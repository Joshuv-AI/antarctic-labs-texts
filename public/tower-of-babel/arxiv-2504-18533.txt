Expository article: “Bounded orthogonal systems and the Λ ⁡ ( p ) \Lambda(p) -set problem” by Jean Bourgain
Hongki Jung, Bartosz Langowski, Alexander Ortiz, Truong Vu

Abstract
. In this paper, we present an exposition of the work [ 3 ] by Jean Bourgain, in which he resolved the well known conjecture posed by Rudin regarding the existence of Λ ⁡ ( p ) \Lambda(p) -sets.



1.
Introduction and statement of the main result

The purpose of this article is to present an exposition of the paper
[
3
]
of Jean Bourgain, where he proves the conjecture posed by Rudin about the existence of
\Lambda(p)
-sets which are not
\Lambda(q)
-sets for any
q<p
.
The methods used in his proof are deeply insightful and have far-reaching implications in analysis and related fields. His argument involves sophisticated probabilistic techniques, metric entropy methods and chaining arguments.

We begin with a short introduction to
\Lambda(p)
-sets.

Definition 1.1
.

For
0<p<\infty
and an integer
d\geq 1
we say that
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a
\Lambda(p)
-set
(equivalently: has the
\Lambda(p)
-property
) if there exists
q<p
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\cdot\theta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\|\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\cdot\theta}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

for all finitely supported multiparameter sequences
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
.

It is not difficult to see, with the aid of Hölder’s inequality, that the
\Lambda(p)
-property from Definition
1.1
depends only on
p
and not on
q
. In particular, if
p>2
, then one can show that (
1.1
) is equivalent to

(1.2)

\displaystyle\|\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\cdot\theta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\cdot\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Note that due to Hölder’s inequality the
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-norm dominates the
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
-norm if
p>2
, so (
1.2
) can be thought of as a strengthening of Bessel’s inequality

\displaystyle\|\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\cdot\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\cdot\theta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

The simplest example of a
\Lambda(p)
-set is the set of dyadic numbers
\{2^{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
which has the
\Lambda(p)
-property for all
0<p<\infty
. More generally, any lacunary sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\subset\mathbb{Z}
, i.e., a sequence satisfying for some
\varepsilon>0
the growth condition

\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_{n}}>1+\varepsilon,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

forms a
\Lambda(p)
-set for any
0<p<\infty
. In
[
10
]
Rudin showed the
\Lambda(p)
-property for
p>1
for Sidon sets, that is subsets
E
of integers for which an estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in E}|\widehat{f}(j)|\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}

holds for any bounded function
f
on the torus.
Every lacunary set is a Sidon set.

Another array of examples of
\Lambda(p)
-sets comes from discrete restriction theory. There has been substantial progress in this area due to the recent development of
\ell^{2}
decoupling by Bourgain and Demeter—see
[
4
]
. In particular, decoupling establishes that the set of integer-lattice points on an elliptic paraboloid
\{(\mathbf{x},|\mathbf{x}|^{2}):\mathbf{x}\in([1,N]\cap\mathbb{Z})^{d-1}\}
is a
\Lambda(p)
-set for
2\leq p\leq\frac{2(d+1)}{d-1}
up to an
N^{\varepsilon}
–loss for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Decoupling for the moment curve, for example as proved by Bourgain, Demeter, and Guth
[
5
]
, allows one to show that the integer-lattice points on the moment curve
\{(n,n^{2},\dotsc,n^{d}):n\in[1,N]\cap\mathbb{Z}\}
form a
\Lambda(p)
-set for
2\leq p\leq d(d+1)
up to
N^{\varepsilon}
–loss.
The ranges of
p
in both these theorems are sharp. It is conjectured that a discrete sphere
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|\mathbf{x}|=N\}
is a
\Lambda(p)
-set for
2\leq p\leq\frac{2d}{d-2}
. The decoupling techniques only yield partial progress on this problem. The difficulty in handling the case of a discrete sphere lies in its arithmetic features, which seem to be beyond the reach of purely analytic methods.

As for negative examples, one can test (
1.1
) with the Dirichlet kernel to see that the set of all integers is not a
\Lambda(p)
-set for any
0<p<\infty
. More involved arguments are needed to show that the set of squares of integers is not a
\Lambda(p)
-set for
p<2
or
p\geq 4
. A famous conjecture of Rudin asserts that squares have the
\Lambda(p)
-property for
p\in(2,4)
.

Sets with the
\Lambda(p)
-property have some interesting structural properties. It was shown by Rudin
[
10
]
that for
p>2
a
\Lambda(p)
-set of size
n
cannot contain an arithmetic progression of length larger than
Cn^{2/p}
; in particular a Sidon set (which, as we already mentioned, belongs to
\bigcap_{p>1}\Lambda(p)
) of size
n
can have at most
C\log n
elements in an arithmetic progression. For more details in that direction and a nice summary of the
\Lambda(p)
-problem and its connections with Rudin’s conjecture we encourage the reader to visit the blog of Ioannis Parissis
[
9
]
.

It is straightforward to see that if
0<p_{1}<p_{2}
then every
\Lambda(p_{2})
-set is also a
\Lambda(p_{1})
-set, which we will write shortly as
\Lambda(p_{2})\subset\Lambda(p_{1})
. That raises a natural question whether this inclusion is necessarily proper. This problem was first posed in the seminal paper of Rudin
[
10
]
.

In the case
p\in(1,2)
the negative answer was provided by Bachelis and Ebenstein in
[
1
]
. More precisely, they showed that for any
S\subset\mathbb{Z}
the set

\{p\in(1,2):S\in\Lambda(p)\}
is an open interval.

The situation turned out to be much more difficult in the case
p>2
. It had been a long standing open problem until Bourgain showed in his celebrated paper
[
3
]
that the inclusion is indeed proper.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
p>2
there exists
S\subset\mathbb{Z}
which is a
\Lambda(p)
-set, but is not a
\Lambda(r)
-set for any
r>p
.

Bourgain proved the above result in the more general setting of
1
-bounded orthogonal systems.
As we will see momentarily, Theorem
1.2
can be derived from the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\Phi=(\varphi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be a sequence of mutually orthogonal real-valued functions on a probability space satisfying a uniform bound
\|\varphi_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
for each
j\in[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
. For any
p>2
there exists a subset of
[n]
such that
|S|\simeq n^{2/p}
and satisfying for any sequence of real coefficients
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
the estimate

(1.3)

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\big(\sum_{i\in S}|a_{i}|^{2}\big)^{1/2},

with the implicit constant depending only on
p
.

Bourgain’s main result in
[
3
]
, Theorem
1.2
, though stated and proved for real-valued functions, applies to the complex exponential system
(e^{2\pi ik\theta})_{k=1}^{n}
by taking real and imaginary parts. See Remark 5.10 of
[
7
]
for additional examples of complex exponential systems in the context of Fourier restriction to which Theorem
1.2
applies.

We also refer the reader to the paper
[
11
]
of Talagrand, where Theorem
1.2
is proved in a more general setting. Unlike Bourgain’s proof, Talagrand’s approach avoids using the special properties of the function
x\mapsto|x|^{p}
appearing in the definition of the
L^{p}
norm. As a result, his argument applies to a broader class of Banach spaces beyond just
L^{p}
spaces.

Remark 1.4
.

The size of
S
in the above theorem is maximal, in the sense that if (
1.3
) holds for all
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
then
|S|\lesssim n^{2/p}
. To see that this is indeed the case, take
S\subset[n]
and consider
f(\theta)=\sum_{k\in S}e^{2\pi ik\theta}
. Letting
D_{n}(\theta)=\sum_{|k|\leq n}e^{2\pi ik\theta}
be the classicial Dirichlet kernel we can use Hölder’s inequality to write

\displaystyle|S|=f(0)=f\ast D_{n}(0)\leq\|f\|_{p}\|D_{n}\|_{p^{\prime}}.

Using the well known estimate
\|D_{n}\|_{p^{\prime}}\lesssim n^{1/p}
and (
1.3
) we get

|S|\lesssim|S|^{1/2}n^{1/p},

which is equivalent to
|S|\lesssim n^{2/p}.

The following result, shows that the proof of Theorem
1.2
reduces to proving Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 1.5
.

Theorem
1.3
implies Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
consider the system
\Phi_{k}=\{e^{2\pi ij\theta}:2^{k}\leq j<2^{k+1}\}
on the 1-torus
\mathbb{T}
. By Theorem
1.3
(applied with
n=2^{k}
) there exists a set
S_{k}\subset[2^{k},2^{k+1})
such that
S_{k}=\lfloor 4^{k/p}\rfloor

and

(1.4)

\displaystyle\|\sum_{j\in S_{k}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\big(\sum_{j\in S_{k}}|a_{j}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

Letting
S=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{k}
we obtain by the Littlewood–Paley inequality

\displaystyle\|\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\bigg\|\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big|\sum_{j\in S_{k}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}.

Next, using Minkowski’s inequality and then invoking the
\Lambda(p)
-property (
1.4
) of each
S_{k}
we get

\displaystyle\bigg\|\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big|\sum_{j\in S_{k}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\bigg\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\big\|\sum_{j\in S_{k}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\big\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\lesssim\big(\sum_{i\in S}|a_{i}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

Thus,
S
is a
\Lambda(p)
-set.

On the other hand, taking

a_{j}=\mathbf{1}_{S_{k}}(j),\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z},

and restricting the region of integration to the range where constructive interference holds, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\sum_{j\in S}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{j\in S_{k}}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\|_{L^{r}(\mathbb{T})}\geq\Big(\int_{-2^{-k}/10}^{2^{-k}/10}\big|\sum_{j\in S_{k}}a_{j}e^{2\pi ij\theta}\big|^{r}\,d\theta\Big)^{1/r}\gtrsim 2^{-k/r}|S_{k}|

\displaystyle=2^{k(1/p-1/r)}|S_{k}|^{1/2}=2^{k(1/p-1/r)}\big(\sum_{i\in S}|a_{i}|^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

Letting
k\rightarrow\infty
we see that
S
is not a
\Lambda(r)
-set for
r>p
.
∎

In the rest of the paper we will present the proof of Theorem
1.3
. For clarity, we will show detailed arguments primarily for the case
2<p\leq 3
. The cases
3<p<4
and
p\geq 4
, which Bourgain treated separately in many arguments, require some technical changes but follow essentially the same ideas. We will outline the approach for these cases and provide detailed explanations only where the arguments significantly differ from the case
2<p\leq 3
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the notation used throughout the article. In Section
3
, we present the proof of Theorem
1.3
, assuming the validity of a key estimate, whose proof is deferred to Section
6
. To prepare for this, we establish a probabilistic inequality in Section
4
and discuss certain entropy estimates in Section
5
.

2.
Notation

In this section we set up our notation that will be used throughout the paper.

For a random variable
X
on a probability space, we will write

\mathbb{E}X=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}X(\omega)=\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)\,d\omega.

For a measure space
(\Sigma,\mu)
, and real-valued functions
f,g\in L^{2}(\Sigma,\mu)
we denote the standard
L^{2}(d\mu)
inner product by

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Sigma}f(u)g(u)\,d\mu(u).

We will frequently use the inequality

(2.1)

A+B\leq 2AB

valid for
A,B\geq 1
.

For vectors
\mathbf{a},\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we will always write
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. Using the same symbol for components of
\mathbf{a}
and
\bm{a}
should not cause any confusion. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we denote
[k]=\{1,2,\dots,k\}.

We denote by
\mathbf{1}_{E}
the indicator function of a set
E
, and use
|E|
to represent either the number of elements in
E
if it is finite, or the Lebesgue measure of
E
if it is a measurable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
E\neq\emptyset
,
\mathbb{E}_{m\in E}f(m)
refers to the average value of a function
f\colon E\to\mathbb{C}
, taken with respect to the appropriate measure.

For two nonnegative quantities
A,B
we write
A\lesssim B
if there
is an absolute constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
A\leq CB
. If we want to emphasize that
C
depends on a parameter
\alpha
, then we write
A\lesssim_{\alpha}B
. We will write
A\simeq B
if
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
.

If
(X,d)
is a bounded metric space and
\delta>0
, then the metric entropy
N_{d}(X,\delta)
with respect to the metric
d
is the minimum number of
d
-balls of radius
\delta
needed to cover
X
.

3.
Setting up the proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we begin discussing the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

3.1.
Overview and beginning of the proof of Theorem
1.3

Let
\Phi=\big(\varphi_{i}(u)\big)_{i=1}^{n}
be a 1-bounded system of real-valued functions defined on a measure space
(\Sigma,\mu)
with total measure
\mu(\Sigma)=1
, orthogonal under the
L^{2}(d\mu)
inner product defined by

\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{\Sigma}f(u)g(u)\,d\mu(u).

In what follows, we will suppress all mention of the space
\Sigma
and the measure
\mu
. We will follow Bourgain’s notational convention and write the integral
\int f(u)\,du
and the
L^{p}
-norm
\|f\|_{L^{p}(du)}
instead of
\int_{\Sigma}f(u)\,d\mu(u)
and
\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Sigma,\mu)}
, respectively.

For
S\subset[n]
define

(3.1)

K_{S}=\sup_{|\mathbf{a}|\leq 1}\|\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{L^{p}(du)},

where we use the notation
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
, and
|\mathbf{a}|
for the Euclidean norm. We suppress
\Sigma
and the measure
\mu
from the notation, since the bounds we obtain will not depend on the particular probability space
(\Sigma,\mu)
we begin from.

To prove Theorem
1.3
we need to show that there exists
S
with
|S|\simeq n^{2/p}
such that
K_{S}\lesssim 1
. This will be accomplished by showing that generic random sets
S
of the desired size satisfy
K_{S}\lesssim 1
, see Theorem
3.9
below. We will set this up appropriately, starting by describing a general decomposition of a function
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}
with
|\mathbf{a}|=1
followed by a discussion of the randomization of
S
.

We will need the following simple numerical lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. If
2<p\leq 3
, then

(3.2)

|x+y|^{p}\leq(x+y)^{2}|y|^{p-2}+(1+|x|)^{p}+2x(1+|x|)^{p-2}y+(1+|x|)^{p-2}y^{2}.

Moreover, if
p>3
, then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

(3.3)

|x+y|^{p}\leq|x+y|^{p-2}|x|^{2}+C(|x|+|y|)^{p-3}|y|^{3}+2x|x|^{p-2}y+(2p-3)|x|^{p-2}y^{2}.

Proof.

To prove (
3.2
) it suffices to write

|x+y|^{p}\leq(x+y)^{2}(|x|^{p-2}+|y|^{p-2})\leq(x+y)^{2}|y|^{p-2}+(x+y)^{2}(1+|x|)^{p-2}.

For (
3.3
) we begin with splitting

|x+y|^{p}=|x+y|^{p-2}x^{2}+|x+y|^{p-2}(2xy+y^{2}).

Now, it remains to note that the second term on the right-hand side above can be bounded with the aid of the inequality

\big||x+y|^{p-2}-|x|^{p-2}-(p-2)|x|^{p-4}xy\big|\lesssim(|x|+|y|)^{p-4}y^{2}.

That completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The first step in the proof of Theorem
1.3
consists of an ingenious decomposition of the generic sequence
(a_{i})
. This allows us to reduce the proof to estimating suitable linearized expressions coming from Lemma
3.1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
S\subset[n]
. For any
p>2
the following estimate holds

\displaystyle K_{S}^{p}\lesssim_{p}

\displaystyle 1+K_{S}^{p-1}+\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}\Big[|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad+|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\Big].

where

\mathcal{A}(S):=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I):I\subset[n],\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in I\cap S},\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i\in S},|\mathbf{a}|,|\mathbf{b}|\leq 1,\max_{i}|b_{i}|\leq|I|^{-1/2}\}.

Remark 3.3
.

Note if
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)
then the sequences
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
are both supported in
S
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We will only present the proof in the case
p\in(2,3]
. The argument for handling the case
p>3
is similar, with the only differences being that (
3.3
) is applied instead of (
3.2
), and condition (
3.5
) below needs to be replaced by

(3.4)

1-\gamma^{2}+C\gamma^{3}<1,

where
C>0
is the constant from (
3.3
).

Choose
0<\gamma<1
satisfying

(3.5)

(1-\gamma^{2})^{(p-2)/2}+\gamma^{p}<1.

This is possible since if
\gamma
is sufficiently small, then by Taylor’s theorem

(1-\gamma^{2})^{(p-2)/2}+\gamma^{p}=1-\frac{p-2}{2}\gamma^{2}+\gamma^{p}+O(\gamma^{4}),

and the expression on the right-hand side is smaller than 1, since
p>2
.
Fix now
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in S}
satisfying
|\mathbf{a}|=1
. By letting
a_{i}=0
for
i\in[n]\setminus S
we may assume that
\mathbf{a}
is a vector with
n
components
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
such that
|\supp\mathbf{a}|\leq|S|
, and moreover, the components of
\mathbf{a}
are arranged in decreasing order of magnitude

|a_{1}|\geq|a_{2}|\geq\dots\geq|a_{|S|}|\geq|a_{|S|+1}|=\dots=|a_{n}|=0.

Define

m_{0}=\begin{cases}\max\{m:\sum_{i=1}^{m}|a_{i}|^{2}<\gamma^{2}\},\quad&\textrm{if}\quad|a_{1}|<\gamma^{2},\\
0,\quad&\textrm{if}\quad|a_{1}|\geq\gamma^{2}.\end{cases}

Since
|\mathbf{a}|=1
it follows that
m_{0}<|S|
. Moreover,

\sum_{i=m_{0}+2}^{|S|}|a_{i}|^{2}\leq 1-\gamma^{2}.

To see this, note that by the maximality of
m_{0}
,

\sum_{i=m_{0}+2}^{|S|}|a_{i}|^{2}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}+1}|a_{i}|^{2}\leq 1-\gamma^{2}.

First assume that
m_{0}\geq 1
. We will comment on handling the case
m_{0}=0
later. Define subsets
I=I_{\mathbf{a}}
and
J=J_{\mathbf{a}}
of
[n]
by

(3.6)

I=\{1,\dots,m_{0}\}\quad\text{and}\quad J=\{m_{0}+2,\dots,n\}.

Then

\min_{i\in I}|a_{i}|\geq\max_{j\in J}|a_{j}|,

and

(3.7)

\sum_{i\in I}|a_{i}|^{2}<\gamma^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j\in J}|a_{j}|^{2}\leq 1-\gamma^{2}.

Furthermore,

1\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m_{0}}|a_{i}|^{2}\geq m_{0}|a_{m_{0}+1}|^{2}\geq m_{0}\max_{j\in J}|a_{j}|^{2},

so that
\max_{i\in J}|a_{i}|\leq|I|^{-1/2}
. In anticipation of the application of Lemma
3.1
, let

x(u)=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u),\quad y(u)=\sum_{j\in J}a_{j}\varphi_{j}(u).

Writing
\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}=x+y+a_{m_{0}+1}\varphi_{m_{0}+1}
, using the
1
-boundedness assumption to estimate
\|a_{m_{0}+1}\varphi_{m_{0}+1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and applying Hölder’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\int(1+|x(u)+y(u)|)^{p}\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int|x(u)+y(u)|^{p}\,du+p\int(1+|x(u)+y(u)|)^{p-1}\,du

\displaystyle\leq\int|x(u)+y(u)|^{p}\,du+p2^{p-1}\big(1+\int|x(u)+y(u)|^{p-1}\,du\big)

\displaystyle\leq\int|x(u)+y(u)|^{p}\,du+p2^{p-1}\big(1+(\int|x(u)+y(u)|^{p}\,du)^{(p-1)/p}\big)

\displaystyle=\|x+y\|_{p}^{p}+C_{p}(1+\|x+y\|_{p}^{p-1})\leq\|x+y\|_{p}^{p}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1}).

Note that in the case
m_{0}=0
one has
x\equiv 0
and the above argument gives

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|y\|_{p}^{p}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1}).

Then, by the definition of
K_{S}
, we have, still in the case
m_{0}=0
,

\displaystyle\|y\|_{p}^{p}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1})

\displaystyle\leq K_{S}^{p}(\sum_{j\in J}|a_{j}|^{2})^{p/2}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1})

(3.8)

\displaystyle\leq K_{S}^{p}(1-\gamma^{2})^{p/2}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1}).

Returning to the case
m_{0}\geq 1
, we use Lemma
3.1
and Hölder’s inequality to get

\displaystyle\|x+y\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|x+y\|_{p}^{2}\|y\|_{p}^{p-2}+\|1+|x|\|_{p}^{p}+2|\langle y,x(1+|x|)^{p-2}\rangle|+|\langle y,y(1+|x|)^{p-2}\rangle|.

By the definition of
K_{S}
, the first term is bounded by
K_{S}^{p}(\sum_{j\in J}|a_{j}|^{2})^{(p-2)/2}
. To estimate the second term, we argue as above getting

\displaystyle\|1+|x|\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|x\|_{p}^{p}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1})\leq K_{S}^{p}(\sum_{i\in I}|a_{i}|^{2})^{p/2}+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1}).

Combining the above estimates with (
3.7
) we obtain for
m_{0}\geq 1

\displaystyle\|\sum_{i\in S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq[(1-\gamma^{2})^{(p-2)/2}+\gamma^{p}]K_{S}^{p}+2|\langle y,x(1+|x|)^{p-2}\rangle|+|\langle y,y(1+|x|)^{p-2}\rangle|

(3.9)

\displaystyle\quad+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1}).

Taking into account (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), and taking the supremum over sequences
|\mathbf{a}|\leq 1
we get

\displaystyle K_{S}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq[(1-\gamma^{2})^{(p-2)/2}+\gamma^{p}]K_{S}^{p}+\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}\Big[2|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad+|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\Big]+C_{p}(1+K_{S}^{p-1}),

where the supremum on the right-hand side is taken over the set

\mathcal{A}(S)=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I):I\subset[n],\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in I\cap S},\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i\in S},|\mathbf{a}|,|\mathbf{b}|\leq 1,\max_{i}|b_{i}|\leq|I|^{-1/2}\}.

Note in particular we do not require that the vectors
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
have disjoint supports, unlike in the decomposition we made using the disjoint sets
I
and
J
. Using the condition
(1-\gamma^{2})^{(p-2)/2}+\gamma^{p}<1
we see that the first term on the right-hand side can be absorbed by the left-hand side, giving

\displaystyle K_{S}^{p}\lesssim_{p}

\displaystyle 1+K_{S}^{p-1}+\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}\Big[|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad+|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\Big].

That concludes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Decoupling

To further decompose the expressions arising from Proposition
3.2
we will need an important probabilistic decoupling lemma. In the proof of the probabilistic decoupling lemma, we will use the following generalization of Khintchine’s inequality, see
[
6
, Theorem 2, Section 10.3]
.

Lemma 3.4

(Marcinkiewicz–Zygmund inequality)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a family of independent random variables with
\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=0
and such that
\mathbb{E}(|X_{i}|^{p})<\infty
. Then

\mathbb{E}\Big(\big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\big|^{p}\Big)\simeq\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}|^{2}\big)^{p/2}\Big),

with an implicit constant that depends only on
p
.

The probabilistic decoupling lemma reads as follows.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider for
\alpha\in[3]
functions
\phi_{\alpha}\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

(3.10)

|\phi_{\alpha}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{p_{\alpha}},

(3.11)

|\phi_{\alpha}(x)-\phi_{\alpha}(y)|\leq C(1+|x|+|y|)^{p_{\alpha}-\delta}|x-y|^{\delta},

where
p_{\alpha}\geq\delta>0
.

Let
\mathbf{u}=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{n},\mathbf{v}=(v_{i})_{i=1}^{n},\mathbf{w}=(w_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\mathbf{u}|,|\mathbf{v}|,|\mathbf{w}|\leq 1
and let
\{\eta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\zeta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables of respective means

\int\eta_{i}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{3}\quad\text{and}\quad\int\zeta_{i}(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{2},\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

Define the disjoint random sets

\displaystyle R^{1}_{t}

\displaystyle=\{1\leq i\leq n:\eta_{i}(t)=1\},

\displaystyle R^{2}_{t}

\displaystyle=\{1\leq i\leq n:\eta_{i}(t)=0,\zeta_{i}(t)=1\},

\displaystyle R^{3}_{t}

\displaystyle=\{1\leq i\leq n:\eta_{i}(t)=0,\zeta_{i}(t)=0\}.

Then

\displaystyle\left|\int\phi_{1}\Big(\sum_{i\in R^{1}_{t}}u_{i}\Big)\phi_{2}\Big(\sum_{i\in R^{2}_{t}}v_{i}\Big)\phi_{3}\Big(\sum_{i\in R^{3}_{t}}w_{i}\Big)\,dt-\phi_{1}\Big(\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\Big)\phi_{2}\Big(\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\Big)\phi_{3}\Big(\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\Big)\right|

(3.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq C\Big(1+\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\Big|+\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\Big|+\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\Big|\Big)^{p-\delta},

where
p=p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}
.

Proof.

Let
U(t)=\sum_{i\in R^{1}_{t}}u_{i}
,
V(t)=\sum_{i\in R^{2}_{t}}v_{i}
,
W(t)=\sum_{i\in R^{3}_{t}}w_{i}
. Then (
3.12
) is equivalent to

\Big|\mathbb{E}\big(\phi_{1}(U)\phi_{2}(V)\phi_{3}(W)\big)-\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\phi_{2}(\mathbb{E}V)\phi_{3}(\mathbb{E}W)\Big|\lesssim(1+|\mathbb{E}U|+|\mathbb{E}V|+|\mathbb{E}W|)^{p-\delta}.

To prove the above estimate we begin by splitting the left-hand side as follows

(3.13)

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\big(\phi_{1}(U)\phi_{2}(V)\phi_{3}(W)\big)-\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\phi_{2}(\mathbb{E}V)\phi_{3}(\mathbb{E}W)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\mathbb{E}[\big(\phi_{1}(U)-\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\big)\phi_{2}(V)\phi_{3}(W)]\Big|+\Big|\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\mathbb{E}[\big(\phi_{2}(V)-\phi_{2}(\mathbb{E}V)\big)\phi_{3}(W)]\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\Big|\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\phi_{2}(\mathbb{E}V)\mathbb{E}\big[\phi_{3}(W)-\phi_{3}(\mathbb{E}W)]\Big|.

Each of the three terms on the right-hand side of (
3.13
) can be treated similarly, so we only provide the details required to estimate the first term. By the smoothness assumption (
3.11
) we have

\displaystyle|\phi_{1}(U)-\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|U|+|\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}-\delta}|U-\mathbb{E}U|^{\delta}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|\mathbb{E}U|+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}-\delta}|U-\mathbb{E}U|^{\delta}

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}-\delta}(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}-\delta}|U-\mathbb{E}U|^{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq(1+|\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}-\delta}(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}}.

In the second-to-last estimate we used (
2.1
).

On the other hand, by the growth condition (
3.10
) applied to the function
\phi_{2}
and (
2.1
) we have

|\phi_{2}(V)|\lesssim(1+|V|)^{p_{2}}\leq(1+|\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}(1+|V-\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}.

Analogously, we get

|\phi_{3}(W)|\lesssim(1+|W|)^{p_{3}}\leq(1+|\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}(1+|W-\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}.

Combining the above bounds we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[\big(\phi_{1}(U)-\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\big)\phi_{2}(V)\phi_{3}(W)]\Big|

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim(1+|\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}-\delta}(1+|\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}(1+|\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}[(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}}(1+|V-\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}(1+|W-\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1+|\mathbb{E}U|+|\mathbb{E}V|+|\mathbb{E}W|)^{p-\delta}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}[(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}}(1+|V-\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}(1+|W-\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}].

It remains to show that

\mathbb{E}[(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}}(1+|V-\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}(1+|W-\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}]\lesssim 1.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|)^{p_{1}}(1+|V-\mathbb{E}V|)^{p_{2}}(1+|W-\mathbb{E}W|)^{p_{3}}]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}[(1+|U-\mathbb{E}U|+|V-\mathbb{E}V|+|W-\mathbb{E}W|)^{p}]

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim_{p}1+\mathbb{E}\Big(|U-\mathbb{E}U|^{p}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(|V-\mathbb{E}V|^{p}\Big)+\mathbb{E}\Big(|W-\mathbb{E}W|^{p}\Big).

Then appealing to Lemma
3.4
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(|U-\mathbb{E}U|^{p}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\eta_{i}^{1}-\frac{1}{3})u_{i}|^{p}\Big)\lesssim\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(\eta_{i}^{1}-\frac{1}{3})u_{i}|^{2}\big)^{p/2}\Big)\leq\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}|^{2}\big)^{p/2}\lesssim 1

and the terms corresponding to
V
and
W
are estimated in the same way. Consequently,

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[\big(\phi_{1}(U)-\phi_{1}(\mathbb{E}U)\big)\phi_{2}(V)\phi_{3}(W)]\Big|\lesssim(1+|\mathbb{E}U|+|\mathbb{E}V|+|\mathbb{E}W|)^{p-\delta}.

The remaining two terms on the right-hand side of (
3.13
) are estimated in the same way. The proof of the lemma is now complete.
∎

In the next proposition we will apply Lemma
3.5
to the linearized expressions arising from Proposition
3.2
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
S\subset[n]
. For any
p>2
the following estimate holds

\displaystyle K_{S}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}1+K_{S}^{p-1}+\int\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}|\langle\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{1}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i},(\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{2}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i})(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{3}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\int\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}|\langle\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{1}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i},(\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{2}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i})(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{3}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\,dt,

where

\mathcal{A}(S):=\{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I):I\subset[n],\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in I\cap S},\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i\in S},|\mathbf{a}|,|\mathbf{b}|\leq 1,\max_{i}|b_{i}|\leq|I|^{-1/2}\}

and
R_{t}^{1},R_{t}^{2}
and
R_{t}^{3}
are the sets defined in Lemma
3.5
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
we have

\displaystyle K_{S}^{p}\lesssim_{p}

\displaystyle 1+K_{S}^{p-1}+\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}\Big[|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad+|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\Big].

We concentrate on estimating the first term within the supremum, as the analysis of the second term follows similarly and can be left to the reader for verification.

The argument relies on applying the probabilistic decoupling Lemma
3.5
pointwise within the integration over

u
. We apply Lemma
3.5
to the functions
\phi_{1}(x)=\phi_{2}(x)=x
, and
\phi_{3}(x)=(1/3+|x|)^{p-2}
with
p_{1}=p_{2}=1
, and
p_{3}=\delta=p-2,

\mathbf{u}=(b_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)\mathbf{1}_{S}(i))_{i=1}^{n}
, and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{w}=(a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)\mathbf{1}_{I\cap S}(i))_{i=1}^{n}
. With
R^{1}_{t},R^{2}_{t},R^{3}_{t}
the random subsets of
[n]
as in Lemma
3.5
, we have a pointwise estimate for any
u

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{\int}\big(\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{1}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)\big)\big(\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{2}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)\big)\big(1/3+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{3}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)|\big)^{p-2}\,dt

\displaystyle\quad-3^{-p}\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)|)^{p-2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim_{p}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)|+|\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)|)^{2}.

Integrating in
u
and using the orthogonality of the system

, we see that

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim_{p}1+\int\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}|\langle\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{1}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i},(\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{2}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i})(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{3}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\,dt.

Similar considerations give

\displaystyle\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}|\langle\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i},\sum_{i\in S}b_{i}\varphi_{i}(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim_{p}1+\int\sup_{(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},I)\in\mathcal{A}(S)}|\langle\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{1}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i},(\sum_{i\in S\cap R^{2}_{t}}b_{i}\varphi_{i})(1+|\sum_{i\in I\cap S\cap R^{3}_{t}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}|)^{p-2}|\rangle|\,dt,

∎

3.3.
Randomization of the set
S

In this subsection we describe the randomization of the set
S
we mentioned earlier. First we need the following elementary lemma, which may be thought of as another variant of probabilistic decoupling.

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose
\{\xi_{i}:i\in[n]\}
are independent and bounded real-valued
random variables defined on some probability space
\Omega
, and
\{A_{l}\}_{l=1}^{k}
are pairwise disjoint subsets of
[n]
. Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\Big|\prod_{l=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i\in A_{l}}a_{i}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big)\Big|=\mathbb{E}_{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}}\sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\Big|\prod_{l=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i\in A_{l}}a_{i}\xi_{i}(\omega_{l})\big)\Big|.

Proof.

We will show the proof in the case
k=2
as the general case follows by induction. For disjoint sets
A_{1}=A
and
A_{2}=B

let
\mathcal{L}
be the law of the random vector
(\xi_{i})_{i\in A\cup B}
, and let
\mathcal{L}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{B}
be
the laws of the random vectors
(\xi_{i})_{i\in A},(\xi_{j})_{j\in B}
, respectively. By the definition of

\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\omega}

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\Big|\big(\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big)\big(\sum_{j\in B}a_{j}\xi_{j}(\omega)\big)\Big|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|A|+|B|}}\sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\Big|\big(\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}x_{i}\big)\big(\sum_{j\in B}a_{j}x_{j}\big)\Big|\mathcal{L}\big(\prod_{i\in A}dx_{i}\prod_{j\in B}dx_{j}\big)

By the independence of
\xi_{i}
, and the disjointness of
A
,
B
, we have the identity

\mathcal{L}\big(\prod_{i\in A}dx_{i}\prod_{j\in B}dx_{j}\big)=\mathcal{L}_{A}\big(\prod_{i\in A}dx_{i}\big)\mathcal{L}_{B}\big(\prod_{j\in B}dx_{j}\big),

which can be written as

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{B}.

Expressing the last integral as an iterated integral and using the definition of
\mathcal{L}
, we get the desired identity.
∎

What we have said up to this point applies to an arbitrary
S\subset[n]
. Now we will specialize and choose a random set
S=S_{\omega}
(note this is an additional independent source of randomness besides the random index sets
R^{1}_{t},R^{2}_{t},R^{3}_{t}
).

We begin with treating the case
p\in(2,4)
, the case
p\geq 4
will require some modification that we will describe later.

3.4.
The case
p\in(2,4)

Let
\{\xi_{i}(\omega):i\in[n]\}
be independent
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables (selectors) on some probability space
\Omega
of mean
\delta=\int_{\Omega}\xi_{i}(\omega)\,d\omega
satisfying

(3.15)

\delta n=n^{2/p}=:n_{0}

and consider the random set

S_{\omega}=\{i\in[n]:\xi_{i}(\omega)=1\},\qquad\omega\in\Omega,

which has expected size
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}|S_{\omega}|=\delta n=n^{2/p}
. Observe that
|S_{\omega}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}(\omega)
, thus by standard large deviation estimates for binomial random variables, as recorded in Proposition
A.2
, we get that
\frac{1}{10}n^{2/p}<|S_{\omega}|<10n^{2/p}
holds with high probability. Denote
K(\omega)=K(S_{\omega})
. We will prove that there exists
C(p)>0
depending only on
p
such that

(3.16)

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}K(\omega)^{p}\leq C(p).

The inequality (
3.16
) implies the following theorem about the
\Lambda(p)
-constant of the random sets
S_{\omega}
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Assume that (
3.16
) holds. Then for each
\varepsilon>0
, there is
N(\varepsilon,p)\in\mathbb{N}
so that for all
n\geq N(\varepsilon,p)
there exists an event
E\subset\Omega
of probability

\mathbb{P}(E)\geq 1-2C(p)\varepsilon^{p}

such that
K(\omega)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and
\frac{1}{10}n^{2/p}<|S_{\omega}|<10n^{2/p}
for all
\omega\in E
.

Proof.

Set
G=\{\omega\in\Omega:\frac{1}{10}n^{2/p}<|S_{\omega}|<10n^{2/p}\}
and
F=\{\omega\in\Omega:K(\omega)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\}
.
By the large deviation estimate of Proposition
A.2
,

\mathbb{P}(G)\geq 1-2e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}}=1-2e^{-\frac{n^{2/p}}{2}}.

This estimate ensures that the random set
S_{\omega}
has the correct cardinality with high probability, provided
n
is sufficiently large.

On the other hand, by Chebyshev’s inequality and (
3.16
) we get

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{p}}\mathbb{P}(F^{{\complement}})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\omega}K(\omega)^{p}\leq C(p).

Then

\mathbb{P}(F)\geq 1-C(p)\varepsilon^{p}.

Finally, set
E:=G\cap F
. Then by the inclusion-exclusion principle and the above estimates we obtain

\mathbb{P}(E)\geq\mathbb{P}(F)+\mathbb{P}(G)-1\geq 1-2e^{-\frac{n^{2/p}}{2}}-C(p)\varepsilon^{p}\geq 1-2C(p)\varepsilon^{p},

provided that
n\geq N(\varepsilon,p)
is sufficiently large so that
2e^{-\frac{n^{2/p}}{2}}\leq C(p)\varepsilon^{p}
.
∎

Theorem
3.9
shows that (
3.16
) in particular implies Theorem
1.3
, because it implies the existence of some
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
such that

.

Remark 3.10
.

K(\omega)\geq 1

In the remaining part of the paper for
\omega\in\Omega
and a sequence

We will prove the following.

Proposition 3.11
.

If

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\sup_{(\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})\in\mathcal{A}_{1}}\bigg|\langle f_{\bm{a},\omega_{1}},f_{\bm{b},\omega_{2}}(1+|f_{\bm{c},\omega_{3}}|)^{p-2}\rangle\bigg|\,d\omega_{1}\,d\omega_{2}\,d\omega_{3}

3.5.
The case
p\geq 4
.

The argument in the case
2<p<4
cannot be applied directly to the case
p\geq 4
as the proof of (
3.20
) as presented in Section
6
, does not work in this case. Bourgain was able to overcome this issue by using an inductive argument.
We assume that Theorem
1.2
holds for some exponent
p_{1}
and prove that it follows for
p
satisfying
p/2<p_{1}<p
. The base for the induction will be Theorem
1.3
with
2<p<4
(this case will be proved independently).

We need to prove that a random subset of
[n]
of size
\simeq n^{2/p}
has the
\Lambda(p)
-property.
To this end consider
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that
n^{2/p}=\delta^{\prime}n^{2/{p_{1}}}
and let
S_{1}
be a random subset of
[n]
of size
n_{1}=|S_{1}|\simeq n^{2/{p_{1}}}
. By the induction hypothesis, with high probability
S_{1}
satisfies the
\Lambda(p_{1})
-property

(3.26)

\|\sum_{i\in S_{1}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{p_{1}}\leq C|\mathbf{a}|,\qquad\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Now let
S\subset S_{1}
be a random subset of
S_{1}
constructed using a collection of selectors of mean
\delta^{\prime}
. Then the expected size of
S
is
|S|\simeq n^{2/p}=\delta^{\prime}n_{1}
and consequently, the proof of Theorem
1.3
reduces to showing that
S
has the
\Lambda(p)
-property. Following the same overall strategy as in the case
2<p<4
, but with the supports of sequences restricted to
S_{1}
, one can in fact show that the generic random subset
S_{\omega}\subset S_{1}
of size
\simeq n^{2/p}
has the
\Lambda(p)
-property.

To make this restriction on the sequence supports to
S_{1}
explicit, we define:

K_{S_{1}}=\sup_{|\mathbf{a}|\leq 1}\|\sum_{i\in S_{1}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{p}.

Now let
S_{\omega}\subset S_{1}
be a random subset of
S_{1}
obtained using independent selectors of mean
\delta^{\prime}
. We define
K_{S_{1}}(\omega)
by restricting the summation in the definition of
K_{S_{1}}
to
S_{\omega}
, specifically:

K_{S_{1}}(\omega)=\sup_{|\mathbf{a}|\leq 1}\|\sum_{i\in S_{\omega}}a_{i}\varphi_{i}\|_{p}.

Assuming that (
3.26
) holds for
S_{1}\subset[n]
of size
n^{2/p_{1}}
with
p_{1}
satisfying
p/2<p_{1}<p
, we will establish in Section
6
the following analog of the key inequality (
3.20
)

(3.27)

\|K^{S_{1}}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega_{1})}\lesssim(\delta^{\prime}m_{3}^{p/p_{1}-1}+\frac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{m_{1}})^{1/2}(1+K_{S_{1}}(\omega_{2})+K_{S_{1}}(\omega_{3}))^{p-\sigma},

with
\delta^{\prime}=n^{2/p-2/p_{1}}
, where

K^{S_{1}}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3}):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset S_{1}\\
|A|\leq m_{1}\end{subarray}}\sup_{\bm{b}\in\Pi^{S_{1}}_{m_{2}}}\sup_{\bm{c}\in\Pi^{S_{1}}_{m_{3}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{1}}}\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega_{1})|\langle\varphi_{i},f_{\bm{b},\omega_{2}}|f_{\bm{c},\omega_{3}}|^{p-2}\rangle|

and

(3.28)

\displaystyle\Pi^{S_{1}}_{m}=\{\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in S_{1}}:|\mathbf{a}|\leq 1,|\supp\mathbf{a}|\leq m\}.

Then, by following the same approach as in the case
p\in(2,4)
, but with definitions adjusted for vectors supported on the fixed set
S_{1}
, one can check that (
3.27
) implies
\|K_{S_{1}}(\omega)\|_{L^{p}(d\omega)}\lesssim 1
. We remark that to obtain the estimate corresponding to (
3.23
) one needs to use the fact that
\delta^{\prime}n_{0}^{p/p_{1}-1}=O(1)
, with
n_{0}=n^{2/p}
.

4.
A probabilistic inequality

In this section we will establish an important probabilistic inequality which will be a key tool in the proof of (
3.20
) and (
3.27
). The inequality controls the
L^{q_{0}}
-norm of an
uncountable
supremum of random variables indexed by points in the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in terms of the entropy of its index set.

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote
|\mathbf{x}|=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2})^{1/2}
. If
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
, denote by
N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)
the metric entropy number with respect to the
\ell^{2}
-distance, i.e., the minimum number of
\ell^{2}
-balls of radius
t
needed to cover
\mathcal{E}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
and
B=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}}|\mathbf{x}|
. Let
0<\delta<1
and
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be a family of independent
\{0,1\}
–valued random variables (= selectors) of mean
\delta=\int\xi_{i}(\omega)\,d\omega
. Then for
m\in[n]
, and
1\leq q_{0}<\infty
, the estimate

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E},|A|\leq m}\Big[\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)x_{i}\Big]\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big[\delta m+\frac{q_{0}}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\Big]^{1/2}B

\displaystyle\quad+(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{B}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)\Big]^{1/2}\,dt,

holds with an implicit absolute constant independent of
n,m,q_{0}
.

We remark that we will apply estimate (
4.1
) with
q_{0}=\log n
, so it is important that the constant
C
does not depend on
q_{0}
.

Remark 4.2
.

To understand the significance of the factor
\big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)\big]^{1/2}
in the expression arising in the estimate of Lemma
4.1
it is useful to consider a simplified model. Assume that
(X_{i})_{i\in[N]}
is a family of random variables such that
\mathbb{E}X_{i}=1
for each
i\in[N]
and satisfying the following concentration of measure inequality

\mathbb{P}(|X_{i}-1|>K)\leq e^{-K^{2}},\qquad K>0.

Suppose that we are interested in estimating the expected value of the supremum of
\{X_{n}:n\in[N]\}
. When
N\gg 1
, a naive bound
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{n\in[N]}X_{n}]\leq N
is overly crude because if it were accurate, it implies that at least one variable deviates from the mean by roughly
N
. However, the probability that any of the
X_{n}
have such a large deviation (via the union bound) is at most
Ne^{-N^{2}}
, which is negligible. Hence, this estimate almost surely overstates the supremum.
Instead, the supremum is governed by the rare events where
X_{n}
deviates significantly above its mean. If K is too large, deviations by K are exceedingly rare, so the supremum cannot realistically scale with K. Conversely, if K is too small, such deviations are common, and we underestimate the supremum.
The balance occurs at
K=\sqrt{\log N}
, where the expected number of deviations by
K
is approximately
1
, that is
Ne^{-K^{2}}\simeq 1
. This is the point where rare large deviations are likely enough (and large enough) to significantly influence the supremum.
Thus, the term
\sqrt{\log N}
represents a balance between complexity (the number or entropy of the collection of random variables) and the concentration of the individual random variables. As
N
increases, the supremum is likely to exhibit larger deviations, but the growth is controlled by
\sqrt{\log N}
.

For a comprehensive discussion of the duality of complexity and concentration, as well as the techniques used to estimate uncountable suprema of random variables, we refer the reader to Talagrand’s book
[
12
]
.

Before we present the proof of the above lemma we will need a simpler probabilistic inequality which essentially captures the large deviations of the individual random variables inside the supremum in Lemma
4.1
. Its proof will require the following Bernstein’s inequality, see
[
2
, Theorem 3]
.

Lemma 4.3

(Bernstein)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{l}
be independent mean-zero random variables. Suppose that
|X_{i}|\leq 1
almost surely, for all
i\in[l]
. Then, for all
u>0
,

(4.2)

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}\geq u\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{l}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]+\frac{1}{3}u}\Big).

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(\xi_{i})
be as in Lemma
4.1
. Then for
q\geq 1

(4.3)

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}\lesssim\delta l+\dfrac{q}{\log(2+q/\delta l)},

with the implicit constant independent of
l,\delta
and
q
.

Proof.

We begin with showing that if
q\leq 2\delta l
, then

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}\lesssim\delta l.

Note that in view of Hölder’s inequality, the above relationship in fact means that for
q\leq 2\delta l
one has

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}\simeq\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{1}(d\omega)}.

Letting
\mathbb{E}f=\int f(\omega)\,d\omega
we write

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}\Big)^{q}=q\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{q-1}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}>\lambda\Big)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq q\int_{0}^{5\delta l}\lambda^{q-1}\,d\lambda+q\int_{5\delta l}^{\infty}\lambda^{q}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}>\lambda\Big)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle=(5\delta l)^{q}+q\int_{5\delta l}^{\infty}\lambda^{q}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}>\lambda\Big)\,d\lambda.

Let

I:=q\int_{5\delta l}^{\infty}\lambda^{q}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}>\lambda\Big)\,d\lambda

be the second term in the last expression above. To estimate it we first rewrite

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}>\lambda\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}>\lambda-\delta l\Big),

where each
X_{i}:=\xi_{i}-\delta
is a mean-zero random variable. By changing the variable
\lambda=\delta l+u
, we get

I=q\int_{4\delta l}^{\infty}(\delta l+u)^{q}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}>u\Big)\,du.

Since
\delta l\leq\frac{1}{4}u
on the region of integration, we can estimate

(4.4)

I\leq q\cdot\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{q}\int_{4\delta l}^{\infty}u^{q}\,\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}>u\Big)\,du.

Now, we apply Bernstein’s inequality (
4.2
) to estimate the tail probability.
We note that the variables
X_{i}
are
\{-\delta,1-\delta\}
-valued, so
|X_{i}|\leq 1
almost surely, for all
i
, and by a quick calculation,

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{i}-\delta)^{2}]=\delta-\delta^{2}\leq\delta.

Plugging
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]\leq\delta
into the right-hand side of Bernstein’s inequality and noting that on the region of integration again,
\delta l\leq\frac{1}{4}u<\frac{1}{3}u
, we get an upper bound of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}>u\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}{\delta l+\frac{1}{3}u}\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\frac{1}{2}u^{2}}{\frac{1}{3}u+\frac{1}{3}u}\Big)=e^{-\frac{3}{4}u}.

Now we plug this estimate of the tail probability back into (
4.4
) to get

(4.5)

I\leq q\cdot\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^{q}\int_{4\delta l}^{\infty}u^{q}e^{-\frac{3}{4}u}\,du.

Next notice that for
u>0
one has
u^{q}\leq(2q)^{q}e^{\frac{1}{2}u}
.
To see that, it suffices to verify that
f(u)=u^{q}e^{-\frac{1}{2}u}
has a global maximum at
u=2q
.
Plugging this inequality into (
4.5
) gives

I\leq q\cdot\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{q}q^{q}\int_{4\delta l}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{4}u}\,du=q\cdot\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{q}q^{q}\big[4e^{-\delta l}\big]\leq 4q\cdot\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{q}q^{q}.

Now we use the assumption
q\leq 2\delta l
to write

I\leq 4q\cdot\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{q}(2\delta l)^{q}=4q\cdot 5^{q}(\delta l)^{q}.

Altogether, we have shown

\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}\leq(5^{q}+4q\cdot 5^{q})(\delta l)^{q}=(1+4q)\cdot(5\delta l)^{q}.

Taking
q^{th}
roots and using
(1+4q)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq 5
for
q\geq 1
, we see that

\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}\leq 5^{2}\,\delta l,

as desired.
It finishes the proof in the case
q\leq 2\delta l
.

It remains to treat the case
q>2\delta l
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
q
is an integer. It suffice to prove that the estimate

(4.6)

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}\leq C^{q}\Big(\frac{q}{\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta l})}\Big)^{q},

holds with some constant
C>0
independent of
q,l
and
\delta
. We remark that in what follows we will let
C
vary from line to line. In particular,
C^{q}
will absorb all multiplicative factors of polynomial growth
q^{K}
for any
K>0
.

Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}
can be viewed as the random variable counting the number of successes in a series of Bernoulli trials of length
l
with the probability of success
\delta
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle=\int\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\Big]^{q}d\omega=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\binom{l}{k}\delta^{k}(1-\delta)^{l-k}k^{q}.

Using the fact that
\binom{l}{k}\leq(\frac{l}{k})^{k}e^{k}
and bounding
1-\delta
trivially by
1
we get

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}\leq C^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\Big(\frac{\delta l}{k}\Big)^{k}k^{q}=C^{q}\sum_{k=1}^{l}F(k),

where
F(x)=(\frac{\delta l}{x})^{x}x^{q}
. In view of Lemma
A.1
from the Appendix (applied with
\kappa=\delta l<q
), the function
F
changes its monotonicity only once on
(1,\infty)
. Thus, we can dominate the sum in
k
by the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}F(x)\,dx
(by two copies of it, strictly speaking). Further, changing the variable
q\alpha=x
and absorbing the Jacobian
q
into
C^{q}
we get

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq C^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(x)\,dx=C^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\delta l}{x}\Big)^{x}x^{q}\,dx=C^{q}q^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\delta l}{\alpha q}\Big)^{\alpha q}\alpha^{q}\,d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq C^{q}q^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{\delta l}{q}\Big)^{\alpha q}\alpha^{q}\,d\alpha=C^{q}q^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha q\log\frac{q}{\delta l}}\alpha^{q}\,d\alpha

where the second inequality uses the bound
(\frac{1}{\alpha})^{\alpha}\leq C
, valid for any
\alpha>0
. By changing the variable
u=\alpha q\log\frac{q}{\delta l}
, and absorbing the Jacobian factor into
C^{q}
again, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle=C^{q}q^{q}\Big(\frac{1}{q\log\frac{q}{\delta l}}\Big)^{q}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u}u^{q}\,du=C^{q}q^{q}\Big(\frac{1}{q\log\frac{q}{\delta l}}\Big)^{q}\Gamma(q-1).

In view of the known asymptotics for the Gamma function

\Gamma(n)=n^{n-1/2}e^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi}\big(1+O(1/n)\big),\qquad n\rightarrow\infty,

we can finally estimate

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}^{q}

\displaystyle\leq C^{q}q^{q}\Big(\frac{1}{q\log\frac{q}{\delta l}}\Big)^{q}q^{-1/2}\frac{q^{q}}{e^{q}}\leq C^{q}\Big(\frac{q}{\log(\frac{q}{\delta l})}\Big)^{q},

which gives (
4.6
), and concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Definition 4.5
.

A
\delta
-net
of a metric space
(X,d)
is any subset
X^{\prime}\subset X
such that for each
x\in X
, there is
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
so that
d(x,x^{\prime})<\delta
. A nonempty
\delta
-net is
minimal
if any proper subset is not a
\delta
-net.

We will need the following result connecting
\delta
-nets to the metric entropy.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
(X,d)
is a bounded metric space, the cardinality of a minimal
\delta
-net is equal to the metric entropy number
N_{d}(X,\delta)
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Without a loss of generality, suppose that
B=2^{-k_{0}}
for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
. For each
k\geq k_{0}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{k}
be a minimal
2^{-k}
-net for
\mathcal{E}
. By the definition of a
net, given
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}
, and
k\geq k_{0}
, we can find a point
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
such that
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)}|<2^{-k}
, so the points
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}
then converge rapidly to
\mathbf{x}
as
k\to\infty
. Setting
\mathbf{x}^{(k_{0}-1)}=0
and using a telescoping sum, we can write

\mathbf{x}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{x}^{(k)}=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}).

We normalize things to scale
\simeq 1
by defining, for each
k
, a new collection

\mathcal{F}_{k}=\{2^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}):(\mathbf{x}^{(k)},\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)})\in\mathcal{E}_{k}\times\mathcal{E}_{k-1},2^{k}|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}|<4\},

so each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}
has a representation as a sum

\mathbf{x}=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}\mathbf{y}^{(k)},

where
\mathbf{y}^{(k)}
are taken from
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. To see that
2^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)})
in the representation of an
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}
indeed belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, we use the triangle inequality

2^{k}|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}|\leq 2^{k}(|\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)}-\mathbf{x}|)\leq 2^{k}(2^{-k}+2^{-k+1})<4,

so that
2^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k-1)})\in\mathcal{F}_{k}\subset B(0,4)
. Note that to justify the above estimate in the boundary case
k=k_{0}
one needs to use the assumption
\mathbf{x}^{(k_{0}-1)}=0
together with the condition
|\mathbf{x}|\leq B=2^{-k_{0}}
.

As the nets
\mathcal{E}_{k}
are minimal, we can use Proposition
4.6
to get the estimate

|\mathcal{F}_{k}|\leq|\mathcal{E}_{k}|\cdot|\mathcal{E}_{k-1}|\leq|\mathcal{E}_{k}|^{2}=N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-k})^{2}

which implies

(4.7)

\displaystyle\log|\mathcal{F}_{k}|\leq 2\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-k}).

By taking the entropy information of
\mathcal{E}
into account this way, we can replace the uncountable supremum on the left-hand side of the equation (
4.1
) by a more manageable expression involving only suprema over finite sets. To do this, we write, for any fixed
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}
and
|A|\leq m
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}x_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}y_{i}^{(k)}\leq\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}|y_{i}^{(k)}|=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}|y_{i}^{(k)}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}\sup_{y\in\mathcal{F}_{k},|A|\leq m}\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}|y_{i}|,

with the inequality in the second line holding because the random variables
\xi_{i}
are nonnegative. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E},|A|\leq m}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)x_{i}\Big)\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}\leq\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{k},|A|\leq m}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)|y_{i}|\Big)\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}.

It now suffices to show for any finite set
\mathcal{F}\subset B(0,4)\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F},|A|\leq m}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big)\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}\leq C\sqrt{\delta m}+C\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1/2}\Big[q_{0}+\log|\mathcal{F}|\Big]^{1/2}.

Indeed, assuming momentarily that (
4.8
) holds, we can apply (
4.7
), sum over
k\geq k_{0}
, and use the inequality
\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}\leq 2^{1/2}\sqrt{a+b}
, valid for
a,b>0
to get

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F},|A|\leq m}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big)\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\delta m+\frac{q_{0}}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\Big]^{1/2}B

\displaystyle\quad+C\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1/2}\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-k})\Big]^{1/2}.

To obtain (
4.1
) we need to replace a sum in
k
by an integral. To this end notice that the function
t\mapsto[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)]^{1/2}
is nonincreasing, so for any
s\in(k,k+1)
we have

2^{-k}[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-k})]^{1/2}\leq 2^{-s+1}[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-s})]^{1/2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-k}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-k})\Big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{k}^{k+1}2^{-s}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-s})\Big]^{1/2}\,ds

\displaystyle=2\int_{k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-s}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-s})\Big]^{1/2}\,ds.

We change variables
t=2^{-s}
to transform the last integration into

\displaystyle\int_{k_{0}}^{\infty}2^{-s}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},2^{-s})\Big]^{1/2}\,ds=\frac{1}{\log 2}\int_{0}^{B}\Big[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)\Big]^{1/2}\,dt

which is the desired form.

It remains to show (
4.8
). Let us define
\rho_{1}=\delta^{1/2}m^{-1/2}
and
\rho_{2}=(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{1/2}q^{-1/2}
with
q=q_{0}+\log|\mathcal{F}|
. For
|A|\leq m
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}y_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y_{i}\geq\rho_{2}}y_{i}+\sum_{i\in A,y_{i}\leq\rho_{1}}y_{i}+\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}y_{i}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{y_{i}\geq\rho_{2}}y_{i}+m\rho_{1}+\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}y_{i}.

Noting that
|\mathbf{y}|\lesssim 1
, we use Cauchy–Schwarz and Chebyshev’s inequalities to estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{y_{i}\geq\rho_{2}}y_{i}\leq|\mathbf{y}|\cdot|\{i:y_{i}\geq\rho_{2}\}|^{1/2}\lesssim\rho_{2}^{-1}.

Combining this with (
4.9
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}y_{i}\lesssim\rho_{2}^{-1}+m\rho_{1}+\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}y_{i}.

Following the argument by Mockenhaupt and Schlag, see
[
8
]
, we write that

\displaystyle\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F},|A|\leq m}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big)\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{2}^{-1}+m\rho_{1}+\left\|\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{2}^{-1}+m\rho_{1}+\left\|\Big(\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F}}\Big[\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big]^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\right\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{2}^{-1}+m\rho_{1}+\Big(\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F}}\Big\|\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big\|^{q}_{L^{q}(d\omega)}\Big)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq\rho_{2}^{-1}+m\rho_{1}+|\mathcal{F}|^{\frac{1}{q}}\cdot\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\right\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\lesssim\rho_{2}^{-1}+m\rho_{1}+\sup_{|\mathbf{y}|\leq 4}\left\|\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\right\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}.

We used the embedding of
\ell^{q}(\mathcal{F})
into
\ell^{\infty}(\mathcal{F})
to show the first inequality above; in the second and the third we used Hölder’s
(q>q_{0})
and triangle inequalities, and finally in the last inequality we used the condition
q=q_{0}+\log|\mathcal{F}|
so that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}|^{\frac{1}{q}}=\exp(q^{-1}\log|\mathcal{F}|)\leq e.

Note the technique of exchanging the supremum over
\mathcal{F}
from inside the
L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)
-norm to the outside of the the
L^{q_{0}+\log|\mathcal{F}|}(d\omega)
-norm.

We now aim at showing

(4.11)

\sup_{|\mathbf{y}|\leq 4}\Big\|\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}\lesssim\delta\rho_{1}^{-1}+q\rho_{2}(\log 1/\delta)^{-1}.

To this end notice that since
|\mathbf{y}|\lesssim 1
, the level set
\{i:y_{i}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}}\}
has cardinality
|\{i:y_{i}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}}\}|\lesssim l
. Using this fact and Lemma
4.4
, we estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{|\mathbf{y}|\leq 4}\Big\|\sum_{\rho_{1}<y_{i}<\rho_{2}}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\Big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\ \text{dyadic}\\
\rho_{2}^{-2}<l<\rho_{1}^{-2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}}\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{l}\xi_{i}(\omega)\Big\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\rho_{1}^{-1}+q\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\ \text{dyadic}\\
l>\rho_{2}^{-2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta l})},

so to prove (
4.11
) it suffices to show

(4.12)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\ \text{dyadic}\\
l>\rho_{2}^{-2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta l})}\lesssim\rho_{2}(\log 1/\delta)^{-1}.

We change the variable
k:=\log_{2}l
in the sum on the left-hand side and then, arguing in a similar way as earlier in the proof, we can estimate the sum by the integral as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\ \text{dyadic}\\
l>\rho_{2}^{-2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{l}\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta l})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>\log_{2}\rho_{2}^{-2}}\frac{1}{(2^{k})^{1/2}\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta 2^{k}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\log_{2}\rho_{2}^{-2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{s/2}\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta 2^{s}})}\,ds.

Next, we change the variable of integration
t:=\rho_{2}^{2}2^{s}
and use the definition of
\rho_{2}
to further estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\log_{2}\rho_{2}^{-2}}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{2^{s/2}\log(2+\frac{q}{\delta 2^{s}})}

\displaystyle\simeq\rho_{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}\log(2+\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\delta t})}.

Therefore, the proof of (
4.12
) reduces to showing the bound

(4.13)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}\log(2+\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\delta t})}\lesssim(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{-1}.

We begin with splitting the region of integration
\int_{1}^{\infty}=\int_{1}^{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}+\int_{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}^{\infty}
. The local part can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}\log(2+\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\delta t})}

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{1}^{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}\log(\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\delta t})}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}(\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\log{\frac{1}{\delta}}-\log t)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}

\displaystyle\simeq(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{-1},

where we used the fact that
(\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\log{\frac{1}{\delta}}-\log t)\simeq\log\frac{1}{\delta}
for
t\in\big(1,(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}\big)
.

For the global part we write

\displaystyle\int_{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}\log(2+\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\delta t})}\lesssim\int_{(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{3/2}}\simeq(\log\frac{1}{\delta})^{-1},

which shows (
4.13
) and consequently also (
4.12
) and (
4.11
).

Combining (
4.10
) and (
4.11
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\|\sup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{F},|A|\leq m}\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega)y_{i}\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim m\rho_{1}+\delta\rho_{1}^{-1}+q\rho_{2}(\log 1/\delta)^{-1}+\rho_{2}^{-1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\delta m}+(\log 1/\delta)^{-1/2}(q_{0}+\log|\mathcal{F}|)^{1/2},

completing the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

We wish to underline and distill a simple version of one of the techniques used in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, since it appears so many times at various points of Bourgain’s overall argument.

Proposition 4.7

(Exchanging the supremum)
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
and
\{X_{j}(\omega):j\in[n]\}
is a collection of nonnegative random variables. Then for each
q\geq 1
,

\int\sup_{j\in[n]}X_{j}(\omega)\,d\omega\leq n^{1/q}\cdot\sup_{j\in\mathcal{[}n]}(\int X_{j}(\omega)^{q}\,d\omega)^{1/q}.

In particular, if
q\geq C^{-1}\log n
, then
\|\sup_{j\in\mathcal{[}n]}X_{j}(\omega)\|_{L^{1}(d\omega)}\leq e^{C}\sup_{j\in\mathcal{[}n]}\|X_{j}(\omega)\|_{L^{q}(d\omega)}
.

5.
Entropy estimates

Denote by
|\cdot|
the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\|\cdot\|
be some other norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
. We will denote by
B^{n}
the closed Euclidean unit ball and by
B_{X}
the closed unit ball in norm
\|\cdot\|
.

For a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
the following quantities will be used to measure its entropy.

(1)

E(U,B_{X},t)=\inf\left\{N\geq 1:\exists\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},1\leq j\leq N,U\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X})\right\}
. Note that here the centers of the balls are not necessarily in
U
.

(2)

\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},t)=\inf\left\{N\geq 1:\exists\mathbf{x}_{j}\in U,1\leq j\leq N,U\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}(\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X})\right\}
. Unlike in the definition of
E(U,B_{X},t)
, the centers of the balls are required to lie in
U
.

(3)

D(U,B_{X},t)=\sup\left\{M\geq 1:\exists\mathbf{y}_{j}\in U,1\leq j\leq M,\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|>t,j\neq k\right\}
.

Remark 5.1
.

The number
E(U,B_{X},t)
agrees with the metric entropy number
N_{d}(U,t)
we defined in Section
2
, where the metric
d
is induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|
of
X
.

The following result shows that the above quantities are comparable, which will allow us to conveniently choose whichever fits our application the best.

Proposition 5.2
.

For any
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0
the following relations hold.

(a)

D(U,B_{X},t)\geq\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},t)\geq E(U,B_{X},t)\geq D(U,B_{X},2t)
,

(b)

E(U,B_{X},t)\geq\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},2t)
.

Proof.

Let
M=D(U,B_{X},t)
. Then there exists a sequence
\{\mathbf{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}\subset U
such that
\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|>t
for
j\neq k
and the collection of balls
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}+tB_{X}\}_{j=1}^{M}
covers
U
, by maximality of
M
. Since
\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},t)
is the infimum over all such collections, it follows that
D(U,B_{X},t)\geq\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},t)
.

The inequality
\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},t)\geq E(U,B_{X},t)
is immediate from the definitions.

To show that
E(U,B_{X},t)\geq D(U,B_{X},2t)
take any cover
U\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\{\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X}\}
and note that if
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{M}
are such that
\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|>2t
for
j\neq k
, then each
\mathbf{y}_{k}
lies in at most one ball from
\{\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X}\}_{j=1}^{N}
. Therefore
N\geq M
and taking the infimum over
N\geq 1
gives the postulated bound.

Finally, to show part (b) consider a cover
U\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\{\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X}\}
with
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and assume that for each
j
there exists
\mathbf{u}_{j}\in U\cap\{\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X}\}
(if such
\mathbf{u}_{j}
does not exist it means that a ball
\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X}
is redundant and can be removed from our cover). Then by the triangle inequality,
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}+2tB_{X}\}\supset\{\mathbf{x}_{j}+tB_{X}\}
for all
j\in[N]
and consequently
E(U,B_{X},t)\geq\widetilde{E}(U,B_{X},2t)
.
∎

Our next goal will be to obtain suitable estimates for the entropy number
E(B^{n},B_{X},t)
. We begin with a simple observation based on volume counting.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a unit ball
B_{X}
. Then

D(B_{X},B_{X},t)\leq(4/t)^{n},\qquad 0<t<1.

Proof.

Let
M=D(B_{X},B_{X},t)
. There exist
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset B_{X}
such that
\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|>t
for
j\neq k
. Then the balls
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}tB_{X}\}_{j=1}^{M}
are pairwise disjoint. Moreover,
\mathbf{y}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}tB_{X}\subset 2B_{X}
for each
j
, since
t<1
. Combining these two observations we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big|\frac{1}{2}tB_{X}\big|=\big|\sum_{j=1}^{M}(\mathbf{y}_{j}+\frac{1}{2}tB_{X})\big|\leq|2B_{X}|,

where
|\cdot|
stands for the Lebesgue measure.

Thus,

M\Big(\frac{1}{2}t\Big)^{n}|B_{X}|\leq 2^{n}|B_{X}|,

and finally

M\leq(4/t)^{n}.

∎

The upper bound for the entropy will be expressed in terms of a quantity called a Lévy mean.

Definition 5.4
.

The
Lévy mean
of the normed space
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
is given by

M_{X}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\|\mathbf{u}\|\,d\sigma(\mathbf{u}),

where
d\sigma
denotes the normalized surface measure on the Euclidean unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

We will estimate
E(B^{n},B_{X},t)
by an expression involving
M_{X}
. We remark that any two norms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are comparable, but only up to multiplicative constants which may depend on
n
(for example one has
\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\cdot\|_{\ell^{2}}
). That dependence is critical for our developments since we will consider large values of
n
.

We begin with a result collecting several representations of the Lévy mean.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. standard normal random variables, let
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the standard orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|)
for some norm
\|\cdot\|
. Then the following identities hold:

M_{X}=\alpha_{n}(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|\mathbf{x}|^{2}/2}\|\mathbf{x}\|\,d\mathbf{x}=\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\omega)\mathbf{e}_{i}\Big\|\,d\omega,

where

\alpha_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n}{2})}{\sqrt{2}\Gamma(\frac{n+1}{2})}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

Let
d\widetilde{\sigma}
denote the (non-normalized) surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Using polar coordinates one gets

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|\mathbf{x}|^{2}/2}\|\mathbf{x}\|\,d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}/2}r^{n}\,dr\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\|\mathbf{u}\|\,d\widetilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=d\widetilde{\sigma}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}/2}r^{n}\,dr\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\|\mathbf{u}\|\,d\sigma(\mathbf{u}).

Thus, the first identity in the statement of the proposition will follow if we show

d\widetilde{\sigma}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}/2}r^{n}\,dr=\frac{(2\pi)^{n/2}}{\alpha_{n}}.

To verify the above it suffices to combine

d\widetilde{\sigma}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})=\frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}

with

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}/2}r^{n}\,dr=2^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\Gamma\big(\frac{n+1}{2}\big).

The latter requires a simple change of variables. We omit the details.

To complete the proof it remains to show

\int_{\Omega}\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\omega)\mathbf{e}_{i}\big\|\,d\omega=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|\mathbf{x}|^{2}/2}\|\mathbf{x}\|\,d\mathbf{x}.

This however follows immediately from the fact that due to the independence of the random variables
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the density of the random vector
(g_{1}(\omega),\dots,g_{n}(\omega))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(\omega)\mathbf{e}_{i}
is simply a product of 1-dimensional densities of the form
(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x_{j}^{2}/2}
, for
j\in[n]
.
∎

Now we are ready to show the following important estimate for the metric entropy of the Euclidean unit ball
B^{n}
in the normed space
X
in terms of the Lévy mean
M_{X}
.

Proposition 5.6
.

For any
t>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\log E(B^{n},B_{X},t)\lesssim n\big(\frac{M_{X}}{t}\big)^{2},

with the implicit constant independent of
t
and
n
.

Proof.

Let
N=D(B^{n},B_{X},t)
. Then there exists a maximal sequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset B^{n}
, such that
\|\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\|>t
for
j\neq k
. By Proposition
5.2
it suffices to prove that

\log N\lesssim n\big(\frac{M_{X}}{t}\big)^{2}.

To this end we will construct a probability space and a collection of
N
disjoint balls inside it, each of them with a relatively large measure.

Consider a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

d\mu(\mathbf{x})=(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|\mathbf{x}|^{2}/2}\,d\mathbf{x}.

By the first identity in Proposition
5.5
we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{-1}M_{X}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\mathbf{x}\|\,d\mu(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\|\mathbf{x}\|).

Now let
R:=2\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\|\mathbf{x}\|)
and notice that as a consequence of Chebyshev’s inequality we have

(5.1)

\mu(RB_{X})=\mu(\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq R)\geq 1/2.

Clearly the balls
\{\mathbf{x}^{(j)}+\frac{1}{2}tB_{X}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are mutually disjoint. We will rescale them to get balls of radius
R
. To this end let

\mathbf{y}^{(j)}=2Rt^{-1}\mathbf{x}^{(j)}
and note that the balls
\{\mathbf{y}^{(j)}+RB_{X}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are also mutually disjoint. Using the symmetry property
B_{X}=-B_{X}
, and later the convexity of the function
e^{-u}
and (
5.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}+RB_{X})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{RB_{X}}e^{-|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2}/2}\,d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{RB_{X}}\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2}/2}+e^{-|\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2}/2}\right)\,d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\geq(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{RB_{X}}e^{-(|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2}+|\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2})/4}\,d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{RB_{X}}e^{-(|\mathbf{y}|^{2}+|\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2})/2}\,d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\mu(RB_{X})e^{-|\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2}/2}\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-|\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|^{2}/2}.

Now, since
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in B^{n}
, we have
|\mathbf{y}^{(i)}|\leq 2Rt^{-1}
, so the estimate derived above gives

\mu(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}+RB_{X})\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-(2Rt^{-1})^{2}/2}.

Hence, due to the disjointness of balls
\{\mathbf{y}^{(j)}+RB_{X}\}_{j=1}^{N}
we get

\displaystyle 1=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}+RB_{X})\geq\frac{N}{2}e^{-(2Rt^{-1})^{2}/2},

which is equivalent to

N<2e^{(2Rt^{-1})^{2}/2}.

Applying
\log
to both sides, using the definition of
R
and the fact that
\alpha_{n}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
we get

\displaystyle\log N\lesssim(Rt^{-1})^{2}\simeq M_{X}^{2}\alpha_{n}^{-2}t^{-2}\simeq n\left(\frac{M_{X}}{t}\right)^{2},

and the proof is finished.
∎

In what follows we will identify
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the
\mathbb{R}
-linear span of
n
mutually orthogonal and
1
-bounded functions
\varphi_{1}(u),\dots,\varphi_{n}(u)
in the
L^{2}
inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int f(u)g(u)\,du

(5.2)

\mathbb{R}^{n}\cong\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\varphi_{j}(u):a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}.

Here as before, we follow Bourgain’s convention of suppressing the domain of the functions
\varphi_{j}
as well as the particular probability measure from the notation. (See the discussion at the beginning of Section
3
for more about Bourgain’s notational convention.)
Note that
\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\varphi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(du)}\leq|(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})|
and for each
j
, the standard basis vector
\mathbf{e}_{j}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
corresponds to
\varphi_{j}
.
With this identification, for a fixed
q\geq 2
we will consider
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{L^{q}(du)})
. In analogy with the case where
\varphi_{j}(\theta)=e^{2\pi ij\theta}
on the circle, we will sometimes refer to the indices
j
as
frequencies
.

Remark 5.7
.

In the rest of the paper, we will assume that the functions
\varphi_{j}
are mutually orthogonal and
1
-bounded. For concreteness, the reader may like to take the functions
(\sin(2\pi j\theta))_{j=1}^{n}
on
(\mathbb{T},d\theta)
.

The following lemma shows that the Lévy mean for the space
X
is controlled by
\sqrt{q}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

For
q\geq 2
let
X=(\mathbb{R}^{n},\|\cdot\|_{L^{q}(du)})
. Then the following estimate holds

M_{X}\lesssim_{q}1,

with the implicit constant independent of
n
.

Remark 5.9
.

The proof below shows in fact that

M_{X}\lesssim\sqrt{q},

with the implicit constant independent of
n
and
q
. However, the exact dependence on
q
will not be relevant for our application.

Proof.

Denote by
\{r_{j}(t)\}_{j=1}^{n}
a system of Rademacher functions, that represents independent random choices of sign. Note that if
\{g_{j}(\omega)\}_{j=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. standard normal variables, then for each fixed
t\in(0,1)
, the random variables
\{r_{j}(t)g_{j}(\omega)\}_{j=1}^{n}
are also i.i.d. standard normal variables.

Using Proposition
5.5
together with the above observation we get for any
t\in(0,1)

\displaystyle M_{X}=\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}g_{j}(\omega)\varphi_{j}(u)\Big\|_{L^{q}(du)}\,d\omega=\alpha_{n}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int_{\Omega}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}(t)g_{j}(\omega)\varphi_{j}(u)\Big\|_{L^{q}(du)}\,d\omega.

Next using Fubini–Tonelli’s theorem and later Hölder’s inequality we can write

\displaystyle M_{X}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}(t)g_{j}(\omega)\varphi_{j}(u)\Big\|_{L^{q}(du)}\,d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}(t)g_{j}(\omega)\varphi_{j}(u)\Big\|^{q}_{L^{q}(du)}\Big)^{1/q}\,d\omega

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\int\int_{0}^{1}|\sum_{j=1}^{n}r_{j}(t)g_{j}(\omega)\varphi_{j}(u)|^{q}\,dt\,du\Big)^{1/q}\,d\omega.

To estimate the latter expression we apply Khintchine’s inequality (to the integral in
dt
), noting that the constant in this inequality is of size
\sqrt{q}
, and then use 1-boundedness of the functions
\varphi_{j}
to get

\displaystyle M_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\int\big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|g_{j}(\omega)\varphi_{j}(u)|^{2}\big)^{q/2}\,du\Big)^{1/q}\,d\omega

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha_{n}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|g_{j}(\omega)|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\,d\omega.

Finally, we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the integral over
\omega
and later use the fact that
\int_{\Omega}g_{j}^{2}(\omega)\,d\omega=1
getting

\displaystyle M_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{q}\alpha_{n}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|g_{j}(\omega)|^{2}\,d\omega\Big)^{1/2}=\alpha_{n}\sqrt{n}\simeq 1,

where the last relation follows from the estimate
\alpha_{n}\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
∎

Combining Proposition
5.6
with Lemma
5.8
we get the following result.

Corollary 5.10
.

For any
t>0
and
n,q\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(5.3)

\log E(B^{n},B_{L^{q}(du)},t)\lesssim_{q}nt^{-2}.

The above estimate will be a key tool in estimating the entropy in this paper. However, as we will soon see, in order to apply it efficiently, we will have to set the stage suitably by “reducing” the dimension of the ball
B^{n}
.

The following is the set whose entropy will be the object of our study in the rest of this section.

Definition 5.11
.

For
m\in[n]
let

\mathcal{P}_{m}=\Big\{\sum_{j\in A}a_{j}\varphi_{j}(u):a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\quad|(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})|\leq 1,\quad|A|=m\Big\}.

Observe that with the identification (
5.2
) we can view
\mathcal{P}_{m}
as the collection of all points lying at the intersection of the Euclidean unit ball
B^{n}
with some
m
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by coordinate vectors. We emphasize that
\mathcal{P}_{m}\ncong\mathbb{R}^{m}
; the set
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is much larger. In fact we have

(5.4)

\mathcal{P}_{m}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset[n]\\
|A|=m\end{subarray}}\mathcal{P}_{A},

where

\mathcal{P}_{A}=\Big\{\sum_{j\in A}a_{j}\varphi_{j}(u):a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\ |(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})|\leq 1\Big\}.

The key result of this section is the estimate of

N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t):=E(\mathcal{P}_{m},B_{L^{q}(du)},t).

Theorem 5.12
.

For any
m\leq n
and
2\leq q\leq\infty
the following bounds hold

(5.5)

\displaystyle\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)

\displaystyle\lesssim mt^{-\nu}\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big),\qquad t\geq 8,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)

\displaystyle\lesssim m\log(1+\frac{1}{t})\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big),\qquad 0<t<8,

with the implicit constant depending only on
q
and
\nu=\nu(q)>2
.

Note that for a single set
A\subset[n]
of cardinality
m
we have
\mathcal{P}_{A}\cong B^{m}
and consequently, the application of Corollary
5.10
gives

\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t):=\log E(\mathcal{P}_{A},B_{L^{q}(du)},t)=\log E(B^{m},B_{L^{q}(du)},t)\lesssim_{q}mt^{-2}.

On the other hand, we clearly have

\mathcal{P}_{m}\subset\Big\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\varphi_{j}(u):a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},|(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})|\leq 1\Big\}\cong B^{n},

so we can use Corollary
5.10
again to get

\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)\leq\log E(B^{n},B_{L^{q}(du)},t)\lesssim_{q}nt^{-2}.

This bound, however, is far from optimal when
n
is large. Theorem
5.12
shows that one can improve it substantially.

Furthermore, notice that for
t\simeq 1
the right-hand sides of (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) are comparable. Therefore, there is nothing special about choosing
8
to separate the estimates for small and large values of
t
; we can replace the range
t\geq 8
by
t\geq a
for any
a>0
, which will only affect a multiplicative constant.

Finally, we remark that, as we will soon see, the estimate (
5.6
) can be obtained from (
5.5
) via a standard covering argument and simple volume counting, so the whole difficulty lies in handling large values of
t
.

We will obtain Theorem
5.12
as a corollary of the following result.

Theorem 5.13
.

For any
m\in[n]
and
2\leq r\leq\infty
the following bound holds

(5.7)

\log N_{r}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)\lesssim mt^{-2}(\log t)\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big),\qquad t>2,

with the implicit constant depending only on
r
.

Proposition 5.14
.

Theorem
5.13
implies Theorem
5.12
.

Proof.

First we show that if (
5.7
) holds for all
r\geq 2
and all
t>2
, then for any
q\geq 2
and
t\geq 8
the estimate (
5.5
) holds with some
\nu>2
.

To this end fix
q\geq 2
and choose any
r>q
. Let
\theta\in(0,1)
be such that

\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1-\theta}{2}+\frac{\theta}{r}.

For any
f,g\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
we have by Hölder’s inequality

\|f-g\|_{q}\leq\|f-g\|_{2}^{1-\theta}\|f-g\|_{r}^{\theta}\leq 2\|f-g\|_{r}^{\theta},

where the last estimate holds since
\|f-g\|_{2}\leq\|f\|_{2}+\|g\|_{2}\leq 2
. Therefore, if
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are such that
\|f_{j}-f_{k}\|_{q}\geq t
for
j\neq k
, then also
\|f_{j}-f_{k}\|_{r}\geq(\frac{t}{2})^{1/\theta}
for
j\neq k
. Thus, in view of Proposition
5.2
, we get for
t\geq 8

\displaystyle\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)\leq\log D(\mathcal{P}_{m},B_{L^{q}(du)},t)\leq\log D(\mathcal{P}_{m},B_{L^{r}(du)},\big(\frac{t}{2}\big)^{1/\theta})\leq\log N_{r}\big(\mathcal{P}_{m},\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{t}{2}\big)^{1/\theta}\big).

Now, since
\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{t}{2}\big)^{1/\theta}>2
for
t\geq 8
and
\theta\in(0,1)
, we can use (
5.7
) to write

\displaystyle\log N_{r}\big(\mathcal{P}_{m},\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{t}{2}\big)^{1/\theta}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim m\Big(\frac{t}{2}\Big)^{-2/\theta}\log\Big(\frac{t}{2}\Big)^{1/\theta}\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big)\simeq mt^{-2/\theta}\log t\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim mt^{-\nu}\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big),

for some
\nu>2
, since
-\frac{2}{\theta}<-2
.

It remains to show that (
5.5
) implies (
5.6
). Let
t<4
and notice that due to (
5.4
) we can write

\displaystyle N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)\leq\#\{A\subset[n]:|A|=m\}\sup_{|A|=m}N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t)=\binom{n}{m}\sup_{|A|=m}N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t).

Applying
\log
to both sides and using the estimate
(\frac{n}{m})^{m}\leq\binom{n}{m}\leq(\frac{en}{m})^{m}
we get

(5.8)

\displaystyle\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},t)\leq\log\binom{n}{m}+\sup_{|A|=m}\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t)\leq m\log(1+\frac{n}{m})+\sup_{|A|=m}\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t).

Next notice that for each set
A
we can dominate
N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t)
by covering
\mathcal{P}_{A}
with
\|\cdot\|_{q}
-balls of radius 4 and then find the minimum number of
\|\cdot\|_{q}
-balls of radius
t
needed to cover each of these balls. That leads to the estimate

N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t)\lesssim D(4B_{L^{q}(du)},B_{L^{q}(du)},t)N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},4)=D(B_{L^{q}(du)},B_{L^{q}(du)},t/4)N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},4).

The last identity above is just rescaling. Using Proposition
5.3
and (
5.5
) (with
t=4
) we get

\displaystyle\sup_{|A|=m}\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t)\leq\sup_{|A|=m}\log\Big(\Big(\frac{16}{t}\Big)^{m}N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},4)\Big)\leq m\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{t}\Big)+m\log\Big(1+\frac{n}{m}\Big).

Plugging the above bound to (
5.8
) and noting that
A+B\lesssim AB
, for
A,B\gtrsim 1
, concludes the proof.
∎

In view of Proposition
5.14
, proving Theorem
5.12
reduces to showing Theorem
5.13
.
Before we proceed with the proof we need the following simple version of the pigeonhole principle.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}
and
X_{3}
be positive random variables on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
such that

\mathbb{E}X_{i}\leq m_{i},\qquad i\in[3],

for some positive constants
m_{1},m_{2}
and
m_{3}
. Then

\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega:X_{1}(\omega)<10m_{1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad X_{2}(\omega)<10m_{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad X_{3}(\omega)<10m_{3}\})\geq\frac{3}{10}.

In particular, there exists
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
such that

X_{1}(\omega_{0})<10m_{1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad X_{2}(\omega_{0})<10m_{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad X_{3}(\omega_{0})<10m_{3}.

Proof.

Let
A_{i}=\{\omega\in\Omega:X_{i}(\omega)<10m_{i}\}
,
i\in[3]
. Note that by Chebyshev’s inequality we have

\mathbb{P}(A_{i})=1-\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\in\Omega:X_{i}(\omega)\geq 10m_{i}\})\geq 1-\frac{\mathbb{E}X_{i}}{10m_{i}}\geq\frac{9}{10},\qquad i\in[3].

Applying the inclusion-exclusion principle and using the above estimate we get for any
i\neq j

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})=\mathbb{P}(A_{i})+\mathbb{P}(A_{j})-\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cup A_{j})\geq\frac{9}{10}+\frac{9}{10}-1=\frac{8}{10}.

Finally, another application of the inclusion-exclusion principle gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3})+\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{2})+\mathbb{P}(A_{1}\cap A_{3})+\mathbb{P}(A_{2}\cap A_{3})

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{P}(A_{1})-\mathbb{P}(A_{2})-\mathbb{P}(A_{3})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{9}{10}+3\frac{8}{10}-3=\frac{3}{10}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
5.13
.

Fix
t>2
and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
2^{k/2}\leq t<2^{1+k/2}
. For a fixed
A\subset[n]
with
|A|=m
and
|a|=1
let
f=\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}\varphi_{i}
. Denoting by
\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
a system of independent Rademacher functions on the interval
(0,1)
we let for
i\in[n]
and
j\in[k]

\varepsilon_{i}^{j}:=r_{i}(\omega_{j}),\qquad\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k})\in(0,1)^{k}.

Then
\mathbf{\varepsilon}:=(\varepsilon_{i}^{j})_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq k}
forms a collection of randomly selected, independent elements of the set
\{-1,1\}
. We will write
d\varepsilon^{j}:=d\omega_{j}
and
d\varepsilon:=d\varepsilon^{1}\dotsb d\varepsilon^{k}
.

For
i\in[n]
consider the decomposition

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{i}^{1}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})=\varepsilon_{i}^{1}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\big(\varepsilon_{i}^{2}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{2})\big)=\varepsilon_{i}^{1}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\varepsilon_{i}^{2}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{2})=\dots

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{i}^{1}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\varepsilon_{i}^{2}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{2})+\dots+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dots(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k-1})\varepsilon_{i}^{k}+(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dots(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k}).

Using the above decomposition for each
i\in A
, we can represent
f
as follows

\displaystyle f(u)=\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}\varphi_{i}(u)=\Phi(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)+E(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u),

where

\Phi(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)=\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{1}\varphi_{i}(u)+\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\varphi_{i}(u)+\dots+\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dots(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k-1})\varepsilon_{i}^{k}\varphi_{i}(u)

and

E(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)=\sum_{i\in A_{\varepsilon}}a_{i}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dots(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k})\varphi_{i}(u),

with

A_{\varepsilon}=\{i\in A:\varepsilon_{i}^{1}=\dots=\varepsilon_{i}^{k}=-1\}.

Note that the range of summation in the definition of
E(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)
can be restricted to
i\in A_{\varepsilon}
, since the summands
a_{i}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dots(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k})\varphi_{i}(u)
vanish for
i\in A\setminus A_{\varepsilon}
.

We will show that for any fixed
u
the following are true

(5.9)

\int\|\Phi(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)\|_{L^{q}(du)}\,d\varepsilon\lesssim t,

(5.10)

\int|A_{\varepsilon}|\,d\varepsilon\lesssim\frac{m}{t^{2}},

and

(5.11)

\int\|E(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)\|_{L^{2}(du)}\,d\varepsilon\lesssim t.

Assume momentarily that (
5.9
), (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) hold. Then by Lemma
5.15
(applied with
X_{1}=\|\Phi(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)\|_{L^{q}(du)},X_{2}=|A_{\varepsilon}|
and
X_{3}=\|E(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)\|_{L^{2}(du)}
) there exists
\varepsilon_{0}=(\varepsilon_{i}^{j})_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq k}
such that

(5.12)

\|\Phi(\varepsilon_{0},u)\|_{L^{q}(du)}\lesssim t,

(5.13)

|A_{\varepsilon_{0}}|\lesssim\frac{m}{t^{2}},

and

(5.14)

\|E(\varepsilon_{0},u)\|_{L^{2}(du)}\lesssim t.

In other words,

(5.15)

\|f(u)-E(\varepsilon_{0},u)\|_{L^{q}(du)}\leq ct,

with
E(\varepsilon_{0},\cdot)\in ct\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor}
and some absolute constant
c>0
.

The equation (
5.15
) means that any function
f\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
can be approximated in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{q}(du)}
within
t
by some function
E(\varepsilon_{0},\cdot)
from
ct\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor}
. Note that for large values of
t
the frequency support of each function in
ct\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor}
is substantially smaller than
m
—the size of the frequency support of
f
. That gain plays a crucial role in the proof. Bourgain refers to the procedure described above as the “method of support-reduction.”

In view of Proposition
5.2
(consider a maximal
ct
-separated subset of
\mathcal{P}_{m}
), we get

\displaystyle N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m},ct)\lesssim N_{q}(ct\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor},ct)=N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor},1),

with the same
c
as in (
5.15
). The last identity follows simply from rescaling. Now using (
5.8
) we obtain

N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor},1)\lesssim\log{\binom{n}{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor}}+\sup_{|A|=\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor}\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},1).

Using Stirling’s formula we can estimate

\displaystyle\log{\binom{n}{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor}}\lesssim\log{\Big(\frac{en}{m/t^{2}}\Big)^{m/t^{2}}}\simeq\frac{m}{t^{2}}\log\Big(\frac{t^{2}n}{m}\Big)\simeq\frac{m}{t^{2}}\Big(\log t+\log\frac{n}{m}\Big).

Let us emphasize, that the extra decay
t^{-2}
in the above bound is due to the “support-reduction” procedure applied before.
Moreover, for any
A
of size
\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor
we can identify
\mathcal{P}_{A}\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor},\|\cdot\|_{L^{q}(du)})
and consequently by (
5.3
) the bound

\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{A},t)=\log E(B^{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor},B_{L^{q}(du)},1)\lesssim mt^{-2}

holds uniformly in
A\subset[n]
satisfying
|A|=\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor
.

Combining the above estimates we obtain

N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{\lfloor m/t^{2}\rfloor},1)\lesssim\frac{m}{t^{2}}\Big(\log t+\log\frac{n}{m}\Big)+mt^{-2}\lesssim mt^{-2}(\log t)\log{(\frac{n}{m}+1)},

where in the last relation we used the fact that product of terms greater than 2 dominates their sum. That gives the postulated bound.

It remains to prove (
5.9
), (
5.10
) and (
5.11
). Let us begin with (
5.9
). By Hölder’s inequality (applied to the integral with respect to
d\varepsilon^{l}
) and Khintchine’s inequality (applied again to the integration with respect to
d\varepsilon^{l}
; the constant in that inequality is of size
\sqrt{q}
) we get

\displaystyle\int\|\Phi(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)\|_{L^{q}(du)}\,d\varepsilon

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\int\big\|\|\sum_{i\in A}a_{i}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dotsb(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{l-1})\varepsilon_{i}^{l}\varphi_{i}(u)\|_{L^{q}(d\varepsilon^{l})}\big\|_{L^{q}(du)}\,d\varepsilon^{1}\dotsb d\varepsilon^{l-1}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim_{q}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\int\Big[\sum_{i\in A}|a_{i}|^{2}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})^{2}\dotsb(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{l-1})^{2}\Big]^{1/2}\,d\varepsilon^{1}\dotsb d\varepsilon^{l-1}.

Now applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we can further estimate it by

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}|a_{i}|^{2}\int(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})^{2}\dotsb(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{l-1})^{2}\,d\varepsilon^{1}\dotsb d\varepsilon^{l-1}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}|a_{i}|^{2}2^{2(l-1)}\mathbb{P}(\{\varepsilon_{i}^{1}=\dots=\varepsilon_{i}^{l-1}=-1\})\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}|a_{i}|^{2}2^{2(l-1)}2^{-(l-1)}\Big)^{1/2}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}2^{l/2}\lesssim t,

which shows (
5.9
).

To prove (
5.10
) we notice that

|A_{\varepsilon}|=2^{-k}\sum_{i\in A}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})\dotsb(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k}),

hence due to the independence of
(\varepsilon_{i}^{j})
we get

\int|A_{\varepsilon}|\,d\varepsilon=2^{-k}\sum_{i\in A}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\int(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{j})\,d\varepsilon_{i}^{j}\Big)=2^{-k}\sum_{i\in A}1=2^{-k}m\simeq\frac{m}{t^{2}}.

Finally, to show (
5.11
) we use orthogonality and 1-boundedness of
(\varphi_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and then the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality getting

\int\|E(\mathbf{\varepsilon},u)\|_{L^{2}(du)}\,d\varepsilon\leq\int\Big[\sum_{i\in A}|a_{i}|^{2}(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{1})^{2}\dotsb(1-\varepsilon_{i}^{k})^{2}\Big]^{1/2}\,d\varepsilon\leq 2^{k/2}\simeq t.

That concludes the proof of Theorem
5.13
.
∎

6.
End of the proof of Theorem
1.3

It this section we complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
. We need to treat separately the cases
2<p<4
and
p\geq 4
.

6.1.
Case
2<p<4

In the previous section we reduced the proof of Theorem
1.3
to showing the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

There exists
\sigma>0
such that for any
\omega_{2},\omega_{3}

(6.1)

\displaystyle\|K_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega_{1})}\lesssim(\delta m_{3}^{p/2-1}+\frac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{m_{1}})^{1/2}(1+K(\omega_{2})+K(\omega_{3}))^{p-\sigma},

where
q_{0}=\log n\simeq\log\frac{1}{\delta},n^{2/p}=\delta n
, with
K_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})
defined by (
3.19
) and
K(\omega)=K_{S_{\omega}}
given by (
3.1
).

Proof.

Fix
\omega_{2}
and
\omega_{3}
.
Letting
\mathcal{E}=\big\{\big(|\langle\varphi_{i},f_{\bm{b},\omega_{2}}(1+|f_{\bm{c},\omega_{3}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\big)_{i=1}^{n}:\bm{b}\in\Pi_{m_{2}},\bm{c}\in\Pi_{m_{3}}\big\}

K_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m_{1}}}\sup_{|A|\leq m_{1}}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}}\Big(\sum_{i\in A}\xi_{i}(\omega_{1})x_{i}\Big).

Thus, by Lemma
4.1
(applied with
m=m_{1}
) we get

(6.2)

\displaystyle\|K_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega_{1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[\delta^{1/2}+m_{1}^{-1/2}\right]B+m_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{B}[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dt,

where
B=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}}|\mathbf{x}|
. It remains to estimate
B
and
\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)
suitably. Following Bourgain, we will use the shorthand notation

g_{\bm{b}}=f_{\bm{b},\omega_{2}},\quad h_{\bm{c}}=f_{\bm{c},\omega_{3}}.

We begin with treating
B
. Take any
\mathbf{x}=\big(|\langle\varphi_{i},g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\big)_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{E}
. By Bessel’s inequality and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}|=\Big(\sum_{i}|\langle\varphi_{i},g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{\bm{b}}\|_{p}\left\|1+|h_{\bm{c}}|\right\|_{2p}^{p-2}

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{\bm{b}}\|_{p}\left(1+\left\|h_{\bm{c}}\right\|_{\infty}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left(1+\left\|h_{\bm{c}}\right\|_{p}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}-1}

\displaystyle\lesssim K(\omega_{2})(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{\frac{p}{2}-1}m_{3}^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{p}{2}-1)},

where in the last inequality we used the estimates

\|g_{\bm{b}}\|_{p}\leq K(\omega_{2}),\qquad\|h_{\bm{c}}\|_{p}\leq K(\omega_{3}),

and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and
1
-boundedness of the system
(\varphi_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
giving

\|h_{\bm{c}}\|_{\infty}\leq|\bm{c}|\cdot(\sum_{i\in\mathrm{supp}\,\bm{c}}\|\varphi_{i}\|_{\infty}^{2})^{1/2}\leq m_{3}^{1/2}.

It follows that

(6.3)

B\lesssim K(\omega_{2})(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{\frac{p}{2}-1}m_{3}^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{p}{2}-1)}.

We turn to estimating
\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)
. Let
\mathbf{x}=\big(|\langle\varphi_{i},g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\big)_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\big(|\langle\varphi_{i},g_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}^{\prime}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\big)_{i=1}^{n}
be two elements in
\mathcal{E}
. Then by Bessel’s inequality,

\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i}\left||\langle\varphi_{i},g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|-|\langle\varphi_{i},g_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}^{\prime}}|)^{p-2}\rangle|\right|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

(6.4)

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}-g_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}^{\prime}}|)^{p-2}\|_{2}.

Depending on the value of
p
, we need to estimate the right-hand side of (
6.4
) differently.

Assume first that
2<p\leq 3
. Using the elementary inequality

(6.5)

\displaystyle|(1+|x|)^{p-2}-(1+|y|)^{p-2}|=\Big|\int_{1+|y|}^{1+|x|}(p-2)s^{p-3}\,ds\Big|\leq(p-2)|x-y|,\qquad p\in(2,3),

we get

RHS of (
6.4
)

\displaystyle=\|(g_{\bm{b}}-g_{\bm{b}^{\prime}})(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}+g_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}\big[(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}-(1+|h_{\bm{c}^{\prime}}|)^{p-2}\big]\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(g_{{\bm{b}}}-g_{{\bm{b}^{\prime}}})(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}\|_{2}+\|g_{{\bm{b}^{\prime}}}|h_{\bm{c}}-h_{\bm{c}^{\prime}}|\|_{2}.

Using Hölder’s inequality with
q=\frac{2p}{4-p}
and
r=\frac{2p}{p-2}
, we further estimate

\displaystyle|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{{\bm{b}}}-g_{{\bm{b}^{\prime}}}\|_{q}(1+\|h_{{\bm{c}}}\|_{p})^{p-2}+\|g_{{{\bm{b}^{\prime}}}}\|_{p}\|h_{{\bm{c}}}-h_{{\bm{c}^{\prime}}}\|_{r}

\displaystyle\leq(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{p-2}\|g_{{\bm{b}}}-g_{{\bm{b}^{\prime}}}\|_{q}+K(\omega_{2})\|h_{{\bm{c}}}-h_{{\bm{c}^{\prime}}}\|_{r}.

Consider the function

T\colon\mathcal{P}_{m_{2}}\times\mathcal{P}_{m_{3}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

defined by

T(f,g)=\big(|\langle\varphi_{i},f(1+|g|)^{p-2}\rangle|\big)_{i=1}^{n}.

Then

\mathbf{x}=T(g_{\bm{b}},h_{\bm{c}})\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=T(g_{\bm{b}^{\prime}},h_{\bm{c}^{\prime}}).

By Proposition
A.3
applied with
\mathcal{E}=T(\mathcal{P}_{m_{2}}\times\mathcal{P}_{m_{3}})
,
c=(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{p-2}
and
c^{\prime}=K(\omega_{2})
, it follows that

\displaystyle N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)\lesssim N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m_{2}},\frac{t}{2}(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{2-p})N_{r}(\mathcal{P}_{m_{3}},\frac{t}{2}K(\omega_{2})^{-1}).

Hence,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)\lesssim\log N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m_{2}},\frac{t}{2}(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{2-p})+\log N_{r}(\mathcal{P}_{m_{3}},\frac{t}{2}K(\omega_{2})^{-1}).

Inserting (
6.3
) and (
6.6
) into (
6.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|K_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega_{1})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[\delta^{1/2}+m_{1}^{-1/2}\right]B+m_{1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{B}[\log N_{2}(\mathcal{E},t)]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\left[\delta^{1/2}+m_{1}^{-1/2}\right]m_{3}^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{p}{2}-1)}K(\omega_{2})(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{\frac{p}{2}-1}

\displaystyle\quad+m_{1}^{-1/2}(\log n)^{-1/2}K(\omega_{2})\int_{0}^{\infty}\log[N_{q}(\mathcal{P}_{m_{2}},t)]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+m_{1}^{-1/2}(\log n)^{-1/2}(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{p-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log[N_{r}(\mathcal{P}_{m_{3}},t)]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dt.

Next, we can apply Theorem
5.12
getting

\displaystyle\|K_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega_{1})}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\left[\delta m_{3}^{\frac{p}{2}-1}+\frac{m_{3}}{m_{1}}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}K(\omega_{2})(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{\frac{p}{2}-1}+\left(\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(1+K(\omega_{3}))^{{p}-2}+\left(\frac{m_{3}}{m_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}K(\omega_{2}),

which implies (
6.1
) with
\sigma=\frac{p}{2}
, since
K(\omega_{3})\geq 1
by (
3.17
). It finishes the proof in the case
2<p\leq 3
.

For
3<p<4
, the inequality (
6.5
) is no longer valid. Instead, we use

(6.7)

|(1+|x|)^{p-2}-(1+|y|)^{p-2}|\leq(p-2)|x-y|(|x|^{p-3}+|y|^{p-3}),\qquad p>3.

Applying the above inequality we get

\displaystyle|g_{\bm{b}}(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}-g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}(1+|h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}|)^{p-2}|

\displaystyle=|(g_{\bm{b}}-g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}+g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}})(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}-g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}(1+|h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}|)^{p-2}|

\displaystyle\lesssim|g_{\bm{b}}-g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}|(1+|h_{\bm{c}}|)^{p-2}

\displaystyle\quad+|g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}||h_{\bm{c}}-h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}|(|h_{\bm{c}}|^{p-3}+|h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}|^{p-3}).

Hence, with
q=2p/(4-p)
, we can compute the distance by

RHS of (
6.4
)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|g_{\bm{b}}-g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}\|_{q}(1+\|h_{\bm{c}}\|_{p})^{p-2}+\|g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}\|_{p}\|h_{\bm{c}}-h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}\|_{q}(\|h_{\bm{c}}\|_{p}^{p-3}+\|h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}\|_{p}^{p-3})

\displaystyle\lesssim K(\omega_{3})^{p-2}\|g_{\bm{b}}-g_{{\bm{b}}^{\prime}}\|_{q}+K(\omega_{2})K(\omega_{3})^{p-3}\|h_{\bm{c}}-h_{{\bm{c}}^{\prime}}\|_{q}

using the general form of Hölder’s inequality with exponents
(\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{4-p},\frac{p}{2(p-3)})
for the second term.
We can follow the rest of the argument from the case
2<p\leq 3
to finish the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\|K^{S_{1}}_{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3})\|_{L^{q_{0}}(d\omega_{1})}

Appendix A
Technical results

Lemma A.1
.

Let
q>1
and let
0<\kappa<q
. Consider the function

F(x)=\Big(\frac{\kappa}{x}\Big)^{x}x^{q}.

Then there exists
x_{0}\in(1,\infty)
such that
F
is increasing on
(1,x_{0})
and decreasing on
(x_{0},\infty)
.

Proof.

We start with writing

F(x)=e^{x\log\frac{\kappa}{x}}x^{q}.

Then the derivative of
F
is

F^{\prime}(x)=x^{q-1}e^{x\log\frac{\kappa}{x}}\Big[x\big(\log\frac{\kappa}{x}-1\big)+q\Big]=x^{q-1}e^{x\log\frac{\kappa}{x}}\Big[x\log\frac{\kappa}{xe}+q\Big].

Thus
F^{\prime}(x)=0
for some
x\in(1,\infty)
if and only if

x\log\frac{xe}{\kappa}=q,\qquad x\in(1,\infty).

Letting
K=\frac{e}{\kappa}
and
u=Kx
the above equation is equivalent to

u\log u=qK,\qquad u\in(K,\infty).

It remains to note that the function
u\mapsto u\log u
has negative values for
u
in
(0,\frac{1}{e})
and it increases unboundedly on
(\frac{1}{e},\infty)
. Therefore the equation
u\log u=qK
has exactly one solution and clearly the desired conclusion about
F
follows.
∎

A.1.
Large deviations

Suppose
\{\xi_{i}:i\in[n]\}
are independent
\{0,1\}
-valued random variables of mean
\delta(n)
, which is allowed to depend on
n
. We can think of
\delta=n^{2/p-1}
as it is in Bourgain’s paper—something which for fixed
p
decays much slower than
n^{-1}
as
n\to\infty
. Set

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}

and note that
\mathbb{E}S_{n}=n\delta
.
A typical large deviations result for the family
\{\xi_{i}:i\in[n]\}
would establish an exponential rate of decay of the probability of the rare event that
S_{n}/n
is larger than 10 times its expected value:

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}/n>10\delta)\leq e^{-cn}.

Many families of independent random variables enjoy such a property as long as
\delta
stays sufficiently large compared to
n^{-1}
.

Proposition A.2
.

For all
n\geq 1
, the following estimates hold.

(i)

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}>10n\delta)\leq e^{-5n\delta}

(ii)

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}<\frac{1}{10}n\delta)\leq e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}}
.

Proof.

First we prove (i). Let
t>0
be an extra parameter we have at our disposal. By Chebyshev’s inequality and independence of the selectors,

\displaystyle p_{n}:=\mathbb{P}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}>10n\delta]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[e^{t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}}\geq e^{10tn\delta}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-10tn\delta}\mathbb{E}[e^{t\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}}]

\displaystyle=e^{-10tn\delta}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}e^{t\xi_{i}}.

A direct computation shows

\mathbb{E}e^{t\xi_{i}}=e^{t}\delta+(1-\delta)=1+(e^{t}-1)\delta.

By what we have so far,

p_{n}\leq e^{-10tn\delta}[1+(e^{t}-1)\delta]^{n}\leq e^{-10tn\delta}e^{(e^{t}-1)n\delta}=[e^{-10t+(e^{t}-1)}]^{n\delta}.

We could optimize to choose the best value of
t
, but setting
t=1
is sufficient because it shows

p_{n}\leq[e^{-8.28...}]^{n\delta}\leq e^{-5n\delta}.

The proof of (ii) is very similar. First we transform the expression so it more closely resembles what we did to prove (i). Let
\eta_{i}=\delta-\xi_{i}
, and let
T_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}
. Then the reader can easily verify

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}<\frac{1}{10}n\delta)=\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>\frac{9}{10}n\delta).

We introduce a free parameter
t
as before, and use Chebyshev and independence of the variables
\eta_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>\frac{9}{10}n\delta)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\frac{9}{10}tn\delta}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}e^{t\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{9}{10}tn\delta}(e^{t\delta}(1-\delta)+e^{t(\delta-1)}\delta)^{n}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{1}{10}tn\delta}(1+(e^{-t}-1)\delta)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\frac{1}{10}tn\delta}e^{(e^{-t}-1)n\delta}

\displaystyle=[e^{\frac{t}{10}+(e^{-t}-1)}]^{n\delta}.

Setting
t=1
, we have

\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>\frac{9}{10}n\delta)\leq[e^{-0.532...}]^{n\delta}\leq e^{-\frac{n\delta}{2}}.

∎

A.2.
Entropy of products

Suppose
(X,d)
,
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
are two metric spaces,
c,c^{\prime}>0
, and consider the product metric space
(X\times X^{\prime},cd\oplus c^{\prime}d^{\prime})
with metric defined by

(cd\oplus c^{\prime}d^{\prime})((x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}),(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime}))=cd(x_{1},x_{2})+c^{\prime}d^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}).

Lipschitz images of such product metric spaces obey natural entropy bounds:

Proposition A.3
.

Let
(X,d)
,
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
and
(\bar{X},\bar{d})
be metric spaces. Assume there exists a
1
-Lipschitz map
T\colon(X\times X^{\prime},cd\oplus c^{\prime}d^{\prime})\to(\bar{X},\bar{d})
, meaning that for any
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}),(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})\in X\times X^{\prime}
,

\bar{d}(T(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}),T(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime}))\leq(cd\oplus c^{\prime}d^{\prime})((x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime}),(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})).

Then the covering number
N_{\bar{d}}(T(X\times X^{\prime}),t)
satisfies the bound

N_{\bar{d}}(T(X\times X^{\prime}),t)\lesssim N_{d}(X,\frac{t}{2c})\cdot N_{d^{\prime}}(X^{\prime},\frac{t}{2c^{\prime}})

for any
t>0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{i}\}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\{B_{j}^{\prime}\}
be minimal coverings of
X,X^{\prime}
by
\frac{t}{2c},\frac{t}{2c^{\prime}}
-balls in their respective metrics. For fixed
i,j
, we claim that
T(B_{i}\times B_{j}^{\prime})
is contained in a
t
-ball of
\bar{X}
. To see this, if
T(x,x^{\prime})\in T(B_{i}\times B_{j}^{\prime})
, then with
B_{i}=B(x_{i},\frac{t}{2c})
and
B_{j}^{\prime}=B(x_{j}^{\prime},\frac{t}{2c^{\prime}})
, by assumption,

\bar{d}(T(x,x^{\prime}),T(x_{i},x_{j}^{\prime}))\leq cd(x,x_{i})+c^{\prime}d^{\prime}(x^{\prime},x_{j}^{\prime})<c\cdot\frac{t}{2c}+c^{\prime}\cdot\frac{t}{2c^{\prime}}=t.

Since
T(X\times X^{\prime})\subset\bigcup_{i,j}T(B_{i}\times B_{j}^{\prime})
, it follows from the definition of the entropy numbers that the minimum number of
t
-balls required to cover
T(X\times X^{\prime})
is

N_{\bar{d}}(T(X\times X^{\prime}),t)\lesssim|\mathcal{B}|\cdot|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}|\simeq N_{d}(X,\frac{t}{2c})\cdot N_{d^{\prime}}(X^{\prime},\frac{t}{2c^{\prime}}),

as desired.
∎

A.3.
Level set decomposition of a sequence

The result below is a version of a level set partition. Its proof is based on the Cauchy condensation test.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
\bm{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots)
be a nonincreasing sequence of nonnegative numbers satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}\leq 1.

Then there exists a family of sequences
\bm{c}(l)=(c_{1}(l),c_{2}(l),\dots)
,
l=0,1,\dots
with mutually disjoint supports and a sequence of nonnegative coefficients
(\gamma_{l})_{l=0}^{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{c}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{l}\bm{c}(l)

and the following conditions hold

1)

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{l}\leq 1,

2)

|\supp\bm{c}(l)|\leq 2^{l},

3)

c_{i}(l)\leq 2^{-l}.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=2^{l}}^{2^{l+1}-1}c_{i}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}2^{l}c_{2^{l}}\sum_{i=2^{l}}^{2^{l+1}-1}\frac{c_{i}}{2^{l}c_{2^{l}}}=:\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{l}\sum_{i=2^{l}}^{2^{l+1}-1}c_{i}(l),

where
\gamma_{l}=2^{l}c_{2^{l}}
and

c_{i}(l)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{c_{i}}{2^{l}c_{2^{l}}},&\qquad i\in[2^{l},2^{l+1}-1],\\
0,&\qquad i\notin[2^{l},2^{l+1}-1].\end{cases}

It remains to notice that using monotonicity of
c_{i}
we get

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{l}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}2^{l}c_{2^{l}}\leq\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i=2^{l}}^{2^{l+1}-1}c_{i}\leq 1,

so condition 1) holds. Moreover, condition 2) holds trivially. Finally, condition 3) holds since
c_{i}\leq c_{2^{l}}\leq 1
for
i\in\supp\bm{c}(l)=[2^{l},2^{l+1}-1]
.
∎

Corollary A.5
.

Let
\bm{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of complex numbers satisfying
|\bm{a}|\leq 1
.
Then there exists a family of sequences
\bm{a}(l)=(a_{1}(l),a_{2}(l),\dots)
,
l=0,1,\dots
with mutually disjoint supports and a sequence of nonnegative coefficients
(\lambda_{l})_{l=0}^{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{a}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{l}\bm{a}(l)

and the following conditions hold

A)

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{l}^{2}\leq 1,

B)

|\supp\bm{a}(l)|\leq 2^{l},

C)

|a_{i}(l)|\leq 2^{-l/2}.

Proof.

Consider the sequence
c_{i}:=|a_{i}|^{2}
of nonnegative numbers. By re-indexing we can assume that
c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\dotsc
. Using Proposition
A.4
there exists a decomposition

\bm{c}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{l}\bm{c}(l)

with
\gamma_{l}
and
\bm{c}(l)
satisfying conditions
1)-3)
of Proposition
A.4
. Then letting

a_{i}(l)=\begin{cases}\frac{a_{i}}{2^{l/2}|a_{2^{l}}|},&\qquad i\in[2^{l},2^{l+1}-1],\\
0,&\qquad i\notin[2^{l},2^{l+1}-1],\end{cases}

and
\lambda_{l}=\gamma_{l}^{1/2}
it is straightforward to see that conditions A)—C) hold.
∎
