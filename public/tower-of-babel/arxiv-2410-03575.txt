A New Algorithm for Computing the Exponential of a Block Triangular Matrix Thanks: Version of August 24, 2026
Awad H. Al-Mohy

Abstract
The exponential of block triangular matrices arises in a wide range of scientific computing applications, including exponential integrators for solving systems of ordinary differential equations, Hamiltonian systems in control theory, sensitivity analysis, and option pricing in finance. We propose a novel algorithm exploiting the block triangular structure for simultaneously computing the exponentials of the diagonal blocks and the off-diagonal block of the matrix exponential without direct involvement of the full block matrix in the computations. This approach generalizes the work of Al-Mohy and Higham [ SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. , 30 (2009), pp. 1639–1657] on the Fréchet derivative of the matrix exponential. The generalization is established through a linear operator framework, facilitating efficient evaluation schemes and rigorous backward error analysis. The algorithm employs the scaling and squaring method using diagonal Padé approximants with algorithmic parameters selected based on the backward error analysis. A key feature is that the selection of the scaling parameter relies solely on the maximal norm of the diagonal blocks with no dependence on the norm of the off-diagonal block. Numerical experiments confirm that the proposed algorithm consistently outperforms existing algorithms in both accuracy and efficiency, making it a preferred choice for computing the matrix exponential of block triangular matrices.

Introduction

Matrix functions play a fundamental role in many scientific and engineering disciplines, as they provide a structured approach to solving complex problems. They have broad applications in fields such as control theory, quantum mechanics, network analysis, and machine learning
[
9
,
13
,
15
]
. As a result, matrix functions have attracted substantial research attention, leading to significant advances in theoretical understanding and computational methods.
In particular, the matrix exponential is one of the most extensively studied matrix functions, both from theoretical and computational perspectives. However, at the algorithmic level, the computation of the matrix exponential for some structured problems has received comparatively less attention, despite its importance in applications where exploiting matrix structure can lead to substantial gains in efficiency and accuracy.
In this context, and motivated by applications, we propose a specialized algorithm for computing the exponential of block triangular matrices that fully exploits the triangular structure without explicitly forming the full matrix.

The contents of this paper are organized as follows. First, for a given analytic function
f
and matrices
A
and
B
of appropriate dimensions, we define a linear operator via the Cauchy integral and derive its fundamental properties. Motivated by the potential of this operator to facilitate the computation of the exponential of block triangular matrices, we highlight several applications. In section
2
, we apply the operator to the optimally formulated evaluation schemes for the diagonal Padé approximants to the exponential function, thereby obtaining optimal evaluation schemes for the operator itself, which, in this setting, represents the off-diagonal block of the matrix exponential. This approach leads to significant computational savings and improved efficiency. The scaling and squaring method is then employed and analyzed.
We present a rigorous backward error analysis in section
3
. In section
4
, we describe our proposed algorithm and review the algorithm of Kenney and Laub. Numerical experiments are provided in section
5
. Finally, we draw some concluding remarks in section
6
. and raise some open questions.

Unless otherwise stated, the matrix norms used in this paper are assumed to be consistent.

Definition 1
.

Let
f
be an analytic function on a simply connected open set
\mathchar 28938\relax\subset\mathbb{C}
containing the union of the spectra of
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
. For every
E\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times d}
we define the operator
\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{n\times d}\to\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times d}
as

\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathchar 28928\relax}f(z)(zI_{n}-A)^{-1}E(zI_{d}-B)^{-1}\mathrm{d}z,

(1)

where
\mathchar 28928\relax\subset\mathchar 28938\relax
is a simple closed rectifiable contour that strictly encloses the union of the spectra of
A
and
B
and winds once around it in the counterclockwise direction.

Obviously
\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,\cdot)
is linear and uniquely determined by the function
f
and the ordered pair
(A,B)
.
This definition follows immediately from the Cauchy integral definition
[
13
, Def. 1.11]
of the matrix function

f\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&E\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}\right)
. That is,

\displaystyle f\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&E\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathchar 28928\relax}f(z)\begin{bmatrix}zI_{n}-A&-E\\
0&zI_{d}-B\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\mathrm{d}z

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathchar 28928\relax}f(z)\begin{bmatrix}(zI_{n}-A)^{-1}&(zI_{n}-A)^{-1}E(zI_{d}-B)^{-1}\\
0&(zI_{d}-B)^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}f(A)&\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)\\
0&f(B)\end{bmatrix}.

The next lemma lists several properties and rules of this operator.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f
and
g
be matrix functions satisfying the assumptions of Definition
1
. Then we have:

1.

\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,A,E)=L_{f}(A,E)
, where
L_{f}(A,\cdot)
is the Fréchet derivative operator of the matrix function

[

2.

\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)=P_{1}\mathcal{D}_{f}(T_{1},T_{2},P_{1}^{-1}EP_{2})P_{2}^{-1}
, where
A=P_{1}T_{1}P_{1}^{-1}

B=P_{2}T_{2}P_{2}^{-1}

3.

\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)=0

4.

\mathcal{D}_{f+g}(A,B,E)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)+\mathcal{D}_{g}(A,B,E)

(sum rule)

5.

\mathcal{D}_{fg}(A,B,E)=f(A)\mathcal{D}_{g}(A,B,E)+\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)g(B)

(product rule)
.

6.

\mathcal{D}_{f\circ g}(A,B,E)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(g(A),g(B),\mathcal{D}_{g}(A,B,E))
,
(chain rule)

assuming further that the union of the spectra of
g(A)
and
g(B)
is strictly enclosed in
\mathchar 28928\relax
.

Proof.

The first point follows from
[

, Prob. 3.9]
. The second point follows directly from the formula

. For the other points, we use the formula
[

, Lem. 1.1]
,
[

0&B\end{bmatrix}\right)=f\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}\right)+L_{f}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0&E\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right).

Aligning this formula with (

L_{f}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0&E\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

Therefore, the sum, product, and chain rules of the operator
\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,\cdot)
are directly inherited from those of the Fréchet derivative operator
[
13
, Thm.’s 3.2–3.4]

These properties of the operator

[

,

[

in view of Lemma

\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)

A significant application arises in exponential integrators, a class of numerical methods used for solving systems of ODEs, particularly those involving stiff or highly oscillatory problems:

\displaystyle x\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu^{\prime}(t)=A\mkern 1.0mux(t)+h(t,x(t)),\quad x(t_{0})=x_{0},\quad t\geq t_{0},

(4)

where
x(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and
h
is a nonlinear vector
function. Numerical solution schemes for such problems involve computing a linear combination of the form

\varphi_{0}(A)w_{0}+\varphi_{1}(A)w_{1}+\dots+\varphi_{p}(A)w_{p},

(5)

where

\varphi_{0}(A)=\mathrm{e}^{A},\quad\varphi_{j}(A)=A\varphi_{j+1}(A)+\frac{1}{j!},\quad j\geq 0

and
w_{j}
,
j=0\colon p
, are vectors that correspond to an integration method. For a comprehensive survey of the exponential integrators, see Hochbruck and Ostermann
[
18
]
. In view of
[
3
, Thm. 2.1]
and the block formula (
2
), the linear combination (
5
) satisfies

\sum_{j=0}^{p}\varphi_{j}(A)w_{j}=\mathrm{e}^{A}w_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,J_{p}(0),W)e_{p},

where
J_{p}(0)
is the Jordan block of size
p\times p
associated with the zero eigenvalue,
W=[w_{p},w_{p-1},\dots,w_{1}]\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times p}
,
and
e_{p}
denotes the
last column of the identity matrix
I_{p}
.

In control theory, Hamiltonian matrices frequently arise in the analysis of linear quadratic regulator (LQR) problems. Consider the Riccati matrix differential equation

X^{\prime}(t)+X(t)A+A^{T}X(t)+X(t)RX(t)-C=0,

where
A
,
R
, and
C
are constant matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, with
R
and
C
being symmetric positive semidefinite. This differential equation is closely related to the linear system
[
23
]

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{bmatrix}u(t)\\
v(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A&R\\
C&-A^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u(t)\\
v(t)\end{bmatrix}=:\widetilde{A}\begin{bmatrix}u(t)\\
v(t)\end{bmatrix}.

The coefficient matrix
\widetilde{A}
is
Hamiltonian
, meaning that
J\widetilde{A}=(J\widetilde{A})^{T}
, where
J=\mathrm{antidiag}(I_{n},-I_{n})
. The matrix exponential
\mathrm{e}^{t\widetilde{A}}
arises in the solution of this system. A fundamental property of Hamiltonian matrices is the existence of
a structure-preserving unitary similarity transformation, known as the
Real Schur-Hamiltonian decomposition

[
22
, Thm. 5.1]
, reducing
\widetilde{A}
to a block upper triangular Hamiltonian matrix

\widetilde{T}:=\begin{bmatrix}T&H\\
0&-T^{T}\end{bmatrix},

(6)

where
T
is upper quasi-triangular with
eigenvalues in the left half plane and
H
is symmetric. It follows that

\mathrm{e}^{\widetilde{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}F&D\\
0&F^{-T}\end{bmatrix},

where
F=\mathrm{e}^{T}
and
D=\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(T,-T^{T},H)
. Since
H
is orthogonally diagonalizable,

H=Q\mathchar 28931\relax Q^{T}
, the second property of Lemma
2
can be used to write

D=Q\mkern 1.0mu\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(T,-T^{T},\mathchar 28931\relax)\mkern 1.0muQ^{T}.

Thus, Algorithm
1
below can be readily
optimized
to compute
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(T,-T^{T},\mathchar 28931\relax)
efficiently.

Another interesting application of the exponential of block triangular matrices arises in option pricing models based on polynomial diffusions, a class of stochastic processes frequently used in mathematical finance to model the evolution of financial variables
[
11
]
. In this context, Kressner, Luce, and Statti
[
20
]
observe that exponentials of a sequence of recursively nested block upper triangular matrices need to be computed. Specifically, a sequence
\exp(G_{0})
,
\exp(G_{1})
,
\exp(G_{2})
,
\dots
, where

G_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{n-1}&E_{n}\\
0&G_{n,n}\end{bmatrix},\quad G_{0}=G_{0,0},

(7)

with
G_{0,0}
being an initial square matrix that serves as the starting point of the recursion. Defining the sequence
F_{n}=\exp(G_{n})
and
F_{n,n}=\exp(G_{n,n})
, the matrix

F_{n}
can be computed recursively as

F_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}F_{n-1}&\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(G_{n-1},G_{n,n},E_{n})\\
0&F_{n,n}\end{bmatrix}

using Algorithm
1
, which simultaneously computes all three blocks.

Finally, a possible application that would enhance research in the computation of matrix functions is to use
\mathcal{D}_{f}
to compute the matrix function itself. Given an
n\times n
matrix, we can transform it to an upper triangular matrix
T
using Schur decomposition. Thus for any index
1\leq k\leq n-1
, we have

f(T)=\begin{bmatrix}f(A_{k})&\mathcal{D}_{f}(A_{k},B_{k},E_{k})\\
0&f(B_{k})\end{bmatrix},

(8)

where
A_{k}=T(1\colon k,1\colon k)
,
B_{k}=T(k+1\colon n,k+1\colon n)
, and
E_{k}=T(1\colon k,k+1\colon n)
.

Although
\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)
can be computed directly by extracting the (1,2) block of the left-hand side matrix in (
2
), increasing the size of the problem is computationally and storage-wise inefficient. We will discuss some disadvantages of this approach below. Therefore, having a specialized algorithm for evaluating the operator
\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)
is highly valuable.

The remainder of this paper is devoted to the computation of
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}
.

2
Evaluation schemes for
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)

The exponential function is entire over the complex plane, thus for any matrices
A
,
B
, and
E
of appropriate matching sizes, the operator
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,\cdot)
always exists, and the matrix
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)
satisfies the Sylvester equation

AX-XB=\mathrm{e}^{A}E-E\mathrm{e}^{B}.

However, the converse is not true in general, since the Sylvester equation has a unique solution if and only if the spectra of
A
and
B
are disjoint. Even in this case, the accuracy of the computed solution depends critically on the separation between the spectra; that is, on how well-conditioned the problem is
[
13
, Ch. 9]
. Due to this classical limitation, we adopt a different approach that imposes no restrictions on the matrices.

In this section we generalize the algorithm of Al-Mohy and Higham
[
1
, Alg. 6.4]
for
simultaneously
computing the matrix exponential and
its Fréchet derivative to an algorithm for simultaneously computing the matrix exponentials
\mathrm{e}^{A}
and
\mathrm{e}^{B}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)
using the scaling and squaring method based on the diagonal Padé approximants,
r_{m}(z)=p_{m}(z)/q_{m}(z)
, for the exponential function
\mathrm{e}^{z}
. The cost analysis of Higham
[
12
,
14
]

suggests the optimal degrees for the Padé approximants are the odd degrees
3, 5, 7, 9, and 13.
For
m=3,5,7,9
, the polynomial

p_{m}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}c_{i}z^{i}
can be written as

\displaystyle p_{m}(z)=z\sum_{k=0}^{(m-1)/2}c_{2k+1}z^{2k}+\sum_{k=0}^{(m-1)/2}c_{2k}z^{2k}=:u_{m}(z)+v_{m}(z).

(9)

Therefore,
q_{m}(z)=-u_{m}(z)+v_{m}(z)
because
p_{m}(-z)=q_{m}(z)
and

\mathcal{D}_{p_{m}}=\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}+\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}},\qquad\mathcal{D}_{q_{m}}=-\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}+\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}},

where
\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}(A,B,E)
and
\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}}(A,B,E)
are obtained by applying the sum and product rules of the operator on
u_{m}
and
v_{m}
, respectively. This yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\sum_{k=1}^{(m-1)/2}c_{2k+1}M_{2k}+E\sum_{k=0}^{(m-1)/2}c_{2k+1}B^{2k}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{(m-1)/2}c_{2k}M_{2k}.

(11)

The matrix
M_{\ell}=\mathcal{D}_{x^{\ell}}(A,B,E)
is evaluated using the recurrence relation

M_{\ell}=A^{t}M_{r}+M_{t}B^{r},\quad M_{1}=E,

(12)

where
t
and

are positive integers such that
\ell=t+r
, resulting from applying the product rule to
z^{\ell}=z^{t}z^{r}
.

For
m=13
, the splitting of
p_{13}(z)=u_{13}(z)+v_{13}(z)
into odd and even terms can be efficiently calculated
[
1
, sect. 6]
as

\displaystyle u_{13}(z)=zw(z),\quad w(z)=z^{6}w_{1}(z)+w_{2}(z),\quad v_{13}(z)=z^{6}y_{1}(z)+y_{2}(z),

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cc}w_{1}(z)=c_{13}z^{6}+c_{11}z^{4}+c_{9}z^{2},&w_{2}(z)=c_{7}z^{6}+c_{5}z^{4}+c_{3}z^{2}+c_{1},\\
y_{1}(z)=c_{12}z^{6}+c_{10}z^{4}+c_{8}z^{2},&y_{2}(z)=c_{6}z^{6}+c_{4}z^{4}+c_{2}z^{2}+c_{0}.\end{array}

Applying the operator
\mathcal{D}
on theses schemes yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{u_{13}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\mathcal{D}_{w}(A,B,E)+Ew(B),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{v_{13}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{6}\mathcal{D}_{y_{1}}(A,B,E)+M_{6}y_{1}(B)+\mathcal{D}_{y_{2}}(A,B,E),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{w}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{6}\mathcal{D}_{w_{1}}(A,B,E)+M_{6}w_{1}(B)+\mathcal{D}_{w_{2}}(A,B,E),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{w_{1}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{13}M_{6}+c_{11}M_{4}+c_{9}M_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{w_{2}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{7}M_{6}+c_{5}M_{4}+c_{3}M_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{y_{1}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{12}M_{6}+c_{10}M_{4}+c_{8}M_{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{y_{2}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{6}M_{6}+c_{4}M_{4}+c_{2}M_{2}.

Then

\mathcal{D}_{p_{13}}=\mathcal{D}_{u_{13}}+\mathcal{D}_{v_{13}}
and

\mathcal{D}_{q_{13}}=-\mathcal{D}_{u_{13}}+\mathcal{D}_{v_{13}}
. We finally solve for

r_{m}(A)
,
r_{m}(B)
, and
\mathcal{D}_{r_{m}}(A,B,E)
the equations

\displaystyle(-u_{m}+v_{m})(A)r_{m}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{m}+v_{m})(A),

\displaystyle(-u_{m}+v_{m})(B)r_{m}(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{m}+v_{m})(B),

\displaystyle\qquad\quad(-u_{m}+v_{m})(A)\mathcal{D}_{r_{m}}(A,B,E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}+\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}})(A,B,E)

(16)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mkern 1.0mu\mkern 1.0mu(\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}-\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}})(A,B,E)r_{m}(B).

Observe that we can obtain
\mathcal{D}_{r_{m}}(A,B,E)
by solving the multiple left-hand

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r_{m}}(A,B,E)(-u_{m}+v_{m})(B)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}+\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}})(A,B,E)

(17)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle r_{m}(A)(\mathcal{D}_{u_{m}}-\mathcal{D}_{v_{m}})(A,B,E),

which results from applying the product rule of the operator
\mathcal{D}
on the equation
r_{m}(z)q_{m}(z)=p_{m}(z)
instead of

that produces
(
16
). Thus, we can obtain
\mathcal{D}_{r_{m}}(A,B,E)
by solving the easiest system. As the exponential function is well-approximated by Padé approximants near zero, if either
A
or
B
has a large spectral radius,
then the scaling and squaring method is used to reduce the spectral radii. This ensures that

and

are good approximations to
\mathrm{e}^{A}
and

and

\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)

, and

\displaystyle D_{i+1}

\displaystyle X_{i+1}

3
Backward error analysis

We analyze the backward error resulting from
the approximations
X_{s}\approx\mathrm{e}^{A}
,
Y_{s}\approx\mathrm{e}^{B}
, and
D_{s}\approx\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)
using the recurrence (
19
). We recall the backward error result from
[
2
, sect. 3]
.

Theorem 3
.

Let

\mathchar 28938\relax^{(n)}_{m}=\{\,Z\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}:\rho(\mathrm{e}^{-Z}r_{m}(Z)-I)<1,\quad\rho(Z)<\mu_{m}\,\},

(20)

where
\mu_{m}=\min\{\,|z|:q_{m}(z)=0\,\}
and
\rho
denotes the spectral radius. Then the functions

g_{2m+1}(X)=\log(\mathrm{e}^{-X}\mkern 1.0mur_{m}(X))

(21)

are defined for all
X\in\mathchar 28938\relax^{(n)}_{m}
, where
\log
is the
principal matrix logarithm, and

r_{m}(X)=e^{X+g_{2m+1}(X)},\quad X\in\mathchar 28938\relax^{(n)}_{m}.

If
X
is not in
\mathchar 28938\relax^{(n)}_{m}
, choose
s

2^{-s}X\in\mathchar 28938\relax^{(n)}_{m}

r_{m}(2^{-s}X)^{2^{s}}=\mathrm{e}^{X+2^{s}g_{2m+1}(2^{-s}X)}=:\mathrm{e}^{X+\mathchar 28929\relax X}

(22)

and the matrix
\mathchar 28929\relax X=2^{s}g_{2m+1}(2^{-s}X)
represents the backward error associated with approximating
\mathrm{e}^{X}
using the scaling and squaring method via Padé approximants.
Over
\mathchar 28938\relax^{(n)}_{m}

“As a compromise between accuracy and efficiency we have modified the above algorithm to a “semirecursive” form: if

This idea does not generally apply to all matrices. The identity

is guaranteed to hold if the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues of the matrix

.

The direct application of
expm
to the block matrix yields satisfactory results for small values of
\alpha_{k}
, but its accuracy declines as
k
increases. For the largest value tested,
\alpha_{6}=2^{600}
, the relative error rises rapidly to approximately
9.39\times 10^{-2}
. This reduction in accuracy is primarily due to the
(1,2)
block norm becoming arbitrarily large, leading to excessive scaling that overscales the diagonal blocks and adversely affects numerical stability. While this represents an extremely exaggerated scenario unlikely to arise in practice,
expm
still outperforms the KL algorithm up to
k=5
. Nevertheless, this behavior highlights the importance of Algorithm
1
as a specialized and reliable method for this class of problems.

Furthermore, both Algorithm
1
and the KL algorithm
preserve the linearity
of the operator
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}(A,B,E)
. This property holds exactly in exact arithmetic, as it is inherent in the derivation of both algorithms.
In finite precision arithmetic, linearity is also effectively preserved under the current experimental conditions. Notably, the parameter
\alpha_{k}
is selected as an
integer power
of 2, ensuring that the scaling operation
\alpha_{k}H
introduces no rounding errors. This is a direct consequence of the fact that scaling by integer powers of 2 in IEEE floating-point arithmetic is exact.
Thus, we have
\widehat{D}(\alpha_{k})=\alpha_{k}\widehat{D}(1)
, and the factor
\alpha_{k}
cancels out in the relative error computation.

The algorithmic structure of Algorithm
1
exploits the triangularity of the input matrices and selects the scaling parameter independently of the matrix
\alpha_{k}H
. Although the KL algorithm similarly does not rely on the norm of
\alpha_{k}H
for scaling, its loss of accuracy is attributed to the nature of its algorithmic design rather than to a loss of linearity.

6
Concluding remarks

We propose a new algorithm for computing the exponential of block triangular matrices that simultaneously computes the blocks of the matrix exponential without directly involving the full matrix.
We have introduced a linear operator
\mathcal{D}_{f}(A,B,E)
, generalizing the Fréchet derivative of matrix functions. We explored its algebraic properties and demonstrated how they facilitate the construction of a computational framework for evaluating this operator, specifically for the matrix exponential. Our rigorous backward error analysis not only ensures high accuracy but also inherently promotes computational efficiency through the optimal selection of scaling parameters using sharp bounds. Our numerical experiments demonstrate the high accuracy of the proposed algorithm, while the theoretical analysis highlights its computational advantages over existing methods.

Moreover, we highlighted several practical applications of
\mathcal{D}_{\exp}
, including its role in exponential integrators for solving systems of stiff or highly oscillatory ordinary differential equations and in computing matrix exponentials for Hamiltonian matrices in control theory. Additionally, we discussed its potential in option pricing models based on polynomial diffusions, where it facilitates the computation of exponentials of recursively nested block upper triangular matrices. Algorithm
1
is a general-purpose method; however, it can serve as a foundation for developing specialized and more efficient algorithms depending on the application and the structure of the inputs.

An open question we raise is whether the bound in Theorem
4
can be expressed in terms of the quantities
\alpha_{p}(A)
and
\alpha_{p}(B)
, defined in (
28
), instead of matrix norms. Achieving this would significantly reduce the scaling parameters and, consequently, the overall computational cost. It is also worth investigating whether the input matrices
A
and
B
can be scaled independently using different scaling parameters, which may lead to further efficiency gains and improved numerical behavior.

Finally, future research could explore extending the use of
\mathcal{D}_{f}
to directly compute matrix functions, particularly by employing the Schur decomposition. This provides a promising direction for simplifying and enhancing various computational challenges in scientific computing and numerical methods.
