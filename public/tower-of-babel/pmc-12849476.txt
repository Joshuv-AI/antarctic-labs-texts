Breaking boundaries: integration of sports and medicine in the community elderly service model
Authors: Yuanli Chen, Mohd Firdaus Bin Abdullah, Nor Eeza Zainal Abidin, Fanghui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina possesses the largest aging population globally and is experiencing the most rapid demographic transitions, presenting substantial public health challenges. In response, the Healthy China strategy has emphasized the integration of sports and medicine as a key initiative for monitoring and promotion, with a particular focus on community services. This study employed grounded theory classification coding to identify core concepts and key factors. It systematically constructed a theoretical model for integrating sports and medicine into community elderly services, while conducting a developmental pathway analysis that compares major international models with China's practical approach. The overarching aim was to jointly advance public health and enhance the nation's overall well-being.MethodsUtilizing grounded theory and NVivo, this study analyzed policies, reports, and cases related to the integration of sports medicine. Through open, axial, and selective coding with constant comparative analysis, it identified key factors of integrated model services. Consequently, a theoretical framework and operational model were constructed for China, elucidating contextual conditions, stakeholder interactions, multi-actor collaboration, and implementation pathways for community-dwelling older adults.DiscussionThis study delineates the operational characteristics, performance outcomes, and bottlenecks of four service typologies. It offers critical appraisal of their respective merits and limitations, while emphasizing how policy directives steer their development. This comparative synthesis substantiates the practical feasibility and conceptual robustness of the proposed model, demonstrating its capacity to enhance integrated community-based service delivery.Conclusion A total of 355,796 characters of text were included, resulting in the construction of 981 coding reference points, identified 13 critical categories and the core concepts underpinning the integration of sports and medicine services for the elderly. The proposal of four distinct and innovative community integration models: the Sports-led Model, the Medicine-led Model, the Technology-driven Model, and the Talent-driven Model. These models offer specific and actionable guidance for developing diversified and efficient elderly health services within the community.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25991-0.

Introduction

Population aging has emerged as a critical global social and public health challenge. China faces unprecedented pressure as it possesses the world’s largest and fastest-growing elderly population. It is projected that by 2025, China’s population aged 65 and above will exceed 400 million, accounting for more than 30% of its total population, and that the country will enter a stage of severe aging around 2035 [1]. This demographic transition is expected to generate a series of socioeconomic consequences, including a shrinking labor force, rising healthcare demand, increased strain on medical and eldercare systems [2, 3], and a growing need for long-term care. These challenges collectively threaten national fiscal sustainability, social stability, and long-term economic development [4]. While China’s healthcare system has historically centered on post-onset diagnosis and treatment, integrating sports into health services represents a significant paradigm shift. It elevates physical activity from generalized health recommendations to structured, standardized interventions. This shifts the healthcare paradigm from simply repairing injuries to proactively building and maintaining health, helping to alleviate the growing burden on the healthcare system.

Recognizing this imperative, the Chinese government launched the Healthy China 2030 Outline in 2016 [5], thereby elevating health promotion to a national strategic priority. This policy explicitly advocates strengthening the integration of sports and medicine and identifies physical activity as a cornerstone of non-medical health interventions. Older adults are designated as a priority population, underscoring the critical role of evidence-based exercise in advancing public health. Aligned with this strategic direction, the General Administration of Sport of China has initiated community sports and health centers in several pilot regions since 2022 [6]. These centers offer physical fitness assessments, health evaluations, evidence-based exercise counseling, and exercise-based rehabilitation services, constituting a substantive step toward operationalizing integrated service delivery.

However, significant barriers, including fragmented sports and medicine systems, misaligned resource allocation, and a shortage of interdisciplinary professionals, hinder the effective translation of policy into scalable, sustainable health promotion solutions. Thus, there is an urgent need for systematic investigation into these operational constraints and for identifying viable pathways to advance community-level integrated services.

The dilemma of the integration of sports and medicine

A significant theoretical gap in current Chinese research lies in the lack of culturally and contextually adapted frameworks for integrating sports and medicine within local communities. As noted by Alrashidi et al., interventions that do not incorporate familiar, community-organized activities significantly compromise adherence and sustainability among Chinese seniors, highlighting the limitations of uncritical adoption of foreign models [7]. Research indicates that collective activities such as Tai Chi [8] are highly prevalent among the elderly, whereas gym-based workouts are less common. Zhang’s [9] survey of the Shanghai Community Active Health Plan revealed that Shanghai still faces a fragmentation between sports and medical systems, as well as management barriers in community services. This deficiency in adaptability stems from profound differences in community infrastructure, healthcare delivery systems, and population health beliefs.

The integration of sports and medicine in China is further impeded by systemic fragmentation, wherein the two sectors operate within isolated professional silos. The medical system remains predominantly curative, relying heavily on diagnostic and pharmaceutical interventions, constrained by reimbursement models that seldom cover exercise counseling. In contrast, the sports system prioritizes athletic performance and general fitness, yet often lacks the clinical expertise necessary for managing chronic diseases. This professional and institutional dichotomy precludes the formation of a synergistic approach to health promotion and chronic disease management.

This institutional divide creates a critical service gap, particularly for the growing elderly population burdened with chronic conditions. For instance, a physician may offer generic advice to increase physical activity, structured protocols, and financial incentives to prescribe a specific, supervised, and safe exercise regimen. Conversely, while exercise professionals can design training programs, they frequently lack the clinical competency to assess medical risks or tailor interventions for complex health conditions. Consequently, physical activity—a crucial non-pharmacological intervention—remains systematically underutilized, leading to suboptimal health outcomes and perpetuating reliance on reactive medical care.

Bridging this systemic divide represents not merely an operational adjustment but a fundamental imperative for constructing a modern, efficient, and sustainable public health system. Such integration is paramount for mitigating the escalating economic burden of chronic diseases and advancing the proactive, wellness-oriented goals of national initiatives notably the Healthy China 2030 strategy. Only through synergistic integration across both policy and practice can the full potential of exercise as a therapeutic modality be realized.

Main models of international development

United States exercise is medicine® (EIM)

By the early 20th century, the paradigm of mainstream Western medicine had increasingly shifted toward disease prevention. A significant portion of physicians’ responsibilities expanded to encompass health protection and promotion, alongside disease prevention. By the latter half of the 20th century, as physical activity became a central tenet of public health agendas, the medical community began to recognize exercise as an integral part of a healthy lifestyle. Structured physical activity was acknowledged to play a vital role in promoting longevity, preventing chronic diseases, and serving as a therapeutic adjunct. Consequently, exercise is increasingly recognized as a fundamental form of medicine and a core component of healthcare. This paradigm has been formalized and globally disseminated through the Exercise is Medicine® (EIM) initiative [10].

Launched in 2007 by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) in collaboration with the American Medical Association, EIM explicitly promotes the integration of sports and medicine by positioning exercise assessment and prescription as essential elements of routine healthcare practice. The foundational objective of the EIM Global Health Program was to cultivate global awareness and institutionalize physical activity within healthcare settings. To this end, the EIM Global Center established National Centers globally, employing a multisectoral approach to implement effective interventions. These sectors included primary care associations, medical associations, sports federations, academic institutions, health departments, and national public health institutes, all of which were aligned within national health strategies [11]. This initiative solidified the integration of sports into medicine, fostering an ecosystem that spans policy development, monitoring, evaluation, healthcare professional education, and fitness professional certification.

Spearheaded by the ACSM, the EIM program leverages initiatives propelled by health insurance agencies and market mechanisms, while being embedded into national health initiatives and physical activity guidelines to improve public health. Within this clinical pathway, physicians utilize screening tools to assess patients’ physical activity levels and risk, subsequently issuing personalized exercise prescriptions. Referral networks connect patients to fitness centers and qualified exercise professionals in the community, ensuring safe and effective exercise instruction. Notably, some programs track patient participation and health outcomes through electronic health record systems, providing evidentiary value to insurance companies. This model successfully bridges clinical care with community resources. Consequently, developed countries have begun to operationalize this health promotion pathway, achieving notable milestones in synergizing sports and medicine.

German insurance bonus policy

The German health insurance system functions as a pivotal intermediary, bridging government organizations and sports clubs to facilitate the integration of sports and medicine. It bears responsibility for harmonizing the interests of the government, insured persons, physicians, and social organizations [11]. The German government mandates universal participation in social health insurance and actively promotes preventive healthcare. Accordingly, insurance companies have established bonus schemes and designed comprehensive sports and health incentive programs to encourage citizens to engage in physical exercise. Furthermore, they subsidize health promotion for chronic diseases, rehabilitation exercises, and functional training.

Germany’s community-based health promotion program for integrating sports and medicine relies primarily on the collaboration between statutory health insurance and certified sports club courses. Community physicians issue exercise prescriptions for patients, who are then referred to community sports clubs or community centers to implement the exercise programs. Upon completing these certified courses, insured citizens can apply to their statutory health insurance company for reimbursement of the associated costs [12]. To ensure accessibility, patient copayments for treatments at accredited sports clubs and community rehabilitation centers are capped at €10 per day [13]. This model has significantly incentivized the participation of community residents in physical exercise through economic leverage.

Germany has achieved remarkable results through years of implementation of this sports-medicine integration model. Prominent examples include the Active Health Promotion for the Elderly program, implemented in 2001 at the Albertinen Haus Geriatric Center in Hamburg [14], and the long-term care provisions at the Evangelical Geriatric Center in Berlin [15]. Life expectancy in Germany has increased from 78.3 years in 2000 to 81.1 years in 2018. Concurrently, overall medical expenses and the incidence of chronic diseases have declined significantly, with mortality rates from diabetes, cervical cancer, and breast cancer decreasing by more than 20% [16].

UK exercise referral scheme

Since Morris’s seminal physical activity epidemiological survey in 1953 [17], the dose-response relationship between physical activity and population health has been firmly established. Globally, a total of 78 countries have implemented evidence-based national physical activity programs. Furthermore, 43 countries have established exercise referral mechanisms and developed resources such as Physical Activity for the Prevention and Treatment of Diseases and National Exercise Prescription Libraries. These initiatives utilize exercise referrals as primary prevention and non-pharmacological treatments for 42 non-communicable diseases and medical conditions [18]. In 1994, the UK established the world’s first Exercise Referral Scheme (ERS), initiated by the Department of Health and Social Care and implemented within the National Health Service (NHS). Referrals are transmitted to exercise professionals via electronic pathways (or spreadsheets), and exercise prescriptions are delivered in leisure, fitness, and community settings.

To codify service standards and guarantee quality assurance, the UK Department of Health (now the Department of Health and Social Care) published the Exercise Referral System: National Quality Assurance Framework in 2001. This seminal framework established national benchmarks for the oversight of Exercise Referral Schemes (ERS), offering strategic guidance on program commissioning, evaluation protocols, and patient follow-up mechanisms. Moving from policy to practice, the UK introduced the Exercise Referral Toolkit in 2010 to further operationalize these standards. This comprehensive resource empowers healthcare and social care organizations to systematically design, implement, and evaluate programs, thereby enhancing the consistency and clinical effectiveness of ERS delivery.

From an operational perspective, ERS models function through a structured clinical pathway. Primary care professionals identify and refer physically inactive or at-risk patients to accredited third-party service providers. Upon referral, these providers stratify risk and develop tailored physical activity plans informed by the patient’s clinical profile. Throughout the intervention, continuous monitoring and assessment are conducted not only to support patient engagement and adherence but also to ensure that the prescribed activities remain strictly aligned with clinical needs and safety protocols.

Research progress in China

The theoretical models proposed by the current academic community, such as the cooperation framework “state – government – society – market” [19] and the “pyramid model” [20], emphasize the collaboration among multiple entities spanning from basic intervention to service delivery. Although offering valuable insights for the systematic construction of sports-medicine integration, their inherent limitations expose a profound theoretical impasse in this field. Medicine operates within a pathology-centered paradigm defined by disease-diagnosis-treatment, emphasizing evidence-based practice, standardization, and risk aversion. In contrast, sports function within a physiological-functional paradigm of health assessment-enhancement, emphasizing load adaptation and potential development. The two paradigms are characterized by a fundamental divergence in terms of terminology, value orientation, and operational procedures. The “pyramid model” attempts to integrate the two into a single framework but fails to resolve the underlying logical incompatibility. Conversely, the cooperation framework tends to sidestep this fundamental contradiction, achieving only external alignment at the organizational level. Consequently, forced attempts to unify these two paradigms frequently devolve into jurisdictional disputes regarding dominance, or mechanistic reductionism—simply equating exercise testing with medical examinations and exercise prescriptions with pharmaceuticals—thus undermining the synergistic value of integration.

In contrast to theoretical discourse, practical developments in sports-medicine integration offer more direct and empirical evidence. Regions endowed with abundant sports, healthcare, and technological resources have emerged as pioneers in this field, leveraging their economic strength, human capital, and digital infrastructure to pilot diversified service models. Prominent operational modalities include sports and health complexes [21], sports rehabilitation centers, community sports clubs, and sports prescription clinics [22]. These initiatives have evolved from fragmented, project-based collaborations to systematic ecosystems characterized by institutional synergy and platform building. However, the scarcity of unified local standards and service guidelines has revealed critical bottlenecks in governance, funding mechanisms, professional competency, and technical specifications [23, 24]. This reality underscores the urgent necessity for a profound system integration reform.

International experience demonstrates that successful models rely on standardized processes, sustainable support mechanisms, and cross-sectoral professional collaboration [25]. While mature frameworks for EIM exist in the United States, Germany, and the UK, these models lack adaptability to China’s distinct culture, policies, and social structure. Conversely, entrenched administrative barriers exist between China’s health and sports systems. The predominantly curative orientation of medical institutions and the fitness-oriented focus of the sports sector lack effective synergy, thereby impeding the implementation of insurance policies comparable to those in Germany and the establishment of exercise referral programs akin to those in the United Kingdom. Furthermore, China has yet to formulate unified national standards for sports-medicine integration. For example, the International Practice Guidelines and Transparent Registration Platform currently includes clinical exercise protocols solely for diabetes, osteoporosis, and osteoarthritis, yet lacks comprehensive operational standards to steer the implementation of sports-medicine integration. The absence of systematic planning and effective methodologies for optimal integration models in this area has resulted in a paucity of theoretical evidence regarding the effectiveness and benefit of integrating exercise services into healthcare.

In summary, the integration of sports and medicine in China has transitioned from strategic planning to the pilot implementation phase. Current regional pilot initiatives necessitate a systematic theoretical framework to analyze the systemic challenges facing community sports-medicine services within the context of China’s rapidly aging population. Establishing such a framework is critical for resolving the structural imbalance between inadequate effective supply and the escalating demand for elderly services, thereby charting sustainable development paths for Chinese communities.

Research objective

To identify the key factors influencing the models of sports-medicine integration for the elderly population.

To develop pathways for synergizing sports and medicine within community-based health services.

Methodology

Text collection and processing

The textual sources utilized in this study are derived from the following three distinct channels. First, comprehensive policy summaries and detailed information regarding pilot initiatives were collected from official websites. This yielded a collection of national, provincial, municipal, and regional policy documents, which helped elucidate the top-level guidance on the integration of sports and medicine. Second, drawing upon summary reports submitted to the General Administration of Sport of China by provincial and municipal authorities regarding integration of sports and medicine, the research team identified key focal points and prominent challenges characterizing development across different regions. Third, supplementary case-specific materials were incorporated, including news articles, reports, and other relevant documentation. Together, these sources constitute the textual corpus for this research, comprising a total volume of 355,796 words.

Grounded theory

Existing research in the field of health promotion predominantly utilizes theoretical frameworks such as Self-Efficacy Theory [26], the Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change) [27], Theory of Planned Behavior [28], PRECEDE-PROCEED [29], Social-Ecological Theory [30], and Biopsychosocial Model [31]. Methodologically, the application of established frameworks is predominantly rooted in a positivist paradigm. These studies typically employ deductive quantitative methods, utilizing standardized instruments and statistical analyses to verify specific variables and linear causalities. However, the integration of sports and medicine constitutes an emerging, multifaceted social phenomenon characterized by systemic complexity and contextual fluidity, rendering standard quantitative measures insufficient for uncovering its deep-seated structural mechanisms. Consequently, this study transcends traditional confirmatory approaches and adopts Grounded Theory [32, 33], an exploratory qualitative methodology [34], to enable the inductive construction of theory [35, 36]. By systematically extracting concepts from empirical data, this approach allows for the generation of a localized theoretical framework that specifically elucidates the unique logic and implementation pathways of sports-medicine integration in the Chinese context [37].

In terms of analytical procedure, the study followed a rigorous bottom-up inductive logic. Assisted by NVivo software, the research team performed systematic classification and coding of the textual data. The analysis progressed through three stages: summarizing and condensing raw content to generate initial concepts; abstracting and clustering these concepts to identify main categories. Finally, synthesizing theoretical connections to establish core categories [38, 39]. Through this iterative process of constant comparison, the analysis moved from particular empirical instances to general theoretical constructs, ensuring that the definitions of categories were precise and the resulting research findings were theoretically saturated and meaningful [40, 41]. Stage 1: the open coding

The open coding refers to a process of identifying substantive codes emergent within the contents [42]. Decompose and summarize the organized raw materials word by word, sentence by sentence, and develop initial concepts from them [43]. The initial concepts from the collected case text materials are searched, the original data are analyzed and summarized word by word, important sentence information is extracted, and then keywords are extracted and named on the basis of coding principles for conceptualization.

During the open coding process, we conducted keyword identification, coding, categorization, and conceptual interpretation of the textual content. Thirteen categories were generated: management, policy, condition, technology, professionals, service content, service location, sports events for the elderly, individual, pattern type, region, elderly health services and implementation barriers.

Stage 2: axial coding

The axial stage is an advanced stage of qualitative data analysis that involves refining and interconnecting categories derived from initial coding. The main task of this stage is to extract the category main factor from all named conceptual codes through relationships and merging. This process establishes a logical structure by elucidating the relationships between categories through their semantic, contextual, procedural, causal, functional, structural, and strategic dimensions. In the paradigm model proposed by Strauss and Corbin [44, 45], these relationships are systematically organized. Specifically, Conditions explain the context (the “where” and “why”) of a phenomenon; Actions address the actors and strategies (the “who” and “how”); Interactions refer to relational dynamics (e.g., mutual or reciprocal relationships); Support involves the coordination of various elements; and Results describe the outcomes emerging from these actions and interactions. Finally, utilizing the high-frequency word query function in NVivo (Supplementary Table 2), the factors within each cluster were systematically organized in accordance with the inductive logic described above. This process culminated in the formation of the study’s main categories. (Fig. 2).

Stage 3: selective coding

Selective coding is the process of extracting core categories from the main category, theoretically integrating the connections between core categories, main categories, and other categories, and connecting core categories and related categories in the form of relational mechanisms to construct a theoretical framework [46]. This process establishes associative mechanisms linking core categories to relevant subcategories, thereby achieving theoretical saturation and conceptual clarity. To ensure consistency following the inclusion of additional policy documents, a targeted validation procedure was conducted. Two independent coders reviewed a 10% sample of the newly added corpus via the same coding framework, achieving a kappa coefficient ≥ 0.75. The results confirmed thematic saturation and methodological consistency with the original dataset (Table 1).

Discussion

This study employed grounded theory’s systematic three-stage coding protocol and key factor analysis to develop a research model for the integration of sports and medicine. This model conceptualizes the integration of physical education and medicine as a complex systems engineering endeavor. Its effective operation hinges not only on optimization within hierarchical systems but also on the synergistic interaction and resource exchange between subsystems. The analytical logic proceeds through the following sequence: textual cases → practical obstacles → multistakeholder integrated actions → key factors→main model (Fig. 3).

Theoretical model for the integration of sports and medicine

Sports-led model

The Sports-led integrated service model fundamentally redefines the locus of health promotion by positioning community sports venues—rather than clinical settings—as the “first line of defense” for population health. Drawing on the settings-based approach to health promotion [47], this model operates by repurposing underutilized infrastructure and infusing medical expertise into non-clinical environments. Unlike traditional fitness centers that focus solely on recreation, these upgraded Community Fitness Monitoring Stations rely on interprofessional collaboration [48]. By establishing structured protocols where professional coaches work alongside healthcare providers, the model ensures that physical activity is not merely recreational but delivered as a standardized, evidence-based intervention [49]. This effectively bridges the gap between public fitness resources and clinical health needs.

Empirical evidence from the pilot regions validates the operational feasibility and clinical efficacy of this approach. In Hubei Province, the government-backed retrofit of community centers successfully transformed passive venues into active health intervention hubs. This operational shift yielded immediate service outcomes: as of November 2024, pilot sites in districts such as Hongshan and Hanyang had served over 8,259 residents. Notably, 1,306 of these individuals received targeted rehabilitation for chronic conditions, demonstrating the model’s capacity to deliver specialized care at the grassroots level. Further validating the model’s scalability and data integration capabilities, the Hangzhou Community National Physical Fitness Monitoring Center has established a data-driven closed-loop management cycle. By systematically correlating resident exercise habits with 25 medical indicators for over 40,000 individuals annually, the system enables precise, individualized preventive interventions. These operational metrics indicate that integrating professional guidance into community sports settings significantly enhances adherence to physical activity and improves chronic disease management outcomes among older adults.

Nevertheless, two critical challenges remain regarding the model’s broader application. First, its sustainability is currently heavily reliant on government subsidies; a mature, market-driven revenue mechanism has yet to be fully established, raising concerns about long-term fiscal viability [50]. Second, the professional competence boundary remains a significant barrier in cross-disciplinary services [51]. Specifically, sports personnel often lack clinical diagnostic authority. Without rigorous risk management protocols, this gap can create safety liabilities when managing complex comorbidities in elderly populations, highlighting the urgent need for standardized certification systems [52].

Medicine-led model

The Medicine-led model represents a paradigm shift from traditional pharmacocentric approaches by systematically incorporating exercise-based health services into the mainstream healthcare system [53]. In this pathway, physicians serve as the primary gatekeepers, embedding evidence-based physical activity directly into clinical practice to establish a continuum of care that extends from initial diagnosis to long-term wellness [54]. The successful implementation of this model necessitates the synergy of critical interconnected factors: institutional coordination, precise medical assessment, effective exercise prescription, and supportive health insurance mechanisms.

Empirical applications in Chengdu and Shanghai illustrate how these factors are operationalized through distinct yet complementary pathways. The Shaocheng community in Chengdu exemplifies the power of institutional coordination through a grid-based organizational approach. By establishing a collaborative framework that interconnects neighborhood committees, community health centers, and fitness stations, the initiative integrates sports services directly into family doctor contracts. Through standardized care plans and health assessments, this grid system has successfully guided over 2,000 elderly residents into regular physical exercise groups, ensuring sustained engagement and effective chronic disease management.

Shanghai’s Yangpu District demonstrates the critical role of health insurance mechanisms. By integrating the local “Ludongbao” initiative with medical insurance reform, the project established Exercise Intervention Clinics across a tiered network comprising two medical centers, 12 community health centers, and 67 service stations. Targeting adults with metabolic conditions or a BMI ≥ 24 kg/m², the program provides individualized exercise prescriptions alongside nutritional guidance. Crucially, the integration of insurance reimbursement incentivizes patient adherence. Consistent with global findings on the cost-effectiveness of physical activity interventions [55], it shows that by 2024, the average monthly outpatient insurance expenses for participating chronic disease patients decreased by up to 16.7% compared with 2021 levels. Notably, patients who adhered to the program for 12 months saw their average monthly insurance costs drop by RMB 139.85, validating the economic feasibility of medical insurance-supported sports interventions.

By embedding standardized assessment and exercise prescription into clinical workflows, the Medicine-led model facilitates a paradigm shift toward preventive and precision-oriented health governance [56]. However, the scalability of this model is impeded by significant structural constraints. First, it places a substantial burden on physicians, whose severe time constraints, variable competencies in sports science, and limited willingness to adopt non-pharmacological interventions often create bottlenecks [57]. Second, regional disparities in institutional coordination and professional resources inevitably lead to heterogeneity in service quality. Furthermore, insurance reimbursement mechanisms for exercise-based services remain fragmented globally [58]; without the unified policy support seen in pilot cities like Shanghai, economic sustainability remains a challenge. Collectively, mitigating these challenges necessitates stronger cross-sectoral coordination, standardized professional training, and coherent insurance reform to fully realize the potential of this clinical approach.

Technology-driven model

The technology-driven model utilizes digital platforms to dismantle institutional and operational silos, facilitating a transition toward integrated smart health ecosystems [59]. By leveraging a suite of technologies, including big data analytics, artificial intelligence, and blockchain. This model enables the provision of personalized health services while optimizing the allocation of human and material resources. The core of this integration is a new health intervention paradigm driven by digital technology, which facilitates smart services through the in-depth mining of data elements and intelligent decision-making [60]. This constitutes a cross-border collaborative innovation among sports science, clinical medicine, and digital intelligence.

Empirical implementations in Xiamen and Zhejiang demonstrate how the technology-driven model operates through advanced digital infrastructures and integrated service networks. In Xiamen, artificial intelligence (AI), Internet of Things (IoT) devices, knowledge graphs, and computer vision technologies underpin automated exercise prescription systems, intelligent movement management platforms, exercise databases, and smart virtual fitness coaches. These advancements significantly reduce the technical threshold for implementing exercise-based interventions, making cross-disciplinary service delivery more feasible. Among the 28 pilot projects, three community smart sports and health centers and more than 60 intelligent fitness cabins have been established, providing personalized exercise prescriptions and guided fitness services to over 100,000 residents, generating more than 150,000 training plans, and delivering AI coaching services to over 2.5 million users. Zhejiang Province provides a broader institutional example through its deployment of precision health platforms and community-based fitness ecosystems, launching over 70 digital applications serving more than 17 million daily users. The “Zheli Fitness” platform integrates mobile terminals, smart devices, and a real-time public fitness map to enhance facility transparency and utilization; by continuously collecting and analyzing users’ exercise trajectories, the platform enables automated posture correction, training optimization, and injury risk warnings based on comparisons with standardized movement patterns. Integrated modules support individualized prescriptions, automated reminders, and real-time facility reservations, while linking online data collection with offline fitness testing and report retrieval to create a unified digital service chain. Complementing these initiatives, the Jiaxing Community Sports Hub integrates sports venues, schools, greenways, and parks into an IoT-enabled ecosystem with personal fitness accounts and smart coordination mechanisms; within three years, 385 hubs have attracted over 706,000 registered users, demonstrating the scalability of digital community integration and its capacity to standardize personalized exercise interventions across diverse settings.

The technology-driven model delivers substantial value by expanding access to personalized exercise services, improving service continuity, and enabling data-informed decision-making across health and fitness sectors; its digital platforms allow large-scale population reach at low marginal cost, enhance preventive health capacity through continuous monitoring, and strengthen user engagement via real-time feedback loops. The model also facilitates cross-sector collaboration by standardizing data flows across medical institutions, sports organizations, and community service providers. Despite these advantages, several structural challenges persist. Data quality, interoperability, and security remain uneven across regions, limiting the accuracy and reliability of digital assessments, while disparities in digital literacy and technological access risk deepening existing health inequalities [61]. Moreover, over-reliance on automated systems may undermine the human–professional component of exercise guidance [62], which remains essential for complex cases. Finally, the long-term sustainability of large-scale digital platforms requires stable financing and continuous technological upgrading. Addressing these structural constraints is essential for ensuring the equitable, scalable, and sustainable development of this model.

Talent-driven model

The convergence of sports and medicine is not merely a juxtaposition but a synthesis anchored in distinct epistemological paradigms, necessitating the integration of multidisciplinary principles and methodologies [63]. While the China Sports Science Society has certified over 1,700 sports prescribers, the systemic efficacy of this integration is currently constrained by deep-seated professional silos and ambiguous career pathways. To overcome these structural obstacles, a University-Hospital-Community-Association consortium model has been proposed as a critical theoretical mechanism. This framework is designed to construct a seamless pipeline for talent cultivation, specifically aiming to equip a new breed of healthcare practitioners with the competency to prescribe evidence-based exercise regimens and facilitate cross-disciplinary knowledge transfer [64, 65].

Empirical validation of these mechanisms is evident in diverse regional pilot models. In Shanghai’s Yangpu District, the Sports Bureau and the Shanghai Institute of Sports Science launched a pilot program that trained 12 community-based service personnel whose curriculum extended beyond theoretical assessment to include mandatory public service. These professionals are now tasked with instructing citizens in scientific exercise, healthy eating, and chronic disease prevention, thereby effectively bridging the gap between fitness services and health management. This cohort delivered scientifically grounded guidance to over 20,000 residents, demonstrating the tangible public health impact of a coordinated workforce. Concurrently, the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University institutionalized clinical integration by establishing multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) comprising cardiologists, Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) practitioners, and biomechanists to offer tailored lifestyle–medical treatment plans. Furthermore, Guangdong Province standardized professional development by upgrading social sports instructors to exercise health specialists through a stratified, tiered training system, thereby expanding the coverage of collaborative exercise prescriptions.

The strategic value of these models lies in their ability to dismantle traditional outpatient barriers and synergize sports, medical care, and rehabilitation into holistic health management [66]. By leveraging the strengths of diverse disciplines, this strategy effectively bridges the gap between theoretical assessment and applied public health interventions [67, 68]. However, significant limitations persist regarding the professional delineation of this emerging workforce. Despite the success of local pilots, the broader professional landscape remains underdeveloped. Future efforts must address the ambiguity in certification and career progression to transform these fragmented pilot successes into a sustainable, system-wide solution for chronic disease prevention.

Policy alignment and practical implications

However, operational feasibility alone does not guarantee systemic integration; the ultimate scalability and sustainability of these models depend on their congruence with the macro-strategic framework of national health governance. Beyond the specific operational mechanisms discussed above, the synergistic implementation of these four models holds profound implications for the Healthy China 2030 initiative and the National Strategy for Active Response to Population Aging. In particular, the Sports-led and Medicine-led models exemplify a critical paradigm shift from treatment-centered to health-centered care. By embedding physical activity into the continuum of health services, these models operationalize the policy mandate to establish exercise as a cornerstone of non-pharmacological intervention. Specifically, the Sports-led model broadens the reach of The National Fitness Program, fostering widespread participation, while the Medicine-led model ensures the clinical safety and scientific rigor of these interventions. Together, they directly address the core policy objective of deepening the integration of sports and medicine.

Concurrently, the Technology-driven model aligns seamlessly with the national strategies of Smart Health. In response to the projected strain on medical infrastructure and the contracting labor force, this model leverages digital innovations to optimize resource allocation. It ensures that high-quality, standardized health guidance can be scaled efficiently to meet the demands of the rapidly expanding elderly demographic, thereby overcoming geographical and capacity constraints.

Crucially, the Talent-driven model addresses the human capital bottleneck that often impedes policy execution. It responds to the urgent imperative for cross-disciplinary professionals who can bridge the gap between clinical medicine and sports science, thus ensuring the long-term sustainability and quality assurance of the Healthy China 2030 initiative.

In summary, these four models collectively constitute a comprehensive implementation framework. They translate top-down national priorities into actionable, bottom-up service strategies, providing policymakers with evidence-based pathways to mitigate the growing fiscal and social burdens of an aging society.

Conclusion

Employing grounded theory, this study constructs a foundational framework to dissect interdisciplinary integration mechanisms. It systematically elucidates the structural dynamics, operational realities, and inherent limitations of four distinct service typologies, revealing how institutional directives and contextual conditions shape their development trajectories within localized contexts.

Diverging from the evolutionary trajectories of developed economies, the integration of sports and healthcare in China exhibits distinct policy-driven and institutionally guided characteristics. Compared with the market-driven ecosystems of the U.S. and Germany—which leverage specialized health industries and insurance mechanisms—or the standardized sports referral model in the U.K., China’s practice predominantly anchors its delivery within community health institutions and national fitness monitoring networks, prioritizing infrastructure revitalization and cross-sectoral linkage.

The four models proposed in this study exemplify the operationalization of national policies. Specifically, the Sports-led and Healthcare-led models catalyze a paradigm shift from passive healthcare to proactive health governance. The Technology-driven model bypasses resource bottlenecks through digital innovation, and the Talent-driven model bridges the interdisciplinary workforce gap, sustaining continuous improvement in service quality. Ultimately, these models mirror the strategic orientation of Healthy China, offering actionable pathways for strengthening community health governance and fostering resilience against the long-term medical burden of an aging society.

Prospects

Future research should prioritize three critical avenues. First, digital health platforms must be developed to streamline exercise referral and tracking, fostering a closed-loop service ecosystem. Second, cost-effectiveness analyses are essential to demonstrate the long-term economic value of interventions, thereby securing sustainable financing from health insurance systems. Finally, future studies should employ mixed-method approaches, specifically structural equation modeling, to empirically validate the proposed framework. This quantitative validation will enhance the model’s generalizability and policy relevance across diverse regional contexts.
