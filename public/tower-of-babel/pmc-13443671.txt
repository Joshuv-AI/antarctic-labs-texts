A longitudinal whole-body CT dataset with manually annotated tumor lesions
Authors: Sergios Gatidis, Felix Peisen, Andreas Wagner, Pauline Ornela Megne Choudja, Ahmed Othman, Antoine Sanner, Nils Grauhan, Suam Kim, Dirk Graafen, Lukas Müller, Tanja Loßau, Jan Hendrik Moltz, Temke Kohlbrandt, Alessa Hering, Christian La Fougère, Konstantin Nikolaou, Thomas Küstner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe introduce Longitudinal-CT, a publicly available resource of whole-body computed tomography (CT) studies with exhaustive expert manual annotations of tumor lesions across two timepoints. The dataset comprises 600 CT studies from 300 patients diagnosed with metastatic malignant melanoma, each including a baseline and a follow-up examination acquired during systemic therapy at the University Hospital Tübingen, Germany. In total, it contains 7,182 manually segmented tumor lesions - 4,079 at baseline and 3,103 at follow-up - each labeled with anatomical location, volume, and longitudinal correspondence to capture lesion evolution such as persistence, regression, merging, or new appearance. All CT data are provided in anonymized NIfTI format with corresponding segmentation masks and lesion metadata. Longitudinal-CT establishes a standardized foundation for developing and validating artificial intelligence methods for automated lesion detection, segmentation, and temporal tracking in oncology. As a reference, a baseline deep learning segmentation model trained using nnU-Net v2 demonstrates the dataset’s potential for advancing research in automated oncologic whole-body CT lesion segmentation.

Background & Summary

Computed tomography (CT) plays a central role in oncologic imaging, enabling detection, staging, and therapy response assessment across a broad range of malignancies. For many cancer types, patient management relies on longitudinal comparison between a baseline examination and subsequent follow-up scans to evaluate disease progression or regression1. Despite its clinical importance, longitudinal CT interpretation remains labor-intensive and subjective, as radiologists must consistently identify, measure, and link lesions across multiple timepoints and body regions2.

Over the past decade, several high-quality annotated datasets have driven remarkable progress in medical image segmentation and detection, particularly in the context of single-timepoint imaging. For example, AutoPET introduced a large collection of hybrid PET/CT studies with comprehensive voxel-level tumor annotations, providing a benchmark for deep-learning–based lesion segmentation in whole-body imaging3,4. The ULS23 challenge introduced a dataset for whole-body single time point CT tumor lesion segmentation5. Similarly, datasets such as MSD (Medical Segmentation Decathlon)6 have established strong foundations for organ-specific segmentation, stimulating algorithmic innovation and comparative evaluation. In contrast to existing datasets such as the Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD), which focus on single timepoint and often organ-specific imaging, Longitudinal-CT provides paired whole-body CT studies with exhaustive lesion annotations and explicit longitudinal correspondence. This enables the study of temporal lesion dynamics, including progression, regression, merging, and splitting, which are not supported by existing datasets.

However, despite their scientific and methodological importance, existing public resources share two main limitations. First, most focus on a single imaging timepoint, preventing direct study of temporal evolution, registration-based alignment, or lesion correspondence across scans. Second, many are restricted to a specific anatomy or imaging modality, such as liver, lung, or head and neck, which limits their applicability for developing generalizable algorithms capable of handling the diversity of lesion appearance and location encountered in full-body oncologic imaging.

Longitudinal imaging, by contrast, introduces additional challenges and opportunities. Algorithms must not only segment lesions accurately within each scan but also establish stable mappings of individual lesions across time, even as their shape, intensity, or location changes under therapy7. The absence of paired, whole-body CT datasets with voxel-wise lesion labels and explicit longitudinal correspondences has thus hindered progress in automated therapy response assessment.

The Longitudinal-CT dataset was designed to address this gap. It comprises paired baseline and follow-up CT scans from 300 melanoma patients undergoing systemic therapy, with over 7,000 manually annotated tumor lesions. Each lesion is characterized by its temporal evolution, whether it persists, regresses, merges, splits or newly appears between scans, providing a unique foundation for modeling disease dynamics. Metastatic melanoma serves as an ideal model system for this purpose, as it exhibits a high variability of metastatic spread across diverse organ systems, including the lungs, liver, lymph nodes, soft tissue and bone. This biological heterogeneity makes it particularly suitable for developing and evaluating generalizable, anatomy-independent tumor segmentation and tracking algorithms.

By providing paired, whole-body CT studies with consistent imaging protocols and detailed longitudinal annotations, Longitudinal-CT bridges the gap between static imaging benchmarks and real-world clinical challenges. It enables research into lesion segmentation, temporal consistency, registration, and therapy response quantification, supporting a new generation of algorithms that reason jointly over space and time.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the University of Tübingen (Project number 700/2024BO2). The ethics approval permitted the use and publication of anonymized imaging data for research and scientific dissemination. Informed consent by patients for the use of their imaging data was waived by the institutional review board due to the anonymized nature of data processing and analysis. All methods were carried out according to relevant guidelines and regulations. All CT data were anonymized and defaced prior to publication to remove patient-identifying information.

Imaging data

The dataset consists of baseline and follow-up CT studies from 300 patients with malignant melanoma who underwent systemic therapy at the University Hospital Tübingen between 2015 and 2019. Among them, 181 patients (60.3%) were male and 119 (39.7%) were female. The mean age for male patients was 64.1 ± 14.9 years, and for female patients 63.6 ± 14.1 years, with an overall age range of 19 to 89 years (Fig. 1).

Patient demographics and baseline lesion characteristics. Histograms illustrate the age and sex distribution of the 300 patients (upper left), the distribution of baseline lesions across organs (upper right), lesion counts per study at baseline and follow-up (lower left), and lesion volume distributions on a logarithmic scale (lower right).

The data are organized into three splits: a training set of 240 patients (480 CT studies), a validation set of 30 patients (60 studies), and a test set of 30 patients (60 studies). The majority of studies (537 in total, 89.5%) contain a single CT acquisition volume, while 56 studies (9.3%) contain two acquisition volumes and 7 studies (1.2%) contain three. Multi-volume studies represent combined imaging protocols acquired for different body regions, such as dedicated head and neck scans in addition to standard whole-body acquisitions. All patients contain two imaging time points: at baseline (BL) and at follow-up (FU).

All in-house CT scans (520) were performed on Siemens scanners, including SOMATOM Sensation 64, SOMATOM Definition AS(+), SOMATOM Definition Flash, SOMATOM Force, and Biograph128 PET/CT, using a whole-body staging protocol. Examinations were conducted in the supine position with arms raised above the head. Standard inhouse procedure was portal-venous phase imaging with intravenous administration of iodinated contrast agent (Ultravist 370), injected in a body-weight-adapted manner via the cubital vein. Imaging parameters were standardized across scanners with a fixed tube voltage of 120 kV, reference tube current-time product of 240 mAs with attenuation-based modulation (CARE Dose), collimation of 64–128 × 0.6 mm, and a rotation time of 0.5 s. Pitch values ranged between 0.6 and 1.0. Images were reconstructed at 3-mm slice thickness and increment using a medium-smooth kernel to ensure consistent soft-tissue representation. No additional intensity normalization or resampling was applied beyond scanner reconstruction; volumes are provided in their native resolution. Although acquisition protocols were standardized, minor inter-scanner variability may persist. No explicit harmonization was applied, reflecting real-world clinical heterogeneity.

All CT volumes were de-identified using an anatomy-based defacing algorithm, which uses anatomical segmentation masks (brain, skull, body, clavicles) to identify and anonymize facial regions in CT scans. The defacing algorithm is provided as part of the dataset code repository (https://github.com/lab-midas/autoPETCTIV). CT volumes are provided in the NIfTI format. Data annotation

The data annotation process was performed in two phases: (1) Initial manual volumetric segmentation of primary tumor lesions and metastases by a radiologist with extensive expertise in oncologic imaging (FP) and (2) after a temporal delay to avoid recall bias a consensus reading review by two radiologists with extensive expertise in oncologic imaging (FP and AW).

The initial segmentation process involved freehand volumetric segmentation of all visible metastases on axial slices using the web-based annotation platform CuraMate (Fig. 3) (former SATORI, Fraunhofer MEVIS, Germany8). Lesion segmentation was exhaustive, i.e. all present lesions were segmented on all CT volumes. No minimum lesion size threshold was applied; all lesions deemed malignant by expert readers were segmented.

Example screenshot of the CuraMate annotation tool in review mode. Axial slices of baseline (left) and follow-up (right) CTs are shown side-by-side. A toolbox including brushing tools, zooming and level-windowing is provided on top. The selected tracked structure is highlighted in blue and all other lesions are shown in yellow. Warnings, tags and the review status per lesion are listed in the sidebar on the left.

The annotation platform presented baseline and follow-up CT volumes in a side-by-side view with slice synchronization based on a deformable image registration9. To avoid automation bias, lesion segmentation was performed from scratch, i.e. without algorithmic guidance. Segmentations were created as contours with sub-voxel accuracy with a brush tool and then voxelized. Geometric interpolation functionality was available to automatically generate contours on intermediate slices. Lesion location was selected manually from a predefined list and target lesions were selected according to RECIST criteria1.

Pulmonary metastases were segmented in the lung window, while osseous metastases were segmented in the bone window. For all other types of lesions, the preset window was set to soft tissue, although adjustments were permitted to enhance the visibility of subtle lesions (e.g., liver metastases).

Lesion correspondence between BL and FU studies was established manually by comparing BL and FU imaging side by side, with particular attention to anatomical sites (e.g. liver- or lung segments) and landmarks (e.g. vascular structures, nearby organs, distinct shapes, etc.). During this process, the annotator documented merging and splitting of lesions between BL and FU studies. Merging was defined as the correspondence of multiple distinct baseline lesions to a single lesion at follow-up, reflecting spatial convergence over time. Conversely, splitting was defined as a single baseline lesion corresponding to multiple distinct lesions at follow-up. These relationships were established manually by expert radiologists based on spatial proximity, anatomical context, and visual assessment of lesion continuity across timepoints.

Consensus reading review was performed after a temporal delay to minimize recall bias. The volumetric segmentations of the BL and FU images were displayed side by side. The reviewers were instructed to assess the accuracy and correspondence of the displayed volumetric segmentations, again restricted to axial slices. In line with the initial segmentation, pulmonary metastases were evaluated using a lung window, and osseous metastases using a bone window. For other lesion types, the preset soft tissue window was used, with the option for adjustments to enhance the visibility of subtle lesions.

To ensure a high dataset quality during annotation of FU studies, automatic quality controls were implemented. The annotator was warned in case of (1) missing connectivity, i.e. a lesion mask consists of multiple connected components; (2) missing tracking consistency, i.e. tracked lesions are not at similar relative positions based on the deformable registration result.

Dataset properties

Across all timepoints, the dataset contains a wide distribution of lesion counts and volumes, representative of the heterogeneity seen in metastatic melanoma. The median number of lesions per study is 7 (range 1–95). At baseline, the median lesion count is 8 (range 1–86), at follow-up 6 (range 1–95). The mean lesion volume across all studies was 7.99 ± 67.36 mL, corresponding to 7.26 ± 63.29 mL at baseline and 8.94 ± 72.36 mL at follow-up. At baseline, the lung was the most common metastatic site (32.1% of all lesions), followed by soft tissue/skin (21.3%), lymph nodes (20.8%), and the liver (14.7%). Less common sites included the skeleton, spleen, adrenals, and other organs (<5% each).

Data Records

The Longitudinal-CT dataset is organized to facilitate use in both deep learning and traditional image-analysis workflows. All data are hosted on the FDAT Research Data Repository of the University of Tübingen and are available under the accession 10.57754/FDAT.75kj1-6474710. The dataset is released under a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) license. The dataset associated with this DOI represents a fixed, versioned release and will not be modified retrospectively. Each patient is identified by a unique ten-character anonymized ID, and all files belonging to that patient share this identifier as their prefix.

The data are divided into two main directories: inputsTr/ and targetsTr/. The inputsTr/ folder contains all baseline (identified as “BL”) and follow-up (identified as “FU”) CT volumes (NifTI format,.nii.gz), corresponding lesion masks for baseline studies (NifTI format,.nii.gz), lesion-center annotations in JSON format (corresponding to lesion center of gravity derived from the segmentation mask), and per-patient CSV files summarizing lesion-level metadata. The targetsTr/ folder provides the corresponding manually generated lesion masks for follow-up studies (NifTI format,.nii.gz), whereby the lesions have unique integer values (lesion_ids) in the lesion mask.

Each CSV file contains per-lesion metadata, including the lesion identifier (lesion_id), three-dimensional coordinates of the lesion center at baseline and follow-up (cog_bl, cog_fu), anatomical site (lesion_type), topology class describing temporal evolution (UNCHANGED, DISAPPEARING, MERGING, NEWLYAPPEARING) as well as baseline and follow-up lesion volumes (volume_bl, volume_fu). The JSON files describe the lesion location in pixel coordinates and follow the Grand Challenge annotation format, ensuring interoperability with commonly used platforms such as MONAI, nnU-Net, and 3D Slicer.

Technical Validation

To demonstrate dataset usability for automated lesion segmentation, a baseline experiment was conducted using the nnU-Net v2 framework11 with its default configuration. This experiment is intended solely to demonstrate dataset usability and does not represent a competitive benchmark. The model was trained on the training subset, validated on the held-out validation set, and evaluated on the independent test set. For this baseline experiment, timepoints were treated as individual volumes and multivolume studies were treated as separate volumes. On the test set, the model achieved a mean Dice score per study of 0.328 ± 0.279 and a mean lesion detection sensitivity per study of 0.486 ± 0.302. The model predicted an average of 27.8 ± 18.7 false positives per study (median 22, range 3–113) and 6.1 ± 7.5 false negatives per study (median 5, range 0–32) (Figs. 4, 5). These results demonstrate the challenges around automated detection and segmentation of metastatic lesions on whole-body CT and the importance of providing public datasets for further research.

The baseline nnU-Net experiment treats baseline and follow-up scans as independent volumes to provide a simple and reproducible reference for dataset usability. The dataset is specifically designed to support advanced longitudinal modeling, enabling the scientific community to address the challenging problem of temporal lesion analysis, as exemplified by its use in the AutoPET IV Challenge 2025 (https://autopet-iv.grand-challenge.org/).

Usage Notes

The dataset can be viewed and processed using open-source software such as 3D Slicer or the Medical Imaging Interaction Toolkit (MITK). For computational analysis, CT volumes and masks can be loaded directly with Python libraries such as nibabel or SimpleITK. The dataset adheres to FAIR data principles, ensuring accessibility, interoperability, and reusability.
