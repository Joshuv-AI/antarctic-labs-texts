Incidental left gastric artery aneurysm during adrenal mass evaluation treated with endovascular coil embolization
Authors: Jacquelyn Fields, Daniel Villegas, Ryan Rimer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeft gastric artery aneurysms (LGAAs) are rare but potentially life-threatening vascular abnormalities often discovered incidentally during imaging for unrelated conditions. We report the case of a 54-year-old woman with a right adrenal mass in whom a partially thrombosed 2-cm LGAA was identified during preoperative evaluation. Endocrine studies confirmed a nonfunctioning adrenal adenoma. The patient successfully underwent endovascular coil embolization of the LGAA prior to her planned adrenalectomy, with only a minor access-site hematoma as a postoperative complication. This case underscores the importance of early recognition and timely intervention of visceral artery aneurysms, particularly when additional abdominal surgery is anticipated, and adds to the limited literature on diagnosis and management of LGAA.Keywords: Left gastric artery aneurysm, Visceral artery aneurysm, Coil embolization, Interventional radiology, Endovascular treatment, Fusiform aneurysm

Introduction

Visceral artery aneurysms are rare entities, accounting for less than 1% of all arterial aneurysms, with splenic and hepatic arteries representing the majority of cases [1]. Among them, left gastric artery aneurysms (LGAAs) are particularly uncommon, representing less than 4% of visceral artery aneurysms, and have historically been associated with high rupture-related mortality rates reported up to 70%-90% [2]. Because of their rarity, diagnosis is often delayed, and many patients present only after rupture.

Reported symptoms of visceral artery aneurysms, including LGAAs, may include epigastric pain, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, and hematemesis, although many lesions remain asymptomatic until rupture [3]. With the widespread use of multiphase computed tomography angiography (CTA), incidental, asymptomatic aneurysms are now being detected at earlier stages [4]. Management options depend on aneurysm size, symptomatology, and patient comorbidities. Current treatments include endovascular coil embolization, stent-graft placement, and, less commonly, open surgical ligation or aneurysmectomy [5]. Endovascular therapy has become the preferred first-line approach due to lower morbidity and high technical success rates.

Here, we present a case of an incidentally discovered left gastric artery aneurysm identified during preoperative imaging for evaluation of a known right adrenal lesion. The aneurysm was successfully treated with endovascular coil embolization.

Case report

A 54-year-old woman presented with intermittent abdominal pain and melena. Her past medical history included hypertension (managed with losartan and hydrochlorothiazide), hyperlipidemia (on ezetimibe), and hypothyroidism well controlled with levothyroxine. She also had a history of internal hemorrhoids identified on colonoscopy on January 24, 2023, which had remained stable without active bleeding. She was a former cigarette smoker and denied prior gastrointestinal bleeding, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, or anticoagulant use. The patient provided informed consent for publication of her clinical information and imaging.

A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) abdomen/pelvis performed on December 27, 2024 demonstrated a partially calcified 3.6 × 2.0 cm right adrenal mass. Further characterization with magnetic resonance (MRI) of the abdomen with and without contrast on March 21, 2025 revealed a 3.6 × 2.0 cm right adrenal nodule consistent with an adrenal adenoma (Fig. 1). The MRI additionally identified an incidental 2-cm partially thrombosed fusiform aneurysm of the left gastric artery (LGA).

Coronal T2-weighted (A) and T1-weighted (B) MRI demonstrating a right adrenal lesion. The lesion was subsequently characterized as a nonfunctioning adrenal adenoma during endocrine evaluation.

As part of the adrenal workup, 24-hour urine metanephrines obtained on May 21, 2025 showed metanephrine 57 µg/d, normetanephrine 241 µg/d, and total metanephrines 298 µg/d, values not consistent with catecholamine-secreting pathology. Urinary free cortisol was 89.9 µg/d, within the upper reference range but without biochemical evidence of hypercortisolism. Overall, there was no indication of a functional adrenal tumor, and the lesion was managed as a nonfunctioning adenoma pending surgical resection.

Baseline laboratory testing on September 18, 2025 demonstrated hemoglobin 14.2 g/dL, hematocrit 43.1%, normal platelet count 282 k/mm3, International Normalized Ratio (INR) 0.96, Prothrombin Time (PT) 10.0 seconds, and activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT) 22 seconds. Comprehensive metabolic panel values were unremarkable aside from mild hypokalemia (K⁺ 3.2 mmol/L), which was corrected prior to the planned intervention. Renal and hepatic function were within normal limits.

During continued evaluation, a PET/CT on June 12, 2025 again demonstrated a lobulated enhancing lesion in the upper abdomen with radiotracer uptake similar to background vasculature, correlating with prior CT and MRI findings of an LGA aneurysm (Fig. 2). Given the risk of rupture, the patient was referred to Vascular and Interventional Radiology (VIR) for definitive management prior to adrenal surgery.

Axial noncontrast CT demonstrating a fusiform left gastric artery aneurysm. Caliper measurements indicate a 2.0-cm partially thrombosed aneurysm arising from the left gastric artery.

On September 22, 2025, the patient underwent endovascular coil embolization of the LGA aneurysm. Under conscious sedation, the celiac artery and left gastric artery were catheterized using standard 5F catheters and a microcatheter system. Selective angiography confirmed a fusiform aneurysm of the LGA, which was successfully embolized using detachable microcoils (Boston Scientific and Terumo). Final angiography demonstrated complete exclusion of the aneurysm with preserved perfusion of surrounding vasculature (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Total fluoroscopy time was 39 minutes, and radiation dose was 352 mGy. Hemostasis was achieved with an Angio-Seal closure device.

Diagnostic angiography of the left gastric artery. Selective angiography demonstrates a fusiform aneurysm of the left gastric artery with focal dilation of the mid-segment, without evidence of rupture or contrast extravasation. The aneurysm morphology and arterial anatomy are well delineated prior to endovascular intervention.

Endovascular coil embolization of a left gastric artery aneurysm. (A) Initial post-deployment angiogram following partial coil placement demonstrates reduced aneurysmal opacification. (B) Further coil deployment results in near-complete exclusion of the aneurysm sac. (C) Completion angiography confirms complete aneurysm occlusion with preserved flow in adjacent vessels and no evidence of contrast extravasation or non-target embolization.

The postoperative course was complicated by right groin pain and swelling, prompting an emergency department visit on September 30, 2025. CTA of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a 2.2 × 2.0-cm soft-tissue hematoma at the right femoral access site without pseudoaneurysm. The adrenal lesion remained stable, and the embolization coils were appropriately positioned along the lesser curvature of the stomach (Fig. 5). Urinalysis was positive for a urinary tract infection, and she was treated with oral cephalexin. She was discharged in stable condition with outpatient VIR follow-up.

Follow-up contrast-enhanced CT after embolization. (A) Coronal and (B) sagittal contrast-enhanced CT images demonstrate stable positioning of embolization coils along the lesser curvature of the stomach (circle), corresponding to the treated left gastric artery aneurysm. No interval aneurysm enlargement, contrast extravasation, or surrounding hematoma is identified.

The patient was reevaluated on October 6 and October 20, 2025, demonstrating progressive improvement in groin swelling and pain with near-complete resolution of ecchymosis. She remained clinically well, and 6-month surveillance imaging was recommended. She is scheduled to undergo surgical management of the right adrenal adenoma following completion of her VIR follow-up.

Discussion

VAAs are rare vascular abnormalities, accounting for less than 1% of all arterial aneurysms [6]. Among these, LGAAs are exceptionally uncommon, representing only 2%-4% of VAAs [7]. Despite their rarity, LGAAs carry a high risk of rupture, with reported mortality rates up to 70%, making timely diagnosis and management essential [8].

Visceral artery aneurysms, including LGAAs, may represent true aneurysms associated with atherosclerosis, medial degeneration, or connective tissue disorders, or pseudoaneurysms secondary to inflammation, pancreatitis, trauma, or prior surgery [9]. In this case, imaging demonstrated a partially thrombosed fusiform true aneurysm measuring 2 cm, without identifiable secondary cause. Many LGAAs remain asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally, as occurred in our patient during imaging for an unrelated adrenal lesion. When symptoms do occur, they are nonspecific and may include epigastric pain, nausea, or gastrointestinal hemorrhage [3].

Cross-sectional imaging plays a pivotal role in the identification and characterization of LGAAs. CTA and MRI are highly sensitive for detecting aneurysms and assessing thrombosis, morphology, and adjacent structures [10]. Although angiography remains the diagnostic gold standard, it is most often performed in conjunction with endovascular therapy [10]. In our patient, the aneurysm was initially detected by MRI and later confirmed on PET/CT, consistent with an arterial structure.

Management guidelines for LGAAs are based largely on expert consensus due to the limited number of cases described in the literature. Treatment is generally recommended for symptomatic aneurysms, those >2 cm in diameter (depending on vessel location), enlarging aneurysms, and aneurysms at high risk of rupture [11]. In this case, coil embolization was performed prior to the patient’s adrenalectomy to mitigate the risk of intraoperative rupture.

Endovascular therapy has become the preferred first-line treatment for most visceral aneurysms due to its minimally invasive nature, high technical success rates, and lower morbidity compared with open surgical repair [12]. Techniques include coil embolization, covered stent placement, or parent artery occlusion depending on vascular anatomy and collateral pathways. Our patient underwent successful coil embolization with complete exclusion of the aneurysm and preserved adjacent perfusion. Follow-up imaging confirmed appropriate coil positioning.

Access-site complications, such as hematoma or pseudoaneurysm, occur in up to 5% of endovascular procedures and are typically self-limited [13]. The patient’s small femoral access-site hematoma was managed conservatively with good recovery. She remains clinically stable on follow-up, and 6-month surveillance imaging is recommended, consistent with standard practice for postembolization monitoring [4].

This case highlights the importance of recognizing incidental visceral aneurysms and underscores the value of early endovascular treatment, particularly when additional intra-abdominal surgery is planned. With fewer than 50 LGAA cases reported in the literature, each case contributes important information regarding optimal diagnostic evaluation and therapeutic decision-making for this rare but potentially life-threatening condition.

Conclusion

Left gastric artery aneurysms are exceptionally rare vascular lesions with a high potential for rupture and significant associated mortality. As demonstrated in this case, they may be discovered incidentally during imaging for unrelated pathology and can remain clinically silent despite their risk profile. Early recognition and timely intervention are essential, particularly when additional intra-abdominal surgery is anticipated, as operative stress may further increase the likelihood of rupture.

Endovascular coil embolization offers a safe, effective, and minimally invasive treatment option with high technical success and rapid recovery. Our patient’s favorable outcome underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving radiology, surgery, endocrinology, and interventional radiology in the evaluation and management of complex vascular and adrenal pathology. With relatively few left gastric artery aneurysms reported in the literature, this case adds to the evolving understanding of optimal diagnostic strategies and reinforces the role of preoperative endovascular therapy to mitigate perioperative risk.
