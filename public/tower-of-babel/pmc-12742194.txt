Mutation patterns and prognostic potentiality of homologous recombination repair genes in gliomas
Authors: Hongmin Bai, Yaqin Liu, Cheng Ji, Qin Zhang, Ningning Luo, Xing Zhang, Zhiming Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGliomas are primary malignant brain tumors with poor prognosis. Conventional therapies have achieved limited improvements, and there is an urgent need for new treatments. Homologous recombination repair (HRR) defects are observed in many types of cancer and confer increased sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapies and poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. To our knowledge, HRR mutations in gliomas have not been extensively reported. To this end, we analyzed alterations in 39 HRR related genes and divided patients with glioma into different groups according to HRR LOF mutation status. We then analyzed the differences in genetic mutation landscape, TMB, CNV burden, prognosis, and immune-related features between the HRR-LOF group and the NonHRR-LOF group. The results demonstrated that compared to the non-HRR-LOF group, the HRR-LOF group exhibited a higher mutation frequency of IDH1, increased TMB, and poor prognosis in IDH-mutant patients. In summary, gliomas patients with HRR LOF have relatively more unstable genomes and might benefit from the combination and sequential use of PARP inhibitors, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15314-2.Keywords: Gliomas, Homologous recombination repair, PARP inhibitors, Radiotherapy, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Gliomas represent the most common type of primary malignant brain tumors, exhibiting diverse disease development, treatment responsiveness, and prognostic outcomes based on their varied histological and molecular characteristics [1]. The intratumoral heterogeneity of glioma and the plasticity of malignant cell states pose significant challenges for therapeutic development [2]. Despite advancements in the management of gliomas, the clinical outcomes of patients remain suboptimal [3], underscoring the critical need for additional studies to evaluate novel therapeutic strategies.

Homologous recombination repair (HRR) is a vital pathway for repairing double-stranded DNA breaks [4]. Mutations in HRR pathway genes may result in homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) [5, 6], which is implicated in the development of various cancer types, such as endometrial, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers [4, 7]. Tumors with HRD are often more sensitively to poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitions and platinum-based chemotherapy [8]. This feature has historically promoted the development of treatment strategies for high incidence HRD tumors, represented by ovarian cancer and breast cancer [9, 10]. However, the potential correlation between the molecular characteristics of the HRR pathway and glioma remains underexplored.

In this study, 1352 glioma samples (our cohort) were enrolled to investigate the molecular characteristics of tumors with HRR loss of function (LOF). Additionally, we included 1094 glioma patients (TCGA cohort) to analyze the correlation between HRR LOF and survival outcomes. Furthermore, we explored the immune microenvironment characteristics of HRR LOF patients through the analysis of immune related gene expression, immune-related signatures and immune cell characteristics.

Materials and methods

Patients and data sources

A total of 1,352 glioma patient samples were analyzed using the NeuroOnco 360 panel (Simcere Diagnostics, Inc., Nanjing, China), which encompasses 353 brain tumor-related genes and common chromosomal structural variants. The Whole-exome sequencing (WES) data of 1094 patients with glioma were extracted from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov). Additionally, gene expression data were retrieved and downloaded from the TCGA database portal. The selection of patients for our analysis was ethically approved, and written informed consent was waived for this retrospective analysis.

DNA extraction and library preparation

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) testing for the panel was conducted at SimcereDx (Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples using the QIAamp® FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The purified tissue DNA was quantified using the Qubit DsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit and Qubit fluorometer 4.0 (Life Technologies, USA). Genomic DNA libraries were constructed with the KAPA HyperPlus Kit (Roche, Swiss), with an enzymatic fragmentation reaction time optimized at 17 min. All DNA libraries were captured using a custom-designed panel targeting 353 brain tumor-related genes and common chromosomal structural variants (Simcere Diagnostics, Inc., Nanjing, China). The prepared DNA libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 150 bp paired end reads.

Data processing

A custom pipeline was developed for reading alignment, variant calling, fusion detection, CNV identification, and quality control. Fastp (v.2.20.0) was utilized for adapter and quality trimming. Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using BWA-mem (v.0.7.17). VarDict (v.1.5.7) was employed for detecting SNVs and Indels, whereas InterVar was utilized for functional annotation [11]. CNVs and chromosome (arm) analyses were performed using the CNVkit (dx1.1).

Fusions were identified by an in-house developed software named SA-Fuse (v1.2.3). Additional filtering and inspection of somatic mutations, CNVs, and fusion results were conducted using custom scripts. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) was defined as the total number of somatic, coding, base substitution, and indel mutations per megabase in the analyzed genome. CNV burden was defined as the total length of autosomal CNVs divided by all autosomal lengths.

HRR LOF mutation

Based on previous studies, 39 HRR-related genes (Supplemental Table 1) were screened for further analysis, including ATM, ATRX, BARD1, BRCA1/2, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK1, CHEK2, and FANCL, etc [12–14]. The 1352 glioma patients in our cohort were divided into three groups: Germline HRR-LOF group, Somatic HRR-LOF group and NonHRR-LOF group. Patients (n = 83) carried the HRR-related pathogenic or likely pathogenic germline mutations, as annotated by the Clinvar were classified into the Germline HRR-LOF group. The Somatic HRR-LOF group (n = 497) included patients with LOF somatic variants (frameshift, splice_acceptor, splice_donor, stop gained, stop_lost, etc.), as defined in prior studies [15–18]. The remaining 772 patients were assigned to the NonHRR-LOF group. The 1094 glioma patients in the TCGA cohort were divided into Somatic HRR-LOF group (n = 369) and Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group (n = 725).

RNA expression profile analysis

CIBERSORT analysis, utilizing RNA-seq data from the TCGA database, was conducted to evaluate the infiltration status of 22 types of immune cells [19]. Gene expression data were retrieved from the TCGA database, and differential gene expression analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 [20]. Immune-related genes from Danaher et al. [21] were used for differential expression analysis between glioma patients with and without somatic HRR LOF mutations in the TCGA cohort.

Statistical analysis

Mutation patterns for each subgroup were analyzed using ComplexHeatmap or maftools (R package). The association between HRR LOF status and TMB and copy number variation (CNV) burden was examined using the Wilcoxon test. Differences in mutation frequency of differential genes among the HRR-LOF groups and NonHRR-LOF group were assessed using the Fishier test. Survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves, with P values determined by the log-rank test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.3 (http://www.r-project.org).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the patients in our cohort

There was no significant difference in the proportion of patients aged < 65 years old among the three groups: 90.36% (75/83) in the Germline HRR-LOF group, 89.34% (444/497) in the Somatic HRR-LOF group, and 87.95% (679/772) in the NonHRR-LOF group (p = 0.657). The treatment status did not differ significantly among the three groups (p = 0.341). The proportion of IDH1 or IDH2 gene mutations was significantly lower in the Non-HRR-LOF group (13.99%, 108/772) compared to the Somatic HRR-LOF group (43.36%, 216/497) and the Germline HRR-LOF group (25.30%, 21/83) (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Mutational landscape in HRR-LOF and NonHRR-LOF groups

The mutational landscape was comprehensively analyzed across three aspects: single nucleotide variation (SNV)/Indel, CNV and fusion. In the Somatic HRR-LOF group, TP53 exhibited the highest SNV/indel mutation frequency at 85%, followed by ATRX (45%), IDH1 (41%), MUC16 (19%), and PTEN (18%) (Fig. 1A, Supplemental Fig. 1 A). For the NonHRR-LOF group, PTEN had the highest SNV mutation frequency at 17%, succeeded by ARID1B (13%), IDH1 (13%), NF1 (12%), and EGFR (11%) (Fig. 1B, Supplemental Fig. 1B). In the germline HRR-LOF group, the top five genes with the highest mutation frequencies were NF1 (28%), TP53(28%), ARID1B(23%), IDH1 (23%), PIK3CA (22%) (Fig. 1C, Supplemental Fig. 1 C). In the Non-HRR-LOF group, the distribution of genes with higher mutation frequencies varied between the IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype groups. However, this phenomenon was not observed in either of the HRR-LOF groups (Supplemental Fig. 1 A, B, C).

Top 10 mutational genes in HRR-LOF group, NonHRR-LOF group and Germline HRR-LOF group. A Top 10 mutational genes in Somatic HRR-LOF group. B Top 10 mutational genes in NonHRR-LOF group. C Top 10 mutational genes in Germline HRR-LOF group

The CNV profiles of the three groups were largely consistent, with common high-frequency mutant genes including deletions of CDKN2B, CDKN2A, and MTAP, as well as amplifications of CDK4 and EGFR (Fig. 1A, B, C, Supplemental Fig. 1D, E, F). The genes harboring high-frequency fusion mutations varied among the three groups, yet EGFR fusion was a common feature in all of them (Fig. 1A, B, C, Supplemental Fig. 1G, H, I).

Differential gene mutation frequency analysis between HRR-LOF and NonHRR-LOF groups

Genes with significant differences in mutation frequency were further analyzed among the Somatic HRR-LOF group, Germline HRR-LOF group and NonHRR-LOF group. Compared to the NonHRR-LOF group, both the Somatic HRR-LOF and Germline HRR-LOF groups exhibited a relatively large number of differentially mutated genes in terms of SNV/Indel, with 266 and 243 genes, respectively (Supplemental Table 2 and 3). In contrast, the number of differentially mutated genes between the Somatic HRR-LOF and Germline HRR-LOF groups was relatively small, with only 2 genes identified (Supplemental Table 4). Given the greater similarities between the Germline HRR-LOF and Somatic HRR-LOF groups, we speculated that the carcinogenic mechanisms of these two groups might be similar.

In HRR-related genes, the mutation frequencies of TP53 (85% vs 4%, p < 0.001) and ATRX (45% vs 2%, p < 0.001) in the Somatic HRR-LOF group were significantly higher than those in the NonHRR-LOF group (Fig. 2A). A similar pattern was observed when comparing the Germline HRR-LOF group with the Non-HRR-LOF group (Supplemental Fig. 2 A). Among non-HRR but glioma related genes, IDH1 was one of the most significant differential mutated genes (41% vs 13%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). In the comparison between the Germline HRR-LOF and Non-HRR-LOF groups, the mutation frequency of IDH1 also showed a statistically significant difference (23% vs 13%, p < 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Additionally, compared to the NonHRR-LOF group, the mutation frequencies of NF1 (p < 0.05) and PDGFRA (p < 0.01) were significantly higher in both the Somatic HRR-LOF and Germline HRR-LOF groups, while the mutation frequency of BRAF (p < 0.001) was significantly lower in the Somatic HRR-LOF group.

Regarding CNV, the Non-HRR-LOF group exhibited significantly higher EGFR amplification (p < 0.001) and significantly lower PDGFRA amplification (p < 0.001) compared to the Somatic HRR-LOF group (Fig. 1C-D, Supplemental Table 5). Moreover, MET and MYC amplifications were significantly higher in the Somatic HRR-LOF and Germline HRR-LOF groups compared to the Non-HRR-LOF group (Supplemental Table 5). In terms of chromosomal structural variations, the Somatic HRR-LOF group showed more significant differences, such as chr1_p_Whole.arm.Loss (p < 0.001), chr1_q25_Gain (p = 0.028), chr10_pq_Whole.arm.Loss (p = 0.002), chr13_q_Whole.arm.Loss (p < 0.001) and chr7_pq_Whole.arm.Gain (p < 0.001), compared with the NonHRR-LOF group (Supplemental Table 6).

Furthermore, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) clustering analysis was conducted on non-HRR differentially genes with SNV/Indel. The GO analysis indicated that more differential genes were enriched in DNA-binding transcription factor binding, protein tyrosine kinase activity, RNA polymerase II-specific and DNA-binding transcription factor binding (Fig. 2C). The KEGG analysis results revealed that PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MicroRNAs in cancer, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance and Rap1 signaling pathway enriched the largest number of differential genes (Fig. 2D). The results of differential gene and pathway enrichment in comparison between the Germline HRR-LOF group and NonHRR-LOF group were similar to those described above (Supplemental Fig. 2).

HRR LOF was associated with TMB, CNV burden and prognosis

We compared the differences in TMB, CNV burden and microsatellite instability (MSI) status among different groups.

In our cohort, both the Somatic HRR-LOF group (p < 0.001) and the Germline HRR-LOF group (p < 0.001) exhibited significantly higher TMB than the NonHRR-LOF group, with the Somatic HRR-LOF group having the highest TMB (p = 0.038) (Fig. 3A). We examined the differences in TMB among the three groups in IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype patients and found that the trend in IDH-wildtype patients was consistent with the overall pattern (Supplemental Fig. 3 A). However, in IDH-mutant patients, neither the Somatic HRR-LOF group (p = 0.61) nor the Germline HRR-LOF group (p = 0.076) showed a statistically significant difference in TMB compared with the Non-HRR-LOF group (Supplemental Fig. 3B).

B Differential analysis of CNV burden among the three groups in our cohort. C Differential analysis of TMB between the two groups (Somatic HRR-LOF group and Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group) in the TCGA cohort. D Kaplan–Meier survival curves of OS between the Somatic HRR-LOF group and the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group in different IDH status in the TCGA cohort. There was no significant difference in CNV burden between the Somatic and Germline HRR-LOF groups (p = 0.85), but both had significantly higher CNV burden than the Non-HRR-LOF group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). In IDH-wildtype patients, the CNV burden trend among the three groups was consistent with the overall pattern (Supplemental Fig. 3 C). However, in IDH-mutant patients, there were no statistically significant differences in CNV burden among the three groups (Supplemental Fig. 3D).

The occurrence of MSI-H was generally low, with only 8 cases in the Somatic HRR-LOF group and 5 cases in the Germline HRR-LOF group. Notably, there were no MSI-H cases in the NonHRR-LOF group (Supplemental Table 7).

In the TCGA cohort, there was no significant difference in TMB between the Somatic HRR-LOF group and the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group (p = 0.052) (Fig. 3C). However, the Somatic HRR-LOF group had significantly higher TMB than the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group in IDH-wildtype patients (p = 0.0017) and in IDH-mutant patients (p = 0.00035) (Supplemental Fig. 4A-B).

Differences in RNA expressions between the Somatic HRR-LOF group and the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group in the TCGA database. Comparison of 17 immune-related signatures (A), 22 predefined immune cells (B) and the expression of 39 immune-related genes (C) between the Somatic HRR-LOF group and the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group

Importantly, a significant difference in the overall survival (OS) was observed between the different HRR and IDH status groups in the TCGA database (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3D). The patients with IDH-mutant showed a trend toward longer OS in the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group (p = 0.015), but this correlation was not observed in IDH-wildtype patients (p = 0.82) (Supplemental Fig. 4C-D). Further multivariate analysis revealed that HRR status was independently correlated with OS (p = 0.036) in IDH-mutant patients (Fig. 3E), but this correlation was not observed in the IDH-wildtype patients (p = 0.829) (Supplemental Fig. 4E).

Immune microenvironment of HRR LOF glioma

Gene expression data from glioma patients were downloaded from TCGA, and immune-related signatures were assessed. The results indicated that the immune-related signatures in the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group were positively associated with immunotherapy and generally higher than in the Somatic HRR-LOF group, including CYT Score, CTL levels and Immune signature (Fig. 4A). We evaluated the abundance of 22 predefined immune cell types and found that the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group had a significantly higher proportion of CD8 T cells, Macrophages M1 cells and Neutrophils cells (Fig. 4B).

Additionally, the expression levels of the immune checkpoint inhibitor pathway-related targets were generally higher in the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group than in the Somatic HRR-LOF group, such as CCL5, CD27, CXCL10, CXCL9 and CXCL11 (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest significant differences in immune escape, immune activation, and immune regulation mechanisms between the Somatic HRR-LOF and the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF groups.

Discussion

Our study categorized glioma patients into distinct groups based on the presence of HRR LOF mutations. We analyzed differences between the HRR-LOF and non-HRR-LOF groups in terms of mutational landscape, TMB, CNV burden, prognosis, and immune-related features. To our knowledge, this study represents the first exploration of the genetic mutation landscape in HRR LOF gliomas. Our results suggest that genome of the HRR-LOF group is more unstable, and the HRR-LOF group and the NonHRR-LOF group may have significantly different carcinogenic mechanisms and immune microenvironment regulatory systems. Research on the association between glioma and the HRR pathway can provide direction for the treatment of patients with glioma.

Malignant gliomas, particularly GBM, IDH-wildtype, have a poor prognosis [22]. Standard therapies are only available for patients with MGMT promoter hypermethylation, and currently, there is no established standard treatment for those with recurrent GBM. Therefore, there is an urgent need for more effective targeted treatment options. Targeted therapies, which are based on biomarker detection throughout the management of glioma patients, are indeed ideal tools for precision and personalized medicine [23].

Tumors with HRR deficiency exhibit increased sensitivity to both DNA-damaging agents and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. PARP inhibitors are currently approved for treating breast cancer, castration-resistant metastatic prostate cancer, ovarian cancer, and metastatic pancreatic cancer [24, 25]. Early-phase studies have demonstrated promising anti-tumor activity of PARP inhibitors in gliomas, which should be further validated in larger, prospective, and randomized trials [26]. The HRR deficiency in H3K27M DMG can be exploited with PARP inhibitors to enhance radiosensitivity and selectively induce an NK cell-mediated antitumor immune response in H3K27M DMG [27].

In our study, the HRR-LOF groups carried significantly higher mutation frequencies of TP53, ATRX and IDH1, compared to the NonHRR-LOF group. In previous studies, IDH1 mutations were considered positive prognostic markers and carry the greatest prognostic significance in adult gliomas patients [28]. It was also reported in gliomas that the ATRXmut/IDH1mut group had a better prognosis compared to the ATRXwt/IDH1wt [29]. In our study, we found that IDH-mutant patients showed a trend toward longer OS in the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group than in the Somatic HRR-LOF group (p = 0.015). Further multivariate analysis revealed that HRR status was independently and positively correlated with OS (p = 0.036) in IDH-mutant patients.

The majority of IDH-wildtype patients have received radiation therapy, and studies have shown that HRR mutations may increase sensitivity to radiotherapy. For instance, HRR-deficient p53abn endometrial cancer (EC) cannot repair radiation-induced double-strand breaks, potentially leading to increased radiosensitivity [30]. The HRR-LOF groups exhibited significantly higher TMB and CNV burden than the Non-HRR-LOF group, especially in IDH-wildtype patients, indicating a relatively more unstable genome. Additionally, the analysis of RNA-Seq data indicated that the immune microenvironment status of the Somatic HRR-LOF group and the Non-Somatic HRR-LOF group was significantly different. Additionally, radiotherapy can improve and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment, transforming it into a “hot” tumor [31]. It has been proven that DNA damage repair (DDR) alterations, including the HRR pathway, significantly contribute to reshaping the glioma microenvironment by modulating the immune response and cytokine pathways [13]. HRR deficiency is sensitive to PARP inhibitors and may also be sensitive to radiotherapy, while PARP inhibitors can enhance the effectiveness of radiotherapy. Furthermore, studies have underscored the correlation of HRR with TMZ resistance in glioma treatment [32]. Therefore, for gliomas with HRR LOF, the combination and sequential use of PARP inhibitors, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy could be a promising approach to improve patient outcomes, warranting further exploration in the future.

Limitations of this study include the reliance solely on DNA-based NGS testing, lacking assessment of the impact of protein-level alterations on protein function. In addition, since CNVs vary greatly in length and copy number, and the accuracy of existing detection results also differs [33, 34]. Furthermore, large-scale studies are necessary to further assess the safety and efficacy of PARP inhibitors or combined therapies in glioma tumors with HRR-LOF.

Conclusion

Compared with patients in the NonHRR-LOF group, those in the HRR-LOF group exhibited a higher mutation frequency of IDH1, a higher TMB value and a greater CNV burden, especially among IDH-wildtype patients. Moreover, HRR status emerged as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in IDH-mutant patients. The distinctions between the HRR-LOF and NonHRR-LOF groups may signal the necessity for distinct therapeutic approaches for glioma patients. Research into the correlation between glioma and the HRR pathway can offer guidance for the treatment of patients with glioma.
