From association to causation: a decision-aware framework for reproducible biomarker discovery and precision intervention design in the human gut microbiome
Authors: AbdulAziz Ascandari, Suleiman Aminu, Rachid Benhida, Daoud Rachid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman gut microbiome research has generated many disease associations, yet few translate into clinical applications. A central obstacle is not a lack of data, but the limited integration of causal reasoning, as most studies report correlations without establishing directionality, confounding control, or mechanistic evidence. We propose a unified causal inference framework that integrates directed acyclic graphs, Mendelian randomization, double machine learning, mediation analysis, and tests of causal reversibility into a single decision-aware workflow. Unlike prior applications of these tools in isolation, our framework explicitly separates assumption mapping, causal identification, effect estimation, and mechanistic interpretation, introducing “assumption guardrails” that constrain interpretation at each stage and prevent overinterpretation of observational findings. Using a colorectal cancer case study with public metagenomic data, we demonstrate how the framework operates under real-world constraints, transforming observational associations into testable, mechanism-based hypotheses. The contribution is architectural in that it organizes existing tools into a disciplined, integrated pipeline that clarifies the strength of evidence at each stage. This operational blueprint provides a reproducible path from correlation to causation in microbiome research and toward precision interventions.Keywords: biomarker discovery, causal inference, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), double machine learning (DML), microbiome, Mendelian randomization (MR), multi-omics, precision medicine

Introduction: from association to causation in human gut microbiome bioinformatics

Advances in high-throughput sequencing have enabled large-scale characterization of the human gut microbiome across diverse diseases [1–5]. Numerous associations between microbial taxa and conditions such as colorectal cancer (CRC), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and metabolic disorders have been reported. However, most findings remain observational and frequently lack reproducibility across cohorts, sequencing protocols, and populations [6–8]. As a result, microbiome research has largely progressed through association mapping, while translation toward mechanistic understanding and clinically actionable biomarkers has remained limited.

A central challenge is distinguishing microbial drivers of disease from downstream consequences or context-dependent bystanders. Microbiome studies are typically observational and therefore highly susceptible to confounding by diet, medication use, host genetics, lifestyle, and environmental exposures [9, 10]. In addition, microbiome data presents unique analytical constraints, including compositional structure, sparsity, and technical heterogeneity across sequencing pipelines. These challenges complicate causal interpretation and frequently lead to unstable or non-reproducible microbiome–disease associations [11–17].

Addressing these limitations requires analytical strategies capable of moving beyond correlation toward causal inference. Several methodological approaches have been applied in microbiome research, including directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to formalize causal assumptions [18], Mendelian randomization (MR) to leverage host genetic instruments, [19, 20], longitudinal models to infer temporal directionality [6, 21], and double machine learning (DML) for effect estimation in high-dimensional data [22, 23]. Mediation analysis further enables exploration of biological mechanisms linking microbial features to host outcomes [24, 25]. While these tools are individually powerful, they are often applied in isolation, making it difficult to interpret how different sources of evidence collectively support causal claims [26].

To address this gap, our contribution is primarily architectural rather than algorithmic. Here, we propose a unified causal inference framework that organizes these complementary methods into a structured, decision-aware workflow with explicit assumption guardrails at each stage, spanning assumption mapping, causal identification, effect estimation, mechanistic interpretation, and causal reversibility testing. This design transforms causal analysis from a collection of isolated techniques into a coherent and disciplined framework that constrains interpretation and reduces the risk of overstatement.

Thus, the novelty of the proposed framework, in contrast to prior studies, lies in its explicit and decision-aware integration of causal inference methods into a single system. It systematically defines the role and limitations of each analytical stage, thereby clarifying when causal claims are supported and preventing overinterpretation of observational microbiome data. We further demonstrate the operational application of the framework using publicly available colorectal cancer metagenomic datasets. Together, this approach provides a transparent and reproducible pathway from observational microbiome associations to testable causal hypotheses and potential intervention strategies.

Methods for causal inference in human gut microbiome research

Advancing from association to causation in human gut microbiome research requires integrating tools that formalize assumptions, establish directionality, estimate effects, and clarify mechanisms. DAG-based modeling, temporal and genetic identification strategies, DML, and mediation analysis fulfill these roles within a unified workflow. The following sections first outline the structural constraints of gut microbiome data and then describe how each layer contributes to a coherent causal framework.

Structural constraints in human gut microbiome causal inference

Causal inference in the human gut microbiome is uniquely complicated by strong host–environment interactions, dietary variability, and rapid ecological responsiveness. In this context, confounding is pervasive because factors such as diet, medication use, host genetics, geography, and lifestyle simultaneously influence both microbial composition and disease outcomes. Failure to account for these shared determinants can lead to spurious associations and unstable biomarkers across cohorts [7, 8, 10, 27].

Beyond confounding, gut microbiome data pose inherent structural challenges. Measured abundances are compositional, reflecting relative proportions rather than absolute quantities, which can induce spurious correlations and violate independence assumptions underlying conventional statistical models [28, 29]. Metagenomic datasets are also typically sparse and zero-inflated due to biological absence and technical detection limits [30]. In addition, batch effects introduced by sequencing protocols, DNA extraction procedures, and study design can generate systematic variability that obscures genuine biological signals and compromises reproducibility [7, 8].

Taken together, these constraints define the analytical landscape of human gut microbiome research. Rather than treating confounding, compositionality, sparsity, and technical heterogeneity as isolated problems, the unified framework addresses them as shared structural limitations that inform assumption mapping, causal identification, and effect estimation throughout the workflow.

Directed acyclic graphs: clarifying assumptions and causal structure

Effective causal inference in the human gut microbiome begins with explicit specification of causal assumptions. DAGs provide a formal framework for representing hypothesized relationships among host factors, microbial features, environmental exposures, and disease outcomes [18]. By encoding these assumptions graphically, DAGs identify admissible adjustment sets and distinguish confounders from mediators and colliders.

In the gut microbiome context, where host and environmental factors strongly influence both microbial composition and disease risk (Section 1.1), DAGs are particularly valuable. For example, in colorectal cancer, dietary fiber intake and antibiotic exposure may influence the abundance of Fusobacterium nucleatum, which may in turn contribute to tumor progression through immune modulation or epithelial adhesion [31]. Representing such relationships within a DAG clarifies whether microbial abundance reflects a causal driver, a downstream mediator, or residual confounding.

DAGs may be constructed from biological knowledge or informed by structure-learning algorithms such as PC, NOTEARS, or GES [32–35]. However, their validity depends on correct specification. Omitted variables, incorrect edge direction, or failure to account for compositional constraints can bias downstream inference. Within the unified framework, DAGs serve as the foundational layer, defining the structure upon which identification, estimation, and mediation analyses depend.

Mendelian randomization: genetic support for causal identification

Following assumption mapping, causal identification can be strengthened through MR. It leverages host genetic variants associated with microbial traits as instrumental variables to assess whether microbial features plausibly influence disease outcomes [20, 36]. Because genetic variants are assigned at conception, they are largely independent of many environmental confounders, provided instrumental-variable assumptions are satisfied.

Microbiome genome-wide association studies and microbial quantitative trait locus analyses have identified host genetic variants associated with microbial taxa and functional pathways in the human gut microbiome [37]. These variants can therefore be used to assess causal direction, for example supporting a microbe-to-disease relationship rather than reverse causation. However, microbial traits often exhibit modest heritability, which can limit instrument strength [19, 38]. Horizontal pleiotropy and population-specific genetic effects further constrain interpretation [39].

To address these limitations, sensitivity analyses such as MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO, multivariable MR, and colocalization testing are commonly employed [40–45]. Within the framework, MR functions specifically as an identification layer. It strengthens directional plausibility but does not estimate effect sizes or elucidate mechanisms. Its results therefore constrain, rather than replace, downstream effect estimation and mediation analyses.

Temporal and longitudinal models: establishing direction in dynamic gut ecosystems

The human gut microbiome is highly dynamic, responding rapidly to dietary shifts, antibiotic exposure, and host physiological changes [46, 47]. While MR provides population-level genetic support for directionality, it does not resolve within-individual temporal ordering. Temporal and longitudinal models address this gap by evaluating whether microbial changes precede, accompany, or follow disease-related shifts [21, 48].

Approaches such as Granger causality, sparse vector autoregressive models, and Bayesian dynamic networks have been applied to gut microbiome time series to infer directional relationships [49–51]. More recent methods accommodate irregular sampling, compositional structure, and high dimensionality [52, 53]. These tools extend beyond prediction to assess causal ordering grounded in ecological dynamics.

However, temporal inference remains sensitive to sampling frequency, sparsity, and time-varying confounding. Methods such as marginal structural models and dynamic treatment frameworks have therefore been proposed to address feedback between exposures and outcomes [54]. In the unified framework, temporal modeling provides complementary identification evidence that strengthens causal interpretation before effect estimation is undertaken.

Double machine learning: effect estimation in high-dimensional gut microbiome data

Once causal direction is plausibly supported, effect sizes can be estimated. DML offers a flexible approach for estimating causal effects in high-dimensional gut metagenomic and multi-omic datasets [22, 23]. By modeling nuisance relationships for both exposure and outcome using flexible learners and applying cross-fitting procedures, DML reduces bias arising from overfitting and complex confounding structures.

In the gut, where thousands of taxa, genes, and pathways may be correlated and sparse, DML is particularly suited for effect estimation. However, it does not establish causal direction and relies on the assumption that relevant confounders have been measured. Compositionality and zero inflation require appropriate preprocessing to avoid biased estimates [28].

Within the framework, DML is restricted to the estimation layer. When upstream identification remains incomplete, DML outputs are interpreted conservatively as prioritization signals rather than definitive causal claims.

Mediation analysis: linking microbial effects to host mechanisms

Beyond estimating whether microbial features influence disease, understanding how they exert effects is essential for translational relevance. Mediation analysis decomposes total effects into direct and indirect pathways mediated through metabolites, immune signals, or secondary microbial interactions [55, 56].

Mediators frequently include short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, inflammatory cytokines, and other host–microbe signaling pathways [57–67] in the human gut. High-dimensional mediation frameworks such as SparseMCMM and CRAmed have been developed to accommodate compositional and sparse microbiome data [24, 59].

Mediation analysis remains sensitive to unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding and collinearity among candidate mediators. However, when applied downstream of assumption mapping, identification, and effect estimation, it provides a mechanistic bridge between computational inference and experimental validation.

A unified causal inference framework for human gut microbiome research

Building on the methodological components described above, we integrate these elements into a unified causal inference framework for human gut microbiome. The key innovation of this framework lies in organizing complementary causal inference approaches into a decision-aware analytical workflow that explicitly separates assumption mapping, causal identification, effect estimation, mechanistic interpretation, and causal reversibility testing. This structure clarifies how different sources of evidence contribute to causal interpretation and helps prevent overinterpretation of observational microbiome associations. The framework therefore guides analyses from association through causal identification, effect estimation, mechanistic interpretation, and, when warranted, tests of causal reversibility within a coherent decision-aware architecture [56, 68–70].

As outlined in Box 1, the framework is structured around a core analytical sequence. Steps i–iv form the core analysis common to most microbiome studies. In contrast, downstream steps (v–viii) are conditional and pursued only when converging evidence supports a plausible causal signal.

Summary

An integrated causal framework is essential for advancing microbiome science from descriptive associations toward mechanistic understanding and translational relevance. By explicitly separating assumption mapping, identification, estimation, and validation, this framework supports robust, interpretable, and reproducible causal inference across diverse microbiome study designs.

Finally, experimental validation is not treated as a mandatory endpoint, but as a targeted follow-up informed by prior causal inference. When supported, interventions testing causal reversibility (Section 4) can be evaluated in appropriate experimental systems (Table 2) to assess biological plausibility and functional impact, rather than to retroactively establish causality. In this way, the unified framework enforces assumption-aware interpretation at each stage while maintaining flexibility across diverse data types and study designs.

Assumption guardrails

Each stage of the framework rests on explicit assumptions that constrain interpretation and determine the strength of causal claims in human gut microbiome studies. These assumptions function as guardrails for inference rather than as additional analytical steps, ensuring that conclusions remain proportional to the available evidence.

Cross-sectional analyses and assumption mapping (Steps i–ii): Interpretation depends on adequate measurement and adjustment of confounders, avoidance of collider bias, and appropriate handling of compositional microbiome data.

These assumptions can be partially assessed using F-statistics, colocalization analyses, and pleiotropy diagnostics.

Across all stages, sensitivity analyses, including E-values, negative controls, and robustness checks, are essential for evaluating stability and reproducibility. Explicit articulation and evaluation of these assumptions enhance transparency and foster trust in causal claims derived from human gut microbiome research.

Synthesis and implications

This illustrative use case demonstrates how the framework transforms a well-established microbiome–CRC association into a structured and testable causal program. Each stage constrains the next. For instance, DAGs define adjustment strategies, causal identification guides interpretation, DML estimates effects, mediation analysis clarifies mechanisms, and causal reversibility guides intervention design. The framework provides a decision-aware blueprint for causal inference in human gut microbiome research under realistic data constraints.

While Box 2 presents the framework in its ideal form, Section 2.2 applies it to a cross-cohort public-data example that reflects the practical limitations of many microbiome datasets. The purpose is to demonstrate, in a focused way, how the framework guides interpretation when key identification layers are unavailable and how its decision logic operates under realistic data constraints. A second case study was therefore not included because the objective is to illustrate the decision-aware structure of the framework through a single representative example rather than to benchmark performance across diseases.

Demonstrating the unified framework using public CRC microbiome data

Data and causal question

To illustrate the operational application of the unified framework, we harmonized publicly available shotgun metagenomic datasets from seven CRC case–control studies (n = 995 samples) [78–81]. Metadata for the datasets used in this demonstration, together with their corresponding references, are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Available host metadata included age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Consistent with the structural considerations discussed in Section 1.1, the abundance data were preprocessed before causal estimation. After adding a pseudocount, we applied a centered log-ratio transformation (CLR) transformation to account for compositionality, removed zero-variance taxa, and corrected batch effects using the limma function removeBatchEffect. The datasets were cross-sectional and did not include host genetic information or molecular mediator measurements which reflects common real-world constraints in microbiome research.

The causal question posed was: does increased abundance of F. nucleatum plausibly contribute to CRC risk?

A directed acyclic graph (DAG), shown in Fig. 2, was constructed using Dagitty to formalize the assumed causal structure for the real-data CRC demonstration [82]. Fusobacterium nucleatum abundance was specified as the exposure and CRC status as the outcome. Age, sex, and BMI were modeled as measured confounders and defined the minimal sufficient adjustment set used in downstream analysis. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), immune modulation, and adhesion signaling were included as hypothesized mediating pathways linking F. nucleatum to CRC. Although these mediators were not measured in the public datasets and were therefore not included in the estimation step, their inclusion in the DAG helped clarify the assumed biological structure.

Identification constraints

Because the datasets were cross-sectional and lacked host genetic or repeated-measures information, temporal direction could not be independently established and instrumental-variable approaches such as Mendelian randomization were not feasible. Causal interpretation therefore depended on the plausibility of the DAG-specified structure and prior experimental evidence. The resulting estimate was interpreted as an adjusted association conditional on measured confounders rather than as definitive evidence of causality. In settings where longitudinal or host genetic data are available, the same framework would allow stronger directional identification before effect estimation, as outlined in Step iii of Box 1. A full demonstration of temporal identification would require longitudinal microbiome datasets, which lie beyond the scope of this illustrative example but represent a natural extension of the framework.

Effect estimation and robustness

Double machine learning (DML) was implemented using the LinearDML estimator from the EconML Python package. CRC status was encoded as a binary outcome, CLR-transformed F. nucleatum abundance was treated as the exposure, and age, BMI, and sex were included as measured confounders. Outcome and exposure nuisance functions were modeled using random forest regressors (n_estimators = 200, min_samples_leaf = 10, random_state = 42), and estimation was performed using five-fold cross-fitting. In this procedure, the outcome and exposure are first modeled conditional on the measured confounders, after which orthogonalized residuals are used to estimate the association with reduced overfitting bias. The estimated effect was 0.026 (95% CI 0.021–0.032).

Bootstrap resampling (300 iterations) confirmed stability (bootstrap mean 0.026, 95% CI 0.019–0.032). As an internal falsification test, we repeated the analysis using Acidaminococcus intestini, a taxon without established involvement in CRC. No association was observed (bootstrap mean 0.0004, 95% CI −0.0035 to 0.0043), supporting exposure specificity.

Sensitivity analysis yielded an E-value of 1.19, indicating moderate susceptibility to unmeasured confounding. These results are interpreted cautiously as associations conditional on measured confounders.

Mechanistic context

Although mediators were not directly measured, experimental studies support plausible pathways linking F. nucleatum to CRC, including disruption of butyrate-producing communities, immune modulation, and activation of oncogenic signaling through epithelial adhesion [83–85]. Incorporating metabolomic or transcriptomic layers would enable formal mediation testing within this framework.

Causal reversibility

The present analysis does not permit direct testing of causal reversibility. Within the unified framework, reversibility requires targeted perturbation of the microbial exposure and evaluation of downstream disease effects. Experimental studies have shown that depletion of F. nucleatum in animal models reduces tumor burden and inflammatory signaling, providing external evidence consistent with reversibility [86–88]. However, such intervention-based validation lies beyond the scope of this observational dataset. This distinction reinforces a central principle of the framework that effect estimation can prioritize plausible drivers, but causal reversibility requires controlled experimental perturbation.

Summary

This demonstration shows how the framework can be applied to a typical cross-sectional microbiome dataset with limited covariates. It is not intended to establish definitive causality. Rather, it shows how the framework improves interpretation in practice by making the assumed causal structure explicit, defining the measured adjustment set, limiting the conclusion to an adjusted association when temporal and genetic identification are unavailable, and confirming exposure specificity through negative control analysis. Together, these steps ensure that the strength of the causal claim remains proportional to the available evidence. The application of this workflow to the real dataset is illustrated in Fig. 3, which summarizes the progression from data harmonization and preprocessing to DAG-based assumption mapping, constrained identification, effect estimation, and robustness analysis. Applying the framework to core challenges in human gut microbiome research

Having formalized the unified causal framework, we now examine how this architecture addresses recurring analytical challenges in human gut microbiome research. Rather than treating these challenges as isolated methodological problems, the framework links them to specific stages of causal inference, including assumption mapping, causal identification, effect estimation, mechanistic interpretation, and generalizability. In this way, the framework provides a structured approach for evaluating microbiome–disease associations while clarifying the strength and limitations of causal claims.

Temporality

Temporality represents a fundamental identification challenge within the unified framework. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, a microbial exposure (X) can plausibly contribute to disease onset (Y) only if changes in X precede Y. Without temporal ordering, associations remain vulnerable to reverse causation.

Addressing core challenges in microbiome causal inference using the unified framework. (A) Temporality: Causal interpretation requires that microbial changes precede disease onset. Longitudinal and interventional designs test whether modifying a microbial factor prior to disease progression reduces disease risk (do-operator principle), e.g. changes in butyrate-producing taxa before colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease. (B) Mechanistic mediation: Microbial effects on host outcomes operate through intermediate biological pathways, including short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, immune signaling, and direct mechanisms such as toxins or virulence factors. Mediation analysis links microbial perturbation to host inflammation, epithelial barrier integrity, and downstream clinical outcomes. (C) Context dependence: The causal impact of a microbial taxon is modulated by host-specific factors such as diet, antibiotic exposure, body mass index, and metabolic state. These contextual modifiers alter disease risk associated with taxa such as Bacteroides fragilis or F. nucleatum, motivating interaction-aware and personalized causal models. (D) Multi-disease inference: Some microbial taxa exhibit consistent causal effects across multiple diseases, whereas others act in disease-specific contexts. Cross-disease analyses summarize shared protective or harmful signals across conditions such as colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, type 1 diabetes, and asthma. For example, Bifidobacterium frequently aligns with SCFA-linked protection, whereas Ruminococcus torques is associated with risk via mucin degradation and barrier disruption.

Within the framework, temporal and longitudinal models (Section 1.4) function as identification tools that evaluate whether microbial shifts occur prior to disease-related changes. In human gut microbiome research, Granger causality and related time-aware approaches have been used to assess whether variations in microbial abundance precede inflammatory or metabolic alterations [21, 48, 49]. Extensions accommodating irregular sampling, compositionality, and nonlinear dynamics have further improved directional inference in realistic gut datasets [52, 53].

However, temporal evidence alone does not establish causality. Its interpretation depends on prior assumption mapping through DAGs (Section 1.2) and careful consideration of time-varying confounding (Section 1.1). When combined with complementary identification strategies such as Mendelian randomization (Section 1.3), temporal modeling strengthens directional plausibility before effect estimation is undertaken. In this way, temporality operates as a decision gate within the framework, constraining downstream interpretation rather than serving as standalone proof.

Mechanistic mediation

Even when directionality and effect estimation are supported, a key challenge remains regarding understanding how microbial exposures influence host outcomes. As depicted in Fig. 4B, mediation analysis addresses this mechanistic layer by decomposing total effects into direct and indirect pathways.

Within the unified framework, mediation is applied downstream of identification and estimation (Sections 1.3–1.5). In the human gut, mediators frequently include short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids, immune signaling molecules, and host–microbe metabolic interactions [57–67]. For example, butyrate-producing taxa have been implicated in maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and modulating inflammatory responses in IBD, suggesting indirect pathways linking microbial composition to disease risk.

High-dimensional mediation methods such as SparseMCMM and CRAmed extend this approach to compositional and sparse gut microbiome data [24, 59]. However, mediation analysis requires strong assumptions regarding mediator–outcome confounding and temporal ordering. Within the unified architecture, mediation does not independently establish causality; rather, it translates directionally supported and estimated effects into biologically interpretable mechanisms, thereby guiding experimental validation and intervention design.

Context dependence

Microbial effects in the human gut are rarely uniform. Contextual factors such as diet, body mass index, antibiotic exposure, and host physiology can modify the magnitude or even the direction of microbial effects on disease outcomes (Fig. 4C). Context dependence therefore intersects both identification and estimation stages of the unified framework.

DAG-based assumption mapping (Section 1.2) helps distinguish effect modification from confounding, while multivariable MR and heterogeneity-aware estimation strategies (Sections 1.3–1.5) allow evaluation of conditional or subgroup-specific effects. For example, Mendelian randomization studies have suggested that dietary preferences may influence IBD risk through specific gut taxa, highlighting interactions between host behavior and microbial pathways [89].

Accounting for heterogeneity prevents overgeneralization of average treatment effects and strengthens the translational relevance of findings. Within the unified framework, context dependence motivates stratified analyses and interaction modeling prior to causal interpretation, ensuring that microbial interventions are tailored to biologically meaningful subgroups.

Multi-disease inference

A further challenge concerns whether identified microbial drivers exert disease-specific or cross-disease effects. As shown in Fig. 4D, certain taxa may influence multiple disease outcomes, raising questions about shared versus condition-specific causal pathways. Within the framework, this challenge corresponds to the generalizability and transportability stage (Box 1, Step vii). Multi-disease Mendelian randomization studies have examined associations between host genetic instruments for gut taxa and diverse disease phenotypes, revealing both shared and distinct microbial influences across gastrointestinal and extra-intestinal conditions [90–93].

Distinguishing broad health indicators from disease-specific mechanisms requires integration of MR, effect estimation, and mechanistic mediation layers. Evaluating stability across populations and longitudinal cohorts further constrains interpretation. In this way, multi-disease inference extends the unified framework beyond single-outcome analysis and supports identification of microbial features with carefully defined translational scope.

Testing causal reversibility through microbiome-based interventions

A defining implication of causal inference is reversibility: if a microbial factor contributes causally to disease, targeted modification of that factor should alter disease risk. Within the unified framework, reversibility represents the translational stage that follows assumption mapping, causal identification, effect estimation, and mechanistic interpretation (Box 1). This stage is operationalized through targeted interventions that directly perturb microbial exposures and evaluate downstream host responses (Fig. 5). These interventions are not intended to retroactively establish causality; rather, they test whether hypotheses supported by prior identification and mechanistic analyses produce biologically consistent and reversible effects. Figure 5A summarizes this conceptual logic.

Testing causal reversibility through microbiome-based interventions. (A) Causal reversibility principle: If a microbial factor causally contributes to disease, removing or modifying it through an intervention (do(Z)) should lead to a reduction in disease risk. (B) Targeted microbial perturbation: Phage therapy tests causal hypotheses by selectively depleting a suspected pathogenic taxon (e.g. Enterobacter cloacae). A two-phage cocktail reduces target abundance (CFU or relative reads), increases beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (such as butyrate), and preserves alpha diversity under controlled dosing, timing optimization, and safety monitoring. (C) Microbiome restoration: Live biotherapeutics (such as RBX2660) or fecal microbiota transplantation administered after antibiotic treatment restore microbial diversity and enrich beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium longum. Reported outcomes include reduced recurrence of Clostridioides difficile infection, decreased inflammation (e.g. C-reactive protein), and increased SCFA production. (D) Context-aware modulation: Precision dietary interventions guided by metagenomic profiling reshape gut microbiota composition toward butyrate-producing taxa and away from pro-inflammatory microbes, leading to improvements in metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers.

Phage therapy

As illustrated in Fig. 5B, selective phage-mediated depletion of a suspected pathogenic taxon provides a precise perturbation for testing causal hypotheses. Phage therapy (PT) targets specific bacterial populations while minimizing disruption to the broader microbial community, making it well suited for evaluating mechanistic links between microbes and disease.

For example, a study applied a two-phage cocktail targeting Enterobacter cloacae, a bacterium associated with obesity, using an ex vivo colonic simulation model derived from obese individuals. This intervention reduced E. cloacae abundance, increased beneficial short-chain fatty acids, and preserved overall microbial diversity and metabolic function [94].

These observations are consistent with a causal role for E. cloacae in obesity-associated metabolic dysfunction, while also highlighting the sensitivity of PT outcomes to dosing and timing [95]. When combined with mediation analysis, phage-based perturbations can help distinguish whether observed host improvements arise through metabolic restoration, immune modulation, or other downstream pathways, thereby strengthening mechanistic interpretation without overstating causal certainty.

Live biotherapeutics

Live biotherapeutics, including probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation, represent an alternative mode of causal reversibility testing through microbial restoration rather than depletion. If reintroduction of specific microbial functions leads to clinical improvement, this supports (but does not prove) their causal relevance. Figure 5C illustrates this logic.

A notable example is the phase III trial of RBX2660, a stool-derived live biotherapeutic evaluated for prevention of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI). Following antibiotic treatment, participants receiving RBX2660 exhibited substantially lower recurrence rates than placebo controls, with sustained remission in a majority of treated individuals over 6 months [96]. These findings demonstrate that restoring microbial community structure can reverse dysbiosis-associated disease states.

Integration of such trials with longitudinal causal modeling may further clarify which microbial changes precede and contribute to clinical recovery.

Precision diets

Precision dietary interventions offer a non-invasive strategy for testing causal reversibility by modulating microbial composition and function in a host-specific manner (Fig. 5D). By tailoring nutritional inputs to individual microbiome profiles, these approaches enable targeted perturbation of microbial pathways implicated in metabolic and inflammatory disease.

In a clinical study of individuals with type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia, personalized diet plans informed by shotgun metagenomic profiling were associated with improvements in glycemic control, blood pressure, and inflammatory markers over a 3-month period, alongside increased microbial diversity and enrichment of beneficial taxa [97–101].

While such outcomes are consistent with microbiome-mediated metabolic improvement, causal attribution requires integration with complementary inference tools. Combining precision nutrition with causal estimation and mediation analysis may help distinguish direct microbial effects from correlated lifestyle changes, thereby refining mechanistic interpretation.

Emerging tools for scaling causal inference and multi-omics integration in the human gut microbiome

As human gut microbiome datasets continue to increase in scale, dimensionality, and complexity, causal inference frameworks must extend beyond single-method applications toward scalable, interoperable, and multi-omic designs. While the core components of the framework, such as DAGs, MR, and DML, remain foundational, their application at scale increasingly benefits from complementary tools capable of handling high-dimensional features, longitudinal sampling, and cross-layer integration.

Progress in this area depends not only on methodological advances but also on standardized metadata, compositional normalization strategies, interoperable data formats, and benchmarking resources that support reproducible causal analyses across cohorts. Emerging cloud-based and containerized workflows are improving transparency and portability, while simulation-based approaches and synthetic microbial systems enable in silico exploration of counterfactual scenarios to guide downstream experimental validation.

Conclusion

This synthesis advances the transition from correlation to causation in human gut microbiome research by integrating statistical, causal, and experimental approaches within a cohesive, assumption-aware framework. We demonstrate how DAGs, temporal evidence, MR, DML, and mediation analysis can be systematically combined within a unified workflow that constrains interpretation at each stage. Using colorectal cancer as an illustrative example, we show how this approach transforms observational associations into testable, mechanism-based hypotheses while explicitly preventing overinterpretation when causal identification is incomplete.

In this context, the primary contribution of this work is not the introduction of a new causal discovery algorithm but the establishment of a structured and practical framework for causal reasoning in microbiome research. By integrating existing methods into a coherent, decision-aware pipeline with explicit interpretative constraints, this work provides a procedural safeguard that aligns the strength of causal claims with the quality of available evidence.

The proposed framework provides a transparent and reproducible route toward clinically actionable microbiome biomarkers by linking effect estimation to mechanistic pathways and principled evaluation of causal reversibility. Importantly, causal reversibility and experimental model systems are positioned as a bridge from computational inference to microbiome-based therapeutic development, rather than as substitutes for causal identification. Continued integration of these components within open, multi-omic analytical pipelines will further support the translation of microbiome discoveries into precision diagnostics and interventions.
