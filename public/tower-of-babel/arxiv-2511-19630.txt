Metal-loaded outflows in sub-Milky Way galaxies in the CIELO simulations
Valentina P. Miranda, Patricia B. Tissera, Emanuel Sillero, Jenny Gonzalez-Jara, Lucas Bignone, Ignacio Muñoz-Escobar, Susana Pedrosa, Rosa Domínguez-Tenreiro

Abstract
Context. Supernova (SN) feedback-driven galactic outflows are a key physical process that contributes to the baryon cycle by regulating the star formation activity, reducing the amount of metals in low-mass galaxies and enriching the circumgalactic (CGM) and intergalactic media (IGM). Aims. We aim to understand the chemical loop of sub-Milky Way (MW) galaxies and their nearby regions. Methods. We studied 15 simulated central sub-MW galaxies ( M ∗ ≤ 10 10 ​ M ☉ \rm M_{*}\leq 10^{10}\ {\rm M_{\sun}} ) and intermediate-mass galaxies ( M ∗ \rm M_{*} ∼ \sim 10 10 M ☉ {\rm M_{\sun}} ) from the CIELO-P7 high-resolution simulations. We followed the evolution of the progenitor galaxies, their properties and the characteristics of the outflows within the redshift range z = [ 0 , 7 ] z=[0,7] . We used two dynamically-motivated outflow definitions, unbound outflows and expelled mass rates, to quantify the impact of SN feedback. Results. At z ∼ 0 z\sim 0 , sub-MW galaxies have a larger fraction of their current oxygen mass in the gas phase but have expelled a greater portion beyond the virial radius, compared to their higher-mass counterparts. Galaxies with M ∗ < ∼ 10 9 ​ M ☉ M_{*}<\sim 10^{9}\penalty\ {\rm M_{\sun}} have 10–40 per cent of their total oxygen mass within R 200 \rm R_{200} in the CGM, and an equivalent to 10–60 per cent expelled into the IGM. In contrast, more massive galaxies have most of the oxygen mass locked by the stellar populations. The CGM of low-mass galaxies predominantly contains oxygen low-temperature gas, acting as a metal reservoir. We find that the outflows are more oxygen-rich for sub-MW galaxies, Z out / Z ISM ∼ 1.5 \rm Z_{\rm out}/Z_{ISM}\sim 1.5 , than for higher-mass galaxies, Z out / Z ISM ≤ 0.5 \rm Z_{\rm out}/Z_{ISM}\leq 0.5 , particularly for z < 2 z<2 . Mass-loading factors of η out ∼ 0 − 6 \rm\eta_{out}\sim 0-6 are detected in agreement with observations. While a weak dependence of η \eta on mass and circular velocity is found at z ∼ 0 z\sim 0 , a stronger anticorrelation appears for higher redshift. Conclusions. Our results suggest that sub-MW galaxies may store a significant fraction of metals in their CGM and that the anti-correlation between η \eta and stellar mass (or circular velocity) is stronger at z ∼ 2 z\sim 2 , likely due to a combination of more intense star formation, a higher merger rate, and shallower potential wells.



1
Introduction

Galaxy formation and evolution is a complex interplay of physical processes such as the accretion of gas and the interactions and mergers of the central systems with satellites
(
81
)
.
These processes also regulate the star formation activity, mix the chemical elements in the interstellar medium (ISM) and induce radial migration
(
45
, e.g.)
. The evolution of stars leads to the synthesis of new chemical elements, which are expelled into the ISM—primarily during the final stages of stellar life—accompanied by significant energy release that can power metal-enriched outflows
(
86
;
53
, e.g.)
.
SN feedback-driven outflows are expected to be one of the major drivers of the baryon cycle
(
65
)
by reducing the baryon fraction in low-mass galaxies
(
23
)
and enriching the CGM and IGM
(
18
)
. The impact on low-mass galaxies is thought to be stronger due to their shallower potential wells, which contributes to facilitating the ejection of material
(
26
)
. Galactic outflows can also shape the mass-metallicity relation (MZR) of galaxies, as they might be able to transport enriched material out of galaxies, particularly in low-mass galaxies
(
9
)
. Additionally, metal-poor gas inflows are also expected to contribute to modulating the global metal content of galaxies and hence, their location on the MZR
(
25
;
97
;
5
, e.g. )
.

Given the important role that SN feedback plays, studying low-mass galaxies becomes particularly relevant. However, low-mass galaxies are challenging to study because they usually have low surface brightness, which makes them harder to detect
(
56
, e.g.)
. Therefore, higher-mass galaxies have been more extensively studied. In addition, low-mass galaxies represent a key element in solving cosmological discrepancies of the standard
\Lambda
Cold Dark Matter Model (
\Lambda
CDM), such as the missing satellites problem or the dark matter distribution in dwarf galaxies
(
75
;
40
)
. Therefore low-mass galaxies play a very important role in constraining galaxy formation theories and the sub-grid physics modelling
(
52
;
10
)
.

A key parameter to study the impact of outflows in galaxies is the mass-loading factor (
\eta
), which is defined as the rate at which gas is transported out of the system with respect to the star formation rate (SFR). Observations reported
\eta
ranging from
\sim
0.5 to 10 for nearby dwarf galaxies
(
54
;
56
)
and up to
\sim
25-60 for extreme starburst systems
(
35
;
66
)
. High-redshift estimates of main-sequence galaxies obtained with the Atacama Large Millimeter/Submillimeter Array (ALMA;
36
;
68
;
8
) and of low-mass galaxies observed with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST;
12
) range between
\eta=0.5
and 10.

In the Local Universe, observational estimations reported by
56
found only a weak dependency on circular velocity or stellar mass. However, for high redshift galaxies (
z\sim 4-9
) there is evidence of a strong dependency on stellar mass,
\eta\propto M_{*}^{-0.43}
and
\eta\propto M_{*}^{-1.3}
, by
68
and
12
, respectively. These dependencies agree
with the predictions from FIRE simulations
(
61
;
63
)
. Additionally,
62
found that the momentum injection by supernovae (i.e. momentum-conserving outflows) into the galaxy scaled as
\eta\propto

\rm V_{c}^{-1}
, where
\rm V_{c}
is the halo circular velocity, whereas energy-conserving outflows seem to correlate the mass-loading factor as
\eta\propto

\rm V_{c}^{-2}

(
16
)
.
At
z\sim 0
, hydrodynamical simulations often estimate
\eta\sim 10-100

(
94
;
28
;
18
;
63
)
, which are higher than the observed values, but not so different from the new estimations reported at high redshift. We note that results from numerical simulations depend on the numerical implementation used to model outflows and hence, the analysis and comparison with observations is crucial to set constraints on these models, and to understand the role and impact played by SN outflows
(
74
;
42
;
72
, see also)
.

The exploration of the chemical abundances of the ISM and the CGM offers the possibility to further understand the role of SN feedback and the baryon-metal cycle
(
65
)
. In this context, cosmological simulations of galaxy formation are a powerful tool to investigate the complex interplay between SN feedback and the evolutionary history of galaxies. By tracking back-in-time the progenitor galaxies and their components, gas and stars, across cosmic time, simulations enable us to directly study the origin and evolution of outflows, as well as the location of the expelled material.

Simulations generally find that dwarf galaxies are more efficient than MW–mass galaxies at ejecting metals due to the lower potential well
(
78
;
19
;
60
;
57
)
. This is in global agreement with theoretical expectations, which consider the relative importance of the injected SN energy and the binding energy of the system
26
. However, there is no clear consensus among different numerical experiments regarding the fraction of metals that remain in the dwarf galaxies and their CGM. For example, the FIRE simulation
(
33
)
reported that dwarf galaxies could retain between 30 to 100 % of their metals within all gas inside the virial radius,
\rm R_{200}
, and between 10% and 60% in their CGM. In contrast,
18
reported lower retention rates, estimating 30% to 60 % for gas within
\rm R_{200}
, and 15 % to 25 % in the CGM. Recently,
67
studied a larger simulated sample of dwarf galaxies, finding that the warm and cold phases retained between 5-10 % of metals formed by the dwarf galaxies. They also found a weak correlation between galaxy mass and the CGM metal retention factor. These differences among numerical results might be due to the differences in the subgrid physics and, potentially, to the different methods and modelling assumed to measure outflows as mentioned before.

In fact, observationally, outflows are often detected using integrated spectra, such as H
α
emission lines as tracers of mass loss in galaxies
(
54
;
56
;
12
, e.g.)
. In local starburst galaxies, galactic winds are detected using far-UV absorption lines
(
35
;
17
)
, whereas at higher redshift are usually detected using rest-frame optical emission lines such as [OII] and [OIII]
(
95
)
or in the rest-frame far-infrared using the [CII] emission line
(
31
;
36
;
68
, e.g.)
. Works using simulations generally define outflows as gas particles that have positive radial velocities
(
94
;
60
;
4
, see)
, or velocities larger than the Bernoulli velocity
(
63
)
. Other works tracked gas particles and measured outflows by following the material that moved beyond the virial radius of a galaxy as a function of redshift
(
28
;
1
;
18
)
. Hence, while global trends might agree, more specific differences might be due to the actual definition of outflows.

In this paper, our main goal is to analyse the impact of SN feedback on the production of outflows and their effect on metal retention in sub-MW galaxies. In particular, we focus on understanding how oxygen is distributed in the different baryonic components in these galaxies and transported via galactic outflows. For this purpose, we studied 15 central galaxies of the high-resolution simulation of the ChemodynamIcal propertiEs of gaLaxies and the cOsmic web (
CIELO
) project
(
89
)
. We followed the evolution of the galaxies across their merger trees. We analysed the oxygen abundances of our galaxies, as well as their history of star formation and triggering of outflows. We explored two dynamically motivated definition of outflows, one based on the binding energy and the other by tracking particles as they move out of the virial radius
(
1
, e.g.)
.

This paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we provide a brief description of
CIELO
simulations, our galaxy sample, and the main methodology.
In Section
3
we analyse the selected galaxies and their properties in time and study the main metallicity relations. In Section
4
, we analyse for the oxygen distribution in our simulated galaxies, unbound outflows, inflows and expelled mass. Section
5
discusses the
\eta
factor and its dependency on circular velocity and stellar mass at
z\sim 0
. We also study the evolution of
\eta
in Sect.
5.2
. Finally, in Sect.
6
we summarise our main results and conclusions.

2.1
The
CIELO
simulations

The zoom-in
CIELO
simulations were performed by using a version of the
gadget-3

(
83
)
. This version incorporates a multiphase model for the gas component, metal-dependent cooling, a prescription for star formation where stars form in dense, cold gas clumps, and feedback by Type Ia and Type II supernovae (SNe)
(
76
;
77
)
. The
CIELO
simulations adopt an Initial Mass Function (IMF) by
15
, with lower and upper cut-offs of 0.1
{\rm M_{\sun}}
and 40
{\rm M_{\sun}}
, respectively.

The chemical SN feedback implemented is taken from
59
. This model considers 12 chemical isotopes:
{}^{1}\mathrm{H}
,
{}^{4}\mathrm{He}
,
{}^{12}\mathrm{C}
,
{}^{16}\mathrm{O}
,
{}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}
,
{}^{28}\mathrm{Si}
,
{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}
,
{}^{14}\mathrm{N}
,
{}^{20}\mathrm{Ne}
,
{}^{32}\mathrm{S}
,
{}^{40}\mathrm{Ca}
and
{}^{62}\mathrm{Zn}
. Primordial abundances are assumed for the initial gas particles:
{\rm X}_{\rm H}=0.76
,
{\rm Y}_{\rm He}=0.24
and
Z=0
, where
Z
is the metallicity defined as the fraction of elements heavier than He in the baryonic component.

SNII are assumed to form at the final phase of the evolution of stars more massive than
8\ {\rm M_{\sun}}
, and their lifetimes are estimated following
70
.
CIELO
adopts the SNII yields by
96
.
SNIa are assumed to originate from CO white dwarf systems, in which mass transfers from the secondary to the primary start until it outreaches the Chandrasekhar mass. SNIa yields were taken from
38
, and the lifetimes of the progenitors are taken at random within
[0.1,1]
Gyr
(
77
)
. This simple model for the lifetime distributions of the progenitors of SNIa reproduces very well with the results provided by the Single-Degenerated (SD) model as discussed by
39
.

The SN feedback model adopted is able to reproduce galactic mass-loaded winds without introducing mass-dependent scaling parameters. It also includes a multiphase model for the ISM that allows the coexistence of the hot, diffuse phase and the cold, dense gas phase, where star formation takes place
(
77
;
78
)
. The energy injected by both types of SNe is distributed equally between the cold and hot phases. The energy injected into the cold phase is stored in a reservoir until the gas particles accumulate enough energy to change their thermo-dynamical properties and join the hot phase. The energy injected into the hot phase is instantaneously thermalised. The injection into a cold energy reservoir may delay the effective injection of energy when a galaxy is forming stars at a low rate, but during this period, no outflows are expected. When a gas particle is transformed into stars, the existing energy reservoir is injected into the surrounding gas. The thresholds to separate cold and hot phases have been extensively tested by
76
;
77
.

The initial conditions of
CIELO
are consistent with the
\Lambda
Cold Dark Matter (
\Lambda
CDM) cosmological model, with
\Omega_{0}=0.317
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.6825
,
\Omega_{b}=0.049
and
h=0.6711

(
69
)
.
The initial conditions of the CIELO project are zoom-in regions around target galaxies selected from a dark matter only cosmological simulation. The target haloes were chosen to map different density regions, excluding large groups and clusters. In this work, we use the higher resolution CIELO-P7, centred around a target halo of virial mass
2
2

2

The virial mass, M
200
, is defined as the mass enclosed within a sphere of radius
\rm R_{200}
, at which the mean mass density reaches 200 times the background density.

\rm M_{200}=1.3\times 10^{12}{\rm M_{\sun}}
.
89
reported that this zoom-in region corresponds to a filamentary structure. The initial mass gas particles have
m_{\rm g}=3.1\times 10^{4}{\rm M_{\sun}}
and the dark matter particles have
m_{\rm dm}=2.0\times 10^{5}{\rm M_{\sun}}
.

CIELO-P7 hosts haloes of virial mass within the range,
\rm M_{200}\sim 10^{10}-10^{12}{\rm M_{\sun}}
. The virialised haloes were identified using a friends-of-friends algorithm
(
24
, FoF,)
, and the substructures within each halo were individualised by using the SUBFIND algorithm
(
82
;
27
)
. The most massive substructure within a viral halo is classified as a central galaxy. Finally, the merger trees were built using the AMIGA algorithm
(
43
)
.

Outflows, inflows and star formation rates

In this section, we describe the methods we used to estimate the rate of gas transported in and out of a galaxy and the analysis performed to compare simulations with observations.
Regarding the estimation of the outflow rates, there are different approaches in the literature
(
94
;
60
;
4
, e.g.)
. In this work, we adopt two dynamically motivated definitions. Our first approach defines unbound outflows by taking into account only the gas moving outwards with enough energy to eventually escape the potential well of its host galaxy,

\rm\dot{M}_{\rm out}(t)=\frac{\sum_{\rm i}m_{\rm i,out}}{\Delta t}

(1)

where the sum is over the
i
gas particles, which are unbound and are dominated by outward radial motions such that
\rm V_{r}/\sigma>1
, with
\rm V_{r}
the radial velocity and
\sigma
, the velocity dispersion of the gas particles. We estimated these rates within a
\Delta t
given by the time interval between two consecutive available snapshots.

The rate
\rm\dot{M}_{\rm out}(t)
was estimated within two radial bins of the CGM:
[1.5\rm R_{opt},0.5\rm R_{200})
and
[0.5\rm R_{200},\rm R_{200})
, of a given
CIELO
galaxy. Hence, for each analysed galaxy,
\rm\dot{M}_{\rm out}(t)
was estimated in two radial bins and as a function of time.

The second approach adopted is based on the determination of the expelled gas particles. These particles are selected as those that reached galactocentric distances larger than the
\rm R_{200}
of a galaxy and never re-entered it at subsequent times, i.e.
r>\rm R_{200}
always remains valid after the particle was expelled the first time.
Furthermore, we restrict the gas particles to those with
\rm V_{r}/\sigma>0.5
from
z=7
to
z=0
, so that at high redshift, we only consider the particles with the highest probability of leaving the system. And, for consistency, we maintain this condition down to low values of
z
.
The expelled mass rate is defined as

\rm\dot{M}_{\rm ex}(t)=\frac{\sum_{\rm i}m_{\rm i,ex}}{\Delta t},

(2)

where the sum is over the mass of gas particles
i
that, at a given snapshot, do no longer belong to the system, but at the previous snapshot did (i.e. r
i

>

\rm R_{200}
with r
i
being the galactocentric distance of a given particle at a given snapshot). We note that
\rm\dot{M}_{\rm ex}(t)
could also include material associated with galaxy interactions or splash satellite galaxies. Hence, it is an upper-limit estimation of the action of SN feedback.

We also calculated the rate,
\eta
, at which the gas mass is transported out of a system (i.e.
\rm\dot{M}_{\rm ex}(t)
or
\rm\dot{M}_{\rm out}(t)
), relative to its SFR,

\eta=\frac{\rm\dot{M}(t)}{\rm SFR}

(3)

where the SFR is defined as
\frac{\sum_{\rm i}m_{\rm i,*}}{\Delta t}
by considering stellar populations with ages younger than
\Delta t=0.1\rm Gyr
. We study the mass-loading factors and their relation with physical properties in Section
5
.

Additionally, we estimate the rate at which the gas that is not associated to the central galaxy at
z=7
enters a given galaxy halo at a determined snapshot (
r<\rm R_{200}
), but stays within
\rm R_{200}
up to
z=0
. For this purpose, we tracked gas particles in time and calculated the inflow mass rate as

\rm\dot{M}_{\rm in}(t)=\frac{\sum_{\rm i}m_{\rm i,in}}{\Delta t}

(4)

where the sum is over the mass of gas particles
i
that, at a given snapshot, is within the
\rm R_{200}
, but in the previous snapshot was not,
r>\rm R_{200}
.

Finally, we defined the effective inflow mass rate,
\rm\dot{M}_{\rm in}^{\rm eff}
by applying Eq.
4
, but considering only those infalling gas particles that, at a given time, reach the ISM of a galaxy (i.e.
r\leq
1.5
\rm R_{opt}
and z
\leq

\rm h_{max}
). This rate considers the gas that effectively fell into the ISM of a galaxy and should be considered a lower limit since we do not track the gas particles that fall between snapshots and are transformed into stars.

6
Conclusions

We study 15 central galaxies of the high-resolution CIELO-P7 cosmological simulations
(
89
)
, focussing on the sub-MW galaxies (
\rm M_{*}<10^{10}{\rm M_{\sun}}
). We follow the progenitor galaxies between redshift
z=[0,7]
and measure unbound outflows in two shells of the CGM [1.5
\rm R_{opt}
, 0.5
\rm R_{200}
] and
[0.5\rm R_{200},\rm R_{200})
and expelled mass rates (
r>\rm R_{200}
). We applied these two dynamically motivated definitions of outflows.

We summarise our main results as follows:

1.

We find minor/major mergers and interactions, as well as SN feedback triggered by these events, can modulate the SFR histories of sub-MW galaxies. A tidally-induced or merger-induced burst of SF triggers mass-loaded outflows, which decreases the star formation activity, as expected, due to the energy release of SN into the ISM, and transports enriched material out of a galaxy. However, we note that the impact of mergers on star formation and the galactic outflow is complicated and might depend on other parameters such as the gas-richness and the orbital characteristics of the encounters. A detailed analysis on these aspects is beyond the scope of this paper.

2.

The outflow metallicity becomes significant relative to the ISM metallicity from
z\sim 2
, as many SF episodes have time to enriched a galaxy. For
z>2
, we estimate a median
\rm Z_{out}/Z_{ISM}\sim 1.5
for the sub-MW galaxies and a median
\rm Z_{out}/Z_{ISM}\leq 0.5
for higher-mass galaxies, in agreement with previous studies
(
60
;
18
, e.g.)
. At higher redshift (
z>2
), the progenitors of the more massive systems have an average
Z_{\rm out}/Z_{\rm ISM}=0.56
, while the sub-Milky-Way systems show a higher average ratio of
Z_{\rm out}/Z_{\rm ISM}=0.67
. Sub-MW galaxies are more efficient in removing metals from their ISM than more massive systems across all redshift.

3.

The MZR of our sample is in agreement with observations. The effective yields show a large diversity in agreement with observations, particularly for sub-MW systems. There is a global agreement with predictions from analytical models that allow mass-loss. Galaxies have also accreted gas which tend to be lower metallicity than the star-forming ISM, particularly for sub-MW systems, although there is a large variation. It remains to be analysed the relative impact of inflows and outflows.

4.

Sub-MW galaxies have a higher fraction of their current total oxygen mass in the gas phase (i.e. ISM or CGM) and have higher fractions of expelled mass at
z=0
than higher-mass galaxies. This implies that sub-MW galaxies are more affected by outflows. We find that in galaxies with
\rm M_{*}<10^{8.7}\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot}
, the CGM contains between 10 and 40 per cent of the oxygen mass within
\rm R_{200}
, whereas in more massive galaxies, this fraction drops below 10 per cent. For sub-MW-mass galaxies, an equivalent to approximately
10–60
per cent of the present-day oxygen mass has been expelled into the IGM, while in more massive galaxies, this fraction is less than 10 per cent. In addition, most of the oxygen in the CGM is in form of low ions gas for
M_{*}<10^{8.7}\penalty\ \rm M_{\odot}
. This component seems to be a metal reservoir in the CGM of these galaxies.

5.

We obtain mass-loading factor between
\eta_{\rm out}\sim
0.7-6 for the unbound outflows and
\eta_{\rm ex}\sim 0.6-5.7
for the expelled mass rate for
z\leq 0.5
. This is consistent with observations of local dwarf galaxies
(
56
)
and field galaxies
(
29
)
. We find no clear signals of correlation between the mass-loading factor and the circular velocity in this redshift range.

6.

At
z\sim 1
we obtain mass-loading factors within the range
\eta_{\rm out}\sim
0.6-4 for the unbound outflows and
\eta_{\rm ex}\sim 0-2
for the expelled mass rate, and for
z\sim 2
between
\eta_{\rm out,ex}\sim 0-3
for the unbound outflows and for the expelled mass rate. The negative correlation is stronger at higher redshift (
z\sim 2
) with a linear fit
\eta_{\rm out}\sim\rm V_{c}^{-0.71}
. This suggests that the progenitors of sub-MW galaxies are more affected by SN outflows than those of higher-mass galaxies. The negative correlation is stronger at
z\sim 2
as progenitors of the selected galaxies are more actively forming stars, have more frequent minor and major mergers, and lower potential wells.

The
CIELO
galaxies are able to reproduce global observed trends of sub-MW galaxies and provide a reference to compare with observations and with simulations using different subgrid physics. Sub-MW galaxies provide important constraints to test subgrid modelling. We predict that a significant fraction of metals in low-mass galaxies are stored in the cool components of their CGM. Due to the high number abundance of dwarf galaxies, they could represent a significant reservoir of chemical elements.

Appendix A
The Tully-Fisher relation

The
CIELO
galaxies follow the Tully-Fisher relation
(
92
, TFR - )
as shown by
89
. In this paper, we estimated it again, but for dwarf galaxies. The main goal is to estimate the potential well of the galaxies by using the rotational velocity as a proxy.

For this purpose, we obtained the rotational curves of the gas component for each galaxy and their progenitors by estimating the tangential velocity of each gas particle. The maximum rotational velocity, V
max
, was measured as the mean tangential velocity within 0.5 kpc radial interval around the maximum value achieved. Additionally, we define the circular velocity,
\rm V_{c}
, as the rotational velocity measured at
\rm R_{opt}
. Both values are given in Table
1
. When there is a well-defined gaseous disc in equilibrium within its potential well, we expect
\rm V_{c}=\sqrt{G\rm M_{*}/r}
. This is the case for the simulated galaxies with a disc. However, there are a few galaxies in which the gas components are not settled onto well-behaved discs, hence, the gas is more turbulent and dominated by dispersion. For these galaxies, we take
\rm V_{c}
as the maximum value of the curve,
\rm V_{max}
.

+ 2.41 determined by
47
of local spiral galaxies was included.

49
, where stellar masses were estimated by this author are based on 3.6
\mu

55
, which used rotational velocities estimated by using PV slices (position-velocity diagram) of HI zones are also included.
