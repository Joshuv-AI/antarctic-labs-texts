Anesthetic management of a patient with malignant right ventricular tumor and pulmonary tumor embolism secondary to cervical cancer: a case report
Authors: Yanjun Lin, Pengzhan Shuang, Deqiang Tao, Liangyuan Lu, Liming Bao, Guohua Zhang, Haijun Hou, Suli Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac metastasis involving the right ventricle and pulmonary artery is extremely rare and poses significant anesthetic challenges due to the high risk of tumor embolization and hemodynamic instability. We report the anesthetic management of a patient with metastatic right ventricular tumor secondary to cervical cancer who underwent surgical resection.Case presentationA 63-year-old woman with a history of cervical cancer presented with respiratory symptoms and was found to have a mobile mass involving the right ventricle and main pulmonary artery on imaging. Given the risk of sudden pulmonary embolism, surgical resection under cardiopulmonary bypass was undertaken. Anesthetic management emphasized careful induction, advanced monitoring including cerebral oximetry, and intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography to guide hemodynamic management and reduce embolic risk. The procedure was completed successfully, and the patient recovered without major complications. Histopathology confirmed metastatic squamous cell carcinoma. At eight-month follow-up, the patient was alive and receiving adjuvant chemotherapy.ConclusionIntracardiac metastatic tumors with pulmonary artery involvement present substantial anesthetic risk. This case highlights the importance of individualized anesthetic planning, vigilant hemodynamic control, and echocardiographic guidance in achieving safe perioperative outcomes.Keywords: Cardiac tumor, Cardiopulmonary bypass, Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), Cervical cancer, Cardiac metastasis, Anesthetic management

Background

Cervical cancer is the second most common malignant tumor in females worldwide [1]. Cardiac metastasis from cervical cancer, including involvement of the right ventricle, is rare, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1.2% based on autopsy studies [2]. Such tumors may grow to a considerable size and give rise to a wide range of clinical manifestations, including arrhythmias, pulmonary or systemic emboli, syncope, and sudden death [3]. Once identified, these masses typically require prompt surgical removal under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) [4]. Perioperative management of these patients presents numerous challenges for the anesthesiologist. This report details the perioperative anesthetic management of a patient with right ventricular and pulmonary artery metastasis secondary to cervical cancer. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 63-year-old woman (height 168 cm; weight 61.5 kg) presented to our hospital with a two-week history of progressive cough and hemoptysis, accompanied by intermittent chest tightness and dyspnea. The cough was initially dry but progressively worsened and became productive with blood-streaked sputum. Chest tightness and dyspnea were initially exertional but had become persistent at rest by the time of admission. One week prior to presentation at our institution, she had been informed of thrombocytopenia at another hospital, where the platelet count was measured at 60 × 10⁹/L. She had been advised to seek further evaluation, which prompted her referral to our center.

The patient had undergone hysterectomy for International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage II–III cervical squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) eleven years earlier, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy. She had remained disease-free thereafter, with regular follow-up until approximately one year before the current presentation. Her past medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, chronic lung disease, or other significant comorbidities. She had no known drug allergies.

On physical examination, the patient had a heart rate of 90 beats per minute, a blood pressure of 118/85 mmHg, a respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Cardiac auscultation revealed a grade II/VI systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border, consistent with tricuspid regurgitation. Breath sounds were clear bilaterally, and no other abnormalities were identified on examination.

She was admitted for further investigation. Initial laboratory tests were notable for a platelet count of 80 × 10⁹/L (reference range: 125–350 × 10⁹/L), squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC-Ag) of 13.5 ng/mL (reference range: 0–1.8 ng/mL), neuron-specific enolase (NSE) of 74.4 ng/mL (reference range: 0–16.5 ng/mL), D-dimer (DD) of 562 µg/L (reference range: 0–500 µg/L), and fibrinogen degradation products (FDP) of 18.5 mg/L (reference range: 0–5 mg/L). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (Fig. 1) demonstrated normal left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 61%, estimated using Simpson’s biplane method. A large echogenic mass measuring approximately 4.6 cm × 2.2 cm was identified, attached to the lateral wall of the right ventricle and extending into the pulmonary artery. The tricuspid valve opened adequately but exhibited incomplete coaptation; Color Doppler Flow Imaging (CDFI) demonstrated moderate tricuspid regurgitation. The peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity was approximately 3.5 m/s on continuous-wave Doppler. The estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) was 54 mmHg; however, given the extension of the mass through the pulmonary artery, the modified Bernoulli Eq. (4v²) could not be reliably applied, as its underlying assumption—that right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) equals PASP—is violated when a mass obstructs the pulmonary artery. This value should therefore be interpreted with caution in the present clinical context. A small pericardial effusion was also detected, estimated to be mild (less than 100 mL) on echocardiographic assessment.The patient was commenced on a therapeutic dose of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH).

CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) confirmed extension of the right ventricular mass into the main pulmonary artery, with the mass measuring approximately 2.4 cm × 1.9 cm × 5.5 cm on three-dimensional reconstruction. The mass adhered to the right ventricular wall and was observed to be mobile, moving with the blood flow (Fig. 2). The apparent discrepancy in mass dimensions between TTE and CTPA is attributable to the inherent differences between imaging modalities: TTE provides two-dimensional measurements in a single plane, whereas CTPA enables three-dimensional reconstruction. Given the irregular geometry of the mass and its extension across both the right ventricle and pulmonary artery, such inter-modality discrepancies are expected. Abdominal and pelvic computed tomography showed no significant abnormalities.

The initial differential diagnosis included: [1] right ventricular thrombus, given the patient’s known thrombocytopenia and elevated D-dimer; [2] primary cardiac tumor, such as angiosarcoma or myxoma; and [3] metastatic cardiac tumor, in the context of the patient’s prior history of cervical cancer. A thrombus was considered less likely given the mass’s attachment to the lateral wall of the right ventricle, its mobility, and the elevated SCC-Ag (13.5 ng/mL)—a specific tumor marker for squamous cell carcinoma. A primary cardiac tumor was also deemed less probable given the known history of cervical SCC. The combination of imaging findings—a mobile mass attached to the right ventricular wall with extension into the pulmonary artery—together with the markedly elevated SCC-Ag and the patient’s prior history of cervical SCC, strongly favored a diagnosis of metastatic cardiac involvement.

Following multidisciplinary consultation involving cardiac surgeons, radiologists, anesthesiologists, gynecologists, and perfusionists, a diagnosis of right ventricular and pulmonary artery tumor with suspected cardiac metastasis from cervical cancer was established. To mitigate the risk of sudden death from pulmonary embolism, surgical resection under cardiopulmonary bypass was undertaken with the informed consent of the patient and family. The patient was classified as ASA Physical Status IV. In accordance with the institutional massive transfusion protocol for high-risk cardiac surgery, eight units of packed red blood cells (RBCs), 1000 mL of fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and three units of platelets (each unit approximately 250 mL) were prepared preoperatively.

Anesthesia management

Standard monitoring (ECG, SpO₂, and non-invasive blood pressure [NIBP]), invasive arterial blood pressure (IBP), and cerebral oximetry (regional oxygen saturation, rSO₂) were established upon the patient’s arrival in the operating room. Baseline rSO₂ was 56–59% bilaterally. Central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring via right internal jugular vein cannulation, body temperature monitoring (rectal and nasopharyngeal), and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) were initiated after induction of anesthesia. The right internal jugular vein was selected for central venous access as it represents the standard approach at our institution for cardiac surgery, providing a direct and reliable route to the superior vena cava. The femoral approach was considered but avoided given the proximity of the tumor to the right ventricular outflow tract and the theoretical risk of tumor-related venous involvement in the lower extremities, although no such involvement was confirmed on preoperative imaging.

The patient was positioned with the right side elevated to approximately 30 degrees according to her comfort preference, as lying supine was associated with worsening dyspnea, likely related to the position of the mass within the right ventricle and pulmonary artery.

Following three minutes of preoxygenation with 100% oxygen, a slow, titrated induction protocol was employed: 3 mg of midazolam was administered initially, followed by incremental doses of etomidate (12 mg total, given in 2 mg increments at 30-second intervals), sufentanil (75 µg in divided doses), and cisatracurium (15 mg). This stepwise approach was selected to minimize hemodynamic fluctuations, prevent abrupt increases in heart rate that could dislodge the mobile tumor mass, and maintain adequate preload throughout induction. Bispectral index (BIS) monitoring was used to guide the depth of anesthesia, with BIS values maintained between 40 and 60 throughout the procedure. During induction, blood pressure decreased from a pre-induction value of 132/76 mmHg to 83/50 mmHg, accompanied by a transient reduction in heart rate from 68 bpm to 55 bpm. Hemodynamics returned to baseline following the intravenous administration of phenylephrine 50 µg, without the need for additional vasopressors. The overall induction process was hemodynamically stable and free of tachycardia. Tracheal intubation was successfully performed using a video laryngoscope with a size 7.0 endotracheal tube. Volume-controlled ventilation was initiated with a tidal volume of 4–6 mL/kg, a respiratory rate of 12–14 breaths per minute, an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:2, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH₂O. End-tidal CO₂ (PETCO₂) was maintained between 35 and 45 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with 1%–1.5% sevoflurane, a continuous propofol infusion at 10–15 mL/h, sufentanil at 30–35 µg/h, and cisatracurium at 6 mg/h.

Intraoperative TEE confirmed the preoperative diagnosis, demonstrating a right ventricular and main pulmonary artery mass with marked mobility between the right ventricle and pulmonary artery with each cardiac cycle. The mass was also in close proximity to the tricuspid valve (Fig. 3).

The surgical team performed a median sternotomy, and cardiopulmonary bypass was established via arterial cannulation of the ascending aorta and bicaval venous drainage. Intraoperatively, a mass was identified beneath the anterior leaflet of the tricuspid valve, encircling the subvalvular chordae tendineae without a discrete pedicle or identifiable lesion of the right ventricular wall. The mass extended into the main pulmonary artery through the pulmonary valve. Its surface was smooth but friable, and it was completely resected (Fig. 4). Tricuspid annuloplasty was subsequently performed. Following removal of the aortic cross-clamp, the heart recovered spontaneously to sinus rhythm. Pre-weaning TEE demonstrated only mild tricuspid regurgitation (Fig. 5) with no other abnormalities. The patient was successfully weaned from CPB with the support of dobutamine at 5 µg/kg/min to enhance right ventricular contractility and reduce pulmonary vascular resistance, and norepinephrine titrated at 0.02–0.1 µg/kg/min to maintain a mean arterial pressure (MAP) above 65 mmHg and an appropriate level of systemic vascular resistance (SVR) for adequate end-organ perfusion. The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) while still intubated. Successful extubation was achieved 8 h postoperatively, following confirmation of adequate respiratory function, hemodynamic stability, and level of consciousness. Postoperative analgesia was managed with intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) using sufentanil. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no arrhythmias, renal dysfunction, or wound complications. The patient was mobilized on postoperative day 2 and transferred to the general ward on postoperative day 3. Postoperative histopathology confirmed moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma consistent with metastasis from cervical cancer. Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated an estimated PASP of 40 mmHg, with mild residual tricuspid regurgitation and no recurrent mass. The patient recovered well and was discharged 10 days after surgery. She was scheduled for PET-CT scanning followed by chemotherapy and immunotherapy. At eight-month follow-up, the patient was alive and undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy, with no evidence of cardiac recurrence on follow-up echocardiography.

Discussion

Anesthetic learning points

This case report focuses on three core anesthetic challenges in managing a patient with a malignant right ventricular tumor and pulmonary tumor embolism: [1] safe anesthesia induction in the presence of a mobile, obstructive cardiac mass; [2] hemodynamic management and embolic risk reduction during surgical manipulation; and [3] perioperative coagulation management in a patient with DIC-like coagulopathy.

Cervical cancer cardiac metastasis and diagnosis

Cardiac metastasis from cervical cancer is rare [4]. The management of such metastases has not been standardized, largely owing to the poor prognosis associated with this condition [5]. Median survival from diagnosis is approximately four months [6, 7]. The most common presenting symptom is dyspnea, which typically progresses rapidly from exertional to resting dyspnea [8]. This may result from pulmonary hypertension (PH), hypoxemia, or obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) [9]. Clinical manifestations are, however, nonspecific and may be mistaken for other conditions, including pulmonary thromboembolism or primary cardiac tumors [10]. In the present patient, the initial symptoms of cough, hemoptysis, and dyspnea were relatively nonspecific, prompting an initial workup that included evaluation for pulmonary embolism.

Echocardiography is a noninvasive modality of choice for detecting and characterizing intracavitary cardiac masses. A recognized limitation of echocardiography is its tendency to misidentify right ventricular masses as thrombi [11]. Cardiac CT angiography and MRI can provide superior delineation of tumor extent and its relationship with adjacent structures, particularly when a malignant mass is suspected [12]. In this case, although cardiac MRI and preoperative biopsy were not performed, cardiac metastasis was strongly suspected on the basis of the patient’s prior history of cervical cancer and the markedly elevated SCC-Ag (13.5 ng/mL). Optimal treatment for these patients—encompassing surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted biological therapy—should be tailored to the individual [13]. Comprehensive and individualized evaluation is therefore essential for treatment planning. In the present case, the possibility of pulmonary tumor embolism—defined as the passage of tumor fragments into the pulmonary arterial circulation—represented a major concern given the location and mobility of the mass extending from the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery. Although no definitive pulmonary embolism was confirmed preoperatively on CTPA, the high risk of intraoperative tumor fragmentation and embolization constituted the primary indication for surgical intervention. Accordingly, right ventricular mass resection was prioritized as the treatment of choice to improve the patient’s outcome [14].

Anesthetic induction and risk of embolization

Anesthetic management of patients with intracardiac tumors carries extremely high risk. Anesthesia induction and surgical manipulation may readily cause tumor fragmentation and precipitate pulmonary embolism; additionally, changes in patient positioning may result in acute RVOT obstruction, which can lead to arrhythmia, hemodynamic instability, and cardiac arrest [15]. Meticulous attention and proactive preventive measures are therefore essential.

Successful surgical outcome depends heavily on thorough preoperative planning and coordinated multidisciplinary teamwork, with particular emphasis on the site and method of CPB cannulation. Femoral artery–femoral vein cannulation to establish peripheral CPB has been advocated, allowing the chest to be opened safely after the heart has been fully decompressed and arrested, thereby minimizing the risk of tumor fragmentation during sternotomy [16]. In this case, central cannulation via the ascending aorta and bicaval venous drainage was selected based on the following considerations: [1] preoperative TEE and CTPA demonstrated that the mass was primarily attached to the right ventricular lateral wall without invasion of the right atrium, atrial septum, or the orifices of the superior and inferior vena cava, making central cannulation technically feasible; [2] antegrade aortic perfusion provides more physiological and hemodynamically controllable systemic organ perfusion while avoiding the risks associated with retrograde perfusion—particularly important in a patient with pre-existing coagulation abnormalities and a potential risk of systemic microembolism; [3] central cannulation affords the surgical team a bloodless, motionless operative field that facilitates complete tumor resection; and [4] intraoperative TEE enables real-time surveillance to confirm the absence of residual tumor fragments before cardiac resuscitation. We acknowledge that this approach carried an inherently higher risk of fatal pulmonary obstruction due to potential fragmentation of the friable, mobile mass during bicaval cannulation, and that peripheral femoral cannulation would have provided an additional margin of safety by permitting CPB to be established prior to sternotomy. This remains an area where alternative cannulation strategies warrant consideration in future cases.

Intraoperative monitoring and hemodynamic management

Advanced intraoperative monitoring is essential to provide real-time data during the most critical phases of the procedure. Invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring should be established prior to anesthesia induction. CVP monitoring is useful for estimating right ventricular preload and right heart filling pressure and for detecting changes in volume status [17]. A rising CVP in association with systemic hypotension often signals the onset of right ventricular failure and impending circulatory collapse [18]. The use of pulmonary artery catheterization in the setting of a right ventricular mass is controversial. Although it can provide valuable hemodynamic data including continuous cardiac output and mixed venous oxygen saturation, it carries a significant risk of dislodging the mass and precipitating acute pulmonary embolism. In this case, pulmonary artery catheterization was not performed, as the risk of tumor fragmentation during catheter passage was judged to outweigh the potential hemodynamic benefits. TEE was therefore used as the primary tool for real-time assessment of ventricular function, valvular competence, and volume status, providing adequate hemodynamic guidance throughout the procedure.

Cerebral oxygen saturation (ScO₂) monitoring provides accurate, real-time information on cerebral tissue perfusion during cardiac surgical procedures. For patients with a baseline ScO₂ greater than 50%, an absolute decrease of 20% warrants intervention; for those with a baseline ScO₂ below 50%, any reduction of 15% should prompt intervention [19]. In this case, regional oxygen saturation was maintained above 50% throughout the procedure, and no clinically significant desaturation events were observed.

Role of transesophageal echocardiography

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) serves as the anesthesiologist’s “eyes” intraoperatively. It provides superior visualization of cardiac structures compared with transthoracic echocardiography, confirming the preoperative diagnosis and offering real-time imaging of tumor movement between the right ventricle and pulmonary artery with each cardiac cycle, although Doppler alignment may be limited in certain views [20]. TEE also enables quantitative assessment of right ventricular function through multiple indices. Specific parameters including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), fractional area change (FAC), and right ventricular strain derived from speckle-tracking echocardiography have been shown to correlate with postoperative outcomes and mortality in patients undergoing cardiac surgery [21]. In the present case, TEE provided continuous real-time guidance throughout the perioperative course—from confirmation of the preoperative diagnosis and guidance during tumor resection to postoperative verification of the adequacy of tricuspid valve repair. TEE was particularly valuable in assessing the degree of residual tricuspid regurgitation following annuloplasty and confirming the absence of residual mass in the right ventricle and pulmonary artery before weaning from CPB.

Hemodynamic strategy

To minimize the risk of catastrophic embolization, anesthesiologists should avoid tachycardia and acute reductions in preload, maintain sinus rhythm, and support systemic vascular resistance without increasing pulmonary vascular resistance [22]. In this case, esmolol [23] and norepinephrine were used in conjunction with fluid therapy guided by TEE and serial blood gas analysis to support hemodynamic stability. Esmolol was selected for its ultra-short-acting β-blocking properties, which allow precise heart rate control to prevent tachycardia-induced tumor fragmentation while offering the ability to rapidly reverse bradycardia if required. Norepinephrine was titrated to maintain systemic vascular resistance and adequate coronary perfusion pressure without exacerbating pulmonary hypertension. Excessive airway pressure during mechanical ventilation was avoided, as elevated plateau pressures can increase pulmonary vascular resistance and impair right ventricular function. Low tidal volumes (5–7 mL/kg) and low PEEP are recommended in this setting [24]. Following weaning from CPB, inotropic support and pulmonary vasodilators may be required to support right ventricular function. Dobutamine, milrinone, and enoximone are preferred agents given their capacity to increase cardiac contractility while reducing pulmonary vascular resistance [25–27]. In this patient, dobutamine provided adequate inotropic support for successful CPB weaning.

Coagulation management

This patient presented with elevated D-dimer and thrombocytopenia, which may represent a manifestation of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Several cases of DIC associated with cardiac angiosarcoma have been reported [28, 29], often related to the Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon (KMP), which is characterized by severe thrombocytopenia [30]. Experimental evidence suggests that tumor cells and damaged endothelial cells may express procoagulant molecules, upregulate the fibrinolytic inhibitor PAI-1 to induce a hypofibrinolytic state, or alternatively express plasminogen-activating factors that drive a hyperfibrinolytic state [31]. The degree of contact activation during cardiac surgery with CPB in patients with malignant tumors may be substantially greater than anticipated [32]. Real-time and accurate coagulation monitoring using rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) or thromboelastography (TEG) is therefore strongly recommended in this context [33]. Unfortunately, viscoelastic coagulation monitoring was not performed in this case, which we recognize as a significant limitation. In the absence of ROTEM or TEG, coagulation management relied on conventional laboratory tests—including serial measurements of prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), fibrinogen, platelet count, and D-dimer (Table 1)—together with clinical assessment of surgical bleeding. Transfusion of FFP, platelets, and cryoprecipitate was guided by these laboratory parameters and the institutional transfusion protocol. We recommend that viscoelastic coagulation monitoring be incorporated as a standard of care in future cases of cardiac surgery involving malignant tumors, as it enables real-time, goal-directed hemostatic therapy [33]. The absence of such monitoring in this case may have resulted in a less optimized coagulation management strategy.

Limitations

Several limitations of this case report warrant acknowledgment. First, viscoelastic coagulation monitoring (ROTEM/TEG) was not performed intraoperatively, limiting our ability to implement goal-directed coagulation management in a patient with DIC-like coagulopathy. Second, central cannulation was chosen over the potentially safer peripheral (femoral) approach; the clinical rationale for this decision, as outlined above, may not be applicable to all similar cases. Third, preoperative cardiac MRI and biopsy were not performed, which limited the certainty of the preoperative diagnosis. Fourth, follow-up data were limited to eight months; longer-term follow-up, including survival and recurrence outcomes, would provide additional clinical value given the poor prognosis associated with metastatic cardiac tumors. Fifth, the decision not to insert a pulmonary artery catheter, while justified on safety grounds, limited the availability of continuous cardiac output monitoring and mixed venous oxygen saturation measurements.

Conclusion

We describe the anesthetic management of a patient with a rare intracardiac metastatic tumor from cervical cancer carrying a high risk of embolization. This case highlights three key anesthetic considerations: [1] meticulous, stepwise induction to minimize hemodynamic disturbance and tumor fragmentation risk; [2] comprehensive monitoring incorporating TEE and cerebral oximetry for real-time hemodynamic surveillance and embolic risk assessment; and [3] multidisciplinary preoperative planning with careful attention to CPB cannulation strategy and coagulation management. The incorporation of viscoelastic coagulation monitoring and consideration of peripheral CPB establishment may further enhance the safety of anesthetic management in similar cases.
