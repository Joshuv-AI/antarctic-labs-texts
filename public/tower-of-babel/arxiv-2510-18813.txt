A Geometric Approach to Steerable Convolutions
Soumyabrata Kundu soumyabratakundu@uchicago.edu, Risi Kondor risi@cs.uchicago.edu

Abstract
In contrast to the somewhat abstract, group theoretical approach adopted by many papers, our work provides a new and more intuitive derivation of steerable convolutional neural networks in d d dimensions. This derivation is based on geometric arguments and fundamental principles of pattern matching. We offer an intuitive explanation for the appearance of the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition and spherical harmonic basis functions. Furthermore, we suggest a novel way to construct steerable convolution layers using interpolation kernels that improve upon existing implementation, and offer greater robustness to noisy data.



1
Introduction

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are one of the most successful neural network architectures, and an essential part of modern computer vision systems. As the name implies, the distinguishing feature of CNNs is that the learnable filters of the network are combined with its activations via
convolutions
. This guarantees that the same set of filters are applied to the image at every pixel position, and hence the network can recognize a given feature or constellation of features equally well in any part of the visual field. In modern parlance, we say that convolutional neural networks are
equivariant to translations
.

Steerable neural networks generalize this framework to also encompass rotations, and hence every point in the output carries additional information about the orientation of features. Consequently, the output of a steerable convolutional layer is a function of both position and orientation. Steerable neural networks offer a clear advantage over classical CNNs by eliminating the need to learn separate filters for each spatial orientation when detecting image constituents like edges and curves. As a whole, the inductive bias of steerable neural networks is more specific and faithful to the structure of most types of natural images than that of simple convolutional networks. This is especially true in applications such as medical imaging, where equivariance to rotations is essential for finding physiologically relevant features.

1.1
Related Work

To achieve invariance to specific transformations, one common approach is to apply these transformations to the input and then pool their responses, as shown in studies by
Sohn & Lee 2012
;
Kanazawa et al. 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2015
. Recently, however, there has been a growing focus on rotation equivariant CNNs, which are designed to handle general rotations rather than being limited to specific transformations. Steerable filters, originally explored in the 1990s by
Freeman & Adelson 1991
;
Reisert & Burkhardt 2009
, have seen renewed interest as key components in modern neural networks.

Dieleman et al. 2016
introduced four core operations that seamlessly integrate into conventional convolutional networks by augmenting both the batch and feature dimensions with transformed versions of the input. Subsequently,
Marcos et al. 2017
enforced rotational equivariance by pooling feature responses across multiple orientations.
Cohen & Welling 2016
developed a theoretical framework for group equivariant convolutional networks, focusing on discrete transformation groups constrained to the pixel grid, such as the wallpaper group
p4
, which includes translations and
90^{\circ}
rotations. Expanding on this,
Cohen & Welling 2017
proposed the use of steerable representations composed of elementary feature types, enabling equivariance to a wider range of orientations. In parallel,
Worrall et al. 2017
introduced circular harmonic filters, marking a significant step towards continuous rotation equivariance. This was further refined by
Weiler et al. 2018b
, who proposed a weight initialization scheme inspired by
He et al. 2015
.
Weiler & Cesa 2019
extended the steerable framework to include reflections in addition to rotations, thereby achieving equivariance under the full Euclidean group
\mathrm{E}(2)
.

In three dimensions,
Worrall & Brostow 2018
introduced an architecture that achieves equivariance to translations and right angle rotations.
Weiler et al. 2018a
broadened the scope by developing models that are equivariant to arbitrary 3D rotations and translations. In a related direction,
Thomas et al. 2018
proposed Tensor Field Networks, which guarantee equivariance to rotations, translations, and permutations for 3D point cloud data. For data defined on a three dimensional sphere,
Cohen et al. 2018
introduced spherical CNNs that are equivariant to rotations. This framework was further refined by
Kondor et al. 2018
, who proposed the Clebsch–Gordan non-linearity, a Fourier space non-linearity that mitigates numerical errors caused by repeated transformations between the spatial and spectral domains. More recently,
Cesa et al. 2022
generalized these ideas to include rotations, translations, and reflections in arbitrary
d
dimensional spaces, providing a principled approach for constructing architectures equivariant to the Euclidean group
\mathrm{E}(d)
.

In a broader and more abstract setting,
Krajsek & Mester 2007
introduced a unified theory of steerable filters grounded in Lie group theory, providing a systematic framework for analyzing their structure and behavior. Building on this foundation,
Cohen et al. 2019
formulated a general theory of group equivariant convolutional networks on homogeneous spaces, offering a flexible and principled basis for designing equivariant architectures.
Kondor & Trivedi 2018
formally proved that convolution is the most general form of equivariant linear maps for compact groups, demonstrating that no broader class of equivariant linear operations exists. Extending these theoretical developments,
Lang & Weiler 2021
generalized the classical Wigner–Eckart theorem to the setting of equivariant convolution kernels, advancing the mathematical understanding of symmetry in machine learning.

1.2
Main Contribution

Existing works on two and three dimensional steerability
(
Cohen & Welling 2017
;
Weiler & Cesa 2019
;
Weiler et al. 2018b
;
Weiler et al. 2018a
)
, along with general theories for
d
dimensional steerability
(
Cohen et al. 2019
;
Cesa et al. 2022
)
, predominantly adopt an algebraic framework to derive the equations for steerable convolutions. While this approach is mathematically rigorous, it often introduces a level of complexity that can hinder the understanding and broader adoption of these methods.
In this paper, we propose a novel and intuitive method for deriving the equations of steerable convolutions by emphasizing on geometric reasoning over traditional algebraic techniques. This geometric perspective simplifies the underlying concepts, and offers a more nuanced understanding of the operations required for the implementation of steerable neural networks. Starting from the foundational principles of classical CNNs and Fourier transforms, we systematically develop the equations for steerable convolutions, making the methodology more accessible and practical for a wider audience. This derivation also leads to an alternative implementation of steerable convolution which, as demonstrated by our experiments, outperforms existing approaches in the literature and exhibits greater robustness to noise perturbations.

We extend the foundational result of
Kondor & Trivedi 2018
, which established that convolutions are the most general equivariant linear maps for compact groups, to a broader setting. While elements of this generalization have been discussed in
Weiler et al. 2018a
for three dimensional setting and in
Cohen et al. 2019
within the context of homogeneous spaces of locally compact groups, our work provides a unified and rigorous formulation. The theorem we present not only consolidates these fragmented insights but also encompasses all prior results as special cases, offering a comprehensive and cohesive extension to the theory.

In the context of three dimensional steerable convolutions, earlier works of
Weiler et al. 2018a
;
Thomas et al. 2018
have recognized spherical harmonics as fundamental building blocks of the architecture. These studies primarily employ spherical harmonics to construct a basis for steerable filters. In contrast, our approach offers a more intuitive interpretation by showing that spherical harmonics naturally arise through the application of the Fourier transform over the rotation group. Furthermore, we extend this perspective to
d
dimensional spaces, demonstrating that steerable filters can be systematically derived using spherical harmonic basis functions, This leads to a unified framework for developing rotation and translation equivariant architectures in arbitrary
d
dimensions.

When applying steerable architectures to real world image data, the discrete nature of images often compromises theoretical equivariance, resulting in small but notable errors. We analyze the
loss of equivariance
in practical implementations, and provide theoretical bounds for these errors, offering deeper insights into the behavior, limitations, and practical applicability of steerable neural networks.

2
The Principles Behind Classical CNNs

As a foundation for a “first principles” approach to steerable neural networks, we begin by revisiting the fundamental concepts underlying classical two dimensional CNNs. While real world neural networks operate on finite resolution rasterized images, we simplify the mathematical treatment by modeling the inputs, filters, and activations as functions defined on continuous domains. Furthermore, we assume that our input image has a single channel. The generalization to multiple channels is relatively straightforward.

2.1
Pattern Matching

In an idealized 2D continuous CNN, the input image is represented as a function
f^{\textrm{in}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, mapping spatial coordinates to intensity values. The intuitive idea behind CNNs is to find visually meaningful features by comparing local regions of the image against a hierarchy of small, learnable filters
w\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, defined on a compact domain
\Omega\hskip-1.00006pt=\hskip-1.00006pt[-h,h]^{2}
for some
h>0
. This pattern matching idea is best expressed as a three stage process. First, around every image location
\mathbf{x}
we extract a “patch function”
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\textbf{y})=f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+\textbf{y}).

(1)

Second, this patch function is compared with a filter
w
, by computing their inner product. This gives the so called “pre-activation function”,

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x})=\langle f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}},w\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\textbf{y})\,w(\textbf{y})\,d\textbf{y}.

(2)

Finally, we get the output by applying a pointwise nonlinearity
\gamma
, such as the popular ReLU function
\gamma_{\textrm{ReLU}}(x)=\textrm{max}(0,x)
, and get

f^{\textrm{out}}(\mathbf{x})=\gamma(f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x})).

Neglecting complications such as pooling and skip connections, classical CNNs simply iterate this pattern matching process over multiple layers. Throughout this paper, we will consistently use the notation
f^{\textrm{in}}
,
w
, and
f^{\textrm{patch}}
, even in more general settings, with the understanding that the underlying domains of the functions will be clear from the context.

2.2
Convolution on a Group

CNNs are named for their close connection between the pattern matching process and the mathematical concept of convolution. Equations
(
1
)
and
(
2
)
imply that
f^{\textrm{pre}}
is the cross-correlation of
f^{\textrm{in}}
and
w
,

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega}f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+\textbf{y})\,w(\textbf{y})\,d\textbf{y}.

(3)

Technically, the convolution of
f^{\textrm{in}}
with
w
is given by

(f^{\textrm{in}}\ast w)(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega}f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}-\textbf{y})\,w(\textbf{y})\,d\textbf{y}.

(4)

However, (
3
) and (
4
) are closely related, and the latter can be transformed into the former by replacing
w
with
\overline{w}(\textbf{y}):=w(-\textbf{y})
. This conflation of convolution and correlation is common, and algorithms implementing (
3
) are still called
Convolutional Neural Networks
.

Equation (
4
) is the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-specific formula for convolution. However, the concept of convolution generalizes naturally to any locally compact group
\mathcal{G}
(see Appendix
A.1
for definition). For functions
f^{\textrm{in}}
and
w
defined on
\mathcal{G}
, the analogue of (
4
) would be

(f\ast\overline{w})(x)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f^{\textrm{in}}(xy^{-1})\,\overline{w}(y)\,d\mu(y).

(5)

Here,
\overline{w}(y)=w(y^{-1})
and
\mu
is the canonical measure on
\mathcal{G}
, called the Haar measure (see Appendix
A.4
for definition).

2.3
Equivariance

The fundamental principle underlying the success of CNNs is that once a set of filters learns to recognize a particular image feature, it should be able to detect that feature equally well at any location in the image plane. Equivalently, if the input to a given CNN layer is translated by some vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the output must also be translated the same way. This property is called
equivariance
to the translation group
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
As we will see now, this idea extends naturally beyond
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, allowing for a broader generalization to other domains and symmetry groups.

Definition 1
.

Given a domain
\mathcal{X}
and group
\mathcal{G}
acting on it (see Appendix
A.2
for definition of group action and continuous group action), a natural action of the group on any function on
\mathcal{X}
is defined as
[g\cdot f](x)=f(g^{-1}\cdot x)
.

Definition 2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are two domains with group
\mathcal{G}
acting on them. Let
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
denote the space of all complex valued functions on a domain. Then
\phi\colon\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{Y})
is said to be
equivariant
if for any
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X})
,
g\cdot\phi[f]=\phi[g\cdot f]
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a measurable space and
\mathcal{G}
a group acting on
\mathcal{X}
, continuously. Denote by
C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
the space of compactly supported continuous functions defined on
\mathcal{X}
, and by
C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
the space of all continuous bounded functions on
\mathcal{G}
. An equivariant linear map
\phi\colon C_{c}(\mathcal{X})\to C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
can be defined as

\phi[f](g)=\langle g^{-1}\cdot f,{w}\rangle,

(6)

where
w\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
. Here,
\mathcal{L}_{1}(\cdot)
denotes the space of integrable functions on a given domain, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represents the inner product with respect to the measure on that domain. Proposition
1
confirms that
\phi
is indeed an equivariant linear map.

Proposition 1
.

The map
\phi
defined in (
6
) is an equivariant linear map.

Proof.

Linearity of
\phi
follows directly from the linearity of the inner product. To verify equivariance, observe that for any
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
, we have

\displaystyle g\cdot\phi[f](h)=\phi[f](g^{-1}h)=\langle(g^{-1}h)^{-1}\cdot f,w\rangle=\langle h^{-1}g\cdot f,w\rangle=\langle h^{-1}\cdot g\cdot f,w\rangle=\phi[g\cdot f](h).

Since
g
and
h
were arbitrary, this identity holds for all
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
, completing the proof.
∎

Equation (
6
) offers a way to construct equivariant linear maps. This leads to a natural question: does this representation capture the most general form such maps can take? Under mild regularity assumptions on the space
\mathcal{X}
, the answer is yes. These assumptions are typically satisfied by spaces encountered in practical applications. The theorem below formalizes this statement.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a locally compact Hausdorff space and
\mathcal{G}
be a group that acts continuously on it. Suppose
\phi:C_{c}(\mathcal{X})\to C_{b}(\mathcal{G})
is a bounded linear map that is equivariant with respect to the action of
\mathcal{G}
.
Then there exists a
unique
complex measure
\lambda
such that for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\phi[f](g)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}g^{-1}\cdot f\,d\lambda.

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{X}
is assumed to be
\sigma
-compact and equipped with a
\sigma
-finite measure, then there exists a
unique
function
w\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and a
unique
singular measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
such that for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
,

\phi[f](g)=\langle g^{-1}\cdot f,w\rangle+\int_{\mathcal{X}}g^{-1}\cdot f\,d\nu.

(7)

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
shows that a general equivariant linear map consists of two main parts: an inner product component and a singular component. The singular component accounts for edge cases, such as when
\phi
is the identity, but it can be ignored in practice. The construction in equation (
6
) corresponds to the special case in which the singular measure
\nu\equiv 0
. Furthermore, when
\mathcal{X}
is the group itself, (
6
) reduces to convolution on the group (
5
). This shows that a classical CNNs can be viewed as convolution on the group
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, or more broadly, as an equivariant linear map under the action of translation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Remark 2
.

Kondor & Trivedi 2018
proved a special case of Theorem
1
for compact groups using a Fourier space based argument. However, their method is more intricate and requires stronger assumptions about the underlying function spaces.
Cohen et al. 2019
proved a related result in the setting of homogeneous spaces of locally compact groups. Their analysis assumes that the linear maps can be expressed in integral form, which excludes the appearance of singular measures in their formulation. In contrast, Theorem
1
applies more broadly. As established in Lemma
6
, if the group
\mathcal{G}
is locally compact and
\sigma
-compact, then a homogeneous space of
\mathcal{G}
(a space where the action of
\mathcal{G}
is continuous and transitive) inherits these topological properties, thereby satisfying the assumptions of the theorem. Specific cases involving locally compact groups such as
\mathrm{SE}(3)
and
\textrm{E}(d)
were previously addressed by
Weiler et al. 2018a
and
Cesa et al. 2022
, respectively. Theorem
1
offers a cleaner and more general formulation, clearly stating the assumptions on the domains and function spaces, and unifying all of these prior results as special cases.

3
Fourier Space Neural Networks

The Fourier transform of a function on
\mathbb{R}
is given by

\widehat{f}(\mathbf{k})=\int f(\mathbf{x})\,e^{\iota\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,d\mathbf{x}.

(8)

In Fourier space, by the well known
convolution theorem
, convolution reduces to

\widehat{(f\ast w)}(\mathbf{k})=\widehat{f}(\mathbf{k})\,\widehat{w}(\mathbf{k}).

(9)

In general, (
9
) is of course much more efficient to compute than (
4
). However, the filters in classical CNNs are typically so small that the Fourier approach would afford little or no advantage, so most CNNs still operate in the time domain. In contrast, when functions are defined on nontrivial groups, the computation of convolution in the time domain becomes considerably more challenging. For example, on the rotation group in
d
dimensions,
\mathrm{SO}(d)
(see Appendix
A.1
for definition), time domain convolution is not only computationally expensive as it requires an exponential number of discretization points with respect to
d
, but also susceptible to numerical instabilities, particularly due to singularities near the poles. These factors make performing direct convolution operations on such groups particularly challenging, which makes Fourier space methods a more practical option. Remarkably, the Fourier transform generalizes to any compact group
\mathcal{G}
in the form

\widehat{f}(\rho)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(g)\,\rho(g)\,d\mu(g).

(10)

Here, instead of just being complex exponential,
\rho
extends over all
irreducible representations (irreps)
of
\mathcal{G}
(see Appendix
A.3
for definition). The irreps of a compact group are finite dimensional unitary complex matrix valued functions
\rho\colon\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho}}
, and hence the Fourier components are now complex matrices instead of a complex scalar. The convolution theorem also generalizes in the elegant form

\widehat{f\ast g}(\rho)=\widehat{f}(\rho)\,\widehat{g}(\rho),

(11)

where the right hand side now involves multiplying
\widehat{f}(\rho)
and
\widehat{g}(\rho)
as matrices (the convolution theorem for compact groups is proved in Lemma
2
). Neural networks operating in Fourier space have become very popular
(
Weiler et al. 2018a
;
Weiler et al. 2018b
;
Kondor et al. 2018
;
Thomas et al. 2018
;
Anderson et al. 2019
)
. We shall see that it is critical for both understanding and implementing steerable neural networks.

3.1
The Clebsch–Gordan Decomposition

A key tool required for deriving steerable neural networks is the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition (CG–decomposition). In general, this is used to decompose the tensor product of two irreps of a given group
\mathcal{G}
into a direct sum of irreps,

\rho_{1}(g)\hskip-1.00006pt\otimes\hskip-1.00006pt\rho_{2}(g)=C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\Bigl[\bigoplus_{\rho}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}\rho(g)\Bigr]C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\dagger},\quad g\in\mathcal{G}.

(12)

Here,
\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)
is the multiplicity of
\rho
appearing in the CG–decomposition of
\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
. The unitary matrices
C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}
are the so called the Clebsch–Gordan matrices (CG–matrices). While at first sight the CG–decomposition may seem complicated, in some of the relevant special cases it reduces to a simple form. For example, in the case of the two dimensional rotation group
\mathrm{SO}(2)
, the irreps are all one dimensional and given by
\rho_{k}(\theta)=e^{\iota k\theta}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. In this setting equation (
12
) reduces to

\rho_{k_{1}}(\theta)\,\rho_{k_{2}}(\theta)=\rho_{k_{1}+k_{2}}(\theta).

While the CG–decomposition has appeared in neural nets in different roles, we argue that the fundamental reason that they appear in steerable convolutions is the following result.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a compact group and
f
a function on the product group
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
with Fourier transform

\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\!\!\int_{\mathcal{G}}\int_{\mathcal{G}}\!\!\!\!f(g_{1},g_{2})\bigl(\rho_{1}(g)\otimes\rho_{2}(g)\bigr)\,d\mu(g_{1})d\mu(g_{2}).

(13)

Then the Fourier transform of the restriction of
f
to the diagonal subgroup,

f^{\textrm{diag}}(g):=f(g,g)
, is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{f^{\textrm{diag}}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}\frac{d_{\rho_{1}}d_{\rho_{2}}}{d_{\rho}\mu(\mathcal{G})}\Bigl[C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\dagger}\,\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\,C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\Bigr]_{\rho,i},

(14)

where
[A]_{\rho,i}
denotes the
i
’th diagonal block in the matrix
A
corresponding to the
irrep
\rho
.

While the CG–decomposition involves the tensor product of group representations, as we will see, it is a crucial component in deriving steerable convolutions. To make the notation more streamlined, we will use the notation
\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{CG}\\
\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\to\rho\end{subarray}\left(\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\right)
to denote the transformation in the right hand side of (
14
).

Remark 3
.

In the relevant cases of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
and
\mathrm{SO}(3)
, the multiplicity of any irrep
\rho
in the CG–decomposition of
\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}
satisfies
\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)\leq 1
. This means that the multiplicity of any irrep
\rho
appearing in the CG–decomposition has multiplicity exactly one. For simplicity, we adopt this assumption going forward. With this assumption, we denote by
C^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho_{1}}d_{\rho_{2}}\times d_{\rho}}
the subset of columns of the CG–matrix
C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}
corresponding to the irrep
\rho
.

4
Steerable Convolutions

Steerable neural networks are constructed to enforce equivariance under rigid body transformations. In mathematical terms, this means that in
d
dimensional space, the network layers exhibit equivariance to the natural action of the
Special Euclidean group
. Theorem
1
provides the foundation for defining equivariant linear maps in this framework. In this section, we will explore how this general form of equivariant linear maps and convolution theorem can be used to derive the equations for steerable convolutions.

4.1
The Special Euclidean Group

The group of encompassing all rotations and translations is referred to as the special euclidean group and is denoted by
\mathrm{SE}(d)
in
d
dimensions. Any element of
\mathrm{SE}(d)
can be expressed as
(\mathbf{t},R)
, where
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the translational component, and
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
denotes the rotational component. This group acts on a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
according to the rule
(\mathbf{t},R)\cdot\mathbf{x}=R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}
, and the group operation is given by

(\mathbf{t}^{\prime},R^{\prime})(\mathbf{t},R)=(\mathbf{t}^{\prime}+R^{\prime}\mathbf{t},\,R^{\prime}R).

From an algebraic point of view,
\mathrm{SE}(d)
is the so called
semi-direct product

\mathbb{R}^{d}\rtimes\mathrm{SO}(d)
(see Appendix
A.1
for more details).
In line with Definition
1
, the action of
(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}
on a function
f
defined over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
would be

[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f](\mathbf{x})=f(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}).

On the other hand, when
f
is defined on the group itself, the action becomes

[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f](\mathbf{x},R^{\prime})=f((\mathbf{t},R)(\mathbf{x},R^{\prime}))=f(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t},RR^{\prime}).

4.2
First Layer

We will now derive the equations of steerable convolutional networks, by applying the general equivariant linear map (
6
) in the context of the group
\mathrm{SE}(d)
.
The input field
f^{\textrm{in}}
is a function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Similar to classical CNNs, the weight function
w
is assumed to be compactly supported on
\Omega:=[-h,h]^{d}
.
With this setup, the equivariant linear map (
6
) becomes

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)=\langle{(\mathbf{x},R)^{-1}}\cdot f^{\textrm{in}},w\rangle=\int_{[-h,h]^{d}}f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+R\textbf{y})w(\textbf{y})d\textbf{y}.

(15)

Equation (
15
) captures the core operation of steerable convolutions. The key difference from classical CNNs is evident in this formulation: instead of applying the filter to a single patch at each spatial location
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, steerable convolutions apply the filter to rotated versions of the patch, for
every
rotation
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
.
Specifically, in two dimensions, the pre-activation function simplifies to

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},\phi)=\int_{[-h,h]^{2}}f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+R_{\phi}\textbf{y})w(\textbf{y})d\textbf{y},

(16)

where
R_{\phi}
is the standard 2D rotation matrix

R_{\phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{bmatrix}.

Intuitively,
f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},\phi)
quantifies how well the filter
w
matches the input
f^{\textrm{in}}
at location
\mathbf{x}
for each orientation
\phi
. This generalizes the pattern matching mechanism in classical CNNs: a single filter
w
is not only applied to all spatial patches, but also to their rotated versions at every possible angle. This added rotational dimension allows steerable neural networks to effectively capture features invariant or equivariant to rotations (see Figure
1(a)
).

\mathbf{x}
, the filter is matched against the input at all possible orientations
\theta
. (a) The patch grid
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x},\theta}
is generally not aligned with the underlying image grid. (b) To address this, both the patches centered at
\mathbf{x}
and the filters are expressed in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
.

Generalizing the pattern matching principle to the first layer of a steerable convolutional network leads to the expression in (
15
). In practice, when working with finite resolution images represented on a square grid, this operation becomes more involved. The added difficulty arises from the need to interpolate values from the original image grid to a grid at arbitrary rotations
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
.
To simplify this process, one can transition from square filters to filters supported on a ball of radius
h
, expressing both the input and filter in polar coordinates (see Figure
1(b)
). In this representation, any
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be written as
\textbf{y}=rR^{\prime}\mathbf{e}
, for some
r\geq 0
,
R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
and a fixed reference vector
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The patch function in polar coordinates is then defined as
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,R^{\prime})=f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+rR^{\prime}\mathbf{e})
.
Using this patch function, the expression in (
15
) can be simplified to

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)=\int_{0}^{h}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,RR^{\prime-1})\overline{w}(r,{R^{\prime}})r^{d-1}\,d\mu(R^{\prime})\,dr,

(17)

where
\overline{w}(r,{R^{\prime}}^{-1})=w(rR^{\prime}\mathbf{e})
. The equality in (
17
) is a direct consequence of Lemma
3
and
4
. Note that, for a fixed
r
, the inner integral is a convolution over the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
. This observation naturally motivates a Fourier approach, as convolutions in the angular domain is more efficient in Fourier space.
Applying the convolution theorem,
f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)
can be represented in Fourier space as

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\int_{0}^{h}\widehat{f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}}(r,\rho)\,\widehat{\overline{w}}(r,\rho)\,r^{d-1}\,dr,

(18)

where the Fourier transform of the patch function
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}
is given by

\widehat{f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}}(r,\rho)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,R)\rho(R)d\mu(R).

(19)

Remark 4
.

The group
\mathrm{SE}(d)
itself is not compact. Without compactness, we cannot ensure that the irreps are finite dimensional. However, the rotation component
\mathrm{SO}(d)
within
\mathrm{SE}(d)
is indeed a compact group. In the derivation of steerable convolutional layers, we capitalize on this fact and employ the convolution theorem solely on the angular component. This results in a mixed Fourier approach, where convolution in the translation component is performed in the time domain, while the convolution in the angular component is carried out in Fourier domain.

4.3
Higher Layers

The output of (
15
) is a function defined on both position
\mathbf{x}
and rotation
R
, meaning that in subsequent layers, both the input field
f^{\textrm{in}}
and the filter
w
are functions defined on
\mathrm{SE}(d)
. To proceed, we need to establish a compact support for the filter
w
. This can be achieved by setting the support to
\Omega:=[-h,h]^{d}\times\mathrm{SO}(d)
, which is compact since
\mathrm{SO}(d)
itself is a compact group. Under this assumption, the equivariant linear map for higher layers takes the form

\displaystyle f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)

\displaystyle=\langle{(\mathbf{x},R)^{-1}}\cdot f^{\textrm{in}},w\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+R\textbf{y},RR^{\prime})w(\textbf{y},R^{\prime})\,d\mu_{\mathrm{SE}(d)}(\textbf{y},R^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{[-h,h]^{d}}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+R\textbf{y},RR^{\prime})w(\textbf{y},R^{\prime})\,d\mu(R^{\prime})\,d\textbf{y}.

(20)

The last equality follows from Lemma
5
. Similar to the first layer, we will express (
4.3
) in Fourier space. By leveraging the compactness of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
, the convolution theorem can be applied to the angular component, leading to a Fourier space representation that simplifies computations. The details of this transformation and its implications for steerable convolutions are provided below.

We begin by defining the patch function at each position
\mathbf{x}
. Since the input
f^{\textrm{in}}
already includes a rotational component, the patch function in polar coordinates involves two rotation arguments:
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})=f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+rR^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{e},R^{\prime})
. Using this formulation, equation (
4.3
) can be simplified to

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)=\int_{0}^{h}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,RR^{\prime-1},RR^{\prime\prime-1})\overline{w}(r,R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})r^{d-1}\,d\mu(R^{\prime})\,d\mu(R^{\prime\prime})\,dr,

(21)

where
\overline{w}(r,R^{\prime-1},R^{\prime\prime-1})=w(rR^{\prime}\mathbf{e},R^{\prime\prime})
. Although equation (
21
) bears a resemblance to a convolution over the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)\times\mathrm{SO}(d)
, it is not quite the same thing. The inner product calculation involves integrating over
R^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime\prime}
, which are coupled through
R
. As a result, applying the convolution theorem is not straightforward. To address this, we introduce “shadow rotations”
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, and define the quantity

q_{\mathbf{x}}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\int_{0}^{h}\!\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,R_{1}R^{\prime-1},R_{2}R^{\prime\prime-1})\,\overline{w}(r,R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})\,r^{d-1}\,d\mu(R^{\prime})\,d\mu(R^{\prime\prime})\,dr,

(22)

which, for a fixed radius
r
, can now be interpreted as a convolution on the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)\times\mathrm{SO}(d)
. In Fourier space, this convolution is expressed as

\widehat{q}_{\mathbf{x}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\int_{0}^{h}\widehat{f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}}(r,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\widehat{\overline{w}}(r,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\,r^{d-1}\,dr,

(23)

where
\widehat{f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}}(r,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
is given by

\widehat{f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}}(r,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})(\rho_{1}(R^{\prime})\otimes\rho_{2}(R^{\prime\prime}))\,d\mu(R^{\prime})\,d\mu(R^{\prime\prime}).

(24)

Given that
f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)=q_{\mathbf{x}}(R,R)
, it remains to extract
\widehat{{f^{\textrm{pre}}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)
from
\widehat{q}_{\mathbf{x}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
. As Proposition
2
suggests, this operation can be carried out efficiently using the CG–decomposition of
\widehat{q}_{\mathbf{x}}
, yielding

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{CG}\\
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\to\rho\end{subarray}\left(\widehat{q}_{\mathbf{x}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\right)=\int_{0}^{h}\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{CG}\\
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\to\rho\end{subarray}\left(\widehat{f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}}(r,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\widehat{\overline{w}}(r,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\right)\,r^{d-1}\,dr,

(25)

Recall the notation
\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{CG}\\
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\to\rho\end{subarray}(\cdot)
, introduced in Remark
3
, which denotes the CG–decomposition (
14
).

Remark 5
.

From Proposition
1
, we know that when an element
(\mathbf{t},R)\in\mathrm{SE}(d)
acts on the input map as
f^{\textrm{in}}\mapsto(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f^{\textrm{in}}=f^{\textrm{in}}(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t})
, the resulting pre-activation function transforms accordingly as

f^{\textrm{pre}}(\mathbf{x},R)\mapsto[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f^{\textrm{pre}}](\mathbf{x},R^{\prime})=f^{\textrm{pre}}(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t},RR^{\prime}).

Since the rotational component is expressed in Fourier space, the translation property of the Fourier transform ensures that
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}
, for each irrep
\rho
, transforms as

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)\mapsto[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}](\mathbf{x},\rho)=\rho(R)^{\dagger}\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t},\rho).

(26)

4.4
Comparison to Previous Derivations

In this section, we will discuss the approach taken by previous works in deriving steerable convolutions. Previous works, such as those by
Cohen & Welling 2017
;
Weiler et al. 2018b
;
Thomas et al. 2018
;
Weiler & Cesa 2019
;
Cesa et al. 2022
introduce steerable convolutions as convolving “steerable features” with “steerable filters”. First, let us define these concepts.

Definition 3
.

A feature map
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}}
is said to be
\rho
-steerable if, under the action of any
(\mathbf{t},R)\in\mathrm{SE}(d)
, it transforms according to
[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f](\mathbf{x})=\rho(R)^{\dagger}f(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t})
.

Definition 4
.

A filter
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho_{1}}}
is called a
(\rho,\rho_{1})
-steerable filter if, for any
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
, it satisfies the condition

K(R\mathbf{y})=\rho(R)K(\mathbf{y})\rho_{1}(R)^{\dagger}
.

In essence, a
(\rho,\rho_{1})
-steerable filter “steers”
\rho_{1}
-steerable features to
\rho
-steerable features. Specifically, when a
\rho_{1}
-steerable feature map
f
is convolved with a
(\rho,\rho_{1})
-steerable filter
K
, the output feature map is
\rho
-steerable:

\displaystyle[K*(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f](\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(\textbf{y})\rho_{1}(R)^{\dagger}f(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}-R\textbf{y},\rho_{1})d\textbf{y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(R^{-1}\textbf{y})\rho_{1}(R)^{\dagger}f(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}-\textbf{y},\rho_{1})d\textbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(R)^{\dagger}K(\textbf{y})f(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}-\textbf{y},\rho_{1})d\textbf{y}=(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot[K*f](\mathbf{x}).

The discussion in Remark
5
establishes that the pre-activation function in Fourier space,
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\cdot,\rho)
, is
\rho
-steerable. The geometric derivation of steerable convolutions, presented in Section
4
, shows that steerable feature maps can be understood as functions defined on the group
\mathrm{SE}(d)
, with the rotational component expressed in the Fourier domain. While the concept of steerable convolutions, when viewed through the lens of steerable features and filters, simplifies the derivation, it does not offer much insight into their underlying principles. Our derivation demonstrates that under the hood, we are essentially matching the same filter across all possible rotations. This can be understood as a generalization of the core principles of CNNs to the group
\mathrm{SE}(d)
.

5
Implementation

In section
4
, we discussed how the pattern matching argument in CNNs can be replaced by a direct appeal to the standard method of generalizing equivariant linear maps (
6
) to
\mathrm{SE}(d)
, leading to the equations of steerable convolutions in first and higher layers. While this derivation provides a solid theoretical basis, it offers limited guidance for practical network design and implementation. In this section, we will build on this foundation to expand on how to implement steerable convolutional layers.

5.1
Interpolation

In practical implementations, the input to a convolution layer are finite resolution rasterized images defined on a grid. These images can be thought of as functions on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, which are zero outside a finite set. As discussed in Section
4
, these images need to be interpolated from a Cartesian coordinate system to a spherical coordinate system. Standard interpolation techniques, such as linear and cubic interpolation, fundamentally operate by computing a weighted sum of values from a grid. Rather than focusing on a specific technique, we take a more general perspective and define interpolation in terms of a discrete kernel, as outlined below.

Definition 5
.

An interpolation kernel is a function
\mathcal{I}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies the following properties:

(a)

For any function
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, the interpolated function
\mathcal{I}[f]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by
\mathcal{I}[f](\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})f(\textbf{y})
.

(b)

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\textbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the kernel satisfies
\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z},\textbf{y}+\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})
.

(c)

For any
\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the function
\mathcal{I}(\cdot,\textbf{y})
is
\alpha
-Hölder for
\alpha\in[0,1]
, i.e., there exists constants
M,C>0
such that for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
|\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})|\leq M
and
|\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})-\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{z},\textbf{y})|\leq C\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{\alpha}
.

(d)

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the quantity
\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})|
is uniformly bounded above by some constant.

Interpolation from a Cartesian grid to a spherical grid introduces errors that result in loss of equivariance. To quantify this loss, we define the following quantity.

Definition 6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is a group acting on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, define

\Delta(g):=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})-\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{z},\textbf{y})\right|.

(27)

The interpolation is said to be exact with respect to the action of
g
, if
\Delta(g)=0
.

In essence,
\Delta(g)
measures the maximum potential information loss incurred when evaluating an input function on the grid obtained by translating it by
g
through interpolation. More precisely, when we evaluate the
g
-translated version of a function
f\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
at a point
\mathbf{x}
, the deviation from the value obtained by directly evaluating the original function at
g\cdot\mathbf{x}
is bounded above by
\Delta(g)\|f\|_{1}
:

\displaystyle\Bigg|\mathcal{I}[f](g\cdot\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{I}[g^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{I}[f]](\mathbf{x})\Bigg|=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})f(\textbf{y})-\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})[g^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{I}[f]](\mathbf{z})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})f(\textbf{y})-\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\mathcal{I}[f](g\cdot\mathbf{z})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})f(\textbf{y})-\sum_{\textbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{z},\textbf{y})f(\textbf{y})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left(\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{x},\textbf{y})-\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\mathcal{I}(g\cdot\mathbf{z},\textbf{y})\right)f(\textbf{y})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Delta(g)\|f\|_{1}

Thus,
\Delta(g)
provides a uniform upper bound on the interpolation error induced by the group action.

Figure 2
:
Illustration of the interpolation kernel
\mathcal{I}
at a fixed
\textbf{y}_{0}
in one dimension. The nearest-neighbor interpolation is an example of a discontinuous kernel, hence corresponding to the case
\alpha=0
in Definition
5
(c). In contrast, linear interpolation varies linearly between adjacent grid points, hence corresponding to the case
\alpha=1
. In
d
dimensions, the kernel
\mathcal{I}
is constructed by multiplying the kernel values along each dimensions.

5.2
Fourier Transform

The interpolation into spherical coordinates is followed by applying the Fourier transform over the rotation group. At first glance, the idea of integrating over a (non-commutative) group might appear intimidating. However, on closer examination, we see that for a fixed radius
r
,
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}(r,\cdot)
is actually a function on the
d
dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. This observation allows us to use
spherical harmonic
basis functions (see Appendix
B
for definition) to simplify the integral.

In
d
dimensions, spherical harmonics form a basis for
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, the space of all square integrable function on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and are closely related to the symmetric traceless irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
(see Appendix
B
for details). A spherical harmonic associated with an irrep
\rho
of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
is a function
\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}}
that satisfies

\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(Rs)=\rho(R)\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s)

(28)

for all
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. The relationship between
\rho
and
\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}
in the above equation simplifies the Fourier transform over the group, as formalized in Proposition
3
.

Proposition 3
.

Fix
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. For any
f\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
,

\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f(R\mathbf{e})\rho(R)d\mu(R)=\begin{cases}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s)\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s)\,d\sigma(s)\bigg)\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(\mathbf{e})^{\dagger}&\rho\text{ is a symmetric traceless irrep},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\sigma
denotes the normalized surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and the spherical harmonics are normalized such that
\|\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s)\|_{2}=1
, for all
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

The expression inside the parentheses is known as the
spherical harmonic transform
of
f
(see Appendix
B
for more details). This result significantly reduces complexity, as the surface measure on the sphere is far simpler to work with than the Haar measure on
\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Furthermore, Proposition
3
highlights that non-symmetric irreps carry no information about
f^{\textrm{patch}}_{\mathbf{x}}
and can be ignored in practice. Interestingly, in two and three dimensions, all irreps are symmetric traceless, so this distinction is not needed. From here on, we will assume the spherical harmonics are normalized such that
\|\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s)\|_{2}=1
, for all
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Remark 6
.

Spherical harmonics are fundamental to the construction of steerable filters. Previous works, such as those by
Weiler et al. 2018a
;
Thomas et al. 2018
in three dimensions and
Cesa et al. 2022
in general
d
dimension, introduced spherical harmonics as basis functions for designing steerable filters. Proposition
3
explains their natural emergence as a direct consequence of applying the Fourier transform on the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
, providing a deeper connection to the symmetry properties of the group.

5.3
Discretization

The discussion in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
demonstrated that steerable convolution layers involve integration over both the radial and rotation components. As discussed in Section
5.2
, the integration over the rotation group can be simplified to an integration over the
d
dimensional sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. In practice, these integrals are approximated numerically through discretization. For the radial part, the interval
(0,h]
can be partitioned into
n_{r}
equally spaced points. To approximate the spherical integration, a discretization of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is necessary.

A point on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
can be parameterized by angular coordinates
\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}:=[0,\pi]^{d-2}\times[0,2\pi)
via the standard mapping

s_{i}(\theta):=\begin{cases}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-2}\sin\theta_{j}\right)\cos\theta_{i}&1\leq i\leq d-1,\\
\,\,\,\,\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sin\theta_{j}&i=d.\end{cases}

(29)

With this parametrization, the integral of any bounded function
f:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{C}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s)\,d\sigma(s)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\int_{\Theta^{d-1}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)\,d\theta,

(30)

\displaystyle\omega(\theta):=\prod_{i=1}^{d-2}(\sin\theta_{i})^{d-1-i},

(31)

where
\sigma
denotes the normalized surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}):=\int\omega(\theta)d\theta
is the area of
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
We use this parameterization to numerically approximate integrals over the sphere by constructing a uniform angular grid, obtained by dividing the intervals
[0,\pi]
and
[0,2\pi)
into
n_{a}
equally spaced points. This yields the discretized grid

\Theta_{n_{a}}^{d-1}:=\left\{\left(\tfrac{\pi}{n_{a}}\left(a_{1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\,\tfrac{\pi}{n_{a}}\left(a_{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\,\dots,\,\tfrac{\pi}{n_{a}}\left(a_{d-2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\right),\,\tfrac{2\pi}{n_{a}}a_{d-1}\right):\;a_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,n_{a}-1\}\right\}.

(32)

The points are shifted from the origin to avoid poles. Using this grid, the the spherical harmonic transform of any
f\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
can be approximated by a Riemann sum:

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s)\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s)\,d\sigma(s)\approx\frac{2\pi^{d-1}}{n_{a}^{d-1}\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{n_{a}}^{d-1}}f(s(\theta))\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s(\theta))\,\omega(\theta).

(33)

Remark 7
.

This uniform angular grid can be used to approximate any integral over the sphere, not only for computing spherical harmonic transforms. Nonetheless, the choice of quadrature weights can be optimized to improve the accuracy of spherical harmonic transform approximations. In three dimensions, for example,
Driscoll & Healy 1994
proposed the quadrature

\omega_{\textrm{DH}}(\theta):=\frac{4}{\pi}\sin\theta_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{n_{a}}{2}-1}\left(\frac{\sin((2k+1)\theta_{1})}{2k+1}\right)

(34)

for the grid
\Theta^{2}_{n_{a}}
, when
n_{a}
is even, that yields the exact spherical harmonic transform exactly for band limited functions. This was used by
Cohen et al. 2018
and
Kondor et al. 2018
in the context of Spherical CNNs. The quadratures (
31
) and (
34
) are asymptotically equivalent as the summation in (
34
) converges to
\frac{\pi}{4}
.

5.4
Implementation in general d dimensions

The input to a steerable convolutional layer is first interpolated onto spherical coordinates at each grid location, followed by a spherical harmonic transformation and finally CG–decomposition (in higher layers). Since all these operations are linear operations, they can be combined into a single “steerable filter”. This steerable filter, combined with learnable weights, can then be applied to the input to generate the pre-activation function. In the following sections, we formalize these concepts and outline a framework for implementing steerable convolutional layers.

5.4.1
First Layer

Let
f^{\textrm{in}}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
denote the input function for a single channel. The output of the steerable convolution corresponding to an irrep
\rho
, consists of
d_{\rho}
columns. For simplicity, in a neural network implementation, these columns are combined into the channel dimension, allowing us to represent the output as
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\cdot,\rho)\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}}
for a single channel. Although
\mathrm{SO}(d)
has infinitely many irreps in theory, we restrict ourselves to a finite subset
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
in computation. With this setup, the theorem below provides a framework for implementing the first layer of a steerable convolutional network. As described in Section
5.3
, the integrals are evaluated by discretizing
(0,h]
into
n_{r}
grid points for the radial component, and using the grid
\Theta_{n_{a}}^{d-1}
for the spherical component.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
f^{\textrm{in}}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be an input map. The pre-activation
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}
for the first layer of steerable convolutional network can be computed as

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}w_{r}^{(\rho)}M_{r}^{(\rho)}(\textbf{y})\right)f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+\textbf{y}),

(35)

where
{w}_{r}^{(\rho)}\in\mathbb{C}
are learnable weights,
\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
, and
M_{r}^{(\rho)}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}}
is defined as

M_{r}^{(\rho)}(\textbf{y}):=\frac{r^{d-1}}{n_{r}^{d}n_{a}^{d-1}}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta_{n_{a}}^{d-1}}\mathcal{I}\left(\frac{rh}{n_{r}}s(\theta),\textbf{y}\right)\mathbf{Y}^{(\rho)}(s(\theta))\,\omega(\theta)

(36)

for
1\leq r\leq n_{r}
.

The function
M_{r}^{(\rho)}
is computed during a precomputation phase
(
Cohen & Welling 2017
;
Weiler et al. 2018a
;
Weiler et al. 2018b
;
Cesa et al. 2022
)
. The forward pass consists of assembling the convolution kernel by combining learned weights with precomputed filters, and then performing convolution for each Fourier component. All these steps are parallelizable and can be efficiently executed on GPUs.

5.4.2
Higher Layers

The higher layers build upon the operations of the first layer by incorporating the CG–decomposition. As this decomposition is also a linear operation, it can similarly be integrated into the steerable filter during the precomputation stage. As a result, the higher layers follow a similar process to the first layer, as established by the following theorem. Recall the CG–matrices
C^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}
introduced in Remark
3
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
f^{\textrm{in}}(\cdot,\rho):\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}}
be the input map corresponding to the irrep
\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
and single input channel. Then,
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}
for the higher layers of steerable convolutions is given by

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}}\left(\sum_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}w_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\tilde{M}_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}(\textbf{y})\right)f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+\textbf{y},\rho_{1}),

(37)

where
w_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\in\mathbb{C}
are learnable weights,
\rho\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
, and the
m^{\text{th}}
row of
\tilde{M}_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}(\textbf{y})\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho_{1}}}
is defined as

\left[\tilde{M}_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}(\textbf{y})\right]_{m,\cdot}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}|}M_{r}^{(\rho_{2})}(\textbf{y})^{\top}\tilde{C}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}_{m}.

(38)

Here,
\tilde{C}_{m}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho_{2}}\times d_{\rho_{1}}}
such that
\left[C^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\right]_{\cdot,m}^{*}=\operatorname{vec}\left(\tilde{C}_{m}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\right)
.

Similar to the first layer, the steerable filter
\tilde{M}_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}
is computed during the precomputation phase. The first layer can be interpreted as a special case of steerable convolutions in higher layer, where the input Fourier cutoff
\rho_{1}
is restricted to the constant representation.

Remark 8
.

Theorems
2
and
3
establish that, unlike conventional convolutions, which directly learn a full weight matrix of size
\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho_{1}}}
, steerable convolutions parameterize the kernel using a single learnable scalar
w^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}
that is linearly combined with a fixed matrix
\tilde{M}_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}(\textbf{y})\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho_{1}}}
. Thus steerable convolutions can be viewed as learning convolutional filters constrained to a predefined steerable basis
(
Cohen & Welling 2017
;
Weiler et al. 2018a
)
.

Remark 9
.

Because matrix multiplication is associative, the operations in equations (
35
) and (
37
) can be executed in two equivalent ways: either by first convolving the input with the precomputed steerable filters and then applying the learned weights, or by combining the weights with the filters beforehand and convolving the result with the inputs. In practice, the latter approach is both faster and more memory efficient, as demonstrated in Figure
3
, which compares runtimes across various parameter settings. The first method is typically slower because it requires storing large intermediate tensors whose size scales with the radial and angular resolutions, and performing weight multiplications at every spatial location. However, if memory is not a limiting factor and sparse convolution for interpolation and Fast Fourier Transform are used, the first approach has the potential to outperform the second in terms of speed. Implementing these optimizations would require writing custom CUDA kernels, which we leave as an interesting direction for future work.

5.5
Loss of Equivariance

While in theory the steerable layers are exactly equivariant, practical implementations deviate from this due to interpolation errors and approximation of integrals by discretization, which result in a loss of equivariance. In this section, we derive bounds that quantify this loss. For brevity, we focus on steerable convolutions in higher layers. However, the results are also applicable to the first layer, as it can be viewed as special cases.

Define the filter
K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho_{1}}}
as

K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}(\textbf{y}):=\sum_{\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}w_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\tilde{M}_{r}^{(\rho,\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}(\textbf{y})

(39)

Suppose
f^{\textrm{in}}(\cdot,\rho):\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}}
is an input feature map, corresponding to irrep
\rho
. According to Theorem
3
, the output of the steerable convolution is computed via a discrete cross-correlation, given by

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\sum_{\rho_{1}}\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}(\textbf{y})f^{\textrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}+\textbf{y},\rho_{1}).

(40)

Since
K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}
is defined over a discrete grid, it does not strictly satisfy the definition of a steerable filter in the continuous sense (as given in Definition
4
). However, if a rotation
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
results in exact interpolation (
\Delta(R)=0
) and maps the spherical grid back onto itself while preserving the quadrature
\omega
, then
K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}
can be interpreted as a discrete counterpart to a steerable filter. This relationship is formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 4
.

Let
K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be as defined in (
39
). Suppose
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
satisfies
\Delta(R)=0
, and the action
s(\theta)\mapsto R\,s(\theta)
defines a bijection on the discretized angular grid
\Theta_{n_{a}}^{d-1}
, with the quadrature
\omega
being invariant under this action. Then, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the filter satisfies

R^{-1}\cdot K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}(\textbf{y})=\rho(R)K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}(\textbf{y})\rho_{1}(R)^{\dagger},

where
R^{-1}\cdot K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}(\textbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}(\mathbf{z})\mathcal{I}(R^{-1}\mathbf{z},\textbf{y})
.

For general transformations, however, the assumptions of Proposition
4
are not satisfied. Even in favorable cases, such as rotations by
90^{\circ}
about the
x
or
y
axes in three dimensions, the transformed angular grid does not align with the original configuration (see Figure
4
). This leads to a loss of equivariance. The following theorem provides an upper bound on this error.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho_{1}}}
be a feature map such that under the action of any
(\mathbf{t},R)\in\mathrm{SE}(d)
, it transforms according to

f(\mathbf{x})\mapsto[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f](\mathbf{x})=\rho_{1}(R)^{\dagger}\mathcal{I}[f](R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}).

Let
K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}
be as defined in (
39
), and assume that its weights are uniformly bounded above by some constant. Then, for any
(\mathbf{t},R)\in\mathrm{SE}(d)
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\|(\mathbf{t},R)\cdot[K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}\star(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\cdot f](\mathbf{x})-[K^{(\rho,\rho_{1})}\star f](\mathbf{x})\|_{\infty}\leq C\bigg(\Delta(\mathbf{t},R)+\Delta(R)+n_{a}^{-\alpha}\bigg)\sum_{\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\|f(\textbf{y})\|_{2}

for some constant
C>0
, which only depends on radius
h
, dimension
d
, choice of
\mathcal{I}
and the bounds on the weights.

Remark 10
.

The error
\Delta(\mathbf{t},R)
arises from applying the back and forth transformation
\mathbf{x}\mapsto R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}
to the grid. The term
\Delta(R)
is introduced by the interpolation performed at each patch location of the rotated feature map. Naturally, if the transformation maps points back onto the integer grid, as in the case of integer translations or rotations by multiples of
90^{\circ}
(about
z
axis), the terms
\Delta(\mathbf{t},R)
and
\Delta(R)
vanish, and no error is introduced. These observations emphasize that interpolation errors depend on how the transformation aligns with the structure of the underlying grid.

Remark 11
.

The term
n_{a}^{-\alpha}
arises from approximating the spherical harmonic transform using a discrete grid, with
\alpha
capturing the smoothness of the interpolation kernel. For nearest-neighbor interpolation (
\alpha=0
), increasing the grid resolution does not reduce the error. In contrast, for linear interpolation (
\alpha=1
), the approximation error decays at the rate
n_{a}^{-1}
, allowing this error to be controlled by using a finer grid.

5.6
Implementation in 2D and 3D

In this section we will discuss some specific details about implementing steerable convolutions in two and three dimensions and contrast them to prior works.

2D steerable convolutions:

Implementing steerable convolutions in two dimensions is relatively straightforward because all spherical harmonics are one dimensional and take the form
\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}(\theta)=e^{\iota k\theta}
, where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. These functions also serve as the irreps
\rho_{k}(\theta)
of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
. In this setting, the precomputed filter for the first layer becomes

M_{r}^{(k)}=\frac{r}{n_{r}^{2}n_{a}}\sum_{a=1}^{n_{a}}\mathcal{I}\left(\frac{rh}{n_{r}}s_{a},\textbf{y}\right)e^{\iota k\frac{2\pi a}{n_{a}}},\quad s_{a}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\frac{2\pi a}{n_{a}}\\
\sin\frac{2\pi a}{n_{a}}\end{bmatrix},

(41)

where
0\leq k\leq n_{a}-1
. Because the product of two irreps
\rho_{k_{1}}
and
\rho_{k_{2}}
results in
\rho_{k_{1}+k_{2}}
, the CG–matrices are given by
C^{(k,k_{1},k_{2})}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{k=k_{1}+k_{2}\}}
. As a result, the steerable filters in higher layers simplify to

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{2}}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}w_{r}^{(k,k_{1},k_{2})}\tilde{M}_{r}^{(k,k_{1},k_{2})}(\textbf{y})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(2)}|}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}\sum_{k_{2}}w_{r}^{(k,k_{1},k_{2})}\mathbbm{1}_{\{k=k_{1}+k_{2}\}}M_{r}^{(k_{2})}(\textbf{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{SO}(2)}|}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{r}}w_{r}^{(k,k_{1})}M_{r}^{([k-k_{1}]_{n_{a}})}(\textbf{y}),

(42)

where
w_{r}^{(k,k_{1})}:=w_{r}^{(k,k_{1},k-k_{1})}
, and
[\cdot]_{n_{a}}
denotes modulo
n_{a}
operation. Since each pair of irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
yields exactly one irrep, this structure naturally leads to a reduction in the number of learnable parameters, as seen in (
42
). Moreover, if the number of angular discretization points
n_{a}
matches the highest Fourier frequency cutoff, the symmetry group can effectively be treated as the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{n_{a}}
. This setting corresponds to the
\mathbb{Z}_{n_{a}}
steerable filters discussed by
Weiler & Cesa 2019
.

3D steerable convolutions:

In three dimensions, the implementation becomes more intricate because the spherical harmonics, apart from the constant representation, are no longer one dimensional. Instead, the spherical harmonic take the form
\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2\ell+1}
, where
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Consequently, the precomputed filter for the first layer in three dimensions is given by

M_{r}^{(\ell)}(\textbf{y})=\frac{r^{2}}{n_{r}^{3}n_{a}^{2}}\sum_{a_{1},a_{2}=1}^{n_{a}}\mathcal{I}\left(\frac{rh}{n_{r}}s_{a_{1},a_{2}},\textbf{y}\right)\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}\left(s_{a_{1},a_{2}}\right)\sin\frac{\pi(a_{1}+0.5)}{n_{a}},\,\,s_{a_{1},a_{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin\frac{\pi(a_{1}+0.5)}{n_{a}}\cos\frac{2\pi{a_{2}}}{n_{a}}\\
\sin\frac{\pi(a_{1}+0.5)}{n_{a}}\sin\frac{2\pi a_{2}}{n_{a}}\\
\cos\frac{\pi(a_{1}+0.5)}{n_{a}}\end{bmatrix}.

(43)

Using the quadrature from
Driscoll & Healy 1994
amounts to replacing the sine with
\omega_{\text{DH}}
, defined in (
34
). Unlike the 2D case, the tensor product of two irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
can decompose into multiple irreps, and as a consequence, we no longer have parameter reduction seen in two dimensions. Therefore, the filters in the higher layers of 3D steerable convolutions retain the general structure described in (
38
).

Remark 12
.

The implementation of steerable convolutions across various works in the literature primarily differs in the definition of
M_{r}^{(\rho)}
. For instance, in the 2D case,
Weiler et al. 2018b
employed the filter

M_{r}^{(k)}(\textbf{y})=e^{-\frac{(||\textbf{y}||-r)^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\textbf{y}\neq 0\}}\,e^{\iota k\angle\textbf{y}},

while in the 3D case,
Weiler et al. 2018a
used

M_{r}^{(\ell)}(\textbf{y})=e^{-\frac{(||\textbf{y}||-r)^{2}}{2\tau^{2}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\textbf{y}\neq 0\}}\,\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}\left(\frac{\textbf{y}}{||\textbf{y}||}\right).

Here,
\angle\textbf{y}
represents the angle that
y
makes with the
x
axis, and
\tau
is a hyperparameter. Since these filter are directly defined using the Cartesian coordinate system, we will refer to these as Cartesian filters, to distinguish from the interpolation based filters.

6
Experiments

We investigated the performance difference between Cartesian filters (Remark
12
) and interpolation based filters in steerable convolutions by benchmarking their performance across four widely used datasets. For each dataset, we systematically varied the Fourier cutoff values to study their effect on model accuracy. Training was carried out both with and without rotation augmentation, and models were subsequently evaluated on rotated test samples to assess generalization to unseen orientations. Each experimental setup was repeated across five independent runs to ensure reliability. To further probe model robustness, we performed a sensitivity analysis by introducing Gaussian noise at multiple intensity levels into the test data and measuring how performance degraded under these perturbations. For each noise level and each trained model, evaluations were repeated over five runs. The following sections describe the datasets, training procedures, and detailed analyses of the experimental findings.

6.3

To examine equivariance error, we conducted a controlled simulation experiment. We used a minimal network comprising two convolutional layers separated by a normalization layer (Appendix

to

. For the 3D case, rotations were applied separately about the
y
and

compares the errors for networks using Cartesian versus interpolation based filters. Cartesian filters yielded substantially larger errors than interpolation based filters suggesting that the latter is better suited for generalizing to unseen rotations.

Across all four datasets and a range of Fourier cutoffs, interpolation based filters achieved comparable or superior performance. The performance gap was especially pronounced when models were trained without rotation augmentation, reflecting the higher equivariance error of Cartesian filters and their reduced ability to generalize to unseen orientations. Furthermore, as shown in Figure

Rotation augmentation enables the model to compensate for these inconsistencies, thereby improving performance (see Section

). The effect is more pronounced in 3D datasets, where accuracy declines more sharply.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel framework for deriving the equations of steerable convolutional networks, adopting a geometric perspective rather than an algebraic one. We established a general formulation of equivariant linear maps in a broad setting, encompassing existing results in the literature as special cases, and provided a new proof of this characterization. Building on this foundation, we systematically derived the equations of steerable convolutions using Fourier space arguments. This approach offers an intuitive methodology that not only facilitates efficient implementation but also enables the exploration of new architectural variations. The derivations further shed light on the natural emergence of spherical harmonics and the Clebsch–Gordan decomposition in the context of steerable convolutions. Furthermore, it motivated a novel implementation of steerable convolutions using interpolation kernels, which our experiments showed to outperform existing methods and to exhibit greater robustness to Gaussian noise perturbations. Collectively, these contributions advance the theoretical understanding of steerable networks and provide practical guidance for developing more robust and efficient architectures.

Appendix A
Background

A.1
Groups

Definition:

A group
\mathcal{G}
is a non-empty set together with a binary operation (commonly denoted by “
\cdot
”, that combines any two elements

a
and
b
of
\mathcal{G}
to form an element of

\mathcal{G}
, denoted
a\cdot b
, such that the following three requirements, known as group axioms, are satisfied:

•

Associativity
: For all
a,b,c\in\mathcal{G}
, one has
(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)

•

Identity Element
: There exists as element
e\in\mathcal{G}
such that for every
a\in\mathcal{G}
, one has
e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a
. Such an
element is unique. It is called the
identity element
of the group.

•

Inverse
: For each
a\in\mathcal{G}
, there exists an element
b\in\mathcal{G}
such that
a\cdot b=b\cdot a=e
, where
e
is the identity element. For each
a
, the element
b
is unique and it is called the inverse of
a
and is commonly denoted by
a^{-1}
.

Examples include set of integers
\mathbb{Z}
with the addition operation and the set of non-zero reals
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
with the multiplication operation.
From here on we will drop “
\cdot
”, for simplicity. The group operation will be clear from the elements of the group concerned.

Group Homomorphism:

Given two groups
\mathcal{G}
and
H
, a function
\phi:\mathcal{G}\to H
is called a group homomorphism if
\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)
for any
a,b\in\mathcal{G}
. If the map
\phi
is a bijection, it is called an
isomorphism
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{G}=H
, then an isomorphism is called
automorphism
. The set of all automorphisms of a group
\mathcal{G}
with the operation of composition form a group in itself and is denoted by
\textrm{Aut}(\mathcal{G})
.

Compact Groups:

A topological group is a topological space that is also a group such that the group operation and the inverse map are continuous. A compact group is a topological group whose topology realizes it as a compact topological space (see
Munkres 1974
for definition of compact topological spaces).
Some classic examples of compact groups are the groups
\mathrm{SO}(d)
(the group of all real orthogonal matrices in
d
dimensions with determinant 1),
\textrm{U}(d)
(the group of all complex unitary matrices in
d
dimensions) and
\textrm{SU}(d)
(the group of all complex unitary matrices in
d
dimensions with determinant 1).

Special Orthonormal Group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
:
This group comprises all real orthogonal matrices in
d
dimensions with a determinant of
1
. These groups are associated with rotations in
d
dimensions. In two dimensions, the group
\mathrm{SO}(2)
can be parametrized by a single angle
\theta
corresponding to the rotation matrix

R(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
R(\theta)
signifies anti-clockwise rotation in the
x
-
y
plane by an angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
radian. In three dimensions, the group
\mathrm{SO}(3)
can be parameterized using the so called
Euler angles
. Consider the rotation matrices
R_{z}(\alpha)
and
R_{y}(\beta)
, defined by

R_{z}(\alpha)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\alpha)&-\sin(\alpha)&0\\
\sin(\alpha)&\cos(\alpha)&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad R_{y}(\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\beta)&0&\sin(\beta)\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin(\beta)&0&\cos(\beta)\end{bmatrix},

which represent anti-clockwise rotations by angles
\alpha
and
\beta
about the
z
-axis and
y
-axis, respectively. Using the
z
-
y
-
z
convention, any rotation matrix
R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
can be expressed as
R=R_{z}(\alpha)R_{y}(\beta)R_{z}(\gamma)
, where
\alpha,\gamma\in[0,2\pi)
and
\beta\in[0,\pi)
are the Euler angles.

Semi-direct Product Groups:

Given two groups
N
and
H
and a group homomorphism
\phi:H\to\text{Aut}(N)
, we can construct a new group
N\rtimes_{\phi}H
defined as follows:

•

The underlying set is the Cartesian product
N\times H
.

•

The group operation is given by
(n_{1},h_{1})(n_{2},h_{2})=(n_{1}\phi_{h_{1}}(n_{2}),h_{1}h_{2})
.

Special Euclidean Group
{\text{SE}(d)}
:
The Special Euclidean group
\text{SE}(d)
consists of all combinations of rotations and translations in
d
dimensions. Translations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
form a group isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
itself, while rotations are represented by the group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
. We define a group homomorphism
\phi:\mathrm{SO}(d)\to\text{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by setting
\phi(R)=(\mathbf{t}\mapsto R\mathbf{t})
, which describes how a rotation acts on a translation vector.
Using this, the group
\text{SE}(d)
can be constructed as the semidirect product
\mathbb{R}^{d}\rtimes_{\phi}\mathrm{SO}(d)
. The group operation for two elements
(\mathbf{t}_{1},R_{1})
and
(\mathbf{t}_{2},R_{2})
in
\mathrm{SE}(d)
is given by

(\mathbf{t}_{1},R_{1})(\mathbf{t}_{2},R_{2})=(\mathbf{t}_{1}+R_{1}\mathbf{t}_{2},\,R_{1}R_{2}).

The identity element of
\text{SE}(d)
is
(\mathbf{0},I)
, and the inverse of an element
(\mathbf{t},R)
is given by
(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}=(-R^{-1}\mathbf{t},R^{-1})
.

A.2
Group Actions

Definition:

If
\mathcal{G}
is a group with identity element
e
, and
X
is a set, then a (left) group action of
\mathcal{G}
on
X
is a function

\alpha:\mathcal{G}\times X\to X
, that satisfies the following two axioms for all
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
and
x\in X
:

•

Identity
:
\alpha(e,x)=x
,

•

Compatibility
:
\alpha(g,\alpha(h,x))=\alpha(gh,x)
.

The group action is said to be continuous if
\alpha
is a continuous map. Often
\alpha(g,x)
is shortened to
g\cdot x
. Any group
\mathcal{G}
acts on itself by the group operation. If
\mathcal{G}
acts on
X
, then it also naturally acts on any function
f
defined on
X
, as
(g\cdot f)(x)=f(g^{-1}\cdot x)
.

Action of
\mathrm{SE}(d)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:
The special Euclidean group acts on a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by first applying the rotation component followed by translation. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(\mathbf{t},R)\in\mathrm{SE}(d)
,

(\mathbf{t},R)\cdot\mathbf{x}=R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t}

gives us the action of
\mathrm{SE}(d)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

A.3
Group Representations

Definition:

A representation of a group
\mathcal{G}
is a group homomorphism from
\mathcal{G}
to
\text{GL}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
(group of invertible linear maps on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
).
Here
n
is called the dimension of the representation, which can possibly be infinite. A representation is
unitary
if
\rho
maps to unitary linear transformation of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Irreducible Representations:

If we have two representations,
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
of dimensions
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
respectively, then the two can be combined by a direct sum to give another representation of dimension
n_{1}+n_{2}
,

\rho_{1}(g)\oplus\rho_{2}(g)=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1}(g)&0\\
0&\rho_{2}(g)\end{bmatrix}.

A representation is said to be
completely reducible
if it can be expressed as a direct sum of other representations after maybe a change of basis, i.e,

U\rho(g)U^{-1}=\bigoplus_{i}\rho_{i}(g)

where
U
is a unitary change of basis matrix and the direct sum extends over some number of representations.
However, for
every
group there are a some representations which cannot be broken further into a direct sum of other representations. These are called the
irreducible representations
or
irreps
of the group. These irreps are the building blocks of the all other representations of the group, in the sense that any representation can be written as a direct sum of the irreps,

\rho(g)=U\left[\bigoplus_{i}\rho^{(i)}(g)\right]U^{-1}

where again
U
is a change of basis matrix and
\rho^{(i)}
are the irreps. The Peter–Weyl Theorem (Theorem
F2
) by
Peter & Weyl 1927
tells us that for a compact group
\mathcal{G}
, any unitary representation
\rho
is completely reducible and splits into direct sum of irreducible
finite dimensional unitary
representations of
\mathcal{G}
.

Irreducible Representations of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
and
\mathrm{SO}(3)
:

\mathrm{SO}(d)
being a compact group, all its irreps are finite dimensional unitary representations. In the case of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
, every irrep is one-dimensional and indexed by an integer. The group
\mathrm{SO}(2)
can be parameterized by an angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, corresponding to an anti-clockwise rotation in the
x
-
y
plane by
\theta
radians. Under this parameterization, the irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(2)
take the form

\rho^{(k)}(\theta)=e^{ik\theta},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

The irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(3)
are indexed by positive integers
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where the
\ell
’th representation is of dimension
2\ell+1
and are given by the so called Wigner D-matrices
(
Wigner 1932
)
:

\rho^{(\ell)}(R)=D^{(\ell)}(R),\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

A.4
Fourier Transform

Haar Measure:

Theorem
F1
guarantees the existence of a unique canonical measure on locally compact groups, known as the (left) Haar measure. One key property of the Haar measure is that
\mu(U)>0
for every non-empty open subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{G}
. In the special case where
\mathcal{G}
is compact, the total measure
\mu(\mathcal{G})
is finite and strictly positive. This allows us to uniquely determine a Haar measure by imposing the normalization condition
\mu(\mathcal{G})=1
, which is especially useful in probability theory and harmonic analysis, where one often interprets the Haar measure as a uniform distribution over the group. Additionally, for compact groups such as
\mathrm{SO}(d)
, the Haar measure is unimodular, meaning it is invariant under both left and right translations, i.e.,
\mu(gA)=\mu(Ag)=\mu(A)
for all measurable subsets
A\subseteq\mathcal{G}
and all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. This symmetry simplifies many computations and ensures that convolution and integration operations behave consistently with the group structure.

Fourier Transform on Compact groups:

A notable and useful property of compact groups is that the set of (isomorphism classes of) their irreps is countable
(
Robert 1983
)
. This fact underpins a powerful generalization of classical Fourier analysis to the setting of compact groups, enabling square-integrable functions to be decomposed into frequency components indexed by group representations. Let
f\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
, where
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
denotes the space of complex-valued, square-integrable functions on
\mathcal{G}
with respect to the (normalized) Haar measure. For each irrep
\rho
, the Fourier transform of
f
at
\rho
is defined as

\widehat{f}(\rho)=\int_{\mathcal{G}}f(g)\,\rho(g)\,d\mu(g),

where
\widehat{f}(\rho)
is a complex matrix of size
d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho}
. The inverse Fourier transform allows one to reconstruct the original function
f
from its Fourier coefficients via the formula

f(g)=\sum_{\rho}d_{\rho}\,\mathrm{Tr}\left(\widehat{f}(\rho)\,\rho(g)^{\dagger}\right),

where the convergence is in the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
-sense. This formula is justified by the Peter–Weyl theorem (Theorem
F2
), which states that the matrix coefficients of all irreps form a complete orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
. Consequently, any square integrable function on
\mathcal{G}
can be expressed as a (generalized) Fourier series in terms of these basis functions.

Appendix B
Spherical Harmonics

Spherical harmonics arise as a natural generalization of trigonometric functions and classical Fourier series to higher dimensional spheres. Just as the functions
\sin
and
\cos
provide a basis for square integrable functions on the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, spherical harmonics furnish an orthogonal basis for functions defined on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. They play a central role in mathematical physics, harmonic analysis, and approximation theory, serving as the fundamental building blocks for problems with rotational symmetry. Applications range from quantum mechanics and geophysics to computer vision and machine learning, where spherical harmonics allow functions on spheres to be analyzed, expanded, and approximated in terms of simple, structured components.

From a mathematical perspective, spherical harmonics in
d
dimensions emerge from the study of harmonic polynomials. These are polynomials that satisfy the Laplace equation and thus capture a notion of “balanced” or “smooth” behavior in all directions. By restricting homogeneous harmonic polynomials to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, one obtains the spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
. In this way, spherical harmonics can be viewed both as natural eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on the sphere and as the higher dimensional analogue of Fourier modes on the circle.

Formally, a polynomial
P(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d})
is called
homogeneous
of degree
\ell
if each of its monomials has total degree
\ell
, that is,

P(\lambda\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\lambda\mathbf{x}_{d})=\lambda^{\ell}P(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d}),\quad\text{for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

For example,
P(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})=\mathbf{x}_{1}^{2}-\mathbf{x}_{2}^{2}
is homogeneous of degree
2
. A homogeneous polynomial is called
harmonic
if it satisfies the Laplace equation,

\Delta P(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d})=0,\quad\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell)}
denote the space of harmonic homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
in
d
variables. The corresponding spherical harmonics are obtained by restricting the elements of
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell)}
to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. An orthonormal basis of this space will be denoted by
\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}
. The dimension of the space of spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
is given by

\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell)}=\frac{(2\ell+d-2)(\ell+d-3)!}{\ell!(d-2)!}.

To obtain explicit formulas for spherical harmonics in
d
dimensions, it is convenient to use the standard angular parameterization of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. For
\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}:=[0,\pi]^{d-2}\times[0,2\pi)
, define
s(\theta)\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
componentwise by

s_{i}(\theta):=\begin{cases}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-2}\sin\theta_{j}\right)\cos\theta_{i}&1\leq i\leq d-1,\\
\,\,\,\,\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\sin\theta_{j}&i=d.\end{cases}

In these coordinates, one explicit choice of spherical harmonics
Y_{d}^{(\ell)}
is given by

\left[\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(s(\theta))\right]_{\mathbf{m}}=\left[\mathcal{K}^{(\ell)}_{d}\right]_{\mathbf{m}}\frac{e^{\iota\mathbf{m}_{d-1}\theta_{d-1}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}(\sin\theta_{j})^{\mathbf{m}_{j+1}}C_{\mathbf{m}_{j}-\mathbf{m}_{j+1}}^{\mathbf{m}_{j+1}+(d-j-1)/2}(\cos\theta_{j})

(44)

where the index
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{m}_{d-1})
satisfies the condition
|\mathbf{m}_{d-1}|\leq\mathbf{m}_{d-2}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{m}_{1}=\ell
, and the normalization constants
\mathcal{K}^{(\ell)}_{d}
are given by

\left[\mathcal{K}^{(\ell)}_{d}\right]_{\mathbf{m}}=\prod_{j=1}^{d-2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mathbf{m}_{j+1}+\frac{d-j+1}{2}\right)}{2\mathbf{m}_{j+1}+d-j-1}\sqrt{\frac{2^{2\mathbf{m}_{j+1}+d-j-1}(2\mathbf{m}_{j}+d-j-1)(\mathbf{m}_{j}-\mathbf{m}_{j+1})!}{\pi(\mathbf{m}_{j}-\mathbf{m}_{j+1}+d-j-2)!}}.

Here,
C_{n}^{(\alpha)}
denotes the Gegenbauer polynomial,

C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}{\frac{\Gamma(n-k+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)k!(n-2k)!}}(2z)^{n-2k},

where
\alpha>0,\,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function
(
Cohl et al. 2023
)
.

The constants
\mathcal{K}^{(\ell)}_{d}
are chosen so that each basis coordinate function is normalized in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
with respect to the surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
:

\int_{\Theta^{d-1}}\left|\left[\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(s(\theta))\right]_{\mathbf{m}}\right|^{2}\omega(\theta)\,d\theta=1,\quad\omega(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{d-2}(\sin\theta_{i})^{d-1-i}.

Additionally, with this normalization, the squared Euclidean norm of the vector
Y_{d}^{(\ell)}(s)
is constant across the sphere. Specifically, for all
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\|\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(s)\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell)}}{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})},

where
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}):=\int_{\Theta^{d-1}}\omega(\theta)\,d\theta
denotes the surface area of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(see Lemma
7
for proof).

In the special cases of two and three dimensions, the general expression (
44
) reduces to the familiar formulas. For
d=2
, corresponding to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the spherical harmonics coincide with the Fourier modes:

\mathbf{Y}^{(k)}_{2}(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{\iota k\theta},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

For
d=3
, corresponding to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and using spherical coordinates
(\theta,\phi)
, the spherical harmonics take the classical form

\big[\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{3}\big]_{m}(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\,\frac{(\ell+m)!}{(\ell-m)!}}\;e^{\iota m\phi}\,P_{\ell}^{-m}(\cos\theta),\quad|m|\leq\ell,\,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

where
P_{\ell}^{m}
denotes the associated Legendre functions
(
Olver 2010
)
.

B.1
Spherical Harmonic Transform

Every square integrable function
f\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
admits a spherical harmonic expansion that converges in the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
sense:

f(s)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(s)^{\dagger}\widehat{f}_{\ell},\quad s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

Here the expansion coefficients
\widehat{f}_{\ell}
are obtained as the inner products of
f
with the basis functions, namely

\widehat{f}_{\ell}=\int_{\Theta^{d-1}}f(s(\theta)){\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(s(\theta))}\omega(\theta)\,d\theta.

B.2
Connection to irreps of SO(d)

A distinguished subclass of representations of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
is realized on the space of symmetric traceless tensors.
For each integer
\ell\geq 0
, these spaces are defined as

V^{(\ell)}:=\left\{T_{i_{1}\dots i_{\ell}}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes\ell}\,\bigg|\,T_{i_{\sigma(1)}\dots i_{\sigma(\ell)}}=T_{i_{1}\dots i_{\ell}}\ \forall\sigma\in S_{\ell};\,\delta_{i_{1}i_{2}}T_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}\dots i_{\ell}}=0\right\}

where
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta. Elements of
V^{(\ell)}
are completely symmetric under permutation of indices and traceless under contraction with the Euclidean metric. The group
\mathrm{SO}(d)
acts naturally on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes\ell}
by rotation of tensor indices, and this action preserves both symmetry and tracelessness.
Explicitly, the representation
\rho^{(\ell)}:\mathrm{SO}(d)\to\mathrm{GL}(V^{(\ell)})
is given by

\left[\rho^{(\ell)}[R](T)\right]_{i_{1}\dots i_{\ell}}=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{d}\cdots\sum_{j_{\ell}=1}^{d}R_{i_{1}j_{1}}R_{i_{2}j_{2}}\cdots R_{i_{\ell}j_{\ell}}T_{j_{1}\dots j_{\ell}},\quad R\in\mathrm{SO}(d).

Consequently,
V^{(\ell)}
is invariant under this action and carries an irrep of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Since its carrier space consists of symmetric traceless tensors, the resulting irrep
\rho^{(\ell)}
are referred to as the
symmetric traceless irrep
of degree
\ell
.

The spaces of spherical harmonics
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell)}
furnish a functional realization of these representations. Under the natural action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
on functions defined on the sphere,

[R\cdot f](s)=f(R^{-1}s),

the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell)}
remains invariant, and its basis functions
Y_{d}^{(\ell)}(s)
transform as

\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(R^{-1}s)=\rho^{(\ell)}(R)^{\dagger}\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)}_{d}(s),

Hence, spherical harmonics provide an explicit realization of the symmetric traceless irreps of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
as functions on the sphere. Moreover, the tensor product of two such symmetric traceless representations decomposes as a direct sum of symmetric and non-symmetric irreps
(
Georgi 2000
)
:

\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell_{1})}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell_{2})}\cong\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\min(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})}\mathcal{H}_{d}^{(\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}-2k)}\bigoplus\text{ non-symmetric irreps}.

Each symmetric traceless irrep appearing in this decomposition occurs with multiplicity exactly one.

Appendix C
Other Steerable Layers

Here we provide the details of other equivariant layers that we used in designing steerable neural networks.

C.1
Non-linearity

All the operations we’ve discussed so far are linear in
f^{\textrm{in}}
, but for a neural network layer, introducing nonlinearity is crucial. A common strategy in Fourier space networks is to first apply an inverse Fourier transform to bring
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}
back to the time domain. Here, a standard nonlinear activation function, such as ReLU, can be applied pointwise before transforming it back to Fourier space. This method allows flexibility in choosing any activation function. However, even with the efficiency of FFTs, the repeated forward and backward transforms can be computationally demanding, potentially becoming a bottleneck for the network. Furthermore, for groups such as
\mathrm{SO}(3)
, discretization on a uniform grid can introduce singularities, making FFT-based computations prone to numerical instability and errors. One strategy proposed by
Worrall et al. 2017
involves applying standard non-linearities, such as ReLU, to the norm of equivariant feature vectors at each spatial location. Another approach, used by
Weiler et al. 2018a
;
Weiler et al. 2018b
, first convolving the input with another steerable filter, then extracting the component corresponding to the constant representation, and finally multiplying it pointwise with the input.

In our experiments, we adopt the Clebsch–Gordan non-linearity used by
Kondor et al. 2018
;
Anderson et al. 2019
. This involves computing the tensor product of the equivariant vector with itself at each location, followed by a CG–decomposition. In the 2D case, the output
\widehat{f^{\textrm{out}}}
at a given location
\mathbf{x}
is given by

\widehat{f^{\textrm{out}}}(\mathbf{x},k)=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k_{\max}}\eta_{k,k^{\prime}}\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},k^{\prime})\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},[k-k^{\prime}]_{n_{a}}),\quad 0\leq k\leq k_{\max},

and in the 3D case, it is given by

\widehat{f^{\textrm{out}}}(\mathbf{x},\ell)=\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=0}^{\ell_{\max}}\eta_{\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}C_{\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}^{\dagger}\left[\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\ell_{1})\otimes\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\ell_{2})\right]C_{\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}},\quad 0\leq\ell\leq\ell_{\max}.

Here,
k_{\max}
,
\ell_{\max}
are the respective Fourier frequency cutoffs, and
\eta_{k,k^{\prime}}
,
\eta_{\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
are learnable weights. The tensor product operation on large tensors is the most computationally demanding step in the network, especially for the 3D case. To optimize this, we utilize the
GElib
library
(
Kondor 2023
)
, which efficiently implements this operation for the 3D case.

C.2
Normalization

Under the action of any
(\mathbf{t},R)\in\mathrm{SE}(d)
, the features extracted from steerable convolutions transform as

\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)\mapsto[(\mathbf{t},R)^{-1}\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}](\mathbf{x},\rho)=\rho(R)^{\dagger}\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(R\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{t},\rho).

Since the matrices
\rho(R)
are unitary, the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
norm of each Fourier component remains invariant under this transformation.
Motivated by this property, we normalize each Fourier vector by its norm to achieve an equivariant form of normalization,

\widehat{f^{\textrm{out}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\frac{\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)}{\sqrt{\sum_{\rho}||\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)||_{2}^{2}}}.

This normalization not only preserves equivariance but also enhances stability within the network by preventing numerical overflow and facilitating faster convergence during training.

C.3
Pooling

Feature maps produced by steerable convolutions store, at each location
\mathbf{x}
, an equivariant vector
\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)
corresponding to each irreducible representation
\rho
. Since the group action is inherently a linear operation, multiple such vectors at different locations can be averaged, and the resulting vector will still remain equivariant. Exploiting this property, we use average pooling (instead of the commonly used max pooling) to ensure that equivariance is preserved.
The operation of average pooling is expressed as

\widehat{f^{\textrm{out}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})}\widehat{f^{\textrm{pre}}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\rho).

Here,
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x})
represents the neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}
. This operation aggregates the angular information from neighboring locations while preserving the equivariant properties of the input field.

C.4
Flattening layer

For the flattening layer, we apply a similar approach to that used for normalization, leveraging the property that the norm of equivariant vectors remains invariant under group actions. Specifically, we compute the mean of the sum of the equivariant vectors across all spatial locations and then calculate its norm

\sqrt{\sum_{\rho}\left\|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{f^{\textrm{out}}}(\mathbf{x},\rho)\right\|_{2}^{2}},

where
\mathcal{F}
represents the set of all spatial locations. This operation makes the network
invariant
to rotations and translations of the input by aggregating equivariant features into a single scalar value. The resulting output can then be passed on to subsequent linear layers for further processing.

E.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Let
e
denote the identity element of
\mathcal{G}
.
Because
\phi
is an equivariant, for every
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we have

\phi[f](g)=\phi[f](g\!\cdot\!e)=g^{-1}\!\cdot\!\phi[f](e)=\phi[g^{-1}\!\cdot\!f](e).

Define
\Lambda:C_{c}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\Lambda(f):=\phi[f](e)
. Since the operator
\phi
is bounded, there is a constant
C>0
such that for all
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})

|\Lambda(f)|\;=\;|\phi[f](e)|\;\leq\;C\|f\|_{\infty}

Thus,
\Lambda
is a bounded linear functional on
C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
. Because
\mathcal{X}
is a locally compact Hausdorff space,
C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
is sup
-
norm dense in

C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, the space of continuous functions in
\mathcal{X}
vanishing at infinity. Boundedness therefore extends
\Lambda
uniquely to
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
.
By the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani theorem (Theorem
F3
), there exists a
unique
complex Radon measure
\lambda
on
\mathcal{X}
such that for all
f\in C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, we have

\Lambda(f)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,d\lambda.

Now suppose
\mathcal{X}
is
\sigma
-compact and equipped with a
\sigma
-finite measure
\sigma
.
By the Hahn–Jordan decomposition theorem (Theorem
F4
) there exists four unique non
-
negative Radon measures
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\lambda_{4}
such that

\lambda\;=\;(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\;+\;i\,(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}).

Since
\mathcal{X}
is
\sigma
-compact, and
\lambda_{j}
is finite on compact sets (by definition of Radon measure), each
\lambda_{j}
is
\sigma
-finite. By Lebesgue decomposition theorem (Theorem
F5
), for each
1\leq j\leq 4
, there exists a unique real valued functions
w_{j}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(\mathcal{X})
and a unique measure
\nu_{j}
, which are singular with respect to
\sigma
, such that
d\lambda_{i}=w_{i}d\sigma+d\nu_{i}
. Set

\displaystyle w:=(w_{1}-w_{2})+i(w_{3}-w_{4}),

\displaystyle\nu:=(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})+i(\nu_{3}-\nu_{4})

so that
d\lambda=w\,d\sigma+d\nu
. Now, for any
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
, let
\Omega(f)\subseteq\mathcal{X}
denote the compact support of
f
. For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the support for the translate
g^{-1}\cdot f
is given by
g^{-1}\cdot\Omega(f)
. Since the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{X}
is continuous,
g^{-1}\cdot\Omega(f)
is also compact, as it is the image of a compact set under a continuous map. Hence, for any
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, we have
g^{-1}\cdot f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{X})
. Therefore,

\phi[f](g)\;=\;\phi[g^{-1}\!\cdot\!f](e)\;=\;\Lambda(g^{-1}\!\cdot\!f)\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{X}}g^{-1}\!\cdot\!f\,d\lambda\;=\;\bigl\langle g^{-1}\!\cdot\!f,\;w\bigr\rangle\;+\;\int_{\mathcal{X}}g^{-1}\!\cdot\!f\,d\nu.

E.2
Proof of Proposition
2

Proof.

Using the inverse Fourier transform on
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
,

f(g,g)\hskip-1.00006pt=\hskip-1.00006pt\sum_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\frac{d_{\rho_{1}}d_{\rho_{2}}}{\mu(\mathcal{G})^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl[\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\,\rho_{1}(g^{-1})\otimes\rho_{2}(g^{-1})\bigr].

Since the trace of a matrix is invariant to similarity transformations, we can rewrite the trace term as

\mathrm{tr}\Bigl[C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\dagger}\,\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\,C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\cdot C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\dagger}\rho_{1}(g^{-1})\otimes\rho_{2}(g^{-1})C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\Bigr]=\\
\mathrm{tr}\biggl[\biggl(C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\dagger}\,\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\,C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\biggr)\biggl(\bigoplus_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}\rho(g^{-1})\biggr)\biggr].

Now, since the second factor under the trace is block diagonal, the first one can be brought to similar form, giving

\mathrm{tr}\biggl[\biggl(\bigoplus_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}F_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\rho,i}\biggr)\biggl(\bigoplus_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}\rho(g^{-1})\biggr)\biggr]=\sum_{\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}\mathrm{tr}\bigl[F_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\rho,i}\,\rho(g^{-1})\bigr],

where

F_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\rho,i}=\Bigl[C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\dagger}\,\widehat{f}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\,C_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\Bigr]_{\rho,i}.

In summary, collecting terms indexed by the same
\rho

f^{\textrm{diag}}(g)=f(g,g)=\sum_{\rho}\sum_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\kappa(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho)}\frac{d_{\rho_{1}}d_{\rho_{2}}}{\mu(\mathcal{G})^{2}}\>\mathrm{tr}\bigl[F_{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}}^{\rho,i}\,\rho(g^{-1})\bigr].

Comparing this to the form of the inverse Fourier transform on the diagonal of
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}
,

f^{\textrm{diag}}(g)=\sum_{\rho}\frac{d_{\rho}}{\mu(\mathcal{G})}\>\mathrm{tr}\bigl[\widehat{f}(\rho)\,\rho(g^{-1})\bigr]

proves our proposition.
∎

Appendix F
Technical Lemmas

Lemma 1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{C}
be an integrable function with respect to the surface measure
\sigma
. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s)\,d\sigma(s)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}f(R\mathbf{e})d\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}(R)

where
\mathbf{e}
is a fixed vector and
\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
is the Haar measure on
\mathrm{SO}(d)
.

Proof.

Define the map
\phi:\mathrm{SO}(d)\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, by
\phi(R)=R\mathbf{e}
. Note that
\phi
is a continuous function, hence measurable. Define a measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that for any measurable set
E\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\nu(E)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\chi_{E}(\phi(R))\,d\mu(R).

Note that
\nu
is a valid measure (the pull back measure) on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. For any
R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
,

\displaystyle\chi_{{R^{\prime}}^{-1}\cdot E}(\phi(R))=1

\displaystyle\iff\phi(R)\in{R^{\prime}}^{-1}\cdot E\iff R\mathbf{e}\in{R^{\prime}}^{-1}\cdot E\iff R^{\prime}R\mathbf{e}\in E

\displaystyle\iff\phi(R^{\prime}R)\in E\iff\chi_{E}(\phi(R^{\prime}R))=1

Due to left invariance of
\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
,
\nu({R^{\prime}}^{-1}\cdot E)=\nu(E)
for any measurable
E
and
R^{\prime}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Hence,
\nu
is a measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
invariant to the left action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
.

Pick a measurable open
E\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that,
\sigma(E)>0
. If
\nu(E)=0
, then by invariance of the measure under rotation,
\nu(R\cdot E)=0
for all
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Since the action of
\mathrm{SO}(d)
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is transitive and

has positive measure under
\sigma
, any point in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
can be covered by an open set of the form

for some
R\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Using compactness, we only need finitely many on these open sets to cover
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and all of them have measure
0
under
\nu
. This implies
\nu\equiv 0
. But, by construction,
\nu(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}(\mathrm{SO}(d))>0
. This is a contradiction. Hence,
\sigma(E)>0
implies
\nu(E)>0
.

The above discussion shows that,
\nu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\sigma
, and hence the Radon-Nikodym derivative exists and using the invariance property of both the measures, we have

\frac{d\nu}{d\sigma}(R\mathbf{e})=\frac{d\nu}{d\sigma}(\mathbf{e})\quad\quad\forall\,R\in\mathrm{SO}(d).

A measurable function on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, which is invariant to all rotations, can only be the constant function. Hence
\exists

c>0
such that,

. Furthermore, if
\sigma
and
\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}
are normalized to integrate to
1
, we have
c=1
. Therefore, for any measurable set
E
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\chi_{E}(s)\,d\sigma(s)=\sigma(E)=\nu(E)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\chi_{E}(\phi(R))\,d\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}(R)=\int_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}\chi_{E}(R\mathbf{e})\,d\mu_{\mathrm{SO}(d)}(R).

Since it is true for indicator functions, it holds for all measurable functions. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Lemma 4

Lemma 9
.

f_{1},f_{2}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be Hölder continuous functions on a space
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with exponents
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]
, respectively. Then the pointwise product

is also Hölder continuous with exponent

.

Proof.

The functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are Hölder continuous means there exists constant

such that, for all
x,y\in X
,

\displaystyle|f_{i}(x)|\leq M_{i}

\displaystyle\|f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y)\|_{2}\leq C_{i}\|x-y\|_{2}^{\alpha_{i}}

. From the boundedness of

and
f_{2}

is also bounded by

. Note that, for any

, we have

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(x)f_{2}(x)-f_{1}(y)f_{2}(y)\|_{2}=

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(x)\big(f_{2}(x)-f_{2}(y)\big)+f_{2}(y)\big(f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(y)\big)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|f_{1}(x)\|\,\,\|f_{2}(x)-f_{2}(y)\|+\|f_{2}(y)\|\,\,\|f_{1}(x)-f_{1}(y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{1}C_{2}\|x-y\|_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}+M_{2}C_{1}\|x-y\|_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{1}C_{2}\|x-y\|_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}+M_{2}C_{1}\|x-y\|_{2}^{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left(M_{1}C_{2}+M_{2}C_{1},2M_{1}M_{2}\right)\|x-y\|_{2}^{\min(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\theta}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)-f(s(\theta^{\prime}))\omega(\theta^{\prime})\,d\theta^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\theta}}\left|\left|f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)-f(s(\theta^{\prime}))\omega(\theta^{\prime})\right|\right|_{2}\,d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\theta}}\frac{C}{2}\left|\left|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\right|\right|_{2}^{\alpha}\,d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\textit{for some constant $C>0$, since $f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)$ is $\alpha$-H\"{o}lder continuous in $\theta$})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{2}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{\theta}}\frac{\pi^{\alpha}d^{\alpha/2}}{n_{a}^{\alpha}}d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle(\textit{using definition of $\mathcal{V}_{\theta}$ in equation }\eqref{eq: V theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Cd^{\alpha/2}}{2n_{a}^{\alpha}}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}\frac{2\pi^{d-1}}{n_{a}^{d-1}}

\displaystyle(\textit{by equation }\eqref{eq: area element})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Cd^{\alpha/2}}{2n_{a}^{\alpha}}n_{a}^{d-1}\frac{2\pi^{d-1}}{n_{a}^{d-1}}

\displaystyle(\textit{since }|\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}|=n_{a}^{d-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{d-1}Cd^{\alpha/2}}{n_{a}^{\alpha}}

(71)

Since
f
is bounded,
[R^{-1}\cdot f]
is also bounded and hence integrable. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(Rs(\theta))\,\omega(\theta)\delta-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(Rs^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}[R^{-1}\cdot f](s(\theta))\,\omega(\theta)\delta-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}[R^{-1}\cdot f](s^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{d-1}Cd^{\alpha/2}}{n_{a}^{\alpha}}

(72)

Combining (
71
) and (
72
) we get

\displaystyle\left|\left|\frac{1}{n_{a}^{d-1}}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)-\frac{1}{n_{a}^{d-1}}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(Rs(\theta))\omega(\theta)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{d-1}}\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)\delta-\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(Rs(\theta))\omega(\theta)\delta\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{d-1}}\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)\delta-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s(\theta^{\prime}))\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})-\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(Rs(\theta))\omega(\theta)\delta\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{d-1}}\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi^{d-1}}\left|\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})-\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(Rs(\theta))\omega(\theta)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi^{d-1}}\left|\left|\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(s(\theta))\omega(\theta)-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(s^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})\right|\right|_{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi^{d-1}}\left|\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f(Rs^{\prime})\,d\sigma(s^{\prime})-\sum_{\theta\in\Theta^{d-1}_{n_{a}}}f(Rs(\theta))\omega(\theta)\right|\right|_{2}

\displaystyle(\textit{since the surface measure $\sigma$ is invariant to rotations})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cd^{\alpha/2}n_{a}^{-\alpha}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix G
Auxilliary

Theorem F1

(Haar Theorem)
.

There is, up to a positive multiplicative constant, a unique countably additive, nontrivial measure
{\displaystyle\mu}
on the Borel subsets of
{\displaystyle\mathcal{G}}
satisfying the following properties:

•

The measure
{\displaystyle\mu}
is left-translation-invariant:
{\displaystyle\mu(gS)=\mu(S)}
for every
{\displaystyle g\in\mathcal{G}}
and all Borel sets
{\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathcal{G}}
.

•

The measure
{\displaystyle\mu}
is finite on every compact set:
{\displaystyle\mu(K)<\infty}
for all compact
{\displaystyle K\subseteq\mathcal{G}}
.

•

The measure
{\displaystyle\mu}
is outer regular on Borel sets
{\displaystyle S\subseteq\mathcal{G}}
:
{\displaystyle\mu(S)=\inf\{\mu(U):S\subseteq U,U{\text{ open}}\}.}

•

The measure
{\displaystyle\mu}
is inner regular on open sets
{\displaystyle U\subseteq\mathcal{G}}
:
{\displaystyle\mu(U)=\sup\{\mu(K):K\subseteq U,K{\text{ compact}}\}}
.

Proof.

See §2.2 of
Folland 2016
.
∎

Theorem F2

(Peter–Weyl Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a compact group, and let
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
denote the space of square integrable complex valued functions on
\mathcal{G}
, with respect to the normalized Haar measure. Then, the matrix coefficients of the irreducible unitary representations of
\mathcal{G}
span a dense subspace of
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
. Moreover, they form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{G})
under the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
inner product.

Proof.

See §5.2 of
Folland 2016
.
∎

Theorem F3

(Riesz–Markov–Kakutani Representation Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space. For any bounded linear functional
\psi
on
C_{0}(X)
, there is a unique complex Radon measure
\mu
on X such that

{\displaystyle\psi(f)=\int_{X}f(x)\,d\mu(x),\quad\forall f\in C_{0}(X).}

Proof.

See Theorem 7.17 in Chapter 7 of
Folland 1999
.
∎

Theorem F4

(Hahn–Jordan Decomposition Theorem)
.

Let
\mu
be a finite complex measure on a measurable space. Then, there exist four mutually singular finite positive measures
\mu_{1},\;\mu_{2},\;\mu_{3},\;\mu_{4}
such that
\mu\;=\;(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\;+\;i\,(\mu_{3}-\mu_{4})
.

Proof.

See Theorem 4.1.5, Corollary 4.1.6 and equation (4) of Chapter 4.1 on page 118 of
Cohn 2013
.
∎

Theorem F5

(Lebesgue Decomposition Theorem)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be
\sigma
-finite measures on a measurable space. Then
\nu
can be uniquely decomposed into
\nu=\nu_{c}+\nu_{s}
where
\nu_{c}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
and
\nu_{s}
is singular with respect to
\mu
.

Proof.

See Theorem C in §32 of
Halmos 2013
.
∎
