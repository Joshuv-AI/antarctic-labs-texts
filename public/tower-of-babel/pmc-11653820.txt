Reducing dropout rates in cardiac rehabilitation among cardiac patients in a vulnerable situation: systematic development and feasibility testing of the Heart Priority Programme
Authors: Charlotte Ibsen, Kirstine Kold Katholm, Anja Jakobsen, Gitte Bastrup Eriksen, Lene Lysdal, Ulla Kirkegård Nielsen, Maiken Bay Ravn, Thomas Maribo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite advancements in reducing cardiovascular disease, it remains a major health concern. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) has a positive impact on morbidity, mortality, and functioning, but faces high dropout rates especially among vulnerable patients, due to social inequalities and insufficiently tailored interventions. To address this, we developed the Heart Priority Programme targeting and supporting cardiac patients at risk of dropout. This study outlines the development of the Heart Priority Programme and share findings from a feasibility test.MethodsAn iterative three-stage process involving cardiac patients and healthcare professionals from three community healthcare services, guided the development of the programme. Stage 1 included reviewing evidence, consulting stakeholders, and observing practice. In stage 2, a project-group of researchers and healthcare professionals was established to co-produce the programme. Stage 3 included field-testing and local adaptions. Subsequently, the programme was feasibility tested in a single-arm follow-up study across two community healthcare services. Over six months, process data on implementation, acceptability, and mechanism of impact were gathered.ResultsThe Heart Priority Programme was development from January 2023 to June 2023, comprising two main parts: 1) an evidence-based identification form with 13 risk factors to identify patients at risk of dropout (referred to as priority patients), and 2) an add-on intervention targeted these patients with three core elements—assigning a contact person, systematic communication, and follow-up. During a six-month feasibility phase, 260 patients were included. Of these, 91 (35%) were identified as priority patients. CR teams found that the programme was relevant, easy to integrate into daily workflow, and applicable in practice. Patient consultations yielded positive feedback on the three core elements of the add-on intervention.ConclusionsThis paper outlines the development and feasibility test of the Heart Priority Programme, co-produced to identify, and support priority patients. The programme, developed through a three-stage iterative co-production process, was found relevant and easy to implement in community healthcare services. CR teams valued its structured approach to supporting priority patients, and patients found it aligned with their needs. Although initial results are promising, further research is needed to evaluate the programme's effectiveness and suitability for widespread implementation.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06575764, registered retrospectively on 28 August 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-12073-x.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) continues to be a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, despite overall declines in the incidence and mortality rates of cardiovascular disease. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is strongly recommended for individuals after ACS (Class 1a recommendation) [1–3]. CR is a multi-component intervention, with a positive impact on recovery, morbidity, mortality, and functioning [1, 2, 4]. Exercise-based CR has demonstrated significant benefits for patients with ACS, including a 26% risk reduction in cardiovascular mortality and a 23% risk reduction in hospital readmissions [3]. These findings underscore the importance of CR in improving survival and reducing hospitalisations among ACS patients. Despite documented benefits of CR, dropout rates remain relatively high, ranging from 12 to 82% worldwide [4–7]. Therefore, it is crucial to address the key barriers to CR, and implement targeted interventions to reduce dropout rates, and optimise secondary prevention [8].

Individuals in a vulnerable situation have a higher risk of developing ACS, and their prognosis is poorer [9, 10]. The expression ‘individuals in a vulnerable situation’ refers to people who are at risk for disparate healthcare access and outcomes because of financial circumstances or social characteristics such as age, race, gender, ethnicity, sexual orientation, spirituality, disability, or socioeconomic or insurance status [11, 12]. Studies have shown that individuals in a vulnerable situation are at a higher risk of dropout from CR [6, 8, 13]. A study revealed a persistent and notable trend of underutilisation of CR among patients with low socioeconomic status highlighting social inequality [13]. Multiple risk factors associated with dropout from CR has been identified in a systematic review [6]. These factors were grouped into six main categories: intrapersonal, clinical, interpersonal, logistical, CR program, and health system factors [6]. While the review enhanced our understanding of these risk factors, there is still a need for tools to identify the patients in clinical practice. To address this, Pack et al. proposed a tool to predict early dropout from CR within an American setting. However, since this tool was based solely on available registry data, it may have overlooked unrecorded risk factors and may not be transferable to other healthcare settings [14].

Another review found that interventions to increase adherence to CR were effective and there was moderate-quality evidence that interventions to increase program completion were effective [4]. The review called for high-quality studies on the effectiveness of interventions aimed at increasing completion and interventions targeting individuals in a vulnerable situation [4]. To implement such interventions, it is essential to first identify the target group and then develop a tailored intervention [15]. A recent study showed that a low-cost peer-mentor intervention significantly improved CR attendance among older patients in a vulnerable situation [16]. However, it lacked a clinical method to identify vulnerability and had high CR attendance indicating potential selection bias towards more resourceful individuals. To address these gaps, we developed the Heart Priority Programme as a supplement to standardised CR, incorporating 1) a method to identify individuals in a vulnerable situation and 2) a targeted intervention to support at-risk cardiac patients likely to drop out of CR. Systematic development of complex interventions is important for maximising their effectiveness and sustainability. However, intervention development is rarely a straightforward, linear process, requiring different methods at various parts of the process [17]. Co-producing is increasingly recognised as a key step in this process, as it incorporates diverse perspectives and expertise, thereby enhancing the real-world application of the intervention [18–22]. Additionally, the development phase often continues with a feasibility phase to assess acceptability and feasibility of an early version of the intervention [22, 23].

The main objective of this paper is to outline the development of the Heart Priority Programme, co-produced to identify, and support cardiac patients at risk of dropout due to being in a vulnerable situation. Additionally, we report findings on the programme’s feasibility, focussing on its implementation (reach and fidelity), acceptability, and mechanisms of impact.

Methods

This study was informed by the framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions, focussing specifically on the development and feasibility phases [17]. The framework guided our understanding of these phases, detailing the target group, context and delivering team.

Target group

Patients aged 18 + with ACS, referred to CR within community healthcare services.

Context and delivering team

The Heart Priority Programme (hereafter referred to as the programme) was designed to be an add-on and integrated part of standard tax-funded CR offered in community healthcare services in Denmark. The community healthcare services are responsible for rehabilitation, working closely in cooperation with the treating hospital [24–26]. Following hospital discharge, the first outpatient CR follow-up is recommended within 14 days, with the CR program ideally starting within four weeks [27]. CR commences with an initial consultation with a healthcare professional specialised in cardiovascular disease. The aim of the initial consultation is to uncover individual recourses and challenges of each patient and evaluate their individual needs, and present the possibilities regarding CR. Subsequently, patients are offered a 12-week CR programme that includes the recommended key components: patient education, exercise, dietary counselling, smoking cessation, psychosocial support, and medication management [1]. The programme was delivered by CR teams including nurses and physiotherapists, who managed daily CR, collaborated with the hospital, conducting CR initial consultation, and communicating with patients opting out of CR. Specialists such as dieticians and consultants on smoking cessation supplemented the CR teams.

Three community healthcare services were involved in the study, representing small (population ~ 56,350), medium (population ~ 97,000), and large (population ~ 361,600) community healthcare services. Collaboration agreements were established with all services prior to the start of the study, outlining expected obligations including time consumption and meeting activities throughout the project.

Development phase

The programme consisted of two main parts: 1) an evidence-based form to identify patients at risk of dropout due to being in a vulnerable situation and 2) an add-on intervention to standard CR targeted these patients. Development was drawing upon an iterative multi-method framework for co-producing and prototyping healthcare interventions [28]. This approach emphasised three stages: 1) Evidence review and stakeholder consultation; 2) Co-production; and 3) Prototyping. Stage 1. Review knowledge base and stakeholder involvement

Stage 1 involved reviewing existing evidence, consulting stakeholders, and observing current practice. Literature on ‘risk factors for dropout from CR programmes’, and ‘interventions to promote patient utilisation in CR’ was reviewed to inform development, focusing on both the identification form and the add-on intervention. Priority was given to systematic reviews to provide a comprehensive overview of risk factors and intervention effectiveness, as well as to recent qualitative studies to capture patient perspectives, healthcare professionals’ insights, and contextual factors. To understand participation barriers and to inform development, we consulted six cardiac patients who opted out of CR and healthcare professionals. Patient consultations were conducted individually by telephone by two of the authors (CI and KK). Healthcare professional consultations were conducted individually at the three study sites by one author (CI). Additionally, three authors (CI, TM, KK) observed practices at stage 1, including: three direct observations of the initial patient consultations, observations of various elements of the CR programme, with one author (TM) embedded in a CR team for six-months at one of the study sites. Informal interactions with CR teams and comprehensive notes further supported development planning.

Stage 2. Co-production of the α-version

We established a project-group of ten members: three researchers and seven healthcare professionals, including four CR team members (three nurses and one physiotherapist) and the team leaders from the three community healthcare services. All researchers and CR team members also contributed as authors. Over six months, an action research cycle was conducted through six meetings, hosted in turn by each community healthcare service. Meetings included collective discussion, facility tours and workflow exchanges. Ideas were presented, refined, and re-evaluated within the project group and with feedback from the CR teams until consensus was reached. Notes were taken and used throughout the development phase. Feedback from six cardiac patients in stage 1 was actively integrated into the process.

Stage 3. Prototyping and local adaption of the α-version

This stage involved field testing and local adaptations to address early issues of acceptability, feasibility, and other potential problems before formal feasibility testing. The three CR teams conducted the field test, making small adaptations to tailor the programme to their specific contexts. As part of the field testing, the healthcare professionals involved in the co-production process provided peer training for the CR team. Additionally, the CR teams at the three community healthcare centres drafted tailored instructions for delivering the program within their specific contexts. Consultations with three cardiac patients and the CR teams informed adjustment to the add-on intervention, its delivery, and the context. Patients were presented with an outline of the intervention and provided feedback. This stage culminated in the prototyping of the β-version, ready for the feasibility phase.

Feasibility phase

This phase was conducted as a single-arm follow-up study collecting qualitative and quantitative data to enhance a comprehensive understanding of the programme’s feasibility and acceptability. The study was conducted in close collaboration with CR teams from two of the original study sites. It involved four healthcare professionals from one site (one nurse and three physiotherapists) and six from the other (two nurses and four physiotherapists). Patients who completed an initial consultation between July 2023 and January 2024 were invited to participate. Eligible patients, meeting the following inclusion criteria—diagnosed with ACS, referred to CR and aged over 18—were recruited by a healthcare professional from the CR team during the initial consultation. A convenience sample of at least 150 cardiac patients were deemed appropriate to represent the target population, provide sufficient data, and allow the CR teams to test the programme with a sufficient number of patients, providing them to gain valuable insights. If more than 150 patients were eligible within the allocated time frame, they were also included. As suggested by Moore et al. the collection of process data, along with ‘acceptability’ can have a vital role in understanding the feasibility of the intervention by providing insights into how the intervention is implemented and received by the target population [29]. Thus, we collected quantitative and qualitative data over a six-month period, focusing on three dimensions: implementation (reach and fidelity), acceptability, and mechanism of impact (Table 1). Process data collected during the feasibility phase

The identification form was used to present patient characteristics, showing the proportion of patients being in risk of drop out due to being in a valuable situation and comparing them to those not identified as such. At the conclusion of the feasibility phase, feedback meetings were held with the two CR teams, comprising of four and six healthcare professionals respectively, to explore their perspectives on how the programme worked in real-world setting. The feedback meetings were held separately, followed a semi-structured interview guide, were audio-recorded, and lasted approximately 25 min. The interview guide focused on the CR teams’ perspectives on the programme’s impact on the collaboration between healthcare professionals and patients, their approach to individuals in a vulnerable situation, and the programmes’ influence on their daily work (see Additional file 1). Two authors (MBR and CI) reviewed the audio files and prepared a summary of each meeting. Concept-driven content analyses were performed using the three dimensions as coding frame [30], and passages were identified for transcription. Throughout this process the data coding and themes were clarified, the final summaries were discussed, and a consensus was reached. In case of disagreement, TM was consulted. Furthermore, during the feasibility phase, one of the authors (KK) conducted telephone consultations with three cardiac patients from stage 1 of the development phase. The aim was to obtain their immediate view on the core elements of the add-on intervention.

Ethics statement

According to Danish legislation and the Act on Biomedical Research Ethics Committee System in Denmark, research without human biological material does not require approval from an ethics committee (§14 Sect. 2). The study was registered and approved by the Data Protection Agency of Central Denmark Region (1–16-02–121-24). Cardiac patients and healthcare professionals provided written informed consent to participate in the co-production process, like participants in the feasibility test.

Results

The development phase followed a three-stage iterative process, involving close collaboration between cardiac patients, healthcare professionals, and researchers.

Development phase

The development phase spanned from January 2023 to June 2023.

Identification form

Resurreccion et al. identified 25 risk factors associated with dropout from CR programmes [6], Ravn et al. found 17 factors [31] and Svendsen et al. highlighted 4 socioeconomic factors as important markers of CR utilisation [13]. As four of the risk factors overlapped between the papers (comorbidity, anxiety/depression symptoms, unemployed or retired, transportation and previous CR participation), this resulted in a total of 42 unique factors associated with dropout. Subsequently, two authors (TM and CI) assessed these factors using three criteria: alignment with the aim of identifying patients in a vulnerable situation, relevance to the outpatient context, and whether they can be combined with other factors to reduce the number of items, and thus reduce the registration workload for professionals. In total 21 risk factors were deemed suitable for the co-production process (Table 3). Add-on intervention

Santiago et al. emphasised the need for further research to understand how to increase completion and to develop specific, implementable interventions [4]. Resurreccion et al. suggested that factors associated with dropout from CR could inform interventions to reduce dropout, particularly emphasising support for patients with a low social profile [6]. Ravn et al. stressed the importance of tailoring CR programmes to patients' social context and fostering a sense of belonging, recommending a holistic and inclusive approach by healthcare professionals throughout the entire treatment process [32].

Consultations with the six cardiac patients who dropped out of CR revealed that they tended to be elderly (age + 65) and found it challenging to participate in large group settings. The patients primarily articulated that CR was solely an exercise programme, and therefore, they did not discuss other components, such as patient education, in their considerations regarding participation. Additionally, patients argued that their lack of participation was due to the insufficient focus on comorbidities, as several patients with cardiac disease have multiple comorbidities. Early dropout (after 1 or 2 sessions) was primarily attributed to the program not meeting their individual needs and their discomfort with participation in CR. Practice observations of interactions between cardiac patients and healthcare professionals helped identify areas for improvement and adaptation. This was summarised and presented at project-group meetings to inform the co-production (stage 2) and prototyping (stage 3) of the programme.

Stage 2. Co-production of the α-version

Stage 2 focused on the continuous development and refinement of the knowledge gained in stage 1, aiming to produce an α-version of the programme ready for field testing [28]. Six project group meetings were held for ongoing adaptations. The first two meetings addressed the risk factors to include in the identification form. Meetings three to five reviewed the field testing of the identification form, discussed stakeholder input, and decided on elements for the add-on intervention. The sixth meeting covered the results from the field testing of the identification form and the subsequently feasibility phase. An additional file shows a detailed description of the six meetings (see Additional file 2).

Identification form, α-version

Discussions at project group meetings and feedback from the CR teams in the three community healthcare services led to 12 risk factors being omitted (see Additional file 3, Table 1). The CR teams suggested to add three risk factors based on practical experience in CR: additional health concerns, illness related to family and ethnicity. Patients suggested adding “unfamiliar with exercising”. This resulted in 13 risk factors being included in the α-version of the identification form ready for field testing. To improve the practical usability of the identification form, simple and intuitive descriptions were created for each risk factor. For example, ‘Ethnicity’ was described as "Linguistic or cultural barriers impacting cardiac rehabilitation," and "Lower educational level" was described as ‘No education or low educational level/short courses’ (see Additional file 3, Table 2). Additionally, the project-group agreed that the identification form should serve as a clinical tool for healthcare professionals and be used during the initial consultation. Based on feedback from the CR teams, detailed definitions of each the risk factor were added to enhance understanding and ensure consistent interpretation in daily practice. The risk factors were assessed using ‘yes / no’ response options, with three of them also allowing for an open ‘free text’ response. The assessment was subjective, meaning that the number of 'yes' responses was not the sole factor for the overall assessment; instead, a single factor could be pivotal in assessing dropout risk. Therefore, there was strong agreement that risk of dropout was a subjective assessment made by healthcare professionals rather than relying on a high total ‘yes’ score to predict the risk of dropout.

Add-on intervention, α-version

During the first discussion regarding the add-on intervention the project group suggested to establish a midterm consultation after 4–6 weeks. However, consultations with six patients in stage 1 quickly rendered this proposal irrelevant because they argued that they dropped out early in the process, usually after only one or two sessions. Through group work, the following elements for the α-version were suggested and discussed: the need for more focus on the content of the invitation letter and the initial consultation to make sure that the patients understand that CR is more than an exercise programme and that participation is crucial; how to improve communication between healthcare professionals in the CR team; how to handle cancellation and absence effectively; and how to ensure that patients feel safe during their first session. Members of the project group shared these discussions with colleagues in the three CR teams. While generally satisfied with the suggested elements, colleagues recommended reducing the numbers of elements to ensure manageability within the existing context, timeframe, and resources.

Stage 3. Prototyping and local adaption of the α-version

During this stage, it became clear that the CR teams needed a practical terminology for patients identified as in risk of dropout due to being in a vulnerable situation. Therefore, several lengthy discussions were held to determine which term to use, and through a consensus process, it was decided to refer to these patients as ‘priority patients.’

Identification form, β-version

An electronic α-version was designed and integrated into electronic patient record to facilitate use in daily practice. The α-version was field tested in the three community healthcare services. During the field testing the identification form was applied in 230 patients of which 45% was identified as priority patients. Overall, the CR teams were satisfied with the α-version. They found it to be valuable in their workflow, they appreciated the design and functionality, as well as the ease of integration into their existing processes. The teams also provided constructive suggestions for improvements, primarily regarding the wording and the detailed description of the risk factors. These suggestions were integrated into the β-version, which was ready for the feasibility phase.

Add-on intervention, β-version

The elements suggested in the α-version was discussed, and a consensus process in the project group resulted in three core elements for the add-on intervention:Assigning a contact person with specific responsibilities (e.g., ensure the patient feels safe and welcome at the first session, and acting as a contact in case of cancellation)Systematically communicating patient information to colleagues in the CR team (e.g., regular team meetings, ensuring that patient information is consistently recorded, and establishing clear communication protocols for what information should be communicated and when)Systematic follow-up (e.g., maintaining continuous contact in case of cancellation/absence, and ongoing support and follow-up).

Feasibility phase

This β-version was feasibility tested in two community healthcare services from July 2023 to January 2024, involving 260 patients referred for CR. The two main components were used in this phase. The identification form comprising 13 risk factors were used by healthcare professionals during the initial consultation to identify priority patients. The second component was the add-on intervention targeted these patients, involving the assigning of a contact person with specific responsibilities, systematic communicating patient information to colleagues in the CR team, and systematic follow-up. Implementation (reach and fidelity)

During the feasibility phase the CR teams identified 91 patients (35%) as priority patients (Table 5). All 13 risk factors were more prevalent in patients identified as priority patients compared to those not at risk. The following risk factors were present in more than 30% of the patients: no education or low educational level / short courses (35.2%), symptoms of anxiety and/or depression (39.6%), unfamiliar with exercising (62.6%), unsure of the benefit from CR / participation (46.2%), comorbidity (64.8%) and additional health concerns (36.3%).

Feedback meetings with the two CR teams revealed that the programme was relevant and applicable because it was easily integrated into their daily workflow. Furthermore, the meetings revealed that the programme led to changes in practice that were not part of the programme. For example, the CR teams reported that they revised the wording of the patients’ invitation letter emphasising that CR is more than solely an exercise programme and that participation is crucial to gain its full benefits. Additionally, they worked on implementing a midterm evaluation for patients to further reduce dropout rates.

Acceptability

The CR teams had a positive view on the programme, considering it approachable and valuable for their daily work with cardiac patients. It helped ensure a more individualised approach to patient care and highlighted the various nuances in each patient's life. According to the CR teams, the programme was easy to implement because it closely aligned with their existing practice.“It's just about systematising something we were already doing. It was something we were doing before; now it's just been organised into a system, which is why it has been easy”. (Physiotherapist, >10 years’ experience)

Another positive reflection came from a healthcare professional who noted that the programme positively impacted job satisfaction by ensuring a more person-centred rehabilitation process. Despite the positive view on the programme, some areas for improvement were suggested. While the CR team found the identification form useful in understanding individual patient needs, they sought a more systematic approach to personalise CR for priority patients. The CR teams disclosed that they sometimes found it difficult to know how to best personalise and assemble the optimal CR programme for each patient. They suggested an overview of different possibilities and pathways such as home training, increased contact, and delayed start. This would create a list of alternatives for healthcare professionals to use besides standard CR, along with sharing experiences of what has been effective in other cases.

Mechanism of impact

The feedback meetings revealed that the programme had established a common language among healthcare professionals. They developed a unified way to discuss patients in a vulnerable situation, ensuring everyone understood what ‘priority patient’ meant. This shared language proved valuable not only within the CR team but also across the entire organisation. According to the CR teams, the programme had enhanced their focus on individualisation and tailoring CR to meet the needs of each patient. They explained that, in the past, they would often discontinue a patient’s CR course if the patient missed a few sessions. However, the programme has helped them shift this practice, allowing more flexibility for individual patients."In general, the framework has become broader and taller in virtually every aspect in order to accommodate the various differences that now exist among patients. The project has allowed us to do this, and it has been interesting." (Physiotherapist, >10 years’ experience)

The increased focus on priority patients also resulted in more attention to patients who were not in a vulnerable situation, confirming that these patients could manage on their own. Hence, from the CR teams’ perspective, the extra focus on patients in a vulnerable situation was a good investment.

Consultations with cardiac patients

Common feedback from three patients regarding the add-on intervention were the importance of continuity and an accommodating approach throughout the process. The patients provided positive feedback on the three core elements of the add-on intervention. Regarding the contact person they expressed that it could help establish a relationship, provide a sense of security, and allow for a better understanding of the patients’ strengths and weaknesses. Systematic communication with colleagues in the CR team would create continuity in the process, which was crucial for the patients. Such systematic communication ensures that needs are addressed, making participation more meaningful. Systematic follow-up would provide a sense of security in the beginning of CR, helping patients feel acknowledged and valued. Additionally, patients appreciated that the follow-up could be individualised in terms of timing and frequency, which should be agreed upon early in the process.

Discussion

This paper outlines the co-production process behind the Heart Priority Programme, designed as a supplement to standardised CR by identifying and supporting cardiac patients at risk of dropout due to being in a vulnerable situation (priority patients). We also share findings from the programme’s feasibility test. The study introduced an evidence-based identification form with 13 risk factors for dropout and proposed an add-on intervention targeted at priority patients, which included assigning a contact person, systematic communication among colleagues, and systematic follow-up for continuity. The programme was tested in two community healthcare services, identifying 35% of 260 patients as priority patients, with all 13 risk factors being more prevalent in this group. Feedback from the CR teams indicated that the programme was relevant, easily integrated into daily workflows, and effective in fostering a common language within CR teams while enhancing individualised patient care. The patients provided positive feedback on the three core elements of the add-on intervention. They emphasised that assigning a contact person could foster strong patient-healthcare professional relationships, enhance security, and help understand individual patient needs. Systematic communication among CR team members could ensure continuity and addresses patient needs, making participation in CR more meaningful. Moreover, systematic, and personalised follow-up can provide patients with a sense of security, making them feel acknowledged and valued.

The heart priority programme

The programme consisted of two main parts: 1) an identification form to identify priority patients and 2) an add-on intervention targeted at these patients. This approach aligns with research suggesting that effective interventions for individuals in a vulnerable situation require identifying the target group before implementing a tailored intervention that addresses their specific needs [15].

In this study we developed, and feasibility tested a simple and practical identification form. The risk factors included was chosen through a combination of factors originated from healthcare professionals with extensive expertise within CR and previously publications [6, 13, 31]. The fact that the identification form included risk factors that have been previously described supports the idea that this form should be applicable to other CR programmes and contexts. Previous research has established a strong association between smoking and dropout from CR [34, 35]. Smoking was excluded from the identification form during the co-production process (see Additional file 3) to reduce the number of items, as the healthcare professionals considered other factors more critical. In the field test, smoking was not mentioned as a missing risk factor for vulnerability. Since all patients report on smoking status, this information is already available to healthcare professionals and may not be necessary for identifying individuals in a vulnerable situation. Future evaluation of the identification form will determine whether smoking is a critical factor for identifying priority patients.

Although multiple tools exist to identify vulnerability in e.g. older adults and cancer patients [36–38], we only found one tool within the field of CR [14]. Since this tool was designed as a predictive model based on retrospect data from an American setting, it could not be directly applied to our study due to lack of transferability. Furthermore, neither patients nor healthcare professionals were involved in the development—a crucial step for tools used in multi-component interventions, particularly when identifying individuals in a vulnerable situation [28].

The add-on intervention comprised three core elements: 1) assigning a contact person with specific responsibilities for the priority patients, 2) systematically communicating patient information to colleagues in the CR team, and 3) systematic follow-up to ensure continuity throughout the process. These elements focus on systematic contacts by the same healthcare professional and ongoing support over the telephone or face-to-face meetings, which aligns with other studies aimed at increasing participation in CR [8, 39, 40]. Prior literature has shown a strong dose–response relationship between the number of CR sessions attended and long-term outcomes. Patients who attend more sessions experience a lower risk of death and adverse events compared to those who attend fewer sessions, highlighting the critical importance of such an intervention [4, 41]. However, there remains a gap in research specifically evaluating intervention effectiveness in under-represented groups such as individuals in a vulnerable situation, as their needs and challenges may differ from those not in a vulnerable situation [4, 42]. Our study seeks to bridge this knowledge gap by proposing a simple and easily implementable add-on intervention, including on-going support, targeted at these patients.

Use of frameworks and co-production

The development of the Heart Priority Programme was inspired by the framework proposed by Hawkins et al. [28]. This framework was suitable and helpful, recommending iterative co-production with continuous feedback loops among researchers, healthcare professionals, and patients to tailor the intervention to the target group and address implementation issues. It also allowed the project group to remain flexible and open-minded during development. This approach allowed the Heart Priority Programme to be refined to address the needs of both healthcare professionals and patients, improving its practicality and effectiveness in community healthcare services.

However, the iterative co-production approach was sometimes challenging. Other studies have found the process to be time- and resource-consuming [43, 44]. This aligns with our experiences; however, initial planning and established collaboration agreements supported the process and allowed the project group members to allocate time and resources. It was also difficult to determine when consensus had been reached, and the uncertainty about the final outcome – since the process is open-ended and evolves over time – could be unsettling for stakeholders who prefer clear and predictable plans. Thus, this iterative and co-production approach requires available resources, detailed timelines as well as project managing. Finally, reporting on an iterative development process can be challenging. The open-ended nature of iterative processes, with continuous feedback loops and evolving project elements, makes it difficult to present a clear, linear narrative. Additionally, capturing the complexity and nuances of the various iterations within the structured format of a scientific article can result in oversimplification or omission of critical details. However, reflecting on our experiences and aligning with Moore et al. [45], we believe that involving the CR teams and utilising their resources was crucial for the result.

Methodological considerations

A strength of the study is the combination of qualitative and quantitative methods, along with stakeholder involvement. This approach enables a broad understanding of implementation, acceptability, and mechanism of impact. However, it also has strengths and limitations. Investing sufficient time in the various settings, co-producing with healthcare professionals and patients, and employing data triangulation were considered a strength in enhancing the credibility of the study [46]. Furthermore, proving detailed context descriptions of the various settings was deemed to increase the study’s transferability [46]. Specifically, the number of patient consultations and the absence of direct presentation of patients in the project group were considered a limitation. Initially, the plan was to include three cardiac patients as active partners. However, due to their vulnerability, this was not feasible. Instead, we conducted individual telephone consultations to gather their perspectives during the development process.

The identification form was systematically used for 260 patients. While the subjective assessment by healthcare professionals were key in identifying ‘priority patients’, conducting an inter-rater reliability test could have enhanced data validity [47]. Despite this, the data showed consistency in the characteristics of identified priority patients, supporting content validity. Although social vulnerability is often estimated using an index aggregated social factors [48], our study demonstrated the feasibility of assessing vulnerability without such an index. As the feasibility phase was limited to two community healthcare services, the findings should be regarded as preliminary indications of the programme’s implementation, acceptability, and mechanisms of impact.

We involved various stakeholders – cardiac patients, healthcare professionals from the CR teams in community healthcare services, and researchers—in the development process, which we believe strengthened the content of the Heart Priority Programme by incorporating multiple perspectives and addressing diverse needs. Furthermore, programs that are co-produced with stakeholder input often enjoy greater acceptance and support from those who will use or be affected by them [18, 19, 22]. Stakeholders are more likely to embrace a program they have helped to shape, leading to higher engagement and successful implementation [22]. Although the Heart Priority Programme was developed within a specific health care system, the identification form and the core components of the add-on intervention may be transferable to other health care systems such as hospitals.

Conclusion

This paper outlines the development of the Heart Priority Programme and present findings from a feasibility test. The study demonstrated that the iterative three-stage co-production process used to develop the Heart Priority Programme resulted in a relevant programme that was relatively easy to implement across community healthcare services of various sizes. The CR teams reported that the programme offered a structured and systematic approach to supporting cardiac patients at risk of dropout due to being in a vulnerable situation, effectively addressing their specific needs. Patients also recognised the relevance of the core elements, indicated that the programme aligned well with their needs for participating in CR. While these initial findings are promising, further research is necessary. A full-scale evaluation, involving a more diverse range of community healthcare services, is crucial to rigorously assess the programme’s effectiveness and generalisability. This next step will be key in determining whether the programme can be recommended for widespread implementation within community healthcare services.
