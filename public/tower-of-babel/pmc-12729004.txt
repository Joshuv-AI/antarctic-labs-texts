Research on China’s population health policy: an analysis based on the PMC index model
Authors: Rongjiang Cai, Congying Shen, Xi Wang, Shufang Zhao, Tao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith China’s profound demographic transition, population health issues have emerged as critical determinants of sustainable national development. Since 2001, the Chinese government has implemented numerous population health policies; however, the absence of a systematic, standardized, and quantifiable evaluation framework has constrained the scientific rigor and effectiveness of policy assessment. This study aims to develop a Policy Modeling Consistency (PMC) Index model tailored to China’s context, enabling systematic quantitative evaluation of population health policies to identify strengths and limitations, thereby establishing an evidence base for policy optimization.This study employs text mining combined with the PMC Index model to construct an evaluation framework comprising 9 primary variables and 40 secondary variables. Twelve representative population health policies were quantitatively assessed, with results visualized through PMC surface maps to analyze internal policy consistency and multidimensional performance.The evaluation revealed that 8 policies achieved “excellent” ratings (PMC index: 6.00-7.31), while 4 received “good” ratings (PMC index: 5.59–5.78), indicating generally high quality with notable variations across China’s population health policies. Most policies demonstrated consistent strengths in timeliness, evaluation mechanisms, and transparency. However, significant disparities emerged in policy functions, target populations, and policy objectives.Through development of the PMC Index model, this study provides systematic, multidimensional quantitative evaluation of China’s population health policies, introducing novel insights and methodological approaches for policy optimization. The findings offer substantial theoretical and practical contributions toward enhancing the scientific precision and effectiveness of China’s population health policies, ultimately promoting improved population health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25566-z.Keywords: Population health policy, Policy modeling consistency index, Policy evaluation, Quantitative analysis.

Introduction

Population health constitutes the foundation of sustainable national development and represents a vital component of long-term strategic security. China’s rapid socioeconomic progress has precipitated profound demographic transitions, elevating population health issues to critical importance for national welfare and people’s livelihoods. By the end of 2023, China’s population aged 60 and above reached 280 million, representing over 19% of the total population. Concurrently, chronic diseases continue to rise in prevalence, accounting for more than 80% of the national disease burden. The floating population exceeds 240 million, facing particularly acute health challenges, while approximately 85 million citizens with disabilities urgently require enhanced health services. These multifaceted challenges systematically test China’s policy response capabilities. Since 2001, the Chinese government has implemented comprehensive population health policies, evolving from an initial family planning focus to establishing an integrated population health management service system. These policies address multiple populations—including the elderly, women and children, migrant populations, and persons with disabilities—spanning prevention, medical care, rehabilitation, and health education. This framework constitutes a distinctive Chinese approach to population health governance, playing a pivotal role in promoting public health, addressing demographic changes, and ensuring social equity. However, as policies proliferate and expand in scope, scientifically evaluating their implementation, identifying strengths and limitations, and optimizing policy design have emerged as key challenges. Given the field’s broad coverage, complex influencing factors, and extended effect cycles, systematic and quantifiable assessment tools are essential.

Current policy analyses predominantly examine individual policies or employ qualitative evaluation methods, lacking systematic, standardized, and quantifiable assessment frameworks. This limitation significantly constrains the scientific rigor and effectiveness of policy evaluations. For China, with its substantial population base and pronounced regional disparities, developing a context-appropriate population health policy evaluation system represents both a theoretical and practical imperative. Despite growing interest in this field, systematic approaches integrating text mining with policy evaluation models, particularly for population health policies, remain scarce. Several critical research gaps persist. First, a systematic quantitative evaluation framework remains absent. Existing studies focus on single policies or qualitative assessments, lacking standardized quantitative systems capable of enabling systematic cross-policy comparisons. Consequently, evaluation outcomes lack comparability and intuitive presentation. Second, current evaluation indicator systems lack scientific comprehensiveness. Existing indicators typically assess single dimensions—policy content, implementation processes, or outcomes—while neglecting multidimensional examination encompassing policy nature, timeliness, functions, target audiences, evaluation mechanisms, objectives, effectiveness levels, thematic content, and transparency. Third, methodological approaches remain limited and lack innovation. Research predominantly relies on traditional content analysis or survey statistics, with minimal adoption of advanced technologies such as text mining and social network analysis, or innovative evaluation tools like the Policy Modeling Consistency (PMC) Index model. This constrains research depth and breadth. Fourth, comparative analysis across different policy types remains insufficient. Studies typically concentrate on specific policy categories (e.g., elderly health policies, medical security policies), lacking horizontal comparisons across diverse policy types, thereby limiting comprehensive understanding of China’s population health policy system’s characteristics and evolutionary trends.

This study systematically evaluates China’s population health policies through: reviewing the developmental trajectory since 2001; constructing a PMC Index evaluation model tailored to China’s context; quantitatively assessing 12 representative population health policies; analyzing their strengths, limitations, and internal consistency; and proposing targeted optimization recommendations. By establishing a scientific evaluation framework, this study aims to conduct comprehensive quantitative assessment, identify policy strengths and weaknesses, provide evidence for policy improvement, and ultimately enhance the scientific precision and effectiveness of China’s population health policies.

The study employs an integrated methodology combining text mining with the PMC Index model. Text mining techniques, including word frequency analysis and social network analysis, extract key information and thematic structures from policy documents. Subsequently, a PMC Index evaluation model comprising 9 primary variables and 40 secondary variables enables quantitative assessment of 12 policies. Results are visualized through PMC surface plots, informing policy optimization recommendations. This approach achieves methodological innovation through two mechanisms: first, integrating text mining technology with policy evaluation models creates a closed-loop research process from text to data to evaluation; second, the PMC Index model establishes a multidimensional, quantifiable evaluation framework, enhancing assessment objectivity and scientific rigor.

For this study, we adopt a comprehensive definition of ‘population health’ that encompasses both demographic dimensions (population structure, fertility, aging) and health service dimensions (disease prevention, medical care, rehabilitation). This integrated approach reflects China’s policy evolution from narrow fertility control to holistic population wellbeing, as articulated in the Healthy China 2030 strategy. Thus, P8 (Disability Prevention and Rehabilitation Regulations) appropriately falls within this scope as it addresses both population quality (prevention of disabilities affecting demographic structure) and health services (rehabilitation for affected populations).

The PMC Index evaluation reveals that among 12 policies, 8 achieved “excellent” ratings (PMC index: 6.00–7.31.00.31) and 4 received “good” ratings (PMC index: 5.59–5.78), indicating generally high quality with notable variations in China’s population health policies. Through constructing the PMC Index model, this study provides systematic, multidimensional quantitative evaluation of China’s population health policies, addressing existing research gaps while offering novel insights and methodological approaches for policy optimization. These findings hold substantial theoretical and practical value for advancing the scientific precision and effectiveness of China’s population health policies, ultimately promoting improved population health outcomes.

This study makes four distinctive contributions to population health policy research: We advance policy evaluation theory by adapting the PMC Index model—originally developed for macroeconomic policy assessment—to the population health domain. Unlike generic policy frameworks, our adapted model incorporates health-specific dimensions (target population diversity, health system integration, equity considerations) while maintaining the PMC model’s quantitative rigor. This represents the first application of PMC methodology to comprehensive population health policy evaluation in China.

We pioneer a two-stage analytical approach integrating: (1) Unsupervised text mining (TF-IDF weighting, co-occurrence network analysis) to inductively derive evaluation indicators from policy documents themselves, ensuring context-specific relevance; followed by (2) PMC Index quantification for systematic scoring and visualization. This integration overcomes limitations of purely qualitative content analysis (subjectivity, non-comparability) and purely theory-driven indicator systems (potential mismatch with policy reality). Our methodology enables replication across policy domains and national contexts.

We provide the first systematic quantitative assessment of China’s population health policy landscape spanning 23 years (2001–2024), evaluating 12 national-level policies across 9 dimensions and 40 sub-indicators. Our findings reveal: (1) Universal policy strengths in timeliness, evaluation mechanisms, and transparency (all policies scored 1.0); (2) Significant variation in functional comprehensiveness (scores 0.20–0.80) and target population specificity (scores 0.00–0.60.00.60); (3) Identification of top-performing policies (P10: PMC = 7.31) and improvement priorities (P8: PMC = 5.59). These evidence-based insights have not been documented in prior literature.

Our PMC surface visualizations provide policymakers with intuitive diagnostic tools identifying dimensional strengths and weaknesses for each policy. Unlike conventional evaluation reports presenting aggregated scores, our multidimensional mapping enables targeted optimization—for example, our analysis reveals that P8 (Disability Regulations) requires functional scope expansion and clearer target population definition, while maintaining its strong hierarchical positioning. This actionable specificity distinguishes our work from existing policy analyses.

Literature review

Theoretical foundations of population health policy evaluation

As a core area of national governance, population health policies have garnered widespread global attention in recent years. This systematic review analyzes the developmental trajectory and theoretical foundations of population health policies through comprehensive examination of domestic and international literature. The theoretical foundation of population health policies has evolved from simple birth control to comprehensive governance approaches. Goldstone and May [1] analyzed the evolution of population policies across the 20th and 21st centuries, documenting a paradigm shift from family planning toward emphasizing individual reproductive rights and multidimensional intervention strategies. Yeh and Lee [2] provided theoretical frameworks for evaluating both anti-natalist and pro-natalist policies. Research demonstrates that governments strengthen population development guidance through “top-level design,” reflecting the dialectical relationship between policy continuity and change [3]. Lutz [4] positioned human resource development as central to sustainable development, emphasizing female education’s critical role in population policies. Demeny [5] identified the need for innovative incentives addressing low fertility rates, including pension system reforms. Wang et al. [6] advocated for developing postmodern population policies with Chinese characteristics, emphasizing humanistic care and individual autonomy [6]. Historical analyses of China’s population policies since the 1970 s provide essential context for contemporary policy adjustments [7]. Research examining population health policies’ impact on fertility behavior yields diverse findings. Chen and Gietel [8] demonstrated through survey data that population health policies significantly influence ideal family size. Studies reveal that fertility subsidy policies substantially increased birth numbers [9]. Wu [10] documented notable fertility rate increases following implementation of the two-child policy. However, policy effects demonstrate considerable regional and demographic variation. Liu and Zhao [11] found education had no significant impact on fertility decisions while negatively affecting fertility intentions. Shen et al. [12] established that anti-natalist policies significantly reduced fertility rates in Asia and Latin America, whereas pro-natalist policies yielded only short-term positive effects. Spéder et al. [13] demonstrated that Hungary’s child-rearing support policies increased fertility intentions among less-educated groups, illustrating differential policy tool effects.

Theoretical foundations of population health policy evaluation

Policy evaluation theory has evolved from linear input-output models to complex systems approaches [14]. Early studies predominantly employed qualitative case analyses or single-indicator assessments [15], lacking systematic frameworks for multidimensional evaluation. Recent advances integrate computational methods with policy science: Ruiz Estrada et al. (2010) developed the PMC Index model for quantitative policy consistency assessment [16], while text mining applications in policy analysis have proliferated [17]. However, these methodologies remain underutilized in population health policy contexts, particularly in developing country settings. Huang et al. [18] documented that declining fertility rates increase unmet demand for community-based elderly care services. López-Hernández et al. [19] analyzed Mexican data, concluding that rising elderly population proportions and dependency ratios necessitate corresponding public policy adjustments. Dennehy et al. [20] conducted comprehensive geriatric assessments of Irish community health organizations, recommending nationally unified evaluation standards to enhance elderly health service quality.

Scholars worldwide have proposed diverse strategies addressing population aging challenges. Research has analyzed the impact of China’s population policies on economic and social systems [21]. Miyazawa and Yamada [22] demonstrated that population policies and delayed retirement ages can significantly improve long-term fiscal conditions. Seid and Babbel [23] identified Ethiopia’s lack of specific policies addressing elderly health and nutritional issues. Ma et al. [24] proposed strategies addressing challenges including uneven resource allocation in managing aging and declining birth rates. Zhang et al. [25] analyzed factors influencing the sustainable development of China’s maternity insurance system. Alberti and Pierce [26] emphasized healthcare organizations’ need for diversified approaches promoting population health and health equity.

The relationship between population policies and economic development has received extensive attention. The challenges of population aging on health systems have received substantial empirical attention. Lopreite et al. [27] employed word embedding techniques to analyze the thematic evolution of aging and healthcare expenditure, demonstrating how computational text analysis can reveal policy priorities and gaps in aging-related health governance. Their scoping review identified critical linkages between demographic transitions and healthcare financing mechanisms, providing methodological precedents for our text mining approach. Furthermore, Lopreite and Zhu [28] quantified the bidirectional effects between China’s aging population and health expenditure growth using Bayesian-VAR modeling, establishing that demographic structural changes significantly increase healthcare costs while potentially constraining economic growth. Their findings underscore the urgency of evidence-based population health policy optimization in China’s context, directly supporting our study’s rationale for systematic policy evaluation. Wolf et al. [29] showed that socially just population policies significantly promote climate mitigation and adaptation. Lupi and Marsiglio [30] found such policies substantially reduce environmental policy social costs. Dalen and Henkens (2021) revealed demographers’ high concern regarding climate change [31]. Studies employing the STIRPAT model demonstrate that population aging and quality inhibit transportation sector CO2 emissions [32], while population aging significantly exacerbates regional carbon emissions [33]. Cafaro [34] explored population growth’s impact on climate change. Remenyi et al. [35] noted that robust socioeconomic development and supportive policies enhance national fertility rates [35]. Budolfson and Spears [36] projected global population exceeding 9 billion by 2060, highlighting limited prospects for fertility reduction as climate mitigation policy. Bontje [37] examined how governance structure changes affect population decline management. Li et al. [38] found approximately 30% of China’s resource-dependent cities experienced population shrinkage, necessitating more comprehensive population policies.

Methodological approaches in health policy analysis

Research methodologies in population health policy demonstrate increasing diversification. Existing health policy evaluations exhibit three methodological limitations: (1) Indicator system incompleteness: Studies typically assess isolated dimensions—policy content [39], implementation processes [40], or health outcomes—while neglecting holistic frameworks integrating policy design, targeting, evaluation mechanisms, and transparency. (2) Quantification challenges: Qualitative content analysis dominates the field [41], with limited adoption of standardized quantitative metrics enabling cross-policy comparisons. (3) Technological underutilization: Despite advances in natural language processing, few studies systematically integrate text mining with policy evaluation models to extract structured insights from policy documents [42]. Nishimura [43] analyzed historical changes and current status of Japan’s low fertility countermeasures since 1974. Kolbina [44] found Russia’s social support measures failed to effectively increase birth rates, exhibiting neoliberal characteristics. Homanen and Meskus [45] revealed fertility issues’ significance in Nordic biopolitics through Finnish research. Pourreza et al. [46] systematically reviewed key factors underlying fertility decline in the Middle East and North Africa. Hale and Hale [47] emphasized comprehensive family planning programs’ necessity for enhancing autonomy. Existing research provides essential foundations for understanding population health policy evolution and impact. However, comprehensive analytical frameworks remain necessary for evaluating policy effectiveness and providing scientific evidence for future policymaking. However, existing research shows limited integration of text mining technologies with policy evaluation models, particularly in the field of population health policies. A systematic review by Mao and Li [48] of computational methods in Chinese policy analysis found that only 8% of studies combined natural language processing with quantitative evaluation frameworks, with no applications identified in population health domains. Similarly, Hu et al. (2024) noted that health policy research remains dominated by traditional content analysis (68% of reviewed studies) and expert consultations (24%), with minimal adoption of advanced text analytics despite their potential for systematic, large-scale policy assessment [49]. This methodological gap is particularly pronounced in comparative policy evaluation, where standardized quantification is essential but rarely achieved. This approach addresses current research gaps while providing enhanced scientific theoretical and empirical support for China’s population health policy formulation.

Research gaps and study positioning

Four critical gaps emerge from our literature review: First, absence of standardized evaluation frameworks for population health policies in China’s context, limiting systematic policy comparison and optimization. Second, methodological innovation deficits—particularly the unexplored integration of text mining (for thematic extraction) with PMC Index modeling (for quantitative consistency assessment). Third, limited cross-policy comparative analysis: Existing research focuses on single policy domains (elderly care, maternal health) without horizontal comparisons revealing system-wide patterns. Fourth, weak linkage between policy design and implementation outcomes, with evaluation studies rarely connecting design features to effectiveness indicators. This study addresses these gaps by: (1) constructing a China-specific PMC Index evaluation framework with 9 primary and 40 secondary variables; (2) integrating text mining for data-driven indicator development; (3) conducting comparative analysis across 12 representative policies spanning 2001–2024; and (4) providing actionable optimization recommendations grounded in quantitative assessment.

Methodology and materials

Data collection

“Population health” represents a key component of China’s national strategy, encompassing comprehensive policies, strategic plans, and data platforms administered by the State Council and relevant ministries, including the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine, the National Health Commission, and the National Healthcare Security Administration. This study conducted a systematic search of population health policies through the State Council’s policy document database and affiliated ministry websites. The State Council’s policy database maintains a dedicated “population health” repository, systematically compiling relevant policies, regulations, news updates, and service information. Retrieved documents include recent initiatives such as the “Guidelines on Accelerating Population Health Informatization,” draft regulations on population health information management, and provincial implementation plans for the “two-child policy,” all accessible through the State Council’s thematic portal. The National Health Commission, National Healthcare Security Administration, and affiliated departments collaboratively implement initiatives encompassing health poverty alleviation, basic medical security, major disease prevention and control, and targeted health improvement for key populations (including economically disadvantaged groups, the elderly, women, and children), ensuring equitable access to essential healthcare services.

Policy selection followed rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) policy content directly addressing population health; (2) issuing authority at central or national ministerial level; (3) policy documentation in standardized formats including “notices,” “opinions,” or “plans”; and (4) policies currently in effect. Exclusion criteria eliminated: (1) documents containing only keyword references without substantive population health content; and (2) peripherally related or subnational policy texts, including activity notices, administrative approvals, and public announcements. Application of these criteria yielded 20 valid policy documents for analysis, From the initial 20 policies identified, 12 were selected based on four criteria: (1) Temporal coverage: ensuring representation across different Five-Year Plan periods (2001–2024); (2) Policy hierarchy: including State Council regulations, ministerial opinions, and implementation plans to capture different governance levels; (3) Target diversity: covering all major population groups (elderly, maternal-child, migrant, disabled); (4) Policy type: encompassing strategic planning (e.g., Five-Year Plans), regulatory frameworks (e.g., Regulations), and implementation guidance (e.g., Action Plans). Eight policies were excluded due to overlap (n = 3), local rather than national scope (n = 2), or primary focus outside population health (n = 3), as presented in Table 1 (retrieval completed: April 28, 2025).

PMC index model construction

The Policy Modeling Consistency (PMC) Index model evaluates policy consistency through the construction of multidimensional variables and systematic analysis of their interrelationships. The PMC surface provides visual representation of model results, enabling comprehensive quantitative analysis and policy optimization through graphical interpretation. This study employed equal weighting for all secondary variables, balanced through binary assignment, with overall policy effectiveness assessed via the PMC Index and corresponding PMC surface visualization. This model demonstrates broad applicability for quantitative policy analysis across diverse domains, establishing a scientific framework for evidence-based policy improvement. Figure 1 illustrates the construction process of the PMC Index model.

Policy text mining analysis

The text mining process involved the following preprocessing steps: (1) Text cleaning: removal of punctuation, numbers, and special characters using Python 3.8 with the jieba library for Chinese text segmentation; (2) Stopword removal: we employed a customized stopword list combining the Baidu stopword dictionary with policy-specific non-informative terms (e.g., ‘hereby’, ‘article’); (3) Synonym handling: we constructed a domain-specific thesaurus mapping synonymous terms (e.g., ‘elderly’/‘aged’/‘senior citizens’ mapped to ‘elderly population’); (4) Word frequency analysis using TF-IDF weighting to identify key terms; (5) Co-occurrence matrix construction using a sliding window of 5 words. All text mining procedures were implemented using Python’s jieba (v0.42.1) for Chinese text processing, scikit-learn (v1.0.2) for TF-IDF calculation, and Gephi (v0.9.2) for network visualization.

Words constitute the fundamental units of textual meaning. Statistical word frequency analysis reveals trends and thematic characteristics within policy documents. This study employed word frequency analysis functions to examine the substantive content of 12 Chinese population health policy texts, with minimum word length set at 2 characters. Word cloud of China’s population health policy

The core semantic network of China’s population health policy anchors on “population” as the central term, forming a historically inherited “triangular core” with “family planning” and “ministry” (representing competent authorities), establishing the foundation for governance transition from quantity control to responsibility-based management. High TF-IDF terms including “construction,” “development,” and “healthcare” radiate outward, signifying a policy discourse shift from “birth control” toward “system-building and health promotion.” The frequent occurrence of “accordance,” “laws,” and “responsibility” indicates that legalization and accountability have emerged as governance imperatives.

This transformation encompasses dual expansions in service scope and target populations. Marginalized groups including “floating population,” “disabilities,” “children,” and “aging” demonstrate high frequency rankings, emphasizing health equity priorities. The parallel prominence of “treatment,” “prevention,” “rehabilitation” alongside “finance,” “social security,” and “funds” reveals an emerging integrated continuum spanning “medical care—disease prevention—functional rehabilitation—multi-channel support.” The emergence of digital terminology including “big data,” “information sharing,” and “population health informatization” demonstrates systematic integration of digital governance as an accelerator for coordination efficiency and resource optimization.

However, the network periphery reveals areas requiring improvement. Weak linkages for “infectious diseases” within aging and floating population contexts indicate gaps in elderly infectious disease prevention and migrant population surveillance. Sparse connections between “financial” and “disabilities” reflect overreliance on single funding sources for disability rehabilitation services. Weak ties for “regional” terms underscore persistent health resource disparities across regions. Future policies should prioritize active healthy aging, integrated infection prevention, diversified disability funding mechanisms, and balanced regional investment to advance China’s population health governance toward greater equilibrium, resilience, and digitally-enabled quality enhancement.

Nodes with multiple connections exhibit higher centrality, indicating greater importance within the policy framework.

China’s population health policy social network map

The six themes were identified through a three-step process combining quantitative and qualitative analysis: (1) Community detection algorithms (Louvain method) identified initial clusters in the co-occurrence network; (2) Manual review of policy texts within each cluster to identify substantive themes; (3) Validation through keyword-in-context analysis to confirm thematic coherence. For example, Theme 1 (Health Management Services) emerged from the clustering of terms like ‘Service’, ‘Management’, ‘System’ but was confirmed through manual verification that these co-occurrences consistently appeared in contexts discussing service delivery infrastructure.

The semantic network of population health policies demonstrates a “dense core-sparse periphery” pattern. “Population” serves as the central hub with highest degree centrality, functioning as a central axis linking all color clusters. This forms a triangular core with “family planning” and “ministry,” reflecting both the historical legacy of quantity control and contemporary governance responsibility chains. High-betweenness nodes including “construction,” “management,” and “development” radiate outward, illustrating policy discourse progression from “control-management” toward “construction-development.” Target population terms—“persons,” “floating population,” “maternal,” and “disabilities”—encircle the core, signaling policy shifts toward diversity and vulnerable group inclusion. The outermost layer comprises modifiers including “local,” “regional” (hierarchical dimensions), “preventive,” “financial,” and “cultural” (implementation means), demonstrating multi-tiered governance and comprehensive support mechanisms. The network progresses hierarchically through “population→actors→construction→targets→means,” highlighting three core characteristics: “population” as anchor, “family planning” as cornerstone, and “construction/development” as accelerators. This structure delineates China’s population health policy evolution from single-core control to multi-core coordination.

Theme 1: Health Management Service System. China’s population health policies prioritize healthcare service system enhancement to ensure accessible, high-quality health services for diverse populations. Policies promote community-based health service network development, enabling prevention and treatment of common geriatric diseases at grassroots levels. They establish maternal and child health service chains providing comprehensive management from pre-pregnancy through postpartum care, develop “fertility-friendly” medical institutions offering integrated services for women of childbearing age and children, and implement targeted interventions for chronic diseases, mental health, and specialized healthcare needs. This foundational service system provides the operational platform for population health policy implementation.

Theme 2: Disease Prevention and Control System. Building upon the service framework, policies reinforce the “prevention first” principle, establishing life-cycle-spanning disease prevention networks. This encompasses three-tier prevention strategies for maternal and child health, early intervention protocols for geriatric diseases, comprehensive prevention programs for disabled populations, and infectious disease control with immunization measures for migrant populations. Through emphasizing source prevention, policies shift toward proactive, cost-effective health intervention models, effectively reducing disease incidence and disability rates while alleviating subsequent medical burdens.

Theme 3: Health Security System. Supporting the operation of service and prevention systems, policies establish multi-layered health protection mechanisms. These include expanding basic medical insurance coverage to encompass elderly, women and children, disabled individuals, and migrant populations; creating special subsidies including hospitalization support for pregnant women and neonatal disease assistance; improving medical aid systems addressing healthcare access barriers for vulnerable groups; and refining fiscal investment mechanisms ensuring public health program sustainability. Through economic support measures, this system effectively addresses healthcare affordability, guaranteeing basic medical needs across all demographic groups.

Theme 4: Health Education and Awareness System. With improved material security, policies increasingly emphasize soft interventions enhancing public health literacy. Initiatives include health education campaigns for migrant populations, self-care knowledge dissemination for elderly populations, promotion of eugenics and prenatal care concepts among childbearing-age groups, and multi-channel disability prevention knowledge dissemination. These awareness-raising measures transform public health perceptions, foster self-management capabilities, cultivate policy-supportive social environments, and extend health responsibilities from sole reliance on medical systems toward shared accountability among individuals, families, and society.

Theme 5: Technological Support System. To enhance system efficiency and quality, policies vigorously promote technological innovation and digital infrastructure. This includes establishing national population health information platforms enabling data sharing for migrant health management; expanding big data applications to innovate medical service models; strengthening scientific research on disability prevention and rehabilitation; and popularizing advanced prenatal screening technologies improving maternal and child health outcomes. Technological integration optimizes traditional service models while providing data-driven policy support, advancing health governance from experience-based to evidence-based approaches.

Theme 6: Policy Institutional System. As the highest-level safeguard, policies establish comprehensive legal, regulatory, and organizational frameworks. These span from specialized legislation to integrated deployments within Five-Year Plans for National Economic and Social Development; from central-to-local work coordination mechanisms to tiered accountability assessment systems; from dual-responsibility management systems for migrant populations (household registration and current residence) to government-led, multi-department collaborative health mechanisms for persons with disabilities. This institutional guarantee ensures all policy measures are legally grounded and procedurally actionable, forming a closed-loop policy implementation system with clear objectives, defined responsibilities, and efficient operation.

Variable classification and parameter identification

The Policy Modeling Consistency (PMC) Index and Omnia Mobilis hypothesis were proposed by Ruiz Estrada et al. The PMC Index model employs binary coding (0 and 1) for each variable, enabling objective analysis of policy strengths, weaknesses, and internal consistency.

The PMC model employs binary scoring (0 or 1) for secondary indicators following the original framework by Ruiz Estrada [16, 50]. This approach offers several advantages: (1) Enhanced objectivity by reducing subjective judgment in scoring; (2) Improved inter-rater reliability as demonstrated by Krippendorff [51] who found binary scoring achieved Cohen’s kappa of 0.85 versus 0.62 for 5-point scales in policy evaluation; (3) Computational simplicity enabling evaluation of large policy sets. While we acknowledge that binary scoring may not capture all policy nuances, the aggregation across 40 secondary variables provides sufficient granularity at the policy level. Future research could explore weighted or multi-level scoring systems as suggested by Hollenstein [52].

Text mining for raw data acquisition enhances policy evaluation precision. The PMC assesses the consistency level of specific policy models, intuitively revealing their advantages, limitations, and the concrete meanings and levels represented by variables. The PMC Index methodology typically involves: (1) constructing multi-input-output tables; (2) calculating two-tier variable values and PMC indices; and (3) generating PMC surface visualizations. Constructing the Multi-Input-Output table

Building upon established document and text mining methodologies, this study comprehensively references and modifies policy evaluation indicators from existing literature, ultimately establishing nine primary variables. Secondary variables were adjusted based on classic indicators discussed in prior research, the specific research questions addressed, and the context of China’s population health policies.

The PMC Index system for quantitative evaluation of China’s population health policies represents a meticulously designed framework conducting comprehensive assessment through nine key primary variables (X₁ to X₉). This evaluation system encompasses critical dimensions including policy nature, timeliness, functional aspects, target populations, evaluation criteria, key objectives, policy hierarchy, thematic content, and transparency. Each primary variable subdivides into specific secondary variables ensuring detailed and thorough evaluation.

Policy Nature (X₁) is assessed through six secondary variables—prediction, regulation, recommendation, description, guidance, and others—evaluating the roles and functions of population health policies, revealing core purposes and mechanisms. Policy Timeliness (X₂) examines implementation duration, categorized as long-term (exceeding 10 years), medium-term (4–10 years), and short-term (1–3 years), facilitating understanding of sustained impact and objective achievement timeframes. Policy Function (X₃) considers population health support promotion, normative standards, systemic coherence, and service optimization to determine whether policies effectively stimulate or constrain stakeholder behavior.

Policy Target Populations (X₄), identified through text mining and social network analysis, reveal that China’s population health policies have established a service system centered on elderly populations, maternal and child groups, migrant populations, and individuals with disabilities. For elderly populations, policies since 2001 have continuously strengthened health security through urban and rural elderly care systems and community service networks. In maternal and child health, the 2010 pre-pregnancy eugenics program and subsequent full-cycle service support following fertility policy adjustments aim to reduce maternal and infant mortality while improving birth population quality. Migrant population management has evolved from the 2009 “apartment management service” model to ensure equal access to basic public health services, guaranteeing equitable health rights. Health security for individuals with disabilities entered the legal framework in 2017, with regulations promoting social integration through prevention, rehabilitation, and barrier-free environment construction. Additionally, policies incorporate impoverished populations into the health security framework, though the four aforementioned groups remain core policy targets.

Policy Evaluation (X₅) conducts in-depth analysis from three perspectives: adequacy of policy foundations, clarity of objectives, and scientific rigor of plans, ensuring rationality and effectiveness of China’s population health policies. Policy Objectives (X₆), based on text mining and social network results, evaluate whether policies aim to improve population quality, optimize elderly service systems, enhance migrant population health management, and ensure basic medical services for individuals with disabilities. Policy Hierarchy (X₇) clarifies applicable targets and tiers, including action plans, guiding outlines, implementation standards, and notices or opinions. Policy Thematic Content (X₈), derived from text mining and social network analysis, examines focal points and development directions in health management services, prevention and control, health security, publicity and education, and technological support. Policy Transparency (X₉) ensures information openness and public access rights, evaluating whether policies are publicly disclosed, with scores of 1 assigned for disclosed content and 0 for non-disclosed content.

This comprehensive PMC Index system provides China with a structured, quantitative evaluation tool for formulating, implementing, and assessing population health policies. Through this framework, policymakers and evaluators can comprehensively understand integrated policy effects and impacts while identifying improvement directions, thereby enhancing policy quality and effectiveness to ensure China’s population health policy objectives are effectively achieved, meeting long-term population health development needs. Results and discussion

Selection of evaluation subjects

This study evaluated 12 representative Chinese population health policies issued between 2001 and 2024, encompassing key developmental stages of population health governance. The mean PMC index was 6.25, ranging from 5.59 (P8) to 7.31 (P10). Eight policies achieved “excellent” ratings (P1, P2, P4, P6, P7, P9, P10, P12), while four received “good” ratings (P3, P5, P8, P11), demonstrating strong overall performance. Score distribution exhibited “high-score concentration, low-score dispersion” characteristics with clear differentiation patterns. Systematic analysis of these policies provides comprehensive insights into China’s population health policy evolution and implementation effectiveness, establishing an evidence base for future policy optimization.

These 12 national-level policies were issued by authoritative state departments. Temporally, the policies span critical developmental stages, reflecting evolution from fragmentation toward systematization and from guidance-oriented to standardization-based approaches. The policy portfolio demonstrates high typological diversity, encompassing guiding opinions, implementation plans, action programs, and legal frameworks. Different policy types provide functional complementarity, establishing a multi-tiered governance system.

Multidimensional analysis of PMC index for China’s population health policies

Analysis of evaluation results across the nine primary variables reveals distinct performance patterns:

X₁ (Policy Nature): Policies P1, P2, P6, and P10 achieved perfect scores (1.0), demonstrating high authority in policy form and standardization. The overall strong performance in this dimension indicates most policies maintain robust standardization, though individual policies exhibit variations in formal completeness. X₂ (Policy Timeliness): All policies demonstrated explicit and valid temporal arrangements, with clear implementation periods or renewal mechanisms. This universal strength aligns with established policy evaluation literature emphasizing temporal requirements, representing a shared characteristic across the policy portfolio. X₃ (Policy Function): Significant disparities emerged, with P1, P2, and P10 achieving the highest scores (0.80), indicating comprehensive functional coverage, while P8 scored lowest (0.20), demonstrating minimal functional scope. Most policies ranged between 0.40 and 0.60, revealing substantial variation in functional comprehensiveness across the policy landscape. X₄ (Policy Target): Generally low scores across this dimension indicate limited beneficiary group specification or narrow applicability scope for most policies, reflecting divergent strategic orientations and targeting approaches. X₅ (Policy Evaluation): All policies incorporated evaluation mechanisms or clear indicators, consistent with comprehensive policy evaluation frameworks assessing benefits, efficiency, effectiveness, and value—representing another universal strength. X₆ (Policy Objective): Marked disparities emerged with P1, P2, P3, P11, and P12 achieving highest scores (0.60), while P7 scored 0.00 (objectives absent) and P10 scored 0.20. Other policies clustered around 0.40. This variation reveals differential clarity in objective articulation: some policies provide comprehensive objective descriptions while others, particularly P7 and P10, demonstrate weak or absent objective specifications. X₇ (Policy Hierarchy): Score distribution reflects variations in issuing authority and regulatory intensity. Some policies represent top-level design (national regulations or guiding documents), while others operate at lower hierarchical levels (0.25), illustrating the multi-tiered nature of the policy system. X₈ (Policy Thematic Content): P1, P2, and P10 achieved highest scores (0.71), demonstrating comprehensive thematic coverage, while P4 scored lowest (0.29). Remaining policies ranged between 0.43 and 0.57. This variation indicates substantial differences in sub-issue coverage: some policies demonstrate rich content breadth while others maintain narrow thematic focus, reflecting individualized approaches to scope definition. X₉ (Policy Transparency): All policies achieved full scores, demonstrating complete openness and transparency in compliance with government information disclosure requirements. This universal characteristic indicates that public accessibility represents a standardized feature across China’s population health policy framework.

Research conclusions and prospects

This study employs text mining and PMC index analysis to conduct an in-depth, quantitative evaluation of 12 representative Chinese population health policies. By constructing a PMC index model, it provides a comprehensive scientific assessment of these policies. The model incorporates 9 primary variables and 40 corresponding secondary variables, covering critical dimensions such as policy nature, timeliness, function, target audience, evaluation, objectives, hierarchical effectiveness, thematic content, and transparency, aiming to analyze the multifaceted characteristics and potential impacts of China’s health policies.

Through text mining and data analysis, we calculated the PMC index for each Chinese population health policy and evaluated their effectiveness accordingly. In terms of rating, 8 policies were graded as “excellent” (including P1, P2, P4, P6, P7, P9, P10, P12), while 4 were rated as “good” (P3, P5, P8, P11), indicating that most policies perform exceptionally well. The successful implementation of these policies has profoundly positive implications for China’s population health policy framework.

Overall, China’s population health policies demonstrate strong effectiveness and implementation potential under the PMC model evaluation. These assessment results enable policymakers and administrators to better understand policy strengths and weaknesses, thereby providing a basis for continuous improvement in policy formulation and execution to ensure these policies effectively meet the long-term needs of China’s population health development.

The PMC index model evaluation reveals: among the 12 policies assessed, 8 were rated as “excellent” (with PMC indices ranging 6.00–7.31.00.31) and 4 as “good” (PMC indices 5.59–5.78), indicating generally high quality across China’s population health policies, though with discernible variations. Across various dimensions, most policies perform consistently well in terms of timeliness, evaluation mechanisms, and transparency, while showing significant differences in dimensions such as policy function, target groups, and policy objectives. Specifically, Policy P10 (“Notice of the National Health Commission on Implementing the Decision of the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and the State Council on Optimizing Fertility Policies to Promote Long-term Balanced Population Development”) demonstrates the best overall performance, scoring high in almost all dimensions, with only slight deficiencies in objective articulation. Policy P8 (“Regulations on Disability Prevention and Rehabilitation of Persons with Disabilities”) has the lowest overall score, primarily due to unclear policy functions and target group definitions.

These findings not only reveal the general characteristics and development trends of China’s population health policies but also identify directions and priorities for policy improvement. Based on the evaluation results, this study proposes specific optimization recommendations for different policies, providing a scientific basis for refining China’s population health policies.

A key limitation of this study is the lack of external validation of PMC scores against policy implementation outcomes or expert assessments. The PMC index evaluates policy design quality rather than implementation effectiveness. Future research should: (1) Correlate PMC scores with implementation indicators (e.g., health outcome improvements, service coverage rates); (2) Conduct Delphi studies with policy experts to validate the scoring framework; (3) Compare PMC assessments with other evaluation methods such as policy impact assessments or cost-effectiveness analyses. Such validation would strengthen the PMC model’s utility for policy decision-making.A limitation of this study is that policy coding was conducted by a single research team without independent validation. To address this, we implemented a consensus-based approach where three researchers jointly reviewed and discussed each policy’s scoring, reaching agreement through deliberation. We acknowledge that formal inter-coder reliability testing (e.g., calculating Cohen’s kappa or Krippendorff’s alpha) would strengthen the methodology. Future research should employ multiple independent coders with reliability testing.

In summary, by constructing a PMC index model, this study conducts a systematic and multi-dimensional quantitative evaluation of China’s population health policies, addressing gaps in existing research and offering new insights and methods for policy optimization. The research outcomes hold significant theoretical and practical value for advancing the scientific, precise, and effective development of China’s population health policies, thereby enhancing overall public health.

Policy implications and recommendations

Our PMC Index evaluation yields actionable insights for optimizing China’s population health policy framework across four dimensions: Dimension 1: Functional Scope Enhancement. Our analysis reveals significant variation in policy functional comprehensiveness (X₃ scores: 0.20–0.80), with lower-performing policies exhibiting narrow functional focus. Recommendation: Policymakers should adopt a “four-function integration” model encompassing: (1) encouragement/support mechanisms (financial incentives, service subsidies); (2) normative guidance (standards, protocols); (3) systemic constraints (accountability, monitoring); and (4) service optimization (delivery model innovation). Policy P8 (Disability Regulations, score = 0.20) exemplifies functional deficiency—while establishing legal frameworks, it lacks implementation incentives and service delivery mechanisms. Concrete action: Revise P8 to incorporate rehabilitation service subsidies, provider quality standards, and local government performance indicators.

Dimension 2: Target Population Specificity. Low X₄ scores (0.00–0.60.00.60) indicate insufficient target population specification in several policies, potentially hindering implementation precision. Recommendation: All population health policies should explicitly define primary and secondary beneficiary groups using demographic, socioeconomic, and health status criteria. Policy P8 scored 0.00 due to generic references to “persons with disabilities” without differentiation by disability type, severity, or age group. Concrete action: Disaggregate target populations (e.g., “elderly with mobility impairments,” “children with developmental disabilities”) and tailor interventions accordingly. This specificity enables local governments to allocate resources effectively and measure coverage rates accurately.

Dimension 3: Objective Clarity and Measurability. Variable X₆ performance (0.00–0.60.00.60) reveals that even high-ranking policies (e.g., P10, score = 0.20) sometimes lack clear, measurable objectives. Recommendation: Adopt SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) objective formulation. Instead of vague goals like “improve population quality” (P7), specify: “reduce infant mortality rate to ≤ 5 per 1,000 live births by 2025; increase elderly health examination coverage to ≥ 70% by 2025.” Concrete action: Require all new policies to include quantitative targets with baseline values, interim milestones, and accountability mechanisms.

Dimension 4: Systemic Integration. Text mining revealed thematic clustering but also identified weak linkages between domains (e.g., sparse connections between “infectious disease” and “elderly population” nodes). Recommendation: Strengthen cross-domain policy coordination. Establish an integrated population health information platform (as called for in P4) connecting elderly care, maternal-child health, migrant health, and disability services. Concrete action: Mandate data interoperability standards and develop unified electronic health records accessible across service systems, enabling comprehensive individual health management across the life course.

Study limitations and future research directions

Our PMC Index assesses policy design quality (consistency, comprehensiveness, clarity) rather than implementation effectiveness or health outcomes. High PMC scores indicate well-designed policies but do not guarantee successful implementation or population health improvements. Future research: Conduct longitudinal studies correlating PMC scores with implementation indicators (e.g., budget allocation rates, service utilization) and health outcomes (e.g., mortality reductions, disease prevalence changes). Validate whether design quality predicts real-world effectiveness.

Our PMC model employs binary coding (0/1) for secondary variables to enhance objectivity and inter-rater reliability. However, this approach may not fully capture policy nuances—for example, a policy with “partial” target population specification receives the same score (0) as one with no specification. Future research: Explore weighted or multi-level scoring systems (e.g., 0/0.5/1.0 scales) while maintaining methodological rigor through formal inter-coder reliability testing (Cohen’s kappa, Krippendorff’s alpha). Alternatively, sensitivity analyses could assess how different scoring thresholds affect policy rankings.

Policy coding was conducted by our research team without independent validation by external experts or practitioners. While we employed consensus-based scoring (three researchers jointly reviewed each policy), formal inter-coder reliability testing would strengthen methodological rigor. Future research: Conduct Delphi studies with policy experts, health administrators, and community representatives to validate: (a) indicator system appropriateness; (b) scoring accuracy; (c) policy recommendations’ feasibility.

Our evaluation provides a cross-sectional snapshot (policies as of 2024) without capturing implementation evolution or policy adjustments over time. Population health governance is inherently dynamic—policies are revised, implementation contexts change, and new challenges emerge (e.g., COVID-19 pandemic impacts). Future research: Develop longitudinal PMC tracking systems monitoring policy revisions and implementation progress. Conduct quasi-experimental studies comparing health outcomes in regions with different policy implementation intensities.

Our study analyzes national policies, but China’s vast geographic, economic, and demographic diversity means implementation varies substantially across provinces and localities. A “one-size-fits-all” national policy may be highly effective in urban coastal regions but poorly suited to rural western areas. Future research: Extend PMC evaluation to provincial-level implementation plans and local adaptation strategies. Conduct comparative case studies examining how similar national policies generate different outcomes across diverse local contexts.

While our text mining approach enables data-driven indicator derivation, it relies on keyword co-occurrence and may miss implicit policy meanings or contextual nuances detectable through interpretive analysis. Additionally, our analysis focused on formal policy documents, excluding implementation guidelines, budget allocations, and stakeholder communications that influence actual policy operation. Future research: Integrate mixed methods combining computational text analysis with qualitative interviews and ethnographic observation of policy implementation processes.
