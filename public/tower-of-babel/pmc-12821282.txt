Introduction

The Gender-based violence (GBV) is defined as violence directed specifically against a man or a woman because of his sex or which affects women or men disproportionately. It can take different forms: verbal, physical, sexual, psychological, and economic within the framework of the key population and it recognizes no boundaries of age, race, culture, wealth, geographical location. GBV is based on an unequal balance of power (inequality between man and woman, inequality of physical strength, financial strength, and position of authority) [1–3].

Although the Central African Republic (CAR) has adopted a National Strategy document to fight GBV, the number of cases of sexual violence remains high year after year [4]. Statistics from the United Nations GBV Information Management System (GBVIMS) in CAR, indicate that in 2020, 9,216 cases of GBV were collected at the service level, including 24% of sexual violence and this in only one third of the country’s sub-prefectures [5].

The CAR has been going through a complex and profound crisis for several years with serious humanitarian and protection consequences. These politico-military conflicts had dramatic consequences on the populations, causing massive displacement of populations and destruction of the state apparatus, basic social services [6]. This situation further exposes the most vulnerable groups (women and girls) to more violence including rape and sexual assault. Female Sex workers (FSW) are among the populations most vulnerable to GBV [7, 8]. Data on violence against sex workers are scarce in CAR, although it is obvious that GBV threatens the health and dignity of sex workers [9]. They remain surrounded by a culture of silence. It is in this context that our study sets out to determine the extent and types of GBV against sex workers in the Central African Republic, to offer targeted interventions for their prevention and adequate cares.

Methods

Setting of the study

Our study took place in the city of Berberati, capital of the prefecture of Mambéré Kadéï. Due to its population, Berberati is the third largest city in the country. The reasoned choice of the city of Berberati is explained by the importance of economic activities (mining, forestry, traffic density), the concentration of socio-economic activities and the major roads that connect the borders with the Republic of Congo and the Republic of Cameroun (Fig. 1).

Type and period of study

This was a cross-sectional descriptive and analytical study. Survey data was collected between 22nd January and 30th march 2022 from FSW as part of a Behavioral and Combined Biology Surveillance (IBBS) study in Berberati.

Study population

A sex worker was defined as any woman who receives money or goods in exchange for sexual services, on a regular or occasional basis, and who consciously or not defines this activity as the main source of income or survival [9]. Were included in the study, all women aged 15 and over who self-identified as sex workers and who gave their consent to participate in the study. All FSW non-consenting are excluded.

Sampling and data collection methods

All FSW meeting the inclusion criteria were recruited. Before the start of the investigation, contacts were made with the administrative and police authorities of the city to explain the merits of the investigation. This study is part of a national survey on mapping key populations in the Central African Republic. Respondent Driven Sampling (RDS) was used to recruit participants. RDS is a peer recruitment method often used to create a sample from seeds [10, 11]. These first participants (10, identified as cluster leader), called “seeds” were then invited to recruit other pairs, from a series of reference sampling chains. Each participant received a number of recruitment coupons to distribute to potential participants within their own network (Fig. 2). Using this method, we recruited 256 FSWs, as part of an exhaustive recruitment process. A weighting was applied using rural and urban residences. A structured and pre-tested questionnaire was used to collect data. The questionnaire is administered in interviews conducted in Sango, the national language, by Master’s students in socio-anthropology who have been recruited and trained for this purpose.

Definition of concepts

In this work, we have considered five (5) types of expression of gender-based violence [12, 13].

Verbal abuse

Consists of controlling, humiliating and intimidating a person through verbal threats. They include, among others, insults (bitch, whore, crazy, imbecile, brothel, etc.); the use of coarse and offensive language, shouting, criticism. Verbal abuse can result in prohibitions, blackmail, orders.

Physical violence

These include gestures such as slapping, pushing, biting, kicking, shaking arms, pulling hair, rapping, throwing objects, choking, kidnapping, attacking with a weapon (gun, knife), making an attempted murder, holding a person against their will. They are a criminal offence.

Sexual violence

It is defined as any act of a sexual nature performed without the consent of the other. They include rape (individual or collective), fondling, and sexual harassment, coercion to adopt dangerous, degrading or hurtful sexual behavior (anal or oral penetration with a penis or other unwanted object. The person feels ridiculed or denigrated sexually. Sexual violence is characterized by the absence of the power of free will.

Psychological violence

This translates into attitudes and behaviors that aim to denigrate a person in such a way as to keep them in a position of inferiority. They lead the abused person to believe and feel that he is a worthless and incapable person. The violent person can use psychological violence through prolonged silences, excessive demands, blackmail, humiliations, reproaches, sarcasm and orders. Psychological abuse is also a criminal offence.

Economic violence

They are used to prevent a person from gaining financial autonomy or also to seize the salary of the other. They can be expressed in various forms: From theft without the knowledge of the person to the refusal of payment through bargaining.

Analysis and statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using Epi info version 7 software. Categorical variables were reported as numbers (percentages) and continuous variables as medians (interquartile range). The outcome of interest was FSW victims of VGB. Risk factors for VGB were assessed using logistic regression to determine unadjusted odds ratios (ORs). The variables from simple regression with p < 20% are included in multiple logistic regression, taking into account potential confounding factors to produce a final model with p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study was formally approved on by the Scientific Ethical Committee Faculty of Health Sciences of Bangui (“Comité Scientifique Chargé de la Validation des Protocoles d’Etudes et des Résultats”/”CSCVPER”) (agreement UB/FACSS/CSCVPER). All participants agreed to a written and signed consent form. Parental authorization was required for FSWs between the ages of 15 and 19. Subsequently, FSWs were offered support from the Civil Protection Service.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

During this study period, 256 sex workers were recruited. The average age of participants in our series was 26 years (± 6). The most represented age group was that of 25 years and over. However, young adolescent girls in the age group under 20 were twice as affected by GBV. The data recorded on GBV in our series show that nearly 38.8% of FSW had sexual intercourse under physical or psychological coercion, more than one in two FSW (56.6%) had been insulted in public and similarly 62.8% said they was physically injured at least once in the past month by a paying sexual partner (customer). Nearly half of the participants consume alcohol every day and use psychoactive substances at least three times a week or live alone (Table 1).

Factors associated with GBV

In univariate analysis, the age group below 24; regular alcohol consumption; having a secondary activity; the notion of living alone and having a client “male in uniform” was identified as factors associated with GBV [OR = 2.15; 95% CI (1.24–3.71), p = 0.003]; [OR = 2.60; 95% CI (1.47–4.61), p < 0.001]; [OR = 2.06, 95% CI (1.11–3.79), p = 0.011]; [OR = 2.35; 95% CI (1.35–4.07), p = 0.001] and [OR = 14.40, 95% CI (7.66–27.06), p < 0.001] respectively. In multivariate analysis by multivariate logistic regression, the main determinants associated with sexual violence among FSW were: the age group below 24 years [aOR = 2.69; 95%CI (1.93–3.28); p = 0.001]; regular alcohol consumption [aOR = 2.13; 95%CI (1.94–3.08); p = 0.002]; FSW as main source income: [aOR = 1.36; 95%CI (1.18–5.15); p = 0.041] and having a military or police partner [aOR = 3.57; 95%CI (2.81–5.23); p = 0.001] (Table 3).

Discussion

Limitations of study

The cross-cutting nature of the study is a limitation. FSWs do not have a formal organization for their recognition but do have clearly identified leaders. The sampling method (RDS) may have limitations because, although used by some authors, it did not allow us to obtain the majority of FSWs. Finally, the study may be subject to information bias due to the stigma that FSWs face in our communities.

Baseline characteristics of FSWs

We collected data from 256 female sex workers (FSWs). The average age of the participants in our series was 26 years. This result was similar to the findings of other studies carried out on female victims of GBV, notably in Bangui by Longo et al. [9] and the baseline survey on GBV in Rwanda [14], which reported an average age of 23 and 24 respectively. Adolescent girls and young women under 24 were twice as exposed to sexual violence. Literature on sex workers in sub-Saharan Africa in general and in the Central African Republic in particular reveals that a significant number of young women are involved in commercial sex transactions before they reach 20 years of age. These young women who sell sexual services are said to be at high risk of experiencing GBV because of their involvement in drug and alcohol use, including exposure to pressure from older sexual partners [15–18].

The majority of participants had a low level of education. Our results corroborate those of several authors who reported respectively that 67.8% and 53.33% of FSWs had a low level of education [9, 19]. Literature data indicate that the level of schooling in CAR remains very low for girls (67.8%) compared with 82.2% for boys. There is also a high drop-out rate among girls at the end of primary school [9, 20]. Regarding the living arrangements of FSWs in our s, the majority of FSWs lived alone and/or with other FSWs (77.80%). Our results corroborate those of Bambara in Burkina Faso [19] who reported that more than three quarters of FSW lived alone or with prostitute friends. This could be explained in part by the fact that most of the women involved in sexual transactions feel that they are more financially independent to meet some of their basic needs, particularly housing and subsistence. Consequently, they tend to live alone or in shared accommodation with their peers [21]. Moreover, in a context where sex workers are generally stigmatized and often disowned by their close relatives, women sex workers prefer to live alone or with other sex workers, in order to preserve a certain degree of privacy and to be able to live with their peers. Female sex workers prefer to live alone or with other FSWs, in order to maintain a certain degree of privacy and to practice their trade freely [22].

GBV and income-generating activities among FSW

Our results showed that more than three quarters had at least one other income-generating activity besides sex work. The main activities carried out by the FSWs in our series were odd jobs (hairdressing, bar maids, etc.), street vendors and small businesses. A sociological and anthropological survey carried out in Bangui, the capital of CAR, analyzed the different characteristics and networks of female sex work and proposed a typology of the different categories of FSW [22]. This analysis of the situation provided important new information about sex work network in Bangui. Women’s work is remarkably heterogeneous. In addition to genuine professional FSWs (who identify themselves as such), so-called “clandestine” FSWs have also been identified. So-called “clandestine” FSW have also been identified, i.e. categories of women who carry out other activities, usually small trades and/or informal businesses, but are involved in the sex trade [22]. Analysis of our results showed that FSWs who engaged in another income-generating activity in addition to sex work were more exposed to the risk of GBV than FSWs who were professionals. In our context of extreme poverty, where sex work is generally stigmatized and often repressed by the forces of law and order. The practice of other activities in addition to sex work is common [18]. Clandestine prostitution thus enables some women involved in commercial transactions to practice this trade in complete safety [9]. Women who practice prostitution clandestinely are generally overlooked by national control programmes and are more exposed to HIV/AIDS and GBV than professional FSWs. The latter are generally grouped into associations and benefit from numerous protection and capacity-building programmes [8, 22].

Association between VGB and FSW

Age

Adolescent FSWs and young people under 24 years of age were more exposed to VGBs. Neglect, abandonment by families and a history of violence or sexual abuse are common characteristics of young women selling sex in an environment of extreme poverty [18]. Without social support, young women, particularly those under the age of 18, are often forced to engage in commercial sex in order to buy food, accommodation, clothing, transport, alcohol or drugs. In addition, some studies have shown that a high proportion of young women engage in paid sex for economic reasons [18]. Many sex workers are children. Numerous studies show that up to 40% of female prostitutes began working before the age of 18. In studies conducted in India, Nepal, Thailand and Canada, it was found that the young age at which sex work begins increases vulnerability to physical and sexual violence [23–25].

VGB and consumption of alcohol and other psychotropic substances

Our results showed that regular alcohol consumption during sex work was linked to sexual violence. FSW who regularly consumed alcohol were 2 times more exposed to sexual violence. Excessive alcohol consumption can lead to impaired consciousness and altercations in the decision to protect oneself in the face of potential danger. Research by Robillard et al. in Bolivia [26], Chersich et al. in Kenya [27] and REHM et al.. in a systematic review [28] explain that alcohol abuse exposes FSW to the risk of sexual violence. Our results also showed that one of the main determinants associated with sexual violence among FSW was regular alcohol consumption. FSWs who drank alcohol regularly were about three times more likely to engage in forced sexual intercourse than those who drank alcohol periodically. These results corroborate those found by Tounkara et al.. who noted a high frequency of GBV among FSW, particularly those working in bars [29].

Types of violence suffered by FSW

In our study, all the different forms of gender-based violence were found among FSW. In sex work, the associative nature of the different types of GBV was revealed. The prevalence of psychological violence was very high. FSWs who worked outside their home town for fear of being stigmatized were more affected. Tounkara et al. [29] stated that psychological violence among FSW is very common and takes different forms. However, although this type of violence has significant consequences for their mental health, FSW are the least prepared to deal with it. Wilson et al. report that 68% of FSW suffer from symptoms of psychological post-traumatic stress. Psychological violence can escalate to physical and/or sexual violence [30].

More than half of the FSWs (58.9%) in our series reported at least one episode of verbal violence. Similar findings were observed by Tounkara et al. [29] in Benin who reported that verbal abuse was ubiquitous in sex work. In a systematic review, Deering et al. [31] specified that verbal violence was very frequent among FSWs, which is said to be the prerogative of undocumented sex workers. It is often trivialized by the majority of FSWs, despite the potential risk to their mental health.

In our sample, 36.6% of FSW was physically injured by their sexual partners in the 30 days preceding the survey. In a study conducted in Benin, 76% of FSW were victims of repeated physical violence [29]. Foldhazi et al. [32]. in Geneva reported that physical violence for no specific purpose is rare, but it does occur. It occurs in places where the influence of alcohol reduces self-control. Most FSWs were beaten for reasons of jealousy or refusal to pay for sex. For Tounkara et al., the main reasons were refusal to use drugs by FSWs or forcible return of the money by the client once sex had taken place [29]. These high rates of physical violence among FSW are thought to be partly due to the criminalization of sex work. In this environment where brutality is commonplace, FSWs become an easy target for violence [29, 33]. Moreover, physical violence is a consequence of the inequality of power between the sexes, which generally gives men the power to control women [34]. Physical violence against FSW is also tolerated and under-reported to the police, as some law enforcement officers are unfortunately among the perpetrators of violent acts [29, 34].

Association between VGB and men in a uniform

We found that 44.6% of FSWs said they had been sexually harassed in the 30 days preceding the survey, and that the main perpetrators of sexual harassment were the Central African police, military and security forces, followed by other uniformed men Force in the Central African Republic, (p = 0.001). This can be explained by the fact that, unlike in Bangui, where FSWs are dealt with by the police, in Berberati the security patrols are generally carried out by the military and gendarmes. Generally speaking, these institutions, which have very little training in human rights, treat FSWs as delinquents who disturb public order and, as a result, they exert all kinds of physical, psychological and sexual violence [33, 34]. WHO reports that military personnel and police forces are potential perpetrators of violence against FSW. In our context of multi-faceted humanitarian crises with forced population displacements that have lasted for some thirty years, sexual violence against women in general and against FSW in particular is very common in CAR [9, 20]. Available data show that violence against women and FSW is not only frequent, but also socially tolerated [20]. Criminalization and punitive laws against sex work also serve as a cover for this violence. However, acts of violence perpetrated by official state services compromise FSWs’ access to basic health services, justice and police protection [35]. Foldhazi et al. [27] in Geneva note that FSWs report clients’ insistence on unprotected sex, which they perceive as painful pressure, including acts of sabotage perpetrated by clients such as trying to remove or damage condoms. It is interesting to note that some FSW protection organizations are increasingly advocating that a client’s insistence on having unprotected sex with an FSW should be considered GBV and punished as such, which was not the case at the start of the HIV/AIDS epidemic [36].

Conclusion

Our study highlighted the extent of violence in all its forms among sex workers. Regular alcohol consumption is a risk factor for violence. Men in uniform are the main perpetrators of such violence. Laws are therefore needed to protect these groups. Not only do these laws need to be drafted, they also need to be enforced. If FSWs continue to avoid health facilities for fear of being stigmatized, this is likely to hinder the fight against AIDS and STIs in the Central African Republic. Fighting poverty and empowering women in general and FSWs in particular is a bulwark against inequality and AIDS.
