Early functional recovery and clinical risk profiles predict distinct trajectories of post-stroke depression: a one-year longitudinal analysis
Authors: Aiqin Pan, Yan Zeng, Zhun Zhang, Yuanqiu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies often investigated risk factors for post-stroke depression (PSD) at a single timepoint, neglecting its dynamic nature. We aimed to identify distinct trajectories of depressive symptoms over the first-year post-stroke and explore their early predictors.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study at a stroke center in China from 2022 to 2024. Stroke patients with HAMD assessments at 3-, 6-, and 12-months were included. We identified trajectories using group-based trajectory modeling. The optimal number was determined by statistical criteria and interpretability. To explore predictors of trajectory membership, we conducted two separate binary logistic regression analyses, adjusted for covariates, to differentiate subtypes within favorable (Resilient vs. Recovering) and unfavorable (Fluctuating vs. Worsening) outcome clusters. We also conducted a separate analysis to identify predictors for developing any form of post-stroke depression (Recovering, Fluctuating, or Worsening) versus remaining in the Resilient group.FindingsThe analysis comprised 634 participants. We identified four distinct trajectories: Resilient (50.8%, n = 322), Recovering (9.6%, n = 61), Fluctuating (18.8%, n = 119), and Worsening (20.8%, n = 132). Compared to Recovering, Resilient patients were less likely to be smokers (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.93) and have hypertension (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.17–0.92), and had lower nutritional risk. Comparing unfavorable trajectories, smoking (OR = 4.17, 95% CI: 1.75–9.96) and hyperlipidemia (OR = 3.83, 95% CI: 1.97–7.47) predicted the Fluctuating group versus Worsening. Functional improvement pace was also significantly associated. A subsequent analysis of overall risk, conducted on 553 participants with complete data, found that male sex was the only significant independent predictor, and was strongly associated with a reduced likelihood of developing any form of PSD (OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.17–0.73).InterpretationThe course of PSD is heterogeneous and classifiable into four trajectories. Our primary analysis identified male sex as a strong protective factor against any form of PSD. Concurrently, secondary analyses suggest that early modifiable factors, particularly smoking and metabolic status, are significant predictors differentiating unfavorable subtypes. The novel association observed with VTE risk scores should be considered exploratory and requires further validation. These findings highlight the value of using clinically accessible markers for early risk stratification, though underscore the complexity of PSD prediction. Introduction

Stroke represents a major public health challenge globally and is a leading cause of death and long-term disability. The burden of stroke is particularly severe in China, where the standardized incidence among adults is rising annually, and the number of prevalent cases has reached over 17 million[1]. While the resulting physical and cognitive impairments receive considerable clinical attention, one of the most common and consequential neuropsychiatric complications, post-stroke depression (PSD), remains relatively under-addressed [2]. This is a critical oversight, as PSD is not only highly prevalent, affecting up to one-third of survivors[3], but also robustly linked to poorer functional recovery and worse rehabilitation outcomes [4]. A key reason for this clinical gap may be an underappreciation of the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of PSD. Its prevalence is not static, with studies showing significant fluctuations from the acute phase to the chronic phase post-stroke [5]. Therefore, viewing PSD as a simple, uniform condition rather than a complex process with diverse longitudinal courses may obscure its true impact and hinder the development of effective, timely interventions.

To overcome the limitations of single-timepoint assessments, recent research has begun to adopt a longitudinal perspective, employing methods like group-based trajectory modeling to delineate the heterogeneous course of PSD [6, 7]. This approach has consistently revealed that stroke survivors follow diverse developmental pathways. However, a clear consensus on the exact number and nature of these trajectories has yet to emerge from the literature. For instance, a large 10-year follow-up study identified four distinct patterns: no symptoms, low symptoms, a complex profile of moderate symptoms worsening early before improving, and a high-increasing symptom pattern [6]. In contrast, another one-year study also reported four trajectories but with different characterizations, such as stable-low, high-remitting, late-onset, and high-persistent courses [7]. Further illustrating this variability, a shorter-term study delineated just two primary trajectories: a moderate-level decreasing course and a high-level increasing course [8].

While these pioneering studies have advanced our understanding, a more critical knowledge gap lies in the prediction of these trajectories. Existing research has often focused on predictors that are either difficult to modify, such as baseline stroke severity and a history of pre-stroke depression [6], or are not readily available in routine clinical practice, such as specific peripheral inflammatory biomarkers [7]. Therefore, a major limitation in the current literature is the lack of knowledge regarding early, easily obtainable clinical and functional markers that could help clinicians identify patients at high risk for unfavorable depression courses in daily practice.

Given the identified gap in predictors, a primary focus of this study is on the role of functional status, a cornerstone of stroke rehabilitation. It is well-established that greater baseline functional impairment is a key determinant of PSD [9, 10]. However, a static view of functional limitation may be insufficient, as the relationship between functional status and depression is dynamic and often bidirectional [11]. Emerging longitudinal research underscores the importance of the recovery process itself, demonstrating that poorer functional recovery is strongly associated with adverse depressive outcomes [12], while improvements in function are linked to the amelioration of depressive symptoms [13]. This suggests that the pace of early functional improvement during the initial hospitalization, a critical window for recovery, could be a more powerful and dynamic predictor of long-term psychological trajectories. Such early gains may foster a greater sense of self-efficacy and hope, providing crucial positive feedback that shapes a patient’s subsequent mental health course [14, 15]. Beyond functional status, this study also explores the predictive value of comprehensive risk assessments that are readily available in clinical practice but under-investigated in the context of PSD trajectories. Nutritional status, for instance, is a critical yet often overlooked factor. Up to half of stroke survivors may be malnourished upon discharge [16], and poor nutritional status has been linked to depression [17, 18]. Crucially, recent longitudinal evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship, where early malnutrition can predict the development of later PSD, and vice versa [19], highlighting nutrition as a potentially modifiable target. Similarly, while risk scores for venous thromboembolism (VTE) are routinely used, their association with depression is unexplored. Given that a higher VTE risk often reflects greater immobility and a pro-inflammatory state, both of which are established contributors to depression [20], and it is plausible to hypothesize that VTE risk scores could serve as a potential, albeit unexplored, marker for depression risk. Finally, we aim to refine the understanding of traditional vascular risk factors by examining their role in differentiating specific unfavorable trajectories. While the “vascular depression” hypothesis provides a broad framework, it is unclear if factors like smoking or hyperlipidemia have a differential impact on whether a patient develops a chronic, high-symptom course versus a progressively worsening one. Clarifying these specific associations could offer new insights into the mechanisms driving the severity and chronicity of PSD.

Therefore, this study aimed to: [1] identify distinct longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms over the first year following stroke in a Chinese cohort; and [2] determine the predictive value of early, clinically accessible factors, particularly the pace of functional recovery, nutritional risk, and VTE risk, for membership in these trajectories. We hypothesized that distinct, heterogeneous trajectories of post-stroke depression would be identified, and that these early functional and clinical risk profiles would be significant predictors differentiating the course of depression.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a prospective, observational cohort study at the People’s Hospital of Meizhou City, a tertiary-level stroke center. Patient recruitment was carried out from January 2022 to December 2024. A total of 634 patients with acute stroke were ultimately included in the final analysis.

The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: [1] age between 18 and 89 years; [2] a confirmed diagnosis of ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or subarachnoid hemorrhage, consistent with the 2019 Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis of major cerebrovascular diseases; [3] clear consciousness and stable vital signs upon admission; and [4] provision of signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: [1] non-cerebrovascular events; [2] concurrent diagnosis of a malignant tumor; [3] depressive symptoms determined to be the direct physiological consequence of another medical condition (e.g., hypothyroidism, severe vitamin deficiency) or substance use; and [4] a primary diagnosis of an Adjustment Disorder with Depressed Mood, where symptoms were a direct response to a recent, identifiable life stressor other than the stroke itself (e.g., bereavement).

Stroke severity, as assessed by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), was not an eligibility criterion, as the study aimed to include a broad spectrum of patients to enhance the generalizability of the findings. It is important to note that patients with a documented pre-stroke history of major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder were not excluded, as a history of mood disorders is a known risk factor for PSD. The eligibility of each participant was determined by the attending physician through a structured clinical interview, a thorough review of medical records, and, when necessary, consultation with the psychiatry department.

Data collection and measures

A comprehensive set of data was collected at baseline (within 72 h of admission) and during scheduled follow-ups by trained medical staff.

Baseline assessments

Demographic and Clinical Data: Information on age, gender, smoking and alcohol history, and comorbidities (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, diabetes) was extracted from electronic medical records.

Longitudinal assessment of depressive and anxiety symptoms

1) Depressive symptoms were quantitatively measured using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17)[23] (see Supplementary Data). Assessments were performed at five time points: at admission, at discharge, and at 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-stroke. The HAMD-17 score was treated as a continuous variable to reflect the severity of depressive symptoms.

2) Anxiety symptoms were assessed at admission using the 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA-14) [24] (see Supplementary Data).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (Version 26.0). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

1) Descriptive Statistics: Baseline characteristics of the cohort were summarized. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared across trajectory groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%) and were compared using the Chi-square (χ²) test.

2) Trajectory Analysis: To identify distinct longitudinal patterns of depressive symptoms, we applied group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to the HAMD-17 scores collected at 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-stroke. We fitted models with varying numbers of trajectory groups (from two to five) and determined the optimal number based on a combination of statistical criteria, including the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the clinical interpretability of the trajectories, and the requirement that each group comprise at least 5% of the total sample.

3) Predictor Analysis: To identify independent predictors of trajectory membership, we conducted three separate binary logistic regression analyses. First, to explore the heterogeneity within clusters of favorable and unfavorable outcomes, we performed two analyses. The first model aimed to differentiate between the two favorable outcome subtypes by setting the Resilient group as the outcome (coded as 1) and the Recovering group as the reference category (coded as 0). The second model aimed to distinguish between the two unfavorable outcome subtypes by setting the Fluctuating group as the outcome (coded as 1) and the Worsening group as the reference category (coded as 0). Subsequently, to identify risk factors for any form of post-stroke depression, we conducted an additional multivariable logistic regression. This analysis was restricted to a sub-cohort of 553 participants who had complete data for all covariates included in the model, particularly the baseline NIHSS score. This standard method of handling missing data ensures the robustness of the regression analysis. This analysis compared all patients in the depression trajectories (Recovering, Fluctuating, and Worsening) against the Resilient group, which served as the reference. In all multivariable models, we included all baseline variables listed in Table 1 that showed a p-value < 0.10 in the initial univariate analysis, alongside age and gender as clinically important covariates. The full list of variables entered into the regression models was: age, gender, smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, diabetes, nutritional risk, and VTE risk. The results were reported as Odds Ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Meizhou City. Results

Identification of Post-Stroke depression trajectories

A total of 634 patients with acute ischemic stroke were included in the final analysis (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for the detailed recruitment flowchart). Based on the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores collected at 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-stroke, group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was employed to identify distinct long-term trajectories of depressive symptoms.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, four unique trajectories were identified. These were termed: [1] the Resilient group (n = 322, 50.8%), characterized by a consistently low level of depressive symptoms throughout the follow-up period; [2] the Recovering group (n = 61, 9.6%), which started with mild-to-moderate depressive symptoms that progressively declined to a subclinical level; [3] the Fluctuating group (n = 119, 18.8%), which presented with and maintained the highest level of depressive symptoms over the year; and [4] the Worsening group (n = 132, 20.8%), which began with mild symptoms that steadily increased over the 12-month period.

To explore the psychological state in the acute phase, we analyzed the correlation between baseline anxiety (HAMA score) and depression (HAMD score). A significant positive correlation was observed between HAMA and HAMD scores at admission across all four trajectory groups (Fig. 2), indicating a high comorbidity of anxiety and depression shortly after the stroke event.

The first analysis compared the two favorable outcome subtypes, with the Recovering group serving as the reference category (coded as 0) and the Resilient group as the outcome (coded as 1). As shown in Table 2; Fig. 4A, non-smoking (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.93), absence of hypertension (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.17–0.92), and a lower nutritional risk score (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.62–0.86) were significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of being in the Resilient group. Interestingly, a higher VTE risk score at baseline was associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to the Resilient group (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.07–1.69). Furthermore, while not reaching statistical significance, male gender showed a trend towards being associated with a reduced likelihood of membership in the Resilient group (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.25–1.08, p = 0.077).

The second analysis compared the two unfavorable outcome subtypes, with the Worsening group as the reference (coded as 0) and the Fluctuating group as the outcome (coded as 1). This analysis, clearly visualized in Fig. 4B, revealed that smoking (OR = 4.17, 95% CI: 1.75–9.96) and a history of hyperlipidemia (OR = 3.83, 95% CI: 1.97–7.47) were strongly associated with an increased likelihood of following the more severe Fluctuating trajectory. Conversely, the presence of coronary heart disease (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.05–0.76) and diabetes (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.24–0.86) was associated with a reduced likelihood of being in the Fluctuating group versus the Worsening group. A higher VTE risk was also a significant predictor in this model (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.13–1.76).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we reframed the longitudinal course of post-stroke depression by identifying four distinct trajectories, including a large Resilient group. Our analyses yielded two key insights. First, our secondary analyses exploring the heterogeneity between specific pathways suggested that traditional risk factors, such as functional recovery, nutritional status, smoking, and hyperlipidemia, remain important for differentiating between specific trajectory subtypes. For instance, smoking and hyperlipidemia were strongly associated with the more severe Fluctuating trajectory. Second, our primary analysis, which controlled for a comprehensive set of covariates including baseline stroke severity, revealed an unexpected and strong protective association for male sex against the overall risk of developing any form of PSD. These dual findings underscore the complexity of PSD prediction and warrant careful discussion.

A key finding of our study is the profound impact of early functional recovery on the long-term trajectory of PSD. Our results, which show that a greater pace of improvement in ADL during hospitalization predicts a more favorable depression course, align with and extend the existing evidence. The fundamental link between functional status and depression is well-established, with a recent meta-analysis confirming that decreased ADL is a significant contributor to the development of PSD [25]. However, our study contributes to a more dynamic understanding of this relationship. The connection appears to be bidirectional; just as depression can impair the efficiency of functional recovery [26] and predict future functional deterioration [27], our findings demonstrate that the initial trajectory of functional improvement is a critical determinant of subsequent emotional well-being. This is consistent with longitudinal observations showing that functional disability and depression are closely intertwined over time post-stroke [28].

The mechanism underlying this association is likely multifactorial. Beyond the direct relief from physical burden, early functional gains may trigger a virtuous psychosocial cycle. For instance, visible progress in daily activities can bolster a patient’s rehabilitation motivation, a factor strongly linked to better functional outcomes [29]. More importantly, this enhanced autonomous motivation may serve as a psychological buffer; as one study demonstrated, autonomous motivation for daily activities can significantly weaken the negative impact of physical fatigue and pain on a patient’s mood [30]. Therefore, it is plausible that the rapid functional improvement observed in our Recovering group did not directly reduce depression, but rather empowered patients with the motivation to better cope with post-stroke stressors. Clinically, these findings underscore that interventions promoting early functional recovery, such as aerobic exercise or mind-body therapies [31, 32], should be viewed not merely as physical rehabilitation but as a potent form of primary prevention for unfavorable psychological outcomes.

The importance of clinical risk scores in differentiating specific pathways was also clear. Our finding that higher nutritional risk at baseline predicted unfavorable outcomes aligns with a growing body of evidence linking malnutrition to PSD. Poor nutritional status, which is highly prevalent in stroke survivors, has been robustly associated with an increased incidence and slower resolution of depressive symptoms [33]. The underlying mechanisms are likely multifactorial, involving pathways that regulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter availability [33], with deficiencies in specific nutrients like antioxidants or Vitamin D being directly linked to a higher risk of PSD [34, 35].

Furthermore, this study is among the first to suggest that a high VTE risk score may predict long-term depression trajectories. While this link has not been directly explored, we hypothesize that VTE risk scores may function as a composite marker for pro-depressive states. A high score often reflects significant immobility and a systemic inflammatory response. Indeed, elevated inflammatory and thrombotic biomarkers are associated with adverse clinical outcomes in stroke [36], and the “inflammatory hypothesis” of depression is well-supported, particularly in the context of medical illness [37].

Our results also provide a more nuanced view of how traditional vascular risk factors operate. We found that while hyperlipidemia and smoking were both predictors of unfavorable trajectories, they showed the strongest statistical association with distinguishing the most severe, fluctuating course from a progressively worsening one. The link between hyperlipidemia and PSD is corroborated by a meta-analysis [35] and studies on metabolic indices like the TyG index [38]. Mechanistically, high-fat diets have been shown to induce neuroinflammation and depressive-like behaviors in animal models [39]. Similarly, and consistent with recent large-scale predictive models [40], smoking likely exacerbates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, thereby perpetuating a more severe and chronic depressive state. The most striking finding from our primary analysis, however, was the emergence of male sex as the sole independent predictor for the overall risk of PSD in the fully adjusted model. This suggests that, in our cohort, the protective effect of being male was so strong that it overshadowed other factors when considered simultaneously. While research on gender differences in PSD is inconsistent, this strong protective association is notable and requires validation. The borderline-significant protective trends for smoking and hypertension are also counterintuitive and further underscore the complexity of PSD prediction, highlighting a critical area for future research.

This study has several notable strengths. The prospective cohort design allowed us to capture the dynamic course of depressive symptoms and establish temporal precedence for our predictors. Our use of trajectory modeling provided a more nuanced understanding of PSD heterogeneity compared to single-timepoint assessments. Furthermore, the novel analytical strategy of comparing subtypes within both favorable and unfavorable outcome clusters offers more granular clinical insights. Finally, our focus on clinically accessible predictors, such as functional recovery pace and routine risk scores, enhances the potential for translating these findings into practice.

Nevertheless, our study has several significant limitations. Primarily, the observational design prevents any causal interpretation; our results represent associations only. This is compounded by the absence of data on critical confounders known to influence depression, such as antidepressant use, psychotherapy, and social support. Furthermore, our predictive models could not account for some of the strongest known predictors of PSD. The study did not include a systematic collection of pre-stroke depression history. While baseline stroke severity (NIHSS) was included in our primary analysis of overall risk, its impact across the specific trajectory subtypes was not fully explored. Finally, the primary analysis was restricted to a sub-cohort with complete data, which introduces a potential for selection bias and may limit the generalizability of that specific finding. Taken together, these factors require that our results be interpreted with considerable caution.

In conclusion, our study identifies four distinct trajectories of post-stroke depression, highlighting its heterogeneous nature. Our dual-level analysis suggests that while male sex is a key factor in overall resilience, early clinical profiles like functional recovery pace are crucial for differentiating adverse pathways. These findings support a shift towards proactive risk stratification using clinically accessible markers. Looking forward, an important next step will be to validate these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts to ensure their generalizability. Future research should also capitalize on larger samples to specifically explore the predictors of the favorable “Recovering” trajectory. To build more comprehensive predictive models, it would be valuable to incorporate a wider range of variables, such as post-stroke fatigue and specific lesion locations. Finally, the exploratory association we observed with VTE risk highlights a promising avenue for future studies aimed at prospectively validating novel biological markers for post-stroke depression.
