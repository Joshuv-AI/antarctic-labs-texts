Nonidiopathic papillary muscle ventricular arrhythmia ablation using the lattice-tip catheter: A case series

Introduction

Papillary muscle (PM) premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) are challenging sites for catheter ablation. PMs are thick and constantly in motion, making it difficult to achieve stable catheter positioning and create durable lesions, limiting the long-term success of standard radiofrequency (RF) ablation.1

Investigational and alternative energy sources remain constrained by safety and efficacy concerns.2 For instance, pulsed field ablation (PFA) is a nonthermal method that induces myocardial cell death through the process of electroporation.3 Preclinical studies have demonstrated PFA’s ability to create effective lesions in mobile intracavitary structures without damaging nearby chordal or valvular tissue.4,5

The Affera lattice-tip catheter system (Sphere-9, Medtronic, Boston, MA) integrates high-density mapping with delivery of both RF and pulsed field (PF) energy. Although the system has been approved and used for atrial fibrillation ablation, its application in ventricular arrhythmia ablation remains investigational.6 Early multicenter and registry data show high success rates and great promise in the ablation of ventricular substrate with dual-energy technology.7,8 We present 2 cases of PM arrhythmias treated with the lattice-tip catheter system, highlighting the potential of dual-energy ablation in these ventricular substrates.

Case series

The following cases were performed using electroanatomic mapping with the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA) integrated with CartoSound and the SoundStar intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) catheter (Biosense Webster) to define PM anatomy and identify optimal ablation targets. The lattice-tip catheter was used exclusively for energy delivery via its dedicated generator console rather than for electroanatomic mapping. Ablation was performed using the standard atrial software presets, the parameters of which have been previously described in detail.9 The use of the lattice-tip catheter for ventricular arrhythmias was investigational at the time of these procedures, given that the system is Food and Drug Administration approved only for atrial fibrillation and flutter. This off-label application was patient-specific based on the challenging anatomy of the PMs and the failure of the previous conventional ventricular tachycardia (VT) ablation attempts. Intracardiac electrograms from the lattice-tip catheter were used to identify fractionated and abnormal signals at target sites. Ablation lesions were manually annotated as circles on the CARTO map (Biosense Webster) using real-time ICE visualization. A formal institutional review board approval was not required.

Case 1

A 58-year-old male with hypertension, cardiac sarcoidosis on methotrexate, heart failure (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] 39%), and complete heart block with cardiac resynchronization therapy defibrillator was referred 7 months after an initial VT and PVC ablation for antiarrhythmic-refractory VT.

When he was diagnosed as having sarcoidosis, the patient underwent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, which showed late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) in the basal anterior, anteroseptal, and inferoseptal walls, as well as multifocal midmyocardial and subepicardial LGE in the midanterolateral wall. More importantly, LGE was present in both the anterior and posterior PMs, consistent with sarcoid-related fibrotic remodeling. He was treated with corticosteroids and methotrexate. He was initially started on amiodarone, then switched to sotalol after experiencing an episode of ventricular fibrillation that required an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) shock. Despite well-controlled sarcoidosis, he had frequent PVCs and nonsustained VT (NSVT), with subsequent ambulatory monitoring confirming a high PVC burden. He underwent VT and PVC ablation using a conventional irrigated RF catheter, which was complicated by pericarditis and failed to adequately suppress his PVCs. He was eventually referred for repeat ablation because of persistent dyspnea with PVCs on device interrogation and VT episodes that were refractory to antitachycardia pacing and required ICD shocks.

At referral, repeat fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography showed resolution of sarcoid inflammation with normal myocardial perfusion. Laboratory studies were unremarkable. His medications included sotalol, methotrexate, and spironolactone.

Under monitored anesthesia care, which was used to preserve VT inducibility, electroanatomic mapping was performed using the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster) with an ICE catheter. A quadripolar catheter was positioned in the right ventricular apex, and the lattice-tip catheter was advanced through an Agilis large-curl steerable sheath (St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN).

The first targeted PVC demonstrated a right bundle branch block (RBBB) morphology, with a biphasic lead I and R-wave predominance in lead II over lead III, consistent with an anterolateral PM (ALPM) origin. The second PVC showed a similar RBBB morphology with a superior axis and a V3 precordial transition, consistent with a posterior-medial PM origin (Figure 1). Activation and pace mapping confirmed these sites (Figure 2). Catheter stability was maintained via a transseptal approach. RF ablation was delivered at 20–30 W with 15–20 Ω impedance drops for 60–120 seconds. Sequential PF and RF energy applications were used in a lesion-stacking strategy to improve depth and durability. Postablation programmed stimulation confirmed noninducibility for VT. The patient recovered uneventfully. Overnight telemetry showed no recurrence of the clinical PVC morphologies, and he was discharged the next day. Case 2

A 52-year-old female with recurrent sustained monomorphic VT despite previous RF ablations of the ALPM was referred for antiarrhythmic-refractory VT. She was initially diagnosed as having VT 2 years earlier and underwent 2 ablations and a dual-chamber ICD placement within the year. After a period of stability, she presented with recurrent symptomatic VT requiring frequent failed antitachycardia pacing therapies, followed by 6 appropriate ICD shocks. On this admission, there was no evidence of acute ischemia or clinical heart failure.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated an LVEF of 30%–35% without significant valvulopathy. Coronary computed tomography angiography confirmed normal coronary arteries without coronary artery calcium. Baseline laboratories were unremarkable. Her home medications included metoprolol and flecainide (started after multidisciplinary discussion as a bridge to planned ablation after NSVT refractory to sotalol and amiodarone, acknowledging its limitations in structural heart disease).

Under monitored anesthesia care, electroanatomic mapping was performed using the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster). At baseline, the patient had frequent PVCs and hemodynamically tolerated VT with RBBB morphology, lead III greater than II, and a V5 precordial transition (cycle length 498 ms), indicating an origin from the ALPM (Figure 3). After induction of anesthesia, the VT was suppressed, but morphologically identical PVCs persisted. Mapping with a Thermocool SmartTouch SF catheter (Biosense Webster) at the PM base showed 18-ms pre-QRS activation and a nearly perfect pace-map match. The Thermocool SmartTouch SF was then exchanged for the Sphere-9 lattice-tip catheter for ablation. RF ablation was delivered at the PM tip, followed by 4 PFA lesions, achieving suppression. Additional lesions at the base of the PM further suppressed the remaining PVCs. Repeat echocardiogram 2 days after ablation demonstrated a significant improvement in LVEF to 51%, consistent with myocardial stunning from previous ICD shocks as well as a contributory PVC-mediated component. At 11 days after ablation, she had 1 episode of NSVT, prompting resumption of metoprolol. ICD interrogation at 2-month follow-up confirmed no sustained VT requiring therapy. In this case series, the lattice-tip dual-energy system effectively eliminated PM arrhythmias without complications. The system’s dual-energy capability and mechanical stability seem to overcome the mechanical and anatomic limitations of traditional RF ablation (Supplemental Videos A and B).

Discussion

These cases demonstrate successful treatment of PM arrhythmias with the lattice-tip catheter after multiple previous failed attempts with conventional RF ablation. The lattice-tip design provided a significant advantage by maintaining stable, broad catheter-tissue contact and addressing the mechanical challenges of PM ablation.

The lattice-tip catheter maintained consistent contact throughout the cardiac cycle, allowing reliable energy delivery to highly mobile PMs. This likely contributed to the durable suppression, as evidenced by previous studies demonstrating that catheter-tissue contact is a key determinant of success.8,10 Although PFA can create lesions with minimal contact, optimal lesions require proximity owing to stronger electric-field penetration.10 Successful PM PVC ablation has been achieved with contact forces as low as 3 g, emphasizing that stability and engagement, rather than high force, are crucial for success.9 These mechanical advantages facilitated the results observed in our 2 cases.

The dual-energy lattice-tip system remains investigational in the United States for the treatment of ventricular arrhythmias. A large multicenter trial, AFFERA Ventricular Arrhythmia Ablation Registry, reported an 84% acute success rate for ventricular arrhythmia ablation and 72% freedom from recurrence at a mean follow-up of 5.6 ± 3.7 months.8 Despite these promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. The ideal sequence of energy delivery for an optimal lesion-stacking strategy to achieve durable suppression of ventricular arrhythmia is unclear. Although the follow-up duration of the current study is consistent with previous research, the 3-month follow-up period may be insufficient to detect late recurrences. This is particularly relevant because the long-term durability of PFA on ventricular tissue remains uncertain. Similar to early feasibility studies, we use stacking PF and RF lesions; the discrete contributions of each modality cannot be determined.8 Thus, although early findings support efficacy, further research is required to address these limitations and establish standardized treatment protocols.

When delivering PF energy near the PMs, the proximity of the coronary arteries and subvalvular structures must be considered. In both cases, we used ICE to visualize the catheter position. In addition, nitroglycerin was available per institutional protocol for the treatment of coronary vasospasm during delivery of PFA lesions. No clinical or hemodynamic evidence of coronary vasospasm was observed.

Sequential RF and PFA application with the lattice-tip catheter has been shown to enable safe lesion stacking in ventricular arrhythmia ablation.11 The growing body of evidence emphasizes that dual-energy delivery, combined with reliable catheter-tissue contact, is fundamental to achieving effective lesion formation.

The lattice-tip design enabled stable catheter positioning and sustained tissue engagement, resulting in consistent lesion formation despite the dynamic motion of the PMs. Beyond individual cases, these findings suggest that, for patients with complex substrates such as PM arrhythmias, this dual-energy platform could improve procedural efficacy and expand therapeutic options for a broader population of patients with challenging ventricular arrhythmias.

Conclusion

Although the use of PF energy in ventricular ablation remains investigational, these cases demonstrate that the lattice-tip catheter eliminated PM arrhythmias after failed conventional RF ablation. Catheter-tissue contact and stability were fundamental to success, assuring stable energy delivery on these highly mobile structures. These results add to the growing evidence that dual-energy lattice-tip technology may advance the management of complex ventricular substrates and offer a new option for refractory cases.
