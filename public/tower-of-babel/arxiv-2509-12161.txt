A branch group with unsolvable conjugacy problem
Alex Bishop, Eduard Schesler

Abstract
. We prove that every finitely generated residually finite group G G can be embedded in a finitely generated branch group Γ \Gamma such that two elements in G G are conjugate in G G if and only if they are conjugate in Γ \Gamma . As an application we construct a finitely generated branch group with solvable word problem and unsolvable conjugacy problem and thereby answer a question of Bartholdi, Grigorchuk, and Šuniḱ.

Primary 20F10; Secondary 20E08

1.
Introduction

Since their formulation by Dehn
[
8
]
in 1911, the word problem, the conjugacy problem, and the isomorphism problem for groups remained three fundamental decision problems in group theory.
Still, the question of decidability of these problems within a class of groups
\mathcal{C}
is often among the first questions that arises in the study of such a class
\mathcal{C}
.
Among the classes of groups whose algorithmic problems received particularly great interest are those that themselves are defined via algorithmic concepts.
One instance of this is the class of (finite state, synchronous) automata groups, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
11
]
for some background on these groups.

Although the word problem is well-known to be solvable for finitely generated automata groups, the picture regarding the solvability of the conjugacy problem in automata groups is far from complete.
First steps in this direction were achieved by Wilson and Zalesskii
[
23
]
who showed that for odd primes
p
the conjugacy problem is solvable in certain
p
-groups that were introduced by Grigorchuk
[
13
]
and Gupta and Sidki
[
14
]
.
This result was complemented by Rozhkov
[
20
]
and Leonov
[
17
]
who showed that the conjugacy problem is solvable for Grigorchuk’s
2
-group, respectively for all members of the family of
2
-groups
G_{\omega}
from
[
9
]
whose word problem is solvable.
In
[
12
]
Grigorchuk and Wilson unified and extended the latter results to more general classes of branch groups, including most of the classical examples of automata groups.

Another milestone in the understanding of the solvability of the conjugacy problem in automata groups and branch groups was provided by I. Bondarenko, N. V. Bondarenko, Sidki, and Zapata
[
7
]
, who showed that the conjugacy problem is solvable in every finitely generated group of bounded automata.
On the other hand, Šunić and Ventura
[
22
]
gave concrete examples of exponential activity automata groups with unsolvable conjugacy problem.
As the above results suggest, many of the most-studied branch groups arise as (bounded) automata groups.
However, unlike the class of automata groups, the class of finitely generated branch groups contains uncountably many isomorphism types so that there exist finitely generated branch groups with unsolvable word problem.
It is therefore a natural question, raised by Bartholdi, Grigorchuk, and Šuniḱ
[
4
, Question 5]
, whether the word problem is the only obstruction for the solvability of the conjugacy problem in the class of finitely generated branch groups.
The following result provides a negative answer to this question in the form of the following theorem.

Theorem A
.

There exist finitely generated branch groups with solvable word problem and unsolvable conjugacy problem.

As usual with results on the unsolvability of algorithmic problems, we will deduce Theorem
A
from the unsolvability of a related problem.
In our case, this related problem is given by the conjugacy problem for finitely generated residually finite groups.
By a result of Miller
[
18
, Theorem 9 on p. 31]
the latter problem is unsolvable even if we assume that the given residually finite group has solvable word problem.
In fact Miller constructs a finitely presented residually finite group
H
with unsolvable conjugacy problem.
Recall that the relevance of finite presentability for the word problem of a residually finite group
G
is that it enables us to decide whether a given assignment
f\colon S\rightarrow Q
from a finite generating set
S
of
G
to a finite group
Q
extends to a homomorphism
\widetilde{f}\colon G\rightarrow Q
.
This allows us to detect the non-triviality of an element
g\in G
by enumerating all homomorphisms from
G
to every symmetric group
\Sym(n)
and applying them to
g
.
The latter procedure is a particular case of an algorithm that takes a word
w
on the generators of
G
as an input and, if
w\neq_{G}e
, produces a morphism
\varphi\colon G\rightarrow Q
to a finite group
Q
that satisfies
\varphi(w)\neq_{Q}e
.
Following
[
19
]
, we will say that a group
G
is
effectively residually finite
if it admits such an algorithm, see Definition
2.1
for a more precise definition.
If in addition
G
is recursively presented it can be easily deduced that
G
has a solvable word problem.

In order to take advantage of Miller’s group
H
, we construct a finitely generated branch group
\Gamma
and an embedding
\iota\colon H\rightarrow\Gamma
for which two elements
g,h\in H
are conjugate in
H
if and only if
\iota(g)
and
\iota(h)
are conjugate in
\Gamma
.
Such an embedding is known as a Frattini embedding and guarantees that the unsolvability of the conjugacy problem in
H
implies the unsolvability of the conjugacy problem
\Gamma
.
Regarding this, we obtain Theorem
A
as a consequence of the following result.

Theorem B
.

For every finitely generated residually finite group
G
, there exists a finitely generated branch group
\Gamma
and a Frattini embedding
\iota\colon G\rightarrow\Gamma
.
Moreover, if
G
is recursively presented and effectively residually finite, then
\Gamma
can be chosen to be recursively presented and effectively residually finite as well.

As pointed out by Rauzy
[
19
, Theorem 2]
, there exist finitely generated residually finite groups with solvable word problem that do not embed in finitely generated effectively residually finite groups.
In particular, this tells us that the assumption on
G
in Theorem
B
to be effectively residually finite cannot be relaxed to the assumption of having a solvable word problem while keeping the effective residual finiteness for
\Gamma
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, it could still be true that the following question has an affirmative answer.

Question 1.1
.

Does every finitely generated residually finite group
G
with solvable word problem embed in a finitely generated branch group with solvable word problem?

As a byproduct of Theorem
B
, we note the following direct consequence for the Boone-Higman conjecture, which asserts that a finitely generated group
G
has solvable word problem if and only if
G
embeds into a finitely presented simple group.

Corollary 1.2
.

If the Boone-Higman conjecture is true for the class of effectively residually finite branch groups, then it is true for every finitely generated effectively residually finite group.

In view of Corollary
1.2
it seems interesting and intriguing that it recently became a successful strategy to apply variations of typical properties of branch groups, such as self-similarity, in order to construct finitely presented simple groups that contain groups with those properties, see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
24
]
.

Notation

In this paper, we write
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
for the natural number starting at 1, and we write
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
for the natural numbers including 0.
Suppose
g,h\in G
are two elements of a group
G
, then we define the conjugate
g^{h}\coloneqq h^{-1}gh
.

2.
Effectively residually finite groups

Let
G
be a finitely generated, recursively presented, residually finite group and let
S
be a finite symmetric generating set of
G
.
Given a word
w\in S^{*}
, we write
\overline{w}\in G
to denote the element of
G
that is represented by
w
.
For each word
w\in S^{*}
with
\overline{w}\neq 1
, let
f_{w}\colon G\to Q_{w}
be a homomorphism onto a finite group
Q_{w}
that satisfies
f_{w}(\overline{w})\neq 1
.
The set of words of length at most
n\in\mathbb{N}
over
S
that represent a non-trivial element in
G
will be denoted by

(2.1)

W_{n}\coloneqq\{w\in S^{*}\mid\overline{w}\neq 1,\ |w|_{S}\leqslant n\}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the homomorphism

f_{n}^{\prime}\colon G\rightarrow\prod\limits_{w\in W_{n}}Q_{w},\ \ g\mapsto(f_{w}(g))_{w\in W_{n}},

whose image we denote by
Q_{n}
.
By taking a corestriciton of
f^{\prime}_{n}
, we obtain a function

(2.2)

f_{n}\colon G\to Q_{n}.

Since
G
is residually finite, the sequence
(N_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that consists of the kernels
N_{n}\coloneqq\ker(f_{n})
is a residual chain
in
G
, i.e. it satisfies
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}N_{n}=1
.
Notice then that if
G
is a nontrivial group, then
|Q_{n}|\geqslant 2
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

It is a direct consequence of the definition of
f_{n}
in (
2.2
) that the word length of every non-trivial element in
N_{n}
is bounded from below by
n+1
.
In what follows we work with the assumption that there is a Turing machine that takes
n\in\mathbb{N}
as an input and returns the map
f_{n}
.
The existence of such a Turing machine can be guaranteed (see
Lemma

2.2
) by combining our assumption that
G
is recursively presented with the following definition, which was introduced by Rauzy
[
19
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be the group with finite generating set
S
as chosen at the beginning this section, then
G
is
effectively residually finite
if there exists a Turing machine that takes as input a word
w\in S^{*}
, and proceeds as follows:

•

if
\overline{w}\neq_{G}1
, then the machine outputs a homomorphism
f_{w}\colon G\to Q_{w}
, with the properties described above;

•

otherwise,
\overline{w}=_{G}1
and the Turing machine does not halt.

Notice then that for every word
w\in S^{*}
if
\overline{w}\neq_{G}1
, then effective residual finiteness would enable us to compute a proof of this fact.

2.1.
Output format

In
Definition

2.1
, it makes sense to speak of a Turing machine which outputs
f_{w}\colon G\to Q_{w}
as such a homomorphism can be completely described by a finite amount of data.
In particular, we need only output the values
f_{w}(s)\in Q_{w}
for each generator
s\in S
, where each such element of
Q
can be described as an element of
\Sym(|Q_{w}|)
, for some fixed enumeration of
Q_{w}
.

2.2.
Computable sequences of normal subgroups

From the definition of effective residual finiteness in
Definition

2.1
, we may prove the following result which is used in the proof of our main theorems.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group with finite symmetric generating set
S
.
If
G
is effectively residually finite and recursively presentable, then there exists a Turing machine that takes as input a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and returns as output a homomorphism
f_{n}\colon G\to Q_{n}
as describe in (
2.2
).

Proof.

Since
G
is recursively presented, there exists a Turing machine that takes as input a word
w\in S^{*}
and terminates if and only if
\overline{w}=1
.
By running this machine in parallel with the machine in Definition
2.1
, we obtain a Turing machine
M
that takes as input a word
w\in S^{*}
and proceeds as follows:

•

if
\overline{w}\neq 1
, then the procedure returns a homomorphism
f_{w}\colon G\to Q_{w}
onto a finite group
Q_{w}
with
f_{w}(\overline{w})\neq 1
,

•

if
\overline{w}=1
, then the procedure terminates and outputs ‘
identity
’

Notice here that we intend that the output ‘
identity
’ cannot be confused with the specification of a homomorphism.

Equipped with such a Turing machine
M
, we can build another one that takes as input a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, enumerates the words of length at most
n
over
S
, applies
M
to each of them, and returns the corestriction
f_{n}
of the homomorphism

f_{n}^{\prime}\colon G\rightarrow\prod\limits_{w\in W_{n}}Q_{w},\ \ g\mapsto(f_{w}(g))_{w\in W_{n}}

onto its image.
By construction, this newly constructed Turing machine fulfills the requirements of the Turing machine from the lemma.
∎

We then obtain the following result as an immediate corollary.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group with finite symmetric generating set
S
.
If
G
is effectively residually finite and recursively presentable, then the word problem is solvable for
G
.

Proof.

Suppose we are given a word
w=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}\in S^{*}
.
We may then use the Turing machine in
Lemma

2.2
to find the homomorphism
f_{n}\colon G\to Q_{n}
as in (
2.2
).
We then see that
f_{n}(\overline{w})=f_{n}(\overline{w_{1}})f_{n}(\overline{w_{2}})\cdots f_{n}(\overline{w_{n}})=1
if and only if
\overline{w}=1
in
G
.
Thus, using
f_{n}\colon G\to Q_{n}
, we may decide the word problem for
w
.
∎

3.
Groups acting on rooted trees

Our objective in this section is to develop notation and basic results concerning groups that act on rooted trees, which we use in
Section

4
to construct the group
\Gamma
used in
B
.
For more background on these groups, and in particular for background on branch groups, we refer the reader to
[
4
]
.

3.1.
Rooted trees

Let
X=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite sets, each with cardinality
|X_{n}|\geqslant 2
, which we will think of as alphabets.
By taking finite products over those alphabets we obtain the sets of words
X^{\ell}\coloneqq X_{1}\times X_{2}\times\cdots\times X_{\ell}
of length
\ell
for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
X^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
.
The
spherically homogeneous rooted tree associated to
X
, denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, is the rooted tree with vertex set
X^{\ast}=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{\infty}X^{\ell}
and root vertex
\emptyset
, where two vertices
v,w
are connected by an edge if and only if there is a letter
x\in X_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that either
v=wx
or
w=vx
.
Let
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
denote the group of all automorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
that fix the root. Note that the latter condition implies that the distance of a vertex
v
to the root, which we call the
level
of
v
, is preserved under the action of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
and coincides with the word length
|v|
of
v
.
Thus for every subgroup
G\leqslant\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
and every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have a natural homomorphism
\pi_{\ell}\colon G\rightarrow\Sym(X^{\ell})
.
On the other hand, every permutation
\sigma\in\Sym(X_{1})
gives rise to an automorphism
\widehat{\sigma}
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
by setting
\widehat{\sigma}(xw)=\sigma(x)w
for every
x\in X_{1}
and every
w\in X_{2}\times X_{3}\times\cdots\times X_{k}
with
k\geqslant 2
.
Automorphisms obtained in this way are called
rooted
.
More generally, we call a tree autormorphism
\alpha\in\Aut(T_{X})

finitary
if there is some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and a permutation
\sigma\in\Sym(X^{n})
, such that
\alpha(vw)=\sigma(v)w
for every
v\in X^{n}
and every
w\in X_{n+1}\times\ldots\times X_{m}
with
m\geqslant n
.
In this case we will write
\alpha=\widehat{\sigma}
.
A natural counterpart to finitary automophisms are those automorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
that are supported on a subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
whose vertex set consists of those words in
X^{\ast}
that start with a given word
v\in X^{\ast}
.
Note that this
subtree is canonically isomorphic to the rooted tree
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
that is associated to the subsequence
(X_{\ell+1},X_{\ell+2},\ldots)
of
X
, where
\ell=|v|
.
Using the tree
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
, we can now introduce the following notation for the subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
that we have just described.

Notation 3.1
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and each
v\in X^{\ell}
we write
v\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
to denote the subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
whose vertex set consists of those words in
X^{\ast}
that start with
v
.

Equipped these trees, we can let the direct sum
\bigoplus_{v\in X^{\ell}}\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
act on
\mathcal{T}_{X}
via
(\alpha_{v})_{v}(uw)=u\alpha_{u}(w)
for every
u\in X^{\ell}
and
w\in\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
.
By combining the latter type of tree automorphism with the finitary ones, we can decompose each tree automorphism
\alpha\in\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
by writing

\alpha=\widehat{\pi_{\ell}(\alpha)}\cdot(\alpha_{v})_{v\in X^{\ell}},

where
\alpha_{v}\in\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
is the unique automorphism that satisfies

\alpha(vw)=\alpha(v)\alpha_{v}(w)

for every
w\in\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
.
In fact, for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it can be easily seen that the map

(3.1)

\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})\rightarrow\widehat{\pi_{\ell}(\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}))}\ltimes\bigoplus\limits_{v\in X^{\ell}}\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}),\ \alpha\mapsto\widehat{\pi_{\ell}(\alpha)}\cdot(\alpha_{v})_{v\in X^{\ell}}

defines an isomorphism, which is referred to as the
permutational wreath decomposition of level
\ell
of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
.
For each
v\in X^{\ell}
, the automorphism
\alpha_{v}
in (
3.1
) is called the
section
of
\alpha
at
v
.
Note that the section of the product
\alpha\beta
of two automorphisms
\alpha,\beta\in\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
at
v
is given by the formula

(3.2)

(\alpha\beta)_{v}=\alpha_{\beta(v)}\beta_{v},

which we will extensively use in what follows.
The following notation is quite useful for working with sections.

Notation 3.2
.

Let
\ell,d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Given a vertex
w\in\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
of level
d
and an element
\gamma\in\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell+d]})
, we write
w\gamma\in\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
to denote the unique element with support in
w\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell+d]}
that is given by
w\gamma(wv)=w\gamma(v)
for every
v\in\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell+d]}
.
More generally, we write
wH\coloneqq\{\,wh\mid h\in H\,\}\subseteq\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
for every subset
H\subseteq\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell+d]})
.

3.2.
Branch groups

Let us now fix a subgroup
G
of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
.
We say that
G
acts
spherically transitively
on
\mathcal{T}_{X}
if the action of
G
on
X^{\ell}
via
\pi_{\ell}
is transitive for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The
level
\ell
stabilizer subgroup
in
G
is defined by

\St_{G}(\ell)\coloneqq\bigcap\limits_{v\in X^{\ell}}\St_{G}(v),

where
\St_{G}(v)
denotes the stabilizer of
v
in
G
.
The
rigid stabilizer
of
v\in X^{\ell}
in
G
is the subgroup
\RiSt_{G}(v)
of
\St_{G}(v)
that consists of those elements in
G
that fix all vertices in
\mathcal{T}_{X}
that are not contained in
v\,\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
.
The subgroup of
\St_{G}(\ell)
that is generated by the groups
\RiSt_{G}(v)
with
v\in X^{\ell}
is called the
rigid level
\ell
stabilizer subgroup
in
G
. We denote this subgroup as
\RiSt_{G}(\ell)
.
If
G
acts spherically transitively on
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, and each
\RiSt_{G}(\ell)
is a finite index subgroup of
G
, then
G
is said to be a
branch subgroup
of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X})
.
A group is called a
branch group
if it is isomorphic to a branch subgroup of the automorphism group of some spherically homogeneous rooted tree.

4.
Constructing branch groups

In this section, we fix a finitely generated infinite residually finite group
G
.
Our aim in this section is to construct a finitely generated branch group
\Gamma
such that
G
embeds as a subgroup of
\Gamma
.
This embedding is used to prove
B
.

4.1.
The alphabets

In order to define our branch group
\Gamma
, we must first define the tree on which it acts, which in turn is based on a sequence of alphabets.
To describe this sequence of alphabets, we fix a descending chain
(N_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite index normal subgroups of
G
that is residual, i.e. satisfies
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}N_{n}=\{1_{G}\}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Q_{n}=G/N_{n}
and let
o_{n}
be the trivial element in
Q_{n}
.
Let
\lambda_{n}\colon G\rightarrow\Sym(Q_{n})
denote the action of
G
on
Q_{n}
by left multiplication of cosets.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
x_{n}
,
y_{n}
,
z_{n}
,
p_{n}
,
q_{n}
be pairwise distinct elements which are also distinct from the elements of
Q_{n}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we then define the set

(4.1)

X_{n}\coloneqq Q_{n}\cup\{x_{n},y_{n},z_{n},p_{n},q_{n}\}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define an action
\varphi_{n}
of
G
on the sets
X_{n}
such that

(4.2)

\varphi_{n}\colon G\rightarrow\Alt(X_{n}),\ \ g\mapsto\begin{cases}\lambda_{n}(g)&\text{if }\sgn(\lambda_{n}(g))=1\\
\lambda_{n}(g)\cdot(p_{k}\ q_{k})&\text{if }\sgn(\lambda_{n}(g))=-1\end{cases}

where
\sgn(\lambda_{n}(g))\in\{1,-1\}
denotes the even/odd parity of the
\lambda_{n}(g)\in\Sym(Q_{k})
.
Notice that the automorphisms
\varphi_{n}(g)
stabilize the letters
x_{n},y_{n},z_{n}\in X_{n}
, and that they only permute
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
if doing so would result in an even permutation.

The family of actions
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the first of three ingredients that we require in order to define the group
\Gamma
in Theorem
B
.
To obtain the second ingredient, we consider the alternating group
A\coloneqq\Alt(\{x,y,z,o,p,q\})
and let it act on each of the sets
X_{n}
in the obvious way, that is, we define a homomorphism
\psi_{n}\colon A\rightarrow\Alt(X_{n})
by setting
\psi_{k}(a)(*_{n})=(a(*))_{n}
for each
*\in\{x,y,z,o,p,q\}
, and
\psi_{k}(a)(\omega)=\omega
for each
\omega\in X_{n}\setminus\{x_{n},y_{n},z_{n},o_{n},p_{n},q_{n}\}
.
Notice that since
A
is an alternating group over 6 elements, it is a perfect group, that is, it is equal to its derived subgroup.

For later reference, we prove the following technical lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
G
is effectively residually finite and recursively presentable.
Then the descending chain
(N_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as above, can be chosen such that

(1)

there is a computable sequence of surjective functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, each of the form
f_{n}\colon G\to Q_{n}
, such that
\ker(f_{n})=N_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and each
g\in N_{n}\setminus\{1\}
, the word length of
g
with respect the generating set
S
is bounded from below by
n+1
;
and

(3)

the sequences of automorphisms
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as described above, are computable.

Notice here that when we say that a sequence of functions
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
computable
, we mean that there exists a Turing machine which takes as input a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and provides as output, a description of
h_{n}
as discussed in
Section

2.1
.

Proof.

From
Lemma

2.2
, there exists a computable sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of surjective homomorphisms
f_{n}\colon G\to Q_{n}
such their kernels
N_{n}=\ker(f_{n})
form a descending residual chain of finite-index normal subgroups, that is,

N_{1}\geqslant N_{2}\geqslant N_{3}\geqslant\cdots\qquad\text{with}\qquad\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}N_{n}=\{1\}.

Recall that these functions
f_{n}
are surjective, in particular,
f_{n}(G)=Q_{n}
.
Moreover, from the definition of the subgroups
N_{n}
, we see that every nontrivial element of
N_{n}
has length at least
n+1
.

Since each function
f_{n}
is surjective, we see that each group
Q_{n}
is computable as the finite group generated by
Q_{n}=\left\langle\{f_{n}(s)\mid s\in S\}\right\rangle
where
S
is a finite set.
We then see that the sequence of functions
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as defined at the beginning of this section, is computable as each function in the sequence is completely defined by finitely many values, i.e.,

\lambda_{n}(s)\cdot q=f(s)\cdot q

for each
s\in S
and
q\in Q_{n}
.

Since the group structures of
Q_{n}
are computable, we then see that each set

X_{n}=Q_{n}\cup\{x_{n},y_{n},z_{n},p_{n},q_{n}\}

and the elements
o_{n}\in Q_{n}
is also computable.
It then immediately follows that a computable description of the sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as defined in (
4.2
), can be constructed from our computable description of
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Moreover, from our computable description of
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, it immediately follows that the sequence of functions
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is computable.
In particular, the Turing machine for such a function need only transform the action of element of the alternating group
\Alt(\{x,y,z,o,p,q\})
to an action on the subsets of the form
\{x_{n},y_{n},z_{n},o_{n},p_{n},q_{n}\}\subseteq X_{n}
which is known from computation of
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

4.2.
The groups
\Gamma_{\ell}

Equipped with the sequence
X=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we may then define the trees
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, as in
Section

3.1
.
Our aim in the remainder of this section is to define and study a sequence of branch groups
\Gamma_{\ell}\leqslant\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
.
In order to define these groups, we first introduce the group
H\coloneqq G\times A
where the group
A\coloneqq\Alt(\{x,y,z,o,p,q\})
is the same as in
Section

4.1
.
For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we then define an action of
H
on
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
as follows.

Recall that in
Section

4.1
, we defined the sequences of functions
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
where each
\varphi_{n}\colon G\to\Alt(X_{n})
and
\psi_{n}\colon A\to\Alt(X_{n})
.
We define a sequence of group actions
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell]}\colon H\to\Alt(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
recursively such that, for each
h=(g,a)\in H
,

\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}=(\gamma_{d})_{d\in X_{\ell+1}}\in\Alt(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell+1]})\wr\Sym(X_{\ell+1}),

where each

\gamma_{d}=(\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]})|_{d}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{h}^{[\ell+1]}&\text{if }d=x_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\widehat{\varphi_{\ell+2}(g)}&\text{if }d=y_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(a)}&\text{if }d=z_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\id&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Notice here that each element
\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}
stabilizes the first level of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
.
We provide
Figure

1
to assist in the understanding of this action.
Note that within this paper, we extend the definitions of
\varphi
and
\psi
to
H
such that, for each
h=(g,a)\in H
, we have
\varphi(h)\coloneqq\varphi(g)
and
\psi(h)\coloneqq\psi(a)
.

Figure 1.
The action of
\widetilde{h}^{[0]}
on
\mathcal{T}_{X}=\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[0]}
.

In order to define our branch groups, we introduce one additional family of tree automorphisms:
For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we define

B_{\ell}\coloneqq\widehat{\Alt(X_{\ell+1})}\leqslant\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}).

That is,
B_{\ell}
is the group of rooted automorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
that correspond to even permutations of
X_{\ell+1}
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we define the group

\Gamma_{\ell}\coloneqq\left\langle\widetilde{H}^{[\ell]},B_{\ell}\right\rangle\leqslant\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}).

That is,
\Gamma_{\ell}
is the subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
that is generated by
\widetilde{H}^{[\ell]}
and
B_{\ell}
.
In the remainder of this section, we show that each such group
\Gamma_{\ell}
is a branch group, and we prove some lemmas which we find useful in the proofs in later sections.

We begin with the following technical lemma which is the proof of
Lemma

4.3
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set and let
A,B\subseteq\Omega
be subsets such that

(1)

\Omega=A\cup B
,

(2)

A\cap B=\{\omega\}
for some
\omega\in\Omega
,

(3)

|A|\geqslant 3
.

Let
G\leqslant\Alt(\Omega)
be a subgroup that fixes at least two elements of
A\setminus\{\omega\}
and acts transitively on
B
.
Then the subgroup of
\Alt(\Omega)
that is generated by the
G
-conjugates of
\Alt(A)
coincides with
\Alt(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
H
denote the subgroup of
\Alt(\Omega)
that is generated by the
G
-conjugates of
\Alt(A)
.
Let
p,q\in A\setminus\{\omega\}
be two distinct elements that are fixed by
G
.
If we conjugate the
3
-cycle
(p\ q\ \omega)
with all elements in
G
, then, by the transitivity of the action of
G
on
B
, we obtain that all
3
-cycles of the form
(p\ q\ x)
lie in
H
, where
x\in B
.
Moreover, since
(p\ q\ x)\in\Alt(A)
for every
x\in A
, it follows that
H
contains the subset
\{\,(p\ q\ x)\mid x\in\Omega\,\}\subseteq\Alt(\Omega)
, which is well-known to generate
\Alt(\Omega)
.
∎

The following lemma appears in several variations within the literature (see for example
[
21
,
15
]
) and is based on the so-called commutator trick, which was extracted by Segal
[
21
, Lemma 4]
from a proof of Grigorchuk
[
10
, Theorem 4]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the group
\Gamma_{\ell}
is a finitely generated branch subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
.
Moreover, the wreath decomposition

\Gamma_{\ell}\rightarrow\Gamma_{\ell+1}\wr_{X_{\ell+1}}B_{\ell},\ \ \gamma\mapsto(\gamma_{a})_{a\in X^{\ell+1}}\cdot\widehat{\pi_{1}(\gamma)},

defines an isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Notice that since
\Gamma_{\ell}
is generated by two finitely generated groups, i.e.
\widetilde{H}^{[\ell]}=\widetilde{G\times A}^{[\ell]}
and
B_{\ell}=\widehat{\Alt(X_{\ell+1})}
, that
\Gamma_{\ell}
itself is finitely generated.
Let us now prove that
\Gamma_{\ell}
is a branch subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
.
In particular, we show that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the rigid level stabilizer subgroup
\RiSt_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(n)
of
\Gamma_{\ell}
coincides with the ordinary level stabilizer subgroup
\St_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(n)
, which is of finite index in
\Gamma_{\ell}
.

From their definitions, it directly follows that the
n
-th level sections of
\widetilde{H}^{[\ell]}
and
B_{\ell}
lie in
\Gamma_{\ell+n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Notice then that this implies that the
n
-th level sections of every element in
\Gamma_{\ell}
lie in the group
\Gamma_{\ell+n}
.
Thus, in order to show that
\Gamma_{\ell}
is a branch group, it is sufficient to show that
v\Gamma_{\ell+n}
is contained in
\RiSt_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(n)
for each vertex
v\in X_{\ell+1}\times X_{\ell+2}\times\cdots\times X_{\ell+n}
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
.
Notice that it suffices to show that for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have
\RiSt_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(1)=\St_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(1)
and that
\Gamma_{\ell}\cong\Gamma_{\ell+1}\wr_{X_{\ell+1}}B_{\ell}
.
Thus, in the remainder of this proof, we show this property.

Let
\sigma\in B_{\ell}
be such that
\sigma(z_{\ell+1})=z_{\ell+1}
and
\sigma(\{x_{\ell+1},y_{\ell+1}\})\cap\{x_{\ell+1},y_{\ell+1}\}=\emptyset
, for example, we could choose
\sigma=(x_{\ell+1}\ p_{\ell+1})\,(y_{\ell+1}\ q_{\ell+1})
.
Then, from the definition of the maps
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell]}\colon H\to\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
, we see that for each
h,k\in H
, we have

[(\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]})^{\sigma},\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}]_{a}=\begin{cases}\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}([h,k])}&\text{if }a=z_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\id&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since
A\cong\Alt(6)
is perfect and
\psi(H)\cong A
, we deduce that
z_{\ell+1}(\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(A)})
lies in
\RiSt_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(z_{\ell+1})
.
Moreover, since
B_{\ell}
acts transitively on
Q_{\ell+1}
, there is a
\tau\in B_{\ell}
with

(z_{\ell+1}\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(A)})^{\tau}=y_{\ell+1}\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(A)}\leqslant\Gamma_{\ell}.

Now we can conjugate
y_{\ell+1}\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(A)}
by
\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}
to deduce that

\left(y_{\ell+1}\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(A)}\right)^{\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}}=y_{\ell+1}\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(A)^{\varphi_{\ell+2}(h)}}

lies in
\Gamma_{\ell}
.
Using that the action of
G
on
Q_{\ell+2}
via
\varphi_{\ell+2}
is transitive, we can apply Lemma
4.2
to conclude that
y_{\ell+1}B_{\ell+1}\leqslant\Gamma_{\ell}
.
As a consequence, it follows that

\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}\cdot\left(y_{\ell+1}\varphi_{\ell+2}(h)^{-1}\right)\cdot\left(z_{\ell+1}\psi_{\ell+2}(h)^{-1}\right)=x_{\ell+1}\widetilde{h}^{[\ell+1]}\in\Gamma_{\ell}

for every
h\in H
.
Thus
x_{\ell+1}\widetilde{H}^{[\ell+1]}
lies in
\Gamma_{\ell}
.
By conjugating
x_{\ell+1}\widetilde{H}^{[\ell+1]}
with an appropriate element from
B_{\ell}
we obtain
y_{\ell+1}\widetilde{H}^{[\ell+1]}\leqslant\Gamma_{\ell}
and hence

y_{\ell+1}\left\langle\widetilde{H}^{[\ell+1]},B_{\ell+1}\right\rangle=y_{\ell+1}\Gamma_{\ell+1}\leqslant\Gamma_{\ell}.

Taking again conjugates by elements of
B_{\ell}
we finally get
a\Gamma_{\ell+1}\leqslant\Gamma_{\ell}
for every
a\in X_{\ell+1}
, which we wanted to prove.
This gives us

\Gamma_{\ell}=\St_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(1)\rtimes B_{\ell}=\bigoplus\limits_{a\in X_{\ell+1}}a\Gamma_{\ell+1}\rtimes B_{\ell}=\Gamma_{\ell+1}\wr_{X_{\ell+1}}B_{\ell},

which completes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we introduce one additional tool which we find useful in
Section

6
.
We begin by defining the group of
germs
as follows.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
G
be a group that acts on a topological space
\Omega
and let
\omega
be a point in
\Omega
.
The group of
germs
of
G
at
\omega
is defined as
\mathcal{O}_{G}(\omega)=\St_{G}(\omega)/\sim
, where
g\sim h
if and only if
g
and
h
coincide on some neighbourhood of
\omega
.
Let
\pi_{\omega}\colon\St_{G}(\omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{G}(\omega)
denote the canonical quotient map.

We then have the following result which applies to the subgroup
\widetilde{H}^{[\ell]}
of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and let
\omega_{\ell}\coloneqq(x_{\ell+1},x_{\ell+2},\ldots)\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{Y}^{[\ell]}
.
Then the map

\iota_{\ell}\colon H\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(\omega_{\ell}),\ \ h\mapsto\pi_{\omega_{\ell}}(\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]})

is injective.

Proof.

It is a direct consequence of the definition of
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell]}
that
\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}
fixes
\omega_{\ell}
, which shows that
\iota_{\ell}
is well-defined.
Suppose now that
\pi_{\omega_{\ell}}(\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]})=\id
, i.e. that there is a neighbourhood
U
of
\omega_{\ell}
on which
\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}
coincides with the identity.
Recall that a neighbourhood base of
\omega_{\ell}
in
\partial\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]}
is given by
(x_{\ell+1}\cdots x_{n}\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[n]})_{n>\ell}
.
Thus we can choose
n
to be such that
x_{\ell+1}\cdots x_{n}\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[n]}\subseteq U
.
Since the section of
\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}
at
x_{\ell+1}\cdots x_{n}
is given by
\widetilde{h}^{[n]}
, we deduce that
\widetilde{h}^{[n]}
is the identity on
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[n]}
.
In that case it follows that
\varphi_{n+k}(h)
and
\psi_{n+k}(h)
coincide with the identity in
\Alt(X_{n+k})
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
In other words, this tells us that
h=(g,\sigma)
satisfies
\sigma=\id
in
\Sym(X_{n+k})
and that
g\in N_{n+k}
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
As
(N_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
was chosen to be a properly decreasing residual chain of
G
, we deduce that
h=1
, which completes the proof.
∎

5.
Preservation of the conjugacy classes

In the remainder of this paper, it is useful to think of
\Gamma_{\ell}
as a quotient of the free product
F_{\ell}\coloneqq H\ast B_{\ell}
via the canonical projection
\pr_{\ell}\colon F_{\ell}\rightarrow\Gamma_{\ell}
, which is defined such that
\pr_{\ell}(h)=\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}
for each
h\in H
, and
\pr_{\ell}(b)=b
for each
b\in B_{\ell}
.
Using
F_{\ell}
, we define a complexity function
\|\cdot\|_{H}\colon\Gamma_{\ell}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
on
\Gamma_{\ell}
, variants of which have turned out to be useful in many inductive arguments in the realm of branch groups, see e.g.
[
1
,
16
]
.
By definition, each element
w\in F_{\ell}
can be uniquely written as

(5.1)

w=b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}h_{2}\cdots b_{n}h_{n}b_{n+1}

where
n\geqslant 0
, each
b_{i}\in B_{\ell}
with
b_{j}\neq 1
for each
2\leqslant j\leqslant n
, and each
h_{i}\in H\setminus\{1\}
.
For each word
w
, as in (
5.1
), we then define
\|w\|_{H}=n
, that is,
\|\cdot\|_{H}\colon F_{\ell}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
counts the number of
H
factors in a normal form of an element in
F_{\ell}
.
Notice that this function satisfies the triangle inequality, that is,
\|uv\|_{H}\leqslant\|u\|_{H}+\|v\|_{H}
for each
u,v\in F_{\ell}
.
We then extend this definition to a map
\|\cdot\|_{H}\colon\Gamma_{\ell}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\|\gamma\|_{H}\coloneqq\min\{\|w\|_{H}\mid\gamma\in F_{\ell}\text{ with }\gamma=\pr_{\ell}(w)\}.

To simplify the proof of
Lemma

5.3
, we introduce
fragmented subwords
as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
A
be an alphabet, and let
v,w\in A^{*}
.
We then say that
v
is a
fragmented subword
of
w
if the word
w
can be factored as

w=u_{1}v_{1}u_{2}v_{2}\cdots u_{k}v_{k}u_{k+1}

where
v=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}
, and each
u_{i}\in A^{*}
.

We prove the following lemma which we then use to prove
Lemma

5.3
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and suppose that
w,w^{\prime}\in F_{\ell}
have the forms

w=b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad w^{\prime}=b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}h_{2}b_{3}.

We write
\gamma=\pr_{\ell}(w)
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\pr_{\ell}(w^{\prime})
for the element of
\Gamma_{\ell}
corresponding to
w
and
w^{\prime}
, respectively.
Then, for each
d\in X_{\ell}
, there are elements
w_{d},w_{d}^{\prime}\in F_{\ell+1}
with

\displaystyle w_{d}

\displaystyle\in B_{\ell+1}\cup\{h_{1}\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{d}

\displaystyle\in B_{\ell+1}\cup\{h_{1}h_{2}\}\cup\{h_{1}\beta_{2}\mid\beta_{2}\in B_{\ell+1}\}\cup\{\beta_{1}h_{2}\mid\beta_{1}\in B_{\ell+1}\}

such that
\gamma|_{d}=\pr_{\ell+1}(w_{d})
and
\gamma^{\prime}|_{d}=\pr_{\ell+1}(w^{\prime}_{d})
.

Proof.

Recall from
Section

4
, that the sections of
\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}\in H
are defined as

\widetilde{h}^{[\ell]}|_{d}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{h}^{[\ell+1]}&\text{if }d=x_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\widehat{\varphi_{\ell+2}(h)}&\text{if }d=y_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(h)}&\text{if }d=z_{\ell+1},\\[3.0pt]
\id&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for each
d\in X_{\ell+1}
, where
\widehat{\varphi_{\ell+2}(h)},\widehat{\psi_{\ell+2}(h)}\in B_{\ell+1}
.
Our result then follows from the interpretation of
\Gamma_{\ell}
as a wreath product
\Gamma_{\ell+1}\wr_{X_{\ell}}B_{\ell}
.
For example, from this interpretation, we see that each section
\gamma^{\prime}|_{d}
can be written as the product of a section of
\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell]}
with a section of
\widetilde{h_{2}}^{[\ell]}
; and each section
\gamma|_{d}
is also a section of
h_{1}
.
Each possible choice of such words is included in this theorem statement.
∎

From the above theorem, we then obtain the following as a corollary.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and let
w\in F_{\ell+1}
be a word in normal form

w=b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}h_{2}\cdots b_{n}h_{n}b_{n+1}\in F_{\ell}

where each
b_{i}\in B_{\ell}
and each
h_{i}\in H
.
Let
\gamma=\pr_{\ell}(w)
, then for each
d\in X_{\ell}
, we may find a word of the form

w_{d}=\beta_{1}h^{\prime}_{1}\beta_{2}h^{\prime}_{2}\cdots\beta_{m}h^{\prime}_{m}\beta_{m+1}\in F_{\ell+1}

such that
\gamma|_{d}=\pr_{\ell+1}(w_{d})
with
m\leqslant(n/2)+1
, each
\beta_{i}\in B_{\ell+1}
, and each
h_{i}^{\prime}\in H^{*}
such that

h_{1}^{\prime}h_{2}^{\prime}\cdots h_{m}^{\prime}\in H^{*}
is a fragmented subword of
h_{1}h_{2}\cdots h_{n}\in H^{*}
.
Notice then that this result implies that
\bigl\|\gamma|_{d}\bigr\|_{H}\leqslant(\|\gamma\|_{H}/2)+1
for each
d\in X_{\ell}
.

Proof.

We begin by collecting adjacent terms of
w
; in particular, depending on the odd/even parity of
n
, we can group the terms of
w
as either

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=(b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}h_{2})(b_{3}h_{3}b_{4}h_{4})\cdots(b_{n-3}h_{n-3}b_{n-2}h_{n-2})(b_{n-1}h_{n-1}b_{n}h_{n}b_{n+1})\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=(b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}h_{2})(b_{3}h_{3}b_{4}h_{4})\cdots(b_{n-2}h_{n-2}b_{n-1}h_{n-1})(b_{n}h_{n}b_{n+1}).

From
Lemma

5.2
and (
3.2
), we see that, for each
d\in X_{\ell+1}
, we may choose a word
w_{d}\in F_{\ell+1}
with
\gamma|_{d}=\pr_{\ell+1}(w_{d})
such that either

w_{d}=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\qquad\text{or}\qquad w_{d}=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}s^{\prime}

where each

s_{k}\in B_{\ell+1}\cup\{h_{2k-1}h_{2k}\}\cup\{h_{2k-1}\beta_{2}\mid\beta_{2}\in B_{\ell+1}\}\cup\{\beta_{1}h_{2k}\mid\beta_{1}\in B_{\ell+1}\}

and

s^{\prime}\in B_{\ell+1}\cup\{h_{n}\}
.
Notice here that each
s_{i}
corresponds to a section of one of the groupings of terms, and
s^{\prime}
corresponds to a section of
b_{n}h_{n}b_{n+1}
.
It is then clear that such a word has each of the desired properties of the lemma.
∎

From
Lemma

5.3
, we can prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then two elements
g,k\in G
are conjugate in
G
if and only if the corresponding elements
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]},\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}\in\Gamma_{\ell}
are conjugate in
\Gamma_{\ell}
.
That is, for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the map
g\mapsto\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
is a Frattini embedding of
G
into
\Gamma_{\ell}
.

Proof.

It follows from
Lemma

4.5
that the map
g\mapsto\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
is a homomorphism.
From this, it is clear that if
g,k\in G
are conjugate in
G
, then
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]},\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}\in\Gamma_{\ell}
must also be conjugate in
\Gamma_{\ell}
. Thus, we only need to show that this is a necessary condition.

Suppose now that
g,k\in G
are elements which are not conjugate in the group
G
.
Thus, to complete our proof, it only remains to be shown that
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
and
\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
are not conjugate in
\Gamma_{\ell}
.
Since
g\mapsto\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
is an isomorphism this property immediately follows if one of
g
or
k
is the identity.
In the remainder of this proof, we assume without loss of generality that neither
g
nor
k
is the identity of
G
.

Suppose for contradiction, that
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
and
\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
are conjugate, that is, that there exists some
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}
for which
\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
.
In the remainder of this proof we show, by induction on
\|\gamma\|_{H}
, that no such element
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}
can exist.
We begin with the base cases
\|\gamma\|_{H}=0
and
\|\gamma\|_{H}=1
, as follows.

Base case
:
\|\gamma\|_{H}=0
.

To begin, suppose that
\|\gamma\|_{H}=0
, that is,
\gamma\in B_{\ell}
.
Recall from the definition of
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell]}
, that

\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}|_{x_{\ell+1}}=\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}
and

\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}|_{x_{\ell+1}}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
, neither of which are finitary;
and that for each
a\in X_{\ell+1}\setminus\{x_{\ell+1}\}
, we have

\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}|_{a}\in B_{\ell+1}
and

\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}|_{a}\in B_{\ell+1}
, which are finitary.
Moreover, notice that, if
\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
, then

(\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma)|_{x_{\ell+1}}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}.

Hence, it must be the case that
\gamma\cdot x_{\ell+1}=x_{\ell+1}
and thus
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
.
Since the map
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell+1]}\colon G\to\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell+1]})
is an isomorphism, this would imply that
g=k
which contradicts our assumption that
g
and
k
are not conjugate in
G
.

Base case
:
\|\gamma\|_{H}=1
.

Suppose that
\|\gamma\|_{H}=1
, then there exists an element
w\in F_{\ell}
with
\gamma=\pr_{\ell}(w)
and

(5.2)

w=b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}\in F_{\ell}

where each
b_{i}\in B_{\ell}
and
h_{1}\in H
.
Since
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\in\St_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(1)
, and
\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
, then there exists some
a,b\in X_{\ell+1}
such that

(5.3)

\bigl(\gamma|_{a}\bigr)^{-1}\cdot(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{b}\cdot\gamma|_{a}=(\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]})|_{x_{\ell+1}}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}.

From the definition of
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
, we then see that either
(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{b}\in B_{\ell+2}
or
(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{b}=\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}
.
Further, from
Lemma

5.2
, we see that

\gamma|_{a}\in B_{\ell+1}\cup\left\{\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]}\right\}.

From (
5.3
), the remainder of the proof of this base case falls into 4 cases as follows.

Case 1:

\tau^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}\cdot\tau=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
for some
\tau\in B_{\ell+1}
.

In this case, our contradiction follows from the previous base case.

Case 2:

\tau^{-1}\cdot\sigma\cdot\tau=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
for some
\sigma,\tau\in B_{\ell+1}
.

In this case, our contradiction follows as
\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
does not belong to
B_{\ell+1}
, in particular,
\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
is not finitary for every
k\neq 1
in
G
.

Case 3:

(\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]})^{-1}\cdot(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]})\cdot(\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]})=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
.

Since the map
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell+1]}\colon H\to\Gamma_{\ell+1}
is injective, it would then follow that
h_{1}^{-1}gh_{1}=k
in
H=G\times A
.
Since
H
is a direct product of
G
and
A
, with
h_{1}=(x,y)\in G\times A=H
, we see that this result implies that
x^{-1}gx=k
in
G
.
This contradicts our assumption that
g
and
k
are not conjugate in
G
.

Case 4:

(\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]})^{-1}\cdot\sigma\cdot(\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]})=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
for some
\sigma\in B_{\ell+1}
.

If
\sigma=1
, then
\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}=1
, which would contradict our assumption that
k
is nontrivial.
Suppose therefore that
\sigma\in B_{\ell+1}\setminus\{1\}
.
Then, a contradiction follows as

(\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]})^{-1}\cdot\sigma\cdot(\widetilde{h_{1}}^{[\ell+1]})\notin\St_{\Gamma_{\ell+1}}(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}\in\St_{\Gamma_{\ell+1}}(1)

This completes all subcases of the base case for
\|\gamma\|_{H}=1
.

Inductive step
:

We now assume for induction that for
m\in\mathbb{N}
(i.e.
m\geqslant 1
), for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there is no word
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\ell}
with
\|\gamma^{\prime}\|_{H}\leqslant m
such that
(\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma^{\prime}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
.
Our intention within this inductive step is then to show that there is no element
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}
with
\|\gamma\|_{H}=m+1
for which
\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
.
To do so, suppose for contradiction that there exists such a choice of element
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}
.
By the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{H}
, there must exist some element
w\in F_{\ell}
which can be written as

w=b_{1}h_{1}b_{2}h_{2}\cdots b_{m}h_{m}b_{m+1}h_{m+1}b_{m+2}

where each
b_{i}\in B_{\ell}
,
h_{i}\in H
, and
\gamma=\pr_{\ell}(w)
.

Notice that if
\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
, then it would follow that

(5.4)

\bigl(\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma\bigr)|_{x_{\ell+1}}=(\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]})|_{x_{\ell+1}}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}.

Let
\beta\in B_{\ell}
be the unique element for which

\gamma\beta\in\St_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(1).

From (
3.2
), (
5.4
) and the definition of
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell]}\colon H\to\Gamma_{\ell}
, we then see that there exists some
d\in X_{\ell+1}
for which

(5.5)

\bigl((\gamma\beta)|_{d}\bigr)^{-1}\cdot(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{d}\cdot(\gamma\beta)|_{d}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}.

From the definition of
\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}
, we then see that either
(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{d}\in B_{\ell}
or
(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{d}=\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}
.
In the remainder of this proof, we consider these two possibilities in separate cases.

Case 1
:
(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{d}\in B_{\ell}
.

Same arguments as in case 4 of the proof of the base case
\|\gamma\|_{H}=1
.

Case 2
:
(\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]})|_{d}=\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}
.

From
Lemma

5.3
, we see that
\bigl\|(\gamma\beta)|_{d}\bigr\|_{H}<\|\gamma\|_{H}
.
Thus, we have a
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\ell+1}
with
\|\gamma^{\prime}\|_{H}<\|\gamma\|_{H}
for which
(\gamma^{\prime})^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell+1]}\gamma^{\prime}=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell+1]}
which contradicts our inductive hypothesis.

Conclusion
:

In all cases, we see that a contradiction arises if we assume that there exists some
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\ell}
for which
\gamma^{-1}\widetilde{g}^{[\ell]}\gamma=\widetilde{k}^{[\ell]}
where
g
and
k
are not conjugate in
G
.
∎

We then obtain the following result as an immediate corollary to
Theorem

5.4
.

Corollary 5.5
.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, if
G
has an unsolvable conjugacy problem, then
\Gamma_{\ell}
also has an unsolvable conjugacy problem.

6.
Preservation of effective residual finiteness

Our aim in this section is to prove the second part of Theorem
B
.
In our current setting this ends up in showing that the effective residual finiteness and the recursive presentability of
G
can be extended to
\Gamma_{0}
.
Let us start by recalling and extending the notation from the previous sections.

6.1.
Setting the stage

Let
G
be a finitely generated infinite residually finite group and let
S
be a finite symmetric generating set of
G
.
We assume from now on that
G
is recursively presented and effectively residually finite.
In this case Lemma
4.1
provides us with a sequence
(N_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite index normal subgroups of
G
such that the following holds.

(1)

There exists a computable sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of surjective homomorphisms
f_{n}\colon G\rightarrow Q_{n}

with
\ker(f_{n})=N_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(2)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and each
g\in N_{n}\setminus\{1\}
the word length of
g
with respect to
S
is bounded from below by
n+1
.

(3)

The sequences
X=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
Q=(Q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

X_{n}\coloneqq Q_{n}\cup\{x_{n},y_{n},z_{n},p_{n},q_{n}\}

are computable.

(4)

The sequences
(\varphi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

of the homomorphisms

\varphi_{n},\psi_{n}\colon G\rightarrow\Alt(X_{k})

from Section
4
are computable.

Recall that for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the group
\Gamma_{\ell}
is defined as the subgroup of
\Aut(\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[\ell]})
that is generated by
\widetilde{H}^{[\ell]}
and
B_{\ell}
.
Thus a finite symmetric generating set of
\Gamma_{\ell}
is given by
\Sigma_{\ell}\coloneqq((\widetilde{S}^{[\ell]}\times\widetilde{A}^{[\ell]})\cup B_{\ell})\setminus\{1\}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
w\in\Sigma_{0}^{\ast}
be a word of length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Then
w
represents the trivial element in
\Gamma_{0}
if and only if
w
represents an element in
\St_{\Gamma_{0}}(2\ell)
.

Proof.

Since the claim is trivial otherwise, we may assume that
\ell>0
.
Let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}
be the element represented by
w
.
Suppose that
\gamma\in\St_{\Gamma_{0}}(2\ell)
.
We want to show that
\gamma
is the trivial element.
To this end, we write

w=v_{1}w_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot v_{m}w_{m}v_{m+1},

where
v_{i}\in B_{0}^{\ast}
and

w_{i}\in(S\times A)^{\ast}
for every
i
.
Without loss of generality we may assume that the words
w_{i}
are non-empty.
Then

m\leqslant\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\|w_{i}\|_{H}\leqslant\|w\|_{H}=\ell.

From Lemma
5.3
we know that for every
a\in X_{1}
, the section
\gamma_{a}
is represented by a word of the form

w_{a}=v_{1}^{\prime}w_{1}^{\prime}\cdot\ldots\cdot v_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}w_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}v_{m^{\prime}+1}^{\prime},

where
v_{i}^{\prime}\in B_{1}^{\ast}
,
w_{1}^{\prime}\ldots w_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}
is a fragmented subword of
w_{1}\ldots w_{m}
, and
m^{\prime}<m
if
m\geqslant 2
.
Using the fact that the section of
\gamma
at a word of the form
u_{1}u_{2}
is given by
\gamma_{u_{1}u_{2}}=(\gamma_{u_{1}})_{u_{2}}
, an inductive application of Lemma
5.3
tells us that for every
u\in X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{\ell}
, the section of
\gamma
at
u
is represented by a word of the form

w_{u}=v_{1}^{(u)}w^{(u)}v_{2}^{(u)},

where
v_{1}^{(u)},v_{2}^{(u)}\in B_{\ell}^{\ast}
and
w^{(u)}
is a fragmented subword of
w_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot w_{m}
.
Note that the latter implies that the word length of
w^{(u)}\in(S\times A)^{\ast}
is bounded above by
m\leqslant\ell
.
As a consequence, we have

\gamma_{u}=\tau_{1}\cdot\widetilde{(s_{1},\sigma_{1})}^{[\ell]}\cdot\ldots\cdot\widetilde{(s_{k},\sigma_{k})}^{[\ell]}\cdot\tau_{2}

for some
k\leqslant\ell
and appropriate elements
s_{i}\in S
,
\sigma_{i}\in A
, and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in B_{\ell}
.
Let
g\in G
be the element represented by
s_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot s_{k}
and let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\sigma_{k}
.
Then

\gamma_{u}=\tau_{1}\cdot\widetilde{(g,\sigma)}^{[\ell]}\cdot\tau_{2}=\tau_{1}\cdot\widetilde{(g,\sigma)}^{[\ell]}\cdot\tau_{1}^{-1}\cdot\tau_{2}.

From our assumption that
\gamma\in\St_{\Gamma_{0}}(2\ell)
it follows that
\tau_{1}\tau_{2}=\id
and hence that
\widetilde{(g,\sigma)}^{[\ell]}
lies in
\St_{\Gamma_{\ell}}(\ell)
.
Regarding the definition of the map
\widetilde{\cdot}^{[\ell]}
, we see that

\phi_{k}(g)=\id\in\Sym(Q_{k})\text{ and }\psi_{k}(\sigma)=\id\in\Sym(Q_{k})

for every
\ell<k\leqslant 2\ell
.
Since
\psi_{k}\colon A\rightarrow\Sym(Q_{k})
is injective, the latter shows that
\sigma=\id
.
To see that
g
is the trivial element, we recall from (
2
) that the word length of
g
with respect to
S
would be at least
\ell+1
if
g
is non-trivial.
However, the word length of
g
is bounded from above by
k\leqslant m\leqslant\ell
, which implies that
g=1
and hence
\gamma_{u}=1
.
Since
u\in X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{\ell}
was arbitrary, it follows that the section of
\gamma
at each word of length
\ell
is trivial.
Together with our assumption that
\gamma\in\St_{\Gamma_{0}}(2\ell)
this implies
\gamma=1
, which completes the proof.
∎

From the above lemma, we may then prove the following result.

Proposition 6.2
.

Under the assumptions of this section.
The group
\Gamma_{0}
is effectively residually finite and recursively presentable.

Proof.

From Lemma
6.1
we know that a word
w
of length
\ell
over
\Sigma_{0}
represents the trivial element in
\Gamma_{0}
if and only if
\pi_{2\ell}(w)\neq\id
, where

\pi_{2\ell}\colon\Gamma_{0}\rightarrow\Sym(X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{2\ell})

denotes the action of
\Gamma_{0}
on the
2\ell
-level of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{X}^{[0]}
.
Regarding this, it suffices to show that there exists a Turing machine
M
that takes as input a word
w\in\Sigma_{0}^{\ast}
and returns the homomorphism
\pi_{2\ell}
, where
\ell
is the word length of
w
.
Recall that to return
\pi_{2\ell}
it suffices to encode the multiplication table of
\Sym(X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{2\ell})
along with the elements
\pi_{2\ell}(\gamma)
for each
\gamma\in\Sigma_{0}
as the output of a Turing machine.
To see that there is a Turing machine doing the latter, recall from (
3
) that the sequence
X=(X_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is computable.
As a consequence, the sequence
(X^{2\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
X^{2\ell}=X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{2\ell}
, is computable.
The latter in turn implies that the sequence of groups
(\Sym(X^{2\ell}))_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
is computable.
Thus it remains to show that for each generator

\gamma\in\Sigma_{0}=((\widetilde{S}^{[0]}\times\widetilde{A}^{[0]})\cup B_{0})\setminus\{1\},

the assignment
\ell\mapsto\pi_{2\ell}(\gamma)
is computable.
To achieve this, it suffices to show that there is a Turing machine that takes as input a number
\ell\in\mathbb{N}

v=a_{i_{1}}\ldots a_{i_{2\ell}}\in X_{1}\times\ldots\times X_{2\ell}

and returns the word
\gamma(v)
.
In the case where
\gamma=\widehat{\tau}\in B_{0}=\widehat{\Alt(X_{1})}

we have

\widehat{\tau}(v)=\tau(a_{i_{1}})a_{i_{2}}\ldots a_{i_{2\ell}}.

To describe
\gamma(v)
in the case where
\gamma=(\widetilde{s}^{[0]},\widetilde{\sigma}^{[0]})\in\widetilde{S}^{[0]}\times\widetilde{A}^{[0]}
, we consider the maximal
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with the property that
a_{i_{k}}=x_{k}
.
In that case there is a word
u\in X_{k+3}\times\ldots\times X_{2\ell}
with

v=x_{1}\ldots x_{k}a_{i_{k+1}}a_{i_{k+2}}u

and we have

(\widetilde{s}^{[0]},\widetilde{\sigma}^{[0]})(v)=\begin{cases}a_{i_{1}}\ldots a_{i_{k+1}}\varphi_{k+2}(s)(a_{i_{k+2}})u&\text{if }k\leqslant 2\ell-2\text{ and }a_{i_{k+1}}=y_{k+1}\\
a_{i_{1}}\ldots a_{i_{k+1}}\psi_{k+2}(\sigma)(a_{i_{k+2}})u&\text{if }k\leqslant 2\ell-2\text{ and }a_{i_{k+1}}=z_{k+1}\\
v&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since the sequences
(\varphi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are computable by (
4
), we deduce from the above descriptions of
\gamma(v)
that the assignment
\ell\mapsto\pi_{2\ell}(\gamma)
is computable for each
\gamma\in\Sigma_{0}
.
To complete the proof, it remains to note the Turing machine that takes a word
w\in\Sigma_{0}^{\ast}
as input and returns the homomorphism
\pi_{2\ell}
can check whether
w
represents the trivial word in
\Gamma_{0}
by evaluating
\pi_{2\ell}(w)
.
Since this solves the word problem in
\Gamma_{0}
, it follows that the group
\Gamma_{0}
is recursively presented, which completes the proof.
∎

We have now all ingredients to deduce Theorem
B
from the introduction.

See
B

Proof.

From Theorem
5.4
, we know that the map

\iota\colon G\rightarrow\Gamma_{0},\ g\mapsto\widetilde{g}^{[0]}

defines a Frattini embedding.
In the case where
G
is recursively presented and effectively residually finite we know from Proposition
6.2
that
\Gamma_{0}
is also recursively presented and effectively residually finite.
∎

Thus, we may now prove
A
from

as follows.

See

Proof.

By a result of Miller
[
18
, Theorem 9 on p. 31]
there exists a finitely presented residually finite group
G
with solvable word problem and unsolvable conjugacy problem.
Since finitely presented residually finite groups are effectively residually finite, we can apply Theorem

to deduce that there exists a finitely generated recursively presented and effectively residually finite branch group
\Gamma
and a Frattini embedding
\iota\colon G\rightarrow\Gamma
.
As a consequence, the unsolvability of the conjugacy problem of

passes to
