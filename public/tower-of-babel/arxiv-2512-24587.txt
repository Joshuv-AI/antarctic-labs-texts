MultiRisk: Multiple Risk Control via Iterative Score Thresholding
Sunay Joshi, Yan Sun, Hamed Hassani, Edgar Dobriban

Abstract
As generative AI systems are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, regulating multiple dimensions of model behavior has become essential. We focus on test-time filtering : a lightweight mechanism for behavior control that compares performance scores to estimated thresholds, and modifies outputs when these bounds are violated. We formalize the problem of enforcing multiple risk constraints with user-defined priorities, and introduce two efficient dynamic programming algorithms that leverage this sequential structure. The first, MULTIRISK-BASE, provides a direct finite-sample procedure for selecting thresholds, while the second, MULTIRISK, leverages data exchangeability to guarantee simultaneous control of the risks. Under mild assumptions, we show that MULTIRISK achieves nearly tight control of all constraint risks. The analysis requires an intricate iterative argument, upper bounding the risks by introducing several forms of intermediate symmetrized risk functions, and carefully lower bounding the risks by recursively counting jumps in symmetrized risk functions between appropriate risk levels. We evaluate our framework on a three-constraint Large Language Model alignment task using the PKU-SafeRLHF dataset, where the goal is to maximize helpfulness subject to multiple safety constraints, and where scores are generated by a Large Language Model judge and a perplexity filter. Our experimental results show that our algorithm can control each individual risk at close to the target level.

Users and developers alike must be able to regulate model behavior to meet standards of safety, fairness, creativity, and helpfulness.

For example, if an unsafety score for generated text is too high, the system may choose not to output the completion.

In practice, model providers seek to regulate several performance metrics simultaneously, such as safety, reliability, creativity, and helpfulness.

Problem formulation

In this section, we formalize the problem of selecting test-time filtering thresholds to control multiple risks.
We consider post-hoc modifications of the output of a generative or predictive model to control desired metrics; below, we refer to these modifications as
behaviors
.
For instance, if a user queries an LLM with prompt
x

S_{1}(x,y)
is high (where
y
denotes the generated response), then a possible behavior would be abstention from responding, as generating unsafe answers is problematic.
Similarly, if an uncertainty score
S_{2}(x,y)
is high, the corresponding behavior could involve prepending generation with “I am highly uncertain”.
Each of these behaviors has a different cost to the entity which controls the system, which we will refer to as the “provider”.
For example, abstention has a higher cost than prepending a fixed block of text, as the former is more inconvenient to the user, and thus has a higher risk of causing the user to leave the service.

Two-score example

To illustrate the framework introduced above, consider a simple setting with two scores
(
m=2
) representing safety and diversity. This example clarifies how sequential constraints
and thresholds interact in practice.

Let
S_{1}
denote an
unsafety score
: if
S_{1}
is high, the system abstains from responding.
Abstention carries a fixed cost
C_{1}>0

Hence the first constraint-loss function is constant,
V_{1}(x,y,y^{*})=C_{1}
.
Next, let
S_{2}
denote the
negative of a diversity score
,
so that larger

means less

For instance, when generating multiple images, it can be important to make them highly distinct and dissimilar from each other
in order to maximize utility.
If
S_{2}
exceeds a certain threshold, we ask the model to

a response, emphasizing
diversity (possibly by changing the prompt),
which incurs a constant cost
C_{2}>0
due to additional computation or delay.
Thus the second loss function is
V_{2}(x,y,y^{*})=C_{2}
.

The objective loss corresponds to the cost to the provider of generating one response.
For simplicity,
we normalize this cost so that the objective loss equals
V_{3}(x,y,y^{*})=1
.

5

5

In this example, all costs are constant for simplicity. However, the monotonicity properties of the risks generalize to arbitrary cost functions. For instance, generating longer textual answers usually incurs a higher cost, and this can be immediately included in our framework by setting

4.2
Multirisk

Motivated by the need for rigorous constraint risk control,
we propose the
multirisk
algorithm (
Algorithm

2
).
Like
multirisk-base
,
multirisk
leverages monotonicity to sequentially set the thresholds,
but refines the baseline by replacing empirical risks with conservative upper bounds and by slightly shrinking each risk budget.
The approach is
inspired by methods from the distribution-free uncertainty quantification literature, such as
nested prediction sets
(
39
)

and conformal risk control
(
4
)
.

In order to construct an upper bound on the empirical risk function,
since we do not have access to the
(n+1)
-st data point,
we replace its loss value
L_{j}^{(n+1)}
by an upper bound.
For
j\in[m]
,
let the constant
V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\geqslant 0

be an a.s. upper bound
on the cost
V_{j}^{(n+1)}=V_{j}(X^{(n+1)},Y^{(n+1)},Y^{*,(n+1)})
.
We define the

bumped empirical risk function

g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)}):\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)

corresponding to the
j
-th constraint by

\displaystyle g_{j}^{+}(\lambda_{j};\lambda_{1:(j-1)})=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{j}^{(i)}(\lambda_{1:j})+\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}{n+1}

for all
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
As in prior work on conformal risk control, the interpretation of this quantity is that it upper bounds the unknown loss value of the test data point by a deterministic value.

Recall that
\Lambda_{j}
,
j\in[m]
,
are the compact intervals to which we restrict our search.
As before,

g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})
is a non-increasing step function for any
\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}
(
Lemma

D.1
).
Thus, as above,
for each
j\in[m]
,
given
\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}

and
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
we can define
the generalized inverse
U_{j}^{+}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)};\cdot)

of
g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})

as the best possible setting of the hyperparameter
\lambda_{j}
that satisfies the constraint for the bumped empirical risk function, namely

\displaystyle U_{j}^{+}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)};\beta_{j})=\inf\left\{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}:g_{j}^{+}(\lambda_{j};\lambda_{1:(j-1)})\leqslant\beta_{j}\right\},

(4)

where the infimum of the empty set is taken to be
\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{max}}
.

Then, analogously to the
multirisk-base
method,

multirisk
(
Algorithm

2
) proceeds inductively to set the thresholds.

5
Theoretical guarantees

In this section, we establish finite-sample guarantees showing that our algorithm tightly controls risk.
Under appropriate regularity conditions (such as feasibility and boundedness conditions on the loss, detailed in Section

Further, we show that

The next theorem establishes that our algorithm satisfies all constraints under mild exchangeability and boundedness assumptions.

Moreover, under additional continuity assumptions on the

Upper bound conditions

In order to ensure risk control,

(Exchangeable observations)

The observations

For

For

a.s.

Recall that in our algorithm, we use upper and lower bounds on the cost functions

The final condition for the upper bound result formalizes this step.

Condition 5.7

(Bounds on

.

For

5.1.2
Upper bounding risks for
symmetric functions

A crucial step in the construction of our algorithm is to define a threshold given all the previous ones.
In order to motivate and analyze this construction,
it will be useful to consider
an (oracle) version of the empirical risk
that is symmetric in all
n+1
datapoints. (Below, when we say that a quantity is
symmetric
, we mean that it is a symmetric function of the
n+1
calibration and test datapoints.)
Given
j\in[m]

and

\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}
,
define the
symmetric oracle empirical risk
function

g^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)}):\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)

by

\displaystyle g^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}(\lambda_{j};\lambda_{1:(j-1)})=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}L_{j}^{(i)}(\lambda_{1:j})

(5)

for all
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Unlike
g_{j}
and
g_{j}^{+}
,
the function
g^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}
is a symmetric version
of the empirical risk corresponding to the
j
-th constraint,
which depends on the unobserved test loss
L_{j}^{(n+1)}(\lambda_{1:j})
, and it is thus purely a tool that is used in our theoretical analysis.

For each
j\in[m]
,
given
\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}

and
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
we define the generalized inverse

of

\displaystyle U^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)};\beta_{j})=\sup\left\{\lambda_{j}\in\Lambda_{j}:g^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}(\lambda_{j};\lambda_{1:(j-1)})>\beta_{j}\right\},

where the supremum of the empty set is taken to be
\lambda_{j}^{\mathrm{min}}
.
Since
g^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}
is symmetric, it follows that
U^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}
is symmetric.

Next, we will show that when working with the symmetric versions of the losses, the crucial step of constructing the next threshold given all the previous ones can be performed in a convenient manner with the help of the generalized inverse
U^{\mathrm{sym}}_{j}
.
However, since we do not have access to these symmetric risks,
we shall introduce intermediate statistics that leverage this construction.

For now, let us

j-1

We will define a choice of such values to be symmetric if each of the
j-1
thresholds are individually symmetric.
The following lemma shows

to ensure the

-th constraint risk is controlled,

\displaystyle(I):\quad|U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)};\beta)-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)};\beta^{\prime})|

Conclusion

This paper introduced a general framework for test-time filtering in generative models, formulated as optimizing an objective subject to multiple risk constraints with a sequential structure. Building on a natural dynamic programming baseline,
multirisk-base
, we proposed
multirisk
, a computationally efficient algorithm that provides distribution-free control of the constraint risks. Theoretically, we established conditions under which the
multirisk
thresholds are near-optimal, and empirically demonstrated that both
multirisk-base
and
multirisk
effectively regulate harmfulness and uncertainty in Large Language Model outputs.

Beyond this setting, the proposed framework suggests new ways to integrate statistical guarantees into AI deployment. Future work could extend
multirisk
to handle more general graph-structured dependencies among risks.

balance an objective and a constraint.

(15)

D.4
Comparing generalized inverses of non-increasing functions

Lemma D.2

(Bounded perturbations imply bounded generalized inverses for non-increasing functions)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an interval. Suppose that
f_{1},f_{2}:I\to[0,\infty)

are non-increasing functions such that

f_{1}\geqslant f_{2}\geqslant f_{1}-C

for some constant
C>0
. Then for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup\{x\in I:f_{2}(x)>\gamma-C\}\geqslant\sup\{x\in I:f_{1}(x)>\gamma\}.

(16)

Similarly,
suppose that
\tilde{f}_{1},\tilde{f}_{2}:I\to[0,\infty)

are non-increasing functions such that

\|\tilde{f}_{1}-\tilde{f}_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\leqslant\widetilde{C}

for some constant
\widetilde{C}>0
.
Then for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma-\widetilde{C}\}\geqslant\sup\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{1}(x)>\gamma\}\geqslant\sup\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma+\widetilde{C}\}.

(17)

Here, we define
the supremum of an empty subset of
I
to be
\inf I
.

Proof.

Since we define
the supremum of an empty subset of
I
to be
\inf I
, note that
given subsets

A,B\subseteq I

with

A\subseteq B
,
we necessarily have

\sup\{A\}\leqslant\sup\{B\}
.
Indeed,
if
A
is nonempty,
then
B
is necessarily nonempty,
and the inequality follows.
Otherwise,
if
A
is empty,
then the inequality vacuously holds.

First claim:

If
f_{1}(x)>\gamma
for some
x\in I
,
then by assumption

f_{2}(x)\geqslant f_{1}(x)-C>\gamma-C
.
Thus, we have the inclusion

\displaystyle\{x\in I:f_{1}(x)>\gamma\}\subseteq\{x\in I:f_{2}(x)>\gamma-C\}.

The result follows.

Second claim:

If
\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma+\widetilde{C}
,
then by assumption
and the triangle inequality,
we have

\tilde{f}_{1}(x)\geqslant\tilde{f}_{2}(x)-\widetilde{C}>\gamma
,
so we have the inclusion

\displaystyle\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma+\widetilde{C}\}\subseteq\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{1}(x)>\gamma\}.

Similarly,
if
\tilde{f}_{1}(x)>\gamma
,
then by assumption and the triangle inequality,
we have

\tilde{f}_{2}(x)\geqslant\tilde{f}_{1}(x)-\widetilde{C}>\gamma-\widetilde{C}
,
so we have the inclusion

\displaystyle\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{1}(x)>\gamma\}\subseteq\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma-\widetilde{C}\}.

Putting these together, we have

\displaystyle\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma+\widetilde{C}\}\subseteq\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{1}(x)>\gamma\}\subseteq\{x\in I:\tilde{f}_{2}(x)>\gamma-\widetilde{C}\},

which implies the result.
∎

we may apply
Lemma

D.15

for sufficiently large
n

to deduce

\displaystyle|U_{1}^{*}(\beta_{1}^{(k-1)})-U_{1}^{*}(\beta_{1}^{(k)})|\leqslant\mu_{1}\delta_{1}^{1/\nu}\leqslant\mu_{1}(V^{\mathrm{max}}_{1}-V^{\mathrm{min}}_{1})^{1/\nu}\frac{1}{n^{1/\nu}}.

Thus, we have

|\lambda_{1}^{*,(2k)}-\lambda_{1}^{*,(2k+2)}|\leqslant\frac{b_{1}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu}}
,
where we defined

b_{1}^{(2k)}:=\mu_{1}(V^{\mathrm{max}}_{1}-V^{\mathrm{min}}_{1})^{1/\nu}
.
If
1\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{discrete}}
,
then by definition,

\mu_{1}=0
,
and so

.
This proves the base case.

Inductive step:

Assume that for some
j\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
,
for each
\ell\in[j-1]
,
and for each
k\in[m-\ell+1]
,
the result holds.
We show the result holds
for
j

and
k\in[m-j+1]

Fix

.
Recall that

\lambda_{j}^{*,(2k)}=U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k)};\beta_{j}^{(k-1)})

and

\lambda_{j}^{*,(2k+2)}=U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})

Thus, we may write

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{*,(2k)}-\lambda_{j}^{*,(2k+2)}

\displaystyle=U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k)};\beta_{j}^{(k-1)})-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})

(23)

\displaystyle=(U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k-1)})-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)}))

\displaystyle+(U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k)};\beta_{j}^{(k-1)})-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k-1)}))

\displaystyle=:\text{Term (A)}+\text{Term (B)}.

To bound Term (A),
note that
by the inductive hypothesis,
if we define

\alpha_{1:(j-1)}=\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)}-\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2)}
,
then for
\ell\in[j-1]
,

,
where

if
\ell\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{discrete}}
.
Thus,

\alpha_{\ell}=0
for
\ell\in[j-1]\cap\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{discrete}}

and

|\alpha_{\ell}|\leqslant\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}b_{\ell}^{(2k^{\prime})}/n^{1/\nu^{\ell}}

if
\ell\in[j-1]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{discrete}}
.
Setting

\varepsilon_{j}=\beta_{j}^{(k-1)}-\beta_{j}
,

\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}=\beta_{j}^{(k)}-\beta_{j}^{(k-1)}
,
and
\alpha_{1:(j-1)}
as above,
since

|\varepsilon_{j}|=(k-1)\delta_{j}=(k-1)\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j}}{n+1}
,

|\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}|=\delta_{j}=\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j}}{n+1}
,
and

\|\alpha_{1:(j-1)}\|_{1}

tend to zero
as

,
we may apply
Lemma

D.15

for sufficiently large

to deduce that

\displaystyle|U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k-1)})-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})|\leqslant\mu_{j}\delta_{j}^{1/\nu}\leqslant\mu_{j}(V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j})^{1/\nu}\frac{1}{n^{1/\nu}}.

To bound Term (B),
note that by the inductive hypothesis,
we have the bounds

|\lambda_{\ell}^{*,(2k)}-\lambda_{\ell}^{*,(2k+2)}|\leqslant\frac{b_{\ell}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{\ell}}}

.
By

,

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}:=V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sum_{\ell\in[j-1]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{discrete}}}K_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{(2k)},

\displaystyle\|g_{j}^{*}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k)})-g_{j}^{*}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}.

17

from

D.2
,

\displaystyle U_{j}^{*}\left(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)}+\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}\right)-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leqslant U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leqslant U_{j}^{*}\left(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)}-\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}\right)-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)}).

Setting

\varepsilon_{j}=\beta_{j}^{(k)}-\beta_{j}
,

\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}=\pm\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}
,
and

\alpha_{1:(j-1)}=\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)}-\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2)}

since

|\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}|

D.15

\displaystyle\left|U_{j}^{*}\left(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)}+\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}\right)-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})\right|\leqslant\mu_{j}\left(\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}\right)^{1/\nu}=\mu_{j}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)})^{1/\nu}}{n^{1/\nu^{j}}}

\displaystyle\left|U_{j}^{*}\left(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)}-\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}\right)-U_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{1:(j-1)}^{*,(2k+2)};\beta_{j}^{(k)})\right|\leqslant\mu_{j}\left(\frac{\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)}}{n^{1/\nu^{(j-1)}}}\right)^{1/\nu}=\mu_{j}\frac{(\tilde{b}_{j}^{(2k)})^{1/\nu}}{n^{1/\nu^{j}}},

,

,

.

Since by

Combining

\displaystyle=V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j}(F(\lambda_{1:(j-1)},\lambda_{j}^{\prime},B_{(j+1):m})-F(\lambda_{1:(j-1)},\lambda_{j},B_{(j+1):m}))

\displaystyle\geqslant V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j}\widetilde{K}_{j}(\lambda_{j}^{\prime}-\lambda_{j})^{\nu},

where in the second step we used

5.7

in the third step we used

5.20

in the fourth step we let

of

5.22

Equation

D.13.3
Empirical process bound

Lemma D.9
.

Under the conditions
in
Section

5.1.1

and under

5.19
,
for
j\in[m]
,
for
n\geqslant 2
,
for any
\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}
,
with probability at least
1-2/\sqrt{n}
,

\displaystyle\|g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})-g_{j}^{*}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant r_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},

where
r_{j}=(16\sqrt{2}+2)V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle g_{j}^{+}(\lambda_{j};\lambda_{1:(j-1)})-g_{j}^{*}(\lambda_{j};\lambda_{1:(j-1)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{j}^{(i)}(\lambda_{1:j})-\mathbb{E}L_{j}^{(i)}(\lambda_{1:j}))+\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-\mathbb{E}L_{j}^{(1)}(\lambda_{1:j})}{n+1}

\displaystyle=:\frac{n}{n+1}W_{\lambda_{j}}+\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-\mathbb{E}L_{j}^{(1)}(\lambda_{1:j})}{n+1},

where by

5.7

and

5.19

\displaystyle W_{\lambda_{j}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{j}^{(i)}(\lambda_{1:j})-\mathbb{E}L_{j}^{(i)}(\lambda_{1:j}))

is an average of
n
i.i.d. mean zero bounded random variables,
each having modulus bounded by
V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j}
.
We want uniform control
of the process
W_{\lambda_{j}}
over
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Recall that
V_{j}^{(i)}=V_{j}(X^{(i)},Y^{(i)},Y^{(i),*})
,
where by

5.7

the function
V_{j}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}

obeys
V_{j}\in[V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j},V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}]

with
V^{\mathrm{min}}_{j}\geqslant 0
.
For fixed nonrandom
\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}
,
define the function class

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{j}

\displaystyle=\{f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ of the form}

\displaystyle f(x,y,y^{*})=V_{j}(x,y,y^{*})I(S_{1:(j-1)}(x,y,y^{*})\leqslant\lambda_{1:(j-1)})I(S_{j}(x,y,y^{*})>\lambda_{j}):\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

For convenience,
let
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
and write
z=(x,y,y^{*})
.
By

5.7
,

\mathcal{F}_{j}
is
an
V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}
-uniformly bounded function class.
By

(
40
, Theorem 4.10)
,
for each
\tau>0
,
with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\frac{n\tau^{2}}{2(V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j})^{2}})
,
writing
Z^{(i)}=(X^{(i)},Y^{(i)},Y^{(i),*})
for
i\in[n]
,
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}}|W_{\lambda_{j}}|=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{j}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(Z^{(i)})-\mathbb{E}f(Z^{(i)}))\right|\leqslant 2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})+\tau.

Note that

\delta=2\exp(-\frac{n\tau^{2}}{2(V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j})^{2}})

is equivalent to

\tau=V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
Thus, for each
\delta>0
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}}|W_{\lambda_{j}}|\leqslant 2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})+V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

which implies that
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\|g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})-g_{j}^{*}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{n}{n+1}\left(2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})+V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+\left|\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}-\mathbb{E}L_{j}^{(1)}(\lambda_{1:j})}{n+1}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})+V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{2\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}{n+1},

where in the second step
we used
5.7
.
Setting
\delta=2/\sqrt{n}
, we deduce that
with probability at least
1-2/\sqrt{n}
,

\displaystyle\|g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})-g_{j}^{*}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 2\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})+V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}{n+1}.

Assume that

\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})\leqslant\tilde{r}_{j}\sqrt{\log n/n}

for some constant
\tilde{r}_{j}>0
,
uniformly
in
\lambda_{1:(j-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{j-1}
.
We deduce that
for
n\geqslant 2
,
with probability at least
1-2/\sqrt{n}
,

\displaystyle\|g_{j}^{+}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})-g_{j}^{*}(\cdot;\lambda_{1:(j-1)})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant 2\tilde{r}_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}+\frac{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}{n+1}

(40)

\displaystyle\leqslant(2\tilde{r}_{j}+2V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j})\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}=:r_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},

where in the second step
we used the inequality

1/(n+1)\leqslant\sqrt{\log n/n}

for
n\geqslant 2
,
and in the third step
we defined

r_{j}=2\tilde{r}_{j}+2V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}
.

It remains only to bound
\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})
.
Note that
\mathcal{F}_{j}=\tilde{f}_{j}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{j}
,
where
\tilde{f}_{j}:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{j}(x,y,y^{*})=V_{j}(x,y,y^{*})I(S_{1:(j-1)}(x,y,y^{*})\leqslant\lambda_{1:(j-1)}),

where the function class
\mathcal{G}_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{j}=\{f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ of the form }f(x,y,y^{*})=I(S_{j}(x,y,y^{*})>\lambda_{j}):\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\},

and where for a fixed
\phi:\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}

and for a class
\mathcal{G}
of functions from
\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
,
we define
\phi\cdot\mathcal{G}:=\{\phi\cdot g:g\in\mathcal{G}\}
.
Note that
since
\mathcal{G}_{j}

is
1
-uniformly bounded
and has VC dimension 1,

(
40
, Lemma 4.14)

implies that

\mathcal{R}_{k}(\mathcal{G}_{j})\leqslant 4\sqrt{\log(k+1)/k}

for all positive integers
k
.
Also, note that by

5.7
,

|\tilde{f}_{j}|\leqslant V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}
.

Fix
z_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
.
For
i\in[n]
,
define the linear function

\varphi_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\varphi_{i}(t)=\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}(z_{i})}{V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}}t

for

.
Clearly

,
and since

is a contraction
for

.
Thus, by the Ledoux-Talagrand contraction lemma in its empirical form

(

, Theorem 4.12)
,
we have the empirical Rademacher complexity bound

.
Plugging in the random variables

and taking the expectation of both sides,
we deduce that

,
so that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(\mathcal{F}_{j})\leqslant 2V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\cdot 4\sqrt{\frac{\log(n+1)}{n}}\leqslant 8V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}\sqrt{\frac{2\log n}{n}}=:\tilde{r}_{j}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},

where in the second step
we used the inequality

\log(n+1)\leqslant 2\log n

for
n\geqslant 2
,
and in the third step we defined

\tilde{r}_{j}:=8\sqrt{2}V^{\mathrm{max}}_{j}
.
The desired bound follows.
∎

.

Lemma D.11
.

Suppose that

non-increasing
step function

|f(\mathbb{R})|<\infty
.

of
\mathbb{R}\setminus f(\mathbb{R})

for any compact interval

of an empty subset of

to be
\min I
.

Sort the elements of
f(\mathbb{R})
as

.
Then we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\setminus f(\mathbb{R})=(-\infty,v_{1})\cup\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{|f(\mathbb{R})|-1}(v_{i},v_{i+1})\right)\cup(v_{|f(\mathbb{R})|},\infty).

f(x)>\theta
iff
f(x)>\psi

f(x)>v_{i}

for all

by our convention

of an empty subset of

\min I

\displaystyle\sup\{x\in I:f(x)>\theta\}=\sup\{x\in I:f(x)>\psi\}=\min I.

if
\theta,\psi\in(-\infty,v_{1})
