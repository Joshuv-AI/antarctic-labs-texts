Localized High‐Concentration Electrolyte in Li‐Mediated Nitrogen Reduction for Ammonia Synthesis
Authors: Hyeju Yun, Chaeeun Lim, Minjun Kwon, Dongmin Lee, Yongju Yun, Dong‐Hwa Seo, Kijung Yong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lithium‐mediated nitrogen reduction reaction (Li‐NRR) is a promising green alternative to the Haber–Bosch process for ammonia synthesis. The solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) is crucial for high efficiency and stability, as it regulates reactant diffusion and suppresses side reactions. The SEI properties are greatly influenced by the Li+ ion solvation structure, which is controllable through electrolyte engineering. Although anion‐derived SEI enhances selectivity and stability, it has typically been engineered using high‐concentration electrolytes (HCEs), which face mass transfer, viscosity, and cost issues. In this study, a localized high‐concentration electrolyte (LHCE) in the Li‐NRR is first introduced, enabling the formation of anion‐derived SEI in a low‐concentration electrolyte (LCE) by enhancing the Li–anion coordination using an antisolvent. Among various antisolvents, 1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethyl‐2,2,3,3‐tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) achieves the highest ammonia Faradaic efficiency (73.6 ± 2.5%), more than double that of the LCE (34.3 ± 2.8%) and exceeding the HCE (56.0 ± 2.8%). Systematic calculations and experimental analyses show that the LHCE exhibits anion‐rich solvation structures and forms thin, inorganic SEI. Moreover, the LHCE has advantages of low viscosity and high N2 solubility, which facilitate mass transport. This study suggests the application of LHCE as an effective electrolyte engineering strategy to enhance the Li‐NRR efficiency.Keywords: ammonia, LHCE, Li‐mediated nitrogen reduction, SEI, solvation, TTE

Introduction

Ammonia is a high‐value‐added chemical used in various industrial fields, which has recently attracted much attention as an energy carrier, owing to its high volumetric energy density (15.6 MJ L−1).[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] The Haber–Bosch process is the conventional method used for ammonia synthesis; however, it requires a high temperature (400–500°C) and pressure (up to 200 bar), accounting for 1–2% of the global energy consumption, and leads to huge amounts of CO2 emissions during hydrogen production through steam reforming.[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] Therefore, the need for green ammonia production is emerging to reduce energy consumption and carbon emissions, and electrochemical ammonia synthesis has recently received great attention as an alternative approach in this context.[ 8 ] In particular, the lithium‐mediated nitrogen reduction reaction (Li‐NRR) has emerged as the most reliable method for ammonia synthesis, because it enables room temperature and ambient pressure reactions based on the high reducing ability of lithium.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ]

The Li‐NRR is known to proceed through a cyclic reaction of 1) lithium plating, 2) lithium nitridation, and 3) protonation reaction. Electrodeposited lithium cleaves the triple bond of N2 to form Li3N. The Li3N is then protonated to form NH3 while simultaneously releasing Li0 as Li+, enabling continuous cycling.[ 12 , 13 ] Considering the kinetics of the above reaction, it is important to control the mass transfer of the main reactants in the Li‐NRR: lithium ions, nitrogen gas, and protons.[ 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] The diffusion rate of these species can be determined by the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formed during Li plating.[ 18 , 19 , 20 ] The SEI is an ion‐conducting and electronically insulating layer composed of electrolyte decomposition products.[ 19 ] The physicochemical properties of the SEI control the permeability to reactants, which determines the uniformity of the Li plating and the NH3 selectivity. The electronic insulating properties of the SEI can also improve the process stability by inhibiting further decomposition of the electrolyte.

Recently, SEI engineering has been actively explored as a way to improve the efficiency of the Li‐NRR process.[ 13 , 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 ] Previous studies have shown that the SEI is generally modulated by the electrolyte components, which mainly consist of Li salt, proton source, and solvent.[ 13 , 17 , 19 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 ] In particular, the Li+ ion solvation structure has a significant impact on the interfacial chemistry, because Li+‐coordinated molecules can be preferentially reduced during the Li‐NRR process.[ 23 ] For low‐concentration electrolytes (LCEs), the Li+ ion solvation structure is dominated by Li+–solvent coordination, which leads to a solvent‐derived organic SEI at the electrode Figure 1a(i). On the other hand, in high‐concentration electrolytes (HCEs), an increased proportion of Li+–anion solvation structures form an anion‐derived SEI Figure 1a(ii). This anion‐derived SEI is known to have LiF‐like inorganic properties, which enhance the lateral diffusion of Li+ and improve the uniformity of Li plating with a high Faradaic efficiency (FE).[ 13 , 19 ] However, HCEs have unavoidable drawbacks such as high viscosity, slow mass transport, and high cost of the concentrated lithium salt.[ 31 ]

a) Schematic illustration of low‐concentration electrolytes (LCE), high‐concentration electrolytes (HCE), and localized high‐concentration electrolytes (LHCE). b) NH3 yield rates and Faradaic efficiencies for various LiTFSI concentrations at −40 mA cm−2 and 20 bar N2. c) NH3 yield rates and Faradaic efficiencies for various solvent compositions in 1 M LiTFSI at −40 mA cm−2 and 20 bar N2. d) Comparison of NH3 yield rates and Faradaic efficiencies of LCE, HCE, and LHCE. e) Dependence of Faradaic efficiency on N2 pressure in HCE and LHCE. The arrows indicate the decreasing ratio of FE compared to the initial value (at 20 bar).

To address the above issues, in this study, a localized high‐concentration electrolyte (LHCE) was introduced into the Li‐NRR for the first time. As shown in Figure 1a(iii), the LHCE can be prepared by adding an antisolvent that barely dissolves Li+ to the LCE. The antisolvent increases the proportion of Li+–anion solvation structures and induces a local high‐concentration state, forming an anion‐derived SEI.[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] Therefore, the LHCE can adopt the SEI structure of the HCE while maintaining the advantages of the LCE, such as low cost and viscosity. An additional advantage of the LHCE is that the low‐polar nature of the antisolvent can enhance the solubility and mass transfer of N2, favoring the production of NH3. In the present study, 1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethyl‐2,2,3,3‐tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) was selected as the antisolvent, owing to its low donor number (DN = 1.9), high electrochemical stability, and low polarity.[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 ] The LHCE was produced by mixing TTE and tetrahydrofuran (THF) in a low‐concentration (1 M) lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) solution. At the optimum TTE/THF mixing ratio, the LHCE achieved the maximum FE (73.6 ± 2.5%) and NH3 yield rate (101.7 ± 3.4 nmol s−1 cm−2), which were significantly improved compared to those of the LCE (34.3 ± 2.8%, 47.4 ± 3.8 nmol s−1 cm−2), and even higher than those of the HCE (56.0 ± 2.8%, 77.4 ± 3.9 nmol s−1 cm−2). Systematic analysis of the SEI and electrolyte showed that the LHCE effectively formed a LiF‐rich, thin, and uniform SEI. This is due to its anion‐rich solvation structure, which was confirmed through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The low viscosity and high N2 solubility of the LHCE represent additional advantages over the HCE. Taking into account all these effects, the application of the LHCE can be considered as a very effective electrolyte engineering strategy to enhance the performance of the Li‐NRR.

Results and Discussion

Effect of LHCE on Li‐NRR Process

Typically, Li‐NRR experiments were performed at −40 mA cm−2 and 20 bar N2 for 1 h using a custom‐designed electrochemical reactor under controlled water content (Figure S1 and Tables S1–S2, Supporting Information). The produced ammonia was quantified and cross‐validated using ion chromatography (IC), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and indophenol blue (IB) titration (Figures S2–S6, Supporting Information). A series of control experiments and isotope tests were also conducted to validate the process and confirm the source of nitrogen (Figures S7–S10 and Table S3, Supporting Information). Figure 1b shows the effect of the Li salt concentration on the NH3 yield rate and FE. Increasing the concentration of LiTFSI from 0.5 to 2.35 M resulted in improved Li‐NRR performances, confirming that the high‐concentration electrolyte led to a high NH3 yield rate and FE values. However, an excessively high concentration (3 M) of electrolyte resulted in the viscosity becoming too high (35.2 cP) and hindered the diffusion of reactants, thereby reducing the performance (Figure S11, Supporting Information). In similar results, computational analysis confirmed that the lowest EtOH diffusivity is observed in a high‐concentration electrolyte compared to low‐concentration ones (Figure S12, Supporting Information). In this study, 1 M electrolyte was assigned as LCE, because it is the most feasible low‐concentration electrolyte with stable electrochemical potential (Figure S13, Supporting Information). In addition, 2.35 M LiTFSI electrolyte was assigned as HCE for comparisons afterward.

Based on the effect of the concentration, TTE was used as an antisolvent to prepare the LHCE from the LCE (Table S1, Supporting Information). Figure 1c shows the trends of the NH3 yield rate and FE as a function of the volume ratio between TTE (V TTE) and THF (V THF) solvents in 1 M LiTFSI. The FE toward NH3 increased with the TTE content; maximum FE (73.6 ± 2.5%) and yield rate (101.7 ± 3.4 nmol s−1 cm−2) values were reached at an optimum V TTE:V THF ratio of 2:1 (Table S4, Supporting Information). We assigned this optimum electrolyte as LHCE for further analysis. Unlike the HCE, the LHCE maintained a low cost and viscosity (4.8 cP), which can facilitate mass transport (Figures S11 and S14, Supporting Information). The decrease in FE at a higher TTE content (V TTE:V THF = 3:1) in the LHCE is ascribed to the need for higher overpotentials, owing to the lower conductivity (Figure S15, Supporting Information).

Cyclic voltammetry in each electrolyte (Figure S16, Supporting Information) compares their reductive/oxidative behavior near lithium plating potential. For LCE, a significantly lower oxidative peak indicates that there was insufficient lithium available for stripping, even though a very high current was applied for reduction. This result indicates that a substantial portion of the charge was consumed by side reactions or that lithium was not plated uniformly. In contrast, HCE and LHCE showed a clear peak in the stripping region and lower voltage hysteresis compared to LCE. Even though the reductive current density was lower in LHCE due to the low conductivity of antisolvent, this did not have a negative impact on FE. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) result shows a reduction in resistance of the bulk SEI layer for LHCE, as shown in the Supporting Information (Figures S17–S18 and Table S5, Supporting Information). During the stability test, the Li‐NRR system in LHCE operated stably for 20 h. However, the ammonia yield became saturated due to the depletion of sacrificial proton donor, EtOH, leading to a decrease in the Faradaic efficiency of longer‐term operation (Figures S19–S20, Supporting Information).

Figure 1d compares the Li‐NRR performances of LCE, HCE, and LHCE. The LHCE showed much higher FE and NH3 yield rate values (73.6 ± 2.5%, 101.7 ± 3.4 nmol s−1 cm−2) compared to those of the LCE (34.3 ± 2.8%, 47.4 ± 3.8 nmol s−1 cm−2), and even higher than those of the HCE (56.0 ± 2.8%, 77.4 ± 3.9 nmol s−1 cm−2). The solubility of N2 is an essential factor for enhancing the FE and alleviating high‐pressure conditions. Figure 1e shows the dependence of the FE on the N2 pressure in HCE and LHCE. As the N2 pressure was reduced from 20 to 5 bar, the FE of the HCE decreased by 70% of the initial value. In contrast, the FE of the LHCE only decreased by 27%, showing a superior performance even at low N2 pressure. This finding is ascribed to the higher N2 solubility and diffusivity in the LHCE (Section D and Figures S21–S24 and Tables S6 and S7, Supporting Information). The N2 solubility in the LHCE was measured to be ≈1.4 times higher than that in HCE. Since TTE has low polarity due to its fluorine‐rich chain structure, the low‐polar LHCE will more easily dissolve non‐polar N2 than THF‐only based electrolytes according to the ‘like dissolves like’ principle.[ 39 , 40 ] Taking into account the sluggish kinetics of lithium nitridation, the high N2 solubility of the LHCE would contribute to its high NH3 selectivity.[ 15 ]

Analysis of Li+ Solvation Structures

MD simulations were performed to reveal the solvation structure of each electrolyte. Figures 2a–c display the final snapshots obtained from the MD simulations of the LCE, HCE, and LHCE systems. To illustrate the distributions of the anions, they were highlighted with green and thick bonds. In the LCE, the anions were found to be well dispersed, because the low concentration of salt enabled them to be separated well by the solvent molecules. In contrast, the anions tended to cluster together in the HCE, which can be explained by the reduced amount of solvent available to separate them, due to the high concentration of salt. Similarly, the anions were found to be aggregated in the LHCE, despite the latter containing the same amount of salt as the LCE. This is due to the presence of the TTE molecules, acting as antisolvent, which does not participate in the solvation structure of the Li+ ions, allowing anions to cluster around them. These results can be confirmed by examining the molecular structures of the first solvation shells around the Li+ ion (insets of Figures 2a–c).

The solvation structures around Li+ ions were quantitatively analyzed through the average radial distribution functions (RDFs) and coordination numbers (CNs) obtained from MD simulations of the LCE, HCE, and LHCE (Figure 2d–f; Figure S25, Supporting Information). The RDF, represented by g(r) in the figures, describes the probability of finding oxygen atoms of salt or solvent molecules at a distance r from Li+ ions.[ 28 ] The CN between Li+ ions and oxygen atoms can be determined by integrating the RDF in spherical coordinates up to a specific distance and multiplying by the number density of oxygen atoms.[ 25 ] The calculation methods are provided in the Supporting Information. Among the three electrolytes, the LCE exhibited the lowest CN of oxygen atoms in TFSI anions (TFSI_O) (2.06), along with the highest CN of oxygen atoms in THF solvent molecules (THF_O) (3.08). This indicates that Li+ ions were predominantly surrounded by solvent molecules. On the other hand, in the HCE, the CN of TFSI_O was higher (2.79), while that of THF_O was lower (2.45), owing to the high concentration of salt. The LHCE showed the highest CN of TFSI_O (2.85) and the lowest CN of THF_O (2.28) among the three electrolytes. This indicates that Li+ ions were mostly surrounded by anions in the LHCE.

Based on the experiments and MD simulations, we can predict the solvation structures of the LCE, HCE, and LHCE electrolytes, as illustrated in Figure 2g. The LCE mainly showed solvent‐separated ion pairs (SSIPs), whereas the HCE contained higher fractions of contact ion pairs (CIPs) and aggregates (AGGs), owing to the high concentration of salt. The LHCE showed similar results to the HCE, despite the low concentration of salt owing to the presence of the TTE antisolvent.

The solvation structures of LCE, HCE, and LHCE were also confirmed by Raman and Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy. In the full Raman spectra of each electrolyte (Figure 3a), the lower Raman shift of the S─N─S peak (738.7 cm−1) for the LCE indicates a weaker Li+–TFSI− interaction. The sharp C─O (911.3 cm−1) peak corresponds to free THF, which was mainly present in the LCE because of the excessive amount of THF creating a solvent‐rich solvation structure.[ 33 ] When the Li salt concentration increased from the LCE to HCE, a blue shift was observed in the S─N─S (740.2 cm−1) vibration region, corresponding to an increased Li+–TFSI− coordination.[ 41 ] An additional blue shift in the C─O (915.0 cm−1) vibration region was due to a change in the THF molecular symmetry caused by the Li+ ion solvation.[ 42 ] For the LHCE, similar shifts to the HCE were observed for the S─N─S (741.6 cm−1) and C─O (915.1 cm−1) peaks, indicating that the LHCE had a similar solvation structure to the HCE. The Raman spectrum of TTE (Figure S26, Supporting Information) also suggests that TTE did not participate in the Li+ ion solvation, but only affected the formation of a localized‐high concentration state, as confirmed in Figure 2.

The deconvolution of the S─N─S vibration peak provides additional information on the Li+–TFSI− ion pair composition in each electrolyte, as shown in Figure 3b. The ion pairs are classified into three groups according to the Li+–TFSI− coordination: SSIPs (736.2 cm−1), CIPs (741.2 cm−1), and AGGs (746.0 cm−1). A significantly higher (CIP + AGG)/SSIP area ratio was observed for both HCE (2.35) and LHCE (2.69) compared to LCE (0.96), indicating the dominant proportion of anion‐rich solvation structures in HCE and LHCE. These solvation structures were also confirmed by the FT‐IR analysis in Figure 3c. The stronger Li+–TFSI− coordination in the S─N vibration region resulted in a blue shift in the HCE (740.7, 790.7 cm−1) and LHCE (742.5, 794.6 cm−1) spectra compared to the LCE (740.5, 788.8 cm−1). In the C─O vibration region, an enhanced Li+–THF coordination (1044.7 cm−1) and a reduced free THF content (1060.8 cm−1) were observed for the HCE and LHCE (Figure 3c; Figure S27, Supporting Information).[ 43 ] Because free THF can occupy the inner Helmholtz layer in electrochemical systems, this coordination can further enhance the stability of the electrolyte.[ 44 ] The full Raman and FT‐IR spectra of all components confirm the origin of the peaks and the accuracy of the analyses (Figures S26–S27, Supporting Information). The 7Li NMR results further confirm the solvation structures in each electrolyte (Figure 3d). The upfield shift observed for the HCE supports the increased ion pairing due to the concentration effect. In contrast, the downfield shift observed in the LHCE reflects the weak solvation ability of TTE, explaining its localized high‐concentration state where it remains outside the solvation shell.[ 45 ]

Analysis of SEI

The physicochemical properties of the SEI are strongly dependent on the Li+ ion solvation structures because the SEI consists of electrolyte decomposition products. First of all, the chemical composition of the SEI varied greatly depending on the Li+ ion solvation structure. Figures 4a–c compare the Li 1s, F 1s, and S 2p XPS spectra for the LCE, HCE, and LHCE systems. The Li 1s spectrum (Figure 4a) shows peaks centered at higher binding energies in the HCE and LHCE, suggesting a higher proportion of LiF (55.8 eV) than Li2O/Li2CO3. The F 1s spectrum (Figure 4b) also shows a significantly higher LiF/C–F ratio for HCE (2.1) and LHCE (6.7) compared to LCE (0.6), indicating the formation of LiF‐rich SEIs in HCE and LHCE. This demonstrates that the anion‐rich solvation structure promoted the preferential reduction of TFSI−, in agreement with the electrolyte analysis discussed above. The additional gap of LiF/C–F value between HCE and LHCE shows that the LHCE could form LiF‐rich SEI more effectively than the HCE despite the similar anion‐rich solvation structures. This effect is ascribed to the lower viscosity of LHCE, which enables faster transport of CIP and AGG to the electrode.

The LiF/C–F area ratio in b) indicates the degree of decomposition degree of TFSI−. d–f) Top‐view SEM images of SEI in LCE (top), HCE (middle), and LHCE (bottom). Scale bars are inserted at the bottom of each image. Insets in e) and f) indicate higher magnification images showing the porous morphology of SEI formed from LHCE compared to HCE.

The presence of the Li2S peak (162.6 eV) in addition to the TFSI− and S─O peaks in the S 2p spectra of HCE and LHCE (Figure 4c) also indicates the complete decomposition of TFSI−, suggesting that their thin SEIs are mainly composed of inorganic components. These inorganic components are known to suppress side reactions and improve stability, which is consistent with the decrease in Li2CO3 content in the C 1s spectrum (Figure S28, Supporting Information).[ 19 ] The complete XPS spectra of all systems confirm the presence of relevant SEI elements (Figure S29, Supporting Information). The presence of Ni peaks in HCE confirms the thin SEI, while the F auger peak in LHCE confirms the formation of a LiF‐rich SEI. In contrast, neither Ni nor F Auger peaks are detected in LCE, indicating that the electrode is fully covered by a thick SEI with a low F content. The 3D image mappings of TOF‐SIMS also confirm that relevant atoms (Li, C, O, N, F, S, Ni) and components (LiF, CF, SO, Li2S) are present in both the outer and inner layer of SEI for LHCE. A higher intensity of LiF compared to CF in the depth profile is also consistent with the XPS results (Figures S30–S31, Supporting Information). Moreover, the X‐ray diffraction (XRD) analysis showed no detectable crystalline components in the SEI under these operating conditions (Figure S32, Supporting Information).[ 46 ]

The physical morphology of the SEI also changed dramatically with the electrolyte composition. In the case of the LCE, an uncontrollably thick SEI was observed in the SEM image of Figure 4d. Moreover, a thick SEI coating was visible on the working electrode after the reaction under LCE conditions (Figure S33, Supporting Information). On the other hand, very thin and uniform SEIs were formed in the HCE (Figure 4e) and LHCE (Figure 4f) cases. Energy dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis also confirmed differences in SEI uniformity formed in each electrolyte (Figures S34–S39, Supporting Information). The Ni atomic ratio of each electrolyte shows significant differences. In particular, low amounts of Ni were exposed in LCE, while more than 50% Ni was detected in HCE and LHCE. This high level of Ni exposure indicates that a very thin SEI is formed in HCE and LHCE, whereas in LCE the electrode is covered with a thick and non‐uniform SEI.

In particular, a more porous SEI morphology was observed in the LHCE than the HCE. Steinberg et al. reported that a porous SEI is formed due to the reaction of EtOH, which simultaneously produces H2 gas and lithium alkoxide (ROLi).[ 27 ] LHCE can maintain this porous structure due to its high LiF content, which is mechanically stable and insoluble in the electrolyte.[ 47 ] However, the porous structure was not well maintained in HCE because the lower LiF content compared to LHCE allowed the less robust SEI to dissolve in the electrolyte. This is consistent with the lower amount of ROLi detected in SEI for HCE. (Figure S28, Supporting Information). Therefore, the porous SEI of the LHCE is expected to increase the permeability to reactants such as N2 and increase the FE.[ 17 , 20 ] These results prove that the formation of a thick organic SEI in the LCE is due to its solvent‐rich solvation structure, while thin and inorganic LiF‐rich SEIs are formed in the HCE and LHCE as a result of their anion‐rich solvation shells. The influence of the antisolvent on solvation and its subsequent effect on the SEI was observed; however, its impact on the protonation process of Li3N is negligible (Tables S8–S9, Supporting Information).

Application of Various Antisolvents in LHCE Formation

For LHCE formation, various antisolvents other than TTE can be applied. Chen et al. proposed a DN < 10 as an effective descriptor to identify suitable antisolvents for the design of LHCEs.[ 32 ] According to this criterion, we tested various representative antisolvents that have previously been employed in Li ion batteries.[ 32 , 33 , 34 , 38 ] Figure 5a summarizes the properties of three groups of antisolvents according to the molecular structure: non‐fluorinated rings (anisole and furan), fluorinated rings (m‐fluorotoluene (mFT)), and fluorinated ethers (TTE) and 1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethyl‐2,2,2‐trifluoroethyl ether (TFETFE)). Some hydrocarbons are also known to have a DN lower than 10, but were not considered in this study owing to their immiscibility with THF.[ 32 ] The results of the application of various antisolvent‐based LHCEs to the Li‐NRR are compared in Figure 5b. Among the three antisolvent groups, the fluorinated ethers (TTE and TFETFE) showed the highest NH3 yield and FE. The other two antisolvent groups (fluorinated and non‐fluorinated rings) showed lower NH3 yield and FE values. The pseudo‐energy efficiency (EE) was also evaluated for each antisolvent (Section C‐3, Supporting information), and showed a similar trend. In particular, furan exhibited the lowest EE, which is further discussed below in relation to its stability.

Along with the performance in the Li‐NRR process, other important factors in the evaluation of an electrolyte are the stability against decomposition and the required power consumption during the reaction.[ 29 , 48 , 49 ] Figure 5c shows the LUMO and HOMO energy levels of each antisolvent. The order of HOMO energies is TFETFE (−9.68) < TTE (−9.61) < mFT (−6.89) < furan (−6.51) < anisole (−6.28); a lower value corresponds to higher oxidative stability.[ 50 ] This order of HOMO energies matches the color of the electrolyte after the reaction, another stability descriptor. (Figure S40, Supporting Information). The UV–vis absorbance spectra of each antisolvent‐based LHCE after 1 h of reaction show that the non‐fluorinated rings showed the lowest stability with the highest light absorbance, making it unsuitable for Li‐NRR (Figure S41, Supporting Information). In contrast, fluorinated ethers exhibited the lowest absorbance, demonstrating their superior stability under the operating conditions of Li‐NRR. The significantly high HOMO‐LUMO energy gap of the fluorinated ethers (TTE: 10.15, TFETFE: 10.69) confirms their high electrochemical stability.

Figure 5d shows the potential profiles of various antisolvent‐based LHCEs. Unlike other antisolvents with similar anode and cathode profiles, furan and anisole showed significantly different counter electrode (CE) potentials. First, the huge CE potential for furan is due to the uncontrollable growth of insulating deposits on the CE (Figure S42, Supporting Information). These deposits are severe polymerization products of furan, which has inferior oxidative stability. Furan can be preferentially oxidized at low onset potential due to its high HOMO energy (Figure S43, Supporting Information). As a result, this inhibits overall current flow, increasing cell potential and lowering NH3 production. Anisole showed the lowest CE potential, contrary to furan, indicating preferential oxidation according to the highest HOMO energy without insulating precipitates. Instead, the oxidation products caused severe electrolyte degradation, as evidenced by the electrolyte color (Figures S40–S41, Supporting Information).

Figure 5e summarizes the performance of each antisolvent‐based LHCE. Five indicators were used for evaluating the antisolvent: 1) FE, 2) EE, 3) ammonia yield, 4) electrochemical stability, and 5) cell potential. According to these indicators, the fluorinated ethers (TTE and TFETFE) showed the best overall performances; fluorinated rings (mFT) also showed moderate stability, but also an inferior performance to fluorinated ethers. These results are related to the proportion of highly electronegative fluorine in the antisolvent. In comparison, the non‐fluorinated rings showed poor electrochemical stability, which negatively affected the overall process performance. This highlights the critical importance of antisolvent stability under high current conditions in the Li‐NRR, which requires a distinct antisolvent selection strategy from that used for Li‐ion batteries.

Combined Effects of LHCE in Li‐NRR

Figure 6 summarizes the characteristics of each electrolyte system in the Li‐NRR. The LCE benefited from the use of low‐concentration (1 m) lithium salts, resulting in relatively low costs as well as a low viscosity (1.1 cP). Although the low‐viscosity LCE had moderate N2 solubility (2.00 mm bar−1), the thick and non‐uniform SEI derived from the solvent‐rich solvation structure led to a low ammonia selectivity (34.3 ± 2.8%). On the other hand, the HCE exhibited a high ammonia selectivity (56.0 ± 2.8%), owing to the thin and uniform LiF‐rich SEI derived from the anion‐rich solvation structure. However, the use of high concentrations (2.35 m) of lithium salts resulted in high costs along with a high viscosity (10.4 cP), associated with a low N2 solubility (1.57 mm bar−1). The LHCE combined the advantages of both LCE and HCE: low cost, low viscosity (4.8 cP), and high selectivity (73.6 ± 2.5%). In particular, it employed low concentrations (1 m) of lithium salts, similar to LCE, while also mimicking the anion‐rich solvation structure of HCE through the weak solvation effect of antisolvents. This led to the formation of a thin and uniform LiF‐rich SEI. Furthermore, the low polarity of the LHCE combined with its low viscosity led to the highest N2 solubility (2.26 mM bar−1), thereby resulting in the highest ammonia production.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced a localized high‐concentration electrolyte for inducing the formation of an anion‐derived SEI in the Li‐NRR and enhanced the ammonia synthesis through electrolyte engineering. The LHCE was successfully formed by adding an antisolvent to a low‐concentration (1 M) LiTFSI solution in THF. The anion‐rich solvation structure of the LHCE was confirmed by MD simulations as well as Raman, FT‐IR, and 7Li NMR spectroscopy. The LHCE promoted the formation of a thin and inorganic LiF‐rich SEI, which suppressed side reactions and regulated the diffusion of reactants. At the optimum mixing ratio of TTE and THF (V TTE:V THF = 2:1), the LHCE achieved the maximum FE (73.6 ± 2.5%) and NH3 yield rate (101.7 ± 3.4 nmol s−1 cm−2), representing a significant improvement over LCE (34.3 ± 2.8%, 47.4 ± 3.8 nmol s−1 cm−2) and also exceeding the HCE performances (56.0 ± 2.8%, 77.4 ± 3.9 nmol s−1 cm−2). In addition to low cost, low viscosity, and high selectivity, which combine the favorable features of LCE and HCE, the LHCE also exhibited the additional advantage of high nitrogen solubility. Among various antisolvents tested in the current study, TTE showed the best performance in terms of ammonia selectivity, low cell potential, and electrochemical stability. Taking into account all these effects, the use of an LHCE can be considered as a promising electrolyte engineering strategy for enhancing the green ammonia synthesis by Li‐NRR.

Experimental Section

Electrochemical Experiment

The Li‐NRR experiments were conducted in a single‐compartment batch reactor with three electrodes: a nickel working electrode, a platinum counter electrode, and a platinum pseudo‐reference electrode. The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (99.95%) in THF (99.9%) or TTE (99.95%) solvent, with absolute ethanol (99.5%) as a proton donor. The cell was assembled in an Ar environment, flushed with N2 (99.999%) or Ar (99.999%) gas, and chronopotentiometry was performed at −40 mA cm− 2. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and cyclic voltammetry were also conducted to characterize the system (Section A, Supporting Information).

Quantification of Ammonia

Ammonia quantification was performed using three methods: colorimetric, ion chromatography (IC), and NMR. The colorimetric method involved measuring absorbance at 630 nm after adding a color agent and calculating ammonia concentration via a calibration curve. In IC, ions were separated based on retention time, with NH4 + quantified by comparing peak areas to a calibration curve. In the NMR method, 1H NMR was used with maleic acid as an internal standard, correlating peak areas to ammonia concentration. Cross‐validation of results was performed using all three methods to ensure accuracy (Section B, Supporting Information).

Characterization

Electrode characterization was performed to analyze SEI using SEM, XPS, TOF‐SIMS, and XRD with all samples prepared in an Ar‐filled glove box to prevent air exposure. Electrolyte characterization involved the use of a Rheometer for viscosity measurements, as well as Raman spectroscopy, FT‐IR, and 7Li NMR for coordination analysis. Detailed specifications and methods for each characterization are provided (Section F, Supporting Information).

Computational method

MD simulations were run in LAMMPS with optimized molecules and ESP charges from second‐order Møller–Plesset theory, using the OPLS force field. The simulations employed minimized boxes generated by PACKMOL, with trajectory analysis performed using VMD and MD Analysis. The HOMO and LUMO energies were calculated using the B3LYP functional and def2‐TZVP basis set in an implicit solvent model. Detailed computational methods are also given (Section E, Supporting Information).
