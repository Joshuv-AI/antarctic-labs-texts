Group-based telehealth stress management—lifestyle interventions are feasible for patients with chronic liver disease
Authors: Donna M. Evon, Chuning Liu, Cathryn Rollason, Suzanne C. Lechner, Ashley R. Brown, Dawn S. Harrison, Paul Stewart, Donald E. Bailey, Leslie A. Morland, A. Sidney Barritt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Group-based, telehealth behavioral interventions may be efficient, effective, and acceptable treatment modalities for patients with chronic liver diseases; however, pilot feasibility and patient satisfaction data are needed to justify efficacy trials. Given the poor quality of life, symptoms, and health risk factors, patients with chronic hepatitis C–associated liver disease (HCV-LD) may benefit from psychosocial interventions. A pilot feasibility study of a videoconferencing-delivered cognitive behavioral coping skills intervention (“VC-CBCS”) was conducted to evaluate multiple indicators of feasibility, levels of patient satisfaction, and changes in patient-reported outcomes (PROs).Methods:We pilot tested a 14-week, 90-minute VC-CBCS intervention by conducting a small randomized controlled trial. Thirty-two adults with HCV-LD and high stress/symptom levels were randomized to the protocolized VC-CBCS intervention (n=24) or standard of care (n=8), in 4 waves of 7–9 participants in 2020–2021.Results:Based upon rates of approach to enrollment (60%), retention (84%), PRO data collection (99.4%), and session attendance (91%), a future efficacy trial appears highly feasible. Levels of participant satisfaction were encouraging for VC delivery, intervention content and structure, and group processes. Technological and behavioral issues were minor. Collection of in-home saliva testing was not feasible. Moderate to large pre–post-intervention effect sizes were observed for some mediators (perceived stress, coping, relaxation, self-efficacy, sleep hygiene), with some of these changes correlating with small to moderate improvements in mental health and symptoms (eg, depression, fatigue; effect size range: 0.23–0.47). No consistent patterns were evident between changes in eating and exercise behaviors and PROs.Conclusions:A group-based VC-CBCS intervention appears feasible and may address unmet psychosocial needs in patients living with chronic liver disease.

INTRODUCTION

Healthcare delivery models are rapidly evolving to reduce patient burden and increase access to effective interventions. Electronic health technologies are diverse and include telehealth services such as live synchronous videoconferencing (VC), remote patient monitoring, mobile health applications, and case-based teleconferencing.1 Telehealth services reduce patient and provider burden, overcome provider shortages for rural and underserved populations, help patients better manage health conditions, and increase access to integrated, multidisciplinary teams.1

Delivering psychosocial interventions to small groups of patients, rather than in one-on-one settings, is efficient and cost-effective. Strong evidence exists for the effectiveness of internet-based cognitive behavioral therapies (iCBT) delivered in routine clinical practice for depression, anxiety, insomnia, and pain.23 VC group-based interventions are comparable to in-person groups.45 Moreover, groups leverage therapeutic processes such as cohesiveness, universality, and peer support that improve engagement, retention, accountability, and outcomes.6 Importantly, group-based VC interventions are reimbursable clinical services for psychologists and mental health professionals, whereas many other electronic health technologies (eg, mHealth apps) are not. Although many forms of telehealth and digital technologies are being studied in patients with chronic liver diseases,789 group-based interventions delivered using synchronous VC technology are still rare.

Hepatitis C viral (HCV) infection affects between 2.5 and 4 million people in the United States and has not decreased despite highly effective therapies.10 Even after achieving viral cure, many patients may benefit from behavioral interventions focused on disease self-management and lifestyle modifications to improve health trajectories. Chronic HCV is a multifaceted systemic disease with extrahepatic disorders.11 Comorbidities and symptoms (eg, fatigue, sleep, pain, neuropsychiatric symptoms) affect over half of patients.12 Longstanding mental health issues and impaired quality of life are common, even after viral cure.1314 Among those who achieve viral cure, unhealthy lifestyle habits perpetuate symptoms, contribute to risk for metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), diabetes, liver fibrosis, and cancer, unless healthier lifestyles are adopted.151617 Hence, there is an enormous unmet need to develop and evaluate psychosocial interventions for patients with chronic liver disease, including the growing population with HCV-associated liver disease (HCV-LD) and MASLD, that contribute to personal, economic, and financial burden.1819

Using the Stage Model of Behavioral Therapies step-by-step plan for intervention development and evaluation20 and building off a large evidence base of theoretically informed efficacious cognitive behavioral stress management interventions developed for other chronic disease populations,212223 our group previously adapted, pilot-tested and refined an in-person, group-based, cognitive behavioral coping skills intervention (CBCS) tailored to the needs of patients with HCV-LD undergoing antiviral therapy.2425 The initial version of the CBCS intervention provided relaxation training, coping skills, and stress management, but did not address lifestyle habits. Moderate to large effect sizes were observed in the change in psychological distress, depression, anger, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and fatigue.25 Based on patient feedback and lessons learned from the in-person group-based CBCS, 2 adaptations were made. First, the number of sessions was increased to incorporate modules for healthy lifestyle habits. Second, based on feedback from those who could not attend in-person sessions due to work/travel issues, we adapted the intervention to be delivered synchronously in small groups using WebEx VC technology. The specific objectives of this pilot feasibility study were to evaluate multiple indicators of feasibility (both intervention and trial feasibility), patient satisfaction, and pre–post changes in potential mediators and outcomes to help inform future randomized controlled trials (RCT) of group-based VC-CBCS interventions.

METHODS

Study design

This was a single-site, pilot feasibility study that employed a small 2-arm RCT design with 32 participants assigned to VC-CBCS or standard of care (SC) by 3:1 randomization at a large tertiary care academic medical center. Four waves of participants were randomized, consisting of 7–9 patients each. For example, wave #1 randomized 7 participants (5=VC-CBCS, 2=SC) and wave #2 randomized 9 participants (7=VC-CBCS, 2=SC). The 3:1 randomization was chosen to provide adequate data from 4 waves (a total of 24 participants) to evaluate the feasibility of the intervention, along with 8 participants randomized to SC to evaluate retention and data collection. In both study arms, assessments were conducted pre-intervention (week 0), week 5, week 10, and post-intervention (weeks 14–15). For the purpose of this report, changes in PROs are reported from pre-intervention to post-intervention (weeks 0–14). In the VC-CBCS cohort, standardized measures of participant satisfaction with sessions were collected weekly, and global satisfaction surveys were collected at weeks 5 and 14. The research team completed the feasibility measures.

Participants

This study and all research was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the University of North Carolina (IRB# 19-2197) and all research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. Prior to the pandemic, written consent was given in writing by participants. After the pandemic began, the UNC IRB approved patients to provide verbal consent over the phone using an IRB-approved process whereby the research coordinator obtaining verbal consent signed/dated the consent form. Participants were mailed a copy of the written consent form for reference, but were not ﻿required to return a signed copy to the study team. Participants were eligible if they were 21 years or older, medically cleared by a hepatology provider, currently or previously diagnosed with chronic HCV-LD, reported needing support for symptoms, stress, or unhealthy lifestyle habits, defined as ≥4 on a numeric rating scale (0=not at all to 10=yes, absolutely) on 2 or more screening questions, and were able to read and speak English (see Supplemental Information, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C194). Participants were excluded if they had decompensated liver disease; a life expectancy of <12 months as estimated by a hepatologist; received or wait-listed for liver transplant; reported current, severe alcohol or substance use disorder, psychiatric disorders, or cognitive impairment that was likely to interfere with group participation. Participants were also required to have a private, quiet space in the home to participate in telehealth sessions.

Screening and recruitment

This study was funded and initiated before the COVID-19 pandemic. Screening and recruitment began in March 2020 in-person, pivoted to all remote procedures, and ended in September 2020. Patients were identified by 2 hepatology providers in outpatient or virtual HCV clinics. Interested patients were referred to the research team and were screened for eligibility by phone. The initial phone screening process included the Substance Abuse and Mental Illness Brief Symptom Screener (SAMISS)26 with additional questions related to mental health and substance use disorders. Positive screens were followed by a more thorough phone assessment by the lead investigator/psychologist to determine safety and appropriateness for group participation.

Randomization

A computer-based procedure for permuted-block randomization and concealment was created by non-study staff in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system that automatically randomized each wave of 7–9 participants. In order to provide the investigators with adequate experience conducting 4 waves of VC-CBCS groups, we selected a 3:1 randomization procedure, randomizing 24 participants to VC-CBCS and 8 to SC, for a targeted enrollment of 32.

Treatment conditions

Standard of care (SC) arm

Participants randomized to the SC condition were managed per HCV guidelines by hepatology providers in the HCV clinics and typically followed every 6–12 months, depending on cirrhosis status. During the COVID pandemic, providers used telehealth (phone, virtual appointments). Co-located psychosocial care was not standard, but patients could be referred to specialists, as needed. Research staff had no contact with SC participants except during survey and saliva collection windows. The purpose of including the SC arm was to evaluate the feasibility of randomization, retention, and data collection, not to draw comparisons between conditions.

VC-CBCS group–based intervention arm

The CBCS intervention was modeled from evidence-based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Informational–Motivation–Behavioral skills (IMB) theoretical frameworks (Figure 1).212326 Development of the CBCS intervention was an iterative process following the Stage Model of Behavioral Therapies steps20 that included a needs assessment, patient feedback, and refinements described in prior CBCS publications.2425 The current VC-CBCS intervention consisted of 14 weekly sessions (duration 90–120 min) that included practice of relaxation techniques and didactics related to liver health, healthy lifestyle habits, stress management, coping skills, and medication adherence (Table 1). Hard copy Patient Workbooks were mailed to participants, and electronic versions were accessible on a private web-based drive. Each session was divided into 3 phases: (i) relaxation/mindfulness practice; (ii) review of the previous session’s content, discussion of participants’ success with meeting weekly goals while leveraging peer support and problem-solving; and (iii) new didactic content and individual goal-setting. Participants were encouraged to log into REDCap after each session to add goals related to that week’s content and to rate the importance, motivation, and confidence associated with each goal.

Content and structure of 14 VC-CBCS group sessions

Abbreviation: VC-CBCS, videoconferencing-delivered cognitive behavioral coping skills intervention.

Sessions were delivered virtually using a university-approved, HIPAA-compliant WebEx VC system. Participants had the choice of using their own computer system with integrated VC capabilities or a study-provided iPad with internet access. VC-CBCS sessions were facilitated by a Master’s-level therapist receiving supervision from the lead investigator (Donna M. Evon). A research coordinator was available before and during sessions to troubleshoot technology/internet issues.

Data collection and assessments

Data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of North Carolina.27 REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies. Pre-intervention, participants entered self-report data using an email link directly into REDCap. SAMISS screening data were reported verbally to the research team and entered into the database.28 Self-reported data were cross-validated with data from participants’ electronic health records (EHRs). The following laboratory and clinical data were abstracted from EHRs by the research team if available at baseline (valid window: 6 months before enrollment) and post-intervention (valid window: within 6 months after week 14): complete blood count (CBC); metabolic and hepatic laboratory panels; cirrhosis status; health comorbidities; number of daily medications; HCV treatment status; weight; and body mass index.

Patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures were collected from all participants at 4 timepoints: baseline (within 30 days before the first session), week 5, week 10, and post-intervention (within 14 days after the final session). Participant surveys took ~20–30 minutes to complete. Participants in both arms received $25 added to a rechargeable Visa gift card as compensation for completing each of 4 surveys and 2 days of saliva samples (total $150).

Saliva samples were collected by participants at home to measure the change in cortisol levels, an objective measure of stress. After enrollment, all participants were shipped 2 sets of salivary cortisol kits, return shipment packing, and written instructions for performing passive drool sample collection. Verbal instructions were provided by the research team on the day before collection. Participants were instructed to collect 4 passive drool saliva samples 1 day before and 1 day after the intervention: (1) immediately upon awakening, (2) 30 minutes after awakening, (3) late afternoon, and (4) before bedtime.

VC-CBCS participants were reimbursed for upgraded internet speed and given 2 options to compensate for session attendance: (a) retain the iPad (worth $300) after the study ends or (b) return the iPad and receive up to $300 added to the rechargeable gift card, pro-rated for the number of sessions attended ($21.40 per session). They were also reimbursed $25 to attend the group exit interview.

Measures

Trial and intervention feasibility

The feasibility of conducting a future RCT and delivering a group-based behavioral intervention using VC technology was measured by first examining the number and proportion of participants approached, screened, eligible for enrollment, consented, randomized, and retained. Secondly, we tracked session attendance, number of PRO assessments completed, data completeness, and frequency of any technological or logistical problems for each wave in a feasibility spreadsheet. Thirdly, we evaluated therapist protocol fidelity to the therapist manual and therapist competency to manage group dynamics using observer-rating forms completed by a research coordinator who coded all sessions. These forms were initially developed at the University of Miami for use in other stress management intervention trials, and adapted for the current study by authors (Donna M. Evon, Suzanne C. Lechner).29 The Therapist Protocol Fidelity form rated the percentage of session material that was covered on a scale of 0%–100%. The Therapist Competency Form used a 14-item scale to rate skills such as “actively maintains good group dynamics,” “genuine,” and “empathetic” on a 1 (not at all) to 4 (extremely) scale. The therapist did not have access to ratings until the completion of each wave.

Participant satisfaction and acceptability

VC-CBCS participant satisfaction was measured quantitatively using process surveys and qualitatively using exit interviews.

Participant satisfaction with VC-CBCS intervention content

A 14-item acceptability/satisfaction survey used in prior cognitive behavioral stress management interventions2429 was completed by VC-CBCS participants after each session. Items included questions like “The content was informative today,” “The content was relevant to me,” I can use what I learned today.” Items are scored on a scale from 1 = “not at all” to 5 = “extremely useful.” Data across all participants and all 14 sessions were averaged into a Total Patient Satisfaction mean score, ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction.

Participant satisfaction with therapeutic group process

Three items from the Working Alliance Inventory (WAI) measured satisfaction with participant–therapist bonding.30 Four items from the Group Climate Questionnaire (GCQ) measured participant-to-participant dynamics and group cohesiveness.31 Eleven items from the Therapeutic Factors Inventory-Short Form (TFI) measured satisfaction with the overall group experience.32 All surveys included 7-point response options with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction. Group processes were evaluated at weeks 5 and 14.

Participant technology acceptability and satisfaction

The Telehealth Patient Satisfaction Questionnaire33 was modified for this context. Participants rated 15 items on a 5-point Likert scale of “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree,” regarding their comfort with telehealth and its potential benefits; higher scores reflected greater satisfaction. A satisfaction change score was computed comparing week 5 to week 14.

Exit group interviews

Group interviews were conducted by the lead investigator after each VC-CBCS cohort ended using a semi-structured interview guide to obtain participant feedback on program content and structure (see Supplemental Information, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C195). Notes were written in real time by 3 research staff during all 4 waves and aggregated into debriefing notes. Two group-based and 1 individual exit interview were audio-recorded, transcribed, and illustrative quotes extracted. Transcripts were reviewed and pertinent details added to the debriefing notes to aggregate feedback from the 4 waves. Formal coding in a qualitative software program was not conducted.

Adverse events

All study participants were asked to report any study-related harms at weeks 5, 10, and 14 by responding to 1 item: “Have you experienced any physical, mental, or social harms or discomforts from participating in this study?

We explored effect sizes of pre-to-post change in VC-CBCS participants’ PROs to inform primary and secondary outcomes for a future trial. Table 2 provides a description of PRO measures. Secondary or “intermediate” outcome measures captured potential mediators or “active ingredients” underlying the positive effects of the intervention. Measures included several items from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement System (PROMIS),3435 including global health, symptoms, and self-efficacy, measures of perceived stress,36 coping skills,37 sleep,40 individual diet and exercise items from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS),43 and individual items from the SAMISS screener regarding alcohol and drug use.28

Patient-reported outcome measures

Data analysis plan

Analytic strategies examined indicators of intervention feasibility, clinical trial feasibility, and patient satisfaction to generate information to inform a subsequent efficacy trial; thus, no hypothesis testing was conducted. Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, range, SD, frequency, proportion, the Cohen d effect size,45 Pearson correlations, and Spearman correlations were computed using R software (version 4.3.2, released October 31, 2023).

Participant (n=32) sociodemographic and clinical data are provided in Table 3. Seventy-five percent of participants were male, with an average age of 56. The sample was racially diverse. In general, participants’ educational attainment was high school (93%), and one-third were employed. Most participants (90%) requested an iPad to participate. Over half (61%) were diagnosed with cirrhosis, and 84% had achieved viral cure.

Trial and intervention feasibility

Recruitment and retention

Of the 53 patients approached about the study, 39 (74%) consented, and 32 (60%) enrolled and randomized. It took ~6 months (March 2020–September 2020) for 2 providers to refer and the research team to approach 53 patients (remote after March 13, 2020), with an average enrollment of 8 patients per month. Of the 32 randomized, 27 (84%) were retained: 8 (100%) in SC arm (CI of 90% [71%, 100%]) and 19 of 24 (79%) in the intervention arm (CI of 90% [62.7%, 89.8%]) Of the 5 participants randomized to VC-CBCS who were not retained, 3 did not initiate treatment (1 reported new medical issues; 2 with unknown reasons), while 2 dropped out after 1–2 sessions and were nonresponsive to outreach efforts (Figure 2).

VC-CBCS session attendance

Of the 24 participants randomized to VC-CBCS, 19 (79%) completed the intervention. These 19 attended the first 3 sessions and completed the 14-week intervention, with an average session attendance of 91% (range: 71%–100%). (Supplemental Appendix S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C179).

Data collection

Of 27 participants (8 SC, 19 VC-CBCS) retained in the study, 99.4% of all PRO measures were completed. Depending on the clinical or laboratory variable, EHR data were missing for ~30%–40% of participants, especially post-intervention, and this was attributed to patients not returning to the clinic during the pandemic. As a result of missing post-intervention data, we were unable to evaluate change in several clinical markers of interest (eg, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, A1C, lipids).

VC-CBCS technical, logistical, and behavioral issues

Twenty issues (several redundant) were recorded in the feasibility spreadsheet. Only 2 out of 24 VC-CBCS participants experienced technology issues with internet speed, iPad use, WebEx, and REDCap. One participant with low internet connectivity required assistance with upgrading their cellular plan, which resolved the issue. Out of 56 sessions delivered (4 waves × 14 sessions), 2 brief privacy incidents occurred, which led us to expand our Behavioral Group Guidelines for subsequent waves with no further incidents.

Harms/adverse events

No study participants reported any physical, mental, or social harms or discomforts as a result of study participation.

Collection of saliva samples

Participants reported multiple barriers related to in-home passive drool saliva collection. Participant-related barriers included difficulty and frustration with passive drool collection, pandemic-related barriers, and post office-related difficulty with returning saliva samples within 24 hours of collection, timing of delivery, and refrigerated storage. In addition, some participants declined to use the kits, did not return samples on time, were employed in third-shift work that impacted sleep schedules and cortisol, or were nonadherent with instructions. Missing data precluded the analysis of salivary cortisol.

Therapist protocol fidelity and therapist competency

Therapist fidelity to the VC-CBCS protocol was high in all waves (wave 1: 92%, wave 2: 92%, wave 3: 93%, wave 4: 98%) and across all 14 sessions (total mean 94%). The lowest fidelity scores were noted for module 12 on activity–rest cycles and anger management (79%), whereas the highest fidelity was observed for module 1 (99.9%). In general, therapists completed the first 2 phases of each module almost 100% of the time; however, they tended to run out of time presenting new didactic material and conducting individual goal-setting exercises. In terms of therapist competency, the average observer-rating of therapist competency was high (total mean score 3.99; range 3.83–4.0) across all session averages.

Participant acceptability and satisfaction with VC-CBCS telehealth intervention (n=19)

Intervention acceptability and satisfaction

Mean satisfaction scores of the content of weekly sessions were 4.22 out of 5 (range: 3.52–4.94), suggesting that participants were “A lot” to “Extremely” satisfied with intervention content. Overall session satisfaction scores for each of the 14 sessions averaged 4.23 out of 5 (range: 4.06–4.49), also demonstrating that each module was “A lot” to “Extremely” satisfying with regard to content, presentation, group dynamics, and other factors.

VC technology acceptability and satisfaction

Participants’ mean satisfaction scores with VC WebEx as a delivery modality for group-based sessions trended upward over time from week 5 (mean = 3.91; range: 2.0–4.80) to week 14 (mean = 4.50; range: 3.86–5.0), suggesting improvements in satisfaction with VC technology over time.

Satisfaction with group process

On a scale of 0 (low) to 7 (high), the WAI Participant–Therapist Bond satisfaction score trended upward from a mean of 5.82 (range: 3.33–7) at week 5 to a mean of 6.37 (range: 4.33–7) at week 14. The GCQ Group Cohesiveness satisfaction scores trended upward from a mean of 5.87 (range: 4–7) at week 5 to a mean of 6.11 (range: 4.25–7) at week 14. The TFI Group Experience satisfaction score also trended upward from a mean of 5.9 (range: 4.55–7) to 6.11 (range: 5–7) at week 14.

Group exit interviews

Feedback during group exit interviews was uniformly positive regarding most aspects of the VC-CBCS intervention (Supplemental Appendix S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C179). There was variability among individuals regarding which module content or relaxation technique was “most useful.” The stress management, sleep hygiene, nutrition, and assertiveness modules were generally reported as “most useful.” The medication adherence module was “least useful” to most. Participants seemed to prefer progressive muscle relaxation, breathing techniques (diaphragmatic breathing), and affirmations over the guided imagery scripts. Most participants felt that weekly sessions were the right frequency and would not recommend sessions every other week because it would reduce momentum and group bonding. Several participants said they would have benefited from more time to practice skills, suggesting that the intervention moved too quickly through the modules. In general, they were reticent to suggest the removal of any material but provided suggestions for where modules could be combined or trimmed. One notable theme was a desire for more open-ended peer time, with several participants suggesting that they be allowed to stay in the videoconference afterward to share stories or review skills. Some participants recommended additional “booster” sessions throughout the year, a 28-week program, or meeting twice a week. All participants felt strongly that the intervention should remain group-delivered rather than delivered one-on-one, highlighting multiple benefits of group dynamics. They preferred the hard copy Patient Workbook to write in and review; none reviewed the materials in the electronic drive. While they appreciated weekly goal-setting and accountability, only some completed the homework assignments and entered goals into REDCap. They recommended keeping goals and homework assignments as “optional tools” but not making them requirements. Anecdotal positive outcomes from participation included consuming more fruits/vegetables, reducing sugar and soda intake, reading nutrition labels, weight loss, improvements in stress, depression, and relationships at home and work, and reducing use of prescription pain medications.

DISCUSSION

Since the pandemic, there has been a dramatic increase in the use of telehealth and videoconferencing technology to provide healthcare services to millions. Group-based, telehealth psychological interventions may be particularly efficient, cost-effective, and highly acceptable to patients with liver diseases, but have been completely understudied and therefore, underutilized in hepatology. The results of this pilot feasibility study indicate that a trial of the VC-CBCS stress management and lifestyle intervention, delivered in small groups using videoconferencing, is feasible and highly acceptable to patients with chronic liver disease who report needing support for baseline stress, symptoms, or lifestyle habits. Despite a plethora of telehealth-digital health technology studies being conducted in chronic liver disease,89 especially for MASLD,4647 the current study is one of the few to specifically pilot a group-based intervention delivered using synchronous videoconferencing.

This pilot study provides compelling evidence of the feasibility of conducting a large-scale efficacy trial. Extrapolation from our data suggests that at least 60% of eligible patients approached would be randomized; a higher conversion rate than other cognitive behavioral interventions for liver disease patients that were delivered in-person, by telephone, or videoconferencing in small groups (24%– 48%).48495051 Our 84% overall retention rate and 79% for participants randomized to VC-CBCS is similar to, if not higher than, retention in other psychological interventions in liver diseases (67%–79%),48495051 and exceeds the recommended threshold of 70%. Due to the pandemic and rapid pivot to remote operations, the project team was very hands-on, suggesting similar “high-touch” encounters with participants could help ensure retention success in future studies. Patient-centered retention strategies to reduce non-initiation after randomization and drop out during the intervention would also help reduce attrition.52

The study methods used in the present trial yielded PRO data collection rates of over 90%; however, the pandemic made some study procedures difficult. Collection of clinical/lab data from EHRs was complicated by low clinic attendance during the pandemic. Thus, future trials should allocate funds for in-person or mobile research visits post-intervention, as EHR-derived clinical data may be unreliable. Collection of salivary cortisol using the passive drool method in the home setting was notably challenging and fraught with myriad problems. However, we demonstrated excellent feasibility of delivering a psychosocial intervention to multiple end-users in home environments using VC technology, as demonstrated by high rates of session attendance and minimal technical, logistical, or participant issues. It is notable that this trial was initiated before the pandemic, when most individuals were still unfamiliar with videoconferencing platforms such as WebEx. Despite initial concerns, technical/internet issues occurred in <8% of VC-CBCS participants, and participants reported greater satisfaction with VC-based groups over the course of the study. Moreover, although our sample reflected a clearly disadvantaged population (eg, lower educational attainment, income, rates of employment; 90% without access to a computer device), most participants learned to access the sessions with ease. Some of the critical study procedures that may have led to smooth intervention delivery and engagement included: (a) High-touch team approach; (b) provision of study iPads pre-loaded with software; (c) technology/tablet training; (d) review of behavioral guidelines and privacy rules before sessions; (e) access to research/technology coordinator during sessions; (f) participant reimbursement; and (g) mailed hard copy Patient Workbooks. As anticipated, participants indicated during exit interviews that a sense of peer belonging and accountability were factors that motivated them to return each week, speaking to the power of group-based interventions. Finally, therapists demonstrated high adherence to the intervention manual, despite experiencing difficulty completing activities at the end of the lengthier modules, suggesting the need to reduce some of the content in these sessions.

Similarly, the VC-CBCS intervention was reported to be “extremely” satisfying and useful to participants. In exit interviews, participants resoundingly suggested that behavioral interventions delivered via VC are not only acceptable but may even be preferred to in-person options. The exit group feedback was essential to understanding the perceived usefulness of modules and whether the “dose” of the VC-CBCS was optimal. Based on this feedback, more time needs to be allocated to the continued practice of new skills. Not surprisingly, the VC-CBCS intervention would benefit from additional content to address healthy eating and physical activity habits, as these effect sizes were small and inconsistent. Allowing for 30 minutes of optional time at the end of each session for more peer connection and offering monthly “booster” sessions were also advocated by participants.

Importantly, this pilot study confirmed that participating in the VC-CBCS intervention improves coping, stress levels, and mental health outcomes, consistent with results from the initial in-person CBCS pilot studies.2425 Despite the correlation heat maps being limited by sample size and confounding, the data indicate participants were able to successfully apply stress management and relaxation skills and appropriate coping strategies, as evidenced by large effect sizes that were associated with improvements in mental health, perceived stress, and symptoms. Taken together, these findings provide evidence that VC-CBCS is primarily a coping skills/stress management intervention that may be valuable to many patients with liver disease. The current VC-CBCS content does not appear to exert a strong effect on dietary changes and physical activity. Only 2 modules were devoted to these topics. In order to impact physical health outcomes for diseases such as MASLD, future adaptations would need to include more sessions on health behavior change. Though it could be argued that lifestyle change can be deleted from the VC-CBCS content, participants indicated that stress management and lifestyle change worked synergistically. The VC-CBCS could also be refined for specific liver disease populations (eg, those with cirrhosis, high distress, symptom burden) to maximize coping, wellness, and liver health. However, in its current format, it is likely insufficient for the behavioral treatment of MASH and alcoholic liver disease. Finally, engagement with patients to determine clinically meaningful benefits [ie, minimal clinically important differences (MCID)] is needed.52 Using the PROMIS metric of 2–6 points as an estimate of MCID53 or a 5% change as MCID as previously endorsed by our HCV patient engagement group,54 it appears that change in several outcomes might be clinically meaningful to patients.

Notable limitations included a short follow-up period of 14 days versus 1–3 months follow-up, and missing lab data and saliva data precluded evaluation of objective stress markers and lifestyle modifications (eg, liver enzymes). There are no gold standards for the collection of salivary cortisol in community-based settings.55 Our protocol (ie, passive drool test, shipping within 24 hours using postmail, written/verbal instructions) proved infeasible. Protocols that include in-person training, the cotton-swap collection method and freezing samples at home until participants return to clinic may be more successful with community-based sampling.55 Other alternatives for objective stress markers include in-lab or mobile phlebotomy for blood draws to evaluate neuroendocrine markers, including immune function indices (eg, proinflammatory cytokines), and cutting-edge objective measures, such as heart rate variability; however, these methods are less patient-friendly and require trained staff.56 Next, we did not conduct structured clinical interviews for formal psychiatric and substance use diagnoses, which would have allowed us to evaluate if specific diagnoses were associated with greater treatment benefits. Finally, our study findings may not generalize to other populations, including those engaged in daily injection drug use, a HCV subpopulation with engagement challenges. The pandemic had both positive and negative repercussions on study engagement, which will need to be considered in post-pandemic videoconferencing studies.

The findings from this study support guidelines and recommendations to use multidisciplinary teams that include behavior experts.57 Compared with the expanding role of GI (gastrointestinal) psychologists to treat gastroenterology disorders (“psycho-gastroenterology”),58 psychologists are relatively scarce and underutilized in treating chronic liver disease patients.59 With billable CPT (Current Procedural Terminology) codes and reimbursement schedules, psychologists with training in behavioral medicine (ie, using lifestyle and health behaviors to prevent, manage, and treat health conditions) are well poised to deliver a wide range of empirically validated psychological interventions using videoconferencing technology embedded in healthcare software programs (eg, Epic). The integration of psychologists into hepatology (“psycho-hepatology”) can expand the armamentarium of services to help patients with chronic liver disease engage in healthy living to mitigate disease progression and improve psychological well-being. With minor adaptations, the VC-CBCS may prove to be a scalable and impactful telehealth intervention for patients afflicted with chronic liver diseases.
