The effect of activities assigned to nursing students on the development of their social responsibility
Authors: Aylin Palloş, Nevin Utkualp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVoluntary social sensitivity activities conducted within universities help students develop awareness of social issues. In this context, social sensitivity projects support individual development while forming the basis of a holistic approach in nursing education.AimThis study aims to examine the relationship between social sensitivity activities assigned to nursing students and their individual social responsibility levels.DesignThis is a descriptive and cross-sectional study.MethodsThis study was conducted during the spring semester of the 2024–2025 academic year with first-year nursing students enrolled in the Social Sensitivity course at a public university in Türkiye. Background

In today’s world, where social problems are diversifying and increasing, it has become increasingly important for both individuals and institutions to develop sensitivity toward these issues [1]. Social sensitivity is defined as individuals’ awareness of the social, cultural, economic, and environmental problems in the society, in which they live, as well as their willingness to assume responsibility for generating solutions to these problems [2]. Such sensitivity can be cultivated and disseminated only through conscious and systematic social responsibility practices.

As stated by Eraslan [3], individual social responsibility refers to individuals’ awareness of social problems, their behavior in accordance with societal values, and their engagement in voluntary activities without expecting any return. Eraslan [3] identifies five fundamental characteristics of individual social responsibility: (1) it is based on voluntariness, (2) it involves awareness of social life, (3) it incorporates an affective and teachable process, (4) it entails project management and collaboration, and (5) it is carried out not only in general areas of society (such as education and environment) but also in more specific areas (such as animal rights, disability rights, women’s rights, consumer rights, disaster relief, history, language, and cultural awareness).

In the 21st century, universities are no longer solely institutions that impart professional knowledge and skills; they have also become multidimensional educational centers aiming to instill a sense of social responsibility in individuals [1, 4]. Voluntary social sensitivity/social responsibility activities conducted within universities or public institutions help students develop awareness of social issues, contributing not only to their academic but also to their affective development. Through such activities, students gain skills such as empathy, understanding different perspectives, and recognizing social injustices [1, 5–10]. Participation in social responsibility projects at the university level may be associated with improvements in students’ self-confidence, communication skills, and leadership abilities, while also fostering 21st -century skills such as teamwork, problem-solving, and decision-making. Moreover, such experiences strengthen students’ self-esteem and sense of belonging. Students perceive themselves as valuable and impactful individuals as they witness their contributions to society [1, 5, 7–10].

For nursing students, social sensitivity activities may contribute to greater awareness of the social structures in which individuals live by enabling interaction with groups from different social, economic, and cultural backgrounds. Such activities may contribute to students’ effectiveness in healthcare delivery and their potential contributions to society. They also support the development of professional competencies such as problem-solving, planning, leadership, and social responsibility. Furthermore, given the community-oriented nature of the nursing profession, educating students as socially sensitive individuals may contribute to their professional satisfaction and integration into the healthcare system. In this context, social sensitivity projects contribute to individual development while forming an important component of a holistic approach in nursing education [11].

In nursing education, social sensitivity courses often include experiential activities such as community-based projects, group work, and reflective discussions aimed at increasing students’ awareness of social issues. These course components are expected to promote learning mechanisms such as reflection, engagement with communities, and the development of empathy. Through these mechanisms, students may develop stronger perceptions of individual social responsibility, which can be assessed using validated instruments such as the Individual Social Responsibility Scale (ISRS). Thus, social sensitivity activities within the course context may provide opportunities for students to translate social awareness into individual responsibility and socially responsive professional attitudes in nursing practice.

Although social responsibility has been examined among university students in different disciplines, studies specifically investigating the relationship between social sensitivity activities and individual social responsibility among nursing students remain limited. In particular, it is still unclear whether the level of participation in course-related activities is associated with differences in students’ individual social responsibility levels, and how students’ perceived gains from these activities (such as increased social awareness, empathy, or communication skills) may relate to their individual social responsibility. Examining these relationships may provide valuable insights into the role of experiential learning activities in nursing education. Therefore, the present study aims to contribute to the literature by examining this relationship within the context of nursing education.

Methods

Design

This was a descriptive and cross-sectional study using a validated online survey to determine the effect of activities assigned to nursing students on the development of their individual social responsibility.

Participants and setting

The population of the study consisted of all first-year students (N = 151) enrolled in the Social Sensitivity course in the Department of Nursing, Faculty of Health Sciences, at a public university during the spring semester of the 2024–2025 academic year. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as the study aimed to include the entire accessible population (census approach) of first-year nursing students enrolled in the course. Therefore, all eligible students (N = 151) were invited to participate. Of these, 112 students voluntarily agreed to participate, corresponding to a participation rate of 74.17%.

Inclusion criteria

Students enrolled in the Social Sensitivity course during the 2024–2025 academic year who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study were included.

Exclusion criteria

Students who did not participate in the course activities or who submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the study.

The Social Sensitivity course is a compulsory course offered to first-year nursing students, consisting of one hour of theoretical instruction per week over a total of 14 weeks. The course covers fundamental concepts related to social sensitivity, the structure and characteristics of society, the significance of social sensitivity practices, available resources that may be utilized in carrying out such practices, and individual responsibilities. In addition, various project examples are presented. Each year, students are encouraged to actively participate in a social responsibility activity aligned with specific themes determined for that year.

For the 2024–2025 academic year, three activities were planned: an association activity, a nursing home activity, and a hospital activity. Students were informed about each activity and were asked to voluntarily choose one in which to participate. Within the scope of the association activity, students collaborated with a volunteer association to prepare Ramadan food parcels for individuals in need. Within the scope of the nursing home activity, students visited a nursing home located within the provincial boundaries once a week in groups; they conversed with the residents, engaged in gardening and handicraft activities, and offered a guitar performance. During these visits, students additionally measured vital signs as part of health screening. In the hospital activity, students prepared star-shaped keychains made of wooden beads and special Mother’s Day message cards for mothers of children receiving treatment in the Pediatric Health and Diseases Clinics of a university hospital. During the week including Mother’s Day, students visited the hospital and presented the prepared gifts to the mothers.

Data were collected immediately after the completion of the Social Sensitivity course activities during the final weeks of the semester. Data were collected online via Google Forms using the “Student Information Form” and the “Individual Social Responsibility Scale (ISRS)” to examine the relationship between the course activities and students’ levels of individual social responsibility. On the first page of the form, students were provided with information about the purpose and content of the study. Those who agreed to participate were asked to click the “I approve” button displayed on the screen. Completing the questionnaire took approximately 10 min.

Variables and instruments

Data were collected using the “Student Information Form (30 items)” and the “Individual Social Responsibility Scale (28 items).”

- Student Information Form: Developed by the researchers by reviewing the literature (1, 11–13], this form includes 30 items designed to gather information on demographic characteristics such as students’ age, gender, grade level, and parental education status, as well as their participation in the activities, their views on the Social Sensitivity course, and their perspectives on social responsibility projects.

- Student Information Form: Developed by the researchers by reviewing the literature (1, 11–13], this form includes 30 items designed to gather information on demographic characteristics such as students’ age, gender, grade level, and parental education status, as well as their participation in the activities, their views on the Social Sensitivity course, and their perspectives on social responsibility projects.

- Individual Social Responsibility Scale (ISRS): Developed by Eraslan [3] to determine individuals’ levels of social responsibility, the ISRS is a unidimensional, five-point Likert-type instrument (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) consisting of 28 items. The scale includes both positively worded items (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11, 13, 14, 16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28) and negatively worded items (5, 7, 9, 10, 12, 15, 17, 18, 19, 27). Scores are calculated by summing the item scores. The minimum possible score is 28 and the maximum possible score is 140, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes and behaviors toward individual social responsibility. The Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of the original scale was 0.86 [3], and it was calculated to be 0.848 in the present study.

- Individual Social Responsibility Scale (ISRS): Developed by Eraslan [3] to determine individuals’ levels of social responsibility, the ISRS is a unidimensional, five-point Likert-type instrument (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) consisting of 28 items. The scale includes both positively worded items (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11, 13, 14, 16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 28) and negatively worded items (5, 7, 9, 10, 12, 15, 17, 18, 19, 27). Scores are calculated by summing the item scores. The minimum possible score is 28 and the maximum possible score is 140, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes and behaviors toward individual social responsibility. The Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of the original scale was 0.86 [3], and it was calculated to be 0.848 in the present study.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics for the data were presented as frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Normality of the data was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The results revealed the non-normal distribution of the data, justifying the use of nonparametric tests. A Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare quantitative, continuous data between two independent groups. A Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare data among three or more independent groups, and Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc test was performed for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was carried out in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Health Sciences Research and Publications of Bursa Uludag University (Date: July 30, 2025, Decision No: 2025-07/3). Institutional permission was also granted by the Dean’s Office of the Faculty of Health Sciences, where the study was carried out. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the data collection process. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all participants received detailed information regarding the purpose, scope, and confidentiality of the study.

The mean ISRS score of male students (103.94 ± 15.74) was significantly higher than that of female students (96.17 ± 12.08) (U = 544.500, p = 0.017), indicating that male participants in this sample reported higher levels of individual social responsibility. However, no significant differences were found in ISRS scores by nationality, family income status, parents’ education and employment status, place of residence, or level of religiosity (p > 0.05). Even though students working part-time had higher mean scores, this difference was not significant (p = 0.162) (Table 2).

The relationship between nursing students’ levels of individual social responsibility and their participation in social responsibility projects was examined. No significant difference was found in the ISRS scores by students’ participation in social responsibility projects (p = 0.433). The mean score of students who participated in such projects was 97.32 ± 12.99, whereas the mean score of non-participants was 97.89 ± 13.22. Likewise, no significant difference was observed in ISRS scores when comparing students whose family members or individuals in their immediate environment participated in social responsibility projects with those whose relatives or acquaintances did not (p > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant differences were found in ISRS scores by the frequency of making donations to charitable organizations or volunteering in community service units (p > 0.05). Similarly, students’ perceptions of the importance of social responsibility projects and their views on assuming responsibility as members of society were not significantly associated with ISRS scores (p = 0.219 and p = 0.384, respectively) (Table 3).

No significant difference was found in ISRS scores by the type of participation in the social sensitivity course (association, nursing home, hospital) (K-W = 0.299, p = 0.861). Students who were actively (97.68 ± 12.39) or partially involved (98.23 ± 14.39) in the activities reported significantly higher ISRS scores than passive participants (82.33 ± 1.53), suggesting that active engagement in course activities may be associated with higher levels of perceived social responsibility (K-W = 6.895, p = 0.032). The scores of students who considered the social sensitivity course necessary (98.65 ± 12.55) were significantly higher than those who regarded it as unnecessary (82.50 ± 0.71) (K-W = 11.844, p = 0.003). Even though the mean score of students who found the course beneficial was higher, the difference was not statistically significant (K-W = 5.290, p = 0.071). All students stated that the activities conducted were beneficial to society (100%). Moreover, 95.5% of students reported that they would like to participate in social responsibility projects in the future. The skills that students felt they had developed most through the course were empathy (80.4%), self-development (75.9%), communication skills (67.9%), sociability (62.5%), and responsibility-taking (53.6) (Table 4).

Discussion

A review of the literature on individual social responsibility revealed that studies carried out in Türkiye focused primarily on university students’ global social responsibility [12–17] and corporate social responsibility [18, 19]. However, the number of studies addressing nursing students’ individual social responsibility is limited [20, 21]. This highlights the gap in literature and the need for further studies in this area. It can be stated that the students who participated in this study demonstrated high levels of individual social responsibility. Similarly, studies carried out by Balun et al. [20], Yetiş and Aktaş [21], and Kim and Lee [22] also reported high levels of individual social responsibility among participants. Examining the curriculum within the Faculty of Health Sciences, Department of Nursing, it can be seen that alongside theoretical knowledge, clinical and field practices are also included. Through these clinical and field experiences, students provide direct support to patients and individuals in need of assistance. This may be considered one of the factors associated with higher levels of individual social responsibility among students. By its very nature, the nursing profession serves as a bridge between individuals, society, and the healthcare system, requiring not only clinical knowledge and skills but also a strong sense of ethics, humanity, and social responsibility. In this regard, nursing students’ high perception of social responsibility is consistent with the professional roles and values of discipline. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as high levels of social responsibility may also be influenced by factors such as social desirability bias, students’ prior experiences, or the specific educational context.

Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships between variables cannot be established. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

The findings of this study suggest that nursing students’ overall levels of individual social responsibility were relatively high. However, no statistically significant relationship was observed between students’ participation in social responsibility projects or volunteer activities and their individual social responsibility scores. This finding may appear to contrast with the assumption that participation in such activities necessarily leads to higher levels of social responsibility. Rather, perceptions of individual social responsibility may be shaped not only by active participation but also by multidimensional factors such as professional values, curricular content, and personal belief systems. The absence of significantly higher levels of individual social responsibility among students who reported participating in volunteer work may be related to the quality, duration, or nature of the projects themselves, as well as to the level of students’ engagement and reflection during these activities. It is also possible that students’ prior participation in social responsibility activities may have been superficial or short-term, lacking structured reflection, which is essential for the internalization of social responsibility. These results may suggest that the development of social responsibility among students could depend not only on the frequency of participation but also on the nature of the activities, the degree of awareness fostered, and the sustainability of such participation. Accordingly, the mere presence of volunteering experience may not necessarily reflect the depth or quality of engagement in these activities. Instead, the development of social responsibility may be more closely associated with reflective learning processes and the educational context in which such activities take place. Furthermore, the fact that a large proportion of students had previously participated in a social responsibility project (83.0%) and had observed similar activities in their environment (70.5%) may also have contributed to their awareness of social issues. In addition, the relatively high overall ISRS scores observed in the sample may suggest a possible ceiling effect, which could have limited the ability to detect stronger differences between groups. This ceiling effect may have reduced the variability of the scores and limited the ability to detect statistically significant differences between groups.

The findings of this study indicate that male students reported significantly higher levels of individual social responsibility compared to female students (U = 544.500, p = 0.017). This finding may be interpreted in relation to the influence of gender norms on individuals’ social roles. In the literature, the relationship between social responsibility levels and gender was reported with varying results. While some studies showed that female students display higher levels of social responsibility [12, 18, 19, 21, 23, 24], others reported no significant difference by gender [24–29]. On the other hand, like the findings achieved in the present study, some research reported higher social responsibility scores among male students [30]. This suggests that social responsibility is shaped by personal values and learning experiences. The findings also demonstrate that social responsibility consciousness is a multidimensional phenomenon that cannot be reduced solely to biological sex. Levels of social responsibility may be influenced by factors such as educational background, sociocultural environment, level of engagement in volunteer activities, and personal values. In a field such as nursing, which has traditionally been female-dominated, the higher levels of social responsibility observed among male students may indicate that these individuals tend to assume professional roles more consciously and voluntarily. However, these differences should be interpreted cautiously, as they may also reflect unmeasured factors such as previous volunteering experience, personal motivation, social desirability bias, or students’ perceptions of the course.

In this study, no significant differences were found in students’ levels of individual social responsibility with respect to variables such as nationality, family income level, parental education and employment status, place of residence, level of religious belief, or part-time employment status. This finding suggests that the perception of individual social responsibility is shaped not only by demographic characteristics but also by the educational process and personal values. The literature presents results both similar to and different from those achieved in the present study. No significant differences were reported with regard to family income [12, 21, 24], parental education [21, 24] and employment status [21], place of residence [12, 21, 24], level of religious belief, and part-time employment status [12]. Contrary to the findings achieved in the present study, however, Ersoy Quadır and Koç [12] reported that students’ religious beliefs, and Sarıakçalı et al. [25] reported that students’ nationality, were significantly related with social responsibility levels.

In the literature, participation in social responsibility activities was reported to contribute both to students’ personal development and to the positive transformation of society [1]. The findings achieved in the present study demonstrate that while the specific type of activity performed within the scope of the social responsibility course did not have a significant relationship on students’ individual social responsibility, their overall engagement in such activities was associated with higher levels of individual social responsibility. Notably, students who actively participated in activities obtained higher scores on the ISRS, underscoring the importance of participatory learning and practice-based education. However, some findings should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively small number of students in certain subgroups (e.g., students who considered the course unnecessary or those who participated passively in the activities), which may limit the robustness of the statistical comparisons. This limitation may have reduced the statistical power and the robustness of the comparisons. Similarly, the literature includes evidence that field-based learning practices have been reported to be associated with higher levels of students’ social awareness and levels of social responsibility [4, 21, 23–25]. In this study, the relationship between students’ perception of activities as beneficial and necessary and their social responsibility scores suggests a possible relationship between individual perception and behavior. Sarıakçalı et al. [25] reported that students with higher levels of individual and social responsibility were more likely to believe that this course was necessary.

One of the expected behaviors of university students is to recognize social issues and to take an active role in the process of generating solutions to these issues. Within higher education institutions, one of the courses designed to help students acquire such behavioral competencies is the “Social Sensitivity” course. This course, which aims to enhance students’ sensitivity toward social issues, is offered under various titles across different higher education programs, such as “Community Service Practices,” “Social Responsibility/Sensitivity,” or “Civic Responsibility.” Through experiential learning activities, the social sensitivity course aims to provide opportunities for students to develop social awareness and sensitivity, collaboration, solidarity, effective communication, and self-assessment skills, while fostering a sense of social responsibility and supporting the internalization of individual and societal values [31]. Previous studies have reported that the social sensitivity course contributes to students’ development in a multifaceted manner [7, 32–34]. Consistent with these findings, the majority of students in the present study reported that the activities increased their awareness of social problems and their responsibilities beyond hospital settings. Şen Atasayar and İşeri [11] found that assigning nursing students to sensitivity projects that benefit society fostered their perceptions of individual entrepreneurship. In a study, Büyüksoy [35] evaluated the skills gained by students through the Service-Learning course under themes such as enhancing communication skills, discovering nursing practices outside clinical settings, developing investigative and inquisitive attitudes, and gaining awareness of social issues. Similarly, Sarıakçalı et al. [25], in their study with medical students, reported that 79% of the participants believed the projects they carried out in social responsibility courses provided benefits to society. In the study carried out by Taş and Eskicumalı [4], the majority of students stated that participating in social responsibility activities during their university education would not only benefit themselves but also the communities they are part of, thereby creating opportunities for positive social change. Tanrısever and Yelken [36] concluded that such projects and activities play an important role in fostering students’ social sensitivity, raising their awareness of social problems, promoting their integration with the community, and strengthening their emphasis on cooperation and solidarity. Another study reported that the course contributed socially by offering students the opportunity to engage directly with the community, recognize existing social problems, and strengthen their social sensitivities and responsibilities [37]. Külekçi and Özgan [1] found that students perceived their participation in social responsibility activities as contributing both individually (e.g., personal development, increased sensitivity, inner peace, happiness, spirituality, sociability) and socially (e.g., building a more conscious society, problem-solving, and increasing sensitivity and awareness). Ersoy [38], in a study carried out with pre-service teachers, emphasized that such courses contributed to students’ general knowledge, self-confidence, empathy, reduced prejudices, and enhanced communication and social skills. Likewise, Külekçi and Özgan [1] reported that engagement in social responsibility activities contributed to both personal and social development of students, while also being effective in the acquisition of skills such as communication, empathy, problem-solving, leadership, entrepreneurship, and responsibility. Sarıakçalı et al. [25] also found that the most positive attitudes students believed they gained from this course included developing awareness of social issues, learning teamwork, feeling more self-confident, and improving communication skills. Asıcı and Külekçi Akyavuz [31] also concluded that social responsibility projects conducted within the scope of the social sensitivity course were effective in enabling students to acquire and strengthen the value of helpfulness. In the study carried out by Taş and Eskicumalı [4], when students were asked how participating in university-led social responsibility projects affected them, their responses included “desire to continue participating in social responsibility projects,” “gaining awareness,” and “experiencing happiness/moral relief.” Findings reported by Toker and Tat’s [10] in their study with 238 university students indicated that 91% supported the implementation of social responsibility initiatives by universities. Studies conducted with pre-service teachers regarding their views on the Community Service Practices course highlighted that such courses fostered behaviors such as community consciousness, social responsibility, and social awareness, whereas the participants’ overall perceptions of the course were positive and at a high level [39]. In this study as well, the fact that the majority of students expressed a desire to continue engaging in social responsibility projects in the future suggests that such courses contribute not only to short-term but also to long-term behavioral change. This indicates that when students are given opportunities to participate in social responsibility activities, their involvement can be sustained, and they may continue to assume active roles in society. Moreover, students’ statements that they developed in areas such as empathy, self-development and sensitivity, interpersonal relationships and communication, and social skills demonstrate that these courses contribute not only academically but also to students’ moral and social development.

Within nursing education, courses such as Social Sensitivity provide structured opportunities for students to interact with communities and better understand the social determinants of health, which are essential components of holistic nursing care. From an educational perspective, such courses may play an important role in nursing curricula by enabling students to engage directly with social issues and vulnerable populations. Experiential learning activities integrated into these courses can support the development of not only professional knowledge and skills but also social awareness, empathy, and civic responsibility. These findings suggest that incorporating structured social responsibility activities into nursing education may contribute to the preparation of socially responsive healthcare professionals. Therefore, integrating experiential and community-oriented learning opportunities more systematically into nursing curricula may further strengthen students’ social responsibility and professional identity.

Conclusion

It was determined in this study that first-year nursing students generally exhibited high levels of individual social responsibility. With respect to gender, male students’ scores were significantly higher than those of female students. While the type of activity (association, nursing home, and hospital) did not lead to differences in ISRS levels, the mode of participation appeared to be influential: students who participated actively or semi-actively scored significantly higher than passive participants. Similarly, the scores of students who considered the course necessary were higher than those of students who regarded it as unnecessary. On the other hand, no significant relationship was found between ISRS scores and students’ previous participation in social responsibility projects or the participation of their close social circles. Students’ self-assessments indicated that course-related activities particularly supported empathy, personal development, communication, social interaction, and responsibility-taking skills. In line with these results, it is recommended that this study be replicated with a multicenter design covering all class levels and involving a larger sample to enhance external validity and reliability of the findings, and that stratified sampling be employed to reduce selection and participation bias. However, future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to better understand causal relationships.

Limitations

Since this study was conducted only with first-year students in the Department of Nursing at the Faculty of Health Sciences of a single university, the findings cannot be generalized to other universities, academic levels (e.g., sophomore–senior), or programs such as midwifery, medicine, or health management. Furthermore, no formal sample size calculation was conducted, as the study was designed to include the entire accessible population. Although this approach strengthens internal representativeness, it may limit the statistical power of the study. The study was limited to students who took the Social Sensitivity course and participated in a social responsibility activity as part of the course. In addition, the voluntary nature of participation may have introduced self-selection bias, as students who were more interested in social responsibility activities may have been more likely to participate in the study. To minimize potential bias, all students enrolled in the course were invited to participate, and the data were collected anonymously through an online survey. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.
