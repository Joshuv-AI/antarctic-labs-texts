Combining automated kinetic model selection with robust optimization and application to maximize cell-free production of UDP-GalNAc
Authors: Nicolas Huber, Tuan Son Hoang, Sebastián Espinel Ríos, Thomas FT Rexer, Udo Reichl, Steffen Klamt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell-free multi-enzyme cascades enable precise biosynthesis, but using them for cost-efficient bioproduction remains challenging. This issue can be addressed through model-based process optimization using kinetic models. However, the predictive power of kinetic models is often limited by structural and parametric uncertainties. In this work, we present MoRSel, a novel method that tackles structural uncertainties through automated refinement and selection of kinetic models. MoRSel starts with a kinetic core model, a library of potential model changes, and available experimental data. The algorithm iteratively expands the model to achieve an optimal balance between fit quality and model complexity according to information-theoretic criteria.We applied MoRSel to a kinetic model of a cell-free enzyme cascade for producing the nucleotide sugar UDP-GalNAc. An initial core model showed significant inconsistencies with measured experimental data. By providing candidates for potential stoichiometric and regulatory changes in the model, MoRSel identified a model that more accurately captured the observed behavior. We then used this model to inform a recently introduced robust (ensemble-based) process optimization strategy that accounts for parametric uncertainty. We addressed two economically relevant problems. First, we determined the optimal initial substrate and enzyme concentrations to maximize the product titer, resulting in a 45 % increase in product titer and yield in a validation experiment. In a second optimization, we minimized the enzyme load and tested the computed optimal enzyme concentrations in another validation experiment. With enzyme costs reduced by 53 %, the relative titer and yield could be improved by 80 % and 75 %, respectively, with small reductions in absolute titer and yield.Overall, MoRSel advances the development of predictive models for complex biochemical systems, with particular relevance for the robust optimization of cell-free bioproduction processes.Keywords: Kinetic modeling, Model selection and identification, Structural uncertainty, Robust optimization, Cell-free systems, Multi-enzyme cascades, Nucleotide sugars, UDP-GalNAc

Introduction

The central goal of bioprocess engineering is to design and optimize biochemical production processes. Two primary strategies exist for synthesizing compounds using biocatalysts: in vivo, employing whole cells (often genetically modified for product synthesis), or in vitro, utilizing isolated cellular components such as enzymes and metabolites in cell-free production systems. While cell-based systems have proven successful across diverse applications and remain the prevailing approach, cell-free systems offer a distinct advantage: by operating independently of cellular constraints (e.g., related to growth, pH, etc.), they enable a wider range of pathway design and operating parameters (Claassens et al., 2019). However, the high costs of cell-free biosynthesis, e.g., due to time-intensive preparatory steps for enzyme production and purification, necessitate robust and first-principles-based optimization methods to bridge the gap to cost-efficient, competitive, and predictable bioproduction (Siedentop et al., 2021).

To address these challenges, the dynamics of cell-free processes, which often involve fewer than a dozen enzymatic steps and thus far fewer components than a cell, can be described, analyzed, and optimized using kinetic models. These models are based on ordinary differential equations composed of kinetic rate laws that describe the kinetics of enzyme-catalyzed reactions in dependency of metabolite concentrations and enzyme abundances (Martin et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2020; Sigg et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2021; Zhong et al., 2017). For instance, Silva et al. (2024) developed a kinetic model for the cell-free production of fructose from lactose and used it to identify initial enzyme activity ratios that maximize volumetric productivity in batch processes and product titer in continuous setups. Similarly, Sun et al. (2021) and Sigg et al. (2021) used kinetic model simulations to improve the cell-free production of various disaccharides from starch and alpha-glycosyl glycerol from sucrose, respectively. Martin et al. (2023) developed a kinetic model of E. coli-based cell-free metabolism and used it to optimize butanol production through model-guided adjustments of cofactor and enzyme levels. Other examples of model-guided exploration and optimization of process dynamics, where optimal enzyme ratios or loadings are identified from kinetic models, include the production of alpha-ketoglutarate from alpha-xylose (Shen et al., 2020) and the cell-free production of cellobiose by Zhong et al. (2017). Notably, many of these model-based optimization studies do not formulate rigorous mathematical optimization problems, defined by explicit objective functions, decision variables, and constraints, that could then be solved using dedicated optimization algorithms. Instead, optimization often relies on heuristic trial-and-error simulations by varying selected parameter values. One central aspect of our recent work (Huber et al., 2024) was therefore to highlight and demonstrate the use of a rigorous optimization framework for identifying optimal process variables (e.g., enzyme ratios) by maximizing or minimizing a given economic objective function.

Another issue addressed in our previous work (Huber et al., 2024) was the adequate consideration of parametric uncertainty when using a kinetic model in a rigorous optimization approach. Rate laws of kinetic models involve (kinetic) parameters, some of which might be available from literature or databases. In many cases, however, parameter values must be estimated from experimental data. Depending on the problem definition, the available experimental data, and the specific parameter estimation methodology, kinetic parameters are often not uniquely identifiable and repeating the parameter estimation with different seeds can lead to different parameter sets that fit the data equally well. Different methods exist to quantify this parameter uncertainty (Heinrich et al., 2025; Mitra and Hlavacek, 2019; Vanlier et al., 2013; Wieland et al., 2021), including profile likelihoods (Kreutz et al., 2013; Raue et al., 2009), sensitivity analysis (Marino et al., 2008), and Bayesian approaches (Schmidt et al., 2020; Vernon et al., 2018). Many of these approaches employ ensemble strategies where, instead of trying to identify one unique set of model parameters, the focus is placed on exploring and capturing a variety of possible model behaviors. In particular, Bayesian approaches often rely on ensemble strategies (Groves et al., 2024; Linden et al., 2022; van Mourik et al., 2014), but they have also been applied in other contexts (Hu et al., 2025). In our previous work (Huber et al., 2024), we proposed a robust ensemble-based optimization strategy to tackle parametric uncertainty. By repeating a least-squares parameter estimation, we generated an ensemble of different parameter sets that all fit the data equally well within a defined tolerance, thus capturing the parameter uncertainty. Then, as a new approach for (robust) optimization on model ensembles, we solved the optimization problem for each parameter set in the ensemble, followed by cross-validation and scoring of all optimization results across the entire parameter ensemble.

However, in addition to parametric non-identifiabilities, there is another source of uncertainty when building enzyme-kinetic models, concerning the model structure itself. Structural (epistemic) uncertainties include, for example, the set of reactions to consider, the kinetic rate laws to use, and potentially relevant regulations of the enzymes. For example, apart from unknown enzymatic mechanisms, enzymes from different organisms, along with their associated substrates and cofactors, are often mixed at non-physiologically high concentrations in cell-free systems, potentially leading to unknown side reactions or alterations in enzyme activity. In such cases, especially when a constructed and fitted kinetic model cannot satisfactorily reflect experimental data, methods to identify missing (or irrelevant) components or interactions would be highly valuable. A variety of methods address this problem in different ways, which can broadly be categorized into three major classes: (1) model building via machine-learning methods involving no or only limited prior knowledge, (2) ensemble-based model selection, and (3) iterative improvement of an initial model candidate (and combinations of these three basic approaches).

Methods with a focus on building models purely from available data include DeepMoD, which uses deep learning for model discovery in noisy data (Both et al., 2021), and SINDy, a sparse regression-based method to find a linear combination of basis functions that best capture the behavior of the dynamical system (Brunton et al., 2016). Other methods in this category make use of symbolic regression for predicting dynamical systems (Quade et al., 2016) or automatic model construction via Gaussian processes (Duvenaud, 2014). Recently, generative machine learning was combined with symbolic regression to enable efficient kinetic model discovery (Medina et al., 2026). Additionally, hybrid modeling approaches, where data-driven machine-learning methods are combined with models from first principles, have also gained increasing interest (Espinel-Ríos et al., 2024; Pennington et al., 2024; Sansana et al., 2021).

The second class of model identification approaches uses model ensembles to identify the model variant that best describes the observed data. For this purpose, all models of the ensemble need to be evaluated by simulating them, which in turn requires parameterization, highlighting the connection between parameter and model structure uncertainty. A basic modeling guideline states that models should only be as large and complex as necessary (Occam's Razor). Therefore, when comparing different model variants, a trade-off between model size and descriptive power needs to be considered. Model selection criteria such as Akaike's Information Criterion (Akaike, 1973) or the Bayesian Information Criterion (Schwarz, 1978) address this issue by introducing penalty terms that scale with the amount of model parameters to promote sparsity during model selection. An example of an ensemble-based model selection approach is the modelMAGE framework (Flöttmann et al., 2008; Schaber et al., 2011). There, a master model is assembled which contains all reactions and associated regulations, including hypothetical candidate reactions and mechanisms. A specific set of user-defined candidate submodels (derived from the master model) is then fitted against experimental data and the best model is selected according to the corrected Akaike Information Criterion for small sample sizes (Hurvich and Chih-Ling, 1989; Sugiura, 1978). Other ensemble-based methods use Bayesian techniques to evaluate and select the most plausible model. Examples include the extension of GRASP (General Reaction Assembly and Sampling Platform) to include model selection via Approximate Bayesian Computation (Matos et al., 2022; Saa and Nielsen, 2016) and Bayesian Model Comparison using Nested Sampling (Pullen and Morris, 2014).

Model identification methods of the third class start with an initial model candidate, that is refined by incorporating additional information from experimental data and/or from first principles. One example of this class is the Numerical Matrices Method for nonlinear system identification (Karnaukhov et al., 2007). In its most general form with no prior knowledge of the reaction dynamics, it starts with a comprehensive model structure that contains all possible uni- and bimolecular reactions for a set of known process species (metabolites and enzymes). Then, by integrating information on observed reaction rates it seeks to identify, in one step, the combination of reactions that best describes the data.

Comparing advantages and disadvantages of the different approaches, data-driven machine-learning methods require no or only some (in case of hybrid models) prior information and assumptions of mechanistic properties of the underlying process. However, they usually require significantly larger datasets and the constructed models are often difficult to interpret biologically. Moreover, typically only simple mechanisms and/or simple combinations of candidate mechanisms (e.g., linear combination in SINDy) are possible. Ensemble-based methods search a broader solution space of possible and biologically motivated model structures and can therefore significantly reduce model structure uncertainty. However, parameterization of each model of the ensemble is required to rank the different model variants, which introduces an additional source of uncertainty. Finally, attempting to improve a single initial model candidate can lead to a model that precisely describes the specific process under investigation. However, completely different alternative model structures that might also describe the observed dynamics are not considered.

In this work, we present MoRSel (model refinement and selection), an automated model selection approach for kinetic models that addresses many of the limitations described above. Our approach starts with a biologically plausible core model and uses a library of possible (candidate) model changes (addition or removal of reactions and/or modifications of kinetic rate laws), which are iteratively introduced into the core model with the goal to identify the model structure that achieves the best balance between fit quality and model size. MoRSel complements our previously developed method of robust ensemble-based optimization under parametric uncertainty (Huber et al., 2024): it provides a model selection module to handle structural uncertainties during the construction of the kinetic model, which is later used for robust process optimization (Fig. 1).

To demonstrate the framework in Fig. 1 end-to-end, we applied it to modeling and optimization of a cell-free multi-enzyme cascade for the production of the nucleotide sugar UDP-GalNAc (Fig. 2). Nucleotide sugars are compounds where nucleoside di- or mono phosphates are bound to sugar molecules. In cells they serve as activated building blocks for the biosynthesis of polysaccharides, which in turn can be used to create glycoconjugates - compounds where proteins, peptides or lipids are covalently linked to glycans. Most of these glycoconjugates in mammals are built from only nine main monosaccharide subunits (del Val et al., 2016; Kightlinger et al., 2020) including N-acetyl-D-galactosamine (GalNAc), which, in its nucleotide sugar form, is bound to uridine diphosphate (UDP). Recent efforts to produce UDP-GalNAc were summarized by Frohnmeyer and Elling (2023), covering both cell-based and cell-free methods. Due to the importance of nucleotide sugars for the industrial production of therapeutic glycoproteins (Blondeel and Aucoin, 2018), glycoconjugate vaccines (Micoli et al., 2019), and human milk oligosaccharides (Walsh et al., 2020), we established a cell-free enzyme cascade for the production of UDP-GalNAc (shown in Fig. 2) in a previous work (Hoang et al., 2025). Apart from the main pathway synthesizing UDP-GalNAc from uridine and GalNAc, it includes a polyphosphate-based in situ ATP regeneration module previously used in other cell-free systems together with the inorganic pyrophosphatase (PPA) for degrading pyrophosphate, a potential inhibitor of the GLMU enzyme. Herein, we first built an initial kinetic model of the cascade, which, due to apparently missing components in the model structure, was refined and modified by applying MoRSel. Afterwards, we used the identified model to optimize the cell-free batch process with regard to different performance metrics (titer, yield, enzyme load).

Results

In the following, we first introduce our new kinetic model refinement and selection module (MoRSel). In the second part, we demonstrate its applicability as an initial module for the robust optimization of a cell-free multi-enzyme cascade for UDP-GalNAc synthesis (Fig. 1).

Development of a new model selection pipeline

MoRSel implements a new model selection approach based on a pipeline that starts from an initial kinetic (core) model, a library of (candidate) model changes (e.g., adding/removing reactions or changes in kinetic rate laws), and experimental datasets. It seeks to identify the model structure with the optimal balance between fit quality and model complexity. The pipeline starts with the construction of an initial core model, which usually contains all known reactions and their assumed kinetic rate laws of the system under study. For the rate laws of all enzyme-catalyzed reactions, we used the convenience kinetics formulation by Liebermeister and Klipp (2006), a generalized version of Michaelis-Menten kinetics, which we modified by integrating the Haldane relationship (leading to terms with the equilibrium constant of the respective reaction). Generally, the core model should contain all (and only) the reactions and regulatory terms that are evident from biological knowledge; these are consequently treated as ground truth.

Initial fitting (parameter estimation) of the core model to experimental data may fail to adequately describe the dynamics of the given enzyme cascade. One typical reason for this is that the initial core model does not include all relevant regulatory interactions between metabolites and enzymes. Such regulatory effects may become relevant due to the mixing of all compounds in a one-pot process at concentrations that may exceed physiological values in cells. For similar reasons, unknown or unconsidered side reactions may occur and affect the overall dynamics of the cascade. Therefore, if the fitted initial core model does not adequately describe experimental data, the next step is to collect information on potential allosteric regulations and/or side reactions of the participating enzymes, resulting in a library of model changes. These model changes are thus either stoichiometric (addition or deletion of entire reactions, including their rate laws) or kinetic (changes in the respective rate law of the reaction). Regarding stoichiometric changes, all reactions (including those not in the core model) are already present in the stoichiometric matrix N, and implementing a stoichiometric change is equivalent to setting the corresponding rate laws either to zero or to the (new) rate law equation. Kinetic changes affect the rate law of a reaction in one of three ways: (a) by replacing it entirely by a new mechanistic description, (b) by incorporating competitive inhibition terms between different reactions catalyzed by the same enzyme, or (c) by combining the existing rate law with multiplicative terms related to allosteric enzyme regulation. Herein, for integrating allosteric inhibition, we multiply the rate law of the respective reaction rate ri with the term KiKi+I ,where Ki is the inhibitory constant and I is the concentration of the metabolite acting as an inhibitor. For allosteric activation terms, we use the factor (1+(Ka·A)) where Ka is the activation constant and A is the concentration of the metabolite acting as an activator. Since Ki and Ka are free parameters (to be estimated during model fitting), it is always possible to restore the behavior of the model before the inclusion of a regulatory term by choosing very high values for Ki and zero for Ka. Hence, it is ensured that the best fit of a model structure modified via allosteric terms cannot be worse than that of a previous model structure when a regulation term is introduced. The same holds true when adding new reactions because the value of their kcat parameter can be set to 0 to restore the previous dynamics. Only the removal of reactions may lead to a worse best model fit.

Even if the optimal parameter set minimizing the RSS were found, the RSS alone is not suitable for comparing models with different numbers of parameters. In particular, adding regulatory terms or reactions (and their parameters) to a model implies that, as explained above, a parametrization always exists that can at least reproduce the model behavior before introducing the modification (i.e., the RSS cannot become worse). To correct for this effect and to promote sparsity, the quality of a given model structure (i.e., its fitness) is evaluated by computing model selection criteria (MSC), which penalize the number of model parameters as an indicator of model complexity. A commonly used model selection criterion, which is based on information-theoretical considerations, is the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (Akaike, 1973). From a given set of different models, it aims to find the model that explains the data with the least loss of information. Assuming a large sample size (number of data points) n, a penalty term of 2k is included in the criterion to account for the number of model parameters k (Table 1). A corrected version of the AIC (AICc) was derived by Sugiura (1978) and Hurvich and Chih-Ling (1989). Compared to the traditional AIC, it features a more complex penalty term, imposing a heavier penalty for smaller n but approaching 2k when n grows larger, making the corrected AIC more robust for smaller sample sizes. Neither the AIC nor the AICc assumes that the true model is included in the set of models to be tested. This contrasts with the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Schwarz, 1978), which selects the model that maximizes its posterior probability given the available data, assuming the true model is among the set of different models variants. Similar to the AICc, but unlike the AIC, the penalty term of the BIC grows with the sample size n.

According to our experience, the effect of the penalty terms of AIC, AICc, and BIC is sometimes too low, leading to the selection of larger models even if they achieve only minor improvements in the fit. To test the impact of penalty terms that scale more severely with the number of model parameters, we consider herein three additional heuristic custom information criteria: (1) CIC1 with an adaptive penalty term that scales with the magnitude of the fitting error (here, one needs to differentiate based on the sign of the term ln⁡(RSSn)), (2) CIC2 that uses the AIC penalty term but with a much stronger weight, and (3) CIC3, which uses a single term containing the fitting error and the number of parameters. These custom criteria are not rooted in any theoretical framework but were instead derived as variations of the basic structure of the traditional MSC (a combination of a log-likelihood term and a penalty term), which proved useful in our applications.

Fig. 3 shows the workflow of MoRSel, which makes use of the concepts introduced so far. The algorithm starts by evaluating the goodness of the initial core model. Generally, herein, the goodness of a model variant refers to computing and then aggregating all six MSC from Table 1 using the best-fitting parameter set (lowest RSS) of a parameter ensemble generated by repeating a least-squares parameter estimation with the respective model variant. When comparing different model variants, we rank them separately for each MSC and then calculate the overall mean rank to reduce all six MSC values to a single measure.

Workflow of the proposed model refinement and selection algorithm (MoRSel). The first iteration starts with the initial core model, where each candidate model change is separately integrated and the resulting model variant evaluated. The model variant with the best aggregate MSC ranking becomes the new model, and the introduced model change is removed from the library. The loop stops when all candidate model changes have been integrated. In this example, the library contains four candidate model changes. A total of 11 out of 16 possible model variants (including the core model) are evaluated during the modification loop across four iterations. During the final model selection, the best variant of each iteration and the initial core model are ranked to identify the overall best model variant.

After evaluating the initial core model, the first round of the iterative modification loop of the model refinement and selection algorithm begins. In its first iteration, each candidate from the library of model changes is individually integrated into the initial core model. For each of these generated model variants, several parameter estimation runs are performed (the parameters are initialized with the values of the best-fitting parameter set of the initial core model ensemble), which serve to assess the goodness of the model variants. Following a greedy strategy, among all model variants tested, the one with the highest goodness is used as the new core model for the subsequent iteration. It is also stored for a final ranking step (performed after the loop has concluded), and the model change introduced in this selected model variant is removed from the library. Additionally, the values of the best-fitting parameter set generated during the evaluation of the selected model variant are stored and used to initialize the parameter estimations performed in the next iteration.

The second and all subsequent iterations repeat the steps of the first, using the current (modified) model variant and the reduced library. Thus, in each iteration, many more model variants are evaluated than are stored in the final output list, which collects the selected best model variant from each iteration. The modification loop stops when no model changes are left in the library. A final model selection step then ranks all stored model variants (including the initial core model) based on the aggregated MSC metrics. This time, both the mean and the median rank are computed across all MSC values. If a model variant achieves both the best mean and median rank, it is directly selected. If the best mean- and median-ranked model variants differ, the sparser one is selected (i.e., the one with fewer model parameters). A new ensemble of parameter sets is computed for the selected final model variant, which is used for the subsequent (robust) optimization steps in the pipeline shown in Fig. 1.

We implemented MoRSel as a Python-based program that utilizes functions for model building, time course simulation, and parameter estimation from COPASI (Hoops et al., 2006) through its Python API, BasiCO (Bergmann, 2023). The program, together with all scripts, data, and models used herein, is available at the repository https://github.com/klamt-lab/MoRSel_UDP-GalNAc_Paper and archived at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19098183.

Testing MoRSel with an example model and synthetic data

We used the kinetic toy model shown in Fig. 4 to test whether MoRSel can "rediscover" the true model structure and dynamics from a given (incomplete) core model, a library with five potential model changes, and data generated from simulating the true model. The true model consists of a substrate S, a product P, and an inhibitor X. S is converted to P by reaction 1, which is inhibited by X. X itself is produced from S and P by reaction 4. The ODEs, reaction rate laws (for sake of simplicity, we use mass-action kinetics), and all parameter values of this toy model are shown in Fig. 4. We simulated the true model (with S0=10; X0=P0=0 and all kinetic parameters set to 1) to generate time course data, to which we added Gaussian noise. We then constructed an initial (incomplete) core model, which only included reaction 1, assuming it was the only reaction known to be part of the system. Experimental tests would then reveal that a compound X appears in the process, which cannot be explained by the initial core model alone. We provided a library of candidate model changes containing three new reactions representing different hypotheses for the production of X (reactions 2, 3, and 4; the latter being part of the true model) and two candidate regulatory mechanisms (X as either an activator or an inhibitor of reaction 1; the latter again being part of the true model).

Overview of the rediscovery test with a simple toy model. Synthetic time course data with a 2-h step were generated by simulating the true model. Gaussian noise (mean: 0, standard deviation: 0.2) was added to the data, which were then used as input for MoRSel, along with an initial (incomplete) core model and a library of five potential model changes. Model selection results show that model variant 3 (the initial core model with the addition of reaction 4 and the allosteric inhibition of reaction 1 by X) best describes the synthetic data, as it achieves the best median rank across all model selection criteria (MSC). Then, a parameter ensemble was calculated for the selected model variant by repeating a least-squares estimation. The parameter histograms show that the estimated values of the optimal parameter set of the ensemble (red lines) are close to the true parameter values (black dashed lines). Also, the time course simulation of the selected model (solid lines) closely matches the synthetic data points, unlike the initial core model (dashed lines). This is to be expected, as both the model structure and the kinetic parameter values were identified successfully.

Applying MoRSel, as described in the previous section, leads to the successful identification of the true model structure. Additionally, the parameter values of the best fit of the parameter ensemble calculated for the identified model structure are close to their true values. Accordingly, the time course simulations of the selected model are much closer to the data compared to the initial core model (Fig. 4).

Using MoRSel to improve a model for the cell-free UDP-GalNAc production system

We applied our model selection pipeline to systematically improve an initial kinetic model describing the dynamics of the cell-free enzyme cascade producing UDP-GalNAc from uridine and GalNAc (see Fig. 2). As the first step, an initial model of the UDP-GalNAc cascade was constructed (see Materials and Methods). We then performed 12 experiments with different initial substrate and enzyme concentrations and measured the time course of metabolites of the cascade (Supplementary File S1). Analysis of the mass balances of the metabolite pools related to adenosine and uridine, respectively, showed only small violations across all experiments (mean deviations of 5.68 % and 0.54 % respectively).

We then fitted the initial core model to these data and found larger discrepancies between the available measurements and the simulations of the fitted core model (Supplementary File S2). This prompted us to compile a list of potentially relevant model changes (Fig. 5, Table 2), which were considered plausible and relevant to improve the model fit of the initial model. The model changes include several potential enzyme regulations (kinetic changes), for which some hints can be found in the literature (Table 2). The library also includes new reactions, which are not directly based on literature information but on inspection of the experimental data. In particular, the core model did not include any reaction that could produce adenosine monophosphate (AMP), adenosine tetraphosphate (ATPP), or adenosine pentaphosphate (ATPPP), although these species appeared with relevant concentrations in all experiments (Supplementary File S1). We therefore included different candidates for possible stoichiometric model changes that enable the formation of AMP, ATPP, and/or ATPPP (the latter two were grouped in the model as one variable: ATPP). One possible explanation for the accumulation of AMP observed in all experiments could be a spontaneous hydrolysis of ADP, leading to the loss of a phosphate group and thereby the formation of AMP. Therefore, two different types of ADP decay reactions with simple mass action (instead of enzyme-kinetic) rate laws were added to the library of model changes. However, it is also possible that AMP is created by kinase reactions (such as UDK, UMPK, and NAHK) if they use adenosine diphosphate (ADP) as a phosphate donor instead of ATP. The formation of adenosine nucleotides with more than three phosphate groups (summarized in the ATPP model variable) could be the result of PPK3 catalyzing the transfer of additional phosphate groups onto ATP. This additional side reaction was also included in the library of model changes, alongside alternative versions of all kinase reactions, enabling them to use ATPP as a phosphate donor, resulting in the production of ATP. If additional enzyme-catalyzed (side) reactions are added to the model, it is ensured that necessary competitive inhibition terms are included in the rate laws of all reactions catalyzed by the same enzyme.

Model-based optimization of the UDP-GalNAc enzyme cascade under parametric uncertainty and experimental validation

We used the parameter ensemble of the selected final model variant to improve the performance of the cell-free UDP-GalNAc batch process compared to a defined baseline behavior (see Supplementary File S1; experiment 4; see also Fig. 7) by applying our methodology of optimization under parametric uncertainty developed in Huber et al. (2024) (see also Fig. 1). The approach consists of three steps: (1) the optimization problem is defined and solved for each parameter set of the ensemble (for this step, we used the genetic algorithm implemented in COPASI), (2) additional model simulations are computed to test how each optimization result performs across all remaining parameter sets in the ensemble (cross-validation), and (3) the performance of each optimization result is scored, and the best result is selected for experimental validation. Two economically relevant process objectives were considered, as outlined below.

Optimization of the UDP-GalNAc titer

The objective of the first model-based optimization problem (Equations (5a), (5b), (5c), (5d), (5e)) is to identify initial enzyme and substrate concentrations xE0 and xS0 (decision variables) that maximize the concentration of the product UDP-GalNAc after 7 h. The problem is constrained by a maximal enzyme load Etot,max, which is set to that of the baseline experiment (experiment 4 in Supplementary File S1; see also Table 4). The selected kinetic model variant is included as a constraint in the optimization problem, with initial concentrations denoted as x(t0)=x0 (xE0,xS0 are included in x0). Discussion

In this work, we presented MoRSel, a new model refinement and selection approach for building kinetic models under structural uncertainties. Starting from an initial core model, a library of possible model changes, and experimental data, the candidate changes are iteratively integrated into the core model, and their respective effects on the model goodness are evaluated by aggregating different model selection criteria. In this way, a curated space of a limited set of initially defined hypotheses is explored using a greedy search algorithm. MoRSel seamlessly integrates with our previously developed framework for robust model-based optimization under parametric uncertainty (Huber et al., 2024), which we applied here to successfully optimize a cell-free enzyme cascade for the production of the nucleotide sugar UDP-GalNAc with respect to product titer and enzyme load.

Different methods have been proposed to solve the problem of selecting the best model from an assortment of different possible variants (see the Introduction). Within this space, MoRSel can be classified as a hypothesis-library-driven refinement of an initial model structure. This increases the biological interpretability of the results and is particularly suited to problems where different structural hypotheses are well defined. Among other available methods, the modelMAGE framework (Flöttmann et al., 2008; Schaber et al., 2011) shares the most similarities with our approach. Their method starts with a master model that contains all possible model components and a set of directives specifying changes to the master model (removing reactions and/or changing their kinetics), which are used to generate model variants. Crucially, the structures of all model variants to be tested need to be explicitly defined by the user and are then subsequently built, evaluated, and ranked by modelMAGE. This requires prior knowledge of which model variants are most promising (from the entirety of models that can be constructed by integrating all combinations of possible mechanistic changes) and makes modelMAGE a method focused exclusively on model selection. By contrast, MoRSel does not require prior definition of specific model variants and instead attempts to explore the entire space of possible model structures emerging from all possible combinations of an initial core model and a given library of model changes. However, the size of this solution space scales exponentially with the number of potential model changes that can be introduced into a given core model: for n model changes, there are 2n possible model structures that can be constructed. This makes evaluating all possible model variants infeasible for larger n, due to combinatorial explosion (for example, in our UDP-GalNAc enzyme cascade with 19 candidate model changes, we would have had to test more than 500,000 model variants). For this reason, MoRSel uses a greedy search algorithm, which tests, in the first iteration, the addition of n individual model changes (from which the best resulting model is selected), in the second iteration the addition of n−1 (remaining) individual model changes to the best model from the first iteration, and so forth. In total, this requires the evaluation of only n·(n+1)2 different model variants. Applying this strategy of iterative refinement of an initial core model in our UDP-GalNAc case, only 190 (instead of 500,000) model variants needed to be tested, from which the overall best model variant could then be selected. In this way, MoRSel combines the advantages of model refinement and model selection approaches. A possible disadvantage of utilizing such a greedy search strategy is that it cannot capture potential synergies between candidate model changes, since model changes are included and tested individually in each iteration of MoRSel's modification loop. While it would be possible to adjust our algorithm to also test (smaller) combinations of candidate changes in each iteration, this would again increase the computational costs in larger networks and may then again become combinatorially intractable. We believe that MoRSel achieves a good balance between search space coverage and computational cost. It is most naturally suited to small-to medium-scale mechanistic kinetic models with a manageable number of plausible structural hypotheses, such as (cell-free) enzyme cascades, pathway modules, or other well-characterized biochemical systems.

The selection of kinetic models is complicated by parameter uncertainty due to practical and structural non-identifiability. Practical non-identifiability, arising from limited or low-quality experimental data, can be mitigated by increasing the amount and/or quality of available data or by subdividing the system into smaller modules. For example, Shen et al. employed a modular approach to first characterize individual enzymes in isolation using initial rate kinetics, which provided a foundation for modeling the full Weimberg pathway cascade (Shen et al., 2020). However, this approach inherently omits interactions between enzymes and metabolites from different reactions, such as substrate channeling or synergistic effects, which can significantly influence the overall cascade performance. As a result, kinetic parameters measured in isolation often require adjustment when applied to the full system. Structural non-identifiability arises due to correlations between parameters, preventing a unique estimation of their values (and thus the goodness of the entire model). This can lead to cases where the same model structure is evaluated differently depending on the underlying parameterization. To handle this kind of uncertainty, any parameter estimation calculated as part of MoRSel is repeated multiple times, and the parameter set with the lowest error is chosen for further computations. Depending on the dimensionality of the parameter space, the number of repetitions can be adjusted, although the same trade-off between higher coverage and higher computational cost also applies here. Furthermore, the opposite problem also needs to be considered: namely, that different model structures can lead to very similar (or even identical) evaluations because combinations of different model changes can compensate for or reinforce each other. Repeating the entire MoRSel framework multiple times and choosing the best result across all runs, as practiced herein, can help alleviate this problem since a larger area of the space of possible model structures is explored. Due to the influence of structural and parametric uncertainties as well as stochastic elements of the chosen numerical solvers, it is very unlikely that multiple runs follow exactly the same path through the solution space.

Model-based optimization requires models with sufficient predictive power, which MoRSel can help to identify. We demonstrated this by using the selected kinetic model for the UDP-GalNAc multi-enzyme cascade to successfully generate predictions of optimal initial enzyme and substrate concentrations that led to improvements in the cell-free process, as validated experimentally. While we applied MoRSel to specifically select a kinetic model of a cell-free enzyme cascade, its approach of combining a greedy search strategy and robust ranking across multiple different model selection criteria makes it a general tool that can be applied to other model types and processes.

In this work, all experiments with the UDP-GalNAc cascade were exclusively performed in batch mode with purified enzymes and all necessary substrates and cofactors mixed in a one-pot reaction. Different process designs could be beneficial, such as a fed-batch approach, where additional amounts of substrates and/or cofactors are added over time (Frohnmeyer et al., 2024). This holds potential for increasing the cost efficiency of cell-free processes, as purified enzymes (the most expensive components) can be utilized for longer periods. Furthermore, since reaction rates depend on substrate concentrations, a feeding strategy can maintain high substrate levels to prevent kinetic rate limitation and sustain maximum productivity. However, feeding also results in enzyme and metabolite dilution, which must be accounted for in fed-batch process optimization. The advantages of fed-batch operation are most evident in processes limited by substrate inhibition or where high initial concentrations are impractical due to solubility issues. In addition, continuous operation, where the reaction volume remains constant, would require the net inflow to equal the outflow. In such cases, enzyme immobilization techniques could be employed to prevent enzymes from being washed out from the reactor (Maghraby et al., 2023). Regardless of the configuration, optimal feeding profiles for cell-free fed-batch processes can again be determined using dynamic optimization (Espinel-Ríos et al., 2022). Our presented modeling and optimization framework can be applied in these cases as well, as it only requires identifying the suitable decision variables for the specific process design.

Combining MoRSel and our robust model-based optimization framework to improve the specific cell-free process that we chose proved to be successful. Nonetheless, some parts of the methodology could benefit from further development and analysis. Aspects for future work include analyzing the impact of our custom model selection criteria on the evaluation of model variants, incorporating hybrid machine-learning-supported approaches, and extending our optimization approach to other process designs or optimization problems.

Material and methods

Recombinant enzyme production

For the cultivation of E. coli BL21(DE3) as expression host, a complex growth media (Terrific Broth, Lessard, 2013) was used. Recombinant protein expression was carried out using the standard T7 expression system, with the pET-28a(+) vector for the enzymes UDK, UMPK, PPK3, NAHK, and PPA and the pET-15b vector for the enzyme GLMU. Cultivation took place at 37 °C and 200 rpm until an OD of 0.8-1.2 is reached. Subsequent induction was initiated by 0.4 mM IPTG, followed by incubation at 16 °C and 200 rpm for 20 h. Protein purification was facilitated by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), using an Äkta Pure 25 system (GE Healthcare, SE) with a 5 mL HisTrap FF column (Cytiva, DE). The equilibration buffer consisted of 50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 20 mM imidazole and 5 % (v/v) glycerol, while the elution buffer contained 500 mM imidazole. The final enzyme stock solutions were stored in 50 % (v/v) glycerol at −20 °C until use. The purity of the protein samples was determined by SDS-PAGE (Supplementary File S4) using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Reaction conditions

The reaction conditions for the multi-enzyme cascade synthesizing UDP-GalNAc were as follows: Synthesis reactions were performed in a total volume of 500 μL in 150 mM Tris (pH 9) containing 120 mM MgCl2 and 32 mM PolyPn. Unless otherwise stated, the initial enzyme concentrations were 0.1 g/L for UDK, GLMU, UMPK, PPK3, and NAHK, and 0.03 g/L for PPA. Initial concentrations of uridine, GalNAc, and ATP were set to 50 mM, 50 mM, and 0.5 mM, respectively. Enzyme, substrate, and ATP concentrations were adjusted according to the optimization results. The UDP-GalNAc synthesis was initiated by adding the intermediates to the enzyme mixture. The reactions were incubated at 39 °C and 500 rpm for 24 h. For quantification, samples were quenched with 90 °C hot water and diluted with ddH2O.

Quantification of UV active components with high performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC)

UDP-GalNAc reaction samples were analyzed by HPAEC-UV (260 nm), using a setup of a CarboPac PA200 analytical and guard column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the HPAEC unit ICS-5000+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The aqueous system consisted of 100 mM NaOH, ddH2O water and 1 M NaOAc. For optimal peak separation, a gradient (Fig. 12) was used at a flow rate of 0.5 ml/min with a column temperature of 30 °C.

Parameter estimation

Kinetic model parameters were estimated using data from 12 experiments (Supplementary File S1). The start values (initial guesses) of some parameters for the estimations were obtained from the literature, and the upper and lower bounds were also derived from these literature values. Equilibrium constants were sourced from eQuilibrator with pH set to 9 and pMg set to 0.9. The start values and bounds of the remaining parameters were set to different default values, depending on their type. The start values for inhibition and activation constants were chosen such that the associated regulation terms are initialized in a deactivated state (meaning no inhibitory or activating effect on the reaction rate) at the beginning of each estimation. All parameter ensembles throughout this work were generated by repeating parameter estimations with the same setup according to Table 7. The estimations were calculated using the Particle Swarm algorithm implemented in COPASI (iteration limit: 800, swarm size: 50, standard deviation: 1e-06).
