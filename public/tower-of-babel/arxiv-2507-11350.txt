Abstract
. Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) is a worst-case approach to decision making when there is model uncertainty. Though formulated as a single-objective problem, we show that it is intrinsically multi-objective in that DRO solutions map out a near-Pareto-optimal frontier between expected cost and a measure of robustness called worst-case sensitivity (WCS). We take this as the starting point and explore robust decision making through a multi-objective lens. We show that WCS is a measure of spread and derive WCS for a collection of uncertainty sets commonly used in DRO. These sensitivity measures identify the errors against which the nominal expected cost is most vulnerable and the uncertainty set for the worst-case problem that most effectively mitigates it. The associated mean-sensitivity frontier is used to select its size. The multi-objective perspective provides a quantitative measure of robustness and a sensitivity-based approach to addressing important conceptual gaps in DRO—how to choose the family and size of uncertainty sets for a given cost distribution, and how this affects the solution.



Key words:
Distributionally robust optimization, multi-objective optimization, worst-case sensitivity, generalized measure of deviation, model uncertainty, uncertainty sets, mean–sensitivity tradeoff.

1.
Introduction

Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) is a worst-case approach to decision making when there is model uncertainty. Though formulated as a single-objective problem, we show that DRO is intrinsically multi-objective in that its solutions map out a near-Pareto-optimal tradeoff between performance (expected cost) and robustness (worst-case sensitivity (WCS)) as the size of the uncertainty set is varied. The multi-objective perspective provides an intuitive non-worst-case interpretation of DRO, and identifies the fundamental role of worst-case sensitivity as a quantitative measure of robustness. This leads to a systematic sensitivity-based approach to selecting the family and size of the uncertainty set for the DRO problem.

Worst-case sensitivity is the rate of increase of the expected cost under small worst-case deviations from the nominal model. It is a measure of robustness in that it quantifies the sensitivity of the expected cost in a stress test defined by an uncertainty set. Different uncertainty sets have different sensitivity measures, but all measure in one way or another the spread of the cost distribution.

The multi-objective perspective shows that the uncertainty set should be chosen according to the sensitivity measures that need to be reduced (which improves performance in the associated stress test). For example, standard deviation is the sensitivity for any smooth
\phi
-divergence uncertainty set; if it is large, the expected cost is sensitive to underestimating (overestimating) the probability of a high (low) cost event. This sensitivity measure can be reduced by solving a DRO problem with a smooth
\phi
-divergence uncertainty set. Other uncertainty sets control the sensitivity to errors in just the left tail or the right. Frontier plots showing the tradeoff between expected cost and worst-case sensitivity can be used to calibrate the size of the uncertainty set. The choice of uncertainty set can have a major impact on the solution. For example, an uncertainty set that controls the sensitivity to errors in the left tail can make a decision less robust to errors in the right.

Summary of contributions

We take the perspective that (single-objective) DRO is a tool for making tradeoffs between expected cost and worst-case sensitivity
and study the implications of this multi-objective perspective for DRO.

•

We define the notion of worst-case sensitivity (WCS), a quantitative measure of robustness. We establish the connection between single-objective DRO and (nearly) Pareto optimal solutions of a multi-objective mean–sensitivity problem.

•

We show that worst-case sensitivity is a

and hence a measure of the spread of the cost distribution. Different uncertainty sets give different sensitivity measures.

and the Wasserstein distance. For clarity we focus on single-period problems: this is enough to convey the key ideas and is the setting for much of the DRO literature. Generalizations (multi-period problems, online problems with learning, etc) build on the intuition developed in this paper and will be reported elsewhere.

3.
Mean–sensitivity Tradeoff

We introduce worst-case sensitivity (WCS) as a measure of robustness and discuss some of its properties. We propose a new formulation of robust decision making as a tradeoff between expected cost and WCS (robustness). Though intuitively appealing, mean–sensitivity objectives can be difficult to optimize due to nonlinearities and non-convexities introduced by the sensitivity measure. We show, however, that (near) Pareto-optimal solutions can be computed by solving a worst-case DRO problem. WCS is defined in terms of an uncertainty set. In subsequent sections, we derive explicit expressions for WCS for commonly used uncertainty sets. This enables us to select uncertainty sets for the DRO problem that reflect the sensitivity control we desire. The size of the uncertainty set can be chosen to achieve the desired mean–sensitivity tradeoff.

3.1.
Distributionally robust optimization and the mean–sensitivity tradeoff

Nominal model.

Consider a cost function
f(x,Y)
where
x
is the decision and
Y
is a random variable with (nominal) distribution
{\mathbb{P}}
. For example,
\mathbb{P}
could be the empirical distribution associated with a historical sample of
Y

A natural candidate is the solution of the nominal/Sample Average Approximation (SAA) problem

\displaystyle\min_{x}~{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f(x,Y)].

(3.1)

However, out-of-sample performance of this decision can be poor if the real-world distribution of
Y
is different from the nominal
\mathbb{P}
and the expected reward under the nominal is sensitive to these errors. We would like to modify (
3.1

A popular approach to account for model misspecification in (
3.1
) is distributionally robust optimization (DRO). DRO is a worst-case approach which replaces the minimization in (
3.1
) with a worst-case minimization over an uncertainty set of alternative probability measures. A common criticism of DRO is that it can be too conservative. Moreover, worst-case solutions can be sensitive to the choice of family and size of the uncertainty set, and different uncertainty sets can give conflicting recommendations on what it means to be “robust” (e.g., “order more” vs “order less”). We now propose a non-worst-case alternative to (
3.1
) that views robust decision-making as a mean–sensitivity tradeoff instead of worst-case optimization over arbitrarily chosen uncertainty sets. We also show that a (nearly) Pareto optimal frontier can be generated by solving the original single-objective DRO problem.

The objectives in a multi-objective problem reflect the tradeoffs in the problem. For example, the choice of risk measure in a mean–risk problem determine properties of the solution. For this reason, it is natural to understand the properties of worst-case sensitivity.

DRO maps out nearly Pareto optimal solutions for a multi-objective problem with expected cost (performance) and worst-case sensitivity (robustness) as objectives. \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]
, variance
\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]
, and worst-case sensitivity
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]
of
\mathsf{f}\equiv f
under
\mathsf{p}\equiv\mathbb{P}
. In addition,
\mathsf{1}
and
\mathsf{0}
denote column vectors with all entries being
1
’s and
0
’s, respectively. Many of our results have generalizations to more complex settings. The restriction to discrete random variables enables us to communicate our message with minimal technical fuss. For the purposes of readability, all proofs can be found in Appendix
B
.

4.1.
Smooth
\phi
-divergences

Consider the worst-case objective

\displaystyle V_{\phi}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mathsf{q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\phi}(\varepsilon)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}f_{i},

(4.1)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\phi}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\mathsf{q}:=(q_{1},...,q_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\Big|\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\phi\Big(\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\Big)\leq\varepsilon,~\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,~\mathsf{q}\geq\mathsf{0}\Big\}.

(4.2)

We assume the following.

Assumption 4.1
.

\phi(z)
is strictly convex, twice continuously differentiable in
z
, with
\phi(1)=0
,
\phi^{\prime}(1)=0
and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)>0
.

Since
p_{i}>0
for all
i
, Assumption
4.1
and
convex duality imply that
for small
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle V_{\phi}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\min_{\delta>0,\,c}\max_{\mathsf{q>0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}f+\frac{1}{\delta}\Big(\varepsilon-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\phi\Big(\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\Big)\Big)+c\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}-1\Big).

The following result characterizes the solution
(c(\varepsilon),\delta(\varepsilon))
of the dual problem and the associated worst-case distribution
{\mathsf{q}}(\varepsilon)=\big(q_{1}(\varepsilon),\cdots,\,q_{n}(\varepsilon)\big)^{\top}
when
\varepsilon
is small.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
\phi
satisfies Assumption
4.1
. Then

\displaystyle c(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}),

(4.3)

\displaystyle\delta(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{2\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)}{{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}}+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

(4.4)

The family of worst-case distributions
\{{\mathsf{q}}(\varepsilon)\,|\,\varepsilon\geq 0\}
satisfies

\displaystyle q_{i}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=p_{i}\Big\{1+\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{{{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}}}\left(f_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})\right)\Big\}+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

The worst-case expected cost is the expected cost under the worst-case distribution

\displaystyle V_{\phi}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{q}(\varepsilon)}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{2\mathbb{V}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)}}+o(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

It follows that
V_{\phi}(\varepsilon)-V_{\phi}(0)
is
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, so worst-case sensitivity (
3.3
) with
g(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
is as follows.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose Assumption
4.1
is satisfied. Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{V_{\phi}(\varepsilon)-V_{\phi}(0)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}=\sqrt{\frac{2{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)}}.

(4.5)

A closely related result for the case
\phi(z)
is relative entropy was derived in
[
34
]
, while
[
24
]
derives worst-case sensitivity for the “penalty formulation” of the DRO model. Extensions to the penalty case can be found in Appendix
F
.

Example 4.4
.

When
\phi
-divergence is modified
\chi^{2}
,
\phi(z)=\frac{1}{2}(z-1)^{2}

\displaystyle c(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f}),\quad\delta(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{{\mathbb{V}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}}}

and the worst-case distribution is

\displaystyle q_{i}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=p_{i}\big\{1+\delta(f_{i}+c)\big\}=p_{i}\Big\{1+\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{{{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}}}\Big(f_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})\Big)\Big\}.

This holds for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
as long as
q_{i}(\varepsilon)\geq 0
, and not just when it is small.
It follows that

\displaystyle V_{\phi}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}f_{i}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\sqrt{2{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}.

Clearly,
V_{\phi}(\varepsilon)-V_{\phi}(0)\sim O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
and worst-case sensitivity is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}.

4.2.
Nonsmooth
\phi
-divergences

Next consider uncertainty sets (
4.2
) defined by
\phi
-divergences with
\phi(z)=|z-1|
or
\phi(z)=\delta_{I}(z)
for a closed interval
I\subset[0,\infty)
such that
1\in I
, where
\delta_{I}(z):=0
for
z\in I;~\infty
, otherwise. Neither satisfies Assumption
4.1
since the former is not differentiable at
z=1
and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)=0
for the latter.

4.2.1.
Total Variation

For
\phi(z)=|z-1|
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the uncertainty set is given as

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\varepsilon):=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}|\mathsf{q}-\mathsf{p}|\leq\varepsilon,~\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,~\mathsf{q}\geq\mathsf{0}\Big\},

where
|\mathsf{z}|:=(|z_{1}|,...,|z_{n}|)^{\top}
, and denote the worst-case expected cost

V_{\rm TV}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f}):=\max_{\mathsf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\varepsilon)}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathsf{f}).

We can focus on
\varepsilon\leq 2
since
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\varepsilon)
coincides with the unit simplex, i.e.,
entire space of all distributions,
for
\varepsilon>2
.

Consider an ordering of the components of the cost vector
\mathsf{f}=(f_{1},...,f_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from largest to smallest and denote the
i^{th}
largest component by
f_{(i)}
, i.e.,

f_{(1)}\geq\cdots\geq f_{(n)},

and let
p_{(i)}
denote the probability mass corresponding to
f_{(i)}
, whereas
q_{(i)}
denotes the worst-case probability mass on
f_{(i)}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose that
\phi
is given by
\phi(z)=|z-1|
. For
\varepsilon\in(0,\min(\mathsf{p}))
,
a worst-case probability distribution is

(q_{(1)},q_{(2)},...,q_{(n-1)},q_{(n)})=\big(p_{(1)}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},~p_{(2)},~...,~p_{(n-1)},~p_{(n)}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)

and the worst-case objective is

V_{\rm TV}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+\frac{\varepsilon(\max(\mathsf{f})-\min(\mathsf{f}))}{2}.

Accordingly, for the uncertainty set
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\varepsilon)
and
g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, worst-case sensitivity is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})=\frac{\max(\mathsf{f})-\min(\mathsf{f})}{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\times\mbox{``Range of }\mathsf{f}.\mbox{''}

(4.6)

4.2.2.
Budgeted uncertainty

With
\phi(z)=\delta_{[0,1+\varepsilon]}(z)
for
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the uncertainty set becomes

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathrm{b}}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,\mathsf{0}\leq\mathsf{q}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{p}\Big\}

(4.7)

and define the associated worst-case expected cost as

V_{\rm b}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f}):=\max_{\mathsf{q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm b}(\varepsilon)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathsf{f}).

(4.8)

We can focus on
\varepsilon\leq\max_{i}\{\frac{1}{p_{i}}-1\}
since
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm b}(\varepsilon)
is equal to the unit simplex, otherwise. The set (
4.7
) can be referred to as the
budgeted uncertainty set
since, for
\varepsilon\in(0,\min_{i}\{\frac{1}{p_{i}}-1\})
, the worst-case distribution is obtained, as below, by applying a greedy algorithm for (a scaled version of) continuous knapsack problem.

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that
\phi
is given by
\phi(z)=\delta_{[0,1+\varepsilon]}(z)
. Then the worst-case distribution is given by

(q_{(1)},...,q_{(k)},q_{(k+1)},q_{(k+2)},...,q_{(n)})=\big((1+\varepsilon)p_{(1)},...,(1+\varepsilon)p_{(k)},1-(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)},0,...,0\big)

and the worst-case expected cost is

V_{\rm b}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f})=(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)}f_{(i)}+\big\{1-(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)}\big\}f_{(k+1)},

where
k
is such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)}\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}<\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k+1}p_{(i)}
.

Worst-case expected cost under budgeted uncertainty is related to
Conditional Value-at-Risk
(CVaR, for short)
[
48
]
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, it is referred to as
\alpha
-CVaR and defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle:=\min_{\gamma}\Big\{\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\max\{\mathsf{f}-\gamma\mathsf{1},\mathsf{0}\})\Big\}

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathsf{q}}\Big\{\mathsf{f}^{\top}\mathsf{q}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,\mathsf{0}\leq\mathsf{q}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathsf{p}\Big\},

(4.10)

where the last equality follows from the duality.
Obviously,
V_{\rm b}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}}(\mathsf{f})
.

Proposition 4.7
.

For uncertainty set (
4.7
) and
g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, worst-case sensitivity of (
4.8
) is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})-\min(\mathsf{f}).

(4.11)

While worst-case sensitivity (
4.11
) is a measure of spread, it only depends on the “good” side of the cost distribution. In contrast, smooth
\phi
-divergence (
4.5
) and Total Variation (
4.6
) depend on the entire distribution.

4.2.3.
Convex Combination of Expected Loss and CVaR

Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
be a fixed parameter, and consider the uncertainty set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\rm c}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=(1-\varepsilon)\{\mathsf{p}\}+\varepsilon\mathcal{Q}_{\rm CVaR}(\alpha)

(4.12)

parameterized by
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\rm CVaR}(\alpha):=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,~\mathsf{0}\leq\mathsf{q}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathsf{p}\Big\}

is the feasible set of (
4.10
).
The worst-case expected cost is

\displaystyle V_{\rm c}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mathsf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{c}}(\varepsilon)}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{q}}(\mathsf{f}).

Observe that
\mathcal{Q}_{\rm c}(0)=\{\mathsf{p}\}
, so there is no robustness if
\varepsilon=0
and the worst-case expected cost is the expected value of
\mathsf{f}
under the nominal distribution
\mathsf{p}
(the SAA cost).
The uncertainty set (
4.12
) was considered in
[
3
]
(see also
[
53
]
) and is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\rm c}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,~\mathsf{p}(1-\varepsilon)\leq\mathsf{q}\leq\mathsf{p}(1-\varepsilon)+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\alpha}\mathsf{p}\Big\}.

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that
\phi(z)=\delta_{[1-\varepsilon,\frac{1-(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}{1-\alpha}]}(z)
. Then the worst-case probability distribution is given by

\displaystyle\big(q_{(1)},~...,~q_{(k)},~q_{(k+1)},~q_{(k+2)},~...,~q_{(n)}\big)

\displaystyle=\Big((1+\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{1-\alpha})p_{(1)},...,(1+\frac{\alpha\varepsilon}{1-\alpha})p_{(k)},(1-\varepsilon)p_{(k+1)}+\varepsilon\big(1-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)}\big),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(1-\varepsilon)p_{(k+2)},...,(1-\varepsilon)p_{(n)}\Big),

where
k
is such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)}\leq 1-\alpha<\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k+1}p_{(i)}
,
and the worst-case expected cost is

V_{{\rm c}}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f})=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+\varepsilon\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{f})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+\varepsilon\big(\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{f})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})\big).

(4.13)

For uncertainty set (
4.12
) and
g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, worst-case sensitivity is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{f})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})~\equiv~``\alpha\mbox{-CVaR Deviation of }\mathsf{f}.\mbox{''}

(4.14)

Expression (
4.13
) suggests that
V_{\rm c}(\varepsilon)
is linearly increasing at a rate of its
CVaR Deviation

[
49
]
for any non-uniform vector
\mathsf{f}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.
The worst-case sensitivity (
4.14
) is the spread of the “bad” part of the cost distribution, which contrasts with (
4.11
).
In particular, for
\alpha\in[1-p_{(1)},1)
,
\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{f})=\max(\mathsf{f}):=\max\{f_{1},...,f_{n}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})=\max(\mathsf{f})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})
.

From (
4.13
) and (
4.14
), we see that the WCS is equal to the average sensitivity, or that

V_{\rm c}(\varepsilon;\mathsf{f})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+\varepsilon\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f}),

so the mean–sensitivity frontier coincides with the original worst-case objective for any
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.
This feature differentiates
V_{\rm c}
from the other
\phi
-divergence-based objectives,
V_{\phi},V_{\rm TV}
, and
V_{\rm b}
.
Also, we can associate the frontier of
V_{\rm c}
with the consistency of risk-averse decision making. Specifically, it is known
[
44
]
that Second-order Stochastic Dominance (SSD) implies the Pareto dominance in terms of the mean cost and CVaR. Based on this, we can say that any point on the mean–CVaR Deviation frontier for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
is SSD efficient if the mean–CVaR Deviation pair
(\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f}),\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f}))
corresponds to a unique
\mathsf{f}
(see
[
42
]
for the details).

Remark 4.9
.

More generally, for any
L,U
such that
0\leq L\leq 1\leq U
, the uncertainty set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\rm h}(L,U)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,~L\mathsf{p}\leq\mathsf{q}\leq U\mathsf{p}\Big\}

corresponds to
\phi(z)=\delta_{[L,U]}(z)
and the worst-case expected cost

\displaystyle V_{\rm h}(L,U;\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle:=L\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})+(1-L)\cdot\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\frac{U-1}{U-L}}(\mathsf{f}).

If
(L,U)=(0,1+\varepsilon)
,
V_{\rm h}(L,U)
is
V_{\rm b}(\varepsilon)
.
If
(L,U)=(1-\varepsilon,\frac{1-(1-\varepsilon)\alpha}{1-\alpha})
,
V_{\rm h}(L,U)
is
V_{\rm c}(\varepsilon)
.
In particular for the latter,
while the parameter
\alpha
for CVaR is usually fixed (e.g., at
0.95
or
0.99
), it can be viewed as another hyperparameter, in addition to
\varepsilon
, that defines the uncertainty set. A reasonable option to merge the two parameters
L,U
into a single one is to set as
U=\frac{1}{L}=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, and the uncertainty set becomes symmetric between
\mathsf{q}
and
\mathsf{p}
since
\delta_{[L,U]}(\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}})=\delta_{[L,U]}(\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}})
or

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\rm s}(\nu)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,(1-\varepsilon)\mathsf{p}\leq\mathsf{q}\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\mathsf{p}\Big\}=\Big\{\mathsf{q}\,\Big|\,\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{q}=1,(1-\varepsilon)\mathsf{q}\leq\mathsf{p}\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\mathsf{q}\Big\}.

For the symmetric case
\mathcal{Q}_{\rm s}(\varepsilon)
, the worst-case expected cost is then equivalent to the convex combination of the SAA cost and
\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}
-CVaR, and the
worst-case sensitivity becomes

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\frac{1}{2}}(\mathsf{f})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})
, i.e.,
\frac{1}{2}
-CVaR Deviation of
\mathsf{f}
.

4.3.
Extensions to Robust CVaR

Consider the distributionally worst-case of
\beta
-CVaR:

\displaystyle\mathrm{RCVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}^{\varepsilon}(\mathsf{f}):=\max_{\mathsf{q}\in\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)}\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{q},\beta}(\mathsf{f}),

where

\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)
denotes an uncertain set of probability distribution
\mathsf{q}
. The worst-case sensitivity can also be defined with a growth rate
g
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathrm{RCVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}^{\varepsilon}(\mathsf{f})-\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})}{g(\varepsilon)}.

(4.15)

To display results simpler, we here suppose the following nondegeneracy condition.

Assumption 4.10
.

There is an integer
k
such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}p_{(i)}<1-\beta<\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k+1}p_{(i)}
.

Note that Assumption
4.10
implies that
\beta
-VaR of
\mathsf{f}
is given as
\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})=f_{(k+1)}
.

Proposition 4.11
.

Suppose that distribution of
\mathsf{f}
satisfies Assumption
4.10
.

(a)

With
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{Q}_{\phi}(\varepsilon)
and
g(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, worst-case sensitivity (
4.15
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsf{p}}\big(|\mathsf{f}-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})\mathsf{1}|_{+}\big)},

where
|\mathsf{f}|_{+}:=\max(\mathsf{f},\mathsf{0})\equiv(\max\{f_{1},0\},...,\max\{f_{n},0\})^{\top}
for
\mathsf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(b)

With
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{Q}_{\rm TV}(\varepsilon)
and
g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, worst-case sensitivity (
4.15
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(1-\beta)}\big(\max(\mathsf{f})-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})\big).

(c)

With
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{Q}_{\rm b}(\varepsilon)
and
g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, worst-case sensitivity (
4.15
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f}).

(d)

With
\mathcal{Q}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{Q}_{\rm c}(\varepsilon)
and
g(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, worst-case sensitivity (
4.15
) is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mbox{``}\alpha\mbox{-CVaR Deviation of }\left|\mathsf{f}-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})\mathsf{1}\right|_{+}.\mbox{''}

Recalling that CVaR is approximately equal to the conditional expected cost over the quantile (VaR), i.e.,
f_{(1)}-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f}),...,f_{(k)}-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})
,
Proposition
4.11
shows that worst-case sensitivity of the robust CVaR objective with different uncertainty set can be captured as different measures of spread of the tail distribution above VaR, i.e.,
|\mathsf{f}-\mathrm{VaR}_{\mathsf{p},\beta}(\mathsf{f})\mathsf{1}|_{+}
.

4.4.
Wasserstein metric

Consider the worst-case expected cost with a constraint on the type-
1
Wasserstein metric
[
12
,
20
,
23
]
:

\displaystyle V_{\rm w}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\gamma\in{\mathcal{X}}}\Big\{\int_{z}f(x,\,z)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}({\rm d}z)\Big)\,\Big|\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{z}\|z-Y_{i}\|\gamma_{i}({\rm d}z)\leq\varepsilon\Big\},

(4.16)

where
\|\cdot\|
is any norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}=\Big\{\gamma\;\Big|\;\int_{z}\gamma_{i}({\rm d}z)=p_{i},\;i=1,\cdots,\,n,\;\gamma_{i}({\rm d}z)\geq 0\Big\}.

The proof of the following result can be found in the Appendix.

Proposition 4.12
.

For (
4.16
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]

\displaystyle=V_{\rm w}^{\prime}(0^{+})=\max_{i=1,\cdots,\,n}\max_{z_{i}}\frac{f(z_{i})-f(Y_{i})}{\|z_{i}-Y_{i}\|}.

(4.17)

It follows that when
\varepsilon
is small

\displaystyle V_{\rm w}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}f(Y_{i})+\varepsilon\Big(\max_{i=1,\cdots,\,n}\max_{z_{i}}\frac{f(z_{i})-f(Y_{i})}{\|z_{i}-Y_{i}\|}\Big)+o(\varepsilon)

and that the DRO problem is (almost) the same as the following mean–sensitivity problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x}\max_{\gamma\in{\mathcal{X}}}\int_{z}f(x,\,z)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}({\rm d}z)\Big)

\displaystyle\quad=\min_{x}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f(x,Y)]+\varepsilon\Big\{\underbrace{\max_{i=1,\cdots,\,n}\max_{z_{i}}\frac{f(x,z_{i})-f(x,Y_{i})}{\|z_{i}-Y_{i}\|}}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f(x,\cdot)]}\Big\}+o(\varepsilon).

The Wasserstein uncertainty set is quite different from the others in that sensitivity is measured by “local perturbations” corresponding to moving probability mass from the support of the nominal distribution to a worst-case point. When the cost is concave in
Y
, it has a particularly simple form.

Example 4.13
.

If
f(z)
is concave in
z
, then the set of optimal dual variables of (
B.24
) is

\displaystyle\Big\{\lambda\,\big|\,\lambda\geq\max_{i=1,\cdots,\,n}\|\nabla f(Y_{i})\|\Big\}=\argmin_{\lambda\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\max_{z_{i}}\Big\{f(x,z_{i})-f(x,Y_{i})-\lambda\|z_{i}-Y_{i}\|\Big\}

and worst-case sensitivity

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f]=\max_{i=1,\cdots,\,n}\|\nabla f(Y_{i})\|.

Example 4.14
.

Consider the cost function

\displaystyle f(x,Y)=-r\min\{x,Y\}-q\max\{x-Y,0\}+s\max\{Y-x,0\}+cx

(4.18)

where
0\leq q<c<r
and
s\geq 0
. The negative of this cost function is the reward function for an inventory problem, so minimizing
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f(x,Y)]
is equivalent to maximizing expected reward. If
x\in(\min_{i}Y_{i},\max_{i}Y_{i})
, it can be shown that for a Wasserstein metric with
p=1

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f(x,\cdot)]=\max\{r-q,s\},

so the SAA optimizer is also the solution of the DRO problem for a large range of
\varepsilon
, beyond which, the order quantity is either smaller than
\min_{i}Y_{i}
or larger than
\max_{i}Y_{i}
, which is not sensible. This suggests that the Wasserstein uncertainty set with
p=1
may not be a good choice for the robust inventory problem.

4.5.
Summary

Solutions of a large class of worst-case DRO problems are (nearly) Pareto optimal for a multi-objective problem with expected cost (performance) and a worst-case sensitivity (robustness) as objectives. The sensitivity measure in this tradeoff depends on the uncertainty set in the worst-case problem. Sensitivity measures are illustrated in Figure
4.1
and summarized in Table
4.1
. The multi-objective perspective provides an intuitive non-worst-case interpretation of DRO as a tradeoff between performance and robustness.

Taking this perspective, it makes sense to select uncertainty sets according to the implied sensitivity measure, giving us a principled way of selecting uncertainty sets. It is clear from Figure
4.1
and Table
4.1
that these can be very different. For example, worst-case sensitivity under smooth
\phi
-divergence is the standard-deviation and hence depends on the entire cost distribution whereas “budgeted uncertainty” is concerned with its left-tail; the “convex combination of expected cost and
\alpha
-CVaR” depends on the right-tail. The uncertainty set should be chosen according to the properties of the cost distribution (e.g., if cost has a long right tail but is bounded below, it makes little sense to use the “budgeted uncertainty” uncertainty set.)
Depending on the cost function different uncertainty sets can give vastly different “robust” solutions because they measure robustness (sensitivity) differently.

Despite its intuitive appeal, the challenge of working directly with the multi-objective problem (
3.4
) is that worst-case sensitivity can be difficult to optimize (e.g., the standard deviation of a convex cost may not be convex). In contrast, the worst-case objective does not destroy convexity and there is a large literature on solving DRO problems efficiently. As such, DRO provides a computationally tractable approach to finding (nearly) Pareto-optimal solutions for the multi-objective mean–sensitivity problem.

5.
Mixtures and Bayesian models

Suppose we have variables
(\theta,Y)
with a joint distribution
{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}\theta,{\rm d}Y)
. We denote the marginal distribution
{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}\theta)=\rho({\rm d}\theta)

and the conditional distribution
{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}Y|\theta)=L^{\theta}({\rm d}Y)
so

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}\theta,{\rm d}Y)=\rho({\rm d}\theta)L^{\theta}({\rm d}Y).

Bayesian models, where
{\mathcal{L}}=\{L^{\theta}(y)|\theta\in\Theta\}
is a parameterized family of likelihood functions and
\rho(\theta)
is the posterior distribution on
\Theta
, belong to this class. The posterior is obtained by updating prior
\rho_{0}({\rm d}Y)
using data
Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{m}
and Bayes’ rule. The likelihood function
L^{\theta}
is a modeling choice, while the posterior
\rho(\theta)
depends on the user-specified prior
\rho_{0}(\theta)
and likelihood
L^{\theta}(y)
. We are concerned about the sensitivity of the expected cost to deviations from
{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}\theta,{\rm d}y)
. Another important class is mixture models with populations indexed by
\theta\in\{\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{n}\}
and population distribution
{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}Y|\theta=\theta_{i})=L^{\theta_{i}}({\rm d}Y)
.
For concreteness, we refer to the marginal distribution
\rho(\theta)
as the posterior and the conditional distribution
L^{\theta}({\rm d}Y)
as the likelihood, fully recognizing that it is more general than a Bayesian learning model.

Let
f
be a cost function of the variables
(\theta,Y)
. The expected cost is

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}[f(\theta,Y)]={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)\,|\,\theta]\big\}.

The expected cost depends of the joint distribution and hence and we are concerned about the sensitivity of the expected cost relative to worst-case deviations from
\mathbb{P}
.

We begin by defining deviations from the posterior
\rho({\rm d}\theta)
and likelihood
{\mathbb{P}}({\rm d}Y|\theta)
.
Let
\eta
be a distribution for
\theta
on
\Theta
and
{\mathcal{Q}}=\{{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}|\theta\in\Theta\}
be a family of probability distributions for
Y
parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
. Let
d(\eta|\rho)
be a deviation measure of
\eta
from
\rho
. For every
\theta\in\Theta
, let
d({\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}|L^{\theta})
be the deviation of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
from
L^{\theta}
. We define the deviation of
{\mathcal{Q}}
from
\mathcal{L}
as the expected deviation of the probability measure
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
from
{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
under the posterior
\rho
:

\displaystyle d({\mathcal{Q}}|{\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{\rho}}\big\{d({\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}|L^{\theta})\big\}.

Let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}(\varepsilon)=\big\{\eta\big|d(\eta|\rho)\leq\varepsilon\big\},\;{\mathcal{L}}(\delta)=\big\{{\mathbb{Q}}\big|d({\mathbb{Q}}|L^{\theta})\leq\delta\big\}

be uncertainty sets for the prior and likelihood distributions.
Consider the worst-case problem

\displaystyle V(\epsilon,\delta)=\max_{\eta\in{\mathcal{P}}(\varepsilon)}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\Big\{\max_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\delta)}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]\Big\}

(5.1)

For the uncertainty sets
{\mathcal{L}}(\delta)
for the inner problem and
{\mathcal{P}}(\varepsilon)
for the outer problem, there are ambiguity costs
{\mathcal{A}}_{L^{\theta}}\big(\varepsilon;f(\theta,Y)\big)
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}\big(\varepsilon;\Psi(\theta)\big)
, respectively, such that

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\delta)}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]+{\mathcal{A}}_{L^{\theta}}\big(\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big)

and

\displaystyle\max_{\eta\in{\mathcal{P}}(\varepsilon)}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\big\{\Psi(\theta)\big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\big\{\Psi(\theta)\big\}+{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}\big(\varepsilon;\Psi(\theta)\big).

It follows that the worst-case expected cost

\displaystyle V(\epsilon,\delta)={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]\Big\}+{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho,{\mathcal{L}}}\big(\varepsilon,\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho,{\mathcal{L}}}\big(\varepsilon,\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big):={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathcal{A}}_{L^{\theta}}\big(\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big)\Big\}+{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}\Big(\varepsilon;{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]+{\mathcal{A}}_{L^{\theta}}\big(\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big)\Big)

is the ambiguity cost for the worst-case problem (
5.1
). We make the following assumptions about the ambiguity costs. The functions
g(\varepsilon)
and
g(\delta)
for the two ambiguity costs need not be the same.

Assumption 5.1
.

The ambiguity costs
{\mathcal{A}}_{L^{\theta}}\big(\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big)
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}\big(\varepsilon;\Psi(\theta)\big)
are such that

(1)

there is a function
g(\delta)
such that
g(\delta)\rightarrow 0
when
\delta\rightarrow 0
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{L^{\theta}}\big(\delta;f(\theta,Y)\big)=g(\delta){\mathcal{S}}_{L^{\theta}}(f(\theta,Y))+o(g(\delta))

for every
\theta\in{\Theta}
;

(2)

there is a function
g(\varepsilon)
such that
g(\varepsilon)\rightarrow 0
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}\big(\varepsilon;\Psi(\theta)\big)=g(\varepsilon){\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}(\Psi(\theta))+o(g(\delta)).

(3)

{\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}(\Psi)
is continuous in
\rho
.

The following result gives an expansion of the value function when
\varepsilon
and
\delta
are small. The proof can be found in Appendix
E
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Suppose that Assumption
5.1
holds. Then

\displaystyle V(\epsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]\Big\}+g(\delta){\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathcal{S}}_{L^{\theta}}(f(\theta,Y))\Big\}+g(\varepsilon){\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}(f(\theta,Y))\Big)

\displaystyle+o(g(\delta))+o(g({\varepsilon})).

We can now define the worst-case sensitivity with respect to the posterior

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}(f)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{V(\epsilon,0)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]\Big\}}{g({\epsilon})}={\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]\Big\},

and the likelihood

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{L}}(f)=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{V(0,\delta)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(\theta,Y)]\Big\}}{g(\delta)}={\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathcal{S}}_{L^{\theta}}(f(\theta,Y))\Big\}.

Then the worst-case problem is a tradeoff between expected cost under the nominal and the posterior and likelihood sensitivities.

\displaystyle\min_{x}\max_{\eta\in{\mathcal{P}}(\varepsilon)}{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\Big\{\max_{{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\delta)}{\mathbb{E}}_{Q^{\theta}}[f(x,\theta,Y)]\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{x}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(x,\theta,Y)]\Big\}+g(\delta){\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big({\mathcal{S}}_{L^{\theta}}(f(x,\theta,Y))\Big)+g(\varepsilon){\mathcal{S}}_{\rho}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(x,\theta,Y)]\Big)

\displaystyle+o(g({\epsilon}))+o(g({\delta})).

Example 5.3
.

Suppose the
\eta
is restricted to being absolutely continuous with respect to

and for every
\theta\in\Theta
,

is absolutely continuous with respect to

. Let
\frac{{\rm d}\eta}{{\rm d}\rho}
denote the Radon-Nikodym derivative of

with respect to

\phi

\displaystyle d(\eta|\rho)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\left[\phi\left(\frac{{\rm d}\eta}{{\rm d}\rho}\right)\right].

\displaystyle V(\epsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big\{{\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(x,Y)]\Big\}+\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}\Big({\sigma}_{L^{\theta}}(f(x,Y))\Big)+\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)}}{\sigma}_{\rho}\Big({\mathbb{E}}_{L^{\theta}}[f(x,Y)]\Big)

and worst-case likelihood sensitivity is

6.
Examples

We consider two examples, inventory control and CVaR minimization. Both illustrate how the multi-objective perspective can be used to select the family and size of uncertainty sets in a DRO model. In particular sensitivity measures can be used to identify vulnerabilities to misspecification from the cost distribution of the nominal solution and the uncertainty set that most efficiently controls these sensitivities. Mean–sensitivity frontiers are used to choose the size of the uncertainty set.

Both examples show that the sensitivity measure of the uncertainty set can have a substantial impact on the DRO solution; a decision is “robust” with respect to the stress test defined by an uncertainty set and DRO solutions reduce sensitivity with respect to its uncertainty set. For the inventory problem, we even have conflicting recommendations on how to be “more robust.” (There are also examples where robust solutions under different uncertainty sets are almost the same (Appendix
D
).)

7.
Conclusion

Though formulated as a single-objective worst-case problem, DRO can be understood as a tradeoff between performance and sensitivity. Each uncertainty set induces a quantitative robustness measure (worst-case sensitivity), and the worst-case DRO problem maps out a (nearly) Pareto-optimal mean–sensitivity frontier as the size of the uncertainty set is varied.

The multi-objective interpretation of DRO resolves many of the misgivings held by applied researchers on the use of worst-case models to make decisions when there is model uncertainty: That it is too conservative, with no systematic approach for selecting the family or size of the uncertainty set, or clear insight on why the resulting decision is even “robust” (particularly when different uncertainty sets give contradicting recommendations).

This reframing lets us choose uncertainty sets that target “large sensitivities” in the cost distribution, and its size from the underlying mean–sensitivity frontier. It shows that DRO is not inherently “conservative” but a computationally tractable tool for mapping out the performance–robustness tradeoff. Under standard assumptions, worst-case sensitivity is a generalized measure of deviation. Intuitively, the expected cost under the nominal distribution can be sensitive to changes in the probability assigned to extreme values so a cost distribution with a large spread is not robust.

We derive sensitivity measures for a collection of commonly used uncertainty sets, for risk-measures, and for Bayesian (mixture) models. For the Bayesian model, worst-case sensitivity can be decomposed into posterior and likelihood sensitivity. The contrast across sensitivity measures explains why DRO solutions with different uncertainty sets can be so different.

By quantifying robustness, and bringing the tradeoff between performance and robustness to the fore, we provide a new way of understanding Distributionally Robust Optimization and building robust optimization models. We focus on the single-period problem as it is sufficient to convey the key ideas and is the setting for much of the DRO literature. The main insights, however, go well beyond the setting of this paper and generalizations will be reported elsewhere.

&\mathsf{Q}-\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mathsf{q}\leq\mathsf{0},\\
&\mathsf{1}^{\top}(\mathsf{u}+\mathsf{v})\leq\varepsilon,\\
&\mathsf{p}-\mathsf{q}=\mathsf{u}-\mathsf{v},\\
&\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{q},\mathsf{u},\mathsf{v}\geq\mathsf{0}\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{|lll}\underset{\mathsf{Q},\mathsf{u},\mathsf{v}}{\mbox{maximize}}&\mathsf{f}^{\top}\mathsf{Q}\\
\mbox{subject to}&\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{Q}=1,&\leftarrow\gamma\\
&\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{u}-\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{v}=0,&\leftarrow\nu\\

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{|lll}\underset{\gamma,\nu,\mathsf{\lambda},\kappa}{\mbox{minimize}}&\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mathsf{p}^{\top}\mathsf{\lambda}+\varepsilon\kappa\\

&-\mathsf{1}\nu-\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mathsf{\lambda}+\kappa\mathsf{1}\geq\mathsf{0},\\
&\mathsf{\lambda}\geq\mathsf{0},~\kappa\geq 0\end{array}

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[t]{lll}\underset{\gamma,\nu}{\mbox{minimize}}&\gamma+\frac{1}{1-\beta}\mathsf{p}^{\top}|\mathsf{f}-\mathsf{1}\gamma|_{+}+\varepsilon\max(\left|\mathsf{1}\nu-\frac{1}{1-\beta}|\mathsf{f}-\gamma\mathsf{1}|_{+}\right|),\end{array}

Consequently, we reach the desired result.

Preliminaries: Some useful results from convex duality

We summarize general properties of the dual problem and the relationship between optimal dual variables and super-gradients of the value function for the optimization problem

\displaystyle V(\varepsilon):=\max_{x\in{\Omega}}F(x)\;\mbox{subject to:}\;G(x)\leq\varepsilon

(B.21)

which we apply to (
4.16
).
Here, we assume that
F:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
G:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, where
X
is a vector space and
\Omega
is a convex subset of
\mathcal{X}
.
The associated dual problem is

\displaystyle D(\varepsilon):=\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\max_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}F(x)+\lambda\big\{\varepsilon-G(x)\big\}\equiv\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{H(\lambda)+\lambda\varepsilon\Big\}

(B.22)

where
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
is the Lagrange multiplier and

\displaystyle H(\lambda):=\max_{x\in{\Omega}}\Big\{F(x)-\lambda G(x)\Big\}.

Note that
H(\lambda)
is convex in
\lambda
.
We denote the derivative of
H
at
\lambda
by
H^{\prime}(\lambda)
, and the directional derivaties

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\lambda^{+})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{H(\lambda+\delta)-H(\lambda)}{\delta},

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\lambda^{-})

\displaystyle:=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{H(\lambda-\delta)-H(\lambda)}{\delta}.

(We refer to
H^{\prime}(\lambda^{+})
and
H^{\prime}(\lambda^{-})
as the right and left derivative of
H
at
\lambda
, respectively).
H(\lambda)
is convex in
\lambda
so
H^{\prime}(\lambda^{+})
is increasing in
\lambda
and
H^{\prime}(\lambda^{-})
is decreasing in
\lambda
.

Lemma B.2
.

Consider the optimization problem (
B.21
). Assume that
F(x)
is concave, that
G(x)
is convex and non-negative, that there is an
x\in\Omega
such that
G(x)=0
, and that
V(\varepsilon)
is finite for every
\varepsilon
.
Then
V(\varepsilon)
is concave and increasing in
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and differentiable at almost every
\varepsilon>0
. When
\varepsilon>0
, strong duality holds
(V(\varepsilon)=D(\varepsilon))
and the maximum of the dual problem is achieved. If
\lambda(\varepsilon)
is a solution of the dual problem corresponding to
\varepsilon>0
, then
\lambda(\varepsilon)
is a super-gradient of
V
at
\varepsilon
. If
V
is differentiable at
\varepsilon>0
, then the dual problem has a unique solution and
V^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\lambda(\varepsilon)
.

Proof of Lemma
B.2

When
\varepsilon>0
the Lagrange Duality Theorem
[
39
]
implies that strong duality holds and that there exists a solution
\lambda(\varepsilon)
of the dual problem.

\Box

Proposition B.3
.

Assume that
F(x)
is concave, that
G(x)
is convex and non-negative, that there is an
x\in\Omega
such that
G(x)=0
, and that
V(\varepsilon)
is finite for every
\varepsilon
.
Let
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that
\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow\varepsilon
. Suppose that

\varepsilon_{i}

\varepsilon_{i}

is increasing as
\varepsilon_{i}\to\varepsilon
. If
\lambda_{i}\uparrow\lambda^{*}<\infty
when
\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow\varepsilon
, then
\lambda^{*}
is a solution of the dual problem at
\varepsilon
.

Proof of Proposition
B.3

Since strong duality holds at
\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle V(\varepsilon_{i})=\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\Big\{H(\lambda)+\lambda\varepsilon_{i}\Big\}=H(\lambda(\varepsilon_{i}))+\lambda(\varepsilon_{i})\varepsilon_{i},

where

is a convex function of
\lambda\geq 0
, and hence is differentiable at almost every
\lambda\geq 0
.

Consider first the case that
\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow\varepsilon
where
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\lambda_{i}\equiv\lambda(\varepsilon_{i})
is a solution of the dual problem

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{+})+\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{-})-\varepsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(B.23)

Since

and

as

, and the right derivative is increasing in

, it follows that

\displaystyle H^{\prime}({\lambda^{*}}^{+})+\varepsilon\geq\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\Big\{H^{\prime}({\lambda_{i}^{+}})+\varepsilon_{i}\Big\}\geq 0.

On the other hand, the left derivative

is left-continuous in

so

\displaystyle H^{\prime}({\lambda^{*}}^{-})-\varepsilon=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\big\{H^{\prime}_{-}(\lambda(\varepsilon_{i}))-\varepsilon_{i}\Big\}\geq 0.

It follows that
\lambda^{*}
is optimal for the dual problem at
\varepsilon
.

When
\varepsilon=0
, we need only consider the derivative from the right at
\lambda(0)
. In particular, it follows from (
B.23
) that

\displaystyle H^{\prime}({{\lambda^{*}}^{+}})\geq\lim_{\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow 0}\Big\{H^{\prime}_{+}(\lambda(\varepsilon_{i}))-\varepsilon_{i}\Big\}\geq 0.

\Box

\begin{array}[]{r|ll}\frac{1}{C_{\alpha,n}}=&\underset{\mathsf{f}}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}}{\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{f})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathsf{p}}(\mathsf{f})}\\

) is equivalently rewritten as

\begin{array}[]{r|ll}\frac{1}{C_{\alpha,n}^{2}}=&\underset{\mathsf{f},\mathsf{z}}{\mbox{minimize}}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{z}^{\top}\mathsf{z}\\
&\mbox{subject to}&\mathrm{CVaR}_{\mathsf{p},\alpha}(\mathsf{z})=1,\\
&&\mathsf{z}=\mathsf{f}-\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{1}\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{f},~\mathsf{f}\neq C\mathsf{1}\mbox{ for any }C,\end{array}

where the objective function is squared for the convenience. Noting that the final constraint implies
\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{z}=0
,
we consider the following relaxed optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{r|ll}&\underset{\mathsf{z}}{\mbox{minimize}}&\frac{1}{n}(z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{n}^{2})\\
&\mbox{subject to}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{k}+(\kappa-k)z_{k+1}\}=1,\\
&&z_{1}\geq z_{2}\geq\cdots\geq z_{n},\\
&&z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}=0,\end{array}

(C.4)

where
\kappa:=n(1-\alpha)
and
k:=\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
.

Let us tentatively consider to remove the inequality constraints from (
C.4
):

\begin{array}[]{r|ll}&\underset{\mathsf{z}}{\mbox{minimize}}&\frac{1}{n}(z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{n}^{2})\\
&\mbox{subject to}&z_{1}+\cdots+z_{k}+(\kappa-k)z_{k+1}=\kappa,\\
&&(1-\kappa+k)z_{k+1}+z_{k+2}+\cdots+z_{n}=-\kappa.\\
\end{array}

The Lagrangian of this is given by

L(\mathsf{z},\lambda,\mu)=z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{n}^{2}-2\lambda\{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{k}+(\kappa-k)z_{k+1}-\kappa\}-2\mu\{(1-\kappa+k)z_{k+1}+z_{k+2}+\cdots+z_{n}+\kappa\}

and the optimality condition is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{j}}(\mathsf{z},\lambda,\mu)=0,&j=1,...,n,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}(\mathsf{z},\lambda,\mu)=0&\leftrightarrow z_{1}+\cdots+z_{k}+(\kappa-k)z_{k+1}=\kappa,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mu}(\mathsf{z},\lambda,\mu)=0&\leftrightarrow(1-\kappa+k)z_{k+1}+z_{k+2}+\cdots+z_{n}=-\kappa.\\
\end{array}\right.

Here the first condition is

\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_{j}}(\mathsf{z},\lambda,\mu)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2z_{j}-2\lambda=0,&1\leq j\leq k,\\
2z_{k+1}-2(\kappa-k)\lambda-2(1-\kappa+k)\mu=0,&j=k+1,\\
2z_{j}-2\mu=0,&k+2\leq j\leq n.\end{array}\right.

Substituting
z_{j}
’s derived from this to the last two conditions, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rrl}\displaystyle\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}\}\lambda&+(\kappa-k)(1-\kappa+k)\mu&=\kappa,\\
\displaystyle(\kappa-k)(1-\kappa+k)\lambda&+\{n-k-1+(1-\kappa+k)^{2}\}\mu&=-\kappa,\end{array}\right.

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{\kappa(n-\kappa)}{n\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}\}-\kappa^{2}}\\
\displaystyle\mu=\frac{-\kappa^{2}}{n\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}\}-\kappa^{2}},\\
\end{array}\right.

so

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle z_{j}&\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa(n-\kappa)}{n\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}\}-\kappa^{2}},&j=1,...,k,\\
\displaystyle z_{k+1}&\displaystyle=\frac{-\kappa^{2}+n\kappa(\kappa-k)}{n\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}\}-\kappa^{2}},&\\
\displaystyle z_{j}&\displaystyle=\frac{-\kappa^{2}}{n\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}\}-\kappa^{2}},&j=k+2,...,n,\\
\end{array}\right.

(C.5)

and
the objective value is then

\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{z}^{\top}\mathsf{z}=\frac{\kappa^{2}}{n\{k+(\kappa-k)^{2}-\kappa^{2}\}}.

(C.6)

The vector

\mathsf{z}=(z_{1},...,z_{n})^{\top}
given by (
C.5
) satisfies
z_{1}\geq\cdots\geq z_{n}
, and turns out to be optimal to (
C.4
) as well.
Furthermore,
for the vector
\mathsf{z}
given by (
C.5
) and any constant
C
,
we can reproduce a nonconstant vector
\mathsf{f}
as
\mathsf{f}=\mathsf{z}+C\mathsf{1}
, which satisfies
\mathsf{z}=\mathsf{f}-\frac{1}{n}\mathsf{1}\mathsf{1}^{\top}\mathsf{f}
. Consequently, the square root of (
C.6
) is the optimal value of
(
C.3
).
