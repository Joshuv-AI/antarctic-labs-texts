Exact Sequence Interpolation with Transformers
Albert Alcalde, Giovanni Fantuzzi, Enrique Zuazua

Abstract
We prove that transformers can exactly interpolate datasets of finite input sequences in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , with corresponding output sequences of smaller or equal length. Specifically, given N N sequences of arbitrary but finite lengths in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} and output sequences of lengths m 1 , … , m N ∈ ℕ m^{1},\dots,m^{N}\in\mathbb{N} , we construct a transformer with 𝒪 ⁡ ( ∑ j = 1 N m j ) \mathcal{O}(\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}) blocks and 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ ∑ j = 1 N m j ) \smash{\mathcal{O}(d\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j})} parameters that exactly interpolates the dataset. Our construction provides complexity estimates that are independent of the input sequence length, by alternating feed-forward and self-attention layers and by capitalizing on the clustering effect inherent to the latter. Our novel constructive method also uses low-rank parameter matrices in the self-attention mechanism, a common feature of practical transformer implementations. These results are first established in the hardmax self-attention setting, where the geometric structure permits an explicit and quantitative analysis, and are then extended to the softmax setting. Finally, we demonstrate the applicability of our exact interpolation construction to learning problems, in particular by providing convergence guarantees to a global minimizer under regularized training strategies. Our analysis contributes to the theoretical understanding of transformer models, offering an explanation for their excellent performance in exact sequence-to-sequence interpolation tasks.

Introduction

Transformers
Vaswani et al. (2017)
have revolutionized machine learning (ML) by outperforming traditional residual networks (ResNets) in applications such as natural language processing
OpenAI (2024)
and computer vision
Dosovitskiy et al. (2021)
, where inputs naturally take the form of finite sequences of
d
-dimensional vectors.
Their practical success relies on the effectiveness of the self-attention layers, which act between feed-forward layers to update each vector in the sequence by aggregating information from all other vectors.

The expressive power of feed-forward layers has already been studied extensively, see, e.g.,
Cybenko (1989)
;
Hornik (1991)
;
Leshno et al. (1993)
;
Pinkus (1999)
;
Yarotsky (2017)
;
Park et al. (2021)
;
Vardi et al. (2022)
;
Li et al. (2022)
;
Ruiz-Balet and Zuazua (2023)
;
Álvarez-López et al. (2025)
. In particular, it is known
Yun et al. (2019)
that sufficiently deep feed-forward networks can interpolate arbitrary input data. Unlike feed-forward networks, transformers rely on self-attention, and thus their interpolation properties and complexity scaling require a distinct analysis.

1.1
Summary of contributions

In this work, we prove that certain transformer architectures can perfectly interpolate data and we provide explicit complexity estimates in terms of layers and parameter count. The key insight of our analysis is that transformers can attain the same expressive power as architectures purely based on feed-forward layers with significantly fewer parameters. In more detail, our core contributions are the following.

•

Exact interpolation with real vector-valued outputs.

We show via an explicit construction that transformer parameters can always be chosen to interpolate arbitrary real vector-valued sequence-to-sequence maps exactly. We therefore overcome previous limitations to discrete outputs
Kim et al. (2023)
or approximate interpolation
Geshkovski et al. (2024)
(see also
Section

1.4
below for further discussion).

•

Complexity independent of input lengths.

The required transformer size depends only on the total output length, not on the (possibly much longer) input lengths. This helps explain why transformers excel in tasks where long inputs must be mapped to shorter outputs, such as text summarization or classification.

•

Parameter efficiency through low-rank attention.

By parameterizing the attention layer of our transformer using matrices that are sums of a rank-one matrix and a multiple of the identity, the number of parameters in our construction grows linearly with the input embedding dimension
d
. This is consistent with efficient transformer designs used in practice.

•

Implications for regularized training.

The existence of exact interpolating transformers ensures that global minimizers of regularized training objectives scale linearly with the regularization parameter, giving a practical criterion to detect whether training converges globally or only to local minima.

We present the problem statement and our results more precisely in
Section

1.2
and
Section

1.3
. Similarities and differences with existing works are discussed in
Section

1.4
. We conclude by comparing the parameter complexity of transformers and ResNets in

1.2
Problem statement

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
the set of tuples of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In keeping with standard terminology, we call

a

and each vector

a

Since our constructions are permutation-equivariant

The set of all finite-length non-empty sequences in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\mathcal{X}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n},

which we view as a topological space equipped with the Hausdorff distance between two sequences
X,Y\in\mathcal{X}

d_{H}(X,Y)\coloneqq\max\left(\max_{x\in X}\min_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|_{2},\max_{y\in Y}\min_{x\in X}\|x-y\|_{2}\right),

(1.1)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the
\ell^{2}
norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
. We consider a dataset of
N

X^{1},\ldots,X^{N}\in\mathcal{X}
of lengths
n^{1},\dots,n^{N}\in\mathbb{N}
, and corresponding outputs
Y^{1},\dots,Y^{N}\in\mathcal{X}
of lengths
m^{1},\dots,m^{N}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
m^{j}\leq n^{j}
for all
j\in[N]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Sequential datasets with the previously described characteristics appear naturally in language and vision applications. In
masked language modeling

Devlin et al. (2019)

X^{j}
corresponds to an incomplete sentence and the output
Y^{j}
consists of the missing (or “masked”) words. Analogously, in
masked image modeling

He et al. (2022)

Bao et al. (2022)

represents an image with missing patches, while the output
Y^{j}
contains the missing content. For further illustration, we consider the following example in language modeling.

Example 1.1
.

Consider the following masked sentences:

\displaystyle X^{1}

\displaystyle=\text{``The [mask1] is blue''},

\displaystyle Y^{1}

\displaystyle=\text{``sky''},

\displaystyle X^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{``She likes eating [mask1] [mask2]''},

\displaystyle Y^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{``ice cream''}.

The inputs
X^{1},X^{2}
have different lengths (
n^{1}=4
,
n^{2}=5
), and the outputs
Y^{1},Y^{2}
also differ in length (
m^{1}=1
and
m^{2}=2
). This illustrates a key feature of our setting: input and output sequences may have different lengths, with the output typically shorter than the input.

The interpolation problem for the dataset consists of constructing a function that maps each input
X^{j}
to the corresponding output
Y^{j}
.
Transformers are an appropriate class of models to solve such problems, since they process variable-length inputs in a permutation-equivariant way. For our purposes, we view a transformer as a parametrized map
\T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
acting on sequences of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Its precise architecture will be described in
Section

3

X^{j}
has length
n^{j}

\T(X^{j})
also has length
n^{j}
. This creates a mismatch with our outputs
Y^{j}

m^{j}\leq n^{j}
). We resolve this mismatch by viewing equality between sequences in the set sense, that is, two sequences are equal when their Hausdorff distance is equal to zero. We are therefore led to the following exact interpolation problem.

Problem 1.2

(Exact interpolation)
.

Construct a transformer
\T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\T(X^{j})=Y^{j}
for all
j\in[N]
, with explicit bounds on the parameter count.

Remark 1.3
.

Problem

1.2
is formulated for permutation-equivariant transformers, where the order of tokens does not matter. Positional encodings
Vaswani et al. (2017)
can be employed in a separate pre-processing step to add information about the order of tokens if desired, but we do not account for it here because this does not affect the behavior of the transformer. For sequence modeling tasks that require auto-regressive next-token prediction, the permutation equivariance can also be removed by employing “masked” self-attention. The relation between this masked auto-regressive setting and
Problem

1.2
is discussed in
Section

7.2.4
, where we show how the former can be reduced to an instance of the latter.

Remark 1.4
.

Viewing the equality
\T(X^{j})=Y^{j}
as a set equality means that, if the output sequence consists of a single token, then
every
token of the sequence
\T(X^{j})
must coincide with the desired output token. This is harder to achieve compared to matching only a specific token of the input sequence to the desired output, but can be done with a transformer with the same leading-order complexity.

To ensure
Problem

1.2
is solvable, we make the following assumptions on the dataset.

Assumption 1.5
.

The input and output sequences
\{(X^{j},Y^{j})\}_{j\in[N]}
have the following properties:

i)

The sequences
X^{1},\ldots,X^{N}
are pairwise distinct.

ii)

Tokens within each
X^{j}
are pairwise distinct.

iii)

Tokens within each
Y^{j}
are pairwise distinct.

Assumption

could be replaced with the weaker requirement that
X^{j}=X^{j^{\prime}}
implies
Y^{j}=Y^{j^{\prime}}
. This condition is necessary to ensure that

1.2
is solvable. However, if
X^{j}=X^{j^{\prime}}
and
Y^{j}=Y^{j^{\prime}}
, then the pair
(X^{j^{\prime}},Y^{j^{\prime}})
can be dropped from the dataset without changing

1.2
to ensure that Assumption

holds.
Assumptions

and
iii)
are mild since positional encoding

usually ensures that tokens in a sequence are distinct.
The pairwise-distinctness requirement is not essential: with minor proof edits (at the cost of heavier notation), repeated tokens in an input sequence

can be replaced by a single token with more “mass”. Concretely, replace the constant
1/|\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)|
in

with
w_{\ell}/\sum_{k\in\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)}w_{k}

Main results

We solve
Problem

1.2

Assumption

in dimension

The case

using a transformer with
\smash{\mathcal{O}(d\sum_{j}m_{j})}
parameters that alternates single-head self-attention and feed-forward layers with ReLU activations and trainable residual connections. We refer to a single pair of feed-forward and self-attention layers as a transformer

We analyze two cases, depending on whether the self-attention layers are defined using a hardmax or a softmax attention mechanism. While only the latter is used in practice, the former allows for a simpler geometric intuition that facilitates analysis

Our first main result establishes that hardmax transformers can exactly interpolate data and solve

Theorem 1.6

(Exact interpolation with hardmax transformers)
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
and a dataset of sequences
\{(X^{j},Y^{j})\}_{j\in[N]}
satisfying

1.5
.
There exists a hardmax transformer
\T^{0}
with feed-forward layers of width

,

L=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}+2N+1\;\text{blocks,}\quad\text{and}\quad P=\mathcal{O}\left(d\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}\right)\;\text{parameters}

such that

\T^{0}(X^{j})=Y^{j}\quad\forall j\in[N].

Remark 1.7
.

The number of parameters in
Theorem

1.6
agrees up to a prefactor with the lower bound of
\smash{P\geq d\sum_{j}m^{j}}
for any function
\T(\cdot,\theta):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
parameterized in a
smooth
way by
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
. Indeed, if a parameter vector
\theta
exists for every choice of output sequences

in a
\smash{d\sum_{j}m^{j}}

of finite-length sequences, then the map from the parameter space

to this space must be surjective. This requires
\smash{P\geq d\sum_{j}m^{j}}

\mathbb{R}^{P}
would be critical and the image

would have measure zero by Sard’s theorem
(

.

The proof of

1.6

Lemma

shows how a self-attention layer can collapse tokens that lie in a suitable geometric configuration. Conversely,

The second main result of this paper, proven in
Section

Theorem 1.8

(Exact interpolation with softmax transformers)
.

Fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
and a dataset of sequences
\{(X^{j},Y^{j})\}_{j\in[N]}
satisfying

1.5
.
There exists

such that, for all

, there exists a softmax transformer

with feed-forward layers of width

,

L=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}+3N\;\text{blocks,}\quad\text{and}\quad P=\mathcal{O}\left(d\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}\right)\;\text{parameters}

(1.3)

such that

\T^{\tau}(X^{j})=Y^{j}\quad\forall j\in[N].

Several comments about
Theorem

1.8
are in order.
First, to the best of our knowledge this is the first
exact
interpolation result for deep softmax transformers with real-valued outputs (see
Section

1.4
for further discussion). Moreover, we derive explicit complexity bounds in terms of both the number of blocks and the parameter count.

Second, the theorem does not follow directly from the hardmax result by taking the “zero-temperature limit” of the softmax
Elfadel and Wyatt (1993)
. Such an argument would only yield
approximate
interpolation, with errors depending on
\tau>0

N-1
additional blocks.

Finally, the temperature can be normalized to
\tau^{\star}=1
by rescaling the self-attention parameter matrix in (
3.2
). However, this rescaling comes at the expense of increasing the norm of the parameters in the self-attention layer. Bounding parameter norms is a substantially more delicate problem, which we do not attempt to solve. In practice, such norms depend nontrivially on the dispersion of the data and the separation of the outputs
Kim et al. (2023)
. See
Section

7.2.1
for further discussion.

1.4
Related work

The interpolation capacity of transformers has been studied in previous works. In
Kim et al. (2023)
, exact interpolation results were established for sequence-to-sequence maps with equal input and output lengths (
m=n
) and integer-valued outputs. The estimates in that work show that the required number of blocks grows as
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n+\sqrt{Nn})
, where the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}
notation hides logarithmic factors in
N
,
n
, and
d
. For real-valued outputs, however, the analysis yields only
approximate
interpolation
(
Kim et al., 2023
, Remark 3.2)
. In
Kajitsuka and Sato (2024)
, the analysis of
Kim et al. (2023)
is extended to vector-valued discrete output sequences using one-layer self-attention with low-rank weight matrices. Regarding next-token prediction capacity of shallow decoder transformers,
Madden et al. (2025)
prove an exact interpolation result where outputs are sequences of probability vectors over a finite vocabulary (see
Section

7.2.2
for further comments on this setting). The interpolation properties of transformers have also been analyzed from a controllability-based viewpoint in
Agrachev and Letrouit (2025)
, where it is proven that exact interpolation is possible using transformers with more general self-attention layers than those commonly used in practice. In contrast, we prove exact interpolation for real-valued vector outputs in sequence-to-sequence tasks using deep single-head transformers with the standard self-attention mechanism. This requires a number of blocks that grows linearly in both
N
and
n
, but crucially remains independent of
d
.

A different line of work is pursued in
Mahdavi et al. (2024)
, which studies interpolation in transformers with a single multi-head attention layer. There, the number of heads plays a role analogous to the width of feed-forward layers, and the authors show that
N
sequences of length
n
can be memorized with at least
\mathcal{O}(N/n)
heads.
Our analysis focuses instead on single-head deep transformers, a structurally different architecture that leads to complementary results (see
Section

7.2.3
for further discussion on the multi-head case).

Finally, beyond interpolation, there are several works investigating the function approximation capabilities and dynamical behavior of transformers. For instance, the asymptotic dynamics of hardmax attention have been shown to induce clustering phenomena in token representations
Alcalde et al. (2025)
.
The seminal result of
Yun et al. (2020)
established that transformers are universal approximators of sequence-to-sequence maps. The work
Alberti et al. (2023)
extends these results to efficient variants of transformers, while
Furuya et al. (2025)
proves that transformers are universal approximators of “in-context maps” (see that work for a definition of this term). Yet another perspective is offered by
Geshkovski et al. (2024)
, where transformers are interpreted as interacting particle systems within a measure-theoretic framework. In this setting, transformers achieve approximate interpolation in the Wasserstein-2 metric for measure-to-measure maps. The construction in
Geshkovski et al. (2024)
can also solve
Problem

1.2
, but requires
\mathcal{O}(Nd+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j})
blocks and is limited to the case of datasets where inputs and outputs have the same length
m^{j}=\len(X^{j})=\len(Y^{j})
.
Theorem

1.8
improves upon these works by achieving lower block complexity and accommodating arbitrary output sequence lengths.

1.5
Parameter complexity of transformers versus ResNets

Since transformers can be viewed as an extension of ResNets, it is natural to compare our exact interpolation results with analogous strategies for ResNets. We discuss two possible approaches.

As a first approach, we remove self-attention layers entirely, which recovers a feed-forward only transformer

\T_{\mathrm{FF}}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n},\qquad\T_{\mathrm{FF}}(X)=\{\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{n})\},

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is realized by a (possibly deep) ResNet. This architecture acts independently on each token, which poses a fundamental limitation for solving
Problem

1.2
under
Assumption

1.5
.
To see this, consider two input sequences that differ only in their last token,

X^{1}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x^{1}_{n}\},\qquad X^{2}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x^{2}_{n}\},

with
x^{1}_{n}\neq x^{2}_{n}
, and corresponding output sequences

Y^{1}=\{y_{1}^{1},\ldots,y_{n}^{1}\},\qquad Y^{2}=\{y_{1}^{2},\ldots,y_{n}^{2}\},

with
y_{i}^{1}\neq y_{i}^{2}
for all
i\in[n]
. Then,

\T_{\mathrm{FF}}(X^{j})=\{\varphi(x_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(x_{n-1}),\varphi(x^{j}_{n})\},\quad j=1,2,

so one can never simultaneously have
\T_{\mathrm{FF}}(X^{1})=Y^{1}
and
\T_{\mathrm{FF}}(X^{2})=Y^{2}
. Thus,
Problem

1.2
cannot be solved by feed-forward only transformers in general.

We next discuss a second approach, which ignores sequence structure altogether. Setting
n=\max_{j\in[N]}n^{j}
,
m=\max_{j\in[N]}m^{j}
, and padding shorter sequences with zeros, each input
X^{j}
can be regarded as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{dn}
. Similarly, each output
Y^{j}
can be viewed as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{dm}
.
In this setting, ResNets operate on
\mathbb{R}^{dn}
, so their parameter count necessarily scales with
n
. For example, the ResNets constructed in
Ruiz-Balet and Zuazua (2023)
require
\mathcal{O}(Ndn)
parameters to interpolate the dataset, while our transformers require
\mathcal{O}(d\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j})
parameters. This comparison yields two regimes:

•

If
m^{j}=n
for all
j\in[N]
, transformers and ResNets have the same parameter order.

•

If some
m^{j}<n
, transformers are strictly more efficient than ResNets.

The efficiency gain of transformers for shorter output sequences stems from the clustering effect of self-attention (see, e.g.,
Geshkovski et al. (2023)
;
Geshkovski et al. (2025)
;
Alcalde et al. (2025)
;
Karagodin et al. (2024)
;
Burger et al. (2025)
). Tokens within a sequence are carefully collapsed to be equal to the output sequence in the set sense. ResNets, in contrast, rely only on geometric correlations in the input, so parameter reductions can be established only probabilistically
Álvarez-López et al. (2025)
.

The key advantage of transformers is that they combine the token-level processing of ResNets with self-attention driven for separation and clustering. This structure enables exact interpolation for sequences of arbitrary input length, with parameter complexity depending only on the total output length. The tradeoff, which we do not address here, is the quadratic computational cost of self-attention with respect to the input sequence length
Vaswani et al. (2017)
.

2
Implications for transformer regularized training

The existence of an exactly interpolating transformer has important implications for regularized training.
In this setting, the parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
of a transformer
\T_{\theta}
are learned by solving the Tikhonov-regularized problem

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}\left\{F_{\varepsilon}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(\T_{\theta}(X^{j}),Y^{j})+\varepsilon\kappa(\theta)\right\},

(2.1)

where
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty)
is a nonnegative lower semicontinuous function such that
f(Y,Y^{\prime})=0
if and only if
Y=Y^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon>0
is a regularization parameter, and the function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{P}\to[0,\infty)
is lower semicontinuous and coercive (i.e.
\lim_{\|\theta\|\to\infty}\kappa(\theta)=\infty
). These assumptions ensure the minimum in (
2.1
) is attained by a minimizer
\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
.

The unconstrained minimization in (
2.1
), which is solved when training transformers in practice, is closely connected to the constrained problem

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{P}}\kappa(\theta)\quad\text{s.t. }\T_{\theta}(X^{j})=Y^{j}\quad\forall j\in[N],

(2.2)

as established by the following standard result in Tikhonov regularization theory (see, e.g.,
Engl et al. (1996)
). We include the proof to make this paper self-contained.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose there exists
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
and a softmax transformer
\T_{\theta_{0}}
satisfying that
\T_{\theta_{0}}(X^{j})=Y^{j}
for all
j\in[N]
. Then:

i)

There exists
\theta^{\star}_{0}
solving
2.2
.

ii)

F_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{0})
. In particular,
\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\leq\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{0})
.

iii)

Any sequence of optimizers
\{\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
has a converging subsequence.

iv)

The limit of any converging subsequence
\{\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
solves
2.2
.

Proof.

To prove i), note that any minimizing sequence
\{\theta_{n}\}
for problem
Eq.

2.2
remains in the sublevel set
\{\theta:\kappa(\theta)\leq\kappa(\theta_{0})\}
, which is compact because
\kappa
is coercive and lower semicontinuous. Pass to a convergent subsequence (not relabeled) and denote its limit by
\theta_{0}^{*}
. This point is feasible for
Eq.

2.2
because the map
\theta\mapsto\T_{\theta}(X^{j})
is continuous for every fixed
X^{j}
. It also satisfies
\kappa(\theta_{0}^{*})\leq\liminf_{n}(\theta_{n})
because
\kappa
is lower semicontinuous. Then, since
\{\theta_{n}\}
is a minimizing sequence,
\theta_{0}^{*}
must be an optimal solution of
Eq.

2.2
.

Next, we prove ii). Since
\theta^{\star}_{0}
is admissible but not necessarily optimal for
2.1
, we obtain

F_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\leq F_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{0})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}f(\mathrm{T}_{\theta_{0}^{\star}}(X^{j}),Y^{j})+\varepsilon\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{0})=\varepsilon\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{0}).

The second equality is true because
\mathrm{T}_{\theta_{0}^{\star}}(X^{j})=Y^{j}
and
f(Y^{j},Y^{j})=0
.

Finally, we prove iii) and iv). Since
f
is nonnegative, we have that
\varepsilon\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\leq F_{\varepsilon}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon})
, which by part ii) implies that
\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\leq\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{0})
. Thus,
\{\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
is uniformly bounded and has a converging subsequence
\{\theta_{\varepsilon_{j}}\}
. Let
\theta^{*}
be its limit. Since
\theta\mapsto\mathrm{T}_{\theta}(X^{j})
is continuous and
f
is lower semicontinuous, the function

\theta\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\mathrm{T}_{\theta}(X^{j}),Y^{j})

is lower semicontinuous. We then find that

0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\mathrm{T}_{\theta^{*}}(X^{j}),Y^{j})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon_{j}\to 0}F_{\varepsilon_{j}}(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon_{j}})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon_{j}\to 0}\varepsilon_{j}\|\theta^{\star}_{0}\|=0.

We deduce that
f(\mathrm{T}_{\theta^{*}}(X^{j}),Y^{j})=0
for all
j\in[N]
, so
\mathrm{T}_{\theta^{*}}(X^{j})=Y^{j}
for all
j\in[N]
. Thus,
\theta^{*}
is feasible for problem
Eq.

2.2
. It is also optimal because
\kappa
is lower semicontinuous and
\kappa(\theta^{*}_{\varepsilon_{j}})\leq\kappa(\theta_{0}^{*})
, so
\kappa(\theta^{*})\leq\liminf\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{\varepsilon_{j}})\leq\kappa(\theta^{\star}_{0})
. The proof is complete.
∎

Several remarks on
Proposition

2.1
are in order.
First, the statement has been formulated for softmax transformers since their continuous dependence on
\theta
, when composed with a suitable lower semicontinuous function
f
, guarantees the lower semicontinuity of
\theta\mapsto F_{\varepsilon}(\theta)
. Consequently, a minimizer of
2.1
exists in this setting. In contrast, the discontinuity inherent to hardmax transformers prevents such a guarantee.

Second,
Theorem

1.8
establishes the existence of a parameter
\theta_{0}
that exactly interpolates the dataset of
N
sequences, provided the softmax transformer has width
d^{\prime}\geq 4
and
L\geq 2\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}+3N
blocks. As a result, the conclusions of
Proposition

2.1
apply directly to this model class.

Finally, although the content of
Proposition

2.1
is relatively standard, it carries practical implications for the training dynamics. In particular, if optimization converges to a global minimizer of the regularized objective
2.1
, then the training loss scales at worst linearly with the regularization parameter
\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Conversely, deviations from this behavior indicate either that the optimization procedure converges only to a local minimizer, or that exact interpolation is not possible with the chosen model architecture. We illustrate this phenomenon for transformer models with a synthetic example

3
The transformer architecture

In this section, we describe the transformer architecture used to solve
Problem

1.2
.
The transformer is defined as a composition of
transformer blocks
, in which a feed-forward (FF) layer is followed by a self-attention (SA) layer. Both layers may or may not include a residual connection. We define these layers in detail next.

3.1
Feed-forward layers

Given a width
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, feed-forward layers are functions
\mathbb{FF}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
parametrized by
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, and an activation function
\sigma
. We fix
\sigma(x)=\max(0,x)
as the usual ReLU activation, and call
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
the
width
of the feed-forward layer. Then, the
i
-th component of the sequence

\mathbb{FF}(X)=\{\mathrm{FF}(x_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{FF}(x_{\len(X)})\}

is given by

\FF(x)=\eta x+W\sigma(Ux+b).

We stress that allowing for a tunable residual parameter
\eta
, rather than fixing
\eta=1
, is crucial to obtain the exact interpolation results in
Theorem

1.6
and
Theorem

1.8
. In particular, it allows the composition of feed-forward layers to behave as multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and ResNets, whose interpolation capabilities have been studied extensively
Hernández and Zuazua (2024)
;
Ruiz-Balet and Zuazua (2023)
;
Álvarez-López et al. (2024)
.

3.2
Self-attention layers

Let
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{A}
be two subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Self-attention layers are defined as functions
\mathbb{SA}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
parametrized by a scalar
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, a matrix
V\in\mathcal{V}
and a matrix
A\in\mathcal{A}
as follows. The
i
-th component of the sequence

\mathbb{SA}(X)=\{\mathrm{SA}_{1}(X),\dots,\mathrm{SA}_{\len(X)}(X)\}

is given by

\mathrm{SA}_{i}(X)=\rho x_{i}+V\sum_{\ell=1}^{\len(X)}\pi_{i\ell}(X,A)x_{\ell},

(3.1)

where
\pi=(\pi_{i\ell})\in[0,1]^{\len(X)\times\len(X)}
is a weighting function parametrized by
A
and whose output depends on the
entire
sequence
X
.

The usual
softmax self-attention
is obtained with
\pi=\pi^{\tau}
, where
\tau>0
is called the “temperature parameter” and

\pi_{i\ell}^{\tau}(X,A)=\frac{\exp{\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\langle Ax_{i},x_{\ell}\rangle\right)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{\len(X)}\exp{\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\langle Ax_{i},x_{k}\rangle\right)}}.

(3.2)

Transformer implementations typically parameterize the attention scores using
query
and
key
matrices
Q,K\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times d}
,
h\leq d

Vaswani et al. (2017)
, with similarities given by
\langle Qx_{i},Kx_{\ell}\rangle
. Our formulation absorbs these projections into a single matrix
A=K^{\top}Q
and reduces precisely to this setting when
\mathcal{A}
is the subspace of
d\times d
matrices with rank
h
.

By taking
\tau\to 0
for fixed
X
and
A
, one obtains the so-called
hardmax self-attention
formulation
Elfadel and Wyatt (1993)
. We denote it by
\pi=\pi^{0}
, where

\pi_{i\ell}^{0}(X,A)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)\right|}&\text{if }\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A),\\
0&\text{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

(3.3)

and

\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)=\left\{j:\;\langle Ax_{i},x_{j}\rangle=\max_{\ell\in[\len(X)]}\langle Ax_{i},x_{\ell}\rangle\right\}

is the set of indices
j
where the inner product
\langle Ax_{i},x_{j}\rangle
is maximized.

Figure 2:
Geometric interpretation of
3.3
for
i=1
with
A=I
. Tokens
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
have the largest orthogonal projection onto
Ax_{1}=x_{1}
, so
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)=\{2,3\}
.

Although rarely used in practice, we use the hardmax self-attention formulation in our proofs due to its simple geometric interpretation: a token
x_{i}
is influenced by the tokens with the largest orthogonal projection onto the direction of
Ax_{i}
(cf.
Figure

2
).
The smooth softmax self-attention formulation is usually preferred in applications because it allows for training with standard gradient-based algorithms.

3.3
The transformer

We are now ready to combine FF and SA layers into a transformer. Fix a depth
L\in\mathbb{N}
and a width
d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
. Given FF layers
\{\mathbb{FF}^{k}\}_{k\in[L]}
with parameters
\{\eta^{k},W^{k},U^{k},b^{k}\}_{k\in[L]}
and SA layers
\{\mathbb{SA}^{k}\}_{k\in[L]}
with parameters
\{\rho^{k},V^{k},A^{k}\}_{k\in[L]}
, a transformer is a map
\T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
defined by

\T=\left(\mathbb{SA}^{L}\circ\mathbb{FF}^{L}\right)\circ\dots\circ\left(\mathbb{SA}^{1}\circ\mathbb{FF}^{1}\right).

(3.4)

For each
k\in[L]
, we call the function
\mathbb{TB}^{k}=\mathbb{SA}^{k}\circ\mathbb{FF}^{k}
a transformer
block
and say that
\T
in
3.4
has
L
blocks (see
Figure

3
).
Note that, from a mathematical perspective, the order of composition of SA and FF layers is irrelevant because one can always choose the parameters of these layers to make them act like the identity map. In particular, all of our results remain true if the transformer blocks are defined as
\mathbb{TB}^{k}=\mathbb{FF}^{k}\circ\mathbb{SA}^{k}
, as in the work introducing the transformer
Vaswani et al. (2017)
.

We will refer to a transformer
\T^{0}
with hardmax self-attention layers as a
hardmax transformer
. Similarly, a
softmax transformer
, denoted by
\T^{\tau}
, will be a transformer with softmax self-attention layers and temperature parameter
\tau>0
.

In this work, we fix the
d\times d
matrix subspaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\{\alpha I_{d}:\;\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\},\qquad\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha I_{d}+uv^{\top}:\;\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\;u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

This implies that our transformer has
\mathcal{O}(d\sum_{j}m^{j})
parameters. Of course, our construction also works if one replaces these matrix subspaces with larger ones.

4
Useful technical results

In this section, we collect a number of auxiliary technical results that will be instrumental in the proof of our main results. We first revisit the analysis in
Alcalde et al. (2025)
concerning the asymptotic behavior of tokens evolving under repeated application of hardmax self-attention layers described in (
3.1
) with
\pi=\pi^{0}
.
This asymptotic analysis provides a simplified setting in which we can gain insight into key features of self-attention layers. Beyond being of independent interest as an extension of previous work, the results of this analysis will also inform the design of self-attention layers in the proofs of
Theorem

1.6
and
Theorem

1.8
.
We then move on to structural lemmas that describe how self-attention layers interact with feed-forward layers inside a transformer block, highlighting geometric constructions that will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

4.1
Asymptotics of hardmax self-attention layers revisited

Our goal is to sharpen the characterization of the equilibria of the discrete-time dynamics

x_{i}(k+1)=\rho x_{i}(k)+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(k),A)|}V\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(k),A)}x_{\ell}(k),

(4.1)

where
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(k),A)
denotes the hardmax-selected cluster for token
i
at time
k
. In
Alcalde et al. (2025)
, convergence was established in the special case where
\rho=1-\gamma
,
V=\gamma I_{d}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, with
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
symmetric positive definite. However, the set of possible equilibria in the full-rank positive definite case is extremely rich: it ranges from a fully concentrated state to a fully dispersed one
Geshkovski et al. (2023)
;
Alcalde et al. (2025)
. This motivates us to study additional structural assumptions on
A
and on the initial configuration to enforce a desired concentration.

Schematic of the transformer architecture described in
Section

3.3
.

Rank-one parameter matrix
A

A natural way to restrict the equilibria is to reduce the rank of
A
. We only consider the symmetric rank-one case
A=vv^{\top}
, where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given an initial configuration
\{x_{i}(0)\}_{i\in[n]}
, suppose
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies
\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle\neq 0
for all
i\in[n]
, and assume there exist unique indices
m,M\in[n]
such that
\langle v,x_{M}(0)\rangle>0
,
\langle v,x_{m}(0)\rangle<0
and

\langle v,x_{M}(0)\rangle>\langle v,x_{j}(0)\rangle\quad\forall j\neq M,\qquad\langle v,x_{m}(0)\rangle<\langle v,x_{j}(0)\rangle\quad\forall j\neq m.

Then, the dynamics governed by (
4.1
) with
\rho=1-\gamma
,
V=\gamma I_{d}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
, and
A=vv^{\top}
converge to an equilibrium consisting of
n_{1}
copies of
x_{m}(0)
and
n_{2}
copies of
x_{M}(0)
where
n_{1}+n_{2}=n
. For
\gamma=1
, convergence happens in one step.

Proof.

If
\gamma=1
, the result follows from a direct calculation using (
4.1
) with
\rho=0
and
V=I_{d}
.
If
\gamma<1
, we start by proving that the following statements hold for all
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\argmax_{j\in[n]}\langle v,x_{j}(k)\rangle=\{M\},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\argmin_{j\in[n]}\langle v,x_{j}(k)\rangle=\{m\},

(4.3)

\displaystyle x_{M}(k)=x_{M}(0)\quad\text{and}\quad x_{m}(k)=x_{m}(0),

(4.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{sign}(\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle)=\operatorname{sign}(\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle)\quad\text{for all }i\in[n].

(4.5)

These properties hold at
k=0
by the assumptions. We now assume they hold at time
k
and prove them at time
k+1
. First, we observe that

\langle Ax_{i}(k),x_{j}(k)\rangle=\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle\langle v,x_{j}(k)\rangle
,
so

\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(k),A)=\begin{cases}\{M\},&\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle>0,\\
\{m\},&\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle<0.\end{cases}

This is because maximizing
\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle\langle v,x_{j}(k)\rangle
over
j
amounts to maximizing

\langle v,x_{j}(k)\rangle
if
\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle>0
and minimizing it if
\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle<0
, and by the induction hypothesis the unique maximizer and minimizer are
M
and
m
.

Next, we compute
x_{i}(k+1)
using the update rule (
4.1
) with
\rho=1-\gamma
and
V=\gamma I_{d}
, and take the inner product with
v
to find that

\langle v,x_{i}(k+1)\rangle=\begin{cases}(1-\gamma)\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle+\gamma\langle v,x_{M}(k)\rangle&\text{if }\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle>0,\\
(1-\gamma)\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle+\gamma\langle v,x_{m}(k)\rangle&\text{if }\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle<0.\end{cases}

(4.6)

Property
4.5
at time
k
and our assumptions that
\langle v,x_{m}(0)\rangle<0<\langle v,x_{M}(0)\rangle
imply that
\langle v,x_{m}(k)\rangle<0<\langle v,x_{M}(k)\rangle
, so we also have
\mathcal{C}_{M}(X(k),A)=\{M\}
and
\mathcal{C}_{m}(X(k),A)=\{m\}
. Then, since
\gamma\in(0,1]
, we conclude from
Eq.

4.6
that properties
4.4
and
4.5
hold at time
k+1
. We also conclude that

\displaystyle\langle v,x_{i}(k+1)\rangle-\langle v,x_{M}(k)\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\bigl(\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle-\langle v,x_{M}(k)\rangle\bigr)<0

\displaystyle\text{if }\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle>0,

\displaystyle\langle v,x_{i}(k+1)\rangle-\langle v,x_{m}(k)\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\bigl(\langle v,x_{i}(k)\rangle-\langle v,x_{m}(k)\rangle\bigr)>0

\displaystyle\text{if }\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle<0.

These inequalities imply that
\langle v,x_{m}(k+1)\rangle<\langle v,x_{i}(k+1)\rangle<\langle v,x_{M}(k+1)\rangle
, which in turn imply that properties
4.2
and
4.3
hold at time
k+1
. This concludes the induction process.

Finally, let
n_{1}=\bigl|\{i:\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle<0\}\bigr|
,
n_{2}=\bigl|\{i:\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle>0\}\bigr|
. For every
i
with
\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle>0
we have

x_{i}(k+1)-x_{M}(0)=(1-\gamma)\bigl(x_{i}(k)-x_{M}(0)\bigr)
,
and therefore

x_{i}(k)=x_{M}(0)+(1-\gamma)^{k}\bigl(x_{i}(0)-x_{M}(0)\bigr)\to x_{M}(0).

Similar arguments show that
x_{i}(k)\to x_{m}(0)
for every
i
with
\langle v,x_{i}(0)\rangle<0
.
Consequently the limiting configuration consists of
n_{1}
copies of
x_{m}(0)
and
n_{2}
copies of
x_{M}(0)
with
n_{1}+n_{2}=n
. This is easily seen to be an equilibrium for the dynamical map
Eq.

4.1
.
∎

This result is advantageous if one seeks equilibria with at most two clusters, and its hypotheses hold for almost every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a given initial configuration
\{x_{i}(0)\}_{i\in[n]}
. However, when more than two distinct clusters are desired, rank-one interactions are too restrictive, and one must instead consider a different structure for
A
together with structured initial conditions.

Scalar multiple of the identity
A

We now study the case
A=\xi I_{d}
with
\xi>0
and dynamics (
4.1
) with
\rho=1-\gamma
,
V=\gamma I_{d}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
We show that the long-term behavior of the dynamics (
4.1
) is highly sensitive to the initial configuration of tokens.
In particular, by arranging the tokens in specific regions of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one can enforce convergence to qualitatively different equilibria: a fully clustered state (all tokens coincide), a fully dispersed state (all tokens remain distinct), or an intermediate configuration with a prescribed number of clusters.
The following lemmas illustrate these three possibilities.

In what follows, we denote the positive orthant as
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
, the negative orthant as
\mathbb{R}_{<0}^{d}
, the open hypercube of side length
R>0
as
H_{R}=(0,R)^{d}
and the vector of all ones in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
1_{d}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Full clustering)
.

Let
A=\xi I_{d}
with
\xi>0
,
\rho=1-\gamma
,
V=\gamma I_{d}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Let the initial configuration
\{x_{i}(0)\}_{i\in[n]}
be such that, for a unique index
i^{\star}\in[n]

i)

x_{i}(0)\in H_{R}
for all
i\neq i^{\star}

ii)

x_{i^{\star}}(0)=R\;1_{d}
.

Then, the dynamics governed by (
4.1
) converge to the fully clustered equilibrium

X^{\star}=(x_{i^{\star}}(0),\dots,x_{i^{\star}}(0)).

For
\gamma=1
, convergence takes place in one step.

Proof.

We first note that

\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(0),A)=\{i^{\star}\}
for all
i\in[n]
, so we have

x_{i}(1)=x_{i}(0)+\gamma(x_{i^{\star}}(0)-x_{i}(0)).

For
\gamma=1
, this proves that
x_{i}(1)=x_{i^{\star}}(0)
, and since all tokens are fixed,
x_{i}(k)=x_{i^{\star}}(0)
for all
i\in[n]
and all
k\geq 1
.
For the case
\gamma\in(0,1)
, notice that
x_{i}(1)\in H_{R}
for all
i\neq i^{\star}
because
H_{R}
is convex. By induction on
k\geq 0
, one finds that

x_{i}(k+1)=x_{i}(k)+\gamma(x_{i^{\star}}(0)-x_{i}(k)),

or equivalently by setting
\delta_{i}(k)\coloneqq x_{i}(k)-x_{i^{\star}}(0)
,
\delta_{i}(k+1)=(1-\gamma)\delta_{i}(k)
. Then,

\|\delta_{i}(k+1)\|\leq(1-\gamma)^{k+1}\|\delta_{i}(0)\|,

which implies convergence as
k\to\infty
.
∎

The next result gives a configuration for which no clustering occurs. We will use
S_{R}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|=R\}
to denote the hypersphere of radius
R
.

Lemma 4.3

(No clustering)
.

Let
A=\xi I_{d}
with
\xi>0
,
\rho=1-\gamma
,
V=\gamma I_{d}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
Any initial configuration
\{x_{i}(0)\}_{i\in[n]}
of pairwise distinct tokens with
x_{i}(0)\in S_{R}
for all
i\in[n]
is an equilibrium for (
4.1
).

Proof.

Since
x_{i}(0)\in S_{R}
for all
i\in[n]
, then is holds that
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(0),A)=\{i\}
. Then,
x_{i}(1)=x_{i}(0)
for all
i\in[n]
and the result follows from straightforward induction.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Partial clustering)
.

Let
A=\xi I_{d}
with
\xi>0
,
\rho=1-\gamma
,
V=\gamma I_{d}
,
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Let the initial configuration
\{x_{i}(0)\}_{i\in[n]}
contain pairwise distinct tokens and the subset
\mathcal{I}=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}\subseteq[n]
with
|\mathcal{I}|=m
satisfy

i)

x_{i}(0)\in H_{R}
for all
i\notin\mathcal{I}
,

ii)

x_{i_{1}}(0)=R\;1_{d}
,

iii)

x_{i_{j}}(0)\in S_{R}\cap\mathbb{R}_{<0}^{d}
for all
j>1
.

Then the dynamics governed by (
4.1
) converge to a permutation of the equilibrium

X^{\star}=\big(\underbrace{x_{i_{1}}(0),\dots,x_{i_{1}}(0)}_{n-m+1\text{ times}},\,x_{i_{2}}(0),\dots,x_{i_{m}}(0)\big),

that is, a partially clustered state with
m
distinct tokens.
For
\gamma=1
, convergence takes place in one step.

Proof.

Arguing as in the proof of
Lemma

4.2
, together with the fact that
x_{i_{j}}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{<0}
for all
j>1
, we obtain
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(0),A)=\{i_{1}\}
for all
i\notin\mathcal{I}
, while arguing as in the proof of
Lemma

4.3
, we have that
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X(0),A)=\{i\}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
. Thus,

\displaystyle x_{i}(1)

\displaystyle=x_{i}(0)+\gamma(x_{i_{1}}(0)-x_{i}(0))\quad\text{for all}\;\;i\notin\mathcal{I},

\displaystyle x_{i^{\prime}}(1)

\displaystyle=x_{i^{\prime}}(0)\quad\text{for all}\;\;i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}.

This immediately proves the result when
\gamma=1
. For
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we run an induction argument on
k\geq 1
as in
Lemma

4.2
to obtain the result.
∎

4.2
Structural lemmas for transformer blocks

We now turn to transformer blocks and investigate how self-attention and feed-forward layers interact, focusing on structural properties that will be essential for proving
Theorem

1.6
. Our first question is whether a self-attention layer can leave a specific token
x_{i}
from a sequence
X^{j}
unchanged. (Throughout this section, superscripts always refer to sequence indices in the dataset, while subscripts refer to tokens indices within the sequence. In particular, we write
x^{j}_{i}
to indicate the
i
-th token from sequence
X^{j}
.)
If
V=(1-\rho)I_{d}
, it is not hard to see from (
3.1
) that
x_{i}
will not be changed by a self-attention layer if
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)=\{i\}
. In such a case, we say that the token
x_{i}\in X
is a
leader
.
The following lemma asserts that a token can be a leader if and only if it is extreme for the closed convex hull of the tokens in the sequence
X
. As usual, we denote by
\co(X)
the closed convex hull of a sequence
X
and by
\ext\co(X)
the set of its extreme points.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X
be a sequence of non-zero tokens. There exists
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
x_{i}\in X
is a leader if and only if
x_{i}\in\ext\co(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is such that
x_{i}
is a leader. Then,

\langle Ax_{i},x_{\ell}\rangle<\langle Ax_{i},x_{i}\rangle\quad\forall\ell\neq i.

(4.7)

It is easy to verify that this implies that
x_{i}
is the unique maximizer of the linear function
x\mapsto\langle Ax_{i},x\rangle
over
\co(X)
. Since linear functions on convex sets are always maximized by at least one extreme point of the set, we conclude that
x_{i}\in\ext\co(X)
. This proves the “only if” part of the lemma.

For the reverse implication, we fix
x_{i}\in\ext\co(X)
and explicitly construct
A
for which
x_{i}
is a leader. Since
x_{i}\in\ext\co(X)
, then
x_{i}\notin\co(X\setminus\{x_{i}\})
, which is a closed and convex set. By the hyperplane separation theorem, there exists a non-zero vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\langle v,x_{i}\rangle>\alpha
and
\langle v,x_{\ell}\rangle<\alpha
for all
\ell\neq i
. Since the maps
v\mapsto\langle v,x_{\ell}\rangle
are continuous for all
\ell\in[\len(X)]
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\langle v,x_{i}\rangle\neq 0
.
Then, if
\langle v,x_{i}\rangle>0
we set
A=vv^{\top}
and verify that

\displaystyle\langle Ax_{i},x_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,x_{i}\rangle\langle v,x_{i}\rangle

(4.8)

\displaystyle>\langle v,x_{i}\rangle\langle v,x_{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Ax_{i},x_{\ell}\rangle\quad\forall\ell\neq i,

because
\langle v,x_{i}\rangle>\alpha>\langle v,x_{\ell}\rangle
. This yields
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)=\{i\}
, as desired. If, instead,
\langle v,x_{i}\rangle<0
, then we set
A=-vv^{\top}
and obtain
\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)=\{i\}
because

\displaystyle\langle Ax_{i},x_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\langle v,x_{i}\rangle\langle v,x_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle>-\langle v,x_{i}\rangle\langle v,x_{\ell}\rangle

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\langle Ax_{i},x_{\ell}\rangle\quad\forall\ell\neq i,

(4.10)

which finishes the proof.
∎

The next two lemmas establish that all tokens in a sequence
X^{j}
can be attracted by a single token in that sequence. This is a crucial result for the proof of
Theorem

1.6
and is inspired by the asymptotic dynamics for
A=vv^{\top}
in
Lemma

4.1
. Moreover, the lemmas allow prescribing the attracting token
x_{i^{\star}}^{j^{\star}}
in a sequence
X^{j^{\star}}
, which is also used in the proof of
Theorem

1.6
iteratively to separate all sequences one by one.

Lemma 4.6
.

Given sequences
X^{1},\dots,X^{N}
of non-zero tokens, fix
j^{\star}\in[N]
and
i^{\star}\in[\len(X^{j^{\star}})]
such that
x_{i^{\star}}^{j^{\star}}\in\ext\co(X^{j^{\star}})
. There exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that:

i)

\langle v,x_{i^{\star}}^{j^{\star}}\rangle\neq 0
.

ii)

For every
j\in[N]
, there exists
i_{j}\in[\len(X^{j})]
such that
\langle v,x_{\ell}^{j}\rangle<\langle v,x_{i_{j}}^{j}\rangle
for all
\ell\in[\len(X^{j})]\setminus\{i_{j}\}
.

iii)

i_{j^{\star}}=i^{\star}
.

Proof.

We assume
j^{\star}=1
without loss of generality, and proceed by induction on
N
. For
N=1
, a vector
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that conditions
i)–iii)
hold can be chosen using the same arguments in
Lemma

4.5
.

Next, we assume that
v_{N-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that
i)–iii)
hold for the first
N-1
sequences, and construct
v_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that they also hold when the last sequence
X^{N}
is considered. Because
j^{\star}=1
, only condition
ii)
can fail for
v=v_{N-1}
when
X^{N}
is added to the problem. If condition
ii)
holds also for
j=N
, we can simply set
v_{N}=v_{N-1}
.

Otherwise, suppose condition
ii)
does not hold for
j=N
. In this case, the quantity
\langle v,x_{\ell}^{N}\rangle
is maximized at
s>1
tokens, which we may take to be
x_{1}^{N},\dots,x_{s}^{N}
without loss of generality after reordering the sequence if necessary. We may also assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}^{N}
is extreme for
\co(x_{1}^{N},\dots,x_{s}^{N})
. Then, by the hyperplane separation theorem, there exists
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\langle u,x_{r}^{N}\rangle<\langle u,x_{1}^{N}\rangle
for all
r\in\{2,\dots,s\}
.
Set
v_{N}=v_{N-1}+\varepsilon u
for some
\varepsilon>0
to be specified below. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle v_{N},x_{r}^{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v_{N-1},x_{r}^{N}\rangle+\varepsilon\langle u,x_{r}^{N}\rangle

\displaystyle<\langle v_{N-1},x_{1}^{N}\rangle+\varepsilon\langle u,x_{1}^{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v_{N},x_{1}^{N}\rangle

for every
r\in\{2,\dots,s\}
.
We now fix
\varepsilon
small enough that
\langle v_{N},x_{r}^{N}\rangle<\langle v_{N},x_{1}^{N}\rangle
also for
r\in\{s+1,\ldots,\len(X^{N})\}
and that conditions
i)–iii)
remain true for sequence indices
j\in[N-1]
. This is possible because the maps
v\mapsto\langle v,x\rangle
are continuous.
∎

Next, we look at how feed-forward layers and the hardmax operation combine. Our goal is to show that alternating feed-forward and self-attention layers can cause all tokens of a sequence to align with a unique leader.

Lemma 4.7
.

Fix
j^{\star}\in[N]
and
i^{\star}\in[\len(X^{j^{\star}})]
such that
x_{i^{\star}}^{j^{\star}}\in\ext\co(X^{j^{\star}})
. There exist parameters
\{\eta,W,U,b\}
of a feed-forward layer
\mathbb{FF}
with width
d^{\prime}=1
, a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and indices
\{i_{j}\}_{j\in[N]}
with
i_{j^{\star}}=i^{\star}
such that, for all
\{\ell,j\}\in[\len(X^{j})]\times[N]
,

\FF(x_{\ell}^{j})\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{C}_{\ell}(\mathbb{FF}(X^{j}),vv^{\top})=\{i_{j}\}.

(4.11)

Proof.

Choose
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in
Lemma

4.6
. We now choose the parameters of the FF layer such that
4.11
holds. For this, we first observe that adding the same constant vector to every token of every sequence does not change properties
ii)–iii)
of
Lemma

4.6
. We then set the parameters of
\mathbb{FF}
as
\eta=1
,
W=v/\|v\|^{2}
,
U=0
and

b=\max_{k\in[N]}\max_{r\in[\len(X^{k})]}|\langle v,x_{r}^{k}\rangle|+\varepsilon,

where
\varepsilon>0
. For this choice,
\sigma(Ux+b)=\sigma(b)=b
, so that
\FF(x_{\ell}^{j})=x_{\ell}^{j}+b/{\|v\|^{2}}v
. It is clear that, for all
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,
\FF(x_{\ell}^{j})\neq 0
for all
\{\ell,j\}\in[\len(X^{j})]\times[N]
, yielding the first condition in
4.11
. For the second one, note that

\displaystyle\langle v,\FF({x}_{\ell}^{j})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,x_{\ell}^{j}\rangle+\frac{\langle v,v\rangle}{\|v\|^{2}}b\geq\varepsilon>0

(4.12)

for all
\{\ell,j\}\in[\len(X^{j})]\times[N]
. With this inequality we find that
i\in\mathcal{C}_{\ell}(\mathbb{FF}(X^{j}),vv^{\top})
if and only if

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\overset{\phantom{\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{eq:lemPosTerms}}}}{\in}\argmax_{r\in[\len(X^{j})]}\left[\langle v,\FF(x^{j}_{\ell})\rangle\langle v,\FF(x_{r}^{j})\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{eq:lemPosTerms}}}{=}\argmax_{r\in[\len(X^{j})]}\langle v,\FF(x_{r}^{j})\rangle.

This fact, combined with property
ii)
of
Lemma

4.6
implies that
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}(\mathbb{FF}(X^{j}),vv^{\top})=\{i_{j}\}
for all
\ell\in[\len(X^{j})]
and all
j\in[N]
. Finally,
i_{j^{\star}}=i^{\star}
by property
iii)
of
Lemma

4.6
.
∎

Finally, the following lemma is related to the behavior of feed-forward layers and their ability to approximate “hat functions”, moving exactly one token to an arbitrary position, while keeping the rest fixed. This is a distinctive trait of ReLU feed-forward layers and key to arranging tokens in the initial configurations of

4.2
,
4.4
and
4.3

Lemma 4.8
.

Fix pairwise distinct
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i\in[m]
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There exist parameters of a
\FF
layer of width
d^{\prime}=3
such that
\FF(x_{i})=y
and
\FF(x_{j})=x_{j}
for all
j\neq i
.

Proof.

Fix the width of the FF layer as
d^{\prime}=3
and parametrize it by

\mathrm{FF}(x)=x+\begin{bmatrix}|&|&|\\
{w}&-2{w}&{w}\\
|&|&|\end{bmatrix}\sigma\left(\begin{bmatrix}\rule{11.38092pt}{0.4pt}&{u^{\top}}&\rule{11.38092pt}{0.4pt}\\
\rule{11.38092pt}{0.4pt}&{u^{\top}}&\rule{11.38092pt}{0.4pt}\\
\rule{11.38092pt}{0.4pt}&{u^{\top}}&\rule{11.38092pt}{0.4pt}\end{bmatrix}x+\begin{bmatrix}{\beta}-{\gamma}\\
{\beta}\\
{\beta}+{\gamma}\end{bmatrix}\right).

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an arbitrary non-zero direction satisfying

\langle u,x_{j}-x_{i}\rangle\neq 0\quad\forall j\neq i.

Such a vector exists because we have a finite collection of points. Now, fix
\beta=-\langle u,x_{i}\rangle
, and define the hyperplane

\mathcal{H}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;:\langle u,x-x_{i}\rangle=0\}.

Moreover, define the “tube”

\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;:|\langle u,x-x_{i}\rangle|\leq\gamma\}.

By construction,
x_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
and every
x_{j}
,
j\neq i
satisfies
x_{j}\notin\mathcal{H}
. Then, for
\gamma>0
small enough,
x_{j}\notin\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}
. This already yields that
\FF(x_{j})=x_{j}
for all
x_{j}
with
j\neq i
. Finally, we choose

to obtain
\FF(x_{i})=x_{i}+w\gamma=y
, as desired.
∎

5.6
Overall construction and complexity estimate

Combining the transformers described in the previous steps results in a transformer satisfying

for all

Next, we count the number of blocks in our transformer. The maximum width

\underbrace{1+2(N-1)\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{N}}}_{\T_{\text{sep}}}+\underbrace{1+\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}}_{\T_{\text{lead}}}+\underbrace{1\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{N}}}_{\T_{\text{col}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}}_{\T_{\text{int}}}=2\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j}+2N+1

More explicitly, ignoring layers that act as the identity, the construction is a sequence of applications of the key technical lemmas from
Section

The separation step uses one FF layer to shift all tokens, followed by

transformer blocks constructed with

In the leader selection step, we shift all tokens to the positive orthant using one FF layer, then apply

The collapse step applies one self-attention layer of the type constructed in

2

Setting

6.1
Softmax separation

The technical construction in
Lemma

5.1
needs to be adapted to ensure that there exists
\tau_{0}>0
such that
\T^{\tau}_{\text{sep}}(X^{j})\cap\T^{\tau}_{\text{sep}}(X^{j^{\prime}})=\emptyset
for all
j\neq j^{\prime}\in[N]
and all
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
.

We will solely invoke continuity arguments for this part, and restrict the presentation to a single sequence
X
(
N=1
) and a single self-attention layer. Since we are working with a finite set of sequences, the continuity arguments are easily extended to general
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Fix a finite set
X=\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Denote for every
i\in[n]

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i}^{0}(X,A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{C}_{i}(X,A)}x_{j},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\tau}(X,A)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{j}\frac{\exp{\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\langle Ax_{i},x_{j}\rangle\right)}}{\sum_{k}\exp{\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\langle Ax_{i},x_{k}\rangle\right)}}x_{j}.

As discussed in
Section

3.2
, for fixed
X
and
A
, the softmax function
\pi^{\tau}
and the hardmax function
\pi^{0}
satisfy
Elfadel and Wyatt (1993)

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\pi_{i\ell}^{\tau}(X,A)=\pi_{i\ell}^{0}(X,A)\quad\text{for all}\;i,\ell\in[n].

Equivalently, for every
\delta>0
there exists
\tau_{0}(\delta)>0
such that

\|\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\tau}(X,A)-\mathcal{M}_{i}^{0}(X,A)\|<\delta\quad\forall\tau\leq\tau_{0}(\delta)

This means that, when comparing softmax and hardmax SA layers,

\displaystyle\SA^{\tau}_{i}(X)

\displaystyle=\rho x_{i}+\alpha\mathcal{M}^{\tau}_{i}(X,A),

\displaystyle\SA^{0}_{i}(X)

\displaystyle=\rho x_{i}+\alpha\mathcal{M}^{0}_{i}(X,A),

we obtain the estimate

\|\SA^{0}_{i}(X)-\SA^{\tau}_{i}(X)\|=|\alpha|\|\mathcal{M}_{i}^{0}(X,A)-\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\tau}(X,A)\|<|\alpha|\delta

(6.1)

for all sufficiently small
\tau
. Moreover, every hardmax SA layer in the proof of
Lemma

5.1
ensures that tokens remain pairwise distinct. In particular, for some
\varepsilon>0
and all
i\neq i^{\prime}\in[n]
we have

\|\SA^{0}_{i}(X)-\SA^{0}_{i^{\prime}}(X)\|>\varepsilon.

Using
6.1
, we can similarly obtain for softmax SA layers that satisfy

\displaystyle\|\SA^{\tau}_{i}(X)-\SA^{\tau}_{i^{\prime}}(X)\|

\displaystyle\geq\|\SA^{0}_{i}(X)-\SA^{0}_{i^{\prime}}(X)\|-\|\SA^{\tau}_{i}(X)-\SA^{0}_{i}(X)\|-\|\SA^{0}_{i^{\prime}}(X)-\SA^{\tau}_{i^{\prime}}(X)\|

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon-2|\alpha|\delta.

Therefore, choosing
\delta<\frac{\varepsilon}{2|\alpha|}
, for
\tau
small enough in each softmax SA layer, we obtain that tokens remain distinct with the same construction.

7
Conclusion

We summarize here the main results of the paper and outline several directions for future work.

7.1
Summary of results

We presented the first exact interpolation result for transformers in sequence-to-sequence tasks that applies to deep transformers with standard self-attention layers and to real vector-valued outputs. Specifically, we constructed an explicit deep transformer that exactly interpolates real-valued sequences with a number of parameters independent of the input sequence length. Our construction mirrors key features of practical transformers, including the alternation of self-attention and feed-forward layers, and the low-rank structure of self-attention.

Our theoretical framework reveals two fundamental roles of self-attention layers. First, they act as a dimensionality reduction mechanism through a clustering effect, enabling efficient interpolation. Second, they leverage the non-locality of self-attention to disentangle tokens shared across different sequences. These insights help explain the practical efficiency of transformers in sequence interpolation tasks and highlight their parameter efficiency compared to traditional architectures like ResNets.
In particular, we have shown that self-attention allows for a significant reduction in the number of parameters: our transformer requires
\mathcal{O}(d\sum_{j=1}^{N}m^{j})
parameters, while a comparable ResNet architecture requires
\mathcal{O}\left(dNn\right)
parameters, where
n
denotes a fixed input length (e.g.,
n=\max_{j}n^{j}
). Since in most practical settings where transformers excel, such as masked language modeling,
m^{j}\ll n
, this translates into a reduction in parameter count, especially for long input sequences. Furthermore, the number of parameters in our transformer is independent of the input sequence lengths
n^{j}
themselves, in contrast to ResNets.

7.2
Perspectives and open questions

Our work leaves room for several future improvements, which we discuss next.

7.2.1
Complexity, parameter norms and separation

While our transformer construction exhibits lower complexity, measured in terms of the number of blocks and parameters, than those proposed in previous works
Geshkovski et al. (2024)
, we do not know whether it is optimal. In practice, transformers often achieve excellent
approximate
interpolation performance with a number of blocks significantly smaller than the dataset size. This raises the question of whether exact interpolation could also be attained with lower complexity. Addressing this question remains an interesting open problem.

Beyond block and parameter counts, an important open direction is to understand the magnitude of the parameters required in our construction. A priori bounds on the norm of the parameters would be relevant, for example, in the context of regularized training as pointed out in
Section

2
. Intuitively, one expects the required norms to depend on how well-separated the tokens are in each step of our construction: well-separated configurations may be updated as desired using transformer blocks with moderate parameter values, whereas tightly clustered data may necessitate larger parameters.
An illustrative example arises in the FF layers acting as “hat functions” of
Lemma

4.8
, where the parameters need to blow up as data separation tends to zero. A systematic study of this effect, both theoretically and numerically, would provide valuable insight into exact interpolation constructions and is left as further work.

7.2.2
Extension to probability vector outputs

An interesting question is related to next-token prediction tasks in language applications. In such settings, it is common that the output sequence is not a single token but a probability vector over the vocabulary (that is, the total number of distinct words in all sequences). One interesting question is then whether transformers can exactly match a finite set of probability vectors. Our construction also applies to this setting under suitable modifications. Specifically, let
\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{N}
be the total number of elements in the vocabulary. Then, if the input sequence is given by
X\in(\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}})^{n}
, the goal of the model is to output the probability vector

Y=(p_{1},\dots,p_{\mathcal{V}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{V}},\quad\text{for}\;p_{i}>0.

One can easily obtain an exactly interpolating transformer
\T
which outputs
Y
, provided
d=\mathcal{V}
. This probability-vector setting is closely related to the next-token prediction capacity studied in
Madden et al. (2025)
, where model complexity bounds are established for one-layer multi-head decoder-only transformers. The observation above shows that exact matching of finitely many probability vectors is also achievable within our construction when
d=\mathcal{V}
. In this sense, our result would be complementary to the results in
Madden et al. (2025)
, as it arises from a deep single-head sequence-to-sequence architecture. In practice, however, the embedding dimension
d
is typically much smaller than
\mathcal{V}
. Extending our framework to this low-dimensional regime remains an interesting direction for future work.

7.2.3
Multi-head attention

Another relevant question is whether our arguments can be efficiently extended to transformers with multi-head attention. As heads can be seen as an analogue of width in ResNets, where a trade-off with depth has been shown
Álvarez-López et al. (2024)
, we wonder if multiple heads can reduce the number of parameters and blocks required to achieve perfect sequence interpolation. For this, one should replace
3.1
with

\mathrm{SA}_{i}(X)=\rho x_{i}+\sum_{h=1}^{H}V^{h}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\len(X)}\pi_{i\ell}(X,A^{h})x_{\ell},

for a given number of heads
H\in\mathbb{N}
and head-specific parameters
A^{h},V^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
h\in[H]
. Our current analysis rigorously covers the case of a single head
H=1
and justifies the good performance of these models given sufficient depth
Michel et al. (2019)
. It would be interesting to investigate whether the additional degrees of freedom provided by multiple heads can reduce the required depth of the transformer, in particular the number of blocks used in the separation step described in
Section

5.2
.

7.2.4
Masked self-attention

Masking is a common feature of self-attention layers for next-token interpolation tasks in natural language processing, where a token
x_{i}
should only be influenced by those preceding it. To model this, one should replace
3.1
with

\mathrm{SA}_{i}(X)=\rho x_{i}+V\sum_{\ell=1}^{i}\pi_{i\ell}(X,A)x_{\ell}.

Notice that, for each
i\in[\len(X)]
, the sum runs only over
\ell\leq i
. This masked self-attention setting can be handled by our results in
Theorem

1.6
and
Theorem

1.8
through a problem reformulation as follows (we assume that
n^{j}=n
and
m^{j}=m
to simplify the exposition). For all
j\in[N]
,

•

each
X^{j}
is split into
n
initial sequences
X_{t}^{j}=\{x_{1}^{j},\dots,x_{t}^{j}\}\subset(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{t}
for all
t\in[n]
,

•

each
Y^{j}
is split into
n
output sequences
Y_{t}^{j}=\{y_{t}^{j}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
t\in[m]
and
Y_{t}^{j}=\{y_{m}^{j}\}
for all
t>m
.

Now, we can apply
Theorem

1.6
or
Theorem

1.8
to the dataset
\{(X_{t}^{j},Y_{t}^{j})\}_{j\in[N],t\in[n]}
consisting of
Nn
sequences, and obtain a transformer
\T
such that
\T(X^{j}_{t})=Y^{j}_{t}
with
\mathcal{O}(Nn)
blocks and
\mathcal{O}(dNn)
parameters. Thus, it holds for a causal transformer
\T_{\text{causal}}
that

\T_{\text{causal}}(X^{j})=\{\T(X_{1}^{j}),\dots,\T(X_{n}^{j})\}=\{y_{1}^{j},\dots,y_{m}^{j}\}=Y^{j},

as desired. This reformulation leads to a complexity that scales with input length
n
rather than output length
m
, a scaling also observed in large-scale models
Hoffmann et al. (2022)
. Designing architectures that avoid this dependence while preserving exact interpolation remains an open challenge.
