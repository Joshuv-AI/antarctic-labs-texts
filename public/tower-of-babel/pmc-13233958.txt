Advances in silicon carbide pressure sensors for high-temperature extreme environment sensing
Authors: Chen Wu, Xudong Fang, Hao Sun, Zhongkai Zhang, Meng Shi, Zhaojun Liu, Ziyan Fang, Yuefei Yan, Qiang Kang, Ryutaro Maeda, Bian Tian, Libo Zhao, Zhuangde Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn fields such as aerospace, geothermal energy, Venus exploration, and nuclear power safety, large-scale equipment often operates under extreme conditions characterized by high temperatures, radiation, corrosive gases, and vibration. Pressure monitoring at critical locations is essential for equipment condition monitoring, fault diagnosis, and improving operational efficiency. Microelectromechanical system (MEMS) sensors, with advantages including small size, high integration, and batch manufacturability, represent the mainstream application of industrial intelligent sensors. However, traditional silicon (Si)-based pressure sensors are limited by material properties, and the performance degrades due to PN junction leakage current and mechanical deformation. While silicon-on-insulator (SOI) sensors still face challenges, such as high-temperature plastic deformation and insufficient reliability. Silicon carbide (SiC), as a third-generation wide-bandgap semiconductor, offers significant advantages, including a high bandgap, radiation resistance, and corrosion resistance. These properties make SiC a promising substrate material for overcoming the bottleneck in high-temperature pressure sensing. Nevertheless, the current study is confined to a laboratory prototype. Performance under extreme conditions needs to be enhanced, especially the allowable operating temperature range, sensitivity, and stability. This paper reviews the evolution of various types of high-temperature pressure sensors systematically. Key technologies for SiC pressure sensors, including structural design, sensor chip fabrication, high-temperature ohmic contacts, packaging and reliability validation, are discussed. The latest research progress is summarized, and existing technical problems are analyzed. Finally, the challenges for SiC high-temperature pressure sensor research are discussed, and perspectives on future development trends are presented. This review aims to provide theoretical support and innovative directions for SiC pressure sensing technologies in extreme environments. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Physics

Introduction

High-temperature pressure sensors employ thermophysical transduction mechanisms to convert mechanical pressure signals into quantifiable electrical outputs, thereby enabling precise pressure measurements under extreme thermal conditions. Compared to conventional room-temperature sensors with operational limitations below 125 °C, these specialized devices maintain measurement fidelity in environments exceeding 125 °C, making them indispensable for demanding applications in different fields where thermal resilience is paramount1. Currently, high-temperature pressure sensors are extensively employed across diverse industrial and scientific domains, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Within the oil and gas sector, these sensors play a crucial role in monitoring downhole, pipeline, and gas pressures. Precise pressure measurement is essential throughout oil and gas operations, from extraction and transportation to processing, to maintain both operational safety and efficiency. In the aerospace field2, high-temperature pressure sensors are employed to monitor engine, cylinder, and atmospheric pressures of aircraft and spacecraft, ensuring operational safety and reliability3. Similarly, in the automotive industry, these sensors measure critical pressures in engine intake systems, brake systems, and combustion chambers. Precise pressure monitoring is particularly essential in vehicle power and braking systems to maintain optimal engine performance and ensure brake system functionality. In the nuclear power sector, precise pressure monitoring within the containment vessel is crucial for maintaining optimal operating conditions of high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs). This monitoring system enables real-time assessment of internal pressure parameters, facilitating timely adjustments to prevent potential nuclear leakage incidents and ensure reactor safety. Furthermore, Venus has evolved into a celestial body enveloped by a dense, high-temperature, and chemically hostile atmosphere due to its runaway greenhouse effect4. Leading space agencies, including NASA, ESA, and Roscosmos, have initiated multiple exploration missions to investigate Venus’s transformation from a potentially habitable planet to its current extreme state. These studies provide crucial insights for understanding and addressing climate change on Earth. Advanced high-temperature sensors, particularly pressure sensors, play a vital role in enabling surface operations and environmental monitoring of Venus landers under these extreme conditions5,6. As illustrated in Fig. 1, high-temperature pressure sensors serve as critical monitoring “eyes” across diverse environments from ocean depths to terrestrial operations, from high-altitude aviation to space exploration. These sensors provide essential pressure data for various applications, including oil and gas extraction, aircraft propulsion systems, automotive electronics, nuclear reactor operations, spacecraft launches, and planetary exploration, ensuring both operational safety and optimal performance of critical equipment.

Application scenarios of high-temperature pressure sensors

The earliest pressure sensors relied on measuring resistance changes resulting from geometric deformation of metals under tensile and compressive forces, yet their sensitivity remained limited. A significant breakthrough occurred in 1954 when C.S. Smith discovered the piezoresistive effect in silicon (Si) semiconductors, marking a pivotal moment that initiated extensive research and practical applications of semiconductor-based pressure sensors7. Room temperature microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) pressure sensors predominantly utilize Si as their core material7. The rapid advancement of diffusion Si ion implantation technology, etching techniques, and micro-nano fabrication processes has enabled the successful commercialization and mass production of Si-based sensors. These devices demonstrate mature manufacturing processes, stable performance, low cost, and compact dimensions when operating within their specified temperature range of 100 °C. However, when deployed in environments exceeding 125 °C, thermal-induced leakage currents in the ion-implanted PN junctions significantly degrade sensor performance, potentially leading to complete device failure8. Moreover, Si exhibits plastic deformation at temperatures exceeding 500 °C and demonstrates poor resistance to corrosive water vapor degradation. These material limitations fundamentally restrict the application of Si-based pressure sensors in high-temperature and harsh environments. To address these challenges, two primary mitigation strategies have been developed: (1) implementing thermal management at the packaging level, such as incorporating water-cooling jackets or thermal dissipation grids to reduce the temperature of the sensing area through physical cooling. (2) relocating the pressure measurement point from high-temperature zones to lower-temperature regions using pressure tubing. However, these solutions inevitably increase sensor volume and cost while compromising dynamic pressure response characteristics, failing to provide a fundamental resolution for high-temperature pressure monitoring. Consequently, researchers worldwide have shifted their focus to alternative high-temperature resistant materials. As illustrated in Fig. 2, extensively investigated substrate materials for high-temperature pressure sensors include silicon on insulator (SOI), sapphire, diamond, fiber, and silicon carbide (SiC). Among various types, fiber-optic principle-based high-temperature pressure sensors have received the most extensive research attention, followed by SiC high-temperature pressure sensors.

The evolutionary trajectory of high-temperature pressure sensor technology

SOI pressure sensors achieve high-temperature sensing by replacing the PN junction isolation with a buried oxide layer that exhibits good insulation properties. Earlier research began in 1992, Pressure sensors on SOI substrates through a combination of trench etching and anisotropic etching techniques were fabricated9. Research in this area has been ongoing. To improve the transient impact performance of the sensor, a high-temperature pressure sensor with a passive resistance compensation network, which effectively reduces the thermal zero-point drift of SOI pressure sensor (Sensor chip structure in Fig. 3)10. This sensor can be used at 200 °C and can withstand the impact of instantaneous high temperatures (less than 0.5 s) of ~1000 °C. After continuous research iterations, they fabricated a micro-pressure sensor with a measurement range of 250 kPa by designing a structure with a widened beam root (Fig. 3a for sensor chip and packaging). Test results showed high response characteristics at 200 °C11. The early research on SOI sensors mainly focused on small ranges, while for some fields such as explosions or oil pipelines, a large range of high-temperature pressure sensors are required. In response to this kind of demand, a piezoresistive pressure sensor based on SOI was designed12, they proposed a sensitivity design method for high-temperature environments, and conducted forward and reverse stroke pressure tests with a range of up to 150 MPa at room temperature (see the practical view of HPTSS in Fig. 3b). In fact, for high temperature pressure measurement, range and temperature are two performance factors that cannot be achieved simultaneously. The combination of high temperature and a large range means that manufacturing and testing are difficult, so the range of common high-temperature pressure sensors generally does not exceed 10 MPa. A typical study on high temperature and appropriate range was completed by Xin Li et al., they studied the variation of piezoresistive coefficient of Si with different crystal orientations on SOI substrates with temperature13, and conducted pressure measurements of sensors at 300 °C and 0–6 MPa (Fig. 3c). To miniaturize the sensor, a 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm miniature high-temperature resistive SOI pressure sensor was proposed (Fig.3d), which completed calibration testing in the full temperature range of -55 °C to 350 °C14. In these studies, there is no detailed packaging description. For SOI high-temperature pressure sensors, testing pressure at temperatures greater than 300 °C and packaging is an inevitable challenge that needs to be addressed. For example, a dielectric push rod during the packaging process to directly transfer the applied pressure to the central bump film of the SOI chip were proposed (see the sensor packaging picture in Fig. 3e), raising the temperature of the sensor to 350 °C and increasing the sensitivity of the sensor chip to 18 mV/μm15. To continuously increase the temperature of SOI sensors, based on smart cut technology, the piezoresistive pressure sensor has achieved pressure calibration in the range of −55 °C ~ 500 °C (Sensor chip structure in Fig. 3f) and has extremely low hysteresis ( < 0.1% FS). It is currently the highest temperature measurement reported in non-commercial research. In addition, it is worth noting that Kulite Corporation has been committed to developing high-temperature pressure sensors for aerospace for many years. The XTEH-10LAC-190 (M) series sensors were developed in 200416, can operate stably within −55 °C ~ 482 °C, which is currently the highest level of commercial SOI pressure sensors. In the past decades, researchers have conducted extensive research on the chip optimization and simulation of SOI pressure sensors17,18, chip fabrication process development19,20, and sensor performance testing applications21,22. At present, the preparation process of SOI high-temperature pressure sensors is relatively mature and is the most common high-temperature pressure product on the market. However, the substrate is still Si. The degradation of the piezoresistive coefficient at high temperatures and the influence of high-temperature Si creep make it difficult for such sensors to work in environments with temperatures of 500 °C or higher for a long time.

Besides the above-mentioned oxide isolation structures, a Silicon on Sapphire (SoS) pressure sensor substrate can be obtained by using sapphire as the insulating substrate, hetero epitaxially growing a monocrystalline Si thin film on it, and forming diffusion-sensitive resistors on the Si film using semiconductor diffusion processes. Currently, the highest reported operating temperature for this type of sensor is achieved by the SoS high-temperature pressure sensor developed by Russia Microelectronic Sensor Industrial Group (Short in MIDA, see the packaged sensor in Fig. 4a), with an operating temperature reaching as high as 550 °C23. An overview of SoS sensor structures and their application as piezoresistive sensing elements in mechanical sensors is proposed (See sensor chip in Fig. 4b), as well as the mechanisms by which temperature effects influence P-type SoS piezoresistive sensors, and they also discussed the development status and trends of SoS pressure sensors24. For the miniaturization of sensors, a miniaturized, portable SoS pressure sensor, along with its temperature compensation system, has been designed and implemented (The sensor chip is illustrated in Fig. 4c), achieving high-precision pressure measurements within the 250 °C range25. Due to the difficulty of forming a vacuum absolute pressure cavity, SoS high-temperature pressure sensors are mostly large-range gauge pressure sensors. Moreover, there is a large lattice mismatch between the epitaxially grown monocrystalline Si thin film and sapphire, leading to significant mismatch stress. For these reasons, research on this type of sensor remains relatively limited.

Taking advantage of the high gauge factor of boron-doped diamond, researchers have been exploring the fabrication of piezoresistive diamond-on-silicon (DoS) high-temperature pressure sensors. These sensors generally use Si as the base chip material, onto which a diamond thin film is deposited. Using etching and patterning techniques, piezoresistive diamond sensing elements are formed. For instance, the piezoresistive effect of doped polycrystalline diamond has been experimentally investigated26. Then, chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is used to deposit boron-doped polycrystalline diamond on a Si substrate to fabricate a diamond pressure sensor, as shown in Fig.4d. This sensor was tested over 0.07 MPa from room temperature to 250 °C27. Both theoretical and experimental studies in the literature have demonstrated that P-type diamond exhibits a relatively high piezoresistive coefficient28, along with a wide bandgap (5.45 eV), making it ideally suited for high-temperature pressure sensing applications29. However, despite its theoretical promise, recent research in this field has been sparse, and devices reported in existing publications have generally failed to fully exploit the superior properties of diamond. One of the core challenges lies in the extreme hardness of diamond30, which makes extensive material removal difficult during microfabrication. As a result, researchers often turn to thin film deposition and photolithography techniques to fabricate boron-doped diamond piezoresistive elements directly on Si substrates. Nevertheless, the lattice mismatch between the Si substrate and the diamond thin film, combined with differences in thermal expansion coefficients, generates significant thermal stress in the heterostructure. This thermal mismatch stress severely degrades the sensor performance and is considered one of the primary reasons why DoS pressure sensors remain underexplored and underutilized in high-temperature sensing applications.

The aforementioned high-temperature pressure sensors are active wired pressure detection systems, where signals are transmitted via lead wires. To reduce the interference on sensing and signal output at high temperatures, some wireless pressure measurement technologies for high-temperature pressure sensors have been researched. Wireless pressure measurement allows for the physical isolation between the electrical connections, conditioning circuits, and the high-temperature heat sources, which helps enhance the sensor’s reliability. Currently, the two main types of wireless high-temperature pressure sensors are surface acoustic wave (SAW) pressure sensors and LC resonance-based pressure sensors.

SAW pressure sensors leverage the piezoelectric effect and the properties of surface acoustic waves to detect external pressure changes by monitoring variations in the amplitude, phase and frequency of the acoustic wave. These sensors are passive, compact, cost-effective to manufacture, and easy to integrate, making them especially suitable for harsh environments like high-temperature, high-pressure conditions or on rotating components30,31. In recent years, SAW pressure sensors based on piezoelectric single-crystal materials such as quartz32, lithium niobate (LiNbO₃)33, and lanthanum gallium silicate (Langasite, LGS) have found wide application. However, both quartz and lithium niobate experience degradation or even complete loss of their piezoelectric properties at elevated temperatures, limiting their use in high-temperature scenarios. By contrast, LGS maintains stable piezoelectric characteristics at temperatures exceeding 900 °C, making it a strong candidate for fabricating high-temperature pressure sensors. For instance, to solve the coupling of temperature and pressure signals in high-temperature environments, an LGS-based SAW pressure sensor reference sensor was designed to detect temperature changes, and a complex decoupling algorithm was designed for temperature compensation (Sensor chip structure is displayed in Fig. 4j). This sensor can operate at temperatures below 1000 °C, achieving a pressure sensitivity of 137 Hz/kPa at 1000 °C34. In this sensor structure, LGS serves as the substrate structural component, while aluminum nitride is the main sensitive structure. Due to the difficulty and high cost of growing LGS single crystals, as well as being a hard and brittle material with high hardness and difficult processing, its micro-nano processing technology (such as wet etching, hot pressing bonding, etc.) is difficult, and the process still needs to be fully overcome. Therefore, such LGS-based SAW pressure sensors have not been able to achieve engineering applications. Compared to bulk piezoelectric crystals, piezoelectric thin-film structures offer the potential to combine the functional advantages of both the piezoelectric material and the substrate, and have thus become a major research focus. Common piezoelectric thin-film materials include aluminum nitride (AlN), zinc oxide (ZnO), and gallium nitride (GaN). At the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China (UESTC), Memon conducted a series of studies on AlN-based SAW pressure sensors on Si substrates. By fine-tuning the device orientation and acoustic wave propagation direction, they significantly enhanced the sensor sensitivity, achieving 228.46 ppm/MPa, which represents a 76.87% improvement over existing sensors. However, one major limitation is that Si substrates exhibit electrical conductivity at elevated temperatures, restricting the operating temperature of AlN-on-Si SAW pressure sensors to below 300 °C35. As a result, for multilayer SAW sensor structures, identifying new substrate materials or architectures is critical. Current research is now exploring the use of SiC and sapphire as alternative substrate materials. These materials promise better high-temperature stability and electrical insulation. However, due to the manufacturing challenges of AlN, particularly its difficulty in etching and patterning, most such designs remain at the simulation or proof-of-concept prototype stage and have yet to see widespread practical application36,37.

High-temperature wireless pressure sensors also include LC resonant-type sensors based on co-fired ceramics, specifically Inductor-Capacitor resonators. These sensors typically embed an LC resonant structure within the sensing chip. The working principle involves the correlation between resonant frequency and pressure; the sensor detects pressure changes by measuring the impedance resonance frequency via inductive coupling. The first passive wireless high-temperature pressure sensor based on LTCC (Low Temperature Co-fired Ceramics) capable of stable operation at 450 °C38. To improve the temperature, a seven-layer LTCC-based LC resonant pressure sensor was proposed (Sensor structure in Fig. 4l)39, pushing the operational limit to 600 °C. By fully embedding the inductor and capacitor within the ceramic body, the sensor exhibited enhanced stability in high-temperature and chemically corrosive environments. In addition to LTCC, HTCC (High Temperature Co-fired Ceramics), along with post-fired platinum paste, is also used to fabricate the pressure-sensitive structure and inductor coil as displayed in Fig. 4m. Their design allowed the sensor to operate up to 1000 °C. Although at this temperature, the Q factor dropped below 3, significantly increasing the difficulty of accurate resonance point detection40. Simultaneously, a high-temperature wireless passive pressure sensor using HTCC technology can couple with an antenna at 600 °C. At room temperature, it supported a maximum readout distance of 2.8 cm, with a sensitivity of around 860 Hz/bar, and exhibited low hysteresis and repeatability errors41. To break through the working temperature, HTCC-based sensors capable of working up to 800 °C were fabricated (in Fig. 4k), with 2 Bar pressure and a linearity coefficient of 0.93, placing their work at the forefront of domestic research42. Despite these advancements, most mainstream high-temperature wireless pressure sensors are still built using ceramic materials. However, LC resonant sensors based on ceramic sintering techniques face structural limitations. The thickness of the pressure-sensitive diaphragm and the gap of the capacitor are often constrained to integer multiples of green ceramic tape layers, which prevents miniaturization and high sensitivity. Additionally, the effective inductive coupling distance depends heavily on the coil’s outer diameter, creating a design conflict between miniaturization and reliable signal transmission. Other issues, such as difficulty in fabricating high-Q inductors for high temperatures, low precision in ceramic sintering, and screen-printing inconsistencies, lead to pressure-sensitive chips that are too large, imprecise, leaky, low in sensitivity, and poor in consistency, making commercial-scale engineering challenging.

To tackle the challenges of pressure monitoring in extreme thermal environments some researchers have turned toward new materials, novel architectures, and innovative sensing methods. Among the most promising directions is the development of miniaturized high-temperature pressure sensors based on third-generation wide-bandgap semiconductors, especially SiC. As a typical representative of third-generation semiconductors43, SiC boasts exceptional properties including wide-bandgap, high breakdown voltage, superior thermal conductivity, high-temperature adaptability and corrosion resistance. These traits make it ideal for high-temperature, high-pressure, and high-frequency devices44. There are >250 polytypes of SiC in nature, but 4H-SiC (hexagonal), 3C-SiC (cubic) and 6H-SiC (hexagonal) are commonly used types45,46. Compared to the first-generation semiconductors (Si) and the second-generation semiconductors (GaAs), SiC offers superior mechanical, chemical, and electrical characteristics47. As summarized in Table 1, SiC supports operation temperatures up to 1000 °C, is compatible with MEMS processes, and enables the fabrication of compact and lightweight devices. As a result, it has become one of the primary directions in MEMS high-temperature pressure sensor research and development48. When applied to MEMS sensors, SiC as a structural material allows pressure sensing in extreme environments without the need for bulky cooling systems or error-prone indirect measurement schemes. Its robust high-temperature performance and integration potential suggest immense promise for future deployment in extreme environment pressure sensing49.

The earliest research into high-temperature pressure sensors based on SiC was initiated by organizations such as NASA in the United States and the Berlin Research Center in Germany. Since then, a variety of SiC-based pressure sensors with different sensing principles and structural designs have been proposed and fabricated. These efforts have collectively demonstrated the feasibility and immense potential of SiC as both the substrate and sensing material for high-temperature pressure sensors. However, despite its excellent theoretical performance, the highest operating temperature reported to date remains at 800 °C, achieved by NASA, which is still far below the material’s intrinsic thermal endurance limit. This discrepancy can largely be attributed to limitations in several key enabling technologies. The following sections focus on SiC high-temperature pressure sensors, outlining the current research progress and providing a comparative analysis of the respective strengths and weaknesses. Additionally, this work analyzes the global research in this field and places particular emphasis on the critical factors that currently limit the high-temperature performance of SiC pressure sensor. A comprehensive analysis of these challenges is presented, as well as an outlook on future development trends.

Sensing principle of SiC high-temperature pressure sensor

Figure 5a illustrates the fundamental operating principle and typical structure of piezoresistive pressure sensor, which operates based on the semiconductor piezoresistive effect48. This sensor configuration has gained significant attention in SiC high-temperature pressure sensor research due to its structural simplicity, straightforward readout circuitry, operational stability, and excellent integration capabilities. Figure 5b demonstrates the working principle and standard configuration of capacitive pressure sensor, whose sensing element primarily consists of a substrate and a diaphragm serving as the capacitor’s dual electrodes. The capacitance variation results from changes in inter-electrode distance and other geometric parameters induced by external pressure. The rapid dynamic response and minimal temperature sensitivity of capacitive pressure sensor have made it a subject of extensive investigation in the field of SiC-based high-temperature pressure sensing.

Piezoresistive SiC pressure sensors

This section presents a comprehensive overview of typical piezoresistive SiC high-temperature pressure sensors. Since the 1990s, the NASA Glenn Research Center has been actively focused on the application of SiC in the MEMS sensor, with particular emphasis on the development of SiC-based pressure sensor50. Kulite Semiconductor Products Inc., in collaboration with NASA, successfully fabricated a 6H-SiC piezoresistive pressure sensor51. Photoelectrochemical etching method is applied to fabricate the sensor piezoresistors, and the sensitive diaphragm is formed by electrochemical back-etching52. This sensor could withstand temperatures up to 260 °C as illustrated in Fig. 6. By 2007, the same team had advanced their design, producing a 6H-SiC piezoresistive sensor that can work at 600 °C high temperature53. Then, in 2014, R S. Okojie et al. proposed a fully integrated pressure sensor fabricated entirely from 4H-SiC54. Based on the previous work, they successfully fabricated a fully monolithic 4H-SiC piezoresistive pressure sensor in 201955, which was able to endure operating temperatures as high as 800 °C. The NASA Glenn team has made notable contributions to extending the upper thermal limit of SiC-based pressure sensors56. Their work includes the implementation of a flip-chip packaging scheme tailored for SiC piezoresistive sensing chips, and a series of studies on achieving high-temperature stability of ohmic contact electrodes, a critical factor in the long-term reliability of such devices under extreme thermal conditions.

The piezoresistive SiC pressure sensor is the most attractive high-temperature SiC pressure sensor, with remarkable research achievements. Typical research results are shown in Fig. 7. Due to the limitation of SiC single crystal growth, initial research focused on 3C-SiC sensitive thin films on Si or SOI. For instance, a study on piezoresistive pressure sensors fabricated by growing 3C-SiC on Si substrates was conducted with two different piezoresistor layout schemes (Sensor chip and piezoresistor layout in Fig. 7b). The sensor achieved high sensitivity (177.6 mV/V·psi) at room temperature, and 63.1 mV/V psi at 400 °C, which demonstrates a decrease in sensitivity with increasing temperature57. Simultaneously, a highly sensitive and robust 3C-SiC/Si piezoresistive pressure sensor was developed (in Fig. 7c), incorporating a stress-amplifying structure to improve sensitivity. The sensor operated accurately within the pressure range of 0–100 kPa and the temperature range of 25 °C-80 °C. In fact, for piezoresistive sensor chips, the deep etching of SiC sensitive diagrams limits the manufacturing of the sensors. And the working temperature of the sensor does not exceed 300 °C. With continuous improvements, they developed a fully 6H-SiC piezoresistive pressure sensor (Packaged sensor in Fig. 7a). This version employed a proof mass structure to enhance sensitivity and provide overload protection. The sensing diaphragm was fabricated using a bulk micromachining technique via ultrasonic drilling, which improved thickness uniformity. A thrust rod-based packaging design was used to transfer the medium pressure to the diaphragm, achieving sensor operation at up to 400 °C and in a pressure range of 0-300 MPa58. In a different approach, a novel design based on a high-purity semi-insulating 4H-SiC substrate was proposed, where P-type SiC piezoresistors are fabricated together with a square-shaped sensing diaphragm, forming a monolithic SiC pressure sensor59. To solve the problem of chip processing, a significant advancement in SiC micromachining was reported. They combined the deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) and laser-assisted direct bonding (LADB) to successfully create a piezoresistive SiC pressure sensor cavity (Enlarged view of the sensor chip structure in Fig. 7d), overcoming the fabrication challenges of SiC wafer60.

The etching difficulty determines the sensitivity of sensors. To solve this problem, several new manufacturing methods have been proposed and applied. Researchers applied SiC bonding technology for fabricating piezoresistive pressure sensor chips. For example, SiC-to-SiC direct bonding techniques were developed to form an absolute pressure cavity for a sensor chip (See sensor chip structure in Fig. 7g), leading to the fabrication of a 4H-SiC piezoresistive pressure sensor capable of withstanding 250 °C. The sensor achieved a sensitivity of 1.56 mV/V/MPa at room temperature61. After that, they continued to fabricate a bonded SiC chip with a side length of 2400 μm and a thickness of 400 μm, using a two-piece SiC bonding method. The sensor produced a full-scale output of 54.62 mV under a 10 MPa pressure load62. The application of bonding technology marks that the processing method of SiC pressure sensors no longer relies on deep etching. Furthermore, a high-temperature piezoresistive SiC pressure sensor using laser micromachining was developed and operates at temperatures up to 300 °C63. The application of laser processing in chip manufacturing further diversifies the sensor manufacturing methods. Xudong Fang et al. developed a N-type 4H-SiC piezoresistive pressure sensor with a sensitivity of 13.2 mV/V/kPa, capable of operating under 7 MPa pressure at 350 °C64. Recently, they focused on exploring the influence of piezoresistor geometry on the piezoresistive effect in N-type 4H-SiC and successfully fabricated a pressure sensor chip with a 2 MPa full-scale range (in Fig. 7k). In cyclic loading tests at 300 °C, the sensor showed a sensitivity of 3.38 mV/V/MPa65. Likewise, a piezoresistive pressure sensor based on P-type 4H-SiC was designed, which verifies that the sensor produced a full-scale output of 1.09 mV/V/bar at room temperature66. To overcome the surface defects induced by laser processing, a femtosecond laser-assisted plasma etching method for SiC diagrams was proposed67 and a sensor with 5 MPa range, further reducing the difficulty of chip processing. In summary, piezoresistive SiC high-temperature pressure sensors offer advantages such as high sensitivity, linear output, and mature fabrication processes. However, the semiconductor piezoresistive effect is significantly temperature-dependent, leading to substantial temperature drift in the sensors at elevated temperatures. On-chip temperature compensation is thus required to ensure long-term stability. Given the strong temperature-dependent variation in the piezoresistive coefficient and the degradation in mobility, which exacerbate the temperature-pressure cross-sensitivity, the temperature drift issue remains a significant challenge. To address this, the structure and circuit should be co-designed with differential/reference cavities or redundant bridge arms for inherent temperature compensation. Studies indicate that piezoresistive sensors are currently the most viable for engineering applications among SiC-based types. Nevertheless, technological challenges, including ohmic contacts, diaphragm etching processes, and high-temperature packaging techniques, constrain the industrial commercialization of piezoresistive SiC pressure sensors. On-chip temperature detection and real-time demodulation compensation should be introduced to mitigate temperature drift, ensuring the stability of high-temperature testing.

Capacitive SiC pressure sensors

In contrast to piezoresistive SiC pressure sensors, capacitive sensors are less sensitive to temperature changes than piezoresistive sensors, and the excellent thermal stability of SiC materials results in smaller output changes and less significant temperature drift across the entire temperature range. In addition, the structural characteristics of the two electrode plates of the capacitive pressure sensor indicate that SiC bonding technology is needed during processing. However, the high-temperature bonding technology of SiC is currently challenging. Therefore, the existing capacitive SiC pressure sensors are mainly based on 3C-SiC thin films. For instance, the APCVD (atmospheric pressure chemical vapor deposition) process was used to deposit 3C-SiC thin-film electrodes on a Si substrate, and successfully fabricated 3C-SiC thin-film capacitive pressure sensor (Sensor chip structure in Fig. 8a). The sensor operated at temperatures up to 400 °C, with a sensitivity of 7.7 fF/torr68. To get rid of the high temperature mismatch of heterostructures, an all-SiC capacitive pressure sensor was fabricated (in Fig. 8b)69. Low-temperature oxide (LTO) was used as both the sacrificial layer and sealing material. The sensor was constructed with polycrystalline SiC diaphragms and substrates, using Si₃N₄ as the insulating layer, and achieved a maximum operating temperature of 574 °C. Moreover, a MEMS-packaged, semi-SiC capacitive pressure sensor prototype was developed (the packaged sensor in Fig. 8c). 3C-SiC thin film was applied as the pressure-sensitive diaphragm bonded to a Si substrate70. The team continued this research71,72, demonstrating that the MEMS-packaged 3C-SiC capacitive pressure sensor maintained high reliability at temperatures up to 500 °C. To address the fabrication difficulty of single crystalline 3C-SiC diaphragm-based high temperature capacitive pressure sensors fabricated by wafer bonding, an alternative polycrystalline SiC-on-Si architecture have developed (in Fig. 8h). To improve the high temperature stability of the sensor from the perspective of structural design, a differential capacitive pressure sensor was proposed which featured two layers of SiC thin films, both acting as capacitor plates on a Si substrate73. Under pressure loading, the upper plate deformed74 (sensor chip in Fig. 8d), altering the plate spacing and resulting in differential capacitance output. It is worth noting that although the above research used a capacitive structure, due to the heterogeneous thermal mismatch of the sensor structure, the working temperature of the sensor did not reach the expected high temperature.

To solve the sensor failure problem at high temperatures, a non-contact SiC-based capacitive pressure sensor for high-temperature applications was designed (in Fig. 8e)75. The device adopted SiC-glass slurry-SiC sandwich structure and used wireless, passive pressure sensing via LC resonance frequency shift, offering a valuable reference for advancing capacitive SiC pressure sensing technologies. All-SiC capacitive pressure sensor proven to avoid high-temperature thermal mismatch76, the sensor achieved 600 °C operating temperature and measuring pressures in the 0–3 kPa range. To increase the working temperature, one viable fabrication route is to form sealed pressure cavities using SiC-SiC homo-junction bonding or SiC ceramic hetero-junction bonding. More recently, a fully 4H-SiC capacitive high-temperature pressure sensor was reported77. They employed deep dry etching and direct bonding to create a hermetically sealed absolute pressure cavity. The sensor achieved a sensitivity of 4.51 × 10⁻³ pF/kPa over a 0–300 kPa range at room temperature, with a non-linearity error of 2.83%. Furthermore, capacitive pressure sensors often suffer from nonlinearity. To address this problem, a novel SiC-AlN-SiC diaphragm structure (see sensor structure in Fig. 8g) with two contact modes was designed to improve the sensor output linearity78. Results elucidate that the double-touch mode capacitive pressure sensor with the defined structural parameters used in this work outperforms all the previously developed models. In summary, compared with piezoresistive counterparts, capacitive designs generally promise lower temperature drift and higher resolution. Efforts toward “All‑SiC” devices introduced bonding and heterogeneous dielectric stacks and even room‑temperature SiC-AlN wafer bonding to enable capacitive architectures with improved linearity and sensitivity, while hermetic high‑temperature packaging, noble‑metal/nitride electrodes, and stable dielectrics have become pivotal for long‑term reliability. In the next step, a clear trajectory toward “All‑SiC structures with co‑integrated high‑temperature electronics,” combined with differential mode designs for linearity and sensitivity gains, and concurrent advances in hermetic packaging and parasitic suppression. Persistent challenges include high‑temperature parasitics and leakage, long‑term cavity hermeticity, material/process cost, and zero‑drift/repeatability under severe thermal cycling.

Optical fiber SiC pressure sensors

Beyond the two major principles discussed above, SiC-based high-temperature pressure sensors that utilize optical components and fiber-optic methods have also emerged as an important area of research. In Fabry-Pérot (F-P) pressure sensors, the principle of F-P interference is employed to measure pressure variations, where pressure information is obtained by analyzing the changes in interference fringes of the reflected light within the optical fiber. These approaches leverage the unique optical and mechanical characteristics of SiC to enable pressure sensing in extreme environments. A wireless pressure sensing system (As demonstrated in Fig. 9a) was proposed using a SiC wafer as the pressure-sensitive diaphragm79. By combining optical path design with image processing, both the mechanical response of the SiC chip within the pressure capsule and the optical response of the pressure measurement system based on image demagnification were investigated. However, due to the complexity of the optical backend processing, this method did not gain traction in follow-up studies or applications. Subsequently, the integration of SiC with fiber-optic sensing techniques has led to various types of SiC-fiber hybrid high-temperature pressure sensors. Among them, the all-SiC Fabry-Pérot (F-P) cavity-based pressure sensor has shown promising early results. Developments in this area date back to 2000, Wade Pulliam et al. fabricated a SiC-based sapphire fiber-optic F-P pressure sensor (in Fig. 9b). Measuring just 3 mm in size, the sensor used a SiC diaphragm to detect external pressure-induced deformation, which was then measured optically via fiber interference80. Moreover, similar structures of SiC-fiber high-temperature pressure sensor were fabricated (in Fig. 9c, d81,82), which demonstrated good linearity over a 0–800 kPa pressure range at room temperature, with a repeatability error of 1.42% and a temperature tolerance up to 400 °C. Notably, Jiang Yonggang et al. have been engaged in long-term research on fiber-optic SiC pressure sensors. They developed a high-temperature bonding process to assemble the SiC diaphragm and base, with the optical fiber fixed through the SiC base. The prototype sensor was produced with a pressure range of 0.1 MPa-0.9 MPa, and evaluated its performance at room temperature. Most recently, a fully SiC-based F-P optical pressure sensor with a pressure range of 1 MPa has been fabricated83. The sensor was calibrated across a temperature range of 25 °C to 700 °C, and supports dual-mode sensing of pressure and temperature, making it suitable for deployment in extreme environments.

In essence, researchers have been devoted to enhancing the sensitivity and stability of SiC fiber-based F-P pressure sensors by optimizing the sensing structure, thereby improving their capability to detect subtle pressure fluctuations. Nevertheless, the primary challenge in developing SiC F-P pressure sensors lies in the exceptional chemical stability and intrinsic brittleness of SiC, which make microfabrication exceedingly difficult. This issue is analogous to the difficulties encountered in processing piezoresistive diaphragms. Achieving diaphragms with high precision and low surface roughness, as well as ensuring vacuum F-P cavities with strong and hermetic bonding, remains technically demanding and poses a significant threat to the long-term stability of the sensors under high-temperature conditions. Another major obstacle is the pronounced cross-sensitivity between temperature and pressure, which severely compromises measurement accuracy. Addressing thermal drift through optimized structural design or effective signal demodulation and compensation strategies is, therefore, critical for practical deployment. In addition, high-temperature packaging remains a weak link. Conventional adhesive-based approaches fail under extreme thermal environments and often introduce residual stress. Although adhesive-free methods such as laser welding have been explored, achieving reliable, hermetic sealing between SiC and metallic or heterogeneous components is still an unresolved challenge. Future research should focus on advancing high-performance SiC deep-etching and surface planarization techniques, developing sensor architectures with inherent temperature compensation, and exploring alternative high-temperature transmission and packaging materials such as sapphire fibers. These efforts will be essential for extending the operational temperature range and enhancing the overall reliability of SiC F-P pressure sensors.

Extensive studies have demonstrated that MEMS pressure sensors based on SiC substrates are well-suited for high-temperature and harsh operating environments. To date, significant research has been conducted on sensors based on the three principles mentioned above. Figure 5 provides a comprehensive summary and comparison of the fundamental principles, advantages, and disadvantages of these three types of pressure sensors. Among them, piezoresistive SiC high-temperature pressure sensors have been studied the longest and exhibit relatively mature technology. Despite performance limitations such as temperature drift, temperature compensation techniques for piezoresistive sensors have been proposed and extensively investigated, achieving high levels of compensation accuracy. Piezoresistive SiC pressure sensors can operate at the highest temperatures, feature simple backend circuitry, and directly output voltage or current signals. Their excellent linearity, ease of signal acquisition, and straightforward processing make them particularly suitable for pressure signal monitoring under high-temperature, harsh, and complex conditions. Consequently, the piezoresistive principle has garnered significant attention and research interest, becoming one of the most extensively studied principles to date. Subsequent sections concentrate on piezoresistive SiC high-temperature pressure sensors, providing a systematic analysis of prevalent critical challenges in this domain.

Design methods of sensor chip

Figure 5 illustrates the typical structural configurations of three types of principle-based sensors, while Fig. 10 further elaborates on the structural design process of these sensors. For SiC pressure sensor chips based on capacitive and optical principles, SiC diaphragms function as structural deformation components, leveraging the mechanical stress-strain properties of SiC. In capacitive sensor chips, the operational principle relies on the change in capacitance resulting from the variation in distance between the upper and lower conductive SiC films. Optical sensing mechanisms exploit strain-induced phase modulation in high-fidelity optical fibers resulting from SiC thin-film deformation81. In piezoresistive SiC pressure transducers84, the diaphragm analogously functions as the core pressure-sensing element through mechanical deflection. However, the core mechanism is rooted in the piezoresistive effect of highly doped SiC on the surface, which arises from the semiconductor physical properties of SiC materials. This will be discussed in detail in “Piezoresistive effect of SiC”. It is evident that all three types of pressure sensor chips incorporate SiC-sensitive diaphragms. Consequently, in the design of pressure sensor chips, the structural design of the sensitive diaphragm is of critical importance, as it directly determines the sensor’s performance in terms of range and sensitivity.

Design methods of a sensitive diaphragm

Pressure sensors typically employ square, circular, and E-type diaphragms with mass blocks as sensitive diaphragms. Compared to square diaphragms, circular diaphragms exhibit more uniform force distribution and reduced stress concentration, resulting in a higher operational limit. Additionally, the inherent challenges in achieving anisotropic etching of SiC materials make circular diaphragms more feasible to fabricate than their square counterparts. The E-type structure with mass blocks is constrained by the limitations of dry etching processes, as the thick masks required for deep etching must be fabricated through electroplating. However, the electroplating process for E-type structures is notably more complex. Consequently, circular and square diaphragms are predominantly utilized in the structural design of SiC pressure sensors. The diaphragm in a piezoresistive pressure sensor serves as both a mechanical stress structure and the carrier for the sensitive element. The shape of the diaphragm directly influences the sensor fabrication process and sensitivity85. A circular diaphragm exhibits a symmetric and uniform stress distribution along its edges, which simplifies the design of the piezoresistor arrangement. This symmetry helps achieve better linear output and long-term stability, making circular diaphragms widely used in conventional and general-purpose pressure sensors. In contrast, while the stress distribution of a square diaphragm is more complex, its shape is often aligned with the chip’s edge in MEMS microfabrication, facilitating processes such as photolithography. However, the square diaphragm is more prone to stress concentration, which can negatively impact performance. Numerous studies have explored the effect of diaphragm shape on sensor performance86. For example, as displayed in Fig. 10a, research by Chen Wu et al. indicates that for diaphragms with the same side length (or diameter), square diaphragms exhibit higher stress at the edges, which corresponds to greater sensitivity84. Some studies have optimized the width-to-thickness ratio of square diaphragms to achieve higher precision within a specific range. Nevertheless, in practical applications, circular diaphragms are generally preferred due to their easier manufacturability. In the design of sensitive diaphragms, preliminary theoretical calculations are generally conducted by integrating plate and shell design theory from structural mechanics. According to the Timoshenko thin plate theory and Hooke’s law87,88, the compression deformation of the diaphragm can be calculated. These principles collectively guide the design methods to achieve optimal functionality. As algorithmic and machine‑learning techniques increasingly inform engineering design, diaphragms for pressure sensors can likewise be synthesized through AI‑assisted workflows. A topology‑optimization framework was proposed for piezoresistive pressure‑sensor diaphragms that recasts the governing equations as a three‑dimensional topology‑optimization problem with design‑dependent loads92. In general, the structural design of sensors is grounded in a well-established theoretical framework. By leveraging existing theory in combination with application-specific requirements, a wide range of innovative architectures can be realized, with sound theoretical design serving as a critical guarantee of device performance. As algorithmic and machine-learning techniques continue to mature, the multi-parameter structural design of sensors is expected to become increasingly streamlined and intelligent.

Piezoresistive effect of SiC

The piezoresistive effect serves as the foundational premise for the study of piezoresistive MEMS pressure sensors and constitutes a critical theoretical basis for the sensor’s sensitive mechanism and structural design95,96. In contrast to the well-established basis for the piezoresistive effect in Si, research on the SiC piezoresistive effect commenced later and is more complex due to the diverse range of crystal types, as well as the limited preliminary research foundation. The resistivity change in semiconductor materials is intrinsically linked to crystal orientation. Specifically, the piezoresistive effect exhibits pronounced anisotropy across different crystal planes and directions, which is determined by the relative relationship between the stress/strain direction and the field direction (e.g., voltage and current). As illustrated in Fig. 11a, when the strain direction of the semiconductor aligns parallel to the external electric field direction, the resulting piezoresistive is termed longitudinal piezoresistive. Figure 11b demonstrates that when these directions are perpendicular, the piezoresistive is referred to as transverse piezoresistive.

Definition of directions of different piezoresistive coefficients.

a Longitudinal piezoresistive. b Transverse piezoresistive. c Tangential piezoresistive

In the early stages of research on the piezoresistive effect in SiC, progress was constrained by limitations in the growth of hexagonal SiC single crystals. As a result, 3C-SiC, which shares the same cubic crystal structure as Si, attracted considerable attention and was extensively studied. For semiconductor Si, it has been well established that the magnitude of the piezoresistive effect varies significantly along different crystallographic orientations. A similar crystallographic dependence of the piezoresistive effect has also been observed in 3C-SiC. As a sphalerite-structured polymorph, 3C-SiC crystallizes in the Si-compatible cubic system and demonstrates historically significant piezoresistive behavior. For instance, the independent differences in the piezoresistive effect between N-type and P-type 3C-SiC across various crystal orientations (as illustrated in Fig. 12a, b) were explored97. Using the cantilever beam bending method, the longitudinal and transverse gauge factor (GF) values in the [100] and [110] crystal orientations were measured. In fact, for experimentally studying the SiC piezoresistive effect, there are two equivalent models, including the bending beam method and the deforming diaphragm method (test method in Fig. 12d). A series of comprehensive studies on the fabrication and test of 3C-SiC piezoresistive properties were conducted as illustrated in Fig. 12e, g98,99. These studies provided detailed values for the longitudinal piezoresistive coefficient, transverse piezoresistive coefficient, and shear piezoresistive coefficient for any crystal direction on the (100) crystal plane. The experimental results revealed that the piezoresistive effect of N-type 3C-SiC is most pronounced in the [100] direction44, whereas that of P-type 3C-SiC is most significant in the [110] direction91,99. When designing piezoresistive sensors, the piezoresistors should be aligned along these two crystal directions. This research has established a robust theoretical foundation for understanding the piezoresistive effect in 3C-SiC. In addition to fabricating 3C-SiC thin film piezoresistors on single-crystal Si substrates100, the feasibility of preparing 3C-SiC piezoresistors on SOI substrates using chemical vapor deposition (CVD) technology was demonstrated.

Pressure sensors based on the 3C-SiC piezoresistive effect typically utilize Si as the substrate, which inherently limits their high-temperature performance. Therefore, with advancements in SiC single-crystal growth technology, the piezoresistive effects of hexagonal wurtzite α-SiC, particularly 4H-SiC and 6H-SiC, have begun to be extensively studied and applied. An experimental study on N-type 4H-SiC has been conducted101, characterizing millimeter-sized 4H-SiC rectangular blocks at room temperature. They reported maximum lateral and longitudinal GF values of 20.8 and -10, respectively, for high-doping concentration N-type 4H-SiC. Additionally, they explored the relationship between the piezoresistive strain coefficient and the dimensions of the piezoresistors. Subsequently, the piezoresistive coefficients of N-type 4H-SiC across varying geometric dimensions were investigated by Xudong Fang et al.65. They fabricated piezoresistors with lengths ranging from 200 μm to 400 μm and widths from 10 μm to 30 μm (the piezoresistive test setup in Fig. 12i), providing evidence that the transverse piezoresistive coefficient of N-type SiC exceeds its longitudinal counterpart.

In addition to N-type doped 4H-SiC, the piezoresistive effect of P-type doped SiC has also been studied. Theoretical and experimental investigations were conducted into the piezoresistive coefficient of P-type 4H-SiC with a doping concentration of 1 × 1018cm−3 in the (0001) crystal direction102,103. Results demonstrated that the longitudinal and transverse gauge factor (GF) values at room temperature were 31.5 and −27.3, respectively. Notably, P-type 4H-SiC retained a high GF value even at elevated temperatures, thereby confirming the suitability of the 4H-SiC piezoresistive effect for high-temperature applications104. Similarly, the highly doped P-type 4H-SiC was experimentally investigated105, determining the longitudinal and transverse GF values to be 26.7 and −21.5, respectively (test methods in Fig. 12f). This research on the SiC piezoresistive effect primarily focuses on the experimental fabrication of piezoresistors and the determination of gauge factor (GF) values. Nevertheless, the semiconductor physical properties underlying the SiC piezoresistive effect were explored from an electronic structure perspective, employing theoretical physics calculations to provide a mechanistic explanation of the phenomenon. For instance, the mathematical model was investigated to describe the influence of intrinsic material properties, such as electron migration and valley scattering, on resistance changes under pressure, thereby elucidating the formation mechanism of the N-type cubic SiC piezoresistive effect106. Subsequently, Takaya Sugiura et al. further refined the fundamental expression of the hexagonal SiC piezoresistive coefficient through experimental studies107. The same team advanced this work by utilizing finite element numerical simulation methods to calculate the piezoresistive coefficient of the p-type 4H-SiC (0001) crystal plane108, obtaining a theoretical value for the GF. The theoretical research on the piezoresistive effect in SiC primarily falls within the domain of semiconductor physics. These work aids researchers in understanding the essential mechanisms of the piezoresistive effect, while also providing a theoretical foundation for doping experiments aimed at regulating the piezoresistive coefficient.

Recent years, significant progress has been made in the study of the piezoresistive effect in SiC, particularly with regard to 3C-SiC epitaxial films and 4H/6H bulk materials. Research has advanced from the empirical validation of the effect to the extraction of tensor-level parameters, demonstrating superior sensitivity and stability compared to Si in pressure, strain, acceleration, and flow MEMS sensors operating in high-temperature, high-radiation, and corrosive environments. The main advancements have focused on the anisotropy analysis dependent on crystal structure and doping. Overall, understanding the piezoresistive effect is crucial for designing high-sensitivity SiC piezoresistive sensors. From 3C-SiC epitaxial films to 4H and 6H bulk materials, the current research has moved from empirical observation to theoretical derivation, showing improved sensitivity and stability in MEMS sensors operating in high-temperature, extreme environments. However, research on piezoresistive strain coefficients still predominantly relies on experimental data. Studies have shown that doping significantly impacts the piezoresistive effect, with higher doping concentrations leading to lower piezoresistive strain coefficients. The stability, uniformity, and reproducibility of the doping process also influence the performance of piezoresistive devices. This is especially problematic in high-temperature environments, where the non-linear changes in piezoresistive properties due to the random motion of charge carriers severely affect the performance of wide-temperature-range devices, such as pressure sensors. Therefore, more research is needed to enhance the piezoresistive strain coefficient and reduce the temperature dependence of the piezoresistive effect.

Micro-manufacturing of SiC

The rapid bulk etching of SiC for sensitive diaphragm fabrication presents a significant challenge across piezoresistive, capacitive, and fiber optic pressure sensor technologies. While sensors based on these principles universally require high-precision diaphragms, current manufacturing methods face substantial limitations due to the dual nature of SiC material properties. The exceptional hardness, corrosion resistance, and mechanical-chemical stability of SiC substrates, while advantageous for sensor durability, paradoxically hinder their processing. Specifically, the high bonding energy (~4.6 eV per atom) and chemical inertness of Si-C bonds, which enable superior survival performance in harsh environments, simultaneously create substantial obstacles in device processing and etching109,110. For MEMS fabrication, the ideal etching process must simultaneously achieve four critical parameters: high speed, excellent anisotropy, superior surface quality, and minimal collateral damage to non-target areas.

As illustrated in Fig. 13, current SiC processing techniques primarily encompass molten salt etching, electrochemical etching, laser ablation, ultrasonic machining, and plasma etching. Among these, molten salt etching demonstrates anisotropic characteristics(the typical etching results and reaction in Fig. 13a), yet its application is constrained by the requirement for alkali-resistant masks and suboptimal etching quality111–115, rendering it unsuitable for fabricating SiC micro/nano-device structures. Electrochemical etching, initially developed for SiC pressure sensor diaphragm fabrication (in Fig. 13b), employs voltage-induced electrochemical reactions between HF and SiC through doped carriers116–118. While exhibiting faster etching rates than molten salt methods, this technique is limited to processing conductive doped SiC119,120. Benefiting from established Si etching technologies121,122, plasma dry etching has gained widespread adoption for SiC processing due to its superior etching quality and precise dimensional control capabilities.

The predominant dry etching techniques for SiC primarily employ Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP) and Reactive Ion Etching (RIE) methodologies122,123. While Si-based dry etching processes have reached technological maturity, researchers have extensively investigated SiC etching parameters to optimize etching quality and device performance. Key parameters under study include etching gas composition123–126, mask layer materials127,128 and plasma power configurations129. Plasma dry etching operates through synergistic physical bombardment and chemical reaction mechanisms130 (see the etching principle in Fig. 13c). This process requires two fundamental conditions: (1) plasma-SiC chemical reactivity (initiation condition) and (2) volatile reaction byproducts (duration condition). Although offering anisotropic etching capability, this method necessitates metal mask barrier layers and exhibits limitations in etching quality, particularly concerning micromasking effects. Current technological advancements have achieved average etching rates of ~150 nm/min125,131. Currently, most SiC-based MEMS devices are fabricated through deposition of SiC thin films on Si or other readily etchable substrates. Direct etching techniques for bulk SiC substrates remain underdeveloped, primarily due to the material's exceptional hardness, thermal stability, and chemical inertness, which pose significant etching challenges. Conventional MEMS fabrication methodologies typically employ masking techniques, crystallographic orientation-dependent wet etching, or reactive ion etching processes. While deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) has emerged as a prevalent technique for SiC processing and is extensively utilized in semiconductor device manufacturing, it necessitates high-selectivity metal hard masks and is susceptible to micro-trenching effects. The practical etching rate for this process generally remains below 2 μm/min. Consequently, for applications requiring extensive material removal in deep etching processes, this method demonstrates limited efficiency, elevated costs, and inadequate suitability for fabricating micro-scale features such as holes, grooves, or intricate three-dimensional microstructures.

In recent years, femtosecond laser processing has emerged as a transformative technique for machining challenging materials132, including high-hardness metals133, dielectrics134, inorganic compounds135, and semiconductors, particularly SiC-based devices. A comparative schematic demonstrates the fundamental distinctions between femtosecond laser processing and conventional pulse laser techniques136 (as illustrated in Fig. 13d). The unique non-thermal interaction mechanism of femtosecond lasers enables nearly complete heat dissipation through high-temperature plasma jets during the ultra-short pulse duration, achieving true “cold processing” that eliminates thermal-induced melting phenomena and significantly enhances machining precision. The exceptional capabilities of femtosecond lasers in ablating superhard materials like SiC stem from their high efficiency, minimal thermal damage, superior resolution, maskless operation, and material independence. These advantages have positioned femtosecond lasers as a viable alternative to reactive ion dry etching. Extensive research and experimental studies have validated their application across various domains, including: Micro-clip fabrication137,138, Pressure sensor and accelerometer cantilever beam etching139–142, SiC substrate backside cavity etching121,132,143–146, Interconnect via formation147, Surface microgroove and microchannel creation148,149, SiC thin film etching on Si-based MEMS devices, complex feature component processing. These applications demonstrate the versatility of femtosecond laser processing in manufacturing advanced /nano devices, offering unparalleled precision and material flexibility.

When interacting with hard and brittle materials such as SiC, femtosecond laser processing effectively suppresses thermal diffusion, enabling a “cold machining” regime that minimizes both the heat-affected zone and the formation of microcracks138. This capability is essential for fabricating SiC-based accelerometer chips with high reliability and performance. By precisely controlling the energy density and scanning trajectory, femtosecond lasers can produce high-quality microgrooves, micropores, and complex cavities on SiC wafers. These finely engineered structures serve as the fundamental components of the accelerometer such as flexural elements, proof mass, and capacitive gaps. For instance, ultraviolet laser cutting was applied for releasing SiC accelerometer cantilever beams (the fabricated sensor chip in Fig. 14a). Through optimization of pulse repetition frequency and processing parameters, they achieved superior surface morphology with minimized thermal effects139. The cantilever beam etching process is completed within 2 seconds, showing significant improvement in etching rates. Likewise, femtosecond laser processing successfully fabricated accelerometer-sensitive beams and proof masses (in Fig. 14c). Their approach, involving optimized scanning paths, effectively eliminated crack propagation during laser etching while maintaining excellent dimensional accuracy and structural consistency141. Beyond accelerometer fabrication, femtosecond laser ablation has also been effectively applied to pressure sensor diaphragm preparation.

Given its relatively simpler structural requirements, the fabrication of SiC pressure sensor diaphragms has so far received more attention and yielded more results with femtosecond laser micromachining than that of accelerometers. Notably, through systematic parameter optimization and modulation, precise control over diaphragm thickness, spatial resolution, and surface quality was achieved, establishing a foundation for subsequent research in this field. The application guarantees the high etching rate and “non-thermal” ablation characteristics of femtosecond lasers for SiC pressure sensor diaphragm fabrication142,143. In addition, hybrid approaches that integrate laser micromachining with other fabrication techniques have also been explored for the processing of pressure sensors. For example, an innovative hybrid processing technique combining femtosecond laser ablation with DRIE was developed to fabricate pyramid/staircase structures on SiC substrates121. This approach effectively addressed the challenge of high-aspect-ratio structure etching, where conventional methods often suffer from sidewall deposition, laser beam defocusing, and premature etching termination. The hybrid technique significantly enhanced processing efficiency while achieving a superior surface finish. Similarly, femtosecond laser deep etching of 4H-SiC substrates can complete the fabrication of a pressure sensor diaphragm132 and blind holes, measuring 1200 μm in diameter and 270 μm in depth within 350 μm-thick SiC, resulting in an 80 μm-thick sensitive diaphragm with exceptional dimensional accuracy (≤2% size error) and surface quality (average roughness: 320 nm). Researchers, including Chen Wu and Lukang Wang from Xi’an Jiaotong University, advanced the field through developing a plasma etching-assisted femtosecond laser composite microfabrication method132,145,146,150. This technique enabled high-quality, high-rate etching of SiC microstructures and batch production of sensitive diaphragms for SiC piezoresistive pressure sensors. These collective studies have conclusively demonstrated that femtosecond laser processing offers both high etching rates and excellent processing quality in SiC deep etching, highlighting its significant potential for precision machining of SiC components and MEMS sensor manufacturing applications.

Except for the application of femtosecond lasers in SiC device fabrication, ultrafast lasers are also capable of processing microgrooves, high aspect ratio micropores, and 3D microchannels in hard, transparent materials. These structures have been widely applied in fields such as microfluidic devices and 3D packaging. Currently, for hard and brittle materials like SiC, there are two main processing approaches: laser-induced material modification followed by chemical wet etching, and direct laser ablation. The laser-assisted wet etching method targets materials that are transparent at specific laser wavelengths. It leverages the contrast in etching rates before and after laser-induced modification to create microstructures. SiC, known for its excellent chemical stability, is not easily corroded by acids at room temperature. However, its bandgap corresponds to photon energies around 400 nm, meaning that SiC is transparent to lasers with wavelengths longer than 400 nm. When exposed to femtosecond laser irradiation, the crystalline structure of SiC can be altered, breaking and rearranging chemical bonds, capturing oxygen from the air, and forming silicon oxides. These oxides can then be etched away using a mixture of hydrofluoric acid and nitric acid.

As exhibited in Fig. 15, 800 nm femtosecond lasers assist chemical wet etching successfully fabricated through-holes with diameters of ~40 μm and aspect ratios of ~10:1 on 6H-SiC147, as well as deep gratings with aspect ratios up to ~25:1. This laser modification combined with chemical etching method can produce micropores or microgrooves with smooth sidewalls, clear boundaries, sharp verticality, and high aspect ratios. Likewise, 515 nm femtosecond laser pulses in a multi-pulse percussion mode were used to drill through 500 μm-thick 4H-SiC crystals (the processed through holes in Fig. 15b)151, achieving vias with aspect ratios over 20 and taper angles <1°. This was the highest aspect ratio and smallest taper achieved under ambient conditions using direct femtosecond laser ablation. However, the process suffers from long etching times, low efficiency, and inconsistent cross-sectional dimensions due to uneven etchant distribution inside deep structures. In contrast, direct laser ablation utilizes mechanisms like micro-explosions, melting, and vaporization to directly create micropores and microchannels through techniques such as single-pulse ablation, multi-pulse percussion processing, and path scanning. The study examined the material removal mechanism, evolution of the heat-affected zone, and methods to control it. For instance, mathematical models were constructed to correlate microgroove depth and surface roughness with laser power, repetition rate, and scanning speed, effectively predicting how laser parameters and their interactions influence microgroove geometry and morphology148. Moreover, the planetary gear components can be fabricated directly on bulk 4H-SiC152, showing that fine structures like vias, channels, steps, and gears could be efficiently and inexpensively etched by optimizing laser line spacing, scanning speed, and number of passes-completing a 4.5 mm diameter central gear in just 15 minutes. Although femtosecond laser has the advantage of ultrashort pulse and claims to have no damage to the processed material outside the processing region, it turns out that issues like poor circularity, surface chipping, and recast layers due to thermal damage persisted.

Furthermore, much of the research has focused on ablation experiments and the characterization of processing outcomes, with limited attention given to analyzing the underlying mechanisms of femtosecond laser irradiation in SiC material removal. It is reasonable to believe that further theoretical advancements will play a key role in overcoming this technical challenge.

SiC ohmic contact

The advent of SiC and its subsequent application in high-temperature sensor devices has effectively addressed the critical material selection challenge for bulk-material-based high-temperature sensors. Nevertheless, measurement methodologies in high-temperature and harsh environments present significantly greater complexities compared to room-temperature applications, as all detection device technologies must incorporate stringent high-temperature resistance considerations. From a material perspective, SiC exhibits superior thermal stability and environmental resilience compared to Si, making it inherently suitable for extreme conditions. However, the high-temperature performance of SiC-based electronic devices is not solely determined by the intrinsic properties of SiC but is also constrained by peripheral processes and packaging factors. Extensive analysis and testing have revealed that performance degradation of ohmic contacts under high-temperature and high-power conditions can precipitate complete sensor device failure156. Figure 16a, b illustrate the ohmic contact architectures in piezoresistive and capacitive SiC high-temperature pressure sensor chips, respectively. Structurally, the ohmic contact, formed through metal layer deposition, constitutes an integral component of the chip. Functionally, it facilitates electrical signal transmission across the chip. Technologically, the fabrication of the ohmic contact metal layer represents a critical step in chip manufacturing. Consequently, the high-temperature stability of ohmic contacts emerges as a pivotal factor determining the overall performance of SiC-based high-temperature pressure sensors.

Theoretical model of SiC ohmic contact and metal selection

Metal-semiconductor contact refers to a crucial component formed by the intimate junction between a metal thin film and a semiconductor substrate, which facilitates the transmission of electrical signals from the device to external circuits157. In practical applications, depending on the material properties, metal-semiconductor contacts can exhibit two distinct behaviors: ohmic contact, characterized by a linear current-voltage relationship, and Schottky contact, which demonstrates rectifying properties. Specifically, Schottky contacts allow current flow only under a voltage bias in one direction. In contrast, under ideal conditions, ohmic contacts on sensors enable bidirectional charge carrier transport at the interface, regardless of whether a forward or reverse voltage is applied. The I-V characteristics show a constant resistance behavior, where lower resistance values are preferred. Taking N-type 4H-SiC as an example, Fig. 17a, b illustrates the energy band diagrams of the metal prior to contact with 4H-SiC. The flow of carriers at the interface after contact is determined by the relative positions of the Fermi levels of the metal and the semiconductor. The electron affinity-work function offset at semiconductor-metal interfaces generates fundamentally distinct electrical contact configurations.

As shown in Fig. 17a, when the metal and semiconductor are in contact equilibrium, their Fermi levels align. When the metals' work function and the semiconductors electron affinity satisfy the specific relationship qϕmå qχs158, the energy band in the contact region bends upward, creating a higher potential barrier at the metal-semiconductor interface, known as the Schottky barrier. The height of this barrier determines the electrical behavior of the contact, whether ohmic or Schottky. Under ideal conditions, the Schottky barrier height (SBH) at the interface is defined by Eq. (1)159, which physically represents the energy required for electrons to transition from the metal into the semiconductor.

When the metal’s work function qϕm is smaller than the semiconductor’s electron affinity qχs(as demonstrated in Fig. 17b), electrons transfer from the metal to the semiconductor at the contact interface, resulting in ohmic contact characteristics between the metal and the semiconductor.

Studies indicate that the electron affinity of 4H-SiC is ~3.6 eV161,162, while most metals exhibit work function values exceeding 4 eV163, making it nearly impossible to satisfy the conditions for direct contact. This highlights the critical importance of selecting an appropriate contact layer metal for the formation of SiC ohmic contacts. Currently, multi-layer thin films combining elemental metals and alloy compounds are widely employed to fabricate SiC ohmic contacts. Common materials include Ni164,165, Ti166, Al167, TiN166, TiW168, NiCr169, W170, WC171, TiAl172, TiC173, Ta174,175, TaSi265,120, Pt176,177, and other metals and alloys. The primary material utilized in the all-SiC piezoresistive high-temperature pressure sensor is N-type SiC, with Ni-based metals serving as the main ohmic contact. For N-type SiC, ohmic contact is typically achieved through a Ni-based metal contact layer combined with an alloy film, which is metallized and annealed at 950 °C–1050 °C. The specific contact resistance usually ranges between 10⁻⁵ Ω cm²–10⁻⁶ Ω cm². However, the mechanism by which Ni readily forms ohmic contact with N-type SiC remains under investigation. Current studies primarily rely on experimental methods, where ohmic contact is first achieved, followed by microscopic analysis to determine its formation mechanisms. Nevertheless, the exact mechanism remains controversial, with two predominant theories. (1) Ni₂Si as the interfacial intermediate layer: E. Kurimoto et al. used Raman spectroscopy to demonstrate that Ni reacts with SiC to form Ni₂Si, NiSi, and NiSi₂ at temperatures below 500 °C. However, ohmic contact is only formed when the annealing temperature exceeds 950 °C, with Ni₂Si being the dominant component. Thus, it is inferred that Ni₂Si is crucial for ohmic contact formation178. (2) Carbon vacancies significantly potentiate carrier tunneling mechanisms. Contrary to the conventional attribution to Ni₂Si dominance, Nikitina et al. postulated that carbon out-diffusion generates substantial vacancy concentrations at the SiC interface. This vacancy-mediated process reduces the Schottky barrier thickness, thereby facilitating ohmic contact establishment. This conclusion was supported by experiments with other Ni-based formulations164. Additionally, some scholars argue that a low vacancy concentration cannot induce tunneling effects179. Instead, they suggest that a transition layer, such as 3C-SiC, plays a decisive role in the formation of 4H-SiC ohmic contacts177,180.

Effect of doping on ohmic contact

Due to the maturity of single-crystal growth technology and its wider bandgap, 4H-SiC is considered to offer superior device manufacturing performance and potential compared to 6H-SiC and 3C-SiC. Existing SiC devices are typically fabricated on doped epitaxial layers, as illustrated in Fig. 14c, d. The structure of 4H-SiC devices generally consists of a substrate layer, an epitaxial layer, and a metal connection layer. Single-crystal substrates are produced through seed crystal growth, drawing, slicing, grinding, and polishing to obtain single-crystal wafer substrates186, followed by the preparation of epitaxial layers. For Si epitaxy, it is generally achieved through high-energy ion implantation and high-temperature annealing. However, due to the high energy of Si-C bonds, ion implantation is challenging for 4H-SiC substrates. Currently, the most common method involves introducing substances containing specific elements into a high-temperature furnace to perform secondary crystal growth on the surface of the single-crystal substrate, forming a doped epitaxial layer with micrometer-scale thickness. In 4H-SiC doping processes123,187, as demonstrated in Fig. 16e, nitrogen incorporation generates N-type epitaxial layers via substitutional doping, whereas aluminum implantation produces P-type epitaxial structures188. Precise modulation of dopant flux and growth duration enables fabrication of SiC epitaxial layers with tailored carrier concentrations and thicknesses for semiconductor device integration.

An essential parameter for forming an ohmic contact is the doping concentration of the topmost epitaxial layer in contact with the metal thin film. The effect of annealing process on ohmic contact

The electrical properties between deposited metal and SiC substrates often exhibit Schottky contact characteristics. In the preparation of ohmic contacts, high-temperature annealing of the contact sample after metal deposition can significantly enhance the formation rate of ohmic contacts189. High-temperature annealing is a critical factor influencing the transformation of electrical properties at the contact interface. Currently, research on high-temperature annealing for ohmic contacts primarily focuses on experimental studies and surface microstructural testing of factors such as annealing temperature, time, and atmosphere. However, there is relatively limited research on the intrinsic mechanisms by which high-temperature thermal annealing promotes the formation of ohmic contacts.

There are two annealing methods for SiC ohmic contacts in high-temperature furnaces190. One is conventional annealing, with a heating rate of approximately 15 °C/min, and the other is rapid thermal annealing, with a heating rate of about 2000 °C/min. The key difference between the two methods lies in the heating rate. Conventional annealing, with its slower heating rate, typically does not exceed a target temperature of 700 °C, and the holding time can extend to 30 minutes or even several hours. In contrast, rapid thermal annealing can exceed 1000 °C, with the holding time generally limited to 5 minutes. The annealing atmosphere is primarily nitrogen, followed by vacuum and argon atmospheres191. Research on high-temperature annealing primarily involves adjusting the annealing temperature and holding time for prepared contact samples to achieve electrode surfaces with lower specific contact resistance values, smoother morphology, and minimal structural damage. Due to the rearrangement of atoms or molecules, research has demonstrated that high-temperature annealing promotes ohmic contact formation through two primary mechanisms. Firstly, a microphysical gap exists between the deposited metal electrode thin film and the SiC substrate, which accelerates the movement of interface atoms or molecules at elevated temperatures192. This results in atomic lattice rearrangement, reducing lattice mismatch, minimizing interface states, and enhancing electron transport efficiency. Secondly, atomic molecules mobilized at high temperatures undergo alloying, forming compounds with lower Schottky barrier heights at the interface177,181,193 thereby reducing contact resistance. These changes are influenced by factors such as electrode type, annealing temperature, and duration. Although high-temperature annealing facilitates ohmic contact formation, fatigue aging experiments reveal that differences in thermal expansion coefficients between SiC and metal layers, coupled with carrier transport and transfer phenomena, induce multi-field coupling (temperature, force, and electric fields) during prolonged high-temperature annealing194. This coupling process leads to surface defects on the electrode, such as increased metal atomic spacing, surface wrinkles, and clusters. Over time, these defects accelerate ohmic contact failure, manifesting as metal layer warping, cracking, or debonding, ultimately reducing the high-temperature operational lifespan of ohmic contacts. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed laser ultra-short pulse annealing as an alternative method109,180,190,195,196. Experimental results indicate that laser annealing leverages the short-term high energy of laser pulses197 to achieve ultra-fast high-temperature annealing while minimizing surface defects. However, due to the convenience of equipment and process integration, high-temperature furnace thermal annealing remains the predominant method for ohmic contact fabrication.

Failure analysis of ohmic contact

Failure mechanism and factors

Failure mechanism and factors

The failure of SiC ohmic contacts at high temperatures primarily stems from three factors: metal interlayer diffusion, physical damage such as film clustering, and the intrusion of oxygen atoms. Among these, metal interlayer diffusion occurs first and represents the primary microscopic behavior between contact atoms. Diffusion causes the positions of atoms initially separated by a certain distance to shift, enabling different atoms to combine and form new compounds. The impact of these compounds on contact performance can be either positive or negative. Simultaneously, under high temperatures, the metal film undergoes macroscopic deformation and physical defects due to thermal stress, primarily manifesting as film cracking and clustering. These physical defects provide pathways for the intrusion of external oxygen atoms, leading to oxidation of the contact interface. To select metals for SiC ohmic contacts, it is essential to ensure that they form thermodynamically stable phases at high temperatures and react with SiC to produce thermodynamically stable silicides and/or carbides. Additionally, high-temperature ohmic contacts must exhibit high oxidation resistance. To meet these requirements, the contact layer is typically composed of multiple metal layers, each serving distinct functions. If any of these layers is improperly selected, it may lead to thermodynamic instability, compromising high-temperature stability. Table 5 summarizes the processes and performance of SiC ohmic contacts prepared using multi-layer metal alloy combinations. In these configurations, the contact layer typically consists of Ni or Ti, while the intermediate barrier layer includes silicides or carbides formed by Ti, Ta, and W elements. Due to their high-temperature stability and high melting points, Pt elements are commonly used for the top anti-oxidation protection layer. The temperature resilience of SiC ohmic contacts was markedly enhanced through the integration of a barrier interlayer and an encapsulation layer. According to published reports, the highest temperature achieved for SiC ohmic contacts in sensors is 800 °C, as demonstrated by R. S. Okejie and colleagues at NASA. By incorporating a TaSi2 intermediate layer, they effectively improved the sensor’s high-temperature performance.

The fundamental characteristics of SiC ohmic contacts are primarily manifested in three key aspects: firstly, compared to Si, SiC possesses a wider bandgap and higher electron affinity, which theoretically makes it challenging to identify suitable metals that can readily form ohmic contacts with SiC. Secondly, while conventional temperature sensors offer a broader selection of contact metals due to the absence of high-temperature operational requirements, SiC-based sensors are specifically designed for high-temperature applications. This operational constraint necessitates the selection of contact metals with superior high-temperature stability, thereby significantly limiting the range of viable metal options. Furthermore, the high-temperature operational environment demands more sophisticated metal stack designs in SiC ohmic contact technology. Consequently, the selection of metals for SiC ohmic contacts must satisfy multiple criteria: thermal stress compatibility at elevated temperatures, the ability to form thermally stable silicides or carbides through reaction with SiC, and exceptional oxidation resistance to ensure reliable high-temperature performance.

(2)Failure analysis methods

Failure analysis methods

Currently, the research on ohmic contacts predominantly centers on the experimental fabrication of ohmic contact samples and their macro-to-microscale analysis utilizing advanced instrumentation. Figure 18 provides a comprehensive summary of the commonly employed research and characterization methodologies in this field. From a macroscopic perspective, the electrical properties of ohmic contacts are initially assessed. The most widely adopted technique is the Transmission Line Method (TLM)198, which is used to measure the current-voltage (I-V) characteristics between electrodes, ascertain whether they exhibit linear ohmic behavior, and calculate the specific contact resistance. Common variants of the TLM include the rectangular TLM, the dot TLM, and the ring TLM, with the first two being the most frequently utilized.

Typical performance characterization and evaluation system of metal and 4H-SiC ohmic contacts

The rectangular TLM was originally introduced by Shockley199 and subsequently refined by Berger. In this approach, a constant current is applied, and the voltage across each rectangular electrode is measured to determine the individual electrode resistances. Using a corresponding mathematical model, parasitic resistances are systematically eliminated through reasonable approximations and assumptions, ultimately yielding the contact resistivity. Nevertheless, due to inherent limitations in the lithography process during electrode fabrication, parasitic resistances are inevitably introduced between the substrate and the electrodes. Furthermore, the rectangular TLM necessitates substrate isolation etching, and the preparation of rectangular electrodes is technically complex. Despite these challenges, the method’s primary advantage lies in its straightforward calculation process, making it the most extensively used technique for investigating the electrical properties of ohmic contacts. To address these limitations, Marlow et al. proposed an alternative approach known as the circular transmission line model, which employs circular electrodes in place of rectangular ones200. This method eliminates the need for substrate etching and simplifies data processing, leading to its widespread adoption in the field.

In addition to evaluating the electrical properties of ohmic contacts, microscopic analysis methods are essential for investigating the underlying mechanisms behind phenomena such as high-temperature annealing, fatigue aging, and performance failure. Figure 18 summarizes the microscopic characterization techniques commonly employed by researchers. Following annealing and high-temperature aging processes, electrode films often exhibit various morphological defects, including cracking, bubbling, and clustering. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) is widely used to observe these surface microstructures. Furthermore, atomic force microscopy (AFM) is utilized to measure surface roughness and quantitatively assess the quality of electrode thin films under varying deposition and annealing conditions. Beyond surface-level analysis, understanding the three-dimensional diffusion of elements is crucial for studying the microscopic dynamic behavior between different metal layers. Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) is frequently employed to quantitatively evaluate the depth and distribution characteristics of elemental interdiffusion along the electrode’s depth profile, thereby elucidating diffusion behavior in the direction of thin film deposition. To further investigate the chemical alloying behavior of electrode thin films, X-ray diffraction (XRD) is used to identify the characteristic peaks of alloy compounds and analyze the formation of new phases. For nanoscale interfacial alloying phenomena, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with focused ion beam (FIB) technology provides atomic-scale resolution, enabling qualitative observation of interfacial alloying with superior microscopic characterization capabilities. Additionally, the mechanical properties of ohmic contact electrodes, such as deposition hardness, can be measured using nanoindentation techniques. By integrating the above methods, a comprehensive evaluation of ohmic contact characteristics and failure mechanisms can be achieved. According to the literature, these experimental and analytical techniques not only enhance the understanding of failure modes but also provide a robust theoretical foundation for implementing preventive measures against ohmic contact failure.

Advances in computational materials science and computer processing power have made first-principles calculations an effective research methodology for studying interfacial reactions between SiC and other substances. The first-principles calculations can be employed to characterize the atomic configuration, charge redistribution, bonding nature, and electronic structure at the 4H-SiC(0001)/TiN(111) heterojunction(the charge density differences and work function results in Fig. 19a). Analysis revealed that strong interfacial charge polarization-electronic state coupling induces a Schottky barrier reduction to 0.03 eV, enabling substantial enhancement in quantum transport efficiency201. Likewise, through density functional theory (DFT) computations, the interfacial stability, energy landscape, adhesion properties, and bonding characteristics of the SiC(111)/W(110) heterostructure were investigated systematically(the atoms stacking sequences and interface charge density in Fig. 19b). Computational results demonstrated the formation of an atomic interdiffusion zone at the interface, primarily composed of tungsten silicide and carbide phases202. To improve the stability of SiC ohmic contact, MoS2 was used as an intermediate layer for ohmic contact between different metals and SiC. The first-principles calculation method is applied to calculate the MoS2 intercalation on the interface properties of metal/SiC system, verifying that the MoS2 intermediate layer helps the conversion from Schottky contact to ohmic contact203. Additionally, the interface properties of various metals (magnesium, silver, aluminum, gold, etc.) and 4H-SiC have been investigated through the calculation method204. Results clarified the interaction and charge transfer between SiC and metals (relaxed models for Ni/SiC(0001), Pt/SiC(0001) interfaces and Zr/SiC(0001) are shown in Fig. 19d). The results indicate that there is an approximate linear relationship between the interface Schottky barrier and work function of the metals. In addition to α-SiC, interfacial behaviors between β-SiC and compounds have also been investigated. For example, four BN contact models were established using the Si terminal and C terminal of SiC as contact surfaces205. They obtained the adhesion force, interface charge density, differential charge density and interface energy of the model interface using the first-principles calculation method, providing new research ideas for the contact between β-SiC(111) and BN(0001). Notably, the effect of graphite carbon on the performance of SiC/Cu and SiC/Ni contacts has been studied206,207. Modeling calculation results show that adding an intermediate layer of graphene to the metal and SiC contacts can effectively reduce the Schottky barrier height of the contact, thereby playing a positive role in the formation of SiC ohmic contacts (the charge density differences result in Fig. 19f). These researches demonstrated that employing first-principles calculation method to investigate SiC ohmic contacts offers interface work function, charge density distribution, charge difference density, state density and so on. These results demonstrated that it has been an effective means of reducing research costs and elucidating the underlying formation and failure mechanisms from a theoretical perspective. Nevertheless, the rationality and credibility of these computational results ultimately require experimental validation.

Extensive research has been conducted on ohmic contacts for SiC. However, current studies predominantly focus on the recombination of commonly used ohmic contact metals in certain SiC high-temperature devices. These investigations are largely confined to experimental exploration of recombined metal combinations, with limited theoretical or mechanistic insights. Furthermore, even for well-established metal combinations, such as Ni-based contacts for N-type SiC and Al/Ti-based contacts for P-type SiC, a coherent set of ohmic contact mechanisms and models remains elusive. Additionally, the resistance performance of these ohmic contacts and the long-term high-temperature stability of sensors incorporating them require further systematic evaluation. A series of critical issues have emerged in the study of ohmic contacts: (1) The lack of standardized scientific guidelines for key process parameters, such as the composition, thickness, alloying annealing conditions (time, temperature, and atmosphere), and other critical factors for each metal layer in ohmic contacts. (2) The inconsistency in specific contact resistance values obtained from identical ohmic contact schemes, coupled with poor process reproducibility. (3) The incomplete understanding of the underlying mechanisms and physical models governing ohmic contact formation, which has yet to reach a consensus within the scientific community. Current research on high-temperature ohmic contacts predominantly emphasizes further process optimization based on comparative experimental results, with limited attention to the theoretical and mechanistic aspects of ohmic contacts in conjunction with the intrinsic properties of metal materials. Even for widely adopted systems, such as the Ni-based ohmic contact system and the Al/Ti ohmic contact system, the mechanisms and models of their formation remain subjects of ongoing debate.

High-temperature pressure sensor packaging

Lead package

The packaging of pressure sensors can be categorized into two types based on the implementation of the wire bonding process: leaded packaging and leadless packaging. Among these, leaded packaging, which utilizes the wire bonding process, represents the simplest and most mature packaging method. Due to its straightforward structure and well-established technology, it has been widely adopted in various applications. For instance, Chuang Li et al. fabricated a piezoresistive SOI pressure sensor chip and engineered a 304 stainless steel packaging shell, as illustrated in Fig. 20a. To enhance sealing and insulation, the shell was filled with an adhesive composed of 60% epoxy resin, 30% quartz powder, and 10% dioctyl phthalate208. The stainless steel core and the fixing ring were joined via laser welding, while the sensor was connected to the aviation plug using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) aviation cables. This design significantly improved the long-term stability and reliability of the sensor, enabling it to withstand temperatures exceeding 200 °C. A packaging structure specifically tailored for capacitive pressure sensors was developed, as depicted in Fig. 20b. The sensor chip was mounted onto a custom-designed PCB using epoxy resin adhesive209. Following gold wire bonding of the sensor chip, the metal cover and Pcb were sealed with silicone rubber. Similarly, Fig. 20d demonstrates another leaded packaging approach, where the sensor chip is affixed to a Pcb serving as the adhesive substrate. A more conventional leaded packaging scheme for pressure sensors is shown in Fig. 20c. In this configuration, the back of the chip is anodically bonded to a glass substrate, while the front is connected to external components via gold wires. The glass substrate is subsequently bonded to a metal core using epoxy resin adhesive. To maximize the operating temperature of the lead package, a leaded packaging scheme was introduced by utilizing Kovar alloy cores65. The SiC piezoresistive pressure sensor chip was bonded to the core base using a dual-layer high-temperature adhesive redundant fixation method (the packaging results in Fig. 20e), enabling the sensor to achieve stable output at temperatures up to 350 °C. Similarly, a resonant pressure sensor chip was mounted onto a custom-designed Kovar base with epoxy resin, as shown in Fig. 20f. In this configuration, the sensitive diaphragm was precisely aligned to cover the pressure port when exposed to the measured medium210. Electrical signal transmission was accomplished through wire bonding, and a Kovar cover was installed atop the assembly to effectively isolate the sensor from moisture and particulate contamination.

Leaded packaging offers advantages such as technological maturity and structural simplicity. However, the leads are susceptible to breakage, detachment, necking, and eutectic failure in environments characterized by strong corrosion and vibration211,212. In high-temperature environments, gold wires exhibit significant grain growth and are prone to diffuse with the bonding pad or even the substrate, forming gold-Si eutectic compounds, which substantially degrade the mechanical strength of the gold wire213. To protect the front-end structure of the sensor chip and enhance its vibration resistance, researchers have proposed oil-filled isolation packaging11,214. Currently, most commercial Si-based pressure sensors employ an oil-filled diaphragm isolation packaging structure12. In this design, incompressible oil-based media are filled into the cavity between the diaphragm and the packaging shell. The dielectric oil serves to transfer the measured pressure load to the pressure-sensitive chip while safeguarding the sensitive resistor circuitry on the chip13. However, this oil-filled medium exhibits poor high-temperature resistance and retains the fluidity of a liquid. Consequently, it occupies considerable space during packaging, rendering it unsuitable for applications requiring compact dimensions, high-temperature operation, and high dynamic performance.

Leadless package

To continuously enhance the temperature resistance limit of pressure sensors, researchers have explored the use of wafers fabricated from various materials. Despite these efforts, the operational temperature range of pressure sensors remains below the desired threshold. This limitation primarily stems from the inability of existing packaging solutions to withstand high-temperature conditions. Although leaded packaging offers structural simplicity and technological maturity, the lead structure is highly susceptible to failure in environments characterized by high temperatures, strong vibrations, and significant mechanical impacts. Furthermore, an isolation packaging solution is essential to ensure the protection and reliability of the sensor chip. Leadless packaging represents a packaging technology that eliminates the need for the wire bonding process. This approach is adaptable to a variety of sensors and offers greater flexibility in packaging design. By avoiding wire bonding, the contact area between the pad and the conductive material at the electrical interface is significantly increased. As a result, leadless packaging exhibits enhanced tolerance to defects in high-temperature, high-vibration, and high-impact environments, thereby reducing the likelihood of failure. It enables higher temperature resistance, improved environmental adaptability, and a more compact package size. As a critical advancement in enhancing the temperature tolerance and operational performance of pressure sensors, leadless packaging holds considerable promise for applications in harsh environments. Consequently, it has emerged as a prominent focus of research in the field.

Europe countries and North America, particularly the United States, have pioneered research in leadless packaging for high-temperature pressure sensors, establishing a robust foundation in this area. As early as 2002, NASA’s Glenn Research Center, in collaboration with Kulite Semiconductor, proposed an innovative leadless packaging solution for 6H-SiC piezoresistive pressure sensors. This approach utilized a flip-chip packaging structure, wherein the sensor chip was affixed to a SiC substrate featuring a through-hole using a high-temperature-resistant glass paste215. After the through-hole was filled with metallized glass paste, the core terminal was inserted into the corresponding through-hole, and the substrate was secured to the core using the same high-temperature-resistant glass paste. Similar structures have also been proven to withstand high temperatures and require sealing, as illustrated in Fig. 20i proposed by Sun et al.216. This design enabled the sensor to operate reliably at temperatures up to 500 °C. The packaging structure developed in this study has provided a valuable reference for subsequent research on leadless packaging for pressure sensors. NASA217 introduced a leadless packaging solution for SiC piezoresistive pressure sensors, the structure of which is depicted in Fig. 20j. In this design, the sensor chip is directly mounted onto an AlN substrate using a direct chip attachment method, ensuring that the chip pads are in direct contact with the conductive traces embedded in the AlN substrate. A glass paste is filled into the narrow gap between the SiC sensor chip and the inner wall of the AlN substrate, which is subsequently sintered to establish a reference pressure within the cavity. Additionally, the AlN substrate is thermally isolated from the metal housing to prevent the transfer of thermal stress generated by the Kovar alloy to the SiC chip. Employing this architecture allows the sensor to maintain functionality at 600 °C with sustained stability.

Inspired by NASA leadless packaging structure, Liu et al. proposed a novel packaging design, as illustrated in Fig. 20k. This structure employs an AuSn metal interlayer bonding technique to affix the sensor chip onto a through-hole substrate composed of the same material as the chip218. Through-holes are filled with electroplated copper, and solder bumps are employed to establish interconnections with the die or circuit board. After optimizing the substrate structure via finite element analysis, the packaging design demonstrated operational capability in environments up to 300 °C. However, during subsequent device-level packaging, the low melting point of the bump material imposed significant limitations on its applicability in high-temperature environments. To solve this problem, an anodic bonding process was employed to affix Si-based pressure sensor chip onto a glass substrate featuring a through-hole219. The assembly was secured within the tube housing using high-temperature glass paste, with the through-hole subsequently filled with conductive adhesive to form an electrical connection between the chip pad and the housing pin, thereby enabling signal transmission. As illustrated in Fig. 20l, SiC was utilized as a substrate and implemented a leadless packaging structure for a SiC pressure sensor by sintering through-hole fillers (in Fig. 20m)220. The zero-point output of the packaged sensor was successfully tested at 600 °C, demonstrating its high-temperature capability. Notably, an innovative packaging solution was proposed for an SOI pressure sensor. This approach involved bonding a borosilicate glass wafer to an SOI wafer using an anodic bonding process, as depicted in Fig. 20n. Conductive silver paste was filled into the SOI wafer and sintered at high temperatures to avoid direct contact between the lead and the chip pad221. The fabricated device demonstrated reliable performance over 0–200 °C and 0–3.5 MPa, exhibiting robust impact resistance and thermal stability at elevated temperatures.

In the field of high-temperature packaging for piezoresistive pressure sensors, leadless packaging exhibits superior high-temperature performance and enhanced environmental adaptability in harsh conditions, demonstrating significant potential for broad application. Discussions and perspectives

Pressure sensors have emerged as the most widely adopted category among conventional MEMS sensors, holding the largest market share. There is a growing and urgent demand for high-temperature pressure sensors in extreme industrial and engineering environments. Although much progress has been made in SiC high temperature pressure sensors in extreme working conditions, the key problems and challenges are further discussed in this work, as well as future trends and potential solutions for environmental adaptability of SiC high-temperature pressure sensors in extreme working conditions testing, as illustrated in Fig. 21. Drawing upon the findings, we give the following discussions and perspectives to propel the continued advancements and implementation of sensing techniques for micro nano pressure sensors.

Summary of challenges and trends for SiC pressure sensors main technologies, including principles and structures，sensor manufacturing and packaging

Exploration of the sensing mechanism and sensitivity improvement

At the level of fundamental theory and design, prevailing methodologies and design rules largely extrapolate from Si‑based MEMS, offering limited guidance for truly innovative, structure‑optimized architectures. The nonlinear behavior of intrinsic parameters in anisotropic materials most notably the temperature dependence of the Young’s modulus of single‑crystal SiC at elevated temperatures remains insufficiently characterized. This gap leaves an inadequate theoretical basis for predicting structural deformation and thermally induced stress in chips operating under high‑temperature conditions. Going forward, research should prioritize the intrinsic properties of SiC itself, establishing temperature‑resolved constitutive models and quantifying variations in key material parameters. Such efforts will provide the foundation necessary for designing sensor chips with robust high‑temperature tolerance and adaptability.

Indeed, to advance the development of micro/nano sensors, researchers have consistently pursued breakthroughs from four key aspects: new materials, new structures, new principles, and new methodologies. As mentioned earlier, various high-temperature pressure sensors with different substrates and operating principles have been investigated and fabricated to enable pressure signal measurement in extreme environments. Examples include early semiconductor silicon substrates, later SOI substrates, as well as materials such as diamond thin films, SiC, and sapphire optical fibers. As a typical representative of third-generation semiconductor materials, SiC exhibits remarkable semiconductor characteristics and is regarded as one of the most promising sensor substrates. With advancements in single-crystal semiconductor technology, fourth-generation ultra-wide bandgap semiconductors, including diamond and aluminum nitride (AlN), have been developed and applied. In future research, exploring the potential of these novel semiconductor materials for extreme-environment pressure sensing is expected to yield immeasurable advancements in testing performance.

In addition to the application of new sensitive materials, the exploration of novel sensing mechanisms is also important. As discussed, the research progress and findings on the piezoresistive effect in SiC have been analyzed. Its unique isotropic properties within the (0001) crystallographic plane are particularly attractive. It is well-established that in conventional silicon semiconductors, the piezoresistive effect varies along different crystal orientations within specific planes, greatly limiting the design and fabrication of sensor chips. In contrast, the distinctive isotropic piezoresistive behavior of 4H-SiC offers vast possibilities in the sensor design process. In existing studies, however, the complexity of the hexagonal crystal system, compared to the cubic structure, has resulted in relatively limited research on the piezoresistive effect in hexagonal SiC, particularly in terms of systematic theoretical investigations. Regarding the piezoresistive effects of SiC with different crystal types and doping concentrations, current research primarily focuses on experimental design and quantitative analysis of piezoresistive coefficients. However, the intrinsic sensitivity mechanisms of piezoresistive effects have not been thoroughly explored, resulting in a lack of innovative and optimized structural designs in SiC-based piezoresistive pressure sensor chips. This represents a current shortcoming but also a significant opportunity. In the study of the piezoresistive effect in SiC materials, it is essential to return to the fundamentals of semiconductor physics and investigate the physical mechanisms underlying the phenomenon. By leveraging computational materials science methods and theoretical analysis, the physical origin of such distinctive piezoresistive behavior can be elucidated. Ultimately, this approach will enable experimental modulation of the piezoresistive effect through deliberate doping strategies.

Furthermore, in the process of sensor chip structural design, the current common approach involves a combination of theoretical modeling and simulation analysis. With the rapid advancement of machine learning and algorithm-based control, future structural design could integrate specific repetitive procedures with advanced technologies, thereby streamlining and algorithmizing the design workflow. Such integration is expected to significantly enhance design flexibility, improve device design efficiency, and reduce design complexity.

Study on thermal stability of SiC ohmic contacts

As discussed, Ohmic contact is a direct factor affecting the performance of pressure sensors in extreme environmental testing, and its importance is self-evident. Although the inherent properties of wide-bandgap semiconductors enable SiC devices to operate at high temperatures, the interfaces between different materials, whether device interconnects or package interconnects, typically undergo interactions such as chemical reactions and atomic diffusion at relatively high temperatures. The electrical failure caused by this is a common challenge faced by extreme environmental testing, whether it is pressure sensors, acceleration sensors, or power devices, including MOSFETs. The research on Ohmic contact mainly includes two aspects: theoretical research and experimental research. The existing research mainly focuses on experimental studies; however, experimental studies have process errors, low reproducibility, and high material and time costs. The previous experimental research on the mechanism of contact formation has yielded inconsistent results, which are not conducive to elucidating the mechanism of contact formation. Therefore, in subsequent research, new methods should be explored to predict experimental results in advance through calculation or simulation methods, and obtain some unobservable results in experiments. This is of great significance for the detailed study of the high-temperature characteristics and high-temperature failure of Ohmic contacts.

In addition, for extreme environmental pressure sensors, the low resistance and high temperature resistance of ohmic contacts are crucial. Firstly, the higher interface state is considered to be the reason for the nonlinear I–V characteristics. Therefore, in order to achieve low contact resistance, in addition to adjusting annealing parameters and substrate doping concentration, corresponding interface research should also be carried out, including studying the interface thermal stress mismatch phenomenon from a macroscopic perspective, as well as studying the diffusion and chemical reactions caused by irregular molecular thermal motion at the interface from a microscopic perspective. Secondly, the prerequisite for increasing the temperature is to clarify the failure factors of contact at high temperatures. According to the previous description, the failure of SiC ohmic contacts at high temperatures is mainly due to three factors: metal interlayer diffusion, physical damage such as thin film clusters, and invasion of oxygen atoms. Among them, metal interlayer diffusion occurs first, representing the main microscopic behavior between contacting atoms. Diffusion causes the positions of atoms initially separated by a certain distance to shift, allowing different atoms to combine and form new compounds. The impact of these compounds on contact performance can be positive or negative. At high temperatures, metal thin films undergo macroscopic deformation and physical defects due to thermal stress, mainly manifested as film cracking and aggregation. These physical defects provide a pathway for the invasion of external oxygen atoms, leading to oxidation at the contact interface. Therefore, in order to improve the high-temperature stability of electrical ohmic contacts, it is necessary to prevent the invasion of oxygen atoms by adding protective layers or changing metal combinations, ensuring the stability of contact electrical properties at high temperatures and delaying contact degradation and failure.

Advanced manufacturing methods for SiC sensor chip

In the realm of processes and manufacturing, significant challenges remain in the etching of SiC, compelling researchers to explore more sophisticated structures or alternative processing techniques. Although four processing methods have been mentioned, the most commonly used approach remains dry etching, as it yields superior surface morphology suitable for electronic device fabrication. For dry etching methods, the two most pressing issues to address are: first, the problem of masks for prolonged etching, and second, the processing of microstructures with high aspect ratios. Conventional photoresist masks cannot sustain long-duration etching, whereas metal masks offer more durable masking capabilities. However, the etching byproducts can contaminate the etching chamber to some extent, thereby imposing more stringent demands on the chamber’s byproduct exhaust capabilities. This is one of the reasons why etching equipment capable of processing SiC is notoriously expensive on the market. Additionally, for high-aspect-ratio microstructures, such as those required for through-silicon vias (TSVs) in flip-chip packaging, the achievable etch depth via dry etching is limited. Consequently, thinning the wafer is often employed to reduce the required etch depth. These limitations significantly hinder the diversification of SiC-based pressure sensors. Although the most widely used dry etching process can meet the manufacturing requirements for most SiC microstructures, the associated equipment costs are prohibitively high, and the etching cost is also expensive.

We have summarized the application of femtosecond laser processing in SiC sensor microstructure manufacturing, indicating that laser special processing is a promising method for SiC microstructure processing. However, the main challenge in the future lies in precise control of machining accuracy and reduction of subsurface damage outside the target machining area. Therefore, in the field of SiC processing technology, future efforts should focus on reducing manufacturing and etching costs, improving processing and etching rates, enhancing quality, and exploring new processing methods and their potential mechanisms. In addition, the processing methods of “femtosecond laser + x” should be explored and innovated in the future, such as “femtosecond laser + dry etching”, “femtosecond laser + surface modification”. New composite processing methods with good adaptability for different device processing requirements.

High temperature packaging challenges for pressure sensors

An unpackaged sensor chip is akin to an unprotected integrated circuit; it lacks the requisite robustness for deployment in real application conditions. This demonstrates that packaging is crucial for sensors, especially those designed for extreme environments. Testing in such environments first requires ensuring the performance of the sensor chip, followed by reliable packaging. Proper packaging can amplify the advantages of the sensor chip and maximize its testing capabilities, whereas improper packaging may adversely affect the chip’s test results. Currently, wire-bonded packaging used for pressure sensors is mainly suitable for temperatures below 200 °C, as structures such as gold wires and adhesives tend to fail at high temperatures. In recent years, leadless packaging, as the most prominent solution for high-temperature pressure sensors, has been extensively investigated. The challenges lie in the precise assembly of components and the selection of materials, involving multiple technical difficulties such as heterogeneous bonding of SiC, through-SiC via processing, and glass sintering processes. Furthermore, for high-temperature packaging, supporting materials, including high-temperature-resistant wires and sealants, they must also be selected with high-temperature conditions in mind. This indicates that the packaging and testing of sensors for extreme environments are not merely issues of packaging technology but also involve materials science. In future research, the challenges in leadless packaging technology will be gradually addressed. The significance of this technology lies not only in enabling high-temperature-resistant packaging but also, more practically, in facilitating the miniaturization and integration of sensor packages. At the system-level packaging stage, it is essential to advance the co-integration with high-temperature electronics. By employing low-stress packaging techniques together with accurate thermo-mechanical coupling models, stability can be engineered from the sensitive unit to the overall system. This approach enables devices to achieve enhanced sensitivity, repeatability, and operational lifetime when subjected to high-temperature, corrosive, and high-vibration environments.

Adaptability of SiC pressure sensors in extreme environments

We should clarify that the research and development of sensors are aimed at practical applications. Therefore, the ultimate goal is to promote laboratory-level sensor prototypes to extreme engineering applications in different scenarios. This means that research is needed on how to improve the environmental adaptability of sensors. For example, for the exploration of outer space, such as Venus, it should be clear that the temperature on Venus exceeds 400 °C and the atmosphere is filled with corrosive gases such as sulfides222. This means that the corresponding test sensors should have both high temperature resistance and corrosion resistance. For pressure monitoring of aircraft engine combustion chambers, the temperature and Mach number of the airflow should be clearly defined, and sensors with appropriate ranges should be selected to ensure the highest pulsation pressure sensing resolution. For monitoring the tail jet flow of rocket launches, the dynamic response capability of the pressure sensor should be evaluated in advance.

Through reasonable packaging and structural design, the frequency response of the sensor can be improved, so as to capture the pressure signal of the launch tail jet flow at extremely high temperatures and in a very short time. In addition, for monitoring the transient pressure of explosions, it is necessary to clarify the radiation on site and the signal interference caused by large equipment on the sensors. Electromagnetic protection and shielding interference should be considered for the sensors to ensure the accuracy of pressure signal testing. In addition, the imperfection of test data under real working conditions requires systematic backend signal acquisition and processing of sensors, including conventional filtering, signal amplification, and temperature compensation based on software algorithms.

In general, the research on pressure sensors under extreme working conditions of SiC should be oriented towards applications. In addition to the common extreme working conditions, pressure testing for geothermal energy, nuclear safety, high-temperature and high-pressure chemical reaction vessels, and other working conditions should also be included in the application scenarios of SiC high-temperature pressure testing, and more attention should be paid to the environmental adaptability design of sensors.

Despite significant progress in the development of SiC high-temperature pressure sensors, the field is still in an early exploration stage. Currently reported operating temperatures for these sensors generally do not exceed 800 °C, and their performance varies considerably. Much of the existing research is confined to laboratory-scale calibration and static performance evaluation, predominantly conducted at room temperature. There is a notable lack of high-temperature measurement data, and reports on real-world engineering application testing are scarce. These limitations highlight common challenges that need to be addressed in future research.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current research status and future directions in key technologies for SiC high-temperature pressure sensors. After decades of development, SiC MEMS pressure sensors represent a highly interdisciplinary field, integrating knowledge from mechanics, mechanical design, semiconductor physics, materials science, chemistry, and mechanical processing. On the contrary, although leadless packaging is difficult, it is considered an effective method for solving the high-temperature packaging of pressure sensors. Nevertheless, for batch engineering applications, there are plenty of opportunities for ongoing development in the future.

Beyond physical quantity sensors such as those for pressure and acceleration, devices such as SiC radiation detectors and SiC ultraviolet photoelectric sensors have garnered significant attention and research interest. For further research, it will be essential to align developments with market demands and industrial trends, aiming to achieve intelligence, integration, and miniaturization of SiC sensors at both the chip manufacturing and packaging levels. We have strong grounds to believe that once the key technologies discussed in this article are successfully addressed and further developed, a wide range of SiC micro- and nano-devices, including SiC pressure sensors, will demonstrate substantial market value and significant engineering application potential.
