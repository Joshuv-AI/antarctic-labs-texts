Rethinking Associative Memory Mechanism in Induction Head
Shuo Wang, Issei Sato

Abstract
Induction head mechanism is a part of the computational circuits for in-context learning (ICL) that enable large language models (LLMs) to adapt to new tasks without fine-tuning. Most existing work explains the training dynamics behind acquiring such a powerful mechanism. However, the model’s ability to coordinate in-context information over long contexts and global knowledge acquired during pretraining remains poorly understood. This paper investigates how a two-layer transformer thoroughly captures in-context information and balances it with pretrained bigram knowledge in next token prediction, from the viewpoint of associative memory. We theoretically analyze the representation of weight matrices in attention layers and the resulting logits when a transformer is given prompts generated by a bigram model. In the experiments, we design specific prompts to evaluate whether the outputs of the trained transformer align with the theoretical results.

In recent years, transformer-based models, such as BERT

Especially, in-context learning (ICL)

Instead, these models leverage patterns from a prompt or input sequence, effectively learning "in context" by interpreting examples or instructions provided in real-time
[

During a phase change, models acquire the ability to complete complex patterns through induction heads, suggesting that induction heads are a key mechanistic basis of ICL
[

It is known that the induction head mechanism can emerge in two-layer transformers
[

[

41
,
10

. Among them,

However, the effectiveness of the induction head mechanism tends to diminish for the latter part of a sequence
[
5
]
, which does not align with the behavior observed in real-world LLM. In addition, while the learning dynamics of the induction head have been elucidated in the existing work, it remains unclear how
in-context knowledge
provided in the prompt and
global knowledge
acquired during pretraining influence the output of two-layer transformers. Understanding how a transformer predicts the next token is crucial, as it allows us to model phenomena such as context hijacking
[
27
]
, in which altering the context disrupts factual recall, causing the model to produce incorrect outputs influenced by misleading in-context information.

In this paper, we theoretically and empirically show, from the perspective of associative memory, how a two-layer transformer with relative positional encoding (RPE) can avoid failure in detecting patterns that appear in the latter part of a sequence.
We also investigate how the model prioritizes in-context knowledge versus global knowledge when predicting the next token, using training data generated by a bigram model with triggered transitions.
To summarize, we make the following contributions:

•

We theoretically show that the induction head learned by a transformer using RPE can avoid oversight of patterns within the framework of associative memory.

•

We experimentally confirmed that the model with RPE can comprehensively use in-context knowledge for the bigram model. We also demonstrated its ability to effectively infer on sequences where the transformer using absolute positional encoding (APE) fails in next-token prediction.

•

We investigated how the model prioritizes in-context knowledge versus global knowledge when generating outputs, using both theoretical analysis and experimental evaluation.

3.2
Associative memory

Associative memory plays an important role in analyzing the behavior of memory recall at induction head in the next section.
Before defining associative memory, we first adopt a technical assumption commonly used in theoretical studies on transformers, that is, that embeddings are high-dimensional random vectors, allowing them to be nearly orthogonal. Mathematically, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 1

(Near-orthogonality
[
24
,
33
,
5
]
)
.

The embeddings
(u_{i})_{i}
are
d
-dimensional vectors with Gaussian entries, each having a unit norm
\|u_{i}\|=1
and
u_{i}^{\top}u_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
. Also,
W_{0}u_{i}
forms a new vector that has a near-unit norm and near-orthogonal to
(u_{i})_{i}
if
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a Gaussian random matrix with appropriate entries.

With the assumption, we define associative memory.

Definition 1

(Associative Memory)
.

Suppose
(u_{i})_{i}
and
(v_{j})_{j}
are the embeddings satisfying Assumption
1
. Then, A matrix is called associative memory when
W
is expressed as the sum of the products of
u_{i}
and
v_{j}
:

\displaystyle W=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{i,j}u_{i}v_{j}^{\top},

(5)

where
\alpha_{i,j}\in\mathbb{R}
is the score for the pair
(u_{i},v_{j})
.

Thanks to orthogonality, we can derive the score
\alpha_{i,j}
for the pair
(u_{i},v_{j})
using the operation
u_{i}^{\top}Wv_{j}
. Henceforth, we consider the weight matrices in the attention layer and the feed-forward network of the transformer as matrices that implement associative memory.

3.3
Induction head

Induction heads are a specific type of attention mechanism observed in transformer models, responsible for detecting patterns in token sequences and utilizing previous occurrences of the same token to improve predictions. The behavior of induction heads is particularly relevant for tasks that require understanding and leveraging repeated patterns, such as in language modeling.

In Fig.
2
, we summarize the process by which the induction head identifies and outputs repetitive patterns in the input. When the prompt
ABC\dots A
is given, the first-layer attention, which is called
previous token head
, copies information from the previous token to the current token. Consequently, the token
B
retains the information of
A
in the form of
\Phi_{1}A
. Then, in the second-layer attention, the associative memory
W_{K}^{2}
matches the pair
\Phi_{1}A
with
A
, attending to the position of token
B
. As a result, the information of
B
is stored in the last token
A
as
W_{V}^{2}B
. Finally, the output matrix
W_{O}^{2}
transforms
W_{V}^{2}B
into
W_{U}(B)
, producing an output that follows the repetitive pattern.

3.4
Bigram model with triggered transitions

This section describes the bigram model with triggered tokens used in
Bietti et al. [5]
.

In this bigram language model over a vocabulary of size
V
, we first prepare trigger tokens
q_{k}
sampled from a distribution
\pi_{q}
and output tokens
o_{k}
sampled from
\pi_{o}
that always follow their corresponding trigger token in every bigram sequence. Then, the sequence
z_{1:T}
is generated accordingly to the bigram conditionals
\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
, but when a trigger token is generated, the next token is guaranteed to be its corresponding output token:

\begin{cases}\pi_{b}(j\mid i)&\text{if }i\notin\{q_{k}\},\\
1\{j=o_{k}\}&\text{if }i=q_{k}.\end{cases}

Motivation for the model

In written texts centered on a particular theme, it is common for identical patterns of tokens to recur multiple times. For instance, in texts about Harry Potter or machine learning, the token "Avada" is often followed by "Kedavra," and "neural" is typically followed by "network."
The induction head is a computational mechanism capable of detecting repeated patterns in context and facilitating the output of the tokens that complete these patterns when a trigger token appears.

The next token following a given token

z
is determined by conditional probabilities in a pure bigram model. As a result, bigram sequences often lack consistent repeated patterns, which prevents the induction head from emerging.
In contrast, pairs of trigger and output tokens in a sequence enable two-layer transformers to learn a circuit that leverages repeated patterns, rather than relying solely on bigram conditionals.

4
Theoretical result

We analyze two key aspects of induction heads: the impact of positional encoding on the oversight of patterns in a sequence, and the contributions of in-context information and pretrained knowledge during inference.

4.1
Oversight of patterns

In this section, we address the theoretical aspects of overlooking patterns in a sequence by the induction head mechanism.

We argue that the learning of the previous token head fails for transformers with APE, and thus, the model cannot take patterns in the latter half of a sequence into account for the prediction.
Mathematically, we observe the representation of the weight matrix
\tilde{W}_{K}^{1}
when weight matrices are sequentially trained, in the order of
W_{O}^{2}
,
W_{K}^{2}
, and
W_{K}^{1}
, through one step of gradient descent. We refer to Theorem 3 from
Bietti et al. [5]
:

Theorem 1

(Theorem 3,
Bietti et al. [5]
)
.

For any
t>1
, the learned matrix achieves the following associative memory:

\displaystyle p_{t-1}^{\top}\tilde{W}_{K}^{1}p_{t}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\eta\alpha\alpha^{\prime}(T-1)}{T^{2}t}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left\{\mathbb{P}(t_{q}=t-1)\left(1-\frac{1}{t}\right)\right.\left.+O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right)\right\},

(6)

where
p_{t}
is the absolute positional encoding for token position
t
,
\alpha
,
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants, and
\mathbb{P}(t_{q}=t-1)
denote the probability that the first trigger token appears at position
t-1
.

From Eq.
6
, we can see that the score for the pair of
p_{t-1}
and
p_{t}
is inversely proportional to
t

increases – meaning as we move toward the latter part of the sequence – the function of the associative memory diminishes.

In contrast, we prove that transformers employing RPE consistently direct the previous token head to attend to the preceding token, regardless of the input sequence length.
We calculate how the transformer’s weight matrices are represented by following the same learning procedure as when using a transformer with APE.
We again present the training outcome of the matrix
W_{K}^{1}
as associative memory, which is the key component for the previous token head.
We provide the complete statement and proof in Appendix

Theorem 2

(

.

Under the setup described in Sec.

\displaystyle r_{-1}^{\top}W_{K}^{1}w_{E}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{P(t_{q}=t-1)}{t}\cdot O\left(1\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\eta\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{P(t_{q}=t)}{t^{2}}\left\{O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)\right\},

where

is the learning rate,
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants,

and

are the first and second occurrence positions of trigger token, and

is the size of the vocabulary set

.

Theorem
2
suggests that the association between

and

is independent of both the vocabulary

and the token position

Remark 1
.

A transformer using RPE learns to attend to the previous token regardless of its position within the sequence. However, if the input sequence contains out-of-distribution tokens, the induction head mechanism does not activate. In such cases, only a transformer with APE can effectively attend to the previous token.

We have seen that positional encoding influences the behavior of induction head, but we also show that a three-layer transformer without positional encoding can implement the induction head mechanism.

Proposition 1
.

A construction exists for a three-layer transformer without positional encoding that successfully achieves the induction head mechanism.

4.2
Global knowledge v.s. in-context knowledge

Here, we discuss how a two-layer transformer uses statistical information obtained from the training data and the information provided in-context by calculating the logits.

Associative memory construction achieving induction head

We first introduce the following lemma, which states that the induction head mechanism is achievable by setting the appropriate weights for the matrices in a two-layer transformer with APE.

Lemma 1

(
Bietti et al. [5]
)
.

Define
Q
as the support of
\pi_{q}
.
The induction head can be achieved by constructing matrices in the following manner.

\displaystyle W_{Q}^{1}=W_{Q}^{2}=I,\qquad W_{K}^{1}=\sum_{t=2}^{T}p_{t}p_{t-1}^{\top},

\displaystyle W_{K}^{2}=\sum_{k\in Q}w_{E}(k)(W_{O}^{1}W_{V}^{1}w_{E}(k))^{\top},

\displaystyle W_{O}^{2}=\sum_{v=1}^{V}w_{U}(v)(W_{V}^{2}w_{E}(v))^{\top},

where
I
is the identity matrix, and the matrices
W_{O}^{1}
,
W_{V}^{1}
, and
W_{O}^{2}
are a Gaussian random matrix.

It is straightforward to prove that a two-layer transformer with RPE also has associative memory construction that achieves induction head by modifying
W_{K}^{1}=\sum_{k\in Q}w_{E}(k)r_{-1}^{\top}
.
Now, using the transformer with RPE architecture, we define an
associative memory transformer
: a model that incorporates an induction head and a feed-forward network storing knowledge learned through pretraining.

Definition 2

(associative memory transformer)
.

A two-layer transformer with RPE is called
associative memory transformer
if its weight matrices are set as in Lemma
1
except for

\displaystyle W_{K}^{1}=\sum_{k\in Q}w_{E}(k)r_{-1}^{\top},\qquad W_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}w_{E}(v_{1})^{\top}\\
w_{E}(v_{2})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
w_{E}(v_{V})^{\top}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v_{1})}w_{U}(u)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v_{V})}w_{U}(u)^{\top}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

This construction reveals that the feed-forward layer in Eq.
3
of Sec.
3.1
functions explicitly as a key-value memory
[
19
]
.
Each row of
W_{1}
(key) acts as a detector for an input pattern
w_{E}(v)
, and each column of
W_{2}
(value) encodes global knowledge, i.e., the distribution
\pi_{b}
over the output vocabulary.
Appendix
B
discusses the difference from the architecture of
Bietti et al. [5]
.
Note that when the probability
\pi_{b}(u\mid v)
approaches
+0
, its logarithm
\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v)}
diverges to
-\infty
. If we admit
\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v)}=-\infty
, the transformer never outputs

Thus, we consider
\epsilon

\pi_{b}(u\mid v)=\epsilon
.

Associative memory transformer on limited input sequences.

We present the following proposition, which focuses on two token patterns in the input sequence.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose a two-layer transformer is an associative memory transformer. Given a length-
T
sequence

where the last token is
z_{T}=q
, let the
t_{1}
-th token be
z_{t_{1}}=v_{1}

-th token be

and
(3\leq)t_{1}<t_{2}

Also assume that there exists only one occurrence for each of

Then, the logits

\xi_{v_{2}}

\displaystyle\xi_{v_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{e}{t_{1}+e-1}}}{Z}+\log{\pi_{b}(v_{1}\mid q)},

\displaystyle\xi_{v_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{1+e}{t_{2}+e-1}}}{Z}+\log{\pi_{b}(v_{2}\mid q)},

(7)

where

is the normalization factor of the softmax function and

is Euler’s number.

We can observe a general trend from Eqs.
7
. First, if the model contains global knowledge of both tokens
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, the output token is influenced by the knowledge, in addition to the in-context knowledge.
In particular, the logits of tokens that rarely appear in the training dataset become significantly low. In our associative memory transformer model, global knowledge never promotes the output of any specific token; rather, it solely suppresses inappropriate predictions, because we always have
\log{\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid q)}\leq 0
.
Second, if token patterns, such as
q\,v_{1}
and
q\,v_{2}
, do not appear in the training data, global knowledge affects the token logits to the same extent, due to the way
W_{F}
is defined using the threshold
\epsilon
in Def.
2
. It is worth noting that an associative memory transformer exhibits a significant difference in scale when handling global knowledge and in-context knowledge. For instance, if
\pi_{b}(v\mid q)=0.1
, then
\log{\pi_{b}(v\mid q)}\approx-2.3
. Even with the functionality of an induction head, it is unlikely to predict the token patterns from the context.
This is avoidable by reformulating

3

If neither token appears during training, the difference between the two logits before applying the softmax function
\xi^{\prime}_{v_{2}}-\xi^{\prime}_{v_{1}}
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{v_{2}}-\xi^{\prime}_{v_{1}}=

\displaystyle\frac{e(t_{1}-t_{2})+t_{1}+e-1}{(t_{1}+e-1)(t_{2}+e-1)}.

(8)

The result implies that the token
v_{2}
is more likely to be generated if
t_{1}
is large and the distance between the tokens
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, i.e.,

is small. This trend arises from the first layer of the attention block, where the attention is not fully concentrated on the previous token, resulting in a diffusion of attention across other tokens as well. In other words, the more tokens that precede the current token, the lower the attention score assigned to the current token.

Stronger associative memory on general input sequences.

Next, we introduce a more sophisticated analysis of in-context and global knowledge by relaxing assumptions in the sequence
z_{1:T}
. The transformer considered here has associative memory with larger scores, characterized by a weighted sum of the individual terms of the weight matrices in Sec.
3.3
, where each term is scaled by an appropriate coefficient. We consider a special case in which each term has the same coefficient
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\in\mathbb{R}

Definition 3

(stronger associative memory transformer)
.

We redefine the following matrices
W_{K}^{1},W_{K}^{2}
and
W_{O}^{2}
in Def.
2

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{K}^{1}=\tau_{1}W_{K}^{1},\quad\hat{W}_{K}^{2}=\tau_{2}W_{K}^{2},\quad\hat{W}_{O}^{2}=\tau_{3}W_{O}^{2},

(9)

with the parameters
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The other weight matrices remain the same.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose a two-layer transformer is a stronger associative memory transformer. Given a length-
T
sequence
z_{1:T}
where the last token is
z_{T}=q

q\,v

v\in\mathcal{V}

\tau_{1}
and

\displaystyle\xi_{v}

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}\\
\tau_{2}\end{subarray}}{\approx}\log{\pi_{b}(v\mid q)}

\displaystyle\quad+\tau_{3}\cdot\frac{f(v)+\mathbf{1}\{v=q\}\mathbf{1}\{z_{1}=q\}}{\left(\sum_{v^{\prime}=1}^{V}f(v^{\prime})\right)+\mathbf{1}\{z_{1}=q\}}.

(10)

It can be observed that the transformer equipped with a general associative memory is affected by the proportions of each token pattern present in the context as well as by the global knowledge. In this case, it can be concluded that the information regarding the positions where token patterns are observed does not influence the final logit. We can easily derive the following properties.

Corollary 1

For vocabulary

\log{\pi_{b}(v\mid q)}
and this means that the candidate for the next token is
\argmax_{v\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{v_{1},v_{2}\}}\pi_{b}(v\mid q)
. On the other hand, The logits for
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
have the other term in Prop.
3
, which makes them other candidates for the next token. Overall, Corollary
1
states that the model output depends on the bigram conditionals
\pi_{b}(\cdot\mid q)
and the frequency of token patterns
qv_{1}
and
qv_{2}
in the context.

We investigated whether the training of transformers directs attention towards the previous token in the first attention layer. Specifically,

Discussion

One of the worst-case scenarios for
\operatorname{TF}_{\text{ape}}
occurs when a trigger token does not appear by chance in the range
t\leq 128
and only appears for the first time at
t>128
. Although such a sequence is contrived, its probability of occurrence is non-zero. For such sequences, models with an incomplete previous token head fail to associate the trigger token’s information with the output token, resulting in incorrect predictions without leveraging in-context information. In contrast,
\operatorname{TF}_{\text{rpe}}
, which links words to their relative positions, ensures that the in-context information is not overlooked, regardless of where the trigger token first appears, thereby enabling correct predictions.

6
Conclusion

We analyzed the influence of interaction between global bigram and in-context knowledge to the two-layer transformer through the lens of associative memory. We theoretically and empirically verified that relative positional encoding empowers transformers with the ability to capture information in longer sequences, and that how the next token prediction is conducted within the transformer that was trained to store global knowledge and to have induction head.

7
Limitations

Our study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the analysis presented in this work is conducted using a two-layer transformer model. While this allows for a controlled and interpretable exploration of the underlying mechanisms, the findings may not directly generalize to LLMs, which typically involve significantly more layers and complex interactions.

Second, while our focus on induction heads provides valuable insights into ICL, it is important to note that ICL leverages additional computational circuits beyond induction heads. As such, our explanation captures only part of the broader mechanisms underlying ICL, leaving room for further investigation into other contributing factors.

Ethics statement

This work is purely theoretical, supported by controlled experiments designed to validate the proposed analyses. No personal or sensitive data were involved at any stage of this research. All experiments were conducted using datasets that are publicly available, ensuring compliance with ethical standards for data usage.

Appendix A
Additional related work

In-context learning and induction head

Bayesian inference is also a popular perspective for comprehending ICL.
Xie et al. [59]
and
Wies et al. [57]
investigated how a pretrained transformer implicitly discovers the underlying concept or latent task of a prompt by leveraging a mixture of distributions. An alternative line of work
[
62
]
relates the Bayesian model averaging to the attention mechanism.
Wang et al. [56]
studies how information is aggregated to label words in ICL.
In terms of two-layer transformer,
Chen et al. [10]
studied ICL on
n
-gram Markov chain, proving that gradient flow optimization under the cross-entropy loss leads to a model that exhibits a generalized induction head mechanism.
Edelman et al. [16]
showed two-layer attention-only transformers learn induction heads that estimate the next token’s conditional probability based on prior occurrences, enabling near Bayes-optimal performance. Similarly,
Nichani et al. [41]
showed that two-layer transformers learn causal structure via gradient descent, suggesting that induction head is a special case of this structure.

Ren et al. [47]
discovered a more generalized version of induction heads, which are referred to as semantic induction heads.

Length generalization and positional encoding

Although we point out the oversight of in-context information by a transformer with APE, this can be considered as the failure of length generalization.
Since the introduction of the Transformer architecture
[
52
]
, there has been a wealth of research on positional encoding
[
63
]
. Originally,
Vaswani [52]
adopted sinusoidal positional encoding that transforms the tokens’ absolute positions. Subsequently,
Kiyono et al. [31]
and
Likhomanenko et al. [34]
proposed shifting absolute sinusoidal positional encoding during training to achieve shift invariance.
Wang et al. [54]
extends word embedding as continuous functions with respect to token position, leading to smooth word representations. Meanwhile, it is demonstrated that RPE is superior to APE for longer sequence generalization in various tasks
[
40
,
34
,
25
,
26
]
. Additionally,
Sinha et al. [50]
questioned whether models can learn the relative distance between words when equipped with APE. The first RPE was formulated by
Shaw et al. [49]
, where a trainable encoding is added to the key before computing the dot product with the query. The transformer in our work also adopts this type of positional encoding, while we fix the positional encodings to randomly initialized vectors. A variety of different RPE methods were presented
[
25
,
30
]
until now, but DeBERTa
[
21
]
, RoPE
[
51
]
, T5-Bias
[
46
]
, and ALiBi
[
43
]
are the most popular choices.
Ruoss et al. [48]
suggested that randomizing absolute and relative positional encoding enhance the model’s ability to generalize to input sequences of lengths not encountered during training, but they used the encoder-ony Transformer, which is different from ours.

Appendix B
MLP as a key-value memory

Geva et al. [19]
discovered that the feed-forward layers in transformer-based language models function as key-value memory. In a transformer, the feed-forward layer is typically computed as

F(x)=W_{2}(\operatorname{ReLU(W_{1}x)}),

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input vector, and
W_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}\times d},W_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{o}\times d_{h}}
.
According to
Geva et al. [19]
, the rows of
W_{1}
(keys) serve to detect specific patterns in the input sequence, while the columns of
W_{2}
(values) have been shown to represent distributions over the output vocabulary.

Bietti et al. [5]
uses a transformer architecture that uses a linear projection
W_{F}
instead of an MLP layer.

W_{F}=\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v)}w_{U}(u)w_{E}(v)^{\top}.

Due to the near-orthogonality of the embedding vectors, this projection maps an input
x=w_{E}(v)
to the output
\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v)}w_{U}(u)
.
This enables the matrix
W_{F}
to function as an associative memory that stores the pairs of an embedding vector and corresponding global knowledge.

Now, suppose we construct the matrices
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}w_{E}(v_{1})^{\top}\\
w_{E}(v_{2})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
w_{E}(v_{V})^{\top}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

\displaystyle W_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v_{1})}w_{U}(u)^{\top}\\
\quad\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v_{2})}w_{U}(u)^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{u=1}^{V}\log{\pi_{b}(u\mid v_{V})}w_{U}(u)^{\top}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},

(12)

Each row of
W_{1}
acts as a detector for an input pattern
w_{E}(v)
, such that only the corresponding entry is activated when
w_{E}(v_{i})
is provided as input. Each row of
W_{2}
, in turn, encodes global knowledge, i.e., the distribution
\pi_{b}
over the output vocabulary conditioned on the corresponding input token.

We can easily find that the resulting computation matches exactly that of the linear projection
W_{F}
under the near-orthgonality condition.

Therefore, this construction confirms that the feed-forward layer operates as a key-value memory.

C.3
Theoretical analysis of learning

Theorem 3

(
formal
)
.

Under the setup described in Sec.
C.2
, a two-layer attention only transformer with relative positional encoding learns associations of the associative memory transformer, i.e., the memories
W_{O}^{2},W_{K}^{2}
and
W_{K}^{1}
can be written as the sum of the terms that constitute the associative memory transformer.

\displaystyle W_{K}^{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in Q}\chi(k)w_{E}(k)r_{-1}^{\top}+(W_{K}^{1})^{\prime},

(13)

\displaystyle W_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in Q}\psi(k)w_{E}(k)(W_{O}^{1}W_{V}^{1}w_{E}(k))^{\top}+(W_{K}^{2})^{\prime},

(14)

\displaystyle W_{O}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{V}\omega(v)w_{U}(v)(W_{V}^{2}w_{E}(v))^{\top}+(W_{O}^{2})^{\prime},

(15)

where
\chi,\psi:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\omega:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the learned score of each association, and
(W_{K}^{1})^{\prime},(W_{K}^{2})^{\prime}
and
(W_{O}^{2})^{\prime}
are the other associations.

Proof.

We will demonstrate that associative memory can be learned by performing one gradient descent step in a top-down manner, starting with
W_{O}^{2}
, followed by
W_{K}^{2}
, and then
W_{K}^{1}
.

Step1: Training of
W_{O}^{2}

We begin with the dynamics of
W_{O}^{2}
. The input to the second layer attention,
x^{(1)}_{t}
, is composed of the sum of the values from the first layer attention,
x^{(1,0)}_{t}
, and the values from the residual connection,
x^{(1,1)}_{t}
. Since
W_{K}^{1}
is initialized to zero, the attention of the first layer is evenly distributed across all the tokens
z_{1:t}
and we have

x^{(1,0)}_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s}),

where
\Phi_{1}=W_{O}^{1}W_{V}^{1}
. Additionally, the residual stream carries the token embedding:

x^{(1,1)}_{t}=w_{E}(z_{t}).

Since the matrix
W_{K}^{2}
is also zero-initialized, the value of the second layer attention can be computed through

x^{(2)}_{t}=W_{V}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}x^{(1)}_{s}\right).

Now the logit prediction is given by
W_{U}(W_{O}^{2}x^{(2)}_{T}+x^{(1)}_{T})
with the residual connection. We note that when
d
is large, thanks to the near-orthogonality,
W_{U}x_{T}^{(1)}=W_{U}\left(\sum_{s=1}^{T}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s})/T+w_{E}(z_{T})\right)
is negligible because
x_{T}^{(1)}
does not contain
w_{U}(v)
for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
. This enables us to use Lemma
2
and one gradient step with learning-rate
\eta
yields

\displaystyle W_{O}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{V}\sum_{k=1}^{V}w_{U}(k)\mathbb{E}[x^{(2)}_{T}\mid y=k]^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{V}\sum_{k=1}^{V}w_{U}(k)\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\hat{p}_{W}(k\mid x)}{p(y=k)}x^{(2)}_{T}\right]^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{V}\sum_{k=1}^{V}w_{U}(k)(\mathbb{E}[x^{(2)}_{T}\mid y=k]-\mathbb{E}[x^{(2)}_{T}])^{\top}.

(16)

This transformation comes from the assumption that every token is sampled from a uniform distribution (
p(y=k)=1/V)
and all the logits are
0
when
W_{O}^{2}=O_{d\times d}
, which means that
\hat{p}_{W}(k\mid x)=1/V
.

Given that
y=k
, it holds that the first output token
z_{t_{o}}=k
. For
z_{i}
(
i\neq t_{o}
), the uniform distribution can possibly generate any token, independently of the condition
y=k
. Since we have

x_{T}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}W_{V}^{2}\left(w_{E}(z_{t})+\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s})\right),

the expression
\mathbb{E}\left[x^{(2)}_{T}\mid y=k\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[x^{(2)}_{T}\right]
leaves only the terms related to
z_{t_{o}}
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x^{(2)}_{T}\mid y=k\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[x^{(2)}_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}W_{V}^{2}(\mathbb{E}[w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})\mid y=k]-\mathbb{E}[w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})])

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{o}}^{T}W_{V}^{2}\frac{1}{t}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{T}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{o}}^{T}W_{V}^{2}\frac{1}{t}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})\right]

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}W_{V}^{2}(w_{E}(k)-\mathbb{E}[w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})])

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\tau}{T}W_{V}^{2}\Phi_{1}(w_{E}(k)-\mathbb{E}[w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})]),

(18)

where
\tau:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=t_{o}}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\right]
. Since each token is generated from a uniform distribution, the expected value of
w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})
, conditioned on nothing, is given by:

\mathbb{E}\left[w_{E}(z_{t_{o}})\right]=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k=1}^{V}w_{E}(k).

Combining eq.
16
and
17
, the near-orthogonality of Gaussian vectors gives us

\displaystyle w_{U}(k)^{\top}W_{O}^{2}W_{V}^{2}w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\eta}{VT}\mathbf{1}(k=j)+O\left(\frac{\eta}{V^{2}T}\right),

(19)

\displaystyle w_{U}(k)^{\top}W_{O}^{2}W_{V}^{2}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\eta\tau}{VT}\mathbf{1}(k=j)+O\left(\frac{\eta\tau}{V^{2}T}\right).

(20)

This is the same result as in the proof from
Bietti et al. [5]
.
So far, the weight matrix
W_{O}^{2}
is trained to perform as an associative memory so that

w_{U}(k)^{\top}W_{O}^{2}W_{V}^{2}w_{E}(z_{t})\approx\alpha\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\},

(21)

with a constant
\alpha=\frac{\eta}{TV}
.

Step2: Training of
W_{K}^{2}

Since the training of
W_{O}^{2}
is finished, we have Eq.
19
.
When
W_{K}^{1}=W_{K}^{2}=O_{d\times d}
, Lemma
4
demonstrates that the next token prediction is distributed among all tokens, which means that
\hat{p}(v\mid x)=1/V
for any vocabulary
v
. According to Lemma
5
, one step of gradient descent yields

\displaystyle W_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{TN}\sum_{k,t}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[w_{U}(k)^{\top}\Phi_{2}x_{t}^{(1)}\cdot(x_{t}^{(1)}-\bar{x}^{(1)})

\displaystyle\qquad\times(x_{T}^{(1)})^{\top}\mid y=k\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{TN}\sum_{k,t}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[w_{U}(k)^{\top}\Phi_{2}x_{t}^{(1)}\cdot(x_{t}^{(1)}-\bar{x}^{(1)})

\displaystyle\qquad\times(x_{T}^{(1)})^{\top}\Bigr],

(22)

with
\bar{x}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{(0)}
.

We simplify our transformer architecture by setting
x_{t}^{(1,1)}
as the queries and values, and
x_{t}^{(1,0)}
as the keys. Furthermore, we add the condition that the trigger token
z_{t_{q}}=j
and rewrite eq.
22
. This conditioning leverages the fact that each token in
z_{t_{q}}
is sampled from a uniform distribution.
Now we have

\displaystyle W_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{TN}\sum_{k,t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[w_{U}(k)^{\top}\Phi_{2}x_{t}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})(x_{T}^{(1,1)})^{\top}\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{TN}\sum_{k,t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[w_{U}(k)^{\top}\Phi_{2}x_{t}^{(1,1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})(x_{T}^{(1,1)})^{\top}\mid z_{t_{q}}=j\Bigr],

where
\bar{x}^{(1,0)}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}^{(1,0)}
. Using the formula
21
, we obtain

\displaystyle W_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha\eta}{TN^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})w_{E}(z_{T})^{\top}\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j\Biggr]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\alpha\eta}{TN^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})w_{E}(z_{T})^{\top}\mid z_{t_{q}}=j\Biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\eta}{TN^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta_{k,j}w_{E}(j)^{\top},

(23)

where
\Delta_{k,j}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,j}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid z_{t_{q}}=j\right].

The sum in
\Delta_{k,j}
can be partitioned into three distinct groups: (i)
\Delta_{k,j}^{o}
, where
t
is the index of the output token; (ii)
\Delta_{k,j}^{q}
, where
t
corresponds to the indices of the trigger tokens; and (iii)
\Delta_{k,j}^{r}
, which includes all other cases.
Mathematically, let
t_{o}\geq 2
be a random variable, and
t_{q}=t_{o}-1
, we write

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,j}^{o}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t_{o}}=k\}(x_{t_{o}}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t_{o}}=k\}(x_{t_{o}}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid z_{t_{q}}=j\right],

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,j}^{q}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{q}}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{q}}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid z_{t_{q}}=j\right],

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,j}^{r}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{r}}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{r}}\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t}=k\}(x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)})\mid z_{t_{q}}=j\right],

where
\mathcal{T}_{q}=\{t_{q},T\}
and
\mathcal{T}_{r}=\{1\leq i\leq T\mid i\in\mathbb{N},i\neq t_{o},i\neq t_{q},i\neq T\}
.

First, we will take a look at
\Delta_{k,j}^{o}
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t_{o}}=k\}\mid y=k,z_{t_{q}}=j]=1
and
\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{z_{t_{o}}=k\}\mid z_{t_{q}}=j]=1/N
, so we find that

\displaystyle\Delta_{k,j}^{o}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t}^{(1,0)}-\bar{x}^{(1,0)}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{t_{o}}\sum_{s=1}^{t_{o}}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{N-1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{k,j,i}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(i),

(24)

where
a_{k,j,i}
is the coefficient of
\Phi_{1}w_{E}(i)
by calculating

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{t_{o}}\sum_{s=1}^{t_{o}}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s})-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s})\right].

The analysis of
a_{k,j,i}
is done by the work
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{k,j,i}\approx\begin{cases}O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{T}\right),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Thus, we have the following approximation:

\displaystyle(\Phi_{1}w_{E}(i))^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta_{k,j}^{o}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\begin{cases}O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),&\text{if }i\neq j,\\
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{T}\right),&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

(25)

Similarly, we have
\Delta_{k,j}^{q}=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
and
\Delta_{k,j}^{r}=O\left(\frac{T}{N}\right)
based on the discussion in
Bietti et al. [5]
. By combining these analysis with Eq.
25
, we multiply
(\Phi_{1}w_{E}(i))^{\top}
from left and
w_{E}(j)
from right to Eq.
23
and we establish that

\displaystyle(\Phi_{1}w_{E}(i))^{\top}W_{K}^{2}w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\alpha\eta}{TN}\left\{\Omega\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{i=j\}+O\left(\frac{T}{N}\right)\right\}

(26)

W_{K}^{1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s}))^{\top}W_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times w_{E}(z_{T})q_{s,t}w_{E}(z_{t})^{\top}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\eta}{V}\sum_{i=k}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{U}(k)^{\top}\Phi_{2}w_{E}(z_{t})\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s}))^{\top}W_{K}^{2}w_{E}(z_{T})q_{s,t}w_{E}(z_{t})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times q_{s,t}w_{E}(z_{t})^{\top}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\eta}{V}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(w_{U}(k)^{\top}\Phi_{2}\bar{x}_{1:T}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}(\Phi_{1}w_{E}(z_{s}))^{\top}W_{K}^{2}w_{E}(z_{T})

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times q_{s,t}w_{E}(z_{t})^{\top}\right],

with

\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle W_{K}^{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{V}\sum_{k=1}^{V}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{t}(\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}q_{t_{q},t}+\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}q_{T,T})

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times w_{E}(z_{t})^{\top}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\alpha^{\prime}}{t}(\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}q_{t_{q},t}+\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}q_{T,T})

With the near-orthogonality of the token embeddings, we write

\displaystyle C_{t,k}

For the term

Lemma 7
.

Given a sequence
z_{1:T}
that satisfies Sec.
C.2
.
Fix some

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $s=t$ and $t=T-1$},\\
\frac{1}{V-1}&\text{if $s\leq t-2$ and $t\neq T$},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(33)

Proof.

First, consider the case
s=t
. Under the condition
t_{q}=s
, we have
t_{q}=s=t
, and thus,
z_{t}
is the trigger token. The only possible case allowing the same token for trigger and output is when

. This yields

because
z_{T}
not only works as trigger, but also output. Therefore, when
s=t

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $t=T-1$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(34)

Next, we focus on
s\leq t-2
. Suppose we are given
t=T
. The token
z_{t}
is then the second trigger token, and
\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}=1
implies that
z_{t_{q}}=k
. However, since the first trigger position is
t_{q}=s\leq t-2
and the output token is also
y=k
,
z_{T}(=k)
cannot be the second trigger occurrence. Hence, it suffices to consider
t\neq T
. We now have
s\leq t-2<T-2
. Assuming
z_{t_{q}}=k
will also lead to contradiction because the trigger token
k
appears more than twice at positions
s,s+1
and
T
. With this in mind, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=P(z_{t}=k\mid y=k,t_{q}=s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}P(z_{t}=k,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid y=k,t_{q}=s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}P(z_{t}=k\mid y=k,t_{q}=s,z_{t_{q}}=q)

\displaystyle\quad\times P(z_{t_{q}}=q\mid y=k,t_{q}=s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{1}{V-1}\frac{1}{V-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V-1}.

Here we use

Lemma 8
.

z_{1:T}
that satisfies Sec.
C.2
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t_{q}+1}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot]=\frac{1}{V-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot]=\frac{1}{V-1}.

Finally, for
t^{\prime}\neq t_{q},t_{q}+1,t
and
T
, the tokens
z_{t^{\prime}}
and
z_{t}
are independent. With Lemma
7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}\neq t_{q},t_{q}+1,t,T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}\right)\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{T-4}{(V-1)^{2}}.

Integrating the equations above allows us to arrive at the conclusion.
∎

Lemma 10
.

Given a sequence
z_{1:T}
that satisfies Sec.
C.2
.
For any vocabulary
k\in\mathcal{V}
, and
t\in[1,T]\subset\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}=t-1)\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle\quad+P(t_{q}=t)\mathbf{1}\{t=T-1\}w_{E}(k)+O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right).

Proof.

We split
\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}
into
\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t-1\}+\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t\}+\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t-2\}
. Then, the expectation can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t-1\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t-2\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right].

Note that when
t_{q}=t-1
,
z_{t}
is the output token. In addition, if we have
t=T
,
z_{t}
works as the second output token as well, which means that
z_{t}=k
and
x_{t_{q}}=w_{E}(k)
. If we find
t\neq T
, the trigger token is not
k
.
Thus, we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t-1\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}=t-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}=t-1)\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle\quad+P(t_{q}=t-1)\mathbf{1}\{t\neq T\}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{V-1}.

Next, we discuss
t_{q}=t
. Since
t_{q}
is the first trigger position, we always observe that
t_{q}\leq T-1
. If two trigger tokens are adjacent to each other, i.e.,
t_{q}=T-1
, we can state that
z_{T-1}=z_{T}=k
because of the equation
y=k
. Otherwise, the trigger token must belong to
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}
, and
\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}=0
. This gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}=t-1)\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}w_{E}(k).

For
t_{q}\leq t-2
, we again analyze the cases of
t=T
and
t\neq T
. Provided that
t=T
,
\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}=1
implies that the trigger token is decided to be
k
, while the output token is also
k
. This leads to more than two occurrences of trigger token, which violates the input sequence condition. Now, we assume
t\leq T-1
. This time, the trigger token
z_{t_{q}}
is not
k
, and
z_{t}
is not a trigger token, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t-2\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}\leq t-2)\mathbb{E}[w_{E}(z_{t_{q}})\cdot\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}

\displaystyle\quad\mid z_{t_{q}},y=k,t_{q}\leq t-2]\mid y=k,t_{q}\leq t-2]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}\leq t-2)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(q)}{V-1}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid z_{t_{q}}=q,y=k,t_{q}\leq t-2]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}\leq t-2)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(q)}{(V-1)^{2}}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Given a sequence
z_{1:T}
that satisfies Sec.
C.2
.
For any vocabulary
k\in\mathcal{V}
, and
t\in[1,T]\subset\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{P(t_{q}=t-1)}{t}(1+\mathbf{1}\{t=T\})w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\mathbf{1}\{t=T-1\}P(t_{q}=t)}{t}w_{E}(k)+O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right).

(40)

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
10
, we analyze each scenario depending on the value of
t_{q}
. Firstly, let us consider
t_{q}=t-1
. This leads to the fact that
z_{t}
is the output token. Given
y=k
, we have
z_{t}=k
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t-1\}\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=P(t_{q}=t-1)\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right].

In the event that
t=T
is valid,
x_{t_{q}}=x_{T}=w_{E}(k)
holds and the other
T-2
tokens are not
k
. Thus, it gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{x}_{1:T}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\left\{2w_{E}(k)+(T-2)\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{V-1}\right\}.

When
t\neq T
holds, we can say that
x_{t_{q}}\neq w_{E}(k)
, while
x_{t_{q}+1}=w_{E}(k)
because of
y=k
. This gives us

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1]=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{V-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x_{t_{q}+1}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1]=w_{E}(k).

Also, we can derive the following equations for
i\in[1,t]\setminus\{t_{q},t_{q}+1\}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x_{i}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}}P(z_{i}=j\mid\cdot)w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{V}\sum_{j=1}^{V}P(z_{i}=j,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}}P(z_{i}=j,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}\\
j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}\end{subarray}}P(z_{i}=j,\mid z_{t_{q}}=q,\cdot)

\displaystyle\qquad\times P(z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{(V-1)^{2}}.

In the above, we use the fact that
P(x_{i}=j,x_{t_{q}}=q)=0
if
j=q
or
q=k
holds.
To summarize, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2w_{E}(k)}{t}+\frac{t-2}{t}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{V-1}&\text{if $t=T$},\\
\frac{1}{t}\left\{w_{E}(k)+\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(q)}{V-1}\right\}\\
\quad+\frac{t-2}{t}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}\\
j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}\end{subarray}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{(V-1)^{2}}\right\}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(41)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{T}w_{E}(k)+O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right)&\text{if $t=T$},\\
\frac{1}{t}w_{E}(k)+O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Next, assume
t_{q}=t
. In this case,
t=T
implies
t_{q}=T
which is a contradiction. Furthermore,
t_{q}=t<T-1
implies
t_{q}<t_{q+1}<T
. Under this condition, we cannot have
z_{t}=k
, because it produces three appearances of the trigger token. Hence, it suffices to consider
t=T-1
. If
t_{q}=t=T-1
is true, we can state that
x_{t}=k
, leading to the formulation below:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left\{1w_{E}(k)+(T-2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq V\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{V-1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T-1}w_{E}(k)+O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right),

where the second equation follows from the same argument in the instance
t=T
in Eq.
41
, but this time
x_{T}
is not contained in
x_{1:t}
.

Lastly, we focus on
t_{q}=s\leq t-2
. We argue that the trigger token
z_{t_{q}}=z_{T}
is not
k
, due to the condition of the number of trigger token in the input sequence. This means that
t\neq T
and
z_{t}
does not serve as a trigger token. Now, we split
\bar{x}_{1:t}
into
\frac{1}{t}(x_{t_{q}}+x_{t_{q}+1}+x_{t}+\sum_{t^{\prime}}x_{t^{\prime}})
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}P(z_{t}=k,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(q)}{(V-1)^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t_{q}+1}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=P(z_{t}=k\mid\cdot)w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}P(z_{t}=k,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(k)}{(V-1)^{2}},

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{E}(k)}{V-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=P(z_{t}=k\mid y=k,t_{q}=s)w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{E}(k)}{V-1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t^{\prime}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}\\
j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}\end{subarray}}P(z_{t}=k,z_{t^{\prime}}=j,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}P(z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}}P(z_{t}=k,z_{t^{\prime}}=j\mid z_{t_{q}}=q,\cdot)w_{E}(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{1}{V-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{q\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{(V-1)^{2}}.

In short, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\bar{x}_{1:t}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]=O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right).

Summing up all the related terms leads to Eq.
40
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 12
.

z_{1:T}
that satisfies Sec.
C.2
.
For any vocabulary
k\in\mathcal{V}
, and
t\in[1,T]\subset\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}\leq t\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2w_{E}(k)}{T}\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}P(t_{q}=t-1)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2w_{E}(k)}{T}\mathbf{1}\{t=T-1\}P(t_{q}=t)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{T}\cdot O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right).

(42)

Proof.

We consider the following cases:

Case 1:

t_{q}=t-1
.

If
t=T
holds, this gives
z_{t_{q}}=z_{T}=y=k
and
\mathbf{1}\{z_{t-1}=k\}=\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}=1
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t-1}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]=w_{E}(k),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]=w_{E}(k).

Since
z_{t^{\prime}}
for
t^{\prime}\neq t_{q},t_{q}+1
is not a trigger token, it follows that
\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}=0
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}\neq t-1,t}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot\right]=0.

Otherwise, when
t\neq T
,
z_{t_{q}}=z_{T}
is not
k
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t-1}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot\right]=0.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{T}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot\right]=0.

Also,
z_{t_{q}}
is sampled from
\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t-1\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(j)}{V-1}.

For the rest of
t^{\prime}
, we note that
z_{t_{q}}\neq k
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}\neq t,t-1,T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid\cdot\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}\\
q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}\end{subarray}}P(z_{t^{\prime}}=k,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(q)

\displaystyle=(T-3)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(q)}{(V-1)^{2}}.

(43)

In summary, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=t-1\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P(t_{q}=t-1)}{T}\mathbf{1}\{t=T\}2w_{E}(k)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{P(t_{q}=t-1)}{T}\mathbf{1}\{t\neq T\}O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right).

(44)

Case 2:

t_{q}=t
.

For this scenario,
\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}=1
holds only when
t_{q}=t=T-1
. Thus, we can state that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t_{q}}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[x_{t_{q}}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t]=w_{E}(k),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{T}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=t]

For the other
t^{\prime}

\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}=0

In short, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{s}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{T}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]=0,

Similarly to Eq.

, we can derive that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[x_{t_{q}}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k,t_{q}=s]

In addition, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}\neq s,s+1,t,T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}x_{t_{q}}\right.

q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}\end{subarray}}P(z_{t^{\prime}}=k,z_{t}=k,z_{t_{q}}=q\mid\cdot)w_{E}(q)

\displaystyle=(T-4)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\{k\}}\frac{w_{E}(q)}{(V-1)^{3}}.

These equations give us that for
1\leq s\leq t-2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}\mathbf{1}\{t_{q}=s\}x_{t_{q}}\right.

(46)

\displaystyle\quad\left.\times\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k\right]

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{V^{2}}\right).

(47)

Gathering all the equations of
1\leq t_{q}\leq t
leads to Eq.
42
and we have proven the statement.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Given a sequence
z_{1:T}
that satisfies Sec.
C.2
.
For any vocabulary
k\in\mathcal{V}
, and
t\in[1,T]\subset\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{P(t_{q}=t-1)}{T}\mathbf{1}\{t\neq T\}O\left(\frac{1}{V}\right).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t^{\prime}}=k\}x_{t}\mathbf{1}\{z_{t}=k\}\mid y=k\right]

\frac{P(t_{q}=t-1)}{T}\left(1+\frac{T-3}{V-1}\right)w_{E}(k)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

D.3
Induction head without positional encoding

In this section, we provide the construction of a three-layer transformer with no positional encoding that achieves the induction head mechanism.

We first introduce the following theorem
[
29
]
stating that transformer can implement APE using one transformer block.

Theorem 4

(Theorem 1,
[
29
]
)
.

Let
z_{1:T+1}
be an input sequence of length
T+1
, where
z_{1}=\langle bos\rangle
. Then, there exist a transformer block consisting of an attention block and feed-froward network that recovers absolute positions
[1,2,\dots T+1]
and writes in the hidden state
H^{(1)}
.

In the proof, they prepare extra
3
dimensions for word embeddings to effectively reconstruct the positional information:

\displaystyle W_{E}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
a^{e}_{1,1}&a^{e}_{1,2}&a^{e}_{1,3}&\cdots&a^{e}_{1,V}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a^{e}_{d,1}&a^{e}_{d,2}&a^{e}_{d,3}&\cdots&a^{e}_{d,V}\end{pmatrix},

where
\langle bos\rangle
is represented by the first column without loss of generality.

Theorem
4
states that the hidden state
H^{(1)}
has the following form after the appropriate transformer block:

\displaystyle H^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&2&3&\cdots&T+1\\
a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}&\cdots&a_{1,T+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{d,1}&a_{d,2}&a_{d,3}&\cdots&a_{d,T+1}\end{pmatrix},

Now we show that a three-layer transformer with no positional encoding can implement induction head mechanism.

Proposition 6

(Restated)
.

There exists construction of a three-layer transformer without positional encoding that achieves induction head mechanism.

Proof.

The first layer of the transformer block is given by the construction in Theorem
4
, which gives us the hidden state:

\displaystyle H^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&2&3&\cdots&T+1\\
a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}&\cdots&a_{1,T+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{d,1}&a_{d,2}&a_{d,3}&\cdots&a_{d,T+1}\end{pmatrix},

where each entry denoted by
a_{*,*}
is a Gaussian random value to guarantee near-orthogonality.

Then, for the second transformer block, we use the following matrix weights.

\displaystyle W_{Q}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&C&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{V}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&w^{v}_{1,1}&\cdots&w^{v}_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&w^{v}_{d,1}&\cdots&w^{v}_{d,d}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{O}^{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&w^{o}_{1,1}&\cdots&w^{o}_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&w^{o}_{d,1}&\cdots&w^{o}_{d,d}\end{pmatrix},

where
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a constant value, and
w^{*}_{*,*}
denotes the Gaussian entry to guarantee the near-orthogonality.
With these matrix, the query and key vectors are represented by

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle=(1,0,\dots,0)^{\top},

\displaystyle k_{i}

\displaystyle=(C\cdot i,0,\dots 0)^{\top}.

Thus, the block outputs

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\sigma(\langle k_{i},q_{t}\rangle)W_{O}^{2}W_{V}^{2}h^{(1)}_{i}

(54)

\displaystyle\approx W_{O}^{2}W_{V}^{2}h_{t-1}^{(1)},

(55)

where the approximation holds for sufficiently large
C
and
h_{t-1}^{(1)}
is the
t-1
-th column of
H^{(1)}
. Here, we assume that the attention block uses strict causal attention, which attends only to positions in
\{1,2,\dots,t-1\}
for the token at position
t
.

Eq.
55
implies that the attention block attends only to the previous token, thus forming a previous token head. With the residual connection, the hidden state
H^{(2)}
is represented as:

\displaystyle H^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
1&2&3&\cdots&T+1\\
a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{1,3}&\cdots&a_{1,T+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{d,1}&a_{d,2}&a_{d,3}&\cdots&a_{d,T+1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\mathbf{0}&\Phi_{2}\mathbf{a}_{1}&\Phi_{2}\mathbf{a}_{2}&\cdots&\Phi_{2}\mathbf{a}_{T}\end{pmatrix},

where
\mathbf{a}_{i}=(a_{1,i},\dots,a_{d,i})^{\top}
and
\Phi_{2}
is the bottom right sub-matrix from
(W_{O}^{2}W_{V}^{2})_{4:d+3,4:d+3}
.

Finally, in the third transformer block, we set the following attention matrix weights:

\displaystyle W_{Q}^{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{K}^{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&w^{k^{\prime}}_{1,1}&\cdots&w^{k^{\prime}}_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&w^{k^{\prime}}_{d,1}&\cdots&w^{k^{\prime}}_{d,d}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{V}^{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&w^{v^{\prime}}_{1,1}&\cdots&w^{v^{\prime}}_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&w^{v^{\prime}}_{d,1}&\cdots&w^{v^{\prime}}_{d,d}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{O}^{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&0&w^{o^{\prime}}_{1,1}&\cdots&w^{o^{\prime}}_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

where

denotes the Gaussian entry to guarantee the near-orthogonality, and the sub-matrices in the bottom right of

and

are constructed the same way as

and

in Lemma

.
The result of this calculation is the same as in Lemma

We discuss the result shown in Fig.

Using Proposition
