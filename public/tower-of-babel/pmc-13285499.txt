Dynamic associations of BMI-metabolic phenotype transitions with incident cardiovascular disease in middle-aged and elderly adults: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Yuhan Zhao, Jiawei Li, Yiqun Miao, Rongrong Guo, Yuan Luo, Huiying Wang, Namuna Dallakoti, Ying Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cardiovascular risk conferred by BMI-metabolic phenotypes is dynamic. However, a comprehensive understanding of how longitudinal transitions influence distinct cardiovascular disease (CVD) subtypes remains limited. We aimed to systematically investigate the associations of BMI-metabolic phenotype transitions with the specific risks of incident heart disease and stroke, and to explore possible sex differences.MethodsThis longitudinal study included 6,274 participants aged ≥ 50 years and free of CVD from two prospective cohorts (CHARLS and ELSA). We defined 16 phenotype transitions based on changes in BMI-metabolic status between a baseline (T0) and a four-year follow-up assessment (T1). Using a landmark approach with follow-up commencing from T1, we used adjusted, stratified Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for incident composite CVD, heart disease, and stroke over a median of 5.0 years. The stable metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW→MHNW) group was the reference.ResultsPhenotype transitions were frequent. In the pooled six-group analysis, compared with the stable MHNW group, persistent metabolic unhealthiness and deterioration were the most consistent predictors of elevated composite CVD risk (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.72–2.41; and HR 1.57, 95% CI 1.29–1.90, respectively), whereas improvement was associated with attenuation but not complete elimination of excess risk (HR 1.33, 95% CI 1.07–1.65). In the more granular 16-transition analysis, associations were endpoint-specific: the transition from MHNW to MHOO was not associated with heart disease risk (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.52–1.28) but was associated with increased stroke risk (HR 2.59, 95% CI 1.44–4.63), and a similar excess stroke risk was observed for transition from MHOO to MHNW (HR 2.80, 95% CI 1.31–6.00).ConclusionsTransitions in BMI-metabolic phenotypes are associated with future cardiovascular risk in an endpoint-specific manner. Persistent metabolic unhealthiness and deterioration were the most consistent predictors of elevated composite CVD risk, whereas improvement did not always fully eliminate residual risk. More detailed endpoint-specific and sex-specific patterns should be interpreted cautiously, particularly where event numbers were limited. These findings underscore the importance of dynamic risk monitoring and suggest the need for prevention strategies tailored to specific cardiovascular endpoints and patient subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05928-0.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading global cause of morbidity and mortality, accounting for approximately 20.5 million deaths in 2021, up from 17.9 million in 2019 [1]. This growing burden underscores the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors for prevention [2]. Among these, obesity has emerged as a major concern due to its rising prevalence worldwide [3, 4]. However, the cardiovascular risk associated with obesity is not uniform. It is increasingly recognized that this risk is influenced by an individual’s underlying metabolic health, encompassing factors such as elevated blood glucose, blood pressure, and lipid abnormalities [5–7]. As a result, individuals with similar levels of adiposity can have markedly different cardiovascular risk profiles. This has led to debate over whether obesity itself or the accompanying metabolic dysfunction is the primary driver of cardiovascular events [8].

To better capture this interaction, previous studies have classified individuals into combined BMI-metabolic phenotypes. These include: metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW), metabolically healthy overweight or obese (MHOO), metabolically unhealthy normal weight (MUNW), and metabolically unhealthy overweight or obese (MUOO). Studies using this approach consistently show that the MHOO group has an intermediate CVD risk—higher than MHNW individuals but lower than those with metabolic abnormalities [9–11]. Notably, even individuals with normal BMI can have elevated CVD risk if they are metabolically unhealthy [12–15], challenging the assumption that normal weight is inherently protective.

Despite the utility of this classification, most existing research evaluates phenotypes at a single time point, ignoring the temporal variability of BMI and metabolic status [16–19]. Longitudinal evidence reveals that these phenotypes are dynamic; an individual’s metabolic and weight status can change significantly over time [20, 21]. For instance, metabolically healthy obese individuals may progress to metabolic dysfunction, increasing their CVD risk. Conversely, improvements in metabolic health or weight may reduce risk, although residual risk from prior metabolic insults may persist. These observations highlight the importance of capturing longitudinal phenotype transitions to more accurately predict cardiovascular risk.

However, the clinical implications of these dynamic transitions remain incompletely understood. Few longitudinal studies have systematically examined the full range of phenotype transitions and their association with CVD outcomes. Most existing research has been limited to select transitions or has grouped all cardiovascular events into a single composite outcome [22, 23]. This approach may mask important differences in risk associations, given that heart disease and stroke exhibit divergent epidemiological relationships with many risk factors. Whether dynamic phenotype transitions influence the risk of these two outcomes to the same extent remains unknown.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to address these evidence gaps by analyzing pooled data from large-scale, population-based longitudinal cohorts. We sought to systematically quantify the associations between all possible transitions in BMI-metabolic phenotypes and subsequent incident cardiovascular outcomes. Crucially, we hypothesized that the strength and direction of these associations would differ for heart disease and stroke. Therefore, we analyzed risks for these endpoints separately. Furthermore, given established sex differences in cardiovascular epidemiology, we conducted exploratory sex-stratified analyses to assess whether these risk associations were consistent between men and women. By providing this detailed epidemiological mapping of dynamic risk pathways, our study aims to generate more precise evidence to inform future research and targeted prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study utilized pooled data from two nationally representative, ongoing prospective cohort studies: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). To assess transitions in BMI-metabolic phenotypes, the analysis used two time points: a baseline assessment (T0) and a follow-up assessment (T1). Time points were selected based on two criteria: the availability of biomarker data and the goal of establishing comparable intervals between assessments in both cohorts. Accordingly, T0 in CHARLS was defined as Wave 1 (2011–2012) and T1 as Wave 3 (2015). To align with this interval, T0 in ELSA was defined as Wave 2 (2004–2005), its first wave with biomarker collection, and T1 as Wave 4 (2008–2009). A landmark design was used, in which BMI-metabolic phenotype transitions were defined between T0 and T1, and follow-up for incident cardiovascular outcomes started at T1 and continued through the latest available waves: Wave 5 (2020) in CHARLS and Wave 9 (2018–2019) in ELSA. Standardized questionnaires and biomarker protocols from the original cohort studies were used to ascertain demographic, clinical, and outcome data, as previously described [24, 25]. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of both cohorts (Peking University: IRB00001052-11015; London Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee: MREC/01/2/91), and all participants provided written informed consent.

The study population selection process is detailed in Fig. 1. Individuals aged ≥ 50 years who participated in baseline blood testing were initially considered. Exclusions were applied to participants with: (1) a baseline BMI < 18.5 kg/m² or missing BMI data; (2) insufficient data to determine metabolic status at either T0 or T1; (3) prevalent cardiovascular disease at T0 or incident cardiovascular disease occurring between T0 and T1; and (4) loss to follow-up before a CVD outcome could be ascertained after T1. Accordingly, all post-T1 analyses were restricted to participants with complete phenotype data at both T0 and T1 who remained free of cardiovascular disease through T1.

Definition of BMI-metabolic phenotypes

BMI-metabolic phenotypes were defined at both T0 and T1 by combining BMI categories with metabolic health status. To account for ethnic differences in body-fat distribution, cohort-specific BMI cutoffs were applied in accordance with established population-specific guidelines. For the CHARLS cohort, representing a Chinese population, normal weight was defined as a BMI of 18.5–23.9 kg/m² and overweight or obesity as a BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m² [26]. In the ELSA cohort, comprising a European population, normal weight was defined as a BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m² and overweight or obesity as a BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m² [27].

Metabolic health was assessed based on the presence of components aligned with established definitions of metabolic syndrome [28, 29]. Participants were classified as metabolically unhealthy if they met two or more of the following four criteria: (1) elevated blood pressure, defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg, or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication; (2) impaired glycemic control, defined as a fasting blood glucose ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.0%, or use of antidiabetic medication; (3) elevated triglycerides (TG), defined as a serum TG level ≥ 1.7 mmol/L or use of lipid-lowering medication; and (4) reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), defined as a serum HDL-C level < 1.03 mmol/L for men or < 1.29 mmol/L for women, or use of lipid-lowering medication.

By cross-classifying BMI and metabolic status, four distinct phenotypes were constructed: MHNW, MUNW, MHOO, and MUOO. The transition in phenotype was determined by comparing an individual’s classification at T0 with their classification at T1, yielding 16 possible transition pathways.

In addition to the 16 transition categories, six pooled transition groups were constructed for the pooled analyses: (1) stable MHNW (MHNW→MHNW); (2) stable MHOO (MHOO→MHOO); (3) persistently metabolically unhealthy phenotype (MUNW→MUNW, MUOO→MUOO, MUNW→MUOO, and MUOO→MUNW); (4) deterioration from metabolically healthy to unhealthy status (MHNW→MUNW, MHNW→MUOO, MHOO→MUNW, and MHOO→MUOO); (5) improvement from metabolically unhealthy to healthy status (MUNW→MHNW, MUNW→MHOO, MUOO→MHNW, and MUOO→MHOO); and (6) weight-change-related transitions (MHNW→MHOO and MHOO→MHNW).

Ascertainment of cardiovascular outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was incident composite CVD, defined as the first self-reported physician diagnosis of either heart disease or stroke during follow-up. The secondary outcomes were incident heart disease and incident stroke, analyzed as separate endpoints. All cardiovascular outcomes were identified through participant responses to structured, interviewer-administered questionnaires at each follow-up wave. Specifically, participants were asked, “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a heart disease, including angina, heart attack, congestive heart failure, and other heart problems?” and “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?” An affirmative response to the respective question was used to define an incident event for each outcome.

To improve the consistency of outcome classification, participant responses were cross-validated across subsequent waves. In cases where previously reported diagnoses were later contradicted, the complete longitudinal response history was reviewed, and discrepancies were resolved retrospectively. This approach to outcome ascertainment is consistent with established methods used in prior research [30–32]. Only events occurring after T1 were counted as incident outcomes in the present analysis. Follow-up time was calculated from the T1 assessment to the date of the first reported CVD diagnosis, death from any cause, or the last completed follow-up interview, whichever occurred first.

Covariate assessments

Covariates included in multivariable adjustment were selected a priori based on their established associations with both BMI-metabolic phenotypes and cardiovascular disease in the scientific literature. All covariates were assessed at baseline (T0) using standardized questionnaires. Demographic variables included age (treated as a continuous variable, in years), sex (female or male), marital status (categorized as married, separated, divorced, widowed, or never married), and educational level (grouped into less than high school, high school/vocational training, and tertiary education). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (categorized as non-smoker or current smoker) and alcohol consumption (categorized as non-drinker or current drinker).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Baseline (T0) characteristics of the study participants were summarized according to their initial BMI-metabolic phenotype, with continuous variables expressed as mean (± standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Characteristics of included and excluded participants were also compared descriptively.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between the 16 phenotype transitions (from T0 to T1) and incident outcomes. Both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted models were fitted. Follow-up time was calculated from the T1 assessment and served as the underlying time scale. The stable metabolically healthy normal weight (MHNW→MHNW) group was the reference category in all analyses.

The primary multivariable model was adjusted for a pre-specified set of baseline (T0) covariates: age, marital status, educational level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. To account for potential differences in baseline hazard functions across cohorts and by sex, all primary models were stratified by both study cohort (CHARLS or ELSA) and sex. The proportional hazards (PH) assumption was formally tested for each final model using Schoenfeld residuals. If a violation of the assumption was identified for an additional covariate (p < 0.05), it was also included as a stratification variable.

We used the 16-transition analysis to characterize detailed transition patterns, and the clinically consolidated six-group analysis to summarize overall associations with greater statistical stability and interpretability. Composite CVD was the primary endpoint; heart disease and stroke were analyzed as pre-specified secondary endpoints.

Several pre-planned sensitivity and subgroup analyses were conducted. First, a landmark sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding events that occurred within the first two years after T1 to reduce potential reverse causation from subclinical illness influencing recent phenotype change. Second, a pooled sensitivity analysis with additional adjustment for physical activity was conducted among participants with harmonized physical activity data available in both cohorts. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex to explore possible heterogeneity of associations. For exploratory assessment of sex heterogeneity, sex-stratified models were fitted and formal interaction tests were conducted in corresponding pooled models including a phenotype-transition × sex interaction term.

Phenotype transitions

A total of 6,274 participants with complete phenotype assessments at both T0 and T1 and free of cardiovascular disease through T1 entered the post-T1 transition analyses. Across the approximately four-year interval, considerable movement occurred across the four defined BMI-metabolic phenotypes (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2). The most frequent transition categories were MHNW→MHNW and MUOO→MUOO, whereas transitions such as MHOO→MUNW and MUOO→MHNW were relatively uncommon. Associations of phenotype transitions with incident cardiovascular outcomes

Over a median follow-up of 5.0 years from T1, a total of 1,363 composite CVD events, 1,090 heart disease events, and 396 stroke events were documented. Compared with the stable MHNW group, the adjusted hazard ratios for incident composite CVD were 1.43 (95% CI, 1.14–1.79) for stable MHOO, 2.04 (95% CI, 1.72–2.41) for persistently unhealthy, 1.57 (95% CI, 1.29–1.90) for deterioration, and 1.33 (95% CI, 1.07–1.65) for improvement, while the estimate for the weight-change group was directionally elevated but did not reach statistical significance (HR, 1.31; 95% CI, 0.97–1.75) (Table 2).

The associations between the detailed 16 phenotype transitions and incident cardiovascular outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table S4. Overall, the more granular results were broadly consistent with the pooled findings, although several categories had limited event counts and were interpreted cautiously. In the 16-transition analysis, most persistently unhealthy or worsening transitions were associated with elevated composite CVD risk. The highest risk was observed among individuals transitioning from MUNW to MUOO (HR, 2.34; 95% CI, 1.69–3.24). Conversely, participants who transitioned from MUNW to MHNW had a risk that was no longer statistically different from the stable MHNW reference group (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.75–1.39).

Distinct patterns emerged when heart disease and stroke were analyzed as separate outcomes (Supplementary Table S4). The transition from MHNW to MHOO was associated with a significantly increased risk for stroke (HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.44–4.63), but not heart disease (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.52–1.28). Similarly, transitioning from MHOO to MHNW was associated with a higher risk of stroke (HR, 2.80; 95% CI, 1.31–6.00), whereas no significant association was observed for heart disease (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 0.76–2.13). For individuals who improved from MUOO to MHOO, a residual increase in risk remained for both heart disease (HR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.21–2.18) and stroke (HR, 1.99; 95% CI, 1.12–3.52). Unadjusted associations from the pooled six-group and sixteen-group analyses are presented in Supplementary Table S5 and were broadly consistent with the adjusted results. Exploratory sex-stratified analyses showed broadly similar patterns in women and men and are presented in Supplementary Table S6.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of these findings against potential reverse causality, a landmark analysis excluding events within the first two years of follow-up was performed. The overall pattern of associations remained similar to that of the primary analysis, with persistently unhealthy and deterioration-related transitions continuing to show elevated composite CVD risk, whereas the estimate for the weight-change group remained non-significant. Notably, for stroke, the association for the pooled weight-change group remained elevated after excluding events within the first two years after T1 (HR 3.20, 95% CI 1.86–5.49). In addition, in the pooled sensitivity analysis with further adjustment for physical activity, the overall pattern of associations remained materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

This large, multinational longitudinal study shows that transitions between BMI-metabolic phenotypes are associated with long-term cardiovascular risk. The principal finding is that persistent metabolic unhealthiness and deterioration to less favorable phenotypes were consistently associated with elevated composite cardiovascular risk, whereas improvement was associated with attenuation, but not complete elimination, of excess risk. These results expand upon existing literature by emphasizing the importance of phenotype trajectory, rather than relying solely on a single static BMI-metabolic classification.

The clinical implications of phenotype improvement are also nuanced. In the pooled analysis, improvement was associated with lower risk than persistently unhealthy or deterioration-related trajectories, but risk was not uniformly normalized across all transitions. We hypothesize that reverse causation may partly explain the paradoxical increase in stroke risk observed with seemingly favorable weight loss, such as MHOO→MHNW. One possible explanation is that unintentional weight loss related to frailty, sarcopenia, or subclinical disease—factors that may themselves be associated with stroke risk—could contribute to this pattern [33, 34]. The persistence of the association in the landmark analysis suggests that reverse causation alone is unlikely to fully explain the observed excess stroke risk, although it may still contribute. In contrast, the near-complete risk reversal for heart disease among those transitioning from MUNW to MHNW is consistent with the potential importance of early metabolic improvement, although causal inferences cannot be made from the present observational data. However, the residual risk observed among those improving from MUOO to MHOO is compatible with the concept of “metabolic memory” [35–37]. This underscores the potential critical importance of early intervention before the onset of metabolic dysfunction, as the vascular damage incurred may not be fully reversible.

An additional observation of our study is the discordant association of certain transitions with incident heart disease versus stroke. The transition from MHNW to MHOO was associated with an increased risk of stroke but not heart disease in the detailed 16-transition analysis. This finding refines the understanding of the MHOO paradox by suggesting its cardiovascular impact is potentially heterogeneous across different vascular beds. While some large-scale studies have reported an elevated risk for composite cardiovascular events in MHOO individuals, few have dissected this risk by endpoint with a dynamic exposure assessment [38, 39]. Our results suggest that the adverse effects of weight gain in the setting of preserved metabolic health may be more apparent for stroke than for heart disease. Potential mechanisms could include direct effects of adiposity on blood pressure independent of metabolic syndrome criteria [40, 41], promotion of atrial fibrillation [42, 43], or subtle endothelial dysfunction and inflammation that precede systemic metabolic derangement [44, 45]. However, these detailed transition-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously, because several 16-group categories had limited event counts.

In sex-stratified analyses, several point estimates appeared higher in women than in men for heart disease, but formal tests for interaction did not provide strong evidence of heterogeneity. A similar directional pattern was seen for the stable MHOO state, although these subgroup-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously. These exploratory patterns may be broadly consistent with known sex differences in cardiac pathophysiology. For instance, women may be more susceptible to coronary microvascular dysfunction or heart failure with preserved ejection fraction following metabolic insults, mechanisms potentially linked to post-menopausal hormonal changes or differential inflammatory responses [46–48]. Although these exploratory patterns may be broadly compatible with known sex differences in cardiac pathophysiology, the lack of strong formal evidence for interaction means that such mechanistic interpretations remain speculative in the context of our data. These observations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, given the exploratory nature of the sex-stratified analyses and the limited number of events in several subgroup comparisons.

Our transition-based BMI-metabolic framework is conceptually related to, but distinct from, the recently proposed cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome staging model. CKM staging provides a broader multisystem clinical framework spanning adiposity, metabolic dysfunction, kidney disease, and established cardiovascular disease [49]. By contrast, our approach focuses specifically on the dynamic joint evolution of BMI category and metabolic health over time, consistent with prior longitudinal work showing that transitions between metabolically healthy and unhealthy obesity phenotypes are associated with later vascular and cardiovascular risk. This transition-based framework may be useful for characterizing short- to mid-term risk trajectories, but it does not capture kidney function or the broader multisystem progression emphasized in CKM syndrome [50]. Future studies should examine whether incorporating renal markers and CKM stages could further refine the interpretation and prediction of longitudinal phenotype transitions.

This study has several strengths, including its large, bi-ethnic population, its longitudinal design with a dynamic exposure assessment, and its detailed analysis of specific cardiovascular endpoints. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was an observational study, and the reported associations cannot establish causality; they should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Second, smoking and alcohol consumption were based on self-report and may be subject to misclassification. Third, although the primary 16-group transition analysis provided greater granularity, some specific transitions had relatively few events, particularly for stroke, which limited statistical power and the precision of subgroup-specific estimates. Fourth, residual confounding remains possible because information on the intentionality of weight change, harmonized dietary measures, and fully comparable physical activity data was not available across both cohorts; physical activity could only be examined in a subset-based sensitivity analysis. Fifth, selection bias cannot be excluded. The transition analyses required complete phenotype data at both T0 and T1 and excluded participants who developed incident CVD before T1, introducing the possibility of survival-related and complete-case selection. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, included participants tended to be younger and somewhat more socially advantaged than excluded participants, which may have attenuated the observed associations. Finally, phenotype transitions were assessed using only two discrete time points, which captured net change over the interval but could not reflect interim fluctuations or more complex longitudinal trajectories.

Conclusion

The trajectory of BMI-metabolic phenotype has emerged as a powerful and complex predictor of long-term CVD risk. In this pooled longitudinal analysis, persistent metabolic unhealthiness and deterioration to less favorable phenotypes were the most consistent predictors of elevated composite CVD risk, whereas improvement was associated with partial attenuation, but not complete elimination, of excess risk. More detailed endpoint-specific and sex-specific patterns should be interpreted cautiously, particularly where event numbers were limited. These findings emphasize that both the phenotypic state and its direction of change are key considerations in the effective prevention of cardiovascular disease.
