Arbitrary Boundary Conditions and Constraints in Quantum Algorithms for Differential Equations via Penalty Projections
Philipp Schleich, Tyler Kharazi, Xiangyu Li, Jin-Peng Liu, Alán Aspuru-Guzik, Nathan Wiebe

Abstract
Complicated boundary conditions are essential to accurately describe phenomena arising in nature and engineering. Recently, the investigation of a potential speedup through quantum algorithms in simulating the governing ordinary and partial differential equations of such phenomena has gained increasing attention. We design an efficient quantum algorithms for solving differential equations with arbitrary boundary conditions. Specifically, we propose an approach to enforce arbitrary boundary conditions and constraints through adding a penalty projection to the governing equations. Assuming a fast-forwardable representation of the projection to ensure an efficient interaction picture simulation, the cost of to enforce the constraints is at most O ⁡ ( log ⁡ λ ) O(\log\lambda) in the strength of the penalty λ \lambda in the gate complexity; in the worst case, this goes as O ( [ ∥ v ( 0 ) ∥ 2 ∥ A 0 ∥ + ∥ b ∥ L 1 ​ [ 0 ; t ] 2 ) ] t 2 / ε ) O([\|v(0)\|^{2}\|A_{0}\|+\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}^{2})]t^{2}/\varepsilon) , for precision ε \varepsilon and a dynamical system d d ​ t ​ v ​ ( t ) = A 0 ​ ( t ) ​ v ​ ( t ) + b ⁡ ( t ) \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=A_{0}(t)v(t)+b(t) with negative semidefinite A 0 ​ ( t ) A_{0}(t) of size n d × n d n^{d}\times n^{d} . E.g., for the heat equation, this leads to a gate complexity overhead of O ~ ​ ( d ​ log ⁡ n + log ⁡ t ) \widetilde{O}(d\log n+\log t) . We show constraint error bounds for the penalty approach and provide validating numerical experiments, and estimate the circuit complexity using the Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation.



I
Introduction

Ordinary and partial differential equations (ODEs, PDEs) describe many processes and phenomena in science and engineering. The modelling of these phenomena typically involves describing interactions of the system studied with its environment through ‘boundaries’, which imposes constraints on the solution of these equations. Hence, for a meaningful numerical solution it is crucial to represent such constraints and boundary conditions.
In addition, solving DEs on classical computers is cursed by dimensionality. Quantum algorithms for differential equations have been developed in order to mitigate exponential cost in the dimension.
Such algorithms are based on, e.g., encoding the solution in a linear system
Berry 2014
;
Berry et al. 2017
;
Berry and Costa 2022
;
Krovi 2023
;
Childs and Liu 2020
;
Childs et al. 2021a
, performing explicit ‘time-marching’
Fang et al. 2023
;
Bharadwaj and Sreenivasan 2024
, or making use of integral identities
An et al. 2022
;
An and Trivisa 2023
;
An et al. 2023a
;
An et al. 2023b
;
Jin et al. 2024a
;
Lu et al. 2025
. Recently, algorithms that map the ODE to a Lindblad evolution has been explored in
Shang et al. 2024
, drawing from quantum algorithms that are able to simulate open quantum systems.
Particular systems studied are mostly focused around popular models from classical differential equations courses such as the Poisson equation
Cao et al. 2013
or more general elliptic equations
Childs et al. 2021a
;
Bagherimehrab et al. 2023
, the advection equation
Novikau and Joseph 2025
or the wave equation
Costa et al. 2019
and also the Maxwell equations
Jin et al. 2024b
or nonlinear equations such as reaction diffusion equations
Liu et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Krovi 2023
;
Costa et al. 2023
or fluid dynamics
Li et al. 2025
;
Bharadwaj and Sreenivasan 2025
;
Penuel et al. 2024
.
Most studies so far focus on input models that assume a structured geometry with no (or, periodic) boundary conditions which limits applicability.
Imposing the boundaries in the encoding of the generator of the dynamics is possible for several techniques
Childs et al. 2021a
;
Novikau et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2024c
;
Liu et al. 2023
, based on other classical techniques such as complex absorbing potentials, perfect matched layers or Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps
Jin et al. 2024d
or analytic continuation
Kharazi et al. 2025
. However, there are some practical limitations to this in the way it can affect the respective access model of the system matrix, e.g. the complexity of a circuit construction of a specific block-encoding. Additionally, it is not immediately obvious how to approach boundary conditions in the recently popular Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulations (LCHS) algorithm
An et al. 2023a
;
An et al. 2023b
, which is promising due to (near-)optimal scaling both in the number of state-preparation queries as well as the system matrix queries.
Furthermore,
Mangin-Brinet et al. 2024a
considers the implementation of complex absorbing potentials, where the main underlying dynamics are unitary.
Beyond the specific concept of boundary conditions, we may be interested in more general constraints.

In this work, we implement boundary values or constraints on evolution equations by separating the dynamics into unconstrained and constrained dynamics using perturbation theory.
Our method is also capable to model interface conditions, which was also explored in the context of quantum algorithms in
Jin et al. 2024c
, as well as Robin boundary conditions.
Conceptually, shows connections to a wide range of existing approaches that relate to perturbation methods and penalty methods
Luenberger et al. 2016
.
Primarily, there are ties to ‘complex absorbing potentials’ for quantum dynamics
Muga et al. 2004
, as previously used in quantum algorithms for non-unitary dynamics
An et al. 2023a
;
Mangin-Brinet et al. 2024b
.
Another interesting study in the simulation of acoustic waves uses counter-acting waves to annihilate the solution
Tsynkov 2003
– imagine numerical noise-cancelling headphones.
Specifically, our approach adds a projection scaled by
\mathrm{i}\lambda
to the generator of a dynamical system. A physical interpretation of this may be seen as measurement with (very fast) frequency
\lambda
on the domain of the projector, similar to the quantum Zeno effect. Thereby, our bounds also closely resemble respective bounds related to the Zeno effect, generalized adiabatic dynamics and the rotating-wave approximation
Burgarth et al. 2019
;
Burgarth et al. 2022
.
General results from perturbation theory suggest what we are looking for is a small signal-to-noise ratio
Van der Sluis 1975
. For an ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}{v(t)}=A_{0}v(t)
, this can be envisioned as the systems ‘energy’ divided by the strength of the perturbation,
\frac{|\langle v(0),A_{0}v(0)\rangle|}{\lambda}
; this gives us intuition about what necessary frequency in the perturbation to expect. Our bounds in
Section

II.2
confirm this intuition.
Using interaction-picture simulation, which is possible if the projection is chosen to be fast-forwardable as will be the case as we demonstrate later, we can expect a modest overhead of
O(\log(\lambda))
to solving differential equations (DEs) when adding constraints in this manner.

Within this work, we focus on ordinary differential equations under constraints. This is equivalent to readily discretized partial differential equations under boundary conditions. We further focus on evolution equations of the kind
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=A(t)v(t)+b(t)
and a “time” parameter
t\geq 0
and will consider stationary boundary value problems in future work.

I.2
Problem Setting

Within this work, we consider the solution of ODEs given constraints, using a quantum algorithm.
To motivate the setting, let
\sf A
be an operator on continuously differentiable functions on some finite-dimensional vector space, let
v(t):[0,T]\to\mathds{C}^{n^{d}}
, and let
\sf P:\mathds{C}^{n^{d}}\to\mathds{C}^{n^{d}}
be a linear constraint function, so that
{\sf P}(v_{\rm bad})=\lambda v_{\rm bad}
with some penalty
\lambda>0
if the constraint is not satisfied and
{\sf P}(v_{\rm good})=0
if it is. Then, the kernel of
{\sf P}
spanned by
v_{\rm good}
makes up the constraint-admissible subspace a degenerate eigenspace of
{\sf P}
with eigenvalue
\lambda>0
spanned by
v_{\rm bad}
forms the constraint-inadmissible subspace. We seek a solution
v(t)
to the constrained ODE such that
v(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)

\displaystyle={\sf A}\big(v(t)\big)

(1)

\displaystyle{\sf P}(v(t))

\displaystyle=0,

which implies that we want
v_{\rm bad}(t)=0
.
Our nomenclature follows penalty methods in constrained optimization
Luenberger et al. 2016
: A penalty function is a function that satisfies (i)
{\sf P}
is continuous, (ii)
{\sf P}(x)\geq 0
for any valid input
x
, and (iii)
{\sf P}(x)=0
if and only if
x
is in the feasible region, i.e., it satisfies the constraint.
Then, an optimization problem via such a penalty function has as limit point the solution to the constrained optimization problem. Later on, we outline that the problem setup in
7
indeed satisfies these requirements.
Moreover, there is an equivalence between stationary solutions of a dynamical system and optimization. Quantum algorithms for this purpose exist, as in
Catli et al. 2025
, and could be extended by the penalty projections in our work to satisfy constraints.

We are particularly interested in settings where the constraint arises from boundary conditions on spatially discretized PDEs. In this case,
{\sf P}
can take the form of a projector being applied to either the solution or its derivative to enforce Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions respectively on a subset of grid points.
Dirichlet boundary conditions are conditions that constrain the solution to follow specific values, whereas Neumann conditions constrain the derivative (within domains in 3D space, typically the surface normal derivative). Linear combination of Dirichlet and Neumann condition leads to what is typically called Robin boundary conditions.

II.1
Motivation

Now, we discuss a method to efficiently implement the solution of such boundary problems on a quantum computer.
To that end, we take inspiration from the so-called complex absorbing potentials in quantum physics and chemistry
Muga et al. 2004
.
Note that for the case when
Eq.

6
has
A=-\mathrm{i}H
anti-Hermitian and operator and a normalized initial state, the dynamics of
\psi(t,x)
follows the Schrödinger equation, i.e.,

-\mathrm{i}\partialderivative{t}\psi(t,x)=H{\psi(t,x)}.

(8)

Then, a modification of the Hamiltonian with
\lambda>0

H\to H-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}

(9)

leads to enforcing that
\psi(t,x^{\prime})\to 0
for all
x^{\prime}\in\Gamma
on the boundary in the limit of
\lambda\to\infty
if
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}=\int_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\outerproduct{x'}{x'}
. In particular, we want
\lambda\gg\norm{H}
. This is easy to see, as then in the solution

\psi(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(H-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}})t}\psi(0,x),

(10)

components of the solution
\psi(t,x)
on
\Gamma
are suppressed to zero. The rest of
\psi(t,x)
remains unchanged.

In what follows, we will show that the modification in
Eq.

9
in the case for boundary conditions over more general, not necessarily quantum dynamics such as in
Eq.

6
can work the same way.
In a general setting, we consider the following modified dynamics governed by a (potentially time-dependent) system matrix
A_{0}(t)
and a forcing term
b(t)
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=(A_{0}(t)-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}})v(t)+b(t).

(11)

Compared to the case of complex absorbing potentials in
Eq.

9
,
A_{0}(t)
need not be Hermitian. As we will be working with orthogonal projections
{\mathsf{P}_{c}},{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{\perp}}
exclusively, this implies their Hermiticity; a more careful analysis could relax the assumptions to non-Hermitian penalty functions in future work. Additionally, a time-dependent constraint region can be thought of via a
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t)
.

We make the following observation here.
In the case of quantum dynamics,
Eqs.

8
and
9
, a perturbation of the form
\mathrm{i}\lambda
as in complex absorbing potentials “dissipates” the wavefunction on the boundaries.
Our approach in
Eq.

11
with positive
\lambda
does not add dissipation per se.
Rather, we can understand this e.g. under the realm of the rotating-wave approximation, where the evolution under a Hamiltonian
H=H_{0}+H_{1}
shows a separation of time-scales with respect to dynamics generated by
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
, respectively. Then, one can say that the solution coming from the perturbative generator is negligible with respect to the initial dynamics in the sense that the on the unperturbed system’s time-scale, the highly oscillatory perturbation averages to zero. In other words, the solution on the infeasible region then is perceived negligible compared to the solution on the feasible region.

Our analysis follows perturbation theoretic arguments, which is reasonable given the perturbation strength
\lambda
is chosen to be much larger than the system
\norm{A_0}
and forcing
\|b(t)\|
. For the following arguments, we change the point-of-view to strong perturbation, in the sense that we consider the system dynamics generated by
A_{0}
as a perturbation of the constraint projection
-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
with perturbation parameter
\frac{1}{\lambda}
.
Then, a natural way to estimate the error is to consider ‘transition elements’ with respect to the projections
{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{\perp}},{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
, as expectations
\langle v(t^{\prime}),{\sf P}v(t)\rangle
. For
t=t^{\prime}
, the Kubo formula
Kubo 1957
gives a natural pathway to find an estimate. We are interested in a non-Hermitian formulation, which was developed in
Sticlet et al. 2022
and further discussed in
Geier and Hauke 2022
. Building on this, we provide a generalization that also covers time-dependent generators for the unperturbed dynamics, and a forcing term.
This enables the remainder of our analysis enforce the constraints.

Before stating the result, we introduce some context and notation. We are looking at a dynamical system
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=A(t)v(t)+b(t)
, where
A(t)=H(t)+\zeta V(t)
so that
V(t)
is a perturbation of the original dynamics
H(t)
in the sense that
\zeta\|V(t^{\prime})\|\ll\|H(t^{\prime})\|
at all times. For now,
A(t),H(t),V(t)
are arbitrary complex, square matrices and we take
1\gg\zeta>0
and note that this can also be simply reformulated to cases of where the scaling term is time-dependent.
The Kubo formula allows to estimate the difference between the expectation of an observable
P
with respect to a perturbed compared to an unperturbed solution vector – in our more general case, the quadratic form of a matrix. We will use the following quantities:

•

Let
\overline{V}(t,t^{\prime})
be the cumulative perturbation in the interval
[t^{\prime};t]
for
t^{\prime}\leq t
,
\overline{V}(t,t^{\prime}):=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,V(\tau)
.

•

We denote by
T_{t}(\cdot)
the time propagation of the argument (borrowing notation from dynamical semi-groups). In
Proposition

6

T_{t}:=\mathcal{T}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\exp(\int_s^t \D s' H(s'))
.
Then, the solution to the inhomogeneous problem is given by
T_{t}(v(0)\delta(t)+b(t))
with the Dirac-delta distribution
\delta(t)
.

•

We use
{\sigma}(t^{\prime},t)
for the outer product of solutions at times
t^{\prime},t
, i.e.,
{\sigma}(t^{\prime},t):=v(t^{\prime})v(t)^{\dagger}
. This notion is similar to the density matrix of pure quantum states, however with non-normalized complex vectors

Proposition 6

(A non-Hermitian, time-dependent, inhomogeneous Kubo formula)

Let

be the solution to

Further, suppose we are interested in measuring the expectation value of a matrix

Then, the effect due to perturbation
\zeta V(t)

\langle P(t)\rangle-\langle P\rangle_{0}={\zeta}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\frac{{\rm tr}\left[\left\{P,\overline{V}(t,t^{\prime})\sigma(t^{\prime},t)\right\}_{\sim}\right]}{{\mathrm{tr}}\left[\sigma(t,t)\right]}+O\big(\zeta^{2}\big)

(12)

where

Remark 3

(Original Kubo formula)
.

For completeness, we briefly restate the original Kubo formula, as derived in
Kubo 1957
, with adapted notation. We are given Hermitian
H,V(t)
as well as a Hermitian observable
P
, so the dynamics of a Schrödinger equation through the Hamiltonian
H
is perturbed by time-dependent
V(t)
. Then, with
P(t)
the observable evolved in the Heisenberg picture,

\langle P\rangle_{t}-\langle P\rangle_{0}=-\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\langle[P(t),V(s)]\rangle_{0},

(13)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
denotes the expectation with respect to the unperturbed dynamics and
P(t)
is the Heisenberg-evolved observable with respect to the perturbed dynamics.

We point out the following differences compared to
Proposition

6

Sticlet et al. 2022
;
Geier and Hauke 2022
), as well as replacing the commutator by a (modified) anticommutator. Additionally, time-dependence of the original dynamics induce the time-cumulative perturbation
\overline{V}
and the definition of

allows to add inhomogeneities to the solution.

The proof of
Proposition

6
is given in
Appendix

.
In the next section, we obtain tighter bounds on the error for special cases – such as time-independent
A_{0}
or
b(t)=0
– with less general technique than
Proposition

6
. Therefore, we are looking into specific bounds under different sets of assumptions.

Lemma 9

(Error in the infeasible space under stable dynamics)

We consider a system as in

the projector on the infeasible space as defined in

We have ODE and solution as

(22)

\left<v(0), \exp(At)^\dagger\exp(At) A v(0)\right>

under the assumption that

\left|\left<v(0),\exp(At)^{\dagger}\exp(At)Av(0)\right>\right|\leq\left\|\exp(A t)^{\dagger}\exp(At)\right\|\left|\left<v(0),Av(0)\right>\right|

\absolutevalue{
\left<v(t) ,A_0 v(t)\right> -\imag\lambda\left<v(t), \projc v(t)\right>}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{
\left<v(t), A_0 v(t)\right> -\imag\lambda\left<v(t) ,\projc v(t)\right>}

\displaystyle\geq\absolutevalue{
\abs{\left<v(t) ,A_0 v(t) \right>} - \abs{\lambda\left<v(t) ,\projc v(t)\right> }

\displaystyle\geq-\absolutevalue{\left<v(t) ,A_0 v(t)\right>}+\lambda\left<v(t),{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)\right>.

∎

Lemma 10

(Error in the infeasible space under dissipative and normal dynamics with an inhomogeneity)

We consider a system as in
5
with dynamics generated by
A=A_{0}-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
, where the real parts of the eigenvalues of
A_{0}
are non-positive,
\lambda>0
and
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
the projector on the infeasible space as defined in

3

Here, we further consider an inhomogeneity

and

Then, the solution error at time

(28)

Proof.

We have ODE and solution as

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=Av(t)+b(t),\quad v(t)=\underbrace{\mathrm{e}^{At}v(0)}_{v_{\text{h}}(t)}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{As}b(t-s)}_{v_{\text{p}}(t)}

This allows us to express a quantity

\displaystyle\left<v(t),Av(t)\right>=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left<v_{\text{h}}(t),Av_{\text{h}}(t)\right>}_{=:(i)}+\underbrace{\left<v_{\text{h}}(t),Av_{\text{p}}(t)\right>+\left<v_{\text{p}}(t),Av_{\text{h}}(t)\right>}_{=:(ii)}+\underbrace{\left<v_{\text{p}}(t),Av_{\text{p}}(t)\right>}_{=:(iii)}.

(30)

The purely homogeneous term follows from

\lvert(i)\rvert=\absolutevalue{\left< v_\mathrm{h}(t), A v_\mathrm{h} (t)\right>}\leq v_{\max}^{2}\norm{A_0}.

Now, we bound the second and third term for the case of time-dependent

Recall that the particular solution is

By assumption,
\exists B>0,\max_{t}\norm{b(t)}_{\ell_{2}}\leq B

\absolutevalue{\left< b(t), A b(t)\right> }\leq B^{2}\norm{A}\;\forall t>0

Next, we consider the third term that comes only from the particular solution,

\displaystyle\lvert(iii)\rvert=\left|\left<v_{\mathrm{p}}(t),Av_{\mathrm{p}}(t)\right>\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\left<\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\mathrm{e}^{As_{1}}b(t-s_{1}),A\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\mathrm{e}^{As_{2}}b(t-s_{2})\right>\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\left<b(t-s_{1}),\mathrm{e}^{A^{\dagger}s_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{As_{1}+A(s_{2}-s_{1})}Ab(t-s_{2})\right>\right|

\displaystyle B^{2}\norm{A_0}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\norm{ \e^{A^\dagger s_1} \e^{As_1 + A(s_2-s_1)}}

\displaystyle B^{2}\norm{A_0}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{1}\norm{\e^{As_1}}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s_{2}\norm{\e^{A(s_2-s_1)}}

t^{2},&\hat{\mu}_{R}(A)=0.\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lvert(ii)\rvert

t,&\hat{\mu}_{R}(A)=0.\end{cases}

\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\to\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}+\frac{1}{\lambda}A_{0}(t)

\frac{\|A_{0}(t)\|}{\lambda}\ll 1

We consider the homogeneous case with

Lemma 12

(Error in the infeasible space under stable dynamics with a time-dependent generator)
.

Here, we have the same assumptions as in
Lemma

, however with a time-dependent generator of the dynamics in the sense that
A(t)=A_{0}(t)-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}

Furthermore, the solution to

(43)

This leads to the following error bound in the infeasible space,

\norm{v(t)}^{2}_{\ell_{2},S_{c}}\leq\varepsilon

Proof.

For the time-dependent case, we make use of Kubo’s formula. Kubo’s formula was first introduced for Hamiltonian dynamics as a linear response result given an expectation value of an operator

,

\delta\langle P(\tau)\rangle=-\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\langle[P(\tau),V(\tau^{\prime})]\rangle_{0}

(45)

for an a constant term

and a time-dependent perturbation

so that the time-dependent generator is

\mathrm{i}H(t)=\mathrm{i}(H_{0}+V(t)).

(46)

Expectations are defined with respect to the current state (referring to the time of the operator),
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}\sim\langle v(t),(\cdot)v(t)\rangle
or the unperturbed expectation
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}\sim\langle(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0)),(\cdot)(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0))\rangle
and are normalized with respect to the unperturbed evolution
v^{(0})(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0))
, so that

\langle P(t)\rangle\eqsim\langle P(t)\rangle_{0}+\delta\langle P(t)\rangle.

(47)

Using a correction as in
Eq.

45
, this is an identity due to the fundamental theorem of calculus and not a first-order approximation.
The dynamics we are interested in stem from

A(\tau)=-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}+A_{0}(\tau);

(48)

note that
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
takes the role of
H_{0}
above in
Eq.

46
. We have a constant constraint projection and a time-dependent
A_{0}(t)
.
There exist generalizations of Kubo’s result for non-Hermitian systems
Pan et al. 2020
and including time-dependency
Sticlet et al. 2022
. Then, we need to express
Eq.

45
more generally using a linear response function
\chi_{PQ}(t,t^{\prime})
for an operator
P
whose expectation we are interested in and a perturbation term
Q
,

\delta\langle P(\tau)\rangle=-\mathrm{i}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi_{PQ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}),

(49)

and neither the original generator nor the perturbation need to be Hermitian. Note that compared to
Sticlet et al. 2022
, we do not separate the perturbation into constant operator and time-dependent forcing that can be pulled out of the response function.
In our case, the constraint projection is Hermitian however, which will lead to a simplification of the general response function form
(
Sticlet et al. 2022
, Eq. (4))
that resembles
Eq.

45
up to a modified commutator
[P,Q]_{\sim}:=PQ-Q^{\dagger}P
, and does not require constant re-normalization.
Note that in
Sticlet et al. 2022
, the generator corresponding to
Eq.

48
is expressed as
-i\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}+\mathrm{i}A_{0}(\tau)
, leading to the commutator expression. Hence, in our case and corresponding to what we will be seeing in
Proposition

6
, the commutator needs to be replaced with an anticommutator
\{P,Q\}_{\sim}:=PQ+Q^{\dagger}P
.
This results in
\chi_{PQ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\mathrm{i}\;\mathbf{1}_{[\tau\geq\tau^{\prime}]}\langle\{P(\tau),Q(\tau^{\prime})\}_{\sim}\rangle_{0}
, or when the dynamics are defined without the imaginary unit in front of
A_{0}(t)
, then
\chi_{PQ}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\mathbf{1}_{[\tau\geq\tau^{\prime}]}\langle\{P(\tau),Q(\tau^{\prime})\}_{\sim}\rangle_{0}
so that

\delta\langle P(\tau)\rangle=\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\left<\{P(\tau),Q(\tau^{\prime})]\}_{\sim}\right>_{0}

(50)

Then, we rephrase the setup as strong perturbation with small
\zeta=\frac{1}{\lambda}
,

A(t)=-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}+\zeta A_{0}(t),

(51)

that we can treat with the Kubo formula.
We look for

\left|\left<P(t)\right>_{t}-\left<P(t)\right>_{0}\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left<[P(t),\zeta A_{0}(\tau)]_{\sim}\right>_{0}\right|.

(52)

Now, recall that expectations are normalized
with respect to unperturbed
unitary
evolution
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau}v(0)
, so that

\left<P(\tau)\right>_{\tau}=\frac{\left<v^{(0)}(\tau),P(\tau)\,v^{(0)}(\tau)\right>}{\left<v^{(0)}(\tau),v^{(0)}(\tau)\right>}=\frac{\left<e^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau}v(0),P(\tau)e^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau}v(0)\right>}{\left<e^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau}v(0),e^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau}v(0)\right>}.

(53)

This is less straightforward if the original dynamics are not unitary, as e.g. would be the case if
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
is
not
orthogonal.
We can use this to continue with
Eq.

52
,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\left<P(t)\right>_t - \left<P(t)\right>_0}

\displaystyle={\rm\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:intermediary-on-kubo-before-dissipation}}\leq\zeta\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left|\frac{\left<\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0),\{P(t),A_{0}(\tau)\}_{\sim}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0)\right>}{\left<e^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0),e^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(0)\right>}\right|

by
Eq.

53

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left\lVert\{P(t),A_{0}(\tau)\}_{\sim}\right\rVert.

(54)

which satisfies the inequality,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{\left<P(t)\right>_t - \left<P(t)\right>_0}\leq\zeta t\max_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\norm{\{P(t), A_0(t')\}_\sim}.

(55)

Next, we can use that the observable of choice is
P(t)={\mathsf{P}_{c}}
, which is constant over time.
Furthermore, the initial conditions are adapted to the constraint and
\langle{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\rangle_{0}=0
consequently. The expectation
\langle{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\rangle
is normalized with respect unperturbed dynamics, thus

\left|\langle{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t)\rangle-\langle{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\rangle_{0}\right|=\left|\frac{\langle v(t),{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)\rangle}{\langle v^{(0)}(t),v^{(0)}(t)\rangle}\right|\leq(\cdot)\Leftrightarrow\left|\langle v(t),{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)\rangle\right|\leq(\cdot)v_{\max}^{2}.

Remembering that
\zeta=\lambda^{-1}
, we can conclude that the final error is given by

\left|\langle v(t),{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)\rangle\right|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}tv_{\max}^{2}\max_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\norm{[\projc, A_0(t')]_\sim}.

(56)

∎

We point out that the framework of Kubo’s formula would also allow us to look at time-dependent strengths of the projection, which may allow to make the overall choice more optimal, and also time-dependent forms of the constraint projection
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
. This is left up to future research.

Lemma 13

Under the same assumptions as in

Proof.

in this setting.

(59)

with

,

(60)

In our setting, we have that

Furthermore, we also use
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
as the observable to measure the

As now, the unperturbed dynamics are unitary,

\displaystyle\sigma(t^{\prime},t)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau^{\prime}}(v(0)+b(\tau^{\prime}))(v^{\dagger}(0)+b^{\dagger}(\tau))\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}\tilde{v}(t^{\prime},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t},

(61)

and therefore we have that

\overline{V}(t,t^{\prime})\sigma(t^{\prime},t)=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,A_{0}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}\tilde{v}(t^{\prime},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}.

(62)

Then, the anti-commutator

\displaystyle\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\left[{\mathsf{P}_{c}}A_{0}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}\tilde{v}(t^{\prime},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\tilde{v}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}A_{0}^{\dagger}(s){\mathsf{P}_{c}}\right].

(63)

Computing the trace of this expression, we get for the left term,

\displaystyle\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm\mathrm{tr}}\left[{\mathsf{P}_{c}}A_{0}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}\tilde{v}(t^{\prime},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\right]=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}w^{\dagger}(\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t-\tau)}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}A_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})}w(\tau^{\prime})

(64)

end for the right term

\displaystyle\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,{\rm\mathrm{tr}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\tilde{v}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}A_{0}^{\dagger}(s){\mathsf{P}_{c}}\right]=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}w^{\dagger}(\tau^{\prime})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(\tau^{\prime}-t^{\prime})}A_{0}^{\dagger}(s){\mathsf{P}_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t-\tau)}w(\tau)

(65)

When we add the two terms together, we can re-label

to add them directly and use that
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
commutes with its time evolution,

\displaystyle\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}w^{\dagger}(\tau)\left({\mathsf{P}_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t-\tau)}A_{0}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime})}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t-\tau)}A_{0}^{\dagger}(s)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime})}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\right)w(\tau^{\prime}).

(66)

Next, we additionally add the integral over
t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}w^{\dagger}(\tau)\left({\mathsf{P}_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t-\tau)}A_{0}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime})}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t-\tau)}A_{0}^{\dagger}(s)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}(t^{\prime}-\tau^{\prime})}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\right)w(\tau^{\prime}).

(67)

We continue as follows: We apply the Cauchy-Schwartz-inequality to
Eq.

67
after taking the absolute value. Then, for each summand, we commute the
\tau,\tau^{\prime}
-
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
-evolutions ‘outwards’ to the

. The order here does not matter insofar as the

-norm that arises from the inner product is unitarily invariant.
Indeed, if we have two terms
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau^{\prime}}w(\tau^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\tau^{\prime}}w(\tau^{\prime})
(there are always

on the
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
-subspace.
Hence this does not pose a complication in application of the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality as the obtained upper bound holds.
Additionally, we use a similar argument regarding mis-aligned complex phases to re-express the matrix norm through the modified anti-commutator
\{X,Y\}_{\sim}=XY+Y^{\dagger}X
.
Hence,

Eq.

67

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,w(\tau)\Bigg\|_{\ell_{2}}\,\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,w(\tau^{\prime})\Bigg\|_{\ell_{2}}\,\left\|\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\left({\mathsf{P}_{c}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}A_{0}(s)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}A_{0}^{\dagger}(s)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t^{\prime}}{\mathsf{P}_{c}}\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,w(\tau)\Bigg\|_{\ell_{2}}\,\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\,w(\tau^{\prime})\Bigg\|_{\ell_{2}}\,\left\|\left\{{\mathsf{P}_{c}},\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,A_{0}(s)\right\}_{\sim}\right\|

\displaystyle{\scriptstyle t^{\prime}\leq t}\quad

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,w(\tau)\Bigg\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\,\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\left\|\left\{{\mathsf{P}_{c}},\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,A_{0}(s)\right\}_{\sim}\right\|

(68)

Recall that
w(t)=v(0)\delta(t)+b(t)
.
Thus, we want to estimate the norm of

(69)

We obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,w(s)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle v(0),v(0)\rangle+2\real\left\langle v(0),\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,b(s)\right\rangle+\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,b(s)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{v(0)}_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+2\norm{v(0)}_{\ell_{2}}\norm{b(s)}_{L^{1}([0,t])}+\norm{b(s)}^{2}_{L^{1}([0,t])},

(70)

where

for

, which implies that
\|\int_{W}\mathrm{d}s\,f(s)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\|f(s)\|_{L^{1}(W)}^{2}

To make notation shorter, we recall that
\left\|v(0)\right\|_{\ell_{2}}=:v_{\max}
(by

) and define
\norm{b(s)}_{L^{1}([0,t])}=:B_{L^{1}}
.
Therefore,

Eq.

70

\displaystyle\leq\left(v_{\max}^{2}+v_{\max}B_{L^{1}}+B_{L^{1}}^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,s\left\|\left\{{\mathsf{P}_{c}},A_{0}(s)\right\}_{\sim}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(v_{\max}^{2}+v_{\max}B_{L^{1}}+B_{L^{1}}^{2}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{2}\max_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\norm{\l\{\projc, A_0(t')\r\}_\sim}.

(71)

To conclude, this means that

\left|\langle v(t),{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)\rangle\right|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(v_{\max}^{2}+v_{\max}B_{L^{1}}+B_{L^{1}}^{2}\right)\frac{t^{2}}{2}\max_{0\leq t^{\prime}\leq t}\norm{\l\{\projc, A_0(t')\r\}_\sim}.

(72)

II.3
Application to Discretized Partial Differential Equations

The type of constraints we are mainly interested in in this work are constraints coming from boundary conditions in the discretization of linear, evolutionary partial differential equations.
Namely, for
\mathcal{L}
a linear differential operator, then for
t\in\mathds{R}_{+}
and
r\in\Omega\subseteq\mathds{R}^{d}
, and
v(t,r)
comes from a function space so that the PDE with respect to
\mathcal{L}
is well-posed,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{v}(t,r)-\mathcal{L}v(t,r)

\displaystyle=f(t,r),

\displaystyle r\in\Omega

\displaystyle v(t,r)

\displaystyle=g(r,t),

\displaystyle{r\in\Gamma_{D}}

(73)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{n},\,v(t,r)\rangle

\displaystyle=h(r,t),

\displaystyle{r\in\Gamma_{N}}

\displaystyle v(0,r)

\displaystyle=v_{0}(r).

(74)

The solution lives on a domain
\Omega
and constrained to a specific value on the Dirichlet boundary
\Gamma_{D}
or a normal derivative on the Neumann boundary
\Gamma_{N}
.
Robin boundary conditions, which specify a linear combination of derivative and value at the boundary can be obtained as a corollary, see
Section

II.4.3
.

In the context of
7
, we have that on a discretized version of
v(t,r)
,
{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{D}}
projects onto the domain
\Gamma_{D}
,
{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{N}}
applies a backwards difference stencil along the surface normal on
\Gamma_{N}
(for details see
Section

II.3.2
) and the differential operator
\mathcal{L}
becomes
A_{0}(t)
. There are no further assumptions beyond linearity on
\mathcal{L}
so far, though most of our error bounds in the previous section require that the for the discretized operator,
\real(A_{0})\preceq 0
.

The methodology we developed so far is able to enforce

and
h_{(}t)=0
, assuming access to boundary projections
{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{D}},{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{N}}
.
Thus, we next consider how to deal with non-zero values as this is an essential aspect of the utility of our method.

II.3.1
Non-zero Dirichlet boundaries

We start by considering the problem in
Eq.

73
, assuming the condition on
\Gamma_{D}
.
Let us further assume for simplicity that
\overline{\Omega}=[0,L]^{d}
. We will construet a function

that satisfies the constraint on
\Gamma_{D}
in (
73
). For the one-dimensional case,
d=1
, this can be found quite easily:

u(r,t)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{r}{L}g(L,t)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{r}{L}\right)g(-L,t).

(75)

Extending this to arbitrary dimension
d\geq 1
follows naturally

u(r,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{r_{i}}{L}g(r,t)\big|_{r_{i}=L}+\left(1-\frac{r_{i}}{L}\right)g(r,t)\big|_{r_{i}=-L}.

(76)

Now, let us consider a new function

v^{\prime}(r,t)=v(r,t)-u(r,t),

(77)

and compute the following,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\prime}(r,t)-\mathcal{L}v^{\prime}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}v(r,t)-\mathcal{L}v(r,t)-\partial_{t}u(r,t)+\mathcal{L}u(r,t)

\displaystyle=f(r,t)+\mathcal{L}u(r,t)-\partial_{t}u(r,t)

(78)

\displaystyle=:\widetilde{f}(r,t)

(79)

where

\partial_{t}u(r,t)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\frac{r_{i}}{L}\partial_{t}g(r,t)\big|_{r_{i}=L}+\left(1-\frac{r_{i}}{L}\right)\partial_{t}g(r,t)\big|_{r_{i}=-L}.

(80)

The initial condition becomes

v^{\prime}(r,0)=v(r,0)-u(r,0)=v_{0}(r)-u(r,0)=v_{0}(r)

(81)

and the boundary condition

v^{\prime}(r,t)\big|_{r\in\Gamma_{D}}=g(r,t)-\underbrace{v^{\prime}(r,t)\big|_{x\in\Gamma_{D}}}_{=g(r,t)}=0.

(82)

Therefore, the differential equation for
v^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\prime}(r,t)-\mathcal{L}(t)v^{\prime}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{f}(r,t),\quad r\in\Omega,t>0

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(r,t)

\displaystyle=0\quad r\in\Gamma_{D},t\geq 0,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(r,0)

\displaystyle=v^{\prime}_{0}(r)\quad r\in\overline{\Omega},t=0.

(83)

That means that the PDE in
u(r,t)
now has Dirichlet boundary conditions of value zero.
The solution
v(r,t)
to the original problem can then be obtained by

v(r,t)=v^{\prime}(r,t)+u(r,t).

(84)

Therefore we easily obtain the following corollary to
Lemma

10
and

13
for the case of a suitable discretization with error at most

, where then the differential operator
\mathcal{L}
has discrete representation
A_{0}
.

II.4.2
Projectors for Neumann conditions and derivative constraints

To obtain a finite-dimensional approximation to a Neumann projection, we employ a discrete finite-difference derivative
{\sf D}
.
As observed in the statement of
5
, a crucial ingredient for our method to work is that the projection
{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{N}}
for the approximate derivative condition is orthogonal to, and thereby commutes with, the projection onto the feasible region and the projection onto the value constraint region. We discuss next how these requirements are satisfied by a simple finite difference stencil evaluated on two points assuming a grid discretization.
The motivation is that this allows us to enforce that the value on the boundary has the same value as the next-closest value within the domain in the direction of interest.

First, we look at a one-dimensional example. Take

Consequently, what we want to enforce is that

.
A swap operation

That means it will allow us to project onto elements that are unacceptable and we can annihilate them.
As we want orthogonal projections as per assumptions in

We continue by discussing how to create the projector for

A general solution vector

Interface conditions via ‘derivative projection’.

The finite difference construction to approximate Neumann conditions is also useful to represent interface conditions, i.e., conditions between regimes that are governed by different PDEs.
Suppose there are
\eta
subdomains which represent a different physical model (expressed by a different linear PDE)

(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}_{1})u_{1}(x;t)=0,\;x\in\Omega_{1},\quad\ldots,(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}_{\eta})u_{\eta}(x;t)=0,\;x\in\Omega_{\eta},

(124)

with

; one can also think of more general relationships.
The numerical treatment of interface conditions in classical numerical methods is described e.g. in
Cen and Zhang 2015
.
Our approach somewhat resembles the ‘penalty method’ described there, in the sense that it also introduces a notion virtual work that is minimized by admissible solutions.

In order to use ‘Neumann projections’ as in

103

{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm interface}
and neighbour sets
\bigcup_{\bm{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm interface}}\zeta_{\bm{j}}

Using a grid discretization of the computational domain, then there are no more ‘true’ boundary points that are in
\Omega_{k}\cap\Omega_{l}
but every point is in either
k
or
l

Upon proper definition of these sets and the according boundary oracles following
Eq.

96

{\mathsf{P}_{c}^{N}}

II.4.3
Input Model for Robin Boundary Conditions

The ability to implement value constraints and derivative constraints allows us to extend the applicability to another class of boundary conditions.
Constraints described by a superposition of Dirichlet and Neumann conditions are called Robin boundary conditions
Gustafson and Abe 1998
; they can be expressed as

\alpha v(x;t)+\beta\,\partial_{n}\cdot v(x;t)=f(x;t),\quad x\in\Gamma_{R},

(125)

with constants
\alpha,\beta
.
Therefore, we can choose a projection

{\sf P}_{c}^{R}=\alpha\sum_{\bm{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}}}\outerproduct{\bm j}{\bm j}+\frac{\beta}{2}\left(\mathds{I}-\prod_{\bm{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}},\bm{k}\in\zeta_{\bm{j}}}\mathrm{SWAP}(\bm{j},\bm{k})\right),

(126)

and additionally assume that
\alpha,\beta\in\mathds{R}
so that
{\sf P}_{c}^{R}
is Hermitian.
The point-wise projection
\sum_{\bm{j}\in{\mathcal{I}}_{R}}\outerproduct{\bm j}{\bm j}
maps any bitstrings from
\mathcal{I}_{\Omega\cup\Gamma}
to those inside the Robin-boundary
\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}}
, whereas the SWAP-circuit takes any from
\mathcal{I}_{\Omega\cup\Gamma}
to those that are considered neighbours for the Robin boundary
\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}\cap\Omega}=\bigcup_{\bm{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}}}\zeta_{\bm{j}}
.
Therefore, the commutativity of these operations depends on how the sets are defined.
One option is to use ‘outside’ ghost points for the finite differences in the Neumann projections for the rather than points inside the domain, and include these to the image of the Dirichlet projection.
Then, the operations commute restricted to the domain of interest (that is, disregarding the ghost points) and one can compile
\exp(-\imag\lambda t {\sf P}_c^R)
with a product formula

\exp(-\imag\lambda t {\sf P}_c^R)=\exp(-\imag\alpha\lambda t \sum_{\bm j\in{\mc I}_{\Gamma_R}}\op{\bm j} )\exp(-\imag\frac{\beta}{2}\lambda t (\identity-{\sf S}_R)),

(127)

or equivalently

{\rm HamSim}({\sf P}_{c}^{R},\lambda t)={\rm HamSim}({\mathsf{P}_{c}^{D}}|_{\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}}},\alpha\lambda t){\rm HamSim}({\mathsf{P}_{c}^{N}}|_{\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{R}}},\beta\lambda t),

(128)

where we know how to implement both from above,
Eqs.

100
and
121
.

In the following, we are presenting numerical experiments as proof-of-concept validation of the penalty projections method via interaction picture based on classical simulation of the dynamics. In addition, we are interested in tightness of our bounds on

.
To that end, we consider the heat equation and the wave equation as two of the canonical examples for ‘simple’ PDEs.
While for the heat equation, a quantum speedup beyond a quadratic one coming from amplitude amplification cannot be expected in general when evaluating an expectation value with respect to the final state (see

In the computations below, we restricted the possible range of

IV
Quantum Algorithm via Interaction-Picture simulation

Recall that the constrained ODEs we consider in this work have the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=A_{0}(t)v(t)-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)+b(t)\equiv A(t)v(t)+b(t),

(140)

with initial data
v(t=0)=v_{0}
and an orthogonal projection
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
.
The general solution to this via the variation of parameters formula is

v(t)=\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}A(s)\mathrm{d}s}v_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}A(s^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}b(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(141)

There exist many quantum DE solvers that we could employ here; a common trait among all of them is that they depend (in the optimal case, linearly) on the block-encoding or subnormalization factor
\alpha_{A}\geq\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\lVert A(t)\rVert
, which itself, by
Eq.

140
, is linear in
\lambda
. As we saw before in
Section

II.2
, a necessary penalty factor is at least of the size
\lambda\gtrsim v_{\max}^{2}\frac{\lVert A\rVert}{\varepsilon}
, which would render implementations of the constraint projection impractical.

We can overcome this limitation by simulating
Eq.

140
in the interaction frame of the projection. Then, the overhead in
\lambda
compared to unconstrained evolution is only logarithmic.
The reason for this is that an interaction picture transformation is unitary, thereby
\alpha_{A_{I}}=\alpha_{A_{0}}
, where
A_{I}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}A_{0}(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}
. The logarithmic overhead in the gate complexity is expected as the number of grid-points to simulate the interaction picture dynamics depends on the maximum frequency
(
Low and Wiebe 2018
, Lemma 5)
, as
\|\partial_{t}A_{I}(t)\|=O(\lambda)
.
A necessary condition of this to work efficiently is that the cost of the interaction picture transformation itself, i.e.,
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}
, does not depend on
\lambda
.
We know from
Section

II.4
that
{\mathsf{P}_{c}}
is fast-forwardable by construction – which makes the setup suited for such an interaction picture implementation
Low and Wiebe 2018
.

Therefore, let
{v_{I}}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(t)
.
A quick calculation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)

(142)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}v(t)+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}(A-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}A\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}}_{={A_{I}}(t)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}v(t)=A_{I}(t)v_{I}(t).

(143)

This means that if we solve for

v_{I}(t)=\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{0}^{t}A_{I}(s)\mathrm{d}s}v_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{T}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{s}^{t}A_{I}(s^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}b_{I}(s)\mathrm{d}s=:S_{I}(t)v_{I}(0),

(144)

we can retrieve the sought-after solution
v(t)
through implementing

{\rm HamSim}({\mathsf{P}_{c}},-\lambda)\underbrace{S_{I}(t)}_{\text{ODE solver}}{\rm HamSim}({\mathsf{P}_{c}},+\lambda).

(145)

IV.1
Time Evolution by LCHS

We use the near-optimal Linear Combination of Hamiltonian simulations approach
An et al. 2023a
;
An et al. 2023b
as a quantum ODE solver.
For our purposes, this choice is more natural to tackle ODEs or discretized PDEs compared to Schrödingerisation
Jin et al. 2024a
;

The LCHS theorem in

allows to write the (time-ordered) exponential of a general matrix

as an integral of a unitary time evolution with a specific kernel function

where the real and imaginary part of a matrix

A_{\real}=\frac{A+A^{\dagger}}{2},\quad A_{\imaginary}=\frac{A-A^{\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}.

(147)

Duhamel’s formula gives an extension to particular solutions given a source term
An et al. 2023a
.
In our case, the ODE system is defined by the interaction picture matrix

A_{I}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}(A_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}(A_{0,\real}+A_{0,\imaginary})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda{\mathsf{P}_{c}}t}.

(148)

We note that
An and Trivisa 2023
provides an algorithm of LCHS with interaction picture, however in a different setting.
In our case, the operator for the interaction frame itself is a Hamiltonian simulation and does not require treatment by the LCHS formula.
Furthermore, in
An and Trivisa 2023
, the authors are using LCHS with the non-optimal kernel function from
An et al. 2023b
which does not achieve high accuracy.
Choosing

would recover the approach in
(
An and Trivisa 2023

which could easily be extended to the higher-order quadratures and improved kernel function of

Before we can do so, we look at the impact of the penalty projection.

IV.2.3
Implications for the solution of discretized PDEs with boundary conditions

Typically, when quantifying the cost of numerical methods, we are interested in the effect of the particular choice of discretization given a target error on the final cost ( with a ‘finer’ discretization meaning more basis functions and thus more accurate representation). In our work, this ‘refinement’ is expressed by a number of grid points

Upon choosing a suitable numerical method, there is also a relationship of approximation error with respect to the discretization.
The example below estimates the complexity through the number of discretization points

for the case of inhomogeneous solutions in

Example 2

(Overhead due to constraint for discrete heat equation)

V
Conclusion and Outlook

We present a quantum algorithm that uses a penalty projection to enforce constraints such as boundary conditions in evolutionary discretized partial differential equations. Particularly, our approach enables arbitrary constraints and boundary conditions for solving differential equations via the LCHS algorithm.
The penalty projections have the advantage that they do not rely on adjustments of the block-encoding of the system matrix, which have the potential to make the algorithm more complicated when compiled to gates.
Moreover, our approach is able to tackle a very general class of constraints and boundary conditions, as well as interface conditions.
Assuming that the constraint projections are orthogonal, our approach only requires a few steps of fast-forwardable Hamiltonian simulation in addition to the usual ODE solution and interaction picture simulation of the constraint comes at a modest logarithmic overhead in the gate complexity when using the LCHS algorithm
An et al. 2023a
;
An et al. 2023b
as ODE solver. The necessary penalty strength goes at most as
O\left(\frac{t^{2}}{\varepsilon}(\max_{t^{\prime}}\|v(t^{\prime})\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\|b\|_{L^{1}}^{2})\right)
; for the example of the heat equation, we can show that the final gate complexity overhead grows at most as
\widetilde{O}(d\log n+\log t)
for
n^{d}
degrees of freedom.
In a simple scenario of a uniform grid, directly enforcing constraints in the system matrix also attains a simple form for linear-systems based and time marching DE solvers. Yet, the algorithmic consequences, especially for LCHS, are not immediately obvious.

Looking ahead, it would be interesting to work out specific costings involving also constant factors of the presented method as compared to other approaches to enforce constraints presented in the literature.
Beyond this, extensions to other constraints that admit decomposition into orthogonal subspaces, such as more general symmetries, seem like a straightforward extension. Less obvious but even more interesting are symmetries with non-local support or situations that lead to non-orthogonal projections, where it is not clear if fast-forwarding in the interaction frame is possible. One could envision this within the framework of scattering theory and build on, e.g.,
Riss and Meyer 1993
.
Furthermore, evaluating the performance on numerical examples of higher practical relevance would be an interesting subject of study.
In particular, we found that our analytical error bounds were somewhat loose compared to the witnessed error in the simulation results of the heat and wave equations. Hence, one could look into exploring analytical methods that would enable tighter bounds, or perform a more thorough analysis of constant factors involved.

\displaystyle\zeta\Bigg(\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left\langle w(\tau),{\overline{V}}^{\dagger}_{I}(t,\tau)T_{t}^{\dagger}PT_{t}w(t)\right\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left\langle w(t),T_{t}^{\dagger}PT_{t}{\overline{V}}_{I}(t,\tau)w(\tau)\right\rangle\Bigg)

Moving along, we define a ‘density’

\nu[A](t,\tau):=\left(T_{t}[A]v(t)\right)\left(T_{\tau}[A]v(\tau)\right)^{\dagger}.

(203)

If we omit the generator in square brackets of
\nu[H]=\nu

.
That means that

\displaystyle\zeta\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,{\rm tr}\left[\nu(\tau,t)^{\dagger}\overline{V}^{\dagger}(t,\tau)T_{t-\tau}^{\dagger}P+PT_{t-\tau}\overline{V}(t,\tau)\nu(\tau,t)\right]

\displaystyle=\zeta\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,{\rm tr}\left[\left\{P,\;T_{t-\tau}\overline{V}(t,\tau)\nu(\tau,t)\right\}_{\sim}\right]=:\zeta\mathds{P}(t)

Then,

\left\langle T_{t}v_{I}(t),T_{t}v_{I}(t)\right\rangle=\left\langle v_{I}(t),T_{t}^{\dagger}T_{t}v_{I}(t)\right\rangle

(205)

Thus, we can treat

analogously to the nominator
\langle v_{I}(t),P_{I}(t)v_{I}(t)\rangle
but replacing

\displaystyle\left\langle w_{0}(t)+\zeta w_{1}(t),T_{t}^{\dagger}T_{t}(w_{0}(t)+\zeta w_{1}(t))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle w_{0}(t),T_{t}^{\dagger}T_{t}w_{0}(t)\right\rangle+\zeta\left(\left\langle w_{1}(t),T_{t}^{\dagger}T_{t}w_{0}(t)\right\rangle+\left\langle w_{0}(t),T_{t}^{\dagger}T_{t}w_{1}(t)\right\rangle\right)+O\big(\zeta^{2}\big)

(206)

where
\mathds{T}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\tau\,{\rm tr}\left[\left\{\mathds{I},\,\overline{V}(t,\tau)\sigma(\tau,t)\right\}_{\sim}\right]
by

.

Appendix D
A lower bound on the final solution norm for the discrete heat equation

Lemma 19
.

Given
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(t)=D\mathbf{L}_{h}v(t)+b(t)
with
v,b\in\mathds{R}^{n^{d}}
and
\mathbf{L}_{h}\in\mathds{R}^{n^{d}\times n^{d}}
a discrete Laplacian in periodic boundary conditions. Thus we also have that
\exists\,\mu_{\min}<0
so that
-\infty\prec\mu_{\min}\mathds{I}\preceq\mathbf{L}_{h}\preceq 0
. Additionally, this means that
\|v(s)\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\|v(0)\|_{\ell_{2}}
for any
0\leq s\leq t
.
Furthermore, in this Lemma, we assume that
b(s)\geq 0
for
0\leq s\leq t
.
Then,

\norm{v(t)}_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq\|v(t)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq 2\mathrm{e}^{D\mu_{\min}t}\|v(0)\|_{\ell_{2}}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}

(212)

Proof.

By the governing equation, we have

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\norm{\e^{D\mathbf{L}_h t} v(0) + \int_0^t\D s\, \e^{D\mathbf{L}_h s} b(s) }_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle v(0),\mathrm{e}^{2D\mathbf{L}_{h}t}v(0)\rangle+2\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\left\langle v(0),\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}s}b(s)\right\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\left\langle b(s^{\prime}),\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}(s+s^{\prime})}b(s)\right\rangle

(213)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{(I)+(II)+(III)}

We can obtain a lower bound using a reverse Cauchy-Schwartz-type inequality due to Pólya and Szegő
(
Hardy et al. 1934
, p. 62, §71)
,
which says that for bounded vectors
x,y
with positive entries,

\langle x,y\rangle^{2}\geq C\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle

(214)

with a constant

\textstyle C=\frac{1}{4}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\max\{x\}\max\{y\}}{\min\{x\}\min\{y\}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\min\{x\}\min\{y\}}{\max\{x\}\max\{y\}}}\right)^{2}.

(215)

For the case of the discretized heat equation here with the solution signifying temperature, the positivity assumption on
v(t)
holds, for the forcing term we invoke a positivity assumption for the sake of this argument. This is physically reasonable when we think of a heat source. In the case of strong heat sinks, we may expect the solution to fully decay to zero (and not become negative to remain physical); we can ensure non-negativity by shifting the solution by
\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}
.
Then, applying the inequality on term (II),

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}t}v(0),\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}s}b(s)\right\rangle^{2}\geq C\left\langle v(0),\mathrm{e}^{2D\mathbf{L}_{h}t}v(0)\right\rangle\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\left\langle b(s^{\prime}),\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}(s+s^{\prime})}b(s)\right\rangle.

(216)

Using this expression in
Eq.

213
,

\displaystyle\norm{v(t)}_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq\mathrm{(I)}+C\sqrt{\mathrm{(I)}}\sqrt{\mathrm{(III)}}+\mathrm{(III)}

(217)

In addition,

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mu_{\min}t}\min_{j}[v(0)]_{j}

\displaystyle\leq[\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}t}v(0)]_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j}[v(0)]_{j}\quad\forall\,j\in[n^{d}]

(218)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mu_{\min}t}\cdot t\min_{j,0\leq s\leq t}[b(s)]_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{D\mathbf{L}_{h}s}b(s)

\displaystyle\leq t\max_{j,0\leq s\leq t}[b(s)]_{j}\quad\forall\,j\in[n^{d}].

(219)

For convenience, we define the amplification factor
Q:=\frac{\max_{j}[v(0)]_{j}\max_{k,s}[b(s)]_{k}}{\min_{j}[v(0)]_{j}\min_{k,s}[b(s)]_{k}}
, noting that
Q\geq 1
, and obtain for the constant (where
\mu_{\min}<0
)

C=\frac{1}{4}\left(\underbrace{\mathrm{e}^{-\mu_{\min}t}\sqrt{Q}}_{>1}+\underbrace{\mathrm{e}^{\mu_{\min}t}\sqrt{Q^{-1}}}_{<1}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2\mu_{\min}t}}{4Q}.

(220)

Now,

\displaystyle\norm{v(t)}_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{(I)}+\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2\mu_{\min}t}}{4Q}\mathrm{(I)}\,\mathrm{(III)}}+\mathrm{(III)}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{2D\mu_{\min}t}\left(\|v_{0}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}+\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}^{2}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2D\mu_{\min}t}\|v_{0}\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}^{2}}{4Q}}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{e}^{D\mu_{\min}t}\left(2+\frac{1}{\sqrt{Q}}\right)\|v_{0}\|_{\ell_{2}}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}

(221)

In the second inequality, we use the bounds
\mathrm{(I)}\geq\mathrm{e}^{2D\mu_{\min}t}\|v(0)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}
and
\mathrm{(III)}\geq\mathrm{e}^{2D\mu_{\min}t}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}^{2}
.
The third inequality follows from bounding the arithmetic with the geometric mean,
\frac{x+y}{2}\geq\sqrt{xy}
.
Recall that
Q\geq 1
to obtain

\|v(t)\|_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq 2\mathrm{e}^{D\mu_{\min}t}\|v(0)\|_{\ell_{2}}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0;t]}.

(222)

∎
