Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty for Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension in an Adolescent With a Novel Frameshift Variant in PROS1
Authors: Hiroyuki Fujii, Sarasa Isobe, Yu Taniguchi, Yuichi Tamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is rare in adolescents, and evidence regarding balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) in young patients with hereditary thrombophilia remains limited. A 16year‐old male was diagnosed with CTEPH and protein S deficiency, characterized by markedly low activity and antigen levels. Genetic analysis revealed a novel PROS1 frameshift variant (c.53del, p.Val18GlyfsTer69) predicted to cause loss of function. Despite anticoagulation and riociguat therapy, pulmonary hypertension persisted, and pulmonary angiography demonstrated predominantly peripheral organized thrombotic lesions. The patient underwent staged BPA at ages 16 and 17, with an additional session at 22. All procedures were completed without complications and were associated with sustained improvement in hemodynamics and exercise capacity during long‐term follow‐up. This case highlights the importance of considering hereditary thrombophilia in adolescent patients with CTEPH and suggests that staged BPA may be a safe and effective treatment option in selected patients with predominantly peripheral disease.

Introduction

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) predominantly affects adults, and cases in children and adolescents are uncommon. Consequently, evidence regarding optimal treatment strategies in younger patients is limited, particularly in those with hereditary thrombophilia. In addition, genetically characterized cases associated with novel PROS1 variants have rarely been reported. We describe an adolescent with recurrent venous thromboembolism and CTEPH associated with congenital protein S deficiency due to a novel frameshift variant in PROS1, who was successfully managed with staged BPA over long‐term follow‐up.

Case Description

A 16‐year‐old male had a history of lower‐extremity pain accompanied by dyspnea and syncope requiring emergency transport. Lower‐extremity venous ultrasonography demonstrated extensive deep vein thrombosis extending from the left femoral vein to the popliteal and calf veins, and contrast‐enhanced computed tomography showed pulmonary arterial thrombi. Unfractionated heparin was initiated. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient of 40 mmHg and diastolic interventricular septal flattening, suggesting pulmonary hypertension. Ventilation–perfusion scintigraphy showed wedge‐shaped perfusion defects with preserved ventilation. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) of 28 mmHg and a pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of 6.48 Wood units. Pulmonary angiography showed organized distal thrombotic lesions, including webs and abrupt narrowing, consistent with CTEPH. Anticoagulation was subsequently transitioned to warfarin, and riociguat was initiated.

Laboratory testing at the time of the patient's initial presentation, before initiation of anticoagulation therapy including heparin or warfarin, revealed markedly reduced protein S activity (17%; reference range, 60.0%–150.0%), as measured by a clotting assay, and a borderline protein S antigen level (66%; reference range, 65.0%–135.0%), as measured by a latex agglutination immunoassay. Genetic testing identified a novel PROS1 frameshift variant, c.53del (p.Val18GlyfsTer69), which introduces a premature termination codon and is predicted to result in loss of function (Figure 1A). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria [1], this variant was interpreted as likely pathogenic. Family screening performed after the patient was diagnosed with protein S deficiency demonstrated reduced protein S activity (23%) and a mildly reduced protein S antigen level (61%) in the patient's father, who had no history of venous thromboembolism. Genetic testing was not performed in the father. In addition, the patient's grandfather had a history of venous thromboembolism, although neither protein S testing nor genetic analysis had been performed. These findings suggested a familial thrombophilic background. A pedigree of the family is shown in Figure 1B.

At 6‐month follow‐up, pulmonary hypertension persisted despite anticoagulation and riociguat therapy (mPAP 24 mmHg and PVR 4.23 Wood units), and pulmonary angiography continued to show residual organized thrombotic lesions (Figure 1A). The 6‐min walk distance was 425 m. After multidisciplinary discussion, the lesions were considered predominantly peripheral, and staged BPA was selected. The patient underwent BPA at 16 and 17 years of age, primarily targeting bilateral lower lobe segmental branches, including the right A8–10 and left A6, A8, and A10 branches. At 18 years, hemodynamics had improved (mPAP 22 mmHg and PVR 1.34 Wood units), and they remained stable at 20 years of age (mPAP 21 mmHg and PVR 2.03 Wood units). At 22 years, mild hemodynamic worsening was observed (mPAP 24 mmHg and PVR 2.23 Wood units) in association with residual organized lesions. A third BPA session targeting the left A3 branch and residual lower lobe branches (left A8–10 and right A8) was subsequently performed. At 23 years, mPAP improved to 21 mmHg and PVR to 1.62 Wood units (Figure 1C), and the 6‐min walk distance increased to 499 m. All BPA procedures were completed without complications. The patient remains on lifelong anticoagulation and continues regular outpatient follow‐up.

Discussion

This case illustrates adolescent‐onset CTEPH associated with congenital protein S deficiency and a novel frameshift variant in PROS1, with successful long‐term management using staged BPA. Reports of genetically characterized PROS1‐associated CTEPH are rare, particularly in adolescents. The present case therefore has value at the intersection of young‐onset CTEPH, hereditary thrombophilia, and catheter‐based therapy.

The occurrence of CTEPH in pediatric and adolescent populations is rare. In the original report describing BPA, a case involving a 14‐year‐old patient was included [2]; however, detailed reports describing the clinical course of early‐onset CTEPH remain limited. Madani et al. reported favorable outcomes of pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) in 17 patients aged 18 years or younger with CTEPH, of whom 88% had underlying thrombophilic conditions [3]. Furthermore, Lian et al. reported that approximately 5.2% of patients with CTEPH had protein S deficiency [4], indicating that congenital thrombophilia represents an important predisposing factor. Protein S deficiency is known to increase the risk of both initial and recurrent venous thromboembolism [5], and incomplete thrombus resolution followed by organization after acute pulmonary embolism has been recognized as an important mechanism in the development of CTEPH [6].

The identified PROS1 variant, c.53del (p.Val18GlyfsTer69), causes a frameshift at codon 18 and introduces a premature termination codon at residue 69. Because loss of function is an established disease mechanism for PROS1 [7, 8], this variant is predicted to impair normal protein S production. Additionally, this variant fulfills the PVS1 criterion at a very strong level, in accordance with the recommendations of the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group [9]. This variant has not been reported in ClinVar and is absent from gnomAD, thereby fulfilling the PM2 criterion and supporting its novelty. The molecular impairment (Figure 1A) is consistent with the biochemical phenotype of markedly reduced protein S activity with reduced antigen levels and supports congenital protein S deficiency as the underlying thrombophilic background in this patient.

Management of CTEPH requires an individualized multimodal approach, including PEA, BPA, and pulmonary hypertension‐targeted medical therapy [10]. In the present case, pulmonary angiography demonstrated predominantly distal organized lesions, and staged BPA was associated with hemodynamic and functional improvement without procedural complications. The need for an additional BPA session in young adulthood despite initial improvement highlights the importance of long‐term surveillance in young patients with CTEPH, especially when hereditary thrombophilia may contribute to recurrent thrombotic burden (38% vs. 1%–4%) [3].

This report has limitations. As a single case, it cannot establish a definitive causal relationship between the PROS1 variant and the development of CTEPH. In addition, long‐term outcome data for BPA in adolescents and young adults remain limited. Nevertheless, this case suggests that staged BPA can be safely performed from adolescence into young adulthood and may be an effective treatment option in selected patients with predominantly peripheral CTEPH associated with congenital thrombophilia.
