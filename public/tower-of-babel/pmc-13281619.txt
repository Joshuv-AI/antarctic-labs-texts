Acarbose modulates microglial Pkm2 acetylation to reshape immunometabolism and preserve retinal neurons after ischemia-reperfusion
Authors: Yuwen Wen, Ya-nan Dou, Xiaohong Chen, Xiuxing Liu, Zhenlan Yang, Yingting Zhu, Zhidong Li, Caibin Deng, Ye Deng, Wenru Su, Yehong Zhuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinal ischemia-reperfusion (IR) elicits microglia-driven neuroinflammation and mitochondrial failure that led to retinal ganglion cell (RGCs) loss, yet effective disease-modifying therapies remain limited. Acarbose (ACA), an α-glucosidase inhibitor widely used for diabetes, has recently been recognized for its dual regulatory potential on immune metabolism and aging-associated neurodegeneration. Here, we demonstrate that intravitreal ACA administration attenuates retinal inflammation and improves RGCs survival following IR injury. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed extensive inflammatory activation and metabolic reprogramming across the retina, characterized by enhanced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) catabolism, particularly in microglia. ACA treatment was associated with reversal of these alterations, replenished NAD levels, and restored mitochondrial integrity. Integrative proteomic and biochemical analyses identified pyruvate kinase, muscle-type 2 (Pkm2) as a candidate regulatory node affected by ACA. Intravitreal delivery of siPkm2 partially protected against IR injury, and co-administration with ACA produced an additive trend in neuroprotection. Mechanistically, ACA upregulated sirtuin 1 (Sirt1) and reduced Pkm2 acetylation at lysine 270 (K270), which was linked to pro-inflammatory microglial activation. Structure-based virtual screening further identified HY-113082, a small molecule targeting Pkm2-K270, which synergized with ACA to suppress inflammation and enhance retinal protection. Moreover, Pkm2fl/flCx3cr1-Cre mice conferred partial resistance to IR injury, but blunted the additional benefit of HY-113082 when combined with ACA, consistent with on-target engagement. Our findings support that ACA exerts retinal protection through the Sirt1-Pkm2-NAD axis, suggesting a metabolic checkpoint that integrates immune and mitochondrial regulation. This study provides mechanistic insight into ACA’s dual immunometabolic and neuroprotective actions, holding promise for therapeutic insights into neuroinflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03838-8.Keywords: Retinal ischemia and reperfusion, Acarbose, Immunometabolism, Microglia, Pkm2, Acetylation

Introduction

Retinal ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury is a major pathological process implicated in vision-threatening diseases such as glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and retinal artery occlusion [1]. Transient interruption and restoration of blood flow trigger oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation, leading to progressive retinal ganglion cell (RGCs) death and irreversible vision loss [2]. Although substantial progress has been made in understanding IR pathology, effective therapeutic strategies remain limited, highlighting the urgent need for novel targets.

Neuroinflammation is a key contributor of neuronal injury. As the resident immune cells of the retina, microglia rapidly respond to ischemic or inflammatory insults and can adopt pro-inflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory (M2) states, thereby exerting either detrimental or protective effects [3]. This plasticity places microglia at the core of the inflammatory cascade in IR injury, making them an attractive therapeutic target for limiting secondary inflammatory damage and supporting neuronal survival.

Recent work has revealed that metabolism is tightly integrated with immune function, a concept broadly defined as immunometabolism [4, 5]. Immune cells adapt their bioenergetic programs to shape effector states, and microglia are no exception [6, 7]. Their ability to proliferate and cluster at sites of injury demands specialized metabolic support, yet the molecular determinants of these bioenergetic programs remain poorly defined.

Mitochondria are central to microglial function, maintaining redox balance and bioenergetic capacity while coupling metabolic status to inflammatory signaling [8–10]. Mitochondrial respiratory complexes and metabolites regulate myeloid immune responses [11, 12], and mounting evidence indicates that metabolic pathway selection licenses or restrains microglial effector states, thereby influencing neuronal fate [13, 14]. In particular, pyridine nucleotide turnover has attracted attention because excessive catabolism of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) may help sustain microglia in a pro-inflammatory state [15]. Interventions that preserve NAD availability not only improves mitochondrial function and limits structural damage but also reduces neuroinflammation, thus protecting neurons [16]. These findings point to NAD metabolism as a tractable entry point for therapeutic intervention.

Acarbose (ACA), a widely used α-glucosidase inhibitor for managing type 2 diabetes [17], has shown potential beyond its glucose-lowering effects. Alongside rapamycin, ACA has been reported to extend lifespan in mice and improves systemic health [18, 19]. Recent evidence has demonstrated that ACA reverses metabolic imbalances and reduces the activation of glial cells in the brain, exhibiting significant therapeutic potential in models of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I deficiency [20]. Furthermore, recent research indicated that oral ACA has both direct and indirect inhibitory effects on pro-inflammatory adipose tissue macrophages in diet-induced obesity, exhibiting local and systemic anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects [21]. Despite these promising observations, the effects of ACA on neuroinflammation remain insufficiently explored.

Given the emerging view that microglial activation and metabolic stress responses are tightly coupled during the propagation and exacerbation of neuroinflammation, we hypothesized that repurposing ACA, a clinically established metabolic drug, might offer a complementary approach to dampen neuroinflammation and improve neuronal resilience. Therefore, in the present study, we investigated whether intravitreal ACA administration can attenuate microglia-associated inflammatory responses and preserve retinal ganglion cell integrity following IR injury. By defining the neuroprotective profile of ACA and clarifying its relationship to retinal immune and metabolic stress pathways, this work aims to provide a mechanistic rationale for exploring metabolic interventions as candidate disease-modifying strategies in retinal IR-related neuroinflammatory conditions.

Results

ACA exerts neuroprotective and anti-neuroinflammatory effects and regulates activation of microglia in the retinal IR injury

To evaluate the neuroprotective effects of ACA in retinal IR injury, we performed intravitreal injection of ACA immediately following anterior chamber perfusion and subsequently validated structural and inflammatory outcomes at 1-, 3-, and 7-days post-IR (Fig. 1A). After a 7-day reperfusion period, we conducted whole-mount immunostaining with RNA binding protein, mRNA processing factor (RBPMS) and βIII-tubulin (TUJ-1), and quantified RGC density in predefined central and peripheral regions (Fig. 1B). We first optimized the ACA dose and found that ACA promoted RGCs survival at 7 days after IR, with 800 µg/mL achieving a robust protective effect in our dose-response evaluation (Fig. S1A-B). To control for potential effects of the injection procedure itself, we additionally included non-IR control groups. Whole-mount immunostaining showed that sham operation and ACA injection alone did not produce detectable changes in RGCs density compared with blank controls (Fig. S1C-D), supporting the ocular safety of the procedure and the selected ACA dosing in the absence of IR. In contrast, IR caused a marked loss of RGCs in both central and peripheral retina, whereas ACA treatment significantly preserved RGC density (Fig. 1C-D). Additionally, we assessed and compared the thickness of the inner plexiform layer (IPL) of the retina across all experimental groups using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. HE analysis further demonstrated that IR reduced the thickness of the IPL, and ACA partially restored this structural loss (Fig. 1E-F).

As previously noted, microglia-mediated neuroinflammation can lead to RGCs degeneration and plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of retinal IR injury [22]. Consequently, we further investigated whether ACA modulates microglial responses together with polarization-associated markers. To evaluate the morphology and distribution of microglia in the retina, we conducted immunofluorescent labeling of the entire retinal tissue using ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1). To control for potential effects of surgical manipulation and intravitreal injection, we additionally examined non-IR control groups, in which Iba-1 whole-mount staining showed no obvious differences in microglial density or morphology among groups (Fig. S2A-B). In parallel, we assessed astrogliosis using GFAP immunofluorescence on retinal frozen sections. The GFAP-positive area showed no obvious differences among the Blank, Blank + ACA, Sham, and Sham + ACA groups (Fig. S2C-D). In contrast, our analysis revealed that, at 3 days post-retinal IR injury, the retinas treated with ACA exhibited a 40–60% reduction in microglial cell numbers compared to untreated controls (Fig. 1G-H). In the blank group, microglia displayed small cell bodies with highly branched processes, whereas in the IR+Vehicle group, microglial cell bodies appeared enlarged, and their processes exhibited partial retraction, consistent with an activated phenotype. Notably, microglia in the ACA-treated group appeared more ramified and less hypertrophic, suggesting that intravitreal injection of ACA is associated with reduced microglial activation. Consistent with these findings, GFAP staining further showed that IR induced a clear expansion of the GFAP-positive area, with reactive astrocytes exhibiting stronger GFAP signals and processes extending downward toward the IPL. This astrogliotic response was attenuated by ACA, as reflected by a reduced GFAP-positive area and less prominent reactive changes (Fig. S2C-D).

Furthermore, the western blotting (WB) analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in Iba-1 protein levels in the IR + ACA group compared to the IR+Vehicle group (Fig. S2E-F), which is consistent with the results obtained from whole retinal labeling. We also assessed the protein expression of CD86 (an M1-pro-inflammational microglial marker) and CD206 (an M2-anti-inflammational microglial marker). The WB analysis indicated that both M1 pro-inflammatory and M2 anti-inflammatory markers were significantly elevated following retinal IR injury (Fig. S2G-I). Notably, after ACA treatment, the expression of the M1 pro-inflammatory marker was significantly downregulated, while the expression of the M2 anti-inflammatory marker was markedly increased compared to the vehicle-treated controls (Fig. S2G-I).

Previous studies have established a strong correlation between microglial activation and increased inflammation [23, 24]. Based on this premise, we evaluated the mRNA levels of the most common pro-inflammatory cytokines: interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). In the non-IR control groups, these cytokines showed no obvious differences, indicating that ACA injection and sham manipulation alone did not measurably alter baseline inflammatory cytokine expression (Fig. S2J). By contrast, we observed a significant upregulation of these cytokines at 1-day post-retinal IR injury, which was notably downregulated following ACA treatment (Fig. 1I). Additionally, WB analysis indicated that retinal IR injury upregulated the expression of proteins associated with the NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain–containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathway, while ACA treatment partially reversed this effect (Fig. 1J-K).

Taken together, these findings provide evidence for the therapeutic potential of ACA in attenuates RGC loss after retinal IR injury and is associated with reduced glial activation and dampened inflammatory responses.

ACA ameliorates immune metabolic dysregulation and neuronal damage in the retinal IR injury as assessed by scRNA-seq

The emergence of unbiased high-throughput scRNA-seq technology has provided profound insights into cell taxonomy and complex cellular interactions, facilitating a better understanding of the pathogenesis of numerous diseases [25]. Utilizing scRNA-seq analysis, we constructed a comprehensive atlas of the Blank, IR, and ACA-treated IR mouse models (Fig. 2A). Based on characteristic marker genes, we identified 16 major cell populations: rod photoreceptor (ROD), cone photoreceptor (CONE), rod bipolar cell (RBC), cone bipolar cell (CBC), amacrine cells (AC), horizontal cell (HC), retinal ganglion cell (RGC), macroglia (MAC), vascular endothelial cells (VEC), microglia, macrophage, monocyte, neutrophil cells (NEU), T cell (TC), dendritic cell (DC), and retinal pigment epithelial cell (RPE) (Fig. 2A, Fig. S3A). Notably, IR injury was accompanied by an increased representation of immune cells, particularly myeloid cells (microglia, macrophage and monocyte), and this shift was attenuated after ACA treatment (Fig. 2B). A similar trend was also observed for astrocytes, whose proportion increased after IR and decreased with ACA treatment (Figs. S3B-C), consistent with our in vivo GFAP findings.

Using differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis, we comprehensively explored the dynamic changes in gene signaling between IR and Blank mice (referred to as IR-DEGs) and between ACA-treated IR mice and IR mice (referred to as ACA-DEGs). The IR-DEGs exhibited a predominantly upward trend in gene expression, particularly in genes associated with inflammatory responses (S100a8, S100a9, Il1b, Cxcl2), myeloid cell differentiation (CD74), oxidative stress responses (Fos, Junb), and microglial activation (Nampt) (Fig. 2C). Conversely, the downregulated genes exhibited a more modest degree of change, with the majority being involved in retinal homeostasis (Arr3, Rlbp1, Opn1sw) (Fig. 2C). Following ACA treatment, gene expression predominantly displayed a downward trend (Fig. 2D). Notably, ACA not only mitigated the expression of genes associated with inflammatory responses (S100a8, S100a9, Il1b, Nfkbia) but also suppressed the expression of metabolism-related genes (Pkm, Ldha), while simultaneously enhancing the expression of some NAD metabolism-related genes (Sirt1, Sirt4) (Fig. 2D).

Subsequent Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that, following retinal IR injury, immune inflammatory pathways, including inflammatory response, oxidative stress responses, myeloid leukocyte migration, and the NF-κB pathway, were significantly enhanced (Fig. 2E). Notably, we also found that metabolism-related pathways, such as ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, generation of precursor metabolites and energy, and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly, were significantly upregulated, indicating concurrent transcriptional activation of immune inflammation and metabolic processes in the retinal microenvironment after IR injury (Fig. 2E). By mapping the IR-DEGs to gene sets associated with metabolic pathways, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further confirmed the significant upregulation of ATP metabolic process, generation of precursor metabolites and energy, and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase following retinal IR injury (Fig. 2F-H). Furthermore, we noted a decline of visual and neural-related GO pathways after retina IR injury, including neurotransmitter secretion, NADH regeneration, and retinal ganglion cell axon guidance (Fig. S3D). To quantify the extent to which ACA alleviates inflammation associated with retinal IR injury, we calculated scores for three gene sets, including the inflammatory response, positive regulation of neuron apoptotic process, and NF-κB signaling pathways. We found that these pathways exhibited elevated scores in the IR group, while ACA treatment significantly decreased the scores for all three pathways (Fig. 2I-K). Correspondingly, GSEA confirmed that ACA downregulated these pathways associated with inflammation and neuronal cell death, as well as the NF-κB pathway (Fig. 2L-M). Notably, ACA also downregulated a series of mitochondrial metabolic pathways, including mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport, mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase (Fig. 2O-Q), indicating that ACA simultaneously regulates inflammation and metabolism. Furthermore, we analyzed DEGs upregulated by ACA, and both GO analysis and GSEA confirmed that ACA upregulates visual-related pathways such as visual perception and phototransduction (Fig. S3E-G). Additionally, scoring of several representative visual and neuro-related pathways showed a significant decrease in scores for phototransduction, neurotransmitter secretion, and other pathways following IR injury, while ACA treatment partially restored the homeostasis of retinal visual conduction and neuronal cells (Fig. S3H-K). Heatmap analysis illustrated the consistent regulatory capability of ACA on gene sets associated with these GO pathways (Fig. S3L-N).

To further investigate the effects of ACA on the immune response, inflammation, and metabolism of various retinal neuronal and immune cells, we conducted GO analysis. Our findings revealed that ACA primarily downregulates apoptosis and metabolism in photoreceptors and RGCs, with more prominent changes observed in RGCs (Fig. 2R). Among immune cells, ACA significantly downregulated inflammation and metabolism, particularly in microglia, with the latter showing a more pronounced effect (Fig. 2S). By comparison, in astrocytes, ACA was associated with a more limited transcriptional shift, with mitochondrial ATP synthesis coupled electron transport emerging as the primary downregulated GO term (Fig. 2S).

Collectively, these results indicate that ACA is associated with reduced inflammatory signatures and altered metabolic activation across the retina after IR injury, together with a partial recovery of neuronal and visual-related transcriptional programs.

ACA attenuates microglial NAD catabolic activation and mitochondrial stress, accompanied by downregulation of Pkm2 as a candidate regulatory node

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the retina and the frontline of the immune defense system, uncontrolled activation can mediate excessive inflammatory responses that may lead to neuronal damage [26]. Our previous results also confirmed that the downregulation of inflammation and metabolism mediated by ACA is predominantly enriched in microglia (Fig. 2S). Accordingly, we focused subsequent analyses on microglial inflammatory and metabolic changes, with emphasis on comparisons between the IR and IR + ACA groups. Under the influence of IR stress, the expression of genes associated with the excessive activation of microglia and their transition to a pro-inflammatory phenotype, including S100a8, S100a9, and CD74, was significantly elevated (Fig. 3A, and Supplementary material 1). These changes were attenuated in the ACA-treated group (Fig. 3A, and Supplementary material 2). To evaluate whether ACA could exert anti-inflammatory effects on overactivated microglia in vitro, BV2 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which has been reported that LPS can induce a robust proinflammatory mediator in BV2 cells [27]. Under stimulation with varying doses of ACA, concentrations ranging from 0 to 50 µM showed no significant impact on the viability of BV2 cells but exhibited a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on the mRNA levels of the M1 marker (inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase, iNOS) (Fig. S4A-B). Consistent with the in vivo results, ACA significantly suppressed the LPS-induced elevation in mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) (Fig. S4C). Furthermore, WB analysis confirmed that ACA inhibited LPS-induced nuclear translocation of the NF-κB pathway in vitro (Fig. S4D, E). Specifically, ACA reduced the BV2 cells from transforming into M1 pro-inflammatory toxic cells triggered by LPS, while enhancing the M2 anti-inflammatory properties (Fig. S4F-H).

It is worth noting that we observed a significant activation of genes related to metabolism following IR, such as Gapdh, Pkm, Aldoa, and Ndufa3, and ACA treatment not only downregulated the expression of these genes but also upregulated the mitochondrial fusion gene (Opa1) (Fig. 3A). Correspondingly, GSEA indicated that microglia exhibit upregulation of inflammation, NF-κB signal transduction, oxidative stress and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex following retinal IR (Fig. 3B-E). Given that mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is a major site of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production because of the propensity for aberrant electron leakage [28], heatmap analysis further demonstrated that genes encoding mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I-IV were markedly upregulated after IR, and these transcriptional changes were partially reduced following ACA treatment (Fig. 3F, Table S1). Besides, we found that IR stress downregulated the apoptotic cell clearance and efferocytosis in microglia, while ACA enhanced reticulophagy and mitophagy (Fig. S4I).

To further investigate the anti-inflammatory effects and metabolic-regulating ability of ACA on microglia, we first identified the intersection of the upregulated DEGs induced by IR and the downregulated DEGs following ACA treatment, referring to these DEGs as “down-rescued DEGs” (Fig. 3G). Then we conducted pathway analysis on the downregulated rescue DEGs. Our enrichment analysis indicated that, following ACA treatment, inflammation and mitochondrial metabolism in microglia, including ATP metabolic process, NAD catabolic process, and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, were reduced, with notable enrichment in the NAD catabolic process (Fig. 3H). Consistent with this pattern, further AUC scoring and complementary in vivo and in vitro assays demonstrated that ACA effectively attenuates NAD catabolism and a relative preservation of NAD pools in response to inflammatory conditions (Fig. 3I-K). Additionally, ACA treatment significantly increased cytochrome c (Cycs) mRNA expression and partially restored mitochondrial morphological and functional impairment by cellular immunofluorescence (IF) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) studies under the LPS-induced inflammation, further supporting the restoration of mitochondrial function (Fig. 3L, Fig. S4J-N).

Consistent with previous studies, mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction is heavily involved in inflammation and possesses significant potential for mitigating inflammation [29, 30]. Our results indicated that, in response to retinal IR injury, ACA may play a dual role in regulating metabolic dysfunction and neuroinflammation in microglia. To validate this hypothesis, we first identified the intersection of the down-rescued DEGs from all retinal cells, immune cell populations, myeloid cell populations, and microglia. The UpSet plot revealed that the intersections among these four populations included pro-inflammatory toxicity genes (S100a8, S100a9, Saa3 and Acod1) as well as metabolism-related genes (Ndufa3, Cox17 and Atp5md) (Fig. 3M). Notably, in immune cells, myeloid cells, and microglia, Gapdh, Pkm, Aldoa, and Eno1 were among closely linked to NAD catabolism (Fig. 3M). Further heatmap revealed a pronounced enrichment of genes associated with NAD catabolism pathways in microglia (Fig. S5A), indicating that microglia may play a pivotal role as key immune mediators in NAD catabolism. Moreover, intersection analysis of down-rescued metabolic GO terms in microglia revealed that Gapdh, Pkm and Aldoa participate in the largest number of pathways (Fig. 3N). And the top three genes exhibiting the most significant alterations in expression before and after ACA treatment in microglia were Gapdh, Pkm, and Aldoa, identifying them as central metabolic hubs through which ACA may reprogram NAD-dependent metabolism and immune responses (Fig. 3O). Subsequently, seven core hub genes, namely, Pkm, Cycs, Eno1, Eno1b, Prdx1, Cox5b and Cox6b1 from the 30 top hub genes among all downregulated rescue genes in microglia, were identified using the cytoHubba plugin with six algorithms: MCC, MNC, EPC, Closeness, Radiality, and EcCentricity (Fig. 3P). Among the core hub genes, Pkm was both highly expressed in microglia following retinal IR injury and significantly downregulated by ACA in microglia as well as other retinal cells (Fig. S5B-C), supporting Pkm as a candidate node potentially linking ACA-associated NAD-related metabolic changes and microglial responses.

Given that Pkm exists in two isoforms: Pkm1 and Pkm2, we further aimed to determine which isoform plays a pivotal role. The WB analysis demonstrated the expression of Pkm2 was significantly elevated on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 following retinal IR injury, with the peak expression occurring on the day 3 post-IR, while the expression of Pkm1 showed no significant changes after IR injury (Fig. 3Q-S). Concurrently, in vivo and in vitro, WB results revealed that ACA treatment significantly reduced the expression of Pkm2, whereas no such changes were observed for Gapdh, Pkm1 and Aldoa (Fig. 3T-U, Fig. S5D-K). Considering that the NF-κB pathway in the entire retina and microglia is activated following IR injury (Figs. 2E and K and 3C), and that Pkm2 is also believed to be highly involved in the activation of the NF-κB pathway [31, 32], we conducted WB analysis of the retinas before and after IR injury (Fig. 3V-W). This indicated a significant decrease in the p-P65/P65 protein ratio following ACA treatment, consistent with the scRNA-seq results.

Altogether, these findings revealed that ACA effectively alleviates the mitochondrial metabolic abnormalities of microglia and Pkm2 may be a candidate regulatory node for ACA in modulating immunity and metabolism during the response to IR injury.

Delivery of siPkm2 combined with ACA effectively alleviates neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction to rescue RGCs

Previous research has confirmed that inhibiting the expression of Pkm2 can restore normal neuronal metabolism [33]. To further investigate the role of Pkm2 downregulation in IR injury, we constructed small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Pkm2 and administered it via intravitreal injection. We found that siPkm2 reduced retinal Pkm2 expression without causing detectable injury to RGCs (Fig. 4A-D). Subsequently, siPkm2 was injected once at 7 days and 3 days prior to retinal IR injury, resulting in a partial salvage of RGCs with a survival rate approaching 40% (Fig. 4E, G). When ACA was administered immediately after IR in siPkm2-pretreated mice, RGC survival increased to 50%-60% (Fig. 4E, G), suggesting that the combination of siPkm2 pretreatment and ACA may provide additive protection in mitigating RGCs mortality. Similarly, siPkm2 can partially alleviate the activation of microglia in the retinas under IR stress, and ACA co-treatment further reduced the activation readout (Fig. 4F, H). Consequently, we assessed the impact of both treatments on the NF-κB pathway and inflammatory factors. The results indicated that, compared with siPkm2 alone, the combination of siPkm2 and ACA was associated with a greater reduction in p-P65/P65 and lower retinal inflammatory factor levels (Fig. 4I-K). Notably, while we previously confirmed that Pkm2 is involved in the aberrant NAD catabolism under IR injury (Fig. 3N-R), we further found that siPkm2 plus ACA raised retinal NAD levels more than siPkm2 alone (Fig. 4L).

To further validate the role of Pkm2 in microglial responses to inflammatory stress, BV2 cells were transfected with siPkm2, resulting in robust knockdown of Pkm2 (Fig. S6A). LPS markedly induced proinflammatory transcripts, which were partially suppressed by siPkm2 and further attenuated by ACA cotreatment (Fig. S6B). At the protein level, knockdown of Pkm2 significantly attenuated the LPS-induced increase in CD86 but did not clearly alter CD206 expression, whereas addition of ACA to Pkm2 knockdown further suppressed CD86 and enhanced CD206 levels, consistent with a shift toward a less proinflammatory phenotype (Fig. S6C-E). Conversely, Pkm2 overexpression mildly elevated inflammatory gene expression, increased CD86, promoted M1 polarization, and amplified NF-κB signaling as shown by enhanced p-p65 (Fig. S6F-M), indicating that Pkm2 activity facilitates microglial proinflammatory activation.

Previous studies have indicated that Pkm2 inhibitors can alleviate mitochondrial structural and functional impairments in osteoblasts induced by LPS [34]. Thus, we hypothesize that knocking down Pkm2 may similarly mitigate mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction in LPS-stimulated microglia. We next examined metabolic and mitochondrial consequences of Pkm2 modulation. LPS reduced intracellular NAD content, whereas siPkm2 partly restored NAD levels, with further improvement upon ACA treatment (Fig. S6N). Functionally, siPkm2 mitigated LPS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, including reducing mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), preserving membrane potential and ameliorating ultrastructural damage, and these improvements were further rescued by ACA (Fig. S6O-S). Together, these data indicate that downregulating Pkm2 in conjunction with ACA treatment more effectively alleviate RGCs damage and neuroinflammation while mitigating the mitochondrial metabolic abnormalities in microglia under IR.

ACA regulates Sirt1-mediated deacetylation of Pkm2 to alleviate neuroinflammation and mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction

Next, we sought to explore the potential molecular mechanisms by which ACA regulates Pkm2 to exert its therapeutic effects. NAD serves as an important co-substrate for the Sirtuins family, which participates in maintaining mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis, neurodegeneration, and regulating immune inflammation by sensing changes in NAD levels [35, 36]. ACA selectively upregulated Sirt1 mRNA in LPS-stimulated microglia, whereas other Sirtuin family members (Sirt2-7) showed no obvious changes under our conditions (Fig. 5A). Consistent with this, STRING protein-protein interaction analysis of key NAD-catabolic genes (Gapdh, Pkm, Aldoa, Eno1, Eno1b) together with Sirt1-7 identified Pkm and Sirt1 as central hubs with a strong predicted interaction, supporting a plausible connection between Sirt1 and Pkm-related NAD metabolism (Fig. 5B). Cellular IF further confirmed that ACA attenuated the LPS-induced upregulation of Pkm2 in BV2 cells while enhancing the expression of Sirt1 (Fig. 5C-E). Sirt1 relies on NAD to maintain its deacetylase activity [37]. Recent studies have found that Sirt1 can inhibit the activity of Pkm2 through deacetylation [38]. Overexpression of Sirt1 in BV2 cells was successfully achieved and confirmed by plasmid transfection (Fig. 5F). Consistent with previous studies [34], co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) confirmed a physiological interaction between Sirt1 and Pkm2 when overexpress Sirt1 and Pkm2 (Fig. 5G-H), and inflammatory stimulation was accompanied by increased Pkm2 acetylation (Fig. 5I-J). To determine whether Pkm2 deacetylation is mediated by Sirt1 rather than class I/II histone deacetylases (HDACs), we treated LPS-activated BV2 cells with the SIRT1 inhibitor sirtinol and the pan-class I/II HDAC inhibitor trichostatin A (TSA) at literature-informed [38], non-cytotoxic working concentrations that was verified by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays before use (Fig. S7A). We showed that only sirtinol increased Pkm2 acetylation, suggesting that the deacetylation of Pkm2 in activated microglia under inflammatory stimulation may predominantly depend on Sirt1 activity (Fig. 5K-L). ACA reduced LPS-induced Pkm2 acetylation, and this effect was reversed by sirtinol (Fig. 5M-N). Consistently, plasmid experiments showed that Pkm2 overexpression increased its acetylation, whereas Sirt1 overexpression suppressed Pkm2 acetylation, and sirtinol partially abrogated this suppression (Fig. 5O-P).

To emphasize the role of Sirt1-mediated Pkm2 deacetylation in the anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects of ACA, co-treatment with ACA and sirtinol revealed that Sirt1 inhibition abolished ACA-associated suppression of proinflammatory mediators and NF-κB signaling, prevented the ACA-driven shift from an M1 to an M2 phenotype in LPS-stimulated microglia (Fig. S7B-I). The metabolic regulation of ACA also appeared to be blocked by sirtinol, which disrupted NAD levels enhanced by ACA in the context of LPS-induced NAD reduction (Fig. S7J). Additionally, sirtinol reversed ACA’s mitochondrial protection such that, in LPS-stimulated microglia, sirtinol induced a marked increase in mtROS and a concomitant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and it attenuated ACA’s restoration of mitochondrial ultrastructure as evidenced by TEM (Fig. S7K-O). Together, these data indicate that ACA exerts its anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects in microglia are partly linked to Sirt1-mediated deacetylation of Pkm2.

ACA enhances Sirt1-mediated deacetylation of Pkm2 at site of K270

Previous studies alongside our findings have indicated an elevated expression of Pkm2 under inflammatory conditions. To pinpoint the critical acetylation sites of Pkm2 in this inflammatory state, we performed acetylation modification proteomics on BV2 cells stimulated with LPS. The Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis revealed that Pkm2 underwent acetylation at five specific lysine residues: K311, K505, K166, K433, and K270 in response to inflammatory stress (Fig. 6A). Subsequently, we engineered mutants of Pkm2, substituting these five residues with alanine (A) to mimic a deacetylated state. Following transfection of these mutants into BV2 cells and subsequent LPS stimulation, we observed a significant decrease in acetylation levels for K166A, K433A, and K270A, with K270 and K433 demonstrating particularly pronounced trends (Fig. 6B-C), supporting that these residues contribute substantially to Pkm2 acetylation under our inflammatory conditions. To further examine the relationship between these sites and Sirt1, we co-transfected K166A, K270A, or K433A mutants with or without Plas-Sirt1 and performed Co-IP assays. Among these mutants, K270A was associated with a marked reduction in detectable Pkm2-Sirt1 interaction (Fig. 6D-E). This finding supports the view that K270 may represent an important deacetylation site associated with Pkm2-Sirt1 interaction. To validate this hypothesis, we mutated K166, K270, and K433 to glutamine (Q) to simulate acetylation, followed by co-transfection with or without Plas-Sirt1. Consistent with previous results, we found that overexpression of Sirt1 diminished the acetylation level at the K270 site (Fig. 6F-G).

Next, we interrogated the three-dimensional structure of Pkm2 (AlphaFold model AF-P52480-F1, residues 1-531) with emphasis on the K270 acetylation site and performed a structure-guided virtual screen from a commercial compound database (MCE Compound Library) containing 80,000 inhibitors. Based on the docking scores of small molecules within the amino acid pocket containing the K270 site of Pkm2, we concentrated our efforts on identifying the top five small molecule inhibitors, including HY-112431 A, HY113082, HY-P0046, HY-F0004, and HY-106443B, which have previously been reported to be associated with inflammation, metabolic disorders, and neurological diseases [39–45]. Guided by previous work, we screened candidate inhibitors in BV2 cells using CCK-8 viability assays and reverse transcription and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT‑PCR) for iNOS, and identified five compounds (HY-112431A, HY-113082, HY-106443B, HY-F0004 and HY-P0046) at concentrations that preserved cell viability while exhibiting reproducible anti-inflammatory activity (Fig. S7A-J). CO-IP assays showed that several candidate compounds reduced Pkm2 acetylation under LPS stimulation, with HY-113082 producing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 6J-K). Molecular docking analysis further revealed that HY-113082 was accommodated within the binding pocket of Pkm2 and established a direct hydrogen bond with the K270 residue, with a bond length of 2.2 Å, which indicates a particularly tight and stable interaction (Fig. 6L). Functionally, HY-113082 markedly attenuated LPS-induced transcriptional upregulation of proinflammatory mediators, confirming its potent anti-inflammatory activity in BV2 cells (Fig. 6M).

To determine the optimal intraocular concentration of HY-113082, we first evaluated its effects on retinal morphology following IR injury. HE staining revealed that IR markedly reduced the thickness of the IPL, whereas intravitreal administration of HY-113082 mitigated this structural loss in a dose-dependent manner, with 5 nM exhibiting the most pronounced protective effect (Fig. S8K-L). Based on these findings, we proceeded to in vivo experiments using the selected concentration. IF staining of RGCs showed that HY-113082 significantly improved RGCs survival after IR injury, and co-administration with ACA was accompanied by further improvement (Fig. 6N-O). Consistent with these histological observations, Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) demonstrated that HY-113082 alone reduced the IR-induced upregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, whereas the combination of HY-113082 with ACA elicited an even stronger suppression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6P). Moreover, WB analysis indicated that HY-113082 decreased the ratio of p-P65/P65, while co-treatment with ACA further reinforced this inhibitory effect on NF-κB activation (Fig. 6Q-R).

Besides, Targeting the Pkm2-K270 site with HY-113082 favorably modulated immunometabolism, as intracellular NAD levels increased and rose further with ACA co-treatment (Fig. S8M). HY-113082 also curtailed LPS-evoked mtROS and restored mitochondrial membrane potential, while ACA potentiated these improvements in mitochondrial metabolic function (Fig. S8N-Q).

Taken together, these results support that Pkm2-K270 is a functionally relevant acetylation site in activated microglia and that HY-113082, a K270-pocket-prioritized compound, can phenocopy part of the anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial benefits observed with ACA. In combination with the Sirt1-dependent deacetylation evidence above, these data are consistent with a model in which ACA-associated microglial protection involves, at least in part, Sirt1-linked regulation of Pkm2 acetylation at K270.

To investigate the role of microglial Pkm2 in retinal IR injury, we inserted LoxP sites into exon 10 of the Pkm2 gene and generated conditional knockout mice using the LoxP/Cre system, with homozygosity confirmed by PCR genotyping (Fig. 7A-C). Baseline retinal architecture was indistinguishable between Pkm2fl/−Cx3cr1Cre and Pkm2fl/flCx3cr1Cre mice, as shown by HE sections and quantification of IPL thickness (Fig. 7D-E). After retinal IR, Pkm2fl/flCx3cr1Cre mice displayed higher RGCs survival than Pkm2fl/−Cx3cr1Cre controls, and intravitreal ACA further improved RGCs preservation (Fig. 7F-G). However, combining ACA with the K270-targeting inhibitor HY-113082 in the knockout background did not yield additional neuroprotection (Fig. 7F-G), indicating that the inhibitor’s efficacy depends on microglial Pkm2. Consistently, microglial activation was attenuated in Pkm2fl/flCx3cr1Cre retinas, as evidenced by reduced Iba-1 + cell density engaging RGCs axons within the ganglion cells layer and a shift away from hypertrophic, amoeboid, and phagocytic morphology, and ACA produced further reductions, whereas HY-113082 conferred no extra effect in the knockout (Fig. 7H-I). ELISA of retinal homogenates showed that Pkm2 deletion lowered IR-induced IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and that ACA further suppressed these cytokines, whereas the anti-inflammatory effect of HY-113082 was not apparent in the absence of microglial Pkm2 (Fig. 7J). In line with these in vivo data, LPS-challenged BV2 cells transfected with siPkm2 did not gain additional suppression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α when HY-113082 was added to ACA (Fig. 7K).

ACA counters RGCs apoptosis via microglial Pkm2 K270 deacetylation rather than direct neuronal action in vitro

To further validate our in vivo findings and explore the interaction between microglia and RGCs, we isolated primary RGCs (pRGCs) and established a co-culture system of pRGCs and BV2 cells to determine whether ACA exerts its effects on RGCs through modulation of microglia (Fig. S9A-B).

We initially extracted pRGCs and confirmed their identity through IF staining for POU domain class 4 transcription factor 1 (BRN3A) and TUJ-1 (Fig. S9A). The WB analysis showed that following oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R), pro-apoptotic markers (Bax, cleaved-casp3) significantly increased while the anti-apoptotic marker (Bcl-2) significantly decreased (Fig. S9C-F). Upon ACA treatment, Bax levels significantly decreased, while cleaved-casp3 showed no significant change, and Bcl-2 levels were significantly elevated (Fig. S9C-F). Notably, when BV2 cells and RGCs were subjected to OGD/R separately before co-culture, pro-apoptotic markers (Bax and cleaved-casp3) in RGCs significantly increased compared to RGCs undergoing OGD/R alone, while anti-apoptotic markers (Bcl-2) further declined (Fig. S9C-F). This indicates that microglia exacerbate RGCs damage in the context of hypoxic-ischemic injury. When BV2 cells and pRGCs were separately treated with ACA after OGD/R and then co-cultured, Bax and cleaved-casp3 in pRGCs were significantly reduced and Bcl-2 was increased compared with pRGCs treated directly with ACA after OGD/R (Fig. S9C-F). These findings support the possibility that ACA may enhance the protective role of microglia on RGCs.

Given the damaging role of Pkm2 during hypoxic-ischemic conditions, we overexpressed Pkm2 in BV2 cells prior to co-culture with RGCs (Fig. S9G). We found that BV2 cells overexpressing Pkm2 increased RGCs apoptosis (Bax and cleaved-casp3) relative to the pcDNA3.1 control group by decreasing their anti-apoptotic capacity (Bcl-2) (Fig. S9H-K). Additionally, we previously confirmed that the Sirt1 inhibitor, sirtinol, weakens the protective effects of ACA. Upon treatment with sirtinol and ACA after OGD/R, we observed a significant increase in the pro-apoptotic marker Bax and cleaved-caspase-3, along with a decrease in Bcl-2 levels, compared to the ACA-only group (Fig. S9L-O). To assess whether inhibiting the K270 site aligns with our in vivo findings, we treated BV2 cells with ACA and HY-113082 after separate OGD/R modeling and subsequently co-cultured them with RGCs. We found HY-113082 significantly enhanced the therapeutic effects of ACA, resulting in a marked decrease of Bax and cleaved-casp3, while an increase in anti-apoptotic levels (Bcl-2) in RGCs (Fig. S9P-S).

In summary, these results suggest that the protective effect of ACA on RGCs in this in vitro system is largely associated with its modulation of microglial state rather than a direct action on RGCs alone. Furthermore, manipulating Pkm2 expression and its K270-associated regulation in microglia appears to influence the efficacy of ACA in protecting RGCs.

Discussion

This study provides evidence supporting intravitreal ACA as a potential neuroprotective strategy for preserving RGCs after retinal IR, possibly through reprogramming microglial immunometabolism. Our data indicate that retinal IR induces pronounced inflammatory activation and mitochondrial dysfunction, accompanied by increased NAD catabolic pathway activity in microglia. ACA treatment was associated with attenuation of these changes, relative preservation of retinal NAD levels, and improvement of mitochondrial structural and functional readouts, together with reduced neuroinflammatory responses and RGCs loss. Mechanistically, the present data suggest that ACA regulates Pkm2 acetylation in a Sirt1-dependent context, with K270 appearing to be a functionally relevant regulatory site. Consistent with this, genetic reduction of Pkm2 enhanced the protective effect of ACA, and the K270-directed inhibitor HY-113,082 decreased Pkm2 acetylation and further increased ACA-associated protection in vivo. This additional effect was no longer observed in microglia-specific Pkm2 conditional knockout mice, supporting the dependence of HY-113,082 on microglial Pkm2 in this context.

Retinal IR is characterized by microglial activation and aggregation around neurons, altering the immune microenvironment through increased cytokine and chemokine secretion and vascular permeability, leads to rapid loss of RGCs with thinning of IPL and pronounced metabolic perturbations, with microglial activation driving and exacerbating this degeneration [46]. Prior studies indicated that modulating microglial neuroinflammation could protect RGCs, although the directness or indirectness of this relationship remains incompletely resolved. In our study, scRNA-seq mapped retina-wide transcriptional changes and highlighted an immune-metabolic response centered in microglia, while complementary in vivo and in vitro experiments showed that IR-associated inflammation coincides with bioenergetic stress characterized by diminished NAD, and disrupted mitochondrial structure and function. Although these observations place retinal IR within an immunometabolic framework in which metabolic disequilibrium co-evolves with inflammatory activation, our interpretation is still based primarily on dissociation-based scRNA-seq and endpoint molecular assays rather than direct metabolic flux measurements. Accordingly, future studies incorporating real-time and cell type-resolved metabolic flux analyses will be important for refining this framework.

Early studies have shown that ACA can mitigate cardiac IR injury by reducing ROS. However, the specific mechanisms underlying this effect remain unclear [47]. As research increasingly elucidates actions of ACA beyond glucose lowering, encompassing anti-inflammatory, mitochondrial, and organ-protective effects, ACA, alongside rapamycin, metformin, and NAD enhancers, is considered one of the most promising interventions for robustly delaying aging [48]. Our data in the retinas showed that ACA conferred measurable neuroprotection and immune quieting in acute IR injury. We demonstrated that intravitreal administration of ACA protects the retina against IR injury, as reflected by partial preservation of IPL thickness and improved RGCs survival. Consistently, GO analysis revealed significant upregulation of pathways related to phototransduction and visual perception, which collectively substantiated the neuroprotective potential of ACA to rescue retinal neuronal injury. From a translational perspective, it is also worth noting that retinal IR is frequently used as an experimental proxy for injurious components that appear across several vision-threatening disorders, including diabetic retinopathy (DR) and glaucoma, where ischemia or hypoxia, metabolic stress, and neuroinflammation can converge to promote progressive neurodegeneration [2]. In DR, where chronic hyperglycemia and retinal metabolic stress are accompanied by inflammatory amplification and microglial activation [49, 50], ACA is conceptually attractive not only because it is an established metabolic drug, but also because its anti-inflammatory and mitochondrial-modulating properties reported in other organs raise the possibility that it could influence the retinal inflammatory microenvironment in a manner complementary to vascular-targeted therapies. In glaucoma and related optic neuropathies, in which RGC vulnerability is increasingly understood to reflect an interaction between mechanical stress, metabolic fragility, and immune activation, our findings provide a mechanistic rationale to consider whether ACA might be leveraged to stabilize NAD-centered metabolic resilience and thereby restrain maladaptive inflammatory signaling [51, 52]. Importantly, these implications should be interpreted cautiously, because the present study does not directly model the chronic pathophysiology of DR or glaucoma, and it remains to be established whether ACA delivers durable benefit when injury evolves over longer time scales. Accordingly, future work will need to evaluate ACA in retinal IR-related disease contexts, including DR and glaucoma, with careful consideration of dosing, treatment window, route of administration, and long-term safety, to clarify how broadly the immunometabolic framework proposed here may extend beyond the acute IR setting.

Microglia, the principal resident immune cells of the central nervous system, can perpetuate tissue damage when dysregulated and thereby endanger neuronal survival through excessive inflammation [53]. Owing to the neuroanatomical continuity of the retina with the brain, a feed-forward loop ensues in which microglial activation amplifies RGC dysfunction and, as injured RGCs release damage-associated signals, oxidative stress intensifies and damages mitochondrial membranes and mitochondrial DNA, diminishing bioenergetic capacity and neural transmission [54]. Prior evidence that ACA suppresses hypothalamic gliosis and cytokine production substantiates its anti-neuroinflammatory profile [55, 56]. Taken together, these observations are consistent with the possibility that ACA can restrain neuroinflammatory responses. Concordant with these observations, our in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrate that ACA attenuates microglial activation, lowers M1-associated markers, increases M2-associated markers, and dampens NF-κB signaling. While these data support microglia as a major contributor to the protective effects observed in our models, a limitation is that we did not mechanistically validate ACA’s effects on other resident immune cells of the retina. Future work should delineate modulatory effects of ACA on these populations to refine our understanding of the retinal immune microenvironment.

Mounting work indicates that mitochondrial metabolism shapes myeloid effector states, and ACA has been reported to reprogram macrophages toward a salutary M2-like phenotype [21]. Consistent with these observations and with evidence that ACA reverses pre-degenerative metabolic imbalance in Leigh syndrome mice [20], we showed that ACA was associated with reduced microglial metabolic stress, including attenuation of NAD catabolic signatures, improvement of mitochondrial ultrastructure, and partial correction of bioenergetic dysfunction. These findings suggest that ACA engages additional metabolic regulatory nodes that influence neuroinflammatory progression and support a model in which ACA modulates microglial immunity through mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming. In line with this framework, our co-culture experiments indicate that, although direct ACA treatment of OGD/R-injured RGCs conferred only limited benefit, a more pronounced anti-apoptotic effect occurred when RGCs were co-cultured with ACA-pretreated microglia. This pattern is consistent with the possibility that ACA alters the microglial state in a manner that is more supportive of neuronal survival. One possible explanation is that ACA shifts microglia toward a less pro-inflammatory and more reparative phenotype, while also preserving NAD homeostasis and thereby improving the metabolic milieu available to stressed neurons. Although our data indicate that ACA is associated with mitigation of retinal IR injury in parallel with reduced NAD catabolism, our scRNA-seq analysis also revealed concurrent down-modulation of glycolysis, pyruvate metabolism, and ATP metabolic programs. The extent to which these pathways are directly regulated by ACA, as opposed to changing secondarily with inflammatory resolution, remains unclear and will require further mechanistic dissection in future studies.

Due to the significant influence of alternative splicing on the activity of Pkm, two mutually exclusive exons 9 form Pkm1, and exon 10 gives rise to Pkm2, thereby presenting different active conformations [57]. Under physiological conditions, Pkm2 predominantly exists as a tetramer in the cytoplasm and mitochondria, but hypoxia or altered metabolic states promote the dimeric form, which supports transcriptional programs and inflammatory signaling [58, 59]. In contrast, Pkm1 remains a constitutively active tetramer that drives oxidative ATP production, highlighting the isoform-specific metabolic roles of Pkm [60]. In our study, we observed that Pkm1 protein levels did not change significantly in the acute phase of retinal IR injury, while Pkm2 was robustly upregulated, suggesting selective involvement of Pkm2 in IR-induced pathology. Pkm2 has gained attention for its dual function in metabolism and inflammation and is increasingly being explored as a potential therapeutic target in neuroinflammatory disorders [61]. It can act as a coactivator for transcription factors implicated in the activation of the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), which regulate proinflammatory genes [62]. Elevated Pkm2 expression correlates with enhanced metabolic demand and M1-biased polarization of microglia [63]. Beyond polarization, Pkm2 facilitates microglial migration to sites of injury, as demonstrated by α-synuclein-mediated engagement of Pkm2 that drives metabolic reprogramming and enhances migration following spinal cord injury [64]. Importantly, accumulating evidence also indicates that Pkm2 regulates mitochondrial metabolism in addition to inflammation. Pkm2 translocation into the nucleus has been shown to influence mitochondrial biogenesis and redox balance, which links its conformational state to bioenergetic stress responses [65, 66]. These studies support a model in which Pkm2 acts at the intersection of inflammatory signaling and metabolic rewiring. While we do not exclude that Pkm2 may also engage other metabolic programs that shape microglial immunity, our current readouts and sampling windows indicate a predominant involvement of NAD catabolic processes in retinal IR, although this inference will require validation with expanded cohorts and orthogonal metabolic assays in future work.

In this context, our work demonstrated that ACA suppresses IR-induced Pkm2 upregulation and potentiates the protective effects of Pkm2 deletion, thereby supporting the possibility that ACA may act as a pharmacological enhancer of Pkm2-targeted interventions. Complementary work in macrophages has shown that Pkm2 knockdown prevents LPS-induced M1 polarization [67] and that myeloid Pkm2 deletion reduces atherosclerosis by suppressing inflammatory signaling and inhibiting inflammation [68]. Our results extend these findings. We found that overexpression of Pkm2 in microglia promoted a shift toward the M1 proinflammatory phenotype and activated the NF-κB pathway, reinforcing the idea that high Pkm2 levels drive inflammatory polarization. Conversely, knockdown of Pkm2 in BV2 cells suppressed LPS-induced inflammation, downregulated proinflammatory markers, and promoted an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype. Importantly, Pkm2 silencing not only reduced inflammatory signaling but also restored mitochondrial morphology and bioenergetic function, revealing a dual role in controlling both inflammation and metabolism. In vivo, targeted knockout of Pkm2 in microglia partially reduced RGC apoptosis-like changes and attenuated neuroinflammation after retinal IR, consistent with prior reports that Pkm2 inhibition exerts neuroprotective effects in other contexts [33, 69]. These findings should nevertheless be interpreted with caution. While our data support Pkm2 as an important regulatory node linking inflammatory activation to metabolic stress, other ACA-responsive pathways may also contribute to the overall protective phenotype. Further studies using broader metabolic profiling and cell type-resolved approaches will be required to clarify the relative contribution of Pkm2 within the wider immunometabolic network engaged by ACA.

Previous studies have shown that the function of Pkm2 is highly dependent on post-translational modifications including acetylation, phosphorylation, and SUMOylation [70, 71]. Protein acetylation is now recognized as a central post-translational modification in metabolic regulation, as nearly all NAD metabolic and TCA cycle enzymes are subject to acetylation [72, 73]. Sirtuins, a family of NAD-dependent deacetylases [74], couple acetylation status to NAD availability. In tumor models, T cell-derived IFN-γ suppresses Pkm2 activity and reduces NAD levels, thereby impairing Sirt1-mediated deacetylation and reprogramming tumor stemness [75]. NAD homeostasis is a critical factor that influences neurodegeneration and neuroprotection. Increasing levels of NAD provide neuroprotection in multiple cell and animal models of disease and in human clinical trials [76]. Recent animal and human studies have uncovered metabolic dysfunction occurring early in RGCs in glaucoma, in particular the critical dependency of RGCs on sufficient levels of NAD [77–79]. Sirt1 can directly interact with and deacetylate the RelA/p65 subunit of the NF-κB complex, thus playing an important role in the termination of NF-κB driven inflammatory responses and enhancing the resolution of inflammation [80]. However, regulation of the Sirt1-Pkm2-NAD axis in neuroinflammation has remained unexplored. Our study reveals that Pkm2 is closely associated with NAD catabolism in microglia, accompanied by reduced NAD levels. Although silencing Pkm2 partly increased NAD, the effect was more pronounced with concurrent ACA treatment, suggesting that ACA may impose additional constraints on NAD catabolism, consistent with GO analysis of scRNA-seq. We further found that the regulation of Pkm2 by ACA appears to involve Sirt1-mediated post-translational acetylation. When Sirt1 deacetylase activity was pharmacologically inhibited with sirtinol, Pkm2 acetylation increased and the anti-inflammatory effect of ACA was correspondingly attenuated. However, this interpretation should be made with caution, as sirtinol-based inhibition does not fully exclude the involvement of other deacetylation-dependent mechanisms. Further studies combining site-specific acetylation analysis with direct metabolic flux measurements will be needed to define the extent to which this axis accounts for the NAD-preserving and anti-inflammatory effects of ACA.

Sirtuin family members are increasingly recognized as regulators of Pkm2, acting through acetylation at discrete sites that differ according to disease context. Sirt2 loss promotes Pkm2 acetylation at K135, altering hepatic glucose metabolism under stress [81], while Sirt3 deacetylates Pkm2 at K433 to suppress apoptosis in lung IR injury [82]. In Parkinson’s disease models, Sirt1 deacetylates Pkm2 at K135 and K206, thereby lowering enzymatic activity and lactate production and reducing glial activation [38]. Nevertheless, under inflammatory conditions the Sirt1 deacetylation sites on Pkm2 remained undefined. Here, we close this gap. Using LC/LC-MS and CO-IP, we identified K270 as a site on Pkm2 associated with inflammatory acetylation changes, and our results further suggested that this residue is responsive to Sirt1 activity and is modulated after ACA treatment. Furthermore, we validated in vitro and in vivo that the K270-targeting inhibitor HY-113082 can enhance ACA-associated protection, with reduced RGC apoptosis-related changes and attenuated neuroinflammation. In this study, leveraging the molecular docking score at the K270 site of Pkm2, we employed virtual drug screening to identify the top five small-molecule inhibitors associated with metabolism or inflammation from a library of 80,000 candidates. These inhibitors showed the ability to reduce Pkm2 acetylation. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that inhibition of Pkm2 K270 residue acetylation in microglia conferred partial anti-inflammatory effects and protection of RGCs, with enhanced therapeutic efficacy observed upon combination with ACA. Among these candidates, HY-113082 emerged as the most effective compound in our screening pipeline, providing a theoretical foundation for future investigations into Pkm2 acetylation-related mechanisms. Nevertheless, constrained by economic and ethical considerations, our screening approach exhibited limitations, as it did not encompass broader libraries of small-molecule inhibitors. Future studies should explore whether additional compounds possess deacetylating activity at K270 to expand therapeutic options.

In summary, our study provides evidence that ACA exerts a protective effect in retinal IR injury and is associated with preservation of RGCs. Combining scRNA-seq with in vivo and in vitro experiments, we delineated a microglial immunometabolic mechanism involving a Pkm2-NAD checkpoint. ACA was associated with reduced NAD catabolism and with enhanced Sirt1-mediated deacetylation of Pkm2 at K270, accompanied by microglia-driven neuroinflammation and restores mitochondrial homeostasis. HY-113082 further strengthened these ACA-associated effects in a Pkm2-dependent context. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for considering ACA-related metabolic modulation as a potential therapeutic direction in retinal IR and possibly other neuroinflammatory conditions.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

For molecular biology experiments, three technical replicate experiments were performed for each sample and the data were averaged. In vitro experiments, assays were performed on cells in three wells for each experiment to obtain an average count. In general, 3 to 30 biological replicates in each group were used in this study. The exact numbers of biological replicates in different experiments were stated in the figure legends. Sample sizes for all experiments were determined on the basis of prior studies with α = 0.05, power = 0.8, and an estimated effect size ≥ 2.

Ethics statement

All animal breeding and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO). The animal protocols used were pre-approved by the animal ethics committee at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China; approval no. ID: 2025021).

Animal and retinal IR model

C57BL/6J male mice (6–8 weeks of age) were purchased from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center and housed in a Specific Pathogen Free animal facility at the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center. Mice were randomly assigned to the following groups: blank group, sham group, and retinal IR group with or without drug treatment. In the blank group, mice received no ocular manipulation. In the sham group, mice underwent anterior chamber cannulation using the same procedure as the IR group, but without elevation of intraocular pressure. The retinal IR models were established as described previously [83]. Prior to the operation, mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium injected intraperitoneally and topical anesthesia of the cornea was administered with tetracaine hydrochloride. We carefully inserted a 30G needle connected to a normal saline (NS) bottle into the anterior chamber of the mice’s eyes. IOP was increased to 110 mmHg by elevating the NS bottle to a height of 150 cm and maintained for 1 h.

Intravitreal injection

Intravitreal injections of the drug were performed immediately after the IR model or sham operation were established. A 3 µl volume was slowly injected into the intravitreal cavity using a Hamilton syringe fitted with a 32-gauge microneedle. ACA (800 µg/ml, MCE, USA) and HY-113082 (5nM, MCE, USA) were dissolved freshly in control vehicle (phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) and were used within 2 h. In addition, to evaluate the potential effect of ACA itself in the absence of IR injury, mice in the Blank + ACA group received the same intravitreal volume and concentration of ACA at the beginning of the experiment. The siPkm2 (2 nmol/eye) and the negative control (NC), provided by KeyGEN BioTECH (China), were dissolved in sterile distilled water and administered three times: 7 days and 3 days prior to the induction of retinal IR, respectively.

Cell culture and treatment

The mouse BV2 cells line (RRID: CVCL_0182) was kindly provided by Wuhan Pricella BiotechnologyCo.,Ltd. Vendor authentication by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling showed > 80% concordance with the reference BV-2 profile, indicating no cross-species or human-cell contamination. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), and 1% penicillin/strep tomycin (PS, Gibco, USA) in a sterile humidified environment at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cultures were inspected daily by light microscopy to confirm the absence of bacterial or fungal contamination, and screened monthly for Mycoplasma Stain Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), with all tests remaining negative throughout the study.

pRGCs were obtained from C57BL/6J mouse pups at postnatal day 2–4. Following euthanasia, the eyes were carefully enucleated, and all surrounding tissues were removed before isolating the retinas. Retinal tissue was finely chopped with ophthalmic scissors and incubated for 30 min in a digestion solution containing papain (8 mg/mL, Worthington, USA) and DNase I (15 U/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The resulting suspension was filtered and then resuspended in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B-27, penicillin–streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine. Cells were seeded onto dishes precoated with poly-D-lysine and laminin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

To establish an inflammatory model, BV2 cells were exposed to 1 µg/ml of LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. The treatment group (50µM ACA, MCE, USA; 10µM HY-113082, MCE, USA) was pre-treated for 6 h before adding LPS, and then they were combined with LPS to treat BV2 cells for 24 h.

HE staining

The eyeballs were enucleated and fixed in FAS Fixator (Servicebio, China) for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 10-µm-thick sections stained with HE solution. Images of the stained sections were obtained with Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar Deutschland, Germany). The thickness of IPL of four cross-sectional areas (500 and 1000 μm from the optic nerve head of both nasal and temporary retina) was measured by CaseViewer v2.4 (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary).

IF staining

For retinal whole mount, the mice were first transcardially perfused with NS solution, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Then, the eyeballs were enucleated and fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h. The retinas were carefully harvested and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100. After blocking, the collected retinas were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the samples were incubated with species-compatible secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature (RT). The retinas were then tiled on glass slides and cut into a four-leaf clover shape. Following previously published approaches for retinal flat-mount regional sampling [84–86], images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar Deutschland, Germany). In each quadrant, regions located approximately 1 mm and 2 mm from the margin of the optic disc were defined as the central and peripheral fields, respectively. The average number of RBPMS or Iba-1-positive cells of both central and peripheral areas was counted as the mean density of all retinas per group.

For retinal frozen section, the eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The eyecups were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose at 4 °C until they sank, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and frozen at -80 °C overnight. Tissues were cryosectioned at 10 μm thickness and collected onto glass slides. Prior to staining, sections were brought to room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% donkey serum supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After primary incubation, sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were washed three times with PBS, counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and washed three additional times with PBS. Finally, sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium, coverslipped, and imaged using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar Deutschland, Germany).

For cell labeling, cells grown on cell slides were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min at RT. The cells were incubated in appropriate concentrations of serum and 0.3%Triton X-100 for 1 h. After incubation with primary antibodies overnight, the samples were washed and incubated with the appropriate secondary anti-body for 1 h. Cell nuclei were stained by 4,6-diamidino 2-phenylindole (DAPI, Bioss, China) for 10 min. Images were captured using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar Deutschland, Germany). The sources and dilutions of all antibodies for IF are listed in Table S2.

qRT‑PCR

The total RNA of retinas and cells were extracted using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (Goonies, China). RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-1000, NanoDrop Technologies, USA). Subsequently, the extracted RNA was transcribed into cDNA using Evo M-MLV RT reagent kit (Accurate Biology, China). qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Accurate Biology, China) in the Light Cycler 480 RealTime PCR System (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., SUR). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to the expression of β-actin gene in the same samples and calculated using the comparative cycle threshold method (2-ΔΔCt method). The primers targeting corresponding mouse genes are listed in Table S3.

WB analysis

Total protein was extracted from the retinas and cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis solution (Beyotime, China) and separated on 8%-12% (wt/vol) gradient polyacrylamide gels according to a standard procedure. The levels of target proteins were normalized to β-actin from the same sample (set as 1.0). The primary antibodies and their respective dilutions are detailed in Table S2.

ScRNA-seq analysis

The data analysis pipeline in our study was described on the 10x Genomics (www.10xgenomics.com/) and the Seurat official website (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat). ScRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the 10×Genomics kit. The CellRanger (version 7.1.0) count commands were utilized to demultiplex and barcode the sequencing data. Downstream analyses were conducted using the R Seurat package (version 4.4.0), unless indicated otherwise. Following rigorous quality control (with a mitochondrial gene ratio of less than 10%, and detected genes ranging from more than 200 to fewer than 7,500), a total of 60,223 cells were retained for further analysis (12,602 cells from Blank, 28,759 cells from IR and 18,862 cells from ACA). The “FindMarkers” function within the Seurat package was employed to carry out differential expression analysis, identifying genes with | Log2 (Fold Change) | > 0.1 and P value < 0.05 as DEGs.

Gene functional analysis

DEGs were mapped to functional Gene Ontology (GO) terms using the web-based tool Metascape. A selection of representative GO terms and pathways was visualized with the R package ggplot2 (version 3.4.3).

GSEA

Gene sets related to selective pathways were downloaded from MSigDb (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp). GSEA of IR-DEGs and ACA-DEGs mapped to corresponding gene sets was performed using R package GSEABase (version 1.56.0).

Scoring of biological processes

The R package AUCell (version 1.8.1) [87] was used to score the pathway activity of individual cells. We used an expression matrix to calculate the gene expression ranking in each cell by the “AUCell_buildRankings” function with default parameters. After downloading the canonical path databases from the KEGG, and GO databases, pathways were used to score each cell. The “AUCell_calcAUC” function was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) value based on gene expression rankings.

Metabolic phenotyping

Metabolic phenotyping for NAD+/NADH quantification in retinas and BV2 cells were dissociated to cell lysate samples and compounds of cell lysate samples were measured following the manufacturer’s instructions (NAD+/NADH assay kit, Beyotime, China). Results were calculated according to the standard curve generated by using standards from the kits and were determined by chemiluminescence.

MMP measurement and mtROS staining

MMP was assessed using a tetramethylrhodamine, methyl ester (TMRM) probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Mito-SOX Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to detect mtROS. The Hoechst (Biosharp, China) was used to stain the nuclei of living cells. Briefly, target cells were seeded in 24-well plates (NEST, USA), treated accordingly, and stained with TMRM or Mito-SOX Red staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were imaged under a Leica DMi8 (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar Deutschland, Germany).

TEM

Cells were collected and fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde-2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), pH 7.3, at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times with phosphate buffer, the cells were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (Structure Probe) for 30 min. The samples were dehydrated and stained with 1% aqueous uranyl acetate (Structure Probe). The structures of mitochondria were observed under an H-7500 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 80 kV.

CCK-8 Assay

Cell viability was assessed using a CCK8 kit (Goonie, China). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with the indicated concentration of specific medicine for 24 h. Next, 10 µl of CCK-8 buffer was added to each well and incubated at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm in each well was then measured via a multimode reader.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction and hub gene prediction

Based on the retinal specific upregulated DEGs, we utilized STRING [88] to conduct the PPI network, which was visualized with Cytascape software (v3.9.1). Then, the hub genes were selected by CytoHubba plugin with six different algorithms [89].

siRNA and plasmids

BV2 cells were plated and grown to 70%-80% confluency at the time of transfection. The transfection complexes were prepared with KeygenMAX 3000 (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) and siRNA (50 nM) in Opti-MEM (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Following transfection for 4–6 h, the transfection complexes were removed, and BV2 cell culture medium was added to continue incubation for 24–48 h before proceeding with subsequent experiments. The sequences of siRNAs are listed in Table S3.

The coding sequence of Pkm2 and Sirt1 were synthesized and incorporated into pcDNA3.1 using endonuclease site of NheI and XhoI (Keygen BioTECH, China). BV2 cells were transfected with plasmid overexpressing Pkm2 or/and Sirt1(0.2 µg/µL) by KeygenMAX 3000 (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) and DNA enhancer (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). Following transfection for 4–6 h, the transfection complexes were removed, and BV2 cell culture medium was added to continue incubation for 24–48 h before proceeding with subsequent experiments. The oligos sequences are listed in Table S3.

CO-IP

After collecting BV2 cells subjected to different treatments, pre-chilled RIPA lysis buffer was added. The cells were subjected to sonication and then left on ice for 30 min. To remove cellular debris, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. One milliliter of the supernatant was then mixed with 0.2 µg of the primary antibody in a 1.5 mL EP tube and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. Following this, the solution was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 20 µL of Protein A/G Plus-Agarose (Santa Cruz, China) on a rotating mixer. The beads were collected by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and washed three times with PBS. After washing, 40 µL of sample loading buffer was added to the beads, and the mixture was boiled in a water bath at 100 °C for 10 min. Following centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and used for WB analysis. The primary antibodies and their respective dilutions are detailed in Table S2.

LC-MS/MS spectrometry analyses

Cells were lysed in pre-cold lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor, 3 µM TSA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 50 mM nicotinamide (NAM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4℃. Cell debris was discarded and supernatant was kept on ice. Protein concentration in super natant was measured by BCA kit according the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, China). The obtained protein samples were subjected to subsequent trypsin enzymatic hydrolysis, peptide modification enrichment and then was analyzed by liquid chromatography followed by liquid chromatog raphy-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (PTM Bio, China).

Computational screening

The data file for the structure of the mouse Pkm2 protein was downloaded from the AlphaFold database. All heterogeneous atoms were removed, followed by molecular docking. High-throughput virtual screening was performed by MCE Co., Ltd. under HTVS, SP, and XP modes. The compounds were selected based on scoring values, conformation and structural diversity. Protein–ligand interactions were obtained by PyMOL version 1.7.4.5.

ELISA

Mice were anesthetized after different treatment and the retinas was used for ELISA. ELISA was performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The following ELISA kits were used for detection: Mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (RayBiotech, China), Mouse IL-6 ELISA Kit (RayBiotech, China) and Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (RayBiotech, China).

Construction and Identification of Transgenic Mice

The Pkm2fl/fl mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (USA) and the Cx3cr1-Cre mice were purchased from Cyagen Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). One Step Mouse Genotyping Kit (Vazyme, China) was used for PCR experiments to identify the genotypes of the mice, with the procedures carried out according to the instructions provided by the reagent manufacturer. Pkm2 primers: F: 5’-CCT TCA GGA AGA CAG CCA AG-3’, R: 5’-AGT GCT GCC TGG AAT CCT CT-3’ (Homozygotes: one band with 680 bp, Heterozygotes: two bands with 680 bp and 578 bp, Wildtype allele: one band with 578 bp). Cx3cr1-Cre primers: F1: 5’-GAC ATT TGC CTT GCT GGA C-3’, R: 5’-GCA GGG AAA TCT GAT GCA AG-3’ (Wildtype: N.A. Targeted: 380 bp).

OGD/R model

The OGD/R model was established as described previously [23]. In brief, OGD/R was induced by washing cells twice with PBS, replacing medium with serum- and glucose-free media and placed under conditions of 5% CO2 and 95% N2 in a 37 °C incubator for 3 h, then returned to the normal environment (37 °C, 5% CO2 and normoxic conditions) and complete medium (DMEM with 10% FBS and glucose [4.9 g/L]) for 24 h.

Cell treatment for co-culture

To maximally simulate interactions between microglia and RGCs, BV2 cells were co-cultured with pRGCs in transwell chambers. In brief, BV2 cells (4 × 10⁴ cells per well) treated with siRNA, plasmid or mutant, or treated with different conditions (ACA, sirtinol or HY-113082) were inoculated into 0.4-micron transwell chambers and placed in a 12-well plate above pRGCs (1 × 10⁵ cells per well), and co-cultured for 24 h.

Quantification

ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/) was used for the quantification of retinal whole mount, retinal section, WB, CO-IP, IF, MMP, and mtROS.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Power analysis by PASS software was used to ensure proper sample size. All values for each group were presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). Parametric and nonparametric tests were used according to the homogeneity of variance. According to different comparison situations, statistical differences were analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, as appropriate, with Sidak’s or Turkey’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as ns for not significant, * for P < 0.05, ** for P < 0.01, *** for P < 0.001, and **** for P < 0.0001.
