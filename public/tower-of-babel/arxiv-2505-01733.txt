Free line arrangements with low maximal multiplicity
Alexandru Dimca, Lukas Kühne, Piotr Pokora

Abstract
. Let 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} be a free arrangement of d d lines in the complex projective plane, with exponents d 1 ≤ d 2 d_{1}\leq d_{2} . Let m m be the maximal multiplicity of points in 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} . In this note, we describe first the simple cases d 1 ≤ m d_{1}\leq m . Then we study the case d 1 = m + 1 d_{1}=m+1 , and describe which line arrangements can occur by deleting or adding a line to 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} . When d ≤ 14 d\leq 14 , there are only two free arrangements with d 1 = m + 2 d_{1}=m+2 , namely one with degree 13 13 and the other with degree 14 14 . We study their geometries in order to deepen our understanding of the structure of free line arrangements in general.

1.
Introduction

Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
be the graded polynomial ring in the variables
x,y,z
with the complex coefficients. Let
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
be a line arrangement of degree
d
in the complex projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Denote by
J_{f}
the Jacobian ideal generated by all partial derivatives of
f
, and we define the Milnor algebra as
M(f)=S/J_{f}
. Recall that if
{\mathcal{A}}:f=0
is a line arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, then
M(f)
admits the following minimal resolution:

0\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s-2}S(-e_{i})\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}S(1-d-d_{i})\rightarrow S^{3}(1-d)\rightarrow S\rightarrow M(f)\rightarrow 0,

where
e_{1}\leq e_{2}\leq\ldots\leq e_{s-2}
and
1\leq d_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d_{s}
, see for instance
[
16
]
. The integers
d_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d_{s}
are called the exponents of
{\mathcal{A}}
. When
s=2
, then
{\mathcal{A}}
is called
free
. If
{\mathcal{A}}
is a free line arrangement and
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is another line arrangement with the same intersection lattice, then
Terao’s Conjecture predicts that
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is also free.

If
{\mathcal{A}}
is a line arrangement and
p
is a point in
{\mathcal{A}}
, then the multiplicity
m_{p}
of
p
in
{\mathcal{A}}
is by definition the number of lines in
{\mathcal{A}}
containing the point
p
. Let
m=m({\mathcal{A}})
be the maximal multiplicity of the intersection points in
{\mathcal{A}}
. It is known that either
d_{1}=d-m
, or

m-1\leq d_{1}<d-m,

see
[
8
]
. Moreover, if
d_{1}\in\{m-1,m\}
, then Terao’s Conjecture holds for
{\mathcal{A}}
, see
[
13
, Remark 5.4]
and Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
, where these two cases are briefly discussed.
The same holds when
d_{1}=d-m
, see
[
8
]
.

From now on we consider free line arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}
such that

d_{1}=m+\epsilon<d-m,

for some integer
\epsilon>0
.
Note that if
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is another line arrangement with the same intersection lattice as
{\mathcal{A}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
is not free, then we have

m\leq\overline{d}_{1}<m+\epsilon,

where
\overline{d}_{1}
is the minimal exponent of
\overline{{\mathcal{A}}}
.

It follows that the bigger
\epsilon
is, the more space there is for counterexamples to Terao’s Conjecture.

In Section
2
, we discuss the case
\epsilon=1
, see Theorems
2.5
and
2.7
, which yields a precise description of the behavior of a free arrangement with
d_{1}=m+1
when deleting or adding a line.

Then, in Section
3
, we consider the case
\epsilon\geq 2
, in which free arrangements with
d_{1}=m+\epsilon
appear to be rather rare.
First we show in Proposition
3.1
a new bound on
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
in terms of
m
and
\epsilon
. In particular, this shows that
d\geq 15
when
\epsilon=3
, see Remark
3.3
.
For
\epsilon=2
and
d\leq 14
, there are only two such arrangements,
{\mathcal{A}}
(resp.
{\mathcal{C}}
) containing
d=13
(resp.
d=14
) lines, and we study them in the remaining part of Section 3.
The fact that there are only two such free arrangements in this range, as well as their equations described below, follows from the article
[
4
]
and the associated database
[
3
]
constructed to verify Terao’s Conjecture up to
d\leq 14

[
5
]
.
Starting with their equations, we give geometric proofs (i.e. without using computer-aided computations) for their freeness, see Theorems
3.8
and
3.12
.

Although the freeness of the arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
can be established more directly by observing that they are divisionally free (see Remark
3.14
), we have chosen to present the longer proofs given in Theorems
3.8
and
3.12
. These arguments rely on more general techniques and may therefore be applicable to similar arrangements that are not necessarily divisionally free.

We hope that further study of such examples will allow us to obtain results for the cases
\epsilon>1
analogous to those established for
\epsilon=1
.

2.
On free line arrangements with
m-1\leq d_{1}\leq m+1

For integers
d>d_{1}>0
, following
[
15
]
, we define

\tau_{max}(d,d_{1})=(d-1)^{2}-d_{1}(d-d_{1}-1).

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be an arrangement of
d
lines in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and let
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
be the smallest two exponents of
{\mathcal{A}}
. It is well-known, see for instance
[
7
,
15
]
, that
{\mathcal{A}}
is free if and only if

(2.1)

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,d_{1}),

where
\tau({\mathcal{A}})
is the global Tjurina number of the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Say
n_{r}
is the number of intersection points in
{\mathcal{A}}
of multiplicity
r
.
The Tjurina number
\tau({\mathcal{A}})
can be combinatorially computed as

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\sum_{r\geq 2}n_{r}(r-1)^{2}.

Since the exponent
d_{1}
is not determined by the combinatorics, we try in this section to describe results similar to the above one, but involving
easy combinatorial properties of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Let
m=m({\mathcal{A}})
be the maximal multiplicity of points of
{\mathcal{A}}
. If
d-m\leq m-1
, then it is known that

d_{1}=d-m

see
[
8
]
. Hence we can apply (
2.1
) to obtain an obvious characterization of free arrangements.
In the remaining cases, when
d+1>2m
, it is known that

m-1\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq d-m,

and the equality
m-1=d_{1}
implies that the line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with exponents

(d_{1},d_{2})=(m-1,d-m)
, see again
[
8
]
. In fact, we have the following more precise result, see
[
13
, Theorem 1.12 (1)]
.

Recall that a point
p\in A
of multiplicity
m_{p}\geq 2
is a modular point for the line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
if for any other point

q\in{\mathcal{A}}
of multiplicity
m_{q}\geq 2
, the line determined by the points
p
and
q
is in
{\mathcal{A}}
. A line arrangement having a modular point is called supersolvable, see
[
17
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
is a line arrangement satisfying

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m-1)

where
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
and
m=m({\mathcal{A}})
. Then
{\mathcal{A}}
is a free line arrangement with exponents

(d_{1},d_{2})=(m-1,d-m)
. In addition,
{\mathcal{A}}
is a supersolvable line arrangement and any point
p
in
{\mathcal{A}}
with multiplicity
m
is a modular point for
{\mathcal{A}}
.

It follows that the interesting cases for understanding the structure of free line arrangements are described by the inequalities

m\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq d-m.

One has the following result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}
is a line arrangement satisfying

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m)

where
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
and
m=m({\mathcal{A}})
. Then
{\mathcal{A}}
is a free line arrangement with exponents

(d_{1},d_{2})=(m,d-m-1)
.

Proof.

We know that
d_{1}\geq m-1
. The equality
d_{1}=m-1
would give by the above discussion

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m-1).
However, a simple computation shows that

\tau_{max}(d,m-1)\neq\tau_{max}(d,m),

and hence
d_{1}>m-1
.
On the other hand, the inequality
d_{1}>m
would imply

\tau({\mathcal{A}})<\tau_{max}(d,m),

see
[
15
]
. Hence the only possibility is
d_{1}=m
, and the claim follows from our discussion above.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}
is a line arrangement satisfying
d_{1}=m=m({\mathcal{A}})
and let
p\in{\mathcal{A}}
be a point of multiplicity
m
.
Then it follows from
[
13
, Theorem 1.12 (2)]
that all the multiple points in
{\mathcal{A}}
not connected to
p
by lines in
{\mathcal{A}}
are situated on a line
L
. The example of the arrangement

{\mathcal{A}}:x(x^{m-1}-y^{m-1})(y^{m-1}-z^{m-1})(x^{m-1}-z^{m-1})=0,

where
p=(0:0:1)
, shows that
L
may not be unique. Indeed, the only point not connected to
p
is
q=(1:0:0)
and hence any line
L
with
q\in L
will do the job. The line
L_{y}:y=0
and the line
L_{z}:z=0
are special, since when we add
L_{y}
or
L_{z}
to
{\mathcal{A}}
we get a supersolvable arrangement. They are characterized by the fact that
q\in L
and the number of points in the intersection
L\cap{\mathcal{A}}
is minimal, namely
m+1
.

It is an interesting open question whether the line
L
in

[
13
, Theorem 1.12 (2)]
can be always chosen such that
{\mathcal{A}}\cup L
is supersolvable. This will give a strong information on the structure of line arrangements satisfying
d_{1}=m({\mathcal{A}})
, namely they are obtained from a supersolvable line arrangement by deleting a suitable line.

We need the following result to continue our discussion.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a line arrangement,
L\in{\mathcal{A}}
be a line and denote by
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
the line arrangement obtained from
{\mathcal{A}}
by deleting the line
L
.
Then

\tau({\mathcal{A}})-\tau({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})=2(d-1)-r_{L}

where
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
and
r_{L}=|L\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}|
.

Proof.

For any point
q\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cap L
, denote by
m_{q}^{\prime}\geq 1
its multiplicity in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
.
One has

\tau({\mathcal{A}})-\tau({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})=\sum_{q\in L\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(m_{q}^{\prime 2}-(m_{q}^{\prime}-1)^{2})=\sum_{q\in L\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(2m_{q}^{\prime}-1)=2(d-1)-r_{L},

since
\sum_{q\in L\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}m_{q}^{\prime}=\deg({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})=d-1
by Bézout’s Theorem.
∎

We now consider line arrangements satisfying
d_{1}=m({\mathcal{A}})+1
. Recall that an arrangement
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
of
d
lines is
plus-one generated
if
s=3
and
d_{1}+d_{2}=d

Theorem 2.5
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}
is a line arrangement satisfying

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)

where
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
and
m=m({\mathcal{A}})\leq\frac{d-3}{2}
. Let
L\in{\mathcal{A}}
and let
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
be the line arrangement obtained from
{\mathcal{A}}
by deleting the line
L
.
If
m=m({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
, then one of the following situations occurs.

(1)

r_{L}=2(d-1)-3m
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is free with exponents
(m-1,d-m-1)
. In this case
3m\geq d-1
and the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is plus-one generated with exponents
(m,d-m,r_{L}-1)
.

(2)

r_{L}=d-m-1
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is free with exponents
(m,d-m-2)
. In this case
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-2)
.

(3)

In all other cases one has
r_{L}<d-1-m
. In particular, if in addition
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with
exponents
(m+1,d-m-2)
, then either
m+1<d-m-2
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-3)
, or
m+1\leq d-m-2
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is a plus-one generated arrangement with exponents
(m+1,d-m-2,d-r_{L})
.

Proof.

The equality
\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)
implies
d_{1}\leq m+1
.
On the other hand, the discussion just before Proposition
2.1
implies that
d_{1}\geq m
.
Let
d_{1}^{\prime}
be the minimal exponent of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
. It is clear that

m-1\leq d_{1}-1\leq d_{1}^{\prime}\leq d_{1}\leq m+1.

Assume first that
d_{1}^{\prime}=m-1
. Then as seen above

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is free and hence

\tau({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})=(d-2)^{2}-(m-1)(d-m-1),

which gives the equality

\tau({\mathcal{A}})-\tau({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})=3m.

Lemma
2.4
implies that
r_{L}=2(d-1)-3m
and since
r_{L}\leq d-1
we get
3m\geq d-1
.
To see that the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is plus-one generated with exponents
(m,d-m,r_{L}-1)
, we apply
[
12
, Theorem 1.4 (3)]
.

Assume from now on that
d_{1}^{\prime}\geq m
. By
[
15
]
we have

\tau({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})\leq\tau_{max}(d-1,m),

and the equality holds if and only if
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is free with exponents
(m,d-m-2)
.
It follows that

\tau({\mathcal{A}})-\tau({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})\geq\tau_{max}(d,m+1)-\tau_{max}(d-1,m)=d+m-1,

and the equality holds if and only if
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is free with exponents
(m,d-m-2)
. Lemma
2.4
tells us that this equality is equivalent to the equality
r_{L}=d-1-m
. To see that in these conditions
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-2)
, we apply
[
12
, Theorem 1.4 (2)]
.

The last claim in
(3)
follows from
[
12
, Theorem 1.3]
, using either claim
(1)
or claim
(3)
there.
∎

.

Theorem 2.7
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}
is a line arrangement satisfying

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1),

where
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
and
m=m({\mathcal{A}})\leq\frac{d-3}{2}
. Let
p\in{\mathcal{A}}
be a point of multiplicity
m
and
L
a line passing through
p
which is not in
{\mathcal{A}}
. Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the arrangement obtained by adding the line
L
to
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then one of the following holds.

(1)

r_{L}=|L\cap{\mathcal{A}}|=3(m+1)-d
and
{\mathcal{B}}
is a free line arrangement with exponents
(m,d-m)
. In this case
m\geq(d-2)/3
and the line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is a plus-one generated arrangement with exponents
(m,d-m,2d-3(m+1))
.

(2)

r_{L}=|L\cap{\mathcal{A}}|=m+2

and
{\mathcal{B}}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-1)
. Then
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-2)
.

(3)

In all the other cases
r_{L}>m+2
. In particular, if in addition
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-2)
, then
either
m+1<d-m-2
,
r_{L}=d-m-1
and
{\mathcal{B}}
is free with exponents
(m+2,d-m-2)
, or

{\mathcal{B}}
is a plus-one generated arrangement with exponents
(m+2,d-m-1,r_{L}-1)
.

Proof.

Note that
m({\mathcal{B}})=m+1
and hence the minimal exponent of
{\mathcal{B}}
, say

d_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, satisfies
d_{1}^{\prime\prime}\geq m
. If
d_{1}^{\prime\prime}=m
, then it follows that
{\mathcal{B}}

is a free line arrangement with exponents
(m,d-m)
and the formula for
r_{L}
comes from Lemma
2.4
. Since
r_{L}\geq 1
, we get

m\geq(d-2)/3
. The claim about
{\mathcal{A}}
follows from
[
12
, Theorem 1.3 (3)]
by observing that the cases
(1)
and
(2)
of that result cannot hold in our situation.

Consider now the case
d_{1}^{\prime\prime}\geq m+1
. Then
[
15
]
implies that

\tau({\mathcal{B}})\leq\tau_{max}(d+1,m+1)
and hence

\tau({\mathcal{B}})-\tau({\mathcal{A}})\leq\tau_{max}(d+1,m+1)-\tau_{max}(d,m+1)=2d-m-2.

This yields the first claim in
(2)
as well as the claims about
r_{L}
.
The claim about
{\mathcal{A}}
follows from
[
12
, Theorem 1.3 (1)]
.

The last claim in
(3)
follows from
[
12
, Theorem 1.4]
, using either the claim
(2)
or the claim
(3)
there.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

(i) Note that the value of

in Theorem

is determined by the intersection lattice of

. On the other hand, the value of

in Theorem

(ii) In Theorem

and hence the case
(1)
,

,

. On the other hand, in Theorem

and hence the case

,

A simpler and complete description of the line arrangements satisfying

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)
similar to Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
is given in the following result.

Theorem 2.10
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{A}}
is a line arrangement satisfying

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)

where
d=|{\mathcal{A}}|
and
m=m({\mathcal{A}})\leq\frac{d-3}{2}
.
Then one of the following situations occurs.

(1)

The arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is plus-one generated with exponents
(m,d-m,2d-3m-3)
.

(2)

The arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
is free with exponents
(m+1,d-m-2)
.

Proof.

The equality
\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)
implies that

d_{1}\leq m+1.

On the other hand, we know that
d_{1}\geq m-1
and the equality

d_{1}=m-1
is not possible by Proposition
2.1
since

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)<\tau_{max}(d,m-1).

If
d_{1}=m
, then we can apply
[
13
, Theorem 1.12 (2)]
.
In addition, since

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=\tau_{max}(d,m+1)<\tau_{max}(d,m),

the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
cannot be free with exponents
(m,d-m-1)
. It follows that
{\mathcal{A}}
is a plus-one generated arrangement with exponents

(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
. In order to determine
d_{3}
we use the formula

\tau({\mathcal{A}})=(d-1)^{2}-d_{1}(d-1-d_{1})-(d_{3}-d_{2}+1),

which comes from
[
10
, Proposition 2.1 (4)]
, using the relation

d_{1}+d_{2}=d
and then we replace
\tau({\mathcal{A}})
by

On free line arrangements with

We start with the following general result that yields bounds for the number of lines of free line arrangements depending on the maximal multiplicity of the intersections.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

be a free arrangement of

lines with

Proof.

The freeness of the arrangement

For the second inequality, recall that by

m+\epsilon=d_{1}\geq\frac{2}{m}\cdot d-2,

which gives us

∎

(x+y+(e-1)z)(y+(e-2)z)(x+(1-e)y+z)(x+(2-e)y+(e-1)z)(x+(2-e)y+ez)=0,

where
e^{2}-3e+3=0
.
This arrangement has
n_{2}=13
,
n_{3}=6
and
n_{4}=10
. Moreover it is free with exponents
(6,7)
. Now we prove its freeness geometrically. If we look at the number of points of multiplicity
4
situated on each line in
{\mathcal{C}}_{14}
, we see that the lines
L_{4}
and
L_{6}
, corresponding to the 4th and the 6th factors above, are exceptional because each of them contains only one point of multiplicity
4
.
As a result, we consider the arrangement
{\mathcal{D}}_{12}
obtained from
{\mathcal{C}}_{14}
by deleting the lines
L_{4}
and
L_{6}
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{D}}_{12}
be the arrangement obtained from
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}_{14}
by deleting the lines
L_{4}
and
L_{6}
. Then
{\mathcal{D}}
is a free arrangement with exponents
(4,7)
.

Proof.

The proof goes along the same lines as for Theorem
3.7
. First of all, we observe that
(0:1:0)
is a point of multiplicity
4
for
{\mathcal{D}}
since the lines
x=0
,
z=0
,
x+z=0
and
x+(e-1)z=0
meet there. It follows that
the minimal exponent
d_{1}
of
{\mathcal{D}}
satisfies

d_{1}\geq\bigg\lceil\frac{1}{2}\cdot 12-2\bigg\rceil=4,

see
[
8
, Theorem 2.8]
.
If
d_{1}=4
, then
{\mathcal{D}}
is free with exponents
(4,7)
, which gives the correct Tjurina number, and
d_{1}>4
yields too small Tjurina numbers for
{\mathcal{D}}
, see also
[
13
, Remark 5.4]
.
∎

Theorem 3.12
.

The arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}_{14}
is a free arrangement with exponents
(6,7)
.

Proof.

To get from
{\mathcal{D}}
to
{\mathcal{C}}
we add first the line
L_{4}:x+y=0
and get a new arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
. Then one has
|L_{4}\cap{\mathcal{D}}|=8
, and the exact sequence (
3.1
) applied to the pair
({\mathcal{C}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{D}})
and to
k=4
yields

0\to D_{0}(g^{\prime})_{3}\to D_{0}(g)_{4}\to R_{-3}

which gives us
D_{0}(g)_{4}=0
since
D_{0}(g^{\prime})_{3}=R_{-3}=0
.
The same exact sequence for
k=5
yields

1=\dim D_{0}(g^{\prime})_{3}=\dim D_{0}(g)_{4},

since
R_{-2}=0
. It follows that the minimal exponent of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
is 5.

Then we add to
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
the line
L_{6}:x+(1-e)y=0
. One has
|L_{6}\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}|=8
and the exact sequence (
3.1
) applied to the pair
({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime})
and to
k=5
yields

0\to D_{0}(g^{\prime})_{4}\to D_{0}(g)_{5}\to R_{-2}

which gives us
D_{0}(g)_{5}=0
since
D_{0}(g^{\prime})_{4}=R_{-2}=0
. It follows that the minimal exponent
d_{1}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies
d_{1}\geq 6
. On the other hand, we have

\tau({\mathcal{C}})=127=\tau_{max}(14,6),

which implies that
{\mathcal{C}}
is free with exponents
(6,7)
.

∎

The proofs above give a new proof for the following known result.

Corollary 3.13
.

The arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}_{14}
satisfies Terao’s Conjecture.

Indeed, for any line arrangement
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
, having the same intersection lattice with
{\mathcal{C}}
, the proofs of Theorems
3.11
and
3.12
can be applied to yield this claim.

Remark 3.14
.

Both arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
are divisionally free
[
1
]
. Indeed the characteristic polynomial
\chi({\mathcal{A}},t)
is

(t-1)(t-6)^{2}
and the characteristic polynomial of the restriction
{\mathcal{A}}^{L}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
to the line
L=L_{8}
considered in the proof of Theorem
3.8
is
\chi({\mathcal{A}}^{L},t)=(t-1)(t-6)
. Similarly, the characteristic polynomial
\chi(\mathcal{C},t)
is

(t-1)(t-6)(t-7)
and the characteristic polynomial of the restriction
{\mathcal{C}}^{L}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
to the line
L=L_{6}
considered in the proof of Theorem
3.12
is
\chi({\mathcal{A}}^{L},t)=(t-1)(t-7)
. Since
\chi({\mathcal{A}},t)
is divisible by

\chi({\mathcal{A}}^{L},t)
and respectively
\chi({\mathcal{C}},t)
is divisible by

\chi({\mathcal{C}}^{L},t)
, it follows that both line arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
are divisionally free.
This fact also, in particular, proves that they are free.
As being divisionally free is a combinatorial property depending on the intersection lattice only, this also yields a second proof of Corollary
3.9
and
3.13
.

In fact, for a line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
being divisionally free is equivalent to the existence of a line
L\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}
and deleted arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{A}}\setminus L
are both free, see
[
1
, Theorem 3.11]
. In particular, we see in this way that the arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}_{12}={\mathcal{A}}\setminus L_{8}
in the proof of Theorem
3.8
and the arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{C}}\setminus L_{6}
in the proof of Theorem
3.12
are both free.

On the other hand, note that the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}
in Theorem
2.5
(2) and the arrangement
{\mathcal{B}}
in Theorem
2.7
(2)
are also divisionally free. However, the monomial arrangements in Example
2.8
are not divisionally free and satisfy
d_{1}=m+1
.
More generally, the arrangement

{\mathcal{A}}^{0}_{3}(r):\prod_{0\leq n<r}(x-\zeta^{n}y)(x-\zeta^{n}z)(y-\zeta^{n}z)=0

satisfies
d_{1}=m+1
and is not divisionally free, see
[
1
, Theorem 5.6]
.

It would be interesting to find free line arrangements with minimal number of lines which are not divisionally free and which satisfy
d_{1}=m+2
.
