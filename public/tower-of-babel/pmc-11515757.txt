What is the impact of acute endocrine and metabolic alterations on long-term ischemic stroke prognosis: a prospective study
Authors: Chunyang Pang, Yufei Chen, Yinuo Chen, Er lin, Xinnan Pan, Yiting Xu, Huan Yu, Wanli Zhang, Binbin Deng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke stress can trigger instant survival but its influence on long-term ischemic stroke outcomes remains controversial. Thus, we sought to explore the associations of acute post-stroke stress evidenced by endocrine and metabolic changes, with long-term ischemic stroke outcomes.MethodsAdmissions for acute ischemic stroke within seven days of onset were prospectively recruited to determine acute endocrine and metabolic variations measured by thyroid parameters and the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR). Long-term ischemic stroke prognoses were followed up for one year, with the primary outcome being a modified Rankin Scale score of 3 to 6.ResultsA total of 887 patients were enrolled, of which 535 reached the final one-year followed up at a poor prognosis rate of 29.3%. Patients with poor outcomes were observed to have lower levels of free triiodothyronine (fT3) and higher levels of SHR on admission. Medium values (fT3, 4.4 mmol/L; SHR, 8 nmol/L) were used to divide patients into four gradient stress degrees. Larger acute endocrine and metabolic variations (fT3 < 4.4 mmol/L and SHR ≥ 8 nmol/L) were independently associated with a poor one-year prognosis (adjusted OR = 4.231, P = 0.001).ConclusionsHigh degrees of acute post-stroke stress may aggravate long-term ischemic stroke prognosis and timely stress-reduced interventions may help promote post-stroke living quality is equally important as survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05453-9.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Stress hyperglycemia, Thyroid hormones, Stroke prognosis, Stress

Background

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is a threatening stressor and an increasingly major cause of death and disability, accounting for the leading criminal of hospitalization for neurologic diseases [1].In the acute early stage, profound alterations of the autonomic, endocrine, metabolic, and immune systems could provide an advantage to balance homeostasis due to the central nervous system’s (CNS) response to the life-threatening insult [2, 3]. Neuroendocrine and subsequent metabolic cross-talks are essential for mounting an effective response to stroke [4]. The hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis is a typical representative of those cross-talks. After a stressful stroke, decreased circulating levels of T3 are often observed, of which degrees of changes can reflect the severity of illness [5, 6]. Additionally, metabolic changes such as stress hyperglycemia are a universal reaction to stress hormones and proinflammatory cytokines [7].

The neuroendocrine response to a threatening stroke follows a biphasic pattern, with potentially conflicting protection effects between acute and later phases [6]. Except it activates a stroke-specific chain of survival [8], poststroke stress alterations may, in turn, aggravate the progression of ischemic brain injury, which accounts for the substantial impact of systemic complications on stroke outcomes [3], such as cognitive impairment, depression, infection etc [4]. Adaptation regulation and dysregulation may be two sides of the same coin. Previous studies have primarily focused on the relationship between stroke and stress-related changes in single systems, with stress hyperglycemia [9] and thyroid parameters [5, 10, 11] being the most commonly used stress markers. However, measurements of single-system stress changes can be easily influenced by underlying diseases. For instance, stress-induced hyperglycemia predicts higher in-hospital mortality and poorer recovery in nondiabetic stroke patients but not in diabetics [12], while lower free triiodothyronine is associated with worse outcomes in clinically euthyroid patients but not in those with thyroid dysfunction [13]. Combining multiple system indicators to quantify stress levels may offer greater objectivity and adaptability, but corresponding studies are scarce. Besides, previous studies have also shown inconsistent conclusions. While higher stress hyperglycemia may increase the occurrence of poor stroke outcomes, including mortality, neurological deficit, HT, and infectious complications [14], it is not independently associated with mortality or disability when stroke severity, as well as an inflammatory component, are taken into account [15]. Furthermore, the reference ranges for thyroid function in relation to stroke outcomes are debated, as it remains unclear whether pre-disease states are related to changes in the acute phase of the disease that affect ischemic stroke prognosis at discharge or in the long term [16–18].

Thus, we aimed to explore the variations and impact of stress responses, as indicated by baseline thyroid hormones and glycemic indices, on the risk of a poor functional ischemic stroke outcome at one year and their predictive capacity in clinical prognosis.

Methods

Patient enrollment and follow-up

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University and was by the Helsinki Declaration. Between June 2019 and June 2022, admissions for AIS were eligible if they met the following criteria: (a) definite diagnosis within 7 days of the event of AIS; (b) no transient ischemic attack, cerebral hemorrhage, or subarachnoid hemorrhage; (c) age ≥ 18 years; (d) no history of thyroid disease, abnormal proteinemia, and malnutrition; (e) no history of medicine affecting the determination of thyroid indexes, such as Phenytoin and Amiodaron for nearly 1 year. Ultimately, 887 admissions were enrolled. National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores [19] were assessed on admission, and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score [20] and the Barthel index (BI) [21] were determined every 3 months for at least 1 year through follow-up after discharge via telephone consultations, questionnaire surveys or outpatient reviews. Good and poor outcomes were defined as an mRS score of 0–2 and 3–6.

Baseline collection and stress indices

Basic information, disease history, and medical records were collected to assess components of a stroke at admission, including the NIHSS score and etiology classification (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment, TOAST [22]) criteria. Minor stroke was defined as a baseline NIHSS score of ≤ 3. Blood parameters were tested in the early morning following admission in a fasting state, including thyroid hormones and glycemic indices. Previous literatures [5, 6] have indicated that the hypothalamus-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis and stress-induced hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) [7] are convenient quantifications of acute endocrine and metabolic alterations. As there is no unified cutoff value for stress degrees, we used median levels of fT3 and SHR to classify high and low-stress levels, thus resulting in four groups according to a combination of different truncation intervals. SHR is calculated by dividing the fasting blood glucose measured on the morning of the second day after admission by the hemoglobin A1c [7].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described as proportions, and continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation. Baseline characteristics grouped by 1-year outcomes were compared using the chi-square test or t-test as appropriate. Univariate and multivariable logistics regression models were performed to estimate the association between stress indices and 1-year outcomes.

All analyses were conducted with SPSS version 26.0 software, R x64 4.1.2, and 2-tailed p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 887 patients with ischemic stroke were included in this study, with a median age of 67 years, 68.1% male, 42.8% classified as large-artery atherosclerosis subtypes, a median NIHSS score of 4, and 52.0% with middle cerebral artery infarctions. Among these, 535 patients completed the 1-year prognosis follow-up, with a poor prognosis rate of 29.3%. The patients who completed the follow-up and those who were lost to follow-up were generally well balanced, except for differences in the proportions of hyperlipidemia, LAA subtypes, smoking status, and baseline levels of NIHSS and BMI (Supplementary Table 1).

Median levels of fT3 (8.8 pmol/L) and SHR (8 nmol/L) were selected to classify stress levels. Patients were classified as: Group1-very low level: SHR < 8 nmol/L, fT3 ≥ 4.4 pmol/L; Group2-low level: SHR ≥ 8 nmol/L, fT3 ≥ 4.4 pmol/L; Group3-high level: SHR < 8 nmol/L, fT3 < 4.4 pmol/L; Group4-very high level: SHR ≥ 8 nmol/L, fT3 < 4.4 pmol/L. The clinical characteristics classified by stress level groups were summarized in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, age (Crude OR = 1.062, P < 0.001), atrial fibrillation (Crude OR = 2.317, P = 0.002), hypertension (Crude OR = 1.982, P = 0.002), hyperlipidemia (Crude OR = 0.499, P < 0.001), lymphocyte (Crude OR = 0.423, P < 0.001), and baseline NIHSS (Crude OR = 1.526, P < 0.001) were significant factors for poor 1-year ischemic stroke outcomes (mRS > 2).

The analysis revealed that PCA infarctions tend to have a better prognosis, even for long-term outcomes, while cardioembolic is a more frequent stroke subtype in very old patients (age > = 85 years).

Acute endocrine and metabolic alterations and stroke prognosis

As shown in Fig. 1, patients attacked by acute ischemic stroke tended to have significantly lower levels of fT3 (4.46 ± 0.83 versus 4.93 ± 0.72 pmol/L, P = 0.002), fast blood glucose (4.69 ± 0.68 versus 5.67 ± 1.75 mmol/L, P = 0.032) and SHR (8.3 ± 1.2 versus 9.1 ± 2.4 nmol/L, P = 0.025) than healthy controls. Furtherly, stroke patients with severe stroke (baseline NIHSS > 3) tended to have significantly lower levels of total thyroxine (100 ± 22.8 versus 103 ± 18.6 nmol/L, P = 0.032) and fT3 (4 ± 0.92 versus 4.5 ± 0.64 pmol/L, P = 0.028). Additionally, those with poor 1-year stroke prognosis (mRS > 2) tended to have significantly lower levels of fT3 (4.3 ± 1.14 versus 4.56 ± 0.73 pmol/L, P = 0.019) and Hb1Ac (6.22 ± 1.38 versus 6.65 ± 1.69%, P = 0.023), and significantly higher levels of SHR (9.4 ± 2.1 versus 8.8 ± 1.9 nmol/L, P = 0.001).

Post hoc analysis

[1] Medium analysis: acute endocrine and metabolic alterations were significantly related to the baseline NIHSS (r=-0.235, P < 0.001), a well-established factor for ischemic stroke prognosis. The further medium analysis uncovered that the relationships between stress and long-term ischemic stroke prognosis were both dependent and independent from baseline ischemic stroke severity (proportions of medium effect = 40.98%, P < 0.00 1; β direct effect = 0.037, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3); [2] Sensitivity analysis considering stress response in different periods: patients admitted 1 day from the onset, 3 days from the onset, and 7 days from the onset were considered to explore associations of the stress response in different periods with long-term stroke prognosis. Very early endocrine and metabolic alterations may not be related to long-term stroke outcomes (within 1 day: adjusted OR = 4.033, P = 0.086). However, changes in the 3-day and 7-day periods may be meaningful (within 1 day: adjusted OR = 2.981, P < 0.026; within 7 days: adjusted OR = 4.231, P = 0.001) (Supplementary Table 4); [3] Sensitivity analysis: Exploration of stress status and long-term ischemic stroke outcomes with additional consideration of arterial territories. As shown in Supplementary Table 5, after accounting for the territories of infarcted arteries, larger acute endocrine and metabolic variations (fT3 < 4.4 mmol/L and SHR ≥ 8 nmol/L) remained independently associated with poorer prognosis at 3 months (adjusted OR = 3.663, P = 0.001), 6 months (adjusted OR = 4.393, P < 0.001), and one year (adjusted OR = 4.621, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Systemic stress responses are adaptive answers to ischemic stroke under many circumstances, yet the correlations between different levels of these changes and different terms of stroke prognosis are inconsistent. In our study, we combined two routine testing items, corresponding to acute endocrine and metabolic alterations, to prospectively examine the causal relationship between variations in admission systemic stress and ischemic stroke functional outcome. We found that endocrine and metabolic alterations at admission were independently associated with after-stroke 1-year functional prognosis (3 months, 6 months, 12 months), and may be indicative of long-term ischemic stroke prognosis (1-year: adjusted OR = 4.231, P = 0.001). It is suggested that stress alterations may be promising for outcome prediction and interventions to reduce stress response during the acute phase of stroke may improve post-stroke outcomes (Fig. 4).

Summary from results to insights: high degrees of stress on acute stroke attacks may aggravate long-term stroke prognosis, and timely stress-reduced interventions may help promote post-stroke qualified living. This study may address a gap in research by evaluating the combined assessment of stress across multiple systems and its impact on long-term ischemic stroke prognosis. Outcome prediction is crucial for decision-making in stroke units to achieve precision medicine [23]. Many stress markers, such as SHR [9], serum catecholamine [24], and free triiodothyronine [5, 10, 11], have been proven to relate to poor outcomes in patients with severe illnesses, including sepsis [6], trauma [25], and acute vascular events [26], but studies on systemic stress levels considering both endocrine and metabolic alterations are scare. The regulation of the HPT axis and glucose are known to occur during a stress response, but they are unable to account for interference from the patient’s baseline conditions [27], and combinations of the two systems may compensate for and increase the specialty for stress. Moreover, prospective follow-up designs have provided strong evidence for causal relationships, and further medium analysis has revealed that the relationship between admission stress and long-term stroke prognosis was partially independent of baseline stroke severity.

A better understanding of the mechanisms through which stress modulates neuron function could lead to improved treatment of stroke. Lower levels of thyroid functions and higher levels of glucose may induce prolonged residual inflammation that further compromises the quality of life [9, 28]. Most triiodothyronine (80–90%) in peripheral blood is converted from thyroxine by deiodinase, which can be inhibited in a clinical stress state as adaptions to spare energy [29], and thyroid hormones may promote post-stroke recovery by regulating several pathways of neuro repair, such as neuronal plasticity, neurogenesis, and angiogenesis, which has been confirmed in some cell and animal studies [30, 31]. Besides, acute hyperglycemia could accelerate neuronal damage in hypoxic brain tissue through stimulating coagulation, and increases oxidative response and inflammation levels [32–34], thereby leading to decreased reperfusion and possibly neuron damage. In our subgroup analysis, despite reports that infarctions not involving the posterior cerebral artery [35] and very elderly individuals [36] typically have worse stroke prognosis, no interaction effects were found between stress status and either arterial involvement or age segment. However, the relationships between admission stress and long-term ischemic stroke prognosis were more pronounced in patients with non-large-artery atherosclerosis and severe stroke (baseline NIHSS > 3). This suggests that the mechanisms may be more specific to stroke etiology and severity, warranting further precision studies.

Considering the clinical implications, further research on stress control, including glucose-lowering interventions and other therapies in acute ischemic stroke, is highly warranted. In the animal model of acute middle cerebral artery occlusion-induced ischemic stroke in rats, the administration of insulin has been shown to reduce infarct size [37, 38], which theoretically suggests it may improve outcomes in ischemic stroke. However, in one of the largest studies to date, the Glucose in Stroke Trial (GIST), which involved 933 patients with hyperglycemia at admission, the use of a glucose-potassium-insulin infusion to maintain capillary glucose levels between 4 and 7 mmol/L did not show a significant reduction in 90-day mortality following insulin treatment [39]. Additionally, another trial observed that glucose-potassium-insulin infusion was associated with greater infarct growth in patients and a high incidence of hypoglycemia [40]. Overall, the efficacy of insulin therapy in acute ischemic stroke requires confirmation through larger trials, with careful consideration of hypoglycemia risk [41].Similarly, the current findings regarding thyroid hormones and stroke are still inadequate for clinical application. Hypothyroid animals exposed to cerebral ischemia exhibited reduced apoptosis, necrosis, oxidative stress, neurological impairments, and infarct size, and T3 administration has shown therapeutic effects in animal studies [42, 43].However, clinical studies examining the use of thyroid hormone therapy in the management of acute stroke are scarce. In summary, there is currently no consensus in guidelines regarding blood glucose control methods and thyroid hormone management during the acute phase of stroke [44]. Future large-scale research is needed to explore the relationships between multi-modal glycemic control strategies (such as long-acting insulin and oral hypoglycemic agents), thyroid hormone supplementation, and long-term stroke outcomes.

The current study had several limitations. First, considering the dynamic stress changes, assessment over multiple time points can better reflect the alterations status over time than only one-time point which was evaluated here, thus we have applied extra sensitivity analysis considering patients’ admission within 1 day2, 3 day3, and 7 days, and found that the potential time window relates to long-term stroke prognosis of stress degrees maybe after 3 days. Second, this is a single-center study despite the study sample capacity was not small, a larger sample size research or external validation is promising to validate our results in the future. Third, we didn’t consider patients’ baseline thyroid before stroke which is hard to obtain as such acute events can not be plannable, but we have compared the group level of stroke patients and healthy controls that could indicate the acute alterations of the HPT axis in some degrees and SHR is a commonly used which take background blood glucose into consideration [9], shooter research can prove the relationship but not the causality between the thyroid parameters and stroke prognosis, more studies on the differences between pre-stroke and stress thyroid hormone abnormity can be done to further validate the link between thyroid hormones and stroke.

In summary, high degrees of stress on acute stroke attacks, evidenced by strong endocrine and metabolic changes, may aggravate long-term ischemic stroke prognosis, and timely stress-reduced interventions may help promote post-stroke qualified living that is equally important as survival.
