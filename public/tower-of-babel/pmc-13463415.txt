Identification of macrophage polarization-related genes for esophageal cancer risk: a multi-omics Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Mumu Shi, Renshi Zhou, Bo Yu, Futian Yu, Jinghai Gao
Published: 2026

Background

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a malignancy of the esophageal epithelium, characterized by rapid progression, high invasiveness, and poor prognosis [1]. It is the seventh most common cancer globally and ranks as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with an estimated 5-year survival rate of only 20% due to late diagnosis and limited treatment options [2]. Despite advancements in chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, effective treatment for EC remains challenging due to issues like drug resistance, toxicity, and the heterogeneity of tumor subtypes [3]. Established risk factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and gastroesophageal reflux disease, significantly contribute to EC progression [4]. Genetic factors also play a critical role in modulating individual susceptibility [5]. These genetic alterations, along with epigenetic modifications, influence tumor behavior and response to therapies, providing key insights that could improve targeted intervention strategies for EC [6].

Macrophage polarization (MP) describes the process by which macrophages differentiate into either a pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype, exhibiting anti-tumor properties, or an anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotype, which supports tumor progression by promoting immune evasion, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [7]. In the tumor microenvironment (TME) of EC, M2-polarized macrophages, also known as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), are frequently observed and facilitate tumor growth and invasion by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors that suppress cytotoxic T-cell responses [8]. The inherent plasticity of macrophages and their ability to shift phenotypes in response to various signaling cues make them promising targets for therapeutic intervention [9]. Recent studies have identified networks of MP-related genes associated with EC progression, which modulate macrophage activity and their phenotypic switching, contributing to immune suppression within the TME [10]. However, the specific genetic and epigenetic regulators that govern MP in EC remain largely unknown, highlighting a critical need for further investigation to uncover potential therapeutic targets.

To investigate the involvement of MP-related genes in EC, we utilized Summary Data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis [11]. SMR combines cis-quantitative trait loci (cis-QTL) data for DNA methylation (mQTL), gene expression (eQTL), and protein expression (pQTL) with genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to differentiate causative relationships from mere correlations [12]. Prior studies have shown that SMR provides a valuable framework for identifying and validating disease-associated genes [13], as well as an effective method for discovering biomarkers and therapeutic targets in various conditions [14]. These findings emphasize the value of SMR in elucidating molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets in complex diseases.

This study aims to examine the roles of MP-related genes in EC by integrating multi-omics data through SMR and colocalization analyses. By analyzing QTL data in association with EC risk, this research seeks to deepen understanding of the immune microenvironment in EC. The ultimate goal is to generate preliminary insights into potential therapeutic targets that may enhance anti-tumor immune responses in EC.

Methods

Study design

The mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL datasets related to MP-related genes were analyzed using SMR to assess their associations with EC risk based on GWAS data from publicly available sources. Colocalization analysis was performed to identify shared causal variants influencing both QTLs and EC risk. Through the integration of multi-omics QTL-GWAS analyses, key genetic variants implicated in EC development were identified. Additionally, eQTL data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database was utilized to investigate tissue-specific gene expression variations associated with EC risk [15]. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Study design. The flowchart illustrates the integration of macrophage polarization (MP)-related gene QTL datasets (mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL) with esophageal cancer (EC) GWAS data from the UK Biobank (UKB). The mQTL dataset contains SNPs associated with DNA methylation levels, the eQTL dataset comprises SNPs linked to gene expression levels, and the pQTL dataset includes SNPs correlated with protein expression. Summary data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis was applied to explore causal relationships between these QTL datasets and EC risk. Significant SMR findings were subjected to colocalization analysis to identify shared causal variants, followed by replication in independent EC cohorts from FinnGen

QTL data source

A total of 731 MP-related protein-coding genes were identified in the GeneCards database using the keyword “Macrophage Polarization” (https://www.genecards.org). Summary data for blood mQTL were derived from a meta-analysis of two European cohorts: the Brisbane Systems Genetics Study (n = 614) and the Lothian Birth Cohorts (n = 1366) [16]. Blood eQTL data were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium, comprising genetic data on blood gene expression from 31,684 European individuals [17]. Blood pQTL data were sourced from a previous study [18], including 10,708 European individuals. The association between MP-related gene expression and EC risk was validated using tissue-specific eQTL data from the gastroesophageal junction, esophageal mucosa, and esophageal muscularis tissues in the GTEx V8 database, which includes 17,382 RNA-seq samples across 54 tissue sites and 2 cell lines (https://commonfund.nih.gov/GTEx).

GWAS data source

The discovery dataset was obtained from the UK Biobank (UKB), including 759 EC cases and 391,483 controls. The validation dataset was sourced from the FinnGen R10 release, comprising 619 EC cases and 314,193 controls. No overlap existed between the participants in the discovery and validation datasets, ensuring independent validation of the results. The details are summarized in Table S1. No additional ethics statement is required, as both datasets are publicly available and were ethically approved for research use. In UK Biobank, EC was defined using ICD-10 code C15, including both esophageal adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. In FinnGen, the EC endpoint (C3_OESOPHAGUS_EXALLC) aggregates esophageal cancer cases across multiple ICD codes and includes both major histological subtypes.

SMR analysis

The study used SMR to assess links between MP-related gene methylation, gene expression, and protein levels with EC risk. Genetic variants were chosen from a ± 1000 kb range around each target gene, with a significance threshold set at p < 5.0 × 10⁻8. Variants with allele frequency differences over 0.2 between datasets (e.g., linkage disequilibrium reference, QTL summary, outcome data) were excluded to enhance consistency. The HEIDI test was used to distinguish whether the QTL and GWAS associations are driven by a shared causal variant or by distinct variants in linkage disequilibrium. Associations with p_HEIDI < 0.01 were considered inconsistent with a shared causal variant [19]. Analyses were conducted using SMR software (v1.3.1) [19]. To further enhance the validity of the findings, SMR_multi was used to incorporate multiple genetic variants simultaneously. All SMR p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and both raw and FDR-adjusted p-values are reported in Supplementary Tables. Only associations with p_SMR < 0.05, p_SMR_multi < 0.05, and P_HEIDI > 0.01 were included for colocalization analysis [16].

Colocalization analysis

A Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed to determine if the same genetic variants underlie associations in both traits. Significant associations (p_SMR < 0.05, p_SMR_multi < 0.05, and p_HEIDI > 0.01) were further evaluated with the coloc R package to identify common genetic variants between cis-QTLs and the disease [20]. The analysis focused on SNPs within ± 1000 kb of the top cis-QTL [21]. Five hypotheses were considered: (H0) no association with either trait, (H1) association solely with the first trait, (H2) association solely with the second trait, (H3) association with both traits via separate causal variants, and (H4) association with both traits due to a common causal variant. Colocalization was confirmed when the prior probability for shared causality (P12) was set at 5 × 10⁻5, and the posterior probability for a shared variant (PPH4) was greater than 0.5 [21].

Integration of multi-omics evidence

In addition to investigating the causal relationships between QTLs and outcomes, we examined how mQTLs influence eQTLs by using mQTL summary data as exposures and eQTL summary data as outcomes to identify methylation events that may affect gene expression. We further analyzed whether eQTLs impact pQTLs. The results may uncover how methylation regulates gene expression and protein levels. To identify key genes associated with EC risk, we integrated results from three tiers of gene regulation. Given that only one protein was significantly associated with EC risk, causal candidate genes were categorized into three tiers based on the following criteria: (1) tier 1 genes were defined as those with associations identified through mQTL-eQTL integration analysis and showing EC risk associations at both the methylation and expression levels; (2) tier 2 genes included those associated with EC risk at both methylation and expression levels, with a colocalization PPH4 > 0.5; (3) tier 3 genes were defined as those associated with EC risk at the methylation, expression, or protein level, with a colocalization PPH4 > 0.5 or validated in an external EC dataset.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R (v4.3.0). Manhattan plots and forest plots were generated using the “ggplot2” and “forestplot” packages, respectively. The locus plots and effect plots were generated as previously described [19].

Results

All SMR p-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, and both raw and FDR-adjusted p-values are reported in the Supplementary Tables. As none of the QTL–EC associations survived FDR < 0.05, the results described below represent nominally associations that require further validation.

MP-related gene methylation and EC risk

Integration of mQTLs and GWAS data suggested 85 methylation loci associated with EC across 49 MP-related genes. Among these, 54 loci corresponding to 29 genes exhibited significant colocalization evidence (PPH4 > 0.5; Fig. 2). For example, INPP5D (cg05299077) methylation was associated with an increased risk of EC (OR = 1.434, 95% CI 1.176–1.748, PPH4 = 0.973). In contrast, PGF (cg15890469) methylation was linked to a decreased risk of EC (OR = 0.822, 95% CI 0.716–0.944, PPH4 = 0.605. Full mQTL analysis results from the discovery EC cohort are provided in Table S2. In the FinnGen EC dataset, SMR analysis identified positive associations of methylation at USP18 (cg18699242) (OR = 2.641, 95% CI 1.023–6.821) and USP18 (cg27281093) (OR = 2.826, 95% CI 1.016–7.864) with EC (Table S3), contrasting with the discovery dataset, in which USP18 (cg18699242) (OR = 0.515, 95% CI 0.293–0.904) and USP18 (cg27281093) (OR = 0.491, 95% CI 0.267–0.904) showed negative associations with EC risk (Table S2).

MP-related gene expression and EC risk

Integration of eQTL and GWAS data suggested 8 MP-related genes with nominal associations with EC. Among these, 5 genes (INPP5D, KARS1, MMP14, PGF, RGCC) showed positive associations, while 3 genes (H2AC6, LEP, UBE2D1) exhibited negative associations. All genes demonstrated significant colocalization evidence (PPH4 > 0.5), except for LEP (Fig. 3). Among these, INPP5D and PGF were notable, as they appeared in both mQTL and eQTL analyses, further supporting their relevance to EC risk. INPP5D showed a positive association with EC (OR = 2.349, 95% CI 1.288–4.284, PPH4 = 0.755), while PGF also exhibited a positive association (OR = 2.473, 95% CI 1.195–5.117, PPH4 = 0.544). No significant associations were observed in the integration of eQTL and pQTL SMR analysis, possibly due to the limited pQTL coverage.

After the integration of evidence from multi-omics, LEP was classified as tier 1 based on consistent evidence. In separate mQTL and eQTL analyses, LEP (cg13454199) methylation demonstrated a positive association with EC risk (OR = 1.475, 95% CI 1.05–2.072, p_SMR = 0.025, p_SMR_multi = 0.025, p_HEIDI = 0.793; Table S2). The chromosomal location of this association is shown in Fig. S1A, while the colocalization and effect size for methylation are detailed in Figs. S2A and S3A, respectively. LEP expression exhibited a negative association with EC risk (OR = 0.204, 95% CI 0.049–0.841; p_SMR = 0.028, p_SMR_multi = 0.031, p_HEIDI = 0.502; Table S3). The chromosomal location is indicated in Fig. S1B, with colocalization and effect size shown in Figs. S2B and S3B, respectively. Based on these results, we hypothesize that increased LEP (cg13454199) methylation may downregulate LEP expression, potentially elevating EC risk. However, in the tissue-specific eQTL validation, no significant associations were observed (Tables S9–11), suggesting tissue-specific regulation in EC risk.

Additionally, INPP5D and PGF were classified as tier 2 genes due to their associations with EC risk at both methylation and expression levels, supported by colocalization evidence with PPH4 > 0.5. SPP1 and USP18 met the criteria for tier 3 genes, showing associations with EC risk at the protein and methylation levels, respectively, with colocalization evidence (PPH4 > 0.5) or validation in the external EC dataset.

Discussion

This study used multi-omics integration to highlight MP-related genes with suggestive associations EC risk, including LEP, INPP5D, PGF, USP18, and SPP1. LEP emerged with the highest evidence level (tier 1). Increased methylation may suppress LEP expression, potentially heightening EC risk. INPP5D and PGF were classified as tier 2 genes due to their significant associations with EC risk at both methylation and expression levels, supported by colocalization evidence. USP18 and SPP1 met tier 3 criteria, showing EC risk associations at the methylation and protein levels, respectively, with colocalization evidence or validation in the external dataset. These findings suggest that the regulation of EC risk by MP-related genes involves complex interactions at multiple molecular levels.

LEP encodes leptin, a hormone involved in energy homeostasis and immune modulation, primarily produced by adipocytes [22]. Our integration of mQTL and eQTL summary data suggests that reduced LEP expression, potentially driven by higher methylation at cg13454199, may increase susceptibility to EC. Evidence from mouse models indicates that leptin promotes M1 polarization, enhancing antitumor immunity [23]. Additionally, in periodontitis models, leptin has been shown to drive M1 polarization through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [24]. Thus, reduced LEP expression could impair immune surveillance, potentially facilitating initial EC development by limiting M1 macrophage activation, which is critical for antitumor immunity. However, leptin can also promote M2 polarization under certain conditions, particularly in obesity-related cancers, via JAK2/STAT3 and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [25]. This M2 polarization creates a tumor-supportive environment through inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, thereby increasing EC risk and enhancing tumor growth and invasion [26]. Additionally, elevated leptin levels in established EC are associated with increased invasiveness, likely through MMP-2 and MMP-9 upregulation, which promotes extracellular matrix degradation and tumor cell invasion [27]. Given leptin’s dual role in MP, reduced LEP expression may decrease M1 macrophage activation, limiting antitumor immunity and increasing EC susceptibility, whereas high plasma leptin levels, particularly associated with obesity, a known risk factor for EC [28], may promote M2 polarization, increasing EC risk and enhancing tumor growth and invasion. Our finding aligns with evidence from acute myeloid leukemia, showing a negative correlation between LEP methylation and expression [29]. However, leptin pQTL data are currently unavailable, limiting our ability to directly assess the impact of protein-level variation on this association.

INPP5D and PGF were identified as tier 2 genes, showing consistent associations with EC risk at both methylation and expression levels, supported by significant colocalization evidence. INPP5D encodes the SHIP1 protein, a phosphatase that promotes M2 macrophage polarization by reducing pro-inflammatory PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby creating an immunosuppressive environment supportive of tumor growth [30]. While direct evidence linking INPP5D expression to EC risk in previous studies is limited, INPP5D may contribute to EC progression by promoting M2 polarization through PI3K/AKT signaling, which supports cellular growth, differentiation, and migration in EC pathogenesis [31]. PGF encodes placental growth factor, a member of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) family, which promotes M2 macrophage polarization by activating pathways like VEGFR1/Flt-1, leading to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that supports cancer progression [32]. Additionally, PGF enhances M2 polarization via exosomal miR-301a-3p, which targets PTEN, further activating the VEGFR1-mediated PI3K/AKT pathway and contributing to EC progression [33]. Together, these findings align with our results, suggesting that INPP5D and PGF may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment in EC, enhancing tumor progression and invasiveness through M2 macrophage polarization.

USP18 encodes ubiquitin-specific peptidase 18, a deubiquitinating enzyme that stabilizes target proteins by removing ubiquitin tags, thereby preventing their degradation and prolonging their cellular presence [34]. USP18 has been shown to promote an immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype by stabilizing colony stimulating factor 1 receptor, preventing MP towards a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor state [35]. This suggests that USP18 may contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment, potentially increasing susceptibility to EC. In the integration of mQTL and GWAS data, USP18 methylation loci (cg18699242 and cg27281093) showed contrasting associations with EC risk, with positive associations in the FinnGen dataset and negative associations in the UKB EC cohort, suggesting potential cohort-specific or situation-dependent effects of USP18 methylation on EC susceptibility. These findings align with recent large-scale mQTL research across multiple cohorts, demonstrating that mQTLs may exhibit population-specific effects due to variations in genetic backgrounds, environmental exposures, or lifestyle factors [36]. This underscores the importance of considering cohort-specific factors when interpreting DNA methylation data in disease studies. Despite these methylation associations, no direct correlation was observed between USP18 expression levels and EC risk, and pQTL data for USP18 is currently unavailable. This suggests that USP18 methylation influences EC susceptibility through indirect mechanisms rather than direct expression modulation. Indeed, DNA methylation can affect the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating immune cell differentiation, infiltration, and immune evasion mechanisms, thereby influencing tumor progression without necessarily altering gene expression levels [37]. In addition, USP18 protein has been implicated in metastasis in EC by stabilizing ZEB1 and driving epithelial-mesenchymal transition, suggesting that USP18 activity may play a role in EC susceptibility and progression independent of expression changes [38].

The opposite directions observed for USP18 methylation between the UKB and FinnGen EC cohorts may reflect several methodological and biological factors. First, the two cohorts differ in phenotype definitions: UKB EC includes both EAC and ESCC, whereas the FinnGen endpoint aggregates multiple esophageal cancer subtypes and related lesions, potentially introducing subtype heterogeneity. Second, the lead mQTL SNPs for USP18 differ between cohorts, and population-specific LD patterns in UK and Finnish ancestries may result in different tagging of causal variants. Third, methylation QTLs are known to exhibit stronger population-specific effects than eQTLs, influenced by environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, and ancestry differences. Finally, the absence of a corresponding eQTL or pQTL association suggests that USP18 methylation may act through broader immune or epigenetic mechanisms rather than direct transcriptional regulation, which may further contribute to cohort-specific variability.

Our results showed that SPP1 was the only protein with a significant positive association with EC risk, supported by strong colocalization evidence. SPP1 (osteopontin) is a glycoprotein involved in inflammation, tissue remodeling, and cancer progression, promoting cell adhesion, migration, and immune modulation in the TME [39]. In EC, SPP1 promotes an M2-like, immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype, which is often associated with resistance to therapies and poorer outcomes. This immunosuppressive effect is facilitated through metabolic pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, enabling SPP1+ macrophages to support tumor progression and immune evasion [40]. Recent studies further demonstrate that SPP1 drives crosstalk between EC cells and TAMs, recruiting macrophages and promoting M2 polarization via CD44/PI3K/AKT signaling, which enhances VEGFA and IL-6 secretion to sustain EC progression [41]. This interaction between SPP1 and macrophages could be a key mechanism linking SPP1 abundance with increased EC risk, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in modulating the TME to improve treatment response.

Although our analyses primarily relied on blood-derived mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL datasets, these resources remain highly relevant for studying macrophage polarization, as immune-cell regulatory variation is predominantly captured in peripheral blood. Many MP-related genes are expressed mainly in circulating monocytes and macrophages rather than epithelial tissues, making blood QTLs reasonable proxies for immune-mediated regulatory mechanisms that may influence cancer susceptibility. However, whole-blood QTLs are subject to cell-composition effects, and aggregation across heterogeneous immune-cell subsets may dilute cell-type–specific regulatory signals. This limitation may partially explain why several associations did not replicate in GTEx esophageal tissues, where immune-cell proportions are substantially lower and sample sizes are more limited.

Ideally, immune-cell–specific QTL datasets (e.g., BLUEPRINT, DICE, or single-cell eQTL resources) would provide more refined resolution of macrophage-related regulatory effects. However, such datasets currently have limited sample sizes or incomplete coverage of MP-related genes, restricting their utility for Mendelian randomization. We have therefore acknowledged that blood-based QTLs may introduce bias and that tissue-specific regulatory effects cannot be fully captured. Future studies incorporating purified immune-cell QTLs or single-cell multi-omics will be essential to validate and refine the immune-mediated mechanisms suggested by our findings.

DNA methylation is highly site-specific, and CpG sites within the same gene often exhibit distinct regulatory functions depending on their genomic location. Promoter methylation typically represses transcription, whereas gene-body methylation is frequently associated with increased gene expression. Therefore, CpG sites located in different regulatory regions of the same gene may show opposite associations with EC risk. In addition, mQTLs reflect genetically driven methylation variation, and different CpG sites may be influenced by distinct causal variants or LD structures, leading to heterogeneous effect directions. Some CpG sites may also affect disease risk through mechanisms independent of gene expression, such as chromatin accessibility or immune-cell differentiation. These factors collectively explain why opposite effect directions can occur among CpG sites mapped to the same gene.

This study highlights notable findings on the association between methylation, expression, and protein levels of MP-related genes, such as LEP, INPP5D, PGF, USP18, and SPP1, with EC risk; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the contrasting associations observed in different cohorts, particularly in the methylation analysis of USP18, suggest potential population-specific effects that were not fully resolved, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the lack of pQTL data for certain genes, like LEP, INPP5D, PGF, and USP18, restricts our ability to draw conclusions about the role of protein-level variation in EC susceptibility. The inability to replicate some associations in the external FinnGen dataset, particularly for SPP1, raises questions about the robustness of the results across diverse populations and environmental conditions. Moreover, while we propose mechanisms linking these genes to MP and immune modulation, direct functional validation of these mechanisms in EC was not conducted, which limits the mechanistic understanding of how these molecular changes contribute to tumor progression. Future studies should incorporate broader cohort validation, pQTL data, and experimental analyses to clarify the impact of MP-related gene regulation on EC pathogenesis and therapeutic response.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study integrates QTLs and GWAS data to highlight MP-related epigenetic and genetic factors with suggestive associations with EC risk, including LEP, INPP5D, PGF, USP18, and SPP1 as potential contributors to EC development, potentially through pathways involving immune modulation and MP. While these findings offer valuable insights, further validation across diverse cohorts and functional studies is essential to fully elucidate these molecular mechanisms and their potential therapeutic implications for EC prevention and treatment.
