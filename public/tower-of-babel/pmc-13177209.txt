H2O2‑Driven Structural and Electronic Modulation of NiMn Electrocatalysts for Enhanced Electrooxidation of HMF to DFF
Authors: Nadia Mumtazah, Nurfadlih Syahlani, Muhammad Ibadurrohman, Mohammad Nasikin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
2,5-Diformylfuran (DFF) is an important oxidation product of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) with significant industrial relevance; however, achieving high selectivity using non-noble metal electrocatalysts remains challenging. Herein, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was introduced during the electrodeposition of NiMn-C to induce in situ oxidative reconstruction. This treatment induces morphological roughening, generates oxygen vacancies, and alters the Mn oxidation state, collectively modifying the structural and electronic properties of the catalyst. The resulting materials were characterized by FTIR, XRD, FE-SEM, and XPS and subsequently evaluated for HMF electrooxidation. The H2O2-treated NiMn-C catalyst exhibits enhanced activity and maintains high DFF selectivity (90.7%) at 1.65 V vs RHE, producing nearly twice the DFF yield compared to the untreated NiMn-F sample. These findings demonstrate that oxidative-agent-driven structural and electronic modulation provides an effective strategy for improving the performance of non-noble metal electrocatalysts for biomass oxidation.

Introduction

2,5-Diformylfuran (DFF) is an important platform molecule with diverse applications. DFF can be converted into a variety of value-added chemicals, including intermediates for biofuels and aromatic ketones, nitrogen-containing compounds and polymer precursors, bulk chemicals such as maleic acid, and hydrogenated derivatives for resins, polymers, and artificial fibers. Among various routes, the oxidation of 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) is considered one of the most effective routes for producing DFF. HMF itself is known for its toxicity, potential health risks to humans, and relatively lower economic value than DFF. However, DFF is not the sole oxidation product of HMF; other products such as 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (HMFCA), 5-formyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (FFCA), and 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) are also typically formed. Among them, DFF is the most valuable due to its broad industrial applicability. The overall oxidation pathways of HMF are illustrated in Scheme .

Selective oxidation of HMF to DFF has been widely studied using homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts. Early studies with homogeneous systems employing dissolved ions of vanadium, copper, and iron achieved high selectivity and yield, but the separation of products from catalysts remained challenging. Moreover, such systems required harsh conditions, including elevated temperatures (80–130 °C) and additional oxidants such as DMF, toluene, TEMPO, or oxygen flow. Subsequently, heterogeneous catalysts like Mn3O4, Fe3O4, CoO, Cu nanoparticles, g-C3N4/Pd, and bimetallic NiMn were explored to simplify catalyst recovery. Despite their advantages, these catalysts still required high temperatures (100–300 °C) and pressures (9–100 bar) to achieve good conversion and selectivity. These harsh conditions increase production costs and often induce humin formation, an undesirable byproduct.

Electrochemical oxidation has emerged as a promising alternative because it operates under mild temperatures and ambient pressure, without the need for auxiliary oxidants. NiMn hydroxide catalysts, in particular, exhibit superior DFF selectivity compared with other transition metals. However, maintaining high DFF yield remains difficult because DFF, as an intermediate, tends to overoxidized into FFCA and FDCA at higher potentials. Therefore, a low applied potential is essential to ensure selectivity. Nevertheless, operating at low potentials limit catalytic activity. Therefore, strategies that simultaneously increase active-site availability, such as enhancing surface area, tuning local coordination environments, and modulating electronic structure through morphological, structural, and defect engineering, are crucial for boosting DFF productivity while preserving high selectivity at low operating potentials.

Several strategies have been developed to improve electrocatalytic activity by increasing active-site accessibility and tuning intrinsic catalytic properties. While morphology control is often employed to enlarge surface area, commonly used approaches such as hydrothermal synthesis require high energy input due to elevated temperature and pressure to regulate precursor reactions. Similarly, chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and pyrolysis also demand high energy input and involve safety risks, making them unsuitable for large-scale applications. Reductive agents such as sodium borohydride and hydrazine generate hazardous residues, whereas template-assisted synthesis requires multiple steps that limit practicality. Recent studies indicate that incorporating hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into the precursor during electrodeposition provides a versatile strategy to simultaneously regulate morphology and electronic structure. However, the detailed mechanism of this H2O2-induced morphological evolution has not yet been clarified. The presence of H2O2 increases the oxidation state of metal ions in the electrolyte, which influences nucleation and growth kinetics during bimetallic hydroxide deposition. The evolution of high-valent species promotes lattice oxygen rearrangement and the transient release of gas-phase species, with locally generated bubbles acting as dynamic masking templates to produce a rough and porous architecture. Beyond morphological restructuring, this oxidative environment also induces oxygen-vacancy formation and shifts in the Mn oxidation state, thereby modifying the electronic structure and further enhancing catalytic performance.

In this work, we report a simple, environmentally benign, and effective strategy to modulate the properties of NiMn electrocatalysts through H2O2-assisted electrodeposition. This approach generates NiMn electrocatalysts with a significantly roughened and coarsened surface morphology (NiMn-C) compared to the unmodified fine-surface counterpart (NiMn-F). In addition to morphological evolution, the oxidative environment introduced by H2O2 also induces electronic and structural modifications, including changes in Mn oxidation state and the formation of oxygen vacancies, which collectively enhance the density and accessibility of active sites. These improvements are expected to increase the electroactive surface area and boost catalytic performance for the selective electrooxidation of HMF to DFF under mild conditions. Moreover, HMF oxidation can serve as a practical alternative to the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), requiring lower energy input and offering faster kinetics. When paired with the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the cathode, this process enables the concurrent production of value-added DFF and hydrogen gas, providing a sustainable dual-output route for chemicals and clean energy.

Results and Discussion

Material Characterization

As described in the Experimental Section, MnSO4 was used as the Mn precursor during electrodeposition, which accounts for the presence of sulfate-related vibrational features observed in the FTIR spectra. The FTIR results for both samples in Figure a show a strong O–H stretching band at ∼3500 cm–1, attributed to hydroxide groups. The ∼1600 cm–1 band corresponds to O–H bending, while the peak at 1080 cm–1 is assigned to SO stretching from intercalated SO4 2– anions, , indicating the characteristic features of layered double hydroxide structures in both samples. XRD analysis was performed to identify the structural characteristics of the electrocatalysts using Co Kα radiation (λ = 1.79 Å). Since this wavelength is longer than that of Cu Kα (λ = 1.54 Å), the reference peaks appear shifted on the 2θ scale. Cu Kα radiation is predominantly used in reference databases and literature, potentially leading to discrepancies when comparing peak positions. In Figure b, the nickel plate substrate exhibits no distinct crystallite features within the examined 2θ range. After NiMn-F and NiMn-C deposition, new peaks emerged at 16.76°, 19.74°, and ∼30.00°, corresponding to d-spacings of 6.14 Å, 5.21 Å, and ∼3.46 Å, respectively. The peak at 16.76° is assigned to the (011) plane of a basic Mn­(II) salt, observed as [Mn­(OH)2]7[MnSO4]2·H2O (JCPDS No. 00–018–0788). Additionally, the reflection at 19.74° corresponds to the (001) plane of brucite-type Mn­(OH)2, and the peak near 30° is attributed to γ-NiOOH (006) (JCPDS No. 06–0075). The stronger diffraction intensity observed for NiMn-F compared to NiMn-C is likely due to the higher amorphous content in NiMn-C. Previous studies have shown that amorphous structures often exhibit superior catalytic performance compared to their crystalline counterparts. In order to further exclude the formation of undesired byproducts during the H2O2 treatment, a full-range XRD pattern (10–90°) was recorded using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) and is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). No additional diffraction peaks corresponding to impurity phases were observed, confirming the phase purity of the electrocatalysts after H2O2 modulation.

Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images (Figure c–d) reveal clear morphological differences between the two samples. The NiMn-F electrocatalyst displays smoother particle surfaces, whereas NiMn-C shows a noticeably rougher texture. This roughened morphology is likely influenced by gas evolution occurring during electrodeposition, which is consistent with the visual observations presented in Figure S2. A more detailed discussion of this phenomenon is provided in the proposed mechanism of morphological transformation. The surface features of NiMn-C also resemble layered structures previously reported for related materials. Moreover, the NiMn-C sample displays a denser particle arrangement than NiMn-F, despite both being prepared under similar total charge (∼0.9 C). The more pronounced Ni signal originating from the substrate in the EDS mapping of NiMn-F (Figure S3) indicates a thinner and less compact catalyst layer. In addition, sulfur was detected in both samples (3–5 wt %), originating from intercalated SO4 2– anions introduced from the MnSO4 precursor. In LDH-type structures, sulfate species are typically located in the interlayer galleries as charge-balancing anions rather than directly coordinated to surface Ni sites. Therefore, they are unlikely to significantly block the electrochemically accessible active sites. The possible influence of sulfate species on catalytic performance is further discussed in the electrochemical section.

Post-reaction FE-SEM images obtained after the electrooxidation of 1 mM HMF at 1.6 V vs RHE for 6 h (Figure S4) indicate that the overall surface morphology of both catalysts remains largely unchanged. The relatively smooth surface observed for NiMn-F and the rougher texture characteristic of NiMn-C are still clearly preserved after the reaction. This observation suggests that both electrocatalysts exhibit good morphological stability under the applied electrochemical conditions. Furthermore, elemental mapping obtained from EDS analysis after the reaction (Figure S5) confirms the presence of Ni, Mn, and O distributed across the catalyst surface. In addition, carbon (C) is detected, which is attributed to residual organic species originating from the HMF electrolyte or its oxidation products adsorbed on the catalyst surface during the electrochemical process.

More specific information on the oxidation states of the materials was examined using the XPS spectra shown in Figure , with quantitative values summarized in Table S1. In both samples, the C 1s spectra display peaks corresponding to CO, C–O, and C–C bonds, originating from surface impurities. In the O 1s spectra, the NiMn-C sample shows a higher relative intensity for the component commonly associated with oxygen-deficient sites, accompanied by a decrease in the O–H contribution. This component increases from 45.22% in NiMn-F to 49.97% in NiMn-C, indicating a higher abundance of oxygen-deficient environments in the H2O2-treated sample. The Ni 2p spectra of both samples contain Ni2+ and Ni3+ species with comparable ratios. In contrast, the Mn 2p spectra exhibit a clear increase in the Mn4+ contribution, while Mn2+ and Mn3+ decrease in NiMn-C compared with NiMn-F, consistent with the oxidizing influence of H2O2 during deposition. A negative shift in the binding energies of Mn species is also observed, with more pronounced shifts for higher oxidation states (Mn4+: 0.44 eV; Mn3+: 0.32 eV; Mn2+: 0.22 eV). These shifts can reasonably be attributed to modifications in the local oxygen environment, including the presence of oxygen-deficient sites that alter the electronic surroundings of Mn. Although other factors such as changes in coordination or electronic redistribution between Ni and Mn may also contribute, the collective XPS features strongly support the formation of oxygen-deficient environments in NiMn-C.

XPS spectra of NiMn-F and NiMn-C for C, O, Ni, and Mn elements.

Furthermore, the XPS spectra of NiMn-F and NiMn-C after HMF oxidation are shown in Figure S6. Following the reaction, the C 1s spectrum exhibits increased intensities for the C = O and C–C bonds, along with the appearance of a new M–C (metal–carbon) bond in NiMn-C. Interestingly, the M–C signal appears only in NiMn-C after the electrooxidation reaction, whereas it is absent in NiMn-F. We attribute this difference to the distinct surface properties of the two electrodes. The rougher morphology and higher density of exposed surface sites in NiMn-C provide more locations where carbonaceous species generated during HMF oxidation can adsorb or accumulate, leading to the detectable M–C feature in the postreaction XPS. In contrast, the smoother NiMn-F surface, with fewer such sites, does not exhibit a measurable M–C signal. In the O 1s spectra, a new peak emerges that corresponds to oxygen-containing organic species, while the M–O–M (lattice oxygen) component becomes more pronounced. In addition, the Ni and Mn signals decrease in intensity relative to the prereaction state, which we attribute to the presence of a thin layer of strongly adsorbed organic molecules formed during the reaction. These surface-bound residues attenuate the metal signals in XPS but do not reflect the bulk product distribution, consistent with the high DFF selectivity observed in solution.

Electrochemical Characterization

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted for NiMn-F, NiMn-C, and the bare Ni plate substrate in 0.1 M Na2B4O7 (Figure a). The Ni plate exhibited minimal electrocatalytic activity, as indicated by its low current density. Upon the formation of NiMn-F onto the Ni substrate, a noticeable increase in current density was observed. Furthermore, the NiMn-C electrode demonstrated the highest current density among all samples. Notably, despite containing a slightly higher sulfur content than NiMn-F, NiMn-C demonstrated superior catalytic performance, suggesting that the presence of intercalated sulfate species does not significantly impede the accessibility of active sites under alkaline conditions. NiMn-C was further evaluated under conditions with and without HMF to compare its electrochemical behavior toward the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and the HMF oxidation reaction (HMFOR). As shown in Figure b, the HMFOR exhibits an earlier onset potential of approximately 1.4 V vs RHE, while the OER begins at around 1.5 V vs RHE. This result indicates that HMF oxidation is thermodynamically more favorable than water oxidation as a competing reaction. In lower potential, the higher HMFOR onset potential observed for NiMn-F may originate from kinetic limitations related to its compact morphology, which can hinder the accessibility of bulky HMF molecules to active sites. In contrast, the more open structure of NiMn-C likely improves surface accessibility and facilitates HMFOR.

From the linear fitting results, NiMn-F displayed a smaller slope than NiMn-C, suggesting that the NiMn-C catalyst possesses a larger ECSA.

DFF Production Analysis

The improved electrocatalytic performance arises not only from the enlarged accessible surface, which increases the density of active sites, but also from the modified surface and electronic properties that collectively facilitate more efficient reaction kinetics. Consistent with these structural advantages, NiMn-C delivers higher DFF formation than NiMn-F across all applied potentials, as shown in Figure a. The DFF yield increases with the applied potential, as observed at 1.55 and 1.65 V vs RHE. However, a different trend is observed at 1.75 V vs RHE, where no further enhancement in DFF yield occurs despite higher HMF oxidation. This behavior is likely attributed to overoxidation at elevated potentials, leading to the formation of other oxidation products besides DFF, thus balancing its production and consumption rates. This observation is consistent with previous reports indicating that optimal DFF formation occurs at relatively low potentials, which inherently limits the attainable yield. Nevertheless, improvements in catalyst structure and surface properties support enhanced DFF production even within this low-potential regime. In addition, a control experiment was performed under identical reaction conditions (same electrolyte composition, stirring rate, temperature, and setup) but without applying any external potential. As shown in Figure S9, no change in HMF concentration was observed over the experimental duration, and no product formation was detected. These results confirm that under the present experimental conditions, HMF remains sufficiently stable and does not undergo spontaneous degradation in the absence of electrochemical bias.

(a) Product distribution after 4 h of HMF electrooxidation at various applied potentials; (b) DFF selectivity; (c) HMF conversion; and (d) DFF yield at 1.65 V vs RHE using 1 mM HMF in 0.1 M Na2B4O7 for 6 h with NiMn-F and NiMn-C.

In this study, 1.65 V vs RHE was identified as the optimal potential for the NiMn electrocatalyst in producing DFF, providing the most appropriate balance between HMF conversion and DFF selectivity. At higher potentials, although HMF conversion increases, DFF formation does not proportionally follow, resulting in a pronounced decline in selectivity, decreasing from 93.3% at 1.65 V to 54.9% at 1.75 V vs RHE for NiMn-F. A similar trend is observed for NiMn-C, where the selectivity decreases from 89.1% to 59.3% under the same conditions. Figure (b-d) presents the product analysis of NiMn-F and NiMn-C at 1.65 V vs RHE, with the reaction monitored over different time intervals. As the reaction proceeds, the production of DFF increases proportionally with the consumption of HMF, resulting in a nearly constant DFF selectivity. NiMn-C exhibits superior catalytic performance compared to NiMn-F. However, its structural modification does not markedly affect the selectivity toward DFF, which remains consistently high at approximately 90.75% after 6 h of reaction. A comparative benchmarking against representative catalysts reported in the literature is summarized in Table S3, further positioning NiMn-C among the higher-performing non-noble systems for HMF oxidation. As summarized in Table S3, although the applied potential is comparable to previously reported systems, the present catalyst demonstrates improved DFF selectivity and yield under similar or milder conditions. This highlights the effectiveness of the structural modulation strategy in steering product distribution.

Furthermore, in order to evaluate the selectivity of HMF oxidation toward DFF, the Faradaic efficiency (FE) was examined at different applied potentials and initial HMF concentrations (Table ). The FE results reveal a strong dependence on both parameters. At 1.55 V (vs RHE) with 1 mM HMF, NiMn-C shows a higher FE (11.71%) than NiMn-F (6.18%). However, increasing the potential to 1.65 and 1.75 V at the same HMF concentration leads to a significant decrease in FE for both catalysts. This trend indicates that higher anodic potentials do not favor selective DFF formation. Notably, NiMn-C consistently exhibits higher FE values than NiMn-F under all examined conditions, indicating improved charge utilization toward DFF formation after H2O2-assisted modification.

The decline in FE at elevated potentials can be attributed to the increasing contribution of the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which becomes kinetically competitive under stronger anodic polarization. As a result, a larger fraction of the current is diverted toward OER rather than HMF oxidation. Moreover, higher potentials may promote further oxidation of DFF to more oxidized products, reducing the apparent FE toward DFF. The effect of substrate concentration is evident at 1.65 V, where increasing the HMF concentration from 1 mM to 5 mM and 10 mM enhances the FE for NiMn-C from 3.30% to 11.03% and 16.66%, respectively. Higher substrate availability improves surface coverage and increases the fraction of charge utilized for HMF oxidation, thereby suppressing competing reactions. Nevertheless, higher HMF concentrations require longer oxidation times to reach the same level of conversion. Mechanism of H2O2-Induced Transformation

The proposed mechanism of the transformation induced by hydrogen peroxide is illustrated in Figure . The incorporation of H2O2 into the precursor solution for NiMn electrodeposition appears to modify both the oxidation state distribution and the local coordination environment of the metal ions. XPS analysis suggests a change in the oxidation state distribution, particularly for Mn, while FTIR reveals a decrease in O–H vibrational intensity, indicating reduced hydroxyl coordination and the formation of oxygen vacancies. These results collectively suggest that H2O2 induces electronic and structural modifications in the catalyst. During electrodeposition, H2O2 promotes the in situ generation of gas-phase species, most plausibly O2. The vigorous evolution of these bubbles disrupts the uniform growth of the solid film, generating voids and surface irregularities that yield a rougher and more porous morphology. This observation is consistent with the FE-SEM results, where the NiMn-C sample displays an open, sponge-like texture in contrast to the smoother surface of NiMn-F.

Proposed mechanism of NiMn electrocatalyst transformation using H2O2.

The resulting porous structure increases the accessible surface area and may also influence the local electronic environment, both of which enhance the interaction between catalytic sites and reactant molecules. Moreover, the improved porosity, surface roughness, and modified oxidation state distribution facilitate more efficient mass transport and charge transfer during HMF electrooxidation. It is proposed that the transient bubbles generated act as dynamic templating agents during electrodeposition, defining not only the surface architecture but also contributing to defect formation and electronic modulation within the catalyst layer, which collectively lead to improved NiMn electrocatalyst performance.

Conclusions

A systematic investigation was conducted to examine how the incorporation of H2O2 into the precursor influences the properties of NiMn electrocatalysts during electrodeposition. The addition of H2O2 induced clear structural changes, yielding a rougher and more open surface compared with the H2O2-free NiMn-F sample. This modification increased the accessible surface area and improved the distribution of catalytically relevant features, as supported by FE-SEM and XPS analysis. Electrochemical measurements showed that the NiMn-C electrocatalyst delivered nearly twice the DFF yield of NiMn-F while maintaining high selectivity of approximately 90% under mild conditions (1.65 V vs RHE, 25 °C, ambient pressure). These findings demonstrate that precursor engineering using H2O2 not only modulates morphology but also adjusts the local chemical environment in ways that collectively enhance catalytic performance. Overall, this strategy offers a sustainable and scalable route for developing efficient transition-metal electrocatalysts for biomass valorization and related electrochemical processes.

Experimental Section

Materials

Nickel­(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O, 99%), manganese­(II) sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4·H2O, 99%), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium tetraborate (Na2B4O7, pH 9.6, 98%) was purchased from Merck. Nickel plates were used as the substrate, and deionized water (aquadest) was used throughout the experiments. 5-Hydroxymethyl-2-furaldehyde (HMF, 99%) and 2,5-furandicarboxaldehyde (DFF, 97%) were also supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. Electrochemical measurements were carried out in an undivided three-electrode cell equipped with a platinum mesh counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) reference electrode, using a PalmSens potentiostat. The reaction products were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Nexera LC-40) equipped with a UV detector and a C18 column maintained at 55 °C. A 5 mM sulfuric acid aqueous solution was used as the eluent.

Synthesis of NiMn-F and NiMn-C Electrocatalyst

NiMn-F (NiMn-Fine) bimetallic hydroxides were synthesized via electrodeposition in a three-electrode configuration using a nickel plate as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) electrode as the reference electrode, and a platinum mesh as the counter electrode. Prior to deposition, the nickel substrate was cleaned ultrasonically by sequential immersion in ethanol, acetone, and 2 M HCl for 15 min each, followed by rinsing with deionized water and drying under ambient conditions. The exposed area of the nickel plate was restricted to 1 cm × 1 cm on each side during electrodeposition.

Electrodeposition was carried out at an applied potential of −1.0 V vs Ag/AgCl for 60 s in a 50 mL electrolyte solution containing 0.10 M Ni­(NO3)2·6H2O and 0.05 M MnSO4·H2O. The resulting sample was denoted as NiMn-F. To obtain the H2O2-modified sample, 300 μL of 30% H2O2 was added to the same electrolyte solution prior to deposition, and this sample was designated as NiMn-C (NiMn-Coarse). Characterization

The surface morphology was observed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM ThermoFisher Scientific Apreo 2S) operated in immersion mode at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV using a Secondary Electron (SE) detector. The chemical bonds were studied by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet IS5, id7 ATR transmission). The FTIR spectra were recorded using a DTGS KBr detector with a KBr beamsplitter. The crystalline structures of the samples were characterized using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker ECO D8 Advance) equipped with a Co Kα radiation source (λ1 = 1.78897 Å, λ2 = 1.79285 Å) operating at 40 kV and 25 mA. The diffraction patterns were collected over the 2θ range of 7°–30° with a step size of 0.019°. The surface chemical compositions and oxidation states were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos AXIS SUPRA PLUS) with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV) operated at 15 mA and 15 kV (225 W). The spectra were collected in hybrid lens mode with a pass energy of 20 eV and a step size of 0.1 eV. All binding energies were calibrated relative to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV.

Electrochemical Measurements

Electrochemical oxidation of HMF was conducted using a potentiostat (PalmSens4) in a three-electrode configuration and single-compartment (undivided) electrochemical cell. The NiMn, NiMn-F, and NiMn-C electrodes were employed directly as the working electrodes, while a platinum mesh (100 mesh) and an Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) electrode served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The measured potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using eq : 3 ERHE=EAg/AgCl+0.059×pH+0.21

The J–V curves of the NiMn-based electrodes were recorded using Cyclic voltammetry (CV) technique at a scan rate of 10 mV s–1 in the absence and presence of 20 mM HMF. Chronoamperometric measurements were carried out to electrochemically oxidize 1 mM HMF in 50 mL of aqueous 0.1 M Na2B4O7 electrolyte under ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure with continuous stirring. To compare the surface area of both catalysts, the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) was determined from double-layer capacitance (Cdl) measurements. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed within the non-Faradaic potential region (0.43–0.68 V vs RHE) at scan rates of 0.05–0.17 V s–1 in 0.1 M Na2B4O7. For each scan rate, the anodic and cathodic capacitive current densities at 0.56 V vs RHE were extracted and individually plotted as a function of the scan rate. The slopes of these linear fits correspond to the double-layer capacitance (Cdl), which is proportional to the ECSA.

Product Analysis

All liquid products were analyzed using a Nexera LC-40 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system equipped with a C18 column. The separation was carried out at 55 °C using phosphoric acid (0.02 mM) as the eluent. For analysis, 200 μL of the electrolyte was diluted with 1800 μL of deionized water and injected into the HPLC system. The total electrolysis time was 6 h. Aliquots were collected every 2 h (at 2, 4, and 6 h) for product analysis. During each sampling, the chronoamperometric measurement was temporarily paused to allow access to the reactor, after which the system was reassembled to continue the reaction. The products of electrochemical HMF oxidation were identified by comparing their retention times with those of standard compounds. Quantification of the chemical was performed using calibration curves obtained from standard solutions of known concentrations (Figure S11, Figure S12).
