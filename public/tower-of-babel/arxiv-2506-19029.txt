Dynamics of Phase-Separated Interfaces in Inhomogenous and Driven Mixtures

Jacopo Romano

,
Ramin Golestanian
a,b
and
Benoît Mahault

We derive effective equations of motion governing the dynamics of sharp interfaces in phase-separated binary mixtures driven by spatio-temporal modulations of their material properties. We demonstrate, in particular, that spatial heterogeneities in the surface tension induce an effective capillary force that drives the motion of interfaces, even in the absence of hydrodynamics. Applying our sharp interface model to quantify the dynamics of thermophoretic droplets, we find that their deformation and transport properties are controlled by a combination of bulk and capillary forces, whose relative strength depends on droplet size. Strikingly, we show that small thermophobic droplets –composed of a material with a positive Soret coefficient– can spontaneously migrate towards high-temperature regions as a result of capillary forces.

Introduction

Phase separation is a prototypical example of pattern formation in soft matter, and naturally emerges when the enthalpic cost of mixing multiple substances outweighs the entropic gain of maintaining a homogeneous blend.
From a theoretical perspective, this phenomenon is routinely described by phase-field models, where the formation of patterns is captured by a conserved field whose dynamics minimizes the free energy of the system
1
,
2
.

Sufficiently far from the critical point, binary phase-separated mixtures consist of nearly homogeneous domains separated by sharp interfaces. Their dynamics is then largely dominated by the motion of the interfaces, whose description involves a significantly reduced number of degrees of freedom. Deriving an effective equation governing the motion of interfaces then enables considerable simplifications in the characterization of the system’s dynamics, and has therefore attracted significant interest
3
,
4
,

while similar approaches have been developed to investigate pattern formation when the order parameter is not conserved
13

14
,
15
,
16
.
Notably, effective sharp interface models find a wide range of applications, including the prediction of coarsening scaling laws
17
and of the roughening
dynamics of fluid interfaces in porous media
18
,
5
,
19
, the description of grain boundaries
20
and crystal growth
21
, as well as the modeling of epithelium and biofilm expansion
22
,
23
.

In many situations, phase separation occurs in the presence of external fields or spatial inhomogeneities that significantly influence the behavior of interfaces.
Common examples include fluids subject to the influence of gravity
6
,
24
or to the presence of impurities
5

Moreover, spatio-temporal control of polymer blends or colloidal suspensions by the application of light

Another field where phase separation in inhomogeneous media is particularly relevant is biology.
Biomolecular condensates, formed through liquid-liquid phase separation, play a central role in regulating key biological processes inside the cell
39
.
In recent years, the characterization of the physics of these emulsions, and how they interact with the highly heterogeneous and dynamic cellular environment, has become the subject of an increasing amount of research
40
,
41
.

Determining how modulations of environmental properties affect the motion of interfaces is often a difficult task. The main reason is that these modulations generally induce multiple changes in the system’s free energy structure, and therefore influence the dynamics in non-trivial ways.
A straightforward example is temperature gradients, which impact all terms of the free energy,
while the same should apply to systems driven by pH
42
,
43
or light
27
,
28
modulations.
To achieve a more quantitative understanding of the influence of heterogeneities on the dynamics of phase separation, having a general theory of how the spatial and temporal dependencies of all the parameters in the free energy influence the motion of interfaces is then particularly valuable.

Here, we consider a binary mixture described by a phase-field model, and derive an effective equation of motion for sharp, phase-separated interfaces in the presence of generic spatio-temporal inhomogeneities. We show that the interface dynamics is controlled by a combination of effective bulk and capillary forces, whose relative magnitude depends on the typical scale of the problem.
In particular, our results indicate that gradients of surface tension can drive the motion of interfaces even in the absence of hydrodynamics driving Marangoni flows
44
.
We apply our framework to systems subject to temperature gradients, which enables us to characterize the thermophoretic motion of phase-separated droplets, and illustrate how their dynamics is affected by the joint influence of bulk and capillary forces. In our current treatment, we ignore the interplay between temperature inhomogeneities and fluctuations, which can manifest themselves in a variety of ways
45
,
46
,
47
.

In Section

Spatially modulated mixtures

2.1
Phase Separation in inhomogeneous conditions

We consider a binary mixture of species
A
and
B
described by a phase field
\phi(\bm{x},t)
, which measures the relative concentration of one of the two species (say
A
) with respect to the total concentration of
A+B
.
This field evolves according to the continuity equation:
\partial_{t}\phi=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}
as a result of total number conservation. When the physical properties of the material vary within the sample, the mass current
\mathbf{J}
is given by a straightforward generalization of model B
48

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}(\phi,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-D(\phi,\bm{x})\nabla\mu(\phi,\bm{x})\penalty\ ,\quad\mu(\phi,\bm{x})=\dfrac{\delta\pazocal{F}}{\delta\phi}(\phi,\bm{x})\penalty\ ,

(1a)

\displaystyle\pazocal{F}[\phi]=\int d^{d}x\;\left[\dfrac{K(\phi,\bm{x})}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+U(\phi,\bm{x})\right]\penalty\ ,

(1b)

where
d
denotes the number of spatial dimensions.
Namely, the concentration
\phi
is advected, up to a mobility factor
D
, by the gradient of the chemical potential
\mu
,
which itself derives from a free energy functional
\pazocal{F}
.
The free energy gathers a bulk contribution,
U
, and an interface-penalizing term with an elastic constant
K
.
Note that we allow all parameters of the theory to vary both with
\phi
and in space.
In what follows,
stated spatial dependencies of parameters are explicit, and thus distinct from those arising through
\phi

For systems with homogeneous material parameters in the phase separation regime, solutions of Eqs. (
1
) correspond to homogeneous domains with equilibrium bulk concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{-}<\bar{\phi}_{+}
, separated by sharp interfaces.
The thickness of these interfaces is associated with a length scale
l\simeq\sqrt{U_{0}/K_{0}}
, where
U_{0}
and
K_{0}
are characteristic scales extracted from
U
and
K
, respectively.
In addition, the relaxation dynamics of the phase field
\phi
toward the equilibrium interface profile
\bar{\phi}
happens over the time scale
t_{l}\simeq l^{2}/D_{0}
, with
D_{0}
a typical mobility scale.
The sharp interface limit is obtained when the macroscopic scales of the dynamics, such as the sizes of the phase-separated domains and the characteristic radius of curvature of their interfaces, are much larger than
l

15
.
This condition ensures a time scale separation between the motion of the interface and the relaxation of the concentration around its equilibrium profile, which can be considered as fast.
A moving interface is then described at all times by the stationary concentration profile
\bar{\phi}
which locally minimizes
\pazocal{F}
and interpolates between the equilibrium concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}
.

The sharp interface limit naturally extends to spatially varying
K
,
U
, and
D
in Eq. (
1
), when these parameters vary over length scales that remain large with respect to
l
.
Under this assumption, the equilibrium bulk densities become local quantities that depend on the position
\bm{x}
, and can be determined from the common tangent construction
49
applied to
the free energy density
U(\phi,\bm{x})
.
In particular, at each point of space the chemical potential
\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})
and phase concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}(\bm{x})
are solutions of:

\begin{cases}\left.\partial_{\phi}U(\phi,\bm{x})\right|_{\bar{\phi}_{+}(\bm{x})}=\left.\partial_{\phi}U(\phi,\bm{x})\right|_{\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x})}=\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})\penalty\ ,\\
U[\bar{\phi}_{+}(\bm{x}),\bm{x}]-U[\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x}),\bm{x}]=\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})\Delta\bar{\phi}(\bm{x})\penalty\ ,\end{cases}

(2)

where
\Delta\bar{\phi}(\bm{x})=\bar{\phi}_{+}(\bm{x})-\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x})
.
In general, spatial variations of
\bar{\mu}
lead to nonvanishing bulk currents, due to Eq. (
1a
).
To keep the corresponding mass transport slow as compared to the relaxation of
\phi
toward the stationary interface profile, we further require variations of
\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})
to be smooth across the sample, i.e.
|\nabla\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})/\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})|\ll l^{-1}
.

In the following section, we derive an effective equation of motion for the interface separating phase-separated domains with concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}(\bm{x})
.
This equation involves the interface surface tension
\sigma(\bm{x})
, which is here position-dependent.
To evaluate
\sigma(\bm{x})
, we define the bulk pressure and an adjusted free energy as

\displaystyle\Pi(\phi,\bm{x})

\displaystyle=U(\phi,\bm{x})-\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})\phi\penalty\ ,

(3a)

\displaystyle\pazocal{F}^{\prime}[\phi]

\displaystyle=\pazocal{F}[\phi]-\int d^{d}x\,[\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})\phi]\penalty\ .

(3b)

When
\bar{\mu}
is constant,
the stationary interface profile
\bar{\phi}
is obtained by minimizing
\pazocal{F}^{\prime}
, whereby
\bar{\mu}
acts as a Lagrange multiplier ensuring mass conservation
15
.
The surface tension, i.e. the free energy of the interface per unit area, is then obtained from Eq. (
1b
) by evaluating
\pazocal{F}^{\prime}[\bar{\phi}]
, as we detail in Sec. A of ESI
{\dagger}
for completeness.
Analogously, for inhomogeneous model parameters,
\bar{\phi}
is obtained by minimizing the r.h.s. of Eq. (
3b

thus accounting for the common tangent construction (
2

\mu[\bar{\phi}(\bm{x}),\bm{x}]\to\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})

Moreover, for smooth modulations, variations of the material parameter across the interface are negligible: the surface tension then only depends on the local values of the coefficients of the free energy.
As a result, its expression is a straightforward generalization of the homogeneous case, and reads

\sigma(\bm{x})=\int_{\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x})}^{\bar{\phi}_{+}(\bm{x})}d\phi\,\sqrt{2K(\phi,\bm{x})[\Pi(\phi,\bm{x})-\Pi_{-}(\bm{x})]},

(4)

where
\Pi_{-}(\bm{x})\equiv\Pi[\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x}),\bm{x}]
is the bulk pressure in the dilute phase.

2.2
The interface equations of motion

We now consider an interface in
d=3
separating domains with concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{+}
and
\bar{\phi}_{-}
. Denoting
G_{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
as the Green’s function solution of
\nabla\cdot[D(\phi,\bm{x})\nabla G_{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})]=\delta^{3}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})

\int d^{3}y\;G_{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{y},t)=\dfrac{\delta\pazocal{F}^{\prime}}{\delta\phi}(\phi,\bm{x})+\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})-\mu_{\infty},

(5)

where
\mu_{\infty}

In addition, for closed systems the total mass conservation imposes a second relation:

\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{x},t)=0.

(6)

Below, we derive from Eqs. (
5

Denoting
V_{+}(t)

\bar{\phi}_{+}

\phi(\bm{x},t)=\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x})+\Delta\bar{\phi}(\bm{x})\,\Theta[\bm{x},V_{+}(t)]\penalty\ ,

(7)

where
\Theta

0

The volume

need not correspond to a connected domain, such that the system may
a priori

The only geometrical constraint imposed by the sharp interface approximation is that the mean curvature
H

Hl\ll 1

Equation (
7

\bar{\phi}_{\pm}

Assuming Eq. (
7

\phi

\bm{v}
:

\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{x},t)=-\Delta\bar{\phi}(\bm{x})\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)\cdot\nabla\Theta[\bm{x},V_{+}(t)].

(8)

Substituting (
8

5

we integrate the gradient of the step function by parts and use the divergence theorem to obtain

\int d^{3}y\;G_{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\,\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{y},t)=\int_{\partial V_{+}(t)}dS_{\mathbf{Y}}\,\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{Y})\cdot\bm{v}(\mathbf{Y},t)\zeta(\bm{x},\mathbf{Y})\penalty\ ,

where the

V_{+}
which defines the interface, while the normal unit vector

points outwards from
V_{+}
and
\zeta(\bm{x},\mathbf{Y})\equiv\Delta\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{Y})G_{D}(\bm{x},\mathbf{Y})

Evaluating the functional derivative of

Therefore,

The thickness of

Before turning to concrete applications of Eqs. (
9
),
we first comment on their structure and the nature of each of their terms.
Equation (
9
) describes the interface motion as an overdamped dynamics,
albeit with a nonlocal drag force consisting in the convolution of the interface velocity with the friction kernel
\zeta
.
Such nonlocal friction originates from the conservation law on
\phi
, as can be observed from the fact that
\zeta(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\propto G_{D}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
(see Eq. (
5
)).
As we show in the next sections, the nonlocality of the interface friction directly influences the scaling of a droplet mobility with its volume.

The effective drag force on the interface is balanced by the r.h.s. of Eq. (
9
), which features three contributions.
The first one is the standard Laplace pressure
15
which results from the elastic cost of interfaces.
When the material properties of the system are homogeneous, this term leads to the minimization of the surface area of the domain
V_{+}
while preserving its total volume.
The second term is also a capillary force, but originates from gradients of the free energy density, and drives the interface towards regions of lower surface tension.
Despite significant interest in droplet dynamics driven by inhomogeneous surface tension
50
,
51
,
52
, this term has been, to the best of our knowledge, unnoticed so far.
Most of the existing literature instead focuses on currents induced by Marangoni flows
44
,
53
,
which lead to qualitatively analogous transport phenomena.
This likely originates from the fact that hydrodynamic effects dominate the transport of sufficiently large droplets that move under a momentum-conserving dynamics, as can be shown from scaling arguments
15
.
However, when flows are suppressed by the presence of a momentum sink, such as
for systems in contact with a substrate or when phase separation happens in a porous medium
54
,
transport induced by the capillary terms in Eq. (
9
) may compete with or even dominate that arising from Marangoni flows.

The thermodynamic force
\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})

results from the fact that the current in Eq. (
1a
) drives the concentration
\phi
to regions with lower free energy.
Contrary to the first two terms described above, it is a bulk contribution and thus dominates the dynamics of large droplets.
Like the gradient of the surface tension, this force vanishes when the material parameters are homogeneous,
since a constant
\bar{\mu}
can be absorbed in a redefinition of
\mu_{\infty}
.
In an inhomogeneous system,
an enlightening example of spatially-varying
\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})
results from the coupling of
a homogeneous free energy
F_{0}(\phi)

with an externally applied potential
V(\bm{x})
,
such that
F(\phi,\bm{x})=F_{0}(\phi)+V(\bm{x})\phi
.
For instance,
V
can be the gravitational potential leading to droplet sedimentation, such that
V(\bm{x})=\Delta mgz
, where
\Delta m
is the mass density difference between the two species,
g
is the standard acceleration induced by gravity, and
z
denotes the vertical coordinate.
Using Eqs. (
2
) and (
4
), one concludes that
V
does not affect the equilibrium concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}
nor the surface tension, both of which remain homogeneous, while it enters the common tangent construction, such that
\bar{\mu}(\bm{x})=V(\bm{x})/\Delta\bar{\phi}
.

Finally, Eq. (
9b
) provides a condition allowing to determine the integration constant
\mu_{\infty}
.
When combined, Eqs. (
9
) thus fully characterize the dynamics of sharp interfaces.
In particular, the interface dynamics only depends on quantities which are directly measurable, such as the binodal densities, the surface tension, the chemical potential and the mixture mobility, and can thus be determined without solving Eq. (
1
) explicitly or even requiring a specific model for the free energy
\pazocal{F}
.

2.3
Dynamics driven by a surface tension profile

To illustrate how Eqs. (
9
) can be applied in practice,
we consider a minimal example of a single droplet moving in a one-dimensional inhomogeneous medium.
In the next section, we will then focus on the case of
systems driven by inhomogeneous temperature profiles.
Here, we consider the free energy:

\pazocal{F}[\phi]=\int dx\left[\dfrac{K}{2}(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}+\dfrac{A(x)}{4}(\phi-\bar{\phi}_{+})^{2}(\phi-\bar{\phi}_{-})^{2}\right],

(10)

and assume a constant mobility
D(\phi,x)=D
.
The free energy (
10
) induces modulations of the surface tension, but leaves the binodal concentrations
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}
constant.
In one dimension, a droplet of phase
+
is described by the position of its left and right interfaces denoted
x_{l}
and
x_{r}
, respectively.
The integral on the l.h.s. of Eq. (
9
) then becomes a point-wise evaluation at the two interfaces, and in a periodic domain of size
L
the Green’s function reads
G_{D}(x,y)=(2DL)^{-1}|x-y|(L-|x-y|)
(see Sec. C of ESI
{\dagger}

\begin{split}\dfrac{\Delta\bar{\phi}|x_{r}-x_{l}|(L-|x_{r}-x_{l}|)}{2DL}\dot{x}_{r}&=-\dfrac{\sigma^{\prime}(x_{l})}{\Delta\bar{\phi}}-\mu_{\infty},\\
\dfrac{\Delta\bar{\phi}|x_{r}-x_{l}|(L-|x_{r}-x_{l}|)}{2DL}\dot{x}_{l}&=-\dfrac{\sigma^{\prime}(x_{r})}{\Delta\bar{\phi}}+\mu_{\infty},\end{split}

(11)

where we have used that the mean curvature
H=0
and the chemical potential is constant.
The surface tension is given in terms of the parameters of the theory by
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{6\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{KA(x)}\Delta\bar{\phi}^{3}
(using Eq. (
4
)).

The mass conservation condition (
9b
) then leads to
\dot{x}_{r}=\dot{x}_{l}
, such that the droplet size
S=|x_{r}-x_{l}|
remains constant over time.
Making use of these relations in Eq. (
11

\mu_{\infty}
and find that the droplet centre of mass

and velocity

v_{\rm c}=-M_{\rm eff}U_{\rm eff}^{\prime}(x_{\rm c})\penalty\ ,

(12)

where the effective mobility and potential read

M_{\rm eff}=\frac{DL}{(\Delta\bar{\phi})^{2}S(L-S)},\quad U_{\rm eff}(x_{\rm c})=\sigma\left(x_{\rm c}+\frac{S}{2}\right)+\sigma\left(x_{\rm c}-\frac{S}{2}\right)\penalty\ .

Equation (
12
) highlights an important feature of the dynamics: in the presence of smooth changes in the material parameters, a droplet moves so as to minimize the total surface tension at its interfaces under the constraint of mass conservation.

To verify that Eq. (
12
) faithfully describes the dynamics, we confront it with numerical simulations of the field equations (
1
) using the free energy (
10
).
In particular, we impose a sinusoidal modulation of
A(x)

x=L/2

(See Sec. D of ESI

As noted previously, spatial variations of

Hence, droplets spontaneously migrate towards this position.
Note that since the effective force in Eq. (
12

for asymmetric profiles the stationary position of its centre of mass does not necessarily coincide with the location of minimum surface tension.
Figure
1

Eq. (
10
) quantitatively captures the droplet trajectory, as reflected by the excellent agreement between the theoretical curve and the data points obtained from simulations.

Hence, the velocity deriving from Eq. (

R
is sufficiently large.

3

Temperature Modulations

3.1
Nonequilibrium thermodynamics of phase separation

The physical properties of a mixture can be straightforwardly modulated by applying an inhomogeneous temperature profile across the sample.
The study of nonisothermal phase separation has received a great deal of attention both from the theoretical
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
35
and experimental
56
,
25
,
36
,
26
viewpoints.
Polymer blends, in which precise temperature modulations can be obtained by focusing laser beams inside the sample
25
,
36
, are particularly well-suited to explore this phenomenon.

The dynamics of a mixture in an inhomogeneous temperature profile
T(\bm{x})
can be modelled by means of nonequilibrium thermodynamics
57
.
The starting point is a generalization of the current in Eq. (
1
), which reads

\mathbf{J}[\phi,T(\bm{x})]=-D(\phi,T)\nabla\mu(\phi,T)-D_{T}(\phi,T)\nabla T,

(14)

where we suppose that
\mathbf{J}
varies in space only through
\phi
and
T

The dependencies of the mobilities
D
and
D_{T}
on the chemical concentration
\phi
and temperature are generally nontrivial, and various models have been considered in the literature
35
,
4
.
Here, we use the forms

\displaystyle D(\phi)=D_{0}\,\phi(1-\phi),\qquad D_{T}(\phi)=D_{T,0}\,\phi(1-\phi),

(15)

which ensure that the current (
14
) vanishes when only a single species is present (
\phi=0
or
\phi=1
).
For simplicity, we have also neglected the dependencies of the mobilities in the temperature,
as for sufficiently smooth modulations they do not significantly affect the dynamics of droplets.

The current in Eq. (
14
) differs from the expression (
1a
) as it includes an additional term modeling thermodiffusion (or Soret effect).
Although generalizing Eqs. (
9
) by taking into account this term is possible,
we show below that many physically relevant scenarios admit an expression for
\mathbf{J}
analogous to Eq. (
1a
), where the contribution from the Soret current enters as an additional term in the free energy
\pazocal{F}
.
In what follows, and for the sake of simplicity, we restrict the analysis to these cases.

We model the bulk potential of the free energy density with the Flory-Huggins theory
58
,
59
:

\displaystyle U(\phi,T)=k_{B}T\bigg[\frac{\phi}{N_{A}}\log(\phi)+\frac{1-\phi}{N_{B}}\log(1-\phi)+\chi\phi(1-\phi)\bigg],

(16)

where
N_{A}
and
N_{B}
are degrees of polymerization of species
A
and
B
, respectively.
The coefficient
\chi
is the interaction parameter,
which can be expressed as a function of the temperature as
60
,
61

\chi=\alpha+\frac{\beta}{T},

(17)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are phenomenological parameters that can be fitted from experimental data.
Additionally, we model the elastic contribution to the free energy using de Gennes’ random phase approximation
62
,
32
:

K(\phi,T)=\dfrac{k_{B}T}{18}\left[\dfrac{\theta_{A}^{2}}{\phi}+\dfrac{\theta_{B}^{2}}{1-\phi}\right]\penalty\ ,

(18)

where
\theta_{A,B}
are the Kuhn lengths associated with
A
and
B
, respectively.
In the presence of an inhomogeneous temperature profile, the chemical potential for the binary mixture is then given by
31

\mu=\dfrac{\delta}{\delta\phi}\left\{\int\dfrac{d^{d}x}{k_{B}T(\bm{x})}\left[\dfrac{K[\phi,T(\bm{x})]}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+U[\phi,T(\bm{x})]\right]\right\}.

(19)

With the bulk potential (
16
),
the system exhibits phase separation when the interaction parameter
\chi
overcomes the critical value
\chi_{c}=(\sqrt{N_{A}}+\sqrt{N_{B}})^{2}/(2N_{A}\,N_{B})
,
which in turn determines the critical temperature
T_{c}
through Eq. (
17
).
In the remainder of this section,
we analyze the dynamics of droplets in systems maintained close to the critical point (
T(\bm{x})\lesssim T_{c}
) and far from it (
T(\bm{x})\ll T_{c}
).

3.2
Dynamics close to the critical point

We focus here on two-dimensional mixtures maintained close to their critical point.
In particular, we show below that Eqs. (

3.3
Dynamics far from the critical point

We now assume that the system is far from criticality, which is achieved for
T\ll T_{c}
.
In this regime, the mixture is almost completely phase-separated, so that
\bar{\phi}_{-}\to 0
and
\bar{\phi}_{+}\to 1
.
Since the resulting concentration field varies significantly when crossing an interface, the
\phi
-dependency of the mobilities appearing in Eq. (
15
) cannot be neglected.
On the other hand, we keep ignoring the variations of
D_{0}
and
D_{T,0}
with the temperature, as we are interested in the effect of weak modulations.
Under this assumption, the Soret current in Eq. (
14
) can again be treated as an additional contribution to the chemical potential (
19
) which renormalizes the bulk free energy as
U[\phi,T(\bm{x})]/[k_{B}T(\bm{x})]\to U[\phi,T(\bm{x})]/[k_{B}T(\bm{x})]+\Sigma_{T,0}\phi T(\bm{x})
, where
\Sigma_{T,0}=D_{T,0}/D_{0}

Hence, we can directly deduce the dynamics of droplets from Eqs. (
9

We demonstrate below that, far from the critical point, the capillary term in Eq. (
9

This contribution, in particular, dominates the thermophoretic transport of small droplets, and may either amplify or compete with the Soret effect.

Using Eqs. (

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x})\simeq e^{N_{A}/N_{B}-1-N_{A}\chi(\bm{x})},\qquad\bar{\phi}_{+}(\bm{x})\simeq 1-e^{N_{B}/N_{A}-1-N_{B}\chi(\bm{x})},

To evaluate the surface tension,

For simplicity, we restrict the calculations to one-dimensional systems, as we do not expect higher-dimensional dynamics to introduce qualitative changes.
As before, we assume a weak temperature modulation

|\delta T(\bm{x})|\ll T_{0}

In particular, the fact that the motion of large droplets is dominated by the bulk Soret currents, while small droplets primarily move as a result of capillary forces, should remain independent of the dimension.

We conclude this section by commenting on nonlinear effects influencing the droplet mobility.
For simplicity, we first consider the case of an isolated droplet evolving in an infinite background of the complementary phase.
The effective mobility appearing in Eq. (

Specifically, assuming one of the species to be much smaller than the other, the dynamics of droplets in which it is dominant is strongly suppressed as compared to droplets of the other phase.

The situation is more subtle in finite systems, where the effective droplet mobility varies with the type of imposed boundary condition.
For periodic systems, in particular,

Hence, in this case,

Dynamics under spatio-temporal modulations

In this section, we extend the above formalism to account for situations where the material properties of the sample are modulated both in space and time.
As before, we assume that the coefficients in the free energy vary on a characteristic length scale much larger than the interface length
l
,
which ensures the validity of the sharp interface approximation.
In addition, we require that they vary over time scales much larger than
t_{l}\simeq l^{2}/D

the typical time associated with the relaxation of the concentration field toward the stationary interface profile.

Using the time and length scale separation between the interface profile dynamics and the applied modulations, the surface tension, binodal densities, and bulk chemical potential can then be deduced from Eqs. (

4

Note, however, that they now all vary both in space and time, such that we generalize Eq. (

\phi(\bm{x},t)=\bar{\phi}_{-}(\bm{x},t)+\Delta\bar{\phi}(\bm{x},t)\,\Theta[\bm{x},V_{+}(t)]\penalty\ .

(36)

Plugging this ansatz in Eqs. (

\displaystyle\int_{\partial V_{+}(t)}dS_{\mathbf{Y}}\,\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{Y})\cdot\bm{v}(\mathbf{Y},t)\,\zeta(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},t)=\Xi(\mathbf{X},t)+\bar{\mu}(\mathbf{X},t)-\mu_{\infty}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\Delta\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{X},t)}\big[2H(\mathbf{X})\sigma(\mathbf{X},t)+\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\cdot\nabla\sigma(\mathbf{X},t)\big],

5

Discussion

In this work, we have developed a general framework for describing the dynamics of interfaces in phase-separating systems that are subject to space- and time-dependent modulations of their material properties.
The equations of motion, Eqs. (
9
) and (
38
), derived in the sharp interface approximation, allow us to quantify the dynamics of interfaces in terms of directly measurable quantities such as surface tension, chemical potential, binodal concentrations, and mixture mobility.
We have shown that, even in the absence of hydrodynamics, the motion of phase-separated droplets is driven by two contributions of distinct physical origins.
The first one is a capillary term, which causes droplets to minimize the overall surface tension at their interface, while the second results from bulk currents induced by a spatially-dependent chemical potential, which drive dense droplets to regions of minimum free energy. Notably, the relative magnitude of these terms is controlled by the droplet volume, with the capillary contribution dominating the dynamics of small droplets.

While we have illustrated the consequences of these results through minimal examples mostly involving a single droplet, Eqs. (
9
) and (
38
) can potentially describe a wide range of physical phenomena. Considering multiple droplets will, for example, enable the exploration of how an inhomogeneous environment affects the properties of nucleation
66
,
67
and ripening
15
,
68
,
69
. Beyond inhomogeneities induced by temperature modulations, our approach is suited to investigate how the dynamics of droplets is influenced by other factors such as gravity
6
, electric fields
70
, and diffusiophoresis
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
.
In particular, a number of works have postulated that the positioning of biomolecular condensates in cells can be driven by diffusiophoresis in response to the presence of biomolecule concentration gradients
75
,
76
. Importantly, current studies have overlooked the potential impact of effective capillary forces induced by gradients of surface tension on the dynamics of these small droplets, whose diameter often ranges below the micrometer.

The framework we have introduced here neglects hydrodynamics, such that Eqs. (
9
) and (
38
) typically describe the motion of slow interfaces in a highly viscous environment. Extensions that take into account the coupling of phase separation with hydrodynamic flows, however, should be straightforward, provided that the typical flow length scale remains much larger than the interface width.
Our approach further offers promising avenues for the study of active phase separation
73
,
77
,
78
,
79
in inhomogeneous media by considering, for example, active agents such as enzymes or bacteria interacting via self-produced chemical fields
73
,
74
,
80
,
81
,
82
.

The surface tension of an inhomogeneous mixture

In this section, we outline the calculation steps leading to Eq. (4) of the main text, which gives the expression of the surface tension for an inhomogeneous mixture in the sharp interface limit.
Here, we work in the regime where the interface is sufficiently thin such that we neglect its curvature and locally approximate it as flat.

First, let us consider a homogeneous mixture, i.e. for which the coefficients in the free energy do not explicitly depend on space.
Supposing that the interface is orthogonal to the
x
axis, the corresponding concentration profile
\bar{\phi}(x)
minimizes the free energy density

F^{\prime}(\phi)=\frac{K(\phi)}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2}+U(\phi)-\bar{\mu}\phi=\frac{K(\phi)}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{2}+\Pi(\phi),

(S1)

where we have kept the notations used in Sec. 2.1 of the main text.
In particular, the chemical potential
\bar{\mu}
enters as a Lagrange multiplier ensuring total mass conservation, while the profile
\bar{\phi}(x)
satisfies the boundary conditions
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\bar{\phi}(x)=\bar{\phi}_{\pm}
with
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}
the binodal concentrations of the two phases.
To determine
\bar{\phi}
, we thus apply the Euler-Lagrange equations to
F^{\prime}
, leading to

K(\bar{\phi})\frac{d^{2}\bar{\phi}}{dx^{2}}+\dfrac{1}{2}K^{\prime}(\bar{\phi})\left(\frac{d\bar{\phi}}{dx}\right)^{2}-\Pi^{\prime}(\bar{\phi})=0.

(S2)

We then multiply both sides of Eq. (
S2
) by
d\bar{\phi}/dx
, and integrate from
-\infty
to
x
, to obtain

\frac{1}{2}K(\bar{\phi})\left(\frac{d\bar{\phi}}{dx}\right)^{2}=\Pi(\bar{\phi})-\Pi(\bar{\phi}_{-}),

(S3)

where we have used that
\bar{\phi}\to\bar{\phi}_{-}
and
d\bar{\phi}/dx\to 0
when
x\to-\infty
.
The surface tension of the interface corresponds to the free energy of the system per unit area relative to an equilibrium homogeneous configuration.
Using, moreover, the phase “
-
” as a reference, we obtain
1

\sigma=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\;\left[\frac{K(\bar{\phi})}{2}\left(\frac{d\bar{\phi}}{dx}\right)^{2}+\Pi(\bar{\phi})-\Pi(\bar{\phi}_{-})\right]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\;K(\bar{\phi})\left(\frac{d\bar{\phi}}{dx}\right)^{2}=\int_{\bar{\phi}_{-}}^{\bar{\phi}_{+}}d\bar{\phi}\;K(\bar{\phi})\frac{d\bar{\phi}}{dx},

(S4)

where the second equality results from Eq. (
S3
), while the last one was obtained through the change of integration variable
x\to\bar{\phi}(x)
.
Solving Eq. (
S3
) for
d\bar{\phi}/dx
, and plugging the corresponding solution in Eq. (
S4
), we then get

\sigma=\int_{\bar{\phi}_{-}}^{\bar{\phi}_{+}}d\bar{\phi}\;\sqrt{2K(\bar{\phi})[\Pi(\bar{\phi})-\Pi(\bar{\phi}_{-})]}.

(S5)

Assuming now that the coefficients in the free energy (
S1
) vary in space,
the surface tension becomes a spatially-dependent quantity.
In the sharp interface limit, the coefficients
K(\phi,x)
,
\Pi(\phi,x)
and
\bar{\phi}_{\pm}(x)
entering the expression of
\sigma
in Eq. (
S5
)
do not vary much over the interface thickness
l
.
We can then simply approximate these coefficients with their value at the centre of the interface, which allows to straightforwardly recover Eq. (4) of the main text.

\int_{dW}d^{3}x\;\hat{\bf n}(\bm{x})\cdot\nabla\phi(\bm{x},t)\int d^{3}y\;G_{D}(\bm{x},\bm{y},t)\,\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{y},t)=-dA\Delta\bar{\phi}(\mathbf{X},t)\left[\int_{\partial V_{+}(t)}dS_{\mathbf{Y}}\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{Y})\cdot\bm{v}(\mathbf{Y},t)\zeta(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},t)-\Xi(\mathbf{X},t)\right],

(S21)

where
\Xi(\mathbf{X},t)=-\sum_{k=\pm}\int_{V_{k}(t)}d^{3}y\;G_{D}(\mathbf{X},\bm{y},t)\,\partial_{t}\bar{\phi}_{k}(\bm{y},t)
as defined in the main text.
In addition, Eq. (38b) of the main text is obtained from a similar calculation after replacing the ansatz for
\phi(\bm{x},t)
in the mass conservation constraint
\int d^{3}x\;\partial_{t}\phi(\bm{x},t)
.

C

Green’s functions in periodic domains

To confront the predictions of the interface equations of motion —Eqs. (9) and (38) in the main text— to numerical simulations of the full phase field model,
we considered one-dimensional systems with periodic boundary conditions.
Here, we detail how we calculate the corresponding Green’s function, which differs from that associated with infinite domains.

Namely, we consider a periodic domain of size
L
, and look for a function
G_{D}(x,y)
satisfying the boundary conditions
G(0,y)=G(L,y)
and
\partial_{x}G(0,y)=\partial_{x}G(L,y)
.
First, we note that the equation

\partial_{x}\,[D(\phi,x)\,\partial_{x}G_{D}(x,y)]=\delta(x-y),

(S22)

does not admit such a periodic solution for
G_{D}(x,y)
, as can be checked by integrating it over
x
in the whole domain.
To find an admissible Green’s function, we instead look for solutions of

\partial_{x}\,[D(\phi,x)\,\partial_{x}G_{D}(x,y)]=\delta(x-y)-\dfrac{1}{L}.

(S23)

The idea behind this approach is that the convolution

\psi(x)=\int_{0}^{L}dy\;G_{D}(x,y)s(y),

(S24)

with
G_{D}(x,y)
a periodic solution of Eq. (
S23
)
is still a solution of the equation
\partial_{x}\,[D(\phi,x)\,\partial_{x}\psi(x)]=s(x)
whenever the source satisfies
\int_{0}^{L}dx\;s(x)=0
,
as can be checked by replacing
\psi(x)
with Eq. (
S24
).
The phase field model with conserved mass precisely falls into the same class of problems, where
\psi
is the mixture chemical potential, while
s=\partial_{t}\phi
whose integral over the whole domain vanishes due to mass conservation.
Crucially, contrary to Eq.(
S22
), Eq. (
S23
) admits a periodic solution, which for constant
D
is given by

G_{D}(x-y)=\dfrac{|x-y|(L-|x-y|)}{2DL}.

(S25)

E

Dynamics of spherical droplets subjected to surface tension gradients

Here, we provide details on the derivation of Eq. (13) of the main text, which describes the dynamics of spherical droplets under the influence of surface tension gradients.
For simplicity, we assume that the droplet shape is not affected by the surface tension landscape, such that it has a constant density and retains its spherical shape at all times.
The dynamics of the droplet is therefore characterized by the motion of its centre of mass,
\bm{x}_{c}=R^{-d}V_{d}^{-1}\int_{V_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}d^{d}x\;\bm{x}
,
where the integration domain
V_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})
denotes the ball of radius
R
centred in
\bm{x}_{c}
, and
V_{d}
is the volume of the unit
d
-sphere.
Using Reynolds transport theorem, the centre of mass velocity follows

\dot{\bm{x}}_{c}=\frac{1}{R^{d}V_{d}}\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\cdot\bm{v}(\mathbf{X},t)\;R\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{R^{d-1}V_{d}}\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;\bm{v}(\mathbf{X},t),

(S27)

where the integration is now performed on the sphere of radius
R
centred in
\bm{x}_{c}
,
S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})
.

We consider the simple case of an infinite domain, for which the Green’s functions in two and three dimensions are given by

G^{(2)}_{D}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})=\frac{1}{2\pi D}\log(|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|),\qquad G^{(3)}_{D}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})=-\frac{1}{4\pi D|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|},

(S28)

respectively.
In particular, these functions satisfy the identity

\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;G^{(d)}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y})\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})=-\frac{R}{dD}\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{Y}),

(S29)

where
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
are two points on the circle (
d=2
) or on the sphere (
d=3
).

Multiplying Eq. (9a) of the main text by
\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})
, and integrating over
S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})
, we then get for the l.h.s.

\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\,\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{Y}}\,\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{Y})\cdot\bm{v}(\mathbf{Y},t)\Delta\bar{\phi}G^{(d)}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y})=-\frac{\Delta\bar{\phi}R^{d}V_{d}}{dD}\dot{\bm{x}}_{c},

(S30)

where we have used that the binodal concentrations are constant in the case we are interested in.
For the r.h.s. of Eq. (9a), we note that the chemical potential is constant and can thus be discarded, such that we are left with evaluating the r.h.s. of

\frac{\Delta\bar{\phi}^{2}R^{d}V_{d}}{dD}\dot{\bm{x}}_{c}=-\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\left[\frac{d-1}{R}\sigma(\mathbf{X})+\hat{n}(\mathbf{X})\cdot\nabla\sigma(\mathbf{X})\right].

(S31)

Using the relation
\frac{(d-1)}{R}\sigma(\mathbf{X})+\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\cdot\nabla\sigma(\mathbf{X})=\nabla\cdot[\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\sigma(\mathbf{X})]
, and applying the divergence theorem separately on each component of the r.h.s. of Eq. (
S31
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;\hat{n}_{i}\left(\frac{d-1}{R}\sigma(\mathbf{X})+\hat{\bf n}(\mathbf{X})\cdot\nabla\sigma(\mathbf{X})\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dV\;\partial^{2}_{\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{j}}[\hat{n}_{j}(\mathbf{X})\sigma(\mathbf{X})]

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dV\;\partial_{\mathbf{X}_{j}}\left[\hat{n}_{j}(\mathbf{X})\partial_{\mathbf{X}_{i}}\sigma(\mathbf{X})+\frac{\delta_{ij}-\hat{n}_{i}(\mathbf{X})\hat{n}_{j}(\mathbf{X})}{R}\sigma(\mathbf{X})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;\partial_{\mathbf{X}_{i}}\sigma(\mathbf{X}),

(S32)

where only the gradient term remains in the last equality, since the other term is orthogonal to
\hat{\bf n}
.
To obtain Eq. (13) of the main text, we finally use the fact that the droplet has a fixed shape, such that applying the change of variables
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}+\bm{x}_{c}
in the integral on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
E
) yields

\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\;\nabla\sigma(\mathbf{X})=\int_{S_{R}(0)}dS_{\mathbf{Z}}\nabla_{\mathbf{Z}}\sigma(\mathbf{Z}+\bm{x}_{c})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{c}}\int_{S_{R}(0)}dS_{\mathbf{Z}}\sigma(\mathbf{Z}+\bm{x}_{c})=\nabla_{\bm{x}_{c}}\int_{S_{R}(\bm{x}_{c})}dS_{\mathbf{X}}\,\sigma(\mathbf{X}).

(S33)
