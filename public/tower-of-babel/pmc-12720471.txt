Global, regional, and national epidemiology of cervical cancer in elderly women from 1990 to 2021 and its prediction to 2035
Authors: Yu Li, Kangtai Zheng, Qin Xu, Lingling Xie, Huaiwu Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer among elderly women poses a significant challenge to the elimination of cervical cancer. This study estimated the burden, trends, and disparities of cervical cancer in women aged ≥ 60 years from 1990 to 2021, with projections extending to 2035 at global, regional, and national levels.MethodsDeriving data from Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, we analyzed the incidence, prevalence, and mortality burden of cervical cancer among women aged ≥ 60 years. The average annual percent change calculated by Joinpoint regression analysis was used to demonstrate the temporal trend during 1990–2021. It highlights the increasing disease burden and significant socioeconomic disparities in this population, warranting further investigation. Background

Cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality and the incidence of cancer cases among women worldwide. With the implementation of evidence-based interventions, including human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, cervical cancer screening, and timely and effective treatments, cervical cancer is now regarded as a preventable and curable disease. WHO is formulating a global strategy with the objective of eradicating cervical cancer as a public health concern [1]. This strategy proposes an objective for all countries to achieve an incidence rate below 4 per 100,000 women-years. Additionally, it outlines triple intervention targets: achieving 90% HPV vaccination coverage, 70% lifetime cervical cancer screening coverage, and 90% access to treatment for cervical pre-cancer and cancer, all by 2030. Previous research has investigated the epidemiology of cervical cancer on a global scale, revealing a decline in the incidence and mortality rates across various populations over the past few decades, supporting the feasibility of the WHO targets [2–4]. However, existing studies have not thoroughly examined the changes in incidence, prevalence, and mortality among elderly women over the past decades, both globally and across different countries. This oversight neglects the significant burden of cervical cancer among older women and the complexities involved in their disease management [5, 6]. The substantial burden of cervical cancer in elderly women may pose a significant challenge to the realization of the WHO targets.

Given the aging population and the global increase in life expectancy, the absolute number of cervical cancers among older women is expected to increase. The incidence of cervical cancer in older women is primarily associated with HPV infection and a high positive screening rate [7, 8]. HPV vaccination is not a preventive measure for older women, as its efficacy is significantly lower in this age group compared to younger women [9, 10]. Cervical cancer deaths are often linked to late diagnosis and delayed treatment [11], which are more common in countries with limited medical resources. Consequently, the insufficient focus on the health needs of older adults, coupled with substantial regional disparities, is likely to exacerbate the burden of cervical cancer.

Comprehensive estimates of the burden associated with cervical cancer in older women are essential for understanding the current situation and call for the attention and care of potential patients within this age group. By utilizing data from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021, we aimed to describe the burden of cervical cancer among elderly women in age-specific group globally and regionally, as well as the progress in reducing incidence, prevalence, and mortality over the past decades. We also aimed to provide an overview of the inequality in burden between countries and to suggest tailored approaches for policymakers and medical providers.

Methods

Data sources

We obtained data on cervical cancer (classified under International Classification of Diseases tenth edition [ICD-10] code C53) in women aged over 60 years from GBD 2021, with the population divided into eight five-year age brackets (60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, and 95 + years); including the number and rate of incidence, prevalence, and deaths; and age-standard population; across 5 socio-demographic index (SDI) regions, 21 GBD regions and 204 countries and territories (with corresponding 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs)). GBD 2021 provides an updated epidemiological data encompassing 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories. Detailed information about GBD can be found in protocol in additional file 1 and other articles [12, 13]. GBD 2021 complies with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) recommendations [14].

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rate of cervical cancer among women aged over 60 years across 204 countries and territories. Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) [15], calculated by Joinpoint regression analysis, was utilized to demonstrate the trends in incidence, prevalence, and mortality from 1990 to 2021, with model specification based on the Weighted BIC method. The calculation formula for AAPC is: , where refers to the length of each segment in the range of years, where the denotes the slope coefficient for each segment within the expected time period. The AAPC value denotes the percentage annual change (increase, decrease, or no change). As the age-standardized rates did not follow a normal distribution, the Locally Weighted Scatterplot Smoothing regression (Loess) model is utilized to generate a fitted curve and Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the relationship between age-standardized rates and SDI quintile.

To project cervical cancer trends through 2035, we applied the Bayesian Age–Period–Cohort (BAPC) model using annually estimated data from the Global Burden of Disease Study, fitted in 1-year intervals. The BAPC model extends the conventional log-linear APC model through Bayesian inference, where age, period, and cohort effects are treated as random variables with prior distributions that are updated to derive posterior distributions for probabilistic inference [16–18]. A key assumption is that effects from adjacent age groups, time periods, or cohorts exhibit continuity and smoothness [18]. To capture this, the BAPC model imposes structural constraints on the age, period, and cohort parameters through second-order random walk (RW2) priors. The priors assume that the second differences of the parameters follow a normal distribution with mean zero [18]. This specification smooths the age, period, and cohort effects, yielding medically plausible trends that emphasize long-term patterns while attenuating short-term fluctuations [19, 20]. Results

Global trends

Globally, from 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized incidence rate per 100,000 population in women aged over 60 years showed a slight decrease (AAPC=−0.75, 95%CI −0.77~−0.73), declining from 42.74 (95%UI 39.34 ~ 46.10) in 1990 to 33.84 (30.15 ~ 37.00) in 2021. In terms of prevalence, the age-standardized prevalence rate initially declined from 97.52 (91.03 ~ 103.70) in 1990 to 87.24 (80.59 ~ 92.06) in 2006, then increased to 94.02 per 100,000 (84.09 ~ 103.69) in 2021, resulting in an overall subtle reduction (AAPC=−0.11, 95%CI −0.14~−0.09). The age-standardized mortality rate decreased from 33.55 per 100,000 (30.62 ~ 6.54) in 1990 to 23.87 per 100,000 (21.26 ~ 26.13) in 2021, with an AAPC of −1.09 (−1.11~−1.07) (Table 1; Fig. 1). In 2021, there were 114.4 thousand new cases of cervical cancer globally, and the number of elderly patients with cervical cancer was 261.0 thousand. Cervical cancer caused 139.8 thousand deaths (Table 1, Table S1−3). The proportion of deaths among those aged over 60 relative to the total number of cervical cancer-related deaths steadily increased, reaching nearly 50% in 2021 (Fig. 2).

Global trends by age subgroup

Across all age subgroups, the number of newly diagnosed cases of cervical cancer increased by at least 1.5 times, and even tripled among those aged over 90 years from 1990 to 2021 (Fig.S1). The age-standardized incidence rate of cervical cancer among seniors increased in all age subgroups at various rate (Fig.S2 and 3). In 2021, the incidence rate was highest among those aged 60–64 (2.07 per 100,000), 65–69 (1.75 per 100,000) (Table 1, Table S1−3). Compared to the newly diagnosed cases and deaths, during 1990–2021, the number of existing cases of cervical cancer among elderly women at least doubled or nearly doubled in every age subgroup (Fig.S2). Except for those aged 75–79 years, age-standardized prevalence rate of cervical cancer increased at a noticeable rate across all age subgroups in the past three decades (Fig.S3).

Global trends by sociodemographic index

During 1990–2021, the age-standardized incidence and mortality rate of cervical cancer among elderly women decreased across all subgroups of SDI regions (Fig.S4). The incidence rate in the low SDI region consistently remained the highest (71.76 in 2021, 95%UI 59.84 ~ 85.37), reaching 3.6 times that of the high SDI region (20.00, 95%UI 17.39 ~ 21.71) in 2021 (Table 1). The mortality rate per 100,000 population of low SDI region (62.64 in 2021, 95%UI 52.25 ~ 74.48) was more than five times that of High SDI region (11.60, 95%UI 9.87 ~ 12.69) (Table 1). During the temporal trend of the age-standardized prevalence rate among seniors from 1990 to 2021, the prevalence rate of high-middle SDI region (AAPC = 0.14, 95%CI 0.04 ~ 0.21) and middle SDI region (AAPC = 0.75, 95%CI 0.72 ~ 0.77) increased over time, while that of high SDI, low-middle SDI and low SDI exhibited an overall downward trend in this period (Table 1). In 2021, both the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates and its AAPCs showed a decreasing trend as SDI increased (Fig. 3, Fig.S5). However, regarding prevalence rates, middle SDI regions had the second highest rate at 109.90 per 100,000 population (94.49 ~ 127.10), following low SDI regions which had a rate of 127.60 per 100,000 population (105.60 ~ 152.42) (Table 1).

Regional trends

Most regions experienced a reduction in age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer among seniors at various rate from 1990 to 2021 (Fig.S6). However, a large increase in these two rates was found in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (AAPC of ASIR = 1.37, AAPC of ASMR = 1.23) (Fig.S7). The most rapid decreases in age-standardized incidence and mortality rate for older with cervical cancer was observed in Australasia (AAPC of ASIR=−2.82, AAPC of ASMR=−3.15), Central Latin America (AAPC of ASIR=−1.93, AAPC of ASMR=−2.64) and Eastern Europe (AAPC of ASIR=−1.94, AAPC of ASMR=−2.35) (Fig.S7). As for prevalence, 7 regions out of 21 including East Asia, Southern Sub-Saharan Africa, Andean Latin America, Southeast Asia, Western Sub-Saharan Africa, Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Caribbean and Southern Latin America showed an increase in prevalence of cervical cancer among seniors, while Central Europe did not show any significant changes overall (Table S4-6, Fig.S7).

National trends

At the national level, from 1990 to 2021, China had the most cases in incidence, prevalence and mortality of cervical cancer among older people. Lesotho had the highest increase in the age-standardized incidence rate, with AAPC of 2.35 (2.27 ~ 2.42), followed by Egypt and Italy. For prevalence rate, Italy showed the highest increase, with an AAPC of 2.76 (2.41 ~ 3.09), followed by Egypt and Lesotho. As for mortality, the country with the greatest increase in age-standardized mortality rate for older people with cervical cancer was Lesotho (AAPC = 2.38, 2.28 ~ 2.47), Egypt (AAPC = 1.75, 1.58 ~ 1.89) and South Africa (AAPC = 1.72, 1.54 ~ 1.88) (Fig. 4). The countries with the highest age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rate per 100,000 population in 2021 were Kiribati (ASIR = 183.73, 115.04 ~ 272.48), Palau (ASPR = 389.44, 232.53 ~ 613.09), Lesotho (ASMR = 167.06, 94.74 ~ 262.42), respectively (Table S7-9, Fig.S8).

Predictions

Without any interventions, BAPC prediction models indicated an overall decrease in the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates. However, the absolute numbers of incident cases and deaths were projected to increase from 2021 to 2035, with an estimated rise of 79.0 thousand new cases and 36.4 thousand additional deaths, respectively. In terms of prevalence, an overall increase in the prevalence rate of cervical cancer from 2021 to 2035. The global age-standardized prevalence rate per 100,000 population is predicted to rise from 94.02 (84.09 ~ 103.69) in 2021 to 103.85 (83.33 ~ 124.37) in 2035 (Fig. 5, Table S10).

Discussion

This study provided a comprehensive analysis of the cervical cancer burden among elderly women at global, regional, and national levels, highlighting an increasing burden and regional disparities. These findings emphasize the urgent need for enhanced focus on healthcare for the elderly and addressing inequalities in medical resource distribution.

We found that over the past three decades, the age-standardized prevalence rate caused by cervical cancer among women over 60 years increased gradually, while the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates showed a downward trend. It is worth noting that the number of cervical cancer cases among the elderly is increasing relative to the total population, and the proportion of deaths is significantly higher. The observed epidemiological shifts are driven by two contrasting forces: while improved and standardized care has led to better survival and thus higher prevalence, accumulated historical risk, limited screening in older age groups, and age-related barriers to diagnosis and treatment have contributed to a disproportionate burden of cervical cancer among elderly women.

The rising number of cases may be partly attributable to the increased life expectancy of elderly women. In 2023, the global life expectancy for female was 75.9 years. 58 of 261 countries and regions had a life expectancies of 80 years or more for both sexes [21]. Another underlying drivers of the higher cervical cancer burden in elderly women is a cohort effect stemming from the Sexual Revolution, as women who came of sexual age during the 1960–1970 s carry a historically elevated cumulative HPV risk that now manifests in older age cohorts [22]. Consequently, the disease burden and quality-of-life of women aged 60 and above warrant heightened attention, given their ongoing social and familial roles.

This study revealed that the age groups with the largest increases in cervical cancer mortality between 1990 and 2021 were women aged 55–59, 65–69, and 70–74 years. Consistent with these findings, previous studies reported that approximately one-quarter of cases were diagnosed in women over 60, and nearly half of all deaths occurred within this age group [23]. Survival outcomes in older women are generally poorer compared with younger patients, largely because diagnosis tends to occur at more advanced stages. Stage I cervical cancer is associated with an excellent prognosis, with stage IA patients achieving nearly 100% survival and often remaining disease-free for life. Data from the California Cancer Registry demonstrated that early-stage patients aged ≥ 65 years had a 5-year relative survival of approximately 81.5%, comparable to younger cohorts, whereas late-stage disease was associated with a dramatic decline in survival to 23.2%–36.8% [24]. Similar results have also been reported in population-based data from Germany [25]. These findings highlight the critical role of early detection. As sexual activity generally declines with age, postcoital bleeding becomes less prevalent in older women [26]. Consequently, the effectiveness of screening over symptom-based approaches is demonstrated in its improvement of detection rates and reduction of disease burden.

Cervical cancer screening among elderly women faces several challenges. First, screening coverage in older populations remains limited. Compared with younger women, cervical cancer screening in elderly women receives less emphasis, partly because most national guidelines recommend regular screening only for women aged 25–65 years and the implementation of cervical cancer screening among elderly women is insufficient [5, 6]. Epidemiological studies indicate that women aged 60 and above remain at substantial risk, and implementing screening up to 70 years or older can significantly decrease late-stage diagnoses and enhance long-term survival [27–30]. Thus, expanding screening, diagnosis, and treatment efforts after age 60 is critical to ensure timely detection and management when prevention is no longer feasible. Second, elderly women often lack repeat screening or long-term follow-up [31, 32]. A considerable number of women have approached or exceeded the “stopping” age for cervical cancer screening without an adequate prior screening history. What’s more, elder women often face both physiological and psychosocial barriers to conventional cervical screening. Post-menopausal changes such as vaginal atrophy and cervical stenosis may increase discomfort during speculum exams, reducing sample adequacy and screening compliance [33]. Psychological factors—including embarrassment, distrust of the procedure, and misconceptions that risk diminishes after menopause—also contribute to low participation, especially among underserved and ethnic minority groups [34, 35]. Health education and alternative approaches are needed. For instance, studies have shown that self-sampling for HPV testing is highly acceptable among older women and performs comparably to clinician-collected samples, particularly in detecting high-risk HPV types [36, 37].

Our study found that the age-standardized prevalence of cervical cancer among women over 60 has gradually increased, while age-standardized incidence and mortality rates have declined. This pattern suggests that, despite the substantial disease burden in elderly women, overall survival and prognosis have improved, reflecting significant advancements in medical care and standardized treatment over the past three decades. Nevertheless, screening and treatment strategies for older women remain crucial. Studies have consistently shown that elderly patients who receive appropriate treatment achieve significantly better outcomes than those who do not, regardless of FIGO stage [38–40]. Elderly patients often exhibit lower treatment proactivity [23, 38, 41] and multiple comorbidities, which can reduce tolerance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery, potentially compromising treatment efficacy compared with younger patients [42].

In our study, significant variations in mortality rates of cervical cancer among elderly patients were observed across countries, ranging from less than 4 to more than 167 per 100,000 women aged over 60 years. In 2021, the incidence of cervical cancer in low SDI regions was more than three times higher, and the mortality rate was over five times greater compared to high SDI regions. Cost-effective measures for cervical cancer prevention and treatment already exist, but these measures remain underutilized in low SDI regions with high disease burdens. The scarcity of medical resources and the limited awareness of cervical cancer among elderly patients are the key factors contributing to this disparity [43].

WHO country capacity survey conducted in 2019 reported that less than 40% of low-income countries had established national cervical cancer screening programs, in contrast to over 80% of high-income countries. About 30% of low-income countries provide pathology services, cancer surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy within the public sector, in contrast to over 90% in high-income countries [44]. The absence of quality control in cervical cancer screening, inadequate contextualization of screening guidelines to reflect local health systems and resources, delays in referrals, and lack of high-quality treatment for advanced cervical cancer are major contributing factors to the highest disease burden in low SDI regions [45]. Sub-Saharan Africa, characterized by a high proportion of low-SDI countries, exhibits the highest prevalence and mortality rates of cervical cancer globally [46]. Among the 21 regions examined, the southern part of Sub-Saharan Africa is the only region where incidence and mortality rates among elderly cervical cancer patients have increased. A meta-analysis of 29 studies reported overall cervical cancer screening uptake of 12.87%, with only 7.65% in southern Sub-Saharan Africa, reflecting limited awareness and negative attitudes toward screening [47]. The region also suffers from insufficient medical infrastructure and oncology personnel [48], resulting in a population where more than half of cervical cancer patients survive less than three years post-diagnosis [49]. Screening coverage alone does not appear to reduce mortality, likely due to inadequate screening practices and limited treatment capacity. WHO is developing a global target “90-70-90” to accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer [1]. However, it is rather overly optimistic to achieve this goal in low SDI regions where screening and treatment have not yet to demonstrate significant impact. Given the limited resources available in these low SDI regions, it is essential to understand the economic, social, and cultural barriers in treating cervical cancer, which can inform the development of tailored approaches. Furthermore, enhancing access to secondary and tertiary preventive interventions is a top priority to alleviate the burden on elderly patients suffering from cervical cancer. For example, the visual inspection of the cervix with acetic acid can serve as an alternative approach in resource-constrained settings, although the effectiveness of this method is highly dependent on the provider and its sensitivity is variable [1]. Cervical cancer screening necessitates a corresponding enhancement in the capacity for treating detected lesions. Otherwise, an increase in the screening coverage would be meaningless. Strategies such as streamlining referral pathways, cultivating skilled surgery, improving access to radiotherapy and chemotherapy could improve treatment outcomes [1]. Additionally, educating women about benefits of screening and treatment, reducing cancer stigmatization, giving enough social support can make a difference. Our study found that the prevalence rate in Italy is increasing at the fastest pace, whereas the incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates in Kuwait are experiencing a significant decline. Notably, Italy is ranked 19th out of 204 countries and territories with respect to the level of population aging. This demographic shift suggests that the development of strategies for managing cervical cancer in elderly women may not be adequately aligned with the rapid growth of this population segment. In contrast, Kuwait’s healthcare system, which is predominantly public, offers free or low-cost medical services to its citizens. This accessibility may enhance the elderly’s willingness to seek medical treatment [50]. And successful implementation of preventive measures and sufficient financial resources for accessing treatment may contribute to the observed decline[51, 52].

China has the highest cases of elderly patients with cervical cancer in the world, both in terms of incidence and mortality. According to GBD estimates, 53% cervical cancer deaths and 32% of diagnosed cases occur within the elderly population. China’s large population base and aging population could explain these numbers. Although there has been a decline in both incidence and mortality rates over the past three decades, the absolute number of cases remains significant. Consequently, China still faces numerous challenges in mitigating the disease burden of cervical cancer among elderly women.

Our study is the first to utilize age-specific epidemiological data to analysis cervical cancer burden focusing on the elderly women. The findings highlight the increasing disease burden within the elderly population and reveal significant disparities across various socioeconomic contexts that require further investigation. Our study also calls for an intensified focus on the elderly demographic and urges countries and regions to refine their guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer, considering their economic conditions, cultural contexts, and disease burden.

The study has several limitations. Firstly, the data were extrapolated from countries with established epidemiological records, and DisMod-MR 2.1 was utilized to estimate the epidemiological data for cervical cancer by age, year and country. Caution is advised when interpreting these results in practical contexts due to potential discrepancies between modelled and actual scenarios. Secondly, while rigorous statistical techniques were utilized, the reliability of data sources varies, especially in low SDI countries and in regions affected by conflict. This variability may introduce biases and inaccuracies into the results, potentially impacting the interpretation of trends and policy formulation in these areas. Thirdly, the study predominantly relies on modelling processes. The selection of models and parameter settings may have impacted the outcomes. Finally, some recent factors, such as COVID-19–related changes in screening, regional conflicts, and fertility shifts, were not included in the model; however, given the 30-year age span, these short-term influences are expected to have minimal impact on the overall trends projected to 2035. As a result, the prediction results may be subject to bias. Therefore, there is a pressing need for high-quality empirical research in the future.

Conclusions

This study is the first to analyze the disease burden of cervical cancer among elderly women by using age-specific epidemiological data from the GBD 2021. The growing burden of cervical cancer among elderly women, particularly in terms of mortality, requires urgent attention. Public health strategies to combat cervical cancer should focus on emphasizing screening for elderly women, expanding screening age ranges, increasing clinical research enrollment of elderly women, exploring effective treatments, and optimizing medical resource allocation. Specifically, in low SDI settings, it is critical to strengthen health systems, enhance community awareness, implement cost-effective screening and treatment methodologies, and develop resource-appropriate management guidelines, in order to alleviate the burden of cervical cancer.
