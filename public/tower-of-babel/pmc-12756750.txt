KOH-Promoted Catalyst Derived from Coffee Husk and Eggshell Composite for Biodiesel Production from Waste Frying Oil
Authors: Seid Mohammed Seid, Girma Gonfa, Sintayehu Mekuria Hailegiorgis, Demelash Tilahun Bekele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, a strongly active and sustainable heterogeneous catalyst for producing biodiesel from waste frying oil (WFO) was successfully developed. The catalyst’s synthesis involved calcining a composite of potassium hydroxide (KOH), eggshell powder (ES), and coffee husk char (CHC) at a remarkably low temperature of 700 °C, which is significantly below the typical 900–1000 °C required for the direct calcination of bare eggshells. This lower calcination temperature was crucial for forming the effective active phases (CaO and K2O) and enhancing the catalyst’s basicity. The catalyst calcined at 700 °C significantly improved the biodiesel yield to 95.33 wt %, a major increase from the 45.87% yield at 600 °C. However, increasing the calcination temperature to 800 °C resulted in a slightly lower biodiesel yield of 93 wt %. Through further optimization using central composite design (CCD) method, an overall optimal yield of 97.5% was achieved, demonstrating a promising pathway for efficiently converting waste resources into valuable biofuel. The work highlights the use of abundantly available waste materials as catalyst precursors, which not only lowers overall production expenses but also reduces the environmental footprint of the process. While the catalyst showed a progressive decrease in activity across three cycles (85, 71, and 52%), these findings underscore the need for further research into improving its durability for industrial application. In summary, this research marks a key advance toward developing a more economical and sustainable method for producing biodiesel.

Introduction

Energy has always been and remains fundamental to human history, driving economic advancement and representing national strength in the modern era. Therefore, ensuring energy access and promoting sustainable energy are key priorities in alignment with the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals. Achieving this goal requires replacing petroleum fuels with renewable and eco-friendly alternatives like biodiesel. Biodiesel is a mixture of fatty acid monoalkyl esters with varying carbon chain lengths and degrees of unsaturation. Its benefits include low sulfur, a high flashpoint, efficient combustion, and biodegradability. Biodiesel can be produced from domestic feedstock and mitigates greenhouse gas emissions. Despite its disadvantages, such as a lower energy density than fossil fuels and the emission of nitrogen oxides during combustion, the main obstacle to biodiesel’s large-scale use is its higher production cost compared to fossil diesel.

The economic viability of biodiesel production is significantly impacted by feedstock costs, which currently account for over 70% of the total expenditure. While transesterification remains the prevalent method for converting these feedstocks, the dominant industrial practice (exceeding 95%) relies on edible oils such as soybean, rapeseed, sunflower, and palm. However, the utilization of these resources raises concerns regarding food security and economic feasibility, rendering this approach increasingly unsustainable. Consequently, contemporary research efforts are predominantly directed toward exploring nonedible alternatives, including nonedible vegetable oils, animal fats, and algal oils, with waste frying oil (WFO) presenting a particularly advantageous option due to its low cost and status as a waste stream, thereby mitigating both feedstock expenses and environmental disposal issues.

While addressing the challenge of feedstock is a major step, the transformation of these nonedible oils into biodiesel faces another significant obstacle, namely the need for efficient and cost-effective catalysts. A variety of catalysts were investigated for the synthesis of biodiesel, including alkali, acid, bifunctional, and enzyme catalysts. The catalysts may be generally classified as homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts. The employment of homogeneous catalysts in biodiesel manufacture elevates costs due to the need for extensive product purification to meet quality standards. Furthermore, these catalysts are corrosive to reactors and cannot be recovered for reuse. While researchers have also explored enzymes for biodiesel synthesis due to their efficiency and nonpolluting nature, enzymatic catalysts have been found to be inhibited by alcohol, costly, and require extended transesterification times for maximum conversion. Consequently, current research increasingly focuses on heterogeneous catalysts, as they offer the advantages of high reusability and simple separation from the final reaction mixture. These properties of heterogeneous catalysts contribute to lowering biodiesel production costs.

Despite the advantages of reusability and easy separation offered by heterogeneous catalysts, issues such as high cost, low stability, low activity, small pore size, and limited surface area still persist. Therefore, much of the current research on heterogeneous catalyst synthesis for biodiesel production aims to address one or more of these limitations, with solutions including the selection of suitable catalyst precursors. , These precursors can include biomasses, chemicals, or a combination thereof. , While chemical precursors can yield highly active catalysts, their synthesis processes are often costly and environmentally hostile. Consequently, the utilization of biomasses has become a prevalent approach in numerous catalyst synthesis investigations, encompassing lignocellulosic biomasses (LBs), animal bones, teeth, and shales, and eggshells. Due to their renewable origin and ability to reduce net CO2 emissions in catalyst synthesis, LBs are becoming more popular as precursors. Furthermore, utilizing LBs can also reduce the overall cost of the resulting catalyst.

Given the significantly higher reaction rates they exhibit compared to acidic and bifunctional catalysts, alkali catalysts have garnered considerable attention for transesterification reactions. Researchers have successfully developed highly active alkali catalysts from various LB sources, including the ashes of passion fruit peel, palm bunch, Musa paradisiaca plant parts, walnut shell, orange peel, and acai seed. These ash catalysts, often rich in compounds like K2CO3, K2Ca­(CO3), CaCO3, and KCl, can be obtained through LB calcination processes. A crucial factor in this process is that the decomposition temperature of these compounds in a mixture differs from their individual values. For example, CaCO3 decomposes at a lower temperature when mixed with K2CO3, and the decomposition temperature of K2CO3 into K2O is also lowered in the presence of carbon (a product of LB decomposition). . By leveraging this phenomenon, highly active metal oxide-containing catalysts can be produced more efficiently and economically.

Although LB ashes are appropriate for this purpose due to their mixed compound content, their low ash (catalyst) yield compared to CaCO3 rich biomasses like eggshells presents a challenge. , For example, the ash content of pineapple leaves was 6 and 2.6% for cupuacu seeds. This is very small compared to the ash content (45.29%) of biomasses such as eggshells. Thus, blending alkali-rich (primarily K) LB ash with CaCO3-rich biomasses ash presents a promising route to enhance catalyst yield. Achieving active CaO from bare eggshells requires high calcination temperatures (800–1000 °C), but a blending approach can produce active mixed oxides at more moderate temperatures. ,, This is a notable advantage, as mixed oxides often demonstrate enhanced catalytic performance compared to single-oxide catalysts. Additionally, a synergy between Ca and K has been reported to enhance feedstock transesterification. The activity of ash catalysts derived from this blended biomass can also be further enhanced through chemical modification. Ultimately, by combining the complementary properties of K-rich LB and eggshell biomasses with chemical modification, we can potentially develop highly efficient and sustainable catalysts for biodiesel manufacturing.

The abundance of coffee husk in Ethiopia, along with the high concentration of K compounds in its ash, presents a promising opportunity for developing an alkali catalyst. This research specifically focused on developing a novel, highly active heterogeneous alkali catalyst by creating a composite from coffee husk and eggshell, and then promoting it with KOH. The primary aim was to assess the overall effectiveness of this novel catalyst as a sustainable solution for producing biodiesel from readily available WFO.

Materials and Methods

Materials

In this research, waste frying oil was sourced from local potato chip sellers. Coffee husks were obtained from coffee processing plants situated in the Jimma region of Oromia, Ethiopia. Eggshells were collected from Addis Ababa Science and Technology University (AASTU) cafeterias. All chemical reagents, including acetic acid, cyclohexane (99%), sodium hydroxide (97%), benzoic acid (≥99.5%), methanol (≥98%), phenolphthalein (≥99%), ethanol (≥98%), potassium iodide, iodine chloride, sodium thiosulfate (Na2SO4), toluene (99.8%), potassium hydroxide (85%), hydrochloric acid (HCl), and starch indicator, were procured from local sellers.

Preparation and Characterization of Waste Frying Oil

To prepare the waste frying oil (WFO), it was first heated and passed through a filter to eliminate any solid particles and contaminants. The filtered oil then underwent further heating at 90 °C for 1 h to eradicate moisture. Finally, the treated WFO was transferred to a clean, sealed container for later use. The saponification and acid values of this treated oil were determined following the standard procedures outlined in AOCS 920.160 and ISO 660:2020, respectively. The peroxide value of the WFO was determined using AOCS Official Method Cd 8b-90.

Preparation and Characterization of Coffee Husk

Raw CH was prepared by first washing it with tap water. It was then air-dried in the sun for 10 days, followed by oven-drying at 105 °C for 3 h. The dried CH was then ground into a fine powder (<250 μm) using a coffee grinder (NIMA NM-124, China) for subsequent use and characterization. Using the test method from ASTM standard E1756-08, the moisture content of the CH powder was determined. Likewise, the ash and volatile matter content were determined using ASTM standards E1755-01 and E872-82, respectively.

Catalyst Synthesis and Synthesis Condition Optimization

The synthesis of the KOH modified coffee husk-eggshell ash based composite catalyst (K-CHC-ES)­A started with CH powder calcination for 2 h at 600 °C, resulting in the formation of coffee husk char. The char was subsequently comminuted using a mortar and pestle to obtain fine coffee husk char (CHC). In parallel, eggshells were washed, membrane-removed, dried, and ground to <250 μm powder (ES). By incipient wet impregnation of KOH solution (0.3 molar) into a CHC-ES (CHC to ES weight ratio of 4:1) for 1 h a catalyst composite material (CCMC) was prepared. The KOH solution concentration and the CHC to ES weight ratio were determined preliminary screening experiments. To determine the most suitable calcination temperature, the CCMC was then calcined for 3 h at 600 °C, 700 °C, and 800 °C based on thermogravimetric analysis, and previous studies including ours and. , The subsequent ash catalysts were labeled (K-CHC-ES)­A-600, (K-CHC-ES)­A-700, and (K-CHC-ES)­A-800, where “A” denotes the ash nature of the final catalyst and the number indicates the calcination temperature.

With the optimal calcination temperature established, a Central Composite Design (CCD) experimental approach was used for further optimization of the (K-CHC-ES)­A catalyst synthesis (Table ). The CCD was selected because it includes axial points (star points), which allow the experimental space to be explored beyond the factor limits. The values of CHC/ES (4:1), KOH solution concentration (0.3 M), and calcination time of 3 h that were used during the optimal calcination temperature were taken as center values. This design systematically investigated the impact of calcination time (ranging from 2 to 4 h), CHC/ES weight ratio (from 2:1 to 6:1), and KOH solution concentration (from 0.2 to 0.4 M) on achieving the highest possible biodiesel yield. The KOH solution was added at a 1:1 volume-to-mass ratio relative to the CHC-ES composite during the catalyst composite material (CCM) preparation.

Catalyst Materials and Catalysts Characterization

To guide the selection of CCMC calcination temperatures, thermal analysis (thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA)) was carried out with a Shimadzu DTG 60H (Japan). This analysis was conducted under a limited air environment (50 mL/min), with the temperature ramped at 10 °C/min from 25 to 1000 °C. The crystalline phases within the catalysts were identified via X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Shimadzu XRD-7000 (Japan) instrument. The analysis was conducted with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) at 30 mA and 40 kV. Diffraction patterns were recorded within a 2θ value of 10° to 80° with a scan speed of 3°/min and a step size of 0.02°. Crystalline phases were identified by comparing observed peak 2θ values with literature data. The specific surface area of the catalysts was determined via the Brunauer–Emmet–Teller (BET) method using a surface area analyzer (Horiba Instruments, Inc. SA-9600 series, USA). Prior to the surface area examination, catalyst samples were dried at 110 °C overnight after being ground with a mortar and pestle. Furthermore, degassing of the catalysts was made at 150 °C for 3 h using the analyzer’s degassing procedure. Measurements for nitrogen adsorption isotherms were performed at a temperature of 77 K. Using data points at relative pressures of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 from the linear portion of the isotherm, the BET surface area was calculated. Using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (JCM-6000Plus, German) with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, the surface morphology of the catalysts was visualized. A Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) (Thermo Scientific Nicolet, IiS50 ABX smartiTX) was used to identify functional groups within the catalysts and intermediate products. The instrument, which featured an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) diamond crystal and a DTGS KBr detector, collected spectra from 400 to 4000 cm–1 at a resolution of 2 cm–1 and with 32 scans. In line with the procedure described by Al-Hamamre et al., the catalysts total basicity of the was measured via the Hammett indicator method with phenolphthalein. A suspension of 0.15 g of catalyst in 2 mL of a 0.1 mg/mL phenolphthalein/toluene solution was prepared and stirred for 30 min. The resulting mixture was then titrated using a 0.01 M solution of benzoic acid in toluene until the color turned from pink to colorless, indicating the end point.

Transesterification and Post-Treatment

An activity test was conducted for each catalyst by performing the transesterification of crude WFO, following procedures established in previous studies. , The transesterification procedures were carried out in a reflux setup consisting of a 250 mL round-bottom flask reactor and a tap water-cooled condenser. First, a 5 wt % catalyst was stirred with methanol for 30 min at 450 rpm while the reaction temperature was maintained at 60 °C. After this methoxide formation period, WFO (preheated to the same reaction temperature) was introduced into the mixture, and the stirring speed was increased to 600 rpm. The transesterification reaction proceeded for 2 h with an alcohol-to-WFO molar ratio of 12:1. Upon completion, the mixture was cooled and then centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 15 min. The resulting separated phase was allowed to age overnight to facilitate further glycerol separation. The upper layer was then washed with hot water to remove remaining impurities, following a procedure of four cycles. Each cycle was 20 min long, at 45 °C, and used a 2:1 volume ratio of distilled water to biodiesel. To remove the residual water, the biodiesel was placed in a static air oven and dried for 24 h at 110 °C. Biodiesel (WFOME) Characterization

The essential fuel properties of the synthesized WFOME were comprehensively analyzed to ensure compliance with international standards. Density, kinematic viscosity, and flash point were measured using standard methods ASTM D1298, ASTM D445, and ASTM D93, respectively, and the results were compared to required ASTM and EN specifications. Additionally, the acid value of the WFOME, a critical indicator of product purity, was determined using a standard titration method.

Catalyst Reusability Test

After the transesterification reaction, the spent catalyst was recovered for reusability assessment by first washing it with methanol and then with acetone. A regeneration step was included, where the recovered catalyst was recalcined at 700 °C for 1 h. The reusability of the catalyst was then evaluated under specific reaction conditions: a 12:1 molar ratio of methanol to WFO, a temperature of 60 °C, a 2 h reaction time, and a catalyst loading of 5 wt %.

Results and Discussion

Waste Frying Oil Characterization

The WFO’s acid value was found to be 2.08 mg KOH/g (representing 1.5% FFA), which indicates a significant concentration of free fatty acids (FFAs). For effective biodiesel production with a base catalyst, the recommended acid value is ideally below 0.5 mg KOH/g. Elevated FFA value hinders the transesterification process by deactivating the catalyst due to soap formation, necessitating a two-step approach for optimal biodiesel production. These approach increase the overall cost of producing biodiesel, making it more difficult to achieve large-scale production. Despite these challenges, our preliminary experiments utilizing the catalyst we developed have shown that single-step transesterification can be successfully achieved. The saponification value for the WFO was determined to be 202 mg KOH/g oil. The average molecular weight of waste frying oil (WFO) was calculated to be 842 g/mol based on its acid and saponification values. High molecular weight suggests the presence of longer-chain fatty acids, which can affect the viscosity and fuel properties of the resulting biodiesel. With a peroxide value of 9.6 mequiv O2/kg, the oil demonstrated moderate oxidation stability, which could, in turn, influence the quality and shelf life of the biodiesel produced.

Ash content is a crucial parameter to consider when synthesizing catalysts from the ash of lignocellulosic biomass. This low ash content indicates that the yield of catalysts derived from these lignocellulosic biomasses will be very low. This limitation is overcome by integrating coffee husk (CH) with eggshell (ES), since the ash content of the eggshell is relatively very high.

Eggshell Powder and KOH-Coffee Husk Char-Eggshell Composite Thermal Analysis

Combined thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) presented in Figure a,b, respectively, elucidated the thermal decomposition of eggshell (ES) and its composite with coffee husk char impregnated with potassium hydroxide (CHC-ES/KOH), providing crucial insights for catalyst synthesis. The TGA curve for pure ES (Figure a, labeled “ES”) exhibited a characteristic multistage weight loss profile: initial moisture release evident below 100 °C, followed by organic matter decomposition between 100 and 600 °C, and a significant weight loss event between 600 and 800 °C mainly corresponding to CaCO3 decomposition yielding CaO (which is catalytically highly active metal oxide). The corresponding eggshell DTA (Figure b, labeled “ES”) showed endothermic peaks associated with moisture loss and CaCO3 decarbonation, as well as an exothermic peak indicative of organic combustion.

Thermogravimetric analysis (a) and differential thermal analysis (b) for ES and CHC-ES/KOH.

The CHC-ES composite with KOH (Figure a,b, labeled “CHC-ES/KOH”) displayed significant differences compared to pure ES. Similar initial weight loss was observed in the TGA curve (Figure a), but a more pronounced weight loss occurred between 200 and 600 °C. This is attributed to CHC combustion and potentially the decomposition/reaction of KOH and its intermediates with the char. The limited air atmosphere would influence the combustion products. The CHC-ES/KOH DTA (Figure b) exhibited a strong exothermic peak between 250 and 550 °C, clearly indicating CHC oxidation, which is likely enhanced by the presence of both the inherent potassium within the CHC and the impregnated KOH, potentially acting synergistically as combustion catalysts.

A key observation, visually supported by the TGA curves in Figure a, was the influence of CHC and KOH on CaCO3 stability. The TGA of CHC-ES/KOH indicated that CaCO3 decomposition commenced and concluded at noticeably lower temperatures than in pure ES. The DTA curves in Figure b also showed a lower peak temperature for CaCO3 decarbonation in the composite. This reduction likely arises from enhanced heat transfer facilitated by the conductive char, a modified gaseous environment resulting from char and KOH decomposition products (e.g., CO2, H2O), and the known catalytic effect of alkali metals (like potassium from KOH) on CaCO3 decomposition. Further CaCO3 decomposition at lower temperatures is evidenced by the shifted DTA peak.

The distinct thermal behaviors of ES and the CHC-ES/KOH composite, as clearly illustrated in Figure a,b, offer significant advantages for tailored catalyst synthesis, particularly for alkali metal oxide applications. Sequential decomposition allows controlled porosity, and in situ potassium species formation. The enhanced reduction in CaCO3 decomposition temperature enables energy-efficient processing and influences catalyst texture/phases. Exothermic CHC combustion, possibly KOH-catalyzed as suggested by the prominent DTA peak, can aid energy balance. This highlights KOH-impregnated eggshell-coffee husk char as a versatile, cost-effective composite for catalysts with tunable structure, chemistry, and active potassium species.

Impact of CHC-ES/KOH Composite Calcination Temperature on Catalyst Properties

Building upon our thermal analysis of the CHC-ES/KOH composite (CCMC) (Figure ), which revealed significant decomposition and phase changes between 600 and 800 °C, this section focuses on comparing the properties of the resulting ash catalysts synthesized at different calcination temperatures within this range ((K-CHC-ES)­A-600, (K-CHC-ES)­A-700, and (K-CHC-ES)­A-800). These catalysts were systematically evaluated using FTIR, XRD, and SEM to understand how the varying calcination temperatures influence their properties, including BET surface area, basicity and ultimately, catalytic activity.

FTIR spectroscopy was used (Figure ) to identify the surface functional groups present in eggshell (ES), the catalyst (CHC-ES)­A (without KOH impregnation), and the KOH-modified catalyst (K-CHC-ES)­A. The FTIR spectrum of the uncalcined eggshell (ES) exhibits prominent bands at 1411 cm–1 and 874 cm–1, and 711 cm–1 which are characteristic stretching, out of plane bending, and inplane bending vibrations of the carbonate (CO3 2–) group, respectively. Upon calcination, the single carbonate peak observed at 1411 cm–1 in uncalcined eggshell (ES) split into two distinct peaks (approximately 1447 and 1368 cm–1) across all catalysts. This likely signifies a less symmetrical arrangement of the carbonate ions, possibly due to the incorporation of potassium (from CHC and KOH) into the ES structure. This potentially indicates the incorporation other metallic carbonates and/or formation of mixed metal carbonates (mainly K2Ca­(CO3)2). The presence of other metallic carbonates (mainly K2CO3) in the catalysts is indicated by the C–O bending frequency observed around 1118–1126 cm–1. This peak may also belong to the asymmetric (ν3) stretching vibrations of the phosphate (PO4 3–) group in carbonated hydroxyapatite (CHAp). The CHAp presence also revealed by the phosphate group symmetric (ν1) stretching vibration in the range of (1028 cm–1 to 1041 cm–1). The decreasing CO3 2– group vibration peaks in the catalysts’ FTIR spectra, intensifying with higher calcination temperatures, clearly shows the alteration of CaCO3 from the original ES and the significant impact of calcination temperature on the catalyst’s structure. Higher calcination also likely promote the formation of highly alkaline K2O (indicated by the K–O peak around 704 cm–1 ) and CaO (peak around 430 cm–1 ). The vibrations observed around 474, 622, and 704 cm–1 might also be due to metal–oxygen bond in carbonates or the phosphate groups. , In conclusion, modifying eggshell with KOH and CHC, especially at higher calcination temperatures, transforms calcium carbonate into potentially mixed metal carbonates and alkaline oxides like K2O and CaO. Higher temperatures intensify this shift, and forming more alkaline sites, vital for catalysis. However, determining the specific crystalline structures formed at different calcination temperatures necessitates further investigation using X-ray diffraction (XRD).

FTIR of the uncalcined eggshell powder (ES) and KOH and CHC modified eggshell ash catalysts.

XRD analysis (Figure ) revealed a clear evolution of crystalline phases in the (K-CHC-ES)­A catalysts as a function of calcination temperature (600 °C, 700 °C, and 800 °C), aligning with the thermal decomposition behavior observed in the TGA/DTA (Figure a,b). At 600 °C, the predominant phase was fairchildite (K2Ca­(CO3)2), with a lower presence of CaO and K2O. As the temperature increased to 700 °C, a significant shift occurred toward the formation of dominant CaO and increased K2O phases, accompanied by fairchildite and other carbonates decomposition. However, the lack of substantial changes in the XRD patterns, particularly for the key active phases (CaO and K2O), between 700 and 800 °C suggests that further increasing the calcination temperature beyond 700 °C may not significantly enhance the catalyst’s crystalline structure and composition. This implies that 700 °C might represent a near-optimal calcination temperature for achieving a well-developed crystalline structure of the active phases.

XRD crystallography for (K-CHC-ES)­A catalyst.

The initial formation of significant amounts of fairchildite at 600 °C emphasizes its role as an intermediate. While higher temperatures are needed to drive its decomposition into the catalytically important CaO and K2O, the minimal structural evolution observed beyond 700 °C indicates that the benefits of higher temperatures might be outweighed by potential energy costs or textural changes not evident in XRD alone. Therefore, considering the crystalline phase development, a calcination temperature of 700 °C appears to be sufficient for achieving the desired active phases with good crystallinity.

The XRD analysis revealed the presence of various alkali active sites in the catalyst, including K2CO3, K2Ca­(CO3)2, K2CO3·1H2O, Ca­(CO3)2, K2O, and CaO, and their composition is directly influenced by the calcination temperature regimes identified in the thermal analysis. An XRD peak observed at 32.23° also suggested the potential presence of CHAp (Ca10 (PO4)6(OH) (CO3)). The relative amounts and structural order of these phases are critical factors determining the overall catalytic performance of the resulting material. The insignificant change in crystalline phase development beyond 700 °C suggests that other factors, such as morphology, surface area, or pore structure, which might be affected differently at 800 °C, could play a more significant role in determining the ultimate catalytic activity.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis was conducted to further explore the impact of calcination temperature on the catalyst’s physical characteristics, especially its morphology. The SEM image of catalysts at different calcination temperatures is presented in Figure . Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed distinct morphological features for the (K-CHC-ES)­A catalysts prepared at different calcination temperatures. At 600 °C, the catalyst exhibited a heterogeneous structure with aggregated particles and an irregular surface. Increasing the temperature to 700 °C resulted in a more uniform particle distribution with better-defined, sometimes layered, structures and a rougher surface. The highest calcination temperature of 800 °C led to the formation of interconnected, porous structures, suggesting some degree of particle fusion. , These observed changes in agglomeration, particle shape, and surface morphology with increasing calcination temperature likely correlate with the phase transformations and crystallinity development identified by XRD, and could significantly influence the catalyst’s surface area and accessibility of active sites.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of (a) (K-CHC-EA)-600, (b) (K-CHC-EA)-700, and (c) (K-CHC-EA)-800.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis was performed to quantify the surface area of the catalysts prepared at different calcination temperatures (Table ). For the (K-CHC-ES)­A catalysts, the BET analysis revealed a clear trend of increasing surface area with higher calcination temperatures, directly aligning with the morphological changes observed in the Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis (Figure ). Specifically, the catalyst prepared at 600 °C exhibited a relatively low surface area of 1.076 m2/g, corresponding to its heterogeneous structure with aggregated particles and an irregular surface. Increasing the calcination temperature to 700 °C resulted in a modest increase in surface area to 1.292 m2/g, associated with a more uniform particle distribution and rougher, sometimes layered structures. Notably, the catalyst calcined at 800 °C showed the most significant increase in surface area, reaching 4.812 m2/g. This substantial impact on the material’s textural properties can be attributed to the formation of the interconnected, highly porous structures evident in the SEM image, which inherently provide a much larger surface area compared to the other samples. ,

This progressive increase in surface area with thermal treatment suggests that calcination effectively promotes the development of a more open and porous texture. This transformation is likely driven by the decomposition of remaining carbonate species (as indicated by XRD in the Figure ), the release of volatile organic matter, and the subsequent formation of the observed interconnected network. This finding underscores the critical importance of calcination temperature in tailoring the textural properties of the (K-CHC-ES)­A catalyst for enhanced performance.

This general trend of increasing surface area with calcination temperature up to an optimum is consistent with observations in other biomass ashes; for example, a study on wood ash reported an increase in surface area from 1.59 m2/g at low temperatures to 3.72 m2/g at 800 °C. However, the same study further demonstrated that exceeding this optimal temperature can lead to a reduction in surface area due to sintering, with wood ash showing decreases to 1.6 m2/g at 1000 °C and 1.33 m2/g at 1200 °C. This highlights the importance of identifying an optimal calcination temperature to maximize surface area. Furthermore, the lower surface area often observed in potassium and calcium-containing alkali catalysts can be attributed to the lower melting points characteristic of ashes rich in these elements. Indeed, studies have reported relatively low BET surface areas for similar catalysts, such as a KNO3-loaded coffee husk ash catalyst (C-25-600) at 0.773 m2/g, and a KNO3-modified coffee husk-teff straw mixed ash catalyst (C-Mix) at 0.873 m2/g (Table ).

The total basicity of the catalysts increased with calcination temperature, measuring 1.92 mmol/g for the (K-CHC-ES)­A-600 catalyst, 2.54 mmol/g for the (K-CHC-ES)­A-700, and 2.92 mmol/g for the (K-CHC-ES)­A-800. This clear trend indicates that higher calcination temperatures are crucial for enhancing the quantity and/or accessibility of basic active sites. Such an increase is likely due to several factors: the complete decomposition of carbonates and other organic matter at elevated temperatures, leading to the formation of more active metal oxides (like CaO and possibly K2O phases, as indicated by the XRD). Additionally, the development of more porous and interconnected structures, particularly evident at 800 °C from SEM analysis, presumably exposes previously inaccessible basic sites. Comparable findings and conclusions were presented by Bekele et al. ,

To evaluate the catalytic activity, as influenced by the calcination temperature-dependent structural, morphological, and textural properties of the catalyst, biodiesel production tests were conducted in the transesterification of WFO (Figure ). The biodiesel yields achieved using the (K-CHC-ES)­A catalysts were found to be strongly dependent on the calcination temperature, which significantly influenced the catalyst’s physicochemical properties. The catalyst calcined at 600 °C yielded the lowest biodiesel (45.87 ± 0.416 wt %), primarily attributed to its lower basicity, likely stemming from the predominance of the less active fairchildite phase. Its lower surface area also contributed to this reduced activity. , Increasing the calcination temperature to 700 °C resulted in a substantial improvement in biodiesel yield to 95.33 ± 0.046 wt %, correlating with the formation of dominant CaO and increased K2O active phases, along with a more uniform particle distribution and a modest increase in surface area. Further increasing the temperature to 800 °C led to a slightly lower yield of 93 ± 0.321 wt %, despite the highest surface area. This suggests that while a high surface area is beneficial, the optimal catalytic activity for biodiesel production using this material is achieved at a calcination temperature of 700 °C, where a favorable balance of active crystalline phases and sufficient surface area with good accessibility is attained. The study highlights the crucial role of controlled thermal treatment in tailoring the physicochemical properties of bioderived catalysts to maximize their efficiency in biodiesel synthesis.

The 3D response surface plots in Figure illustrate the interactive effects of catalyst synthesis parameters on biodiesel yield, with other variables held at midrange values. The CHC/ES weight ratio, KOH concentration, and calcination time similarly influenced high biodiesel yields. Further refinement of the CHC/ES ratio, calcination time, and KOH concentration is essential to maximize catalytic performance and achieve optimal biodiesel yields.

Each experimental run was performed in triplicate, with average WFO methyl ester (WFOME) yields (determined by eq ) presented in Table . WFOME yield was the chosen response for optimization. While individual experimental runs, such as number 2, showed the highest mean WFOME yield (and number 1 the lowest), the optimal parameter set was not directly taken from these discrete points. Instead, the optimal catalyst synthesis parameters were determined by a regression model (eq ), which correlates these parameters to the WFOME yield (wt %).

Specifically, as shown in Figure a, manipulating the CHC/ES ratio and calcination time demonstrates a significant impact on biodiesel yield, starting at 78.74% with a 2:1 ratio and 2 h of calcination and peaking at 98.91% with a CHC/ES ratio of approximately 5.1:1 and a calcination time around 3.57 h. This highlights that achieving a specific balance between the CHC and ES content, along with a carefully controlled calcination time, is essential for synthesizing a catalyst with optimal textural properties and active site development, as suggested by the supporting discussion on porosity and potassium species formation during the SEM image analysis.

Similarly, Figure b illustrates the combined effect of KOH concentration and calcination time, starting at a yield of 78.32% with 0.2 M KOH and 2 h of calcination and reaching an optimum yield of 98.46% at approximately 0.371 M KOH and a calcination time of 3.72 h. This underscores the critical role of KOH loading in generating a sufficient density of alkaline active sites for efficient transesterification, while also emphasizing the need for an optimized calcination time to properly activate these sites without causing detrimental structural changes.

Figure c examines the interaction between KOH concentration and CHC/ES ratio, starting at a yield of 83.63% with 0.2 M KOH and a 2:1 CHC/ES ratio and achieving a high yield of 98.83% at approximately 0.371 M KOH and a CHC/ES ratio of around 5.4:1. This further emphasizes that the amount of KOH used for impregnation is intricately linked to the initial composite ratio, requiring a specific balance to ensure optimal dispersion and interaction of the active components, ultimately leading to maximized catalytic activity.

The significant curvature observed in all surface plots confirms a strong interaction among the CHC/ES ratio, KOH concentration, and calcination time; achieving high biodiesel yields (consistently in the high 90% range) critically depends on finding the precise, optimized combination of these parameters, as deviations from these ideal ranges will impair catalytic performance by negatively impacting the catalyst’s structure, composition, and textural properties, thereby necessitating a systematic optimization approach. This intricate interplay underscores the complexity of designing highly efficient catalysts. Consequently, fine-tuning each parameter is not an isolated task but part of a holistic optimization strategy. Such targeted adjustments are key to unlocking the full potential of the catalyst for maximum biodiesel production.

Catalytic Reaction Mechanism

The proposed transesterification reaction mechanism is presented in Figure . The catalyst’s active sites are the K+σ and Ca2+σ cations, along with oxygen anions, primarily originating from CaO and K2O phases, as well as surface carbonate species. The mechanism is initiated by these basic oxygen sites exploiting the polarity of the O–H bond in methanol (CH3OH). Due to the high electronegativity of oxygen, the O–H bond is polarized, giving the hydrogen a partial positive charge (+σ) and the oxygen a partial negative charge (−σ). The strong basic catalyst sites abstract this +σ hydrogen in Catalyst Activation (Step 1), generating a highly nucleophilic methoxide ion (CH3O–). This methoxide then performs a Nucleophilic Attack (Step 2) on the carbonyl carbon of the triglyceride (WFO), forming an unstable, short-lived tetrahedral intermediate. In Intermediate Breakdown and Regeneration (Step 3), this intermediate collapses, expelling a diglyceride anion and forming the first molecule of Fatty Acid Methyl Ester (FAME) (biodiesel). The diglyceride anion subsequently picks up a proton from the catalyst to form the diglyceride product, thereby regenerating the original basic catalyst site for the next cycle (diglyceride to monoglyceride, and monoglyceride to glycerol). ,

Qualitative Analysis of the Biodiesel Using FTIR

Figure presents the FTIR spectra of WFO and WFO biodiesel (BD) produced using the C-Optimal catalyst assisted transesterifiction. The presence of strong characteristic peaks observed within the 2922–2853 cm–1 range are attributed to the C–H stretching of the methyl group. The BD showed a CH3 asymmetric bending peak at 1433 cm–1, indicating the presence of (CO)–O–CH3. Additionally, a peak between 1180 and 1220 cm–1 was observed, attributed to −O–CH3 stretching within the biodiesel’s structure. Notably, both of these peaks were absent in the WFO FTIR spectrum, highlighting their formation during the transesterification process. The presence of −C–O ester groups is further supported by a peak at 1169 cm–1. The peak at 721 cm–1 for both spectra corresponds to the rocking of the CH2 group. These findings are consistent with previous studies on biodiesel production from waste cooking oil (WFO) using KNO3-loaded CH ash catalyst and from Argemone seed oil using CaO catalysts.

Catalyst Reusability Test

The reusability of the optimized catalyst was assessed over multiple cycles, with the results presented in Figure . The fresh catalyst exhibited an optimal biodiesel yield of 97.5 wt %. Upon the first reuse cycle, the yield decreased to 85 wt %, indicating an initial reduction in activity. The decline in performance continued in subsequent cycles, with the second cycle yielding 71 wt % and the third cycle showing a further significant drop to 52 wt %. This progressive deactivation across consecutive cycles, particularly the notable decline from the first to the third, highlights a critical limitation in the catalyst’s long-term reusability. Potential factors contributing to this rapid deactivation include surface fouling by impurities from the waste frying oil, loss of active sites due to leaching of active components (e.g., K or Ca species), or structural degradation of the composite material. Addressing this reusability challenge is crucial for ensuring the economic viability and sustainability of the biodiesel production process, thereby necessitating further investigation into more robust catalyst designs or efficient regeneration methods.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed and characterized a highly active heterogeneous alkali catalyst derived from KOH-promoted coffee husk and eggshell composites for biodiesel production from waste frying oil. The comprehensive characterization elucidated the optimal catalyst synthesis conditions. It was found that a calcination temperature of 700 °C effectively promotes the formation of key active phases like CaO and K2O, while also enhancing the catalyst’s basicity, which is crucial for catalytic activity. The research achieved an optimum biodiesel yield of 97.5 ± 0.52%. However, the catalyst’s reusability was significantly limited, with biodiesel yield dropping from 71 to 52% by the second round. This necessitates further research into more robust catalyst designs or efficient regeneration for practical application. The research underscores the synergistic effect of K and Ca components from the coffee husk and eggshell, further boosted by KOH promotion, offering a promising solution for converting low-cost waste frying oil into biodiesel through a single-step transesterification process.
