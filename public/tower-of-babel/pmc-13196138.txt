Mechanisms underlying immunodynamics of layered defenses elicited by mRNA vaccination in children
Authors: Yiyu Liao, Hoong Kai Chua, Boon Kiat Law, Yuqian Wang, Franciszek Polak, Allyson N X Choi, Carina J X Tay, Hui’En Li, Lynette P Shek, Elizabeth Huiwen Tham, Youjia Zhong, Keisuke Ejima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDefining the ever-evolving correlates of protection against symptomatic infection is critical in light of the widespread deployment of mRNA vaccines in children. Since immune maturation and exposure histories differ between children and adults, immune kinetics and correlates of protection identified in adult cohorts may not directly generalize to pediatric vaccine recipients.MethodsA prospective cohort of 5–12-year-old children (N = 70) was monitored longitudinally for 12 months after SARS-CoV-2 mRNA (BNT162b2) vaccination. Four immunological biomarkers were assessed: anti-Spike immunoglobulin G (anti-S IgG), neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), Spike-specific memory B cells (S+ MBCs), and S-reactive T cell response. We utilized mathematical models to reconstruct time-varying biomarker trajectories, while accounting for multiple immunity-conferring events (i.e., primary vaccination, breakthrough infection, and booster vaccination). Using the biomarker levels as time-varying covariates in survival analyses, we evaluated the circulating correlates of protection against symptomatic breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection across three post-primary vaccination periods (30 days to 3 months, 3 to 6 months, and 6 to 12 months), as an interpretable summary of early, intermediate, and late phases. We also repeated this evaluation in a global analysis without considering specific time windows.ResultsSerological markers were best described by the exponential model, while S+ MBC and T cell responses followed the inactivation model. While early protection was associated with antibodies, multivariable analyses identified T cell responses as the primary correlate of protection from 3 months onward, and this association was strongest in the presence of hybrid immunity. Under our prespecified threshold for the T cell response, the model-projected protection duration under hybrid immunity was estimated to be approximately 500 days after primary vaccination; however, empirical validation in independent cohorts will be required. Importantly, we obtained similar conclusions on the identity and direction of the correlates of protection in several sensitivity analyses adjusting for model structure, accounting for age and sex, and including asymptomatic infections.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the time-dependent nature of correlates of protection, emphasizing the need for employing mathematical models to reinforce the accuracy and reliability of clinical analysis so as to prevent overreliance on single-timepoint measurements of immune parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04852-6.Keywords: mRNA vaccine, Children, Biomarkers, Antibodies, B cell, T cell, Mathematical modeling, Immunodynamics, Survival analysis

Background

Despite their novelty, mRNA vaccines were administered to millions of children worldwide in response to the public health emergency of the COVID-19 pandemic. They proved to be highly efficacious in protecting against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection [1–3], as well as severe complications such as multi-system inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) [4]. In addition to robust immunogenicity and efficacy, mRNA vaccines showed excellent safety profiles in pediatric populations [5, 6]. mRNA vaccine candidates are currently being trialed against a variety of other pathogens—including influenza [7], cytomegalovirus (CMV) [8], and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [9]—many of which children constitute a primary group for vaccination. The efficacy of mRNA vaccines is attributable to the robust immunogenicity in multiple compartments of the adaptive immune system: circulating antibodies, memory B cells (MBCs), as well as CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [10–12]. The T cell response, being the first response to breakthrough infections [13], is resilient against immune evasion driven by changes in the viral genome, thereby providing cross-protective cellular immunity against emerging viral variants [13–16]. In fact, we have previously identified the T cell response to be the most important correlate of protection against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection in vaccinated children [17].

Children represent a distinct immunological and epidemiological setting for SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. Compared with adults, children differ in immune maturation, baseline cross-reactive immunity, and exposure histories, and they experience a higher proportion of asymptomatic infections [18, 19]. These factors can shift both the kinetics of vaccine-induced responses and the relative importance of antibodies, MBCs, and T cell responses as correlates of protection, motivating studies focused on pediatric-specific analyses.

Circulating antibodies, which are well-characterized by their rapid induction and significant waning over the months following mRNA vaccination, are widely recognized correlates of protection, especially in the early weeks to months post-vaccination [20–23]. Yet, we and others have alluded to the layered and concerted way in which the immune system works to deliver clinical protection beyond just circulating antibodies [17, 24]. Importantly, correlates of protection may evolve over time as vaccinated individuals acquire hybrid immunity (i.e., immunity acquired through a combination of vaccination and natural infection). Since the clinical endpoint of vaccination for most infectious diseases is to reduce clinical severity rather than to achieve sterilizing immunity, hybrid immunity is a likely outcome for most endemic pathogens. Natural infection by respiratory pathogens can broaden the binding repertoire of antibodies, establish localized site-specific immunity at mucosal surfaces, and induce a broader T cell response against viral epitopes spanning the full viral genome—a breadth of immunity that is likely unachievable by single-protein-restricted vaccines [15]. As such, investigating the long-term dynamics of both vaccine-induced and natural infection-induced MBC and T cell responses is of paramount importance. This gap in knowledge hinders our attempts to accurately assess the quality and longevity of immunological memory elicited after these events, as well as our ability to predict the necessity of booster doses in maintaining said memory.

Additionally, the lack of integrated frameworks to analyze complex and multidimensional immunological data over extended time horizons remains a challenge. The complexity of such datasets is twofold. Firstly, immunity-conferring events occur at different time points for different individuals, and this heterogeneity in immune history complicates the accurate assessment of immune dynamics. Secondly, using immune responses measured at fixed follow-up time points to correlate with infection risk that occurs at a point later in time assumes that immune responses remain static over time, which is biologically implausible.

Mathematical models explicitly account for time-varying immune trajectories and have been used to quantify post-vaccination antibody kinetics and waning using both phenomenological and biologically motivated formulations [25–28]. More mechanistic frameworks have also integrated several immune components to study disease outcomes or vaccination strategies [29, 30]. Recent work in vaccinated adults has combined high-dimensional serology with machine learning approaches to disentangle vaccine-only versus hybrid immunity and to identify previously unreported infections [31]. Complementary pediatric studies have also characterized humoral and T cell responses following breakthrough infection, providing context for interpreting hybrid immunity in children [32]. Here, we complement these efforts by providing a tractable framework tailored to sparse longitudinal cohort sampling, reconstructing individual-level trajectories across multiple immune compartments, and linking the reconstructed biomarker levels to the risk of symptomatic infection at the precise moment it occurs.

In this study, we used a longitudinal cohort of children undergoing serial immunological assessment for 12 months after primary vaccination with two doses of the BNT162b2 mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccine to reconstruct the temporal trajectories of four key immunological biomarkers—anti-Spike immunoglobulin G (anti-S IgG), neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), Spike-specific MBCs (S+ MBCs), and Spike-reactive T cell response—for children with vaccine-only as well as hybrid immunity. We then identified the circulating correlates of protection against symptomatic breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection across three distinct post-primary vaccination periods using individualized biomarker trajectories to account for the dynamicity of immune responses. By integrating time-varying biomarker profiling with clinical outcomes, our study provides comprehensive insights into the durability of mRNA vaccine-induced and hybrid immunity in children. We also address the multidimensional nature of correlates of protection, providing a foundation for future implementation of mRNA vaccines for endemic pathogens in pediatric populations.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

This study examined both male and female children. In our primary analyses, sex was not considered as a potential covariate as no correlation was found with any of the adaptive immune parameters in our previous study [17]. Nonetheless, we performed sensitivity analyses of the immunodynamic models and found no statistical improvement in model fit when sex was included as a covariate.

Study design and sample collection

One hundred ten healthy and SARS-CoV-2-naïve children aged 5 to 12 years were recruited between 20 December 2021 and 8 March 2022 and were followed up for 12 months after primary vaccination with two 10 µg doses of monovalent BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine based on the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain, 21 days apart. At parental discretion, several children received a homologous booster vaccination (third dose) at least 5 months after the second dose. Peripheral venous blood was sampled at the pre-vaccination baseline as well as at 10 days, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the first dose. At each of these time points, four immunological biomarkers were quantified: anti-S IgG via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using the full-length trimeric S protein, nAbs against the Wuhan-Hu-1 strain of SARS-CoV-2 determined via a surrogate virus neutralization test (sVNT), S+ MBCs as a proportion of total B cells via flow cytometry, and S-reactive T cell response using a cytokine release assay, in which interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) levels were measured in the supernatant of whole blood stimulated with overlapping peptides of the SARS-CoV-2 S protein. Full details on how the immunological biomarkers were measured can be found in Additional file 1: SupplementaryMethods [13, 17, 33–42].

We did not specifically include variant-specific nAb titers against Omicron BA.2 (which was the predominant variant during the study follow-up period) as a candidate correlate of protection as they were not induced in meaningful titers after Wuhan-Hu-1-based mRNA vaccination in the absence of recently acquired hybrid immunity [17]. Instead, we modeled vaccine-induced antibody responses based on the ancestral Wuhan-Hu-1 as antigenic mismatch can be reasonably expected given inevitable divergence between the current circulating variant and the previous vaccine rollout (until a globally effective pan-beta-CoV vaccine is available). While antigenic mismatch may possibly reduce the direct neutralizing capacity against BA.2, we deliberately included anti-S IgG as an aggregate readout of antibodies with any binding capacity for all SARS-CoV-2 variants, including nAbs targeting the receptor binding domain (RBD). These broad binding antibodies and their associated Fc-mediated effector functions remain critical correlates of protection in this setting as they are more resilient to viral evolution as a whole group [24].

Symptomatic antigen rapid test positive (ART+) SARS-CoV-2 infections were monitored for and documented throughout the follow-up period. Asymptomatic infections were detected with anti-nucleocapsid (N) antibodies, anti-S IgG, and N-reactive T cell response as previously described [17]. We excluded children who were lost to follow-up and those who became infected between vaccine doses because immunity conferred by a single dose differs from that after the full primary two-dose regimen [43, 44]. For the primary analysis, we also excluded children who experienced asymptomatic infection as the exact timing of infection was not known. Full details on how breakthrough infections were defined can be found in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods [13, 17, 33–42].

Mathematical modeling of immunodynamics

To investigate the temporal dynamics of immunological biomarkers following vaccination and breakthrough infection, we developed two phenomenological models—an exponential model and an inactivation model—which share the same general pattern (i.e., increasing after a stimulation event, reaching a peak, and then declining over time) but have distinct underlying mathematical structures. Both models incorporate known timings of the immunity-conferring events (i.e., primary vaccination, booster vaccination, and breakthrough infection) and are designed to estimate key features of the immune response, such as the magnitude, duration, and waning of biomarker levels over time. We selected these two models to capture two distinct but biologically plausible response patterns. The exponential model reflects discrete, event-driven transitions by assuming a stepwise immune activation pattern: biomarker levels rise exponentially following each immunity-conferring event and subsequently wane over time, with explicitly defined peak timings. In contrast, the inactivation model allows for a continuous, smoother response, where the production rate of the biomarker gradually decreases over time without explicitly parameterizing the peak timing.

For simplicity and due to the lack of sufficient early data points for parameterization, we modeled the primary vaccination series as a single immunization event starting at day 0 with an aggregated immune response rather than treating each dose as a separate event. This was consistent with the overall kinetics observed in prior large-scale studies and clinical trials evaluating vaccine immunogenicity at early time points [20, 21]. We describe the two mathematical models in more detail in the following sub-sections.

The parameter φmi is the timing of the m th immunity-conferring event (i.e., primary vaccination, breakthrough infection, booster vaccination, reinfection) for individual i, thus m∈1,2,3,4. Note that the time of the primary vaccination is φ1i=0, and the biomarker level at this timepoint is parameterized as A0i=Ai(0). After the m th immunity-conferring event, Ai(t) increases at a rate of kma for a defined period of τm before reaching a peak and declining at the rate of kmb. In other words, kma controls the post-event rise (growth/production) of the biomarker, kmb controls the waning (decay) after the peak, and τm is the duration of the rising phase (time-to-peak). The timing of each event (φmi) is taken from observed vaccination and infection dates, and the model reconstructs each child’s continuous biomarker trajectory between sparse sampling timepoints.

For the primary analysis, to reduce the number of model parameters for simplicity, we assumed that the growth and decay rates for subsequent immunity-conferring events after primary vaccination are the same (i.e., k2a=k3a=k4a and k2b=k3b=k4b) and the time to the peak after each immunity-conferring event is the same (i.e., τm=τ). Although τ is estimated from the data, it is shared across events to avoid over-parameterization given sparse early measurements.

Inactivation model

In the inactivation model, the biomarker’s effective production rate decays over time, producing a smoother rise-to-peak behavior without explicitly estimating a single peak time. The model has the following form:

After the m th immunity-conferring event, Ait increases at the rate of αme-γmt-φmi, where the initial increasing rate αm (i.e., the maximum growth rate) slows by a time-dependent rate of γm (i.e., the degradation rate of the growth rate). Ait also decreases by a rate of βm due to the natural competing waning process. Similar to the exponential model, the time of the primary vaccination for individual i is φ1i=0, and the biomarker level at this timepoint is parameterized as A0i=Ai(0). For the primary analysis, in order to reduce model complexity, we also assumed all the parameter values are the same except those for the first immunity-conferring event (i.e., α2=α3=α4, γ2=γ3=γ4, and β2=β3=β4). We also considered a correlation structure between α1 and β1 to represent the possible production and decay correlations.

Nonlinear mixed-effect modeling approach

We fitted the two models independently to each immunological biomarker on their original scale using a nonlinear mixed-effect (NLME) approach and jointly estimated the parameters. Each model parameter is composed of fixed and random effects. The fixed effect is constant across individuals, representing the typical population value of the parameter, whereas the random effect reflects the inter-individual variability. Specifically, for an individual i, the parameter set, θi, is the product of the fixed effect θpop and the random effect πi, which is assumed to follow a normal distribution: πi∼N(0,Ω). This hierarchical structure enables the model to borrow strength across individuals, allowing for more stable parameter estimation even in cases with sparse data. Fixed effects and random effects were estimated via the stochastic approximation expectation–maximization (SAEM) algorithm and empirical Bayes estimation (EBE), respectively.

We assessed identifiability empirically using parameter uncertainty (i.e., relative standard errors), parameter correlation matrices, and the individual-level observed-versus-predicted fits. Model selection was performed using Akaike information criteria (AIC) and bias-corrected Bayesian information criteria (BICc) that balance model fit and parsimony. We prioritized BICc over the standard BIC to account for the finite sample size of the pediatric cohort relative to the number of estimated parameters, as BIC can underestimate the penalty for model complexity in smaller datasets [45]. While BICc assumes normality, this aligns with the structural assumptions of the framework used here, which presupposes log-normally distributed random effects (eπi, where πi∼N(0,Ω)) and normally distributed residual errors.

Note that for anti-S IgG, as the raw data contained right-censored observations with 500 U/mL as the limit of detection, we imposed an upper limit of 5000 U/mL on these censored values to prevent extreme parameter estimates during model fitting. For S+ MBCs, we assumed no direct event-driven increase from immunity-conferring events other than the primary vaccination, based on biological plausibility [46, 47].

Survival analyses with time-varying immunological biomarkers

We first estimated biomarker trajectories for each child using the best-fit immunodynamic model. For survival analyses, biomarkers were log10-transformed and entered as time-varying predictors in Cox proportional hazards (PH) models. We first fit single-biomarker univariable models and followed by multivariable models. The Cox model baseline hazard h0t allows the underlying infection pressure to vary over time at the cohort level. Accordingly, the hazard ratios (HRs) represent the change in the risk of symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection per one log10-unit increase (i.e., a tenfold increase on the original scale: anti-S IgG [U/mL], nAbs [IC50], S+ MBCs [%], and IFN-γ [pg/mL]). To enable comparisons across biomarkers with different units, we also fitted models using standardized log10-transformed predictors (Z-scores); in these models, a one unit increase corresponds to a one standard deviation increase in the log10 biomarker level, allowing direct comparison of effect sizes across immune compartments.

We chose 30 days post-primary vaccination to be the starting point for survival analyses as the best-fit exponential models suggested that the anti-S IgG and nAbs levels peak after approximately 30 days. As we treated the initial two-dose primary vaccination as a single immunity-conferring event, we therefore made a simplifying assumption that 30 days is an appropriate time point where immunity is well-established to provide stable protection, and an earlier starting point prior to the peak may distort survival outcomes. This is in line with other studies that measured the levels of binding antibodies and nAbs at 4 weeks following the first dose of primary vaccination (i.e., 2 weeks after second dose) [21, 23, 48, 49].

To elucidate potential temporal shifts in the association between immunological biomarkers and protection against symptomatic infection, we conducted analyses across three post-primary vaccination periods: 30 days to 3 months (30D–3M), 3 to 6 months (3M–6M), and 6 to 12 months (6M–12M). These period-based analyses are presented as the primary results for interpretability as early/intermediate/late phases and are not intended to represent biologically discrete transitions. Children who remained uninfected throughout the respective periods were treated as right-censored.

Sensitivity analyses for immunodynamic models

For our primary analysis, to maintain identifiability under sparse sampling and heterogeneous exposure histories, we treated the two-dose primary vaccination as a single immunity-conferring event and imposed shared kinetic parameters across subsequent antigenic exposures. Here, we evaluated alternative specifications that relax constraints on each immunity-conferring event following primary vaccination and allowed event-specific kinetics (i.e., booster vs. infection events). First, we allowed event-specific growth and decay rates for post-primary vaccination events instead of constraining them to be equal. Second, for the exponential model only, we allowed the peak timing to vary across immune events rather than being shared. To visualize the differences in model outputs, we overlapped the population immunological biomarker trajectories of the complex model together with the simplified one. We also evaluated age and sex as covariates by including them as fixed effects on decay kinetic parameters and assessed for any improvements in model fit.

Following model selection, we evaluated the precision and stability of the population parameter estimates using a non-parametric bootstrap approach (B = 200). Bootstrap datasets were generated by resampling individuals from the original cohort with replacement. The final population model was refitted to each resampled dataset using the final estimates as initial values. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the fixed effects and inter-individual variability terms were derived using the percentile method (i.e., 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution).

Sensitivity analyses for survival analyses

In our primary analysis, we considered vaccine-only children as the population at risk and excluded hybrid-immune children (i.e., those who received two doses of primary vaccination, and then experienced an episode of breakthrough infection) in the preceding period. We thus also performed a sensitivity analysis including these hybrid-immune children as the population at risk. As a complementary robustness check that does not rely on discrete temporal cutoffs, we additionally conducted a global survival analysis allowing associations to evolve over time.

A subset of children had evidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection without symptoms, for whom the exact infection date was unknown but could be localized to an interval between two consecutive study visits. Here, we included these children by imputing the infection time as the midpoint of the interval between the last visit without evidence of infection and the first visit with evidence of infection. We then repeated the immunodynamic reconstruction and survival analyses using this augmented dataset. We also fitted multivariable Cox PH models including age and sex as covariates; these adjusted models were used to evaluate robustness of immune correlates to measured confounding. However, because individual-level exposure intensity (e.g., household contacts or behavioral risk) was not measured, between-child heterogeneity in exposure could not be explicitly adjusted for.

In addition to comparing the magnitudes of the coefficients, we calculated the concordance index (C-index) to evaluate the discriminatory performance of the multivariable models and quantify the contribution of individual biomarkers (i.e., variable importance). The C-index which estimates the probability that, for a randomly selected pair of individuals, the individual with a higher predicted risk experiences the event sooner. We computed the drop in C-index by comparing the full multivariable model against reduced models where single biomarkers were iteratively excluded. A larger reduction in C-index indicated a greater contribution of the excluded biomarker to the model’s predictive accuracy.

Finally, to internally validate the robustness of the identified immune correlates of protection and thoroughly account for model uncertainty, we performed a bootstrap validation analysis using two distinct resampling approaches. In both approaches, bootstrap samples were generated by resampling the study cohort with replacement while preserving the longitudinal structure of the data (B = 1000). In the first approach, designed to isolate the stability of the survival associations, individual model-predicted biomarker trajectories from the primary analysis were treated as fixed inputs. Time-dependent Cox PH models were then refitted for each resampled cohort. In the second, more rigorous approach, we fully propagated the underlying estimation uncertainty from the immunodynamic modeling stage into the survival analysis. For each resampled cohort, immunodynamic models were completely re-estimated to generate new individual parameters and corresponding individual biomarker trajectories. The period-based time-dependent Cox PH models were subsequently refitted using these newly estimated, iteration-specific trajectories. For both approaches, the robustness of the immune correlates was assessed by calculating the non-parametric bootstrap 95% CIs for the HRs.

Protection durations for children with hybrid immunity

To estimate the duration of protection against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 reinfection for children with hybrid immunity—reflecting endemic COVID-19 in which most children would have experienced breakthrough infection—we employed two complementary approaches. In the first approach, we simulated biomarker trajectories for a virtual cohort of 1000 children, and determined the mean population response, as well as the 30% strongest and weakest responders. The timing of the first infection event for the acquisition of hybrid immunity was set as the average value of all hybrid-immune children in the original data. The protection duration was thus calculated as the time after primary vaccination to the timepoint after the acquisition of hybrid immunity at which the biomarker falls below a defined protection threshold, using the 80% sensitivity point on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve as previously established [17, 50]. In the second approach, we based our analysis on the univariable Cox PH model incorporating simulated daily T cell trajectories as a time-varying covariate. The duration of protection was defined as the timepoint post-primary vaccination at which the predicted cumulative survival probability drops below 75%.

For projections beyond the study follow-up period (> Day 392), we assumed the baseline hazard remained constant at the last estimated value, such that the subsequent decline in survival probability was driven exclusively by the projected waning of T cell levels derived from the immunodynamic model. Lastly, we also performed a sensitivity analysis using different infection times to evaluate the robustness of the computed protection durations. Note that we defined an operational threshold for the T cell response and computed the time until the projected trajectory crossed this threshold to summarize the protection duration. This duration is a model-derived estimate and is not directly observed beyond the 12-month follow-up.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) (https://www.r-project.org/). Baseline characteristics between included and excluded children were compared using appropriate statistical tests (i.e., Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test for continuous and categorical variables, respectively). NLME modeling was conducted in Monolix 2024R1 (Lixoft) (https://www.lixoft.com) using the SAEM algorithm, and model selection was based on the lowest AIC and bias-corrected BICc. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to assess associations between biomarkers. Univariable and multivariable Cox PH models with time-varying covariates were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs; the proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld’s global test. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Study cohort and infection events

We utilized data obtained from a cohort study—MARkers of Vaccine Efficacy and Longevity in SARS-CoV-2 (MARVELS)—as previously described [17]. Of the 110 healthy SARS-CoV-2-naïve children undergoing primary mRNA vaccination, serial blood sampling was performed at five time points: pre-vaccination baseline, and at 10 days, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months following primary vaccination (Fig. 1A). We excluded 16 children who were infected between the first two doses and 4 children who were lost to follow-up. For the main analysis, we also excluded 20 children with asymptomatic infection as the exact date of infection was unknown, resulting in 70 children (63.6% of the original cohort) remaining (Fig. 1B). Of these 70 children, the median age was 8.0 years, 65.7% were female, and 77.1% were Chinese. During the follow-up period, 55 (78.6%) children experienced symptomatic breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections, while 35 (50.0%) children received a booster (third dose) vaccine at least 5 months after primary vaccination. Formal comparisons of baseline characteristics between the full cohort and the analyzed subset showed no meaningful imbalances, supporting the representativeness of the participants included (Table 1). All infections were mild (Additional file 1: Figure S1).

As the study period coincided with the emergence and circulation of the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant (predominantly BA.2) in Singapore, we leveraged on this epidemiological opportunity to identify circulating correlates of protection against breakthrough infections in children undergoing primary mRNA vaccination. However, while the follow-up visits were scheduled for 10 days, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months post-primary vaccination, the actual follow-up times varied among participants (mean of 10, 123, 217, and 392 days, respectively). We thus divided our analyses into three post-vaccination periods—30 days to 3 months (30D–3M), 3 to 6 months (3M–6M), and 6 to 12 months (6M–12M)—according to these mean follow-up times and the corresponding number of breakthrough infections during the respective periods were 22 cases, 18 cases, and 16 cases. Details of the population at risk and the number of infection events in each period are summarized in Fig. 1C, while a timeline of events for each child in relation to the follow-up visits is illustrated in Additional file 1: Figure S2. Note that 1 child was infected prior to 30 days and 2 children experienced reinfections.

Immunological biomarkers follow distinctive trajectories

We reconstructed the longitudinal trajectories of each biomarker by fitting both exponential and inactivation models to the data on their original scales. As an example, Fig. 1D depicts the immunological dynamics of a hypothetical individual who receives the full primary vaccination series (i.e., first and second doses), experiences breakthrough infection, receives booster (i.e., third dose) vaccination, and then experiences the second breakthrough infection. Model selection was based on the lowest AIC and sample-size corrected BICc values (Additional file 1: Table S1). The dynamics of anti-S IgG and nAbs were best described by the exponential model, suggesting that serological markers are best captured with an exponential growth and decay pattern. In contrast, the dynamics of S+ MBC and S-reactive T cell response (as measured by IFN-γ and IL-2) were best captured by the inactivation model, indicating that these responses follow an attenuated contraction phase.

Figure 2 depicts the model-based population trajectories for each biomarker, with hypothetical timings of breakthrough infections and booster vaccinations set according to the mean timings observed in the cohort, which were 152 and 303 days, respectively. While parameter estimation was performed on the original scale, we plotted the biomarker trajectories on a log10-transformed scale for visual comparability across biomarkers with different ranges. The estimated population-level model parameters for the exponential and inactivation models are summarized in Additional file 1: Tables S2–3. Estimation uncertainty of the population parameters is reflected by the standard errors. While the standard deviation of the random effects captures the between-child biological heterogeneity, these variance estimates also inherently incorporate individual-level estimation uncertainty driven by the sparse temporal sampling of the cohort. For IFN-γ and IL-2, high correlations were observed between the estimated growth (α₁) and decay (β₁) parameters, which jointly determine the overall trajectory shape (Additional file 1: Table S3). While this may reflect partial non-identifiability of individual kinetic parameters—a common feature of nonlinear dynamical models fitted to sparse longitudinal data—the strong parameter correlation does not necessarily imply instability of the model-predicted biomarker trajectories or derived quantities. We assessed the quality of the model fits via visual predictive checks and inspected the individual-level trajectories, which captured the distinctive dynamics of individual children well, with observed data points distributed randomly around the predictions without systematic bias (Additional file 1: Figures S3–S7).

Both anti-S IgG and nAbs displayed similar dynamics, with lower growth rates but higher decay rates with each subsequent immunity-conferring event as compared to primary vaccination (Fig. 2A, B and Additional file 1: Table S2). The estimated peak timings were 30.7 days for anti-S IgG and 28.7 days for nAbs after the first dose, aligning with previously reported dynamics in healthy adults undergoing primary vaccination [20, 21]. To assess the temporal relationship between these two biomarkers, we conducted Spearman’s rank correlation analyses across the entire study period and within each time period (i.e., 30D–3M, 3M–6M, and 6M–12M). Since the measured nAbs were only specific to ancestral Spike encoded by vaccine mRNA, whereas variant-specific binding antibodies would generally still be captured by the anti-S IgG assay, the consistent and strong correlation of anti-S IgG and nAbs (ρ > 0.75) suggested that primary vaccination elicited a broad repertoire of S+ MBCs, leading to a robust boost of both anti-S IgG and nAbs with each immunity-conferring event (Additional file 1: Figure S8).

S+ MBCs, as a proportion of total B cells, continued to increase slowly during the 3M–6M period post-primary vaccination before remaining stable at 12 months (Fig. 2C). The more gradual increase over time is consistent with reports of a sustained germinal center reaction 4 to 6 months after primary vaccination, with evidence of somatic hypermutation and affinity maturation [11, 51]. S+ MBCs appeared to exhibit minimal changes in response to subsequent immunity-conferring events, which supports the notion that immune homeostatic mechanisms are in place to limit the proportion of specific pools of antigen-specific MBCs in circulation [10, 17]. While S+ MBCs initially demonstrated a weak correlation with serological biomarkers, this became insignificant over time (Additional file 1: Figure S8), consistent with our knowledge of the germinal center reaction, which gives rise to two distinct populations of B cells: long-lived plasma cells which home into the bone marrow and continually produce circulating antibodies, and S+ MBCs which do not produce antibodies but remain in circulation [52].

The S-reactive T cell response, as measured by post-stimulation IFN-γ, was rapidly induced and detectable as early as 10 days in most children (Fig. 2D), as previously shown [17, 53]. Interestingly, we observed that the growth and decay rates of the T cell response were lower after subsequent immunity-conferring events as compared to primary vaccination (Additional file 1: Table S3). This pattern contrasted with the antibody response, which decayed faster after subsequent immunity-conferring events compared to primary vaccination (Additional file 1: Table S2). This suggests that once hybrid immunity was established, the T cell response waned more slowly and could thus be more durable. We also analyzed the dynamics of the S-reactive T cell response, as measured by IL-2 (Additional file 1: Figure S7), and found that IFN-γ and IL-2 were strongly correlated across multiple time periods (ρ > 0.70) (Additional file 1: Figure S8), suggesting that the two cytokines could be used interchangeably as readouts for the T cell response. As in previously published work using the cytokine release assay [17, 40], we henceforth selected IFN-γ as the main cytokine representing the S-reactive T cell response. Lastly, the T cell response was weakly correlated to serological markers (ρ < 0.50), consistent with our findings of diverging kinetics between cellular and antibody immune responses over time (Additional file 1: Figure S8).

To assess the robustness of the final model structure, we performed additional sensitivity analyses that relaxed the kinetic constraints on each immunity-conferring event after primary vaccination or incorporated event-specific peak timings (Additional file 1: Table S4 and Figure S9). These modifications, however, did not yield any statistically significant improvements in model fit (AIC/BICc), nor did they materially alter the reconstructed biomarker trajectories. Furthermore, we tested the addition of age and sex as covariates on the decay parameters, based on prior studies suggesting differential waning immunity based on age and sex [22]. Likewise, this did not improve the model, indicating that immune waning rates were largely consistent across the demographic spectrum of this cohort. Finally, the statistical stability of the model parameter estimates was confirmed by non-parametric bootstrap validation (B = 200). The fixed-effect and random-effect parameters fell within their derived 95% CIs, demonstrating that the model structure is identifiable and the estimated kinetic rates are robust to sampling variability (Additional file 1: Table S5).

In summary, our modeling framework not only captures the distinctive biomarker trajectories over time but is also able to recapitulate realistic biological patterns and key features of the underlying immune response, thereby providing a basis for identifying the dynamic profiles that may underlie differences in protection and infection risk.

T cell response is the most important correlate of protection against symptomatic breakthrough infection

Analysis per time period

As we were interested to use our modeled time-varying biomarker trajectories to identify correlates of protection against symptomatic breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection in this cohort, we evaluated the association between the four immunological biomarkers—anti-S IgG, nAbs, S+ MBCs, and S-reactive T cell response—and the risk of symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection using a time-to-event survival analysis across three post-primary vaccination periods: 30D–3M, 3M–6M, and 6M–12M. Henceforth, we refer to “protection” as the reduced hazard (lower risk) of symptomatic breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection, which is the clinical endpoint in this study. Note that the analyses presented below were conducted twice for each biomarker: on the original log10-transformed scale to enhance interpretability of the HRs (Fig. 3A and Additional file 1: Table S6) and on the standardized log10-transformed scale with standardized coefficients (where one unit represents one standard deviation) so that the relative importance of the biomarkers can be fairly compared (Fig. 3B, C).

We first considered a univariable Cox PH analysis with vaccine-only children. We found that anti-S IgG and nAbs were the only statistically significant covariates which reduced the risk against symptomatic breakthrough infection during the 30D–3M period, with HRs of 0.13 (95% CI [0.02, 0.70]) and 0.06 (95% CI [0.004, 0.89]) per unit increase, respectively (Fig. 3A). The relative importance of these two biomarkers, as measured by standardized coefficients, was comparable (Fig. 3B). In direct contrast to the first time period, S+ MBC and the T cell response became the only covariates which significantly reduced the risk of symptomatic breakthrough infection during the 3M–6M period, with HRs of 0.04 (95% CI [0.004, 0.59]) and 0.17 (95% CI [0.05, 0.55]) per unit increase, respectively (Fig. 3A). By the 6M–12M period, only the T cell response continued to show a strong association with protection, with a HR of 0.35 (95% CI [0.13, 0.95]) per unit increase (Fig. 3A). When including hybrid-immune children, we found that serological biomarkers became significantly correlated with protection across all three periods (Fig. 3A, C). However, the T cell response was still the most significant predictor of protection 6 to 12 months after the primary dose, as evidenced by the largest negative coefficients (Fig. 3C).

We also fitted a multivariable Cox PH model accounting for the combined effects of antibody, S+ MBCs, and S-reactive T cell response. To mitigate multicollinearity arising from correlated covariates (Additional file 1: Figure S8), we selected three out of four biomarkers for the multivariable analysis: nAbs (for its coefficient which was larger than anti-S IgG), S+ MBCs, and T cell response (Fig. 4A, B and Additional file 1: Table S7). In the multivariable analysis of the overall cohort (including hybrid-immune children), the T cell response was the only significant correlate of protection from 3 months onwards, highlighting that the T cell response had the strongest statistical association with reduced risk in later periods (Fig. 4B). However, when restricted to the vaccine-only cohort, this association did not reach statistical significance in the 6M–12M period after adjusting for concurrent antibody and S+ MBC levels (Fig. 4A). This loss of statistical signal—despite being significant in univariable analysis—likely reflects the reduced statistical power in the waning vaccine-only subgroup (N = 29) compared to the robust total cohort (N = 70). Consequently, this suggests that while T cells are critical for long-term durability, the maintenance of protective immune levels that statistically predict clinical outcomes is more robust in the presence of hybrid immunity.

We validated our findings using two complementary approaches. First, variable importance analysis using the C-index confirmed that removing the T cell response as a covariate significantly reduced predictive accuracy in the late phase (Additional file 1: Figure S10). Second, cluster bootstrap validation was performed to confirm the stability of these correlates, utilizing both a fixed-trajectory approach and a highly rigorous full kinetic parameter re-estimation approach to account for underlying model uncertainty (Additional file 1: Table S7). This re-estimation confirmed the robust contribution of T cells: the association between T cell responses and reduced infection risk remained highly significant in the total cohort (including hybrid-immune children), with 95% CIs remaining strictly below 1.00 during the late phases (HR 0.39, 95% CI: 0.21–0.62 for 6M–12M). Notably, accounting for parameter uncertainty also revealed a residual protective effect for nAbs in the total cohort during these later periods (HR 0.56, 95% CI: 0.21–0.93 for 6M–12M); however, the stronger effect size and narrower CIs for T cells still support our primary finding of a temporal shift toward T cell-dominant protection in the late phase.

Lastly, we further assessed the robustness of our findings against potential biases. Despite adjusting for age and sex in the multivariable Cox PH models, the associations remained consistent: nAbs were the primary correlate of protection in the early phase, while T cell responses became the sole significant correlate of protection in the intermediate and late phases (3M–12M). Neither age nor sex showed a statistically significant association with infection risk in these sensitivity analyses (Additional file 1: Table S8). Including children with asymptomatic infection by imputing their plausible infection times to the midpoint of their observation intervals yielded qualitatively similar results to the primary analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. 11). As the identity and direction of the main correlates were unchanged (compared to Fig. 4), this suggests that the correlates of protection against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection may be relevant to asymptomatic infections as well.

Global survival analysis

To verify whether our conclusions depend on the selected period boundaries, we conducted a global survival analysis focusing on the first breakthrough infection. This global analysis serves as a robustness check that does not rely on discrete temporal cutoffs and corroborates the overall time-dependent pattern observed in the previous period-based analyses. Here, the follow-up period was defined from 30 days after primary vaccination until the timing of first infection for all infected children, or the maximum follow-up timepoint for uninfected children who were treated as censored.

The global univariable Cox PH analysis identified T cell response as the only significant correlate of protection (Additional file 1: Table S9). Schoenfeld’s global test indicated that anti-S IgG and nAbs violated the proportional hazards assumption, suggesting time-varying effects. We observed consistent results with the multivariable Cox PH model with the three selected biomarkers (i.e., nAbs, S+ MBCs, and T cell response). Furthermore, the coefficient for nAbs changed markedly over time, shifting from around − 1 to approximately 0 before 200 days (Fig. 4C). To account for this, we then restricted the analysis to the early phase and found that nAbs remained the only significant correlate of protection for cut-offs up to 150 days. Conversely, T cell response emerged as the significant correlate of protection when analyzing the entire follow-up period. The global analysis was consistent with our analysis per time period, supporting our findings that antibody responses were the dominant correlate of protection in the early post-vaccination period, whereas T cell response became more important in later periods (Fig. 5).

Taken together, our survival analysis reveals how fluctuating biomarker levels over time influence the risk of symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection, emphasizing the value of accounting for time-dependent immune dynamics.

Durable T cell-correlated protection for children with hybrid immunity

Since the T cell response plays the dominant role in long-term protection, we focused on this biomarker to estimate the duration of protection against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 reinfection using two complementary approaches. Furthermore, as hybrid immunity against SARS-CoV-2 is almost universal in the context of endemic transmission, we directed our analyses at model-based protection estimates under hybrid-immunity scenarios.

In the first approach, we simulated trajectories of the T cell response for three groups: weakest responders with the bottom 30% T cell response, mean responders, and strongest responders with the top 30% T cell response within a virtual population of 1,000 hybrid-immune children (Fig. 6A). We fixed the acquisition of hybrid immunity at 150 days post-primary vaccination based on the mean timing of the first breakthrough infection in the cohort. The protection duration was determined by the intersection between simulated trajectories and the predefined protection threshold of IFN-γ = 19.55 pg/mL, which was adopted from our prior study (80% sensitivity) [17, 50] and was pre-specified for interpretability; it was not re-estimated in the current cohort. Notably, in the 30% weakest responders, the T cell response fell below the threshold between 100 and 200 days after primary vaccination, during which the first breakthrough infection occurred, and hybrid immunity was subsequently established. Upon acquiring hybrid immunity, the secondary immune response in the cellular compartment was largest for the strongest responders, as evidenced by the higher peak responses attained; this then determined the duration that T cell response remained above the threshold of protection upon subsequent decay. We identified different protection durations of 469 days, 564 days, and 722 days for the weakest, mean, and strongest responders, respectively.

In the second approach, we fitted the trajectories of the T cell response into the univariable Cox PH survival model and defined the protection duration as the time that the cumulative survival probability dropped below 75%. In other words, this reflects the period after primary vaccination during which fewer than three out of four hybrid-immune children remain protected against reinfection. Here, we identified the protection durations of 487, 582, and 740 days for the weakest, mean and strongest responders, respectively (Fig. 6B). It is noteworthy that both approaches yielded consistent results.

We performed a sensitivity analysis where we simulated trajectories of the T cell response for the mean responders with different timings of the first breakthrough infection (i.e., acquisition of hybrid immunity), which were chosen at specific quantiles (10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 90%) of the distribution of the cohort’s observed infection times. Interestingly, we found that while the timing of the first infection for the acquisition of hybrid immunity may influence the subsequent protection duration, this effect was very limited. All simulated mean trajectories intersected the threshold between 586 and 631 days, whereas an estimated 580 to 631 days was required before less than three out of four children were protected from infection (Additional file 1: Figure S12).

Collectively, our findings suggest that in children with hybrid immunity, T cell-correlated protection is projected to be sustained for approximately 500 days on average under our pre-specified protection thresholds. If continual protection against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection is desired, booster doses may be considered after approximately 1.5 years after primary vaccination.

Discussion

By leveraging a unique cohort of children undergoing primary vaccination with BNT162b2 mRNA SARS-CoV-2 vaccination during an Omicron BA.2 wave of infections, we applied mathematical modeling to reconstruct the temporal trajectories of anti-S IgG, nAbs, S+ MBCs, and S-reactive T cell response to both vaccination and natural infection and showed that S+ MBCs and T cell responses have distinct kinetics compared to serological biomarkers. Using individual-level biomarker trajectories to assess the association with subsequent risk of symptomatic breakthrough infection, we identified serological biomarkers as being associated with reduced risk mainly in the early post-vaccination period, whereas the T cell response showed the strongest association with reduced risk as antibody levels waned.

As the goal of vaccination is to induce long-term immunological memory, the durability of S+ MBCs and cellular immune responses is critical. Consistent with the long-lived nature of cellular immunological memory, SARS-CoV-1-specific T cell responses have been detected up to 17 years after infection [54]. Separately, vaccine-induced MBCs can persist for decades—for example, MBCs induced by childhood vaccination against pertussis and measles have been detected ~ 50 years later even in the absence of circulating antibodies [55]. With our pediatric cohort, serial immunological monitoring and clearly demarcated time points of each immunity-conferring event enabled us to accurately capture the long-term kinetics of S+ MBC and T cell response to mRNA vaccination in a mathematical model. While serological biomarkers were best described by exponential growth and decay pattern, the dynamics of S+ MBC and T cell response were best captured by the inactivation model that has an attenuated contraction phase. The increased durability of T cell response compared to antibodies was also demonstrated in the recent work by Park et al. [28], which is consistent with the notion that immunological memory resides in S+ MBC and memory T cells.

While clinical studies have often focused on circulating antibodies as potential systemic correlates of protection and neglect other compartments of adaptive immunity, our findings point to the multifaceted and temporally dependent cellular arm of the adaptive immune system that is associated with clinical protection [17, 24]. In the first 3 months post vaccination, protection was mostly associated with circulating antibodies, consistent with prior studies in which antibodies are associated with short-term protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection [27, 48, 49, 56, 57]. Here, we demonstrate that the protective associations of circulating antibodies declined after 3 months post-primary vaccination. In later periods, S+ MBC and T cell responses showed stronger associations with reduced risk, with the T cell response showing the largest magnitude of risk reduction beyond 6 months. This observation cannot be attributed to viral evolution, as the Omicron variant had already emerged in Singapore when our cohort began receiving vaccines in December 2021. Subsequent Omicron subvariants (primarily BA.2) contracted by children during the follow-up period remained equally antigenically distant from the ancestral strain from which the monovalent BNT162b2 was designed. Interestingly, including children with pre-existing hybrid immunity in our analysis revealed that protection was also conferred by circulating antibodies (as observed in our univariable models); this likely reflects an expansion of binding repertoire after vaccine-induced S+ MBCs re-enter the germinal center for further affinity maturation upon the first breakthrough infection [58]. However, direct measurements of receptor breadth were not available in this study and can be evaluated in future cohorts using variant-panel binding and neutralization assays and/or epitope-level mapping approaches to validate this hypothesis.

Lastly, we were able to leverage on our modeling of immune kinetics to predict the duration of protection that children with hybrid immunity could anticipate. Our model projects that, under the pre-specified IFN-γ threshold (19.55 pg/mL), the T cell response may remain above this putative protection threshold for approximately 500 days; notably, this estimated duration was consistent with the independent estimate derived from the continuous time-dependent Cox survival model (corresponding to a 75% cumulative probability of remaining infection-free). During the COVID-19 pandemic, the initially recommended duration for a booster dose after primary vaccination was 6 to 12 months (i.e., 183 to 365 days), which evidently differs from our estimate because it was inferred solely based on the kinetics of nAbs instead of T cells [56]. This illustrates the importance of holistically measuring immunological responses in multiple compartments of adaptive immunity in order to identify correlates of protection apart from circulating antibodies. Nonetheless, while our estimates suggest that an additional booster dose could be considered around 1.5 years after primary vaccination if sustained protection is desired, it is important to acknowledge that the estimated duration is intrinsically dependent on the specific definition of protection applied, such that stricter thresholds (e.g., corresponding to 90% sensitivity or > 80% survival probability) would yield shorter estimated durations, whereas more lenient thresholds would extend them. Consequently, the reported durations should be interpreted cautiously as model projections conditional on these specific immunological and statistical benchmarks, rather than as absolute biological limits.

Mathematical modeling has previously been employed to estimate protection durations and waning immunity in adult populations, often highlighting the predictive value of nAbs in the immediate post-vaccination phase and the potential role of cellular immunity in long-term durability [56, 59]. While previous adult studies suggested a divergence between humoral and cellular waning [60], our study extends these observations by applying dynamic modeling specifically to a pediatric cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first time such an approach has been used to quantify the “cutoff period” (3–6 months) where T cell responses become the primary statistical predictor of protection in children. While our parameter estimates were obtained in a pediatric cohort receiving BNT162b2 mRNA vaccination with infections occurring during the Omicron wave, the main contribution of this study is a general, biomarker-driven modeling framework that reconstructs individual immune trajectories from sparse longitudinal sampling and temporally links the reconstructed immune state to the risk of symptomatic infection. Moreover, we incorporated the effect of additional antigenic exposures—primary breakthrough infection that defines hybrid immunity, and booster vaccination—which closely mirrors the real-world immunity-conferring events in endemic COVID-19. In fact, since a combination of vaccine-conferred and infection-conferred immunity can be expected for most endemic pathogens for which vaccination does not achieve sterilizing immunity, our model demonstrates considerable potential for application in real-world contexts, regardless of pathogen. In contemporary settings with variant-updated vaccines and repeated exposures, the same framework can be re-parameterized using updated immunogenicity data, and—when supported by sufficient sample size—extended to allow event-type–specific kinetics (e.g., variant-updated booster versus infection). Accordingly, our results should be interpreted as pediatric circulating correlates of protection for the study context, while the modeling approach is intended to be extensible to evolving variants and vaccine formulations.

A strength of our study is in the simplicity of our models, which captured biologically plausible patterns from limited sample data without overfitting, while complementing more mechanistic immune-dynamics frameworks that require extensive parameterization and denser sampling [29, 30, 61]. While semi-mechanistic formulations incorporating explicit stimulation terms and/or production delays (e.g., after primary vaccination) could provide additional mechanistic insight [62], this would require denser sampling and richer immunological inputs than available in this cohort. Toggling strategically between simplicity and biological realism remains critical given the limited knowledge on how multiple immune exposures (and the time intervals between them) influence cell functions, intracellular signaling, and subsequent recall responses in hybrid-immune individuals. Secondly, our study specifically addresses the ever-vulnerable pediatric population that is a key target group for novel vaccine development against all infectious diseases. Third, while our findings were consistent with previous results [17, 48, 49, 57], this study value adds in that a more accurate snapshot of biomarker levels precisely at the point of infection can be estimated using individual-level modeled trajectories instead of relying solely on measurements from sparsely observed timepoints. Such an approach negates the improbable assumption that biomarkers remain static over time, adding confidence and resolution to our findings. Data gleaned from our study have broad implications for how we evaluate immunogenicity of mRNA vaccines against any and all endemic pathogens, even if clinical protection against symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection may not be of utmost priority.

Our study, however, has the following limitations. First, we treated the two-dose primary vaccination as a single immunity-conferring event and constrained post-primary exposures to share kinetic parameters. These assumptions may obscure genuine differences between booster- and infection-induced kinetics and potential nonlinearities across repeated exposures [63, 64]. However, less constrained models were not supported by the model selection criteria (Additional file 1: Table S4), and relaxing these constraints increased parameter uncertainty without materially changing reconstructed trajectories or downstream inferences (Additional file 1: Figure S9). Accordingly, our primary specification should be interpreted as a parsimonious, data-supported approximation to enable cohort-scale inference, rather than evidence that booster vaccination and infection induce biologically identical kinetics. Larger cohorts with denser sampling and more balanced exposure types will be needed to resolve exposure-specific kinetics. Second, in children without breakthrough infection or booster vaccination, biomarker trajectories were relatively flat, which limits identifiability of production-phase assumptions. Furthermore, the sparse temporal sampling design—specifically the wide intervals between the early acute timepoints (e.g., ~ 10 days) and the subsequent follow-ups (e.g., ~ 120 days)—limits the precision to anchor the onset and rate of the decay phases. This physical absence of intermediate temporal data mathematically resulted in large estimated inter-individual variances (i.e., standard deviations of random effects) for these specific kinetic decay rates within the NLME models. Nonetheless, trajectory reconstructions remained stable and survival analyses relied on predicted biomarker levels rather than individual kinetic parameters (Additional file 1: Table S7). Third, asymptomatic infections were excluded from the primary analysis due to interval-censored timing; thus, our primary findings strictly define correlates of protection against symptomatic infection. While future work utilizing denser surveillance or machine-learning approaches could better resolve this uncertainty [31], a sensitivity analysis incorporating these cases via midpoint imputation yielded qualitatively identical results (Additional file 1: Figure S11). This consistency suggests that the identified biomarkers are not limited to modulating disease severity but likely correlate with protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection per se (i.e., viral acquisition including asymptomatic infection), extending their applicability beyond the symptomatic endpoint. Fourth, our survival analyses use a two-stage framework where model-predicted biomarker trajectories were treated as covariates. When fully propagating kinetic parameter uncertainty via a re-estimation bootstrap (Additional file 1: Table S7), we observed methodological sensitivity: late-phase antibody effects became statistically significant in the total cohort with hybrid-immune children, contrasting with our primary analysis. While the protective effect of T cells remained robust across all methods, this inconsistency indicates that conclusions regarding the absolute absence of late-phase antibody protection are sensitive to underlying estimation uncertainty. Fifth, we did not assess mucosal immunity through nasal sampling. While intramuscular mRNA vaccines primarily induce systemic immunity, recent studies indicate that mucosal immunoglobulin A (IgA) and airway-resident T cells are critical for blocking viral entry and reducing transmission [65–67]. Since we relied solely on peripheral blood samples, the correlates of protection identified here are likely to be statistical rather than mechanistic [68]. Future work should prioritize non-invasive nasal sampling in pediatric cohorts to directly evaluate the contribution of mucosal immunity. Sixth, because this is an observational cohort, residual confounding is possible. For example, booster uptake may correlate with unmeasured behaviors (e.g., risk avoidance) or exposure patterns that also influence infection risk, and individual-level exposure intensity (e.g., household contacts, school outbreaks) was not fully captured. Although we adjusted for age and sex in sensitivity analyses (Additional file 1: Table S8), between-child heterogeneity in infection pressure could not be explicitly adjusted for; therefore, our findings should be interpreted as adjusted associations conditional on unmeasured exposure variation rather than causal effects. Relatedly, the discrete period boundaries used for interpretability are heuristic and may influence period-specific estimates; accordingly, we corroborated the period-based results with a global analysis that does not rely on strict cutoffs and yielded consistent qualitative conclusions (Fig. 4). Seventh, the primary vaccination series in this cohort used the manufacturer-recommended 21-day dosing interval; subsequent studies suggest that longer intervals (e.g., ~ 8–12 weeks) can yield stronger immunogenicity, including higher nAb responses, and have been adopted in some settings. Therefore, the absolute magnitude and durability of the fitted antibody kinetics (and any derived projections) may not directly generalize to cohorts vaccinated under extended-interval schedules [69–71]. Lastly, our follow-up period was 12 months; therefore, estimates of protection duration beyond this window (e.g., ~ 500 days) are model-based projections that depend on the assumed threshold and extrapolated waning trends. Real-world factors such as variant evolution, additional exposures, and longer-term immune dynamics could shift these trajectories, and thus these projections should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Furthermore, although our findings provide a trajectory-based view of circulating correlates of protection in children, differences in immune maturation, exposure history, and vaccine dosing or interval will mean that the quantitative effect sizes and relative contributions of immune compartments may not be directly generalizable to adult cohorts.

Conclusions

In summary, evaluating the immunogenicity of mRNA vaccine platforms requires longitudinal tracking of immunological biomarkers in both humoral and cellular compartments of adaptive immunity. Mathematical modeling can help identify circulating correlates of protection with increased confidence and predict the durability of immunological memory and clinical protection. This is because mathematical models allow us to reconstruct the likely state of those immune parameters at the precise moment of pathogen exposure, helping us better capture the biological reality that could underlie an individual’s eventual clinical outcome. Using a pediatric SARS-CoV-2 cohort, we demonstrated how this integrated framework reveals the relative contributions and importance of different immune compartments to inform optimal booster timings. Beyond this study, our model serves as a versatile tool to identify circulating correlates of protection and estimate protection durations of varied populations against prospective pandemic-causing pathogens.
