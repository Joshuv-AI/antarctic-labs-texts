Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Among Sexually Active Individuals Toward Trichomoniasis in the Ashaiman Municipality, Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Christopher Yaw Dumevi, Percival Delali Agordoh, Yaw Osei Owusu, Josephine Afi Nukpedu Banini, Roberta Araba Amoquandoh, George Boateng Kyei, Patience B Tetteh‐Quarcoo, Irene Ayi, Patrick F Ayeh‐Kumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTrichomoniasis is a significant public health concern due to its implications for reproductive health and HIV transmission. Despite its prevalence, there is limited understanding of community knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding trichomoniasis in Ghana. This study aimed to assess KAP toward trichomoniasis among sexually active individuals in the Ashaiman Municipality of the Greater Accra Region.MethodsA hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from February to April 2024 among 397 sexually active individuals aged 15–65 years. Participants were selected via simple random sampling. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 and Stata version 17. Descriptive statistics summarized socio‐demographic characteristics and knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Chi‐square tests assessed bivariate associations, while multivariable linear and logistic regression models identified independent predictors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsOnly 37.1% of respondents demonstrated good knowledge, 23.5% positive attitudes, and 34.1% good preventive practices regarding trichomoniasis. While 98% were aware of at least one STI, only 34.3% had heard of trichomoniasis, and 20.9% correctly identified its parasitic cause. Key information sources included television (21.5%) and health workers (20.2%). Bivariate analysis identified number of sexual partners (p = 0.001) and occupation (p = 0.011) as significant risk factors. Multivariable regression confirmed that multiple sexual partners strongly predicted poorer preventive practices (p < 0.001).ConclusionsKnowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices related to trichomoniasis were generally poor among respondents. The findings underscore an urgent need for targeted, theory‐based health education and integration of trichomoniasis services into primary healthcare to improve awareness and promote safer sexual practices in Ghanaian communities.Keywords: attitudes; Ghana; practice; sexually transmitted infection; trichomoniasis, knowledge

Introduction

Trichomoniasis, caused by the protozoan Trichomonas vaginalis, is the most common non‐viral sexually transmitted infection (STI) globally, with an estimated 156 million new cases annually [1]. This public health burden is disproportionately borne by women in resource‐limited settings, particularly in sub‐Saharan Africa, where prevalence rates can exceed 30% [1, 2]. The clinical presentation is highly gendered; women frequently experience symptomatic infection characterized by inflammation and discomfort, while male infections are often asymptomatic, facilitating silent transmission and under‐diagnosis [3, 4]. Although primarily sexually transmitted, the parasite's ability to persist on fomites underscores a need for broader infection control awareness [5, 6, 7]. This is because trichomoniasis‐induced genital inflammation significantly elevates the risk of HIV acquisition and transmission, making its control an essential public health priority [8, 9].

Effective management of this infection extends beyond clinical treatment to address underlying behavioral drivers. The Health Belief Model provides a valuable framework for this, positing that health behaviors are influenced by an individual's perceived susceptibility to and severity of an illness, balanced against the benefits of and barriers to action [10]. In this context, community knowledge and beliefs are foundational; they directly shape perceptions and, by extension, practices. Misconceptions or a lack of awareness can diminish the perceived risk of infection, foster stigma, and perpetuate high‐risk behaviors that facilitate transmission.

While studies in Nigeria and Kenya have reported findings on community knowledge and attitudes toward trichomoniasis [11, 12, 13], a significant knowledge gap remains within specific socio‐cultural contexts in Ghana. Studies in Ghana have focused largely on clinical prevalence, leaving a paucity of data on the community‐level understanding, perceptions, and preventive practices specifically related to this infection. Understanding the specific knowledge gaps and attitudinal barriers in Ashaiman is essential for designing targeted health education campaigns within the Ghana Health Service's existing STI framework, ultimately working toward the national goal of reducing STI incidence.

This study therefore aims to address this gap by assessing the knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices (KAP) toward trichomoniasis among sexually active individuals in the Ashaiman Municipality of Ghana. The findings will provide crucial evidence to inform the development of targeted, theory‐based, and culturally appropriate interventions designed to reduce transmission and mitigate the associated health impacts.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

This study employed a cross‐sectional study design to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of sexually active individuals seeking healthcare services in the Ashaiman Municipal Hospital of the Greater Accra Region, Ghana, from February 5 to April 30, 2024.

Sample Size

The sample size was determined using the following equation [14]: n = z 2 pq/d 2;

where n = number of samples; z = 1.96 (95% confidence level); p = prevalence estimate (50% or 0.5); q = (1 − p); and d = precession of the prevalence estimate (10% of 0.05).

We estimated a prevalence (p) of 50% and initially calculated a minimum sample size of 384. After accounting for a 5% non‐response rate, the sample size was adjusted to 403. However, after excluding incomplete responses, we analyzed data from 397 participants.

Study Design, Participants and Procedure

This was a hospital‐based cross‐sectional study conducted among males and females in the Ashaiman Municipality of the Greater Accra Region between February 5 and April 30, 2024. Ashaiman Municipal Hospital was purposively selected as the primary public healthcare facility in this densely populated and cosmopolitan municipality. It provides care to a socio‐demographically diverse population, making it a reasonable proxy for many urban Ghanaian settings. The study population comprised sexually active individuals aged 15–65 years residing in the municipality for at least 3 months. In this study, “sexually active” refers to individuals who reported at least one act of vaginal, anal, or oral intercourse within the 12 months preceding the survey, consistent with the definition used in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [15]. To minimize potential selection bias associated with hospital‐based sampling, simple random sampling was applied among all eligible attendees, ensuring that the study sample was representative of the hospital's client population. Written informed consent was obtained from the study participants after the objectives of the study were explained them in a language they understood (Twi, Ewe, or English).

Prior to recruitment, the study objectives and consent statement were presented to potential participants. Data were collected via a structured questionnaire from individuals who met the following inclusion criteria: (i) residence in the Ashaiman Municipality for a minimum of 3 months, and (ii) provision of written informed consent. For minors, both their assent and written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian were required. Participation was entirely voluntary.

A total of 443 individuals were assessed for eligibility. Forty‐three were excluded: three did not meet the inclusion criteria, 31 declined participations, and nine were excluded for other reasons. The remaining 400 participants were enrolled in the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) survey. Three participants did not complete the questionnaire, leaving 397 participants whose data were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Study Instrument Validity and Reliability

Data were collected using a standardized questionnaire we developed based on the WHO fact sheet on trichomoniasis [1] and similar studies conducted in Ethiopia [16] and Iran [17] (Supplementary File S1). The questionnaire was adapted to the local context and administered face‐to‐face to the study participants. The questionnaire was also translated into the two predominant local languages spoken by the residents (Twi and Ewe) using forward and backward translation to ensure accuracy. The questionnaire comprised four sections: socio‐demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding trichomoniasis. To ensure content and face validity, two medical doctors and two medical microbiologists (YOO, GBK, PBT‐Q, and CYD) reviewed it. Pretesting with 20 sexually active individuals in the study area led to minor revisions. Pretest data were excluded from the final analysis. All study participants were interviewed by the researcher between 15 and 20 min within the hospital setting.

The reliability and validity assessment of the questionnaire indicated satisfactory internal consistency and construct validity across most domains. The knowledge section, with 23 items, demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.793) and strong sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.848), with Bartlett's test confirming factorability, χ² (2663.37), p < 0.001, and 62.57% of the variance explained. The attitude section, which comprised nine items, showed acceptable reliability (Cronbach's α = 0.599), a modest KMO value of 0.599, and significant Bartlett's test, χ² (276.73), p < 0.001, accounting for 59.97% of variance. The practices section performed strongly, with 11 items yielding a good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.820), a KMO value of 0.688, significant Bartlett's test, χ² (921.97), p < 0.001, and 64.12% of variance explained.

Measurements

Assessment of Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice

Knowledge

Contained 23 structured questions on knowledge on trichomoniasis with three likely responses: “yes,” “no,” and “I don't know.” A three‐point scale was used instead of a five‐ or seven‐point Likert scale to minimize respondent burden and enhance comprehension, particularly in a population with diverse educational backgrounds. This approach is recommended for KAP surveys in settings where health literacy may vary [17, 18]. These questions precisely elicited responses and tested the knowledge of respondents on STIs and trichomoniasis in particular. The correct knowledge item reported was awarded a score of 1 point while incorrect knowledge, including “I don't know” was awarded a 0 score. In the current study, if “yes” is the correct answer, then “yes” is a score of 1 point while “no” or “I don't know” is a score of 0 point or otherwise reverse.

Attitudes

Contained nine structured queries on the attitude of respondents on trichomoniasis with two likely responses: “true” or “false.” This section assessed the psychological state of the respondents regarding their opinions, views, morals, and character to act in particular. Each correct attitude reported was awarded a score of 1 point while a wrong attitude option was rated a 0 score. In the current study, if “true” is the correct answer, then “true” is a score of 1 point while “false” is a score of 0 point or otherwise reverse.

Sexual Practice

Assessed the sexual practices of the respondents with respect to trichomoniasis. The instrument contained 11 structured queries with three likely responses: “no,” “once in a while,” or “always.” Each correct sexual practice reported was awarded a score of 1 point while an incorrect option was rated a 0 score. In the current study, if “no” is the correct answer, then “no” is a score of 1 point while “once in a while” or “always” is a score of 0 point or otherwise reverse. The sectioning of the responses of the respondents is the most suitable way for studies allied to the assessment of the KAP of respondents on trichomoniasis.

Knowledge and attitude questions with “once in a while” or “I don't know,” thus the third option, had been presented to enable simplicity of responding by sexually active individuals regarding trichomoniasis for fascinating thoughts considered by undecided or doubtful respondents. The third option “once in a while” or “I don't know” always scores a 0‐point due to the cumulative percentage approach adapted which considered only the acceptable response or the correct answer. The cumulative percentage method of assessment only considered a correct answer and the total cumulative score is converted to 100%. A cumulative score below 70% of the correct responses on the knowledge, attitude, and practice was considered as “poor” while a cumulative score of 70% and higher was considered as “good.” The 70% threshold for categorizing “good” vs. “poor” KAP is consistent with established benchmarks used in public health research to denote adequate knowledge or practice levels [19, 20].

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel (2021), IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., NY, USA), and Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, TX, USA). Excel was used for data cleaning, coding, and sorting, and the finalized dataset was imported into SPSS to compute descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, summarizing the socio‐demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of respondents. Bivariate associations were assessed using Chi‐square tests of independence (reported as χ² and p‐values). To identify independent predictors of outcomes, multivariable analyses were performed in Stata. Linear regression models were fitted to examine predictors of continuous knowledge, attitude, and practice scores, while logistic regression was used to assess factors associated with a dichotomized outcome (results presented in Table 9).

Results

General Characteristics of Participants

The demographic profile of the study participants is presented in Table 1. A total of 397 respondents were included, with females comprising a larger proportion (63.0%) compared to males (37.0%). The majority of participants were aged between 15 and 35 years, with 33.2% in the 15–25 age category and 32.7% in the 26–35 category. Respondents aged 36–45 years accounted for 20.9%, while those aged 46–55 and 56–65 years represented smaller proportions (8.8% and 4.3%, respectively). Regarding marital status, half of the participants were single (50.6%), 42.1% were married, and fewer were divorced (3.5%), widowed (3.3%), or widowers (0.5%). Christianity was the predominant religion (74.3%), followed by Islam (21.2%) and African traditional religion (4.5%).

Educational attainment showed that more than one‐third of the respondents had completed senior high school (34.5%), while 28.0% had tertiary education, 20.2% had junior high education, and smaller proportions had primary (12.1%) or no formal education (5.3%). Knowledge of Trichomoniasis

Respondents demonstrated varying levels of awareness of STIs and trichomoniasis, with HIV (38.0%), gonorrhea (28.7%), and syphilis (18.4%) being the most commonly identified, while candidiasis (3.3%), chlamydia (6.3%), herpes (4.3%), and warts (1.0%) were rarely mentioned (Table 2). Nearly half of the participants (47.9%) reported having experienced a prior genital infection, yet only 45.6% sought care at a health facility, indicating gaps in health‐seeking behaviors. In terms of information sources on trichomoniasis, television (21.4%) and health workers (20.4%) were the leading channels, followed by college workers (18.4%) and friends (15.9%), while parents (3.8%) and social media (7.1%) contributed minimally. These findings highlight that while general awareness of common STIs exists, knowledge of T. vaginalis and consistent health facility utilization remain limited (Table 2).

The responses to the knowledge questions on trichomoniasis reflected notable gaps in awareness among the respondents (Table 3). Nearly half of the participants (49.6%) recognized that trichomoniasis is curable, while 35.8% indicated they did not know, and 14.6% believed it was not curable. A majority (58.4%) correctly acknowledged that both men and women of reproductive age can be infected, although 32.0% were uncertain and 9.6% disagreed. Similarly, half of the respondents (50.1%) agreed that the risk of infection increases with multiple sexual partners, but more than one‐third (37.5%) did not know. Awareness of the link between trichomoniasis and other STIs such as HIV was reported by 47.6% of respondents, while 16.1% disagreed and 36.3% indicated they were unsure. Knowledge of complications was inconsistent; although 48.4% knew that trichomoniasis infection could cause cervical cancer and infertility in women, 42.8% were uncertain, and 44.6% identified that infection during pregnancy could lead to severe complications.

Misconceptions were evident in responses to preventive and symptomatic items. While 44.6% believed having sex with a trusted partner was a form of protection, 23.7% disagreed and 31.7% were unsure. Less than half (43.6%) knew that males may present with penile irritation and urethritis, and only 27.0% rejected the misconception that infection can be judged by appearance, while 36.8% remained unsure. Regarding reinfection, 39.0% agreed that a previous infection does not confer immunity, whereas 41.6% did not know. Preventive misconceptions were common: 40.6% believed washing genitals after sex could protect against infection, and 22.4% thought emergency contraceptives could prevent T. vaginalis. Recognition of symptoms among women was also limited, with 40.8% linking infection to pain during sex, 39.8% to painful urination, and only 33.0% to vaginal discharge, while over half (52.1%) did not know. Finally, 33.5% acknowledged that infected individuals may not show symptoms, but 42.8% were uncertain, highlighting significant knowledge deficits regarding the asymptomatic nature of the infection.

Attitudes Toward Prevention

The attitudes of respondents toward the prevention of trichomoniasis revealed both positive perceptions and notable reservations (Table 4). A majority endorsed protective behaviors, with 65.5% supporting abstinence until marriage and 80.9% preferring one faithful sexual partner. Similarly, 73.6% recognized condoms as a reliable means of protection, and 72.8% reported they would insist on condom use before sex with a casual partner. However, ambivalence and misconceptions were also evident. While 44.8% acknowledged the risk of trichomoniasis through unprotected sex, more than half (55.2%) did not accept this risk. Additionally, 59.4% believed condoms decrease sexual pleasure, 64.2% considered condom use to provide an unnatural sexual experience, and 55.2% agreed that condoms cause unnecessary delay and interference during intimacy. Furthermore, 40.6% admitted feeling embarrassed to ask a partner to use a condom. These findings highlight that although respondents expressed awareness of the protective value of condoms and fidelity, negative attitudes toward condom use remain prevalent.

Sexual and Hygiene Practices

As shown in Table 5, respondents indicated varying patterns of risk‐related behaviors on sexual and hygiene practices. The majority reported not engaging in practices that could increase vulnerability to infection, with 86.4% not sharing underwear and 78.6% not sharing towels, although 12.1% and 19.9% admitted doing so occasionally, respectively. Sharing of toilet seats was more common, as 41.8% did so occasionally and 21.7% always, compared to 36.5% who never engaged in this practice. Regarding the use of sex toys, 81.4% reported never using them, while 12.8% used them occasionally and 5.8% always. Sharing of sex toys was largely uncommon, with 86.6% reporting never sharing, though 12.8% admitted occasional sharing.

In terms of sexual practices, 23.9% of respondents reported always having unprotected vaginal intercourse, while 40.1% did so occasionally and 36.0% abstained from it. Oral sex was practiced occasionally by 21.9% and always by 0.8%, while 77.3% reported never engaging in the act. Anal sex was even less common, with 12.3% reporting occasional practice and 87.7% denying it entirely. Hygiene practices following sexual intercourse were relatively positive, as 54.2% indicated they always washed or wiped their genitals, 20.4% did so occasionally, and 25.4% did not. These findings suggest that although a proportion of respondents engaged in risky sexual behaviors such as unprotected sex, most reported relatively safe hygiene practices following intercourse.

Religious affiliation was not a significant predictor across any of the KAP domains. The number of sexual partners stood out as an important predictor, particularly for preventive practices. Respondents with two partners (β = –1.013, p = 0.001, 95% CI [–1.617, –0.410]) or three partners (β = –1.655, p < 0.001, 95% CI [–2.414, –0.897]) reported significantly lower practice scores compared to those with one partner, indicating riskier sexual behaviors in these groups. Multivariable Logistic Regression of Predictors of Good Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice

The multivariable logistic regression identified several factors that significantly predicted knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward trichomoniasis among respondents (Table 9). Sex did not emerge as a significant determinant across the three domains. Compared to females, males were more likely to demonstrate good knowledge (AOR = 1.983, p = 0.188, 95% CI [0.715, 5.522]) and good practices (AOR = 1.483, p = 0.335, 95% CI [0.666, 3.305]), though the effects were not statistically significant. Similarly, the odds of positive attitudes were nearly identical between males and females (AOR = 0.979, p = 0.964, 95% CI [0.396, 2.421]). These findings suggest that gender differences did not independently account for variations in knowledge, attitudes, or practices when controlling for other factors.

Age group analysis revealed some noteworthy trends, though statistical significance was limited. Respondents aged 56–65 years were more likely to have good knowledge (AOR = 6.284, p = 0.089, 95% CI [0.757, 52.164]) compared to those aged 15–25, though this finding was marginal. Age showed no significant influence on attitudes, with odds ratios close to one across groups, such as 26–35 years (AOR = 1.155, p = 0.681, 95% CI [0.579, 2.302]). Practice scores were similarly unaffected by age, with respondents aged 36–45 years, for instance, having an AOR of 0.687 (p = 0.367, 95% CI [0.303, 1.552]). Collectively, this indicates that age did not substantially influence attitudes and practices in this population, though older respondents showed a tendency toward better knowledge.

Marital status, in contrast, revealed significant associations. Married participants were less likely to demonstrate good knowledge compared to single respondents (AOR = 0.367, p = 0.039, 95% CI [0.141, 0.952]). Attitudes were strongly affected among divorced respondents, who were significantly less likely to exhibit positive attitudes (AOR = 0.080, p = 0.011, 95% CI [0.012, 0.564]). Practices, however, did not vary significantly by marital status, with odds ratios such as 0.806 (p = 0.493) for married respondents and 0.627 (p = 0.560) for divorced respondents. This pattern suggests that marital context plays an important role in shaping knowledge and attitudes, but not necessarily preventive behaviors.

Education and occupation did not significantly predict knowledge, attitudes, or practices in the adjusted models. Compared to those without formal education, respondents with primary schooling had similar odds of good knowledge (AOR = 0.824, p = 0.864, 95% CI [0.090, 7.580]), positive attitudes (AOR = 3.072, p = 0.125, 95% CI [0.731, 12.893]), and good practices (AOR = 1.122, p = 0.879, 95% CI [0.252, 4.998]). Likewise, students, informally employed, and formally employed individuals showed no significant differences across outcomes, though formally employed respondents had somewhat higher odds of good knowledge (AOR = 5.394, p = 0.182). These findings indicate that educational attainment and occupational status, while often predictors of health literacy, were not strong determinants of knowledge, attitudes, or practices in this sample.

Reproductive history showed more relevance in predicting outcomes. While parity did not influence knowledge, biparous respondents were significantly more likely to report good practices (AOR = 3.629, p = 0.032, 95% CI [1.114, 11.823]) compared to nulliparous participants. This may reflect increased exposure to reproductive health services among women with multiple pregnancies. Attitudes, however, did not vary significantly across parity groups, with primiparous respondents having an AOR of 1.579 (p = 0.463, 95% CI [0.466, 5.343]). Number of children was also not predictive of KAP, as none of the categories achieved statistical significance. These results highlight that while reproductive experiences do not uniformly shape knowledge or attitudes, they may encourage safer practices through health system interactions.

The most consistent predictors were number of sexual partners and prior awareness of T. vaginalis. Respondents with two partners had lower odds of good practices (AOR = 0.362, p = 0.005, 95% CI [0.178, 0.737]), and those with three partners showed even stronger negative associations (AOR = 0.014, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.002, 0.119]). In contrast, sexual partners did not significantly influence knowledge or attitudes. Awareness of trichomoniasis was strongly associated with higher knowledge (AOR = 2.949, p = 0.002, 95% CI [1.464, 5.938]) but was unexpectedly associated with lower odds of good practices (AOR = 0.607, p = 0.042, 95% CI [0.375, 0.983]), suggesting that knowledge does not always translate into protective behavior. These findings emphasize the complex relationship between information and behavior and point to multiple sexual partnerships as a critical barrier to effective prevention.

Discussion

This study found low levels of knowledge, predominantly negative attitudes, particularly toward condom use, and moderate levels of self‐reported preventive practices regarding trichomoniasis. Our results identify that behavioral factors, specifically having multiple sexual partners, were the strongest independent predictor of poorer practices, while socio‐demographic factors like formal education showed limited predictive power when accounting for other variables. This triad of findings, such as severe knowledge deficits, attitudinal barriers, and the primacy of behavioral risk, highlights that current preventive actions are likely not rooted in a firm understanding of the infection, leaving the community vulnerable to infection and its sequelae.

The profound knowledge deficit identified reflects a broader, global pattern of neglect toward trichomoniasis, which remains one of the most common yet overlooked STIs, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) where its burden is highest [16, 21, 22]. This neglect is evident in our data; while participants demonstrated high awareness of HIV and syphilis, knowledge of trichomoniasis was critically low (34.3% had heard of it), confirming that it is overshadowed by higher‐profile infections [22, 23, 24]. This finding is consistent with the alarmingly low levels of awareness reported in other LMIC settings across sub‐Saharan Africa [11, 12, 16], underscoring that inadequate community knowledge is a pervasive regional challenge. Consequently, individuals are ill‐equipped to accurately assess their personal risk or recognize symptoms, a cornerstone of many health behavior models.

The Health Belief Model (HBM) posits that perceived susceptibility and severity are prerequisites for action [25]. Our findings indicate that these perceptions are critically low for trichomoniasis. Furthermore, the prevalence of misconceptions, such as the belief in the protective effects of genital washing or emergency contraceptives, reveals a fertile ground for misinformation that public health initiatives must actively counter with clear, accurate messaging.

The paradoxical attitudes toward prevention by endorsing protective methods like condom use and fidelity while simultaneously holding strong negative beliefs about condoms reducing pleasure and causing embarrassment reflect a significant cognitive dissonance. This conflict can be interpreted through the lens of the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), where attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control interact to shape intention and, ultimately, behavior [26]. The negative attitudes and perceived social barriers appear to be formidable obstacles that likely weaken the intention to use condoms consistently, despite knowledge of their benefits. This highlights that interventions aiming to increase condom use must move beyond mere provision and information dissemination to address these deeply ingrained attitudinal and social barriers through skills‐building and normative change interventions.

Our multivariate analysis offers crucial insights for tailoring public health interventions. The strong independent association between having multiple sexual partners and poorer preventive practices underscores that behavioral factors are paramount in determining risk. This reinforces the need for targeted interventions for individuals with multiple partners, focusing on accessible testing, partner notification strategies, and reinforced condom negotiation skills. Conversely, the finding that formal educational attainment was not a significant independent predictor of KAP in the adjusted model is revealing. It suggests that general education, while important, does not automatically translate into specific knowledge about trichomoniasis. This implies that awareness of this infection is not being systematically integrated into standard educational or health curricula, pointing to a clear opportunity for the development and integration of structured, STI‐specific educational modules within school and community health programs.

From a public health perspective, these findings compel a move beyond generic health education campaigns toward targeted, evidence‐based interventions. Specifically, three key strategies are supported by our data: First, given that healthcare workers were a key information source, our findings support the integration of brief, structured trichomoniasis education into routine consultations at antenatal clinics, HIV testing centers, and youth‐friendly health services in Ghana. Second, public health campaigns should explicitly debunk the prevalent misconceptions identified (such as the ineffectiveness of emergency contraceptives and genital washing) using local mass media channels like television. Third, Interventions must specifically target individuals with multiple sexual partners, a strong predictor of poor practices, through peer‐led initiatives and community‐based screening.

It must be emphasized that the role of healthcare workers is pivotal. They are a trusted information source and represent a critical touchpoint for improving diagnosis, treatment, and patient education. Strengthening the capacity of primary health services to offer routine, non‐stigmatizing screening for trichomoniasis, coupled with effective patient counseling, could dramatically improve case detection and management while simultaneously spreading accurate information within the community [27, 28, 29]. Integrating trichomoniasis screening into existing reproductive health and HIV service platforms presents a pragmatic and efficient strategy to increase its prioritization.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study has several strengths. First, it applied a rigorous analytical approach, combining bivariate and multivariable models to capture the complex factors shaping knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to trichomoniasis. Second, participants were selected through random sampling within a hospital that serves a socio‐demographically diverse municipality, which improves the representativeness of the findings for comparable urban populations in Ghana.

However, the study has some limitations worth noting. First, the cross‐sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences between the variables studied and KAP outcomes. Second, reliance on self‐reported data for sensitive sexual behaviors may have introduced social desirability bias, with the possibility of under‐reporting risky practices. Third, as a single‐center study, external validity is limited, and the findings are best generalized to urban or peri‐urban settings in West Africa.

Conclusion

This study revealed critically low levels of knowledge and predominantly negative attitudes toward trichomoniasis among sexually active individuals in the Ashaiman Municipality, despite moderate self‐reported preventive practices. The findings indicate a substantial gap in specific knowledge about trichomoniasis with only a minority of respondents accurately identifying its cause, symptoms, and prevention methods, even though general awareness of STIs was high. Notably, having multiple sexual partners emerged as the strongest independent predictor of inadequate preventive practices. These results underscore that current preventive actions are not firmly rooted in a comprehensive understanding of trichomoniasis, leaving the community vulnerable to infection and its associated health sequelae. This study provides critical evidence that effective public health interventions must address both the profound knowledge deficits and behavioral risk factors through targeted, culturally appropriate strategies.
