Neurodevelopmental delays in children born after medically assisted reproduction: a national population cohort study
Authors: Seung-Ah Choe, Eunseon Gwak, Juyoung Lee, Jung Hye Byeon, Ju-Young Shin, Seungbong Han, Jee Hyun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImportance: The neurodevelopmental outcomes of children born after medically assisted reproduction remain incompletely characterized on a population level. We were to assess the incidence and relative risk of neurodevelopmental delay in the children of infertile women born after unassisted conception, intrauterine insemination (IUI), and in vitro fertilization (IVF).MethodWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the National Health Insurance Service database of South Korea from October 2017 through December 2023. The study included 115 839 singleton, full-term, non–low–birthweight children born to women aged 20–49 years with a diagnosis of female infertility. The exposure was mode of conception—spontaneous, IUI, or IVF—balanced by propensity score matching on preconceptional maternal factors. Outcome measure was neurodevelopmental delay, defined as scoring below the cutoff in any of six functional domains on the Korean Developmental Screening Test across six consecutive rounds. Crude incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsAmong 115 839 children, 6 575 (5.7%) exhibited delay in at least one domain. Crude IRRs for any delay were 1.19 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.07, 1.61) for IUI and 1.10 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.17) for IVF versus unassisted conception. In the survival models, higher risks persisted for both IUI (aHR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.06, 1.34) and IVF (1.09, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.06) compared with unassisted conception. When comparing IVF and IUI, IVF conferred a lower risk of fine motor delay than IUI (0.84, 95% CI: 0.72, 0.99). Among IVF births, risk of developmental delay was similar across all six domains for frozen and fresh embryo transfers.ConclusionsChildren conceived via IUI or IVF demonstrated a modest but significant increase in screen-positive neurodevelopmental delay compared with those conceived spontaneously. These findings highlight the need for neurodevelopmental monitoring in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11689-025-09658-w.Keywords: Assisted reproduction, Neurodevelopment, Developmental screening test

Background

Assisted reproductive technologies (ART) have revolutionized the management of infertility, providing viable solutions for millions of couples worldwide. Since the first live birth following in vitro fertilization (IVF) nearly five decades ago, ART procedures have been responsible for the birth of approximately 12 million children globally. This remarkable advancement has not only transformed clinical practice in reproductive medicine but has also stimulated ongoing research into optimizing treatment protocols and understanding the long-term outcomes associated with these technologies [1].

As ART births continue to rise, interest in understanding the long-term health and developmental outcomes of children born after these methods has also grown. Some studies indicate a generally higher risk of cerebral palsy, learning and motor function disorders, autism spectrum disorder, and developmental delays, although these outcomes are largely attributed to parental factors, the prevalence of multiple births, and prematurity [2]. For example, compared with natural conception, IVF/ICSI pregnancies are independently associated with higher risks of both spontaneous and iatrogenic preterm birth, a major intermediate factor in the association between ART and neonatal development [3]. In the context of maternal and child safety, reducing multiple births represents the most significant safety advancement in ART [4]. However, concerns persist due to the increasing number of ART recipients, the limited long-term data on the health and development of children born after ART, and the potential impact of residual confounding factors. Some have attributed the higher risk associated with medically assisted reproduction to the generally unfavorable health conditions of subfertile patients [5]. Most prior studies, however, suffered from cross-sectional design, small sample sizes, relied on medical diagnoses of neurological disorders that capture only the most severe cases, and depended on healthcare utilization data, potentially introducing bias [6].

In October 2017, the South Korean government introduced universal health insurance coverage for IVF procedures for individuals experiencing infertility. As ART procedures have become more prevalent amid rising infertility rates and delayed parenthood, national population data offers a unique opportunity to explore developmental trends in ART-conceived children. Developmental delays—in cognitive, motor, and social domains—are critical markers of child health that can significantly influence future academic achievement and social functioning. Leveraging the comprehensive national data, we assessed the risk of developmental delays among children born after ART compared with those born after intrauterine insemination (IUI) or unassisted conception. By identifying patterns and potential risk factors, the research aims to enhance clinical guidelines and promote early interventions, ultimately fostering improved outcomes for this growing population.

Methods

Data

This retrospective cohort study used a customized data provided by the National Health Information Database of the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of South Korea from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2023. Covering >95% of all Korean nationals living in South Korea, the database contains clinical information on diagnoses, prescribed medications, procedures, and treatments covered by the NHIS for every visit to a health facility, as well as demographic information including employment status, household income in percentile-ranking, and residential region. To minimize the baseline differences between mother-child pairs according to the mode of conception, we constructed a national cohort of women with the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision code of ‘female infertility (N97)’ and their babies. Although preterm birth, low birthweight, and multiple gestations are important intermediaries in the pathway from mode of conception to child health outcomes, our primary analysis restricted to singleton, full-term, and non-low birthweight children to reduce confounding due to birth complications and isolate the direct association [7].

Among the 264,963 women diagnosed with female infertility during the study period, 213,651 were successfully linked to children’s data born in 2018–2021 (Fig. 1). We restricted the children’s data to those born in 2021 or earlier. This ensured at least a two-year observation period per child. Exclusion of those with no record of first-trimester obstetric ultrasound (T1US, n = 47,096), no record of Korean Developmental Screening Test (K-DST, n = 3,203), siblings of multiple gestation (n = 12,532), low birth weight (n = 26,323), and preterm birth (n = 13,178) resulted in a final study population of 115,839 children. This study was carried out in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data guidelines.

Identification of children born after IUI or IVF

We identified children conceived with IUI or IVF based on the corresponding treatment codes (Table S1). All relevant treatment and procedure codes were referenced the official repository that provides the continually updated code list (https://opendata.hira.or.kr/op/opc/olapDiagBhvInfoTab1.do#none). The complete set of procedure codes used in this study was reviewed and approved by the two board-certified reproductive endocrinologists in South Korea, thereby supporting both the comprehensiveness and the clinical validity of our coding approach [8]. Despite the availability of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) procedure codes, assessment of ICSI exposure or male-factor infertility was not possible since, for frozen ET cycles, the NHIS data do not indicate whether fertilization was via ICSI or IVF.

Because the expected date of birth was recorded only for 17% of children, we estimated last menstrual period (LMP) for each birth based on the date of IUI or ET procedure for those missing for gestational age. For those born after IUI, LMP was defined as 14 days before the day of IUI. LMP for the pregnancies with IVF was calculated 17 days before the day of ET. To compare neurodevelopmental delay risk between fresh and frozen ET, we identified births from ET cycles classified as fresh—defined by the presence of an oocyte retrieval code within seven days of ET.

For children conceived spontaneously, the date of second round of T1US was used to calculate LMP [9]. In South Korea, two rounds of T1US are covered by the NHIS when conducted during first trimester. The Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service applies an adjudication process that enhances the validity of NHIS claims—including prescription codes—by verifying that billed services meet coverage criteria. For pregnancy-episode identification, we used an algorithm built on administrative claims and iteratively adapted to data availability and clinical practice patterns to minimize misclassification of pregnancy outcomes [9, 10]. Since the median interval between LMP and the second T1US was 85 days among children with available gestational age data, we estimated LMP as occurring 85 days before the second T1US date.

Outcome of neurodevelopmental delay

The K-DST was first developed in 2014 by the Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare along with the Korean Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Since its inception, the K-DST has been implemented nationwide by the National Health Screening Program for Infants and Children (NHSPIC) to screen neurodevelopmental delays in all infants and young children living in Korea. The NHSPIC consists of a total of seven rounds. Excluding the first screening conducted at 4 to 6 months of age, the K-DST is administered during six subsequent rounds. Specifically, the K-DST is conducted at the following ages: 9–12 months (1st test), 18–24 months (2nd test), 30–36 months (3rd test), 42–48 months (4th test), 54–60 months (5th test), and 66–71 months (6th test). The proportion of all eligible children nationwide who received the test ranged from 69.8% to 87.1% between 2015 and 2023 [11]. The K-DST has shown satisfactory reliability and validity. Its internal consistency levels are notably high, with scores ranging from 0.73 to 0.93. Additionally, test-retest reliability indices between 0.77 and 0.88 indicate good temporal stability. The sensitivity and specificity at the cutoff point were 0.83 and 0.98, respectively, demonstrating strong validity for the diagnosis of developmental delay in the pediatric population [12].

The K-DST evaluates six domains: gross motor, fine motor, cognition, language, social skills, and self-help, with self-help domain assessments beginning at 18 months (Table S2). Children with K-DST scores less than two standard deviations of the domain scores for each age group are considered abnormal, warranting a “recommendation for further evaluation” [12]. Those who exhibit abnormal results in any domain were defined as having neurodevelopmental delays. Because the K-DST identifies children screen-positive, it does not establish a diagnosis and requires subsequent clinical evaluation.

Covariates

Our explanatory model for neurodevelopmental delay in children incorporated pre-conceptional factors previously identified in the literature as being associated with both the exposure and the outcomes [8, 13–15]: maternal age, maternal employment status (employed vs. non-employed), residence in the Seoul capital area, household income level, presence of maternal disability, obesity, smoking history, anemia, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), substance use disorder, and asthma. Income was categorized into five groups for analytical convenience: medical beneficiaries, lowest income (0–30th percentile), lower income (30–60th), higher income (60–80th), and highest income (80–100th percentile). Residence in the Seoul capital area (comprising Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi Province) was used as a proxy for high socioeconomic status, given the region’s urban setting, elevated cost of living, and greater healthcare accessibility.

Data on maternal obesity, smoking, and anemia were obtained from health examinations conducted before pregnancy. Obesity and anemia were defined as a body mass index (BMI) >25 kg/m² and hemoglobin < 12 g/dL, respectively. A CCI score ≥ 3 was used to indicate maternal multimorbidity before conception [16]. Substance use disorder and asthma, additional conditions included in the Obstetric Comorbidity Index (OCI), were incorporated to better capture the risk of severe maternal morbidity compared with the CCI alone [17]. Because the information on paternal clinical status was not available, covariates included in the model were restricted to maternal characteristics. Recognizing that obstetric complications and adverse perinatal factors may act as mediators of the association between mode of conception and neurodevelopmental outcomes, these factors were not included in our models (Supplemental Fig. 1) [18–20].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics of mothers and children according to mode of conception. Incidence rates of neurodevelopmental delay (per 1,000 person-months) were calculated overall and separately for each of the six domains of the K-DST. To balance the baseline characteristics across the groups, propensity score (PS) matching was conducted separately for each pairwise comparison, using varying matching ratios to maximize sample size: 1:4 for unassisted conception versus IUI, 1:3 for unassisted conception versus IVF and IVF versus IUI. Frozen ET births were 1:1 matched with fresh ET births. Matching employed a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations of the logit of the PS. Covariate balance was assessed using absolute standardized differences, with values below 0.1 considered acceptable.

Incidence rates were estimated using Poisson regression model with a log link function. Associations between mode of conception and neurodevelopmental delay were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models. For each child, we calculated the observation time from birth until first diagnosis of neurodevelopmental delay according to the K-DST or date of the latest round of the K-DST, whichever came first. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed with scaled Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant time-dependent trend was observed. Adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) for covariates were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models to compare the risk of neurodevelopmental delay across four pairs: IUI with unassisted conception, IVF with unassisted conception, IVF with IUI, and frozen versus fresh ET cycles. To estimate the total effect of IUI or IVF procedures, we also presented aHRs for neurodevelopmental delay in models that included pregnancies with multiple gestation, preterm birth, and low birth weight.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we calculated E-values for any delays in the six domains of K-DST for pairs with positive associations. The E-value represents the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome, beyond the measured covariates, in order to fully explain the observed treatment–outcome relationship [21, 22]. All analyses and plotting were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (v4.3.2; The R Foundation 2023).

RESULTS

Characteristics of mother-child pairs by mode of conception

A total of 115,839 children met our inclusion criteria: 85,513 (73.8%) with unassisted conception, 5,926 (5.1%) after IUI and 24,400 (21.1%) after IVF—of the latter, 9,929 (8.6%) were from fresh ET cycles and 14,471 (12.5%) from frozen ET cycles. Mean maternal age was higher for those born after IVF (unassisted 34.2 ± 4.0; IUI 34.8 ± 3.4; IVF 36.2 ± 3.5 years; Table 1). Maternal employment during pregnancy was less common in the IVF group (34.0%) than in IUI (38.9%) or unassisted conception (38.4%). Residence outside the capital area was slightly more frequent in the IVF (57.1%) and IUI (57.4%) groups compared to unassisted conception (54.9%). The proportion of those with the highest income quartile were higher in the IVF group. Pre-pregnancy anemia, obesity, CCI ≥ 3 and substance use disorder did not differ across groups, while past smoking and asthma prevalence was marginally lower among IUI and IVF groups than in the unassisted conception group.

Among the 37,192 IVF births, those born after frozen ET involved slightly older mothers (36.2 ± 3.5 vs. 36.0 ± 3.6 years) and lower maternal employment rates (33.3% vs. 34.9%) than fresh ET (Table S3). A higher proportion of frozen ET group fell into the richest income quartile (34.9% vs. 31.7%). Obesity prevalence was lower in the frozen ET group (3.1% vs. 4.0%), while anemia was slightly more common (32.1% vs. 30.0%). Residence in non-capital areas, smoking history, disability status, CCI ≥ 3, substance use disorder, and asthma rates were comparable.

Incidence of neurodevelopmental delay in the matched datasets

Among 115,839 children born to women diagnosed with infertility, the overall prevalence of neurodevelopmental delay in any of the six domains assessed by the K-DST was 5.7% (n = 6,575). After PS-matching, the pre-conceptional characteristics were well balanced across matched groups (Table S4). In the matched datasets, the incidence rates of any neurodevelopmental delay ranged from 1.52 to 1.82 per 1,000 person-months, lowest in the unassisted conception and highest in the IUI group. Children born after IUI and IVF consistently exhibited higher proportions and incidence rates of delay across all domains compared with those conceived spontaneously (Table 2). For example, incidence rate ratios (IRR) for any delay of the six domains were 1.19 for IUI versus unassisted conception (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.07, 1.34) and 1.10 for IVF versus unassisted conception (95% CI: 1.03, 1.17). While the point estimate for developmental delay was lower for children conceived via IVF than for those born after IUI, overlapping confidence intervals indicate this difference was not statistically significant.

In the matched analyses comparing children from frozen versus fresh ET cycles, the distribution of characteristics was generally balanced between the two groups, except for living in non-capital areas, income quartiles, presence of disability, and prevalence of asthma (Tables S5). Risk of neurodevelopmental delay

In the comparative analysis for the risk of neurodevelopmental delay, children born after IUI had higher risk of any delay (aHR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.34) and across all six domains of the K-DST than those conceived spontaneously (Fig. 2). In comparison of IUI against unassisted conception, the largest point estimate was observed for fine motor function (1.45, 95% CI: 1.24, 1.69), although the CIs broadly overlapped with those of the other domains. Similarly, those born after IVF exhibited a modest but consistent elevation in risk across each domain compared with those conceived spontaneously (1.09, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.06 for any delay). Although the point estimates of the hazard ratios relative to the unassisted conception group were generally higher for IUI than for IVF, the confidence intervals overlapped across all six domains. Comparison between IVF and IUI revealed no higher risk of neurodevelopmental delay for IVF. For fine motor function, those born after IVF showed a lower risk of developmental delay compared to those born after IUI (0.84, 95% CI: 0.72, 0.99). When the analyses restricted to children born after IVF, risk of neurodevelopmental delay was similar between frozen ET and fresh ET across six domains (Fig. 3). In the model that retained multiple gestation, preterm birth, and low birth weight, children conceived via IUI or IVF consistently showed higher risks of screen-positive neurodevelopmental delay than those with unassisted conception across most K-DST domains (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analysis

Our bias analysis using E-value indicated that an unmeasured confounder associated with both the exposure and the outcome by a risk ratio of at least 1.67 could explain away the observed association. To move the confidence interval of the observed aHR to include the null, an unmeasured confounder associated with both the exposure and the outcome by a risk ratio of at least 1.32 could do so, but weaker confounding could not. For the comparison of IVF and unassisted conception groups, the magnitude of association was 1.09. The lower bound of the confidence interval corresponded to an E-value of 1.19, indicating a relatively modest level of unmeasured confounding would be sufficient to move the confidence interval to include the null.

DISCUSSION

In a large cohort of 115,839 children of women who were diagnosed with infertility, we observed a higher risk of neurodevelopmental delay among children born after medically assisted reproduction compared with spontaneously conceived children. These findings were observed consistently across different key domains of neurodevelopment, suggesting that IUI or IVF procedures may influence neurodevelopmental outcomes of children. Although the magnitude of the observed risk was modest, small relative effects can translate into meaningful population impact when baseline risks are substantial, and exposure prevalence is high. The risk for any delay remained high when we expanded the study population to include multiple gestations, preterm, and low birthweight infants. Our findings highlight the importance of monitoring neurodevelopment in children born after IUI or IVF.

Our findings are consistent with those of prior studies reporting worse neurodevelopmental outcomes among children born after ART. Although poorer outcomes in IVF-conceived children have largely been attributed to their higher rates of multiple gestation and low birth weight [23], singleton IVF offspring nonetheless exhibited an higher risk of learning disorders, motor function impairments, and autism spectrum disorder [24]. Higher risks of autism spectrum disorder and developmental delay in offspring born after IVF was observed compared with those natural conception [25]. On the other hand, in a study utilizing population-based survey data, IVF-conceived term children showed a lower risk of cognitive delays at 5.5 years and attention problems at 8 years of age [26]. When comparing children conceived with ART and those of couples with known infertility, no differences in the risk of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder were observed. The inconsistency will be attributed to the compatibility of IVF and non-IVF groups and heterogeneity in control groups across studies which may include births from IUI procedures [26]. However, it is important to note that the absolute risk increase is relatively small, and many children born after IUI or IVF do not exhibit neurodevelopmental delays. Additionally, residual confounding factors such as frequent healthcare utilization among ART users may contribute to over-reporting of developmental delays.

The observed higher risk associated with medically assisted reproduction may reflect both subfertility, defined by overall health status and underlying medical conditions, and procedural factors such as the use of medications, manipulation of gametes, and exposure to the in vitro environment, especially for IVF. The prevalence of neurodevelopmental delay in our cohort of women with infertility diagnosis, 5.7%, was higher than the 3.1% observed in the general population of South Korea [11]. This aligns with the prior observation of the association between subfertility and developmental problems in offspring [5]. Notably, IVF pregnancies exhibit higher rates of obstetric complications, compared to unassisted conceptions [27, 28]. These complications are well-established risk factors for developmental delays, thus possibly mediating some of the higher risks observed in ART offsprings [29, 30]. Our restriction of study population to singleton, term, and non-low birthweight offsprings would imply additional mechanisms beyond adverse perinatal outcomes may also contribute to the observed risk.

Direct comparisons between IUI and IVF conceptions revealed a slightly lower risk of developmental delay in fine motor function among IVF-conceived children, a novel finding warranting cautious interpretation. The outcome of fine motor function among children born after IUI or IVF has not been extensively reported. This nuanced difference highlights the potential heterogeneity in the medically assisted reproduction technique or underlying infertility etiologies affecting developmental outcomes [4]. One possible explanation is that IVF cycles more frequently employ progesterone support during early pregnancy than IUI cycles. Although animal studies suggest a neuroprotective effect of progesterone, this has not been translated into clinically significant differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes in human offspring [31]. Because progesterone prescriptions are not covered by the NHIS and thus are absent from its dataset, it was impossible to assess the impact of antenatal progesterone use. Given the overlap in confidence intervals across domains, further research is necessary to elucidate clinical relevance and underlying mechanisms.

Among ART-conceived children, the risk of neurodevelopmental delay did not significantly differ between fresh and frozen ETs. This finding coincides with studies reported comparable developmental outcomes between frozen and fresh ETs [32, 33], challenging previous concerns regarding potential epigenetic alterations and developmental differences due to cryopreservation techniques [6]. In a previous randomized controlled trial comparing frozen and fresh ET cycles, some developmental domains, such as problem-solving, were slightly better in the frozen ET group, but the differences were not clinically significant [32]. Given prior evidence of higher birthweight in frozen ET cycles [34, 35] and the potential mediating role of birthweight in neonatal neurodevelopment [36, 37], a lower risk of neurodevelopmental delay in frozen versus fresh ET would be expected. Our finding of comparable risk between the two groups therefore warrants replication.

The findings of this study need caution in interpretation. First, although we employed propensity score matching and conducted sensitivity analyses to enhance the robustness of our findings, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded due to the observational nature of the study. A persistent concern is that couples undergoing medically assisted reproduction may differ fundamentally in baseline health from those who conceive without treatment, such that observed risks reflect underlying infertility rather than effects of the treatment itself. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed information on genetic factors, environmental exposures, and parental psychosocial characteristics are warranted to better elucidate the nature and magnitude of neurodevelopmental risks associated with assisted reproductive technologies. Second, we could not evaluate the impact of ICSI or paternal-side risk factors. Studies incorporating detailed paternal health information such as paternal age, infertility etiology, and genetics would help confirm the independent effects of ART. Third, there is a potential misclassification inherent to screening tools. Differential care-seeking or surveillance could have inflated screen-positive rates. However, since our study population is restricted to mothers with an infertility diagnosis, such misclassification is likely non-differential with respect to conception mode, which would typically bias estimates toward the null. Lastly, we did not exclude or adjust for congenital anomalies because their diagnosis timing varies widely by condition and ascertainment pathway (e.g., prenatal vs. perinatal vs. postnatal detection up to several years), unlike preterm birth or low birth weight. Conditioning on these post-conception outcomes can introduce differential misclassification and, if on the causal pathway between ART and neurodevelopment, constitute overadjustment that biases affect estimates [38]. We believe our findings from primary analyses would not change significantly by excluding or adjusting for congenital anomaly, given the result from total effect analysis.

CONCLUSION

In summary, while medically assisted reproduction appears associated with modestly higher risks of neurodevelopmental delays compared to unassisted conception, the absolute risk remains relatively low. These findings emphasize the importance of providing comprehensive counselling to prospective parents undergoing assisted reproduction procedures, highlighting both the generally favorable prognosis and the necessity of careful developmental follow-up in children born after medically assisted reproduction.
