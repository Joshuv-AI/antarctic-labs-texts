Cyclic Tetraindoles with a Cyclooctatetraene Core: Controlling Planarity and Antiaromatic Character via N‐Alkylation
Authors: Keisuke Fujimoto, Ryo Hirose, Hyuma Masu, Masaki Takahashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyclooctatetraene (COT), a representative 4n π‐electronic system, typically exhibits nonaromatic character in highly twisted structures, but planarization significantly alters its electronic properties, increasing antiaromaticity. Therefore, investigations on the structure‐property relationships of COT derivatives are essential for the development of future functional materials. In this study, we have developed a series of cyclic tetraindoles possessing a central COT ring. The degree of molecular distortion can be controlled by N‐alkylation. Specifically, substitution of the N‐butyl groups increased the distortion, while the structural fixation by ethylene linkers enhanced its planarity. As planarity increased, a noticeable red‐shift in the absorption wavelength, a decrease in oxidation potential, and a reduction in the HOMO‐LUMO gap were observed. These results can be attributed to the more effective conjugation of the COT ring with increased planarity, which led to an enhancement of the antiaromaticity.

Introduction

Cyclooctatetraene (COT), a representative 4n π‐electronic system, possesses a nonplanar D 2d boat‐like structure where the dihedral angles between neighboring double bonds are around 56°.[ 1 , 2 ] The highly twisted connection inhibits the effective cyclic π‐conjugation in the COT ring, generally making the COT derivatives nonaromatic or very weakly antiaromatic.[ 3 ] The D 2d boat‐like structure undergoes a fast ring inversion at room temperature through a planar D 4h transition state with the activation barriers of 10–13 kcal/mol (Figure 1a).[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] This value represents the inherent energy difference between the nonplanar D 2d structure and the planar D 4h structure. The instability of the planar D 4h structure is mainly attributed to the angle strain caused by large inner angles (135°), with the resonance effect, specifically antiaromatic destabilization, playing a secondary role.[ 6 , 7 , 8 ]

a) Ring inversion of COT, b) COT derivatives planarized by small‐ring annelation and embedding into a fused molecular framework, c) nitrogen‐substituted PHA with multiple redox states containing vinylene linker, and d) cyclic tetraindoles developed in this work.

To date, numerous planar COT derivatives have been discovered through chemical modifications that modulate the energetic relationship between the D 2d and D 4h structures. For example, the release of angle strain through small‐ring annelation[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] and the embedding of the COT ring into highly fused molecular frameworks[ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ] are effective design strategies to overcome the energetic disadvantage in the planar structure (Figure 1b). The theoretical and experimental investigations revealed that the planar COT derivatives exhibit considerable antiaromatic character, such as distinct paratropicity and narrow HOMO‐LUMO gaps. Furthermore, stabilization from Baird aromaticity[ 18 , 19 , 20 ] is the driving force that planarizes the COT core in the excited state, leading to photofunctionality based on the structural flexibility.[ 21 ] Therefore, research on the structure‐property relationship of COT is a crucial area of study, serving as the foundation for the development of organic semiconductors and photofunctional materials.[ 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 ]

In our previous research on nitrogen‐substituted polycyclic heteroaromatics (PHA) exhibiting multiple redox states, a reversible interconversion was observed in a molecule in which two nitrogen atoms are connected via a vinylene linker (Figure 1c).[ 26 , 27 ] Building on this work, we synthesized cyclic tetraindoles in which the vinylene linker was replaced with cyclooctatetraene (COT), a representative 4n π‐electronic system (Figure 1d), and found that the distortion of the COT ring can be controlled through N‐alkylation, allowing systematic tuning of molecular planarity and electronic properties.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of the cyclic tetraindoles 2–4 was summarized in Scheme 1. First, starting from 4‐tert‐butyl‐2‐ethynylaniline,[ 28 ] butadiyne precursor 1 was prepared by the Glaser reaction (see the Supporting Information).[ 29 ] Then, we attempted COT ring formation via a one‐pot intramolecular cyclization‐dimerization reaction using an Au/carbon catalyst.[ 30 ] The Au/carbon catalyst was prepared by the incipient wetness method, in which a solution of gold in aqua regia was slowly added to the activated carbon while stirring. The cyclization‐dimerization of 1 was efficiently promoted by heating a p‐xylene solution of 1 in the presence of the Au/carbon catalyst, resulting in the formation of 2 with a yield of 57%.

Synthesis of cyclic tetraindoles 2–4. a) Au/Carbon catalyst, p‐xylene, 120 °C, 57%; b) 1‐chlorobutane, KOH, DMSO, 50 °C, 75%; c) ethylene ditosylate, KOH, DMSO, 50 °C, 65%.

To explore structurally diverse tetraindole derivatives, we conducted fourfold N‐alkylation reactions. The N‐butyl derivative 3 was synthesized by a nucleophilic substitution with an excess amount of 1‐chlorobutane in 75% yield. In this compound, it is expected that steric hindrance between the butyl groups will induce greater ring strain. In the next step, the synthesis of the ethylene‐bridged derivative was pursued with the aim of developing a compound with reduced strain through structural fixation. After optimization of the electrophilic reagents, we found that ethylene ditosylate smoothly reacted with 2 to afford the bridged structure in a high yield of 65%. In this reaction, intramolecular nucleophilic substitution is significantly faster than the intermolecular one despite the use of an excess amount of ethylene ditosylate. These tetraindoles 2–4 proved to be sufficiently stable under ambient conditions despite the expected highly electron‐rich nature and some extent of antiaromaticity.

The 1H NMR of 3 provided information about the molecular stereochemistry and dynamic behavior. While all indole units are chemically equivalent, the protons Ha and Hb are diastereotopic and thus independently observed at 4.21 and 3.87 ppm. This fact indicated that the compound 3 possesses helical chirality and the conformational exchange between the enantiomers (P)‐3 and (M)‐3 is slower than the NMR time scale (∼ms−1) (Scheme 2).[ 31 ] A similar situation was also observed for compound 4, where the diastereotopic protons Hc and Hd were independently detected at 4.50 and 4.26 ppm. Even at a higher temperature of 100 °C, these signals were independently observed, indicating the high activation barrier of the ring inversion in the tetraindole derivatives.

a) Chemical shifts of diastereotopic protons Ha‐Hd and b) hypothetical racemization reaction of 3.

The differences in the molecular distortion of 2, 3, and 4, as well as a comparison with a pristine COT, were evaluated through density functional theory (DFT) calculations[ 32 ] and X‐ray crystallographic analysis[ 33 ] (Figure 2 and Table 1). The calculation on 2 showed that the bending angle was 37° and the dihedral angles were 45–47°. These values were slightly smaller than those of the pristine COT (39° and 53°), indicating that the indole‐fused structure slightly enhanced the planarity of the central COT ring. Compared to 2, the N‐butyl derivative 3 exhibited significantly increased bending angle (43°) and dihedral angles (55–58°), indicating that the steric hindrance around the N‐butyl groups certainly increases the molecular distortion. On the other hand, these angles of the ethylene‐bridged derivative 4 (30° and 32–40°) were much smaller than those of 2 and 3. This result indicates that structural constraints due to ethylene cross‐linking significantly enhance the planarity of the COT ring.

The differences in the molecular distortions of cyclic tetraindoles 2–4 were also confirmed experimentally by X‐ray crystallographic analysis. The NH derivative 2 formed a 1 : 1 cocrystal with AcOEt through bidentate hydrogen bonding on the NHs at indoles A and B. The bending angles (35–37°) and the dihedral angles (40–48°) showed good agreement with the results of the DFT calculations except for the hydrogen‐bonded site. The dihedral angle between indoles A and B decreased to 40°, highlighting the importance of intermolecular interactions in determining its structural strain. In the unit cell, two molecules of 2 formed a racemic pair through herringbone‐type CH‐π interactions. Furthermore, multiple CH‐π and π‐π interactions constructed a 2D π‐electronic network in the crystal packing (Figure S13). The crystal structure of N‐butyl derivative 3 showed a more distorted structure exhibiting large bending angles (42–44°) and dihedral angles (48–61°). The butyl groups extend out‐of‐plane, with the terminal CH3 positioned over the diagonal benzene ring. In the unit cell, two molecules of 3 form a racemic pair, but their π‐surfaces are spatially separated due to steric repulsion of the alkyl groups, preventing any close contact (Figure S14). In the crystal structure of 4, two independent molecules were observed in the unit cell. The smaller bending angles (30–31°) and dihedral angles (34–40°) indicate a more planar structure, which is attributed to the presence of the ethylene bridges. In the packing structure, a 2D molecular network was constructed, in which compound 4 aligned along the a‐axis through CH–π interactions and along the c‐axis through both CH–π and π–π interactions. (Figure S15). To evaluate the antiaromatic character of the central COT ring, we also calculated the harmonic oscillator model of aromaticity (HOMA) values[ 34 ] for each structure (Table S2). Compared with the negative HOMA value of pristine COT (–0.23), cyclic tetraindoles 2–4 exhibited small positive values in the range of 0.21–0.36, indicating relatively small bond‐length alternation.

The photophysical properties were evaluated by UV/vis absorption and fluorescence spectra in CH2Cl2 (Figure 3 and Table 2). These compounds exhibited weak visible absorption and strong UV absorption. The weakness of the S0‐S1 transition is a typical characteristic of the 4n π‐electronic system.[ 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ] Despite the same π‐conjugated networks, these tetraindoles 2–4 showed quite different absorption wavelengths depending on the extent of distortion. Namely, as the planarity of the COT ring increases, the absorption edge (λ abs.edge) shifts to longer wavelengths in the order of 3 (423 nm), 2 (466 nm), and 4 (565 nm). The optical HOMO‐LUMO gaps (ΔE opt) were calculated from the absorption edges to be 2.93 eV for 3, 2.66 eV for 2, and 2.19 eV for 4. While the ethylene‐bridged 4 are nonemissive, compounds 2 and 3 showed fluorescence emission at 572 and 505 nm with the fluorescence quantum yields of 0.6% and 12.8%, respectively. These results suggest that planarization leads to a significant quenching of the fluorescence of the tetraindole chromophore. Large Stokes shifts, especially in 2, suggested the substantial structural relaxation in the excited states.

Cyclic voltammetry also revealed that the electronic properties of 2–4 are highly dependent on their planarity (Figure 4 and Table 2). The measurements were conducted in CH2Cl2 utilizing the ferrocene/ferrocenium ion redox couple as an external standard. The NH‐form 2 exhibited reversible oxidation waves at –0.05 V (E ox.1) and 0.31 V (E ox.2), indicating its highly electron‐rich nature. Moreover, the relatively low and reversible second oxidation potential reflects a characteristic feature of the 4n π‐electron system: the stability of the dicationic species with aromatic character. The HOMO level of 2 was calculated to be –4.75 eV by following the equation E HOMO = –4.8 – E ox.1 [eV].[ 39 ] Compounds 3 and 4 similarly exhibited two‐step reversible oxidation waves, but their potentials were significantly different. Namely, the HOMO levels of 2–4 arranged in the order of 3 (–4.96 eV), 2 (–4.75 eV), and 4 (–4.60 eV), corresponding to their structural distortion. The LUMO levels, which were calculated from their HOMO levels and optical HOMO‐LUMO gaps, were also in the order of 3 (–2.03 eV), 2 (–2.09 eV), 4, and (–2.41 eV). Therefore, we can conclude that the HOMO‐LUMO gap narrows due to both the destabilization of the HOMO and the stabilization of the LUMO, which result from the more effective π‐conjugation in the COT ring as planarity increases.

Similar to the experimental findings, DFT calculations on compounds 2–4 showed that the HOMO‐LUMO gap decreases as planarity increases, with both the HOMO and LUMO levels fluctuating accordingly (Figure S9). In addition, time‐dependent (TD) DFT calculations provided simulated UV/vis absorption spectra, where the weak HOMO‐LUMO transitions were predominantly red‐shifted in the order of 3 (381 nm), 2 (434 nm), and 4 (525 nm) (Figure S10), showing a trend similar to the experimental spectra in CHCl3. Moreover, the antiaromaticity was evaluated by nucleus independent chemical shift (NICS)[ 40 ] at the center of the eight‐membered ring (Table 2). As for the N‐butyl derivative 3, small positive NICS(0)zz and NICS(1)zz values indicated its weakly antiaromatic character. As the molecular planarity increased, the NICS values increased significantly, and the large positive NICS(0)zz (+41.5 ppm) and NICS(1)zz (+25.8 ppm) values for the ethylene‐bridged derivative 4 indicated a certain degree of antiaromatic character.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully demonstrated that the electronic properties of cyclic tetraindoles can be systematically tuned by controlling the degree of planarity of the central COT core. The correlation between structural distortion and optoelectronic behavior clearly indicates that planarization enhances conjugation and increases antiaromatic character, leading to pronounced red‐shifted absorption and lowered oxidation potentials. These findings provide direct experimental evidence for the structure‐property relationships governing antiaromatic π‐systems and highlight the delicate balance between molecular geometry and electronic delocalization. This work establishes fundamental insights into the interplay between molecular distortion and antiaromaticity, paving the way for future studies on functional π‐conjugated systems with tunable electronic characteristics.
