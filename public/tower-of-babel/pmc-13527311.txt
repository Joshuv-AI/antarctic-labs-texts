Analysis of the curative effect of arthroscopic coracoid process drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation in the treatment of acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation
Authors: Gushang Xia, Yongsheng Liu, Daohong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the clinical effect of arthroscopic coracoid process drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation in the treatment of acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation.MethodsThis retrospective clinical study analyzed the data of 25 patients with acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation. The patients were divided into a drill-free group and a drilling group. The University of California at Los Angeles Shoulder Rating Scale, visual analog scale, and Constant scores as well as the shortest distance between the coracoid process and clavicle were evaluated before and after the surgery. Complications such as re-dislocation, loss of reduction, coracoid fracture, and clavicle fracture were evaluated.ResultsThe averaging follow-up duration was 36 months. The shortest distance between the coracoid process and clavicle was significantly reduced immediately postoperatively and at the final follow-up in both groups. At the final follow-up, the postoperative University of California at Los Angeles Shoulder Rating Scale, Constant, and visual analog scores of both groups were significantly improved compared with the preoperative scores (p < 0.01). However, two patients in the drilling group developed coracoid fractures and underwent surgery; no coracoid fractures were observed in the drill-free group.ConclusionThe use of arthroscopic coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation to treat acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation achieves satisfactory clinical outcomes and considerably reduces the incidence of coracoid fractures compared with the coracoid drilling technique.

Introduction

Acromioclavicular joint dislocation is a relatively common shoulder injury observed in young individuals who engage in sports and those who have experienced high-energy trauma, accounting for approximately 9%–12% of all shoulder injuries.1–3 According to the Rockwood classification system, injuries are divided into types 6 types (I to VI). 4 Currently, types I and II can be treated non-surgically, whereas types IV to VI require surgery. However, the optimal treatment for type III injuries remains controversial. Ceccarelli et al. have reported that there is lack of evidence to guide the treatment choice for acute type III acromioclavicular dislocation. 5 A review of the literature revealed no significant difference in clinical outcomes between nonsurgical and surgical treatments in patients with type III injuries.6–8 There may be no additional benefits of surgical treatment. However, surgery is often necessary for patients who do not exhibit improvement with nonsurgical treatment. Additionally, certain groups, such as those with high shoulder joint function demands, workers with heavy shoulder joint loads, and individuals who prefer surgery, may consider these factors when deciding on surgical treatment. There is no clear consensus on whether nonsurgical or surgical treatment is the optimal treatment approach for acromioclavicular dislocation. 9

In addition, there is no gold standard for the choice of surgical treatment modality. For instance, the choice of the clavicular hook plate can fully reduce the acromioclavicular joint and firmly fix it, which is beneficial to early functional exercise after shoulder surgery.10,11 However, this approach is associated with certain drawbacks, including acromion impingement and the necessity of subsequent surgery for removal. The TightRope fixation technique may more effectively restore the anatomical relationship of the coracoclavicular ligament; however, it involves a risk of coracoid clavicular fracture. According to Jeong and Chun, the surgical method should be selected according to the comprehensive situation of the patient and surgeon. 12 At present, the concept of arthroscopic anatomical treatment is globally recognized; however, most coracoclavicular ligament anatomical reconstruction techniques require the establishment of the clavicle and coracoid tunnels This technique may lead to iatrogenic fractures of the clavicle and coracoid iatrogenic fractures, fixation failure, reduction failure, and many other complications. Such patients often require revision surgery, which involves substantial challenges, increases the hospitalization costs for patients, and exacerbates the contradiction between doctors and patients.

The arthroscopic coracoid drilling TightRope fixation technique we used earlier also requires the establishment of a coracoid tunnel, 13 which is associated with considerable risks. This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical treatment of patients with acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation. The study used the arthroscopic coracoid drilling TightRope fixation technique in the early stage from May 2015 to June 2021, and the arthroscopic coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation technique in the later stage. No studies have compared these two techniques. We hypothesized that the arthroscopic coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation can achieve similar clinical efficacy with fewer complications.

Patients and methods

Patient selection criteria

From May 2015 to June 2021, 25 adult patients with acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation were treated at our hospital. Thirteen patients were treated using the arthroscopic coracoid drilling TightRope fixation technique and 12 using the arthroscopy coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation technique. The following inclusion criteria were applied: (a) time since injury ≤3 weeks, with no surgical treatment; (b) Rockwood classification type III to VI; (c) no chronic shoulder injury (such as rotator cuff tear, shoulder joint instability, anterior and posterior upper labrum lesions, shoulder joint adhesion, and acromioclavicular arthritis) and adjacent tumor diseases; and (d) no history of shoulder surgery or inferior vascular injury. All patient-related information has been fully deidentified to protect patient privacy. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before study enrollment, and all patients were treated by a senior surgeon. This retrospective clinical study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 as revised in 2024. This study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University and received their exemption.

General information

Drilling group. Eight men and five women aged 22–55 years, with an average age of 32.5 years were included. Six patients had lesions on the left and seven on the right. With respect to Rockwood classification, there were five, six, and two cases in groups III, IV, and V, respectively. The time from injury to surgery was 4–10 days, with an average of 5.8 days.

Drill-free group. Eight men and four women aged 19–52 years, with an average age of 30.5 years were included. Five patients had lesions on the left, and seven on the right. According to Rockwood classification, four, seven, and one cases were types III, IV, and V, respectively. The time from injury to surgery was 3–10 days, with an average of 6.5 days. There was no significant difference in the general characteristics, injury grade, time from injury to surgery, or body mass index between the two groups of patients (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Surgical methods

Patients in the drill-free group underwent arthroscopic coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation. All patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia in the beach-chair position with their heads turned as far as possible toward the healthy side. A standard posterior shoulder arthroscopic portal was first created as the viewing portal to access the glenohumeral joint for routine arthroscopy to rule out concomitant intra-articular injuries. The arthroscope was then withdrawn, and a subacromial working portal was established through the posterior portal after debridement of the subacromial bursa. Subsequently, anterolateral and anterior portals were created, and the coracoid process was identified along the course of the coracoacromial ligament. The coracoacromial ligament and conjoint tendon were used as core anatomical landmarks, and soft tissues were bluntly dissected layer by layer. Full subperiosteal dissection was performed to expose the coracoid body and base bony cortex. This allowed for exploration of the acromioclavicular joint and coracoclavicular ligament by entering from the surface of the coracoacromial ligament; complete rupture of the conoid ligament and trapezoid ligament was confirmed in all enrolled acute dislocation cases. Special attention was given to protect the stump of the ligament during the procedure. To achieve this, an anteromedial portal was used (located between the coracoid process and the clavicle). Creation of the anteromedial portal is guided by three key anatomical landmarks, including the coracoid tip, long head of the biceps tendon, and subscapularis tendon, to prevent neurovascular injury. The arthroscope is first inserted 0.5 cm medial to the coracoid tip, with the lens tip positioned at the coracoid tip initially. Subsequently, coracoid exposure is performed along the medial bony surface from the coracoid tip toward its base. Since all manipulations are maintained tightly against bone intraoperatively, exploration of the brachial plexus nerves is unnecessary. The medial side of the coracoid process was prepped, and partial release of the medial attachment of the pectoralis minor tendon to the coracoid process was performed. A right-angle forceps thread guide was then inserted from the medial side of the coracoid process, passed through the lower surface; it emerged from the lateral side. An absorbable thread was then inserted for use. An epidural needle was used to assist in positioning the clavicular tunnel. The medial tunnel was established at the anatomical location of the conoid ligament, whereas the lateral tunnel was placed at the anatomical point of the trapezoid ligament. Two 1-cm incisions were made on the upper surface of the clavicle to access the bone surface. For Rockwood Type III injuries, an additional small auxiliary incision was made to address the trapezoid fascia. Under guidance, two 4-mm bone tunnels were created, and the two ends of the absorbable wire encircling the coracoid were pulled through the tunnels. To secure the ligament, one end of the adjustable TightRope fixation sets was connected to one end of the absorbable wire and pulled into the bone tunnel. The other end of the absorbable wire was then tightened and pulled out from the other bone tunnel. When the loop plate reached the surface of the clavicle, the adjustable wire of the fixation set was tightened until the acromioclavicular joint was completely reduced, and the reduction process was completely monitored in real time via arthroscopic visualization (Figures 1 and 2).

In the drilling group, traditional arthroscopic guides were used to create three bone tunnels in total, including one coracoid tunnel and two clavicle tunnels. The diameter of the coracoid process tunnel was 4.5 mm, and the diameter of the clavicle tunnel was 4.0 mm, and a Twin Tail TightRope fixation device was placed to tighten and fix them. The detailed technique, representing our previously proposed arthroscopic coracoid drilling Twin Tail TightRope fixation technique, has been presented in Figures 3 and 4. 13 The Twin Tail TightRope Y-type looped titanium plate fixation system is manufactured by Arthrex. The clavicular titanium plate measures 8 mm in length, the coracoid titanium plate is 11 mm long, and No. 2 FiberWire sutures are utilized in the system.

Postoperative exercise

The affected limb was immobilized with a forearm sling within 6 weeks postoperatively. Passive shoulder rehabilitation began 1 week postoperatively, with active shoulder movements starting at the 6-week mark. Strength training commenced 3 months later. Participation in overhead and contact sports typically resumed 1 year postoperatively, once mobility and strength had been restored. Annual activities and strength were regained with the help of a professional rehabilitation specialist.

Clinical evaluation

Each patient underwent preoperative assessment and postoperative follow-up assessment using the University of California at Los Angeles (UCLA) Shoulder Rating Scale Shoulder Rating Scale score, Constant score, and visual analog scale (VAS) pain score. Horizontal stability of the acromioclavicular joint was assessed via anterior–posterior stress test during physical examination and axial x-ray radiography at each follow-up visit.

Imaging assessment

Each patient underwent x-ray imaging before, immediately after, and at the final follow-up of the surgery. These included frontal radiographs of the left and right shoulder joints. The shortest distance between the coracoid process and the clavicle (CCD) was measured as the distance between the uppermost edge of the coracoid process and the lowermost edge of the clavicle's distal lower cortex (Figure 5). This was used to assess the superior and inferior displacements of the clavicle. Loss of reduction was defined as ≥25% increase in CCD compared with that in the contralateral side; failure was defined as concurrent acromioclavicular dislocation at the final follow-up. 14

CCD: the shortest distance between the uppermost edge of the upper cortex of the coracoid process and the lowermost edge of the distal lower cortex of the clavicle.

Statistical analyses

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 17.0) was used to analyze the data, and data were expressed as mean ± SD values. Independent samples t-test was used for between-group comparisons, and repeated measures analysis of variance was used for pre- and postoperative within-group comparisons; the test level was set α =0.01.

Results

The incisions healed by primary intention in both groups, and no complications, such as clavicle fracture, osteolysis around the loop, infection, neurovascular juries, and internal leakage, were observed. Both groups of patients were followed up after surgery for 24–72 months, with an average follow-up duration of 36 months. In terms of imaging evaluation, the average CCD in the drilling group decreased from 21.8 mm before surgery to 8.8 mm immediately after the surgery and was 10.8 mm at the final follow-up. The average difference between the left and right sides decreased from 11.8 mm preoperatively to 1.1 mm immediately after the surgery and was 3.1 mm at the final follow-up. Meanwhile, in the drill-free group, the average CCD decreased from 22.1 mm preoperatively to 9.0 mm immediately after the surgery and was 10.5 mm at the final follow-up. The average difference between the left and right sides decreased from 12.5 mm preoperatively to 1.2 mm immediately after the surgery and was 3.5 mm at the final follow-up. Immediately after the surgery and at the final follow-up, the CCD of both groups was significantly reduced compared with that before the surgery, and the difference between the left and right was within the acceptable range (Table 3). At the final follow-up, the postoperative UCLA Shoulder Rating Scale, Constant, and VAS scores of both groups were significantly higher than those before the surgery, and the difference was significant (p < 0.01); however, there was no significant difference between the groups (Table 2). However, in the drilling group, the postoperative x-ray of two patients demonstrated that the graft construct had migrated superiorly through the coracoid, and computed tomography scan confirmed developed coracoid fracture complications (Figure 6); surgery was performed on these patients. No cases of coracoid fractures were observed in the drill-free group (Figure 7).

Discussion

The key finding of this study is that in patients with acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation, at the final follow-up after the arthroscopic coracoid process drill-free U-suspension fixation, clinical and radiological outcomes were comparable with those in patients who had undergone coracoid tunnel TightRope fixation; additionally, there were no coracoid fractures in the group that underwent the arthroscopic coracoid process drill-free U-suspension fixation.

The current coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction techniques require the establishment of a clavicular tunnel and, in most cases, a coracoid tunnel. An anatomical study of the coracoid process conducted by Lian et al. revealed that the thickness between the tip and base of the coracoid process is 11.12 mm. 15 Another anatomical study by Rios, Arciero and Mazzocca have reported that the mean width and height of the coracoid process are 24.9 mm and 11.9 mm, respectively, and the mean length is 45.2 mm. 16 This evidence suggests that the coracoid process is a short, flat, narrow bony structure with limited maneuvering space for surgeons, making tunneling difficult. Once the tunnel is established, there may not be any opportunity to adjust the tunnel position. Simultaneously, the coracoid tunnel is associated with a higher risk of fracture, and the double tunnel is associated with an even greater risk. A biomechanical study has shown that a 4-mm tunnel was created in the coracoid process with an average width of 25.1 mm, and the coracoid process was at a higher risk of fracture. 17 Those with short stature and a small coracoid process are at a higher risk of fracture. In addition, Ferreira et al. shown that when the coracoid tunnel was established, the upper entrance and lower exit of the tunnel in the center-center or middle-center direction could reduce the fracture risk, with a higher risk of coracoid fracture in the other tunnel directions. 18 This highlights the need for surgeons with a high level of expertise. For most surgeons, it is challenging to establish a standard direction tunnel, and the coracoid process is associated with a higher risk of fracture. To date, for the anatomy of the coracoid process in Chinese patients, although some studies based on fresh specimens, dried specimens, and computed tomography three-dimensional reconstructions have given more detailed anatomical parameters, used to guide intraoperative positioning and reduce fracture risk, the risk of coracoid fracture objectively exists.19–21 If the coracoid process fractures early on, the reconstruction of the coracoclavicular ligament may fail, causing the acromioclavicular joint to dislocate again, presenting significant challenges for revision. Milewski et al. conducted a study on coracoid single-tunnel reconstruction of the coracoid ligament. 22 The coracoid process was fractured 10 weeks postoperatively. This coracoid fracture was treated with revision surgery utilizing open reduction and internal fixation of the coracoid with screw fixation and revision coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction using a hamstring autograft utilizing a coracoid loop technique, and the reduction of the acromioclavicular joint was lost 8 weeks after the reoperation. In our study, two cases of coracoid fractures occurred in the drilling group, and surgery was performed; meanwhile, a coracoid tunnel was not established in the drill-free group, and coracoid fractures were prevented to a great extent.

Lu et al. used the Ligament Augmentation and Reconstruction System (LARS) artificial ligament for coracoid process drill-free suspension to reconstruct the coracoclavicular ligament in a study involving 24 patients with acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation. At a mean follow-up of 36 months, 20 anatomical reductions occurred, four patients lost the reduction slightly, and no coracoid fracture occurred. 23 In a study on coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction involving 17 patients conducted by Banffy, van Eck CF and ElAttrache, an artificial material was used to form a ring in the coracoid process for suspension, and satisfactory results were obtained at the 2-year follow-up, with no coracoid fractures. 24 In a case series involving 48 cases conducted by Li et al., only 8.3% of the patients had partial CCD loss; none experienced pain, instability, or coracoid fracture-related complications. 25 This evidence suggests that coracoclavicular ligament reconstruction with drill-free suspension fixation of the coracoid process not only achieves satisfactory clinical results but also prevents coracoid fractures, which is consistent with our results.

The technique we used provides effective support for the coracoclavicular ligament to maintain its anatomical position. It also preserves the ligament footprint and stump of the conoid and trapezoid ligaments, creating conditions for promoting ligament healing. The double-tunnel anatomical reconstruction is superior to the single-tunnel non-anatomical reconstruction mentioned in the literature in terms of strength, anti-rotation, and anatomy. Furthermore, it can prevent fixation failure and wire knot erosion, both of which are documented in the literature. These issues may arise from tunnel widening due to excessive stress, clavicle fractures, reduction loss caused by loosening square knots, and other associated complications.14,24

A potential complication of coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation technique is the Gigli saw effect, which refers to progressive bone erosion and cortical cutout induced by long-term frictional shear between high-strength braided suture and bare bony surface under cyclic shoulder loading. In our modified U-shaped suspension technique, two key strategies were adopted to mitigate this risk. First, the dual-loop U-shaped design distributes vertical and horizontal shear force across a broad contact area rather than a single focal point. Second, partial preservation of conjoint tendon and pectoralis minor soft tissue around the coracoid creates a biological buffer layer separating suture from cortical bone, reducing direct friction. Over a mean follow-up period of 36 months, no radiographic signs of coracoid osteolysis or suture-induced bone cutout were detected in the drill-free group, indicating that this U-shaped anatomical suspension design effectively lowers the risk of the Gigli saw effect.

This study has certain limitations. First, our overall sample size was small, which may have led to statistical biases in our conclusions, and future studies involving larger sample sizes are required to support our conclusions. Second, we followed patients for a short period, and some complications may not have been detected during this short-term follow-up.

Conclusion

The arthroscopic coracoid drill-free U-shaped suspension fixation technique is effective for treating acute acromioclavicular joint dislocation and provides satisfactory clinical outcomes while reducing the risk of coracoid fractures.
