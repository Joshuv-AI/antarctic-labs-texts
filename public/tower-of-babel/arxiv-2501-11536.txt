Navigating the Complexities of Multiple Redox State Interactions in Aqueous Systems
Shishir Mundra, Mohit Pundir, Barbara Lothenbach, David S. Kammer, Ueli M. Angst

Abstract




Abstract

Numerous aqueous systems host elements in multiple redox states, with wide ranging implications such as their influence on the formation/dissolution of minerals, water toxicity, and nutrient cycling. To uncover governing mechanisms and complex chemical interactions in aqueous systems, reactive-transport models have increasingly gained importance. However, their predictive capabilities remain limited because existing approaches struggle to accurately account for the full complexities of redox reactions. Here, we develop a reactive-transport framework that leverages recent advancements in thermodynamic modelling, speciation chemistry, and redox kinetics. Distinct from traditional models, we uniquely treat redox kinetics along with transport as transient phenomena, decoupled from Gibbs free energy minimisation. Ensuring ion concentrations are governed by non-equilibrium rate laws, this approach allows predicting the tempo-spatial distribution of speciation and precipitation of species across oxidation states. We illustrate the versatility of our framework through two case studies: manganese speciation in natural waters and the fate of dissolved iron in aqueous/porous media. Our framework significantly enhances the modelling of a diverse range of redox-sensitive environments.

Keywords:
Redox reactions; Reactive-transport framework; Oxidation states; Thermodynamic speciation; Kinetics

We employ a staggered approach, where we first consider kinetic rate laws governing redox reactions along with other transient phenomena such as transport. We take advantage of the availability of accurate thermodynamic datasets to iteratively solve the decoupled systems. 2  Fundamental concept behind the proposed reactive-transport framework

We propose a reactive-transport framework that incorporates the key mechanisms affecting a redox chemical system, such as hydrolysis and complexation, precipitation, kinetics of redox reactions, and the transport of aqueous and solid species (
Figure

1
a). We differentiate the above mechanisms as either transient (time-dependent) or equilibrium processes (indicated in blue and red, respectively in
Figure

1
e). A unique feature in our approach is that we consider the kinetically-controlled oxidation and reduction of metal ions, along with the transport of all aqueous and solid species, as transient processes (
Figure

1
e). By considering the rate laws governing redox reactions as transient processes, thus separately from thermodynamic modelling, we ensure that the concentrations of metal ions in different oxidation states are determined by non-equilibrium processes
[
31
]
(
Figure

1
b). Numerically, we implement this by introducing variables
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{a}$}}\text{]}
and
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{b}$}}\text{]}
for aqueous ions in each redox state, formed either because of oxidation or reduction. Here,
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{a}$}}\text{]}
and
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{b}$}}\text{]}
denote the sum of the concentration of all aqueous complexes of an element M in oxidation state
\mathtt{a}
and
\mathtt{b}
, respectively (
Figure

1
a, e). This approach enables us to use rate constants of oxidation (
\text{$\kappa$}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{ox}}}
) or reduction (
\text{$\kappa$}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{red}}}
), that can be experimentally determined and for which generally reliable data is available in the literature
[
4
,
2
]
. Consequently, we tackle the challenges pertaining to the limited availability of kinetic rate constants for each of the species participating in redox reactions (
Figure

SI 1
b). The spatial and temporal evolution of
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{a}$}}\text{]}
depends not only on its diffusion but also on its rate of conversion (oxidation or reduction) to
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{b}$}}\text{]}
(shown within blue regions in
Figure

1
e). Within each time increment, we first address the transient phenomena for each aqueous species, see (
Section

5
for more details) and then utilise the acquired concentration values to address the equilibrium phenomena (red regions in
Figure

1
e).

After solving the transient processes, we utilise the variables
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{a}$}}\text{]}
and
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{b}$}}\text{]}
to solve for the speciation and precipitation of ions in multiple redox state at every spatial point (
Figure

1
c, d). Here, we separate speciation and precipitation of one oxidation state from the speciation and precipitation of the other oxidation state (represented by red in
Figure

1
e). For this, we take the
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{a}$}}\text{]}
corresponding to oxidation state
\mathtt{a}
at a spatial point, obtained after diffusion and oxidation/reduction from the first stage (indicated in blue in
Figure

1
e), along with the remaining amount of background species. We solve this chemical system under thermodynamic equilibrium (detailed in
Section

5
) under varying environmental conditions such as pH, partial pressures of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and temperatures. Next, we consider a separate chemical system that comprises the
\text{[}\text{M}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{$\mathtt{b}$}}\text{]}
corresponding to a different oxidation state,
\mathtt{b}
, (obtained from the oxidation or reduction considered as transient processes) and the remaining background species (obtained from the previous chemical system). We solve the chemical systems corresponding to different oxidation states in parallel and in an iterative manner where all the background species are constantly exchanged between the systems, ensuring they converge to the same pH value (see
Figure

1
) and are mass balanced. The concentration of different species determined in this way (including multiple redox complexes, background species, and pH) reflects the concentration at the end of the time interval and serves as the starting point for the subsequent time step. Please refer to
Section

5
for a detailed description of the methodology.

3  Application of the reactive-transport framework

Their redox chemistry not only influences several biogeochemical cycles but also plays an important role in the functioning of critical infrastructure systems. In the selected examples, both Mn and Fe can exist in multiple oxidation states over different length and time scales and influence a multitude of local environmental conditions and processes. 4  Outlook

We set out the fundamental basis for a methodology to gain insights into the evolution of the aqueous chemistry of man-made and natural environments. This opens up several avenues for future research. The innate flexibility within our framework allows future work to integrate key transient processes such as the sorption of aqueous species onto solid surfaces, transformations of solid phases from thermodynamically less stable to more stable forms, and the influence of precipitation/dissolution on the pore structure, without compromising on the thermodynamic equilibrium calculations of the aqueous and solid phases of metals in several oxidation states. Moreover, we consider it a major advantage that our framework can also account for the precipitation of solid phases containing metals in mixed oxidation states, for example,
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{II}}
and
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
in
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
(s) or
\text{Mn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{II}}
,
\text{Mn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
and
\text{Mn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{IV}}
in
\text{MnO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{x\/}}}
(s)/
\text{Mn}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
(s). We propose to take into account the precipitation of such solid phases along with other transient processes, provided availability of accurate experimentally determined rate laws. As with most reactive transport models, predicting long-term behaviour is computationally intensive, particularly when large time steps are required. This presents a challenge that could be addressed by developing more efficient and innovative approaches to speed up thermodynamic calculations
[
52
,
53
]
.

Our reactive transport framework showcases a novel approach in addressing the challenges in mapping the spatial and temporal distribution of elements across multiple redox states in aqueous systems. This capability is particularly valuable in fields such as toxicity assessment, groundwater remediation, and corrosion, among others, where metals can exist in multiple oxidation states and significantly influence aqueous chemistry and transport behaviour.

5  Methods

5.1  Operator-split algorithm for thermodynamic modelling

As experimental data are scarce on individual formation rates
\kappa_{i}
of different
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
complexes from
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{a}}
complexes, it is not possible to model the chemical reactions as a whole, that is, under partial equilibrium conditions
[
26
]
. To overcome this problem, we propose using an operator-split approach to simulate chemical reactions in two separate but parallel chemical systems (see
Figure

1
). In the first system, we consider the speciation of
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{a}}
and its precipitation, and in the second system, we consider the speciation and precipitation of
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
. We consider the oxidation of
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{a}}
to
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
by introducing an auxiliary variable [
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
], which represents the sum of all species in the oxidation state
\mathtt{b}
. Similarly, to account for the reduction of
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
to
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{a}}
, we consider an auxiliary variable [
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{a}}
], which represents the sum of all species in the oxidation state
\mathtt{a}
. The formation of each of the auxiliary variables is kinetically controlled with an overall rate constant
\kappa
that is representative of the combined formation rates of each
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
complex
i.e.

\kappa=f(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},...\kappa_{n})
. Our choice is motivated by the fact that in experiments, the parameter
\kappa
is measurable instead of the individual parameters
\kappa_{i}

[
2
]
. We use an iterative approach to solve the two systems. Initially, the focus is on the first chemical system, which involves the equilibrium speciation and precipitation of
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{a}}
. We employ the Gibbs minimisation principle for this purpose
[
22
]
. The pH value obtained from this process, along with the equilibrium amount of background species (excluding
\mathrm{M}
species), serves as the initial condition for the second chemical system. The second system is then solved to determine the speciation and precipitation of
\mathrm{M}^{\mathtt{b}}
. At this point, the pH value obtained from the second system is checked. If the pH values of the first and second systems are within a specified tolerance, it is assumed that the two systems have reached equilibrium. However, if the pH value from the second system is outside the tolerance, it is passed along with the amount of background species to the first system for another iteration. This process is repeated until the pH values of both systems are within the chosen tolerance.
Figure

SI 3
shows a schematic representation of the operator-split algorithm for the thermodynamic modelling. It is worth noting that the proposed operator-split approach is robust, and the order in which the systems are solved does not impact the results.

Supplementary Information Supplementary Note 5
Processes that can influence the interfacial chemistry in the context of steel corrosion in concrete

The pore solution of the concrete is generally highly alkaline (pH:
>
12.5), facilitating the development of a robust passive film on the steel. The breakdown of steel passivity in concrete can be either local due to the ingress of chlorides or uniform due to the lowering of the pH of the pore solution due to carbonation. When considering localised corrosion, the breakdown of passivity may cause a change in the local pH in the area of active corrosion. However, this change in the local pH might be buffered for a significantly long time due to the dissolution of
\text{Ca}\text{(}\text{OH}\text{)}\text{}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(s) present in cementitious matrix. The carbonation of the concrete cover (i.e. decalcification of Ca-bearing cement hydrate phases and the precipitation of
\text{CaCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
) and the associated loss of alkalinity (pH
<
9) is often ’perceived’ as a cause for the corrosion of the embedded steel reinforcement
[
61
]
. However, in the context of infrastructure corrosion, steel corrosion rates are generally only of concern when the internal relative humidity at the steel-concrete interface (SCI) is
\geq
80 – 95 %
[
62
,
63
]
. In the case of fully saturated and completely carbonated concretes, the corrosion of the embedded reinforcement may be sustained.

The sustained anodic dissolution of iron leads to the production of
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{2+}}
(
Equation

132
) and the simultaneous cathodic generation of
\text{OH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
(the flux of [

Supplementary Note 5.5  Framework limitations

First, we assume that
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
formed as a result of the oxidation of
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{II}}
ions instantly precipitate to its thermodynamically favoured solid state - goethite, and aqueous [
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
] is dictated by the solubility limit of goethite (
Figure

SI 9
and
Figure

SI 8
). It has been recently shown that thermodynamically less stable phases such as ferrihydrite may also precipitate
[
70
]
in the porous medium. It is certainly the case that thermodynamically less stable
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
(hydr)oxides (such as ferrihydrite) may transform to thermodynamically more stable states with time, which is kinetically controlled by the dissolution rate of the preceding solid phase
[
40
]
. As a first approximation, here, we do not consider the time-dependent transformation rate of different solid
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
(hydr)oxides. Furthermore, also the precipitation of corrosion products with iron in different oxidation states, such as magnetite (
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
), has not been considered in this work but, will be addressed in detail in future studies (as highlighted in
Section

4
).

Aqueous
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
is assumed to sorb on the surfaces of solid phases and cannot diffuse away. This assumption may not be entirely true. Therefore, in addition to the precipitation of
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
hydr(oxides), another sink term taking into account
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{III}}
sorption must be considered within the transient processes in our framework.

A second limitation of our study pertains to the reactive solid phase,
\text{CaCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, surrounding the electrolyte in the pore network. While we allow for the congruent dissolution of
\text{CaCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, we do not consider the kinetics of
\text{CaCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
dissolution as a function of the gradients in the pH, and speciation of aqueous inorganic carbon at the surface of the dissolving
\text{CaCO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
solid. To incorporate this, one would have to simulate processes occurring at the pore-scale within both the transient and equilibrium stages of this framework.

Thirdly, the precipitation of solid corrosion products, with a relatively higher molar volume compared to the bare metal within the pore network will change the local porosity and diffusivity of individual pores. It will also result in an increase in the expansive stresses exerted on the surrounding solids leading to the development of micro-cracks and subsequently altering the local pore structure. While, this is not the focus of this manuscript, one could incorporate temporal and spatial changes in the pore structure into the framework.

Finally, in the relevant example of steel corrosion in carbonated concrete and to illustrate the applicability of our reactive transport framework, we did not alter the
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{0}}
corrosion rate over time and instead, used a constant flux of
\text{Fe}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{II}}
and correspondingly, [
\text{OH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{\hskip 0.90417pt--\hskip 0.90417pt}}
].
Stefanoni et al. [35]
established that the corrosion rate, under practical exposure conditions depends on the physical and chemical characteristics (fraction of saturated pores at the steel/porous media interface, the concentration of iron ions at the interface, diffusion of iron ions away from the corroding interface - dependent on porosity and connectivity) of the steel/porous media interface. In future studies we aim to incorporate this by changing the input flux accordingly in our framework.
