Taxonomic distribution of alkane synthesizing aldehyde dehydrogenases across bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals
Authors: Masakazu Ito, Yu-An Sui, Natsumi Okada, Nahoko Kitamura, Shigenobu Kishino, Masayoshi Muramatsu, Jun Ogawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHydrocarbon-based biofuels, known as drop-in fuels, which are chemically similar to petroleum, have gained significant attention. Microorganisms that produce medium-chain alkanes hold promise for the bioproduction of drop-in fuels. Previous studies identified Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048 as having aldehyde-decarbonylating activity, enabling it to convert aldehydes into alkanes. Using a genomic fosmid library from Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048, we identified open reading frame 2991 (orf2991), which catalyzes the conversion of tetradecanal to tridecane. This gene shares high sequence similarity with the aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) family in Escherichia coli.ResultsALDH homolog genes from Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048 and E. coli W3110 were cloned and expressed in E. coli to assess their potential alkane-synthesizing activity. Approximately one-fifth of the tested enzymes exhibited this function, with basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) analysis classifying them under the phenylacetaldehyde dehydrogenase, succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase, or aldehyde dehydrogenase B families. Testing additional ALDH homologs from diverse organisms—bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals—revealed that ALDHs with alkane-synthesizing activity are widespread, occurring in Gram-positive bacteria, actinomycetes, lactic acid bacteria, and yeast species. Alkane-synthesizing activity was observed with resting cells and cell-free extracts of the E. coli transformants expressing ALDH (ORF2991) from Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048 with aldehyde as the substrate in the presence of NADH. However, under the tested conditions, the purified enzyme alone did not show detectable decarbonylase activity. These results suggest that additional cellular components, cofactors, or specific conditions may be required for the purified enzyme to exhibit the activity.ConclusionsWe cloned several aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs) from bacteria and yeast that have aldehyde decarbonylase activity to convert aldehydes to alkanes. Alkane-synthesizing activity was observed through the assays using resting cells and cell-free extracts of the E. coli transformants expressing ALDH. This novel function of aldehyde dehydrogenase introduces a new pathway for hydrocarbon fuel production and offers novel insights into microbial processes that may explain the natural origins of petroleum.

Background

Biofuels derived from plant materials present a promising alternative to fossil fuels. Since the carbon dioxide released during biofuel combustion is reabsorbed by plants through photosynthesis, biofuels offer significantly lower net carbon emissions compared to fossil fuels. This cycle aids in reducing greenhouse gas emissions [1–3]. However, current biofuels like ethanol and fatty acid methyl esters are not ideal for internal combustion and jet engines due to issues like metal corrosion [4], resin degradation [5], low energy density, and poor performance at low temperatures [6, 7].

Hydrocarbon-based biofuels, known as drop-in fuels, which are chemically similar to petroleum, have gained significant attention. Common production methods include biohydrogen-refined diesel through hydrogenating vegetable fats and oils [8], biomass-to-liquids processes that combine biomass gasification with Fischer–Tropsch synthesis [9], and hydrocarbon production using genetically modified organisms such as algae [10], bacteria, yeast, and fungi [11–13]. However, these technologies are still in the early stages, with challenges in catalyst cost reduction, energy efficiency, and productivity. Currently, these biofuels are not economically viable.

In our previous study, we screened various bacteria and yeasts for alkane-synthesizing activity and found that Klebsiella, a genus of the Enterobacteriaceae family, efficiently converted tetradecanal to tridecane [14]. Further screening of alkane-synthesizing genes using a genomic fosmid library from Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048 identified orf2991, which encodes the enzyme catalyzing the same conversion of tetradecanal to tridecane [15]. BLAST analysis showed that the cloned gene’s amino acid sequence was highly homologous to E. coli aldehyde dehydrogenase B (ALDH B). ALDHs are NAD(P)-dependent enzymes that oxidize aldehydes to carboxylic acids, detoxifying cytotoxic aldehydes. The ALDH family is evolutionarily conserved across bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals and participates in functions like ester hydrolysis, nitrate reduction, and aldehyde oxidation. However, no information exists on aldehyde decarbonylase activity of ALDH for medium-chain aldehydes.

In this study, we cloned ALDH homologs from bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals to assess their alkane-synthesizing potential. We found this activity across multiple species, with Corynebacterium glutamicum, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Schizosaccharomyces pombe exhibiting particularly high activity. BLAST analysis linked the enzymes with alkane-synthesizing activity to phenylacetaldehyde dehydrogenase, succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase, or aldehyde dehydrogenase B families.

Evaluation of the alkane-synthesizing activity of ALDH from Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048 and E. coli W3110

We cloned 13 ALDH genes from Klebsiella sp. NBRC100048 and 17 from E. coli W3110, expressing them in E. coli JM109 to assess alkane productivity. In addition to orf2991-expressing E. coli, significant alkane production was observed in three recombinants with ALDH genes from Klebsiella sp. Enzymatic evaluation of the ORF2991 enzyme

Alkane production was absent when microorganisms were cultured under oxidative conditions (130 strokes/min) but observed under microaerobic conditions (100 strokes/min). This suggests that alkane production is linked to anaerobic/microaerobic conditions, likely due to providing reducing cofactors such as NADH. To explore whether ALDH, which typically catalyzes aldehyde dehydrogenation to acids with NAD+, could perform NADH-dependent decarbonylation (Fig. 2), E. coli BL21 harboring pRSF_ORF2991 was constructed, and enzyme activity was measured using resting cells, cell-free extracts (CFE), and purified ORF2991 with NADH (Fig. 3a). Resting cells and CFE produced 2.2 μM and 0.3 μM tridecane, respectively, in the presence of NADH. However, no tridecane was detected with the purified ORF2991 enzyme (Fig. 3a). In contrast, in the presence of NAD+, tetradecanoic acid was generated via tetradecanal oxidation (Fig. 3b), indicating ALDH’s oxidative dehydrogenation activity. The result using CFE indicated that the decarbonylation activity requires NADH. However, the purified ORF2991 enzyme did not show detectable decarbonylation activity under the tested conditions. Discussion

ALDH plays roles in various metabolic pathways [16]. This study examined alkane-synthesizing activity using 30 different ALDH genes, spanning 16 families, from Klebsiella sp. and E. coli. Three families, phenylacetaldehyde dehydrogenase, succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase, and aldehyde dehydrogenase B, showed aldehyde-decarbonylating activity, converting straight medium-chain aldehydes into alkanes with one less carbon. These enzymes, typically studied for converting aldehydes into carboxylic acids via NAD+-dependent oxidation [17, 18]. In this study, we found that they also commonly converted medium-chain aldehydes into alkanes.

Enzymes involved in alkane biosynthesis include CER1, which plays a role in plant cuticular wax production [19], and aldehyde deformylating oxygenase (ADO), which is responsible for hydrocarbon production in cyanobacteria [20]. CER1 forms a complex with ER-localized cytochrome b5 (CYTB5) and is known to catalyze the oxidation–reduction reaction forming long-chain alkanes from long-chain acyl CoA by the action of iron ions located in the active site [21]. It is also known to have strict substrate specificity for compounds with 29 or more carbon atoms. Similarly, cyanobacteria-derived ADO is a non-heme di-iron enzyme, with two iron ions (di-iron center) positioned within its active site [22]. ADO catalyzes the conversion of medium-chain aldehydes into alkanes and formic acid. Recent structural analyses have suggested that ADO forms a complex with acyl-ACP reductase, facilitating the efficient transfer of aldehyde substrates [23].

ALDH differs from CER1 and ADO, existing as an active form in dimers or tetramers. Metal ions are not essential for the active site, and it is known to catalyze coenzyme-dependent redox reactions through cysteine residues and cofactors such as NAD(P) ⁺/NAD(P)H in its active site [24]. In our experiments, decarbonylation activity was observed with resting cells and cell-free extracts of the ALDH-expressing E. coli transformant using aldehyde as the substrate in the presence of NADH, suggesting that ALDH has unrevealed decarbonylase activity. However, the purified ORF2991 enzyme did not show the decarbonylation activity under the tested conditions. This suggests that additional cellular components or factors may be required for the decarbonylation reaction, or that the enzyme may lose activity during purification. The precise mechanism underlying ALDH-mediated alkane synthesis from aldehyde thus remains unclear, and further investigation is needed to clarify the roles of possible cofactors, electron donors, or protein partners.

As described above, CER1, ADO, and ALDH each possess distinct evolutionary backgrounds and reaction mechanisms, having developed specific adaptations suited to their respective organisms and environments. The elucidation of ALDH’s mechanism not only suggests the existence of a novel alkane biosynthetic pathway, but also has the potential to deepen our understanding of the diversity of biological metabolism.

Molecular phylogenetic tree analysis of ALDH homologs from Klebsiella sp. and E. coli based on amino acid sequences showed that ALDHs with aldehyde-decarbonylating activity were positioned closely (Fig. 4), suggesting that specific amino acid sequences are responsible for this activity against medium-chain aldehydes. Multiple sequence alignments of ALDHs with alkane-synthesizing activity revealed five highly conserved regions (Fig. 5). Regions B and C are known as active site motifs, while the roles of the other regions remain unclear. The alkane-synthesizing activity of ALDH, confirmed in this study, includes ALDHs from microorganisms previously shown to produce alkanes [14]. This strongly suggests that alkane production in these microorganisms is substantially due to the unique function of ALDH. Ecologically, it is intriguing why various microorganisms retain ALDHs to convert aldehydes to alkanes. Since aldehydes are highly toxic, they must be quickly detoxified. ALDH typically converts aldehydes to carboxylic acids via an oxidative reaction involving NAD+, but many microorganisms exist in reducing conditions, such as underground. To evaluate oxygen’s effect on enzyme activity, ALDH-catalyzing reactions were conducted under aerobic and anaerobic conditions. For aerobic conditions, glass tubes with the reaction mixture were filled with air, while pure nitrogen gas was used for anaerobic conditions. Results indicated that tridecane production was higher in anaerobic conditions (data not shown), suggesting that oxygen negatively influences the decarboxylase reaction catalyzed by ALDH. Our previous research also showed that alkane production during the cultivation of the strain occurs under microaerobic conditions (100 strokes/min) but not under oxidative conditions (130 strokes/min) [14], suggesting this process helps detoxify aldehydes using the reducing power of NADH and other reducing agents under anaerobic conditions. The unknown factors required for the decarbonylation activity of the purified enzyme might be involved in maintaining such reductive conditions. We observed some positive effects of reducing reagents such as 2-mercaptoethanol and NADH-regeneration system that maintain the reduced conditions (data not shown). Further detailed analysis of these factors will be presented elsewhere.

Alkanes, being non-toxic and easily separated from cellular water, are excreted outside the cell. These observations imply that diverse microorganisms use ALDHs for reductive detoxification, potentially contributing to biogenic petroleum formation [28]. Our findings have implications not only for enzyme function and microbial ecology, but also for understanding petroleum origins. We will continue investigating ALDH’s unique role through reaction mechanism and protein structure analysis and explore its potential for future commercial alkane production.

Conclusions

We have shown that ALDH with decarbonylase activity is present in a wide range of species, from bacteria to yeast, by expressing the genes in E. coli. The ALDH with aldehyde decarbonylase activity has been found in three ALDH families: phenylacetaldehyde dehydrogenase, succinate-semialdehyde dehydrogenase, and aldehyde dehydrogenase B.

The alkane-synthesizing activity was observed in resting cells and cell-free extracts of ALDH-expressing E. coli transformants in the presence of NADH. However, the activity was not detected with the purified enzyme under the tested conditions. These results suggest that additional cellular components, cofactors, or specific conditions may be required for the purified ALDH enzyme to show decarbonylase activity. We believe that exploring this pathway is valuable because ALDHs are universally distributed across many species, providing a vast enzyme screening pool.

Alkane production test

A single colony of E. coli containing cloned genes was cultured in 1 mL LB medium (with 50 μg/mL ampicillin) at 37 °C, 130 strokes/min, for 24 h (the culture was carried out using linear reciprocal shaking). A 250 µL aliquot was then transferred to 2.5 mL of fresh LB (with 50 µg/mL ampicillin and 0.1% Triton X-100) in a glass test tube and incubated at 30 °C with shaking (130 strokes/min) for 3 h. After adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), the culture was shaken at 30 °C for 3 h, followed by the addition of 5 mM tetradecanal and further incubation at 30 °C, 100 strokes/min, for 18 h. To prepare the tetradecanal solution, we dissolved tetradecanal in dimethylformamide (DMF) to make a 100 mM stock solution. This stock was added to the culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 5 mM. The final DMF concentration in the medium did not exceed 5% (v/v), and no observable effects on cell growth or enzyme activity were detected under our experimental conditions. A 1 mL sample was taken for GC–MS analysis.

GC–MS conditions for the alkane production test

Alkane production was analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS, HP6890/5973, Agilent) with a headspace sampler (HP7694, Agilent) and an HP-INNOWAX analytical column (0.32 mm i.d. × 30 m × 0.5 µm thickness, Agilent). The supernatant was obtained by centrifuging 1 mL of post-culture at 8,000 g for 5 min, transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial, and sealed with a 20 mm aluminum crimp cap-PTFE/silicone septa. The vial was heated at 80 °C for 15 min, pressurized to 15 psi with helium, and analyzed by GC–MS (conditions: 1 min injection time, 50 mL/min fill flow, 150 °C loop, 200 ℃ transfer line, 1.0 mL/min helium carrier gas, oven at 60 °C for 10 min, raised at 25 °C/min to 260 °C, held for 1 min).

Expression of the recombinant enzyme

A single E. coli BL21 (DE3) colony harboring pRSF_ORF2991 was grown in 10 mL of LB medium (50 μg/mL kanamycin) at 37 °C and 130 strokes. The overnight starter culture (10 mL) was transferred to 1 L of modified M9 medium (12.8 g/L Na2HPO4 × 7H2O, 3 g/L KH2PO4, 0.5 g/L NaCl, 1 g/L NH4Cl, 0.25 g/L MgSO4 × 7H2O, 0.015 g/L CaCl2 × 2H2O, 2 g/L yeast extract, 0.2% (v/v) glycerol, 50 μg/mL kanamycin) and incubated at 37 °C, shaking at 100 rpm for 4–6 h. At OD600 0.8, IPTG was added (final 0.5 mM), and the culture was incubated at 20 °C, 100 rpm for 18 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C (portable refrigerated centrifuge MX-30, 1) (TOMY digital biology Co., Tokyo, Japan), and the pellet was stored at −20 °C for further use.

Resting cell reaction

The frozen pellet was thawed, and two volumes of 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) were added per cell volume. The resting cell reaction mixture contained 30 mg cells, 10 mM NADH, and 1 mM tetradecanal substrate in 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) in a final volume of 1 mL. It was incubated at 30 °C with shaking (130 rpm) for 18 h, then extracted with 500 μL toluene containing 100 μM 1-pentadecanol (internal standard) and analyzed by GC and GC/MS.

Preparation of the cell-free extract

The frozen pellet was thawed, and two volumes of 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) were added per cell volume. The cells were lysed by five 1 min sonication cycles with 1 min rests using a sonicator (Yamato Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) at 0 °C. The cell-free extract was separated by centrifugation at 8,500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and used for the cell-free reaction or recombinant enzyme purification.

Purification of the ORF2991 enzyme

All steps were performed at 0–4 °C using 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) as the standard buffer.

Step 1: Ni-Sepharose column chromatography.

The cell-free extract was filtered through a 0.45 µm filter and applied to a HisTrap HP 5 mL Ni-Sepharose column (GE Healthcare Biosciences, Uppsala, Sweden) equilibrated with the standard buffer. After washing with the buffer, the enzyme was eluted with a 0–1 M imidazole gradient in 50 mL of buffer. Fractions containing the target enzyme were collected, concentrated via filter centrifugation, and used for step 2.

Step 2: Anion exchange chromatography.

Concentrated fractions were applied to a MonoQ 5/50 GL 1 mL anion-exchange column (GE Healthcare Biosciences Co., Uppsala, Sweden) equilibrated with 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). After washing, the enzyme was eluted with a 0–500 mM NaCl gradient in 20 mL of buffer. Fractions containing purified ORF2991 were collected, concentrated, and stored as 50% glycerol stocks at −20 °C. The concentration of the purified enzyme was determined by the Bradford method.

Enzyme assay for cell-free extract and purified enzyme

The assay mixture included the cell-free extract from 30 mg of cells or 100 μg/mL of purified enzyme, 10 mM NADH, and 1 mM tetradecanal in 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), with a final volume of 1 mL. The reaction was incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 130 rpm for 24 h. After incubation, the reaction was extracted with 500 μL toluene containing 100 μM 1-pentadecanol as an internal standard and analyzed by GC and GC/MS.

GC and GC/MS conditions for the enzyme assay

Enzymatic products were analyzed by a Shimadzu GC-1700 gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a flame ionization detector, splitless injector, and Nukol capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm I.D., Supelco, USA). Helium was the carrier gas; nitrogen, hydrogen, and air were used in the detector. The initial column temperature of 80 °C was raised to 112 °C at 5 °C/min and then to 190 °C at 40 °C/min, held at 190 °C for 56 min. The injector and detector operated at 200 °C with helium as the carrier gas at 100 kPa/cm2. GC/MS analysis was performed on GC/MS-QP5050 with GC-17A gas chromatography (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) using the same column and method, with the MS system in electron impact mode at 70 eV and 200 °C.
