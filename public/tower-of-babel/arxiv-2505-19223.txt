LLaDA 1.5: Variance-Reduced Preference Optimization for Large Language Diffusion Models
Fengqi Zhu, Rongzhen Wang, Shen Nie, Xiaolu Zhang, Chunwei Wu, Jun Hu, Jun Zhou, Jianfei Chen, Yankai Lin, Ji-Rong Wen, Chongxuan Li

Abstract
While Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs), such as LLaDA, present a promising paradigm for language modeling, there has been relatively little effort in aligning these models with human preferences via reinforcement learning. The challenge primarily arises from the high variance in Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)-based likelihood estimates required for preference optimization. To address this issue, we propose Variance-Reduced Preference Optimization (VRPO), a framework that formally analyzes the variance of ELBO estimators and derives bounds on both the bias and variance of preference optimization gradients. Building on this theoretical foundation, we introduce unbiased variance reduction strategies, including optimal Monte Carlo budget allocation and antithetic sampling, that significantly improve the performance of MDM alignment. We demonstrate the effectiveness of VRPO by applying it to LLaDA, and the resulting model, LLaDA 1.5, outperforms its SFT-only predecessor consistently and significantly across mathematical (GSM8K +4.7), code (HumanEval +3.0, MBPP +1.8), and alignment benchmarks (IFEval +4.0, Arena-Hard +4.3). Furthermore, LLaDA 1.5 demonstrates a highly competitive mathematical performance compared to strong language MDMs and ARMs. Project page: https://ml-gsai.github.io/LLaDA-1.5-Demo/ .

. Furthermore, explorations of the scaling properties of MDMs have revealed their excellent scalability in various downstream tasks

and achieve competitive results to representative autoregressive models of the same size (e.g., LLaMA 3

]
).

Despite these advancements, existing work on MDMs has primarily focused on unsupervised pre-training and supervised fine-tuning (SFT) paradigms
[
40
]
. There has been relatively little effort in aligning these models with human preferences via reinforcement learning, even though RL has emerged as a powerful and essential paradigm for aligning LLMs
[
48
,
69
,

,

49
,

.

In this paper, we systematically investigate the RL-based alignment of MDMs, focusing on the direct preference optimization (DPO)
[
45
]
algorithm for its simplicity and strong empirical success.
A key challenge in applying DPO to MDMs lies in the intractability of the exact log-likelihoods required by the original DPO formulation. A natural solution is approximating these log-likelihoods by their ELBOs, which involve double expectations over the diffusion time and masked data. This substitution yields a
preference score
expressed as a linear combination of four ELBO terms (see Eq. (
7
)).

In practice, the ELBOs in the preference score are estimated via a doubly Monte Carlo method
[
56
,
17
]
. We demonstrate that this estimation process introduces additional bias and variance into the estimated loss and its gradient.
To mitigate these errors, our theoretical analysis reveals a crucial insight: the introduced bias and variance are governed by the variance of the preference score estimator.
This finding underscores the need to control this variance for effective preference optimization.

Building upon this, we introduce
Variance-Reduced Policy Optimization
(VRPO), a method integrating principled techniques to reduce the variance of the preference score estimator:

(1)
increasing the sampling budget for ELBOs,

(2)
allocating sample budget across distinct diffusion timesteps with one masked sample per timestep, and

(3)
applying
antithetic sampling

[
33
]
between ELBO estimates of the model and reference policies.
Remarkably, such techniques have been theoretically proven to reduce the variance of the preference score estimator in an unbiased manner and empirically validated in both synthetic and large-scale real-world ablation studies (see Section
4.2
). We further discuss on potential generalization of our variance reduction techniques to other alignment algorithms
[
48
,
49
]
.

Finally, we show the effectiveness of VRPO by applying it to LLaDA 8B Instruct
[
40
]
, a leading language MDM, using 350k preference pairs. As shown in Figure
1
, the resulting model, LLaDA 1.5, improves LLaDA consistently and significantly on mathematics, coding, and alignment tasks. In addition, LLaDA 1.5 maintains a highly competitive mathematical performance compared to strong MDMs
[

2.1
RL-based Alignment Methods

Traditional alignment approaches
[
48
,
69
,
43
]
consist of two main stages.

Reward modeling.

In the first stage, a static dataset of preference comparisons
{\mathcal{D}}=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}
is constructed. For each prompt
x
,
y_{w}
denotes the human-preferred response and
y_{l}
denotes the less preferred one, respectively.
A parameterized reward model
r_{\phi}
is trained to reflect these preferences by minimizing the following loss based on the Bradley-Terry formulation
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Reward}}(\phi)\triangleq-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\right)\right],

(1)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. This encourages
r_{\phi}
to assign higher scores to preferred responses.

Reinforcement Learning (RL).

In the second stage, the language model policy
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
, which defines the probability of generating response
y
given prompt
x
, is then optimized via RL to maximize:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\ y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x,y)\right]-\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right),

(2)

where
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a fixed reference policy, often chosen as a frozen SFT model, and
\beta
is a coefficient controlling the regularization strength.
Notably, in autoregressive models (ARMs), both sampling and likelihood evaluation for the policy are exactly characterized by the language model distribution.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).
DPO
[
45
]
offers a simplified alternative to the two-stage paradigm above by avoiding explicit reward model training, while maintaining both theoretical grounding and strong empirical performance
[
23
]
. The DPO loss is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)\triangleq-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\right)\right].

(3)

2.2
Masked Diffusion Models

Masked Diffusion Models (MDMs) define a model distribution via a forward–reverse framework
[
52
,
2
]
.
Starting from the original input at
t=0
, the forward process progressively masks the input tokens with a masking probability increasing over time, producing a fully masked sequence at
t=1
. The reverse process learns to denoise this sequence by iteratively predicting the masked tokens as time reverses from
t=1
to
t=0
.
This framework enables principled modeling of complex data distributions, offering a feasible exploration for non-autoregressive generation approaches.

Likelihood estimation in MDMs.

Unlike ARMs, the exact log-likelihood
\log\pi(y\mid x)
in MDMs is often approximated by its evidence lower bound (ELBO)
[
37
,
42
,
51
,
47
]
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\pi}(y\mid x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim q(y_{t}\mid t,y,x)}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y\mid x)\leq\log\pi(y\mid x),

(4)

where
q(y_{t}\mid t,y,x)
denotes the forward diffusion process at time
t
given the full response
y
and prompt
x
, and
\ell_{\pi}
represents the per-step loss of the mask prediction model, which admits multiple equivalent formulations elaborated in Appendix
A
.
Notably, for a continuous-time diffusion process (or equivalently, when the step size is infinitesimal), the
bias
of the ELBO for a well-trained model relative to the exact likelihood is negligible, as demonstrated in prior works
[
52
,
28
,
53
]
.

Computing
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}(y)
exactly is generally intractable due to the double expectations. In practice, we approximate it by a
doubly Monte Carlo
method. Letting
n_{t}
and
n_{y_{t}}
be the numbers of samples for timesteps and masked data per timestep, we draw:

\displaystyle\mathsfit{S}_{t}\triangleq\{t^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{U}[0,1]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\triangleq\{y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}}\}_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}q(y_{t}\mid t^{(j)},y),\ j=1,\dots,n_{t},

(5)

where we omit the conditions on
x
for brevity.
The ELBO is then estimated by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)\triangleq\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\ell_{\pi}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y).

(6)

The estimator in Eq. (
6
) is an unbiased approximation of the ELBO. However, computational constraints prevent us from using large values for
n_{t}
and
n_{y_{t}}
, as doing so would increase the batch size and training FLOPs by a factor of
n_{t}\times n_{y_{t}}
. Consequently, the
variance
of the estimator must be accounted for. In this work, we explore how to mitigate the adverse effects of ELBO estimation variance on preference optimization while operating under a fixed computational budget.

Large-scale language MDMs.

LLaDA
[
40
]
is an 8B-parameter masked diffusion model for language generation. LLaDA is pretrained on 2.3 trillion tokens and fine-tuned on 4.5 million pairs of SFT data. It exhibits outstanding capabilities comparable with representative ARMs
[
19
]
in scalability, in-context learning, and instruction-following.
In this paper, we adopt LLaDA 8B as a base model to explore reinforcement learning–based alignment methods for MDMs.

3
Method

We investigate how to align MDMs with human preferences using the DPO framework
[
45
]
. To address the intractability of the required log-likelihoods, we approximate them by unbiased ELBO estimators. Remarkably, we prove that the bias and variance introduced by the approximation can be bounded by the variance of a score estimator (a linear combination of four ELBOs). Therefore, we propose
Variance-Reduced Preference Optimization
(VRPO) by integrating multiple unbiased variance reduction techniques for better alignment. We also discuss potential extension beyond DPO.

3.1
Substituting Likelihoods with ELBOs in DPO

We begin by adapting the DPO loss in Eq. (
3
) by substituting log-likelihoods with their ELBOs:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)\triangleq-\mathbb{E}_{(y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}{\log\sigma\left({\color[rgb]{0.6992,0.1328,0.1328}{\beta\left(\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})\!-\!\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w})\right)\!-\!\beta\left(\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})\!-\!\mathcal{B}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l})\right)}}\right)}}\right],

(7)

where the term highlighted in
red
is referred to as the
preference score
and denoted by
{\color[rgb]{0.6992,0.1328,0.1328}s_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}
.

Intuitively, the loss encourages the current model
\pi_{\theta}
to better prefer
y_{w}
over
y_{l}
than reference
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
by comparing the ELBOs.
As discussed around Eq. (
4
), the ELBO provides a principled approximation to the log-likelihood with negligible bias. Moreover, the structure of the DPO loss—specifically its symmetric form and the smoothness of the sigmoid function—inherently helps mitigate the overall approximation gap, making
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)
a reliable surrogate for the original DPO objective.

In practice, Eq. (
7
) is evaluated over a batch of preference data
\mathsfit{S}_{\mathrm{data}}\triangleq\{(y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{m}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}
, and each ELBO is estimated by Eq. (
6
).
The resulting empirical loss is:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\log\sigma\left({\color[rgb]{0.6992,0.1328,0.1328}\beta\left(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w}^{(i)})\!-\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w}^{(i)})\right)\!-\!\beta\left(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l}^{(i)})\!-\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l}^{(i)})\right)}\right)},

(8)

where we denote the preference score estimator, highlighted in
red
, by
{\color[rgb]{0.6992,0.1328,0.1328}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})}
, and we use
\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}
to denote the stochastic sampling introduced by ELBO estimators. We assume that the sampling procedures throughout the paper follow the standard independence conditions specified in Assumption
1
.

Notably, the stochasticity of the preference score estimator introduces additional variance into the estimates of the overall preference optimization loss (i.e.,
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)
) and its gradient. Besides, due to the nonlinearity of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.8047}\log\sigma(\cdot)}
, this also results in a bias in these estimates (see Figure
2
(a)), although

s_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
itself is an unbiased estimator for true preference score.

In the remainder of this section, we address these challenges by first establishing how the variance of the preference score estimator (i.e.
{\color[rgb]{0.6992,0.1328,0.1328}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}
) governs the overall bias and variance, and then proposing multiple principled variance reduction strategies to mitigate them.
For clarity, we focus on the loss analysis in the main paper and defer the analogous gradient analysis to Appendix
B.4
.

3.2
Error Analysis of the Empirical Loss

According to Eq. (
8
), in addition to the stochasticity introduced by the preference score estimator, the overall loss estimator is also affected by another irreducible source of randomness—namely, the sampling of training preference data—which is shared across all preference optimization methods. To disentangle these effects, we establish a lemma that isolates and attributes the bias and variance in the overall loss estimation specifically to the ELBO-based stochasticity as follows.

Lemma 1
.

The bias and variance of
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)
over stochastic sampling satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)}\right]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[{\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))-\log\sigma(s_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))}\right]}_{\triangleq{\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)\right]=\frac{1}{m}\underbrace{\mathbb{V}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right]}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{data}}}+\frac{1}{m}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right]}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}}.

This identifies three error terms for
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)
:

(i)

\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}
, the expected gap between
\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta})
and
\log\sigma(s_{\theta})
, arising from the nonlinearity of
\log\sigma(\cdot)
applied to the preference score estimator
\hat{s}_{\theta}
;

(ii)

\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{data}}
, the variance over preference data of the expected
\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta})
;

(iii)

\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}
, the expected variance of
\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta})
over the Monte Carlo sampling in
\hat{s}_{\theta}
.

Among these,
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{data}}
captures the inherent variability in the preference dataset and is independent of the preference score estimation strategy. In contrast,
\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}
and
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}
arise specifically from the score estimation required in MDMs and are absent in the original DPO formulation.
The proof is provided in Appendix
B.2.3
.

Building on Lemma
1
, the following theorem demonstrates how preference-induced errors
\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}
and
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}
can be directly bounded in terms of the variance of
s_{\theta}
. Intuitively, our goal is to address the effect of the nonlinearity introduced by
\log\sigma(\cdot)
, laying a theoretical foundation for directly applying principled variance reduction techniques to
s_{\theta}
in Section
3.3
.

Theorem 1
.

The errors induced by score estimation in Lemma
1
can be bounded as:

\displaystyle{\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\left[\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})\right]}\right],\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}}\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\left[\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})\right]\right].

Intuitively, the proof of Theorem
1
(see Appendix
B.2.4
) utilizes the Lipschitz continuity of
\log\sigma(\cdot)
and the unbiasedness of
\hat{s}_{\theta}
, which ensures that the variability in
\hat{s}_{\theta}
leads to controlled changes in the loss and keep it close to the true objective. Besides, in the toy example shown in Figure
2
(b),
we plot how the variance of a random variable
X
influences the bias and variance of
\log\sigma(X)
. These curves exhibit trends that align well with Theorem
1
. Collectively, these findings suggest that reducing the variance of
\hat{s}_{\theta}
can simultaneously mitigate both errors, motivating the design of VRPO.

3.3
Variance-Reduced Preference Optimization

Building on Lemma
1
and Theorem
1
, we now present VRPO (see Figure
3
), a set of principled and effective techniques designed to reduce the variance of the preference score estimator, thereby mitigating the bias and variance in the overall preference optimization as follows:

(1)

Sampling budget:
Increase the number of samples
n=n_{t}\times n_{y_{t}}
used to estimate each ELBO.

(2)

Optimal allocation:
Allocate the full budget to timesteps by setting
n_{t}=n
and
n_{y_{t}}=1
.

(3)

Antithetic sampling:
Share the same sampled timesteps and masked data between the ELBO estimates of the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
for the same input
y_{w}
or
y_{l}
.

Practically, the first component increases the batch size and FLOPs of preference optimization by a factor of
n
. However, in our default setting (
n=8
), the additional overhead is fully affordable relative to the overall pretraining cost (see Section
4
). Notably, the latter two components incur no additional sampling cost under a fixed computational budget
n
: optimal allocation redistributes the existing samples across timesteps without increasing the total sample count, and antithetic sampling reuses samples across ELBO estimates, effectively serving as a “free lunch” for preference optimization.

Theoretically, none of the proposed techniques introduces bias into the preference score estimator.
The first two components preserve the unbiasedness as they do not change the ELBO estimation form in Eq. (
6
), while antithetic sampling remains unbiased due to the linearity of expectation applied to the linear combination in Eq. (
8
) (proof in Appendix
B.3
). Below, we present the main theoretical results on variance reduction, with proofs deferred to Appendix
B.3
.

We begin by characterizing the variance of the score estimator in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

The variance of the preference score estimator takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})

\displaystyle\!=\!\beta^{2}\left[\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})\!+\!\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w})\!-\!2\mathrm{Corr}\left(\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w}),\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w})\!\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w})}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\beta^{2}\left[\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})\!+\!\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l})\!-\!2\mathrm{Corr}\left(\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l}),\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l})\!\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l})}\right].

In Lemma
2
, the subscript of variances and square brackets
[\cdot]
are omitted for brevity.

This decomposition reveals two strategies to reduce
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}
:
(i)
first, decreasing the variance of each ELBO estimation
\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)
;
(ii)
second, increasing the correlation between the ELBO estimates for the same input
y
.
The following theorem formalizes how the sampling budget and optimal allocation techniques in VRPO reduce variance through the first strategy.

Figure 3
:
Illustration of VRPO.

We compare VRPO (right) with a naive strategy (left) without optimal allocation and antithetic sampling.
VRPO allocates the sampling budget across timesteps and samples only one masked sequence per timestep (indicated by red arrows) and shares Monte Carlo samples between paired ELBOs (highlighted with the red annotations above the blocks).

Theorem 2
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)
be estimated using a total of
n=n_{t}\times n_{y_{t}}
masked samples. Then we have:

(i)

\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=\Theta(\frac{1}{n})
, and

(ii)

\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)
is minimized when
n_{t}=n
and
n_{y_{t}}=1
.

Notably, Theorem
2
guarantees the effectiveness of allocating the entire sampling budget across timesteps: drawing one masked sample per timestep. Furthermore, the following theorem formally establishes how antithetic sampling leads to variance reduction as realized by the second strategy.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose
\mathrm{Corr}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y),\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\rm ref}}(y))>0
for
y\in\{y_{w},y_{l}\}
. Then we have: Using shared Monte Carlo samples
\mathsfit{S}_{t}
and
\{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\}_{j=1}^{n_{t}}
to compute
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\rm ref}}(y)
yields lower
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
than using independent samples.

Theorem
3
is inspired by the classical antithetic variates method
[
33
]
, where shared randomness is leveraged to reduce the variance of the difference between paired estimates. In our setting, the assumption of positive correlation between the reference and current policies on the antithetic sample is natural: they typically share initialization and exhibit similarities in preferences on the same inputs.

Finally, we validate the effectiveness of VRPO by applying it to LLaDA
[
40
]
(see details in Section
4
), improving the performance significantly and consistently on extensive benchmarks. Besides, we provide systematic ablation studies to see the contribution of each component in Section
4.2
.

3.4
Extension to Other Alignment Methods

The variance reduction techniques in VRPO are not limited to DPO but have broader applicability to other alignment algorithms that involve estimating the ELBO or subtracting two correlated ELBOs.
This scenario commonly arises in algorithms
[
48
,
43
,
49
]
. When applied to MDMs, these methods require ELBOs to approximate intractable log-likelihoods within the KL divergence term in their objective functions (see Eq.(
2
) and Eq.(3) in
Shao et al. 2024
), as shown below:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\!\left[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}\right]\approx\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{(i)}|x)-\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y^{(i)}|x)}\right].

(9)

The expression in
magenta
is in a similar form of ELBO differences as in DPO (Eq. (
8
)), where VRPO techniques can be directly applied to reduce its variance.

Other components in the objectives (e.g., reward signals
[
48
,
43
]
or weighted per-token likelihood ratios
[
49
]
) can be exactly computed or approximated without requiring additional Monte Carlo sampling
[
65
]
, and hence do not introduce extra variance that requires reduction.

4
Experiments

We collect data across rich scenarios to align MDMs for general tasks and evaluate VRPO extensively on common benchmarks.
We briefly present the setup, with more details provided in Appendix
C
.

Data.
We train LLaDA 8B Instruct
[
40
]
for one epoch on 350K preference pairs using VRPO, resulting in LLaDA 1.5. The data are collected internally at scale and undergo several processing steps, including filtering out low-quality samples, removing duplicates via similarity matching, ranking samples using a reward model to select high-quality data, and replacing some responses with outputs from advanced LLMs. This process yields a high-quality and diverse dataset covering a wide range of topics such as writing, dialogue, knowledge Q&A, reasoning, mathematics, and coding.

Training Efficiency.

This results in an approximate 8 times increase in computation compared to methods without Monte Carlo estimation (e.g., ARMs or setting

Despite this, we argue that the overall cost of VRPO remains modest —less than 0.5% of pre-training—making the added overhead practically acceptable.
Moreover, under fixed

We conduct ablation studies to evaluate the impact of each variance reduction component in VRPO. We vary sampling configurations in three factors corresponding to these components:

,

n_{t}/n_{y_{t}}
, and
(3)

We set the base configuration as
n=4
,
n_{t}/n_{y_{t}}=4/1
, with antithetic sampling used.
For each configuration, we measure:
(i)
the variance of preference score estimator
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}
,
(ii)
the preference-induced variances
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}
of the loss and gradient (as defined in Lemma

benchmark results spanning mathematics, code, and alignment.
Details of the empirical variance computation are provided in Appendix

Table

summarizes all the results. We highlight four key observations:

•

Effect of preference score estimator variance.
Lower
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}
generally leads to lower
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}
in both the loss and gradient, along with improved task performance. This empirical trend supports our theoretical insight from Theorem
1
to control the errors by
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}
.

•

Sampling budget.
Increasing the sampling budget
n
consistently reduces estimator variance and improves task performance. For instance, increasing
n
from 1 to 8 reduces
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}
from 44.0 to 1.0 and improves GSM8K accuracy from 80.1 to 83.3, validating our finding in Theorem
2

(i)

•

Optimal allocation.
Under a fixed budget, allocating samples to distinct timesteps generally yields better results than repeating multiple mask samples per timestep. For example,
n_{t}/n_{y_{t}}=4/1
outperforms
n_{t}/n_{y_{t}}=1/4
across all metrics, supporting our theoretical result on the optimal allocation strategy in Theorem

(ii)
.

•

Antithetic sampling.
Removing antithetic sampling causes a sharp rise in estimator variance, which confirms the theoretical prediction in Theorem
3
.
While we also observe that the downstream performance degrades more moderately. We argue that this is understandable. VRPO is theoretically designed to reduce the bias and variance in the DPO loss and gradient estimates, which directly improves optimization rather than generalization. In contrast, downstream task performance is influenced by a multitude of factors, making its sensitivity to estimator variance harder to isolate. We hypothesize that disabling antithetic sampling may expose the model to a broader diversity of data patterns, which could be beneficial for certain downstream tasks.

To summarize, these results demonstrate a strong empirical correlation between variance reduction and downstream task performance, supporting the theoretical analysis in Section
3
. Crucially, they validate that the techniques in VRPO are necessary for effective preference optimization.

5
Related Work

Alignment of LLMs and diffusion models.
LLMs have achieved remarkable success through alignment with human preferences
[
54
,
43
,
4
]
and have demonstrated strong reasoning capabilities
[
41
,
24
]
. With proximal policy optimization and variants
[
48
,
49
,
62
]
being prominent RL-based alignment methods. DPO
[
45
]
offers a simple alternative by directly optimizing the model with preference data, eliminating explicit reward modeling. In contrast, the alignment of diffusion models has been less explored, with most existing research focused on the image domain
[
57
,
7
,
20
,
14
,
44
]
.

Masked diffusion models.
The inspiration for MDMs stems from advances in discrete diffusion models
[
52
,
2
]
, which introduced novel forward and reverse transition mechanisms and facilitated the development of various model variants
[
11
,
30
,
25
,
59
,
66
]
. Empirical studies have shown that MDMs can achieve perplexity comparable to ARMs, and some works have proposed simplified objectives for masked diffusion processes to enable efficient model training theoretically
[
37
,
47
,
51
,
42
]
. Further research has explored the scaling properties of MDMs
[
39
]
, including investigations into both training from scratch and adapting from pre-trained autoregressive models
[
40
,
21
,
61
]
. However, most existing studies on MDMs have primarily focused on pre-training and supervised fine-tuning paradigms, while RL-based alignment method remains relatively underexplored.

Variance reduction techniques.

Our work relates to the broad fields of variance reduction in Monte Carlo methods, doubly stochastic optimization, and variational inference.
In Monte Carlo methods, variance reduction aims to enhance estimation accuracy by improving sampling strategies.
Classic techniques include control variables and stratified sampling
[
33
]
, where our approach adapts antithetic variates to couple correlated ELBO terms.
The nested expectation in ELBOs further parallels variance analysis in doubly SGD
[
17
,
56
,
22
,
32
]
, motivating decomposition via the law of total variance to isolate distinct variance sources.
Our approach also conceptually aligns with importance weighted variational inference
[
10
,
31
]
, where the outer bias is decreased by reducing the inner variance.

6
Conclusion

We analyze the sources of bias and variance when applying DPO to MDMs, and propose the VRPO framework with theoretically grounded variance reduction techniques. Empirically, the resulting model LLaDA 1.5 with VRPO demonstrates improved performance in mathematics, coding, and alignment benchmarks compared to LLaDA, and ablation studies show the effectiveness of VRPO. The work provides both a theoretical and practical foundation for RL-based alignment of MDMs, and the proposed variance reduction techniques are potentially applicable to other RL alignment frameworks, paving the way for future research in the alignment of MDMs.

Limitations.
First, VRPO requires additional computational resources to achieve lower variance, resulting in a slight increase in computational overhead. Second, although VRPO effectively reduces variance, the benefits are limited for certain downstream benchmarks, such as MTBench and Arena-Hard. We leave further investigation into improving performance on these tasks for future work.

Broader Impacts.
Although LLaDA 1.5 demonstrates improved alignment with human preferences, it may still generate hallucinations—factually incorrect content or information not present in the input. Nonetheless, leveraging larger-scale preference data and more advanced alignment methods may help mitigate this issue.

Appendix A
Additional Formulation of MDMs

For completeness, we introduce the formulation of MDMs in this section. For further details, please refer to prior works
[
51
,
47
,
42
]
.

In the forward process, MDMs corrupt an original sequence
y
by progressively masking tokens. Each token is masked independently at a noise level
t\in[0,1]
. Let
y\in\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}^{L}
be the original full response, where
K
denotes the vocabulary size and
L
denotes the sequence length, given a prompt
x
, the forward process is formulated as:

\displaystyle q(y_{t}|t,y,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{L}q(y_{t}^{i}|t,y^{i},x),\quad q(y_{t}^{i}|t,y^{i},x)=\begin{cases}1-t,&y_{t}^{i}=y^{i},\\
t,&y_{t}^{i}=\textbf{M},\\
\end{cases}

(10)

where
y^{i}
denotes the
i
-th token of response
y
, and
M
denotes the mask token.

The reverse process starts from a fully masked sequence and gradually unmask tokens to recover meaningful language sequences. For timesteps
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, the reverse process is defined as:

\displaystyle q(y_{s}|s,t,y_{t},x)=\prod_{i=1}^{L}q(y_{s}^{i}|s,t,y_{t},x),\quad q(y_{s}^{i}|s,t,y_{t},x)=\begin{cases}\frac{t-s}{t}p_{\theta}(y^{i}|y_{t},x),&y_{t}^{i}=\textbf{M}\land y_{s}^{i}\neq\textbf{M},\\
\frac{s}{t},&y_{t}^{i}=\textbf{M}\land y_{s}^{i}=\textbf{M},\\
1,&y_{t}^{i}\neq\textbf{M}\land y_{s}^{i}=y_{t}^{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

(11)

where
p_{\theta}
is modeled by the mask prediction model.

As stated in Section
2.2
, the exact log-likelihood
\log\pi(y|x)
in MDMs is typically approximated by its ELBO
[
37
,
42
,
51
,
47
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\pi}(y|x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\sim q(y_{t}|t,y,x)}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y|x),

(12)

where

\displaystyle\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y|x)\triangleq\left[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[y_{t}^{i}=\mathbf{M}]\log p_{\theta}(y^{i}|y_{t},x)\right].

(13)

As noted in
[
42
,
40
]
, the following formulation is an equivalent approximation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\pi}(y|x)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{l\sim\mathcal{U}(\{1,2,\dots,L\})}\mathbb{E}_{y_{l}\sim q(y_{l}|l,y,x)}\ell^{\prime}_{\pi}(y_{l},l,y|x),

(14)

where

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}_{\pi}(y_{l},l,y|x)\triangleq\left[\frac{L}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[y_{l}^{i}=\mathbf{M}]\log p_{\theta}(y^{i}|y_{l},x)\right],

(15)

with
l
uniformly sampled from
\{1,2,\dots,L\}
, and
y_{l}
denoting the sequence obtained by masking
l
tokens without replacement.

In practice, although Eq. (
14
) and Eq. (
12
) are equivalent in expectation
[
42
]
, the former typically yields lower variance during estimation
[
40
]
. Intuitively, Eq. (
14
) deterministically masks exactly
l
out of
L
tokens in each sequence, providing more consistent samples. In contrast, Eq. (
12
) relies on masking an expected fraction
t
of the tokens, which introduces greater variability into the estimation process. In practice, we apply Eq. (
14
) as our log-likelihood estimator.

Lemma 3

(Properties of
\log\sigma(x)
)
.

Let

, where

and
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
denotes the sigmoid function. Then
f
satisfies the following properties:

concavity:

f(x)
is concave;

(ii)

continuity:

f
is 1-Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lvert f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})\rvert\leq\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\rvert;

(iii)

smoothness:

f
is
\frac{1}{4}
-smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e., its derivative is
\frac{1}{4}
-Lipschitz continuous: for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lvert f^{\prime}(x_{1})-f^{\prime}(x_{2})\rvert\leq\frac{1}{4}\lvert x_{1}-x_{2}\rvert.

Proof.

We first compute the first and second derivatives of
f
. Note that

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}\in(0,1),

and

(i)

Since
f^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
f
is concave.

(ii)

We observe from above that
\lvert f^{\prime}(x)\rvert\leq 1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, implying that
f
is 1-Lipschitz continuous.

(iii)

Since
\lvert f^{\prime\prime}(x)\rvert\leq\frac{1}{4}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, the derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
is
\frac{1}{4}
-Lipschitz continuous, and thus
f
is
\frac{1}{4}
-smooth.
∎

Lemma 4

(Interchangeability of expectation and gradient)
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
X
be a random variable (or random vector) taking values in a measurable space
\mathcal{X}
. Suppose
f_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable with respect to
\theta
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
, and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\lVert\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(X)\rVert_{2}\leq C
for all
X\in\mathcal{X}
.
Then the expectation and gradient operators are interchangeable:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}f_{\theta}(X)=\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(X).

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed. For all
X
, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, define
g_{i}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}f_{\theta}(X)
, which exists since
f_{\theta}(X)
is differentiable w.r.t.
\theta
.
By assumption, we have

\lvert g_{i}(X)\rvert\leq\lVert\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(X)\rVert_{2}\leq C.

For each
i
, by the mean value theorem and dominated convergence theorem
[
5
, Chapter 5]
, we can interchange the expectation and derivative:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\mathbb{E}f_{\theta}(X)=\mathbb{E}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}f_{\theta}(X).

Applying this for each coordinate and stacking the results gives the full gradient interchangeability:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}f_{\theta}(X)=\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(X).

∎

Lemma 5

(Bias and variance of transformed random variable)
.

Let
X_{\theta}
be a real-valued random variable with
\mathbb{E}X_{\theta}=\mu_{\theta}
with parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

f(x)=\log\sigma(x)
on
\mathbb{R}

\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}

Then:

The transformed random variable satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f(X_{\theta})-f(\mu_{\theta})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}f(X_{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}.

(17)

(ii)

Suppose there exists a constant
C\geq 0
such that the gradient of
X_{\theta}
is uniformly bounded as
\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\|_{2}\leq C
.
Then, the gradient satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}f(\mu_{\theta})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}}+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{8}\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}+\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}.

(19)

Proof.

(i)
As

3
, for Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f(X_{\theta})-f(\mu_{\theta})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lvert X_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sqrt{(X_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(X_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta})^{2}}

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}}

(
\mathbb{E}X_{\theta}=\mu_{\theta}
)

For Eq. (
17
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}f(X_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{\theta})-\mathbb{E}f(X_{\theta})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left\lvert f(X_{\theta})-f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right\rvert+\left\lvert f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-\mathbb{E}f(X_{\theta}))\right\rvert\right)^{2}

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{\theta})-f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-\mathbb{E}f(X_{\theta}))\right)^{2}

(
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{\theta})-f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right)^{2}+\left(f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-\mathbb{E}f(X_{\theta}))\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{\theta})-f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right)^{2}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left(f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-f(X_{\theta})\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{\theta})-f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-f(X_{\theta})\right)^{2}

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{E}\left(f(X_{\theta})-f(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}\left(X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}X_{\theta}\right)^{2}

(

is
1
-Lipschitz continuous by Lemma

)

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}

(ii)
Using the chain rule and the bounded gradient assumption, for Eq. (

), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})-\nabla_{\theta}f(\mu_{\theta})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-f^{\prime}(\mu_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\|\left(f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(\mu_{\theta})\right)\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\|_{2}+\mathbb{E}\left\|f^{\prime}(\mu_{\theta})\left(\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\nabla_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}

(triangle inequality)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[|f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(\mu_{\theta})|\cdot\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\|_{2}]+|f^{\prime}(\mu_{\theta})|\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\nabla_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\mathbb{E}\left|f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(\mu_{\theta})\right|+\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\nabla_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}\right\|_{2}

(
f
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous by Lemma
3
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{4}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left|X_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta}\right|+\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\nabla_{\theta}\mu_{\theta}\right\|_{2}

(
f
is
\frac{1}{4}
-Lipschitz smooth by Lemma
3
)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{4}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left|X_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta}\right|+\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\|_{2}

(
\mathbb{E}X_{\theta}=\mu_{\theta}
and Lemma
4
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(X_{\theta}-\mu_{\theta})^{2}}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\|_{2}^{2}}

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}}+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}}.

To prove Eq. (
19
), we begin by decomposing the variance of the estimated gradient into three terms:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})-\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left\lVert f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left\lVert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{(II)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left\lVert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{(III)}}.

We now bound each term separately.

Term (I). Using the bounded gradient assumption
\|\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\|_{2}\leq C
and the
\frac{1}{4}
-Lipschitz smoothness of
f
(by Lemma
3
), we have:

(I)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right\rvert^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{2}}{16}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}X_{\theta}\right\rvert^{2}=\frac{C^{2}}{16}\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}.

Term (II). Since
f^{\prime}
is bounded by
1
(by Lemma
3
), we have:

(II)

\displaystyle=\left\lvert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\right\rvert^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}.

Term (III). Applying Jensen’s inequality and again using the smoothness of
f
and boundedness of
\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}
, we have:

(III)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\mathbb{E}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-\mathbb{E}f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right]\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}-f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\right\rvert^{2}\cdot\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\lvert f^{\prime}(\mathbb{E}X_{\theta})-f^{\prime}(X_{\theta})\right\rvert^{2}\leq\frac{C^{2}}{16}\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}.

Summing all three terms yields:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{\theta})\leq\frac{C^{2}}{8}\mathbb{V}X_{\theta}+\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}X_{\theta}.

∎

B.1.4
Variance of ELBO Estimator (Lemma
6
)

Lemma 6

(Variance of ELBO estimator)
.

Letting
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)
be as defined in Eq. (
6
), we have:

(i)

The variance of the ELBO estimator satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\underbrace{\mathbb{V}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\mid t,y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y)}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{t}}+\frac{1}{n_{t}n_{y_{t}}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t}\mathbb{V}_{y_{t}\mid t,y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y)}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{y_{t}}}.

(ii)

The variance of the gradient of the ELBO estimator for the model policy
\pi_{\theta}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\underbrace{\mathbb{V}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\mid t,y}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{t},t,y)}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{t}^{\nabla}}+\frac{1}{n_{t}n_{y_{t}}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t}\mathbb{V}_{y_{t}\mid t,y}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{t},t,y)}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{y_{t}}^{\nabla}}.

The
\mathsfit{V}_{t}
(or
\mathsfit{V}_{t}^{\nabla}
) and
\mathsfit{V}_{y_{t}}
(or
\mathsfit{V}_{y_{t}}^{\nabla}
) capture variance across timesteps and variance due to the noise at each step, which are inherently determined by the data and the forward process and cannot be reduced.

Proof.

For
(i)
, by the law of total variance,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\}_{j=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)}_{\mathrm{(I)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\mathbb{V}_{\{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\}_{j=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)}_{\mathrm{(II)}}.

Term (I). Conditioned on the
t
-sample, the inner expectation is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\}_{j=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\ell_{\pi}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\mid t^{(j)},y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t^{(j)},y).

Since terms in
\mathsfit{S}_{t}
are i.i.d. sampled, the outer variance is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{(I)}=\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\}_{j=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\mid t^{(j)},y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}}\mathbb{V}_{t}\mathbb{E}_{y_{t}\mid t,y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y).

Term (II). Conditioned on the
t
-sample, the inner variance is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\}_{j=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t^{(j)}}\mid y}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\ell_{\pi}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\mathbb{V}_{y_{t}\mid t^{(j)},y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t^{(j)},y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{2}n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\mathbb{V}_{y_{t}\mid t^{(j)},y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t^{(j)},y).

Taking the expectation over
\mathsfit{S}_{t}
yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{y_{t}}\mid\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}^{2}n_{y_{t}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\mathbb{V}_{y_{t}\mid t^{(j)},y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t^{(j)},y)=-\frac{1}{n_{t}n_{y_{t}}}\mathbb{E}_{t}\mathbb{V}_{y_{t}\mid t,y}\ell_{\pi}(y_{t},t,y).

Combining (I) and (II) gives the result:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\mathsfit{V}_{t}+\frac{1}{n_{t}n_{y_{t}}}\mathsfit{V}_{y_{t}}.

For
(ii)
, as
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)
has similar structure as
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)\triangleq\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y),

the proof closely follows that for
(i)
, and thus we omit the details here.
∎

\mathrm{Corr}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y),\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\rm ref}}(y))>0

\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)+\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)-2\mathrm{Corr}\left(\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y),\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)\!\right)\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y)}<\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)+\mathbb{V}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y).

∎

B.4
Deferred Analysis of Estimated Gradient

In this section, we present a theoretical analysis of the effect of VRPO on gradient estimation, following a structure analogous to the loss estimation analysis in the main paper.

We first introduce an additional assumption on the gradient of per-step mask prediction loss
\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}
, which serves as a necessary condition for the subsequent derivations.

Assumption 2

(Bounded gradient of per-step mask prediction loss)
.

The gradient of the per-step masked prediction loss
\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{t},t,y)
(Eq. (
4
)) is uniformly bounded. Specifically, there exists a constant
0\leq C<\infty
such that

\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{t},t,y)\|_{2}\leq C
for all
\theta
in the model parameter space,
y
in
{\mathcal{D}}
, and all
t\in[0,1]
.

This boundedness assumption is reasonable in practice and leads directly to the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Bounded gradient of preference score estimator)
.

Under Assumption
2
, the gradient of the preference score estimator
\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
is uniformly bounded. Specifically, there exists a constant
0\leq\tilde{C}<\infty
such that
\|\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})\|_{2}\leq\tilde{C}
for all
\theta
in the model parameter space and
(y_{w},y_{l})
in
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Proof.

Recall that the preference score estimator is defined as:

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})=\beta\left(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})\!-\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{w})\right)\!-\!\beta\left(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})\!-\!\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}(y_{l})\right),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi}(y)=\frac{1}{n_{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\frac{1}{n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\ell_{\pi}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y).

Taking the gradient with respect to
\theta
leads to:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})=\beta\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})-\beta\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l}).

Now expand each gradient term to get:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)=\frac{1}{n_{t}n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y).

By Assumption
2
, each term
\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y)\right\rVert_{2}\leq C
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y)\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{t}n_{y_{t}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{t}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{y_{t}}}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\pi_{\theta}}(y^{(k)}_{t^{(j)}},t^{(j)},y)\right\rVert_{2}\leq C.

Thus,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\beta\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})\right\rVert_{2}+\beta\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})\right\rVert_{2}\leq 2\beta C<\infty.

Setting
\tilde{C}=2\beta C
gives the desired result.

∎

B.4.1
Bias and Variance Analysis of the Estimated Gradient (Lemma
7
)

We begin by observing the bias and variance of the estimated gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)
in order to distinguish the errors introduced by preference score estimation.

Lemma 7
.

The bias and variance of
\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)
over stochastic sampling satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)\right]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))-\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(s_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right]}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{DPO-E}}(\theta)\right]=\frac{1}{m}\underbrace{\mathbb{V}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right]}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{data}}^{\nabla}}+\frac{1}{m}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right]}_{\triangleq\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}}.

Proof.

The proof proceeds analogously to the proof of Lemma
1
given in Appendix
B.2.3
, and is therefore omitted for brevity.
∎

B.4.2
Effect of Preference Score Estimator Variance (Theorem
6
)

Building on the above analysis, we identify
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{data}}^{\nabla}
as the inherent variance due to data, while
\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}
and
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}
arise from preference score estimation and can be mitigated by improving the sampling strategy.
We now present a theorem that characterizes how the variance of the score estimator and the variance of its gradient influence
\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}
and
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds.
Then, there exists a constant
0\leq\tilde{C}<\infty
such that the errors induced by score estimation in Lemma
7
can be bounded as:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\left[\frac{\tilde{C}}{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}{\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}}+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}\right],

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\left[\frac{\tilde{C}^{2}}{8}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}{\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}+\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\nabla_{\theta}{\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}\right].

Proof.

The proof is essentially based on the analysis of the bias and variance of the transformed random variable in Lemma
5
presented previously.

By the definition of
\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}
and
\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}
in Lemma
1
, we know that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathsfit{B}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))-\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(s_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right\rVert_{2},

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{\mathsfit{V}_{\mathrm{pref}}^{\nabla}}=\mathbb{E}_{y_{w},y_{l}}\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}{\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))}.

According to Corollary
1
, under Assumption
2
, there exists a constant
0\leq\tilde{C}<\infty
such that the gradient of
\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
is uniformly bounded as
\|\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})\|_{2}\leq\tilde{C}
.
Then by Lemma
5
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))-\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(s_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{4}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})}+\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})},

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\nabla_{\theta}\log\sigma(\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}))\leq\frac{\tilde{C}^{2}}{8}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})+\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}_{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l}).

Applying these bounds to the above equations gives the desired results.

∎

B.4.3
Variance Analysis of Preference Score Gradient Estimator (Lemma
8
)

Given Theorem
6
, our goal is to reduce the variance associated with the preference score estimator, specifically
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
and
\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
(we omit the subscript on
{\mathsfit{S}_{\hat{s}\mid y_{w},y_{l}}}
for brevity). The variance
\mathbb{V}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
has been thoroughly analyzed in Appendix
B.3
, where VRPO is proposed as the variance reduction strategy.
Now, we turn our focus to
\mathrm{tr}\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
, showing that the first two techniques in VRPO—increasing the sampling budget and applying optimal allocation—effectively reduce this term.

We begin by expanding
\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
for detailed analysis.

Lemma 8
.

The variance of the gradient of the preference score estimator takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})=\beta^{2}{\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})}+\beta^{2}{\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})}.

Proof.

According to the definition of
\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})
in Eq. (
8
), we have:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})=\beta\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})-\beta\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l}).

Under the second condition in Assumption
1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\hat{s}_{\theta}(y_{w},y_{l})=\mathbb{V}\beta\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})+\mathbb{V}\beta\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})=\beta^{2}{\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{w})}+\beta^{2}{\mathbb{V}\nabla_{\theta}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{\pi_{\theta}}(y_{l})}.

∎

Appendix C

C.1
Implementation of VRPO

We implement VRPO using a packing strategy, where multiple preference data samples are packed into a single sequence to maximize hardware utilization. For each sequence, we construct an attention mask so that tokens from distinct samples within the sequence cannot attend to each other. Furthermore, all sequences are padded to a fixed length of 4096 with
|\text{EOS}|
tokens, which is consistent with the default pre-training context length used in LLaDA. During VRPO training, these padded
|\text{EOS}|
tokens are excluded from the loss calculation.

C.3
Training

To enhance the general capabilities of LLaDA, we use 350K preference pairs as our training data. These pairs were collected internally on a large scale and processed by filtering out low-quality samples, removing duplicates based on similarity matching, ranking samples with reward models to select high-quality data, and replacing some

We trained the model for one epoch with a batch size of 64 using the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 0.01,
\beta_{1}

\beta_{2}

\beta=0.2
and initialize
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
with LLaDA Instruct for VRPO. Training consumed approximately 405 H100 GPU hours for 8 Monte Carlo samples. Due to hardware resource constraints, we did not perform any hyperparameter search.

C.4
Evaluation

Similar to ARMs with diverse sampling methods
[
29
,
9
]
, MDMs also benefit from various sampling strategies that can enhance sample quality. Following prior work
[
12
,
40
,
47
]
, we employ multiple methods to sample text from MDMs, including diffusion sampling, diffusion semi-autoregressive sampling, and low-confidence remasking.

In diffusion semi-autoregressive sampling, to generate a fixed length of
L
tokens, the method divides the generation process into
\frac{L}{B}
blocks, where
B
is the number of tokens generated per block. Within each block, tokens are generated using the original reverse process, and then each block is generated autoregressively. Furthermore, the low-confidence remasking method remasks predicted tokens that exhibit the lowest confidence, based on the predictions.

Additionally, we observed that for LLaDA SFT, due to the padding of
|\text{EOS}|
tokens during its SFT phase, tends to generate an excessive number of
|\text{EOS}|
tokens. This often leads to incomplete content generation, resulting in notably truncated outputs and adversely affecting model performance. Inspired by this, we set the confidence score for the
|\text{EOS}|
token to zero and observe improved performance for LLaDA. For example, using the same inference configuration as LLaDA, setting the
|\text{EOS}|
token’s confidence score to zero improved HumanEval scores from 47.6 to 49.4 and Math scores from 26.6 to 27.4. Consequently, we adopted this setting for evaluation.

To ensure a fair comparison, we employ both diffusion sampling and semi-autoregressive sampling for LLaDA and LLaDA 1.5 and report the best results. We tuned the answer length over {64, 128, 256, 512, 1024}, for semi-autoregressive sampling, we tuned the block length over {8, 16, 32, 64, 128}. As shown in Table
4
, we detail the best inference configurations employed for each benchmark.

We evaluated LLaDA 1.5 on general tasks using the same inference configurations, as presented in Table
5
. Compared to LLaDA, the improvements of LLaDA 1.5 on general tasks are limited, and its performance even decreases on MMLU-pro. We hypothesize that this phenomenon may be attributed to the alignment tax
[
1
,
4
,
18
]
, meaning that aligning the model with human preferences can sometimes lead to a decrease in its fundamental capabilities. A more rigorous understanding of this issue requires further investigation.
