Psychosocial experiences and challenges of women following pregnancy loss or termination: A phenomenological study
Authors: Latif Hacıoğlu, Çiçek Ediz, Sevda Uzun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to explore the psychosocial experiences and challenges of women who experienced pregnancy loss or termination, and to understand how they coped with the emotional, social, and spiritual effects of this process within the cultural context of Türkiye.MethodsThis study was conducted using a phenomenological qualitative design and employed semi-structured, in depth interviews with 23 women who presented to the Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic of a state hospital in eastern Türkiye for follow-up within six months after pregnancy loss or termination. Data were collected between September and October 2024. Criterion sampling method, one of the purposive sampling methods, was used to reach the sample group. Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved, which was determined when no new information or perspectives emerged and participants began to repeat similar statements. All interviews were audio recorded and then transcribed. The data of the study were evaluated using thematic analysis. The study was conducted and reported according to the COREQ checklist.ResultsIn the analysis of the data, three themes (Psychosocial Experiences Before and After Abortion; Physical, Social, and Psychological/Spiritual Impacts of Abortion; and Coping and Psychosocial Support Needs) and seven sub- themes (Before abortion, After abortion, Physical effects, Social effects, Psychological/spiritual effects, Process management and coping, and Psychosocial support) were identified.ConclusionThe study revealed that women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination experience multifaceted psychosocial challenges and have a strong need for comprehensive and individualized care. Psychosocial support was identified as an essential component in helping women cope with the emotional and social consequences of abortion. The findings emphasize the need to integrate structured psychosocial support into reproductive health services and policy frameworks to enhance the quality, accessibility, and continuity of care for women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02199-4.

Introduction

Pregnancy, which is a period of mental, social and physical changes in a woman’s life, is an important process that requires adaptation to these changes [1]. Due to the nature of women, although it is usually possible for the pregnancy process to be realized with a healthy birth, there may be pregnancies that require abortion. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines abortion as the termination of pregnancy for any reason before the fetus gains the ability to survive outside the uterus [2]. Pregnancy loss or termination is defined as the process of termination of pregnancy by the physician through various methods with written consent due to medical necessity [3].

Pregnancy loss or termination is a process that has negative physical, psychological, social and economic effects on women’s lives [4, 5]. In some studies, on this subject, it has been reported that women experience physical problems such as pain, infection, and sexual life after abortion [5–7], while in some studies, women whose pregnancies resulted in abortion expressed feelings of loss, stress, sadness and guilt [8–10]. Other studies have reported that women after abortion experience serious psychosocial problems such as deterioration in family relationships, stress, anxiety, depression and suicide attempts [5, 11]. Uncertainties about abortion, distrust of health personnel, the thought that the mother will be harmed, fear of death and negative sensations about abortion can negatively affect women’s mental health [12]. Women who decide to abort may experience feelings of anger, rage, fear, sinfulness, guilt, regret, loss and shame [12–14].

According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, an average of 56 million abortions occour each year between 2010 and 2014, accounting for approximately 25% of all pregnancies [15]. In Türkiye, the 2018 Turkey Demographic and Health Survey reported that the rate of spontaneous abortions was 13%, while the rate of induced abortions was 6% [16]. An unexpected abortion during pregnancy may lead to various physical and psychological difficulties for women experiencing this loss. Studies have shown that while health professionals closely follow the physical problems of women who have experienced abortion, they neglect their mental problems [17–19]. It is important for health professionals to provide the support that pregnant women need and to provide psychosocial support interventions to prevent a negative experience even if there is a complicated abortion [19–21]. Many studies emphasize that psychosocial support provided during this period can reduce feelings such as sadness and regret that may be experienced due to the loss of the baby after abortion [8–10, 22]. The process of coping with psychosocial problems experienced by women who have undergone abortion is largely determined by the religious beliefs, social values and cultural structure of the society in which they live [5]. Because the negative consequences of a pregnancy loss or termination may not only affect the woman, but may also be reflected on other women in the same environment. Abortion, which is a traumatic experience, can affect society’s perception and experience of abortion through intergenerational transmission [23, 24]. Therefore, it is important to know women’s experiences, perceptions and psychosocial problems related to abortion in order to ensure that pregnancy loss or termination factors are reduced with supportive care and information [14].

When the literature is examined, it is seen that mostly quantitative studies have been conducted on the problems experienced by women after abortion [3, 4, 25]. However, qualitative studies on the experiences, psychosocial problems and needs of women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination were not found and this study was needed. The information obtained is expected to contribute to the understanding of the experiences of women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination, to determine the psychosocial problems and needs they experience, and to contribute to the provision of pregnancy-specific and holistic care. Accordingly, the aim of this study is to evaluate the psychosocial experiences and challenges of women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination with a phenomenological approach.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted using a phenomenological qualitative design, which aims to explore and interpret the lived experiences of womens who have undergone pregnancy loss or termination. This design is suitable for understanding the psychosocial experiences and challenges experienced by women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination [7]. The study was carried out in the Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic of a state hospital located in eastern Türkiye. The hospital serves a wide population from both urban and rural areas, and most patients come from low to middle income backgrounds. The region is characterized by traditional family structures and strong cultural and religious values, which can influence women’s reproductive health decisions and experiences. The clinic provides routine follow-up services for women after pregnancy loss or termination, offering an appropriate and accessible setting for recruiting participants and exploring their psychosocial experiences within their reallife context. Throughout this study, the authors followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Annex 1) and reported accordingly [26].

Research team and reflexivity

Two members of the research team are associate professors in nursing schools with PhDs in psychiatric nursing, and one is a specialist in obstetrics and gynecology. All three researchers have prior clinical experience in hospital settings and were formally trained in qualitative research methods. Throughout the research process, the team maintained reflexivity by continuously discussing their assumptions and potential biases. For instance, the researchers acknowledged their prior professional experiences with women’s reproductive health and their possible preconceptions about the emotional impact of abortion. Regular team meetings were held to critically reflect on these perspectives and to minimize their influence on data collection and interpretation. To minimize researcher–participant power dynamics, particularly due to the presence of a male obstetrician and the participants’ expressed discomfort with male physicians, all interviews were conducted by the second author, a female researcher with expertise in women’s health and qualitative interviewing. This approach helped create a more comfortable and trusting environment for participants to share their experiences openly. The researchers applied bracketing to set aside their professional preconceptions during data collection and analysis. Member checking was not performed due to the sensitive nature of the topic.

Participants

Criterion sampling method, which is one of the purposeful sampling methods, was used to determine the study group of the research. Criterion sampling is the sampling of people, events, objects or situations that have the qualities determined for the problem [27]. The study was conducted with semi-structured, in-depth individual interviews with 23 women who presented to the Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic of a state hospital located in a province in eastern Türkiye. The participants were women who presented to the clinic for follow-up within the previous six months after experiencing pregnancy loss or termination.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) being 18 years of age or older (b) having undergone pregnancy loss or termination within the previous six months and (c) being open to communication, meaning that the participant was willing and able to express her thoughts and feelings about her experience during the interview process. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) having a speech, language or hearing impairment that would prevent communication.

Data collection

Data collection this study was conducted between September and October 2024. The data collection method involved semi-structured in depth interviews. An interview guide was developed by the researchers based on a comprehensive review of the relevant empirical and theoretical literature on pregnancy loss, abortion experiences, and psychosocial well-being [5, 8, 13, 14, 20, 23]. The guide consisted of open-ended questions designed to explore the psychosocial experiences and challenges of women who had undergone pregnancy loss or termination. The interview guide questions consist of two parts.

In the first part, the interview guide included questions about the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, (age, marital status, education status, employment, household income(self-reported), consanguineous marriage status, substance use (smoking/alcohol)) and obstetric characteristics (number of pregnancies, pregnancy week, is the pregnancy planned, how many abortion experiences, cause of abortion, time since abortion (month), receiving information about pregnancy, status of receiving psychosocial support during pregnancy) [5, 8, 13, 14, 20, 23].

The second section included seven basic open-ended questions to be used in the semi-structured interview [5, 8, 13, 14, 20, 23]. In order to ensure the content and face validity of the interview form, the opinions of 3 faculty members who are experts in the field of obstetrics and gynecology nursing and 3 faculty members who are experts in the field of mental health nursing were obtained. These questions were finalized based on the expert opinions.

Women who visited the Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic of a state hospital in eastern Türkiye for follow-up within six months after pregnancy loss or termination were screened according to the inclusion criteria. Those who met the criteria were invited to participate in the study during their routine follow-up visits. The women who agreed to participate were interviewed face-to-face in a private counseling room within the same clinic. No one other than the participants and the researcher was present during the interview. Each interview lasted 45–60 min and was audio recorded with the consent of the participants. In the interviews, women who underwent pregnancy loss or termination were asked to describe their experiences and psychosocial problems (the physical, social and psychological/spiritual effects of abortion on them) and were asked intermediate questions such as “Could you elaborate on your answer?” and “What do you mean by that?“. All interviews were conducted by (second author). Interviews continued until data saturation was reached, which was determined when no new information or perspectives emerged. Pre-application

A pre-application was conducted with 3 women who underwent pregnancy loss or termination in order to improve the comprehensibility and applicability of the data collection forms and to improve and standardize the interview skills of the researcher. As a result of the pre- application, there was no need to make any changes in the questions. However, due to the standardization of the interview, the women who were included in the pre-application were excluded from the analysis.

Data analysis

After all interviews were completed, the audio recordings were transcribed for verbatim analysis by three researchers. The thematic analysis method was used to analyze the qualitative data obtained from the interviews. Since the study was based on an inductive approach, no categories were defined in advance. In the first stage, the interviews were transcribed and the interview texts were read independently and repeatedly by three researchers. This process aimed to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the participants’ narratives and to identify initial meanings and patterns emerging from the data. Significant statements from the interview transcripts were identified, reorganized, and synthesized into general concepts to capture the essence of participants’ experiences. Then, the underlying meanings within the participants’ statements were identified and analyzed. The researchers discussed these meanings collaboratively until a consensus was reached, thereby ensuring the validity of the interpretations. The researchers then identified and organized the themes into main and sub-themes. The themes and sub-themes of the study were developed through clear articulation. In addition, participants’ statements were included so that the reader could verify the interpretation and analysis of the data [28].

Study rigor

To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of this qualitative study, the four fundamental criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba (1985) were applied. Accordingly, the study’s reliability was established through the principles of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. For credibility, participants were verbally informed and provided with an information sheet outlining the aim and procedure of the study prior to inclusion. Written consent was obtained from each participant. Interviews were carried out at times and in locations convenient for both the researcher and the participants. The first researcher possessed prior training in qualitative interviewing and data collection techniques. The interview guide was pilot tested to ensure its clarity and appropriateness. All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ permission and transcribed on the same day. For dependability, the transcripts were carefully reviewed multiple times to correct potential errors. Two independent researchers coded all transcripts separately and discussed any discrepancies until consensus was reached. Regarding confirmability, all researchers jointly reviewed and verified the codes, themes, and sub-themes to ensure objectivity. Finally, transferability was supported by employing purposive sampling and continuing data collection until data saturation was achieved, which was determined when no new information or perspectives emerged and participants began to repeat similar statements [29].

Ethical aspects of the research

This research was approved by the X University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (dated: 12.09.2024 numbered: 2024/143:1). Informed consent was obtained from the participants before starting the interview. All participants were informed that participation in the study was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any penalty or loss of rights. All participants have been anonymized by assigning them numbers (e.g., P1, P2, etc.). Recordings and transcripts were stored on a password-protected device. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical standards of the National Research Committee.

Theme 1. psychosocial experiences before and after abortion

This theme addresses the physical and emotional difficulties women experience during pregnancy loss or termination in a comprehensive manner, divided into two sub-themes: pre-abortion and post-abortion. Participants reported experiencing physical problems such as pain, bleeding, and heaviness both before and after the abortion. Emotionally, they reported experiencing uncertainty, fear, anxiety, disappointment, and shock before the abortion, and a sense of emptiness, grief, mourning, hopelessness, and a psychological process in which traumatic memories were frequently replayed after the abortion. The findings strongly contribute to the aim of the study by revealing that women’s experiences are not limited to a medical procedure but also involve psychosocial and existential dimensions.

Subtheme 1. before abortion

Most participants reported intense emotional distress before the abortion procedure, including fear, anxiety, and sadness. These feelings were often linked to health concerns, uncertainty about the procedure, or prior negative reproductive experiences. A few women also mentioned experiencing physical symptoms such as pain and bleeding.

“I was very scared. This was my 2nd abortion experience. I was very worried about my health and I was afraid that I would not be able to have children.” (P2).

“I was very scared. This was my 2nd abortion experience. I was very worried about my health and I was afraid that I would not be able to have children.” (P2).

“I felt severe pain, it is a very difficult process, I wish I had never experienced it.” (P4).

“I felt severe pain, it is a very difficult process, I wish I had never experienced it.” (P4).

Subtheme 2. after abortion

Following the abortion, participants described ongoing psychological and emotional challenges such as feelings of emptiness, fear, and persistent negative memories. Some women reported intrusive thoughts and avoidance behaviors associated with clinical settings.

“The delivery room, where I had given birth before and was happy, seemed very dark and scary to me this time. It is constantly in front of my eyes. I never want to go in there again (P7).

“The delivery room, where I had given birth before and was happy, seemed very dark and scary to me this time. It is constantly in front of my eyes. I never want to go in there again (P7).

“Physically I felt incomplete… I had a psychological breakdown.” (P5).

“Physically I felt incomplete… I had a psychological breakdown.” (P5).

Theme 2. physical, social, and psychological/spiritual impacts of abortion

This theme addresses the multifaceted effects of abortion on women through its physical, social, and psychosocial/spiritual sub-themes. Participants reported experiencing physical effects such as fatigue, bleeding, insomnia, and weight loss, as well as intense feelings of guilt, loneliness, worthlessness, and stigmatization. Socially, peer pressure, shame, and family conflicts were frequently mentioned. Some participants stated that they had difficulty communicating with male doctors due to cultural and religious values, and that this affected their perceptions of privacy and trust. Overall, this theme shows that women’s abortion experiences are shaped not only by individual factors but also by their social environment and cultural context. These findings highlight the need for a more holistic approach that addresses the psychosocial dimensions of abortion’s impact on women’s health, not just the medical aspects.

Subtheme 1. physical effects

Several participants reported physical symptoms following the abortion, including fatigue, bleeding, weight loss, and disrupted sleep. These symptoms often co-occurred with emotional exhaustion, making daily activities and child care challenging for some.

“The abortion procedure itself was quite traumatic for me as it was both physically and mentally exhausting. I felt very tired both physically and mentally for a long time (P8).

“The abortion procedure itself was quite traumatic for me as it was both physically and mentally exhausting. I felt very tired both physically and mentally for a long time (P8).

“I was not affected socially, but physically and psychologically it was very destructive for me.” (P9).

“I was not affected socially, but physically and psychologically it was very destructive for me.” (P9).

Subtheme 2. social effects

Participants described social difficulties such as fear of being judged, feelings of inadequacy, and strained family dynamics. Some expressed a strong preference for female healthcare providers due to concerns about privacy and cultural expectations.

“Since it was my second abortion experience, there were arguments between my family and my husband’s family blaming each other, which affected me very negatively socially.” (P15).

“Since it was my second abortion experience, there were arguments between my family and my husband’s family blaming each other, which affected me very negatively socially.” (P15).

“My mother had a heart attack when she found out that I had an abortion, which created a sense of guilt in me.” (P20).

“My mother had a heart attack when she found out that I had an abortion, which created a sense of guilt in me.” (P20).

Subtheme 3. psychological/spiritual effects

Many participants expressed a profound sense of spiritual and emotional pain following the abortion. Feelings of guilt, loneliness, and existential sorrow were common. For some, the connection they had formed with the fetus intensified these emotions.

This theme consists of two sub-themes addressing women’s coping methods and psychosocial support needs during and after the abortion process. Most participants indicated that they turned to spiritual coping methods such as praying, trusting in God, or seeking refuge in their understanding of fate. Some women emphasized that family and spousal support played a critical role in the psychological healing process. However, most participants stated that they did not have access to professional psychosocial support services and therefore experienced loneliness and helplessness. This theme shows that women’s efforts to make sense of their loss and restore emotional balance often remained at an individual or religious level. The findings indicate that women’s emotional and social needs during and after the abortion process must be integrated into the healthcare system.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to address the psychosocial experiences and challenges of women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination. The experiences of women who underwent pregnancy loss or termination and the psychosocial experiences and challenges they experienced were addressed in three categories.

Most of the women who participated in this study were married, unemployed, had moderate income levels, and were high school graduates. The majority had unplanned pregnancies and experienced pregnancy loss or termination during the early or middle stages (first trimester and second trimester) of gestation. Many participants had previously experienced similar reproductive losses. Although most had access to basic information during pregnancy, few reported receiving adequate psychosocial support. These characteristics provide an essential context for interpreting the psychosocial experiences identified in this study, as women’s marital, educational, and economic status, along with limited access to psychosocial support, can significantly shape their emotional reactions, coping mechanisms, and social interactions following pregnancy loss or termination.

Psychosocial experiences before and after abortion

As a result of the study, participants in this study described experiencing a range of emotional and physical challenges before, during, and after the abortion process. These challenges varied across individuals but commonly included heightened anxiety, sadness, and fear. Although the problems experienced by women at the stage of deciding on abortion, during and after abortion vary, the most prominent one is the change in feelings of anxiety [30, 31]. This finding is consistent with prior research, which highlights women facing miscarriage threats often experience significant psychological distress [5, 9, 30, 31]. Our participants similarly reported anxiety and emotional turmoil even prior to the abortion procedure. However, it is reported that 98% of miscarriages can be prevented by identifying and eliminating anxiety and depression disorders of pregnant women who experience the threat of miscarriage [11].

The World Health Organization (WHO) honored this agreement in its first abortion guidelines and limited its policy recommendations to situations where abortion was not illegal [32]. In the second edition, the WHO tested the limits of the Cairo consensus by referring to the relationship between abortion laws and unsafe abortion. “Where abortion is legal and safe services are accessible, both unsafe abortion and abortion-related mortality and morbidity rates decline [33]. A study by Pinar (2017) demonstrated that women who experienced miscarriage felt stigmatized, suffered a sense of loss, had limited access to available services, experienced mistreatment, and lacked trust and knowledge toward healthcare providers [20]. Another study by Punches (2018) demonstrated that women who experienced the threat of abortion encountered complex language and inappropriate communication, dissatisfaction with the hospital environment, lack of emotional care, and lack of follow-up and care [34]. In line with the results of the study, it can be said that abortion is a situation that needs to be addressed in all aspects and women need help at every stage of abortion.

Physical, social, and psychological/spiritual impacts of abortion

As a result of the study, it was determined that individuals reported experiencing various physical, psychological, and social effects as part of the abortion experience. In this study, it was observed that women who experienced pregnancy loss or termination experienced emotional problems such as fear, anxiety, guilt and hopelessness, and physical problems such as dizziness, weakness, bleeding, pain, fatigue. A study by Keten (2015) demonstrated that women with a history of miscarriage experienced emotional experiences such as fear, sadness and self-blame [30]. In a similar study by Eleje (2024) demonstrated that women who had miscarriage commonly experienced sadness, crying and guilt [31]. Another study by Bahar (2018) demonstrated that women who experienced pregnancy loss experienced fear and wanted to get information from health personnel [35]. According to these results, it can be said that pregnancy loss or termination affects individuals psychologically and physically very negatively. Pregnancy loss or termination is a process that has physical, psychological, social and economic effects on women’s lives and can cause problems for them. Such experiences may lead to long-term emotional responses, including trauma, sadness, fear, and in some cases, post-abortion stress symptoms. Fetal anomaly, which is among the causes of pregnancy loss or termination, is among the problems that affect women psychologically the most. Studies have shown that women may experience these symptoms intensely after fetal anomaly [7, 35, 36]. In addition, some women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination may experience guilt, anger, posttraumatic stress and anxiety about their next pregnancy [31, 36–38]. The results of the study are similar to the literature and the findings suggest that pregnancy loss or termination affects individuals psychosocially [30, 31, 35–38].

Coping and psychosocial support needs

The analysis revealed that the participants experienced difficulties in managing the abortion process. The process of coping with psychosocial problems experienced by women who have undergone abortion is largely determined by the religious beliefs, social values and cultural structure of the society in which they live [5]. Because the negative consequences of a pregnancy loss or termination may not only affect the woman, but may also be reflected on other women in the same environment. Abortion, which is a traumatic experience, can affect the community’s perception and experience of abortion through intergenerational transmission [3]. In line with the results obtained, it can be said that the abortion process is a very difficult process.

In the study, it was determined that psychosocial support is very important in the abortion process. It can be said that family support is very important for women. In addition, it was observed that individuals needed physical, emotional, social support, medical assistance and information during their stay in the hospital. A study by Punches (2018) demonstrated that women with pregnancy loss who applied to the emergency department applied directly to the hospital when they realized that there was a problem in their pregnancy and that they needed emotional care and help [34]. In a similar a study by Mcmorrow (2024) demonstrated that women who experienced pregnancy loss needed support for their experiences of disappointment, anger, grief, and self-blame [8]. Another study by Pinar (2017) demonstrated that women wanted to be with their relatives during their hospital stay related to infant loss, they wanted to communicate with people and they wanted to receive attention from their relatives and health personnel [20]. In a systematic review conducted by Petersen (2018), it was determined that most women experienced more severe pain and discomfort than expected during the abortion procedure. While the approach of healthcare personnel to women’s physical needs was described as good and professional, there was little or no focus on women’s emotional and existential needs. Furthermore, the study revealed that young women expressed ambivalent feelings after elective abortions, as a sense of loss was often accompanied by the relief of ending the pregnancy. Some women reported feelings of guilt, whereas others experienced regret [39]. According to these results, the study reveals that women experiencing pregnancy loss or termination need more emotional and social support and information.

Conclusion and recommendations

As a result of the study, it was found that women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination experience multifaceted psychosocial challenges and have a strong need for comprehensive and individualized care. The findings also revealed that women had difficulty coping with the pregnancy loss or termination process and that psychosocial support was perceived as an essential component of care.

A novel aspect of this study is its in-depth exploration of the psychosocial experiences of women undergoing pregnancy loss or termination in the context of Türkiye, where qualitative research on this sensitive topic remains limited. The study was conducted in a less developed city located in eastern Türkiye, making the sample particularly valuable as it represents a region and participant profile that have been rarely explored in previous studies. The study contributes to the literature by providing culturally grounded insights into women’s emotional, social, and spiritual needs throughout the abortion process. Taking women’s lived experiences and care expectations into account in diagnosis, decision-making, and care provision processes may help improve the quality, sensitivity, and continuity of reproductive health care.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, the sample consisted of women recruited from a single public hospital in eastern Türkiye, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, as this study employed a qualitative phenomenological design, the results are based on participants’ self-reported experiences, which may have been influenced by recall bias or emotional distress. Due to the sensitive nature of the topic, some participants may not have fully expressed their emotions despite efforts to ensure a safe and confidential environment. Third, the study focused solely on women’s perspectives and did not include insights from healthcare professionals or family members, which limits a more holistic understanding of the pregnancy loss or termination experience. Finally, although interviews were conducted with women at different gestational stages, no separate analysis was performed considering gestational week or the abortion timeline (whether the abortion occurred in the past or recently). This may have restricted a more detailed evaluation of potential variations in psychosocial experiences according to gestational period.
