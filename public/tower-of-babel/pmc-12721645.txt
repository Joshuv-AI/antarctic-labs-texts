A Common Ancestry-Specific Sodium Channel Variant Increases Susceptibility to Drug-Induced QTc Prolongation: Evidence From a Large Biobank
Authors: Russell Stewart, Brandon D. Lowery, Eric Farber-Eger, Yuko Wada, Dan M. Roden, Quinn S. Wells, Giovanni Davogustto
Published: 2025

The risk for QT prolongation and torsades de pointes after exposure to drugs that block the rapidly activating delayed rectifier potassium current (IKr) varies among patients, even accounting for clinical risk factors. This heterogeneity is attributed to genetic variation, including S1103Y, which is the most prevalent variant (minor allele frequency ≈12%) in the cardiac sodium channel gene (SCN5A) among individuals of African ancestry, but rare in individuals of European ancestry.1 Patients carrying 1 S1103Y variant (S/Y) have higher risk of sudden infant death syndrome1 and sudden cardiac death2 than patients without variants (S/S). We previously demonstrated that S1103Y variant cardiomyocytes induced from pluripotent stem cells had baseline action potential durations similar to S/S cells but were hypersensitive to IKr blockade by dofetilide. There was no effect of S1103Y genotype on corrected QT (QTc) in 1479 patients without heart disease or QT-prolonging drug exposure.3 Here, we report that S1103Y carrier status affects QTc at baseline and upon drug exposure in a larger cohort.

BioVU, the Vanderbilt University Medical Center biobank, links genetic data with clinical records, including ECG and medication reconciliation.4 Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was recently completed in 250 000 samples. We focus here on the first 34 523-sample cohort, which was enriched for participants of non-European ancestry.

Patients with WGS passing quality control and at least 1 ECG were included. Children (age <18 years), nonsinus rhythm, and paced ECGs were excluded. Nonphysiologic ECG measurements were excluded. R-R, QRS, and QT values were drawn from those tabulated by reading cardiologists during their ECG clinical attestation. QTc was then computed using the Bazett formula. S1103Y genotypes and principal components of genetic ancestry were extracted from WGS data. QT-prolonging drug exposure was defined as ≥1 medication from a previously curated list3 present in a medication reconciliation <48 hours before ECG. We excluded as-needed or intraoperative drugs due to dosage and administration frequency uncertainty. QTc was modeled as a function of S1103Y carrier status, drug exposure, age, sex, the first 5 principal components of genetic ancestry, R-R interval, and QRS interval using a multivariable linear regression. Genotype–drug and genotype–sex interactions were also included. Statistical analyses were performed in Julia; visualizations were created in Makie.jl. The Vanderbilt University Medical Center institutional review board approved this protocol. Participants provided informed consent during BioVU enrollment. Data will be made available upon reasonable request.

After WGS quality control, there were 34 131 patients, and 11 926 met all inclusion criteria. Most (91%) self-identified as Black. There were 1566 (13%) S/Y heterozygotes and 64 (0.54%) homozygotes for the S1103Y variant in the SCN5A gene (Y/Y). A total of 1244 ECGs were obtained during exposures to 30 distinct drugs (Figure [A]). The median observed QTc for the S/S, S/Y, and Y/Y genotypes were 433, 434, and 439 ms, respectively. Adjusting for covariates, each year of age increase was associated with a 0.26-ms increase in QTc (95% CI, 0.24, 0.27 [P<0.001]). Female sex was associated with a longer QTc than male sex (+6.8-ms difference; 95% CI, 6.3, 7.3 [P<0.001]), as previously established.5 Interaction effects between genotype and sex revealed that baseline QTc was longer in female Y/Y homozygotes, but not male Y/Y homozygotes, when compared with S/S homozygotes (Y/Y–female sex interaction: +18.7-ms difference; 95% CI, 11.5, 25.9 [P<0.001]; Y/Y–male interaction: −4.86-ms difference; 95% CI, −10.8, 1.11 [P=0.11]). S/Y heterozygotes’ QTc did not differ from that of S/S homozygotes in either sex (S/Y–female sex interaction: −0.56-ms difference; 95% CI, −0.77, 0.44 [P=0.44]; S/Y–male sex interaction: +0.23-ms difference; 95% CI, −0.69, 0.90 [P=0.69]; Figure [C] and [D]).

After adjusting for covariates, QT-prolonging drug exposure was associated with a predicted 10.5-ms QTc increase (95% CI, 9.7, 11.4 [P<10−99]; Figure [C]). S/Y heterozygotes experienced a larger-magnitude QTc prolongation than S/S controls (+3.9 ms; 95% CI, 1.09, 6.70 [Pinteraction=0.006]). These findings remained significant in sensitivity analyses excluding R-R and QRS intervals as covariates. Only 7 participants with Y/Y genotypes had drug exposures, limiting the power to detect Y/Y–drug interactions. However, the genotype–drug interaction remained significant when comparing the S/S genotype versus the combined S/Y and Y/Y populations in a separate linear model (+3.3 ms; 95% CI, 0.52, 6.05 [P=0.019]; Figure [D]).

Leveraging a large biobank including mostly participants of African ancestry, we demonstrate that female S1103Y homozygotes have prolonged baseline QTc. This contrasts with the findings of our previous study’s 1479-subject cohort, which did not reach statistical significance.3 Furthermore, S/Y heterozygotes were hypersensitive to drug-induced QT prolongation, recapitulating our previous in vitro findings.3 Our study’s limitations include retrospective design and insufficient power to assess Y/Y genotype–QT-prolonging drug interactions.

Our findings suggest that pharmacogenetic risk stratification by SCN5A genotype could help prevent QT-prolongation–related adverse drug reactions, especially in haplogroups in which this minor allele is prevalent. Further studies should consider broader end points, including sudden cardiac death,2 and additional ancestry-specific in vitro QT-prolonging SCN5A variants (eg, R1193Q in Asian individuals).3
