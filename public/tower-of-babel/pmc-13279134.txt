ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) expands to younger, lower‐risk populations, the need for repeat procedures due to valve degeneration is expected to increase. TAVR‐in‐TAVR has emerged as a feasible strategy, although outcomes across supra‐annular (SAV) and intra‐annular (IAV) valve combinations remain unclear. The PANDORA (Supra‐Annular Versus Intra‐Annular Devices for TAVR‐in‐TAVR) international registry study assessed safety, hemodynamic performance, and clinical outcomes of TAVR‐in‐TAVR according to prosthetic configurations.MethodsFrom an international multicenter registry (2011–2024), 172 TAVR‐in‐TAVR cases were identified among ≈30 000 TAVR procedures, with a median interval of 1401 days. Patients were stratified into 4 groups: SAV‐IAV (n=32), SAV‐SAV (n=29), IAV‐SAV (n=74), and IAV‐IAV (n=37).ResultsCoreValve/Evolut (49.4%) and Edwards SAPIEN (35.5%) were the most frequent index prostheses, whereas the second valve was mainly Edwards SAPIEN (60.5%), followed by Evolut (35.5%) and Myval/Octacor (4.0%). Structural valve deterioration was the leading failure mechanism (77.9%), while nonstructural valve deterioration dysfunction, alone or combined with structural valve deterioration, occurred in 40.7%. Overall Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 technical success was 91.3%, numerically highest in SAV‐IAV and IAV‐SAV (P=0.090). Thirty‐day device success was 68%, also higher in SAV‐IAV (75.9%, P=0.301), mainly influenced by elevated residual gradients (≥20 mm Hg in 12.7%) and the 30‐day mortality rate (7.3%). At 1 year, the IAV‐IAV group showed the numerically lowest freedom from death and heart failure hospitalization (76.1%, P=0.734). Male sex and chronic kidney disease independently predicted death at follow‐up.ConclusionsTAVR‐in‐TAVR is feasible with generally favorable outcomes, although clinical and procedural profiles vary by the different prosthesis combinations. These findings highlight the need for further studies to refine device selection strategies.

As the use of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) progressively expands to younger, lower‐risk patients with longer life expectancies, the occurrence of bioprosthetic valve failure due to structural valve deterioration (SVD) is expected to rise significantly. Moreover, other mechanisms including nonstructural valve dysfunction, thrombosis, and endocarditis may also be involved in worsening hemodynamic performance and lead to reintervention. 1

Transcatheter aortic valve‐in‐valve replacement (TAVR‐in‐TAVR) has emerged as an appealing strategy for the management of degenerated transcatheter heart valves (THVs) and offers a consistent solution, particularly in patients deemed at high or prohibitive risk for surgical explantation. 2 , 3 , 4 However, this procedure poses unique anatomic and technical challenges, such as preserving coronary access, ensuring favorable hemodynamics and minimizing the risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM). 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In this context, several design features should be considered, including the implantation mechanism (balloon‐expandable versus self‐expanding), valve height (short versus tall, relevant for relationships with the aortic root and coronary reaccess), and supra‐ or intra‐annular configuration (impacting hemodynamic performance).

The procedural success of TAVR‐in‐TAVR relies deeply on careful planning and appropriate THV platform selection. In this context, the choice between a supra‐annular (SAV or “tall frame”) and intra‐annular (IAV or “short frame”) platform, their hemodynamic impact, as well as the midterm outcomes are pivotal, yet the comparative performance of different iterations of bioprostheses remain poorly understood.

The present study sought to investigate the failure mechanisms of the initial THV and to assess the clinical and procedural outcomes of TAVR‐in‐TAVR procedures according to the supra‐annular or intra‐annular design of the bioprosthesis used.

Methods

The data, analytic methods, and study materials will not be made available to other researchers for purposes of reproducing the results or replicating the procedure.

Registry Design

The PANDORA (Supra‐Annular Versus Intra‐Annular Devices for TAVR‐in‐TAVR) international registry is an investigator‐initiated, multicenter, retrospective study designed to collect data on consecutive patients with failure of a previously implanted THV subsequently treated with TAVR‐in‐TAVR. All patients were evaluated by a local multidisciplinary heart team and deemed suitable candidates for TAVR‐in‐TAVR after comprehensive clinical and anatomic assessment and exclusion of surgical intervention. Preprocedural planning was systematically based on contrast‐enhanced computed tomography to evaluate the risk of coronary obstruction and assess valve‐to‐coronary distances, commissural alignment, and sinus anatomy. When elevated risk of coronary occlusion or sinus sequestration was identified, additional preventive techniques, including leaflet modification strategies, were used at the operator's discretion. Specifically, oversizing or downsizing was defined according to the nominal label size of the second THV relative to the true internal diameter of the index prosthesis on the basis of manufacturer‐provided sizing charts and computed tomography–derived measurements. Specifically, for both SAPIEN implantation (IAV, short frame) inside Evolut (SAV, tall frame) or vice versa, oversizing was considered when the selected THV had a nominal diameter larger than the inner diameter of the index valve, whereas downsizing was defined as the choice of a smaller nominal size. The same criteria were applied for SAPIEN or Evolut implantation inside Lotus (IAV, short frame) or ACURATE (SAV, tall frame), with reference to the respective valve‐in‐valve sizing recommendations. In all cases, the decision on oversizing or downsizing was at the operator's discretion, aimed at achieving optimal device expansion, secure anchoring, and favorable hemodynamic performance, while minimizing the risk of malapposition, underexpansion, or coronary obstruction.

For the present analysis, patients were stratified according to the annular design combination of the failed and second THV, yielding 4 different permutations: SAV‐IAV, SAV‐SAV, IAV‐SAV, and IAV‐IAV (Figure S1).

Data from the index TAVR procedure were analyzed, focusing on procedural outcomes, postprocedural echocardiographic parameters, and mechanisms of failure documented at the most recent follow‐up before the second TAVR. Furthermore, baseline, echocardiographic, and computed tomography characteristics at the time of the second TAVR were collected and assessed, as well as procedural and follow‐up information on postprocedural death and adverse events, including stroke, myocardial infarction, major bleeding, acute kidney injury, valve thrombosis, and endocarditis.

Baseline and follow‐up information was obtained through review of the institutional clinical database, and in‐person clinic visits and structured telephone interviews. The study was conducted with institutional review board approval, and all participants provided informed consent for data collection at each participating site.

Definitions

Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 (VARC‐3) definitions were adopted for preprocedural screening, procedural and clinical end points, despite these definitions not being specifically devised for TAVR‐in‐TAVR procedures. 1 On the basis of this, mechanisms of dysfunction for the index THV were categorized into: SVD, including intrinsic permanent changes to the prosthetic valve, wear and tear, leaflet disruption, flail leaflet, leaflet fibrosis and calcification, or strut fracture or deformation; andNon‐SVD valve dysfunction, including any nonintrinsic abnormality to the prosthetic valve, as paravalvular leak and PPM, inappropriate positioning, and thrombosis.

Infective endocarditis was excluded given the contraindication for transcatheter treatment. Similarly, VARC‐3 definitions for moderate and severe hemodynamic valve deterioration were used, as well as postprocedural definitions of acute technical success and 30‐day device success.

Study Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome of the study was 30‐day device success, whereas the primary safety outcome included 30‐day freedom from all‐cause death. A composite of freedom from all‐cause death and heart failure (HF) rehospitalization was used as the co–primary end point at the longest available follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median (interquartile range) on the basis of their distribution, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared across groups using 1‐way ANOVA. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson's χ2 test (with df according to the number of groups) or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Survival curves were plotted according to the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log‐rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to investigate variables associated with all‐cause death at the longest follow‐up available and to estimate corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. Variables were selected for inclusion in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model on the basis of clinical relevance and statistical significance at univariable analysis (P<0.10). Statistical significance was set at a 2‐tailed P value ≤0.05.

Analyses were performed with SPSS statistical software version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among ≈30 000 TAVR procedures performed between 2011 and 2024 across 12 high‐volume experienced centers in Europe and the United States, a total of 172 TAVR‐in‐TAVR cases (0.4%) were finally included. Thereafter, patients were grouped according to the design combination between the index and second THV: SAV into IAV (n=32 [18.6%]), SAV into SAV (n=29 [16.9%]), IAV into SAV (n=74 [43.0%]), and IAV into IAV (n=37 [21.5%]) (Figure S1).

The median time from the index to the second TAVR procedure was 1401 (interquartile range, 464–2251) days. The most implanted index prostheses were the CoreValve/Evolut (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) and Edwards SAPIEN (Edwards LifeSciences, Irvine, CA) (49.4% and 35.5%, respectively), with longer time to degeneration for these 2 platforms (Figure 1).

Procedural and In‐Hospital Details

The SAPIEN platform was the most‐used second valve (60.5%), followed by Evolut (35.5%) and Myval/Octacor (4.0%; Meril Life Sciences, Vapi, Gujarat, India), as outlined in Table 3. Different iterations were used for all platforms, particularly SAPIEN S3 (47.7%) and Evolut R (22.7%). In most cases, prostheses of the same size as the index THV were used (55.2%), particularly when an SAV was chosen as the second valve (P≤0.001). In contrast, downsizing occurred more frequently in the IAV‐SAV combination (70.3%, P≤0.001).

During a median follow‐up of 391 (interquartile range, 150–1240) days, no intergroup differences were observed on the incidence of adverse events including stroke, myocardial infarction, major bleeding, acute kidney injury, valve thrombosis, or endocarditis (Table 5). Valve Design and Hemodynamic Considerations

The increasing demand for TAVR‐in‐TAVR poses significant technical and clinical challenges, particularly on the choice of the second prosthesis. Although initial outcomes are promising, several questions remain about TAVR‐in‐TAVR, particularly the hemodynamic implications of implanting 2 stent valves in aortic position. Key concerns include the risk of leaflet thrombosis, the long‐term valve durability, the risk of coronary occlusion, and the feasibility of future coronary access. These considerations extend beyond the choice of the prosthesis (eg, balloon‐expandable versus self‐expanding) but also include technical aspects such as sizing, alignment, and postdilatation strategies. 6 , 10 , 11 , 12

In this context, Landes et al recently evaluated outcomes from the Redo‐TAVR (Outcomes of Redo Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement According to the Initial and Subsequent Valve Type) registry according to THV expansion mechanism. While no differences in early or midterm death were observed between balloon‐expandable and self‐expanding THVs, device success was significantly higher with self‐expanding valves due to lower postprocedural gradients. 13 However, their analysis did not specify the annular design of the THVs included in the study. In our experience, the supra‐annular or intra‐annular configuration of both the first and the second THV deserve careful assessment: Our analysis showed that patients with a prior IAV had significantly higher baseline mean transvalvular gradients (P=0.002), reflecting the well‐known propensity toward higher postprocedural gradients. This may explain the higher prevalence of IAV failure in women, who are more prone to PPM due to smaller annular dimensions, and aligns with prior studies indicating that SAVs perform better in terms of hemodynamics, particularly in smaller annular sizes and women. 7 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Our analysis confirmed a sex‐related pattern, with men more often presenting with a degenerated SAV and women with a degenerated IAV. The higher prevalence of SVD (77.9%), particularly severe stenosis, and PPM among patients with prior IAVs, mostly women, highlights the influence of prosthesis design and annular size on long‐term valve performance, emphasizing the need for careful valve selection and sizing, particularly in women with small annuli at risk of PPM. The finding that SVD was the predominant mechanism of failure also reinforces the importance of minimizing post‐TAVR gradients to delay further degeneration. 18 , 19 , 20

Moreover, the prolonged time to degeneration observed in CoreValve/Evolut and Edwards SAPIEN prostheses is consistent with prior data demonstrating relatively favorable long‐term hemodynamic profile and durability of these platforms. 21

Technical Aspects

Analysis of the 4 TAVR‐in‐TAVR configurations (SAV‐IAV, SAV‐SAV, IAV‐SAV, and IAV‐IAV) provides an important perspective on procedural outcomes. TAVR‐in‐TAVR appears to be a safe option, with all approaches demonstrating comparable appropriateness. The highest VARC‐3 technical success rates observed in SAV‐IAV and IAV‐SAV (P=0.090), although not significant, may suggest that a mixed strategy may confer procedural advantages.

It is well known that a supra‐annular configuration generally achieves larger effective orifice area and a lower postprocedural gradients (with the exception of the Portico/Navitor THVs that, although self‐expanding, is intra‐annular, and hence associated with similar effective orifice area and gradients compared with the self‐expanding supra‐annular Evolut THV family) 22 , 23 : As such, implanting an SAV inside a failed IAV may reduce residual gradients and improve valve function. Conversely, IAVs offer greater radial force and provide more annulus‐conforming capacity which may aid in stabilizing a previously placed (or misplaced) SAV, particularly in the setting of leaflet overexpansion, calcification, frame integrity issues, or late recoil. 24 , 25

The use of same‐type THVs (SAV‐SAV and IAV‐IAV) present distinct challenges: the SAV‐SAV iteration raises concerns about sinus sequestration and impaired coronary access. 26 , 27 In our cohort, coronary occlusion was rare (1.2%): 1 case in SAV‐IAV and 1 case in IAV‐SAV; moreover, none of the cases of SAV‐SAV required the BASILICA nor the chimney technique, likely reflecting careful patient selection, ensuring a large anatomy of the aortic root. In such settings, estimation of “neo‐skirt” height and geometry, along with assessment of coronary alignment and the spatial relationship between THVs, aorta, and coronaries, is critical: The use of dedicated simulation tools can facilitate this assessment and guide THV selection and positioning. 28 , 29

Similarly, to improve the feasibility of TAVR‐in‐TAVR, improve hemodynamics and ensure selective coronary cannulation, it is crucial to aim for optimal commissural alignment. 30 , 31 , 32 Conceivably, in case of severe misalignment of an index SAV and when a progression of coronary artery disease is likely, implanting a second SAV may not be the preferred strategy.

The use of IAV‐in‐IAV clearly poses a higher risk of significant gradients and thus more rapid leaflet degeneration or PPM, which is known to be associated with worse long‐term outcomes. 22

In our cohort, the BASILICA technique 33 was used in 3 cases (1.7%), and all of them were characterized by an IAV (Edwards SAPIEN) as the first prosthesis. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as leaflet modification is also relevant in SAV with their taller frames. 34 The observed pattern likely reflects anatomic selection or procedural planning factors rather than a true mechanistic association. As regards follow‐up, unfortunately, no specific information on coronary reaccess was reported, and only 2 cases (2/150 [1.3%]) of myocardial infarction occurred.

Periprocedural Complications

The observed 7% rate of new pacemaker implantation after TAVR‐in‐TAVR is noteworthy. Unlike TAVR‐in‐surgical aortic valve replacement, where the surgical sewing ring may attenuate force transmission, the TAVR‐in‐TAVR setting may pose a distinct risk. Although the initial valve frame might be expected to protect the conduction system, cumulative radial force from frame‐over‐frame expansion, and valve‐specific factors such as oversizing or postdilatation may increase septal trauma. Patients in the IAV‐SAV and SAV‐SAV groups (those who initially received an SAV self‐expanding valve) showed a significantly higher baseline rate of left bundle branch block (P=0.040; Table 1); however, postprocedural pacemaker implantation rates did not differ significantly across groups (P=0.348; Table 4). Patient‐related factors, including preexisting right bundle branch block and septal anatomy, may likely influence susceptibility to advanced atrioventricular block. 35

Acute kidney injury (13.5%, P=0.011) and postprocedural sepsis (P=0.037) were more frequent in IAV‐IAV cases, plausibly reflecting differences in procedural complexity baseline patient risk profiles not fully captured by this nonrandomized investigation, rather than THV design.

Outcomes and Clinical Implications

In our cohort, the in‐hospital mortality rate was 5.9% (10/172), with an overall 30‐day all‐cause mortality rate of 7.3% (11/150). The reported 30‐day mortality rate after valve‐in‐valve TAVR ranges around 4% to 6%, while TAVR‐in‐TAVR series show variable outcomes depending on patient selection and valve type. In contrast, surgical explant or redo‐surgical aortic valve replacement after failed TAVR carries a markedly higher operative mortality rate, around 14%. 3 , 36 , 37 Therefore, our rate, although slightly higher than pooled valve‐in‐valve estimates, remains within the expected range for high‐risk, real‐world redo‐TAVR populations. The observed mortality rate likely reflects urgent referrals, significant comorbidity burden, and procedural complexity.

Although mixed combinations (SAV‐IAV or IAV‐SAV) demonstrated numerically higher procedural success, these findings were not statistically significant and should be interpreted as descriptive trends rather than definitive results. The low VARC‐3 device success rate in IAV‐IAV (54.8%) may reflect the hemodynamic limitations of repeat intra‐annular valve implantation, particularly in terms of high residual aortic valve gradients, 22 whereas all‐cause death and significant intra‐ and peri‐aortic regurgitation had a less significant impact. Nonetheless, cardiovascular death (6.7%) and noncardiovascular death (0.7%) showed no significant intergroup differences, underscoring the importance of patient selection rather than valve design per se in determining early survival.

Although differences in residual transaortic gradient were not initially significant, during the follow‐up IAV‐IAV group exhibited the highest significant proportion of high (≥20 mm Hg) residual gradient (29.0%, P=0.030). This observation reinforces concerns about suboptimal effective orifice area with 2 intra‐annular frames. 38 On the other hand, SAV‐SAV showed the lowest rate of residual gradient ≥20 mm Hg (7.4%), which could be attributed to the favorable hemodynamic profile of SAV in maintaining a large effective orifice area. 39

Overall, at 1‐year Kaplan–Meier analysis, among the 4 possible combinations (SAV‐IAV, SAV‐SAV, IAV‐SAV, and IAV‐IAV), no significant differences were observed in survival or in the composite end point of freedom from death and HF hospitalization. The IAV‐IAV configuration showed the lowest freedom from death or HF (76.1%), yet this difference was not significant (P=0.734), suggesting that a suboptimal hemodynamic performance rather than intrinsic device configuration may underlie this trend. 40 The longest follow‐up will be needed to confirm this result and a potential significant impact upon outcome.

Although differences in all‐cause death and HF hospitalization among groups did not reach statistical significance, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a nonsignificant trend toward better survival when the first valve was an SAV. In these cases, freedom from death and from the composite end point appeared numerically higher compared with configurations involving a degenerated IAV (Figure S2). As illustrated in Figure 2A, not only the IAV‐IAV group, but also the SAV‐IAV group (ie, patients with an index IAV) demonstrated a trend toward higher mean gradients across all time points, reaching statistical significance at last follow‐up (P=0.030), suggesting a less favorable hemodynamic performance when the degenerated THV is IAV. Although exploratory and hypothesis generating only, this observation could represent a potential risk factor for suboptimal valve performance and warrants further investigation in larger, prospective studies.

Multivariate analysis identified male sex and chronic kidney disease as independent predictors of death, whereas VARC‐3 technical success and SAPT were protective. This reinforces prior evidence that technical success significantly impacts long‐term outcomes. 41 Furthermore, the benefit of SAPT aligns with growing data, suggesting a role in reducing bleeding complications after TAVR without increasing thrombotic risk. 42 , 43 However, the strong protective association observed with SAPT in our analysis should be interpreted with caution. It is conceivable that this finding may partly reflect patient selection rather than a direct causal effect of antiplatelet strategy. Despite multivariate adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as frailty, comorbid burden, or procedural complexity, cannot be excluded. Thus, while our results are consistent with emerging evidence favoring SAPT in contemporary TAVR practice, they should be considered hypothesis generating and highlight the need for dedicated prospective studies to better define optimal antithrombotic therapy in the TAVR‐in‐TAVR setting.

Limitations

This investigator‐initiated registry offers real‐world insights into TAVR‐in‐TAVR outcomes; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, its observational retrospective nature inherently introduces the potential of selection bias and unmeasured confounders that may have affected results, as procedural decisions were left to operator discretion and not randomized. For this purpose, only patients with preserved or mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction were included, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with reduced systolic function. Moreover, the relatively small sample size of the 4 groups may have limited statistical power, and the inclusion of different THV platforms may have introduced additional heterogeneity that could have influenced outcomes. Similarly, our results cannot be generalized for all the TAVR platforms available that were not part of the present analysis, such as the Portico/Navitor devices. Second, while the study adhered to VARC‐3 definitions for consistency, no central adjudication of clinical events or imaging data was performed.

Third, the wide time frame of the registry (2011–2024) encompasses substantial heterogeneity in device platforms, iteration generations, and implantation techniques, limiting generalizability to contemporary practice. We acknowledge that the retrospective, long‐term design introduces heterogeneity related to device evolution and changing practice patterns. However, all participating centers are high‐volume, highly experienced TAVR institutions, which likely mitigates variability and ensures procedural consistency across eras.

Fourth, the use of advanced coronary protection techniques such as BASILICA or chimney stenting was low, suggesting a degree of patient selection toward anatomies perceived at lower risk. Additionally, while coronary occlusion was rare, our study did not report detailed data on coronary reaccess at follow‐up. Fifth, although SAPT was associated with improved survival, the present study did not stratify patients according to anticoagulation regimens or thrombotic complications, which is the current focus of a subanalysis of the current registry.

Finally, only 1‐year results were reported; thus, long‐term outcomes and hemodynamic performance of different TAVR‐in‐TAVR iterations remain uncertain. Despite these limitations, this study represents one of the most comprehensive multicenter evaluations of TAVR‐in‐TAVR and provides valuable hypothesis‐generating data to guide future prospective studies.

Conclusions

This large multicenter study provides new insights into the technical and clinical implications of THV design selection for TAVR‐in‐TAVR. The 4 possible iterations did not differ at 1‐year follow‐up with respect to the clinical outcome, although the IAV‐IAV configuration presented the worst hemodynamic performance in terms of residual transvalvular gradient. Thorough procedural planning is of paramount importance to maximize the risk–benefit profile in this complex scenario.
