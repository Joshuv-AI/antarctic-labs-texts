Intrinsic and relative characterization results for logics with negative modalities
Jim de Groot, João Marcos, Rodrigo Stefanes

Abstract
We introduce simulations for modal logics with subclassical negations and restoration modalities, establish an adequacy theorem, and prove intrinsic (Hennessy–Milner-type) and relative (Van Benthem-type) characterization results. These results identify each restorative language with the fragment of first-order logic invariant under its simulations and delineate the expressive profile of modal logics with non-classical negations.



1
Introduction

Two fundamental results in the model theory of modal logic are associated to the names of Hennessy, Milner, and Van Benthem.
While first-order logic cannot distinguish between isomorphic models, since such models satisfy exactly the same first-order formulas, the «expressive granularity» of modal logic favors, for the same job, the weaker notion of bisimulation.
In this respect, the Hennessy–Milner theorem provides an
intrinsic characterization
of modal expressivity:
it establishes a correspondence between bisimilarity, for certain classes of models, and modal equivalence.
The Van Benthem theorem, in turn, offers a
relative characterization
of expressivity: it demonstrates that the modal language is precisely as expressive as the bisimulation-invariant fragment of first-order logic.

In the present work, we arrive at Hennessy–Milner-type and Van Benthem-type results for modal logics and languages endowed with negative modalities and additional restoration operators that enable these modalities to recover the behavior of classical negation. We begin by briefly situating this study within its broader theoretical setting.

Negative modalities over a positive basis.

Assuming logic to be grounded on the complementary and mutually exclusive judgments of
assertion
and
denial
, one may take the
conjunction
of two formulas to be asserted iff both formulas are asserted; dually, the
disjunction
of two formulas may be taken to be denied iff both formulas are denied; the nullary forms of these connectives, called
top
and
bottom
, are by default asserted and denied, respectively.

Negation
, in turn, is construed as an operation that inverts judgments: it denies an asserted formula and asserts a denied one.
A paracomplete negation arises when both a formula and its negation are jointly denied,
while not denying all other formulas;
a paraconsistent negation arises when both a formula and its negation are jointly asserted,
while not asserting all other formulas.
In what follows, we focus on logics containing negations with a modal character, for which a certain
global contraposition principle
is expected to hold: whenever one formula follows from another, the negation of the latter follows from the negation of the former. Such behavior often results as a by-product of a
classical-like negation
, namely one that is neither paracomplete nor paraconsistent, and that systematically converts all assertions into denials and vice versa. In the present study, nonetheless, negations will not be automatically assumed to behave classically.

Restorative modal logics.

The modal negations considered here fail to respect certain fundamental classical principles. To
recover
such lost assumptions and
recapture
the full deductive power of classical propositional logic, we introduce a collection of standard
restoration connectives
into the language:
the consistency of a formula is denied precisely when the formula is asserted together with its (paraconsistent) negation; dually, the undeterminedness of a formula is asserted precisely when the formula is denied together with its (paracomplete) negation. In this framework, notions of [
in
]
consistency
and [
un
]
determinedness
also receive a modal interpretation.
Modal logics enriched with these connectives, namely subclassical negations and their restoration companions, will here be called
restorative modal logics
. As will be shown, they correspond to a highly expressive fragment of classical first-order logic, a fragment that is capable of simulating the behavior of non-classical negations while fully reinstating classical reasoning, despite the fact that, in general, no classical-like negation is therein definable.

Bisimulations.

A bisimulation is a relation between worlds of Kripke models that preserves and reflects the truth of propositional letters and mirrors the accessibility relation in both directions.
As a result, worlds linked by a bisimulation satisfy exactly the same formulas.
Also known as
p-relations
and
zig-zag relations
,
bisimulations were first introduced by Van Benthem as part of his work on
correspondence theory
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
They were independently discovered in computer science
as an equivalence relation between process graphs
[
54
,
38
]
,
and later employed as a notion of equality in
non-well-founded set theory
[
1
,
7
,
6
]
.

From a (modal) logical point of view, bisimulations often serve a double purpose.
First, they provide a structural notion that guarantees
logical equivalence. Here by «structural» we mean that the definition
of a bisimulation only refers to the interpretation of propositional letters
and to the modal accessibility relation,
but not to the interpretation of arbitrary formulas.
In special cases, the converse also holds: if two
worlds satisfy the same formulas then they are linked by some bisimulation.
This property is called the
Hennessy–Milner property
, and classes of models satisfying it are called
Hennessy–Milner classes
,
after the authors of
[
38
]
.
Second, bisimulations establish a precise correspondence between modal logic and first-order logic: under its usual interpretation, the standard modal language corresponds exactly to the bisimulation-invariant fragment of first-order logic with one free variable.
This result was first proven by Van Benthem
[
8
]
, and is commonly referred to as the
Van Benthem characterization theorem
.

Following Hennessy and Milner’s theorem and Van Benthem’s theorem for normal modal logic in its customary language with positive modalities,
analogous results were obtained for a wide range of logics, including non-normal modal logics
[
36
,
37
]
, modal fixpoint logics
[
40
,
25
]
,
and (bi-)intuitionistic (modal) logic
[
55
,
3
,
34
]
.
Each modal language is equipped with its own appropriate notion of bisimulation.

Simulations.

The symmetrical nature of bisimulations entails that they automatically preserve the truth of negations. While this feature is appropriate for modal extensions of classical propositional logic, it is undesirable when seeking characterization results for logics lacking classical-like negations,
such as positive modal logic
[
42
]
.
In such settings, bisimulations are sometimes replaced with weaker relations known as
simulations
. The key difference between bisimulations
and simulations is that the latter preserve the truth of formulas without necessarily reflecting it.

Similar strategies have been employed, for instance, in the characterization of non-distributive positive
logic as a fragment of first-order logic
[
33
]

and in analyses of fragments of the calculus of binary relations
[
29
]
.
Since the restorative modal logics examined in this paper can all be viewed as positive logic augmented with unary modal operators, we likewise make use of simulations.

Contributions.

In this paper we develop a notion of simulation tailored to restorative modal logics.
More precisely, we define a notion of simulation that is parametric in a
similarity type and that describes the intensional operators in the language.
This poses an interesting challenge: although simulations
are used to prevent the preservation of the truth of negations, some of the modal operators
under investigation exhibit an inherently negative character
that must, in fact, be preserved.
For one of the restoration modalities, the so-called «consistency operator», this leads to a notion of
simulation closely resembling the one in
[
27
, Section 5]
.

After identifying the correct notion of simulation, in each case, we establish
intrinsic (that is, Hennessy–Milner-type) characterization results
for the class of modally saturated Kripke models.
This, in turn, gives rise to
relative (that is, Van Benthem-type) characterization results
for the modal languages that are here under scrutiny.
Finally, we outline how analogous results can be obtained for restorative modal logics with a classical-like negation, and we speculate how these findings may
fit into a general pattern of simulations for «combined modalities».

Classical and normal modal logics.

The defining feature of
classical modal logics

[

19

is the so-called

(also known as

\alpha
and
\beta

(that is,
\alpha\vartriangleright\beta

\beta\vartriangleright\alpha

\varphi[p\mapsto\alpha]

\varphi[p\mapsto\beta]

\varphi[p\mapsto\gamma]
indicates that all occurrences of the propositional letter
p

\varphi

\gamma

Intuitively, in classical modal logics equivalent formulas are logically indistinguishable, or interchangeable in all contexts. In other words, from an algebraic viewpoint, all
schematic contexts

\varphi(p)
are quotient-compatible with the underlying notion of logical equivalence. If each connective of a given logic —thought of as inducing, on each of its arguments, a schematic context— is quotient-compatible with the underlying notion of logical equivalence, the replacement property clearly holds.

Given a unary connective
\heartsuit
, sufficient conditions for ensuring its quotient-compatibility with logical equivalence consist in, for all formulas
\alpha
and
\beta

modal normality
in one of the two following guises
[
21
]
:

[prs]

if
\alpha\vartriangleright\beta
, then
\heartsuit\alpha\vartriangleright\heartsuit\beta

[rev]

if
\alpha\vartriangleright\beta
, then
\heartsuit\beta\vartriangleright\heartsuit\alpha

The «preservation» condition [prs] can be viewed as the monotonicity of
\heartsuit
with respect to
\vartriangleright
, and is characteristic of
positive modalities
.
The «reversal» condition [rev], on the other hand, can be viewed as antitonicity of
\heartsuit
with respect to
\vartriangleright
and is characteristic of
negative modalities
. In this sense, it is clear that the box and the diamond connectives of usual modal languages are positive modalities, whereas negation in intuitionistic (or classical) logic is a negative modality.

Restoration connectives.

When a classical-like negation is replaced by a subclassical one, some assumption or principle characteristic of classical negation is inevitably absent. A richer language may be often devised, though, in order to allow for such lost assumptions to be explicitly recovered. This is the very idea behind the introduction of the so-called
restoration connectives
: here, their role is to allow for the properties of classical negation to be recaptured.

Recall that a paraconsistent negation
\mathord{\smallsmile}

\mathord{\smallsmile}

, in such a way that the formula
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}p

p
and
\mathord{\smallsmile}p

\mathord{\smallsmile}

3
Kripke semantics for modal logics

As we shall entertain a range of unary operators, we start by introducing notation for the languages thereby induced:

3.1 Definition
.

By a
similarity type
we shall refer to a collection of unary connectives.
Fix some arbitrary set
K
and let
\Lambda
be a similarity type.
We define
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
to be the language generated by the grammar

\varphi::=p_{k}\mid\top\mid\bot\mid\varphi\wedge\varphi\mid\varphi\vee\varphi\mid\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}\varphi

where
k
ranges over
K
and
\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}\in\Lambda
.

3.1
Modal logic as you know it

We briefly recall some basic definitions regarding
relational semantics for modal languages.
Formulas in
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond}
will be interpreted as follows:

3.2 Definition
.

A
Kripke model

\mathfrak{M}
is a structure
\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
consisting of a nonempty set

W
of «worlds», a binary «accessibility relation»
R
on
W
, and a unary predicate
P_{k}
on
W
for each
k\in K
.
Each such
P_{k}
represents the «proposition expressed by the propositional letter
p_{k}
within
\mathfrak{M}
».
We refer to
P_{k}
as the
truth set of
p_{k}
, and denote it by
\llbracket{p_{k}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}
.
Given
w\in W
, we let
R[w]:=\{v\in W\mid wRv\}
be the set of worlds accessible from
w
.
The truth set
\llbracket{\varphi}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}
of a formula
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond}
in
\mathfrak{M}
is then recursively defined by:

(TrS
\top
)

\llbracket{\top}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=W

(TrS
\bot
)

\llbracket{\bot}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=\varnothing

(TrS
\land
)

\llbracket{\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=\llbracket{\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}\cap\llbracket{\varphi_{2}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}

(TrS
\lor
)

\llbracket{\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=\llbracket{\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}\cup\llbracket{\varphi_{2}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}

(TrS
{\sim}
)

\llbracket{{\sim}\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=W\setminus\llbracket{\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}

(TrS
\Box
)

\llbracket{\Box\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=\{w\in W\mid R[w]\subseteq\llbracket{\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}\}

(TrS
\Diamond
)

\llbracket{\Diamond\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}:=\{w\in W\mid R[w]\cap\llbracket{\varphi_{1}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}\neq\varnothing\}

Clearly,
\Box
and
\Diamond
are interdefinable with the help of
{\sim}
. This interdefinability ceases if
{\sim}
is omitted from the language.

3.3 Remark
.

As usual, when
w\in\llbracket{\varphi}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}
we say that
\varphi
is
satisfied at the world
w
of the model
\mathfrak{M}
, and we denote this by
\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\varphi
.
It is a straightforward exercise to rewrite the conditions on truth sets as
satisfaction conditions
. For example:

(Sat
{\sim}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash{\sim}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\not\Vdash\varphi

(Sat
\Box
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\Box\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }v\in W,\text{ if }wRv\text{ then }\mathfrak{M},v\Vdash\varphi

(Sat
\Diamond
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\Diamond\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{there exists }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ and }\mathfrak{M},v\Vdash\varphi

We note that the negation symbol
{\sim}
is interpreted «classically» (at each given world).
In fact, using the terminology and definitions introduced in Section
2
, and identifying assertion (resp. denial) with satisfaction (resp. non-satisfaction), one may now easily check that:
(0)
\land
,
\lor
,
\top
,
\bot
respect all the corresponding standard principles;
(1)
\Diamond
is a diamond-plus connective;
(2)
\Box
is a box-plus connective;
(3)
\sim
is a classical-like negation.

3.4 Remark
.

Kripke models are customarily presented in the literature as structures of the form
\langle\langle W,R\rangle,V\rangle
, where the directed graph
\langle W,R\rangle
is a «Kripke frame» and the «valuation» mapping
V:\{p_{k}\mid k\in K\}\to 2^{W}
assigns to each propositional letter the set of all worlds in which it is true, that is, for each
k\in K
we have that
V(p_{k})=\{w\in W\mid\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash p_{k}\}=\llbracket{p_{k}}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{M}}=P_{k}
. However, for our present purposes we find it more perspicuous to present Kripke models simply as first-order structures interpreting a signature containing one binary relation symbol,
\mathbf{R}
, and one unary predicate symbol,
\mathbf{P}_{k}
, for each
k\in K
.
For that matter, see also Definition
7.1
.

We will sometimes merge two Kripke models as follows:

3.5 Definition
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{1}=\langle W_{1},R_{1},\{P_{k,1}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}=\langle W_{2},R_{2},\{P_{k,2}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
be two
Kripke models. Let:

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle:=\{(\ell,w)\mid\ell\in\{1,2\}\text{ and }w\in W_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle:=\{((\ell,w),(\ell,v))\mid\ell\in\{1,2\}\text{ and }(w,v)\in R_{\ell}\}

\displaystyle P_{k}

\displaystyle:=\{(\ell,w)\mid\ell\in\{1,2\}\text{ and }w\in P_{k,\ell}\}\text{, for each }k\in K

Then the
disjoint union
of
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}
is the Kripke
model
\mathfrak{M}_{1}\uplus\mathfrak{M}_{2}:=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
.

The following result confirms that the two Kripke models given as input in the definition above live inside their disjoint union. It may be proven using a routine inductive argument, or using the fact that the inclusion maps are bounded morphisms:

3.6 Proposition
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}_{1}=\langle W_{1},R_{1},\{P_{k,1}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}=\langle W_{2},R_{2},\{P_{k,2}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
be two
Kripke models, and for each
\ell\in\{1,2\}
let

\incl_{\ell}:\mathfrak{M}_{\ell}\to\mathfrak{M}_{1}\uplus\mathfrak{M}_{2}

be the inclusion map given by
w\mapsto(\ell,w)
.
Then for all
w\in W_{\ell}
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond}
,

\mathfrak{M}_{\ell},w\Vdash\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt(\mathfrak{M}_{1}\uplus\mathfrak{M}_{2}),\incl_{\ell}(w)\Vdash\varphi.

3.2
Interpreting negative modalities and their restoration companions

In this paper, by «positive logic» we mean the logic of top, bottom, conjunction and disjunction, all interpreted as in classical logic.
We introduce below the unary connectives
\mathord{\smallsmile}
and
\mathord{\smallfrown}
in order to represent «subclassical negations», and the unary connectives
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
,
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
,
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}
, and
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}
to represent «restoration connectives».

3.7 Definition
.

A
restorative similarity type
is a subset of

\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\}
.

Restorative modal logics will be interpreted in Kripke models as follows:

3.8 Definition
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
be a Kripke model
and let
\Lambda
be a restorative similarity type.
Then the interpretation of a formula
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}

at a world
w\in W
is recursively defined via the relevant
clauses from Definition
3.2
plus the following clauses in the style of Remark
3.3
:

(Sat
\mathord{\smallsmile}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\mathord{\smallsmile}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{there exists }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ and }\mathfrak{M},v\not\Vdash\varphi

(Sat
\mathord{\smallfrown}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\mathord{\smallfrown}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ we have }\mathfrak{M},v\not\Vdash\varphi

(Sat
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\not\Vdash\varphi\text{ or }(\text{for all }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ we have }\mathfrak{M},v\Vdash\varphi)

(Sat
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\varphi\text{ or }(\text{for all }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ we have }\mathfrak{M},v\not\Vdash\varphi)

(Sat
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\varphi\text{ and }(\text{there exists }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ and }\mathfrak{M},v\not\Vdash\varphi)

(Sat
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}
)

\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\varphi\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\not\Vdash\varphi\text{ and }(\text{there exists }v\in W\text{ such that }wRv\text{ and }\mathfrak{M},v\Vdash\varphi)

Let
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
be two worlds in two Kripke models

\mathfrak{M}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}
, respectively.
Then we write
\mathfrak{M}_{1},w_{1}\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}\mathfrak{M}_{2},w_{2}

if

\mathfrak{M}_{1},w_{1}\Vdash\varphi
implies
\mathfrak{M}_{2},w_{2}\Vdash\varphi
, for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
.
If the Kripke models are clear from the context, we simply write

w_{1}\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}w_{2}
.
We will refer to
\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}
as the
subsumption relation
.

Rewriting the above satisfaction conditions as conditions on truth sets would be straightforward.
Crucially, all the above modalities may be viewed as abbreviations in
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond}
.
For example,
\mathord{\smallsmile}\varphi
corresponds to
\Diamond{\sim}\varphi

(a.k.a. «negation as nonnecessity»)
and
\mathord{\smallfrown}\varphi
corresponds to
\Box{\sim}\varphi
(a.k.a. «negation as impossibility»).
The restoration modalities then arise from conjoining or disjoining
these with either
\varphi
or
{\sim}\varphi
.
Besides, some restoration modalities can be defined using
\mathord{\smallsmile}
or
\mathord{\smallfrown}
.
For example,
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\varphi
clearly corresponds to
\varphi\wedge\mathord{\smallsmile}\varphi
.
However, in absence of a classical negation,
most of these interdefinability features vanish.

Using the terminology and definitions introduced in Section
2
, one may now easily check that: (1)
\mathord{\smallsmile}
is a diamond-minus (paraconsistent) negation; (2)
\mathord{\smallfrown}
is a box-minus (paracomplete) negation;
(3)
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
,
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}
,
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
and

are all standard restoration connectives.
In addition, the semantic clauses in Definition

3.3
On the (un)definability of classical negation

In this section we will focus on restorative modal logics whose semantics satisfy all clauses mentioned in Definition
3.8
. As usual within the context of Kripke semantics, we will dub
classical negation
a connective interpreted according to condition (TrS
{\sim}
) from Definition
3.2
. Several results concerning the undefinability of a classical negation were established in
[
45
]
. Some of these results are recalled in what follows.

3.13 Proposition
.

A classical negation is not definable in the minimal restorative normal modal logic
PK_{\Lambda}
, determined by the class of all Kripke frames and based on
\Lambda=\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\}
.

The proof of the above result, given in
[
45
, Theorem 6.1]
, is quite involved, using a sophisticated semantical argument and an induction over all unary formulas that could be considered as candidates for playing the role of a classical negation. In contrast, a much simpler proof of undefinability of classical negation, using the tools we introduce in the present paper, may be found in Example
4.5
below.

3.14 Remark
.

As mentioned in Example
3.11
, deductively stronger restorative modal logics have also been studied, by restricting attention to specific classes of frames. In contrast to the above result, one may show that a classical negation is indeed available at some of these logics. Over the logics
PKT_{\Lambda}
determined by the class of reflexive frames, for instance, the classical negation of
p
may be explicitly defined by considering
\mathord{\smallsmile}p\land\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}p
or by considering
\mathord{\smallfrown}p\lor\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}p
, according to which restorative similarity type
\Lambda
one happens to entertain. Moreover, over the weaker logics
PKD_{\Lambda}
determined by the class of serial frames one may consider, for instance,
(\mathord{\smallsmile}p\lor\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}p)\land\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}p
or
(\mathord{\smallfrown}p\land\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}p)\lor\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}p
.

Undefinability results are also known for stronger restorative modal logics (again, see
[
45
, Theorem 6.1]
):

3.15 Proposition
.

A classical negation is also not definable in the restorative modal logics
PKB_{\Lambda}
and
PK4_{\Lambda}
, respectively determined by the class of symmetric frames and by the class of transitive frames, based on
\Lambda=\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\}
.

Note that as soon as positive logic is upgraded through the presence a classical negation
{\sim}
, then all classical connectives turn out to be definable as usual, and if negative modalities are available then the positive modalities are also definable: indeed,
\Box\varphi
corresponds to
{\sim}\mathord{\smallsmile}\varphi
(or to
\mathord{\smallfrown}{\sim}\varphi
), and
\Diamond\varphi
corresponds to
\mathord{\smallsmile}{\sim}\varphi
(or to
{\sim}\mathord{\smallfrown}\varphi
). As the negative modalities from
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown}}
are obviously definable in
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond}
, then these are essentially the same logics.
This explains our focus on logics with negative (and restorative) modalities in which classical negation is
not
definable. Analogously, in the presence of a connective
\to
that behaves as classical implication, it is easy to define a classical negation if, say, a diamond-minus connective is also available. Accordingly, we are primarily interested in logics
without
a classical implication.

3.16 Remark
.

In Example
3.9
a language containing a box-minus connective and an intuitionistic implication is mentioned. The problem of adding an intuitionistic-like implication to a restorative modal logic containing a classicality connective (see Example
3.12
) is a rather non-trivial one. On this topic, the interested reader is invited to consult
[
32

6
Intrinsic characterization results

We have seen that the existence of a
\Lambda
-simulation implies subsumption.
A straightforward adaptation of
[
12
, Example 2.23]
shows, however, that the
converse does not hold in general.
As is customary, we can show the
converse holds for the class of image-finite Kripke models, and the result further extends to the class of modally saturated Kripke models.

For the cases of
\mathord{\smallsmile}
and
\mathord{\smallfrown}
the proof is analogous to the classical
or the positive case — see e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 2.24]

and
[

However, the simulation conditions for the restoration modalities contain
additional
S
-connections (the dashed arrows in Figure
3

which reflect the fact that these modalities are, in a certain sense, made up of
\mathord{\smallsmile}\varphi

or
\mathord{\smallfrown}\varphi
together with truth or falsehood of
\varphi

The additional
S
-connections provide an alternative way of satisfying a simulation condition. They are essential to the proof of the intrinsic characterization results in this section because the absence of such connections allows one to assume the
existence of a formula
\chi
that is true in one world and false in another.
In Example
6.5
we illustrate the failure of the intrinsic characterization result when we omit these dashed arrows from the simulation conditions.

We now introduce the notions of satisfiability and refutability. The latter is going to be particularly useful for dealing with negative modalities.

6.1 Definition
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
be a Kripke model and

A set

such

\mathfrak{M},u\Vdash\varphi
for all

Analogously,

is said to be

such
that

for all
\varphi\in\Delta

The set

is called

U

if
every finite subset of
\Delta
is satisfiable in
U
, and

finite refutability
is defined analogously.

We also recall the definitions of image-finiteness and of modal saturation
[
12
]
, a compactness property that will be of interest to us:

6.2 Definition

Let

We call

of formulas and

In the proofs of the following lemmas, we shall freely make use of the modal interpretations of the connectives (Definition

{\sim}\st(x,\psi)\in\mathcal{T}(x)
,
and
\mathfrak{N}\Vdash\alpha(x)[v]
.
Therefore, by construction of
\mathcal{T}(x)
, if
\mathfrak{N}\Vdash\st(x,\varphi)[v]
then
\mathfrak{M}\Vdash\st(x,\varphi)[w]
,
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
, hence, by Lemma
7.3
,
w
subsumes
v
.

Let
\mathfrak{N}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{M}^{*}
be
\omega
-saturated elementary extensions
of
\mathfrak{N}
and
\mathfrak{M}
, respectively, and let
v^{*}
and
w^{*}
be the
images of
v
and
w

[
18
, Theorems 4.1.9 and 6.1.1]
.
Then
\mathfrak{N}^{*},v^{*}\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}\mathfrak{M}^{*},w^{*}
.
Moreover, since every
\omega
-saturated model is modally saturated
(see e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 2.65]
),
Theorem
6.8
yields
\mathfrak{N}^{*},v^{*}\mathrel{\kern 1.0pt\underline{\kern-0.5pt\to\kern-0.5pt}\kern 1.0pt}_{\Lambda}\mathfrak{M}^{*},w^{*}
.
Finally, since
\mathfrak{N}\Vdash\alpha(x)[v]
we have

\mathfrak{N}^{*}\Vdash\alpha(x)[v^{*}]
and because
\alpha(x)
is preserved by

\Lambda
-simulations we get
\mathfrak{M}^{*}\Vdash\alpha(x)[w^{*}]
.
Invariance of truth of classical first-order formulas under elementary embeddings then gives

\mathfrak{M}\Vdash\alpha(x)[w]
, as desired.
∎

Since
\Lambda
-similarity coincides with directed
\Lambda
-similarity
when
\Lambda\subseteq\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown}\}
(recall Corollary
6.9
), we also get:

7.6 Corollary
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown}\}
and
let
\alpha(x)
be a
\mathrm{FOL}
-formula with one free variable
x
.
Then
\alpha(x)
is equivalent to the standard translation of
a
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
-formula if and only if it is preserved by
directed
\Lambda
-simulations.

Proof.

Combine Proposition
5.5
with Theorem
7.5
.
∎

8
Symmetrical simulations for restorative modal logics with a classical negation

The main logics considered so far in this work had their languages induced by restorative similarity types.
By adding a classical negation to some of those logics they become exactly as expressive as «standard» modal logics (the logics having
\{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond\}
as their similarity type).
For an example, if one simply adds a classical negation to a restorative modal logic with a diamond-minus connective, an appropriate notion of bisimulation is already known
[
12
, Section 2.2]
, because
\mathord{\smallsmile}
and
\Box
are interdefinable in the presence of classical negation.
However, this is not the case for every restorative similarity type
(see Example
8.7
),
so the study of bisimulations does not always boil down to the bisimulations for the language
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Box,\Diamond}
, which we will refer to as
Kripke bisimulations

[
12
, Section 2.2]
.

In this section, we extend the results presented for logics with language
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
with
\Lambda\subseteq\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\}
to logics with language
\mathcal{L}_{\{{\sim}\}\cup\Lambda}
,
which we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Lambda}
.
In this way, we obtain some results already studied in the literature, as in
[
27
]
, as well as new ones.
We do so by restricting our focus to
symmetrical

\Lambda
-simulations.

8.1 Definition
.

Let
\Lambda
be a restorative similarity type.
A
symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulation
on a Kripke model

\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
is a
\Lambda
-simulation

S
on
\mathfrak{M}
such that

(w,v)\in\text{S}
implies
(v,w)\in\text{S}
, for all
w,v\in W
.
We write
w\mathrel{\kern 1.0pt\underline{\kern-0.5pt\leftrightarrow\kern-0.5pt}\kern 1.0pt}_{\Lambda}v
if there exists a symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulation
linking
w
and
v
.

8.2 Example
.

If
\Lambda
contains
\mathord{\smallsmile}
or
\mathord{\smallfrown}
,
then it follows immediately from symmetry that any
symmetric
\Lambda
-simulation satisfies the usual
Kripke bisimulation conditions
[
12
, Definition 2.16]
.
Conversely, every Kripke bisimulation
B
such that
B
is
symmetric clearly satisfies all simulation conditions from
Definition
4.1
, so it is a symmetric
\Lambda
-simulation for any restorative similarity type
\Lambda
.
Since every Kripke bisimulation can be extended to a symmetric one, it follows that whenever either
\mathord{\smallsmile}\in\Lambda
or
\mathord{\smallfrown}\in\Lambda
,
symmetric
\Lambda
-bisimilarity and Kripke bisimilarity
coincide.
So in these cases the study of symmetric
\Lambda
-simulations boils down to Kripke bisimulations.
∎

8.3 Example
.

The restorative modal language with
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
and a classical negation was introduced in
[
47
]
, where it was shown to be strictly less expressive than
\mathcal{L}_{\sim,\Box,\Diamond}
.
Subsequently, in
[
27
]
, a notion of
\circ
-bisimulation for the corresponding logic, as well as
intrinsic and relative characterization results,
were provided.
The notion of a
\circ
-bisimulation is similar to our symmetrical
\{\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\}
-simulation,
but they are not exactly the same:
every symmetrical
\{\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\}
-simulation is a
\circ
-bisimulation,
but an arbitrary
\circ
-bisimulation
\mathrm{B}
is a symmetrical
\{\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\}
-simulation
if and only if
\mathrm{B}
is a symmetric relation.
Since every
\circ
-bisimulation
\mathrm{B}
can be extended to a symmetrical
\circ
-bisimulation, its induced notion of bisimilarity coincides with
symmetrical
\{\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\}
-similarity.
In other words, two worlds can be linked by a
\circ
-bisimulation if and only if they
can be linked by a symmetrical
\{\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\}
-simulation.
∎

If
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are worlds in two Kripke models
\mathfrak{M}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{2}
,
then we write
w_{1}\mathrel{\leftrightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}w_{2}
when they satisfy precisely the
same
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Lambda}
-formulas. We note that this coincides with
logical subsumption in
\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Lambda}
,
because preservation of the truth of the classical negation of a formula implies reflection of the truth of that formula.

The symmetrical aspect of symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulations ensures that it at once
preserves and reflects the truth of formulas. That is, worlds linked by a
symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulation satisfy precisely the same formulas:

8.4 Theorem
.

Let
\Lambda
be a restorative
similarity type and
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle

a Kripke model. Then for all worlds
w,v\in W
,

w\mathrel{\kern 1.0pt\underline{\kern-0.5pt\leftrightarrow\kern-0.5pt}\kern 1.0pt}_{\Lambda}v\hskip 10.00002pt\text{implies}\hskip 10.00002ptw\mathrel{\leftrightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}v.

Proof.

We use induction on the structure of
\varphi
to prove that for any
symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulation
S
on
\mathfrak{M}
and all worlds
w,v\in W
,

w\Vdash\varphi
implies
v\Vdash\varphi
.
This suffices because, in the presence of a classical negation,

w\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}_{{\sim},\Lambda}v
coincides with
w\mathrel{\leftrightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}v
.
All cases except for negation proceed as in the proof of
Theorem
4.3
. The induction case for negation follows
immediately from the symmetry of
S
.
∎

The intrinsic characterization result for modally saturated Kripke models
from Theorem
6.8
also readily carries over.
Using the same proofs as in Section
6
one may check that

\mathrel{\rightsquigarrow}_{{\sim},\Lambda}
is a
\Lambda
-simulation. But since
this coincides with
\mathrel{\leftrightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}
, it follows that the resulting

\Lambda
-simulation is also symmetric. Accordingly:

8.5 Theorem

(Symmetrical intrinsic characterization)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a restorative similarity type and
let
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
be a modally saturated Kripke model.
Then, for all
w,v\in W
,

w\mathrel{\leftrightsquigarrow}_{\Lambda}v\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002ptw\mathrel{\kern 1.0pt\underline{\kern-0.5pt\leftrightarrow\kern-0.5pt}\kern 1.0pt}_{\Lambda}v.

This allows us to derive a relative characterization result via a minor adaptation.
First, we extend the standard translation from Definition
7.2
to
a translation
\st:\mathrm{Var}\times\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Lambda}\to\mathrm{FOL}
by adding
the recursive clause

\st(x,{\sim}\varphi)={\sim}\st(x,\varphi).

Second, we say that a first-order formula
\alpha(x)
with one free
variable
x
is
invariant under symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulations

if for every Kripke model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,R,\{P_{k}\}_{k\in K}\rangle
,
all worlds
w,v\in W
, and every symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulation
S

such that
(w,v)\in\text{S}
, we have

\mathfrak{M}\Vdash\alpha(x)[w]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M}\Vdash\alpha(x)[v].

Finally, we generalize Theorem
7.5
to:

8.6 Theorem
.

Let
\Lambda
be a restorative similarity type, and
\alpha(x)
a
\mathrm{FOL}
-formula
with one free variable
x
.
Then
\alpha(x)
is equivalent to the standard translation of a

\mathcal{L}_{{\sim},\Lambda}
-formula if and only if it is invariant under
symmetrical
\Lambda
-simulations.

Proof.

The proof is exactly the same as that of Theorem
7.5
,
except that we appeal to the symmetrical intrinsic characterization result (Theorem
8.5
)
instead of its counterpart from Theorem
6.8
.
∎

This theorem allows us to recover (a slight variation of) the characterization
result from
[
27

restorative similarity types are subsets of

\{\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}\}

9
Outlook: combined modalities

Each of the restoration modalities
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
and
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}

is equivalent to an affirmation or a classical negation combined with one of

\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown},\Diamond
and
\Box
.
It is not fortuitous, indeed, that this gets reflected on the clauses defining the standard translation, in Definition
7.2
.
Furthermore, the simulation conditions of the restoration modalities are
closely related to the simulation conditions of their «ingredients».
We briefly sketch a line of research concerning this pattern, starting with
the notion of a
combined modality
.

9.1 Definition
.

Let
\mathord{\text{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{${\sim}$}}\kern 1.29167pt}}
be a classical-like modal
affirmation
operator, that is, assume that
\mathord{\text{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{${\sim}$}}\kern 1.29167pt}}\varphi
and
\varphi
are true at precisely the same worlds.
Let
\Omega:=\{\mathord{\text{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{${\sim}$}}\kern 1.29167pt}},{\sim},\Diamond,\Box,\mathord{\smallsmile},\mathord{\smallfrown}\}
.
For each
\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1},\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2}\in\Omega
, we specify the
combined modalities

(\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}\mid\mid\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2})
and
(\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}\;\&\;\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2})
semantically by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash(\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}\;\&\;\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2})\varphi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}\varphi\text{ and }\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2}\varphi

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash(\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}\mid\mid\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2})\varphi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{iff}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}\varphi\text{ or }\mathfrak{M},w\Vdash\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2}\varphi

We will refer to
\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{1}
and
\text{\scalebox{.75}{\faIconFromMacro{faStarO}}}_{2}
as the
components
of the
combined modality.

9.2 Example
.

Each of the restoration modalities
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.15pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}},\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.08263pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}
and
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 3.75pt\hskip-3.75pt\hbox{\normalsize${\CIRCLE}$}\hskip-3.75pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{-2.13818pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallfrown}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 3.75pt}}}

may be viewed as a combined modality. For example
the modality for essentiality
\mathord{\textup{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{\normalsize${\Circle}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-3.40282pt\raisebox{5.09444pt}{\hbox{\tiny$\mathord{\smallsmile}$}}\hskip-3.40282pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}

.
Additionally, combined modalities encompass the contingency

51
,
20
]
.
We note that
\Box
is not in general definable from

or
\vartriangle

[
41
,

10
Conclusion

In this work, we investigated simulations for logics equipped with subclassical negations and their associated restoration modalities. Parametric in a restorative similarity type, we developed appropriate notions of simulation, and established both an intrinsic (Hennessy–Milner-type) and a relative (van Benthem-type) characterization theorem.
These results were obtained for a range of known as well as novel modal logics.
The findings pave the way for various avenues of further research, including the following:

Combined modalities.

While our approach already encompasses a wide variety of logics, in
Section
9
we speculated about an even more general perspective on simulations and their associated characterization results.
Pursuing this line of research requires a deeper understanding of the underlying pattern of «gluing conditions».
The concrete instances examined in the present paper may serve as useful case studies in uncovering this pattern.
Taking this one step further, one could even aim to extend the analysis to
combined modalities constructed from repeated use of
||
and
\&
.

Relation to coalgebraic logic.

In the setting of classical coalgebraic logic, i.e. coalgebraic logic
used to model modal extensions of classical propositional logic,

\Lambda
-bisimulations provide a generic notion of bisimulation for
an arbitrary modal signature
[
4
]
.
In the special case of non-contingency logic, the resulting notion was shown to be closely aligned with the existing one, studied in
[
28
,
5
]
.
It would be worthwhile to investigate how
\Lambda
-bisimulations
behave in the presence of restoration modalities.

A generalization of
\Lambda
-bisimulations beyond classical coalgebraic logic was proposed in
[
35
]
. However, this extension does not always yield an intrinsic characterization result for modal extensions of positive logic
(see
[
35
, Remark 4.6]
).
Starting instead with a dual adjunction between the category
of sets and the category of distributive lattices
—the latter being the setting adopted in the present paper— may provide a solution to this issue.

More on restorative modal logics.

In an orthogonal direction, further model-theoretic results could be pursued, such as a finite-model property or Sahlqvist-style theorems for restoration modalities. For the latter, one could draw on Sahlqvist’s original result
[
56
]
and its topological proof
[
58
]
, as well as on its adaptation to positive modal logic
[
17
]
.
