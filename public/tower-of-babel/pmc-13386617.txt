Neuromuscular exercise combined with pain neuroscience education for knee osteoarthritis management in older women: protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Farzaneh Saki, Farzaneh Ramezani, Sepideh Eizadi, Raziyeh Taheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee osteoarthritis is a prevalent chronic condition in older adults, particularly postmenopausal women, affecting physical, psychological, social, and economic aspects of life. Existing neuromuscular exercise programs for knee osteoarthritis are often incomplete, focusing on isolated components such as strength or balance, without simultaneously targeting all key elements, including core stability, balance, and lower-limb strength. Additionally, these programs rarely address psychosocial factors or integrate pain neuroscience principles. Evidence suggests that combining a comprehensive neuromuscular exercise program with Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE) may offer superior benefits in reducing pain, improving function, and decreasing fear of movement compared with conventional exercise alone. This study aims to develop and present a novel integrated protocol and to evaluate its effectiveness compared with usual care in older women with knee osteoarthritis.MethodsThis double-blind randomized controlled trial will enroll 60 women aged 60 years and older with knee osteoarthritis, randomly assigned to either an intervention group or a usual-care control group (30 per group). Outcome assessors and the statistician will be blinded to group allocation. The intervention group will receive an 8-week supervised neuromuscular exercise program combined with PNE, while the control group will receive usual care, defined as continuation of any ongoing medical management without any structured exercise or PNE added. The primary outcome is knee pain intensity, measured using the Visual Analog Scale. Secondary outcomes include functional disability, assessed using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS); physical performance, evaluated via the Timed Up and Go and Sit-to-Stand tests; balance assessed through plantar pressure analysis and the Berg Balance Scale; fear of falling; and pain-related psychological factors including catastrophizing, self-efficacy, and kinesiophobia. Knee proprioception, lower-limb muscle strength, and muscle activity will also be assessed.DiscussionThis protocol describes a novel, integrated approach that simultaneously targets the physical and psychosocial dimensions of knee osteoarthritis in older women. If the findings demonstrate effectiveness, this low-risk, non-pharmacological intervention has the potential to inform clinical rehabilitation guidelines and offer a scalable, accessible treatment option for this growing population.Trial RegistrationStudy is registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20190224042827N7), registration date: 17 November 2025.

Background

Osteoarthritis is one of the most common chronic conditions among the elderly population, and its impact extends far beyond physical pain. It affects multiple dimensions of an individual’s life, including social, psychological, and economic domains. This disorder imposes significant consequences on both individuals and society, such as reduced functional capacity, increased disability, psychological distress, and substantial financial burdens on healthcare systems [1, 2]. The global prevalence of osteoarthritis is growing steadily and currently affects approximately 9% of the world’s population [3]. It is reported to be more common in women than in men, particularly after menopause, when declining estrogen levels heighten the risk of developing and progressing osteoarthritis [4]. Aging-related processes and hormonal changes make joints more vulnerable and contribute to their gradual degeneration [3, 4]. As individuals age, the prevalence of osteoarthritis increases accordingly. Cellular and molecular changes associated with aging diminish the regenerative capacity of bone and cartilage, consequently predisposing individuals to osteoarthritis [5].

Among the therapeutic approaches for osteoarthritis, neuromuscular exercises have gained considerable attention from researchers in recent years. Evidence indicates that, compared with traditional strength training, these exercises provide superior benefits for improving pain, functional performance, and balance in individuals with mild to moderate osteoarthritis [6]. By targeting neuromuscular control, enhancing proprioception, and optimizing movement patterns, neuromuscular exercises can facilitate nervous system retraining and contribute to pain reduction. Older women with knee osteoarthritis commonly present with a combination of reduced quadriceps strength, impaired proprioception, diminished balance, and elevated fear of movement, a clinical profile that conventional exercise programs targeting isolated muscle groups are ill-equipped to address comprehensively [7, 8].

Individuals with osteoarthritis frequently experience chronic musculoskeletal pain, and because many patients are unfamiliar with appropriate treatment strategies, patient education is considered an effective intervention [9, 10]. Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE), a contemporary approach to managing chronic musculoskeletal pain, operates by reshaping patients’ beliefs and perceptions about pain. Specifically, PNE conveys that pain is a complex, multidimensional experience generated and modulated by the central nervous system, rather than a direct reflection of tissue damage. This understanding has been shown to reduce pain catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and avoidance behaviors, and to increase self-efficacy and engagement in physical activity in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal conditions [11, 12]. A systematic review investigating the combined effects of PNE and exercise on chronic musculoskeletal pain demonstrated that this integrated approach is more effective for chronic pain management than exercise alone. These findings underscore the superiority of combining exercise with PNE over implementing exercise in isolation [13].

Previous neuromuscular exercise protocols for knee osteoarthritis have primarily focused on strengthening the quadriceps and hamstrings [7, 8]. Although core exercises have been examined independently [14, 15], they have rarely been integrated into comprehensive lower-limb strengthening programs. Moreover, psychosocial and cognitive aspects of pain management have largely been overlooked. To date, no protocol has simultaneously integrated core stability, lower-extremity strengthening, balance training, sensorimotor control, and PNE into a single, unified intervention for this population. To address these gaps, the present study proposes a novel protocol that integrates neuromuscular exercises with PNE into a unified intervention targeting lower-limb strength, core stability, sensorimotor control, and psychosocial factors. This study will be the first to combine all these components into a single protocol specifically designed for older women with knee osteoarthritis. The combined intervention is designed to promote neural retraining and enhance sensorimotor regulation to improve the management of chronic pain associated with osteoarthritis. The protocol has been developed specifically for older women with knee osteoarthritis and will be evaluated in a future randomized controlled clinical trial. It is hypothesized that an 8-week combined intervention involving neuromuscular exercises and PNE will lead to significant reductions in pain, pain catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and functional disability, along with improvements in balance, proprioception, muscle strength and activation, self-efficacy, and overall function in older women with knee osteoarthritis compared with a control group.

The primary aim of this study is to develop and present a comprehensive therapeutic protocol that integrates neuromuscular exercises with PNE for the management of knee osteoarthritis in older women, and to establish the framework for a randomized controlled clinical trial to evaluate its effectiveness. Specific objectives include providing a detailed description of the neuromuscular exercise program, outlining the content and integration of the PNE component, and assessing the feasibility and applicability of the protocol in both clinical and research settings.

Methods

Study design

This study is designed as a double-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial with two parallel groups. The trial will be conducted at the Sports Rehabilitation Laboratory of Bu-Ali Sina University, Hamedan, Iran. The intervention group will receive a combined neuromuscular exercise program and PNE, while the control group will receive usual care. Blinding will be applied to the outcome assessors and the statistician; due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and therapists is not feasible. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Bu-Ali Sina University (IR.BASU.REC.1404.018), and the trial has been registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20190224042827N7). This study will be conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.

Participants

Sample collection and recruitment

Participants will be recruited through two primary pathways: (1) dissemination of study announcements via social media and online platforms, and (2) referrals from orthopedic clinics and specialist physicians in hospitals in Hamedan. A qualified orthopedic physician will confirm the diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis and subsequently refer eligible patients to the research team. All prospective participants will receive detailed information regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, potential benefits, and risks before to enrollment, and each individual will be required to sign an informed consent form.

Eligibility criteria

To be enrolled in this trial, participants must meet all of the following criteria: a confirmed diagnosis of unilateral or bilateral knee osteoarthritis by an orthopedic specialist; morning joint stiffness lasting less than 30 min; a history of knee pain on most days of the past month; radiographic evidence of osteophytes; postmenopausal status, defined as at least 12 months since the last menstrual period; the presence of knee crepitus during movement, included as a confirmatory clinical sign of knee osteoarthritis consistent with American College of Rheumatology (ACR) diagnostic criteria [16]; mild to moderate knee pain intensity, defined as a score of 5.0–7.4 cm on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) [17, 18]; and no intra-articular corticosteroid injections within the past six months.

Exclusion criteria

Participants who meet any of the following conditions will be excluded from the study: a body mass index (BMI) greater than 35 kg/m2, as severe obesity significantly alters lower-limb biomechanics and may limit the ability to perform neuromuscular exercises safely [19]; a history of surgery or fracture in the lower limb joints within the past six months; grade four knee osteoarthritis according to the Kellgren-Lawrence classification or the need for joint replacement surgery; the presence of active cardiovascular disease that limits physical activity; a history of hip osteoarthritis or lower limb joint replacement surgery; knee ligament injuries confirmed by clinical tests such as the Lachman or drawer tests; and a diagnosis of malignant diseases or severe cognitive disorders.

Study method

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1) based on a repeated-measures ANOVA. An effect size of 0.24 based on the pain variable [20], a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 90% were used, yielding a minimum of 24 participants per group. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the sample size was increased to 30 participants per group, resulting in a total of 60 participants to will be recruited.

Randomization

Randomization will be performed by an independent researcher not involved in the intervention or outcome assessment, using Random Number Generator software. Group allocation will be conducted using the Sequentially Numbered, Opaque, Sealed Envelopes (SNOSE) method. A randomization list will be generated before recruitment, and each sealed envelope will be opened sequentially only after a participant has been confirmed eligible and has provided informed consent.

Blinding

In this study, blinding will be applied at two levels. First, all outcome assessments will be conducted by laboratory technicians who are fully blinded to group allocation and intervention type. Second, statistical analyses will be performed by a statistician blinded to group assignments. Due to the nature of the exercise and educational intervention, blinding of participants and therapists is not feasible. All blinded personnel will be instructed not to discuss group assignments or intervention details with participants or other study staff.

Interventions

Rationale and theoretical framework of the intervention

PNE is designed to enhance participants’ knowledge of the neurobiological mechanisms of pain and to modify their perception of painful experiences [21]. The core concept of this approach is to convey that pain is a complex, multidimensional experience generated and processed by the brain, rather than merely a physical sensation resulting from tissue damage. In other words, participants learn that pain intensity does not necessarily reflect the degree of structural damage and can be influenced by central sensitization, psychological factors, and social context [22]. Individuals with knee osteoarthritis often hold misconceptions about the origin of pain, experience fear of movement, and engage in avoidance behaviors. PNE is expected to play a key role in reducing pain catastrophizing and correcting maladaptive beliefs about injury and movement. In addition, it may help reduce central sensitization and increase self-efficacy, as well as promote active participation in physical activities. After each educational session, participants will receive a one-page brochure containing key points accompanied by simple illustrations. Short homework assignments, such as five-minute mindfulness exercises, completion of a pain self-monitoring diary, or cognitive restructuring exercises, will be assigned to reinforce and consolidate learning.

The neuromuscular exercise program in this study is designed to overcome the limitations of conventional exercise programs and consists of four main components: core stability exercises, balance training, lower-limb strengthening, and functional exercises [23]. These four exercise domains will be implemented in all sessions, with variations across sessions limited to intensity, volume, and movement diversity, which will be progressively adjusted according to participants’ abilities. After each exercise level, structured verbal feedback will be collected from participants to assess fatigue, pain, difficulty, and functional capacity. Participants will advance to the next exercise level only when their ability and motor control are deemed sufficient, ensuring safe and individualized progression throughout the protocol.

Exercise selection is based on key principles of neuromuscular training: improving motor control, activating deep muscles, strengthening muscles responsible for lower limb joint stability, increasing tissue capacity, and enhancing performance in daily activities. The protocol begins with core stability exercises, as the quality of lower-limb movement is directly dependent on adequate core control. Three primary core stability exercises, including abdominal breathing, bridges, and modified Bird-Dog (Pointer), target the main stabilizing components of the trunk and are safe, progressive, and suitable for older adults [24–26]. In the second phase, balance exercises including Tai Chi walking and standing on unstable surfaces will be performed. Tai Chi, with its slow rhythm and controlled weight shifting, directly contributes to fall prevention [27]. Standing on an unstable surface is also one of the most effective exercises for improving static balance and activating proprioception, which are often reduced in older adults and patients with osteoarthritis [28]. Combining these two exercises targets different aspects of the balance system.

Strengthening exercises will include straight leg raises in supine and seated positions, hip abduction to the side, and knee extensions while seated. These exercises safely activate the quadriceps and gluteus medius muscles, compensating for common weaknesses in osteoarthritic patients [29, 30]. Functional exercises, including lunges, squats, and sit-to-stand movements, are selected to strengthen essential movement patterns required by older adults. Squats strengthen the quadriceps, gluteal muscles, and core while simultaneously improving balance control and lumbopelvic stability. Lunges, by introducing controlled asymmetry, help identify and correct lower-limb weaknesses and closely resemble functional daily activities such as stair climbing [29, 30].

Intervention group

The exercise protocol consists of an 8-week program with three supervised sessions per week (24 sessions in total) (Table 1). Two of the three weekly sessions will consist of neuromuscular exercises only, each lasting approximately 50–60 min. The third weekly session will combine PNE with neuromuscular exercises and will last approximately 60–70 min. The PNE component will be delivered at the beginning of the combined session, followed immediately by the neuromuscular exercise component. PNE sessions will be introduced from week one and delivered once per week throughout the entire 8-week period, following the structured curriculum outlined in Table 2. Each neuromuscular exercise session will include the following elements: warm-up (stretching exercises and brisk walking), circuit exercises (core muscle activation, balance and postural control exercises, lower limb strengthening, and functional movement patterns), and cool-down (Table 1). The exercises will start at a basic level and gradually progress to align with the principle of progressive overload [23].

Control group

Participants allocated to the control group will receive usual care, defined as continuation of any ongoing medical management they were receiving before enrollment, including physician visits and prescribed analgesic medication, without any structured exercise intervention or PNE added during the 8-week study period. They will be instructed to refrain from initiating any new exercise programs, physiotherapy, or other rehabilitation interventions. To monitor adherence and identify potential concurrent interventions, control group participants will be required to complete a weekly activity log, documenting any physical activity, medication use, or visits to other healthcare providers. In addition, brief telephone interviews (5–10 min) will be conducted in weeks 2, 4, and 6 of the study to assess general health status, activity levels, and compliance with study restrictions. In accordance with ethical principles and to ensure fairness in access to treatment, all control group participants will be offered the opportunity to participate in the supervised comprehensive exercise program after the post-intervention assessment and data collection.

Outcome measures

Knee pain intensity will be considered the primary outcome of the study. Disability, physical performance, balance, fall risk, pain-related psychological factors, knee proprioception, and lower-limb muscle strength and activity will be considered secondary outcomes. Primary outcomes

Pain assessment

Knee pain intensity will be measured with the VAS, a 10 cm horizontal line anchored at "no pain" (0) and "worst imaginable pain" (10). Participants will be asked to rate their average knee pain over the past 48 h.

Secondary outcomes

Disability assessment

Functional disability will be evaluated using two complementary questionnaires: the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) and the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). Although both instruments assess disability in knee osteoarthritis, they offer distinct and complementary information: WOMAC provides a concise, widely validated measure of pain, stiffness, and physical function, while KOOS additionally captures sport and recreational activity and quality of life, making it more sensitive to functional improvements in active individuals. Using both instruments allows for a more comprehensive assessment of disability across multiple dimensions [31].

WOMAC: This WOMAC consists of 24 items assessing three domains: pain (5 items), joint stiffness (2 items), and physical function (17 items). Each item assesses the severity of the participant’s problem over the past 48 h using a 0–4 scale, where 0 indicates no problem and 4 indicates an extreme problem. Domain scores will be summed and converted to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better function. The questionnaire demonstrates high validity and reliability (ICC = 0.96) [32].

KOOS: The KOOS is a self-reported questionnaire derived from WOMAC and includes 42 items across five dimensions: pain, other symptoms, activities of daily living, sport and recreational activities, and quality of life. Each item is scored from 0 to 4 and converted to a percentage, with scores near 0 indicating poor function and scores near 100 indicating normal function. The KOOS demonstrates acceptable validity and reliability in individuals with mild to moderate osteoarthritis (ICC > 0.82) [33, 34].

Functional performance tests

Timed Up and Go (TUG) test: The TUG test will assess mobility and balance. Participants will stand from a chair, walk 3 m, turn, return, and sit down. The time from standing to sitting will be recorded. This test demonstrates very high reliability (ICC = 0.95–0.97) [35, 36]; durations > 12 s indicate fall risk.

Sit-to-Stand Test: The Sit-to-Stand test will assess lower-extremity function. Participants will perform as many complete sit-to-stand repetitions as possible from a standard chair within 30 s. The test demonstrates very high reliability (ICC = 0.98) [37]. This test has been previously validated for assessing lower-extremity function in geriatric populations with chronic musculoskeletal conditions [38], will be employed to evaluate functional performance in the present study.

Balance

Static Balance: Static balance will be assessed using plantar pressure analysis under eyes-open and eyes-closed conditions for 20 s. This method has demonstrated high reliability (ICC = 0.85–0.98) [39, 40].

Berg Balance Test: The Berg Balance Test consists of 14 functional items, including sitting to standing, standing with eyes closed, turning 360 degrees, and bending forward. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, with a total possible score ranging from 0 to 54. Lower scores indicate a higher risk of falling. This test has been reported to have high reliability (ICC = 0.95) [41, 42].

Fear of Falling Questionnaire: This questionnaire assesses an individual’s confidence in maintaining balance to prevent falls during daily activities, such as housecleaning, dressing, and simple shopping. It comprises 16 items, including 10 basic activities and six more challenging activities [43].

Psychological factors related to pain

Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS): The PCS consists of 13 items rated on a five-point scale and evaluates exaggerated negative thoughts related to pain. Higher scores indicate greater pain catastrophizing. The Persian version demonstrates high reliability (ICC = 0.93) [12, 44].

Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire (PSEQ): The PSEQ includes 10 items assessing an individual’s confidence in performing activities despite pain, such as completing household tasks. Scores range from 0 to 60, with lower scores indicating lower self-efficacy. The Persian version demonstrates high reliability (ICC = 0.92) [12, 45].

Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK): The TSK includes 17 items assessing fear of movement and the perceived risk of re-injury. Scores range from 17 to 68; scores above 37 indicate low fear, whereas scores below 37 indicate high fear. The Persian version demonstrates acceptable reliability (ICC = 0.77) [12, 46].

Knee proprioception

Knee joint proprioception will be measured using a goniometer. The participant sits on a table with legs fully hanging and thighs supported [47]. First, the examiner passively flexes the knee to 45 degrees and holds the position for 5 s. The leg is then returned to the starting position, and the participant is asked to reproduce the same angle. The error relative to the 45-degree target angle is recorded. This test has very high reliability (ICC = 0.98) and is recognized as a valid method for assessing knee joint proprioception [48].

Muscle strength assessment

Isometric strength of three major muscle groups commonly affected by knee osteoarthritis, including knee flexors (ICC = 0.83) [49], knee extensors (ICC = 0.95) [49], and hip abductors (ICC = 0.95), will be measured using a handheld dynamometer. This device (ICC = 0.78–0.95) is manufactured by North Coast Medical, USA [50, 51]. Before the primary test, participants will perform one or two submaximal practice trials to become familiar with the procedure. The primary test will be performed on the more painful leg. Each participant will complete three maximal efforts with a 30-s rest between trials. Straps will be used for limb stabilization. The dynamometer will be positioned vertically and secured firmly, and participants will be instructed to gradually exert their maximal force. The examiner will provide verbal encouragement to ensure maximal effort [52].

Lower-limb muscle activity

Lower limb muscle activity will be assessed using an eight-channel surface electromyography (sEMG) system (Biometrics Ltd.). The evaluated muscles include the vastus medialis, vastus lateralis, medial and lateral hamstrings, and medial and lateral gastrocnemius. Data will be recorded during a standardized walking test. To synchronize EMG signals with the gait cycle, foot switches will be placed on the first metatarsal and the heel [53]. All assessments will be performed on the participants’ dominant limb. Electrode placement on the selected muscles will follow established guidelines from validated previous studies [54, 55]. To improve contact and reduce skin impedance, the skin area will be shaved, cleaned, and prepared before electrode placement [56]. The site will be marked and electrodes attached. To verify proper electrode placement and signal quality, maximal voluntary isometric contractions (MVIC) will be performed for each muscle in an isolated position. For EMG normalization, participants will perform a single seven-second isometric contraction for each muscle, with manual resistance applied by the tester to ensure proper positioning. A rest interval of 10–15 s will be provided between repetitions, and a two-minute rest will be given between testing each muscle. For data analysis, the middle three seconds of each seven-second contraction will be extracted. The mean of three repetitions for each muscle will be calculated and used as the normalized EMG value. All MVIC tests will be completed before the main functional task (walking) so that EMG signals during walking can be normalized accordingly.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize demographic characteristics and baseline variables. The normality of continuous variables will be assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent t-tests will be applied to compare baseline demographic variables between the intervention and control groups. If significant differences are observed in covariates such as age, BMI, disease severity (Kellgren-Lawrence grade), comorbidities, or number of chronic conditions, these variables will be controlled for using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA).

To examine the interaction between time (pre- vs. post-intervention) and group (intervention vs. control), a mixed-model repeated-measures ANOVA will be employed. The assumption of sphericity will be evaluated using Mauchly’s test, and if violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction will be applied. For within-group comparisons over time, post hoc pairwise analyses with Bonferroni correction will be conducted. Given the large number of outcome variables in this study, the false discovery rate (FDR) method will be applied to control for familywise Type I error inflation arising from multiple comparisons across all dependent variables. Effect sizes will be reported using partial eta squared (η2) and interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14) [57].

Discussion

The present study describes the rationale and protocol for a randomized controlled trial evaluating the effectiveness of an 8-week combined neuromuscular exercise and PNE program in older women with knee osteoarthritis. The intervention was designed to address a recognized gap in the literature: the absence of a unified protocol simultaneously targeting physical impairments (lower-limb muscle activity and strength, balance, and proprioception) and psychosocial contributors to pain (catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, and self-efficacy) in this population.

Exercise is recommended as a core treatment for knee osteoarthritis by all major international clinical guidelines, including the updated 2024 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) guidelines. However, a recent individual participant data meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found that the effects of exercise alone on pain and function were only small and of questionable clinical importance, and that these modest gains declined at 6- and 12-month follow-up [59]. These findings highlight the need for enhanced exercise programs that incorporate complementary strategies, such as patient education targeting pain beliefs and psychological barriers, to maximize and sustain treatment gains.

Several studies have investigated the addition of PNE to exercise programs for musculoskeletal conditions. A systematic review and meta-analysis (2022) found that combining PNE with exercise produced greater short-term reductions in pain than exercise alone in chronic musculoskeletal conditions [13]. Regarding PNE specifically combined with exercise in knee osteoarthritis, a pilot RCT found significant within-group improvements in WOMAC pain, physical limitation, pain catastrophizing, and kinesiophobia following PNE combined with Pilates exercise, with the combined group demonstrating numerically greater improvements than exercise alone [12]. Similarly, a systematic review (2023) reported that PNE is effective in improving pain, disability, and psychosocial factors in patients with fibromyalgia, chronic low back pain, especially if associated with other therapeutic approaches [11]. These findings provide support for the combined approach adopted in the present study.

With respect to neuromuscular exercise specifically, previous protocols have predominantly focused on isolated quadriceps and hamstring strengthening, with limited integration of core stability, balance, and sensorimotor training components [8, 15, 23, 60]. The present protocol addresses this limitation by incorporating all four domains, core stability, balance, lower-limb strengthening, and functional movement patterns, into every session, progressively adapted to each participant's ability. This comprehensive approach is consistent with the theoretical framework that adequate core stability underpins lower-limb neuromuscular function and that multi-component training is necessary to address the full spectrum of impairments in older adults with knee osteoarthritis.

An important operational consideration of this study is the duration of the intervention. Eight weeks is a relatively short timeframe, and some reviewers may question whether meaningful clinical changes can be achieved within this period in an older population with established osteoarthritis. However, previous studies have reported significant improvements in pain, balance, and functional outcomes following six to eight weeks of supervised exercise in this population [6, 8], and the structured progressive overload design of the present protocol is intended to optimize neuromuscular adaptations within this timeframe. Nevertheless, the absence of a long-term follow-up assessment remains a limitation, and future studies should include follow-up evaluations at three, six, and 12 months to assess the durability of treatment effects.

This study is designed to evaluate the effectiveness of an 8-week neuromuscular training program combined with pain neuroscience education in older women with knee osteoarthritis. Given the increasing population of older adults with osteoarthritis, this novel and low-risk protocol may provide an effective strategy to improve the quality of life for these individuals. If the study results are successful and lead to improvements in functional and psychological outcomes in older adults with osteoarthritis, this intervention could be valuable and applicable in social and clinical settings where pharmacological treatments or invasive interventions have limited effectiveness. Furthermore, the findings of this research have the potential to inform rehabilitation guidelines and clinical strategies related to knee osteoarthritis.

Limitations

The first limitation of this study is its focus on participant gender: only older women with knee osteoarthritis will be included, which limits the generalizability of the findings to men or other age groups. Additionally, although efforts will be made to control variables such as BMI, disease severity, comorbidities, and psychological factors using statistical methods, complete control over these factors is not feasible, and they may influence the outcomes. Another limitation is the absence of long-term follow-up, as the protocol includes only post-intervention assessments without evaluations at later time points. Consequently, the durability of the intervention effects over time cannot be determined. Furthermore, the use of a waiting-list control group, rather than an active placebo or alternative treatment comparator, represents a limitation of the study design. This design cannot fully isolate the specific effect of the intervention from non-specific effects such as increased attention, social interaction, and expectation of improvement. Future studies should consider including an active control condition to address this limitation. Future studies should consider these items to address limitations.

Strengths

The strengths of this study include its randomized controlled design, which enhances internal validity and reduces selection bias. Adherence to international standards such as SPIRIT increases transparency and enables replication by other researchers. Another advantage is the safe, low-impact nature of the exercises, making them feasible for older adults. The use of standardized methods and blinded outcome assessors will minimize measurement error and bias. A further strength is the novelty of the intervention design, which is the first protocol to simultaneously integrate core stability, lower-extremity neuromuscular training, balance exercises, and PNE into a single unified program specifically tailored for older women with knee osteoarthritis. Considering the growing elderly population and the increasing need for non-pharmacological interventions in chronic disease management, if this novel and comprehensive protocol proves effective, it could offer a cost-effective, safe, and accessible rehabilitation option for knee osteoarthritis at both national and potentially global levels.
