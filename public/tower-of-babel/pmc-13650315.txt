ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlpha-particle spectroscopy is widely used in nuclear physics, radiation monitoring, safeguards, and environmental measurements. Conventional silicon detectors provide excellent spectrometric performance; however, their application in portable or harsh-environment instrumentation may be limited by mechanical fragility, radiation sensitivity, and operational constraints. Scintillator-based detectors coupled to silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) represent a promising alternative because of their compactness, robustness, and simplified integration with modern electronics. Despite the favorable scintillation properties of yttrium orthosilicate (YSO), its applicability for alpha-particle spectroscopy has received limited investigation.MethodsThe response of a YSO scintillator coupled to a Hamamatsu S13360–3050 multi-pixel photon counter (MPPC) array was investigated using alpha particles emitted from a 238Pu source. A 10 × 10 × 10 mm 3 YSO crystal was coupled to the SiPM array and read out using CAEN digitization electronics. Alpha-particle energies in the approximate range from 0.4 MeV to 5.5 MeV were obtained by varying the air-layer thickness between the radioactive source and detector. Although the energy resolution remains inferior to conventional silicon alpha detectors, the investigated system offers important practical advantages including operation under ambient conditions, simplified electronics integration, scalability, and suitability for portable or harsh-environment nuclear instrumentation.

1. Introduction

Alpha-particle spectroscopy plays an important role in nuclear physics, environmental monitoring, nuclear safeguards, contamination assessment, and radiation-security applications. 1– 3 Accurate alpha-particle measurements are commonly required for isotope identification and characterization of radioactive materials. Silicon semiconductor detectors are widely considered the standard instrumentation for high-resolution alpha spectroscopy because alpha particles generate charge directly within the semiconductor material, resulting in excellent intrinsic energy resolution. 4 However, silicon detectors also exhibit several limitations including mechanical fragility, sensitivity to radiation damage, relatively high fabrication costs for large-area systems, and operational constraints associated with vacuum or carefully controlled environmental conditions. 5, 6

Scintillation detectors provide an alternative approach for alpha-particle measurements because of their mechanical robustness, scalability, fast timing properties, and compatibility with compact electronics. Conventional alpha-sensitive scintillators such as ZnS (Ag) and CsI (Tl) are frequently used because of their strong scintillation response to heavily ionizing particles. 7, 8 Nevertheless, these materials may suffer from disadvantages including long scintillation decay times, afterglow effects, hygroscopicity, and integration limitations in compact detector systems.

Recent developments in silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) have enabled compact scintillation detector systems characterized by high photon-detection efficiency, low operating voltage, magnetic-field insensitivity, and fast timing capability. 9– 13 Compared with conventional photomultiplier tubes, SiPMs provide substantially improved compactness, mechanical stability, and simplified integration with modern digital electronics.

YSO scintillators possess several favorable properties for compact radiation-detection systems, including fast scintillation decay, non-hygroscopic behavior, mechanical stability, and emission spectra well matched to silicon photodetectors. 14– 16 In comparison with conventional silicon alpha detectors, scintillator–SiPM systems may offer improved operational robustness, higher tolerance to harsh environmental conditions, easier scalability to larger active areas, and simpler electronics integration. Furthermore, the relatively fast scintillation decay time of YSO supports high count-rate capability and timing applications important for practical radiation-monitoring instrumentation.

Despite these advantages, the alpha-particle response of YSO scintillators coupled to SiPM arrays has received comparatively limited investigation. The present study therefore evaluates the response characteristics of a YSO scintillator coupled to a multi-pixel photon counter (MPPC) array for alpha-particle spectroscopy in the approximate energy range from 0.4 MeV to 5.5 MeV. The primary objectives are to investigate detector linearity, achievable energy resolution, low-energy response, and applicability for compact alpha-spectrometric systems.

2. Methods

2.1 Detector configuration

A 10 × 10 × 10 mm 3 yttrium orthosilicate (YSO) scintillation crystal was optically coupled to a Hamamatsu S13360–3050 MPPC array using optical silicone grease. 17 The crystal dimensions were selected to match the active area of the photodetector array. White PTFE reflector tape was applied to all scintillator surfaces except the optical coupling surface in order to improve scintillation-light collection efficiency. The MPPC array consisted of sixteen SiPM elements operated from a common high-voltage bias channel. The detector was biased at 54.5 V, corresponding to the experimentally determined optimal operating voltage. Signals from all MPPC channels were combined into a single readout output.

2.2 Electronics and data acquisition

Figure 2 shows the experimental block diagram. Signal acquisition was performed using a CAEN DT5720 digitizer with 12-bit resolution and a sampling frequency of 250 MS/s. The bias voltage was supplied using a CAEN DT5733 high-voltage module. The detector assembly and radioactive source were positioned inside a light-tight and electromagnetically shielded enclosure.

Measured energy spectrum of 238Pu at the initial position using Timepix detector.

Data acquisition was performed in self-trigger mode, while offline waveform processing and charge integration were carried out using ROOT-based software. 18 A charge-integration gate of 450 ns was selected according to the scintillation decay characteristics of the YSO crystal.

2.3 Alpha-particle measurements

An unsealed 238Pu alpha source was used for the measurements. The alpha-particle energy reaching the detector was controlled by varying the air-layer thickness between the source and detector. The source was mounted on an adjustable holder allowing controlled variation of the source-detector distance with an estimated positioning precision of approximately ±0.5 mm. Measurements were performed under ambient laboratory conditions at approximately 23 °C. The acquisition time for each measurement was fixed at 5 min. To validate alpha-particle propagation in air, SRIM simulations 19 were performed for 5500 keV alpha particles.

3. Results

As a reference measurement, the alpha-particle spectrum from the 238Pu source was initially measured using a Timepix hybrid pixel detector 20– 22 ( Figure 2, left). At the minimum source-detector spacing, the measured alpha peak energy was approximately 4915 keV, which is lower than the nominal alpha emission energy of approximately 5.5 MeV because of energy losses occurring before detection.

To estimate alpha-particle propagation and energy loss in air, SRIM simulations were performed for 5500 keV alpha particles. The calculated ionization-loss distribution and ion-range distribution are presented in Figure 3. The ionization profile shown in Figure 3 (left) demonstrates the progressive increase in energy loss near the end of the alpha-particle trajectory, corresponding to the Bragg peak region, where ionization density reaches its maximum immediately before the particles stop. The simulated ion-range distribution shown in Figure 3 (right) yielded a mean alpha-particle flight distance in air of approximately 40.6 mm with a longitudinal straggling of approximately 1.08 mm. The relatively narrow stopping distribution indicates small statistical fluctuations in alpha-particle propagation under the investigated conditions. These simulation results were used to estimate the expected alpha-particle energy degradation in air and to validate the experimental source-detector geometry.

SRIM simulation results for 5500 keV alpha particles propagating in air: ionization energy-loss distribution exhibiting the Bragg peak behavior (left) and calculated ion-range distribution in air (right).

Measured alpha-particle spectra obtained at different source-detector distances are presented in Figure 4 (left). The measurement sequence was subsequently repeated using the YSO–SiPM detector system. Starting from a source-detector spacing of 2 mm, the air gap was increased in 5 mm increments, thereby progressively reducing the alpha-particle energy reaching the detector. Under the selected experimental conditions, alpha-particle peaks remained clearly distinguishable down to energies of approximately 500 keV. At energies below approximately 250 keV, alpha-induced signals increasingly overlapped with the gamma-induced background and could no longer be separated reliably. The detector response exhibited approximately linear behavior throughout the investigated energy interval, as shown in Figure 4 (right).

Measured alpha-particle spectra from 238Pu source with the YSO scintillator (left) and linearity of the channel-distance dependence (right).

The experimentally determined alpha-particle flight distance for 5500 keV alpha particles was measured as 40.58 ± 0.87 mm, which is consistent with the SRIM prediction. This level of agreement supports the consistency of experimental geometry and confirms that the YSO-MPPC configuration can track alpha-energy degradation in air with acceptable accuracy for practical spectrometric use.

The measured energy resolution varied from approximately 10.5% at 5.5 MeV to approximately 21% at alpha-particle energies near 0.4 MeV, as presented in Figure 5 (left). The estimated deviation from linearity across the investigated energy interval was approximately 125 keV, corresponding to a relative deviation of approximately 3%, as shown in Figure 5 (right).

Dependence of energy resolution (left) and energy linearity (right) on alpha-particle energy from 238Pu.

The deterioration of energy resolution at lower energies is primarily attributed to reduced scintillation-photon statistics, increased relative contribution of electronic noise, and energy straggling in air. In addition, alpha-induced quenching effects in YSO scintillators may contribute to non-proportional scintillation response at low alpha-particle energies because of ionization-density-dependent quenching mechanisms.

4. Discussion

The obtained results demonstrate that the investigated YSO-SiPM detector configuration can provide practical alpha-particle spectrometric capability over a broad energy interval extending from approximately 0.4 MeV to 5.5 MeV. Compared with conventional silicon alpha-particle detectors, the investigated system provides substantially lower intrinsic energy resolution. Silicon detectors typically achieve superior spectrometric performance because alpha particles generate charge directly within the semiconductor material without intermediate scintillation processes. Nevertheless, scintillator-SiPM systems provide several practical advantages important for applied nuclear instrumentation. The investigated YSO-SiPM detector demonstrated stable operation under ambient conditions while providing improved mechanical robustness, simplified electronics integration, and scalability to larger active detection areas. In addition, the relatively fast scintillation decay time of YSO supports high count-rate capability and timing applications. These characteristics may be advantageous for portable radiation-monitoring systems, safeguards instrumentation, environmental contamination measurements, and other field-deployable nuclear-detection applications where practical operation and detector robustness are prioritized over ultimate spectrometric resolution.

The measured agreement between experimental alpha-particle flight distance and SRIM simulation further supports the reliability of the detector calibration and measurement geometry.

Several limitations remain. The present study did not investigate pulse-shape discrimination capability or detailed alpha-quenching behavior in the YSO scintillator. Furthermore, operation at lower alpha-particle energies was limited by increasing overlap between alpha-induced and gamma-induced signals.

Future investigations should therefore focus on optimization of scintillation-light collection, digital pulse processing, alpha/gamma discrimination capability, and detailed characterization of ionization-density-dependent quenching effects in YSO scintillators.

5. Conclusions

The response of a YSO scintillator coupled to a Hamamatsu MPPC array for alpha-particle spectroscopy was investigated in the approximate energy range from 0.4 MeV to 5.5 MeV. The detector exhibited stable and approximately linear response characteristics with an estimated deviation from linearity of approximately 3%. The measured energy resolution varied from approximately 10.5% at 5.5 MeV to approximately 21% at lower alpha-particle energies near 0.4 MeV. The experimentally measured alpha-particle flight distance in air was consistent with SRIM simulations.

Although the investigated detector does not provide the spectrometric resolution achievable with conventional silicon alpha detectors, the obtained results demonstrate that the YSO–SiPM configuration represents a compact and mechanically robust detector solution suitable for portable and field-deployable alpha-particle instrumentation.

The following list of comments are intended to improve interpretation of the work and quality of the reviewed paper (MAx - Major comments, MIx - Minor comments):

MA1 - The choice YSO scintillator geometry

The choice of the 10 × 10 × 10 mm³ YSO geometry should be discussed in greater detail. While the 10 × 10 mm² lateral dimensions appear to be motivated by matching the MPPC array, the 10 mm scintillator thickness is substantially larger than the range of several-MeV alpha particles in YSO. The increased thickness may improve scintillation-light collection and homogenize the light distribution over the MPPC array, but it also increases the detector volume and consequently its sensitivity to X-ray and gamma-ray background. This may be particularly relevant to the reported deterioration of alpha/gamma separation at low alpha-particle energies. The authors should discuss the trade-off between alpha detection efficiency, optical light collection/uniformity, photon-background sensitivity, and if possible, justify the selected thickness.

MA2 - Charge-integration window

The choice of the 450 ns charge-integration window should be justified in greater detail. The authors are encouraged to provide representative digitized waveforms (documenting a typical detector response to 5 MeV and 0.5 MeV alpha particles) of the YSO–SiPM signals recorded with the CAEN DT5720. Such waveforms would help the reader assess the actual scintillation pulse shape, rise and decay characteristics, baseline fluctuation and signal-to-noise ratio. This would also provide experimental support for the selected 450 ns charge-integration window, which corresponds to approximately 7.3 times the reported 62 ns YSO decay constant.

MA3 - Clarification of the reported energy resolution

The manuscript reports an energy resolution of approximately 10.5% at 5.5 MeV, increasing to approximately 21% at lower alpha-particle energies. It would be important to clarify whether these values represent the intrinsic energy resolution of the YSO–SiPM detector or the overall experimental resolution obtained under the particular measurement conditions. In the present experimental approach, the alpha-particle energy incident on the scintillator is determined indirectly from the source-to-detector distance and the corresponding energy loss in air. Consequently, the measured values may include not only contributions from scintillation-photon statistics, SiPM response and electronic noise, but also the energy straggling of alpha particles in air and the uncertainty associated with the source-detector geometry and positioning (which may become increasingly important at lower alpha-particle energies).

MA4 - Reference Timepix measurement

More details should be provided on the Timepix reference measurement used to determine the alpha-particle energy, including the detector configuration, calibration procedure, measurement geometry and the uncertainty/accuracy of the obtained energy value. In particular, the authors should clarify how the reported 4915 keV was determined.

MA5 - Clarification of the energy calibration and linearity analysis

The manuscript presents the dependence of the measured peak channel on the source-to-detector distance in Figure 4 and describes the detector response as approximately linear. However, the channel–distance dependence is not equivalent to an energy–channel calibration, since the alpha-particle energy loss in air is nonlinear with propagation distance. The authors should clarify how the alpha-particle energy corresponding to each source-to-detector distance was determined and how the reported approximately 3% deviation from linearity was calculated. The procedure used to convert the measured peak positions into an energy scale should be described in sufficient detail to assess the validity of the reported energy linearity (it is not clear if it was determined based on measured signal versus alpha-particle energy or simply as an agreement between the distance-dependent alpha energy and the assumed/SRIM energy-loss model).

MA6 - Inconsistency of the reported energy resolution

There appears to be a discrepancy between the energy-resolution values stated in the text and those shown in Figure 5. The manuscript reports an energy resolution of approximately 21% at alpha-particle energies near 0.4 MeV, whereas the lowest-energy data point in Figure 5 appears to correspond to a substantially larger resolution. The authors should verify the plotted data and clarify the corresponding alpha-particle energy and FWHM value.

MI1 - Introduction - More suitable wording of YSO based solution benefits

The statements concerning the limitations of silicon alpha detectors in portable and harsh-environment instrumentation should be somewhat more carefully qualified, since compact and ruggedized silicon alpha spectrometers are also commercially available.

MI2 - Introduction - Detailed overview on available alpha particle detection scintillator materials

The Introduction would benefit from a more quantitative comparison of YSO with other scintillators used for alpha-particle detection and spectroscopy. In particular, the authors should clarify what specific advantage of YSO motivates its selection over established materials such as ZnS(Ag), CsI and other modern scintillators coupled to SiPMs.

MI3 - Figure 3 – axis labeling

The x-axes in Figure 3 are labeled only at the endpoints of the displayed range, which makes it difficult to quantitatively read the position and evolution of the simulated quantities. Additional tick marks and numerical labels (e.g. at regular 5 or 10 mm intervals) should be added to improve the readability and quantitative interpretation of the figure.

MI4 - More information on peak fitting

The manuscript would benefit from a brief description of how the alpha-particle peak positions and FWHM values were obtained from the measured spectra. It would be useful to specify whether Gaussian or another fitting function was used, how the background was treated. This information is particularly important for the low-energy spectra, where the alpha peak increasingly overlaps with the background.

MI5 - Count-rate and timing capabilities

The authors may consider slightly qualifying the statements regarding high count-rate capability and timing applications, since these aspects were not directly evaluated in the present study. For example, the fast scintillation decay of YSO could be described as a property favorable for or potentially beneficial to high-rate and timing applications.

Investigation of YSO–SiPM detector response for alpha spectroscopy applications

The manuscript presents an experimental investigation of a YSO scintillator coupled to a SiPM array for alpha-particle spectroscopy. YSO scintillators are more commonly employed in photon-detection applications, particularly gamma-ray spectroscopy and related radiation-detection systems, whereas their response and applicability for charged-particle detection have been investigated to a much more limited extent. From this perspective, the present study is relevant not only to the development of compact and robust radiation-detection systems, but also to extending the potential application of YSO-based scintillation detectors to charged-particle spectroscopy.

The experimental results demonstrate an approximately linear detector response over a broad alpha-particle energy range and show reasonable agreement between the experimentally determined alpha-particle range in air and SRIM simulations. The manuscript is generally clear and the experimental approach is appropriate. However, several aspects should be clarified before indexing. In particular, the authors should provide a clearer description of the energy-calibration procedure, explain how the experimental alpha-particle range and its uncertainty were determined, define the method used to calculate the energy resolution, and resolve minor inconsistencies concerning the lowest detectable alpha-particle energy and the simulated alpha-particle range. Additional clarification of the MPPC readout configuration and improvement of several figure captions would further strengthen the manuscript.

Minor presentation issues, including inconsistent terminology and figure numbering, should also be corrected. Overall, the manuscript presents useful experimental results and addresses a comparatively less explored application of YSO scintillators for charged-particle detection. The manuscript may be considered for indexing after the points listed below are adequately addressed.
