Infinite polynomial patterns in large subsets of the rational numbers
Ethan Ackelsberg

Abstract
. Inspired by a question of Kra, Moreira, Richter, and Robertson [ 36 ] , we prove two new results about infinite polynomial configurations in large subsets of the rational numbers. First, given a finite coloring of ℚ \mathbb{Q} , we show that there exists an infinite set B = { b n : n ∈ ℕ } ⊆ ℚ B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q} such that { b i , b i 2 + b j : i < j } \{b_{i},b_{i}^{2}+b_{j}:i<j\} is monochromatic. Second, we prove that every subset of positive density in the rational numbers contains a translate of such an infinite configuration. The proofs build upon methods developed in a series of papers by Kra, Moreira, Richter, and Robertson [ 34 , 35 , 37 ] to translate from combinatorics into dynamics, where the core of the argument reduces to understanding the behavior of certain polynomial ergodic averages. The new dynamical tools required for this analysis are a Wiener–Wintner theorem for polynomially-twisted ergodic averages in ℚ \mathbb{Q} -systems and a structure theorem for Abramov ℚ \mathbb{Q} -systems. The end of the paper includes a discussion of related problems in the integers.

1.
Introduction

Looking for a “sumset” generalization of the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem, Kra, Moreira, Richter, and Robertson posed the following question in
[
36
, Question 3.15]
.
Suppose
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
has positive upper Banach density, i.e.

d^{*}(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{|A\cap\{x+1,x+2,\ldots,x+n\}|}{n}>0.

Must there exist an infinite set
B=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots\}
and a shift
t\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(1.1)

\left\{b_{i},b_{i}^{2}+b_{j}:i<j\right\}

is contained in
A-t
?
Note that taking
x=b_{j}+t
,
y=b_{i}^{2}+b_{j}+t
, and
n=b_{i}
gives a pair of points
x,y\in A
with
y-x=n^{2}
, so an affirmative answer to this question strengthens the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem
[
21
,
43
]
.
A weaker question that was also asked in
[
36
, Question 3.13]
is whether every set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of positive upper Banach density contains a shift of a set of the form

(1.2)

\left\{b_{i}^{2}+b_{j}:i<j\right\}

for some infinite set
B=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots\}
.

We now discuss evidence in favor of an affirmative answer to both of the questions from the previous paragraph.
To enable this discussion, we recall some definitions.
A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is

•

thick
if it contains arbitrary long intervals,

•

syndetic
if it has bounded gaps, and

•

piecewise syndetic
if it can be written as the intersection of a thick set with a syndetic set.

Piecewise syndetic sets are an important class of sets in Ramsey theory; for example, van der Waerden’s theorem can be rephrased as the statement that every piecewise syndetic set contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.
1
1

1

This formulation of van der Waerden’s theorem appears to have first been observed by Furstenberg and Glasner
[
23
]
, who provide a short argument in the opening paragraph of their paper.
Furstenberg and Glasner use the term “big” instead of piecewise syndetic and refer to thick sets as “replete” sets.
For a rendering of their argument in modern terminology, see the first page of
[
6
]
.

We will now show that every piecewise syndetic set contains a configuration of the form (
1.2
) up to a shift.
Suppose that
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is a piecewise syndetic set.
Then there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
T=A\cup(A-1)\cup\ldots\cup(A-k)
is thick.
Define a sequence
c_{1}<c_{2}<\ldots
as follows.
Let
c_{1}=1
.
Given
c_{1}<\ldots<c_{n}
, we use the fact that
T
is thick to choose
c_{n+1}
such that
c_{n+1}+\{c_{1}^{2},\ldots,c_{n}^{2}\}\subseteq T
.
We thus have an infinite sequence
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\{c_{i}^{2}+c_{j}:i<j\}\subseteq T
.
Now we define a
(k+1)
-coloring of the 2-element subsets of
\mathbb{N}
by

\chi(\{i,j\})=\min\{t\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}:c_{i}^{2}+c_{j}\in A-t\}

for
i<j
.
By Ramsey’s theorem, there is an infinite subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and a color
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
such that
\chi(\{i,j\})=t
for every
i,j\in S
,
i<j
.
Enumerating
S=\{s_{1}<s_{2}<\ldots\}
and putting
b_{n}=c_{s_{n}}
, we see that
A-t
contains a set of the form (
1.2
).
Thus, the second question in the previous paragraph (
[
36
, Question 3.13]
) has a positive answer for piecewise syndetic sets.

The above discussion does not rely on any particular properties of the set of squares, which may be hiding potential issues that arise when trying to upgrade from piecewise syndetic sets to arbitrary sets of positive upper Banach density or from the more limited configuration in (
1.2
) to the configuration in (
1.1
).
This is where the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem plays a role: using the fact that
\{n^{2}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a set of strong recurrence

2

Subsequent work by Luke Hetzel (personal communication) establishes a similar result for sets of recurrence without assuming that they are also sets of strong recurrence.
, it was shown in
[
36
, Corollary 3.33]
that for any set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
of positive density, there exist infinite sets
B,C\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
\{c,b^{2}+c:b\in B,c\in C,b<c\}\subseteq A
.
Asking for a configuration of the form (
1.1
) is equivalent to asking for
B
and
C
to be equal (at the cost of allowing
A
to be shifted).

Despite the evidence in its favor, even the weaker question of Kra, Moreira, Richter, and Robertson has a negative answer, as shown in
[
2
]
:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
2
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
d^{*}(A)>1-\varepsilon
such that if
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

\{b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}:b_{1},b_{2}\in B,b_{1}<b_{2}\}\subseteq A-t,

then
B
is finite.

Though more intricate than other instances of this phenomenon, the counterexample relies on the presence of “local obstructions”: the sets
A
constructed in
[
2
]
are of the form
A=\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{t}
, where
A_{t}
is (up to finitely many elements) a union of finitely many infinite arithmetic progressions.
Replacing the ring
\mathbb{Z}
by the field
\mathbb{Q}
eliminates local obstructions, and we show that large subsets of
\mathbb{Q}
contain infinite polynomial sumset configurations.

1.1.
A density result

To give a precise formulation of our first main result, we need a definition.
A sequence
\Phi=(\Phi_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite subsets of
\mathbb{Q}
is an (additive)
Følner sequence
if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\left|(\Phi_{N}+x)\triangle\Phi_{N}\right|}{|\Phi_{N}|}=0

for every
x\in\mathbb{Q}
.
An example of a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
is the sequence

\Phi_{N}=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{a_{n}}{n}:a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z},-N\leq a_{n}\leq N\right\}.

The
upper Banach density
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
is the quantity

d^{*}(A)=\sup_{\Phi}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\Phi_{N}|}{|\Phi_{N}|},

where the supremum is taken over all Følner sequences
\Phi
in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
with
d^{*}(A)>0
.
Then there exists
t\in\mathbb{Q}
and an infinite set
\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
such that

\{b_{i},b_{i}^{2}+b_{j}:i<j\}\subseteq A-t.

We can replace the polynomial term
b_{i}^{2}
by a more general polynomial expression
P(b_{i})
but with some restriction on the polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
.
To aid with the discussion, call a polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]

good for sumsets
if Theorem
1.2
holds with
P(b_{i})
in place of
b_{i}^{2}
.
We will now describe the family of polynomials that are good for sumsets.

One relatively simple obstruction to being good for sumsets is when
P=c
for some nonzero constant
c\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
.
Indeed, we can find a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
such that
A\cap(A+c)=\emptyset
and
\mathbb{Q}=A\cup(A+c)
.
Such a set
A
must have density
\frac{1}{2}
, so we see that
P=c
is bad for sumsets.

A more subtle obstacle arises from certain linear polynomials.
By modifying a construction of Straus (never published by Straus but described, e.g., in
[
7
]
and
[
2
, Section 2]
), we show in Section
10
that there exists a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
with
d^{*}(A)>0
such that no translate of
A
contains a
\Delta
-set, i.e. an infinite set of the form
\{b_{j}-b_{i}:i<j\}
.
As a consequence, the polynomial
P(x)=-x+c
is bad for sumsets for every
c\in\mathbb{Q}
.

As we will show, there are no other obstacles to producing polynomial sumsets.
That is,
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
is good for sumsets if and only if
P=0
or both
P(x)
and
P(x)+x
are nonconstant, as demonstrated by the following theorem (we do not account for the trivial
P=0
case explicitly):

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that both
P(x)
and
P(x)+x
are nonconstant.
Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
with
d^{*}(A)>0
.
Then there exists
t\in\mathbb{Q}
and an infinite set
\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
such that

\{b_{i},P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}\subseteq A-t.

1.2.
A coloring result

Using the methods developed for proving Theorem
1.3
, we are also able to prove a result about monochromatic infinite polynomial sumset configurations in finite colorings of
\mathbb{Q}
, answering an analogue of a question asked by Kra, Moreira, Richter, and Robertson in the integers (see
[
36
, Remark 3.17]
).

Theorem 1.4
.

For any finite coloring of
\mathbb{Q}
, there exists an infinite set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

\{b_{i},b_{i}^{2}+b_{j}:i<j\}

is monochromatic.

That one can find a monochromatic triple
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}\}
in any finite coloring of
\mathbb{Q}
(in fact, in any finite coloring of
\mathbb{N}
) is a nontrivial result of Bergelson; see
[
8
, p. 53]
.
Theorem
1.4
is a substantial strengthening for colorings of
\mathbb{Q}
, providing an infinite set
B
rather than a set with just two elements.

As with Theorem
1.2
above, Theorem
1.4
actually holds for more general polynomials.
With our method of proof, we can replace the term
b_{i}^{2}
by
P(b_{i})
for an arbitrary polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
with
\deg{P}\geq 2
:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
with
\deg{P}\geq 2
.
Then for any finite coloring of
\mathbb{Q}
, there exists an infinite set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

\{b_{i},P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}

is monochromatic.

This leaves open the linear case.
That is, for which linear polynomials
P(x)=cx+d\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
does the following hold: for any finite coloring of
\mathbb{Q}
, there is an infinite set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that
\{b_{i},P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}
is monochromatic?
This problem is surprisingly recalcitrant, and the only linear polynomial for which we know the answer is
P(x)=x
: by Hindman’s theorem
[
27
]
, if
\mathbb{Q}
is finitely colored, then there exists an infinite set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that
\{b_{i},b_{i}+b_{j}:i<j\}
is monochromatic.

1.3.
Overview of the paper

We will prove Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.5
using techniques from ergodic theory.
For problems concerning patterns in sets of positive density, dynamical tools have in most cases been deployed in the language of abstract ergodic theory with no reference to an underlying topological structure (see, e.g.,
[
22
, Chapters 3–7]
).
In
[
34
]
, it was observed that one can make appreciable use of topology in conjunction with more standard ergodic-theoretic tools to move beyond the classical results in ergodic Ramsey theory (which concern finite configurations) and find infinite configurations in sets of positive density.
Our proof of Theorem
1.3
builds upon the arguments developed in
[
34
]
(and subsequent work of the same group of authors in
[
35
,
37
]
) and utilizes topological as well as measure-theoretic tools.

On the other hand, in most applications of dynamical methods to coloring problems (first established in a systematic way in
[
24
]
; see also
[
22
, Chapters 1, 2, and 8]
), the natural setting is in the realm of topological dynamics.
In keeping with this tradition, we give a reformulation of Theorem
1.5
as a statement in topological dynamics in Section
2
(see Theorem
2.6
).
However, in the course of the proof, we introduce a measure and carry out the majority of the argument in the context of measure-preserving systems in order to make use of many of the same ingredients that appear in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
uses the framework introduced in
[
35
]
to translate Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.5
into dynamical language.
There is substantial overlap in the arguments for both of the main theorems, and the common tools for both results are developed in Sections

–

.
We complete the proof of Theorem
1.3
in Section

; the finishing touches for the proof of Theorem

appear in Section

.
Two important technical results about polynomial structures in measure-preserving systems play a central role in our argument: (1) a Wiener–Wintner-type theorem for ergodic averages twisted by polynomial phases (Theorem

), and (2) a structure theorem for Abramov systems (Theorem

).
Both of these theorems have known analogues in the case of (totally ergodic)

The proofs of these new results, which may be of independent interest, appear in Section

As promised in the discussion following Theorem

2.
Dynamical reformulation of the main theorems

The Furstenberg correspondence principle, originating in Furstenberg’s ergodic-theoretic proof of Szemerédi’s theorem
[
21
]
, is a tool that allows one to encode combinatorial statements in a dynamical framework.
Before stating the version of the Furstenberg correspondence principle that we will need, we introduce basic objects from ergodic theory.

A
topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system
is a pair
(X,T)
, where
X
is a compact metric space and
T
is an action of
(\mathbb{Q},+)
by homeomorphisms
T^{q}:X\to X
.
By a (measure-preserving)
\mathbb{Q}
-system
, we will mean a triple
(X,\mu,T)
, where
(X,T)
is a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system and
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
X
that is
T
-invariant, meaning that
\mu(T^{-q}A)=\mu(A)
for every Borel set
A\subseteq X
and every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
(Note that the notion of a measure-preserving system is often more general than we have stated here, allowing for
(X,\mu)
to be an arbitrary probability space and
T
a measurable map.
Several arguments in this paper rely on simultaneously keeping track of both topological and measure-theoretic properties, which is why we impose additional topological constraints in the definition of a system.)

A system
(X,\mu,T)
is
ergodic
if whenever
A\subseteq X
is a Borel set satisfying
\mu(A\triangle T^{-q}A)=0
for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, one has
\mu(A)\in\{0,1\}
.
A point
a\in X
is
transitive
if the orbit
\{T^{q}a:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is dense in
X
.
We say that
a
is
generic for
\mu
along a Følner sequence
\Phi
, abbreviated
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, if
\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\delta_{T^{q}a}\to\mu
in the weak
∗
topology.

Theorem 2.1

(Topological Furstenberg correspondence principle)
.

Let
\chi:\mathbb{Q}\to\{1,\ldots,r\}
be an
r
-coloring of
\mathbb{Q}
for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exists a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,T)
, a transitive point
a\in X
, and a partition
X=\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}E_{k}
of
X
into clopen sets such that
T^{q}a\in E_{\chi(q)}
for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Encoding a finite coloring in a topological dynamical system has its origins in
[
24
]
, where a corresponding statement for
\mathbb{Z}
appears implicitly.
We give a short self-contained proof for the convenience of the reader.

Let
\Omega=\{1,\ldots,r\}^{\mathbb{Q}}
be the space of all
r
-colorings of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Equipping
\Omega
with the product topology makes it into a compact set with compatible metric given, for example, by

d(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\min\{j\in\mathbb{N}:\omega(q_{j})\neq\omega^{\prime}(q_{j})\}},&\text{if}~\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}\\
0,&\text{if}~\omega=\omega^{\prime},\end{cases}

where
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots
is an enumeration of
\mathbb{Q}
.

For
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, let
T^{q}:\Omega\to\Omega
be the shift map
(T^{q}\omega)(s)=\omega(s+q)
, and let
X=\overline{\{T^{q}\chi:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}}\subseteq\Omega
.
Then
(X,T)
is a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and
a=\chi
is a transitive point by construction.
Let
E_{k}=\{x\in X:x(0)=k\}
for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
.
Then the sets
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
form a partition of
X
into clopen sets, and for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we have

T^{q}a\in E_{k}\iff(T^{q}a)(0)=k\iff\chi(q)=k.

∎

Theorem 2.2

(Furstenberg correspondence principle)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
with
d^{*}(A)>0
.
Then there exists an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,\mu,T)
, a transitive point
a\in X
, a Følner sequence
\Phi
such that
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and a clopen set
E\subseteq X
with
\mu(E)>0
such that
A=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}a\in E\}
.

Theorem
2.2
can be proved similarly to the proof of Theorem
2.1
, with the added step that one must construct a
T
-invariant ergodic measure
\mu
on the space
X
.
A full proof of Theorem
2.2
(in the wider generality of amenable groups) appears in
[
17
, Theorem 2.15]
.

In order to complete the translation of Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
into dynamical language, we need an appropriate notion of dynamical progressions, which generalizes the notion of Erdős progressions from
[
35
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(X,T)
be a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
.
A triple
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{3}
is an
Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy
P
-progression
if there exists a sequence of distinct elements
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(T^{s_{n}}x_{0},T^{P(s_{n})}x_{1}\right)=(x_{1},x_{2}).

We will denote the set of Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy
P
-progressions by
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}\subseteq X^{3}
.
For a point
x_{0}\in X
, we let
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(x_{0})=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X^{2}:(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}\right\}
.
We have the following variant of
[
35
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(X,T)
be a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system,
x_{0}\in X
, and
U,V\subseteq X
open sets.
If
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(x_{0})\cap(U\times V)\neq\emptyset
, then there exists a sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of distinct elements such that

\displaystyle\{b_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}x_{0}\in U\}

\displaystyle\{P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}x_{0}\in V\}.

Proof.

Let
(x_{1},x_{2})\in U\times V
with
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}
.
Let
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(T^{s_{n}}x_{0},T^{P(s_{n})}x_{1}\right)=(x_{1},x_{2}).

By refining the sequence
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we may assume
(T^{s_{n}}x_{0},T^{P(s_{n})}x_{1})\in U\times V
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We will construct an increasing sequence
(n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
by induction and take
b_{i}=s_{n_{i}}
.
Let
n_{1}=1
.
Suppose we have chosen
n_{1}<n_{2}<\ldots<n_{k}
such that the elements
b_{i}=s_{n_{i}}
satisfy

x_{0}\in\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq k}T^{-P(b_{i})-b_{j}}V.

(Note that this condition is vacuously satisfied for
k=1
.)
The set
\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq k}T^{-P(b_{i})}V
is an open neighborhood of
x_{1}
, so we may choose
n_{k+1}>n_{k}
such that

T^{s_{n_{k+1}}}x_{0}\in\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq k}T^{-P(b_{i})}V.

Let
b_{k+1}=s_{n_{k+1}}
.
By the induction hypothesis, this implies

x_{0}\in\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq k+1}T^{-P(b_{i})-b_{j}}V.

By construction,
T^{b_{i}}x_{0}\in U
for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and
T^{P(b_{i})+b_{j}}x_{0}\in V
for
i<j
, so this completes the proof.
∎

a transitive point,
\Phi
a Følner sequence such that
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and
E\subseteq X
a clopen set with
\mu(E)>0
such that
A=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}a\in E\}
.
Then by Theorem

, there exists
t\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(T^{t}a)\cap(E\times E)\neq\emptyset
.
Applying Proposition
2.4
, we obtain a sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of distinct rational numbers such that

\{b_{i},P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}\subseteq\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}(T^{t}a)\in E\}=A-t.

∎

We similarly give a dynamical formulation of Theorem
1.5
:

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
with
\deg{P}\geq 2
.
Let
(X,T)
be a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
Suppose
a\in X
is a transitive point.
If
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}\subseteq X
is a finite open cover of
X
, then there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
with points
x_{1},x_{2}\in E_{k}
such that
(a,x_{1},x_{2})
is an Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy
P
-progression.

Proof that Theorem
2.6
\implies
Theorem
1.5
.

Suppose Theorem
2.6
holds.
Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be a nonconstant polynomial, and let
\chi:\mathbb{Q}\to\{1,\ldots,r\}
be a finite coloring of
\mathbb{Q}
.
Then by Theorem
2.1
, let
(X,T)
be a topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system,
a\in X
a transitive point, and
X=\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}E_{k}
a partition into clopen sets such that
T^{q}a\in E_{\chi(q)}
for every

.
By Theorem

, there exists
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and points
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)\cap(E_{k}\times E_{k})
.
Hence, by Proposition

, there exists an infinite sequence

such that

\{b_{i},P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}\subseteq\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}a\in E_{k}\}=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:\chi(q)=k\}.

That is,

is monochromatic of color

∎

The basic strategy for proving these dynamical statements is as follows.
First, we show that Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy
P
-progressions are “controlled” in some sense by the Abramov factor of order
d=\deg{P}
.
The main tool here is a polynomial variant of the Wiener–Wintner theorem (Theorem
3.4
).
Using a structure theorem for Abramov systems (Theorem
4.1
), we are able to define a measure on Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions starting at a fixed point in the Abramov factor of order
d
.
We then lift this measure to the original system and prove that it remains supported on the closure of the space of Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions.
From this point on, the proofs of Theorem
2.5
and Theorem
2.6
diverge, as we need to establish different properties of the measure.

It turns out that the proof of Theorem
2.5
is simpler, so we deal with it first.
The remaining step is to average the measures corresponding to the spaces
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(T^{t}a)
over
t\in\mathbb{Q}
and show that this averaged measure give positive mass to the product set
E\times E
.
By introducing the average over
t
, we are able to obtain this positivity result by an application of a version of the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem over
\mathbb{Q}
.

To prove Theorem
2.6
, we need to show that the measure supported on the closure of
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)
gives positive mass to
\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}(E_{k}\times E_{k})
.
The inability to shift the point
a
means that recurrence results from ergodic theory are no longer helpful.
However, we can compute polynomial orbits in Abramov systems rather explicitly in order to reduce to an expression that can be controlled by an application of Ramsey’s theorem for hypergraphs.

3.
Characteristic factors for polynomial averages

The goal of this section is to show that polynomial ergodic averages related to Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions are “controlled” by certain structured factors (the Abramov factors, to be defined later in this section).

3.1.
Factors of measure-preserving systems

A system
(Y,\nu,S)
is a
(measurable) factor
of another system
(X,\mu,T)
if there exists a measurable map
\pi:X\to Y
such that
\pi_{*}\mu=\nu
and
\pi\circ T^{q}=S^{q}\circ\pi
almost everywhere for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
We will say that
\pi
is a
topological factor map
if
\pi
is continuous and surjective and the identity
\pi\circ T^{q}=S^{q}\circ\pi
holds everywhere.

Measurable factors of
(X,\mu,T)
correspond to
\sigma
-algebras that are invariant under the action of
T
.
For this reason, while dealing with factors, it is often convenient to make explicit reference to
\sigma
-algebras.
We will denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on a space by calligraphic letters; for example, we use
\mathcal{X}
to denote the
\sigma
-algebra on
X
,
\mathcal{Y}
to denote the
\sigma
-algebra on
Y
, and so on.
In the other direction, given a
T
-invariant
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we will denote the correspond space by
Y
.
In general, such a set
Y
is an abstract space about which we can say very little.
However, for the structured factors of interest, we will be able to produce a concrete realization of
Y
.

We caution the reader that in a standard terminological abuse, we will use the term
factor
to refer both to the system
(Y,\nu,S)
and to the corresponding sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, using whichever notion is more convenient for the specific problem at hand.

Each factor comes with a corresponding conditional expectation operator.
Given a sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, the
conditional expection

\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}\right]:L^{1}(\mu)\to L^{1}(\mu)
is the operator defined by the properties: for any
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
,

•

\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{Y}\right]
is
\mathcal{Y}
-measurable, and

•

if
g\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is
\mathcal{Y}
-measurable, then

\int_{X}fg~d\mu=\int_{X}\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{Y}\right]\cdot g~d\mu.

Restricting
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\mid\mathcal{Y}\right]
to
L^{2}(\mu)
produces the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of
L^{2}(\mu)
of
\mathcal{Y}
-measurable square-integrable functions.

Suppose
\pi:(X,\mathcal{X},\mu,T)\to(Y,\mathcal{Y},\nu,S)
is a measurable factor map.
(Here, we have included the Borel
\sigma
-algebras explicitly for clarity in the ensuing discussion.)
A function
f:X\to\mathbb{C}
is measurable with respect to the sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})
if and only if there is a measurable function
g:Y\to\mathbb{C}
such that
f=g\circ\pi
.
We will write
\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid Y\right]
for the function on
Y
determined by
\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid Y\right]\circ\pi=\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\pi^{-1}(\mathcal{Y})\right]
.

3.2.
A variation on characteristic factors

Let us now give meaning to “control” by factors.
Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system and
a\in X
.
The space of progressions
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)
is intimately related to polynomial orbits of the form
(T^{q}a,T^{P(q)}x)_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
for
x\in X
(see Definition
2.3
).
In order to understand such orbits from a measure-theoretic point of view, it is natural to study ergodic averages taking the shape

A_{N}(x)=\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot g(T^{P(q)}x).

Here, we will assume (as we may in Theorem
2.5
) that
\Phi
is a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
for which
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
.
To take advantage of genericity of
a
, we will also assume that
f
is a continuous function.
It turns out that an appropriate and convenient space to work in is the space
L^{2}(\mu)
: we assume
g\in L^{2}(\mu)
and study convergence of
A_{N}
in
L^{2}
norm.
What we will mean by factors “controlling” the average
A_{N}
is a result of the following kind: there exists a pair of factors
(\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{2})
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a decomposition
f=f_{s}+f_{u}
as a sum of continuous functions and a decomposition
g=g_{s}+g_{u}
as a sum of measurable functions satisfying:

•

the “structured” functions
f_{s}
and
g_{s}
are measurable with respect to factors
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
respectively,

•

the “uniform” function
f_{u}
is
\varepsilon
-almost orthogonal to
\mathcal{B}_{1}
, i.e.
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f_{u}\mid\mathcal{B}_{1}\right]\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}<\varepsilon
,

•

the “uniform” function
g_{u}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, and

•

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left(f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g-f_{s}(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g_{s}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\varepsilon.

This closely resembles the notion of
characteristic factors
for multiple ergodic averages as introduced by Furstenberg and Weiss
[
25
]
.
The difference is that here we must allow for a small error tolerance
\varepsilon
in order to ensure that the function
f_{s}
is continuous.
Without continuity, we would be unable to say anything about the evaluation of the function
f_{s}
along the specific orbit
(T^{q}a)_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
.

We will carry out our argument in two steps, first identifying the factor
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and then using the structure of
g_{s}
to more easily identify
\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

3.3.
The van der Corput Lemma

One of the main tools we will use for producing the factors
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
described in the previous subsection is the van der Corput differencing trick.

Lemma 3.1

(van der Corput lemma, cf.
[
44
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space, and let
u:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathcal{H}
be a bounded sequence.
Let
\Phi
be a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Suppose that:

•

the limit

z(r)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left\langle u(q+r),u(q)\right\rangle

exists for every
r\in\mathbb{Q}
, and

•

there exists
K<\infty
such that for any Følner sequence
\Psi
in
\Gamma
,

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{M}|}\left|\sum_{r\in\Psi_{M}}z(r)\right|\leq K.

Then

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q)\right\|^{2}\leq K.

3.4.
The Kronecker factor is partially characteristic

The first structured factor of importance for the present analysis is the Kronecker factor.
Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
The
Kronecker factor
is the maximal factor of
(X,\mu,T)
that is measurably isomorphic to a compact group rotational system, i.e. a system of the form
(Z,m_{Z},R)
, where
Z
is a compact abelian group with Haar measure
m_{Z}
and
R^{q}z=z+\psi(q)
for some homomorphism
\psi:\mathbb{Q}\to Z
.
There are many equivalent characterizations of the Kronecker factor, of which we reproduce a selection below (cf.
[
30
, Chapter 4]
):

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
f,g\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
Consider the statements:

(i)

f
is measurable with respect to the Kronecker factor;

(ii)

the orbit
\{T^{q}f:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is pre-compact in
L^{2}(\mu)
;

(iii)

f
can be approximated arbitrarily well in
L^{2}(\mu)
by linear combinations of eigenfunctions
3
3

3

We say that
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
is an
eigenfunction
if there is a group character
\chi:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
such that for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
,
T^{q}f=\chi(q)f
.
;

(iv)

g
is orthogonal to the Kronecker factor;

(v)

g
is orthogonal to every eigenfunction;

(vi)

there exists a Følner sequence
\Phi
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left|\left\langle T^{q}g,g\right\rangle\right|=0;

(vii)

for every Følner sequence
\Phi
, one has

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left|\left\langle T^{q}g,g\right\rangle\right|=0.

Each of the statements (i)–(iii) is equivalent, and the statements (iv)–(vii) are equivalent to each other.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system and
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
.
Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be a nonconstant polynomial.
For any
f\in C(X)
and
g\in L^{2}(\mu)
, if
\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right]=0
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g=0.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on the degree of
P
.
First, suppose
\deg{P}=1
, and write
P(q)=cq+d
with
c,d\in\mathbb{Q}
,
c\neq 0
.
Let
u(q)=f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{cq+d}g\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\langle u(q+r),u(q)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{f(T^{q}a)}\cdot f(T^{q+r}a)\int_{X}T^{cq+d}\overline{g}\cdot T^{c(q+r)+d}g~d\mu

\displaystyle=(\overline{f}\cdot T^{r}f)(T^{q}a)\int_{X}\overline{g}\cdot T^{cr}g~d\mu,

so

z(r)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left\langle u(q+r),u(q)\right\rangle=\left(\int_{X}\overline{f}\cdot T^{r}f~d\mu\right)\left(\int_{X}\overline{g}\cdot T^{cr}g~d\mu\right).

Therefore, by the assumption
\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right]=0
, given any Følner sequence
\Psi
in
\mathbb{Q}
, we have (see (viii) in Proposition
3.2
)

\limsup_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{M}|}\sum_{r\in\Psi_{M}}|z(r)|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{u}^{2}\cdot\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{M}|}\sum_{r\in\Psi_{M}}\left|\int_{X}\overline{g}\cdot T^{cr}g~d\mu\right|=0.

Thus, by Lemma
3.1
, we conclude

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q)=0.

Now suppose the proposition holds for polynomials of degree at most
d
, and assume
\deg{P}=d+1
.
As before, let
u(q)=f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\langle u(q+r),u(q)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{f(T^{q}a)}\cdot f(T^{q+r}a)\int_{X}T^{P(q)}\overline{g}\cdot T^{P(q+r)}g~d\mu

\displaystyle=(\overline{f}\cdot T^{r}f)(T^{q}a)\int_{X}\overline{g}\cdot T^{P(q+r)-P(q)}g~d\mu.

For fixed
r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
, the polynomial
Q_{r}(q)=P(q+r)-P(q)
has degree at most
d
.
Therefore, by the induction hypothesis,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}(\overline{f}\cdot T^{r}f)(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{Q_{r}(q)}g=0.

Hence,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left\langle u(q+r),u(q)\right\rangle=0

for every
r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Lemma
3.1
, it follows that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q)=0.

∎

3.5.
The Abramov factor is partially characteristic

We now wish to describe a factor that will allow us also to replace
f
by a structured function when dealing with the limiting behavior of averages of the form

(3.1)

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g.

Since the Kronecker factor is generated by eigenfunctions (see item (iii) in Proposition
3.2
), Proposition
3.3
shows that the key to understanding the behavior of (
3.1
) is to analyze “polynomially-twisted” ergodic averages

(3.2)

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot\chi(P(q))

for characters
\chi:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}

Let us begin by introducing some notation for describing characters on
\mathbb{Q}
.
For each prime number
p

p

\mathbb{Q}
by
d_{p}(x,y)=p^{-n}

The completion of

p

c_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}

The
p

\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}p^{j}:c_{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_{p}

The ring of adeles is defined by

\mathbb{A}=\left\{(a_{\infty},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots)\in\mathbb{R}\times\prod_{p}\mathbb{Q}_{p}:a_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}~\text{for all but finitely many}~p\right\}.

We embed
\mathbb{Q}
as a subspace of
\mathbb{A}
by identifying

with the element

The ring of adeles

is a topological space with the subspace topology inherited from the product space

.
One can show that

is discrete and co-compact, so
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
is a compact group.
In fact,
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
is isomorphic to the Pontryagin dual

.
We let
e_{\mathbb{Q}}:\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
such that each character
\chi\in\widehat{\mathbb{Q}}

.
(For a concrete description of the function

[

We may thus express the averages (

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q))

with
P
a polynomial taking values in
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.

As may be expected, averages of the form (

) are controlled in a certain sense by polynomial structures in the system
(X,\mu,T)
.
Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
For a function
g\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
and an element
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we define the multiplicative derivative
\Delta_{q}g=\overline{g}\cdot T^{q}g
.
Given
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}

\Delta^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}
inductively by
\Delta^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}g=\Delta_{q_{k}}\left(\Delta^{k-1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1}}g\right)
.
We say that
g\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is a
phase polynomial of degree at most

if for each
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k+1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}

\Delta^{k+1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k+1}}g=1~\text{a.e.}

Let
\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
be the set of phase polynomials of degree at most

.
The
Abramov factor of order

is the factor
\mathcal{A}_{k}
for which

is the closure of the linear span of
\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)

.
(We have thus far reserved the letter

for the Haar measure on a compact group.
We will show in Theorem

Note that

by item (iii) in Proposition

, then

(3.4)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)\right|\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\right\|_{1}.

Proof.

If
\alpha=0
, then by the assumption
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, the left-hand side of (
3.4
) is equal to
\left|\int_{X}f~d\mu\right|
, and in this case the inequality is trivial.
We will therefore assume
\alpha\neq 0
.

We define an action of
\mathbb{Q}
on
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}
by

S^{q}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})=\left(v_{1}+q\alpha,v_{2}+2qv_{1}+q^{2}\alpha,\ldots,v_{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\binom{d}{j}q^{d-j}v_{j}+q^{d}\alpha\right).

One can show that
((\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d},S)
is minimal and uniquely ergodic.
The argument appears in
[
22
, Lemma 1.25 and Theorem 3.12]
for a corresponding action of
\mathbb{Z}
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and is easily adapted to the
\mathbb{Q}
-action on
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}
.
Writing
P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_{j}x^{j}
, we have

e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)=g\left(S^{q}(\bm{0})\right)

for the function
g(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(c_{0}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}v_{j}\right)
.

Now let
(N_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)\right|=\left|\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{k}}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N_{k}}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)\right|.

Refining to a further subsequence if necessary, we may assume that the weak* limit

\lambda=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{k}}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N_{k}}}\delta_{T^{q}a}\times\delta_{S^{q}(\bm{0})}

exists.
Since
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
by assumption and
\bm{0}\in\textup{gen}(m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{d},\Phi)
by unique ergodicity of the system
((\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d},S)
, the measure
\lambda
is a joining of the systems
(X,\mu,T)
and
((\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d},m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{d},S)
.

Claim.
For
h\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
k\in L^{2}((\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d})
,

(3.5)

\int_{X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}}h\otimes k~d\lambda=\int_{X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[h\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\otimes k~d\lambda.

Proof of Claim.

By linearity, it suffices to prove the identity when
\mathbb{E}\left[h\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]=0
and
k(\bm{v})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}r_{j}v_{j}\right)
for some
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
.
Moreover, if
\lambda=\int_{\Omega}\lambda_{\omega}~d\rho(\omega)
is the ergodic decomposition of
\lambda
, then each of the ergodic components
\lambda_{\omega}
is an ergodic joining of
(X,\mu,T)
and
((\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d},m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{d},S)
(see, e.g.,
[
42
, Proposition 1]
), and it suffices to prove (
3.5
) with
\lambda_{\omega}
in place of
\lambda
.

Fix a tempered Følner sequence
\Psi
in
\mathbb{Q}
(such a sequence exists by
[
40
, Proposition 1.4]
).
By Theorem
3.4
, let
X_{0}\subseteq X
with
\mu(X_{0})=1
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Psi_{N}}h(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(Q(q)\right)=0

for every
Q(x)\in\mathbb{A}[x]
of degree at most
d
and every
x\in X_{0}
.
By Lindenstrauss’s pointwise ergodic theorem (see
[
40
, Theorem 1.2]
), let
Y\subseteq X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}
with
\lambda_{\omega}(Y)=1
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Psi_{N}}h(T^{q}x)\cdot k(S^{q}y)=\int_{X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}}h\otimes k~d\lambda_{\omega}

for every
(x,y)\in Y
.

Let
(x,y)\in Y\cap(X_{0}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d})
.
Note that

k(S^{q}y)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(Q_{y}(q)\right)

for some polynomial
Q_{y}
of degree at most
d
depending on the point
y
.
Thus,

\int_{X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}}h\otimes k~d\lambda_{\omega}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Psi_{N}}h(T^{q}x)\cdot k(S^{q}y)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Psi_{N}}h(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(Q_{y}(q)\right)=0.

This proves the claim.
∎

Now, using the claim, we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)\right|=\left|\int_{X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}}f\otimes g~d\lambda\right|=\left|\int_{X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\otimes g~d\lambda\right|,

and the inequality (
3.4
) follows by an application of Hölder’s inequality.
∎

3.6.
An approximation theorem for averages in systems with topological Abramov factors

We now have nearly everything in place to obtain the desired type of “control” described at the beginning of the section.
The final missing piece is a small technical assumption on the system
(X,\mu,T)
.
We say that
(X,\mu,T)

has topological Abramov factors
if there are topological (continuous) factor maps
\pi_{d}:X\to A_{d}
for every
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
We will see later on that this is not overly restrictive: every system has a topological extension that is measurably isomorphic and has topological Abramov factors (see Lemma
6.1
below).
Having topological Abramov factors ensures that continuous functions on
A_{d}
lift to continuous functions on
X
via the factor map
\pi_{d}
.

We collect the main results of this section in the following theorem:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
.
Assume that
(X,\mu,T)
has topological Abramov factors.
Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be a nonconstant polynomial of degree
d
.
Let
f\in C(X)
and
g\in L^{2}(\mu)
.
Then given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a decomposition
f=f_{s}\circ\pi_{d}+f_{u}
with
f_{s}\in C(A_{d})
and
f_{u}\in C(X)
such that
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f_{u}\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}<\varepsilon
and

(3.6)

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left(f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g-f_{s}(T_{d}^{q}\pi_{d}(a))\cdot T^{P(q)}\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right]\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\varepsilon.

Proof.

We will assume
\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=1
.
To deduce the general case, one can scale and apply the theorem with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{-1}\cdot\varepsilon
.

Since the space of continuous functions
C(A_{d})
is dense in
L^{1}(m_{A_{d}})
, we may find
f_{s}\in C(A_{d})
such that
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid A_{d}\right]-f_{s}\right\|_{L^{1}(m_{A_{d}})}<\varepsilon
.
Let
f_{u}=f-f_{s}\circ\pi_{d}
.
The factor map
\pi_{d}
is continuous by assumption, so
f_{u}
is continuous.
Moreover,
\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f_{u}\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}<\varepsilon
.
It remains to check (
3.6
).

For functions
h\in C(X)
and
k\in L^{2}(\mu)
, let

A_{N}(h,k;x)=\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}h(T^{q}a)\cdot k(T^{P(q)}x).

We want to show

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|A_{N}(f,g)-A_{N}(f_{s},\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\varepsilon.

By Proposition
3.3
, we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|A_{N}(f,g)-A_{N}(f,\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0,

so it is enough to show that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\underbrace{A_{N}(f,\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])-A_{N}(f_{s},\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])}_{A_{N}(f_{u},\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\varepsilon.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
be the countable set of eigenvalues of
T
, and let
h_{\alpha}:X\to S^{1}
be an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\alpha
for each
\alpha\in\sigma
, i.e.
T^{q}h_{\alpha}=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)\cdot h
.
By item (iii) in Proposition
3.2
and orthogonality of eigenfunctions, we may write
g=\sum_{\alpha\in\sigma}c_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}
for some coefficients
c_{\alpha}
satisfying
\sum_{\alpha\in\sigma}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}=1
.
Then

A_{N}(f_{u},\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])=\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f_{u}(T^{q}a)\cdot\sum_{\alpha\in\sigma}c_{\alpha}e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)h_{\alpha},

so by Theorem
3.5
and Fatou’s lemma,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|A_{N}(f_{u},\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid\mathcal{Z}\right])\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\alpha\in\sigma}|c_{\alpha}|^{2}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f_{u}(T^{q}a)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q)\alpha)\right|^{2}<\varepsilon^{2}.

∎

4.
Equidistribution of polynomial orbits in Abramov systems

Theorem
3.6
shows that the Abramov factors of
(X,\mu,T)
play a fundamental role in the behavior of polynomial orbits in
X
and hence in the structure of the space of Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions.
The goal of this section is to show that polynomial orbits in Abramov systems have a simple algebraic description.
This will later allow us to construct a measure supported on Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions that we can lift back to the original space
X
.

The main tool that allows us to get a handle on polynomial orbits in Abramov systems is the following structure theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
(X,\mu,T)
is an ergodic Abramov
\mathbb{Q}
-system of order at most
k
.
Then there exists a compact
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
5
5

5

The notion of a compact
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space may sound rather exotic, but we have in fact already encountered an example of one in Section
3
: the space
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
Indeed,
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
is a compact group that is both torsion-free (since
\mathbb{Q}
is divisible) and divisible (since
\mathbb{Q}
is torsion-free), so there is a natural multiplicative action of
\mathbb{Q}
on
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
that makes
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
into a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
.

V
and an element
\alpha\in V
such that
(X,\mu,T)
is a factor of the system
(V^{k},m_{V}^{k},S)
, where

S^{q}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=\left(v_{1}+q\alpha,v_{2}+qv_{1}+\binom{q}{2}\alpha,\ldots,v_{k}+qv_{k-1}+\ldots+\binom{q}{k-1}v_{1}+\binom{q}{k}\alpha\right).

Moreover, up to replacing
(X,\mu,T)
by a measurably isomorphic system
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{T})
, we may assume that the factor map is continuous.

A similar structure theorem was established by Abramov
[
1
]
for totally ergodic
\mathbb{Z}
-systems.
We give a proof of Theorem
4.1
in Section
9
.

Observe that for a system of the form given in Theorem
4.1
, the orbit of any point along any polynomial sequence will have polynomial coordinates.
Consequently, we can prove an equidistribution theorem for orbits in Abramov systems, which will be essential for defining a measure on the space of Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions.
Before giving a statement of the relevant equidistribution results, we recall some necessary terminology and notation.
Given a function
v:\mathbb{Q}\to V
taking values in a topological vector space
V
, we say that the
uniform Cesàro limit
of
v
is equal to
L\in V
if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}v(q)=L

for every Følner sequence
\Phi
.
If the uniform Cesàro limit exists, we denote it by
\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}v(q)
.
A function
x:\mathbb{Q}\to X
taking values in a compact metric space
X
is said to be
well-distributed
with respect to a Borel probability measure
\mu
if
\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{x(q)}=\mu
in the weak* topology.
That is, for every Følner sequence
\Phi
and every continuous function
f\in C(X)
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(x(q))=\int_{X}f~d\mu.

When
X
is a group and
\mu
is the Haar measure on a subgroup
H\subseteq X
, we will simply say that
x
is well-distributed in
H
.

We have the following variant of Weyl’s equidistribution theorem for polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}
:

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
V
be a compact
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in V
, and let
H_{j}\subseteq V
be the closed
\mathbb{Q}
-vector subspace generated by
\alpha_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Given any family
P_{1}(x),\ldots,P_{k}(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of nonconstant linearly independent polynomials with zero constant term, the
\mathbb{Q}
-sequence

\left(P_{1}(q)\alpha_{1},P_{2}(q)\alpha_{2},\ldots,P_{k}(q)\alpha_{k}\right)

is well-distributed in the closed subspace
H_{1}\times H_{2}\times\ldots\times H_{k}\subseteq V^{k}
.

Proof.

First we reduce to the case
P_{j}(x)=x^{j}
.
Suppose Proposition
4.2
holds for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
for the polynomials
P_{j}(x)=x^{j}
.
Now for a family
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
of nonconstant linearly independent polynomials with zero constant term, let us write
P_{i}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{ij}x^{j}
with
d=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\deg{P_{i}}\geq k
.
The condition that
P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}
are linearly independent means that the matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq d}
has trivial cokernel, so
A(\mathbb{Q}^{d})=\mathbb{Q}^{k}
by the fundamental theorem of linear algebra.

Let
V^{\prime}=V^{k}
and
\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})\in V^{\prime}
.
Let
H^{\prime}\subseteq V^{\prime}
be the closed vector space generated by
\alpha^{\prime}
.
By assumption, the sequence

s(q)=(q\alpha^{\prime},q^{2}\alpha^{\prime},\ldots,q^{d}\alpha^{\prime})

is well-distributed in
(H^{\prime})^{d}
.
Let
\pi:(V^{\prime})^{d}\to V^{k}
be the linear map

\pi(\bm{v}^{1},\ldots,\bm{v}^{d})=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{ij}v^{j}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{k}.

Then

\left(P_{1}(q)\alpha_{1},P_{2}(q)\alpha_{2},\ldots,P_{k}(q)\alpha_{k}\right)=\pi(s(q))

is well-distributed in
\pi((H^{\prime})^{d})
.
It remains to check that
\pi((H^{\prime})^{d})=H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{k}
.
On the one hand, if
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, then

\pi(q_{1}\alpha^{\prime},\ldots,q_{d}\alpha^{\prime})=\left(\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{ij}q_{j}\right)\alpha_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{k}\in H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{k},

so by continuity of
\pi
, we have
\pi((H^{\prime})^{d})\subseteq H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{k}
.
On the other hand, if
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
, then there exist
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
such that

q_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{ij}r_{j}

for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Hence,
(q_{1}\alpha_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\alpha_{k})=\pi(r_{1}\alpha^{\prime},\ldots,r_{d}\alpha^{\prime})\in\pi((H^{\prime})^{d})
.
Thus, since
\pi((H^{\prime})^{d})
is closed, we have
H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{k}\subseteq\pi((H^{\prime})^{d})
.

Now we want to show: if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}:V\to\mathbb{C}
are continuous functions, then

(4.1)

\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}f_{j}(q^{j}\alpha_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\int_{H_{j}}f_{j}~dm_{H_{j}}.

Approximating the functions
f_{j}
by linear combinations of group characters, it suffices to prove (
4.1
) for
f_{j}=\chi_{j}\in\widehat{V}
.
Note that if
\chi_{j}\in\widehat{V}
, then
\lambda_{j}(q)=\chi_{j}(q\alpha_{j})
is a group character on
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\lambda_{j}
is the trivial character if and only if
\chi_{j}\in H_{j}^{\perp}
.
Therefore, writing
\lambda_{j}(q)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\beta_{j})
and disregarding any high degree trivial terms, it suffices to prove: if
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{d}\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
and
\beta_{d}\neq 0
, then

\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}q^{j}\beta_{j}\right)=0.

This was proved in
[
12
, Theorem 5.2]
.
∎

Using the observation that (polynomial) orbits in Abramov systems have polynomial coordinates, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an Abramov
\mathbb{Q}
-system with Kronecker factor
(Z,m_{Z},R)
.
Assume that the factor map provided by Theorem
4.1
is continuous.
Then the weak* limit

\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{T^{q}x}\times\delta_{R^{P(q)}z}

exists for every
(x,z)\in X\times Z
and every polynomial
P
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and fix a polynomial
P
of degree
d
.
We fix a topological model for the Abramov factor
A_{d}
such that the factor map in Theorem
4.1
is continuous.
Assume moreover that there is a continuous factor map
\pi_{d}:X\to A_{d}
.
Let
\pi_{1}
be the factor map
\pi_{1}:X\to Z
.
This factor map will automatically be continuous, since it can be written as a composition of
\pi_{d}
with a topological factor map
\pi^{d}_{1}:A_{d}\to Z
.
For points

, define (by Corollary

)

\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(u,z)}=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{T_{d}^{q}u}\times\delta_{R^{P(q)}z}.

Then define for

on

by

\int_{X\times X}f\otimes g~d\lambda^{P}_{(x_{1},x_{2})}=\int_{A_{d}\times Z}\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid A_{d}\right]\otimes\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid Z\right]~d\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(\pi_{d}(x_{1}),\pi_{1}(x_{2}))}

\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{T_{d}^{q}\pi_{d}(x)}

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}

Let

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)g\left(T^{P(q)}x\right)=\int_{X^{2}}f\otimes g~d\lambda^{P}_{(a,x)}

in

.

Proof.

By scaling if necessary, we may assume
\left\|g\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=1
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
By Theorem
3.6
, we may decompose
f=f_{s}\circ\pi_{d}+f_{u}
and

such that
f_{s}\in C(A_{d})
,

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f_{u}\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\left(f(T^{q}a)\cdot T^{P(q)}g-f_{s}(T_{d}^{q}\pi_{d}(a))\cdot T^{P(q)}(g_{s}\circ\pi_{1})\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Now, by the definition of the measures

, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f_{s}(T_{d}^{q}\pi_{d}(a))\cdot g_{s}(T^{P(q)}x)=\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f_{s}\otimes g_{s}~d\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(\pi_{d}(a),\pi_{1}(x))}

in

, since this identity holds pointwise for continuous

.
From the definition of

, we also have

\int_{X^{2}}f\otimes g~d\lambda^{P}_{(a,x)}=\int_{A_{d}\times Z}\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid A_{d}\right]\otimes g_{s}~d\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(\pi_{d}(a),\pi_{1}(x))},

so

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\left\|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)g\left(T^{P(q)}x\right)-\int_{X^{2}}f\otimes g~d\lambda^{P}_{(a,x)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\\
\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left\|\int_{A_{d}\times Z}(f_{s}-\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid A_{d}\right])\otimes g_{s}~d\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(\pi_{d}(a),\pi_{1}(x))}\right\|_{L^{2}(m_{A_{d}})}\\
\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[f_{u}\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]\right\|_{L^{1}(\mu)}<\varepsilon.

∎

5.
Progressive measures

Motivated by the method in
[
37
]
, we define a notion of progressive measures for detecting Erdős–Furstenberg–Sárközy progressions.
For a compact metric space
Y
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}(Y)
the space of Borel probability measures on
Y
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
.
A probability measure
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{2})
is
P
-progressive from a point
a\in X
if
\textup{supp}\left(\tau\right)\subseteq\overline{\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)}
.

The goal of this section is to construct a progressive measure.
Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system with topological Abramov factors, and let
P
be a polynomial of degree
d
.
For a point
u\in A_{d}
, we define a measure
\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{u}
by

\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{u}=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{T_{d}^{q}u}\times\delta_{R^{P(q)+q}\pi^{d}_{1}(u)}.

This measure is well-defined by Corollary
4.3
.
We then define
\sigma^{P}_{x}\in\mathcal{M}(X^{2})
for
x\in X
by

\int_{X\times X}f\otimes g~d\sigma^{P}_{x}=\int_{A_{d}\times Z}\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid A_{d}\right]\otimes\mathbb{E}\left[g\mid Z\right]~d\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{\pi_{d}(x)}

for
f,g\in C(X)
.
Once again, we need to ensure that the marginals of
\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{\pi_{d}(x)}
are absolutely continuous so that
\sigma^{P}_{x}
is well-defined.
Noting that
\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{\pi_{d}(x)}=\widetilde{\lambda}^{Q}_{(\pi_{d}(x),\pi_{1}(x))}
for the polynomial
Q(x)=P(x)+x
, we see that
\sigma^{P}_{x}
is well-defined whenver
P(x)+x
is nonconstant.

The main result of this section is:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system with topological Abramov factors, and let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that both
P(x)
and
P(x)+x
are nonconstant.
If
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
for some Følner sequence
\Phi
, then
\sigma^{P}_{a}
is
P
-progressive from
a
.

For the remainder of this section, we fix an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,\mu,T)
with topological Abramov factors, a Følner sequence
\Phi
, a generic point
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, and a polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that
P(x)
and
P(x)+x
are nonconstant.

We have the following criterion for a measure to be progressive:

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{2})
and
a\in X
.
Suppose that for any open sets
U,V\subseteq X
with
\tau(U\times V)>0
, there are infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
T^{q}a\in U
and

\tau\left((U\times V)\cap(T^{-P(q)}V\times X)\right)>0.

Then
\tau
is
P
-progressive from
a
.

Proof.

A closely related result is proved in
[
37
, Proposition 3.2]
.
We adapt the argument to our setting of polynomial progressions.

In order to show that
\textup{supp}\left(\tau\right)\subseteq\overline{\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)}
, it suffices to prove that if
U,V\subseteq X
are open sets and
\tau(U\times V)>0
, then
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)\cap(U\times V)\neq\emptyset
.
Let
U,V\subseteq X
be open set with
\tau(U\times V)>0
.
We will construct sequences
(U_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(V_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of open subsets of
X
with
U_{0}=U
and
V_{0}=V
and a sequence
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of distinct elements of
\mathbb{Q}
such that the following properties hold for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

(1)

\overline{U}_{n}\subseteq U_{n-1}
and
\overline{V}_{n}\subseteq V_{n-1}
,

(2)

\textup{diam}\left(U_{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textup{diam}\left(U_{n-1}\right)
and
\textup{diam}\left(V_{n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textup{diam}\left(V_{n-1}\right)
,

(3)

\tau(U_{n}\times V_{n})>0
,

(4)

T^{s_{n}}a\in U_{n-1}
, and

(5)

U_{n}\subseteq T^{-P(s_{n})}V_{n-1}
.

First we handle
n=1
.
Since
\tau(U_{0}\times V_{0})=\tau(U\times V)>0
, we use the hypothesis for
\tau
to find
s_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
T^{s_{1}}a\in U_{0}
and

\tau\left((U_{0}\times V_{0})\cap(T^{-P(s_{1})}V_{0}\times X)\right)>0.

We can then use regularity of the measure
\tau
to find open sets
U_{1},V_{1}\subseteq X
such that
\overline{U}_{1}\subseteq U_{0}\cap T^{-P(s_{1})}V_{0}
and
\overline{V}_{1}\subseteq V_{0}
with
\textup{diam}\left(U_{1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textup{diam}\left(U_{0}\right)
,
\textup{diam}\left(V_{1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textup{diam}\left(V_{0}\right)
, and
\tau(U_{1}\times V_{1})>0
.

Suppose now that we have constructed
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
,
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
, and
s_{1},\ldots s_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By the induction hypothesis,
\tau(U_{n}\times V_{n})>0
, so by assumption, there exists
s_{n+1}\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
such that
T^{s_{n+1}}a\in U_{n}
and

\tau\left((U_{n}\times V_{n})\cap(T^{-P(s_{n+1})}V_{n}\times X)\right)>0.

As in the base case, we may therefore find open sets
U_{n+1},V_{n+1}\subseteq X
such that
\overline{U}_{n+1}\subseteq U_{n}\cap T^{-P(s_{n+1})}V_{n}
and
\overline{V}_{n+1}\subseteq V_{n}
with
\textup{diam}\left(U_{n+1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textup{diam}\left(U_{n}\right)
,
\textup{diam}\left(V_{n+1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textup{diam}\left(V_{n}\right)
, and
\tau(U_{n+1}\times V_{n+1})>0
.

By (1) and (2), the sets
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}U_{n}
and
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}V_{n}
are singletons.
Let
x_{1}
be the unique element of
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}U_{n}
and
x_{2}
the unique element of
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}V_{n}
.
Then by (4),
T^{s_{n}}a\to x_{1}
as
n\to\infty
.
Moreover, by (5),

T^{P(s_{n})}x_{1}\in V_{n-1}

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so
T^{P(s_{n})}x_{1}\to x_{2}
as
n\to\infty
.
That is,
(a,x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}
.
Since
x_{1}\in U_{0}=U
and
x_{2}\in V_{0}=V
, this proves
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(a)\cap(U\times V)\neq\emptyset
as desired.
∎

The following variant of Proposition
5.3
will be more directly applicable with the methods we employ.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{M}(X^{2})
and
a\in X
.
Let
\Phi
be a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Suppose the following property holds: for all
f,g\in C(X)
, if
f,g\geq 0
and
\int_{X^{2}}f\otimes g~d\tau>0
, then

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\int_{X^{2}}(f\cdot T^{P(q)}g)\otimes g~d\tau>0.

Then
\tau
is
P
-progressive from
a
.

Proof.

We reduce to Proposition
5.3
.
Let
U,V\subseteq X
be open sets with
\tau(U\times V)>0
.
Then there exist continuous functions
f,g:X\to[0,1]
such that
\textup{supp}\left(f\right)\subseteq U
,
\textup{supp}\left(g\right)\subseteq V
, and
\int_{X^{2}}f\otimes g~d\tau>0
.
By assumption, we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}a)\int_{X^{2}}(f\cdot T^{P(q)}g)\otimes g~d\tau>0.

In particular,

(5.1)

f(T^{q}a)\int_{X^{2}}(f\cdot T^{P(q)}g)\otimes g~d\tau>0.

for infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Suppose
q
satisfies (
5.1
).
Then
f(T^{q}a)>0
, so
T^{q}a\in U
.
Moreover,

\tau\left((U\times V)\cap(T^{-P(q)}V\times X)\right)=\int_{X^{2}}(\mathbbm{1}_{U}\cdot T^{P(q)}\mathbbm{1}_{V})\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{V}~d\tau\geq\int_{X^{2}}(f\cdot T^{P(q)}g)\otimes g~d\tau>0.

Thus,
\tau
satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition
5.3
, so
\tau
is
P
-progressive from
a
.
∎

The main combinatorial input required to prove Theorem
5.2
is the following version of the Ajtai–Szemerédi theorem in compact abelian groups:

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
G
be a compact abelian group.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
with the following property: if
F:G^{2}\to[0,1]
is a measurable function such that
\int_{G^{2}}F~dm_{G^{2}}\geq\varepsilon
, then

\int_{G^{3}}F(x,y)F(x+t,y)F(x,y+t)~dx~dy~dt\geq\delta.

Proof.

Let us first prove a corresponding statement for sets: for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a positive constant
ASz(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
E\subseteq G^{2}
is a measurable subset of measure at least
\varepsilon
, then the set

C(E)=\{(x,y,t)\in G^{3}:\{(x,y),(x+t,y),(x,y+t)\}\subseteq E\}

has measure at least
ASz(\varepsilon)
.
(The notation
ASz
stands for “Ajtai–Szemerédi” and
C
stands for “corners.”)
This is essentially a statement of the Ajtai–Szemerédi theorem
[
4
]
and can be proved using standard regularity methods.
For a reference, the exact result claimed here follows from the much stronger popular difference variant in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Now we deduce the functional statement.
Let
F:G^{2}\to[0,1]
be a measurable function with
\int_{G^{2}}F~d\mu\geq\varepsilon
.
Let
E=\left\{(x,y):F(x,y)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right\}
.
Then
m_{G^{2}}(E)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
by Markov’s inequality:

m_{G^{2}}{(G^{2}\setminus E)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\int_{G^{2}}(1-F)~dm_{G^{2}}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}=1-\frac{\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\leq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{G^{3}}F(x,y)F(x+t,y)F(x,y+t)~dx~dy~dt

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{3}\int_{G^{3}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(x,y)\mathbbm{1}_{E}(x+t,y)\mathbbm{1}_{E}(x,y+t)~dx~dy~dt

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{3}\cdot m_{G^{3}}(C(E))

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{3}ASz\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right).

Thus, we can take
\delta=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)^{3}ASz\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)
.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that the following holds: if
f:A_{d}\to[0,1]
and
g:Z\to[0,1]
are measurable functions,
b\in A_{d}
, and

\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f\otimes g~d\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{b}\geq\varepsilon,

then

\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f(u)g(z)\left(\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f\otimes g~d\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(b,\pi^{d}_{1}(u))}\right)~d\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{b}(u,z)\geq\delta.

Proof.

We claim that it suffices to prove the proposition for continuous functions
f
and
g
.
Indeed, once we have the proposition for continuous functions, we may approximate measurable
f
and
g
by continuous functions in
L^{2}
to deduce the general case.

We assume from now on that
f
and
g
are continuous.
Expanding the definition of
\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{b}
, we have

\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f\otimes g~d\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{b}=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}f\left(T_{d}^{q}b\right)\cdot g\left(R^{P(q)+q}\pi^{d}_{1}(b)\right).

In order to compute this limit, we work with the more convenient extension provided by Theorem
4.1
.
Let
V
be a compact
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space and
\alpha\in V
such that
(A_{d},m_{A_{d}},T_{d})
is a topological factor of
(V^{d},m_{V}^{d},S)
, where

S^{q}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})=\left(v_{1}+q\alpha,v_{2}+qv_{1}+\binom{q}{2}\alpha,\ldots,v_{d}+qv_{d-1}+\ldots+\binom{q}{d-1}v_{1}+\binom{q}{d}\alpha\right).

Let
\rho:V^{d}\to A_{d}
be the factor map, and let
\bm{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in V^{d}
such that
\rho(\bm{v})=b
.
Expand
P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_{j}\binom{x}{j}
.
Then we can write the point
\left(S^{q}\bm{v},v_{1}+(P(q)+q)\alpha\right)
as

(\bm{v},v_{1}+c_{0}\alpha)+q\left(\alpha,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d-1},(c_{1}+1)\alpha\right)+\binom{q}{2}\left(0,\alpha,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d-2},c_{2}\alpha\right)+\ldots+\binom{q}{d}(0,\ldots,\alpha,c_{d}\alpha).

Let

H=\overline{\left\{q(\alpha,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d-1}):q\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}}\subseteq V^{d}.

We write points
\bm{x}\in H
as
\bm{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-1})
in order to match indices from
\bm{v}
.
In the product space
H^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we write coordinates with superscripts, reserving the usage of subscripts for the coordinates of points in
H\subseteq V^{d}
.
That is,
\bm{x}\in H^{k}
takes the form
\bm{x}=(\bm{x}^{1},\ldots,\bm{x}^{k})
with
\bm{x}^{j}=(x^{j}_{0},x^{j}_{1},\ldots,x^{j}_{d-1})\in H
.
By Proposition
4.2
, the sequence
\left(S^{q}\bm{v},v_{1}+(P(q)+q)\alpha\right)_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}
is well-distributed in the space

\left\{(\bm{v},v_{1}+c_{0}\alpha)+\left(\bm{x}^{1},(c_{1}+1)x^{1}_{0}\right)+\left(0,x^{2}_{0},\ldots,x^{2}_{d-2},c_{2}x^{2}_{0}\right)+\ldots+(0,\ldots,x^{d}_{0},c_{d}x^{d}_{0}):\bm{x}\in H^{d}\right\}.

Define
\varphi:H^{d}\to[0,1]
by

\varphi(\bm{x}^{1},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d})=(f\circ\rho)\left(\bm{v}+\bm{x}^{1}+(0,x^{2}_{0},\ldots,x^{2}_{d-2})+\ldots+(0,\ldots,x^{d}_{0})\right)

and
\psi:H^{d+1}\to[0,1]
by

\psi(\bm{x}^{1},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d},\bm{y})=(g\circ\rho^{\prime})\left(v_{1}+c_{0}\alpha+y^{1}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}x^{j}_{0}\right),

where
\rho^{\prime}
is the factor map
\rho^{\prime}:V\to Z
.
Let
\xi(\bm{x})=\varphi(\bm{x})\psi(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{1})
.
Then by construction,

\int_{H^{d}}\xi~dm_{H^{d}}=\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f\otimes g~d\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{b}\geq\varepsilon.

Expanding

I(f,g)=\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f(u)g(z)\left(\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f\otimes g~d\widetilde{\lambda}^{P}_{(b,\pi^{d}_{1}(u))}\right)~d\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{b}(u,z)

as an average

\textup{UC-}\lim_{(q,r)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}}f\left(T_{d}^{q}b\right)\cdot g\left(R^{P(q)+q}\pi^{d}_{1}(b)\right)\cdot f\left(T_{d}^{r}b\right)\cdot g\left(R^{P(r)+q}\pi^{d}_{1}(b)\right)

and repeating the previous calculations, we have

I(f,g)=\int_{H^{d}\times H^{d}}\varphi(\bm{x})\psi(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{1})\varphi(\bm{y})\psi(\bm{y},\bm{x}^{1})~d\bm{x}~d\bm{y}.

Making a change of variables
\bm{y}=(\bm{x}^{1}+(1+c_{1}^{-1})\bm{t}^{1},\bm{x}^{2}+\bm{t}^{2},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d}+\bm{t}^{d})
and using the definition of
\psi
, we have

\displaystyle I(f,g)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{d}\times H^{d}}\varphi(\bm{x})\psi(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{1})\varphi(\bm{x}^{1}+(1+c_{1}^{-1})\bm{t}^{1},\bm{x}^{2}+\bm{t}^{2},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d}+\bm{t}^{d})\psi(\bm{x}+\bm{t},\bm{x}^{1}+\bm{t}^{1})~d\bm{x}~d\bm{t}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{H^{d}\times H^{d}}\xi(\bm{x})\xi(\bm{x}+\bm{t})\xi(\bm{x}^{1}+(1+c_{1}^{-1})\bm{t}^{1},\bm{x}^{2}+\bm{t}^{2},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d}+\bm{t}^{d})~d\bm{x}~d\bm{t}.

Now letting
p:H^{d}\times H^{d}\to H^{d}
be the map

p(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\left(\bm{x}^{1}+(1+c_{1}^{-1})\bm{y}^{1},\bm{x}^{2}+\bm{y}^{2},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d}+\bm{y}^{d}\right)

and defining
F:H^{d}\times H^{d}\to[0,1]
by
F=\xi\circ p
, we have

I(f,g)\geq\int_{H^{d}\times H^{d}\times H^{d}}F(\bm{x},\bm{y})F(\bm{x}+\bm{t},\bm{y})F(\bm{x},\bm{y}+\bm{t})~d\bm{x}~d\bm{y}~d\bm{t}

and

\int_{H^{d}\times H^{d}}F~dm_{H^{d}}^{2}=\int_{H^{d}}\xi~dm_{H^{d}}\geq\varepsilon.

Applying Theorem
5.5
for the group
G=H^{d}
, we are done.
∎

Proof of Theorem

\Phi

A related result was shown in

The only thing we need to check in order to apply the same argument to obtain topological Abramov factors is that the Abramov factors are topologically distal

6

6

\mathbb{Q}
-system

if for every pair of distinct points

For the proof of Theorem
2.5
, we will use a variant of the Furstenberg–Sárközy theorem over
\mathbb{Q}
:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
Then for any nonconstant polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
and any
f\in L^{2}(\mu)

\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}T^{P(q)}f=\int_{X}f~d\mu,

where the limit is taken in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

Proof.

This result can be proved using spectral methods and the Weyl-type equidistribution theorem for polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}
provided by
[
12
, Theorem 5.2]
.
We give a different proof here based on a general polynomial ergodic theorem for field actions.

Writing
\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}T^{P(q)}f=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}T^{P(q)-P(0)}(T^{P(0)}f)
and noting that
\int_{X}T^{P(0)}f~d\mu=\int_{X}f~d\mu
, we may assume without loss of generality that
P(0)=0
.
Then the polynomial ergodic theorem for actions of fields due to Larick
[
38
, Theorem 1.1.1]
shows that
\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}T^{P(q)}f
is equal to the orthogonal projection of
f
onto the space of
P
-invariant functions
I_{P}=\{g\in L^{2}(\mu):T^{P(q)}g=g~\text{for every}~q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
.
We want to show that
I_{P}
is the space of constant functions.

For a given
g\in L^{2}(\mu)
, the set of
r\in\mathbb{Q}
for which
T^{r}g=g
defines a group.
Hence, if
g\in I_{P}
, then
T^{r}g=g
for every
r
belonging to the subgroup
G_{P}=\left\langle P(q):q\in\mathbb{Q}\right\rangle\leq(\mathbb{Q},+)
generated by the values of the polynomial
P
.
Since
T
is ergodic by assumption, it suffices to show that
G_{P}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Suppose
d=\deg{P}
, and write
P(x)=a_{d}x^{d}+\ldots+a_{1}x
with
a_{d}\neq 0
.
Let
P_{0}=P
, and define
P_{j}(x)=P_{j-1}(x+1)-P_{j-1}(x)-P_{j-1}(1)
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
By construction, we have
G_{P_{j}}\subseteq G_{P}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
One can check that
\deg{P_{j}}=d-j
for
j\leq d
, and in particular,
P_{d-1}(x)=d!a_{d}x
.
Therefore, for each
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have
q=P_{d-1}\left(\frac{q}{d!a_{d}}\right)\in G_{P}
, so
G_{P}=\mathbb{Q}
as desired.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
2.5
.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Since
a\in\textup{gen}(\mu,\Phi)
, the measure
\mu
is supported on the orbit-closure
\mathcal{O}(a)=\overline{\{T^{q}a:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}}
.
Hence, replacing
X
by
\mathcal{O}(a)
if needed, we may assume that
a
is a transitive point.
Then after passing to an extension if necessary, we may assume by Lemma
6.1
that
(X,\mu,T)
has topological Abramov factors.

For each
t\in\mathbb{Q}
, the measure
\sigma^{P}_{T^{t}a}
is
P
-progressive from
T^{t}a
by Theorem
5.2
.
Our goal is thus to show
\sigma^{P}_{T^{t}a}(E\times E)>0
for some
t\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Averaging over
t
and applying the Fubini property of uniform Cesàro limits (see
[
11
, Lemma 1.1]
), we have

\displaystyle\textup{UC-}\lim_{t\in\mathbb{Q}}\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{T^{t}a}

\displaystyle=\textup{UC-}\lim_{t\in\mathbb{Q}}\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{T_{d}^{q+t}\pi_{2}(a)}\times\delta_{R^{P(q)+q+t}\pi_{1}(a)}

\displaystyle=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\textup{UC-}\lim_{t\in\mathbb{Q}}\delta_{T_{d}^{q+t}\pi_{2}(a)}\times\delta_{R^{P(q)+q+t}\pi_{1}(a)}

\displaystyle=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}(T_{d}^{q}\times R^{P(q)+q})_{*}m_{\Delta},

where
m_{\Delta}
is the Haar measure on the “diagonal”

\Delta=\{(u,z)\in A_{d}\times Z:\pi^{d}_{1}(u)=z\}.

The set
\Delta
is
(T_{d}\times R)
-invariant, so

\textup{UC-}\lim_{t\in\mathbb{Q}}\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{T^{t}a}=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}(\textup{id}_{A_{d}}\times R^{P(q)})_{*}m_{\Delta}.

Given continuous functions
f\in C(A_{d})
and
g\in C(Z)
, we apply Theorem
6.2
to compute

\displaystyle\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\int_{A_{d}\times Z}f\otimes R^{P(q)}g~dm_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}\int_{A_{d}}f\cdot T_{d}^{P(q)}(g\circ\pi^{d}_{1})~dm_{A_{d}}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{A_{d}}f~dm_{A_{d}}\right)\left(\int_{A_{d}}g\circ\pi^{d}_{1}~dm_{A_{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{A_{d}}f~dm_{A_{d}}\right)\left(\int_{Z}g~dm_{Z}\right).

Thus,

\textup{UC-}\lim_{t\in\mathbb{Q}}\widetilde{\sigma}^{P}_{T^{t}a}=m_{A_{d}}\times m_{Z}.

It follows that

\textup{UC-}\lim_{t\in\mathbb{Q}}\sigma^{P}_{T^{t}a}=\mu\times\mu.

Hence, by the portmanteau lemma (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
),

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{N}|}\sum_{t\in\Psi_{N}}\sigma^{P}_{T^{t}a}(E\times E)\geq\mu(E)^{2}

for every Følner sequence
\Psi
in
\mathbb{Q}
.
In particular, there exists
t\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\sigma^{P}_{T^{t}a}(E\times E)>0
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

If one is interested in finding the pattern
\{P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}
for an infinite set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
without worrying about the location of the set
B
, then the shift
t
in Theorem
2.5
is no longer needed.
This follows easily from the observation (using Theorem
6.2
) that the second marginal of
\sigma^{P}_{a}
is equal to
\mu
, so
\sigma^{P}_{a}(X\times E)=\mu(E)>0
.

However, for the full configuration
\{b_{i},P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}
, the shift
t
cannot be omitted, which we show via the following example.
Consider the space
X=(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{2}
with Haar measure
\mu=m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
and the skew-product action

T^{q}(x,y)=(x+q\alpha,y+2qx+q^{2}\alpha)

for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.
Then
(X,\mu,T)
is a uniquely ergodic system.
Let
E\subseteq X
be the set

E=U\times V,

where
U
is a small open neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
, and
V
is an open set away from 0 so that
(V+U)\cap U=\emptyset
.
We take
a=(0,0)\in X
, which is generic for
\mu
along every Følner sequence by unique ergodicity of
(X,T)
.
Put
A=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:T^{q}a\in E\}
.
We can compute
T^{q}a=(q\alpha,q^{2}\alpha)
, so
A=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:q\alpha\in U,q^{2}\alpha\in V\}
.
Thus, if
b_{1},b_{2}\in A
, then
(b_{1}^{2}+b_{2})\alpha=b_{1}^{2}\alpha+b_{2}\alpha\in V+U
.
Therefore,
b_{1}^{2}+b_{2}\notin A
.
It follows that
\mathbf{EFS}^{q^{2}}_{X}(a)\cap(E\times E)=\emptyset
.
Given a polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of degree
d
, it is possible to generate similar examples with skew-product actions on
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d}
.

7.1.
Reducing to averages in skew products

The measure
\sigma^{P}_{a}
is defined in terms of a polynomial average on the Abramov factor of order
d=\deg{P}
.
In order to prove Theorem
7.1
, it is therefore enough to establish the following variant in Abramov systems with the functions
f_{k}
equal to the projection of
\mathbbm{1}_{E_{k}}
to the Abramov factor.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
.
There exists a constant
c=c(d,r)>0
such that the following holds:
for any polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of degree
d
, any order
d
ergodic Abramov
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,\mu,T)
, any
a\in X
, and any measurable functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}:X\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{r}\geq 1
, one has

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{X\times X}f_{k}\otimes f_{k}~d\sigma^{P}_{a}\geq c(d,r).

Imposing a lower bound
c(d,r)
depending only on the degree
d
of
P
and the number of functions
r
appears to make the problem more challenging.
However, it affords us significantly more flexibility to make additional simplifications.
First, we may assume that the functions
f_{k}
are continuous using a standard approximation argument.
(Note that this would not be possible if we were only establishing positivity, but the uniformity of the lower bound
c(d,r)
ensures that there is a main term to dominate the error terms.)
Next, by lifting the functions
f_{k}
to a more convenient extension, we may assume that
(X,\mu,T)
is of the form described by Theorem
4.1
.
That is,
X=V^{d}
for some compact
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
V
, and
T
is an affine skew-product action.
The way the vector space
V
is constructed is as the dual of a countable vector subspace of
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
Approximating the countable subspace by finite dimensional spaces gives
V
as an inverse limit of vector spaces of the form
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
with
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
Finally, approximating the functions
f_{k}
by functions defined on the spaces making up this inverse limit, we may assume that
V=(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
for some
l\in\mathbb{N}
.
It therefore suffices to prove the special case of Theorem
7.2
stated in Theorem
7.3
below.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
.
There exists a constant
c=c(d,r)>0
such that the following holds:
for any polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of degree
d
, any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, any
\alpha\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
with
\overline{\{q\alpha:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}}=(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
, any
v\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{ld}
, and any continuous functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}:(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{ld}\to[0,1]
with
\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{r}\geq 1
, one has

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}f_{k}\left(\left(v_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{q}{j-i}v_{i}+\binom{q}{j}\alpha\right)_{j=1}^{d}\right)\widetilde{f}_{k}\left(v_{1}+(P(q)+q)\alpha\right)\geq c(d,r),

where
\widetilde{f}_{k}:(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}\to[0,1]
is defined by
\widetilde{f}_{k}(v)=\int_{(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l(d-1)}}f_{k}(v,w)~dw
.

7.2.
Linearizing the average

Let
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
.
Fix a degree
d
polynomial
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
, a number
l\in\mathbb{N}
, an element
\alpha\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
satisfying
\overline{\{q\alpha:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}}=(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
, and an element
v\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{ld}
.
Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}:(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{ld}\to[0,1]
with
\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}\geq 1
.
By subtracting appropriately in places where the sum exceeds 1, we may assume
\sum_{k=1}^{r}f_{k}=1
everywhere.
We define a quantity

M=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\textup{UC-}\lim_{q\in\mathbb{Q}}f_{k}\left(\left(v_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{q}{j-i}v_{i}+\binom{q}{j}\alpha\right)_{j=1}^{d}\right)\widetilde{f}_{k}\left(v_{1}+(P(q)+q)\alpha\right).

Our goal is to show
M\geq c(d,r)
.

Grouping terms by degree and rearranging, we can write

\left(v_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{q}{j-i}v_{i}+\binom{q}{j}\alpha\right)_{j=1}^{d}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})+q(\alpha,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d-1})+\ldots+\binom{q}{d}(0,\ldots,0,\alpha).

For notational convenience, let
v_{0}=\alpha
.
Expand
v_{i}=(v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,l})
in coordinates for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
.
Put
F_{0}=\{1,\ldots,l\}
.
The assumption
\overline{\{q\alpha:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}}=(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l}
means that
v_{0,1},\ldots,v_{0,l}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.
By an inductive construction, we can choose sets
F_{i}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,l\}
such that for each
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
,

\{v_{i,j}:0\leq i\leq k,j\in F_{i}\}

is a basis for the subspace of
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})
spanned by
\{v_{i,j}:0\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq l\}
.
By construction, for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
, there is a linear map
L_{i}:(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}\times\ldots\times F_{i}}\to(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{\{1,\ldots,l\}\setminus F_{i}}
such that
(v_{i,j})_{j\notin F_{i}}=L_{i}\left((v_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{i^{\prime}\leq i,j^{\prime}\in F_{i^{\prime}}}\right)
.
We can thus rewrite

(\alpha,v_{1},\ldots,v_{d-1})=\left(v_{0},\left((v_{1,j})_{j\in F_{1}},L_{1}(v_{0},(v_{1,j})_{j\in F_{1}})\right),\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,\left((v_{d-1,j})_{j\in F_{d-1}},L_{d-1}(v_{0},(v_{1,j})_{j\in F_{1}},\ldots,(v_{d-1,j})_{j\in F_{d-1}})\right)\right).

Write
P(x)+x=\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_{j}\binom{x}{j}
.
Then applying Proposition
4.2
,

M=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}\times\ldots\times F_{d-1}}\times\ldots\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}}}g_{k}\left(x^{1}_{0},x^{2}_{0}+\left(x^{1}_{1},L_{1}(x^{1}_{0},x^{1}_{1})\right),\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,x^{d}_{0}+\left(x^{d-1}_{1},L_{1}(x^{d-1}_{0},x^{d-1}_{1})\right)+\ldots+\left(x^{1}_{d-1},L_{d-1}(x^{1}_{0},x^{1}_{1},\ldots,x^{1}_{d-1})\right)\right)\cdot\\
\cdot\int_{(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l(d-1)}}g_{k}\left(a_{0}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}x^{j}_{0},z\right)~dz~d\bm{x},

where
g_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=f_{k}(v_{1}+x_{1},\ldots,v_{d}+x_{d})
.
Performing a change of variables to replace
a_{0}\alpha+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}x^{j}_{0}
by
x^{d}_{0}
, we have

M=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}\times\ldots\times F_{d-1}}\times\ldots\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}}}g_{k}\left(x^{1}_{0},x^{2}_{0}+\left(x^{1}_{1},L_{1}(x^{1}_{0},x^{1}_{1})\right),\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{d}}x^{d}_{0}+\left(x^{d-1}_{1},L_{1}(x^{d-1}_{0},x^{d-1}_{1})\right)+\ldots+\left(x^{1}_{d-1},L_{d-1}(x^{1}_{0},x^{1}_{1},\ldots,x^{1}_{d-1})\right)-\frac{a_{0}}{a_{d}}\alpha-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{a_{j}}{a_{d}}x^{j}_{0}\right)\cdot\\
\cdot\int_{(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{l(d-1)}}g_{k}\left(x^{d}_{0},z\right)~dz~d\bm{x}.

Define functions
h_{k}:\left((\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}\times\ldots\times F_{d-1}}\right)^{d}\to[0,1]
by

h_{k}(\bm{x}^{1},\bm{x}^{2},\ldots,\bm{x}^{d})=g_{k}\left(x^{1}_{0},x^{2}_{0}+\left(x^{1}_{1},L_{1}(x^{1}_{0},x^{1}_{1})\right),\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{d}}x^{d}_{0}+\left(x^{d-1}_{1},L_{1}(x^{d-1}_{0},x^{d-1}_{1})\right)+\ldots+\left(x^{1}_{d-1},L_{d-1}(x^{1}_{0},x^{1}_{1},\ldots,x^{1}_{d-1})\right)-\frac{a_{0}}{a_{d}}\alpha-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\frac{a_{j}}{a_{d}}x^{j}_{0}\right).

Using translation invariance in the
z
-coordinate and writing
Y=(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{F_{0}\times\ldots\times F_{d-1}}
, the expression for

simplifies to

(7.1)

M=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{Y^{2d-1}}h_{k}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})h_{k}(y_{d},\ldots,y_{2d-1})~d\bm{y}.

7.3.
Applying Ramsey’s theorem

We will apply a form of Ramsey’s theorem for ordered hypergraphs to bound the right hand side of (
7.1
) from below (in terms of
r
and
d
).
Given a set
S
and a number
l\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\binom{S}{l}
the family of all
l
-element subsets of
S
.
An
l
-uniform hypergraph
is a pair
H=(V,E)
, where
V
is a finite set of
vertices
and
E\subseteq\binom{V}{l}
is the set of
edges
.
If the vertex set is totally ordered (we will take
V=\{1,\ldots,m\}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
), we say that
H
is an
ordered
l
-uniform hypergraph
.
The
complete ordered
l
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices
, which we denote by
K^{(l)}_{n}
, is the hypergraph with vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and edge set
\binom{\{1,\ldots,n\}}{l}
.

Suppose
H
is an ordered
l
-uniform hypergraph on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
G
is an ordered
l
-uniform hypergraph on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
for some
m<n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that
G

contains an ordered copy
of
H
if there is a strictly increasing map
f:\{1,\ldots,m\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that every edge
\{i,j\}
in
H
maps to an edge
\{f(i),f(j)\}
in
G
.
The
r
-color
ordered Ramsey number
of an ordered
l
-uniform hypergraph
H
, denoted by
R_{<}(H,r)
, is the smallest number
n
such that every
r
-coloring of the edges of
K^{(l)}_{n}
contains a monochromatic ordered copy of
H
.
It is an easy exercise to check that the ordered Ramsey number of a complete ordered hypergraph coincides with the usual Ramsey number of the unordered version of the complete hypergraph.
In particular,
R_{<}(H,r)
exists and is finite by Ramsey’s theorem.

Ordered Ramsey numbers were formally introduced only in the last decade (see
[
18
,
5
]
), but several early results in Ramsey theory such as the Erdős–Szekeres theorem on monotone paths and the “happy ending problem,” also of Erdős and Szekeres, have natural formulations in the language of ordered Ramsey numbers of graphs or hypergraphs.
We refer the reader to the introduction in
[
18
]
and
[
5
, Section 1.2]
for more discussion.

The following measure-theoretic version of Ramsey’s theorem for ordered hypergraphs (applied to the hypergraph on
2d-1
vertices with edges
\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
\{d,\ldots,2d-1\}
) gives a lower bound on
M
via (
7.1
) and thus establishes Theorem
7.3
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
H
be an ordered
l
-uniform hypergraph on vertex set
\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and let
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
There exists a constant
c=c(H,r)>0
satisfying the following:
for any probability space
(X,\mu)
and any measurable functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{r}:X^{l}\to[0,1]
satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{r}\phi_{k}=1
, one has

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{X^{m}}\prod_{e\in E(H)}\phi_{k}((x_{j})_{j\in e})~d\mu^{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\geq c.

Proof.

The idea of the proof of Theorem
7.4
is to view the functions
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{r}
as a model for a random
r
-coloring of the edges of a complete
l
-uniform hypergraph in such a way that

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{X^{m}}\prod_{e\in E(H)}\phi_{k}((x_{j})_{j\in e})~d\mu^{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

computes the expected proportion of ordered copies of
H
that are monochromatic.

Let
n=R_{<}(H,r)
.
Define a random
r
-coloring of the edges of
K^{(l)}_{n}
as follows.
First, we let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in X
be i.i.d. random variables sampled according to the probability measure
\mu
.
Then, for each
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{l}\leq n
, we assign the color of the edge
c(\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}\})\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
as the random variable taking value
k
with probability
\phi_{k}(v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{l}})
, with the color assignment done independently for the different subsets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}\}
.

For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, let

M_{k}=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}):1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq n,\forall e\in E(H),c(\{i_{j}:j\in e\})=k\}

be the (random) set of monochromatic ordered copies of
H
of color
k
, and let
M=\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}M_{k}
.
By the choice of
n
via Ramsey’s theorem,

|M|\geq 1.

On the other hand, we can compute the expected number of monochromatic ordered copies of
H
by

\mathbb{E}[|M|]=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[|M_{k}|]=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq n}\mathbb{P}[\forall e\in E(H),c(\{i_{j}:j\in e\})=k]\\
=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq n}\int_{X^{m}}\prod_{e\in E(H)}\mathbb{P}[c(\{i_{j}:j\in e\})=k\mid(v_{i_{1}},\ldots,v_{i_{m}})=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})]~d\mu^{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\\
=\binom{n}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{X^{m}}\prod_{e\in E(H)}\phi_{k}((x_{j})_{j\in e})~d\mu^{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

Thus,

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\int_{X^{m}}\prod_{e\in E(H)}\phi_{k}((x_{j})_{j\in e})~d\mu^{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\geq\binom{R_{<}(H,r)}{m}^{-1}.

∎

8.
A polynomial Wiener–Wintner theorem for
\mathbb{Q}
-actions

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
3.4
, restated below for convenience.

See
3.4

Our method of proof is based on a proof of a corresponding result for totally ergodic
\mathbb{Z}
-systems due to Lesigne
[
39
]
.

8.1.
Spectral disjointness

Given an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, we denote by
\sigma(T)
the group of eigenvalues of
T
:

\sigma(T)=\left\{t\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}:\exists g\in L^{2}(\mu),g\neq 0~\text{such that}~\forall q\in\mathbb{Q},T^{q}g=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(qt)g\right\}

We then let
\sigma^{\prime}(T)=\{qt:q\in\mathbb{Q},t\in\sigma(T)\}
be the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space spanned by
\sigma(T)
.
The sets
\sigma(T)
and
\sigma^{\prime}(T)
are countable, since
L^{2}(\mu)
is a separable Hilbert space.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
Let
\Phi
be a tempered Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
.
Then there exists a set
X_{0}\subseteq X
with
\mu(X_{0})=1
satisfying the following property: if
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha\notin\sigma^{\prime}(T)
, and
Q(x)\in\mathbb{A}[x]
with
\deg{Q}<d
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d}\alpha+Q(q)\right)=0.

for every
x\in X_{0}
.

Remark 8.2
.

Taking
f=1
, Lemma
8.1
says that polynomial sequences are equidistributed in
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
This was previously observed (along with a multidimensional generalization) in
[
12
, Theorem 5.2]
.

Proof.

Note that

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{P(x)\in\mathbb{A}[x]}\left|\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(P(q))\right|\leq\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}|f(T^{q}x)|.

Therefore, by the maximal ergodic theorem (see
[
40
, Theorem 3.2]
), it suffices to prove the lemma under the assumption
f\in C(X)
.

Let
X_{0}
be the set of generic points for
\mu
along
\Phi
.
That is,
x\in X_{0}
if and only if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}g(T^{q}x)=\int_{X}g~d\mu

for every
g\in C(X)
.
By Lindenstrauss’s pointwise ergodic theorem (see
[
40
, Theorem 1.2]
),
\mu(X_{0})=1
.
We will prove that this set
X_{0}
has the desired property.

We induct on
d
.
Suppose
d=1
.
We want to show: if
\alpha\notin\sigma^{\prime}(T)
and
x\in X_{0}
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)=0.

Let
(\Phi_{N_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary subsequence such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{k}}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N_{k}}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)

exists.
Passing to a further subsequence if necessary, we may assume that the sequence

\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{k}}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N_{k}}}\delta_{T^{q}x}\times\delta_{q\alpha}

converges in the weak* topology to a probability measure
\lambda
on
X\times\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
The measure
\lambda
is a joining of
(X,\mu,T)
and
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q},m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}},R_{\alpha})
, where
R_{\alpha}^{q}z=z+q\alpha
for
z\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The system
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q},m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}},R_{\alpha})
is a system with discrete spectrum, and
\sigma(R_{\alpha})=\{q\alpha:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is disjoint from
\sigma(T)
.
Therefore, the systems
(X,\mu,T)
and
(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q},m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}},R_{\alpha})
are spectrally disjoint, hence disjoint in the sense of Furstenberg (this implication was shown in
[
26
, Theorem 2.1]
for
\mathbb{Z}
-systems and generalizes to actions of abelian groups; see, e.g.,
[
33
, p. 114]
).
Thus,
\lambda=\mu\times m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}
, so

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N_{k}}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N_{k}}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)=\int_{X\times\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}f\otimes e_{\mathbb{Q}}~d\lambda=\left(\int_{X}f~d\mu\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}e_{\mathbb{Q}}~dm_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}\right)=0.

Suppose now that the lemma holds for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
with
\alpha\notin\sigma^{\prime}(T)
, and suppose
Q(x)\in\mathbb{A}[x]
is a polynomial of degree at most
d
.
Let
u(q)=f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q^{d+1}\alpha+Q(q))\in\mathbb{C}
for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Fix
r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
, and note that

u(q+r)\overline{u(q)}=(\Delta_{r}f)(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d}r\alpha+Q_{r}(q)\right)

for some polynomial
Q_{r}
of degree
\deg{Q_{r}}\leq d-1
.
We have
r\alpha\notin\sigma^{\prime}(T)
and
\Delta_{r}f\in C(X)
, so by the induction hypothesis,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q+r)\overline{u(q)}=0.

Hence, by Lemma
3.1
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q)=0

as desired.
∎

8.2.
Systems with divisible discrete spectrum and a skew-product construction

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
There exists an ergodic extension
(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},T^{\prime})
of
(X,\mu,T)
such that
\sigma(T^{\prime})=\sigma^{\prime}(T)
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
.
There is one subtlety to point out:
[
3
, Theorem 3.2]
is stated for abstract measure-preserving systems without any underlying topological structure.
However, the extension
(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},T^{\prime})
is constructed as a joining of
(X,\mu,T)
with an ergodic group rotation, so forgetting temporarily the measure
\mu^{\prime}
, we can construct
(X^{\prime},T^{\prime})
as a product of the topological system
(X,T)
with a rotation on a compact group, so
(X^{\prime},T^{\prime})
is a meaningful topological dynamical
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and
\mu^{\prime}
is a Borel measure on
X^{\prime}
.
Moreover, the canonical projection from the product space
X^{\prime}
onto
X
is a continuous factor map.
∎

Definition 8.4
.

We say that an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,\mu,T)
has
divisible discrete spectrum
if the discrete spectrum
\sigma(T)
is a divisible group.

Note that
(X,\mu,T)
has divisible discrete spectrum if and only if
\sigma(T)=\sigma^{\prime}(T)
.
Hence, Lemma
8.3
shows that every ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system has an ergodic extension with divisible discrete spectrum.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system with divisible discrete spectrum.
Suppose
\alpha\in\sigma(T)
.
Then there exists a measurable function
g:X\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
such that

T^{q}g=g+q\alpha

for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

The lemma states that there is a factor map
g:(X,\mu,T)\to(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q},m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}},R_{\alpha})
, where
R_{\alpha}^{q}
is the rotation by
q\alpha
for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
This can be seen as a consequence of the Halmos–von Neumann theorem together with the assumption that
\sigma(T)
is divisible.
We give a sketch below.

If
\alpha=0
, we can take
g
to be a constant function.
Suppose
\alpha\neq 0
.
By the Halmos–von Neumann theorem (see, e.g.,
[
31
, pp. 46–48]
), the Kronecker factor of
(X,\mu,T)
is isomorphic to the rotational system
(Z,m_{Z},R)
, where
Z
is the dual group of
\sigma(T)
and
R
is the
\mathbb{Q}
-action given by

(R^{q}z)(\beta)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\beta)z(\beta)

for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
,
z\in Z
and
\beta\in\sigma(T)
.
Since
\sigma(T)
is divisible, we have
H=\{q\alpha:q\in\mathbb{Q}\}\subseteq\sigma(T)
.
The map
\pi_{H}:z\mapsto z|_{H}
is a surjective group homomorphism from
Z
to
\widehat{H}
.
The rotational action
R
induces a rotation
S
on
\widehat{H}
given by

(S^{q}w)(r\alpha)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(qr\alpha)w(r\alpha)

for
q,r\in\mathbb{Q}
and
w\in\widehat{H}
.
Now, the map
q\mapsto q\alpha
is an isomorphism from
\mathbb{Q}
to
H
, which induces an isomorphism
\phi:\widehat{H}\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
determined by

e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\phi(w))=w(q\alpha)

for
w\in\widehat{H}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Let us now consider the map
\phi\circ\pi_{H}:Z\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
First, for any
z\in Z
, the element
(\phi\circ\pi_{H})(z)\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
is determined by the identity

(8.1)

e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(r\cdot(\phi\circ\pi_{H})(z)\right)=z(r\alpha)\qquad(\forall r\in\mathbb{Q}).

Next, for any
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
z\in Z
, we have
(\phi\circ\pi_{H})(R^{q}z)=\phi(S^{q}\pi_{H}(z))
, so for
r\in\mathbb{Q}
,

(8.2)

e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(r\cdot(\phi\circ\pi_{H})(R^{q}z)\right)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(r\cdot\phi(S^{q}\pi_{H}(z))\right)=(S^{q}\pi_{H}(z))(r\alpha)=(R^{q}z)(r\alpha)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(qr\alpha)z(r\alpha).

Combining (
8.1
) and (
8.2
), we have

(\phi\circ\pi_{H})(R^{q}z)=(\phi\circ\pi_{H})(z)+q\alpha

for
z\in Z
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
We can then take
g
to be the composition of
\phi\circ\pi_{H}
with the factor map from
(X,\mu,T)
to the Kronecker factor
(Z,m_{Z},R)
.
∎

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
Suppose
g:X\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
satisfies
T^{q}g=g+q\alpha
for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Then there exists
c\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
such that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the action

S^{q}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=\left(T^{q}x,y_{1}+2q(g(x)+c)+q^{2}\alpha,y_{2}+3qy_{1}+3q^{2}(g(x)+c)+q^{3}\alpha,\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,y_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k+1}{j}q^{j}y_{k-j}+(k+1)q^{k}(g(x)+c)+q^{k+1}\alpha\right)

is ergodic on
\left(X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k},\mu\times m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{k}\right)
.

Proof.

A similar statement for
\mathbb{Z}
-actions appears in
[
39
, Lemma 3]
.
We follow essentially the same strategy of proof.

Suppose
f\in L^{2}(X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k})
is
S
-invariant.
We do a Fourier expansion in the adelic coordinates.
Given
\bm{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
, let
f_{\bm{r}}:X\to\mathbb{C}
be the function

f_{\bm{r}}(x)=\int_{(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}}f(x,\bm{y})e_{\mathbb{Q}}(-\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y})~d\bm{y},

where
\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}y_{j}\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
Then
f(x,\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}f_{\bm{r}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y})
.

Applying
S
, we have

\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}f_{\bm{r}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}f_{\bm{r}}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}(j+1)q^{j}r_{j}(g(x)+c)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}q^{j+1}r_{j}\alpha\right)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q}\cdot\bm{y}),

where

\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q,j}=\sum_{i=j}^{k}\binom{i+1}{j+1}q^{i-j}r_{i}.

Then matching Fourier coefficients,

f_{\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q}}(x)=f_{\bm{r}}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}(j+1)q^{j}r_{j}(g(x)+c)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}q^{j+1}r_{j}\alpha\right).

In particular,
\left\|f_{\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\left\|f_{\bm{r}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
.
By Parseval’s identity,

\sum_{\bm{r}}\left\|f_{\bm{r}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mu\times m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{k}\right)}^{2}<\infty,

so
\left\|f_{\bm{r}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}\to 0
as
|\bm{r}|\to\infty
.
It follows that
f_{\bm{r}}=0
unless
r_{2}=\ldots=r_{k}=0
.

Put
f_{r}=f_{(r,\bm{0})}
for
r\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Then
f(x,\bm{y})=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Q}}f_{r}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(ry_{1})
and using
S
-invariance,

\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Q}}f_{r}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(ry_{1})=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Q}}f_{r}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(2qr(g(x)+c)+q^{2}r\alpha)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(ry_{1}).

Thus, if
f_{r}\neq 0
, we have

f_{r}(x)=f_{r}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(2qr(g(x)+c)+q^{2}r\alpha).

Our goal is thus to choose
c\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
such that the equation

(8.3)

\forall q\in\mathbb{Q},\Delta_{q}h=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(2qr(g(x)+c)+q^{2}r\alpha)

has no solutions with
h:X\to S^{1}
measurable and
r\neq 0
.
Fix
r\neq 0
.
We observe that if (
8.3
) has solutions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
corresponding to distinct values
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
, then

\left\langle h_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle T^{q}h_{1},T^{q}h_{2}\right\rangle=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(2qr(c_{1}-c_{2}))\left\langle h_{1},h_{2}\right\rangle

for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, from which we deduce that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are orthogonal.
Since
L^{2}(\mu)
is separable, it follows that for each
r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
, the set of values
C_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
for which (
8.3
) has solutions is countable.
Therefore,
\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}}C_{r}
is countable, and for any choice of
c\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}\setminus\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}}C_{r}
, the action
S
is ergodic.
∎

8.3.
Key induction step

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Suppose
g:X\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
satisfies
T^{q}g=g+q\alpha
for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Assume that

S^{q}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})=\left(T^{q}x,y_{1}+2qg(x)+q^{2}\alpha,y_{2}+3qy_{1}+3q^{2}g(x)+q^{3}\alpha,\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,y_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{k+1}{j}q^{j}y_{k-j}+(k+1)q^{k}g(x)+q^{k+1}\alpha\right)

is ergodic on
\left(X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k},\mu\times m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{k}\right)
.
If
h\in\mathcal{E}_{j}(S)
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, then there exist
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j})\in\mathbb{Q}^{j}
and
h_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{j+1}(T)
such that
h(x,\bm{y})=h_{0}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}t_{i}y_{i}\right)
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
j
, adapting the argument from
[
39
, pp. 782–783]
.
Suppose
h\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)
, say with
T^{q}h=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\beta)h
.
Expanding
h(x,\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}h_{\bm{r}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y})
as a Fourier series in the
\bm{y}
coordinates as in the proof of Lemma
8.6
, the Fourier coefficients satisfy

h_{\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\beta)=h_{\bm{r}}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}(j+1)q^{j}r_{j}g(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{k}q^{j+1}r_{j}\alpha\right)

for

\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q,j}=\sum_{i=j}^{k}\binom{i+1}{j+1}q^{i-j}r_{i}.

In particular,
\left\|h_{\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\left\|h_{\bm{r}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
, so arguing as in Lemma
8.6
,
h_{\bm{r}}=0
whenever
(r_{2},\ldots,r_{k})\neq\bm{0}
.

Now let
h_{r}=h_{(r,\bm{0})}
for
r\in\mathbb{Q}
.
If
r,s\in\mathbb{Q}
and
h_{r},h_{s}\neq 0
, then

(8.4)

\frac{h_{r}}{h_{s}}=T^{q}\left(\frac{h_{r}}{h_{s}}\right)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(2q(r-s)g+q^{2}(r-s)\alpha\right).

Let
\psi(x,\bm{y})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}((r-s)y_{1})
.
Then

\psi\left(S^{q}(x,\bm{y})\right)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(2q(r-s)g(x)+q^{2}(r-s)\alpha\right)\psi(x,\bm{y}).

Hence, the function
\left(\frac{h_{r}}{h_{s}}\circ\pi_{X}\right)\cdot\psi
is
S
-invariant.
By ergodicity of
S
, it follows that
r=s
.
Thus, there is a unique
t_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
h_{t_{1}}\neq 0
, whence
h(x,\bm{y})=h_{t_{1}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1}y_{1})
as desired.

Suppose now that
j\in\{2,\ldots,k-1\}
and the lemma holds for
j-1
.
Let
h\in\mathcal{E}_{j}(S)
.
By definition, this means that for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, the multiplicative derivative
\Delta_{q}h
belongs to
\mathcal{E}_{j-1}(S)
, so by the induction hypothesis, there exist functions
\psi_{q}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}(T)
and elements
(t_{q,1},\ldots,t_{q,j-1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{j-1}
such that

(8.5)

h\left(S^{q}(x,\bm{y})\right)=\psi_{q}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(t_{q,1}y_{1}+\ldots+t_{q,j-1}y_{j-1}\right)h(x,\bm{y}).

We once again express
h
as a Fourier series
h(x,\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}h_{\bm{r}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y})
.
The identity (
8.5
) then yields

(8.6)

\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}h_{\bm{r}}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}(l+1)q^{l}r_{l}g(x)+\sum_{l=1}^{k}q^{l+1}r_{l}\alpha\right)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q}\cdot\bm{y})\\
=\psi_{q}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(t_{q,1}y_{1}+\ldots+t_{q,l-1}y_{l-1}\right)\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}h_{\bm{r}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y}),

where

\bm{r}^{\prime}_{q,l}=\sum_{i=l}^{k}\binom{i+1}{l+1}q^{i-l}r_{i}.

Let
\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}=(r^{\prime}_{q,1}-t_{q,1},\ldots r^{\prime}_{q,j-1}-t_{q,j-1},r^{\prime}_{q,j},\ldots r^{\prime}_{q,k})
.
Then multiplying both sides of (
8.6
) by
e_{\mathbb{Q}}(-t_{q,1}y_{1}-\ldots-t_{q,j-1}y_{j-1})
, we have

\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}h_{\bm{r}}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}(l+1)q^{l}r_{l}g(x)+\sum_{l=1}^{k}q^{l+1}r_{l}\alpha\right)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}\cdot\bm{y})\\
=\psi_{q}(x)\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}h_{\bm{r}}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(\bm{r}\cdot\bm{y}),

so

(8.7)

h_{\bm{r}}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}(l+1)q^{l}r_{l}g(x)+\sum_{l=1}^{k}q^{l+1}r_{l}\alpha\right)=\psi_{q}(x)h_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}}(x).

In particular,
\left\|h_{\bm{r}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\left\|h_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
, so as in Lemma
8.6
,
h_{\bm{r}}=0
unless
\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}=\bm{r}
for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The equation
\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}=\bm{r}
is equivalent to the system of equations

\sum_{i=l+1}^{k}\binom{i+1}{l+1}q^{i-l}r_{i}=\begin{cases}t_{q,l},&\text{if}~l\leq j-1\\
0,&\text{if}~l\geq j.\end{cases}

Therefore, if
\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}_{q}=\bm{r}
for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
,then
r_{j+1}=\ldots=r_{k}=0
, and then taking
q=1
, we have

(8.8)

t_{1,l}=\sum_{i=l+1}^{k}\binom{i+1}{l+1}r_{i}

for
l\leq j-1
, which uniquely determines the values of
r_{2},\ldots,r_{j}
as certain linear combinations of
t_{1,1},\ldots,t_{1,j-1}
.
Let
s_{2},\ldots,s_{j}
be the unique solution
(r_{2},\ldots,r_{j})=(s_{2},\ldots,s_{j})
to the system of equations (
8.8
).
For
r\in\mathbb{Q}
, let
h_{r}=h_{(r,\bm{s})}
.
Then (
8.7
) becomes

(8.9)

h_{r}(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(2qrg(x)+q^{2}r\alpha+\sum_{l=2}^{j}(l+1)q^{l}s_{l}g(x)+\sum_{l=2}^{j}q^{l+1}s_{l}\alpha\right)=\psi_{q}(x)h_{r}(x).

Let

\phi_{q}(x)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(g(x)\left(2qr+\sum_{l=2}^{j}(l+1)q^{l}s_{l}\right)\right).

Then for
t\in\mathbb{Q}
,

\phi_{q}(T^{t}x)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left((g(x)+t\alpha)\left(2qr+\sum_{l=2}^{j}(l+1)q^{l}s_{l}\right)\right)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(t\alpha\left(2qr+\sum_{l=2}^{j}(l+1)q^{l}s_{l}\right)\right)\phi_{q}(x),

so
\phi_{q}
is an eigenfunction of
T
with eigenvalue

\left(2qr+\sum_{l=2}^{j}(l+1)q^{l}s_{l}\right)\alpha.

Thus, (
8.9
) expresses
\Delta_{q}h_{r}
as a product of the form

(\text{constant})\cdot(\text{eigenfunction}~\phi_{q})\cdot(\text{phase polynomial}~\psi_{q}~\text{of degree at most}~j),

so
h_{r}\in\mathcal{E}_{j+1}(T)
.

If
r,s\in\mathbb{Q}
and
h_{r},h_{s}\neq 0
, then dividing (
8.9
) for
r
by (
8.9
) for
s
, we have

\left(\frac{h_{r}}{h_{s}}\right)(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(2q(r-s)g(x)+q^{2}(r-s)\alpha\right)=\left(\frac{h_{r}}{h_{s}}\right)(x).

This is the same equation (
8.4
) encountered in the
j=1
case above, so we again conclude
r=s
.
Hence, letting
r
be the unique value for which
h_{r}\neq 0
and taking
(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{j})=(r,s_{2},\ldots,s_{j})
, we have
h(x,\bm{y})=h_{r}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+t_{j}y_{j})
, which expresses
h
in the desired form.
∎

8.4.
Phases with spectral frequency

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system with divisible discrete spectrum, and let
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\Phi
be a tempered Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
, and suppose
\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]=0
.
For each
\alpha\in\sigma(T)
, there exists a set
X_{\alpha}\subseteq X
with
\mu(X_{\alpha})=1
satisfying the following property: if
Q(x)\in\mathbb{A}[x]
with
\deg{Q}<d
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d}\alpha+Q(q)\right)=0.

for every
x\in X_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

We induct on
d
.
Suppose
d=1
.
We want to show that if
\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{Z}\right]=0
, then there exists a full measure subset
X_{\alpha}\subseteq X
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)=0.

for every
x\in X_{\alpha}
.
We will take
X_{\alpha}
to be the set of points
x\in X
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}(\Delta_{r}f)(T^{q}x)=\int_{X}\Delta_{r}f~d\mu

for every
r\in\mathbb{Q}
.
By Lindenstrauss’s pointwise ergodic theorem (see
[
40
, Theorem 1.2]
), the set
X_{\alpha}
has
\mu(X_{\alpha})=1
.
Now fix
x\in X_{\alpha}
, and let
u(q)=f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)\in\mathbb{C}
for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Note that

z(r)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q+r)\overline{u(q)}=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(r\alpha)\cdot\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}(\Delta_{r}f)(T^{q}x)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(r\alpha)\int_{X}\Delta_{r}f~d\mu.

Therefore, for any Følner sequence
\Psi
,

\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{M}|}\sum_{r\in\Psi_{M}}|z(r)|=\lim_{M\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Psi_{M}|}\sum_{r\in\Psi_{M}}\left|\left\langle T^{r}f,f\right\rangle\right|=0

by Proposition
3.2
(vii).
Applying Lemma
3.1
, we conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q\alpha)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}u(q)=0.

Suppose
d\geq 2
and the proposition holds up to degree
d-1
.
Fix
\alpha\in\sigma(T)
.
By Lemma
8.5
, let
g:X\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
be measurable with
T^{q}g=g+q\alpha
.
Up to modification by a constant, we may assume that the skew-product action

S^{q}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{d-1})=\left(T^{q}x,y_{1}+2qg(x)+q^{2}\alpha,y_{2}+3qy_{1}+3q^{2}g(x)+q^{3}\alpha,\ldots\right.\\
\left.\ldots,y_{d-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}\binom{k+1}{j}q^{j}y_{d-1-j}+dq^{d-1}g(x)+q^{d}\alpha\right)

is ergodic on
\left(X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d-1},\mu\times m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{d-1}\right)
by Lemma
8.6
.

Define a function
\widetilde{f}:X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d-1}\to S^{1}
by
\widetilde{f}(x,\bm{y})=f(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(y_{d-1})
.
We claim the
\widetilde{f}
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{E}_{d-1}(S)
.
Indeed, given any function
h\in\mathcal{E}_{d-1}(S)
, we may write
h(x,\bm{y})=h_{0}(x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1}y_{1}+\ldots+t_{d-1}y_{d-1})
for some
h_{0}\in\mathcal{E}_{d}(T)
and
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d-1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{d-1}
by Lemma
8.7
, so

\left\langle\widetilde{f},h\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mu\times m_{\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}}^{d-1}\right)}=\left\langle f,h_{0}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{t_{1}=\ldots=t_{d-2}=0,t_{d-1}=1\}=0,

since
f
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{E}_{d}(T)
.
Thus, by the induction hypothesis, there exists, for each
\beta\in\sigma(S)
, a full measure subset
Y_{\beta}\subseteq X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d-1}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\widetilde{f}(S^{q}(x,\bm{y}))\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d-1}\beta+Q(q)\right)=0.

for every
(x,\bm{y})\in Y_{\beta}
and every polynomial
Q
of degree at most
d-2
.
Also, by Lemma
8.1
, there exists a set
Y^{\prime}\subseteq X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d-1}
of full measure such that if
\beta\notin\sigma(S)
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\widetilde{f}(S^{q}(x,\bm{y}))\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d-1}\beta+Q(q)\right)=0

for every
(x,\bm{y})\in Y^{\prime}
and every polynomial
Q
of degree at most
d-2
.
Let

Y=Y^{\prime}\cap\bigcap_{\beta\in\sigma(S)}Y_{\beta},

Then

(8.10)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\widetilde{f}(S^{q}(x,\bm{y}))\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(Q(q)\right)=0

for every
(x,\bm{y})\in Y
and every polynomial
Q
of degree at most
d-1
.

Note that

(8.11)

\begin{split}\widetilde{f}(S^{q}(x,\bm{y}))&=f(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(y_{d-1}+\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}\binom{k+1}{j}q^{j}y_{d-1-j}+dq^{d-1}g(x)+q^{d}\alpha\right)\\
&=f(T^{q}x)e_{\mathbb{Q}}(q^{d}\alpha+Q_{(x,\bm{y})}(q)),\end{split}

where
Q_{(x,\bm{y})}
is a polynomial of degree at most
d-1
depending on
(x,\bm{y})\in X\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{d-1}
.

By Fubini’s theorem, there exists a set
X_{\alpha}\subseteq X
with
\mu(X_{\alpha})=1
such that
(\{x\}\times\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})\cap Y\neq\emptyset
for each
x\in X_{\alpha}
.
Suppose
x\in X_{\alpha}
, and let
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d-1}
such that
(x,\bm{y})\in Y
.
Let
Q
be an arbitrary polynomial of degree at most
d-1
.
Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d}\alpha+Q(q)\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}\widetilde{f}(S^{q}(x,\bm{y}))\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\underbrace{Q(q)-Q_{(x,\bm{y})}(q)}_{\text{degree at most}~d-1}\right)=0

by (
8.11
) and (
8.10
).
∎

8.5.
Finishing the proof

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

We want to reduce to a system with divisible discrete spectrum in order to apply the tools we have developed.
Let
f\in L^{1}(\mu)
with
\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]=0
.
By Lemma
8.3
, we may find an extension
\pi:(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},T^{\prime})\to(X,\mu,T)
such that
\sigma(T^{\prime})=\sigma^{\prime}(T)
.
Let
f^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\to\mathbb{C}
be the function
f^{\prime}=f\circ\pi
.
Then
\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{d}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f\mid\mathcal{A}_{d}\right]=0
.
Define
Y_{0}
to be the set of
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f^{\prime}((T^{\prime})^{q}x^{\prime})\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(P(q)\right)=0

for every polynomial
P
of degree at most
d
.
Similarly, let
X_{0}
be the set of
x\in X
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f(T^{q}x)\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(P(q)\right)=0

for every polynomial
P
of degree at most
d
.
The sets
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
are measurable by construction, and since
f^{\prime}=f\circ\pi
, we have
Y_{0}=\pi^{-1}(X_{0})
.
Therefore,
\mu^{\prime}(Y_{0})=\mu(X_{0})
, so it suffices to show that
\mu^{\prime}(Y_{0})=1
.

By Lemma
8.1
, let
X^{\prime\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}
with
\mu^{\prime}(X^{\prime\prime})=1
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f^{\prime}((T^{\prime})^{q}x^{\prime})\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d}\alpha+Q(q)\right)=0

for every
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime\prime}
, every
\alpha\notin\sigma(T^{\prime})
, and every polynomial
Q
of degree at most
d-1
.
Then by Proposition
8.8
, for each
\alpha\in\sigma(T^{\prime})
, let
X^{\prime}_{\alpha}\subseteq X^{\prime}
with
\mu^{\prime}(X^{\prime}_{\alpha})=1
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f^{\prime}((T^{\prime})^{q}x^{\prime})\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q^{d}\alpha+Q(q)\right)=0

for
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}_{\alpha}
and every polynomial
Q
of degree at most
d-1
.
Taking an intersection, we have that if
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime\prime}\cap\bigcap_{\alpha\in\sigma(T^{\prime})}X^{\prime}_{\alpha}
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Phi_{N}|}\sum_{q\in\Phi_{N}}f^{\prime}((T^{\prime})^{q}x^{\prime})\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(P(q)\right)=0

for every polynomial
P
of degree at most
d
.
That is,
X^{\prime\prime}\cap\bigcap_{\alpha\in\sigma(T^{\prime})}X^{\prime}_{\alpha}\subseteq Y_{0}
.
Moreover, the discrete spectrum
\sigma(T^{\prime})
is countable, so
\mu^{\prime}(X^{\prime\prime}\cap\bigcap_{\alpha\in\sigma(T^{\prime})}X^{\prime}_{\alpha})=1
.
Thus,
\mu^{\prime}(Y_{0})=1
as desired.
∎

9.
Abramov
\mathbb{Q}
-systems

The goal of this section to prove Theorem
4.1
about the structure of Abramov systems.

9.1.
Skew-products

We begin by analyzing the skew-product systems of the form appearing in the statement of Theorem
4.1
.
Consider a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
V
, an element
\alpha\in V
generating a dense subspace, and let
S
be the
\mathbb{Q}
-action on
V^{k}
given by

S^{q}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=\left(v_{1}+q\alpha,v_{2}+qv_{1}+\binom{q}{2}\alpha,\ldots,v_{k}+qv_{k-1}+\ldots+\binom{q}{k-1}v_{1}+\binom{q}{k}\alpha\right).

Let
W=\widehat{V}
.
Then
W
is a countable discrete abelian group (in fact,
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space), and by the Stone–Weierstrass theorem,
L^{2}(m_{V})
has an orthonormal basis
(g_{\bm{w}})_{\bm{w}\in W^{k}}
given by
g_{\bm{w}}=w_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes w_{k}
.
We will write
W
with additive notation.

Let us compute the multiplicative derivatives of the functions
g_{\bm{w}}
,
\bm{w}\in W^{k}
.
For
\bm{v}\in V^{k}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have

\displaystyle g_{\bm{w}}(S^{q}\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}\left(v_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\binom{q}{j-i}v_{i}+\binom{q}{j}\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\left(qw_{1}+\ldots+\binom{q}{k}w_{k}\right)(\alpha)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{j=i}^{k}\binom{q}{j-i}w_{j}\right)(v_{i}).

Hence,

\Delta_{q}g_{\bm{w}}=\left(qw_{1}+\ldots+\binom{q}{k}w_{k}\right)(\alpha)\cdot g_{\bm{w}^{\prime}(q)},

where

(9.1)

w^{\prime}_{i}(q)=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}\binom{q}{j-i}w_{j}.

The upshot of this calculation is that there is a natural association between the functions
g_{\bm{w}}
and polynomial maps from
\mathbb{Q}
to
W
.
Namely, if we let
P_{\bm{w}}:\mathbb{Q}\to W
be the map

P_{\bm{w}}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{t}{j}w_{j},

then using the Vandermonde identity for binomial coefficients, we have

P_{\bm{w}}(t+q)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{j}\binom{t}{i}\binom{q}{j-i}w_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{t}{i}\sum_{j=i}^{k}\binom{q}{j-i}w_{j}.

Therefore,
P_{\bm{w}}(t+q)-P_{\bm{w}}(t)=P_{\bm{w}^{\prime}(q)}(t)+\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{q}{j}w_{j}
with
\bm{w}^{\prime}
as in (
9.1
).
Write
\partial_{q}
for the discrete differencing operator
\partial_{q}P(t)=P(t+q)-P(t)
.
Then the map
\psi
from the space of polynomial functions
\mathbb{Q}\to W
to
L^{2}(m_{V})
sending
P_{\bm{w}}+w_{0}
to
w_{0}(\alpha)g_{\bm{w}}
is a group homomorphism satisfying

\psi(\partial_{q}P)=\Delta_{q}\psi(P).

In order to realize an Abramov system as a factor of a system of the form above, we therefore want to find a space of functions with a similar encoding in terms of polynomial functions on
\mathbb{Q}
.
For technical reasons, it will be easier to work with polynomials in terms of their coefficients.
Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
we may associated to a tuple
(c,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
the polynomial
q\mapsto c\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}t_{j}\binom{q}{j}\right)
.
(We will show in Corollary
9.6
below that every polynomial function from
\mathbb{Q}
to
S^{1}
is uniquely representable in this form.)
The precise result we will prove is the following.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system.
There is a family of injective group homomorphisms
\phi_{k}:\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)\to S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
for
k\geq 0
such that:

(1)

\phi_{0}(c)=c
for every
c\in S^{1}
;

(2)

for every
j\leq k
and
f\in\mathcal{E}_{j}(T)
,

\phi_{k}(f)=(\phi_{j}(f),\bm{0});

and

(3)

for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
, if
\phi_{k}(f)=(c,t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
, then for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
,

\phi_{k-1}(\Delta_{q}f)=(e_{\mathbb{Q}}(t^{\prime}_{0}(q)),t^{\prime}_{1}(q),\ldots,t^{\prime}_{k-1}(q)),

where

t^{\prime}_{i}(q)=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}\binom{q}{j-i}t_{j}.

The main effort of this section will be devoted to proving Theorem
9.1
.
Let us first show that Theorem
4.1
follows from Theorem
9.1
.
We will need two small lemmas for this deduction.
The first lemma, which will be proved in Subsection
9.3
below, shows that phase polynomials form natural bases for the Abramov factors.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
f,g\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
, then either
f
and
g
are constant multiples of one another or
f
and
g
are orthogonal.

The second lemma allows us to reduce from the difficult task of finding a factor map to the easier task of constructing a map between Hilbert spaces.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
(X,\mu)
and
(Y,\nu)
be standard Borel probability spaces, and let
\Phi:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(\nu)
be a linear map.
Suppose
\Phi
satisfies:

(a)

for any
f,f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

\left\langle f,f^{\prime}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\left\langle\Phi(f),\Phi(f^{\prime})\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\nu)};

(b)

for any
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)\iff\Phi(f)\in L^{\infty}(\nu);

and

(c)

for any
f,f^{\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
,

\Phi(ff^{\prime})=\Phi(f)\cdot\Phi(f^{\prime}).

Then there exists a measure-preserving map
\widetilde{\Phi}:Y\to X
such that
\Phi(f)=f\circ\widetilde{\Phi}
.

Proof.

A map
\Phi
satisfying (a), (b), and (c) is always induced by a homomorphism of measure algebras (see
[
45
, Theorem 2.4]
).
The assumption that our probability spaces are standard Borel ensures that the measure algebra homomorphism is realized by a measure-preserving map (see
[
45
, Theorem 2.2]
).
∎

Proof that Theorem
9.1
\implies
Theorem
4.1
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an Abramov system of order
k
.
Let
\phi_{k}:\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)\to S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
be the map provided by Theorem
9.1
.
Note that
\phi_{k}(cf)=\phi_{k}(c)\phi_{k}(f)=(c,\bm{0})\phi_{k}(f)
, so the image of the map
\phi_{k}
is of the form
S^{1}\times H
for some subgroup
H\subseteq(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
.
By Lemma
9.2
, if we let
f_{\bm{t}}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
be the function with
\phi_{k}(f_{\bm{t}})=(1,\bm{t})
for
\bm{t}\in H
, then
(f_{\bm{t}})_{\bm{t}\in H}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
In particular,
H
is countable.
Let
W\subseteq\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
be the countable
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space generated by the coordinate projections of
H
.
That is,
W
is the smallest
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space such that
H\subseteq W^{k}
.

Let
V=\widehat{W}
, where we treat
W
as a discrete group.
We will write the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
V
with additive notation.
Let
\alpha\in V
be the element defined by
\alpha(w)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}(w)
for
w\in W
.
We then define an action
S
on
V^{k}
by

S^{q}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})=\left(v_{1}+q\alpha,v_{2}+qv_{1}+\binom{q}{2}\alpha,\ldots,v_{k}+qv_{k-1}+\ldots+\binom{q}{k-1}v_{1}+\binom{q}{k}\alpha\right).

For
\bm{w}\in W^{k}
, we let
g_{\bm{w}}
be the function
g_{\bm{w}}(\bm{v})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}v_{j}(w_{j})
.
As described at the beginning of this subsection, the functions
(g_{\bm{w}})_{\bm{w}\in W^{k}}
form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(m_{V}^{k})
and satisfy

(9.2)

\Delta_{q}g_{\bm{w}}=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{q}{j}w_{j}\right)\cdot g_{\bm{w}^{\prime}(q)}

with
\bm{w}^{\prime}
given by (
9.1
).

Define
\Phi:L^{2}(\mu)\to L^{2}(m_{V}^{k})
by

\Phi\left(\sum_{\bm{t}\in H}c(\bm{t})f_{\bm{t}}\right)=\sum_{\bm{t}\in H}c(\bm{t})g_{\bm{t}}

for
c\in\ell^{2}(H)
.
Since
(f_{\bm{t}})_{\bm{t}\in H}
and
(g_{\bm{t}})_{\bm{t}\in H}
are orthonormal systems in
L^{2}(\mu)
and
L^{2}(m_{V}^{k})
respectively,
\Phi
is a linear isometry.
Let us check that
\Phi
also satisfies conditions (b) and (c) from Lemma
9.3
.

Observe that for
\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}\in H
,
f_{\bm{t}}\cdot f_{\bm{t}^{\prime}}=f_{\bm{t}+\bm{t}^{\prime}}
and
g_{\bm{t}}\cdot g_{\bm{t}^{\prime}}=g_{\bm{t}+\bm{t}^{\prime}}
.
It follows that

\Phi(ff^{\prime})=\Phi(f)\cdot\Phi(f^{\prime})

when
f
and
f^{\prime}
are finite linear combinations of the basis functions
(f_{\bm{t}})_{\bm{t}\in H}
.
But finite linear combinations are dense in
L^{\infty}(\mu)
with respect to the
L^{2}
norm and
\Phi
is continuous, so (c) holds.

It remains to check (b).
This property is more subtle than the others, and we proceed in stages.
We will first prove the property: if
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
f\geq 0
, then
\Phi(f)\geq 0
.
To see this, note that every nonnegative function can be approximated by a nonnegative simple function, so by linearity of
\Phi
, it suffices to show that
\Phi(\mathbbm{1}_{E})\geq 0
for every measurable set
E\subseteq X
.
But using property (c), the function
g=\Phi(\mathbbm{1}_{E})
satisfies
g=g^{2}
, so
g
is almost everywhere
\{0,1\}
-valued.

As a consequence, if
f\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is real-valued, then by linearity of
\Phi
, we have

0\leq\Phi(\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}-f)=\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}-\Phi(f),

so
\left\|\Phi(f)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
.
The map
\Phi^{-1}:\Phi(L^{2}(\mu))\to L^{2}(\mu)
is a linear isometry satisfying the multiplication property (c), so the same argument applied to
\Phi^{-1}
shows that
\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\Phi(f)\right\|_{\infty}
.
Thus, (b) holds.

By Lemma
9.3
, there exists a measure-preserving map
\pi:Y\to X
such that
\Phi(f)=f\circ\pi
.
To show that
\pi
is a factor map, we need to check the additional property: for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
f\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

(9.3)

\Phi(T^{q}f)=S^{q}\Phi(f).

Applying property (3) from Theorem
9.1
,

T^{q}f_{\bm{t}}=f_{\bm{t}}\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{q}{j}t_{j}\right)\cdot f_{\bm{t}^{\prime}(q)},

so by property (c),

\Phi(T^{q}f_{\bm{t}})=g_{\bm{t}}\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{q}{j}t_{j}\right)\cdot g_{\bm{t}^{\prime}(q)}.

By (
9.2
), this is equal to
S^{q}g_{\bm{t}}=S^{q}\Phi(f_{\bm{t}})
.
Therefore, (
9.3
) holds for
f=f_{\bm{t}}
and hence for general
f
by applying linearity and continuity of
\Phi
.

To see that we have the topological statement claimed at the end of Theorem
4.1
, we make the following observation.
The space
\Phi(L^{2}(\mu))\subseteq L^{2}(m_{V}^{k})
is spanned by the functions
\{g_{\bm{t}}:\bm{t}\in H\}
.
If we let
\widetilde{X}=\widehat{H}
, then there is a continuous surjective group homomorphism
\pi:V\to\widetilde{X}
.
Using the map
\Phi
, it is not hard to check that
(X,\mu,T)
is measurably isomorphic to the system
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{\mu},\widetilde{T})
, where
\widetilde{\mu}=m_{\widetilde{X}}
is the Haar measure on
\widetilde{X}
and
\widetilde{T}
is the action induced by
S
.
∎

9.2.
Polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}

The goal for the remainder of the section is to prove Theorem
9.1
.
An important intermediate step is to classify polynomial functions from
\mathbb{Q}
to
S^{1}
using a similar labeling by tuples in
S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
.

We define a differencing operator on functions from
\mathbb{Q}
to
S^{1}
by
\partial_{q}\varphi:t\mapsto\varphi(t+q)\overline{\varphi(t)}
.
We then recursively define
\partial^{k+1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1}}
by
\partial^{k+1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1}}=\partial_{q_{k+1}}\circ\partial^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}
.
For
k\geq 0
, we say
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
is a
polynomial of degree at most
k
if
\partial^{k+1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1}}\varphi(t)=1
for every
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1},t\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}
denote the space of polynomials of degree at most
k
for each
k\geq 0
.

We want to produce a canonical form for polynomials over
\mathbb{Q}
.
We start by producing a canonical form for multilinear functions, which we will then lift to polynomials.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{M}_{k}
be the space of
\mathbb{Z}
-multilinear functions from
\mathbb{Q}^{k}
to
S^{1}
.
That is
\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
if and only if for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{j-1},q_{j+1},\ldots,q_{k})
, the map
q\mapsto\eta(q_{1},\ldots,q_{j-1},q,q_{j+1},\ldots,q_{k})
is a group homomorphism.

For
k\geq 0
and
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
, we define
D^{k}\varphi:\mathbb{Q}^{k}\to S^{1}
by

D^{k}\varphi(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})=\partial^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}\varphi(0)=\sum_{J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}C^{k-|J|}\varphi\left(\sum_{j\in J}q_{j}\right),

where
C
is the complex conjugation map.

Lemma 9.4
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then

(1)

\varphi\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
if and only if
D^{k}\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, and

(2)

the map
D^{k}:\mathcal{P}_{k}\to\mathcal{M}_{k}
is a group homomorphism with
\ker(D^{k})=\mathcal{P}_{k-1}
.

Proof.

Let us first prove (1).
The function
D^{k}\varphi
is symmetric, so it suffices to prove that for fixed
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1})
, the function

\psi(q)=D^{k}\varphi(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1},q)

is a homomorphism.
Letting
\xi=\partial^{k-1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1}}\varphi
, we can write
\psi(q)=\partial_{q}\xi(0)
.
Note that
\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
for every choice of
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k-1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k-1}
if and only if
\varphi\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
.
Thus, we are reduced to showing that the function
\psi(q)=\partial_{q}\xi(0)
is a homomorphism if and only if
\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
.
In other words, it suffices to prove the
k=1
case of the lemma.

Observe that for
q,r\in\mathbb{Q}
,

D\varphi(q+r)=\varphi(q+r)\overline{\varphi(0)}=\varphi(q)\overline{\varphi(0)}\cdot\varphi(r)\overline{\varphi(0)}\cdot\varphi(q+r)\overline{\varphi(q)}\overline{\varphi(r)}\varphi(0)=D\varphi(q)\cdot D\varphi(r)\cdot\partial^{2}_{q,r}\varphi(0).

Hence,
D\varphi
is a homomorphism if and only if
\partial^{2}_{q,r}\varphi(0)=1
for every
q,r\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Recalling that
\varphi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
if and only if
\partial^{2}_{q,r}\varphi(t)=1
for every
q,r,t\in\mathbb{Q}
, we are now reduced to showing that if
\partial^{2}_{q,r}\varphi(0)=1
for every
q,r\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
\partial^{2}_{q,r}\varphi
is the constant 1 function for
q,r\in\mathbb{Q}
.
This follows immediately from the identity

(9.4)

\partial^{2}_{q,r}\varphi(t)=\partial^{2}_{t+q,r}\varphi(0)\cdot\overline{\partial^{2}_{t,r}\varphi(0)}.

Now we prove (2).
By definition, it is clear that
D^{k}(\varphi\psi)=D^{k}\varphi\cdot D^{k}\psi
, so
D^{k}
is a group homomorphism.
Observe that
\varphi\in\ker(D^{k})
if and only if for every
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
,

D^{k}\varphi(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})=\partial^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}\varphi(0)=1.

Using the identity (
9.4
), this is equivalent to having
\partial^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}\varphi(t)=1
for every
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
and
t\in\mathbb{Q}
, but this is exactly what it means for
\varphi
to be a polynomial of degree at most
k-1
.
∎

We now want to better understand the multilinear map
D^{k}\varphi
.
The following proposition characterizes multilinear maps.

Proposition 9.5
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
.
Then there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
such that for every
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})\in\mathbb{Q}
,

\eta(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{k}\alpha\right).

Proof.

When
k=1
, this is a statement that the Pontryagin dual of
\mathbb{Q}
(as a discrete group) is isomorphic to
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
, which we discussed above in Subsection
3.5
.

Suppose the proposition holds for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\eta\in\mathcal{M}_{k+1}
.
By the induction hypothesis, there exists a map
\alpha:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
such that

(9.5)

\eta(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{k}\alpha(q_{k+1})\right)

for every
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}
.
For fixed
q_{k+1}
, since (
9.5
) holds for
q_{1}=\ldots=q_{k-1}=1
and arbitrary
q_{k}
, the element
\alpha(q_{k+1})
is uniquely determined.
Moreover, since
\eta
is multilinear,
\alpha
is in fact a homomorphism from
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
.
Thus,
\alpha(q_{k+1})=q_{k+1}\alpha(1)
and we are done.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain an analogue of Theorem
9.1
for polynomial functions defined on
\mathbb{Q}
.

Corollary 9.6
.

Let
k\geq 0
, and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
.
Then there exists a unique tuple
(c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
such that for every
q\in\mathbb{Q}
,

\varphi(q)=c\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\binom{q}{j}\right).

Moreover, the map that sends
\varphi
to
(c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})
is a group isomorphism, and
\partial_{q}\varphi
corresponds to the tuple
(\varphi(q)\cdot\overline{c},a^{\prime}_{1}(q),\ldots,a^{\prime}_{k-1}(q),0)
with

a^{\prime}_{i}(q)=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}a_{j}\binom{q}{j-i}.

Proof.

The “moreover” statement follows from the first statement by a straightforward computation, so we prove only the first part of the corollary.

We induct on
k
.
The space
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is the space of constant functions, so there is nothing to prove in the base case
k=0
.

Suppose the corollary holds for some
k\geq 0
, and let
\varphi\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}
.
By Lemma
9.4
(1) and Proposition
9.5
, there exists a unique
a_{k+1}\in\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
such that for every
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1})\in\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}
,

D^{k+1}\varphi(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{k}\cdot q_{k+1}a_{k+1}\right)

Define
\psi:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
by
\psi(q)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(a_{k+1}\binom{q}{k+1}\right)
.
Then
D^{k+1}\psi=D^{k+1}\varphi
, so
\varphi\cdot\overline{\psi}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}
by Lemma
9.4
(2).
Thus, by the induction hypothesis, there is a unique choice
(c,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\in S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
such that

(\varphi\cdot\overline{\psi})(q)=c\cdot e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\binom{q}{j}\right).

Multiplying both sides by
\psi
, we obtain the desired expression for
\varphi
.
∎

9.3.
Phase polynomials

We now turn our attention to the spaces of phase polynomials
\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
as preparation for proving Theorem
9.1
.

Lemma 9.7
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
k\geq 0
.
Then the set
\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
of phase polynomials of degree at most
k
is a group under pointwise multiplication.

Proof.

We induct on
k
.
For
k=0
,
\mathcal{E}_{0}(T)
is the set of constant functions taking values in the unit circle, so
\mathcal{E}_{0}(T)
is isomorphic to the group
S^{1}
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
is a group for some
k\geq 0
.
Let
f,g\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(T)
.
Then there are functions
\zeta_{q},\xi_{q}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
such that
T^{q}f=\zeta_{q}\cdot f
and
T^{q}g=\xi_{q}\cdot g
for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Hence,
T^{q}(fg)=(\zeta_{q}\xi_{q})\cdot fg
, and
\zeta_{q}\xi_{q}\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
by the induction hypothesis, so
fg\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}
.
∎

For a function
f:X\to S^{1}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Delta^{k}f:\mathbb{Q}^{k}\times X\to S^{1}
be the map defined by
(\Delta^{k}f)(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k};x)=\Delta^{k}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}}f(x)
.
If
(X,\mu,T)
is ergodic and
f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
, then
\Delta^{k}f
is equal almost everywhere to a constant function in
x
, so we may view
\Delta^{k}f
as a function from
\mathbb{Q}^{k}
to
S^{1}
.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
, then
\Delta^{k}f\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

Proof.

For
k=1
, we note that if
f\in\mathcal{E}_{1}(T)
, then
T_{q}f=\lambda(q)f
for some
\lambda:\mathbb{Q}\to S^{1}
, and
\lambda=\Delta f
must be a homomorphism, since
T
is a group action.

Suppose the lemma holds for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
f\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(T)
.
Then for each
q_{k+1}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
\Delta_{q_{k+1}}f\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
, so
\Delta^{k}\left(\Delta_{q_{k+1}}f\right)\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
by the induction hypothesis.
Hence, the map

\Delta^{k+1}f:(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1})\mapsto\Delta^{k+1}_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q_{k+1}}f=\left(\Delta^{k}(\Delta_{q_{k+1}}f)\right)(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})

is multilinear in the first
k
coordinates.
But
\Delta^{k+1}f
is a symmetric function, so
\Delta^{k+1}f\in\mathcal{M}_{k+1}
.
∎

By Proposition
9.5
and Lemma
9.8
, we may define a map
\delta_{k}:\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
by

(\Delta^{k}f)(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{k}\cdot\delta_{k}(f)\right).

The element
\delta_{k}(f)
will serve as the “leading coefficient” for the encoding of
f
as a polynomial in Theorem
9.1
.

Proposition 9.9
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Q}
-system, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
\delta_{k}:\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)\to\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q}
is a group homomorphism and
\ker(\delta_{k})=\mathcal{E}_{k-1}(T)
.

Proof.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
, we have
\Delta^{k}(fg)=(\Delta^{k}f)(\Delta^{k}g)
, so
\delta_{k}(f\cdot g)=\delta_{k}(f)+\delta_{k}(g)
.
That is,
\delta_{k}
is a group homomorphism.

Note that
\delta_{k}(f)=0
if and only if
\Delta^{k}f=1
.
Thus, by the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{k-1}(T)
, we have
\ker(\delta_{k})=\mathcal{E}_{k-1}(T)
.
∎

We can now give a short proof of Lemma
9.2
, restated below for convenience.

See
9.2

Proof.

By considering the function
h=f\overline{g}
, it suffices to prove that if
h\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
h
is nonconstant, then
\int_{X}h~d\mu=0
.

Assume that
k\in\mathbb{N}
is minimal.
That is,
h\in\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)\setminus\mathcal{E}_{k-1}(T)
.
Then
\delta_{k}(h)\neq 0
, so we may compute the Host–Kra uniformity seminorm

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|h\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{U^{k}}^{2^{k}}=\textup{UC-}\lim_{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}\int_{X}\Delta^{k}_{\bm{q}}h~d\mu=\textup{UC-}\lim_{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}}e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(q_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot q_{k}\cdot\delta_{k}(h)\right)=0.

By monotonicity of the Host–Kra seminorms (see
[
28
, Lemma 3.9]
and
[
13
, Lemma A.20]
), we conclude that
\int_{X}h~d\mu=0
.
∎

9.4.
Encoding of phases polynomials as
\mathbb{Q}
-polynomials

We have gathered all of the ingredients to prove Theorem
9.1
, which we reproduce below for the convenience of the reader.

See
9.1

Proof.

We construct the isomorphisms
\phi_{k}
by induction on
k
.
The isomorphism
\phi_{0}
is defined by (1).

Suppose we have constructed
\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{k}
for some
k\geq 0
.
Let
f\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(T)
.
Put
t_{k+1}=\delta_{k+1}(f)
.
For each
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, the function
\Delta_{q}f
is a phase polynomial of degree at most
k
, so let
(c(q),t_{1}(q),\ldots,t_{k}(q))=\phi_{k}(\Delta_{q}f)\in S^{1}\times(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
.

Claim.
There exists a unique tuple
\bm{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}
such that

(9.6)

c(q)=e_{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\binom{q}{j}t_{j}\right)

and

(9.7)

t_{i}(q)=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k+1}\binom{q}{j-i}t_{j}

for
q\in\mathbb{Q}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

Before proving the claim, let us show how it can be used to finish the proof of the theorem.
For
\bm{t}\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k+1}
and
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, let
\bm{s}(\bm{t},q)\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k+1}
be the tuple
(s_{0}(\bm{t},q),\ldots,s_{k}(\bm{t},q))
with coordinates

s_{i}(\bm{t},q)=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k+1}\binom{q}{j-i}t_{j}.

Then for each
\bm{t}\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k+1}
, we consider the space

\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(T):\phi_{k}(\Delta_{q}f)=(e_{\mathbb{Q}}(s_{0}(\bm{t},q)),s_{1}(\bm{t},q),\ldots,s_{k}(\bm{t},q))~\text{for every}~q\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}.

The space
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}
may be empty.
If
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}\neq\emptyset
and
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}
, then
f
and
g
are constant multiples of each other: indeed, since
\phi_{k}
is injective, we have
\Delta_{q}(f\overline{g})=1
, so
f\overline{g}
is constant by ergodicity of
T
.

By the claim,
\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(T)=\bigcup_{\bm{t}\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k+1}}\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}
.
Moreover, for
\bm{t},\bm{t}^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k+1}
, since
\bm{s}(\bm{t}+\bm{t}^{\prime},\cdot)=\bm{s}(\bm{t},\cdot)+\bm{s}(\bm{t}^{\prime},\cdot)
and
\phi_{k}
is a homomorphism, we have
\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}\cdot\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}+\bm{t}^{\prime}}
.
Let
H=\{\bm{t}\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k+1}:\mathcal{F}_{\bm{t}}\neq\emptyset\}
, and let
K=\{\bm{s}\in(\mathbb{A}/\mathbb{Q})^{k}:(\bm{s},0)\in H\}
.
By Proposition
9.9
,
\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)=\bigcup_{\bm{s}\in K}\mathcal{F}_{(\bm{s},0)}
, so
\phi_{k}(\mathcal{E}_{k}(T))=S^{1}\times K
.
Let
\psi_{k}:S^{1}\times K\to\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
be the inverse map.
We can extend the map
\psi_{k}(1,\cdot):K\to\mathcal{E}_{k}(T)
to a homomorphism

10.
A set of positive upper Banach density in the rationals not containing any shifted
\Delta
-set

The goal of this short section is to elaborate on the discussion after Theorem
1.2
and show that polynomials of the form
P(x)=-x+c
are bad for sumsets.
Given an infinite set
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
, we call the set of differences
\{b_{j}-b_{i}:i<j\}
a
\Delta
-set
.
In comparison to sets of the form
\{P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}
for polynomials
P(x)\neq-x+c
,
\Delta
-sets possess additional arithmetic structure, which allows for constructions of large subsets of
\mathbb{Q}
not containing any shifted
\Delta
-set.
Recall that a set
R\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
is a
set of (measurable) recurrence
if for every
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,\mu,T)
and every measurable set
E\subseteq X
with
\mu(E)>0
, there exists
r\in R
such that
\mu(E\cap T^{-r}E)>0
.

Proposition 10.1
.

Suppose
B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
is infinite.
Then the set of differences
\{b_{j}-b_{i}:i<j\}
is a set of recurrence.

Proof.

This can be seen by carefully examining the standard proof of the Poincaré recurrence theorem, as noted by Furstenberg in the context of the integers; see
[
22
, p. 74]
, where
\Delta
-sets are given as one of the first examples of
Poincaré sequences
(Furstenberg’s term for sets of recurrence).
For completeness, we include the argument here.
Suppose
(X,\mu,T)
is a measure-preserving
\mathbb{Q}
-system and
A\subseteq X
is a measurable set with
\mu(A)>0
.
Consider the sequence of sets
A_{n}=T^{-b_{n}}A
.
Since
\mu
is a probability measure and
T
is measure-preserving, if
N>\frac{1}{\mu(A)}
, then we must have
\mu(A_{i}\cap A_{j})>0
for some
1\leq i<j\leq N
.
Using the measure preserving property again, we conclude that
\mu(A\cap T^{-(b_{j}-b_{i})}A)>0
.
∎

Theorem 10.2
.

Let
\Phi
be a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Q}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
.
There exists a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
such that
\underline{d}_{\Phi}(A)>1-\varepsilon
and

is a set of nonrecurrence for every

.
In particular,
A-t
does not contain a
\Delta
-set for any
t\in\mathbb{Q}
.

We will use the following general result from
[
2
]
:

Theorem 10.3

(
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be an abelian group, and let
\Phi
be a Følner sequence in
\Gamma
.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a family of infinite subsets of
\Gamma
, and suppose there is a family
\mathcal{D}
of subsets of
\Gamma
with the following properties:

(1)

For any
C\in\mathcal{C}
and any

, one has

, and

(2)

\inf_{D\in\mathcal{D}}\overline{d}_{\Phi}(D)=0
.

Then for any

, there exists

with

such that for any

.

Proof of Theorem
10.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the family of sets of recurrence and
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{C}^{*}
the family of sets of return times.
It suffices to check that
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
satisfy the conditions in Theorem
10.3
for an arbitrary Følner sequence
\Phi
.

Property (1) is a standard result in ergodic theory.
We reproduce a proof here.
Let
C
be a set of recurrence, and let
D=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:\mu(E\cap T^{-q}E)>0\}
for some measure-preserving
\mathbb{Q}
-system
(X,\mu,T)
and measurable set

with
\mu(E)>0
.
We want to show

is a set of recurrence.
Let
(Y,\nu,S)
be another measure-preserving
\mathbb{Q}
-system and

a measurable set with
\nu(Y)>0
.
Then since

is a set of recurrence, there exists

Therefore,

\mu(E\cap T^{-c}E)>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu(F\cap S^{-c}F)>0.

The first inequality shows that

To prove property (2), consider the action of
\mathbb{Q}
on

by rotations

Let

Then the set of return times

Given any Følner sequence

Polynomial sumsets in the integers

The counterexamples produced in

11.1.
Totally ergodic sets

Fix a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with

By the Furstenberg correspondence principle (Theorem
2.2

\mathbb{Z}

\Phi

Before stating the result, we introduce the requisite terminology.

The next theorem implies that if

7

A natural follow-up to Theorem
11.1
is to describe sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
having a totally ergodic Furstenberg system.
Essentially what this says is that for any bounded function

in the algebra generated by

and its shifts (i.e.,

is a linear combination of indicator functions of sets of the form

does not correlate with any periodic function.
A precise statement can be found in
[

.

One may ask what happens if we impose only the condition that
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
does not correlate with periodic functions, rather than imposing such a condition on the entire algebra generated by
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
and its shifts.
Interpreting “correlation” along an appropriately chosen Følner sequence, this corresponds dynamically to the set
E
from the Furstenberg correspondence principle satisfying
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{E}-\mu(E)\mid\mathcal{Z}_{rat}\right]=0
, where
\mathcal{Z}_{rat}
is the factor generated by periodic functions (sometimes called the
rational Kronecker factor
or the

).
A more precise description of such sets is given in
[
10
, Section 3.2]
under the moniker

.
Relatively totally ergodic sets contain a rich variety of polynomial patterns (see
[
10
, Section 3.3]
), so it is reasonable to make the following conjecture, which we state in dynamical language:

Conjecture 11.2
.

Let
(X,\mu,T)
be an ergodic
\mathbb{Z}
-system,
\Phi
a Følner sequence, and

.

be an open set with

.

P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
such that both
P(x)
and
P(x)+x
are nonconstant, there exists
t\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathbf{EFS}^{P}_{X}(T^{t}a)\cap(E\times E)\neq\emptyset
.

The methods of this paper do not seem to be sufficient to address Conjecture

required absolute continuity of a certain measure on the Kronecker factor, and this property may fail in systems with nontrivial rational Kronecker factor.

11.2.

.

Proof.

The key observation is the following:

Claim.
For every Borel set
B\subseteq X
and every
\varepsilon>0
,

d^{*}\left(\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:\left|\mu\left(B\cap T^{-P(n)}E\right)-\mu(B)\mu(E)\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\right)=0.

Proof of Claim.

Let
f=\mathbbm{1}_{E}-\mu(E)
and
g=\mathbbm{1}_{B}
.
First we will show

\textup{UC-}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle f,T^{Q(n)}f\right\rangle\right|^{2}=0

for every nonconstant integer-valued polynomial
Q(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
.
By Herglotz’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
41
, 4.13]
), let
\nu_{f}
be a positive measure on
\mathbb{T}
such that

\left\langle f,T^{n}f\right\rangle=\widehat{\nu}_{f}(n)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}e(nt)~d\nu_{f}(t)

for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
f
is a weakly mixing function, the measure
\nu_{f}
is continuous (see, e.g.,
[
41
, Corollary 4.17]
).
Let
\Theta
be a Følner sequence in
\mathbb{Z}
.
Then

\frac{1}{|\Theta_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Theta_{N}}\left|\left\langle f,T^{Q(n)}f\right\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{|\Theta_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Theta_{N}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}e(Q(n)(t-s))~d\nu_{f}(t)~d\nu_{f}(s).

Now, by Weyl’s equidistribution theorem, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Theta_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Theta_{N}}e(Q(n)x)=0

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.
Therefore,

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Theta_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Theta_{N}}\left|\left\langle f,T^{Q(n)}f\right\rangle\right|^{2}\leq\left(\nu_{f}\times\nu_{f}\right)\left(\left\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}:t-s\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}\right).

For each
s\in\mathbb{T}
, the set
\{t\in\mathbb{T}:t-s\in\mathbb{Q}\}
is countable, so
\nu_{f}\left(\{t\in\mathbb{T}:t-s\in\mathbb{Q}\}\right)=0
.
By Fubini’s theorem, it follows that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\Theta_{N}|}\sum_{n\in\Theta_{N}}\left|\left\langle f,T^{Q(n)}f\right\rangle\right|^{2}=0.

Now we want to prove

\textup{UC-}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle g,T^{P(n)}f\right\rangle\right|^{2}=0.

Let
u_{n}=\left\langle g,T^{P(n)}f\right\rangle T^{P(n)}f
.
Then for any
h\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\left\langle u_{n+h},u_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle g,T^{P(n)}f\right\rangle\left\langle g,T^{P(n+h)}f\right\rangle\left\langle T^{P(n+h)}f,T^{P(n)}f\right\rangle.

But
T
is measure-preserving, so we can rewrite this as

\left\langle u_{n+h},u_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle g,T^{P(n)}f\right\rangle\left\langle g,T^{P(n+h)}f\right\rangle\left\langle T^{P(n+h)-P(n)}f,f\right\rangle,

and so by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\left|\left\langle u_{n+h},u_{n}\right\rangle\right|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|g\right\|_{2}^{2}\left|\left\langle T^{P(n+h)-P(n)}f,f\right\rangle\right|.

If
\deg{P}\geq 2
, then
Q_{h}(n)=P(n+h)-P(n)
is a nonconstant polynomial, so

\textup{UC-}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle u_{n+h},u_{n}\right\rangle\right|=0.

On the other hand, if
P(n)=an+b
, then
P(n+h)-P(n)=ah
, and we have

\textup{UC-}\lim_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\textup{UC-}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle u_{n+h},u_{n}\right\rangle\right|\leq\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|g\right\|_{2}^{2}\textup{UC-}\lim_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle T^{ah}f,f\right\rangle\right|=0.

Hence, by van der Corput (Lemma
3.1
), we have

\textup{UC-}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{n}=0

in
L^{2}(\mu)
.
Taking the inner product with
g
gives

\textup{UC-}\lim_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\left\langle g,T^{P(n)}f\right\rangle\right|^{2}=0

as desired.
∎

Let
A=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:T^{n}a\in E\}

By the portmanteau lemma,

Therefore by the claim, we may choose

Our second question concerns the extent to which we can recover a density statement similar to Theorem

Question 11.6
.

Let
P(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
be an integer-valued intersective polynomial, and let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}

Which of the following conditions (from strongest to weakest) are sufficient to guarantee a configuration
\{P(b_{i})+b_{j}:i<j\}\subseteq A

B=\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}

•

A

•

A

•

There exists
\Phi

•

The first bullet has a positive answer by Theorem

We note that the third bullet (and consequently also the fourth) is a partition regular condition.
