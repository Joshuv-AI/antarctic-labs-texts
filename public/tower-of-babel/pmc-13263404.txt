Lived Experiences of Teens and Adults in North America Who Grew Up With a Sibling Diagnosed With Prader Willi Syndrome
Authors: Lauren Schwartz, Abigail TurnWald, Madelyn Roth, Theresa V Strong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPrader‐Willi syndrome (PWS) is a complex disorder with severe behavioural symptoms that affects the entire family. Relatively little research has focused on the lived experiences of siblings growing up in this unique family environment.MethodSemi‐structured interviews were performed with 25 siblings of individuals with PWS from North America. Participants reflected on their experiences including interpersonal relationships, impacts on well‐being, coping, and relationship with food.ResultsUsing inductive thematic analysis, we identified several themes: (a) emotion and well‐being impacts, (b) impacts on their relationship with food, and (c) relational/social issues. Positive aspects of their family experience and impacts on job path were identified. Key coping resources were highlighted.ConclusionsThis study can inform more family‐oriented support efforts in PWS care models to include sibling support. The findings suggest sibling support should focus on addressing difficult emotional experiences, problematic eating behaviours, and seeking connection with other siblings with similar experiences.Keywords: eating behaviours, family impact, Prader‐Willi syndrome (PWS), siblings, support

Introduction

While the emotional well‐being of parents of children with disabilities has been the focus of previous research, the experiences of siblings of people with disabilities have received comparatively less attention, especially in the context of rare diseases (Heaton et al. 2023; Marquis et al. 2023). Sibling experiences in these families are often multilayered and complex. These siblings often face increased emotional stress, disrupted family routines, and reduced parental attention, all of which can negatively impact their emotional and social development and overall well‐being (Moyson and Roeyers 2012; Hanvey et al. 2022). Some studies have also found positive impacts for siblings growing up with a disabled family member including greater empathy, resilience, and maturity (Rum et al. 2022; Shivers 2019; Opperman and Alant 2003). Still, most of the literature has tended to focus on negative outcomes and primarily on parent reports of the impact on the non‐disabled sibling. Previous research suggests that one aspect that can greatly influence sibling experiences in these families are the behaviours and symptoms associated with the specific disability diagnosis (Rossetti et al. 2020; Tomeny et al. 2017). Thus, siblings of individuals with conditions known to have significant behavioural symptoms may be particularly vulnerable to negative consequences, underscoring the importance of studying the well‐being of siblings in these families who may be at the greatest risk for difficulties.

Prader‐Willi syndrome (PWS) is a rare, complex neurodevelopmental disorder caused by the loss of paternally expressed genes on chromosome 15q11‐q13 (Driscoll et al. 2024), with an estimated prevalence of 1 in every 20,000 to 30,000 births (Daley et al. 2025). PWS is characterized by hypotonia, hyperphagia, intellectual disability and a distinct behavioural phenotype including temper outbursts, anxiousness, cognitive rigidity and compulsive behaviours (Schwartz et al. 2021). PWS presents many challenges for individuals with the syndrome and their families (Dempsey et al. 2025; Dykens et al. 2022). Individuals with PWS need constant support and/or supervision, which can create high caregiver burden on the parents leading to downstream effects on the siblings (Allen 2011). Families bear the burden of managing life‐threatening food‐related behaviours and complex psychiatric comorbidities, which can have far‐reaching effects on family dynamics (Dempsey et al. 2025; Kayadjanian et al. 2021). Studies on caregiver/parent stress related to PWS have shown high levels of caregiver burden, as well as emotional, relationship and health impacts for parents (Pulè and Hughes 2025; Usubini et al. 2024; Kayadjanian et al. 2021). Interestingly, the Kayadjanian et al. (2018) study found that caregiver burden scores for PWS caregivers were higher than those measured in caregivers for persons with dementia, Alzheimer's, and traumatic brain injury, all conditions known to be difficult for caregivers.

Although the challenging behavioural phenotype of PWS suggests the potential for significant impacts on the sibling experience, studies are just beginning to explore this impact (Kamble et al. 2025, 2026). Available studies highlight the potential long‐term effects on these siblings. For example, siblings of those with PWS may experience heightened levels of anxiety, depressive symptoms, and symptoms of post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Pulè and Hughes 2025; Bennett Murphy et al. 2023; Mazaheri et al. 2013; O'Neill and Murray 2016). Other studies on this topic also indicate that these siblings may develop problematic relationships with food themselves (Kamble et al. 2025), likely shaped by the prescribed controlled food environments in PWS households (Dykens et al. 2025) needed to manage the person with PWS. Collectively, these studies underscore the potential complexity of the effects on the PWS siblings' psychological wellbeing, their coping strategies, and potentially their relationship with food.

Most of the existing research has relied on parental reports regarding the impact on these siblings (Kamble et al. 2026), often utilizing quantitative methods with standardized assessments. While these studies contribute valuable insights into the effects of PWS on siblings, they may fail to capture more nuanced and varied impacts regarding the lived experiences of these siblings. Thus, the current literature in this area is limited by the absence of direct sibling perspectives, especially in qualitative frameworks that can facilitate deeper exploration of meaning and context.

The current study employed a qualitative design to capture the voice of the sibling and examine directly the lived experiences of adolescent and adult siblings of individuals with PWS. Participants in this study were asked to reflect on their experiences growing up in a family affected by PWS and the impact those experiences have had on their life especially in terms of their emotional wellbeing and coping, their relationship with food, and their schooling and career choices. By centering on the voice of the sibling, this study aims to deepen the understanding of how PWS affects the broader family system and to inform future family‐centered and sibling‐focused support interventions for this challenging rare disease.

Methods

The study, including interview protocol and recruitment materials, was reviewed and approved by the North Star Review Board (#NB300127). North Star Review Board is a non‐profit external research ethics review board.

Participants were recruited by sharing approved posts on Facebook groups focused on PWS and through emails to parents and family members connected with two major patient advocacy organizations supporting families with a family member who has PWS. To be included in the study, individuals had to be at least 12 years old, be a sibling of someone diagnosed with PWS, and express interest in participating in a research study that involved a video interview about their experiences growing up with a sibling diagnosed with PWS. Participants had to be able to speak and read English. Participants were told that the aim of this research was to understand how siblings and families manage the specific challenges and experiences associated with having a family member with PWS. All data collected was de‐identified.

Data Collection and Interview Methods

Informed consent was obtained from all participants including parental consent and participant assent for minors. After consenting, siblings participated in a semi‐structured interview. Interviews were conducted using the HIPAA‐compliant version of Zoom, a secure videoconferencing platform designed to meet U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) standards for protecting the privacy and security of personal health information. Participants were asked to join from a private location without other individuals present. Parents were not present during interviews to avoid responses being influenced by the presence of a family member.

An interview guide was developed by the study team based on published studies that examined the experiences of unaffected siblings of children with chronic illnesses and genetic conditions and on previous quantitative studies exploring the impact of growing up with PWS on siblings. For one of the questions, the interviewer presented a verbal list of coping resources, and participants were asked which supports they believe would be most helpful. This list was created using insights from existing literature and clinical experience with families of individuals with special needs. The list included common coping domains such as emotional support, psychoeducation, peer connection, and individual strategies, serving as a flexible prompt for participants to reflect on supports they might find most helpful (See Supporting Information S1 for details of the coping resources list).

The interview guide was reviewed by a group of parent and sibling advisors who had a family member with PWS. Interview questions explored the following topics: how living with a sibling with PWS affected the sibling's relationship with food, the impacts on the sibling's emotional well‐being and mental health, the most challenging aspects of PWS for them to cope with, positive aspects of having a sibling with PWS and coping strategies that the sibling thought were or would have been helpful for growing up with a sibling with PWS. Finally, interview participants were also asked about the potential influence of growing up with someone with PWS on their potential career paths and future.

The qualitative interviews were conducted by the principal investigator, a clinical psychologist and researcher. To minimize intrusiveness and support openness around sensitive topics, detailed notetaking rather than audio recording was used. Following each interview, notes were typed for clarity and readability by two other members of the research team. These typed interview records were subsequently coded and analysed using ATLAS.ti Web (v9.24.1; 2023) qualitative data analysis software. To maintain confidentiality, transcripts were identified only by a participant number.

Data Analysis

Demographic data was collected on all participants and is shown in Table 1. When sharing qualitative data quotes, participant ages were grouped as either adolescents (aged 12–18) or adults (19 and older). After the qualitative interview data was de‐identified, the data was coded by three members of the research team through an iterative process. After reaching a consensus on codes most relevant to the research, themes and subthemes were developed by inductive thematic analysis. The coding team (LS, AT and MR) continued to work together to develop consensus regarding the themes to ensure meaningfulness and that the themes were grounded in the data. Once codes and code groups were finalized, the ATLAS.ti program (2023) was used to analyse the code data, generate linked quotations and apply inductive codes.

Disclosure and Understanding of PWS Symptoms

Approximately two thirds (65%) of the sibling interview participants (interview participants) reported that their parents told them about their sibling's PWS when they were young, for example, school age. One sibling reported that their parents “hid it” from them but they reported eventually learning about their sibling having PWS “on their own.”

When asked about their understanding of PWS and the most common symptoms, interview participants indicated a good understanding of the syndrome, noting that hyperphagia and behaviour challenges such as temper outbursts were the most common symptoms. Intellectual disability and low muscle tone was also mentioned as common characteristics of the syndrome by about half of the sibling interviewees.

Emotional and Food Impacts for Siblings

Two of the major themes that emerged from the interview data regarding growing up with a sibling with PWS were impacts on emotional well‐being and relationships with food. Regarding effects on the sibling's emotional well‐being, there were four subthemes that arose, characterized as stress, anxiety, embarrassment, and frustration (Table 2). Many of the interview participants seemed to internalize the emotional environment of the household, and anxiety for some was tied to their sibling's outbursts or disciplinary moments. Embarrassment and frustration were also notable subthemes, often with respect to experiences in social or public settings (Table 2).

“I felt embarrassed in public and on vacations”. – Adult male.

As a follow up to this, interview participants were asked if they had participated in therapy or counselling; 38% reported they had. Several siblings shared that the therapy was most helpful when the therapist was open to learning about PWS. An additional 24% of the interview participants shared that, in the future, they would like to participate in therapy related to their family experiences.

The second major theme focused on the sibling's relationship with food, with the majority of the interview participants in the study indicating that their eating habits and relationship with food were impacted by growing up with a sibling with PWS. Several subthemes emerged, with approximately two‐thirds (64%) of these siblings reporting that they were personally highly focused on food, especially compared with peers. Three of the siblings (all females) reported they had been diagnosed with an eating disorder. Finally, several of the interview participants also revealed feeling that they needed to have secrecy around food (Table 2).

“I used to lock myself in the pantry and eat snacks and stuff.” – Adolescent female.

Relationship‐Based and Personal Identity Impacts

The third major theme focused on impacts of PWS on the sibling's relationships and personal identity. Three subthemes were identified, including impacts on the relationship with parents. Siblings felt at times overlooked and felt they received less attention from parents due to the demands of caring for the sibling with PWS (Table 3). Friendship struggles were also discussed, related to embarrassment or having to ‘explain’ their home environment, especially related to food being locked up. Finally, some of the interview participants shared feelings of perfectionism and suppression of personal struggles in an attempt to “not be a burden” to their parents, who they felt were managing significant responsibilities related to caring for their sibling with PWS (Table 3).

“The focus is on him. Everything is about him.” – Adolescent female.

“I have a personal trait from growing up with my brother where I have to be perfect at everything.” – Adult female.

Other Information Gathered in Interviews: Biggest Challenges and Positive Impacts

Interview participants were asked about the most difficult aspects of PWS for them to cope with while growing up with a sibling with PWS. Three themes were identified including PWS‐related tantrums mentioned by 64% of siblings, food‐related behaviours, and their sibling's rigidity and obsessiveness mentioned by 44% and 40% of the siblings, respectively, as very difficult behaviours for them to cope with related to their sibling's PWS.

When asked about positive attributes of their sibling with PWS, the interview participants talked about many positive qualities they noted in their sibling with PWS including being friendly, strong, kind, hardworking, smart, caring, loyal, creative and having a good sense of humour. Siblings also discussed personal positive qualities they believe developed from their experiences growing up that set them apart from their peers. These themes included greater empathy, patience and compassion (see Table 4). Additionally, 36% (36%) of the interview participants indicated that they were interested in studying fields related to advocacy for people with disabilities or pursuing a career in healthcare, science or teaching, influenced by their experiences growing up with their sibling with PWS (Table 4).

“He helped me be more compassionate. He sees the good in people and helps me do that.” – Adult female.

Highest‐Rated Coping Resources Ranked by Interview Participants

During the interview, participants were asked to select, from a verbal list (See Supporting Information S1 for details of the coping resources list used during the interviews), the top two coping resources they felt would have helped them better cope with growing up with their sibling with PWS. The two most frequently identified resources were the opportunity to connect with other siblings of individuals with PWS and the ability to spend individual time with parents apart from the family member with PWS. Additionally, siblings commonly ranked learning specific coping tools and strategies for managing PWS‐related behaviours as valuable, with several expressing interest in resources addressing how to manage or respond to temper outbursts exhibited by their sibling with PWS.

“Seeing other PWS siblings and hearing about their experiences would have been really helpful.” – Adult male.

“It's very helpful to get it (alone time with parents), and seeing that they wanted to spend time with me alone.” – Adolescent female.

Discussion

The findings of this study contribute to a growing body of literature on the impact of disability on non‐disabled siblings. Additionally, the findings extend the limited research available focused on the voice of the sibling and their direct lived experiences related to growing up with a sibling with PWS, a highly complex disorder associated with significant behavioural symptoms. Consistent with prior work in the broader disability field, our results underscore that siblings are not passive bystanders but are directly and often substantially affected by their family context (Wolff et al. 2022; O'Neill and Murray 2016). Our findings suggest that having a sibling with PWS may challenge the coping resources of non‐disabled siblings, which we hypothesize could place these siblings at increased risk for significant psychological difficulties. About one‐third of participants reported seeking professional therapeutic support. Another 24% of the sibling interview participants indicated that they want to pursue therapy related to their family experiences in the future. For interview participants who did, the perceived effectiveness of therapy was linked to the clinician's openness to learning about PWS and its distinctive features. This finding highlights the importance of specialized knowledge in delivering effective psychological support to this population. Additionally, providers, schools and parents are likely to be important facilitators in encouraging siblings who appear to be struggling to seek support.

Several additional findings appear to be specific to the PWS family context. Two‐thirds of the interview participants reported heightened preoccupation with food relative to peers, and 12% of the sample, all female, reported a diagnosis of an eating disorder, higher than general population rates which are listed as 8.6% for females in the US (Deloitte Access Economics 2020). This is the first study to document elevated prevalence of eating disorder diagnoses among PWS siblings, but the sample size is limited. Future research should examine this association in larger, more diverse samples and explore the potential for different mechanisms underlying eating‐related challenges in this population.

The siblings in this study were well‐informed regarding their sibling's diagnosis of PWS and the symptoms of the syndrome, with the majority indicating that they were told about their sibling's PWS diagnosis during childhood. Siblings also demonstrated strong knowledge of the core features of the syndrome, referencing hyperphagia, temper outbursts, behavioural challenges, and intellectual disability. These typical symptoms of the syndrome were described as shaping daily family life, and a high percentage of the participants highlighted the particular burden of PWS‐related temper outbursts, which were identified as the most difficult symptom for them to cope with. This finding aligns with prior research documenting the disruptive and distressing impact of emotional outbursts on family functioning (Bennett Murphy et al. 2023). For siblings, repeated exposure to these episodes may contribute to heightened anxiety, hypervigilance, and trauma‐related symptoms. It should be noted that the current study did not replicate previously reported rates of PTSD among PWS siblings; however, it is possible that the qualitative approach used here was less sensitive in detecting PTSD diagnoses compared with studies that employ structured assessments and questionnaires specific to these conditions.

In addition to symptom‐related impacts, interview participants described relational challenges, particularly reduced parental attention due to the demands on the parents of caring for the person with PWS. Similar patterns have been documented in families of children with other disabilities (Blamires et al. 2024). Siblings in this study also shared that they tended to minimize their own distress or concerns due to a worry about overburdening their parents. Some siblings shared that they had tendencies toward perfectionism related to this. These findings suggest that the PWS sibling experience may have enduring psychosocial consequences.

At the same time, some interview participants also emphasized potential positive outcomes, reflecting themes consistent with models of post‐traumatic growth (Tedeschi and Calhoun 1996). Participants described cultivating greater empathy, patience, and compassion, and some linked their family experiences to subsequent educational and career choices. Over one‐third (36%) reported pursuing or aspiring to careers in healthcare, science, education, or disability advocacy, echoing prior findings that disability‐related sibling experiences can influence vocational trajectories (Beffel et al. 2023).

Several practical implications emerged from the study findings. Many participants emphasized that PWS presents a unique sibling experience distinct from other disability contexts. Connecting specifically with other siblings of individuals with PWS, rather than more general sibling‐support groups, was described as particularly meaningful. This suggests that peer support interventions tailored specifically to the PWS sibling community may be especially impactful. Additionally, siblings expressed a strong interest in having dedicated one‐on‐one time with parents, a finding consistent with prior research on siblings of individuals with disabilities (Bastiaansen et al. 2022; Turnwald et al. 2022). A 2025 study of families of individuals with chronic conditions found that better parental‐sibling communication was associated with improved quality of life and prosocial behaviour, which may have relevance for families affected by PWS as well (Kirchhofer et al. 2025). Further research is needed to better understand the impact of individualized parent‐sibling time for unaffected siblings.

Some study limitations should be noted. Data were derived from interviews supported by detailed field notes rather than recordings. While this approach is consistent with qualitative traditions that prioritize naturalistic dialogue (Mack et al. 2005), it may have constrained some of the data capture. At the same time, foregoing recordings may have allowed participants to feel less scrutinized, thus encouraging a more natural conversation that potentially enhanced the quality of the data. Future research could consider combining qualitative and quantitative methods to enhance both breadth and depth of understanding. This would also allow for more direct assessment of specific clinical issues such as PTSD and more detailed reporting of symptoms related to eating disorders.

Finally, it should be noted that the participants all resided in North America, and mostly in the United States. Therefore, this study was not able to address cross‐cultural or societal factors that could affect how stressful or manageable PWS is for families. For example, other countries may have more options available for respite care and supported living for adults with PWS, particularly compared with the United States.

The findings of this study highlight the critical need to recognize siblings of individuals with PWS as deserving of support and attention in their own right. While parents routinely receive support for caring for a child with special needs, siblings often are not offered such supports despite growing evidence of their vulnerability to anxiety, eating‐related challenges, and difficulties coping with PWS‐related behaviours and family stress. By highlighting the unique challenges and strengths that characterize the lived experience of these siblings, the current study adds further evidence of the importance of addressing their needs proactively. The results of this study point to the value of tailored resources for siblings, including structured opportunities for PWS peer‐to‐peer connection, psychoeducation about ways to manage PWS behaviours, and intentional parent–sibling time to promote resilience and well‐being for this underserved population.
