Aqueous extract of erigeron annuus ameliorates OVA-induced atopic dermatitis-like skin lesions through modulation of Th2-mediated immune responses and MAPK signaling
Authors: Yejin Ahn, Min Ji Gu, Ho-Young Park, Seol Ji Baek, Seong Un Jeong, Sang Keun Ha, Donghwan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextAtopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by epidermal barrier dysfunction and dysregulated type 2 helper T cell (Th2)-mediated immune responses. Erigeron annuus (L.) Desf. is traditionally used for inflammatory conditions, but its effects on atopic dermatitis remain unclear.ObjectiveThis study evaluated the protective effects of E. annuus aqueous extract (EA) and associated mechanisms.Materials and methodsAtopic dermatitis-like lesions were induced in 5-week-old female BALB/c mice by repeated epicutaneous ovalbumin (OVA) sensitization, and EA was orally administered for six weeks. Epidermal barrier function, histopathology, immunoglobulins, cytokines, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling were evaluated. Pyromeconic acid (PA), a representative constituent of EA, was examined in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages.ResultsEA attenuated dermatitis-like lesions and reduced dermatitis scores in OVA-sensitized mice. EA decreased transepidermal water loss, increased skin hydration, restored filaggrin expression, and reduced epidermal and dermal thickening, mast cell infiltration, CD4+/CD8+ T-cell accumulation, splenomegaly, serum total and OVA-specific IgE, and skin interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 levels. EA also reduced extracellular signal-regulated kinase and p38 phosphorylation and interleukin-1β expression in dorsal skin. PA suppressed MAPK activation and inflammatory mediator expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages.Discussion and conclusionEA attenuated OVA-induced atopic dermatitis-like inflammation in association with improved epidermal barrier function, reduced Th2-associated immune responses, and attenuated MAPK signaling. PA showed anti-inflammatory activity in vitro, although its contribution to the in vivo effects of EA remains to be established. EA warrants further investigation as a plant-derived candidate for complementary intervention in atopic dermatitis.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, erigeron annuus, epidermal barrier, MAPK signaling, pyromeconic acid

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease that predominantly affects infants and children and is characterized by recurrent eczematous lesions and intense pruritus (Weidinger and Novak 2016). Multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, including genetic susceptibility, epidermal barrier dysfunction, immune dysregulation, and alterations in skin microbiota, are involved in the development of atopic dermatitis (Sroka-Tomaszewska and Trzeciak 2021). Persistent itching leads to repetitive scratching, which exacerbates skin damage and significantly reduces quality of life. Furthermore, patients with atopic dermatitis are at increased risk of developing other allergic diseases (Hill and Spergel 2018). Immunologically, atopic dermatitis is characterized by a predominance of type 2 helper T cell (Th2)–mediated responses, particularly during the acute phase. Elevated levels of Th2-associated cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 promote immunoglobulin E (IgE) class switching and drive allergic inflammation (Brandt and Sivaprasad 2011; Schuler et al. 2023). Accordingly, increased serum levels of total and allergen-specific IgE are commonly observed in patients with atopic dermatitis and in experimental atopic dermatitis models (Jin et al. 2009; Vakirlis et al. 2011). Concurrently, impaired expression of epidermal barrier proteins, including filaggrin, facilitates allergen penetration and further amplifies immune activation (Stefanovic and Irvine 2024). Current therapeutic strategies for atopic dermatitis include topical corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and targeted biologics such as IL-4 receptor antagonists and IL-13 antagonists (Meledathu et al. 2025). Although these agents effectively alleviate symptoms, their long-term use is often associated with adverse effects, including skin atrophy and systemic immunosuppression (Drucker et al. 2018). Therefore, the development of safer therapeutic approaches capable of simultaneously regulating immune responses and epidermal barrier dysfunction remains necessary.

Erigeron annuus (L.) Desf. is an annual herb belonging to the Asteraceae family that is native to North America and has been introduced to parts of Europe and Asia (Edwards et al. 2006; Sennikov and Kurtto 2019). It contains a variety of bioactive phytochemicals, including flavonoids, phenolic compounds, and terpenoids (Abd El-Razek 2006; Thakur et al. 2025). In East Asian traditional medicine, the whole plant of E. annuus has long been used to treat gastrointestinal disorders, including indigestion and enteritis, as well as epidemic hepatitis and hematuria (Jiangsu College of New Medicine 1977; Rana et al. 2023). Furthermore, plants of the genus Erigeron L. (1753) have been traditionally used to treat inflammatory and painful conditions, highlighting their ethnopharmacological relevance in inflammation-related disorders (Asongalem et al. 2004; Sharma et al. 2014). Consistent with these traditional uses, pharmacological studies have reported diverse biological activities of E. annuus extracts, including antidiabetic (Lee et al. 2023), anti-obesity (Choi et al. 2019), and neuroprotective effects (Lee et al. 2020). In particular, an aqueous extract of E. annuus and its representative compound, pyromeconic acid, have been shown to suppress TNF-α/IFN-γ-induced inflammatory responses in HaCaT keratinocytes and to attenuate sodium dodecyl sulfate-induced skin barrier disruption (Kang et al. 2024). More recently, Jeong et al. (2024) reported that oral administration of a hot-water extract of the whole E. annuus plant alleviated 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (DNCB)-induced atopic dermatitis-like symptoms, including epidermal thickening, mast cell infiltration, and scratching behavior, in association with activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway. In addition, Kim et al. (2021) discussed Erigeron species in the context of pollen-associated allergenic weeds in Korea rather than the pharmacological effects of E. annuus extracts. Collectively, these previous reports have focused on cellular skin models, DNCB-induced dermatitis, or pollen-associated allergic exposure. However, the effects of orally administered E. annuus aqueous extract on allergen-driven Th2 inflammation, epidermal barrier dysfunction, and MAPK signaling remain insufficiently characterized.

Available toxicological information on E. annuus remains limited. A previous 28-day repeated-dose oral toxicity study conducted according to OECD Test Guideline 407 reported no significant treatment-related adverse effects in mice administered an E. annuus extract at 500 or 1000 mg/kg/day (Jeong et al. 2024). However, the oral LD50 of the specific aqueous extract used in the present study has not been established. Pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which are recognized as a toxicological concern in herbal medicines (Letsyo et al. 2017), have been associated mainly with the tribes Senecioneae and Eupatorieae within the Asteraceae (Anke et al. 2004), whereas E. annuus belongs to the tribe Astereae. Their occurrence in E. annuus has not been established, and pyrrolizidine alkaloids were not specifically analyzed in the EA preparation used in the present study. In this study, we evaluated the protective effects of E. annuus aqueous extract (EA) in an ovalbumin (OVA)-induced atopic dermatitis mouse model. This model employs repeated epicutaneous sensitization with OVA patches to recapitulate skin barrier disruption and scratching-associated damage observed in patients with atopic dermatitis (Kim et al. 2017). Given the involvement of Th2-mediated immune responses and epidermal barrier dysfunction in atopic dermatitis, this model provides a suitable experimental system to evaluate the effects of EA. The protective effects of EA were assessed through histopathological, immunological, and molecular analyses, with a focus on epidermal barrier integrity and immune responses. In addition, the anti-inflammatory activity of pyromeconic acid and its effects on inflammatory signaling were examined in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated macrophages.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

The aerial parts of Erigeron annuus (L.) Desf. were collected in Muju, Republic of Korea, in June 2024. The botanical identity of the plant material authenticated by Dr. Byeong Cheol Moon at the Herbal Medicine Resources Research Center, Korea Institute of Oriental Medicine (KIOM; Naju, Republic of Korea), using internal transcribed spacer (ITS) DNA barcoding. According to a previously issued authentication report from KIOM, the ITS sequences were compared with reference sequences in NCBI GenBank using BLAST and were further evaluated by Clustal multiple-sequence alignment with E. annuus and related Erigeron species, supporting the identification of the material as E. annuus. A voucher specimen was deposited in the Korean Herbarium of Standard Herbal Resources at KIOM under accession number KIOM202403308. An additional reference specimen was retained at the Hamsoapharm R&D Center under voucher number HAM-K063-001. The dried aerial parts were subsequently extracted with 30-fold distilled water (1:30, w/w) at 50 °C for 7 h. The extract was sequentially filtered through a 50-mesh basket and a 100 μm filter and concentrated under reduced pressure at ≤65 °C to a final concentration of 78°Brix. The concentrate was sterilized at 90 °C for 30 min and subsequently filtered through a 60-mesh filter. The E. annuus aqueous extract (EA), prepared from the authenticated plant material, was supplied by the Hamsoapharm R&D Center (Iksan, Republic of Korea) and stored at 4 °C until use. The production yield was 27.23 ± 0.12% (w/w), calculated as the weight of the final concentrated extract relative to the initial dried plant material. For the in vivo experiments, the concentrated EA was administered orally at 200 or 500 mg/kg body weight, with the doses calculated based on the weight of the final concentrated extract.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis

Pyromeconic acid (PA; purity 99.7%; catalog no. CFN96870; ChemFaces, Wuhan, China) was used as the reference standard. The PA content of EA was analyzed using a 1260 Infinity II HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies). The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% phosphoric acid in distilled water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B) and was delivered isocratically at a ratio of 90:10 (A:B, v/v) for 10 min. The flow rate was 0.8 mL/min, the injection volume was 10 μL, the column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, and the detection wavelength was set at 270 nm. PA in EA was identified by comparing its retention time with that of the authentic PA reference standard (4.66 min). Quantification was performed using an external-standard calibration curve constructed at five concentrations ranging from 3.125 to 50 μg/mL, which showed good linearity (R2 = 0.9999). The method showed acceptable accuracy (recovery, 90.6%–97.8%) and precision, with relative standard deviation values of 1.7%–2.2% for repeatability and 2.7% for reproducibility. The PA content of EA was determined to be 25.65 ± 0.33 mg/g extract (Supplementary Figure 1).

Animals

BALB/c mice (female, 5 weeks old, 18 ± 1 g) were supplied by Koatech (Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea). BALB/c mice were selected because repeated epicutaneous OVA sensitization in this strain induces Th2-dominant atopic dermatitis-like skin inflammation that reproduces key features of human atopic dermatitis (Wang et al. 2007; Jin et al. 2009). The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle), with free access to food and water. Major clinical symptoms, such as changes in behavior, appearance, body weight, body temperature, and appetite, were monitored daily during the experiment. The sample size was determined with reference to previous studies using OVA-induced atopic dermatitis models (4–6 mice/group) (Kim et al. 2012, 2017) and plant extract-treated atopic dermatitis models (8 mice per group) (Lee et al. 2020; Mechesso et al. 2019), while also considering the 3Rs principle. A sensitivity power analysis based on the final sample size (G*Power 3.1) indicated that, with eight mice per group across five groups (α = 0.05, power = 0.80), the study was able to detect an effect size of Cohen’s f = 0.58 using one-way ANOVA (Faul et al. 2009).

After a one-week acclimatization period, the initial body weight of each mouse was measured, and the mice were randomly allocated to five groups (n = 8 per group) while ensuring that the mean initial body weights were comparable among groups: control (CON; saline), OVA control (OVA-C; saline), low-dose EA (EA-L; EA, 200 mg/kg), high-dose EA (EA-H; EA, 500 mg/kg), and positive control (DEX; dexamethasone, 1.5 mg/kg). EA or DEX was administered orally once daily from week 3 until the end of the experiment, beginning concurrently with the first epicutaneous OVA sensitization (i.e., after systemic sensitization but before the development of skin lesions), to evaluate the preventive effect of EA on the development of atopic dermatitis-like skin lesions. The EA doses of 200 and 500 mg/kg/day were selected with reference to previous oral studies of E. annuus extracts and botanical extracts used in experimental models of atopic dermatitis. An E. annuus water extract has been evaluated at 50–200 mg/kg/day in mice (Zheng et al. 2021), whereas an E. annuus extract was administered at 500 and 1000 mg/kg/day in a DNCB-induced atopic dermatitis model (Jeong et al. 2024). Botanical extracts have also been orally administered at 50–200 mg/kg and 300–500 mg/kg in DNCB-induced atopic dermatitis models (Park et al. 2012; Yang et al. 2016).

At the end of the experiment, the mice were anesthetized in an induction chamber using 2–4% isoflurane in oxygen (Isotroy 250; BKPharm, Goyang, Republic of Korea). Whole blood was then collected from the abdominal aorta, and the mice were euthanized by exsanguination under deep anesthesia. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Food Research Institute (KFRI-M-24047; approval date: 16 July 2024) and were conducted in accordance with the approved institutional protocol. The study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

OVA sensitization

All groups except the CON group were sensitized with OVA (#A5503; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to induce atopic dermatitis (Kim et al. 2017; Han et al. 2022). For systemic sensitization, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 10 μg of OVA emulsified with Alhydrogel (#vac-alu-50; InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) in 200 μL of saline on days 0, 7, and 14. For epicutaneous sensitization, 100 μg of OVA dissolved in 100 μL of saline was applied to the shaved dorsal skin and covered with a sterile Tegaderm patch (4.4 × 4.4 cm; 3 M, St. Paul, MN, USA) on day 21. Epicutaneous sensitization was performed for 7 days in weeks 3, 5, and 7, and the patches were replaced every 2 days.

Dermatitis score and skin parameter measurements

Dermatitis severity was assessed during week 8 of the experiment. Dermatitis severity was assessed by visually scoring four clinical signs: erythema, edema/papulation, excoriation, and scaling/dryness (Yatsuzuka et al. 2007). Each sign was graded as 0 (none), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), or 3 (severe), and the scores were summed to obtain a total score per mouse. Clinical scoring was performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation. Skin hydration and transepidermal water loss (TEWL) were measured on the dorsal skin using a Corneometer CM 825 and a Tewameter TM 300, respectively, connected to a Multi Probe Adapter MPA 6 (Courage + Khazaka Electronic GmbH, Cologne, Germany).

Skin histopathological analysis

Dorsal skin tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-μm-thick sections. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or toluidine blue (Kim et al. 2012). Epidermal and dermal thicknesses were quantified from randomly selected microscopic fields using ImageJ software. For immunohistochemical (IHC) assessment, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against filaggrin, CD4, and CD8 according to previously described methods (Han et al. 2016), and immunoreactivity was visualized using a chromogenic detection system. Details of all antibodies are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Stained sections were scanned using a digital slide scanner (Motic Easyscan One, Motic, Hong Kong), and staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Serum biochemical and immunological analyses and skin cytokine measurements

Whole blood was allowed to clot at room temperature and centrifuged (3,000 × g, 4 °C, 10 min) to obtain serum. Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels were measured using an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Hitachi 7180, Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). Serum levels of total IgE (#MBS564074; MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA), OVA-specific IgE (#MBS764157; MyBioSource), and IgG (#ab151276; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For cytokine measurements, dorsal skin tissue (100 mg) was homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. IL-4 (#MBS2504956; MyBioSource) and IL-13 (#MBS703716; MyBioSource) levels in the resulting supernatants were then measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cell culture

Murine macrophage RAW 264.7 cells were procured from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Pure pyromeconic acid (PA; purity 99.7%; catalog no. CFN96870; ChemFaces, Wuhan, China) was used for all cell-based experiments. PA was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted with culture medium to the final concentrations, with the final DMSO concentration not exceeding 0.1% (v/v), and the control groups received the same concentration of vehicle. For the cell viability and nitric oxide (NO) assays, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well. For the initial cytotoxicity assessment, RAW 264.7 cells were treated with PA at 14.1, 18.8, 28.2, or 35.25 μg/mL for 24 h, and cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CK04; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To assess cell viability under inflammatory conditions, cells were pretreated with PA at 14.1, 18.8, or 28.2 μg/mL for 2 h and subsequently stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 μg/mL; L2630; Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h, followed by CCK-8 analysis. For NO measurement, cells were pretreated with PA at 7.05, 14.1, 18.8, or 28.2 μg/mL for 2 h and then stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 24 h. Nitrite accumulation in the culture supernatant was measured using a Griess Reagent Kit (G7921; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the subsequent MAPK and inflammatory mediator analyses, cells were seeded at 2 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Based on the cell viability results, PA concentrations of 14.1, 18.8, and 28.2 μg/mL were used. Cells were pretreated with PA for 2 h and subsequently stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 1 h for MAPK analysis or 24 h for inflammatory mediator analysis. The experimental design included an untreated control and an LPS-treated control; no separate pharmacological positive control was included.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting

Total RNA was extracted from spleen tissue (100 mg) using TRI reagent (Sigma-Aldrich), and cDNA was synthesized using the iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Relative mRNA levels were quantified using qRT-PCR with SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad). Gene-specific primers targeting glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; NM_008084.3), IL-4 (NM_021283.2), and IL-6 (NM_021168.1) were obtained from Bioneer (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Total protein was isolated from dorsal skin tissue (100 mg) or RAW 264.7 cells using RIPA buffer (#89901; Thermo Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. For the in vivo Western blot analysis, dorsal skin samples from three individual mice per group were analyzed independently (n = 3 biological replicates), and tissues were not pooled. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay, and equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western blotting substrates (Bio-Rad) and quantified using ChemiDoc XRS+ System (Bio-Rad). Detailed information on the primary and secondary antibodies is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

In vitro results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments, whereas in vivo data are reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) with the individual mouse as the experimental unit. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance were assessed for each endpoint using the Shapiro–Wilk and Brown–Forsythe tests, respectively, and no significant violations of these assumptions were detected. Each endpoint was then analyzed independently using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test, with the adjustment applied separately within each endpoint. In the figures, comparisons between the CON and OVA-C groups and between the OVA-C and treatment groups are presented. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of EA on atopic dermatitis–related and physiological parameters in OVA-sensitized mice

The OVA-C group developed pronounced atopic dermatitis-like symptoms relative to the CON group (Figure 1(A)). Consistently, dermatitis scores were markedly elevated in OVA-C mice (Figure 1(B); p < 0.001). Oral administration of EA significantly reduced dermatitis scores to 0.61-fold and 0.39-fold of OVA-C levels in the EA-L and EA-H groups, respectively (p < 0.001). Barrier impairment was further evaluated by measuring TEWL and skin hydration. TEWL levels were markedly elevated in OVA-C mice (2.74-fold vs. CON; p < 0.001), whereas EA treatment significantly suppressed this elevation in both EA-L and EA-H groups (Figure 1(C); p < 0.001). Conversely, skin hydration was markedly decreased following OVA sensitization (0.34-fold vs. CON; p < 0.001), and EA administration dose-dependently restored skin hydration (Figure 1(D); p < 0.001).

Final body weight and serum hepatic enzyme levels were additionally evaluated at the end of the experiment (Supplementary Figure 2). No significant differences in final body weight or serum ALT levels were observed among the groups (Supplementary Figure 2(A–B)). Serum AST levels were significantly increased in the OVA-C group compared with the CON group (p < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed between the OVA-C group and the EA-treated or DEX groups (Supplementary Figure 2(C)). In addition, no mortality or abnormal clinical signs were observed in any group during the experimental period.

Effects of EA on serum immunoglobulin levels in OVA-sensitized mice

To assess systemic allergic responses, serum immunoglobulin concentrations were determined (Figure 2). OVA sensitization resulted in a pronounced increase in total IgE levels compared with the CON group (Figure 2(A); 15.47-fold, p < 0.001). EA treatment significantly reduced total IgE levels in both EA-L and EA-H groups (0.66-fold and 0.55-fold vs. OVA-C, respectively; p < 0.001), reaching levels comparable to those observed with DEX treatment. OVA-specific IgE levels were significantly elevated in OVA-C mice compared with the CON group (Figure 2(B); p < 0.001), whereas EA administration significantly suppressed OVA-specific IgE production (p < 0.001). Furthermore, serum IgG levels were significantly increased in OVA-C mice (5.59-fold vs. CON; p < 0.001). High-dose EA treatment led to a significant reduction in IgG levels (0.69-fold vs. OVA-C; p < 0.001), suggesting that EA attenuated OVA-induced humoral immune activation.

Effects of EA on splenic morphology and cytokine expression in OVA-sensitized mice

Spleen morphology and cytokine/chemokine expression were evaluated to assess systemic immune activation in OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mice (Figure 3). The OVA-C group exhibited an apparent enlargement of the spleen compared with the CON group (Figure 3(A)). Consistently, spleen weight and length were significantly increased in OVA-C mice relative to the CON group (Figure 3(B–C); p < 0.001). EA administration attenuated OVA-induced splenomegaly. Although spleen weight showed a decreasing tendency in EA-treated mice, a statistically significant reduction was observed only in the DEX group compared with the OVA-C group (p < 0.01). In contrast, spleen length was significantly reduced in both EA-L and EA-H groups relative to the OVA-C group (p < 0.01), indicating partial normalization of spleen morphology following EA treatment.

To further evaluate inflammatory responses in the spleen, mRNA expression levels of cytokines were measured. OVA sensitization markedly increased IL-4 and IL-6 mRNA expression compared with the CON group (Figure 3(D–E); p < 0.001). EA administration significantly suppressed IL-4 mRNA expression in a dose-dependent manner compared with the OVA-C group (EA-L: p < 0.01; EA-H: p < 0.001). Similarly, IL-6 mRNA expression was significantly decreased in the EA-H group relative to the OVA-C group (p < 0.001).

Effects of EA on histopathological alterations in the dorsal skin of OVA-sensitized mice

Histopathological changes in the dorsal skin were examined to assess the effects of EA on skin inflammation and barrier disruption in OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mice (Figure 4). H&E staining revealed marked epidermal hyperplasia and dermal thickening in the OVA-C group relative to the CON group (Figure 4(A)). Consistently, quantitative analysis showed that epidermal and dermal thicknesses were significantly increased in the OVA-C group relative to the CON group (Figure 4(B–C); p < 0.001). EA administration significantly attenuated these histopathological alterations. Both EA-L and EA-H groups exhibited a significant reduction in epidermal and dermal thickness compared with the OVA-C group (p < 0.001), indicating amelioration of OVA-induced epidermal and dermal thickening.

Toluidine blue staining revealed marked infiltration of mast cells in the dermis of OVA-C mice relative to controls (Figure 4(A)). Quantitative analysis showed that mast cell numbers were significantly increased in the OVA-C group (Figure 4(D); p < 0.001), whereas EA treatment significantly reduced mast cell infiltration in both EA-L and EA-H groups (p < 0.001). To further evaluate epidermal barrier integrity, filaggrin expression was analyzed by IHC. OVA sensitization markedly decreased the filaggrin-positive area compared with the CON group (Figure 4(A,E); p < 0.001). In contrast, EA treatment significantly restored filaggrin expression in the dorsal skin (p < 0.001), reaching levels comparable to those observed in the DEX group.

Effects of EA on T cell infiltration and Th2 cytokine production in the dorsal skin of OVA-sensitized mice

To further investigate the effects of EA on local immune responses in the skin, T cell infiltration and Th2 cytokine levels were examined in the dorsal skin of OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mice (Figure 5). IHC analysis revealed a marked increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration in the OVA-C group relative to the CON group (Figure 5(A)). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the CD4+- and CD8+-positive areas were significantly elevated in the OVA-C group relative to the CON group (Figure 5(B–C); p < 0.001). EA administration significantly attenuated T cell infiltration in the dorsal skin. Both EA-L and EA-H groups showed a significant reduction in CD4+- and CD8+-positive areas compared with the OVA-C group (p < 0.001), indicating that EA suppressed OVA-induced T cell infiltration in the skin.

To assess local Th2 cytokine production, IL-4 and IL-13 levels in dorsal skin tissue were measured by ELISA. The OVA-C group exhibited significantly increased levels of IL-4 and IL-13 compared with the CON group (Figure 5(D–E); p < 0.001). EA administration significantly reduced IL-4 levels compared with the OVA-C group (EA-L: p < 0.05, EA-H: p < 0.01). In addition, IL-13 levels were significantly decreased in both EA-treated groups (p < 0.001), demonstrating that EA effectively suppressed Th2-polarized cytokine responses in the skin.

Effects of EA on MAPK signaling-related protein levels in the dorsal skin of OVA-sensitized mice

Figure 6 illustrates the effects of EA on MAPK signaling pathway activation and IL-1β expression in the dorsal skin of OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mice. The OVA-C group exhibited a marked increase in extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) phosphorylation compared with the CON group, as indicated by a significant elevation in the p-ERK/ERK ratio (Figure 6(A); p < 0.001). EA administration significantly attenuated ERK activation, with both EA-L and EA-H groups showing significantly reduced p-ERK/ERK levels relative to the OVA-C group (p < 0.001). Similarly, the p-p38/p38 ratio was significantly elevated in the OVA-C group compared with the CON group (Figure 6(B); p < 0.001), whereas EA administration markedly suppressed p38 phosphorylation (p < 0.001).

Consistent with the suppression of MAPK signaling, IL-1β protein levels in the dorsal skin were significantly elevated in the OVA-C group relative to the CON group (Figure 6(C); p < 0.001). EA administration significantly reduced IL-1β expression compared with the OVA-C group (EA-L: p < 0.01, EA-H: p < 0.001). Collectively, these results indicate that EA alleviates OVA-induced skin inflammation, at least in part, through inhibition of MAPK signaling pathways and subsequent reduction of pro-inflammatory IL-1β expression.

Effects of PA on MAPK signaling and inflammatory mediator protein levels in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells

To further examine the anti-inflammatory activity of PA, a representative constituent of EA, we evaluated its effects on MAPK signaling and inflammatory mediator expression in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages (Figure 7). PA showed no significant reduction in cell viability at concentrations up to 28.2 μg/mL, whereas cell viability was significantly decreased at 35.25 μg/mL (Supplementary Figure 3A). Accordingly, 14.1–28.2 μg/mL PA was used for the subsequent mechanistic analyses. PA pretreatment at these concentrations did not significantly affect cell viability following LPS stimulation (Supplementary Figure 3B) and significantly reduced LPS-induced NO production (Supplementary Figure 3C). LPS stimulation markedly enhanced the phosphorylation of ERK, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), and p38 compared with the NOR group, as reflected by increased p-ERK/ERK, p-JNK/JNK, and p-p38/p38 ratios (Figure 7(A–C); p < 0.001). Pretreatment with PA significantly inhibited MAPK activation induced by LPS in a concentration-dependent manner. PA treatment significantly reduced ERK, JNK, and p38 phosphorylation compared with the LPS-treated CON group (p < 0.01–0.001).

In addition to MAPK signaling, the protein levels of downstream inflammatory factors were examined following prolonged LPS stimulation. The protein levels of COX-2, iNOS, and TNF-α were substantially elevated in the CON group relative to the NOR group (Figure 7(D–F); p < 0.001). PA pretreatment significantly reduced the expression of these inflammatory mediators compared with the CON group. COX-2 expression was significantly decreased at all tested PA concentrations (p < 0.01–0.001), whereas iNOS and TNF-α expression were markedly suppressed in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 7(E–F); p < 0.001). Taken together, these findings suggest that PA contributes to the anti-inflammatory activity of EA by suppressing LPS-induced MAPK activation and reducing the expression of key inflammatory mediators in macrophages, thereby the contribution of PA to the anti-inflammatory effects of EA observed in the OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mouse model.

Discussion

Atopic dermatitis is a multifactorial inflammatory skin disorder characterized by epidermal barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation (Bieber et al. 2012). The prevalence of atopic dermatitis has steadily increased, particularly in industrialized regions, and its hallmark symptoms—including eczematous lesions, severe pruritus, and xerosis—substantially impair patients’ quality of life (Xiao et al. 2025). Recent progress in atopic dermatitis research has facilitated the development of therapeutic strategies tailored to barrier characteristics, immune phenotypes, and genetic backgrounds, highlighting the potential for personalized treatment approaches (Bieber 2022; Hartmann et al. 2025). However, despite these advances, effective long-term therapeutic options for atopic dermatitis remain limited compared with those for other chronic inflammatory diseases (Seremet et al. 2024), underscoring the need for safer and more effective natural product-derived approaches. In the present study, an OVA-induced epicutaneous sensitization model was employed to mimic atopic dermatitis-like epidermal barrier disruption and inflammatory responses. This model involves repeated tape stripping followed by allergen exposure and is widely used to reproduce key pathological features of human atopic dermatitis (Kim et al. 2017; Han et al. 2022). Notably, oral administration of EA significantly attenuated OVA-induced pathological features associated with atopic dermatitis-like skin inflammation.

Impairment of the epidermal barrier is recognized as a fundamental pathogenic driver in atopic dermatitis and plays a decisive role in disease initiation, chronicity, and exacerbation (Luger et al. 2021). Structural abnormalities in the stratum corneum, including defects in cornified envelope formation and reduced expression of key barrier-associated proteins such as filaggrin, lead to increased TEWL and enhanced penetration of external allergens. These barrier defects facilitate sustained cutaneous immune activation and promote Th2-polarized inflammatory responses (Elias and Schmuth 2009; Agrawal and Woodfolk 2014). Filaggrin, a major structural protein of the stratum corneum, plays an essential role in maintaining epidermal barrier integrity by contributing to epidermal hydration, pH regulation, antimicrobial defense, and the generation of natural moisturizing factors (Sandilands et al. 2009). Loss-of-function mutations in the filaggrin gene are strongly associated with the pathogenesis of atopic dermatitis and have also been implicated in the progression of the atopic march, including the development of asthma and allergic rhinitis (Morar et al. 2007; van den Oord and Sheikh 2009; Cepelak et al. 2019). In the present study, an OVA-induced epicutaneous sensitization model was used to evaluate the effects of EA on atopic dermatitis-like skin inflammation. Oral administration of EA significantly attenuated OVA-induced pathological features.

Th2 cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-13, are well established as central mediators of allergic inflammation in atopic dermatitis, driving B-cell isotype switching, immunoglobulin production, eosinophil expansion, and mast cell degranulation (Brandt and Sivaprasad 2011). IL-4 further promotes Th2 cell differentiation and IgE synthesis, thereby contributing to the activation of basophils and mast cells associated with pruritus and erythema during allergic inflammation (Torres et al. 2025). Several previous studies have reported that natural products targeting Th2-dominant immune responses exert protective effects in experimental models of atopic dermatitis. Min et al. demonstrated that Lycopus lucidus Turcz extract significantly ameliorated DNCB-induced atopic dermatitis-like lesions in BALB/c mice by suppressing Th2 cytokine production and reducing mast cell infiltration in the skin (Min et al. 2021). Similarly, oral administration of Aloe polysaccharides attenuated ovalbumin-induced atopic dermatitis by restoring epidermal tight junction integrity and improving skin barrier function (Na et al. 2020). In addition, 3′-sialyllactose, a prebiotic component, was shown to prevent skin inflammation in atopic dermatitis mouse models by promoting regulatory T-cell differentiation (Kang et al. 2020). Consistent with previous studies, EA attenuated Th2-polarized immune responses and reduced serum IgE and IgG levels.

The MAPK signaling pathway, particularly ERK, JNK, and p38, contributes to atopic dermatitis by regulating inflammatory responses, immune cell activation, and epidermal barrier function (Tan et al. 2017; Kumar et al. 2025). Increased levels of phosphorylated ERK have been consistently observed in tissues from both patients with atopic dermatitis and experimental models, and topical inhibition of ERK signaling has been shown to significantly alleviate atopic dermatitis symptoms by suppressing cutaneous inflammation and restoring barrier function (Zeze et al. 2022). In addition, pharmacological inhibition of p38 MAPK attenuates atopic dermatitis-like skin lesions and inflammatory responses in hapten-induced mouse models, further supporting the therapeutic relevance of MAPK targeting (Lee et al. 2022). Beyond synthetic inhibitors, several natural compounds have been reported to ameliorate atopic dermatitis pathology through suppression of MAPK signaling. Natural compounds such as mangiferin and neferine have also ameliorated experimental atopic dermatitis in association with suppression of MAPK signaling (Yang et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2024). In the present study, EA significantly suppressed ERK and p38 phosphorylation in dorsal skin tissue, indicating attenuation of MAPK-mediated pro-inflammatory signaling. These findings complement those of Jeong et al. (2024), who reported that a freeze-dried hot-water extract of the whole E. annuus plant ameliorated DNCB-induced atopic dermatitis-like lesions in association with Nrf2/HO-1 signaling. In contrast, the present study used an aqueous extract of authenticated aerial parts with quantified PA content in an OVA-induced epicutaneous sensitization model and focused on epidermal barrier function, Th2-associated immune responses, and MAPK signaling.

PA is a naturally occurring constituent of E. annuus and has previously been isolated from its aerial parts together with the glucosidic derivatives erigeroside (PA 3-O-β-D-glucoside) and 3′-O-caffeylerigeroside (Hashidoko 1995). Kang et al. (2024) reported anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antihistaminic, and skin barrier-protective activities of a PA-enriched E. annuus water extract and PA in mast cells and keratinocytes, including reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokine production and oxidative stress. PA also attenuated histamine release and sodium dodecyl sulfate-induced barrier dysfunction and increased filaggrin mRNA expression. These effects are directionally consistent with the reduced mast cell infiltration and restored filaggrin expression observed following EA administration. In the present study, PA suppressed MAPK activation and inflammatory mediator expression in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages, supporting its anti-inflammatory activity under the tested in vitro conditions. Nevertheless, this model primarily reflects macrophage-mediated innate inflammatory responses and does not reproduce Th2-mediated inflammation or epidermal barrier dysfunction; therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as direct mechanistic validation of the role of PA in atopic dermatitis. Furthermore, because no pharmacological positive control was included in the LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 experiments, the magnitude of the anti-inflammatory effects of PA could not be directly compared with that of an established reference compound. PA accounted for approximately 2.57% of EA, corresponding to estimated oral PA intakes of approximately 5.13 and 12.83 mg/kg at EA doses of 200 and 500 mg/kg, respectively. For context, the PA concentrations used in vitro (14.1–28.2 μg/mL) correspond to approximately 0.55–1.10 mg/mL of EA based on its PA content. Because pure PA was not evaluated in the OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mouse model and its pharmacokinetic properties, including oral bioavailability and skin distribution, have not been characterized, the relationship between these in vitro concentrations and in vivo exposure, as well as the specific contribution of PA to the effects of EA, remains uncertain.

With regard to safety, EA administration did not significantly affect final body weight or serum ALT levels, and no mortality or abnormal clinical signs were observed during the experimental period. Although serum AST levels were increased in the OVA-C group, EA administration did not further increase AST levels relative to the OVA-C group. In addition, a previous 28-day repeated-dose oral toxicity study reported no significant treatment-related adverse effects in mice administered an E. annuus extract at 500 or 1000 mg/kg/day (Jeong et al. 2024). The doses used in the present study (200 and 500 mg/kg/day) were therefore at or below the doses previously evaluated in that study. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the extract evaluated by Jeong et al. (2024) was not identical to the EA preparation used in the present study, and the oral LD50 of the present EA preparation has not been established. Furthermore, hematological parameters, renal function, cardiovascular measurements, and other organ-specific safety indices were not evaluated; therefore, further systematic toxicological studies are required to establish the safety profile of EA. Second, EA is a crude aqueous extract containing multiple constituents, and additional peaks observed in the HPLC chromatograms were not structurally identified or quantified. Contributions from other constituents, including possible additive or synergistic interactions with PA, cannot be excluded. Thus, comprehensive phytochemical characterization of EA remains incomplete. Further LC–MS/MS analyses would provide additional structural evidence for PA identification and facilitate the characterization of other constituents present in EA. Third, because this study used a single OVA-induced atopic dermatitis mouse model, it may not fully recapitulate the chronic and relapsing characteristics of human atopic dermatitis. Further studies using chronic or relapsing atopic dermatitis models and well-designed clinical investigations are required to clarify the long-term efficacy and clinical applicability of EA.

Conclusions

EA ameliorated atopic dermatitis-like skin lesions in an OVA-induced mouse model in association with improved epidermal barrier function and attenuation of Th2-dominant immune responses. These protective effects were associated with the attenuation of MAPK signaling and the downregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators in the skin. Furthermore, pyromeconic acid, a representative constituent of EA, suppressed MAPK activation and inflammatory responses in macrophages, supporting its anti-inflammatory activity under the tested in vitro conditions, although its specific contribution to the in vivo effects of EA remains to be established. Taken together, these results suggest that the protective effects of EA against atopic dermatitis-like skin inflammation are associated with coordinated modulation of epidermal barrier integrity, Th2-associated immune responses, and MAPK signaling. EA may therefore represent a plant-derived candidate for further investigation as a complementary intervention for atopic dermatitis, although further studies are required to characterize its active constituents, pharmacokinetic properties, toxicological profile, and clinical efficacy.
