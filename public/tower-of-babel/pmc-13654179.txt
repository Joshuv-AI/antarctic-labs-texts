Multimodality imaging of a ruptured true splenic artery aneurysm with delayed transcolonic coil migration: a case description
Authors: Tianyu Wu, Na Hu, Pengyu Li, Yixiang Ma
Published: 2026

Introduction

Splenic artery aneurysm (SAA) is one of the most common visceral artery aneurysms; however, gastrointestinal bleeding resulting from rupture of a true SAA is rare and difficult to diagnose (1,2). Most reported cases of SAA-related gastrointestinal bleeding involve pseudoaneurysms, often associated with pancreatitis, trauma, or postoperative complications. Rupture of a true SAA into the colon is particularly uncommon.

Patients with ruptured SAAs often present with nonspecific symptoms, including left upper abdominal pain, gastrointestinal bleeding, and, in severe cases, hemorrhagic shock. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), computed tomography angiography (CTA), and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) are critical for detecting aneurysmal rupture, identifying the source vessel, localizing active bleeding, and guiding treatment (3). Current management strategies include endovascular intervention and definitive surgical treatment, with the choice of therapy depending on hemodynamic status, aneurysm anatomy, rupture pattern, and involvement of adjacent organs (4).

We report a case of a ruptured SAA with colonic fistulization that was initially treated with emergency coil embolization and subsequently complicated by delayed transcolonic coil migration. The prolonged clinical course, serial multimodality imaging findings, and staged management strategy provide useful insights into imaging-based diagnosis and post-embolization surveillance. Notably, in patients with a true SAA complicated by a colonic fistula, emergency embolization may control hemorrhage without eliminating the underlying aneurysm-enteric fistula. Thus, after stabilization, staged definitive resection and long-term surveillance should be considered.

Case presentation

Presenting symptoms

The patient, a woman in her 40 s, was admitted with dark-red hematochezia with clots that had persisted for 7 days and worsened over the preceding 24 hours.

History of present illness

The patient first developed dark-red hematochezia with clots 7 days before admission, without an obvious precipitating factor. She subsequently visited three hospitals; however, the source of bleeding could not be determined. After admission, her vital signs were initially stable and no further hematochezia occurred immediately. Initial management included bowel rest, intravenous fluids, acid suppression, tranexamic acid, and packed red blood cell transfusion.

On hospital day 3, she suddenly passed a large amount of fresh bloody stool and developed altered consciousness. Her blood pressure decreased to 57/35 mmHg. Emergency laboratory testing revealed a red blood cell count of 0.85×1012/L and a hemoglobin level of 26 g/L. She was treated with dual-channel pressurized infusion, vasoactive drugs, packed red blood cells, and fresh frozen plasma. Her systolic blood pressure ranged from 60–90 mmHg, and her consciousness partially improved.

History of past illness

The patient had previously been healthy. She had a history of two pregnancies and one delivery. Her menstrual cycles were regular. She had no history of pancreatitis, abdominal pain, abdominal surgery, blunt abdominal trauma, portal hypertension, primary hypertension, or use of oral anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents. She had no phenotypic features or clinical history suggestive of a heritable connective tissue disorder.

Physical examination

On admission, her body temperature was 36.7 ℃, blood pressure was 117/71 mmHg, heart rate was 96 beats/min, and respiratory rate was 16 breaths/min. She was alert but appeared pale. The abdomen was soft and non-tender, with no signs of peritoneal irritation. Neither the liver nor the spleen was palpable below the costal margin. Bowel sounds were present at a rate of 8/min. Murphy’s sign and shifting dullness were negative.

Laboratory examinations

Initial laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 9.56×109/L, a red blood cell count of 1.84×1012/L, and a hemoglobin concentration of 57 g/L. During hemorrhagic shock, the red blood cell count decreased to 0.85×1012/L, and the hemoglobin concentration decreased to 26 g/L.

Imaging examinations and diagnostic work-up

Because the patient remained hemodynamically unstable and could not initially be transported safely to the radiology suite, bedside colonoscopy was performed first for emergency localization and temporary hemostasis. Emergency bedside colonoscopy revealed a congested and swollen mucosal protrusion at the splenic flexure, with superficial erosion and an adherent white thrombus. No active bleeding was initially observed; however, bright-red blood spurted from the lesion when the thrombus was touched. Six titanium clips were applied for temporary hemostasis, after which active bleeding stopped (Figure 1).

Colonoscopic findings. (A) White thrombus at the splenic flexure (arrow). (B) Endoscopic titanium clips placed at the bleeding site.

After initial resuscitation and improvement following endoscopic clipping, contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic CT was performed. CT revealed a regular, round lesion between the pancreatic tail and splenic flexure, with enhancement similar to that of the adjacent arteries during the arterial and portal phases. Maximum-intensity projection images showed continuity between the lesion and the splenic artery. The aneurysm wall appeared continuous, with a regular contour and no marked perianeurysmal inflammatory changes, suggesting a true SAA; however, definitive differentiation from a pseudoaneurysm required histopathologic examination. Delayed-phase imaging showed increased attenuation in the lumen of the splenic flexure, indicating contrast extravasation into the bowel lumen and suggesting active communication between the aneurysm and the colon. These findings raised the initial suspicion of an SAA-colonic fistula (Figure 2).

Contrast-enhanced abdominal and pelvic computed tomography during the first admission. (A) Maximum-intensity projection image showing the SAA, the SA, SF, and PF. (B) Delayed-phase image showing IC in the splenic flexure. IC, intraluminal contrast; PF, presumed fistulous; SA, splenic artery; SAA, splenic artery aneurysm; SF, splenic flexure.

DSA subsequently demonstrated a 4.2-cm SAA-like dilatation adjacent to the endoscopic clips. No obvious contrast extravasation was observed, likely because bleeding had been temporarily arrested by hypotension and endoscopic clipping. Given persistent hemodynamic instability, the need to minimize the duration of the emergency procedure, and the technical difficulty of safely catheterizing the distal segment beyond the aneurysm, six coils were deployed to occlude the proximal parent splenic artery; neither distal embolization nor aneurysm-sac packing was performed. Completion angiography showed cessation of antegrade flow into the aneurysm, with no contrast extravasation or residual aneurysm opacification (Figure 3). No blood flow was visualized in the distal splenic artery on angiography; however, retrograde collateral perfusion could not be completely excluded.

DSA. (A) The parent splenic artery and 4.2-cm SAA are indicated. (B) After proximal splenic artery coil occlusion, the coil and PA are shown; distal splenic arterial blood flow is not visualized. DSA, digital subtraction angiography; PA, parent artery; SAA, splenic artery aneurysm.

Final diagnosis

The final clinical diagnosis was massive lower gastrointestinal bleeding resulting from rupture of an SAA into the splenic flexure through an SAA-colonic fistula. A true SAA was suspected on imaging and subsequently confirmed by histopathologic examination after surgery. Similarly, the SAA-colonic fistula could not be established by imaging alone; rather the diagnosis was inferred from the presence of intraluminal contrast, the corresponding bleeding site identified on colonoscopy, and the subsequent angiographic relationship between the aneurysm and the endoscopic clips.

Treatment and follow-up imaging

Emergency endoscopic titanium clipping was performed to achieve temporary hemostasis, followed by proximal parent-artery coil embolization under DSA guidance. The patient had no recurrent hematochezia or abdominal pain during the initial hospitalization. After resumption of oral intake, her hemoglobin level increased to 97 g/L, and routine stool and occult blood tests were negative. She was discharged on hospital day 16. Although regular imaging follow-up was recommended, the patient did not undergo follow-up imaging after discharge.

At 34 months after embolization, the patient noticed a metal wire protruding from the anus during defecation but was unable to remove it. Approximately 45 cm of the protruding wire was cut off before she developed moderate-to-severe left upper abdominal pain and returned to our hospital. Physical examination showed a soft abdomen without tenderness or rebound pain. Laboratory tests showed a white blood cell count of 5.45×109/L, a red blood cell count of 4.08×1012/L, a hemoglobin level of 124 g/L, a platelet count of 203×109/L, and a C-reactive protein level of <1 mg/L.

Plain abdominal and pelvic CT showed a high-density embolization coil in the left upper abdomen and a linear high-density structure extending from the descending colon to the pelvic cavity, ending in the sigmoid colon. Volume-rendering reconstruction clearly depicted the migrated coil extending through the colon, supporting delayed transcolonic coil migration through the persistent SAA-colonic fistulous tract (Figure 4). On two-dimensional images, the pelvic portion of the coil appeared to be close to the bladder; however, multiplanar and volume-rendered images localized it to the sigmoid colon. Because the intraluminal foreign body, persistent coil protrusion, and possible SAA reperfusion posed a risk of recurrent massive lower gastrointestinal bleeding, hemorrhagic shock, and potentially fatal complications, definitive surgery was performed after discussion with the patient and her family.

Abdominal and pelvic computed tomography after splenic artery aneurysm embolization. (A,B) Scout image and volume-rendering reconstruction showing the compact coil mass in the left upper abdomen, MLS in the descending/sigmoid colon, PR, and ESC. ESC, extension toward the sigmoid colon; MLS, migrated linear structure; PR, presumed route of migration.

After multidisciplinary review, the patient underwent laparoscopic splenectomy, resection of the aneurysm and fistulous tract, partial colectomy, and intraoperative colonoscopic removal of the residual wire. Severe adhesions around the SAA and preoperative imaging findings demonstrating an extensive SAA-colonic fistula suggested that spleen-preserving surgery might not adequately preserve splenic perfusion. In addition, surgical exposure required division of a portion of the gastrosplenic ligament and several short gastric vessels, after which the spleen showed marked ischemic changes. After discussion with the patient’s family, splenectomy was performed.

Pathological findings

Histopathology showed variable thickening and distortion of the splenic arterial wall, with preserved arterial wall layers on elastic-fiber staining, supporting a true aneurysm rather than a pseudoaneurysm. The fistulous tract and adjacent tissues showed chronic inflammatory cell infiltration and fibrous adhesion around the coil pathway. No gross abscess was identified. These findings confirmed a true SAA with chronic SAA-colonic fistulization.

Outcome

The patient recovered well after surgery. At the 3-month follow-up, she reported no abdominal pain, hematochezia, or other symptoms.

Discussion

SAA is defined as a focal dilatation of the splenic artery exceeding 50% of its normal diameter. Anatomically, SAAs can be classified as distal hilar, proximal hilar, or intermediate types. Distal hilar aneurysms arise from the main splenic artery trunk more than 5 cm from the spleen, while proximal hilar aneurysms arise from branches at the splenic hilum and may extend into the splenic parenchyma, and intermediate aneurysms exhibit features between these two types (5). Histologically, SAAs are classified as true aneurysms or pseudoaneurysms. True aneurysms retain the three-layer structure of the arterial wall, whereas pseudoaneurysms lack normal arterial wall architecture and are typically surrounded by fibrous tissue. Certain imaging features may also help differentiate true aneurysms from pseudoaneurysms (6).

The etiology and risk factors of SAA are diverse and include pathological factors such as arterial degeneration, portal hypertension, and local inflammation, physiological factors such as pregnancy and multiparity, and iatrogenic factors related to invasive procedures or abdominal surgery (7,8). Most SAAs are asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally. Some patients develop abdominal pain, abdominal distension, or a pulsatile abdominal mass as the aneurysm enlarges. Rupture is the most dangerous complication. Patients may present with severe abdominal pain, peritoneal irritation, and hemorrhagic shock. When an SAA forms a fistula with adjacent structures, hemorrhage may enter the gastrointestinal tract, pancreatic duct, or biliary tract, causing gastrointestinal bleeding, pancreatitis, or jaundice depending on the site of communication.

This case highlights the difficulty of diagnosing ruptured SAA when the initial manifestation is intermittent hematochezia. The patient underwent evaluation at several hospitals without identification of a definite bleeding source. Conventional endoscopy may fail because the lesion is extraluminal and bleeding may be intermittent. In this setting, contrast-enhanced CT is particularly valuable because it can simultaneously identify an enhancing vascular lesion, demonstrate its arterial continuity, and detect active extravasation into the bowel lumen. In the present case, maximum-intensity projection images demonstrated continuity between the lesion and the splenic artery, and delayed-phase CT showed contrast material within the splenic flexure. These findings were pivotal in localizing the source of the lower gastrointestinal bleeding and raising suspicion of an SAA-colonic fistula.

The reported frequency of rupture among diagnosed SAAs varies across heterogeneous series and populations, as it is influenced by multiple factors, including aneurysm type, aneurysm diameter, patient characteristics, and clinical presentation (9). In a large institutional series, approximately 5% of diagnosed SAAs were ruptured at presentation, with ruptured aneurysms being larger on average than unruptured aneurysms (10). The rupture of an SAA may result from multiple factors, including persistent high pressure within the aneurysm, high-flow hemodynamic stress, pregnancy, pancreatitis, abdominal trauma, infection, and connective tissue disorders. Based on the clinical characteristics of this patient, we speculate that the rupture and bleeding were caused by the combined effects of the relatively large aneurysm size, local inflammatory adhesion, and pulsatile erosion.

Treatment is indicated for ruptured or symptomatic true SAAs. For unruptured true SAAs, management is individualized according to aneurysm diameter and growth, pregnancy or childbearing potential, portal hypertension, and other patient factors. Pseudoaneurysms lack the normal arterial wall structure and therefore carry a higher risk of rupture and poorer outcomes after rupture; prompt treatment is thus recommended once the diagnosis is established (11,12).

Treatment options include endovascular intervention and definitive surgery, which may be performed using open or laparoscopic approaches. Endovascular therapy is generally favored in patients with severe clinical conditions or high perioperative risk, provided that there are no contraindications to intervention. The specific endovascular technique should be selected according to aneurysm size, morphology, and anatomical location (12). Coil embolization, which has a reported technical success rate of approximately 90–100%, is widely used because the spleen has an extensive collateral blood supply, including through the short gastric arteries (13). Important complications include splenic infarction, aneurysm reperfusion, and coil migration; therefore, regular post-embolization surveillance is necessary.

The choice of surgery depends on aneurysm location, rupture status, hemodynamic stability, and involvement of adjacent organs. Splenectomy is commonly performed for ruptured SAAs and for aneurysms involving the splenic hilum (14). Surgical repair has a reported success rate of up to 98%, with low rates of postoperative complications and lower recurrence, although operative risk increases in patients with unstable hemodynamics or severe comorbidities (15). Laparoscopic treatment is feasible in hemodynamically stable patients and may reduce complication rates, operative time, and hospital stay compared with open surgery (16,17).

In the present case, emergency coil embolization was appropriate during the first admission because the patient had recurrent hemorrhagic shock and hemodynamic instability. However, a persistent fistulous tract may have contributed to subsequent coil migration. This case therefore reports a staged strategy involving emergency endovascular embolization to control bleeding, followed by careful imaging surveillance and consideration of definitive surgical treatment when a persistent fistula, aneurysm reperfusion, or coil migration is suspected.

Delayed coil migration was the most distinctive imaging finding in the present case. With the increasing use of endovascular therapy for SAAs and other vascular diseases, embolization-related complications have become increasingly recognized. Coil migration can result from aneurysm morphology, local hemodynamic forces, tissue fragility, coil size, or procedural factors (18). The overall reported incidence of coil migration is approximately 2–6%, while delayed migration is much rarer (19). Clinical manifestations and management strategies vary widely. Depending on the symptoms, the extent of migration, and the risk of recurrent bleeding, patients may be managed with close observation, endovascular retrieval, or surgery (20).

Delayed coil migration into the gastrointestinal tract is rare but has been reported after embolization of visceral arterial aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms (21-23). Proposed mechanisms include persistent fistulous communication, residual or recurrent perfusion, retrograde collateral flow, chronic inflammation, foreign-body reaction, pressure erosion, and coil configuration.

In the present case, operative and pathological findings demonstrated a persistent fibrotic, and chronically inflamed SAA-colonic fistula, and CT showed the coil extending along this fistulous tract into the colon. The most plausible mechanism was gradual erosion and migration of the coil through the pre-existing fistulous tract. Because interval imaging was not performed, earlier reperfusion, progressive coil displacement, or collateral flow could not be evaluated and thus could not be excluded. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of delayed coil migration through an SAA-colonic fistula following embolization of a true SAA.

SAA-colonic fistulization may develop through repeated minor leakage or contained rupture, followed by chronic inflammatory adhesion between the aneurysm and the adjacent colon. Persistent pulsatile pressure, local ischemia, and pressure necrosis can then erode the adherent colonic wall, resulting in fistulous communication. Published reports predominantly describe splenic artery pseudoaneurysms related to pancreatitis or pseudocysts communicating with the transverse colon or stomach; while true SAA-colonic fistulization is much rarer (21,22). The present case is distinctive because histopathology confirmed a true SAA, the fistula initially presented as massive lower gastrointestinal bleeding, and the persistent fistulous tract subsequently served as the route for transcolonic coil migration.

This case also illustrates the potential consequences of absent postoperative surveillance. There is currently no consensus regarding postoperative surveillance for patients with an SAA-colonic fistula. We believe that follow-up should be long-term, close, and regular. For imaging surveillance, we recommend CTA. For patients with SAA-colonic fistulas treated by emergency embolization, we propose initial follow-up imaging before discharge or within 1 month to establish a baseline, followed by repeat imaging at approximately 3–6 months and 12 months, and annually thereafter for at least 3 years; longer follow-up is reasonable when the fistula persists, the aneurysm is incompletely excluded, or embolization coils are located adjacent to the bowel. New gastrointestinal bleeding, pain, fever, or suspected device displacement should prompt immediate imaging and surgical review. Patient education should also be strengthened to improve adherence to postoperative surveillance and reduce the risk of delayed complications, as illustrated by this case, in which the patient did not undergo follow-up after embolization. Given the extreme rarity of this condition, the proposed follow-up schedule should be considered a reference.

Conclusions

A true SAA-colonic fistula is an exceptionally rare but life-threatening cause of lower gastrointestinal bleeding. In patients with unexplained massive hematochezia, particularly when bleeding originates near the splenic flexure, early contrast-enhanced CT, CTA, or DSA should be considered. Emergency embolization can stabilize an unstable patient, but it should not automatically be regarded as definitive therapy when a fistulous tract persists. After hemostasis, structured imaging surveillance and early multidisciplinary assessment for staged definitive surgical resection should be considered to reduce the risks of recurrent bleeding, infection, and delayed coil migration.
