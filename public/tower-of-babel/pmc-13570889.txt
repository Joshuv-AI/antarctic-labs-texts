On the correlation between mechanical and electrical properties of pure and Ag doped ZnO nanoparticles
Authors: Mansour Mohamed, Yasser A. M. Ismail, M. Bouzidi, Z. R. Khan, A. Sedky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present here structural, mechanical, and electrical properties at different values of temperatures (30–150 °C) for Zn1-xAgxO nanoparticles (NPs) with x = 0.00, 0.02, and 0.04. All NPs samples show wurtzite structure, tensile stress, and brittle character. Ag doping resulted in a slight increase in crystallite size, along with reduction in dislocation density, micro-strain, specific surface area, porosity, particle size, and M–O spacing. The FTIR spectra exhibit various bands between 3450.69 and 411.3 cm−1 for all samples, with most shifting to lower wavenumbers as x increases. With increasing Ag doping content up to 0.04, the Debye temperature, effective mass, elastic moduli, hardness, and wave velocities all gradually increased. The dielectric constant, dielectric loss, AC conductivity, and F-factor increase with increasing temperature (T) or doping content (x). In addition, the activation energy of the charge carriers, determined using the Arrhenius equation, decreased with increasing x or f, ranging from 0.187 to 0.68 eV across all samples. In general, no relaxation peaks are observed in the tan δ curves for x = 0.00 and x = 0.02 across the entire temperature range; however, for x = 0.04, clear relaxation peaks appear at 110 °C and 150 °C. The relaxation peaks are also evident in the electric modulus (M) and impedance (Z) curves for all samples, with their peak frequencies increasing with T or x. The frequency exponent (0.05 < s ≤ 0.55) indicates the conduction mechanism is the correlated barrier hopping. Meanwhile, the binding energy (0.163–0.345 eV) decreases as either T or x increases. The Cole–Cole plots reveal ideal semi-circular arcs corresponding to parallel RC representations for all samples. The impedance of grains, grain boundaries, and the series resistance (ranging from 2 × 104 to 108 Ω) decrease with increasing x or T, whereas the effective capacitance increases (0.005–38.73 µF). These findings establish a correlation between the mechanical and dielectric properties and highlight the promising potential of Ag-doped ZnO nanoparticles for further exploration in electronic and energy-related applications.

Introduction

The ZnO wurtzite (hexagonal) octahedral (6-fold) structure is an n-type semiconductor with lattice constants of a = 3.382 Å and c = 5.502 Å, space group P63mc, and 3d104s2 and 2s22p4 electronic configurations for Zn and O, respectively1–3. The wurtzite structure of ZnO is formed when the O2− atoms are arranged in a close-packed type with Zn2+ occupying half the tetrahedral sites. During the synthesis of ZnO, some intrinsic defects could be formed due to oxygen vacancies (Ov), which usually introduce donor states and tune its properties">4–8. Additionally, the properties of ZnO could be controlled through the replacement of Zn2+ ions by 3d transition metals (3d-TM) through individual doping or a composite6,9–11. The broadening of the XRD peaks of ZnO has shown that ZnO is not a completely perfect crystal due to its finite size, and such deviation from the perfect can be evaluated through structural likes crystallite size, micro-strain, lattice strain, and residual stress12,13.

The presence of more than one valence state on TM during the ZnO doping process suggests that polar-ionic conduction is attributed to a hopping mechanism of electrons from lower to higher valence states14. It is found that the interior defects such as Zn-site vacancies and oxygen vacancies (Ov) induced during the synthesis of ZnO NPs have a great influence on the mechanism and properties of ZnO. The proper selection of dopant ions and their content play a very crucial role in achieving the desired physical characteristics. For example, the dielectric properties of ZnO could be tuned by introducing dopants into the ZnO host matrix with 3d-TMs such as Ni, Co, Cr, Fe, Cu, V, Al, Mn, etc.">15–19. Silver (Ag) is classified as a transition metal; however, unlike the commonly investigated 3d transition metals (such as Ni, Co, Fe, and Mn), Ag belongs to the 4d-block with an electronic configuration of [Kr]4d105s1. While Ag shares transitional properties with 3d metals, its filled 4d orbital distinguishes it from the 3d transition metals typically studied in ZnO doping. It is often grouped with Cu and Au, such that Ag fills its 4d orbital rather than 3d20. Anyhow, Ag+1 of the [Kr] 4d9 5 s−1 configuration is the most common oxidation state compared to Ag+2, since it has an unpaired electron, which, despite this, displays many of the TMs, such as Zn, as presented here.

It has further been reported that the transition energy for Ag is lower than that of other Group IB elements such as Cu and Au ions because Ag creates a shallow acceptor level in ZnO, making Ag-doped ZnO useful for achieving p-doping in ZnO21. Furthermore, the difference in ionic radii between Zn (0.74 Å) and Ag+1 (0.63–1.11 Å) indicates that the Ag ion may enter into the ZnO lattice. Moreover, the incorporation of the Ag+ ions in place of Zn in ZnO NPs causes an imbalance of charges in the ZnO lattice, leading to lattice defects in the form of Ov and enhanced electron–hole pair separation in the NPs, as reported for Ag-doped TiO4 NPs22. However, some of the previous studies reported that Ag ions occupy substitutional or interstitial sites within the ZnO host lattice23, while other findings24 indicated that substitutional sites were energetically favourable compared to interstitial ones, and the final response mainly depends on the Ag content.

The absorption bands of ZnO due to vibration of the lattice are due to metal oxides arising from interatomic vibrations, stretching and deformation of O–H assigned to water adsorption on the metal surface, and some other bands due to Zn–O stretching and deformation vibrations25. However, FTIR spectra assess a number of thermal and mechanical parameters, including Debye temperature, elastic modulus, hardness, and elastic wave velocities26,27. The elastic modulus tests the NPs’ capacity against elastic deformation, whereas the elastic wave velocities are useful in drug delivery, comprehending and reducing NP pollution, and dispersion or accumulation in specific regions.

The electronic traps of ZnO are mainly localized at the grain boundaries and usually adsorb oxygen and capture some donor state electrons, and consequently, the Schottky barrier of the deep trap states generated in the depletion layer is found to be frequency dependent28,29 Ac impedance spectroscopy is able to separate the electric response in different regions of ZnO within the range of time constant τ (τ = (1/ω) = RC)30. This response is entirely characterized by the complex dielectric parameters versus the frequency range of 0–10 GHz31. However, materials with high dielectric constants can be used in telecommunications, discrete, and microwave integrated circuits32,33. In contrast, materials of low dielectric constant are potential candidates for nonlinear optical and high-frequency devices34,35.

However, Koop’s theory indicated that solid material usually consists of conducting grains dominant at high f separated by boundaries of poor conductivity at low f such that the current flows along parallel alignments of grains36–38. In addition, the f-dependent ac conductivity follows Jonscher’s power law, indicating a hopping mechanism and non-Debye relaxation and being convenient for solar cell efficiency19. The development of a good dielectric medium of high values of ε\ and q-factor up to 106 and loss of 10 is necessary39,40. It can also give an idea for the energy storage attitude against dielectric response, but it is mainly controlled by the type and content of doping and synthetic methods. Therefore, the effects of doping frequency as well as temperature on behaviour of dielectric mediums to be used in superconductors and solar cell devices, for example, are necessary41,42.

The correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model43 is a conduction mechanism theory used to describe how charge carriers (electrons or ions) move through a material by hopping over energy barriers between localized defect sites. During the hopping, the carriers change the local charge distribution, which in turn affects the height of the barriers against the other carriers. At lower temperatures, the conduction occurs via a bi-polaron hopping process wherein two electrons simultaneously hop over the potential barrier between the states of charged defects (D+ and D−), where the barrier height is correlated with the spacing between them. While at high temperature, some of the Do states are produced by thermal excitation of D+ and D− defect states, and therefore a single polaron hopping becomes a dominant process. (e-hopping between Do and D+, whereas h-hopping between Do and D−)44.

The dielectric properties of the Ag-doped ZnO NCs have revealed that the incorporation of 20% of Ag-doped ZnO NPs increased the dielectric constant of ZnO by 100 times at 100 Hz, with minimal tangent loss in Ag/ZnO (20%)45. The Cole–Cole plots (Nyquist plot) disclosed the presence of two relaxation processes with different relaxation times. In contrast, ZnO showed more resistive grains and grain boundaries than Ag/ZnO (10%) and Ag/ZnO (15%), demonstrating similar impedance characteristics of grain and grain boundaries. The effects of Ag addition on the electrical properties of ZnO have been investigated44. It is found that a small amount of Ag, around 0.08 mol% or slightly higher, can dissolve into the ZnO lattice. The presence of Ag solutes increases the rates of densification and grain growth. The Ag+1 solute tend to segregate at the grain boundaries and significantly raise the grain boundary resistance and establish an electrostatic barrier against electron transportation. The barrier is approximately 2 V for a single grain boundary.

A similar trend has been reported for Ag-doped ZnO NPs46. At different frequencies and temperatures, under specific conditions, the dielectric properties like dielectric constant, dielectric loss, and AC conductivity of Ag-doped ZnO NPs were analysed. DC electrical conductivity of Ag-doped ZnO NPs has been enhanced with an increase in temperature range (300–650 K)47, while AC conductivity was decreased with Ag concentration at RT. The dielectric constant and dielectric loss tangent were also reduced with increased frequency, while AC conductivity was enhanced with increased frequency at room temperature. In contrast to ZnO, the Ag doping within TiO2 NPs was found to decrease the dielectric constant, dielectric loss, and complex impedance while increasing the AC conductivity as a function of frequency22. Silver (Ag)-doped zinc oxide (ZnO) NPs with different compositions, Zn1-xAgxO (x = 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, 0.10), have been reported48. It is found that dielectric properties and alternating current conductivity of ZnO NPs were significantly affected by Ag doping.

Despite extensive research on Ag-doped ZnO nanoparticles, the existing literature reveals critical gaps: most studies have focused on structural, optical, and photocatalytic properties, with mechanical and thermal parameters receiving marginal attention, while dielectric investigations have been predominantly conducted at room temperature and limited to narrow frequency ranges. Most importantly, no previous study has systematically explored the correlation between mechanical properties and dielectric behavior in Ag-doped ZnO nanoparticles using the same batch of samples. The present work uniquely addresses these gaps by providing a comprehensive investigation of mechanical parameters including Debye temperature, elastic moduli, hardness, and wave velocities and correlating them with dielectric properties across a wide frequency range (0.1–20 MHz) and temperature range (30–150 °C). This integrated approach reveals, for the first time, that Ag doping simultaneously enhances mechanical strength (increased elastic moduli and hardness) while improving dielectric performance (increased dielectric constant, AC conductivity, and effective capacitance reaching 38.73 µF), establishing a fundamental structure property relationship. These findings provide a comprehensive framework for designing multifunctional oxide nanomaterials for advanced electronic and energy-related applications.

Experimental

The Zn1-xAgxO with x = 0.00, 0.02, and 0.04 NPs were synthesized via the co-precipitation method as reported in our recent data49. For the synthesis of ZnO NPs, 0.1 M of zinc acetate dihydrate [Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O] was dissolved in 200 ml of distilled water in a round-bottom flask. In a similar manner, NaOH pellets were dissolved in 200 ml of distilled water to form 0.2 M NaOH solution. For doped samples (Zn1-xAgxO with x = 0.02 and 0.04), appropriate amounts of silver acetate [Ag(C2H3O2)] were added to maintain the total metal concentration at 0.1 M while substituting Ag for Zn: for x = 0.02, 0.067 g (0.0004 mol) of silver acetate; for x = 0.04, 0.134 g (0.0008 mol) of silver acetate. The NaOH solution was added dropwise to the metal acetate solution at a rate of 2 ml/min under continuous stirring at 800 rpm. The pH was maintained at approximately 10.5 ± 0.2. The mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 2 h. The resulting precipitate was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min. The precipitates were washed three times with distilled water and once with acetone, with each wash followed by centrifugation. This washing procedure was repeated four times. The final precipitate was dried at 80 °C for 6 h, ground in an agate mortar, and annealed at 450 °C for 2 h in air with a heating rate of 5 °C/min and natural cooling to room temperature. Identical thermal histories were used for all samples. The powder X-ray diffraction (Philips PW-1700, Netherlands, with Cu-Kα radiation) operating at 40 kV and 30 mA within the angle range (20–80°) is used to investigate the structural properties. The TEM micrographs are well investigated by a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM) model JEM-2100, JEOL, Japan, operated at 200 kV. The samples’ FTIR absorption spectra were repeated further to record the spectra in the region of 400–600 cm−1, which was absent in our previous data, using a PerkinElmer Spectrum 400 FT-IR, FT-NIR spectrometer in the range of 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and a scanning speed of 2 cm/s. The dielectric measurements, at different frequencies (0.1–20 MHz) and temperatures of 30, 70, 110, and 150 °C, of pure and Ag-doped ZnO NPs are measured using broadband dielectric spectroscopy (BDS), utilizing a high-resolution Alpha analyser with an active sample head (Novo Control GmbH). In parallel plate geometry, the pellets are sandwiched between two gold-plated stainless-steel electrodes 20 mm in diameter. The BDS was assisted by a Quatro temperature controller using pure nitrogen as a heating agent and assuring temperature stability better than 0.2 K. The AC applied voltage is 1 V, and the diameter is 8 mm for all samples. The density and thickness are (5.56 g/cm3, 0.481 mm), (5.64 g/cm3, 0.453 mm), and (5.73 g/cm3, 0.463 mm) for the x = 0.00, 0.02, and 0.04 samples, respectively. The empty cell of similar dimensions is taken as a reference in order to minimize the error of measurements to be less than 1%. To prevent temperature drift, many setups rely on a step-and-measure approach. You heat the sample at a standard 1 K/min to 10 K/min rate, wait for the temperature to stabilize, and then perform the scan. The dielectric parameters reported represent the average of consistent readings obtained during each measurement run, with typical instrumental fluctuations within ± 5%.

Results and discussion

Figure 1 shows the XRD pattern of the samples, in which the XRD lines of x = 0.02 and 0.04 show nearly similar peaks of the same positions as well as appear for the pattern of the undoped ZnO NPs (x = 0.00). The XRD depicts typical ZnO crystal planes of the hexagonal wurtzite ZnO phase (Card No. 00–036-1451)50,51. But the sharp intensity and narrow diffraction peaks are increased by increasing the doping of Ag content, indicating an improvement of the crystallinity of the doped samples. This behaviour indicates that Ag occupies the Zn sites within the ZnO lattice with a well-crystalline structure, thereby decreasing the number of defects and enhancing the ZnO overall crystallinity. Furthermore, an expanded view of the (101) diffraction peak (inset) reveals a non-monotonic shift with increasing Ag content. For x = 0.02, the (101) peak shifts to slightly lower 2θ angles, indicating a lattice expansion along the corresponding crystallographic direction. This expansion is consistent with the substitution of Zn2+ (ionic radius 0.74 Å in tetrahedral coordination) by the larger Ag+ ion (ionic radius 1.02 Å). However, for x = 0.04, the peak shifts back to higher angles (36.22°), suggesting a lattice contraction despite the higher Ag concentration. This non-monotonic behavior can be attributed to a combination of competing effects: For x = 0.02: at low doping concentration, Ag+ ions predominantly substitute Zn2+ sites, introducing lattice expansion due to the larger ionic radius. This substitution creates oxygen vacancies (Ov) to maintain charge neutrality. The formation of oxygen vacancies provides additional lattice relaxation that may further contribute to the observed expansion. For x = 0.04: at higher Ag concentration, the accumulation of Ag at grain boundaries may introduce compressive stress. Additionally, the increased oxygen vacancy concentration may lead to lattice distortion and defect clustering that effectively reduces the average lattice parameter.Fig. 1XRD patterns of the pure and doped Zn1-xAgxO nanoparticles

Additionally, to confirm the incorporation of Ag into the ZnO lattice and determine its chemical state, we performed Rietveld refinement of the XRD data (see Fig. 2). The refinement was conducted using the FullProf suite with the wurtzite structure as the starting model. The refined lattice parameters, reliability factors (Rwp), goodness-of-fit (χ2), are summarized in Table 1. The refinement results show that all samples maintain the hexagonal wurtzite structure with no detectable secondary phases. The lattice parameters exhibit subtle variations with Ag doping: for x = 0.02, a slight increase in a-parameter (from 3.247018 Å to 3.247275 Å) and c parameter (from 5.202165 to 5.202234 Å) is observed, consistent with the substitution of Zn2+ (0.74 Å) by the larger Ag+ ion (1.02 Å). This can be attributed to competing effects: (i) lattice expansion due to Ag+ substitution at Zn sites, and (ii) lattice contraction or compensation effects arising from defect complex formation, grain boundary segregation, or the formation of minor Ag-rich phases at higher doping concentrations. Such non-monotonic behavior has been previously reported for Ag-doped ZnO systems46 and other transition metal-doped ZnO systems10. Figure 3 shows the TEM images and electron diffraction pattern (SEAD) of the samples, in which the SEAD pattern reveals rings of white and sharpness spots that prove the high degree of crystallinity for the doped samples as obtained from XRD. The ZnO NPs display larger, more agglomerated nanoparticles of nanosphere and nanorod-like shapes. Particle size histograms (Fig. 3) were constructed by analyzing TEM images. The histograms reveal Gaussian-like distributions with mean particle sizes of 122 nm (x = 0.00), 59.54 nm (x = 0.02), and 47.03 (x = 0.04), confirming the systematic decrease in particle size with Ag doping. In addition, an interesting observation is the opposite trends in crystallite size (D) and particle size (PZ) with Ag doping: D increases from 22.83 to 25.91 nm, while PZ decreases from 122 to 47.03 nm. This behavior is explained by two competing effects. First, Ag ions incorporated at Zn sites promote atomic rearrangement and reduce defects during annealing, facilitating the growth of larger coherent crystalline domains, as reflected in the reduced micro-strain and dislocation density. Second, Ag ions that segregate to grain boundaries or particle surfaces act as barriers that hinder particle coalescence and agglomeration, resulting in smaller overall particle sizes. The TEM images support this interpretation, clearly showing less agglomeration and more dispersed particles with increasing Ag content Furthermore, The SAED patterns show rings of white and sharpness spots that confirm the high degree of crystallinity for the doped samples, consistent with the XRD results. Notably, an increase in bright contrast features (white spots) is observed with increasing Ag dopant concentration. These features may be attributed to several factors: (i) local strain fields arising from the substitution of Zn2+ (0.74 Å) with Ag+ (0.63–1.11 Å) causing lattice distortion, (ii) possible surface segregation of Ag atoms at grain boundaries, or (iii) the presence of Ag-rich clusters that are too small to be detected by XRD. The overall crystallinity enhancement is supported by the increased intensity and narrowing of XRD peaks, as well as the sharp SAED spots, which indicate improved long-range atomic ordering despite these local contrast variations.Fig. 3TEM images, SEAD and histogram plots of the pure and doped Zn1-xAgxO nanoparticles

Regardless of the effects of frequency and temperatures, the successful synthesis of Ag to ZnO NPs has confirmed that the Ag could exhibit an increase in most of the thermal, mechanical, and dielectric properties of ZnO NPs, such as Debye temperature, elastic moduli, hardness, wave velocities, dielectric constant, dielectric loss, AC conductivity, and F-factor. In contrast, the q-factor and activation energy of charge carriers was decreased. The dielectric data analysis approved the correlated barrier hopping for the conduction mechanism corresponding with a decrease of binding energy. A parallel representation of resistance and capacitance was obtained from the Cole–Cole plots for all samples where the impedance of grains and their boundaries and series resistance decreased, whereas the effective capacitance increased.

Conversely, an inverse correlation was obtained against x for the behaviours Ea, Z\g and Rs, which in turn is consistent with those obtained for the PZ and PS.

These results reflecting enhanced charge carrier mobility and confirming semiconducting behaviour, suggesting improved charge carrier mobility and promising for some of potential applications. However, under the application of an external alternating electric field, the charge carriers can easily migrate across the grains, but their motion is hindered at the grain boundaries. In ZnO, grain boundaries are considered resistive compared to grains because of their trapping nature due to uncompensated bonds and non-stoichiometric oxygen composition at the grain-grain interface. These insulating grain boundaries act as a thin dielectric layer between relatively conducting grains, exhibiting higher capacitance due to their low thickness. Therefore, the arcs can be associated with the impedance of grain and grain boundaries. Pristine ZnO showed the largest arc diameters, indicating relatively more resistive grains and grain boundaries as compared to the Ag-doped ZnO NPs, implying the conducting nature for Ag-doped ZnO NPs. Our strange point is based on the increase of dielectric constant and dielectric loss with increasing Ag, although most dielectric parameters related to grain and their boundaries, such as impedance and binding energy, are decreased. It has been reported45 that the increase of dielectric constant and loss could be attributed to the formation of nano/micro capacitors due to the presence of metallic Ag on the surface of ZnO NPs or electrode polarization effect, which enhances the formation of these capacitors and helps for increasing both of them. In other words98, dielectric loss increases in Ag-doped ZnO NPs due to enhanced charge carrier concentration and defects (vacancies/interstitials) that cause dipole relaxation, while binding energy decreases because Ag substitution for Zn weakens the Zn–O lattice structure and lowers the energy required for electron transfer, accelerating dipole relaxation. However, the present investigation opens the way for further research based on similar types of pure and Ag-doped ZnO NPs.

Additionally, to quantitatively assess the relationship between mechanical and electrical properties, we performed linear regression analysis on four key parameter pairs (Fig. 19). The correlation between Young’s modulus and dielectric constant shows a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.82), indicating that enhanced interatomic bonding simultaneously promotes higher dielectric response. The correlation between hardness and AC conductivity is moderate (R2 = 0.66), suggesting that improved crystallinity contributes to both mechanical strength and charge carrier mobility, though other factors such as defect states also play a role. Remarkably, the correlation between porosity and effective capacitance is nearly perfect (R2 = 1.00), confirming that porosity reduction is the primary mechanism behind the significant capacitance enhancement with Ag doping. Finally, the correlation between particle size and activation energy is moderate (R2 = 0.50), indicating that smaller particles facilitate charge carrier hopping, though other factors such as defect concentration also influence the activation energy. Conclusions

In this work, we systematically investigated the structural, mechanical, and electrical properties of pure and Ag-doped ZnO nanoparticles (Zn1-xAgxO with x = 0.00, 0.02, and 0.04) across a wide frequency range (0.1–20 MHz) and temperature range (30–150 °C). Structurally, all samples exhibit the hexagonal wurtzite structure with tensile stress and brittle character. Ag doping increases crystallite size (from 22.83 to 25.91 nm) while reducing dislocation density, micro-strain, specific surface area, porosity, and particle size. A non-monotonic shift in the (101) XRD peak suggests complex defect chemistry involving Ag substitution and grain boundary segregation at higher doping levels. Mechanically, Ag incorporation systematically enhances the Debye temperature (from 610.87 to 632.57 K), elastic moduli (Y: from 4.96 to 5.71 × 1012 D/cm2), hardness (from 2.29 to 4.51 × 1011 D/cm2), and elastic wave velocities. These improvements are attributed to increased density, reduced porosity, and stronger interatomic bonding. Dielectrically, all parameters (ε’, ε’’, tan δ, σ_ac) increase with both temperature and Ag content, attributed to enhanced interfacial and space charge polarization. The CBH conduction mechanism is confirmed with frequency exponent (0.05 < s < 0.55) and binding energy decreasing with temperature and doping (0.163–0.345 eV). Relaxation peaks appear in M’’ and Z’’ curves, with peak frequencies shifting to higher values with increasing temperature or doping. Impedance analysis reveals single semicircular arcs in Cole–Cole plots. Grain and grain boundary impedance, along with series resistance (2 × 104 → 10⁸ Ω), decreases with temperature and doping, while effective capacitance increases significantly (0.005 → 38.73 µF). The activation energy decreases with frequency and doping (0.187–0.680 eV), consistent with enhanced conductivity. Correlation analysis establishes strong links between mechanical parameters (elastic moduli, hardness, porosity) and electrical properties (dielectric constant, AC conductivity, activation energy, grain boundary resistance, effective capacitance), demonstrating that the same microstructural features that strengthen the material also enhance its electrical performance. These findings establish a correlation between the mechanical and dielectric properties and highlight the promising potential of Ag-doped ZnO nanoparticles for advanced technological applications.
