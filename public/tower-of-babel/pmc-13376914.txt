A synergistic framework integrating CPO-VMD with BiLSTM-TimesNet for accurate prediction of nonlinear and nonstationary runoff time series
Authors: Dong-mei Xu, Qian Wang, Wen-chuan Wang, Kun-mei Luo, Zong Li, Miao Gu, Qi-qi Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate runoff prediction is essential for effective water resource management, yet the complex characteristics of runoff sequences—such as nonlinearity, non-stationarity, and intricate temporal dependencies—pose significant challenges. This study proposes a hybrid prediction framework that integrates the Crested Porcupine Optimizer (CPO), Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM), and Temporal 2D-Variation Modeling for General Time Series Analysis (TimesNet). With the minimum envelope entropy as its objective, the CPO adaptively optimizes key VMD parameters to decompose raw sequences efficiently, generating refined input features for subsequent models. BiLSTM captures bidirectional temporal dependencies, and TimesNet performs multi-scale periodic feature analysis, thereby constituting a feature learning framework. The proposed hybrid model aggregates predictions from each sequence component to deliver accurate runoff forecasts. The study utilizes daily runoff data from the Quinebaug River in the United States and the Elbe River in Germany for validation (Quinebaug: 1997–2001; Elbe: 2019–2022). Comparative analysis shows that the proposed model outperformed the benchmark models across all evaluation metrics. Taking the Quinebaug station as an example, compared to the LSTM model, the Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency coefficient (NSE) improved by 16.15%, the Kling-Gupta efficiency coefficient (KGE) improved by 19.34%, the root mean square error (RMSE) decreased by 60.38%, and the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) decreased by 72.56%. The results indicate that this model possesses significant advantages in forecasting nonlinear, non-stationary runoff series and can effectively improve prediction accuracy and stability. Although there is room for improvement in terms of model interpretability, the three-stage collaborative framework of “parameter optimization–signal decomposition–deep modeling” proposed in this study provides a reference technical solution for forecasting complex hydrological time series.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52745-8.Keywords: Runoff prediction, Variational modal decomposition, Crested porcupine optimizer, Deep learning modelSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Research background and review on methodology

Accurate runoff forecasting plays a critical role in the optimal allocation of water resources1 and the efficient operation of water conservancy projects2, as well as in the protection of river health and the maintenance of ecological balance3,4. However, under the combined influence of multiple factors such as global climate change5,6 and intensifying anthropogenic perturbations7, runoff patterns have become increasingly complex8. This complexity is typically manifested in runoff series as pronounced nonlinearity, non-stationarity, and intricate temporal dependencies, which create substantial obstacles for accurate predictions9,10.

Current runoff prediction models are primarily categorized into process-driven and data-driven types11,56. Process-driven models are constructed based on the physical mechanisms of the hydrological cycle, but their performance heavily relies on high-quality and complete input data. In contrast, data-driven models do not require explicit representation of complex physical processes and instead learn the mapping function directly from data, though their performance can be constrained by data bottlenecks. With advantages of simplicity and strong adaptability, these models perform well in most runoff prediction tasks12,13 and thus are increasingly accepted and prevalent in practical applications14,15.

Among various data-driven models, deep learning (DL) models have emerged as a cutting-edge technology for runoff prediction due to their robust feature learning and sequence modeling capabilities16,17. Among these, the superiority of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network lies in its unique gating mechanism, enabling the effective capture of long-term dependencies within time series18. Through comparative studies, LSTM models demonstrate higher accuracy and robustness in predicting runoff in catchments without gauging stations compared to traditional hydrological models19. However, this advantage is offset by structural limitations in practical applications:

Although LSTM models perform well in time series forecasting, their unidirectional processing mechanism relies primarily on historical information and struggles to effectively incorporate future-informed context20. When dealing with the complexity of watershed hydrological processes and long-range temporal dependencies in runoff sequences, this structural limitation poses significant challenges for runoff prediction tasks21,22. To overcome this limitation, Graves and Schmidhuber proposed BiLSTM, which combines forward and backward LSTM layers to effectively integrate past and future information20. Consequently, for data with time-series characteristics, BiLSTM delivers superior performance compared to unidirectional neural network models23. However, the significant periodicity and multi-scale fluctuations inherent in runoff processes make it challenging for the aforementioned prediction methods to explicitly capture these core patterns. To address the shortcomings of the aforementioned method, Wu et al.24 pioneered the formal integration of multi-periodicity with two-dimensional temporal variations, proposing the TimesNet model. Systematic experiments validated its capability for both long-term and short-term forecasting tasks24. Peng, et al.25 employed TimesNet as the core architecture of Bayesian TimesNet to transform one-dimensional time series data into two-dimensional tensors, extracting multi-scale features that significantly enhanced the accuracy of multi-step groundwater level forecasting. This approach holds broad applicability for hydrological prediction tasks.

Given complex dynamic variations and noise in raw sequences, direct modeling often obscures critical hydrological signals and fails to fully capture runoff characteristics. Therefore, incorporating sequence decomposition techniques during data preprocessing has become a key strategy for enhancing model performance.

Currently, commonly used methods include Wavelet Decomposition (WD)26, Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD)27,28, and Variational Mode decomposition (VMD)29,30. These methods decompose complex time series data into several simple sub-sequences, then model each sub-sequence separately to fully extract key information at different time scales31. As a representative decomposition method, the wavelet transform has shown excellent performance in handling nonlinear runoff series. Syed et al.32 proposed a WT-ANN hybrid model that effectively improved prediction stability and obtained high-precision forecasting results for the Gilgit River Basin. There are significant differences in the effectiveness of the various methods: Seo et al.26, Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis33 significantly improved feature extraction capabilities using WD34. Ran et al.35 provided structured inputs for grey forecasting models through EMD decomposition; Hu et al.36 employed the EMD to decompose non-stationary meteorological time series into a set of intrinsic mode functions (IMF) components characterized by distinct physical timescales, thereby extracting underlying hydrological signals at different frequencies36. However, wavelet decomposition is non-adaptive; EMD decomposition suffers from modal aliasing, endpoint effects, and sensitivity to noise and sampling37,38. To overcome these limitations, the VMD proposed by Konstantin Dragomiretskiy was introduced39. VMD can adaptively decompose non-stationary, complex time series into several relatively smooth modal components with distinct center frequencies in the frequency domain. Furthermore, VMD incorporates noise robustness constraints during its solution process, which makes the decomposition results less susceptible to noise and effectively suppresses modal overlap40,41. This enables more accurate and stable decomposition of time series10. To overcome mode overlap and boundary effects in EMD, Zhang et al.42 applied VMD to decompose non-stationary coal price series into stationary components, whereas Qian et al.37 further formulated VMD as a variational optimization problem. This endows VMD with adaptive signal decomposition capabilities, enabling it to decompose input signals into multiple modes with finite bandwidth37,42. Currently, VMD has gained extensive adoption within a broad spectrum of research fields43.

While decomposition models have shown promising results in forecasting non-stationary runoff series, more accurate predictions are still needed44. For VMD, an effective solution for model separation and avoiding modal confusion involves selecting appropriate key parameters, including the number of decomposition layers (K) and the penalty factor (α)45,57. Therefore, researchers employ meta-heuristic algorithms to achieve adaptive optimization of VMD parameter configuration. In the field of daily solar radiation forecasting, the simulated annealing (SA) algorithm has been used to select the IMF with optimal features after VMD decomposition46. However, SA suffers from slow convergence and is prone to exploration–exploitation imbalance33. The Crested Porcupine Optimizer (CPO) is a novel natural heuristic algorithm proposed by Abdel-Basset et al., characterized by fast convergence and effective balancing of global exploration and local exploitation47. It effectively addresses problems across various domains and excels at identifying optimal parameter combinations. CPO finds particularly extensive applications in forecasting domains. For instance, in short-term photovoltaic power forecasting, Zhang et al.48 effectively optimized key hyperparameters of the BiLSTM model using CPO, significantly enhancing prediction accuracy. Regarding long-term deformation prediction of tunnel rock mass, Zhang et al.49 accelerated model convergence while preventing local optima by optimizing hyperparameters of ensemble models using CPO49. Additionally, in an application to electricity price forecasting, Li et al. employed the CPO to optimize VMD’s decomposition level (K) and penalty factor (α). This approach enhanced signal decomposition quality and reduced reconstruction loss, thereby strengthening the hybrid model50.

In summary, the considerable work devoted to improving model accuracy and performance can be broadly categorized into three main avenues: First, improving the internal structure of models, such as constructing hybrid deep learning architectures to strengthen feature extraction capabilities; second, employing data preprocessing techniques, including signal decomposition methods to reduce the complexity and noise interference in raw sequences; third, building upon the second approach by introducing intelligent optimization algorithms to search for optimal combinations of key parameters, thereby further improving model accuracy51. Methodology

CPO

CPO is a nature-inspired heuristic algorithm that balances between local optimization and global search, characterized by its ability to avoid local optima and its fast convergence rate. Its design draws inspiration from the crested porcupine’s four-tiered defense mechanism, which intensifies from weak to strong: visual, auditory, olfactory, and physical attacks. The first two mechanisms (visual and auditory) represent exploration behavior, used for broad-scale search of the solution space; while the latter two (odor and physical attack) represent exploitation behavior for localized refinement. Additionally, CPO introduces a cyclic population reduction technique by simulating the phenomenon where only a portion of individuals activate defense mechanisms upon perceiving threats, thereby accelerating convergence while preserving population diversity. VMD

VMD, introduced by Dragomiretskiy and Zosso, is an adaptive and non-recursive signal decomposition method grounded in variational optimization theory30. The fundamental principle involves formulating and solving a variational problem to break down a complex, nonlinear, and non-stationary raw signal into a set of intrinsic mode functions. Each modal function exhibits sparse characteristics, oscillates around a central frequency, possesses a finite bandwidth, and remains mutually isolated in the frequency domain. Ultimately, the superposition of all modal functions equals the original signal.

Considering the non-stationarity of runoff sequences and the presence of noise contamination, coupled with superiority of VMD in non-recursive parallel decomposition mechanisms and noise robustness, this study selects VMD for decomposing the time series.

CPO-VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet model principle

The hybrid CPO-VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet forecasting model in this study achieves tight synergistic integration of parameter optimization, signal decomposition, and predictive modeling through a multi-stage collaborative optimization framework (as illustrated in Fig. 3).

The effectiveness of VMD decomposition heavily relies on two critical parameters: the penalty factor α and the number of modes K. Traditional methods rely on manual empirical settings for these parameters, which are not only highly subjective but also struggle to achieve optimal decomposition. An excessively small α weakens the constraint on IMF bandwidth, leading to spectral overlap among components and causing mode aliasing. Conversely, an excessively large α imposes overly stringent constraints, potentially resulting in mode splitting. Similarly, an excessively large K value may cause VMD to erroneously decompose noise or minor fluctuations into independent IMFs, increasing computational complexity and compromising convergence. Conversely, an insufficient K value prevents VMD from separating all features, resulting in under-decomposition.

To address these limitations, this study proposes the BiLSTM-TimesNet hybrid model. It adopts a hierarchical architecture where BiLSTM first extracts bidirectional temporal dependency features from runoff sequences. After adjusting feature dimensions via an adaptation layer, the time projection layer extends the input sequence length from historical observations to encompass the entire prediction period. The sequence is then fed into the TimesNet module for frequency-domain periodicity detection and multi-scale feature analysis. TimesNet identifies dominant periods via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), constructs spatiotemporal structures via one-dimensional-to-two-dimensional sequence reshaping, extracts multi-scale patterns using Inception convolutional blocks, and adaptively fuses multi-period representations based on frequency-amplitude characteristics. Prediction results are finally output through a projection layer, forming a progressive feature learning framework spanning from temporal dependency modeling to periodic pattern mining.

Step 1: Divide the original runoff time series into training, validation, and test sets in chronological order.

Step 2: Based on the training set, use the CPO algorithm to optimize the VMD decomposition parameters (K, α) to minimize the envelope entropy, thereby obtaining the optimal parameter combination.

Step 3: Using the optimized parameters, perform independent VMD decompositions on the training, validation, and test sets to obtain the components of each subset.

Step 4: Normalize each decomposed component; calculate the mean and standard deviation of the normalized values based on the training set, and apply these to the validation and test sets.

Step 5: Input the normalized decomposed components into the BiLSTM-TimesNet hybrid model to generate prediction results for each component.

Step 6: Combine the prediction results of each component to generate the final prediction result.

Research domains and datasets

Two river basins—the Quinebaug River Basin, USA, and the Elbe River Basin, Germany—were selected as validation sites, with the objective of evaluating the model’s performance under distinct climatic conditions and underlying land surface characteristics. The distribution of these watersheds is presented in Fig. 4.

Geographical distribution of the two watersheds.

The Quinebaug River (41° 35′ 50.97″ N, 71° 59′ 02.74″ W) originates in southern Massachusetts and flows into the Thames River basin in eastern Connecticut. The river spans approximately 240 km with a catchment area of 1864 square kilometers, and its annual precipitation ranges from 763 to 1701 mm. Frequent rainfall events and their uneven spatial distribution result in complex and variable runoff processes.

The Elbe River (51° 03′ 36″ N, 13° 44′ 24″ E) originates in the Karkonosze Mountains on the Czech-Polish border and flows into the North Sea at Cuxhaven. The river spans approximately 1165 km in length, with a drainage basin of about 144,000 square kilometers. Annual precipitation ranges between 500 and 1000 mm, decreasing from west to east. Hydrological processes are jointly driven by precipitation and snowmelt, exhibiting significant seasonal and interannual variability, resulting in complex runoff patterns.

This study utilized observed daily runoff data from the Quinebaug River (January 1, 1997, to December 31, 2001) and the Elbe River (January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2022). To further compare the optimization performance of CPO and SA, S Figs. 1 and 2 in the appendix show the convergence curves of the envelope entropy for both algorithms during the iterative process. For both sites, CPO achieved lower envelope entropy values than SA, and the convergence process was more stable. Since envelope entropy reflects the sparsity of the signal envelope distribution, a lower value indicates that the IMFs obtained from the decomposition exhibit more pronounced periodic or abrupt features, and the degree of modal aliasing is lower. The above results demonstrate that CPO can more effectively optimize the VMD parameters (K,α), thereby providing a more favorable foundation for subsequent predictive modeling.

All model training in this study is conducted in the following computational environment: Windows 11 operating system, Intel Core i7-14650HX processor, 16 GB DDR5 RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 graphics card with 8 GB VRAM, CUDA version 12.2, Python 3.11, and the PyTorch deep learning framework. Results

Based on daily runoff records from the Quinebaug River and the Elbe River gauging stations, this study systematically evaluated ten models: SVM, Informer, LSTM, BiLSTM, TimesNet, TimesNet-BiLSTM, BiLSTM-TimesNet, VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet, CPO-VMD-BiLSTM, and CPO-VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet. To comprehensively evaluate model performance, five metrics were selected: MAPE, RMSE, correlation coefficient (R), NSE, and KGE. Among these, MAPE expresses error as a percentage; RMSE is more sensitive to larger errors, making it suitable for scenarios emphasizing extreme errors; R measures the degree of linear correlation between predicted values and observed values, and its results reflect the accuracy of trend forecasting; NSE evaluates the overall goodness-of-fit of the model; KGE comprehensively assesses model consistency and robustness across three dimensions: correlation, bias, and variability. Model effectiveness

This study selected research findings from previous literature that employed hybrid framework models for runoff prediction and conducted an objective and reasonable comparison of the predictive capabilities of the CPO-VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet model. For example, Xu et al.10 utilized a VMD combined with LSSVM based on DBO optimization, whereas this study introduced CPO-optimized VMD, which significantly improved decomposition quality and subsequent prediction accuracy through adaptive parameter tuning. Furthermore, while Wu et al.54 utilized quadratic decomposition and the BiGRU ensemble strategy for runoff prediction, the proposed model in this paper adopts a novel cascaded fusion architecture. On the one hand, this overcomes the technical limitations of traditional LSTM and BiLSTM models in effectively capturing the multiscale periodic characteristics of runoff sequences; on the other hand, it addresses the shortcomings of the standalone TimesNet model in modeling long-term temporal dependencies, thereby achieving complementary advantages between the two model types. The study by Wei et al.55 indicates that, due to limitations in the scale of sample data, Transformer models perform worse than LSTM models in runoff forecasting. This corroborates the conclusion that, for runoff sequences with strong periodicity and moderate data scale, the specialized TimesNet periodic modeling module yields better forecasting results than Transformer models employing general self-attention mechanisms.

Overall, the forecasting framework developed in this paper achieves an efficient integration of adaptive signal decomposition techniques with hybrid deep learning models, providing a novel research approach and technical reference for the accurate forecasting of non-stationary runoff sequences.

Conclusion

To tackle the nonlinear, non-stationary, and intricate temporal dependency challenges of daily runoff series, we propose the CPO-VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet that synergistically integrates CPO, VMD, and BiLSTM-TimesNet deep learning predictor. This model aims to enhance daily runoff prediction accuracy through a synergistic framework of parameter optimization-signal decomposition-deep modeling. Based on case studies of two representative basins-the Quinebaug River and the Elbe River-the following conclusions are drawn:

Effectiveness of the BiLSTM-TimesNet hybrid architecture: By cascading BiLSTM and TimesNet in series, this architecture achieves the synergistic extraction of bidirectional temporal dependencies and multi-scale periodic features. Compared to the standalone LSTM model, BiLSTM-TimesNet improves KGE by 15.36% at the Quinebaug River station and reduces MAPE by 45.90% at the Elbe River station, demonstrating the clear advantage of integrating bidirectional temporal modeling with periodic feature extraction.

Compared to the standalone LSTM model, BiLSTM-TimesNet improves KGE by 15.36% at the Quinebaug River station and reduces MAPE by 45.90% at the Elbe River station, demonstrating the clear advantage of integrating bidirectional temporal modeling with periodic feature extraction.

Contribution of CPO-optimized VMD: The CPO algorithm effectively explores the parameter space and avoids suboptimal solutions, yielding near-optimal parameter combinations and thereby enhancing overall model performance. The parameter-optimized VMD method proves more effective in extracting meaningful features from runoff sequences and robustly handles nonlinear and non-stationary signals, contributing to improved model generalization capability.

The parameter-optimized VMD method proves more effective in extracting meaningful features from runoff sequences and robustly handles nonlinear and non-stationary signals, contributing to improved model generalization capability.

Effective integration of the CPO-VMD-BiLSTM-TimesNet framework: Through tight integration of the three-stage “optimization-decomposition-modeling” process, the proposed model achieves optimal predictive performance on the test set, significantly outperforming all benchmark models. CPO-optimized VMD lays a solid foundation for subsequent deep modeling, upon which BiLSTM-TimesNet enables multi-dimensional accurate characterization of runoff processes, forming a complementary and mutually reinforcing synergy. Furthermore, compared to the nine benchmark models, the proposed model reduces peak and trough prediction errors by 74.63% and 75.36% on average, respectively, demonstrating its exceptional capability in capturing extreme hydrological events.

Despite its superior predictive performance, the proposed model still presents several limitations that warrant attention. Model interpretability remains a critical research direction. Enhancing transparency will facilitate understanding of the underlying causes and internal mechanisms of prediction outcomes, thereby strengthening credibility in practical applications. Furthermore, the model’s performance may be constrained by the specific hydrological conditions of the studied basin, and its generalizability to data-scarce regions requires further validation. Consequently, future research should focus on developing explainable technologies to facilitate the transition of intelligent hydrological models from theoretical frameworks to practical applications.
