Growth hormone is required for hippocampal engram cell maturation
Authors: Chang-Ho Kim, HyoJin Park, Chuljung Kwak, Dae Hee Han, Yeojin Jung, Binod Timalsina, Ilgang Hong, Yeonjun Kim, Yong-Seok Lee, Nam-Kyung Yu, Jun Cho, Bong-Kiun Kaang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Memory is stored in specialized cells known as engram cells, but the molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain unclear. We discovered that the initial translation leading to growth hormone (GH) synthesis in engram cells is required for their maturation in the mouse dentate gyrus. Time-course experiments with a translation inhibitor suggested that the key molecule is immediately translated during learning. We identified GH as the crucial factor, specifically translated within the first few minutes of consolidation in engram cells. Blocking GH activity with a dominant-negative (G118R) mutation blocked engram maturation, whereas facilitating activity-dependent GH uptake alleviated engram maturation deficits caused by translation inhibition. Together, our findings propose GH as a key mediator of hippocampal engram cell maturation.

INTRODUCTION

The engram represents the physical substrate of memory (1). The activity of engram cells is necessary and sufficient for memory recall (2, 3). The properties (synaptic connectivity and neuronal excitability) of the engram cells are different from those of non-engram cells before learning (4–6). This suggests that a distinct subset of cells, characterized by unique properties, undergoes maturation in a learning-dependent manner, thereby strengthening its functional attributes and contributing to the formation of the engram. Nevertheless, the critical time window and molecular pathways necessary for engram cell maturation remain elusive (2, 7).

De novo protein synthesis is a critical mechanism of long-term memory formation, represented by impaired retrieval of long-term memory once protein synthesis is blocked at the time of learning (7–13). However, despite the protein synthesis blockade, several properties of the engram remain intact. The sufficiency of the hippocampal engram cell for memory recall and engram-specific presynaptic strengthening is not disturbed under short inhibition of the translational process (7). By extending the inhibition, we previously found that the sufficiency of circuit-level activation in the ventral CA1 to the basal amygdala was impaired; however, the extension did not block presynaptic strengthening (13, 14).

RESULTS

Translation during learning is critical for hippocampal engram cell maturation

To identify the time window for the maturation of engram cells, we revisited the nature of learning. Intuitively, learning does not occur after the termination of a specific experience. It begins during the experience. Such characteristic is represented by differences in memory depending on the timing of protein synthesis inhibition (8). Because the properties of the engram are partially intact when protein synthesis is inhibited after learning (7, 13–16), we hypothesized that the critical translational processes for engram maturation might occur immediately at the time of learning.

Anisomycin inhibits protein synthesis for 2 hours when administered through intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection in mice (15, 17). We thus divided memory consolidation into three distinct phases: the initial phase (corresponding to the learning period), the early phase (immediately following the learning period), and the late phase (associated with the second wave of translation). We then disrupted protein synthesis in these phases by administering anisomycin (i.p.) at various time points relative to the contextual fear conditioning (CFC) process. In the [Multiple (−1 hour)] group, all three phases were disrupted, whereas in the other groups, no phases [Saline], only the early phase [Single (0 hour)], or two phases [Single (−1 hour) (initial and early) and Multiple (0 hour) (early and late)] were selectively disrupted (Fig. 1A and figs. S1A and S3A). We targeted the dentate gyrus (DG) as our brain region of interest because the engram cells in the DG were sufficient for memory recall under protein synthesis blockade (7). To identify the changes in dendritic spine density, engram or activated cells (E or A) were labeled in an activity (c-fos) and doxycycline-dependent (rtTA3G and TRE3G) manner (mScarlet-I+). Non-engram cells were identified among the randomly and sparsely labeled cells using the Cre recombinase virus (mTurquoise2+) that were not labeled by activity (mScarlet-I−) (Fig. 1B and figs. S1B and S2, A and B) (18). Anisomycin did not significantly alter the expression of virally expressed proteins compared to saline-injected controls at 2 to 4 hours post-CFC (fig. S2, D to G). Furthermore, 2 days after labeling, the number of labeled cells was indistinguishable among the groups (fig. S2, H to I). In line with previous studies, long-term memory tested 2 days after the CFC was impaired under all conditions where anisomycin was administered (Fig. 1C and fig. S1C) (7, 13, 16). The freezing response in the Multiple (−1 hour) group was indistinguishable between the pre-exposure and the retrieval session. Analysis of the dendritic spine density indicated that activated dendrites in the Multiple (−1 hour) group had lower spine density than non-engram dendrites (Fig. 1, D and E, and fig. S1, D and E). This difference was not due to artifacts caused by fluorescence proteins (fig. S2, J and K). These results are reminiscent of our previous study, in which cells that eventually became the engram had less synaptic connection than non-engram cells before learning (6).

Next, we tested whether the initial protein synthesis has a key role in the presynaptic properties of the engram cells. The synaptic input from the medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) to the DG [medial perforant pathway (MPP)] transmits contextual information associated with an experience (19, 20). Therefore, we labeled the excitatory connections from the MEC to the DG using channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) and measured the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) as a proxy for the presynaptic release probability (Fig. 1F and fig. S1F). In all experimental conditions other than the Multiple (−1 hour) group, engram cells showed a reduced PPR at the 50-ms interstimulus interval (ISI), but not at the 100-ms ISI, suggesting presynaptic strengthening occurred through learning (Fig. 1G and figs. S1G and S3B). However, engram-specific synaptic plasticity was not observed in the Multiple (−1 hour) group. To confirm the changes in release probability, we examined the difference in miniature excitatory postsynaptic current (mEPSC) frequency (Fig. 1H and fig. S1H). In accordance with the PPR data, engram-specific increase in mEPSC frequency was observed in all groups except the Multiple (−1 hour) group (Fig. 1I and fig. S1I) (21).

To assess differences in the postsynaptic properties, we measured the ratio of α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor and N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor mediated current (AMPA/NMDA ratio) (MPP stimulation), capacitance, and the proportion of mushroom spines in engram and non-engram cells. Anisomycin administration resulted in indistinguishable properties between engram and non-engram cells, regardless of the disrupted consolidation phase (fig. S3, C to E) (7). These results were not due to artifacts caused by viral overexpression or doxycycline injections (fig. S2C).

We speculated that the maturation of engram cells indicated by engram-specific synaptic plasticity would be crucial for the sufficiency of engram for memory recall. Optogenetic activation of the engram cells elicited aversive memory-dependent freezing behaviors (fig. S4, A and B) (2, 7). We confirmed that anisomycin injections do not significantly alter the functionality of ChR2 through c-fos immunohistochemistry (IHC) and ex vivo electrophysiology (fig. S4, C to I). Last, we tested whether the sufficiency of the engram for memory recall was altered (Fig. 1J and fig. S1J). All behavioral groups except for the Multiple (−1 hour) groups showed significantly higher freezing responses in the light On sessions than the Off sessions (Fig. 1K and fig. S1K).

Collectively, in the Multiple (−1 hour) group, the properties of the mature engram were not observed. This suggests that blocking all three consolidation phases (initial, early, and late) arrests the maturation of hippocampal engram cells. Injecting anisomycin before learning did not interfere with memory acquisition or the Ca2+ response during learning in the CA1 (fig. S5).

Growth hormone is translated in highly active hippocampal cells

Next, we explored the molecular mechanisms that drive cellular maturation in engrams. Because the results from anisomycin injections indicated that the key translational regulation occurs during or immediately after learning, we reasoned that the regulation may occur through changes in translational efficiency (TE). Thus, we reanalyzed our data on the mouse hippocampal learning-associated changes in TEs, which were calculated by normalizing the ribosome-protected mRNA fragments against the mRNA level obtained through RNA sequencing (22). Multilevel principal components analysis [multilevel-PCA (23)] on the genome-wide TE data revealed distinct changes in TEs at the 5 min following learning (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, we performed orthogonal-partial least squared analysis (OPLS-DA) on the TE data (5 min versus others) and acquired the t1 (orthogonal component) variable influence in projection (VIP) score (Fig. 2B). Last, we classified the genes based on the VIP score (VIP > 1.1) and fold change (5 min/others >1.1), and conducted functional annotation analysis (https://geneontology.org/) (24). The results overrepresented the growth hormone receptor complex, proposing the complex as the critical mediator of engram cell maturation (Fig. 2C). Because GH harbored the highest VIP score within the complex (Fig. 2D), it was selected as the primary focus of our analysis. This overrepresentation appeared only in the translatome, while not in the transcriptome (Fig. 2D), suggesting that this regulation occurs at the level of translation.

GH is up-regulated after learning, modulates memory, causes postsynaptic chemical long-term potentiation (cLTP), increases mEPSC frequency and spine density, and allocates engram cells (25–30). To address whether GH participates in engram maturation, we asked whether hippocampal GH is translated in a learning-dependent manner. We euthanized animals immediately after the CFC and performed GH-IHC (Fig. 2E). We noticed translation- and CFC-dependent rise of sparse cell body-like patches of GH (Fig. 2, F and G), which was similar to the sparse nature of engram cells in the DG (fig. S2, H and I) (2). Subsequently, we asked if GH is locally translated in the DG using CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) (Fig. 2H). Knocking out neuronal GH using the CRISPR-Cas9 blocked the rise in growth hormone signals in the DG (Fig. 2, I and J). Similarly, infusing anisomycin directly into the DG also blocked the rise in GH signals (fig. S6). This indicates that GH is directly translated in the DG.

Last, we asked if GH was translated specifically in the highly active hippocampal cell. Using the rapid transcription of c-fos mRNA following learning and neuronal activity at the 5-min time point, we performed GH-IHC and c-fos mRNA in situ hybridization (ISH) to label GH in active cells immediately after the CFC (Fig. 2K) (22, 31, 32). All cells showing c-fos mRNA signals in both the nucleus and cytoplasm (Nuc+Cyt+) also exhibited strong GH signals, and vice versa. In contrast, GH signals were not detected in cells with weak intranuclear c-fos mRNA signals and no cytoplasmic expression (Nuc+Cyt−) (Fig. 2L).

Growth hormone is required for hippocampal engram cell maturation

To test whether GH is required for the maturation of engram cells, we disrupted the GH-mediated signaling by overexpressing dominant negative (G118R) GH (DN-GH) in the DG using the CaMKIIα promoter (Fig. 3A) (33). This expression strategy labeled nearly all neurons within our region of interest (fig. S7). Overexpression significantly impaired contextual fear memory and disrupted the synaptic changes of the engram as indicated by the dendritic spine density, and electrophysiological properties (mEPSC frequency, PPR, AMPA/NMDA ratio, and capacitance) (Fig. 3, B to G). These changes were specific to the activated cell, and the number of labeled cells was not altered (fig. S8). The sufficiency of the engram for memory recall was disrupted by the DN-GH (Fig. 3, H and I). Similarly, neuronal GH-knockout using CRISPR-Cas9 also disrupted synaptic changes of the engram cell (fig. S9). Expressing DN-GH in the MEC did not alter the PPR changes of the engram cell (fig. S10). To test whether DN-GH could act in a paracrine manner, we sparsely expressed DN-GH using the Cre recombinase virus (fig. S11A). We compared engram cells that expressed DN-GH intracellularly (mScarlet-I+ and mEmerald+) and engram cells that did not express DN-GH (mScarlet-I+ and mEmerald−) but were located within 10 μm of DN-GH–expressing cells (mEmerald+) in the same tissue slice (fig. S11, B and D). We could not find evidence of DN-GH acting in a paracrine fashion (fig. S11, C and E). Collectively, disrupting postsynaptic GH-mediated signaling results in a similar phenotype as the Multiple (−1 hour) anisomycin injections, highlighting its requirement for the maturation of engram cells.

Intracellular GH function was required for the presynaptic changes (PPR and mEPSC frequency) of engram cells, the properties identified as the major difference between the Multiple (−1 hour) and Multiple (0 hour) groups. However, while recombinant human GH (rhGH) in bath artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) does not affect the PPR, its i.p. administration induces alteration in both PPR and mEPSC frequency (27, 28, 34). One potential explanation is that intracellular GH exerts distinct functional effects, and when external GH is introduced via i.p. injection, it may be internalized to mediate these effects. We hypothesized that this process parallels that of brain-derived neurotrophic factors (BDNFs), which are internalized in response to neuronal activity (35). Supporting this notion, during the early postnatal period (P14–21), when hippocampal inhibitory circuits remain immature and engram cells are not sparse, i.p. administration of rhGH resulted in a global increase in mEPSC frequency (28, 36). To test if internalization of external GH occurred, we incubated brain slices in His tag rhGH for 10 min and stimulated the MPP with 4× intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS). Subsequently, we performed His tag–IHC, and found that the His tag was highly localized in the stimulated granule cell layer (fig. S12A). We examined whether the internalized rhGH caused presynaptic strengthening (fig. S12B). Cells stimulated with iTBS under bath rhGH showed lower PPR than those stimulated without rhGH or those incubated with rhGH but without stimulation (fig. S12C). In line with our PPR data, the spontaneous EPSC (sEPSC) frequency was significantly higher for those stimulated under bath rhGH (fig. S12D). Last, to test whether highly active cells, identified by c-fos mRNA signals in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, could internalize exogenous GH in vivo under the influence of anisomycin, we performed His tag–IHC and c-fos mRNA ISH immediately after CFC. The colocalization of His tag and c-fos mRNA signals in these cells indicated that GH internalization had occurred (fig. S12E).

Engram cells are highly active during memory formation. If the activity of the engram cell is sufficient for external GH internalization, acute administration of rhGH would potentially alleviate the maturation arrest caused by anisomycin. We therefore tested whether rhGH administration alleviates the inhibitory effect of anisomycin on engram cell maturation (Fig. 4A). Animals injected with rhGH showed significantly higher freezing levels than baseline (Fig. 4B). The dendritic spine density of engram cells reduced by anisomycin was partially rescued by the rhGH injection (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, rhGH injection rescued the electrophysiological properties blocked by anisomycin (Fig. 4, D to G). Last, the sufficiency of the engram cell for memory recall was also partially rescued (Fig. 4H). The changes in spine density and electrophysiological properties were specific to the activated cell, and the number of labeled cells was not altered by the rhGH injections (fig. S13). Using biorthogonal noncanonical amino acid tagging (BONCAT), we confirmed that the effect of GH was not due to the disinhibition of the general translational process in the hippocampus (Fig. 4, I to K) (37–40).

To determine whether the effects of GH were sex specific, we examined the impact of DN-GH on mEPSC frequency, capacitance of engram cells, and presynaptic strengthening induced by intracellular GH in female animals. The results indicated that the GH effects were not exclusive to males (fig. S14).

DISCUSSION

The sufficiency for memory recall is a defining trait of the engram (2). By extending the protein synthesis block (>6 hours), we have previously identified the synaptic engram as a neural correlate of memory recall at the circuit level via axon terminal activation (13). However, the cellular correlate of memory recall through cellular activation and the mechanism for engram cell maturation have remained elusive (7, 16). This study demonstrated that the key translational mechanism driving engram cell maturation occurs immediately during the learning process, marked by significant alterations in spine density, capacitance, mEPSC frequency, AMPA/NMDA ratio, and PPR once it is blocked. Next, we found that GH is synthesized specifically in highly active cells and is required to develop the properties of engram cells. Last, we showed that rhGH can be internalized through neural activity, which can partially rescue the anisomycin-induced maturation arrestment.

GH is synthesized in both the pituitary gland and the hippocampus (29, 41–44). While neurons appear capable of internalizing externally derived GH (fig. S12), evidence suggests that the hippocampus serves as the principal source of GH required for hippocampal engram maturation under physiological conditions. Previous studies have identified GH mRNA transcription as being regulated by the cytoplasmic polyadenylation element-binding protein 1 (CPEB-1), with a rapid increase in hippocampal GH mRNA observed immediately following learning (29, 42). Supporting this, CRISPR-Cas9–mediated knockout and intrahippocampal anisomycin infusion blocked the up-regulation of GH patches within the DG (Fig. 2, H to J, and fig. S6). In addition, knocking out GH in the DG blocked engram cell maturation (fig. S9), further indicating that GH primarily originates locally within the hippocampus.

Our findings suggest that intracellular GH rapidly increases the presynaptic release probability of axons innervating engram cells (Fig. 2L and fig. S12). This proposes that intracellular GH may initiate a form of retrograde signaling to presynaptic axons, leading to engram-specific presynaptic strengthening. The ability of rhGH to alleviate engram maturation despite protein synthesis blockade (Fig. 4) points to mechanisms involving endocannabinoid signaling and actin polymerization-mediated LTP (45). Beyond its direct effects on presynaptic terminals and engram cell maturation in the hippocampus, GH may serve as a broader modulator of neuronal plasticity, and consequently, of learning-related changes in engram cells. Supporting this, DN-GH overexpression increased CA1 place cell firing activity in a familiar environment, while GH overexpression enhanced place cell remapping in a novel environment (46).

Overall, our observations highlight GH as a critical mediator of engram cell maturation. Further investigation into the molecular pathways involving intracellular GH and possibly other early mediator(s) will be essential for a deeper understanding of its role in memory consolidation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Most experiments were conducted using male C57BL/6J mice purchased from DBL Korea. Female mice were not used unless otherwise stated (fig. S14), due to the potential influence of estrogen levels on growth hormone (43). The age of the animals at the time of the stereotaxic surgery was 3 to 4 weeks for electrophysiology and 6 to 8 weeks for other experiments (spine density, natural memory retrieval, and optogenetic engram reactivation). Mice were given ad libitum access to food and water and were raised in a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Mice were caged together (maximum of 5) until the day before handling and were individually caged afterward. An enriched environment was not provided for the full duration of the experiments. All procedures and animal care followed the regulations and guidelines of the institutional animal care and use committees of the Institute for Basic Science (reference number: IBS-2023-46).

The target sequence for sgGH was GAGGGACAGCGCTATTCCATT (modified from Addgene plasmid no. 124844, a gift from Larry Zweifel). Because of the potential anterograde transsynaptic expression of AAV serotype 9 at high titer, AAV5 was chosen for the presynaptic DN-GH expression (47). The original viruses were diluted with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; added NaCl to a final concentration of 10 mM) to match the aforementioned titers.

Stereotactic surgery

DG viral injections were bilaterally targeted to [anterior-posterior (AP): −2.1 mm, medial-lateral (ML): ±1.3 mm, and dorsal-ventral (DV): −2.0 mm]. Optic cannulas (ferrule material: black ceramic, ferrule diameter: 1.25 mm, fiber diameter: 300 μm, numerical aperture: 0.37, and fiber length: 2 mm, Newdoon) were bilaterally inserted 0.2 mm above the viral injection sites (AP: −2.1 mm, ML: ±1.3 mm, and DV: −1.8 mm). Drug infusion cannulas (C232G-3.0/SPC, 22G, guide length: 2 mm, P1 Technologies) were bilaterally targeted to (AP: −2.1 mm, ML: ±1.5 mm, and DV: −2.0 mm). MEC viral injections were bilaterally targeted to (AP: −4.6 mm, ML: ±3.5 mm, and DV: −3.5 mm). For all experiments other than optogenetic engram reactivation, a 500- or 800-nl mixture of virus cocktail was injected into the DG or MEC, respectively. For optogenetic engram reactivation, a 300-nl mixture of virus cocktail was injected into the DG. The stereotaxic surgery was divided into two sessions due to limitations in viral titer for DN-GH optogenetic engram reactivation and its control experiments.

Behavior

Fear conditioning

Animals were conditioned 2 to 4 weeks after the AAV injection and were single-caged 6 days before the CFC. Each animal was habituated to the hands of the investigator and the anesthesia chamber for 3 min each for 5 days before the CFC. On the conditioning day, animals were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane in the anesthesia chamber, and 280 μl of doxycycline (5 mg/ml; in saline) was injected (i.p.) 2 hours before the conditioning. Similarly, following anesthesia with isoflurane, all animals were injected (i.p.) with either anisomycin (Hello Bio) or an identical volume of saline at −1, 0, 2, and 4 hours after conditioning. For the initial injection, anisomycin was injected with a dosage of 150 mg/kg. The dosage of the subsequent three injections was lowered to 50 mg/kg (13, 14). Anisomycin was freshly dissolved in 1 M HCl and saline, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 to 7.2 with 1 M NaOH to a final concentration of 10 mg/ml before the experiment. Recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) (Cell Guidance Systems) and His tag rhGH were dissolved in distilled water to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml. Aliquoted tubes (200 μl) were frozen in the −20°C freezer until the day of the experiment. The dosage for the injections was 4 mg/kg. The fear conditioning session lasted for 300 s, and animals received three 0.75 mA shocks of 2 s duration at 208, 238, and 268 s (Med Associates Inc., St Albans, VT). The baseline freezing level was quantified using the first 180 s of the fear conditioning session. Long-term memory was quantified using the retrieval session that lasted for 180 s without any shocks 2 days after fear conditioning.

Drug infusion

All animals were conditioned 2 weeks after the cannula implantation. One hour before the CFC paradigm, the animals were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane, and the dummy cannula was replaced with the internal cannula. Next, anisomycin (100 μg/μl) or saline was bilaterally infused (0.8 μl per site, 1.6 μl in total, at the speed of 0.15 μl/min) into the DG. After the infusion, the internal cannula was replaced with a dummy cannula, and the animals were returned to their home cage.

Optogenetics

All animals were conditioned 2 weeks after the AAV injection and went through identical procedures with the CFC paradigm. On the reactivation day, animals were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane, and the optic fiber was connected to the implanted optic ferrule. The animals recovered from the isoflurane for at least 30 min in their home cages before the optogenetic experiments. After the recovery, the animals were placed in a novel context (context B, an opaque white acrylic chamber) and ChR2 was stimulated using a 473-nm laser (20 Hz, 20 mW, and 15 ms pulse width) for a set period (alternating 3-min OFF and ON sessions, four sessions) (2). Freezing bouts and durations were manually detected by a blinded investigator.

Perfusion, cell counting, and spine density analysis

Animals were anesthetized and perfused with 15 ml of ice-cold PBS and 15 ml of ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; in PBS). The isolated brains of the animals were kept at 4°C for 24 hours in 4% PFA. After 24 hours, the solution was replaced with a 30% sucrose solution (in PBS) and was kept at 4°C for 48 hours. Subsequently, the brains were frozen in the −80°C deep freezer and then cryosectioned for imaging. Engram and non-engram cells in the DG were identified and imaged with a confocal microscope (Stellaris 5 or 8, Leica) equipped with a 20× objective lens. The dendrites in the DG were imaged with a 63× objective lens. We used the Imaris software (Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland) to process and reconstruct the dendrite filaments. Dendrites from cells expressing mScarlet-I were considered as engram dendrites, whereas dendrites exclusively expressing mTurquoise2 were counted non-engram dendrites. For fluorescent intensity comparison, the mean brightness of the cell body in the granule cell layer was used. All investigators were blinded to the groups throughout the analysis.

Spine morphology

The classification criteria for mushroom, stubby, and thin/filopodia spines were slightly modified from a previous study (48). Mushroom spines were defined as spine length >1 μm with either a head diameter >0.5 μm or a head-to-neck diameter ratio >2. Stubby spines were defined as spines with a length ≤1 μm and a head-to-neck diameter ratio ≤1.5. Thin/filopodia spines were defined as spine length >1 μm with a head diameter <0.5 μm. Spines that did not meet any of these criteria were classified as unknown.

Electrophysiology

General preparation

Animals were subjected to electrophysiology 36 to 60 hours after CFC. Animals were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine/xylazine mixture. Once deeply anesthetized, transcardial perfusion was performed with 20 ml of ice-cold sucrose cutting solution (210 mM sucrose, 3 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM glucose, 5 mM MgSO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, and 3 mM sodium ascorbate). Subsequently, hippocampal slices (350 μm) were prepared using a vibratome (VT 1200S; Leica) in the ice-cold sucrose cutting solution and then recovered in 32° to 34°C ACSF (2 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 2 mM MgSO4, 15 mM glucose, and 2 mM CaCl2) for 30 min. Next, the chamber containing the slices was transferred to a water tank at room temperature (RT) and recovered for at least 1 hour. All recordings were performed with ACSF at 30° to 32°C at a flow rate of 2 ml/min. A 4- to 6-MΩ tip resistance recording pipette was used for whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. ACSF and the sucrose cutting solution were carbogenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) before and during usage. Recordings were discarded whenever the resting membrane potential was depolarized above −50 mV or when the access resistance was over 30 MΩ.

Recording

Capacitance and mEPSC were isolated with 0.5 μM tetrodotoxin (Hello Bio) and 100 μM picrotoxin (Hello Bio) in ACSF and recorded in cells in which the membrane potential was held constant at −70 mV. The frequency of mEPSC was calculated using MiniAnalysis (Synaptosoft Inc., Decatur, GA). The recording pipettes were filled with a potassium gluconate–based internal solution (8 mM NaCl, 130 mM K-gluconate, 10 mM HEPES, 0.5 mM EGTA, 4 mM MgATP, 0.3 mM Na3GTP, 5 mM KCl, and 0.1 mM spermine), and the liquid junction potential was not corrected. The pH of the internal solution was adjusted to 7.2 to 7.3 with KOH and the osmolarity was set to 285 to 290 mOsm/liter.

PPR and AMPA/NMDA EPSC ratio were acquired with 100 μM picrotoxin (Hello Bio) in ACSF and were recorded in cells in which the membrane potential was held constant at −70 mV. The photostimulation (473 nm and 1 ms width) intensity (−1 mW) was adjusted so that the peak amplitude of AMPAR EPSC was 100 to 300 pA. NMDAR EPSC was defined as the amplitude of the EPSC 50 ms (40 mV) after the onset of photostimulation. For PPR calculations, three to five traces (−70 mV) in 6 s intervals were recorded, and the median PPR was defined as the final PPR value. ISI of 50 and 100 ms was acquired from each cell, and the peak value of the first (P1) and second (P2) EPSC was used to calculate the PPR (P2/P1). For AMPA/NMDA EPSC ratio calculations, three to five traces of AMPAR EPSCs (6 s interval, −70 mV), two to three traces of evoked EPSCs (6 s interval, 0 mV), three to five traces of NMDAR EPSCs (6 s interval, 40 mV), and three to five traces of AMPAR EPSCs (6 s interval, −70 mV) were recorded sequentially. The median value was used for the ratio calculations. The recording pipettes were filled with a CsMeSO3-based internal solution (8 mM NaCl, 130 mM CsMeSO3, 10 mM HEPES, 0.5 mM EGTA, 4 mM MgATP, 0.3 mM Na3GTP, 5 mM QX-314 Br, and 0.1 mM spermine), and the liquid junction potential of 14 mV was corrected. The pH of the internal solution was adjusted to 7.2 to 7.3 with CsOH, and the osmolarity was set to 285 to 290 mOsm/liter.

Growth hormone internalization

All experiments were performed with 100 μM picrotoxin (Hello Bio) in ACSF. The brain slices (350 μm) were pre-incubated in the ACSF with His-rhGH (100 ng/ml, Sino Biological) for at least 10 min before the iTBS (2 s protocol, 8 s delay). TBS was performed as described elsewhere (49). In brief, the tip of the stimulating electrode was placed in the medial molecular layer, and each stimulation of the iTBS was adjusted so that the peak amplitude of AMPAR EPSC was 400 to 500 pA. For PPR and sEPSC recordings, after four rounds of iTBS the brain slices were further incubated in the rhGH-containing ACSF for at least 5 min until the sEPSC or PPR recordings started. For His tag IHC, the brain slices were submerged in ice-cold 4% PFA (in PBS) immediately after four rounds of iTBS. Brain slices were fixed in PFA for approximately 16 hours and were subjected to IHC.

Paracrine DN-GH expression experiments

Recording

Engram cells expressing mScarlet-I were patch-clamped to record mEPSC frequency and capacitance. Following recordings, each engram cell was classified based on mEmerald expression: Cells expressing mEmerald were designated as DN-GH+, while neighboring cells lacking mEmerald expression but located within 10 μm of an mEmerald-positive cell were classified as DN-GH−. Cells that did not express mEmerald and were located beyond 10 μm from any mEmerald-positive cell were excluded from analysis.

Spine density

Engram cells either expressing mEmerald (DN-GH+) or lacking mEmerald expression but located within 10 μm of an mEmerald-positive cell (DN-GH−) were identified in the DG granule cell layer. The dendrites of these cells were then traced into the molecular layer and imaged. Colocalization of the mEmerald signal in DN-GH+ dendrites and its absence in DN-GH− dendrites were confirmed before analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

For c-fos and GH-IHC, coronal sections (30 μm) of the frozen brain samples were acquired using a cryostat (Leica). Sections were blocked with a blocking solution containing 5% goat or donkey serum (Rockland) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) (in PBS) for 1 hour at RT. For His and GH-IHC on the stimulated brain slices (350 μm), samples were blocked in a blocking solution containing 10% goat or donkey serum and 0.5% Triton X-100 (in PBS) for 1 hour at RT. Next, sections were incubated with the appropriate primary antibody at 4°C overnight [rabbit anti–c-fos 1:1000 (Synaptic Systems, 226 008), goat anti-GH 1:40 (R&D Systems, AF1067), and rabbit anti–6XHis tag 1:500 (Abcam, AB9108)]. Following overnight incubation, the sections were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 2 hours at RT and were mounted with Vectashield (Vector Laboratories) [goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) Alexa Fluor 568 1:500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11011) and donkey anti-goat IgG Alexa Fluor 568 1:500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11057)]

In situ hybridization

Animals were anesthetized and euthanized immediately after the CFC. The isolated brains of the animals were kept at 4°C for 24 hours in 4% PFA. After 24 hours, the solution was replaced with a 10, 20, and 30% sucrose solution (in PBS) and was kept at 4°C for 18 hours each. Subsequently, the brains were frozen in the −80°C deep freezer and then cryosectioned (10 μm). ISH (c-fos mRNA) and IHC (GH or His) were performed on the sectioned coronal slices using the RNA scope Multiplex Fluorescent version 2 (323100, ACD) and RNA-protein co-detection ancillary kit (323180, ACD) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For GH-IHC, goat anti-GH 1:10 and donkey anti-goat IgG Alexa Fluor 488 1:500 (A-11055, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used. For His IHC, mouse anti-His 1:50 (G020, Applied Biological Materials) and rabbit anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 1:500 (A-11059, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used.

Miniscope

The experiments were performed with Miniscope version 4.4 (OEPS). The Ca2+ data were motion corrected with CaImAn, and the regions of interest were extracted with CNMF-E (50). The criteria for SR was dF/F0 during shock >6 * dF/F0 during baseline.

BONCAT and Western blot

The procedures of the BONCAT were slightly modified from a previous study (13, 37). Before the experiment, the animals were put on a methionine-deficient diet (Dooyeol Biotech) for at least a week. The azide-containing noncanonical amino acid l-azidohomoalanine (AHA, Vector Laboratories) 100 μg per gbw was injected (i.p.) 2 hours before the CFC. As previously described, saline or anisomycin was injected with the GH or vehicle −1, 0, and 2 hours following CFC. Four hours after the CFC, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized. The hippocampi were immediately dissected and snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen. The frozen hippocampi were kept in the −80°C deep freezer until the subsequent experiments. For the BONCAT experiment, hippocampi were homogenized in 1× RIPA buffer (LPS solution) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The protein concentration of the homogenized samples was measured with the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the nascent protein synthesis was tagged with biotin using the Click iT protein reaction buffer kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In brief, AHA incorporated into the newly synthesized proteins was detected with the biotin alkyne (Vector Laboratories) through a copper-catalyzed click reaction. After the reaction, the samples were resuspended in 1× RIPA with 5× SDS buffer (LPS solution) and were separated using the 4 to 12% gradient gel. After the separation, the samples were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The total protein was quantified using the Ponceau S staining. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin 1:1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, N100) at 4°C overnight. Chemiluminescent signals were imaged and quantified using a ChemiDoc MP device (Bio-Rad).

Statistics

All statistical tests were conducted with either custom scripts written in R version 4.2.2 and RStudio version 2022.12.0+353 or GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0 (Graphpad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA). The base R stats package was used for the Shapiro-Wilk test and the chi-square test. MixOmics version 6.22.0 was used for the multilevel PCA; ellipses in the score plot indicate the 95% confidence level, multivariate t distribution. The ropls version 1.30.0 was used for the OPLS-DA. Before using parametric tests, all data was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test. If the data did not follow the normal distribution, the nonparametric counterpart of the tests was used. Post hoc tests (Dunn test) were not performed unless the data passed the omnibus test (Kruskal-Wallis test). In all cases, two-tailed statistical tests were used. GraphPad Prism, ggplot2 version 3.4.4, Adobe Illustrator (Adobe Systems Inc., San Jose, CA, USA), and biorender.com were used to visualize and finalize the figures. All data were analyzed by the experimenter blinded to the experimental conditions. For parametric data sets using parametric statistical tests, the mean ± SEM was used for error bars. For nonparametric data sets using nonparametric statistical tests, the median and interquartile range were used. For freezing levels in optogenetic engram reactivation, the mean ± SEM is indicated in the line plot.
