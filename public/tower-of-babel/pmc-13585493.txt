A pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis for severe acetabular bone defects
Authors: Qian Wan, Haotian Bai, Meng Xu, Haowen Xue, Qing Han, Xin Zhao, Wenbo Yang, Xingchen Guo, Lanfeng Song, Bin Zhou, Jincheng Wang, Hao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsSevere acetabular bone defects generally refer to Paprosky III defects and pelvic discontinuity. Reconstruction surgery for these is complex, and requires innovative strategies to address implant failure and reconstruct the acetabulum. Traditional reconstruction methods rely on the acetabular inner surface bone stock for prosthetic support, which sometimes proves inadequate for reconstructing severe acetabular bone defects. This study proposes a pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis (PFRP) that stabilizes the acetabular component by means of pile screws anchored in the deep periacetabular bone stock, offering a promising solution for addressing severe acetabular bone defects.MethodsThis article elucidates the fundamental principles, design, fabrication, and implantation technique of the PFRP, and verifies its mechanical stability through finite element analysis (FEA) and biomechanical testing, exploring its biomechanical characteristics.ResultsInitial FEA of the prosthesis determined that the posterior iliac pile and the pubic pile serve as the primary load-bearing components. Furthermore, a minimum triangular configuration of the posterior iliac pile, pubic pile, and ischial pile is required to stably support the acetabular component. Through fatigue mechanical testing and FEA, the efficacy of the PFRP in reconstructing Paprosky IIIB acetabular defects has been validated. Furthermore, a cluster analysis was performed on a historical cohort of 137 acetabular revision cases, categorizing the bone defects into four distinct types. The effectiveness of the PFRP was validated by applying it to the most severe defect within each category, thereby demonstrating its broad clinical applicability.ConclusionThe PFRP exhibits excellent stability. The three-pile fundamental configuration can stably reconstruct acetabular bone defects of various severities. This represents a promising novel strategy for reconstructing acetabular bone defects.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(9):1147–1158.

Introduction

According to a recent population-based statistical study conducted in the UK, the number of hip revision arthroplasty procedures is projected to increase by 36.7% in 2040 compared with that in 2022,1 with up to 76.1% of these cases presenting Paprosky type II or greater acetabular bone defects.2 Severe acetabular bone defects generally refer to Paprosky III defects and pelvic discontinuity;3 their reconstruction remains a substantial clinical challenge.

Reconstruction options for severe acetabular bone defects primarily include trabecular titanium cups, structural bone grafting, metal augments, cup-cage constructs, and triflange cups.4 Trabecular titanium cups are indicated for clinical scenarios where the acetabular bone-cup contact area between the acetabular component and host bone exceeds 50%,5 with clinical studies reporting an overall cumulative survival rate of 93.4% at a mean follow-up of 56.1 months.6 In cases of extensive acetabular bone defects, augmentative constructs are required to provide structural support for the acetabular component. Structural bone grafting is not recommended for standalone use due to the high prosthesis failure rate resulting from bone absorption during the creeping substitution process;7,8 3D-printed metal augments, a currently prevalent option for bone defect reconstruction, feature a microporous surface that facilitates osseointegration with the surrounding bone.9 However, it is difficult to match these precisely with the anatomical contour of bone defects, which may necessitate the resection of additional host bone to achieve a proper fit.10,11 Cup-cage systems provide initial stability by protecting the cup with the cage, but require contact and osseointegration between the cup and the inner acetabular wall to achieve long-term stability.12,13 It is evident, therefore, that the aforementioned reconstruction methods require the presence of normal bone within the acetabulum to provide a supporting function. The degree of bone defect and the quality of bone at the inner acetabular wall determine their fixation outcomes. If the inner acetabular wall exhibits an extensive transmural bone defect, it cannot provide support. Custom triflange cups achieve fixation through the outer periacetabular bone stock and are renowned for their patient-specific design, extensive reconstruction capability, and satisfactory outcomes. However, their use is limited by factors such as the complexity of custom design, lengthy manufacturing time, and high cost.10,14 In contrast to the inner and outer acetabular walls, the deep periacetabular bone stock typically remains unaffected by osteolysis and maintains good quality. Therefore, if stable support for the augment and the acetabular component could be achieved through engagement with the deep intact bone stock, it would offer a promising strategy for reliable reconstruction of severe acetabular defects.

In architecture, the method of supporting superstructures like bridges or buildings by driving piles into deep, stable soil is known as pile foundation. Drawing on this concept, we propose the concept of a pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis (PFRP) for the acetabulum. This involves inserting pile screws (hereinafter referred to as piles) into the healthy bone stock deep within the ilium, pubis, and ischium to support the caps. The acetabular component is connected to these three caps, forming a pile-group cap system to achieve stable acetabular reconstruction. The LUMiC Endoprosthetic Reconstruction system has previously adopted an identical core concept.15 However, the PFRP in this study is characterized by the following features: 1) reconstruction of the ‘pelvic ring’ using the pile-group restores the mechanical transmission and balance of the pelvis; 2) fixation is achieved in the distant normal bone stock around the acetabulum, providing an optimal environment for osseointegration; and 3) dual osseointegration, i.e. osseointegration at the cap-host bone interface plus osseointegration of the piles.

In this study, we designed and prepared the PFRP, verified the feasibility of the surgical procedure and installation through in vitro simulation, and validated its mechanical stability for reconstructing severe acetabular bone defects via finite element analysis (FEA) and mechanical testing.

Methods

Design and surgical application workflow of the pile-supported reconstruction prosthesis

The PFRP consists of three integral components: piles, caps, and the acetabular component (Figure 1). The piles employ thick, long locking screws. A tri-pile configuration anchored in the posterior ilium, pubis, and ischium serves as the foundational stabilization framework (a configuration validated in this study). Depending on bone stock quality, an auxiliary pile may be added in the anterior ilium. The piles are connected to the caps via a threaded interface. The fixation between the cap and the acetabular component can be achieved by two approaches: either through locking screw fixation (Figure 1b), or via bone cement bonding at the interface of the matched porous surfaces (Figure 1c). To accommodate intraoperative scenarios where the trajectory of the pile screws is not perfectly aligned with the geometrical centre of the acetabular component, caps with a range of pre-set oblique angles at varying inclinations were designed (Figure 1b). These caps can be selected for implantation based on the results of preoperative surgical simulation and actual intraoperative conditions.

Design of the pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis (PFRP). a) 2D conceptual drawing of the PFRP; b) PFRP with a locking connection mechanism; c) PFRP with bone cement connection mechanism.

Preoperative planning begins with CT scanning of the patient’s pelvis to reconstruct a 3D model. A patient-specific guide is then designed and 3D-printed using light-cured resin (Figure 2). Robot navigation is also an available option.16 Intraoperatively, the guide is positioned to direct Kirschner wires. Trajectory accuracy is confirmed via intraoperative radiograph before drilling pile channels with cannulated drills and implanting the piles and caps. The titanium mesh cup or the 3D-printed porous cup is then installed at optimal anteversion and abduction angles and fixed by locking screws or bone cement. Finally, the acetabular liner is cemented in place to complete the revision surgery.

Finite element analysis

Initially, to determine the fundamental design of the prosthesis, the following questions needed clarification. Among the four piles (anterior iliac column, posterior iliac column, pubic, and ischial piles) (Figure 3a), which are the primary load-bearing components? Which piles are indispensable for maintaining cup stability? FEA is an effective method for investigating the mechanical characteristics of the prosthesis. We established a finite element model that reflects the load-bearing capacity of each pile: this model supports the cup solely via the piles embedded in the bone stock, without any cup-bone contact. Using this model, we studied the stability, load types, and magnitudes for each pile, thereby identifying the primary load-bearing piles. Based on these findings, we tested the prosthetic stability under various pile configurations, including three-pile and two-pile support, to determine the essential pile configuration required for stability.

Setup for biomechanical testing and finite element analysis (FEA). a) Finite element model for investigating the mechanical characteristics of the prosthesis. b) Finite element model used to validate the stability of the pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis (PFRP) for Paprosky IIIB defects. c) Applied loads and boundary conditions for the FEA. d) Load curves of four daily activities applied in the FEA. e) Fatigue testing setup: left shows the PFRP with bone cement filling the defect, right shows the configuration after removal of the iliac defect bone cement.

Subsequently, we employed FEA to validate the stability of reconstructing severe Paprosky IIIB acetabular defects using fundamental three-pile supported prosthesis. This FEA model was consistent with the realistic mechanical testing model (Figure 3b), utilizing caps to connect the piles and the cup, and employing bone cement to fill the bone defects and fix the polyethylene liner. Furthermore, to verify the clinical applicability of the PFRP, we acquired 137 revision pelves from January 2018 to December 2024. The bone defect depths in the posterior iliac column, pubis, and ischium were measured and subjected to K-means clustering analysis, categorizing the bone defects into four types. For the most severe case within each defect type (i.e. taking the maximum defect depth in the posterior iliac column, pubis, and ischium observed among all pelves in that category), the PFRP reconstruction was performed and its stability was validated through FEA. Accordingly, the PFRP reconstruction can be deemed to provide stable fixation for all 137 clinical cases.

Based on experience with mesh sensitivity analysis from a previous study,17 a global element size of 1 mm was defined, with refinement to 0.3 mm in critical regions such as screw threads, which is sufficient to ensure accuracy in stress and interface micromotion results. All bone and prosthesis components were modelled as linear elastic materials: the Ti6Al4V prosthesis was assigned an elastic modulus of 110 GPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.3;18 the polyethylene liner an elastic modulus of 1,000 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.46;19 and the polymethyl methacrylate bone cement an elastic modulus of 2000 MPa and a Poisson’s ratio of 0.33.20 To better reflect the heterogeneous nature of bone material, the bone’s material properties were derived from CT grayscale values using an established formula:21

Where HU is the Hounsfield unit from pelvic CT scans, ρ is the bone density, and Ε is the elastic modulus.

FEA boundary conditions setup and calculation were implemented in Abaqus software (Dassault Systèmes Simulia Corp., USA) (Figure 3c). Interfaces bonded by bone cement were constrained with tie constraints. Frictional contact was defined between the piles and bone, with a coefficient of friction set to 0.6.22 Hip joint loads replicated the joint contact forces during various daily activities as measured by Bergmann et al23 (Figure 3d).

The stability of the PFRP depends on two primary factors: first, the stability at the pile-bone interface, which is determined by the micromotion at this interface; and second, the structural integrity of the prosthesis itself, which is assessed via the von Mises stress distribution. Therefore, the FEA primarily focused on monitoring prosthesis stress and the micromotion at the prosthesis-bone interface.

Establishment of the mechanical testing model and setup for mechanical testing

Mechanical testing was conducted using three distinct reconstruction protocols for the treatment of severe Paprosky Type IIIB acetabular bone defects, as detailed below.

The first protocol was the PFRP with a locking connection design (hereinafter referred to as the locking protocol) (Figure 4a). In this protocol, small-diameter locking screws were applied to connect the titanium mesh cup and the cap, and bone cement was used to fix the polyethylene liner. To faithfully simulate the clinical scenario, bone cement was packed into the bone defect site to further augment prosthetic fixation. Gait cycle-based cyclic load fatigue testing was performed on this model. To further evaluate prosthetic stability, a second round of fatigue testing was conducted after removing the bone cement packed in the iliac bone defect site following the successful completion of the initial fatigue test. Upon passing the second round of testing, a third round of fatigue testing was performed after complete removal of bone cement from all bone defect sites.

Grouping of biomechanical testing protocols. a) Pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis (PFRP) group using the locking connection protocol between the cap and titanium mesh cup. b) PFRP group using the bone cement bonding protocol between the porous-surfaced cap and 3D-printed porous acetabular component. c) Control group with customized triflange cup reconstruction protocol.

The second protocol was the PFRP with a bone cement connection design (hereinafter referred to as the cement protocol) (Figure 4b). In this protocol, the porous cap and the porous-surfaced acetabular component were bonded together with bone cement. Fatigue testing was performed directly on this model. No bone cement packing or augmentation was performed at the bone defect site of this model, to evaluate the sole load-bearing capacity of the piles.

The third protocol was the customized tri-flange cup protocol (Figure 4c). For this protocol, a tri-flange cup prosthesis was custom-fabricated, with the iliac, ischial, and pubic flanges fixed with four, two, and one screws, respectively. Fatigue testing was also performed directly on the model of this protocol.

A Paprosky type IIIB bone defect was created, and the prosthesis system was then navigated and installed according to the steps described in the previous section. The loads applied during mechanical testing represented the peak loads during gait as reported by Bergmann et al.23 The sacroiliac joint and the pubic symphysis were fixed (Figure 3e).24 Mechanical testing was performed using a uniaxial mechanical testing machine (Sinter, Changchun, China). Load was applied at a rate of 5 mm/min until the peak load was reached,25 at which point the test was stopped to observe whether prosthesis failure occurred. Additionally, a Bluebox optical strain measurement system (Haytham, China) was used to measure the vertical strain in the iliac region. These measured strain values were compared with the FEA results to validate the accuracy of the finite element model. In addition to the single compression test, a fatigue test was also conducted. The peak gait load was applied to the acetabulum at a rate of 1 Hz. The load was applied for 42,000 cycles to simulate six weeks of loading, assuming 1,000 steps per day. To measure the overall structural displacement and monitor for any structural failure, the load and displacement data from the actuator were recorded for each loading cycle. To measure the displacement of the acetabular component, video images were captured every 1,800 cycles using a Digital Image Correlation (DIC) device. After image calibration, the displacement of the acetabular component relative to the surrounding iliac bone was tracked in each video frame to calculate the displacement and the permanent subsidence of the cup. All resulting data were statistically processed using GraphPad 10.1 Software (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons of the mean data from the FEA were performed using a paired t-test.

Results

Load-bearing patterns, stress, and micromotion characteristics of the four piles

The load-bearing patterns of the four piles when supporting the cup under various daily activity loads are shown in Figure 5a. The posterior iliac pile and the pubic pile served as the primary load-bearing components. The posterior and anterior iliac piles primarily sustained axial compressive loads, whereas the pubic and ischial piles were subjected to axial tensile forces. Substantial radial bending loads were observed in the posterior iliac pile and the pubic pile.

The stress and interface micromotion of all piles remained below the fracture/loosening thresholds (Figure 5b). Peak stress and micromotion values are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. High-stress concentrations were localized at the pile-cup junctions of the posterior iliac pile and the pubic pile. The region of larger micromotion was observed in the distal half of the posterior iliac pile. Across the four activities, the mean peak stresses for the posterior iliac pile, anterior iliac pile, pubic pile, and ischial pile were 139.1 MPa, 36.1 MPa, 145.2 MPa, and 50.9 MPa, respectively. The corresponding mean peak micromotion values were 22.4 μm, 8.4 μm, 11.2 μm, and 6.5 μm, respectively.

Removing the ischial pile increased the radial bending load on the posterior iliac pile. While its peak stress remained largely unchanged, the peak micromotion surged to 45.7 μm, occurring at the pile root. Conversely, removing the pubic pile amplified both the axial compressive load and the radial bending load on the posterior iliac pile, elevating its peak stress and micromotion to 474.5 MPa and 43.1 μm, respectively. Configurations retaining only iliac piles, or only pubic and ischial piles, failed to meet stability requirements. For the latter configuration (only pubic and ischial piles), both piles sustained high-magnitude radial bending loads, with peak stresses approaching the yield strength of the titanium alloy, indicating a high risk of long-term fatigue fracture.

Results of mechanical testing and FEA after PFRP reconstruction for Paprosky type IIIB acetabular defects

Biomechanical testing confirmed the efficacy of the PFRP in reconstructing severe Paprosky type IIIB defects. During peak gait loading, the surface strain of the pelvis was significantly lower than the bone’s failure threshold (6,500 to 7,500 µε) (Figure 7a). FEA revealed a strain distribution trend on the bone surface that was consistent with the mechanical testing results. A statistical comparison of surface strains at eight measurement points between the FEA and the DIC mechanical testing showed no significant difference (p = 0.249), validating the accuracy of the finite element model. The maximum stress in the pelvis reconstructed with the PFRP was 27.9 MPa, slightly higher than that of a healthy pelvis (24.4 MPa) (Figure 7b). A greater proportion of the stress was transferred internally through the piles, leading to reduced surface stress on the pelvis. The highest stress within the prosthetic system (292 MPa) occurred at the locking threads connecting the titanium mesh cup to the cap. Both the pelvis and the prosthesis exhibited no risk of failure.

Fatigue mechanical testing and finite element analysis (FEA) results of the pile foundation reconstruction prosthesis (PFRP) for severe acetabular defect reconstruction. a) Comparison of iliac surface strain between FEA and mechanical testing using DIC. b) FEA showing stress distribution in the healthy pelvis, the pelvis reconstructed with the PFRP, and the prosthesis system under peak gait loading. c) Displacement and permanent subsidence of the acetabular component under various cyclic loads during fatigue testing.

Cyclic fatigue testing for Paprosky Type IIIB acetabular bone defects showed that the tri-flange cup had the largest displacement, and thus lowest structural stiffness, while the locking protocol PFRP with bone cement augmentation had the smallest displacement and highest stiffness; the cement protocol PFRP without bone cement packing had the second largest displacement after the tri-flange cup. No prosthetic structural failure occurred in all five test runs. Permanent migration analysis showed that all prostheses exhibited initial subsidence that stabilized in the late phase, with only the locking protocol showing additional subsidence after iliac bone cement removal, and no further subsidence after complete bone cement removal. These findings confirmed that both prostheses only underwent compaction subsidence under fatigue loading, with subsequent stabilization and no structural failure (Figure 7c).

After PFRP reconstruction of the most severe defect per category, the peak prosthesis stresses measured 183 MPa, 189 MPa, 206 MPa, and 204 MPa, respectively, indicating no risk of mechanical failure. The maximum pile-bone interface micromotion values were 31 μm, 37 μm, 36 μm, and 46 μm, respectively (Figure 8b).

Discussion

In this study, we proposed a PFRP for the revision of severe acetabular bone defects, and verified its stability through FEA and biomechanical testing. These two complementary research methods were adopted in combination due to their respective unique strengths and limitations.

Initial FEA was performed to elucidate the biomechanical characteristics of the PFRP. We confirmed that the posterior iliac pile and the pubic pile serve as the primary load-bearing components, and that the posterior iliac pile, pubic pile, and ischial pile form the essential configuration for maintaining prosthetic stability. Prosthesis configurations retaining the posterior iliac pile exhibited better stability than those retaining the anterior iliac pile, as the posterior iliac pile lies along the mechanical load transfer pathway from the acetabulum to the sacroiliac joint.26 The posterior iliac pile was mainly subjected to compressive and bending loads, while the pubic and ischial piles predominantly sustained bending and tensile pull-out forces. These load analysis findings can guide the targeted design of individual piles to optimize resistance to compression, pull-out, and bending. Basic analysis of prosthesis stress and interface micromotion validated the stability of the prosthesis. Of note, during the chair-rising activity, the posterior iliac pile sustained the maximum axial load yet exhibited relatively low micromotion. The loading rate of axial load during chair-rising is slower (Figure 3), suggesting that micromotion may be correlated with loading rate. Based on this finding, we recommend that patients undergoing acetabular reconstruction with the PFRP adopt a slow, controlled walking pace before stable osseointegration is achieved, to reduce the loading rate, thereby minimizing prosthesis-bone interface micromotion and promoting osseointegration.

After elucidating the biomechanical characteristics of the prosthesis, we validated the stability of the PFRP for reconstructing severe Paprosky Type IIIB acetabular defects via FEA. We further verified the accuracy of the FEA model through biomechanical testing, and confirmed the fatigue stability of the prosthesis over the early postoperative period via fatigue testing. This test cycle is a widely accepted standard for hip prosthesis fatigue testing, corresponding to the critical time window for osseointegration at the prosthesis-bone interface. If the prosthesis can withstand fatigue loading over this cycle, its stability will be further enhanced by subsequent osseointegration. Test results showed that both the PFRP and the conventional tri-flange cup prosthesis had sufficient fatigue stability, with no prosthesis fracture or loosening observed throughout the test. The core differences between the two prostheses lay in the displacement amplitude under loading (reflecting structural stiffness) and permanent subsidence after cyclic loading. The tri-flange cup was fixed via flanges attached to the lateral cortical bone of the pelvis, with a long force arm and a prominent cantilever beam effect, resulting in greater deformation under dynamic loading.27 In contrast, the PFRP adopted an intramedullary fixation mode, which conformed to the force transmission axis of the pelvis to the sacroiliac joint with an extremely short force arm, thus its structural stiffness was superior to the tri-flange cup. In terms of permanent subsidence, the PFRP fixed only by pile screws without bone cement augmentation in the defect showed greater subsidence than the tri-flange cup, which was attributed to the gradual compaction process of the pile screws in the bone tract. Meanwhile, the prosthesis with bone cement filling and support at the defect site exhibited markedly reduced subsidence. This result indicated that in clinical application of the PFRP, even with insufficient intraoperative bone cement filling or bone grafting with weak initial support, the prosthesis can still maintain basic mechanical stability through the multi-pile group structure, providing a stable mechanical environment for bone graft healing. In summary, the PFRP exhibited equivalent overall stability to the tri-flange cup in mechanical testing for the reconstruction of severe Paprosky IIIB acetabular defects, with no prosthesis failure or loosening during the entire test. Meanwhile, the non-customized design of this prosthesis can shorten the preoperative preparation cycle, making it more conducive to clinical popularization.

Finally, to verify the broad clinical applicability of the PFRP and confirm its suitability for common clinical scenarios, we classified 137 consecutive acetabular revision cases from our institution over the past five years into four distinct defect types based on bone defect depth, and validated the stability of the PFRP for reconstructing the most severe defect within each category via FEA. The results showed that prosthesis stress and interface micromotion generally remained within safe limits. Only in cases with extensive ischial defects did the peak micromotion reach 46 µm, slightly exceeding the 40 µm threshold considered optimal for osseointegration.28 Nevertheless, only a minimal portion (0.4%) of the screw-bone interface area exceeded this threshold, while the vast majority of the contact area exhibited micromotion within the safe range for osseointegration. Furthermore, in clinical practice, the use of bone graft and bone cement would further enhance the overall stability of the construct. Therefore, the PFRP can achieve stable reconstruction for the vast majority of clinical defect presentations.

This study has two key limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although FEA and mechanical testing have validated the stability of the PFRP for severe Paprosky Type III acetabular defects, the definitive clinical applicable boundary of the prosthesis has not been fully delineated, particularly the critical threshold of bone defect depth beyond which the PFRP can no longer provide sufficient and stable fixation for the acetabular component. Second, all biomechanical validations in this study were performed under optimal prosthetic implantation and alignment conditions; systematic mechanical testing under sub-optimal prosthetic alignment scenarios,29 as well as higher-cycle long-term fatigue testing targeting the core pile-cap-cup connection of the prosthesis, have not been completed in the current work and will be the focus of our subsequent preclinical validation.

In conclusion, the PFRP utilizes the deep periacetabular bone stock to support the acetabular component, with pile placement aligned with the mechanical load transfer pathway of the pelvis. FEA and mechanical testing demonstrate that this prosthesis achieves excellent stability. Its three-pile fundamental configuration enables stable reconstruction of acetabular defects across a broad spectrum of severities, representing a promising novel strategy for acetabular bone defect reconstruction.
