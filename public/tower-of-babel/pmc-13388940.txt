Telomere-to-telomere genome assembly of Oryza australiensis reveals transposon-driven centromere repositioning and shared EE–DD ancestry
Authors: Yu Bao, Hanli You, Shuo Liu, Guanqing Liu, Yuechao Wu, Qingqing Yang, Qi You, Peng Liu, Chuandeng Yi, Wenli Zhang, Zhukuan Cheng, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOryza australiensis (EE genome), the sole representative of the EE genome in the genus, holds significant value for deciphering polyploid evolution and mining stress-resistance genes owing to its extreme environmental adaptability. Here, we report a telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assembly of O. australiensis, yielding a 901 Mb genome with 12 complete chromosomes. This enables comprehensive annotation of centromeres, telomeres, and 36,742 genes, revealing non-canonical telomeric repeats and three distinct centromere repositioning mechanisms: inversion-driven shifts, duplication-mediated neocentromerization, and transposon burst-induced neocentromere formation. Crucially, transposon dynamics analysis reveals synchronized amplification of Angela LTR retrotransposons in the EE genome and the DD subgenome of allotetraploid O. alta (CCDD) prior to CCDD formation. Phylogenetic and synteny analyses of Angela LTR integrase domains, alongside a 275-bp repeat marker exclusive to EE and DD, indicate that the ancestral lineage that contributed the DD subgenome share common ancestry with an EE-like progenitor. These findings refine Oryza evolutionary history and highlight transposon dynamics in reconstructing deep evolutionary relationships.Subject terms: Genome evolution, Evolutionary genetics, Plant evolution, Agricultural genetics

Introduction

The genus Oryza comprises 27 species, including 25 wild rice species and two independently domesticated cultivated species1–8. Based on molecular markers and subsequent cytogenetic analysis, the genus Oryza is classified into 11 distinct genome types, including six diploids (AA, BB, CC, EE, FF, GG) and five allotetraploids (BBCC, CCDD, HHJJ, HHKK, KKLL)9. Among these, O. australiensis, a unique EE genome clade in Oryza, originated from Australia and is mainly distributed in the tropical north of Australia. O. australiensis is the closest extant relative to the DD genome8,10. The CCDD genome allotetraploid rice originated from a single hybridization event, where the CC genome species (O. officinalis or O. rhizomatis) served as the maternal parent and the extinct species with the DD genome type served as the paternal donor5,7,10,11. In the absence of an extant diploid DD genome, researchers often use the DD subgenome of the CCDD allotetraploid as a proxy to study the evolutionary relationship between the EE and DD lineages in Oryza6,10,12. In 2010, Ammiraju et al. studied the evolution of Oryza by cloning the Adh1 fragment of Oryza genomes and revealed that EE genome and DD subgenomes (from O. alta) diverged at 7.08 Mya6. Since this divergence time significantly predates the formation of CCDD allotetraploids (~1.6 Mya) and no marked difference in evolutionary rates was observed between DD and EE lineages, Ammiraju et al. excluded the EE genome as the direct donor of the DD subgenome. Recently, Long et al. and Fornasiero et al. revealed that the divergence time of the EE genome and the DD subgenomes at 4.44 and 4.9 Mya8,13, respectively, by single-copy gene analysis within the rice genus. Although phylogenomic studies using single-copy genes have provided valuable insights and increasingly consistent time estimates for DD-EE divergence, their conclusions might be constrained by the inherent properties of coding sequences in a polyploid context. Post-polyploidization evolutionary dynamics, including potential rate shifts and selection pressures on genes, could complicate interpretations based solely on this data type14,15. Consequently, evidence independent of single-copy genes, such as transposon dynamics and chromosomal structural evolution, is urgently needed to reassess the DD-EE relationship.

Chromosome-level reference genome of O. alta has been released16. However, a high-quality O. australiensis genome sequence and its annotations are still lacking, which hinders in-depth analysis of the evolutionary relationship between DD and EE. The genome of O. australiensis was estimated to be 965 Mb, which is more than twice the size of that of O. sativa17. The large size of the genome was thought to be caused by the expansion of long terminal repeats (LTR), with three retrotransposon elements (RIRE1, Kangourou, and Wallabi) accounting for about 60% of the genome18. Recently, Phillips et al. combined Oxford Nanopore (ONT) long reads and Illumina short reads to complete a ~858 Mb genome assembly with 850 contigs19. In addition, Long et al., Fornasiero et al., and Morrison et al. completed the O. australiensis genome assembly using long-read sequencing technologies, with genome sizes of 881 Mb (PacBio), 903.3 Mb (ONT), and 909 Mb (PacBio), respectively8,13,20. Despite these advancements, the available assemblies vary in contiguity and completeness, and none of them achieved a gap-free, telomere-to-telomere (T2T) standard. Besides, centromere and telomere annotations, and in-depth investigations into genome architecture and repetitive sequence dynamics remain lacking. These critical gaps severely hinder the elucidation of structural evolution and donor relationships between the DD and EE genomes.

Here, we report a T2T genome assembly of O. australiensis. This assembly enables comprehensive annotation of centromeres, telomeres, and genes, revealing non-canonical telomeric repeats and widespread centromere repositioning events. We further use transposon dynamics to reassess the evolutionary relationship between the EE genome and DD subgenome, providing evidence that the ancestral lineage contributing the DD subgenome shares common ancestry with an EE-like progenitor prior to CCDD formation.

Results

Telomere-to-telomere genome assembly of O. australiensis

In this study, the O. australiensis genome was de novo assembled using 88.71× ONT PromethION long reads sequencing, 22.65× Ultra-long reads sequencing, and 113.15× Illumina short reads sequencing (Supplementary Table 1). For the raw datasets, the N50 length of the PromethION and Ultra-long reads was greater than 33 and 100 Kb (Supplementary Fig. 1), respectively. The PromethION and Ultra-long reads were initially assembled by Nextdenovo software into 14 contigs with a total length of 899.3 Mb (Supplementary Fig. 2). Then, the duplicated contigs and contaminants were removed. Further assembly steps involved telomere patching and gap filling by examining the coverage relationship between ONT reads and the assembled contigs (Supplementary Fig. 3). Subsequently, the assembled genome was polished by three rounds of iterative error correction using ONT and Illumina short reads via automated_polishing.sh (https://github.com/arangrhie/T2T-Polish/tree/master/automated_polishing)21. The final assembly genome size was ~901 Mb and consisted of 12 T2T chromosomes, with scaffolds N50 size of 76.6 Mb (Fig. 1a and Table 1).

Statistics of Oryza australiensis genome assembly and annotation

The quality of the T2T genome was evaluated through mapping Illumina and ONT sequencing reads to the O. australiensis genome. The high reliability of the genome assembly was supported by high genome coverage rates (99.12% WGS, 99.97% ONT) and mapping rates (98.62% Illumina, 99.79% ONT), as well as rigorous validation through Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCOs) values of 99.8%, LTR assembly index (LAI) values of 23.79, and consensus quality values (QV) of 41.76 (Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Annotation of the O. australiensis genome assembly

In O. australiensis genome, a total of 36,742 protein-coding genes have been annotated, using ab initio, protein homology, and RNA-seq assembly prediction methods (Supplementary Table 3). These genes are predominantly located in distal regions of the chromosome arms, with a total length of 110.3 Mb, occupying 12.2% of the genome (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 3). The average lengths of these protein coding genes and corresponding proteins are 3004 bp and 362 aa, respectively.

The O. australiensis genome is predominantly composed of repetitive sequences. A total of 611.6 Mb of the sequence has been annotated as repetitive, which represents 67.8% of the O. australiensis genome. Long terminal repeat retrotransposon (LTR-RTs), including Copia, Gypsy, and unclassified LTR are the dominant components of repeats, making up 91.5% of all repeats and 62.1% of the assembled genome; non-LTR-RTs account for less than 0.25% of the genome (Supplementary Table 4). The length of DNA transposons in O. australiensis genome is only 55.7 Mb, occupying 6.1% of the genome (Supplementary Table 4). Among DNA transposons, En-Spm emerged as the predominant type, representing 5.73% of all repetitive elements and 4.5% of the assembled genome.

Eukaryotic telomeres represent a specialized class of microsatellite sequences characterized by 4–10 bp core motifs. The TTTAGGG telomeric repeat predominates in flowering plants22. O. sativa telomeres comprise 5.1–10.8 Kb repetitive regions containing canonical arrays of TTAGGG monomers23,24. The telomere length of O. australiensis ranged from 9 to 42 Kb (Supplementary Table 5). Unlike O. sativa, the telomeric region of the O. australiensis genome exhibits both a canonical array composed of TTTAGGG monomers (Fig. 1b) and a non-canonical array composed of derivative (CCCAGG and TTTAAAA) monomers (Fig. 1c). Their non-canonical array is present in multiple chromosome-ends, including Chr2L (the long arm of chromosome 2), Chr5L, Chr7S (the short arm of chromosome 7), and Chr1S, Chr9S, Chr11L (Fig. 1c). These telomere derivative sequences also occur in Chenopodium25. These results indicate the diversity of telomere sequences in the genus Oryza and the universality of the non-canonical arrays of telomere sequences.

Satellite repeats, also known as highly repetitive tandem repeats, are the primary components of heterochromatin in most higher eukaryotes, and they are often organized as long arrays in the pericentromeric, centromeric, and subtelomeric regions of chromosomes26,27. The rice genome has been identified to contain numerous satellite repeat sequences, including 45S rDNA, 5S rDNA, and Os48 (a subtelomeric repeat)28,29. Similar to japonica rice, 45S rDNA and 5S rDNA of the O. australiensis genome are located at the end of Chr9S and the pericentromeric region of Chr11 (Fig. 1a), respectively30. However, unlike O. sativa, the O. australiensis genome does not contain any Os48 repeats.

Centromere mapping reveals repositioning and transposon-driven neocentromere formation in O. australiensis

Centromere is a constricted region on a chromosome that plays a critical role in properly segregating chromosomes during cell division. In plants, functional centromeres are epigenetically defined by the localization of the histone H3 variant CENH331. To investigate the centromere landscape within the O. australiensis genome, we used a rice CENH3 antibody for chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing (ChIP-seq) of the captured DNA fragments. The CENH3 binding sites precisely demarcated functional centromere boundaries on each chromosome within the EE genome. High-quality centromere assembly was supported by a 98.96% alignment rate of CENH3 ChIP-seq reads, absence of ONT sequence breakpoints in centromeric regions, and consistent assembly results across multiple software tools (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6 and Supplementary Table 1). Centromeres in the EE genome ranged from 0.9 to 2 Mb in size, comparable to those in O. sativa (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 6). Tandem arrays of highly repetitive satellite sequences make up most of the plant and animal centromeres29. In O. sativa, the tandem repeats satellite sequence of 155 bp (CentO) was found in each centromere region29,32. In EE genome, we identified and experimentally validated via Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) a 155 bp satellite sequence within centromeric regions, which exhibited a high sequence identity of 87.09% to CentO (Figs. 1d and 2b and Supplementary Fig. 7). To explore CentO sequence divergence across Oryza species, we identified centromeric satellite repeats using RepeatExplorer33. Using RepeatExplorer software, we obtained the centromeric satellite sequences of 155 bp of BB genome, 366 and 126 bp of CC genome, 155 and 125 bp of CCDD genome, and 154 bp of FF genome, respectively (Supplementary Table 7). The 155 and 125 bp satellite repeats of the CCDD genome were most similar to 155 bp satellite repeats of the EE genome (similarity: 94.2%) and 126 bp satellite repeats of the CC genome (similarity: 92.9%), respectively (Fig. 2b, c). The high similarity of satellite sequences suggested that the DD subgenome in CCDD may have directly inherited the centromeric characteristics of the EE-like ancestor, supporting the common ancestral hypothesis.

The EE genome exhibits centromere repositioning and neocentromere formation on multiple chromosomes. Neocentromeres are present in Chr2, Chr6, and Chr12, where Chr2 has a large number of satellite repeats at a distance of 1.5 Mb from the functional centromere, Chr12 contains fewer satellite repeats, while Chr6 contains almost no satellite repeats (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 8). These cases demonstrated that CENH3 binding alone can define a functional centromere when its position has shifted, a pattern consistent with neocentromere formation in satellite repeats depleted regions reported in other plant species34,35. This gradient in satellite repeat abundance varies among chromosomes and may be consistent with ongoing centromere reorganization, though this pattern cannot demonstrate temporal sequence. According to the centromere maturation model, neocentromeres can acquire satellite repeats over evolutionary time after their initial formation. Consistent with this model, we interpret the pattern on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, and 10 as representing mature neocentromeres. On these chromosomes, CENH3-binding assays revealed two distinct satellite repeat-enriched regions, with the larger region serving as the functional centromeric region (CENH3-binding site) and the smaller region lacking CENH3 association. Recent studies have indicated that transposon insertions may initiate centromere repositioning events, leading to the formation of neocentromeres36. In the EE genome, approximately 69.7% of centromeric sequences are composed of TEs (Fig. 2d). Among the TEs found in all centromeres, 98% are LTR elements, with 38.3% belonging to the Gypsy family and 22.1% to the Copia family (Fig. 2e). We further compared the LTR insertion times between active centromeres (defined by CENH3 binding) and inactive centromeres (enriched in satellite repeats but devoid of CENH3). The results showed that active centromeres are enriched with younger LTRs, indicative of more recent insertion events (Fig. 2f), suggesting that TE insertions may contribute to the formation of neocentromere (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Studies have shown that plant centromere regions contain transcriptional genes31,37,38. There were 81 genes in the centromere region of the EE genome, 34 of which had transcriptional activity (Supplementary Data 1). Further comparative analysis with the Nipponbare genome revealed that 30 of these 34 expressed genes were syntenic orthologs, and all expressed genes were located at non-CENH3 binding sites.

Centromere repositioning reshapes chromosome arm ratio in Oryza species

Chromosomes are divided into short arms and long arms by centromeres, and their structural features provide critical insights for gene mapping, evolutionary studies, and functional genomics39–41. To compare the chromosomal arm distribution patterns between O. australiensis and Nipponbare, we oriented the chromosomes of O. australiensis based on the syntenic alignment of homologous genes with Nipponbare, ensuring consistent chromosomal orientation prior to analyzing the relative lengths of short-arms and long-arms. Comparative analysis revealed conserved long-short arm ratios (>1 for most chromosomes) but species-specific divergences (Fig. 3a). For example, Chr4 in Nipponbare showed a long-short arm ratio of 2.73, compared to 1.73 in O. australiensis; Chr7 in Nipponbare had a long-short arm ratio of 1.44 versus 2.07 in O. australiensis (Fig. 3a). Strikingly, Chr3 and Chr12 exhibited reversed long-short arm trends between species (Fig. 3a). In Nipponbare, Chr3 had a long-short arm ratio <1 (short-arm longer than long-arm), whereas O. australiensis, displayed long-short arm ratio = 1.22 (long-short arm ratio >1); Chr12 showed a long-short arm ratio of 1.3 in Nipponbare but 0.7 in O. australiensis. These reversed patterns indicate that Oryza chromosomes may have experienced unique structural rearrangements or centromere positional shifts during speciation.

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying these arm ratio differences, we performed whole-genome synteny analysis. Results demonstrated that the centromere of Chr3 in O. australiensis was shifted ~4 Mb toward the short-arm compared to Nipponbare, resulting in short-arm shortening and long-arm elongation (Supplementary Fig. 9). Similarly, the centromere of Chr12 in O. australiensis shifted ~6 Mb toward the long-arm, leading to short-arm elongation and long-arm shortening (Supplementary Fig. 10). Furthermore, comparative syntenic analysis revealed that the centromere on chromosome 11 of O. australiensis underwent a short-armward shift relative to Nipponbare, resulting in increased arm ratios in the O. australiensis. Strikingly, the 5S rDNA locus—positioned immediately adjacent to the centromere within the short-arm of Nipponbare—was shifted to the long-arm region in O. australiensis (Fig. 3b). Pachytene chromosome FISH using CentO and 5S rDNA as probes confirmed these structural rearrangements, directly demonstrating both the centromere shift and the 5S rDNA relocated on chromosome 11 of O. australiensis (Fig. 3c). This concerted repositioning of both centromere and repetitive elements strongly suggests their co-regulation by a pericentric inversion event. In general, the directional consistency between centromere shifts and long-short arm ratio alterations across chromosome confirms that centromere repositioning is the primary driver of arm ratio divergence.

In humans, short arms always harbor higher proportions of repetitive sequences42,43. We further investigated the relationship between arm lengths and transposon distribution in Oryza. The transposon density was significantly higher (p < 0.032) in short-arms than long-arms across most chromosomes (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Method 1). Despite the arm ratio reversal in both Chr3 and Chr12 between species, the transposon density disparities persisted. For instance, the short-arm of Chr12 in O. australiensis, elongated to 34.5 Mb due to centromere shifts, still exhibited higher transposon density than its long-arm (TE-length/arm-length: short-arm: 0.64 vs. long-arm: 0.58). This phenomenon suggests that inherent chromosome arm properties—such as chromatin 3D architecture or epigenetic modifications—predominantly govern transposon distribution preferences, while arm length changes exert limited direct influence on transposon density.

Evolutionary trajectory of centromeres uncovers three distinct repositioning mechanisms in Oryza

Studies on centromere evolution in Oryza species provide crucial insights into the fundamental mechanisms and driving forces behind chromosomal structural remodeling. To reconstruct the dynamic evolutionary pathways of ancestral centromeres, we took Leersia perrieri as an outgroup and constructed a multi-genome synteny map based on the collinearity of protein-coding genes among rice varieties (Nipponbare and MH63) and wild species (O. australiensis and O. brachyantha) (Supplementary Method 2). We then projected the genomic positions of two key centromeric features—the core centromeric satellite repeats (CentO) and the CENH3-binding domains defined by ChIP-seq—onto this syntenic framework to compare centromere positions and structures across species. Ancestral centromeres were identified by the presence of functional centromeric regions (CENH3-binding domains) within syntenic blocks conserved in at least two of the three independent evolutionary lineages (AA, EE, and FF genomes) (Supplementary Method 3). This multi-lineage conservation provides strong evidence that these centromere positions date back to their last common ancestor. Comparative genomic analysis revealed positional synteny of centromeric locations across chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 10, and 11 in both AA and FF genomes, indicating retention of ancestral centromere positions. In contrast, the EE genome exhibited pronounced dynamic remodeling of centromeric positions across these chromosomes (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). Specifically, a pericentric inversion of Chr11 resulted in centromere repositioning, while Chr1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 10 formed neocentromeres at locations far from the ancestral position (2.3–5 Mb). It is worth noting that Chr1, 2, 4, 5, and 10 of the EE genomes still retain satellite repeats in the ancestral centromere position, and the ancestral centromere archaeological sites of Chr1, 2, and 5 have undergone small-scale inversion (Supplementary Fig. 11). For Chr9, syntenic conservation of functional centromeres between EE and AA genomes identifies this locus as ancestral, whereas two pericentric inversions in FF repositioned the centromere toward the long-arm, leaving archaeological traces of residual satellite repeats at the ancestral position (Supplementary Fig. 10). In Chr3 and Chr8, the ancestral centromere position can be determined by the synteny between the inactive centromere loci of the EE genome and the functional centromere loci of the AA genome. This suggests that the AA genome retains the ancestral centromere loci, whereas the EE genome has formed neocentromeres ~3.4 and ~2.3 Mb away from the ancestral loci, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). In contrast, the centromere position of the FF genome in Chr3 and Chr8 has shifted due to pericentric inversions. In Chr12, the functional centromere locus of the AA genome and the centromeric satellite repeats locus of L. perrieri exhibit synteny, supporting this region as the ancestral centromere position in Oryza (Fig. 4c). This conclusion is further strengthened by the presence of residual satellite repeats at the ancestral centromeric position in both EE and FF genomes. The neocentromere position on Chr12 in EE and FF genomes is located at ~6 Mb and ~400 Kb away from their ancestral positions, respectively.

To investigate the factors driving neocentromere formation, we performed a detailed sequence analysis of the ancestral centromere position and neocentromere position in the EE and FF genomes. In the FF genome, a segment duplication exists near the ancestral and new centromeric positions of Chr12 (Fig. 4d), suggesting that this segment duplication may drive the formation of the neocentromere on Chr12, consistent with previous report44. In contrast, segment duplication driving neocentromere formation was not observed in the EE genome. Neocentromeres in the EE genome exhibit a high density of TEs, with CRR LTR specifically enriched in centromeric regions (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13), suggesting that CRR insertion may facilitate CENH3 binding and drive neocentromere formation, similar observations have been reported in Capsicum45.

Here, three centromere repositioning models during Oryza evolution were proposed (Fig. 4f): 1. Pericentric inversion-driven local relocation: Inversion events alter the genomic structure of the original centromeric region, leading to small-scale shifts in functional centromere positions. 2. Duplication-mediated neocentromerization: Duplication of large genomic segments near centromeres induces the emergence of new centromere loci. 3. Transposon burst-driven neocentromere formation: Large-scale transposon amplification induces rapid genome expansion while disrupting epigenetic stability at ancestral centromeres, ultimately driving neocentromere formation at adjacent loci through epigenetic reprogramming.

Phylogenetic tree construction and divergence time estimation in Oryza

To investigate the evolutionary history of the Oryza genus, we generated a phylogenetic tree using OrthoFinder software based on 1460 single-copy genes shared among 18 chromosome-level Oryza genomes, with Brachypodium distachyon as the outgroup. The tree resolved six highly supported monophyletic clades (AA, BB, CC, DD/EE, FF, and GG; Fig. 5a), consistent with previous studies6,8,13,46. The diploid CC genome (O. rhizomatis, O. eichingeri, O. officinalis) clustered with the CC subgenome of O. alta (CCDD), while the DD subgenome of O. alta clustered with the EE genome. Next, the divergence time of Oryza species was estimated by analyzing the coding sequences (CDS) of 1460 single-copy genes using MCMCtree software47. The estimated divergence time between the CC subgenome and O. rhizomatis genome at around 3.9 Mya (95% highest posterior density (HPD): 1.57–6.09 Mya) (Fig. 5a), which is longer than the 1.7 Mya estimated by Ammiraju et al.6 and the 2.71 Mya estimated by Long et al.13. The divergence time between the DD subgenome of O. alta and EE genome occurred at 5.13 Mya (95% HPD: 1.71–8.89 Mya) (Fig. 5a), placing it intermediate between the 7.086 and 4.44 Mya13 estimates.

Transposon evidence supports shared ancestry between the DD subgenome donor and an EE-like progenitor

Transposons play a crucial role in shaping eukaryotic genomes, particularly in plants, contributing to genome size expansion and evolution through amplification and insertion into different genomic locations48–50. To analyze the evolutionary histories of EE genome and DD subgenomes based on transposon characteristics, the transposon sequence of the Oryza genomes was annotated using mipsREdat_9.3p and Viridiplantae_version_3.0 database. LTR-RTs are the main component of Oryza transposon sequences, with a proportion of 91.36% found in the transposon sequences of the EE genome, and proportions of 87.03 and 81.92% found in the DD subgenome and CC subgenomes, respectively (Supplementary Table 9). DNA transposons constitute a low percentage of transposon sequences in the Oryza genus, with proportions of 8.3, 12.5, and 17.5% found in the EE genome, DD subgenome, and CC subgenomes, respectively. Intriguingly, although the DD and CC subgenomes of the allotetraploid CCDD are similar in size, the DD subgenome harbored larger LTR size and proportions (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 9). Given that subgenomic transposons are activated to balance genome size and evolutionary rate in polyploid plants51,52, these inter-subgenomic differences likely predate CCDD formation.

To reconstruct the transposon-driven evolutionary history of EE and DD, we calculated LTRs insertion time across EE and CCDD genomes. The EE genome displays a high proportion of young Copia and Gypsy LTR insertions, with amplification peaks occurring at approximately 0.32 and 0.43 Mya, respectively (Fig. 5c). LTRs, particularly Gypsy LTR, exhibited an additional amplification peak at 1.7 Mya. These results indicate that the EE genome may have experienced two transposon amplification events at approximately 0.4 Mya and around 1.7 Mya, consistent with previous reports of two transposon amplifications in the EE genome17,53. In the CC and DD subgenomes, Copia and Gypsy have an amplification peaks at 0.25 and 0.14 Mya (Supplementary Fig. 14), respectively.

The similarity of repetitive sequences between species has been demonstrated to be effective in studying the evolutionary history of plant and animal groups54,55. Here, we specifically focus on the dynamic changes in the similarity of repetitive sequences between the polyploid genome and its donor genome over evolutionary time. To investigate this, we calculated the sequence similarity of LTRs between CC genome and CC subgenome, CC subgenome and DD subgenome, and EE genome and DD subgenome within different time intervals (Supplementary Method 4). As expected, LTR sequence divergence between the CC subgenome and DD subgenomes increased with time, whereas divergence between the CC genome and CC subgenome and between EE genome and DD subgenome decreased (Fig. 5d). Within the time interval of approximately 1.52 to 2.1 Mya, the fitted curves of CC genome and CC subgenome, as well as EE genome and DD subgenome, intersected with the fitted curve of CC subgenome and DD subgenome around 1.81 and 1.73–1.92 Mya, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15). These intersection points indicate the approximate formation period of CCDD at around 1.73–1.92 Mya. Although the formation time of the CCDD predicted by transposon sequence features is significantly later than the divergence time of CC genome and CC subgenome, as well as EE genome and DD subgenome predicted by single-copy genes, it still falls within the 95% HPD predicted by single-copy gene prediction. Furthermore, the highly concordant intersection times of the divergence trajectories reveal that the evolutionary histories of the EE lineage and the DD subgenome were temporally coupled with the formation of the CCDD polyploid, supporting the hypothesis of shared common ancestry between an EE-like progenitor and the ancestral lineage that contributed the DD subgenome prior to CCDD formation.

Repeat analysis implicates an EE-like progenitor for the DD subgenome

To identify evolutionarily informative LTR sequences linking EE genome and DD subgenomes, we constructed a standardized dataset containing 1,000,000 Illumina sequencing reads per genome (AA, BB, CC, EE, FF, GG, CCDD) and performed comparative analysis using RepeatExplorer to detect shared/lineage-specific LTR patterns33. Comparative analysis of repetitive sequences across rice species revealed three Copia/Angela family clusters enriched in EE and DD genomes but rare/absent in others (Fig. 6a). This lineage-restricted Angela LTR enrichment implicates their role in the distinct evolutionary trajectories of the EE genome and DD genomes.

Complete LTR retrotransposons were annotated across 16 Oryza genomes using TEsorter56. Seven LTR subfamilies from Copia and six LTR subfamilies from Gypsy exist in the Oryza genomes (Supplementary Table 10). Of them, Ale, Ikeros, and Ivana are lineages enriched in the number of intact LTR-RTs of the Copia superfamily among AA, BB, CC, CCDD, EE, and GG genotypes. Retand is the lineage with the most abundant intact LTR-RTs of the Gypsy superfamily among most diploid Oryza genotypes, while Orge lineage is the most abundant intact LTR-RTs in CC subgenome and DD subgenome (Supplementary Table 10). Among all LTRs, the number of Angela LTRs varies greatly among the Oryza genomes, with rare or absent in AA, CC, and FF genomes, and the most abundant in EE genome, followed by DD subgenome (Fig. 6b). This pattern suggests differential evolutionary dynamics of Angela LTR-RTs across Oryza genomes, characterized by their prominent expansion and retention in O. australiensis contrasted with their comparatively lower abundance in other lineages. This is consistent with the observation that transposon evolutionary histories can be independent of host genome divergence in Oryza57. Analysis of Angela LTR insertion times revealed that prior to CCDD formation (1.73–1.92 Mya), insertions were abundant in the EE genome and DD subgenome but almost absent in the CC subgenome and O. officinalis. This indicates that the amplification histories of Angela LTRs in the EE lineage and the DD subgenome were temporally coupled before CCDD formation (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 16).

To elucidate the evolutionary relationship between the EE genome and DD subgenomes at the transposon dynamics level, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis of the integrase domain sequences of Angela LTR retrotransposons inserted prior to 1.7 Mya. The results revealed that Angela LTR sequences from EE genome and DD subgenome formed multiple highly supported clades on the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 6d), with a significantly smaller genetic distance (average p-distance = 0.06) compared to their divergence from the GG genome (average p-distance = 0.12), indicating high homology between EE genome and DD subgenome. Further whole-genome synteny analysis demonstrated conserved chromosomal positions and flanking sequences of Angela LTRs inserted >1.7 Mya in both EE genome and DD subgenome (Supplementary Table 11). For instance, two LTRs on Chr4 of the DD subgenome and their counterparts on Chr4 of the EE genome were located within syntenic regions (Fig. 6e), suggesting that these LTR insertion events occurred prior to the divergence of the EE genome and DD subgenome and remained unaffected by subsequent chromosomal rearrangements. Collectively, the sequence conservation, synteny, and synchronized amplification history of Angela LTRs prior to CCDD formation suggest a close relationship between the EE lineage and the DD subgenome, supporting the hypothesis of shared common ancestry between an EE-like progenitor and the ancestral lineage that contributed the DD subgenome.

Transposon characterization was employed to further test whether, prior to CCDD formation, the ancestral lineage that contributed the DD subgenome shared common ancestry with an EE-like progenitor. Comparative analysis identified 275-bp repetitive sequences uniquely enriched in the EE genome and DD subgenome when contrasted with the CC subgenome of O. alta (Fig. 6f, Supplementary Fig. 17, and Supplementary Table 12). FISH results confirmed the specific enrichment of this repetitive sequence only in the EE genome and DD subgenome (Fig. 6h). As expected, this specific sequence is a repeat sequence of the LTR terminal in the Angela LTR structure and was named the Angela-specific repeat sequence in DD and EE (Angela-Rep-DDEE) (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 17). Genome-wide screening revealed maximal Angela-Rep-DDEE abundance in the EE genome (3709 loci) and DD subgenome (2974 loci), with secondary enrichment in GG (897) and BB (259) genomes (Fig. 6f). The number of Angela-Rep-DDEE loci in the rice genome is significantly higher than the number of Angela LTRs because many Angela LTRs are structurally incomplete due to transposition. In the DD subgenome, intact Angela LTRs matching the Angela-Rep-DDEE sequence exhibit insertion times predominantly older than 1.73 Mya (ranging from 1.73 to 3 Mya), whereas non-matching LTRs are concentrated in the 0–1.5 Mya window (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Method 5). A similar pattern is observed in the EE genome, where matching LTRs are significantly older than non-matching ones. This temporal distinction—with matching LTRs predating the estimated CCDD formation interval (1.73–1.92 Mya)—suggests that the Angela LTRs inserted in the DD subgenome prior to CCDD formation share structural characteristics with those in the EE genome. However, with the formation of CCDD, the evolutionary rate of the DD subgenome significantly accelerated, leading to substantial changes in the Angela LTR sequences. In the EE genome, continuous amplification events of Angela LTRs occurred between 0 and 3 Mya, and as the evolutionary time progressed, significant differences emerged between the younger Angela LTR sequences and the older ones. In general, Angela LTR in EE and DD genomes had similar amplification events and structural characteristics before CCDD formation, and the sequence of Angela LTR in EE and DD genomes changed significantly with transposon burst or polyploid formation. This suggests that the DD-subgenome donor and an EE-like progenitor shared common ancestry prior to CCDD formation.

Discussion

The completed T2T assembly of the O. australiensis (EE) genome provides a foundational resource for elucidating the evolutionary history of wild rice. Our integrated analysis, combining this high-quality assembly with comparative genomics, clarifies two key aspects: the relationship between the EE genome and the DD subgenome of CCDD tetraploids, and the unique mechanisms of centromere evolution in the EE lineage.

The absence of diploid DD genome species and restriction of DD genomes to three CCDD polyploids have prompted multiple hypotheses regarding DD genome origins and relationships to other Oryza genomes1,3,10,12,58–60. O. australiensis is the sole representative of the EE genome clade in Oryza genus. Several studies have suggested that the EE genome is a potential donor to the DD genome10,12,60. Recent phylogenomic studies using large sets of single-copy genes, including our own analysis of 1460 single-copy genes, have yielded increasingly consistent estimates for the divergence time between the EE genome and the DD subgenome, converging around ~5 Mya6,13. However, plant polyploidy has been shown to increase meiotic recombination frequency, facilitate speciation and adaptation, and lead to increased genetic variation14,15. To study the evolutionary rates of tetraploid subgenomes and diploid genomes, we calculated the Tajima’s relative rate score of homologous genes between the B. distachyon and CCDD subgenomes, as well as between the B. distachyon and diploid genomes (CC and EE genomes). The results confirm that the DD subgenome has evolved at a significantly accelerated rate (p < 0.001) compared to the diploid EE genome (Supplementary Table 13). This acceleration introduces a potential bias, likely causing an overestimation of the divergence time when calculated solely from single-copy gene sequences.

As neutral evolutionary markers, transposons provide independent evidence for reconciling these conflicts61. The evolutionary relationship between EE and DD genomes was analyzed through transposon evolutionary history. Angela LTR retrotransposons were identified as marker repeats in both genomes, indicating that the DD-subgenome donor and an EE-like progenitor shared common ancestry prior to CCDD formation. Three evidences support this conclusion: 1, The DD subgenome underwent similar transposon amplification events during 1.7–3 Mya as the EE genome; 2, Similar transposon amplification events led to significant enrichment of Angela LTR in EE and DD; 3, The structure of the tetraploid DD genome Angela LTR can be distinguished according to the formation time of CCDD. The Angela LTRs inserted between 1.7 and 3 Mya exhibit sequence conservation, collinearity, and synchronous amplification histories with the EE genome. The evidence supports a close homologous relationship between the EE lineage and the DD subgenome, consistent with common ancestry between the DD-subgenome donor and an EE-like progenitor. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the direct progenitor of the DD subgenome was an extinct lineage more closely related to O. australiensis than any extant species, or that an undiscovered diploid DD species exists.

Centromere repositioning and neocentromere formation are increasingly documented across plant species36,62,63. In wheat, the formation of polyploids has resulted in the repositioning of centromere regions of multiple chromosomes63. The change of centromere position on Chr6 and Chr7 of cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) and melon (Cucumis melo L.) were revealed by comparing FISH localization62. In cotton, an inversion near the D08 centromere of Zhongmian 113 led to the emergence of a neocentromere64. Centromere evolutionary dynamics were investigated using L. perrieri as an outgroup, with syntenic relationships compared across AA, EE, and FF genomes. Ancestral centromeres were defined by syntenic conservation across more than two evolutionary lineages, enabling systematic identification across the Oryza genus. Minimal centromere shifts were observed in the AA genome, whereas five FF chromosomes exhibited repositioning or neocentromere formation. Further studies revealed that pericentric inversions drove most FF genome centromere shifts, consistent with mechanisms in humans and einkorn wheat65,66. Additionally, the formation of a neocentromere on chromosome 12 of the FF genome was attributed to the segmental duplication near the centromere, consistent with the findings of Liao et al.44. In the EE genome, centromere shifts on Chr11 were caused by pericentric inversions. For other chromosomes, except for chromosome 9, neocentromere loci emerged on all remaining chromosomes. Unlike the FF genome, the formation of neocentromeres in the EE genome was not caused by inversions or segmental duplications, but rather demonstrates a rare transposon burst-driven mechanism. These neocentromeres in the EE genome represent distinct evolutionary stages. We observed two distinct satellite repeat-enriched regions on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, and 10 —one associated with CENH3 and the other lacking CENH3 binding. The neocentromeres on chromosome 2, 6, and 12 are located in a satellite-depleted region, representing a neocentromere at an early stage of formation. In contrast, neocentromeres on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, and 10 are embedded within satellite repeat-enriched regions, indicating they have progressed to a mature stage by gradually acquiring satellite repeats over evolutionary time. This pattern aligns with the centromere maturation model, where neocentromeres initially arise in satellite-poor regions and become satellite-rich as they mature67–70.

Given the considerable phylogenetic distance between O. australiensis and cultivated rice (O. sativa), the observed centromeric changes could plausibly be attributed to artificial selection and diversification within the O. sativa lineage. To determine whether centromere repositioning is indeed a distinctive feature of the EE genome, we performed a synteny analysis of centromere positions among wild rice (O. rufipogon), cultivated rice (O. sativa), and O. australiensis. The results show that centromere positions are highly conserved between cultivated and wild rice across most of chromosomes (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). This indicates that the major centromere shifts we detected are specific to O. australiensis and are not a byproduct of domestication or lineage-specific evolution in cultivated rice.

To investigate whether these changes are consistent within the EE lineage, we further compared centromere positions between the diploid EE genome and the DD subgenome of the CCDD tetraploid. Due to the lack of CENH3 data for the tetraploid, CentO satellite repeat enrichment was used as a proxy for centromere loci. Comparison across all 12 chromosomes revealed four distinct evolutionary patterns: absolute positional conservation (Chr2, 9, 10), lineage-specific shifts (Chr1, 4, 7, 11), and cases on several chromosomes (Chr3, 5, 6, 8, 12) where ancestral CentO loci remained collinear despite neocentromere formation in one or both lineages (Supplementary Fig. 20). The prevalence of shared ancestral loci suggests that EE and DD inherited a congruent centromere architecture prior to their divergence, with subsequent modifications driven primarily by lineage-specific mechanisms. These results provide further structural evidence supporting a shared ancestral origin for EE and DD.

We propose the following model for EE genome centromere evolution. Over the last ~3 Mya, massive, targeted integration of CRR LTR retrotransposons into ancestral centromeres disrupted native chromatin and CENH3 nucleosome assembly. This targeting mechanism closely resembles the invasion of centromeric satellite arrays by ATHILA transposons in Arabidopsis71. The subsequent interference with ancestral centromere organization drove the establishment of new centromeric sites within 6 Mb of the original loci in the EE genome. These neocentromeres likely formed through CRR LTR insertions. Following neocentromere establishment, epigenetic silencing mechanisms suppressed the original centromeres, gradually eliminating satellite sequences. The three centromere repositioning mechanisms we delineated inversion-driven shifts, duplication-mediated neocentromerization, and transposon burst-induced neocentromere formation-represent fundamental evolutionary strategies for chromosomal restructuring across eukaryotes. While inversion-driven shifts and duplication-mediated neocentromerization align with established models in taxa like wheat and cotton, transposon burst-induced neocentromere formation unveils transposons as potent architects of centromere plasticity through epigenetic disruption.

In summary, the T2T assembly of O. australiensis was completed, and transposon characteristics indicate that, prior to CCDD formation, the ancestral lineage that contributed the DD subgenome shared common ancestry with an EE-like progenitor. This work resolves a key gap in Oryza genus evolutionary history concerning DD genome origins and yields additional insights into tetraploid genome evolution.

Methods

Plant materials and sequencing

We sequenced the O. australiensis genome using a combination of ONT long-read and Illumina short-read technologies. For Nanopore sequencing, high-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted from young seed tissue. To generate standard long-read data, the DNA was processed following the manufacturer's protocol: size selection was performed using the BluePippin system, and a sequencing library was constructed with the SQK-LSK109 Ligation Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). For ultra-long-read data, an optimized extraction and size selection protocol was applied to enrich for exceptionally long DNA fragments, followed by library preparation also using the SQK-LSK109 kit. Both libraries were sequenced on an Oxford Nanopore PromethION platform. Additionally, for high-accuracy base correction, a paired-end library was prepared and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. This yielded ~20 Gb of ultra-long reads (~22.65× coverage), ~79.8 Gb of long reads (~88.71× coverage), and ~84 Gb of 150-bp paired-end reads (~113.15× coverage).

mRNA-seq library preparation

For RNA-seq analysis, total RNA was extracted from four tissues (young leaves, roots, flag leaves, and panicles) of O. australiensis using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (NanoDrop). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions, which included mRNA purification, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The resulting libraries were quantified by Qubit and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform to generate paired-end reads. We generate total ~50 Gb 150-bp paired-end reads.

ChIP-seq library preparation

Young leaves were collected from O. australiensis plants. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using published protocols with minor modifications72. As above, 2% PVP40 was added to the nuclei isolation buffer and nuclei washing buffer. The antibodies directed against CENH3 were used to obtain immunoprecipitated DNA. The anti-OsCENH3 antibody was custom-prepared in rabbits using synthetic peptides (C-AEPKKKLQFERSPR) and diluted to 1:200 in TNB buffer (0.5 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.75 M NaCl, and 2.5% blocking reagent). ChIP-seq libraries were prepared from the immunoprecipitated DNA using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7645S). Briefly, the DNA was subjected to end-repair, dA-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification according to the manufacturer's instructions. Libraries were sequenced using the paired-end 150 bp (PE150) strategy.

Genome de novo assembly and assessment

Organelle-related reads were removed, and ultra-long together with PromethION reads were assembled using Nextdenovo v2.3.1 (https://github.com/Nextomics/NextDenovo), yielding 13 contigs longer than 1 Mb. Telomere repeat analysis revealed that 11 contigs contained telomeric repeats at both ends, corresponding to complete chromosomes; remaining contigs were joined and gaps were filled with LR_Gapcloser73. The final assembly spanned ~901 Mb and comprised 12 T2T chromosomes. The genome was polished automatically over multiple rounds using both ONT and Illumina short reads, followed by further refinement with NextPolish74. Assembly quality was evaluated using BUSCO75 completeness, LTR Assembly Index (LAI), Merqury consensus quality value (QV), and mapping rates of ONT, Illumina, and RNA-seq reads. Detailed procedures and parameters are provided in Supplementary Method 6.

Identification and annotation of repetitive sequences

Repetitive sequences were identified and annotated using RepeatMasker (http://repeatmasker.org/) with the mipsREdat and Viridiplantae repeat libraries. Full-length LTR retrotransposons were identified using LTR_retriever76, classified by TEsorter56, and their insertion times were estimated. Briefly, the genome was indexed, followed by parallel structural prediction using LTRharvest77 and LTR_FINDER78. Results were integrated and filtered by LTR_retriever to generate a high-confidence set of full-length LTRs. Detailed workflow and parameters are provided in Supplementary Method 7.

Gene prediction and functional annotation

Gene prediction was performed by integrating multiple lines of evidence. ab initio prediction was conducted with BRAKER279 (v.2.1.5) using protein sequences from rice cultivars Nipponbare and R498. Transcriptome-based assembly was performed using HISAT280 and StringTie81, and homology-based prediction was carried out with GenomeThreader82. These three sets of evidence were integrated by EVidenceModeler83 to produce the official gene set, which was manually validated using IGV-GSAman (https://gitee.com/CJchen/IGV-sRNA). Completeness of the gene set was assessed with BUSCO. Functional annotation included protein domain prediction with InterProScan84, orthology assignment and GO enrichment with eggNOG-mapper85, and transcription factor identification with iTAK86. Detailed procedures and parameters are provided in Supplementary Method 8.

Centromere analysis

For identification of the centromere region, we used BWA87 to align the CENH3 ChIP-seq and Input-seq reads to O. australiensis genome and used SAMtools87 to obtain reads with map quality greater than 30, followed by Picard MarkDuplicates to remove PCR duplicate reads. We used MACS288 to call the peaks of CENH3 with parameters "-f BAM -g 9e8 –broad –broad-cutoff 0.1". We picked peaks with ChIP signals greater than 50 and used BEDtools89 to merge peaks within 150 kb. Finally, the start and end points of each chromosomal centromeric region based on the CENH3 ChIP-seq signal were obtained. To obtain higher-confidence centromeric regions, we used BEDtools intersect to identify the overlapping regions between the two replicates of CENH3 ChIP-seq data, and defined this intersection as the final set of centromeric regions.

Assessment of centromere region structural concordance

To evaluate the structural accuracy of centromeric regions in the final assembly, we performed a comparative dot-plot analysis against an independently assembled genome. Two assemblies were generated from the same set of ultra-long and PromethION reads: one using NECAT90 and the other using NextDenovo (the T2T assembly used in this study). Centromeric sequences were extracted from both assemblies based on annotated coordinates, aligned with Minimap291, and visualized as dot plots using dotPlotly. Structural concordance and continuity in these repetitive regions were assessed by examining the presence of unbroken diagonal lines spanning the entire centromeric interval. Detailed software parameters and procedures are provided in Supplementary Method 9.

Identification of centromeric satellite repeat sequences

To identify the centromere satellite sequence, we used a previously described method92. In brief, 1,000,000 reads were randomly selected from the input data of O. granulate (GG) (SRR6175405), O. brachyantha (FF) (DRR053294), O. australiensis (EE) (CRR1936088), O. alta (CCDD) (CRR123261), O. officinalis (CC) (DRR014203), O. minuta (BB) (DRR056215), and O. sativa (AA, NIP) (SRR25203306). The selected reads were then uploaded to RepeatExplorer and the putative satellites' repeat sequence was calculated using Tandem Repeat Analyzer33. In order to compare the differences in repetitive sequence composition within the Oryza genus, we randomly extracted 1,000,000 reads of AA, BB, CC, tetraploid CC (reads uniquely mapped to the CC subgenome), tetraploid DD (reads uniquely mapped to the DD subgenome), EE, FF, and GG, and pooled them into a single dataset with species-specific labels appended to each read name. Next, we used RepeatExplorer254 to analyze the merged file and obtain the results on differences in repetitive sequence composition within the Oryza genus.

Phylogenetic analysis

Protein homology of B. distachyon (GCA_000005505.4), O. granulate (GWHAAEL00000000), O. brachyantha (GCA_000231095.2), O. alta (GWHAZTO 00000000), O. eichingeri (https://datacommons.cyverse.org/browse/iplant/home/ shared/commons_repo/curated/Shenton_OofficinalisAnnotation_2019), O. rhizomatis (https://datacommons.cyverse.org/browse/iplant/home/shared/commons_repo/curated/Shenton_OofficinalisAnnotation_2019), O. officinalis (https://figshare.com/s/32d 0ac68cee7f647e1e3), O. minuta (https://figshare.com/s/32d0ac68cee7f647e1e3), O. punctata (GCA_000573905.1), O. meridionalis (GCA_000338895.2), O. longistaminata (GCA_000789195.1), O. glumipatula (GCA_000576495.1), O. glaberrima (GCA_000147395.1), O. barthii (GCA_000182155.2), O. nivara (GCA_000576065.1), O. rufipogon (GCA_000817225.1), O. sativa (NIP: GWHDRIN00000000; MH63: CP054676–CP054688) and O. australiensis were calculated by OrthoFinder93. To construct phylogenetic relationships, 1460 single-copy orthologues were extracted from all 19 species, and multiple alignment analysis was performed with MUSCLE v.5.194. All alignments were combined into one supergene, and a phylogenetic tree was analyzed with IQ-TREE 295. The divergence time was estimated using MCMCTree from the PAML package v.4.10.796, and the following constraints were used for time calibrations: 15–24 Mya for the origin and divergence of Oryza97 and 6–17 Mya for GG and FF genome split97.

Evolutionary rate test

To compare the relative evolutionary rates of EE genome with CC subgenome and DD subgenomes, 1460 orthologue proteins were aligned and concatenated into a super alignment. With B. distachyon as an out-group, and Tajima’s relative rate test analysis was conducted using MEGAX98.

Identification of DD and EE genomic repeat markers

The following steps were used to identify whether conserved repeat sequence markers existed between DD and EE genomes. We split the O. alta CCDD genome into CC subgenome and DD subgenome. KMC99 software was used to generate 21 bp short sequences of the CC subgenome, DD subgenome, and EE genome. Next, we screened for short sequences enriched between EE and CC subgenomes and short sequences enriched between EE and DD subgenomes, respectively. The WGS short reads related to the short sequence were extracted, and the short reads were assembled by RepeatExplorer33 software. The EE genome and CC subgenome enriched short reads did not obtain assembly results, while the EE genome and DD subgenome enriched short reads assembled consistent repeats. The assembled repeats were mapped to CC subgenome, DD subgenome, and EE genome using BLASTN (E-value ≤ e−4), respectively. It was found that a 275 bp repeat sequence was enriched only in the DD subgenome and EE genome but was lacking in the CC subgenome. Primers were designed according to the matching sequence of Angela-Rep-DDEE of the EE genome (Supplementary Table 14), and amplified in the EE genome. The biotin labeling was added to the amplified sequence, and FISH experiments were performed.

Chromosome preparation and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

Chromosome preparations were performed using an enzymatic maceration and squashing protocol100. For mitotic chromosome analysis, fresh root tips (1–2 cm) were collected from either field-grown plants or germinated seeds. The roots underwent pretreatment with 0.002 M 8-hydroxyquinoline at 20 °C for 2 h to arrest cells at prometaphase, followed by fixation in Carnoy's solution (3:1 ethanol:acetic acid) and storage at −20 °C. After thorough washing, samples were enzymatically digested in 2.5% cellulase at 37 °C for 1.5 h, then fixed overnight before being squashed onto pre-chilled slides.

For meiotic chromosome analysis, young panicles at various developmental stages were fixed in Carnoy's solution. Anthers containing microsporocytes at pachytene stage were carefully dissected from spikelets, treated with acetocarmine stain, and gently squashed under coverslips. Slides were briefly heated over a flame, then destained with 45% acetic acid. After freezing in liquid nitrogen for coverslip removal, samples were dehydrated through an ethanol series.

For FISH analysis, chromosome slides were denatured at 80 °C for 2 min in 70% formamide/2 × SSC buffer, followed by dehydration in an ethanol series. The hybridization mixture (20 μL per slide), containing 50–100 ng of labeled probe in 2 × SSC buffer with 50% formamide and 10% dextran sulfate, was applied to denatured chromosomes. Hybridization proceeded overnight at 37 °C in a humidified chamber. Post-hybridization washes consisted of 2 × SSC at 42 °C for 10 min followed by two 5-min room temperature washes.

Three DNA clones were employed as probes: (1) pAtT4 containing telomeric repeats (TTTAGGG), (2) pTa794 harboring 5S rRNA genes, and (3) pRCS2 carrying centromeric CentO repeats. Whole-genome DNA probes from O. australiensis and O. eichingeri were additionally used for two-color FISH to distinguish the EE and DD genomes. Biotin-labeled probes were detected with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated streptavidin, while digoxigenin-labeled probes were visualized using rhodamine-conjugated anti-digoxigenin antibodies. Chromosomal DNA was counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) in antifade mounting medium. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Zeiss A2 fluorescence microscope equipped with a high-resolution CCD camera.
