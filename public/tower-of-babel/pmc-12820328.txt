Whole transcriptome analysis reveals T cell signaling activation in septic patients with thrombocytopenia
Authors: I-Chieh Chen, Yu-Han Jiang, Tzu-Hung Hsiao, Wen-Cheng Chao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis, a life-threatening condition triggered by a dysregulated immune response to infection, remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide. Thrombocytopenia, a common sepsis complication, is linked to poor outcomes and reflects disease severity and specific immunological changes affecting patient outcomes. This study aimed to investigate transcriptomic differences between septic patients with and without thrombocytopenia and to explore immune features related to clinical outcomes using whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). We conducted a prospective study on 52 patients who were admitted to ICU for sepsis, including 22 with thrombocytopenia and 30 without. Blood samples were collected on both day 1 and 8, and RNA-Seq was performed to analyze transcriptomic profiles. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, and functional enrichment analyses were conducted to explore biological pathways and processes. T-cell receptor (TCR) diversity was assessed using MiXCR and VDJTools software. Thrombocytopenic critically ill septic patients exhibited significantly higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores and mortality rates compared to non-thrombocytopenic patients. Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct gene expression profiles between the two groups, with marked upregulation of myeloproliferative and T-cell activation signaling pathways in thrombocytopenic patients on day 1. Additionally, TCR diversity counts showed no statistically significant differences between thrombocytopenic and non-thrombocytopenic patients at day 1 or day 8. Thrombocytopenia in septic ICU patients is associated with altered heme/porphyrin metabolism, erythrocyte development, and immune activation. Lower TCR diversity on day 1 in thrombocytopenic patients suggests early immune repertoire perturbations; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and validated with targeted immune repertoire sequencing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32584-9.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening disease caused by an impaired immune response to infection and a leading cause of death. The death toll from this infection is staggering, accounting for 20% of deaths worldwide. Despite advances in critical care medicine, the complex and dynamic pathophysiology of sepsis poses significant obstacles to a comprehensive understanding and improvement of patient outcomes1. However, despite these advances, the complex and dynamic nature of sepsis still presents significant challenges in understanding its pathophysiology and improving patient outcomes2. Moreover, coagulation dysregulation and endothelial dysfunction, known as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), have been identified as one of the major problems in critically ill patients with sepsis, but the relationship between DIC and immune responses remains unclear3–5. Recent studies, including ours, have highlighted the importance of immune responses, especially T cell-mediated immunity, in the progression and resolution of sepsis6–9.

Thrombocytopenia is one of the fundamental prognostic factors in patients with sepsis and is thought to be a consequence of sepsis-associated DIC10. DIC may result from an uncontrolled activated coagulation system, which causes the formation of blood clots in small blood vessels throughout the body, which leads to organ dysfunction and organ failure11,12. In addition, more and more studies have shown that DIC is not only involved in the coagulation system, but also associated with dysregulation of inflammation in septic patients13,14. Furthermore, recent studies used unsupervised analysis of aggregated transcriptomic and clinical data to elucidate the high heterogeneity of sepsis and found that thrombocytopenia is an important phenotype associated with mortality in septic patients15,16. Of note, in the above transcriptome study of 700 patients with sepsis, in addition to coagulation disorder, adaptive immune dysfunction is another important phenotype associated with mortality16. However, recent studies have mainly focused on the hemostatic aspects of thrombocytopenia in sepsis, and its immunological effects have only been limitedly explored. Hence, understanding the relationship between thrombocytopenia and immune responses in sepsis may provide valuable insights into disease mechanisms and help identify therapeutic targets for personalized medicine in septic patients5,17. To gain such insights, we utilized whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing analysis (RNA-Seq), a revolutionary technology that allows the simultaneous study of multiple biological pathways and processes in complex diseases like sepsis8,18. RNA-Seq allows the study of multiple biological pathways and processes simultaneously. Thus, we were able to investigate the relationship between thrombocytopenia and immune responses in critically ill patients with sepsis. In this study, we hypothesized that thrombocytopenia in sepsis is not only a marker of disease severity but also reflects specific immunological changes that may affect patient outcome. To this end, we performed whole-transcriptome analysis on days 1 and 8 in patients with severe sepsis, with or without thrombocytopenia.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

The study was approved by the Taichung Veterans General Hospital (TCVGH) Institutional Review Board (IRB No. CE22110A), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Between December 2018 and January 2022, fifty-two thrombocytopenia ICU patients with sepsis were recruited from TCVGH between January 2019 and April 2022. Demographic information was collected at enrollment, and blood samples were collected on days 1 and 8. Two 4 mL aliquots of blood were collected in sterile EDTA vacuum containers and immediately transferred to RNase-free vials containing RNAlater® (ThermoFisher) for transcriptome stabilization.

Thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count ≤ 150 × 103/µL at ICU admission11. Disease severity was assessed using two standard ICU scoring systems: the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health.

Evaluation II (APACHE II) score and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score19,20. APACHE II incorporates age, chronic health status, and physiologic parameters to classify disease severity, whereas SOFA quantifies the extent of organ dysfunction across six organ systems on a scale of 0–421. Notably, thrombocytopenia is one of the components of the SOFA score but not the APACHE II score. These scoring systems were collected at enrollment to characterize baseline clinical severity.

Sample preparation and RNA sequencing

In this study, the blood samples were collected using PAXgene Blood RNA tubes (PreAnalytiX, Qiagen/BD), which are specifically designed to stabilize and preserve RNA in blood. Approximately 2.5 milliliters of whole blood was collected, processed, and stored from each participant according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were kept at room temperature for 2 h, then stored at − 20 °C overnight, and transferred to − 80 °C for long-term storage until RNA extraction. RNA extraction was performed using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit, yielding an average RNA integrity number (RIN) of 8.21 ± 0.59. Library construction followed the manufacturer’s instructions, utilizing 1000 ng of fragmented RNA for subsequent experiments. For RNA-Seq conducted on the NovaSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), 150 bp paired-end reads were generated, with each sample producing at least 50–60 million reads. The RNA-Seq dataset has been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under accession number GSE273700. This dataset was employed for cellular proportion and pathway analysis.

Bioinformatics analyses

Sequencing data achieved high quality with a Phred score of 30, and sequence reads were mapped to the reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using HISAT222. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the R package DESeq223, and read counts were quantified using featureCounts24. On average, the mapping rate and read counts were 87.6% ± 4.8% and 65.5 million ± 17.6 million reads, respectively. Functional enrichment and pathway analysis based on the KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/)25 and Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org/) databases were performed using the R package gprofiler26. Significance was determined using a corrected p-value threshold of less than 0.05. All DEGs were functionally annotated using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)27. To visualize the results of GSEA, enrichment maps were created that were visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.028. Enrichment maps organize gene sets in a network based on similarity: nodes represent gene sets, links indicate overlap of member genes, and node colors represent enrichment values27.

T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire analysis was performed using RNA-Seq data. MiXCR v3.0.1329,30 was used to assemble complementarity-determining region-3 (CDR3) sequences and quantify unique clonotypes based on the amino acid CDR3 region31. Subsequently, VDJTools v1.2.132 software was then used to calculate clonotype counts, total CDR3 counts, and Shannon diversity indices for each sample.

TCR diversity was evaluated using one predefined comparison: thrombocytopenia versus non-thrombocytopenia groups on days 1 and 8, which were selected to reflect differences in immune recovery trajectories related to clinical outcomes. No additional normalization beyond the default VDJTools workflow was applied. Given that TCR repertoires were inferred from bulk RNA-Seq rather than dedicated TCR-Seq, all TCR metrics are interpreted as exploratory indicators of clonal diversity rather than absolute repertoire measurements. Variations in TCR diversity were considered as potential indicators of adaptive immune competence, which may be associated with clinical outcomes in septic patients. Higher TCR diversity could reflect a broader immune response capacity and potentially better prognosis.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are reported as numbers (percentages) and medians with interquartile ranges. Fisher’s exact test and the Mann–Whitney U test were employed to compare responses and non-responses. A significance level of 0.05 was used for clinical data, and all statistical analyses were two-sided. R version 4.1.1 was used for all data analyses.

Results

Characterization of the ICU patients

In this study, we enrolled 52 critically ill patients with sepsis at TCVGH, of which 22 had thrombocytopenia (case group) and 30 did not have thrombocytopenia (control group). The case group had a mean age of 69 years (range: 56.3–78.5), while the control group had a mean age of 78 years (range: 62–85.75) (Table 1). Gender distribution and BMI showed no significant differences between the groups. Severity scores such as acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) were similar between the groups (case: 29.5 [26.00–31.75] vs. control: 27.523–31, P = 0.163), indicating comparable illness severity upon admission. Patients in the thrombocytopenia group exhibited significantly higher SOFA scores on days 1, 3, and 7 compared to the control group (P < 0.001). No significant differences were found in comorbidities such as diabetes, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and end-stage renal disease. Na, K and creatinine levels were comparable between the groups. But, hematocrit (Hct) levels were significantly lower in the case group (P = 0.022). Of note, the case group had significantly fewer high platelet counts and lower platelet counts (both P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in white blood cell counts and HbA1C levels. The results showed that the number of ventilation days, length of ICU stay, and length of hospitalization were not significantly different between the groups. On day 8, the overall mortality rate was 26.9% (14/52). Notably, 3 patients (10%) in the initial control group died, highlighting that normal initial platelet counts do not guarantee survival. Conversely, 11 patients (50%) in the initial thrombocytopenia group died, indicating a significantly higher mortality risk (P = 0.004). Moreover, the Kaplan-Meier survival curve was used to analyze the cumulative mortality, as shown in Fig. 1. The cumulative incidence of death in the case group (50%) was significantly higher than that in the control group (10%, p = 0.0059 by log-rank test). These results indicate that thrombocytopenic ICU patients with sepsis had higher SOFA scores and mortality, and lower platelet counts and hematocrit values, highlighting the severity and poor prognosis associated with thrombocytopenia in septic patients.

Functional enrichment analysis

In Fig. 2A, using principal component analysis (PCA) on the top 500 variable genes from all of the samples, we discovered that thrombocytopenic ICU patients with sepsis had significantly different transcriptomes between the case (red triangles) and control (green circles) groups. First principal component (PC1) explained 23% of the variance, and PC2 explained 18% of the variance, demonstrating a clear separation between the two groups. Transcriptomic features on day 1 showed marked upregulated myeloproliferative and slightly upregulated T cell activation signaling in critically ill septic patients with thrombocytopenia. The differential expression genes (DEGs) were then identified by comparing gene expression profiles between the case and control groups using the criteria of p < 0.01 and log fold change > 0.25 or < -0.25. As shown in Fig. 2B, the DESeq2 volcano plot displays the log2 fold change versus -log10(p-adjusted) values. Red dots indicate the significantly differentially expressed genes between the case and control groups. These significant DEGs, such as HBG1, HBG2, SERPINB10, RHAG, and CA1 play crucial roles in various biological processes related to the thrombocytopenia. In the case group, 533 genes were upregulated and 158 genes were downregulated. Gene Ontology (GO) and Metascape-immune pathway of these DEGs and enrichment analyses were performed based out (Supplemental Fig. 1). In Fig. 2C, GO bubble plots were constructed for the biological processes of the top 25 DEGs from the dataset. We found that the DEGs from the thrombocytopenic ICU patients with sepsis were enriched in the heme/porphyrin metabolism, erythrocyte development/homeostasis, peroxidase activity, oxidoreductase activity, and immune functions like T cell costimulation. In the Metascape enrichment analysis, the results showed significant enrichment pathways of DEGs contained immune-related processes, such as hematopoiesis, adaptive immune system, porphyrin metabolism, autophagy regulation, T cell signaling pathway and erythrocyte functions (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, GO analysis of DEGs between the two groups was also confirmed by GO-Emap plot (Fig. 2D), which grouped the significant pathways into three major clusters: heme/porphyrin metabolism and erythroid processes, immune/inflammatory processes, and processes of cellular component organization. Highly interconnected nodes were observed within and between these clusters, suggesting that the DEGs are involved in the coordinated dysregulation of an integrated biological program spanning hematopoietic processes, immune responses, and cellular homeostatic functions.

Immune dysregulation at day 8 transcriptomics

On day 8, the DESeq2 PCA plot (Fig. 3A) showed a clear separation between control and case samples, indicating distinct differences between the two groups. The volcano plot (Fig. 3B) revealed prominent upregulation in the T cell activation cluster, highlighting significantly differentially expressed genes and suggesting a dysregulated T cell response that may drive immunopathology in critical illness. The enrichment dot plot (Fig. 3C) presents the GO enrichment analysis, showcasing notable processes such as T cell activation, leukocyte migration, and cytokine production, which are key to immune and inflammatory responses. Moreover, significant enrichment of gene ontology terms and pathways related to immune response, cell activation, and pathogen-related processes are shown by functional enrichment analysis (Supplemental Fig. 2). The GO-Emap plot (Fig. 3D) identifies three major clusters: T cell activation, heme/porphyrin metabolism and erythrocyte development, and vesicle transport and cellular homeostasis. This highlights the functional interplay between disrupted modules related to activated T cell responses, altered hematopoiesis, and disrupted cellular processes in these patients.

GO enrichment in thrombocytopenia patient groups

To investigate molecular pathways associated with thrombocytopenia, we performed GO term enrichment analysis for each patient group. In Day 1 patients, enriched pathways included T cell receptor signaling, gas transport, hydrogen peroxide catabolism, porphyrin metabolism, and erythrocyte development (Supplementary Table 1). By Day 8, immune-related pathways such as lymphocyte differentiation, regulation of inflammatory response, and myeloid leukocyte activation were enriched (Supplementary Table 2). Among Day 8 patients who died, enrichment was primarily in immune cell functions, including lymphocyte differentiation, leukocyte adhesion, and leukocyte-mediated immunity (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, to explore coagulation pathway activity, we examined coagulation-related GO terms (e.g., blood coagulation, regulation of coagulation, protein activation cascade), which were not significantly enriched in any group. All enrichment results are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

TCR diversity analysis in thrombocytopenic sepsis patients

To investigate whether alterations in T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire diversity were associated with thrombocytopenia in sepsis, we analyzed TCR clonotypes inferred from RNA-Seq data using MiXCR and VDJTools. Three complementary diversity metrics were assessed: total diversity counts (number of unique clonotypes), D50 index (percentage of dominant clonotypes accounting for 50% of the total repertoire), and the inverse Simpson index (a measure of clonal evenness) (Fig. 4A–C).

Comparisons between thrombocytopenia cases and non-thrombocytopenia controls revealed no statistically significant differences in any of the three TCR diversity metrics at either day 1 (diversity counts: p = 0.075; D50 index: p = 0.28; inverse Simpson index: p = 0.46) or day 8 (diversity counts: p = 0.70; D50 index: p = 0.67; inverse Simpson index: p = 0.36). Functional enrichment analysis in ICU patients with sepsis categorized by overall hospital-mortality

We categorized thrombocytopenic ICU patients with sepsis based on their hospital mortality outcomes and performed functional enrichment analysis on the day 8 transcriptomic data. As shown in Fig. 5A, the PCA indicated that PC1 accounted for 24% of the variance in gene expression patterns between survivors (live group) and non-survivors (mortality group). A volcano plot representing DEGs between the live and mortality groups is shown in Fig. 5B. Red points mark the genes with significantly increased expression in the mortality group compared to the live group (FDR < 0.01).

GO analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed enrichment of biological processes related to T cell activation (Fig. 5C). Notably, processes such as T cell activation, T cell differentiation, lymphocyte activation, and regulation of T cell activation were among the top enriched terms (Fig. 5C). The GO-Emap plot of 20 enriched gene ontology “biological process” terms (FDR < 0.01) showed that the majority of the ontology terms were associated with T cell activation-related processes (Fig. 5D). These enriched ontology terms included T cell activation, T cell proliferation, T cell differentiation, T cell costimulation, and other processes related to T cell activation signaling pathways. It suggested that dysregulation of T cell activation signaling pathways were observed in the mortality groups.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the clinical and molecular characteristics of thrombocytopenic critically ill patients with sepsis. The complex pathophysiology underlying the poor outcome was highlighted by a significant upregulation of immune and hematopoietic pathways, especially T cell activation signaling. This dysregulated T cell activation signaling was associated with higher disease severity and mortality in these patients. This finding is crucial, as immunotherapies are increasingly being trialed aiming to restore dysregulated adaptive immunity in sepsis. However, the optimal characterization of immunity remains a niche7,33,34. In our study, on day 1, a significant upregulation of myeloproliferative signaling and a slight increase in T cell activation in the case group were observed. However, by day 8, T cell activation became the dominant feature in the transcriptomic landscape of the case group. This persistent T cell activation could represent an ‘overshooting’ of the adaptive immune system, potentially contributing to ongoing inflammation and organ dysfunction.

Previous studies suggest that sepsis-induced morbidity and mortality result from persistent immune activation and inflammation35. Xiao et al. proposed a model elucidating the host immune response in trauma and sepsis, characterizing sepsis pathophysiology as a prolonged inflammatory state driven by the innate immune system, which leads to organ dysfunction and failure. In addition to the difference of myeloproliferative and T cell activation between case and control, we also find that the enriched pathways between two groups are distinct. Through Gene Ontology mapping of the DEGs, we identified that the major differences in pathways between day 1 and day 8 were primarily related to the regulation of myeloproliferative and T cell activation, respectively. On day 1, the volcano plot highlights several upregulated genes in the case group, including HBG1, HBG2, CA1, RHAG, RIPOR3, RNF14, and SERPINB10, which are associated with an early compensatory response in erythropoiesis, possibly due to the developing thrombocytopenia or broader hematopoietic dysregulation36–40. Conversely, genes such as CD8, ZNF683 and KLRG1 were downregulated in this group. By day 8, as shown in Fig. 3, the gene expression landscape had evolved considerably, with a shift towards differences in T cell activation pathways. In the case group, we observed upregulation of genes such as THBS1, ADAMTS3, VSIG4, QSOX1, PGD, and AREG, which are associated with various aspects of immune modulation and cellular stress responses41–43. In addition, CD8A was downregulated, suggesting altered T cell functionality in these patients44. The persistent downregulation of CD8A from day 1 to day 8 in thrombocytopenic patients is particularly noteworthy, as it may indicate an ongoing suppression or dysfunction of cytotoxic T cells throughout the course of sepsis45,46. This could have implications for the patient’s ability to combat secondary infections or modulate the inflammatory response. These findings highlight the complex and dynamic nature of the immune response in sepsis, particularly the evolving role of adaptive immunity.

Recent studies indicated the significant association between DIC and T cell activation in sepsis47. The inflammatory milieu in sepsis, exacerbated by DIC, creates a complex microenvironment that influences T cell responses through hyper-inflammation (cytokine storm), immune cell trafficking, and platelet-T cell interactions, collectively contributing to thrombocytopenia in septic patients48. Microthrombi formation in DIC may lead to endothelial dysfunction, impeding the migration of immune cells, including T cells, to sites of infection or injury49. The endothelium, acting as a critical interface between coagulation and immune systems, can have its barrier function disrupted by inflammatory cytokines and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)50, exposing the subendothelial matrix and promoting platelet adhesion and T cell aggregation. This interaction between activated endothelial cells and T cells may further amplify the inflammatory response, creating a vicious cycle of endothelial damage and immune activation, potentially leading to areas of inadequate immune surveillance or excessive inflammation14. In the acute phase of sepsis, high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines driving DIC may act on T cells, leading to their activation. This cytokine storm can result in an overactive immune response, causing further tissue damage and perpetuating the cycle of inflammation and coagulation7. However, sustained high levels of these cytokines may also lead to T cell exhaustion, contributing to the immunoparalysis observed in the subacute stage of sepsis. Furthermore, platelets, central to the coagulation process, interact with T cells. Platelet-derived signals can modulate T cell function, either enhancing their activation in early sepsis or contributing to their exhaustion and dysfunction in later stages13. Activated platelets can adhere to leukocytes, including T cells, through P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) and other adhesion molecules51, facilitating the transfer of pro-inflammatory and immunomodulatory signals. Platelets release various soluble factors such as cytokines (e.g., IL-1β), chemokines (e.g., RANTES), and growth factors (e.g., TGF-β) that can modulate T cell responses52. Moreover, platelet-derived microparticles (PMPs) can deliver bioactive molecules to T cells, influencing their activation, differentiation, and function53. These complex interactions underscore the intricate relationship between DIC, thrombocytopenia, and adaptive immune responses in the pathophysiology of sepsis.

Finally, we conducted a detailed transcriptomic analysis of ICU patients with sepsis, categorizing them based on overall mortality on day 8. Functional enrichment analysis of the day 1 and day 8 transcriptomic data revealed crucial differences between survivors and non-survivors. On day 8 of hospitalization, significant differences in TCR diversity counts were observed between survivors and non-survivors (p = 0.034, Supplemental Fig. 3A), highlighting the potential prognostic value of TCR diversity in sepsis patients. Our findings demonstrate that higher TCR diversity counts are associated with better survival outcomes, suggesting a robust and diverse T-cell response is beneficial in overcoming sepsis. It is consistent with our previous study8, TCR diversity with improved ability to respond to infection, potentially offering a survival advantage in the face of severe infections like sepsis54. Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in the D50 index or inverse Simpson index between survivors and non-survivors on either day 1 or day 8. This could indicate that while overall TCR diversity is important, specific metrics of TCR clonality or evenness may not be as critical in determining sepsis outcomes. It is possible that the ability to mount a broad immune response, as reflected by overall diversity counts, is more crucial in the early phases of sepsis recovery than the distribution or dominance of specific TCR clones.

To further explore the relationship between TCR diversity and immune functional status, we examined the correlation between TCR diversity metrics and T cell exhaustion marker expression. We analyzed the expression levels of TBX21 and GZMK genes from our RNA-seq data against TCR diversity indices. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 4, we observed significant positive correlations between TCR diversity and both TBX21 (R = 0.35, p = 0.01) and GZMK (R = 0.30, p = 0.018) expression. These findings suggest that higher TCR diversity is associated with increased expression of T cell exhaustion markers, which may reflect an activated yet partially exhausted T cell state in sepsis patients. This correlation provides additional mechanistic insights into how TCR diversity relates to T cell functional status during sepsis progression.

Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 5, a dysregulated immune response that may contribute to poor outcomes. The enriched biological processes, including T cell activation, differentiation, and proliferation, underscore the critical role of adaptive immunity in sepsis prognosis. T cell activation might be associated with mortality in sepsis cases. These deaths are likely secondary to an overexuberant immunoinflammatory response, characterized by extremely elevated proinflammatory cytokines46,55–57.

The immune response during early sepsis is not static but fluctuates between pro-inflammatory activation and counter-regulatory suppression. Indeed, multiple studies have shown that hyperactivation and immunosuppression can occur simultaneously in sepsis rather than sequentially58. The enrichment of T cell–activation pathways in our data may reflect upstream signaling and compensatory proliferation, while the reduction of effector markers such as CD8A is consistent with effector T-cell dysfunction, apoptosis, or exhaustion described in septic patients59. This dynamic equilibrium between activation and inhibition may explain why pathway-level analyses and single-gene expression trends appear divergent, and is consistent with the concept of an oscillatory or “immune-rebalancing” process during sepsis recovery60. In our study, this pattern likely reflects the well-recognized “mixed immune phenotype” of sepsis, in which T-cell activation pathways are enriched due to compensatory or upstream signaling activity, whereas effector markers such as CD8A are reduced because of lymphocyte dysfunction, exhaustion, or depletion. Therefore, pathway-level activation signatures and single-gene suppression can coexist and are not biologically contradictory.

Consistent with these observations, a recent study61 demonstrated that platelet subpopulations undergo profound phenotypic alterations during systemic inflammation, including shifts toward pro-inflammatory, pro-coagulant, and immunomodulatory states. These abnormal platelet subsets were linked to dysregulated immune-coagulation crosstalk, particularly affecting T cell activation, endothelial interactions, and microthrombi formation. Collectively, these findings support our observations that thrombocytopenia and DIC-related pathways may modulate adaptive immunity in sepsis. The study also highlights that platelets possess previously underappreciated transcriptional diversity and immunologic functions, reinforcing the biological plausibility of our findings regarding platelet–T cell interactions and sustained T cell activation in thrombocytopenic septic patients. Future research should integrate single-cell platelet profiling to more precisely define the contribution of platelet heterogeneity to immune dysregulation in sepsis.

Our study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, our analysis was based on a relatively small sample size from a single medical center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should aim to include larger, more diverse patient populations across multiple centers to validate and extend our results. Second, while our transcriptomic analysis provides valuable insights into gene expression changes associated with sepsis outcomes, the use of bulk RNA-Seq presents inherent limitations in resolving cell-type-specific signals. Specifically, bulk RNA-Seq cannot distinguish between effector T cell and regulatory T cell signatures with sufficient granularity, as these cell types are intermixed in the sequencing library. Although we identified broad T cell activation pathways through pathway enrichment analysis, we acknowledge that dissecting the relative contributions of distinct T cell subsets—such as effector T cells (CD4 + Th1/Th17, CD8 + cytotoxic T cells) versus regulatory T cells (Tregs)—requires single-cell resolution technologies. Future studies employing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq), paired with surface marker profiling and T cell receptor sequencing at single-cell resolution, would enable precise characterization of T cell subset dynamics, functional states, and clonal expansion patterns during sepsis progression62. Third, our study design was limited to transcriptomic analysis at two specific time points (days 1 and 8). Longitudinal studies with more frequent sampling in critically ill patients with sepsis would be essential to fully characterize the dynamic changes in the adaptive immune response over the course of the disease33,63,64. Additionally, functional validation through in vitro assays and animal models could provide crucial mechanistic insights into the dysregulated adaptive immunity observed in sepsis.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrate a transcriptomic profile of critically ill septic patients with thrombocytopenia, including dysregulation of heme/porphyrin metabolism, erythrocyte development and T cell activation pathways. Thrombocytopenic patients have higher SOFA scores and mortality, which may be due to upregulation of T-cell activation genes. Higher TCR diversity counts were associated with better survival outcomes. Despite TCR diversity increased during recovery, no significant differences were found between groups. These findings provide new insights into the pathophysiology of sepsis-associated thrombocytopenia and suggest potential targets for future therapeutic interventions.
