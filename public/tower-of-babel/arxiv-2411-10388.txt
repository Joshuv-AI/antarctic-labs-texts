When alpha-complexes collapse onto codimension-1 submanifolds
Dominique Attali, Mattéo Clémot, Bianca B. Dornelas, André Lieutier

Abstract
Given a finite set of points P P sampling an unknown smooth surface \M ⊆ ℝ 3 \M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3} , our goal is to triangulate \M \M based solely on P P . Assuming \M \M is a smooth orientable submanifold of codimension 1 1 in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , we introduce a simple algorithm, Naive Squash , which simplifies the α \alpha -complex of P P by repeatedly applying a new type of collapse called vertical relative to \M \M . Naive Squash also has a practical version that does not require knowledge of \M \M . We establish conditions under which both the naive and practical Squash algorithms output a triangulation of \M \M . We provide a bound on the angle formed by triangles in the α \alpha -complex with \M \M , yielding sampling conditions on P P that are competitive with existing literature for smooth surfaces embedded in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , while offering a more compartmentalized proof. As a by-product, we obtain that the restricted Delaunay complex of P P triangulates \M when \M is a smooth surface in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} under weaker conditions than existing ones.

Techniques.

Our proof of correctness for the squash algorithms is more
compartmentalized than the ones present in the literature: we first
consider a smooth orientable submanifold
\M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
codimension one and a general simplicial complex
K
embedded in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
and contained within a small tubular neighborhood of

\M
. We introduce
vertical
collapses (relative to
\M
) in
K

which remove
d
-simplices of
K
that either have no
d
-simplices of

K
above them in directions normal to
\M
or no
d
-simplices of
K

below them in directions normal to
\M
.

NaiveVerticalSimplification

iteratively
applies vertical collapses relative to
\M
.

PracticalVerticalSimplification

does not depend on knowledge of
\M
and applies vertical collapses
relative to a hyperplane, constructed dynamically based on the simplex
currently considered for collapse.

We examine conditions for the correctness of these algorithms. Apart
from the requirement that
K
has no vertical
i
-simplices relative
to
\M
for
0<i<d
and that its support projects onto
\M
and
fully covers it, we require the
vertical convexity
of
K

relative to
\M
. This means that each normal line to
\M
at a point

m
(restricted to a small ball around
m
) intersects the support of

K
in a convex set. For

PracticalVerticalSimplification

, an additional
requirement is that the
(d-1)
-simplices of
K
must form an angle of
at most
\frac{\pi}{4}
with
\M
.

Afterwards, we present
\operatorname{\tt PracticalSquash}
and
\operatorname{\tt NaiveSquash}
, which
initialize the previous algorithms with
K
as the
\alpha
-complex of
a point set
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
that samples
\M
. We show that
correctness is guaranteed when the
i
-simplices in the
\alpha
-complex form small angles
with
\M
for
0<i<d
. We provide explicit upper bounds for these
angles, expressed in terms of
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and
\alpha
,
where
\varepsilon
and

control the sample density and noise
in
P
.

We analyze the case
d=3

\alpha

Related work.

The Squash algorithms (both practical and naive) are similar to Wrap

[
34
,
13
]
in that they compute a subcomplex

of the Delaunay complex
of

and the nature of the collapses differ between the

\M

unlike Wrap, which uses flow lines derived from

collapsing sequence. This distinction allows us to address the general
case first and then focus on the specific case of the

\alpha
-complex. Moreover, while we guarantee correctness for a
larger interval of the ratio
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{R}}
compared to
previous literature, most existing work addresses non-uniform sampling
cases, whereas our work focuses on uniform sampling.

The vertical convexity assumption, crucial for the correctness of our
algorithms, has been employed in various forms to establish
collapsibility of certain classes of simplicial complexes

[
1
,
22
,
11
]
.
Similarly, bounding the angle between the manifold and the
simplices used for reconstruction has been essential in prior work

[
25
,
24
,
23
,
6
,
10
]
.
Our bound (
1
) remains true when replacing

\mathcal{R}
with the local feature size of

, as explained in

. The thus modified bound improves upon

[
25
, Lemma 3.5]
.

At last, for

, we show in

that the restricted Delaunay complex is generically homeomorphic to

for

. In contrast, it is proven to
be homeomorphic to
\M
only if
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{R}}\leq 0.09

[
24
, Theorem 13.16]
, a result based on the

[
24
, Theorem 13.1]
. Our
proof bypasses this requirement, relying instead on

.

Subsets and submanifold.

Given a subset

X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

X
is denoted as
\operatorname{conv}(X)
and the affine space spanned by

\operatorname{Aff}(X)

. The

of

For any point

-offset of

The

of

that have at least two nearest points in

. The

of

, denoted as

and

.
Furthermore, we define the projection map
\pi_{X}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\operatorname{axis}(X)\to X
, which associates each point

with its unique closest point in
X
. This projection map is well-defined on every subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that does not intersect
\operatorname{axis}(X)
, particularly on every
r
-offset of
X
with
r<\operatorname{Reach}(X)
.

Throughout the paper, we designate
\M
as a compact

submanifold
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of codimension one, and, therefore, orientable (see

[

Given

We define

[

. We
let

Definition 2

Definition 3

The next lemma is a key property of vertically convex

Lemma 4

Suppose that

\pi_{\M}(\boldsymbol{\lvert}K\boldsymbol{\rvert})

\partial\boldsymbol{\lvert}K\boldsymbol{\rvert}=\operatorname{UpperSkin}_{\M}(\boldsymbol{\lvert}K\boldsymbol{\rvert})\cup\operatorname{LowerSkin}_{\M}(\boldsymbol{\lvert}K\boldsymbol{\rvert}).

Lemma 5

Let

\displaystyle\operatorname{UpperComplex}_{\M}(K)

\displaystyle=\{\nu\subseteq\partial K\mid\operatorname{conv}(\nu)\subseteq\operatorname{LowerSkin}_{\M}(\boldsymbol{\lvert}K\boldsymbol{\rvert})\}.

Lemma 15

(Upon termination)
.

Consider

A.1
Definitions and basic properties

Consider two affine spaces
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
U
and
V

be the vector spaces associated to
X
and
Y
, respectively. We
recall that the angle between two affine spaces
X
and
Y
is the
same as the angle between the two vector spaces
U
and
V
and is
defined as

\angle(X,Y)=\angle(U,V)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in U\\
\|u\|=1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v).

The definition is not symmetric in
X
and
Y
, unless the two affine spaces
X

and
Y
(or equivalently, the two vector spaces
U
and
V
) have same dimension. Given a vector space
V
, denote by

V^{\perp}
its orthogonal complement. The following properties will be
useful:

Property A30
.

Let
U
and
V
be two vector spaces. Then,

\angle(U,V)=\angle(V^{\perp},U^{\perp}).

Property A31
.

Let
U
and
V
be two vector spaces, with
\dim U=1
. Then,

\angle(U,V)+\angle(U,V^{\perp})=\frac{\pi}{2}.

The angle between two vector spaces can also be written in terms of all hyperplanes that contain the second vector space:

Lemma A32
.

Let
U
,
V
be two vector spaces and
u\in U
. Then,

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\supseteq V\\
\operatorname{codim}(H)=1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h)

(8)

and

\displaystyle\angle(U,V)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\supseteq V\\
\operatorname{codim}(H)=1\end{subarray}}\angle(U,H),

(9)

where
H
designates a vector space and
\operatorname{codim}(H)=\dim(H^{\perp})
.

Proof.

For any codimension one vector space
H\supseteq V
, it holds that

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v)\geq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h).

Thus, we have one direction of (
8
):

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v)\geq\max_{{H\supseteq V}\atop{\operatorname{codim}(H)=1}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h).

Note that if
u\in V
, (
8
)
is trivially satisfied as both sides are
0
. Hence, for the
other direction of (
8
), we consider
u\notin V
.
Denote the vector in
V
closest to
u
by
\pi_{V}(u)=\arg\min_{v\in V}(v-u)^{2}
. From the definition of the angle, it
holds that

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v)=\angle(u,\pi_{V}(u))=\arcsin\frac{\|\pi_{V}(u)-u\|}{\|u\|}.

Next, define

w=u-\pi_{V}(u)\in V^{\perp}

and let
W
be the vector space spanned by
w
. Since
\omega\in V^{\perp}
, one has
V\subseteq W^{\perp}
. Moreover,
\pi_{W^{\perp}}(u)=\pi_{V}(u)
because
u-\pi_{V}(u)=\omega\in W
. Hence,

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v)

\displaystyle=\angle(u,\pi_{V}(u))=\angle(u,\pi_{W^{\perp}}(u))=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in W^{\perp}\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{{H\supseteq V}\atop{\operatorname{codim}(H)=1}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h).

Finally, (
9
) holds because

\displaystyle\angle(U,V)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in U\\
\|u\|=1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in V\\
\|v\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,v)

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in U\\
\|u\|=1\end{subarray}}\max_{{H\supseteq V}\atop{\operatorname{codim}(H)=1}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h)

\displaystyle=\max_{{H\supseteq V}\atop{\operatorname{codim}(H)=1}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in U\\
\|u\|=1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\in H\\
\|h\|=1\end{subarray}}\angle(u,h)

\displaystyle=\max_{{H\supseteq V}\atop{\operatorname{codim}(H)=1}}\angle(U,H).\qed

Lemma A33

Let

be a compact submanifold of

with positive reach.
If

at

∎

Let
\rho({\tau})

Lemma A34
.

Let
\M
be a compact submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with positive reach.
If

bc
, for

\displaystyle\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(abc),\mathbf{T}_{a}\M\,

\displaystyle\leq\,\frac{\rho({abc})}{\operatorname{lfs}(a)}

is an obtuse triangle and

\displaystyle\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(abc),\mathbf{T}_{a}\M\,

\displaystyle\leq\,\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\rho({abc})}{\operatorname{lfs}(a)}

is an acute triangle.

If

is obtuse, the bound is tight and happens when

is a

Proof.

Let
A=\angle cab
,
\vec{u}=\frac{b-a}{\|b-a\|}

\vec{v}=\frac{c-a}{\|c-a\|}

H
be any hyperplane that contains

\mathbf{T}_{a}\M

\vec{n}
be an unit vector orthogonal to

\vec{n}
is orthogonal to
\M
at

\displaystyle\theta_{u}

\displaystyle=\arccos(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{n}),

\displaystyle\theta_{v}

\displaystyle=\arccos(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}).

Thanks to Lemma

, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\cos\theta_{u}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\vec{u}\cdot\vec{n}\right|=\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(ab),H\leq\frac{\|b-a\|}{2\,\operatorname{lfs}(a)},

(10)

\displaystyle\left|\cos\theta_{v}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\right|=\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(ac),H\leq\frac{\|c-a\|}{2\,\operatorname{lfs}(a)}.

(11)

Consider the vector

\vec{w}=\frac{\vec{v}-(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v})\,\vec{u}}{\left\|\vec{v}-(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v})\,\vec{u}\right\|}=\frac{1}{\sin A}\left(\vec{v}-\cos A\,\vec{u}\right).

By construction,
\vec{w}
is a unit vector parallel to the affine

and orthogonal to
\vec{v}
. It

\displaystyle\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(abc),H

\displaystyle=\max_{t\in[0,2\pi]}(\cos t\,\vec{u}+\sin t\,\vec{w})\cdot\vec{n}

\displaystyle=\max_{t\in[0,2\pi]}(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{n})\cos t+(\vec{w}\cdot\vec{n})\sin t.

The maximum over
[0,2\pi]
of the map

is
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left(\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(abc),H\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{n})^{2}+(\vec{w}\cdot\vec{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\theta_{u}+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}A}\left(\cos\theta_{v}-\cos A\cos\theta_{u}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}A}\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{u}+\cos^{2}\theta_{v}-2\cos A\cos\theta_{u}\cos\theta_{v}\right).

For fixed

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\operatorname{lfs}(a)^{2}}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}A}\|b^{\prime}-c\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\sin\angle\operatorname{Aff}(abc),H\right)^{2}
