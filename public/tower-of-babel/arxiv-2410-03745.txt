Abstract
. These notes explore three amazing formulas proved by Abel in his 1826 Paris memoir on what we now call Abelian integrals. We discuss the first two formulas from the point of view of symbolic computation and explain their connection to residues and partial fractions. The third formula arises from the first two and is related to the genus and lattice points in the Newton polygon.

2.
Our Approach to Abel’s Formulas

Formulas (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) are a bit overwhelming at first glance. For the modern reader, however, they are relatively easy to understand in terms of residues, as will be explained in Sections
3.1
and
4.1
. One goal of these notes is to prove these formulas and understand what they mean in terms of symbolic computation and residues.

But rather than just jump into the proof, let us say a bit more about how people have reacted to Abel’s memoir.
We begin with two quotes from the papers of Boole and Rowe:

Boole
: As presented in the writings of
Abel
, and of those who immediately followed in his steps, the doctrine of the comparison of transcendents is repulsive from the complexity of the formulæ in which its general conclusions are embodied.
[
8

Rowe
: The generality and power of this memoir are well known, but its form is not attractive.
[
34
, p. 713]

One reason for saying “repulsive” and “not attractive” is that Abel’s memoir begins with
(
1.3
) and (
1.5
). The complexity of these formulas is indeed overwhelming, not to mention (
1.6
) and the many other formulas that appear in
[
1
]
. Abel’s memoir is not easy reading.

For some commentators, another challenge of
[
1
]
is that while it implicitly involves key ideas in the theory of algebraic curves, there “is no geometry in Abel’s work” in the words of Kleiman
[
29
, p. 403]
. Although contour integrals are hinted at in

]
(see Section

In his 1857 treatise
Theorie der Abel’schen Functionen

[

]
, Riemann studied the integrals (

) from the point of view of Riemann surfaces, though as noted by Edwards, Riemann’s approach

… gave no insight into Abel’s

which, for Abel at least, represented the true substance of the memoir. After Riemann, the theory of Abelian functions became firmly embedded within the burgeoning theory of functions of a complex variable.
[
19

For us, things like contour integrals and Riemann surfaces are less relevant since Abel’s approach is closer to symbolic computation than to geometry or complex analysis.

Abel’s first proof that
dv
is a rational differential uses the theory of symmetric functions. Although Dieudonné admires Abel’s result, he also says that the proof is “hardly more than an exercise in the theory of the symmetric functions of the roots of a polynomial”

.

These notes will argue that the symbolic aspects of Abel’s formulas for
dv
and
v
go much deeper. In the sections that follow, we will prove modern versions of Abel’s formulas (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) in a way that emphasizes the role of symbolic computation. In fact, one way to think about them is that they are not just formulas; rather, they are

Although we will not provide detailed implementations of the algorithms involved (that is not the goal of these notes), Sections
3

4

) and (

Section

will describe how Abel used his formulas to study questions related to holomorphic differentials and the genus, though he did not think in these terms. We will give a complete proof of (

Section

His generalizations of these results are encapsulated in versions of Abel’s Theorem in which the genus plays a prominent role. Finally, Appendix

This will not be easy—some hard work will be required—but at the end, you will see that Abel’s complicated formulas (

3.1.
A Modern Version of Abel’s Formula for

Abel’s original version of his formula (

dv=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big),

Note that the same sum over the roots

The examples in Section

Solutions of

Since

.

Assumption (
3.6
) has the following nice consequence.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(x_{i},y_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,\mu
, be as above. If (
3.6
) holds, then

y-y_{i}=\gcd(\chi(x_{i},y),\theta(x_{i},y)),\quad i=1,\dots,\mu.

Furthermore,
x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}
are distinct, and for all
i
,
y_{i}
can be expressed as a rational function in
x_{i}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}(\underline{a})
.

Proof.

We first show that
x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}
are distinct.
Let
m_{i}\geq 1
be the multiplicity of
(x_{i},y_{i})
as a solution of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y)=0
. Since
\chi(x,y)
is monic in
y
, Theorem 1.1 of
[
13
]
implies that the resultant
r(x)
is, up to a constant, the product of
(x-\gamma_{1})^{m_{\gamma}}
for all solutions
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
of the system, where
m_{\gamma}
is again the multiplicity (
\chi(x,y)
being monic in
y
guarantees that there are no solutions at
\infty
in the sense of
[
13
]
).
Since
F(x)
collects the factors of
r(x)
where
\gamma_{1}
is transcendental over
\mathbb{K}
, it follows that

F(x)=c\prod_{i=1}^{\mu}(x-x_{i})^{m_{i}}

for some constant
c
. If
x_{i}=x_{j}
for
i\neq j
,
F(x)
has a root of multiplicity
m_{i}+m_{j}\geq 2
, which contradicts (
3.6
). Thus
x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}
must be distinct.

We now compute the
\gcd
. Let
y_{i}=y_{i}^{(1)},\dots,y_{i}^{(n)}
be the roots of
\chi(x_{i},y)=0
. The
y_{i}^{(\ell)}
are distinct since the discriminant of
\chi(x,y)\in\mathbb{K}[x,y]
with respect to
y
lies in
\mathbb{K}[x]
and
x_{i}
is transcendental over
\mathbb{K}
. If
\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})=0
for some
\ell\geq 2
, then since
\chi(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})=0
, we would have solutions
(x_{i},y_{i})\neq(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})
of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y)=0
. But we showed above that the solutions have distinct
x
-coordinates. Thus
\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})\neq 0
for
\ell\geq 2
, which implies

\gcd(\chi(x_{i},y),\theta(x_{i},y))=\gcd((y-y_{i}^{(1)})\cdots(y-y_{i}^{(n)}),\theta(x_{i},y))=y-y_{i}^{(1)}=y-y_{i}

since the factors
y-y_{i}^{(1)},\dots y-y_{i}^{(n)}
are distinct and only the first divides
\theta(x_{i},y)
. The final assertion of the proposition follows by computing the
\gcd
via the Euclidean Algorithm over the field
\mathbb{K}(x_{i},\underline{a})
(remember that
\theta(x_{i},y)\in\mathbb{K}[x_{i},y,\underline{a}]
).
∎

Abel
[
1
, p. 148]
says that the value of
y_{i}
can be expressed as a rational function of
x_{i}
and
\underline{a}
. According to Cooke
[
12
, pp. 407–408]
, Abel was using the “important principle” that when
y_{i}
is the only solution of
\chi(x_{i},y)=\theta(x_{i},y)=0
, the gcd of
\chi(x_{i},y)
and
\theta(x_{i},y)
gives the desired representation of
y_{i}
. Proposition
3.3
justifies this principle in our situation.

The Rational Function
f(x,y)

Next consider the rational function
f(x,y)
that appears in
dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
. Since
\chi(x_{i},y_{i})=0
, we only need
f(x,y)
modulo
\chi(x,y)
. Following Abel, we represent
f(x,y)
in the form

(3.7)

f(x,y)=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)}

for polynomials
f_{1}(x,y)
and
f_{2}(x)
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
. This defines the polynomial
f_{2}(x)
mentioned in the discussion following (
3.2
). The presence of
\chi^{\prime}(y)
in the denominator will be useful.

Abel proves (
3.7
) in
[
1
, pp. 151–152]
. For a modern proof, consider the extension
\mathbb{K}(x)\subseteq\mathbb{K}(x)[y]/\langle\chi(x,y)\rangle
of degree
n
. Then, regarding
\chi^{\prime}(y)f(x,y)
as an element of the larger field, we can write

\chi^{\prime}(y)f(x,y)=b_{0}(x)+b_{1}(x)y+\cdots+b_{n-1}(x)y^{n-1},\quad b_{0}(x),\dots,b_{n-1}(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x).

The desired representation follows after dividing by
\chi^{\prime}(y)
and letting
f_{2}(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]
be the least common denominator of the rational functions
b_{0}(x),\dots,b_{n-1}(x)
.

Differentials and Differential Fields

Abel and his contemporaries used informal notions of differentials. We will use the modern language of differential forms and differential fields to make this precise.

For our computable field
\mathbb{K}
, we have the field of rational functions
\mathbb{K}(x,\underline{a})=\mathbb{K}(x,a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
, where
h\in\mathbb{K}(x,\underline{a})
gives the rational
1
-form

dh=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\hskip 1.0ptdx+\frac{\partial h}{\partial a_{1}}\hskip 1.0ptda_{1}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial a_{2}}\hskip 1.0ptda_{2}+\cdots.

By the theory of differential fields (see
[
10
]
), the commuting derivations
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{i}}
extend uniquely to any algebraic extension of
\mathbb{K}(x,\underline{a})
by
[
10
, Theorem 3.2.2]
and still commute. For example, if
(x_{0},y_{0})
is a solution of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y,\underline{a})=0
, then
x_{0}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{K}(\underline{a})
by the paragraph preceding (
3.5
), so that
\frac{\partial x_{0}}{\partial a_{i}}
and hence
dx_{0}
are all defined. In particular, we now have a precise meaning for Abel’s differential form

dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}

from (
1.2
). We will give an explicit formula for
dx_{i}
in (
3.15
).

Residues

We recall some standard facts about residues and define the operator
\varPi
used in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). We begin with residues. As is well known, if
g(x)
has a meromorphic Laurent series expansion in powers of
x-\beta
, say

(3.8)

g(x)=\frac{a_{-M}}{(x-\beta)^{M}}+\cdots+\frac{a_{-1}}{x-\beta}+a_{0}+a_{1}(x-\beta)+a_{2}(x-\beta)^{2}+\cdots,

then

(3.9)

\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(g(x))=a_{-1}=\text{coefficient of $\frac{1}{x-\beta}$ in the expansion at $x=\beta$}.

Turning to
x=\infty
, suppose that
g(x)
has a meromorphic Laurent series expansion in powers of
u=1/x
, say

\displaystyle g(x)=g(1/u)

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{-M}}{u^{M}}+\cdots+\frac{b_{-1}}{u}+b_{0}+b_{1}u+b_{2}u^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle=b_{-M}x^{M}+\cdots+b_{-1}x+b_{0}+\frac{b_{1}}{x}+\frac{b_{2}}{x^{2}}+\cdots.

This is an expansion in descending powers of
x
. Following Abel
[
1
, p. 155]
, we define

(3.10)

\text{\large$\varPi$}(g(x))=b_{1}=\text{coefficient of $\dfrac{1}{x}$ in the expansion of $g(x)$ at $x=\infty$.}

In terms of differential forms, let
\omega=g(x)\,dx
. For
\beta
as above,

\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(\omega)=\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(g(x))=a_{-1}

since
x-\beta
is a local coordinate at
x=\beta
and
d(x-\beta)=dx
. On the other hand,
u=1/x
is a local coordinate at
x=\infty
, so that

\omega=g(1/u)\,d(1/u)=g(1/u)\cdot\frac{-1}{\ u^{2}}\,du.

Hence

\mathrm{res}_{\infty}(\omega)=\text{coefficient of $u^{-1}$ in the expansion of $-u^{-2}g(1/u)$},

which easily seen to equal
-b_{1}
in the above expansion of
g(x)
in
u=1/x
. Thus

(3.11)

\mathrm{res}_{\infty}(\omega)=\mathrm{res}_{\infty}(g(x)\,dx)=-b_{1}=-\text{\large$\varPi$}(g(x)).

This justifies our comment that
{-}\text{\large$\varPi$}
can be regarded as a residue at
\infty
.

We next recall some well-known methods for computing residues. First consider
g(x)
as in (
3.8
) and let
\nu\geq M
. Then

(3.12)

\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(g(x))=\frac{1}{(\nu-1)!}\frac{d^{\nu-1}}{dx^{\nu-1}}\big((x-\beta)^{\nu}g(x)\big)\Big|_{x=\beta}.

This formula will enable us to interpret various terms in (
1.3
) as residues, though Abel did not think in terms of residues. Notice that in (
3.12
),
\nu
is greater than or equal to the order of
\beta
as a pole of
g(x)
.

Another useful formula is that if
h(x)
is a polynomial with
\beta
as a simple root and
g(x)
has an expansion in nonnegative powers of
x-\beta
, then

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathrm{res}_{\beta}\Big(\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{res}_{\beta}\Big(\frac{g(\beta)+g^{\prime}(\beta)(x-\beta)+\cdots}{h^{\prime}(\beta)(x-\beta)+\cdots}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{res}_{\beta}\Big(\frac{g(\beta)}{h^{\prime}(\beta)}\cdot\frac{1}{x-\beta}+\cdots\Big)=\frac{g(\beta)}{h^{\prime}(\beta)}.

The Global Residue Theorem for
\mathbb{P}^{1}

This important result states that for any rational function
\varphi(x)
, the sum of the residues of the differential form
\varphi(x)\,dx
is zero, i.e.,

(3.14)

\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(\varphi(x)\,dx)=0.

Serre gives simple proof using partial fractions in
[
35
, Lemma 3 on p. 21]
. The Global Residue Theorem will play a key role in our proof of Abel’s formula. Here is an easy corollary of (
3.14
).

Corollary 3.4
.

If
H(x)=a_{m}x^{m}+\cdots
is a nonconstant polynomial with simple roots
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}

and
H_{1}(x)
is any polynomial of degree
\leq m-2
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x_{j}^{m-1}}{H^{\prime}(x_{j})}=\frac{1}{a_{m}}\ \,\text{and}\ \,\,\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{H_{1}(x_{j})}{H^{\prime}(x_{j})}=0.

Proof.

The only possible poles of the differential form
\omega=\dfrac{x^{m-1}}{H(x)}\hskip 0.5ptdx
are
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
and
\infty
, so by (
3.14
),

0=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathrm{res}_{x_{j}}(\omega)+\mathrm{res}_{\infty}(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{x_{j}^{m-1}}{H^{\prime}(x_{j})}-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{x^{m-1}}{H(x)}\Big),

where the second equality uses (
3.13
) and (
3.11
). Then we are done since by the definition of
\varPi
,

\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{x^{m-1}}{H(x)}\Big)=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{x^{m-1}}{a_{m}x^{m}+\cdots}\Big)=\frac{1}{a_{m}}.

The proof of the second assertion is equally easy and hence omitted.
∎

The formulas in Corollary
3.4
were well known in Abel’s time. He calls them “formules connues”
[
1
, p. 153]
. We will say more about Corollary
3.4
in Section
3.3
.

3.3.
Proof of Abel’s Formula for
dv

Here is what we have so far:

•

The polynomials
\chi(x,y)\in\mathbb{K}[x,y]
and
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})\in\mathbb{K}[x,y,\underline{a}]
.

•

The roots
y^{(\ell)}
,
\ell=1,\dots,n
, of
\chi(x,y)
.

•

The solutions
(x_{i},y_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,\,\mu
, of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y,\underline{a})=0
with
dx_{i}\neq 0
.

•

The resultant
r(x)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\theta(x,y^{(\ell)},\underline{a})=F_{0}(x)F(x,\underline{a})
, where
F_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]
and
F(x)=F(x,\underline{a})\in\mathbb{K}[x,\underline{a}]
.

•

The rational function
f(x,y)=\dfrac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)}
.

•

The residue operators
\mathrm{res}_{\beta}
and
\varPi
from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
).

The goal is to prove the version of Abel’s formula for
dv=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
stated in (
3.4
), namely

dv=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x)),

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
and

S(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}.

The first step of the proof is to interpret
f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
as a residue.

Lemma 3.5
.

With the above setup, assume (
3.6
). Then

f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=-\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x)),\quad i=1,\dots,\mu.

Remark 3.6
.

For
\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x))
, we regard the
da_{i}
appearing in
\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})
as new variables, so that
\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x))
is computed by regarding
S(x)
as a rational function.

Proof.

We follow Abel, who begins by noting that
F(x_{i},\underline{a})=0
implies

0=dF(x_{i},\underline{a})=F^{\prime}(x_{i},\underline{a})\,dx_{i}+\delta F(x_{i},\underline{a}),

which can be written more simply as

0=F^{\prime}(x_{i})\,dx_{i}+\delta F(x_{i}).

Since
r(x)=F_{0}(x)F(x)
and
F_{0}(x)\in\mathbb{K}[x]
does not involve
\underline{a}
, multiplying by
F_{0}(x)
gives

0=F_{0}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})\,dx_{i}+\delta r(x_{i}),

so that

(3.15)

dx_{i}=-\frac{\delta r(x_{i})}{F_{0}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}.

Note that
F_{0}(x_{i})\neq 0
by the definition of
F_{0}(x)
, and
F^{\prime}(x_{i})\neq 0
by assumption (
3.6
). Thus the denominator is nonvanishing.

For the numerator of (
3.15
), note that
r(x)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\theta(x,y^{(\ell)})
implies

\delta r(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Big(\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x,y^{(\ell)}).

Evaluating this at
x_{i},y_{i}=y_{i}^{(1)},y_{i}^{(2)},\dots,y_{i}^{(n)}
gives

\delta r(x_{i})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\Big(\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})=\Big(\prod_{k=2}^{n}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(1)}),

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(1)})=\theta(x_{i},y_{i})=0
is a factor of
\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})
for
\ell\geq 2
. Hence (
3.15
) can be written in the form

dx_{i}=-\frac{1}{F_{0}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}\Big(\prod_{k=2}^{n}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(1)}).

This is the third display on
[
1
, p. 151]
. Now multiply both sides by our rational function
f(x_{i},y_{i})=f(x_{i},y_{i}^{(1)})
to obtain

(3.16)

\displaystyle f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{F_{0}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}f(x_{i},y_{i}^{(1)})\Big(\prod_{k=2}^{n}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{F_{0}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})\Big(\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)}).

As above, the last line follows since
\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})=0
for
\ell\geq 2
.

To relate this to
S(x)
, note that

(3.17)

\displaystyle S(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\cdot\frac{\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})}{\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})}\cdot\frac{\delta\theta(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(x,y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{F_{0}(x)F(x)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\Big(\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x,y^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{F_{0}(x)}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\Big(\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x,y^{(\ell)})}{F(x)}

Since
x_{i}
is a simple root of
F(x)
and the numerator of the above expression for
S(x)
is defined at
x_{i}
,
(
3.13
) implies that

\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x))=\frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{F_{0}(x_{i})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})\Big(\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(k)})\Big)\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x_{i},y_{i}^{(\ell)})}{F^{\prime}(x_{i})}=-f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i},

where the last equality follows from (
3.16
). This proves the lemma.
∎

Adding up (
3.5
) for
i=1,\dots,\mu
, we obtain

(3.18)

dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x)).

The basic idea is that Abel’s formula (
3.4
) follows from (
3.18
) and the Global Residue Theorem. This requires knowing the poles of
S(x)
, which leads to the next lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

There is a polynomial differential form
R(x)
such that

S(x)=\frac{R(x)}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)F(x)},

where
f_{2}(x)
comes from the representation
f(x,y)=\dfrac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)}
.

Proof.

If we combine the second line of (
3.17
) with the formula for
f(x,y)
in the statement of the lemma, we obtain

S(x)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\,\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})\,\delta\theta(x,y^{(\ell)})}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)F(x)}.

To analyze this, fix
\ell
and consider the differential form in
da_{1},da_{2},\dots
defined by

\Omega_{\ell}=f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})\,\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})\,\delta\theta(x,y^{(\ell)}).

Then

(3.19)

S(x)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\Omega_{\ell}}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)F(x)}.

Note that
\prod_{k\neq\ell}\theta(x,y^{(k)})
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{K}[x,y^{(\ell)},\underline{a}]
since
y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(n)}
are the roots of
\chi(x,y)\in\mathbb{K}[x,y]
, which is monic in
y
. It follows that
\Omega_{\ell}
can be written as a differential form with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x,y^{(\ell)},\underline{a}]
, and since
\chi(x,y^{(\ell)})=0
and
\chi(x,y)
has degree
n
in
y
, we can assume that
\Omega_{\ell}
has degree at most
n-1
in
y^{(\ell)}
, still with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x,y^{(\ell)},\underline{a}]
, again because
\chi(x,y)
is monic in
y
. Thus we can write

\Omega_{\ell}=R(x)\big(y^{(\ell)}\big)^{n-1}+R^{(1)}(x,y^{(\ell)}),

where
R(x)
has coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x,\underline{a}]
and
R^{(1)}(x,y^{(\ell)})
has coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x,y^{(\ell)}),\underline{a}]
and has degree
\leq n-2
in
y^{(\ell)}
. The automorphism taking
y^{(\ell)}
to
y^{(\ell^{\prime})}
shows that
R
and
R^{(1)}
do not depend on
\ell
.

The notation
R
and
R^{(1)}
is due to Abel, as is the following analysis of the numerator of (
3.19
) (see
[
1
, pp. 152–153]
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\Omega_{\ell}}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{R(x)\big(y^{(\ell)}\big)^{n-1}+R^{(1)}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=R(x)\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\big(y^{(\ell)}\big)^{n-1}}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{R^{(1)}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}.

Using the standard formulas (“formules connues”
[
1
, p. 153]
)

(3.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\big(y^{(\ell)}\big)^{n-1}}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{R^{(1)}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=0\ \ (\text{since $R^{(1)}(x,Y)$ has degree $\leq n-2$ in $Y$),}

Abel concludes that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{\Omega_{\ell}}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}=R(x)\cdot 1+0=R(x).

The lemma follows by substituting this into (
3.19
).
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The formulas (
3.20
) are proved in Corollary
3.4
. There are two interesting things to note here:

•

The algorithm for computing the global residue of a rational function described in
[
11
, pp. 7–8]
is
precisely
the method used by Abel in 1826.

•

The vanishing result on the second line of (
3.20
) is noted by Jacobi in his 1835 paper
[
28
]
, which begins “Of the theorems which are given in the elements of algebra, there is scarcely anything more useful in the most diverse equations” than this vanishing result. In
[
28
]
, Jacobi proves a similar vanishing for two equations in two unknowns, a result now called the
Euler-Jacobi Vanishing Theorem
. See
[
11
, 1.5.5]
for details and applications.

We can now prove Abel’s formula (
3.4
) for
dv
.

Theorem 3.9
.

With the above setup, assume (
3.6
). Then

dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x)),

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
.

Proof.

The proof uses the Global Residue Theorem (
3.14
). As noted in (
3.18
), Lemma
3.5
implies that

dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x)).

In order to apply the Global Residue Theorem, we regard the differential form
S(x)
as a rational function, which we do as above by thinking of
d\underline{a}=da_{1},da_{2},\dots
as variables. Applying the Global Residue Theorem to

S(x)\,dx
gives

\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(S(x)\,dx)=0.

The roots
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
are algebraic over
\mathbb{K}
and hence distinct from the roots
x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}
of
F(x)
, which are transcendental over
\mathbb{K}
since
dx_{i}\neq 0
. By Lemma
3.7
, the only possible poles of
S(x)\,dx
are
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha},x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu},\infty
(some may fail to be poles since we do not assume that
R(x)
is relatively prime to
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)F(x)
). Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(S(x)\,dx)+\mathrm{res}_{\infty}(S(x)\,dx)+\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x)\,dx)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x))+\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x)),

where the second line uses (
3.11
). When we combine this with the above expression for
dv
, the theorem follows immediately.
∎

3.4.
Examples

Here we give examples of how to use the formula

(3.21)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x))

from Theorem
3.9
, where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
.

The first step in computing (
3.21
) is to compute
S(x)
. Recall that

S(x)=\frac{R(x)}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)F(x)}.

To find
R(x)
, we follow the proof of Lemma
3.7
and write

(3.22)

\Omega_{1}=f_{1}(x,y^{(1)})\,\prod_{k\neq 1}\theta(x,y^{(k)})\,\delta\theta(x,y^{(1)})=R(x)\big(y^{(1)}\big)^{n-1}+R^{(1)}(x,y^{(1)}),

where
R^{(1)}
has degree
\leq n-2
in
y^{(1)}
. (The proof of the lemma explains why we only need
\Omega_{1}
.)

The next step is to compute the roots
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
by symbolic methods. From here, one could find the Laurent expansions of
S(x)
at these roots and also at
\infty
, and then compute (
3.21
).

There is also an approach that avoids Laurent expansions. Let
\nu_{j}
be the multiplicity of
\beta_{j}
as a root of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
. Since
R(x)
is a polynomial differential form and
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
and
F(x)
have no roots in common,
\nu_{j}
is greater than or equal to the order of
\beta_{j}
as pole of

S(x)=\frac{R(x)}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)F(x)}.

Thus, by (
3.12
), we have

\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(S(x))=\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{dx^{\nu_{j}-1}}\big((x-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}S(x)\big)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}}.

We now present examples of (
3.21
) where
\chi(x,y)
and
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
are given by

\chi(x,y)=y^{2}-x^{3}-1\ \text{ and }\ \theta(x,y,a,b,c)=x^{2}+ax+b+cy.

Let the roots of
\chi(x,y)
be
y^{(1)}={\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}
and
y^{(2)}=-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}
, where
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}^{2}=x^{3}+1
. Then

\displaystyle r(x)

\displaystyle=\theta(y^{(1)})\theta(y^{(2)})=\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})\theta(-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})

\displaystyle=(x^{2}+ax+b+c{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})(x^{2}+ax+b-c{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})=(x^{2}+ax+b)^{2}-c^{2}(x^{3}+1).

Note that
F_{0}(x)=1
, so
r(x)=F(x)
. The solutions of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y)=0
are

(x_{1},y_{1}),\ (x_{2},y_{2}),\ (x_{3},y_{3}),\ (x_{4},y_{4}),

where
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
are the roots of
r(x)
and
y_{i}=-(x_{i}^{2}+ax_{i}+b)/c
for
1\leq i\leq 4
.

For a rational function of the form

f(x,y)=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)}=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\cdot 2y},

we can compute
\sum_{i=1}^{4}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
using (
3.21
). We illustrate how this works for two choices of
f(x,y)
.

Example 3.10
.

Consider

f(x,y)=\frac{1}{xy}=\frac{2}{x\cdot 2y}\ \text{ with }\ f_{1}(x,y)=2,\ f_{2}(x)=x.

Here,
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)=x
and (
3.21
) imply

(3.23)

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}y_{i}}=\mathrm{res}_{0}(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x)).

To find
S(x)
, we first compute the rational differential form
R(x)
. By (
3.22
),

(3.24)

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}

\displaystyle=f_{1}(x,y)\hskip 1.0pt\theta(x,y^{(2)})\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x,y^{(1)})=2\hskip 1.0pt\theta(x,-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})\hskip 1.0pt\delta\theta(x,{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})

\displaystyle=2(x^{2}+ax+b-c{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})(x\,da+db+{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}\,dc)

\displaystyle=2(x^{2}+ax+b)(x\,da+db)

\displaystyle+2\big({-}c(x\,da+db)+(x^{2}+ax+b)\hskip 1.0ptdc\big){\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}-2c\hskip 1.0ptdc\hskip 1.0pt{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}^{2}.

Since
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}^{2}=x^{3}+1
and
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}=y^{(1)}
, we obtain

\Omega_{1}=\underbrace{2\big({-}c(x\,da+db)+(x^{2}+ax+b)\hskip 1.0ptdc\big)}_{\displaystyle R(x)}y^{(1)}+\cdots.

Thus

R(x)=2(-cx\,da-c\,db+(x^{2}+ax+b)\hskip 1.0ptdc).

Here,
S(x)=\frac{R(x)}{xr(x)}
. Then
x=0
is a simple pole of
S(x)
, so by (
3.12
), the residue is

\mathrm{res}_{0}(S(x))=\frac{R(0)}{r(0)}=\frac{2(-c\hskip 1.0pt0\,da-c\,db+(0^{2}+a\hskip 0.5pt0+b)\hskip 1.0ptdc)}{(0^{2}+0x+b)^{2}-c^{2}(0^{3}+1)}=\frac{-2c\,db+2b\,dc}{b^{2}-c^{2}}.

The
\varPi
term is also easy since

\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x))=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{2(-cx\,da-c\,db+(x^{2}+ax+b)\hskip 1.0ptdc)}{x((x^{2}+ax+b)^{2}-c^{2}(x^{3}+1))}\Big)=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{2x^{2}\,dc+\cdots}{x^{5}+\cdots}\Big)=0.

It follows from (
3.23
) that

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}y_{i}}=\frac{-2c\,db+2b\,dc}{b^{2}-c^{2}}.

One can check that the differential form on the right is closed. In fact, it is exact provided that logarithms are allowed:

\frac{-2c\,db+2b\,dc}{b^{2}-c^{2}}=\delta\log\Big(\frac{b+c}{b-c}\Big),

so that

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}y_{i}}=\log\Big(\frac{b+c}{b-c}\Big)+C.

We will see in Example
4.5
that when we compute
\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int\!\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}y_{i}}
using Abel’s formula for
v
, the logarithm appears in a completely natural way.
\Diamond

Example 3.11
.

Next, consider

f(x,y)=\frac{1}{x^{2}y}=\frac{2}{x^{2}\cdot 2y}\ \text{ with }\ f_{1}(x,y)=2,\ f_{2}(x)=x^{2}.

Then
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)=x^{2}
and (
3.21
) imply that

(3.25)

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}^{2}y_{i}}=\mathrm{res}_{0}(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x)).

Here,
R(x)
is unchanged from Example
3.10
, and since
S(x)=\frac{R(x)}{x^{2}r(x)}
, it is easy to see that the
\varPi
term vanishes. So we need only find the residue at
x=0
.
Since
x=0
is a double pole of
S(x)=\frac{R(x)}{x^{2}r(x)}
, (
3.12
) tells us that the residue is

\mathrm{res}_{0}\Big(\frac{R(x)}{x^{2}r(x)}\Big)=\frac{d}{dx}\frac{R(x)}{r(x)}\Big|_{x=0}=\frac{R^{\prime}(0)r(0)-R(0)r^{\prime}(0)}{r(0)^{2}},

and then (
3.25
) leads without difficulty to the formula

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}^{2}y_{i}}=\frac{-2c(b^{2}-c^{2})\hskip 1.0ptda+4abc\,db-2a(b^{2}+c^{2})\hskip 1.0ptdc}{(b^{2}-c^{2})^{2}}.

It is less obvious that

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int\!\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}^{2}y_{i}}=-\frac{2ac}{b^{2}-c^{2}}+C,

but this will become clear in Example
4.6
.

4.
Abel’s Formula for
v

The goal of this section is to state and prove a modern version of Abel’s formula for
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
. Compared to our discussion of
dv
in Section
3
, this will take more thought because of the presence of logarithms. Examples will be given in Section
4.4
, and Section
4.5
will discuss
v
as a function on the Riemann surface of
\chi(x,y)=0
.

4.1.
A Modern Version of Abel’s Formula for
v

In one sense, a modern version of Abel’s Formula for
v
is easy to state. Recall from (
3.2
) that

dv=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big),

where the
\beta_{j}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
. As usual,
\delta
is the differential with respect to the indeterminates
\underline{a}=a_{1},a_{2},\dots
. Since the
a_{i}
only appear in the
\theta
terms and

\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}=\delta\log\theta(y^{(\ell)}),

we see that, at least formally, the integrated version of the above formula for
dv
is given by

(4.1)

v=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)+C,

where
C
is a constant of integration. This can be regarded as a modern version of Abel’s original formula

v=C-\text{\large$\varPi$}\frac{F_{2}x}{\theta_{1}x}\sum\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{\chi^{\prime}y}\log\theta y+\sum{}\rule{0.0pt}{12.0pt}^{\prime}\nu\frac{d^{\nu-1}}{dx^{\nu-1}}\bigg\{\frac{F_{2}x}{\theta_{1}^{(\nu)}x}\sum\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{\chi^{\prime}y}\log\theta y\bigg\}.

from (
1.5
). It is first main result of Abel’s Paris memoir
[
1
, (37) on p. 159]
.

Making all of this rigorous will take some work. First, we need to give an algorithmic meaning to the expression

(4.2)

\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)

and second, with this algorithmic meaning in mind, we need to prove that

(4.3)

dv=\delta\Big(\!\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 0.5pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 0.5pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)\!\Big).

Since
dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is rational differential in the indeterminates
\underline{a}
and
\delta
is the differential with respect to
\underline{a}
, (
4.3
) makes it clear what (
4.1
) means.

4.2.
Puiseux Series and Logarithms

To make sense of (
4.2
), we need residues and logs. For residues, the series expansions are more complicated because they often involve fractional exponents. Given
\beta\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
(the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{K}
), it is well known that the field

\bigcup_{h=1}^{\infty}\overline{\mathbb{K}}((x-\beta)^{1/h}))

is algebraically closed (see, for example,
[
38
, Chapter IV, Theorem 3.1]
). Elements of this field are series in
x-\beta
with fractional exponents and coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
, where the exponents have bounded denominators and only finitely many are negative. These are commonly called
Puiseux series
in
x-\beta
. Puiseux series in
1/x
are defined similarly.

It follows that
\chi(x,y)
splits completely in this field, so that the roots
y^{(\ell)}
can be written as Puiseux series in
x-\beta
or
1/x
. Algorithms for computing these Puiseux series are described by Walker
[
38
, Chapter IV, §3]
and (in full detail) Edwards
[
19
, Essays 4.4 and 8.4–8.7]
.

The version of Abel’s formula for
v
given in Theorem
4.3
uses the logarithms of certain nonzero elements
c\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}[\underline{a}]
. Recall that
\overline{\mathbb{K}}[\underline{a}]
has commuting derivations
\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{i}}
. By
[
10
, Section 3.2]
, there is a differential extension of
\overline{\mathbb{K}}(\underline{a})
containing an element
L
such that

\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{i}}=\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial a_{i}}

for all
i
. The discussion following
[
10
, Definition 5.1.3]
allows us to write
L=\log(c)
, so that the above equation can be written

(4.4)

\frac{\partial\log(c)}{\partial a_{i}}=\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial c}{\partial a_{i}}

for all
i
. Note that the derivations
\frac{\partial}{\partial a_{i}}
continue to commute in this extension.

Turning to Abel’s formula for
v
, the idea is to introduce only logs needed to compute the final answer. A naive reading of (
4.2
) would be to start from

(4.5)

\widehat{S}(x)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\log\theta(y^{(\ell)}).

and then interpret (
4.2
) to mean

\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}(x)),

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
. The challenge is that
\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})
can be poorly behaved at
\beta_{j}
and
\infty
. It may fail to have Puiseux expansions in the usual sense, and even when it does, the expansions will contain additional logs.

Since we want to minimize the number of logs that appear, we will dispense with
\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})
. Instead, for each
\beta_{j}
, we will replace it with a Puiseux series denoted
\log_{\beta_{j}}\!\theta(y^{(\ell)})
. There will be a similar Puiseux series
\log_{\infty}\!\theta(y^{(\ell)})
at
\infty
.

To define these series, take
\beta\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}
. Since
y^{(\ell)}
is integral over
x
(remember that
\chi(x,y)
is monic in
y
), its Puiseux expansion in
x-\beta
has coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
and
no negative exponents. For the polynomial
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
, the indeterminates
\underline{a}
are some of the coefficients of
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
with respect to
x
and
y
. It follows that the Puiseux expansion of
\theta(y^{(\ell)})=\theta(x,y^{(\ell)},\underline{a})
in
x-\beta
has no negative exponents and coefficients in

\overline{\mathbb{K}}[\underline{a}]_{\leq 1}=\{\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}a_{1}+\lambda_{2}a_{2}+\cdots\mid\lambda_{i}\in\overline{\mathbb{K}}\}.

Thus the Puiseux expansion is
\theta(y^{(\ell)})=c_{\beta,\ell}\,(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}+\cdots
, where
c_{\beta,\ell}\in\underline{\mathbb{K}}[\underline{a}]_{\leq 1}
,
e_{\beta,\ell}\geq 0
in
\mathbb{Q}
, and the omitted terms all involve higher powers (possibly fractional) of
x-\beta
. This enables us to write

\theta(y^{(\ell)})=c_{\beta,\ell}\hskip 1.5pt(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}\hskip 0.5pt(1+W_{\beta,\ell}),

where
W_{\beta,\ell}
is a Puiseux series involving only positive powers of
x-\beta
. In a similar way, for
\beta=\infty
,
\theta(y^{(\ell)})
can be written

\theta(y^{(\ell)})=c_{\infty,\ell}\hskip 1.5ptx^{e_{\infty,\ell}}\hskip 0.5pt(1+W_{\infty,\ell}),

where
W_{\infty,\ell}
is now a Puiseux series involving only positive powers of
\frac{1}{x}
.

Definition 4.1
.

For
\theta(y^{(\ell)})=c_{\beta,\ell}\hskip 1.5pt(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}\hskip 0.5pt(1+W_{\beta,\ell})
as above, we define

\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})=\log(c_{\beta,\ell})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}W_{\beta,\ell}^{k}}{k}.

Similarly, when
\theta(y^{(\ell)})=c_{\infty,\ell}\hskip 1.5ptx^{e_{\infty,\ell}}\hskip 0.5pt(1+W_{\infty,\ell})
, we define

\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})=\log(c_{\infty,\ell})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}W_{\infty,\ell}^{k}}{k}.

The logarithms
\log(c_{\beta,\ell})
and
\log(c_{\infty,\ell})
in Definition
4.1
are from (
4.4
). Hence

\delta\log(c_{\beta,\ell})=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\log(c_{\beta,\ell})}{\partial a_{i}}\hskip 1.0ptda_{i}=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{c_{\beta,\ell}}\frac{\partial c_{\beta,\ell}}{\partial a_{i}}\hskip 1.0ptda_{i}=\frac{1}{c_{\beta,\ell}}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial c_{\beta,\ell}}{\partial a_{i}}\hskip 1.0ptda_{i}=\frac{\delta c_{\beta,\ell}}{c_{\beta,\ell}},

and similarly for
\log(c_{\infty,\ell})
. Note that
\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
and
\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
are Puiseux series whose constant terms involve logs and whose other coefficients lie in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}(\underline{a})
.

The reader may wonder why Definition
4.1
ignores
(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}
and
x^{e_{\infty,\ell}}
. One reason is that in spite of missing these factors,
\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
and
\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
still behave correctly with respect to
\delta
, as shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

\displaystyle{\delta\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})=\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}}
and
\displaystyle{\delta\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})=\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}}
.

Proof.

For simplicity, let
W=W_{\beta,\ell}
. Then

\displaystyle\delta\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\delta\Big(\!\log(c_{\beta,\ell})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}W^{k}}{k}\Big)=\frac{\delta c_{\beta,\ell}}{c_{\beta,\ell}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k-1}W^{k-1}\,\delta W

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta c_{\beta,\ell}}{c_{\beta,\ell}}+\frac{\delta W}{1{+}W}=\frac{\delta c_{\beta,\ell}}{c_{\beta,\ell}}+\frac{\delta(1{+}W)}{1{+}W}=\frac{(\delta c_{\beta,\ell})(1{+}W)+c_{\beta,\ell}\,\delta(1{+}W)}{c_{\beta,\ell}(1{+}W)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(c_{\beta,\ell}(1{+}W))}{c_{\beta,\ell}(1{+}W)}\cdot\frac{(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}}{(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}}\!=\!\frac{\delta\big(c_{\beta,\ell}\,(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}(1{+}W)\big)}{c_{\beta,\ell}\,(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}(1{+}W)}\!=\!\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})},

where the second equality of the last line follows because
(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}
is constant with respect to
\delta
. The proof for
\delta\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
is similar and hence omitted.
∎

Another way to think about Definition
4.1
is to naively assume the properties of logarithms and write

\displaystyle\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\log\big(c_{\beta,\ell}\hskip 1.5pt(x-\beta)^{e_{\beta,\ell}}\hskip 0.5pt(1+W_{\beta,\ell})\big)

\displaystyle=\log(c_{\beta,\ell})+e_{\beta,\ell}\log(x-\beta)+\log(1+W_{\beta,\ell})

\displaystyle=\log(c_{\beta,\ell})+e_{\beta,\ell}\log(x-\beta)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}W_{\beta,\ell}^{k}}{k},

which agrees with
\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
except for the term
e_{\beta,\ell}\log(x-\beta)
. This term is unwanted since it requires adding another log and unneeded since it is constant with respect to
\delta
.

4.3.
Proof of Abel’s Formula for
v

We begin with the algorithmic meaning of (
4.2
). First modify (
4.5
) by defining

\widehat{S}_{\beta}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)}),

where
\log_{\beta}\theta(y^{(\ell)})
is from Definition
4.1
and
f(x,y^{(\ell)})
is computed using the Puiseux series for
y^{(\ell)}
at
x=\beta
. Thus
\widehat{S}_{\beta}
is a Puiseux series in
x-\beta
, where as many terms as needed can be computed algorithmically. In a similar way, define

\widehat{S}_{\infty}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})

as a Puiseux series in
\frac{1}{x}
. Then we interpret (
4.2
) to mean

\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x)).

Only finitely many terms of the Puiseux series are needed to compute
\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))
and
\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))
. Thus (
4.2
) now has a rigorous algorithmic meaning. It follows that Abel’s formula for
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
will be proved once we prove (
4.3
), which we rewrite as

(4.6)

dv=\delta\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))\hskip-1.0pt-\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))\Big).

This leads to the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assuming (
3.6
),
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is given by

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log_{\beta_{j}}\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log_{\infty}\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)+C

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))+C.

Proof.

As noted above, it suffices to prove (
4.6
). By Theorem
3.9
, we know that

dv=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x)).

Comparing this to (
4.6
), we see that the theorem will follow once we prove that

\delta\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))=\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(S(x))\ \text{ and }\ \delta\hskip 1.0pt\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))=\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x)).

Since
\delta
is the differential with respect to
\underline{a}=a_{1},a_{2},\dots
, it operates on a Puiseux series in
x-\beta_{j}
or
\frac{1}{x}
by applying
\delta
to the coefficients. It follows that

\delta\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))=\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}(\delta\hskip 1.0pt\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))\ \text{ and }\ \delta\hskip 1.0pt\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))=\text{\large$\varPi$}(\delta\hskip 1.0pt\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x)).

We now have a straightforward computation:

\displaystyle\delta\hskip 1.0pt\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x))

\displaystyle=\delta\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\log_{\beta_{j}}\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\delta\log_{\beta_{j}}\theta(y^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}=S(x).

On the first line, the second equality follows since the indeterminates
\underline{a}
do not appear in
f(x,y^{(\ell)})
. On the second line, the first equality is Lemma
4.2
, and the second equality is the definition of
S(x)
given in (
3.3
). The equality
\delta\hskip 1.0pt\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))=\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x))
is proved similarly, so we are done.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Theorem
4.3
gives an algorithm for computing
\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
and shows that the only logs that appear in the final answer (if any) are logs of linear combinations of 1 and
\underline{a}=a_{1},a_{2},\dots
with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
.

Example 4.5
.

First consider
f(x,y)=\frac{1}{xy}=\frac{2}{x\cdot 2y}
, so

. It follows that

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int\frac{dx_{i}}{x_{i}y_{i}}=\mathrm{res}_{0}(\widehat{S}_{0}(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x))+C.

We begin with
\widehat{S}_{0}(x)
, which uses the Puiseux expansion

{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}=\sqrt{x^{3}+1}=1+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{3}-\tfrac{1}{8}x^{6}+\tfrac{1}{16}x^{9}-\cdots.

Thus

\displaystyle\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})

\displaystyle=x^{2}+ax+b+c\big(1+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{3}-\tfrac{1}{8}x^{6}+\cdots)=b+c+ax+x^{2}+\tfrac{c}{2}x^{3}-\cdots

\displaystyle=(b+c)\Big(1+\frac{a}{b+c}x+\frac{1}{b+c}x^{2}+\cdots\Big).

It follows that

\frac{1}{x{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}}\log_{0}\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})=\frac{\log(b+c)+(\frac{a}{b+c}x+\frac{1}{b+c}x^{2}+\cdots)-\cdots}{x(1+\frac{1}{2}x^{3}+\cdots)}=\frac{\log(b+c)}{x}+\cdots.

In a similar way,

\theta(-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})=x^{2}+ax+b-c\big(1+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{3}-\tfrac{1}{8}x^{6}+\cdots)=b-c+ax+x^{2}+\tfrac{c}{2}x^{3}-\cdots

\frac{1}{x(-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})}\log_{0}\theta(-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})=-\frac{\log(b-c)}{x}+\cdots.

Adding these gives

\widehat{S}_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{x{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}}\log_{0}\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})+\frac{1}{x(-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})}\log_{0}\theta(-{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})=\frac{\log(b+c)-\log(b-c)}{x}+\cdots.

The next step is to compute
\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x)
.
The Puiseux expansion of
{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}
in
\frac{1}{x}
is easy to compute since

\displaystyle=\sqrt{x^{3}+1}=x^{3/2}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{x^{3}}}=x^{3/2}\Big(1+\frac{1}{2x^{3}}-\frac{1}{8x^{6}}+\cdots\Big)

\displaystyle=x^{3/2}+\frac{1}{2x^{3/2}}-\frac{1}{8x^{9/2}}-\cdots.

Then

\displaystyle\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})

\displaystyle=x^{2}+ax+b+c{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}=x^{2}+ax+b+c\Big(x^{3/2}+\frac{1}{2x^{3/2}}-\frac{1}{8x^{9/2}}-\cdots\Big)

\displaystyle=x^{2}+cx^{3/2}+ax+b+\frac{c}{2x^{3/2}}-\cdots

\displaystyle=x^{2}(1+W),\ W=\frac{c}{x^{1/2}}+\frac{a}{x}+\frac{b}{x^{2}}+\frac{c}{2x^{7/2}}-\cdots,

\displaystyle\log_{\infty}\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})

\displaystyle=W-\frac{1}{2}W^{2}+\cdots=\Big(\frac{c}{x^{1/2}}+\frac{a}{x}+\cdots\Big)-\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{c}{x^{1/2}}+\frac{a}{x}+\cdots\Big)^{2}+\cdots

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{x^{1/2}}+\frac{a-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}}{x}+\cdots.

\frac{1}{x{\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}}}\log_{\infty}\theta({\scriptstyle\mathscr{Y}})=\frac{\frac{c}{x^{1/2}}+\frac{a-\frac{1}{2}c^{2}}{x}+\cdots}{x^{5/2}\big(1+\frac{1}{2x^{3}}-\frac{1}{8x^{6}}-\cdots\big)}=\frac{c}{x^{3}}+\cdots.

from which we conclude that

(4.8)

where we regard

4.5.
Indefinite Integrals and Limits of Integration

Let us say more about the integrals in the sum
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
.

For the most part, Abel regards these as indefinite integrals, i.e., antiderivatives (see
[
1
, p. 145]
). We say “most part” because Abel mentions limits of integration exactly once in
[
1
]
, where he says that
dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
gives
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
“by integrating between certain limits”
[
1
, p. 149]
. Definite integrals appear in many of Abel’s papers, though ones with a variable upper limit of integration are rare. An example occurs in his great paper on elliptic functions, where he writes
[
2
, p. 266]

\alpha=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{(1-c^{2}x^{2})(1+e^{2}x^{2})}}.

(This makes
\alpha
a function of
x
; the inverse function studied by Abel in
[
2
]
is the first published appearance of an elliptic function in the modern sense.) See
[
29
, p. 403]
for further comments about how Abel thought about Abelian integrals.

We can use limits of integration to give an analytic definition of
v
as follows. Consider the Riemann surface
S
associated to the algebraic curve
\chi(x,y)=0
. One can show that the differential form
f(x,y)\,dx
represents a rational differential form on
S
(and all such forms arise in this way). Fix a base point
O\in S
. Given another point
P\in S
, pick a path
\Gamma
from
O
to
P
that avoids the poles of
f(x,y)\,dx
. Then
\int_{\Gamma}\hskip-0.5ptf(x,y)\,dx
is defined, and if we vary
P
slightly, the integral becomes a holomorphic function of
P
. Informally, we write the integral as
\int_{O}^{P}\!f(x,y)\,dx
since changing
\Gamma
to a different path from
O
to the initial choice of
P
changes the function by a constant.

With this in mind, recall that the
(x_{i},y_{i})
are solutions of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y,\underline{a})=0
. They are smooth points of
\chi(x,y)=0
since
x_{i}
is transcendental over
\mathbb{K}
and hence give points on
S
. Using
\int_{O}^{(x_{i},y_{i})}\!f(x,y)\,dx
instead of
\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
gives the function

(4.9)

v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int_{O}^{(x_{i},y_{i})}\!f(x,y)\,dx.

But
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are algebraic functions of the indeterminates
\underline{a}=a_{1},a_{2},\dots
, so that if we vary the
a_{i}
slightly (Abel expresses this by saying that the values of the
a_{i}
“are contained within certain limits”
[
1
, p. 149]
), the points
(x_{i},y_{i})
will also vary slightly, with the result that (
4.9
) is a holomorphic function of the
a_{i}
. This approach to
v
is used by Brill and Noether
[
9
, p. 322]
in 1894, Baker
[
7
, p. 210]
in 1897, and Forsyth
[
20
, p. 585]
in 1918 in their discussions of Abel’s Theorem. Also, in 1857, Boole wrote his version of (
4.9
) as
v=\sum\int^{x_{i}}\!X\,dx

[
8
, p. 745]
.

The treatment by Forsyth
[
20
, pp. 579–586]
is especially useful since he proves

v=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)+C,

where
v
is as above and the right-hand side uses the analytic interpretation of
\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})
(he uses
Boole’s
\Theta
operator from Definition
A.13
).
As noted by Forsyth,
“To evaluate the right-hand side, only algebraic expansions are necessary; and the result will be some function of the
parameters”
[
20
, p. 585]
. These “algebraic expansions” are (up to a constant) the Puiseux series used in Theorem
4.3
. The result is that the version of Abel’s formula for
v
stated in Theorem
4.3
gives an explicit formula for
v
from (
4.9
) as a rational and logarithmic function of the
a_{i}
.

We should also mention that the discussion of Abel’s Theorem in Dieudonné
[
18
]
and Kleiman
[
29
]
uses (
4.9
). The interpretation of
v
given in (
4.9
) is needed in order to understand some of the deeper aspects of Abel’s memoir.

5.
Abel’s Formula for
\gamma

Abel’s formula for
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
states (in modern form) that

v=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\log\theta(y^{(\ell)})\Big)+C.

He proves his version of the formula in Section 4 of
[
1
]
. In the next section, he notes that in this equation,

the second member [the right-hand side] is in general a function of the quantities
a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}
, etc. If one assumes that it is equal to a constant, this will generally result in certain relations among the quantities; but there are also certain cases for which the second member reduces to a constant, regardless of the values of the quantities
a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}
, etc. Let’s seek out these cases:
[
1
, p. 164]

The cases Abel proposes to study lead to conditions on the differential
f(x,y)\,dx
, and the integer
\gamma
appearing in (
1.6
) counts the number of “arbitrary constants” that appear when
f(x,y)\,dx
satisfies these conditions. In the 19th century, the dimension of a vector space was described by the minimum number of constants needed to express a general element of the space. It follows that
\gamma
is the dimension of a vector space of differential forms that satisfy Abel’s conditions.

From a modern perspective,
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is guaranteed to be constant when
f(x,y)\,dx
is a holomorphic differential on the Riemann surface
S
associated to
\chi(x,y)=0
. Recall that
f(x,y)\,dx
is
holomorphic
on
S
if it has no poles on
S
. When
f(x,y)\,dx
is holomorphic, one can prove that
dv=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=0
by the methods of
[
19
, Essay 9.8]
. It follows that
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is constant. A more elementary proof that
v
is constant is sketched in
[
29
, p. 406]
.

The genus of the Riemann surface
S
is the dimension of the vector space of holomorphic differentials on
S
. In Theorem
5.8
, we will prove that for most choices of
\chi(x,y)
,
Abel’s
\gamma
is the genus of the Riemann surface
S
. To prove Theorem
5.8
, we begin with Abel’s conditions on
f(x,y)\,dx
(Section
5.1
) and then follow Baker
[
6
]
to interpret Abel’s formula (
1.6
) for
\gamma
in terms of the Newton polygon of
\chi(x,y)
(Section
5.2
). From here, Theorem
5.8
is an easy consequence of standard results about toric surfaces (Section
5.3
).

The relation between
\gamma
and the genus is our first hint of the real depth of Abel’s memoir
[
1
]
. We will glimpse more of the depth in Sections
5.4
when we discuss holomorphic differentials. Although Abel never defines the genus in
[
1
]
, he keeps asking questions where the genus is the answer.

5.1.
Abel’s Inequalities

Requiring that
v
be constant is equivalent to the equation

0=dv=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big).

The first sum is over the roots
\beta_{j}
of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
, where
f(x,y)=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)}
and
r(x)=F_{0}(x)F(x,\underline{a})
is the factorization of the resultant from (
3.5
). One way to guarantee the vanishing of the first sum is to assume that
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
is constant. Abel makes this assumption and then turns his attention to the equation

(5.1)

0=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big).

Houzel
[
24
, pp. 92–94]
gives a nice description of Abel’s analysis of (
5.1
). Our approach will be slightly different.

Given a Puiseux series
R
in
1/x
(what the 19th century would call “descending powers of
x
”), Abel
[
1
, p. 161]
defines
hR
to be the highest exponent (“haut exposant”) of
x
that appears. We denote this by
h(R)
, so that

h(R)=h\text{ when }R=cx^{h}+\hskip 0.5pt\text{smaller powers of $x$ and $c\neq 0$}.

Writing
R
as a standard Puiseux series in
u=1/x
with increasing exponents, the usual valuation
\nu
satisfies
\nu(R)=-h(R)
. When
R
is a polynomial in
x
,
h(R)
is just its degree.

An easy calculation shows that

h\Big(\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)\leq 0,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n.

Since
\varPi
takes the coefficient of
\frac{1}{x}
, it follows easily that (
5.1
) holds whenever

(5.2)

h\Big(\frac{f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)<-1,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n.

Our study of (
5.2
) begins with the simpler inequalities

(5.3)

h\Big(\frac{x^{a}(y^{(\ell)})^{m}}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)<-1,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n,

where
a
and
m
are nonnegative integers, i.e.,
(a,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
. Equivalently,

a<h(\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)}))-m\hskip 0.5pth(y^{(\ell)})-1,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n.

We will show in Theorem
5.6
below that Abel’s formula for
\gamma
in (
1.6
) counts the number of pairs
(a,m)
that satisfy (
5.3
).

Since
h(y^{(\ell)})
appears frequently in what follows, we will write it more simply as
h_{\ell}=h(y^{(\ell)})
(Abel uses
\varphi\ell
instead of
h_{\ell}

[
1
, p. 175]
). Thus (
5.3
) is equivalent to

(5.4)

a<h(\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)}))-m\hskip 0.5pth_{\ell}-1,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n.

Following Abel
[
1
, p. 162]
, we order the
y^{(\ell)}
so that

(5.5)

h_{1}\geq h_{2}\geq\cdots\geq h_{n},\quad h_{\ell}=h(y^{(\ell)})

and assume that the
y^{(\ell)}
have distinct highest terms. This implies that

(5.6)

h(y^{(\ell)}-y^{(k)})=h(y^{(\ell)})=h_{\ell}\ \text{whenever }\ell<k.

Since
\chi(x,y)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}(y-y^{(\ell)})
implies
\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})=\prod_{k\neq\ell}(y^{(\ell)}-y^{(k)})
, (
5.6
) easily implies that (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) are equivalent to

(5.7)

a<h_{1}+h_{2}+\cdots+h_{\ell-1}+(n-\ell-m)h_{\ell}-1,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n.

These inequalities have some surprises to offer, as we now explain.

5.2.
The Newton Polygon and Abel’s Number
\gamma

To analyze (
5.7
), Abel writes
h_{\ell}=h(y^{(\ell)})
as a fraction in lowest terms with positive denominator. After some further work, he arrives at the complicated but explicit formula for
\gamma
given in (
1.6
). We will derive his formula in the proof of Theorem
5.6
given below. Our approach will use a connection between Abel’s
\gamma
and the Newton polygon of
\chi(x,y)
noticed by Baker
[
6
]
in 1894.

Recall that the
Newton polygon
of
\chi(x,y)
is the convex hull of the exponent vectors of the nonzero terms of
\chi(x,y)
. We start with an example studied by Abel and Baker.

Example 5.1
.

Section 8 of Abel’s memoir
[
1
, pp. 181–185]
is devoted to the case where

\chi(x,y)=p_{0}+p_{1}y+p_{2}y^{2}+\cdots+p_{12}y^{12}+y^{13}

and
p_{0}=p_{0}(x),\dots,p_{12}=p_{12}(x)
are polynomials of respective degrees

2,3,2,3,4,5,3,4,2,3,4,1,1.

Following Baker
[
6
, Fig. 7 on p. 139]
, Figure
5.1
shows the Newton polygon of

of the solutions

) for

Methods for computing of Puiseux series in descending powers of

have been known since Newton. Unfortunately, most expositions of Puiseux series, including
[

,
38
]
, focus on series in ascending powers of
x-\beta
. The only treatment for descending powers we know appears in the commentary to Minding’s 1841 paper

. This reference explains the link to the Newton polygon.

The above ordering of
h_{1},\dots,h_{13}
follows (

), which is needed for Abel’s calculation of
\gamma
. However, this ordering is also relevant to the Newton polygon in Figure

. Ignoring the coordinate axes, the outer normals of the other edges of the Newton polygon are of the form
(1,h_{\ell})
, which gives the outer normals

\nu_{1}=(1,\tfrac{4}{3}),\ \nu_{2}=(1,\tfrac{1}{5}),\ \nu_{3}=(1,\tfrac{-1}{\ 2}),\ \nu_{4}=(1,-1).

The convexity in Figure
5.1
forces the

-coordinates of the

to decrease.

Lemma

below will prove that
(a,m)
satisfies Abel’s inequalities (

) if and only if
(a+1,m+1)
is an interior lattice point of the Newton polygon of
\chi(x,y)
. Proposition

and Remark

will then show that Abel’s initial definition of

is the number of interior lattice points, which for Figure

easily gives

.
\Diamond

In general,
\chi(x,y)
is monic of degree
n
in
y
with Newton polygon
P
. We will assume that
\chi(x,y)
has a nonzero constant term, so that the line segment from the origin to
(0,n)
is an edge of
P
, as in Figure
5.1
. Aside from the coordinate axes, the remaining edges of
P
are made up of
n
line segments, where as we go from top to bottom, the endpoints of the line segments are

Q_{0}=(0,n),Q_{1}=(h_{1},n-1),Q_{2}=(h_{1}+h_{2},n-2),\dots,Q_{n}=(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n},0),

where
h_{\ell}=h(y^{(\ell)})
. This follows from the commentary to
[
30
]
.
The
\ell
th segment
Q_{\ell-1}Q_{\ell}
satisfies
Q_{\ell-1}-Q_{\ell}=(-h_{\ell},1)
with outer normal
(1,h_{\ell})

relative to
P
(so each edge of
P
consists of one or more of these line segments).
By (
5.5
), the outer normals
(1,h_{1}),\dots,(1,h_{n})
have decreasing
y
-coordinates as we go from top to bottom. This is consistent with the convexity of
P
.

In Figure
5.1
, the line segments
Q_{0}Q_{1},Q_{1}Q_{2},Q_{2}Q_{3}
have outer normal
(1,\frac{4}{3})
and form the top edge. Similarly, the next five line segments have outer normal
(1,\frac{1}{5})
and form the second edge, and so on.

The above description of
P
implies that a point
Q=(u,v)
in the plane lies in
P
if and only if

(5.8)

u\geq 0,\ v\geq 0,\text{ and }(Q-Q_{\ell-1})\cdot(1,h_{\ell})\leq 0\text{ for }\ell=1,\dots,n.

To explain the final inequalities, note that
Q
lies on the line connecting
Q_{\ell-1}
and
Q_{\ell}
if and only

(Q-Q_{\ell-1})\cdot(1,h_{\ell})=0
. Since
(1,h_{\ell})
is an outer normal, we must have
(Q-Q_{\ell-1})\cdot(1,h_{\ell})\leq 0
for points in
P
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
P
be the Newton polygon of
\chi(x,y)
, and assume that
\chi(x,y)
has a nonzero constant term. Also, for the solutions
y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(n)}
of
\chi(x,y)=0
, assume that the highest terms of their Puiseux expansions in
1/x
are distinct. Then a lattice point
(a,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\geq 0}
satisfies the inequalities (
5.7
), or equivalently
(
5.3
), if and only if
(a+1,m+1)
is an interior lattice point of
P
.

Proof.

First, the hypothesis on the highest terms of the
y^{(\ell)}
guarantees that (
5.3
) and (
5.7
) are equivalent. Then observe that
(a+1,m+1)
lies in the interior of the first quadrant. Thus it is an interior point of
P
if and only if

\big((a+1,m+1)-Q_{\ell-1}\big)\cdot(1,h_{\ell})<0,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n.

Using
Q_{\ell-1}=(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{\ell-1},n-\ell+1)
, these inequalities can be written as

a+1+(m+1)h_{\ell}-\big(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{\ell-1}+(n-\ell+1)h_{l}\big)<0,\quad\ell=1,\dots,n,

which are easily seen to be equivalent to (
5.7
).
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

With the same hypotheses as Lemma
5.2
, consider the vector space
\Omega
of differential forms
f(x,y)\hskip 0.5ptdx=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{\chi^{\prime}(y)}\hskip 0.5ptdx
, where

f_{1}(x,y)=\sum c_{a,m}x^{a}y^{m},\quad c_{a,m}\in\mathbb{C}

and the sum is over all pairs
(a,m)
for which
(a+1,m+1)
is an interior lattice point of
P
. Also, for
m=0,\dots,n-2
, let
\tau_{m}
be the largest integer such that
(\tau_{m}+1,m+1)
is an interior lattice point of
P
, with
\tau_{m}=-1
when
P
has no such interior lattice point. Then
:

(1)

v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is constant for every
f(x,y)\,dx
in
\Omega
.

(2)

\tau_{m}=\lceil h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n-m-1}\rceil-2
.

(3)

\Omega
is a vector space of dimension

(5.9)

\dim(\Omega)=\#\{\text{\rm interior lattice points of }P\}=\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(\tau_{m}+1).

Proof.

For (1), let
x^{a}y^{m}
be a monomial appearing in
f_{1}(x,y)
. Then
(a,m)
satisfies (
5.3
) by Lemma
5.2
, which easily implies that
f_{1}(x,y)
satisfies (
5.2
). By the discussion preceding (
5.2
), it follows that
f_{1}(x,y)
satisfies (
5.1
). As explained at the beginning of Section
5.1
, we conclude that
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is constant.

For (2), the discussion leading up to (
5.8
) shows that
Q_{\ell}=(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{\ell},n-\ell)
is on the boundary of
P
for
\ell=0,\dots,n
. Thus
Q_{n-m-1}=(h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n-m-1},m+1)
is on the right-hand side of the boundary of
P
. If
P
has a right-most interior lattice point at height
m+1
, then it can be written
(\tau_{m}+1,m+1)
, where

\tau_{m}+1=\lceil h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n-m-1}\rceil-1,\text{ so }\hskip 1.5pt\tau_{m}=\lceil h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n-m-1}\rceil-2.

If
P
has no interior lattice points at height
m+1
, then
0<h_{1}+\cdots+h_{n-m-1}\leq 1
. In this case, the above formula gives
\tau_{m}=-1
, which is what we want.

Finally, for (3),
\dim(\Omega)
is clearly the number of interior lattice points of
P
. Every interior lattice point can be written
(a+1,m+1)
for integers
a,m\geq 0
, and note that
m+1<n
since
(0,n)
is the only point of
P
at height
n
. Thus
0\leq m\leq n-2
. Then
0\leq a\leq\tau_{m}
by the definition of
\tau_{m}
, so that the total number of interior lattice points is
\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(\tau_{m}+1)
. Note that this works when
\tau_{m}=-1
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The sum
\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(\tau_{m}+1)
is closely related to the definition of
\gamma
given in
[
1
]
. To analyze
(
5.2
), Abel writes
f_{1}(x,y)
in the form

f_{1}(x,y)=t_{0}+t_{1}y+\cdots+t_{n-1}y^{n-1},

where
t_{m}=t_{m}(x)
is a polynomial in
x
of degree
ht_{m}

[
1
, p. 162]
. On p. 163, he takes “la plus grande valeur de
ht_{m}
”, which is his version of our
\tau_{m}
from Proposition
5.3
. For this value of
ht_{m}
, he designates by
\gamma
the number

ht_{1}+\cdots+ht_{n-2}+n-1=\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(ht_{m}+1)

(see
[
1
, p. 167]
).
This is consistent with the sum
\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(\tau_{m}+1)
in (
5.9
). For Abel,
\gamma
is the number of “arbitrary constants” in
f_{1}(x,y)
and hence in
\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{\chi^{\prime}(y)}\hskip 0.5ptdx
.

The formula for
\tau_{m}
given in Proposition
5.3
compares nicely with Abel’s formula for the biggest value of
ht_{m}

[
1
, (53) on p. 163]
, namely

ht_{m}=hy^{\prime}+hy^{\prime\prime}+\cdots+hy^{(n-m-1)}-2+\varepsilon_{n-m-1},

where
hy^{(\ell)}=h_{\ell}
and
\varepsilon_{n-m-1}
is “a positive number less than unity which makes this equation possible” (as noted by Houzel
[
24
, p. 92]
,
\varepsilon_{n-m-1}
can be zero).

We now turn our attention to Abel’s formula (
1.6
) for
\gamma
, where we use (
5.9
) from Proposition
5.3
as the definition of
\gamma
. Our main tool will be Pick’s formula, which states that

(5.10)

\#\{\text{\rm interior lattice points of }P\}=\mathrm{Area}(P)-\tfrac{1}{2}\#\{\text{lattice points of }\partial P\}+1,

where
\partial P
denotes the boundary of
P
.
A proof of (
5.10
) using toric geometry is given in
[
15
, Example 9.4.4]
. See
[
22
]
for references to more elementary proofs.

As above, we assume that
\chi(x,y)
has a nonzero constant term. The first step in using (
5.10
) is to compute the area of
P
. Number the first quadrant edges of
P
from
1
to
\varepsilon
, where the first edge starts at
(0,n)
and the
\varepsilon
th edge ends on the
x
-axis. Write the outer normal of the
i
th edge as

\nu_{i}=\Big(1,\frac{m_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\Big),

where
m_{i},\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\mu_{i}>0
, and
\gcd(m_{i},\mu_{i})=1
. Also let
n_{i}+1
be the number of lattice points on the
i
th edge. Suppose the top endpoint of the
i
th edge is the lattice point
(a,b)
. The edge has slope
\frac{-\mu_{i}}{\ m_{i}}
with
\gcd(m_{i},\mu_{i})=1
, so the next lattice point on the edge must be
(a+m_{i},b-\mu_{i})
. Since the edge has
n_{i}+1
lattice points, the bottom endpoint of the
i
th edge is
(a+n_{i}m_{i},b-n_{i}\mu_{i})
. Thus the
i
th edge is made up of
n_{i}\mu_{i}
of the line segments
Q_{\ell-1}Q_{\ell}
defined above. Figure
5.2
shows the first two edges of
P
when
m_{2}\geq 0
.

Lemma 5.5
.

When the constant term of
\chi(x,y)
is nonzero, the Newton polygon
P
of
\chi(x,y)
has area

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}(P)=\

\displaystyle n_{1}\mu_{1}\cdot\frac{n_{1}m_{1}}{2}+n_{2}\mu_{2}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+\frac{n_{2}m_{2}}{2}\Big)+n_{3}\mu_{3}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+n_{2}m_{2}+\frac{n_{3}m_{3}}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon-1}m_{\varepsilon-1}+\frac{n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}}{2}\Big).

Proof.

Slice
P
horizontally at the endpoints of the first quadrant edges. This gives
\varepsilon
slices, where the top slice is a triangle and the remaining slices are trapezoids (except possibly for the bottom slice, which might be a triangle). The first two slices are shown in Figure
5.2
. The area of the triangular first slice is

\frac{1}{2}\cdot n_{1}\mu_{1}\cdot n_{1}m_{1}=n_{1}\mu_{1}\cdot\frac{n_{1}m_{1}}{2},

and for the trapezoidal second slice, the area is

n_{2}\mu_{2}\cdot\frac{n_{1}m_{1}+(n_{1}m_{1}+n_{2}m_{2})}{2}=n_{2}\mu_{2}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+\frac{n_{2}m_{2}}{2}\Big).

The areas of the remaining slices are computed similarly, and the lemma follows.
∎

The next step is to compute the number of lattice points on the boundary of
P
. The left edge of
P
goes from the origin to
(0,n)
and has
n+1
lattice points, and the first quadrant edges have
n_{1}+1,n_{2}+1,\dots,n_{\varepsilon}+1
lattice points. The bottom edge goes from the origin to
(n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon},0)
(this follows by extending Figure
5.2
to show all edges). Hence the bottom edge has
n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}+1
lattice points. (When
n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}=0
, the bottom edge reduces to the origin, but
n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}+1=1
still counts the number of lattice points.) Putting this all together and being careful not to double-count the vertices of
P
, we get

n+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}+n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}

lattice points on the boundary of
P
. Using
n=n_{1}\mu_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}
(the heights of the
\varepsilon
slices add up to the total height
n
), we can rewrite this as

(5.11)

n_{1}\mu_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}+n_{1}(m_{1}+1)+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon}+1).

We are now ready to state and prove Abel’s formula for
\gamma

[
1
, (62) on p. 168]
.

Theorem 5.6
.

When the constant term of
\chi(x,y)
is nonzero, the number
\gamma
of interior lattice points of the Newton polygon
P
of
\chi(x,y)
is given by

\displaystyle\gamma=\

\displaystyle n_{1}\mu_{1}\Big(\frac{n_{1}m_{1}-1}{2}\Big)+n_{2}\mu_{2}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+\frac{n_{2}m_{2}-1}{2}\Big)\ +\

\displaystyle n_{3}\mu_{3}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+n_{2}m_{2}+\frac{n_{3}m_{3}-1}{2}\Big)+\cdots+\

\displaystyle n_{\varepsilon}\mu_{\varepsilon}\Big(n_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+n_{\varepsilon-1}m_{\varepsilon-1}+\frac{n_{\varepsilon}m_{\varepsilon}-1}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle-\ \frac{n_{1}(m_{1}+1)}{2}-\cdots-\frac{n_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon}+1)}{2}+1.

Proof.

By Pick’s formula (
5.10
), the number of interior lattice points of
P
is

\mathrm{Area}(P)-\tfrac{1}{2}\#\{\text{boundary lattice points of }P\}+1.

We computed the area of
P
in Lemma
5.5
and the number of boundary points in (
5.11
). Substituting these results into Pick’s formula and doing some easy algebra gives the formula for
\gamma
stated in the theorem.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

With some slight adjustments in notation (replace
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{\varepsilon}
with
\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime},\dots,\mu^{(\varepsilon)}
, and similarly for
n_{i}
and
m_{i}
), the formula in Theorem
5.6
is identical to (
1.6
), which is Abel’s formula (62) on
[
1
, p. 168]
.

Abel introduces the fractions
\frac{m_{1}}{\mu_{1}},\dots,\frac{m_{\varepsilon}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}
in equation (54) on
[
1
, p. 164]
as the distinct values that occur among
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
, just as we did above. However, his definition of
n_{i}
differs from ours. Given one
\ell
with
h_{\ell}=h(y^{(\ell)})=\frac{m_{i}}{\mu_{i}}
, the Puiseux algorithm creates
\mu_{i}
values of
\ell
for which
h(y^{(\ell)})=\frac{m_{i}}{\mu_{i}}
(these
\mu_{i}
solutions have the same highest exponent but their highest terms differ by
\mu_{i}
th roots of unity). It follows that the number of
\ell
’s for which
h_{\ell}=h(y^{(\ell)})=\frac{m_{i}}{\mu_{i}}
is a multiple of
\mu_{i}
. This is Abel’s
n_{i}
, which he defines in equation (55) on
[
1
, p. 164]
. The Puiseux algorithm shows that Abel’s
n_{i}
agrees with ours.

Our proof of Abel’s formula for
\gamma
is very different from Abel’s original argument
[
1
, pp. 164–168]
, though his proof, summarized by Houzel in
[
24
, pp. 92–93]
, is complete modulo some minor details.

5.3.
Relation to the Genus

It remains to discuss when Abel’s
\gamma
is the genus in the modern sense. This is a complicated question, especially because the curve
\chi(x,y)=0
may be singular, and further problems can arise at infinity when working in the projective plane. Sylow [4, Vol. 2, pp. 296–300], Brill and Noether [7, pp. 215–222], and Houzel [20, pp. 92–96] explore some of these challenges, and Kleiman discusses Abel’s
\gamma
and the genus in
[
29
, pp. 408–409]
.

We will take a different approach which focuses on the Newton polygon
P
of
\chi(x,y)=0
. In toric geometry, the polygon
P
determines a projective toric surface
X_{P}
that contains a projective curve defined by
\chi(x,y)=0
, similar to how this equation defines a curve in the projective plane. The advantage of
X_{P}
is that the curve defined by
\chi(x,y)=0
in
X_{P}
often avoids the problems at infinity mentioned in the previous paragraph. We refer to
[
15
]
for an introduction to toric geometry.

Here is our main result about
\gamma
and the genus.

Theorem 5.8
.

Assume that the constant term of
\chi(x,y)
is nonzero.
If
\chi(x,y)
is sufficiently general within its Newton polygon
P
, then the genus of the Riemann surface
S
associated to
\chi(x,y)=0
is the integer
\gamma
in Abel’s formula (
1.6
), and
S
can be identified with the smooth projective curve in
X_{P}
defined by
\chi(x,y)=0
.

Proof.

The proof follows from results in
[
15
, §10.5]
. As explained in the discussion preceding
[
15
, Proposition 10.5.8]
, standard Bertini Theorems imply that if
\chi(x,y)
is sufficiently general within its Newton polygon
P
, then
\chi(x,y)=0
defines a smooth projective curve in
X_{P}
. When this happens, the resulting smooth curve is the Riemann surface
S
associated to
\chi(x,y)=0
.

The same discussion in
[
15
]
shows that when
\chi(x,y)=0
defines a smooth curve in
X_{P}
, its genus can be computed by the adjunction formula (see
[
15
, (10.5.1)]
). Then the toric interpretation of the adjunction formula and Pick’s formula (
5.10
) imply that the genus of
S
is the number of interior lattice points of
P
(see
[
15
, Prop. 10.5.8]
for the details). By Theorem
5.6
, we conclude that the genus is given by Abel’s formula for
\gamma
.
∎

5.4.
Holomorphic Differentials

Abel’s
\gamma
uses the inequalities (
5.7
) that lead to the
vector space of differentials
\Omega
in Proposition
5.3
. On the other hand, the genus uses holomorphic differentials. Theorem
5.8
shows that
\gamma
equals the genus in many cases but says nothing about how Abel’s differentials relate to the holomorphic ones. In fact, these differentials are the same in the situation of the theorem.

To see why, we use the classical theory of adjoint curves, as suggested by the reviewer and explained in
[
29
, pp. 407 and 410–412]
. Recall that Abel assumes that
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
is constant, so that the differential form is

(5.12)

f(x,y)\,dx=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{\chi^{\prime}(y)}\hskip 0.5ptdx.

Proposition
5.3
can be interpreted as saying that (
5.12
) is one of Abel’s differentials precisely when
xyf_{1}(x,y)
has exponent vectors in the interior of the Newton polygon
P
of
\chi(x,y)
. When
\chi(x,y)
is generic within
P
, Proposition I of Baker’s paper
[
6
]
then implies that Abel’s differentials come from adjoint curves and hence are holomorphic by the theory of adjoint curves.

Thus Abel’s differentials are contained in the holomorphic ones. These vector spaces have the same dimension by Theorem
5.8
, so generically, Abel’s differentials are precisely the holomorphic differentials on the Riemann surface
S
of
\chi(x,y)=0
.

Once we connect holomorphic differentials to Abel’s question about when
v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}
is constant, other powerful ideas come into play, including linear equivalence of divisors, the Jacobian, and the Abel map (sometimes called the Abel-Jacobi map). These topics are important but lie beyond the scope of these notes. See
[
29
, Sections 3 and 6]
for further discussion.

We began this section with Abel’s question about when his formula for
v
reduces to a constant. This led to some wonderful mathematics—far beyond anything Abel was thinking about in 1826—and gives another example of his amazing ability to ask the right question.

6.
Other Versions of Abel’s Theorem

In this section we discuss the motivation for Abel’s Paris memoir and say more about the other versions of Abel’s Theorem that appear in this amazing paper.

6.1.
From Elliptic Integrals to Abel’s Theorems

Elliptic integrals have a long and rich history (see
[
23
]
for a nice overview). They enjoy many wonderful properties, including the classic addition theorem

(6.1)

\int_{0}^{x}\!\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{1{-}t^{4}}}\,dt+\!\int_{0}^{y}\!\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{1{-}t^{4}}}\,dt=\!\int_{0}^{z}\!\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{1{-}t^{4}}}\,dt,\ z=\frac{x\sqrt{1{-}y^{4}}+y\sqrt{1{-}x^{4}}}{1{+}x^{2}y^{2}}

proved by Euler in 1753. By Abel’s time, it was known that the sum of any number of elliptic integrals (which he called “elliptic functions”) “can be expressed by a single function of the same form, by adding a certain algebraic and logarithmic expression”
[
3
, p. 444]
. When this happens, the variable in the “single function” is an algebraic function of the variables in the elliptic summands, as in (
6.1
), where the variable
z
of the “single function” on the right is related algebraically to the variables
x
and
y
of the integrals on the left.

In (
6.1
), also notice that the two integrals sum to a constant when we impose a single condition on
x
and
y
by requiring that
z
be constant.
This is true for general elliptic integrals, provided we allow the sum to be an algebraic and logarithmic function. In the introduction to his Paris memoir, Abel states this as follows:

we can express any sum of similar functions [elliptic integrals] by an algebraic and logarithmic function, provided that we establish a certain algebraic relationship between the variables of these functions.
[
1
, pp. 145–146]

He goes on to explain his motivation for writing
[
1
]
:

This analogy between the properties of these functions [elliptic integrals] led the author to investigate whether or not it would be possible to find analogous properties of more general functions [the Abelian integrals (
1.1
)]
[
1
, p. 146]

Abel then states two versions of what we now call “Abel’s Theorem”. For the first, he says:

”
If one has several functions whose derivatives are roots of the same
”
algebraic equation, all of whose coefficients are rational functions
”
of the same variable, one can always express the sum of a number
”
of similar functions by a
logarithmic
and
algebraic
function, pro-
”
vided that a certain number of
algebraic
relations are established
”
between the variables of the functions in question.“
[
1
, p. 146]

(The odd format using
”
and “ is how Abel indicates a theorem in the introduction to
[
1
]
.)
The functions described at the beginning of this quote are Abelian integrals (
1.1
), so Abel is saying that the sum of any number of Abelian integrals is a logarithmic and algebraic function when a certain number of algebraic relations are satisfied. He goes on to say:

The number of these relations does not depend on the number of summands, but only on the nature of the particular functions that one considers. Thus, for example, for an elliptic integrand this number is 1; for an integrand that contains no irrationalities but a radical of the second degree, under which the variable has degree five or six, the number of necessary relations is 2, and so forth.

In the last sentence, Abel gives examples where the number of relations is easily seen to be the genus the curve in question. This suggests that the genus may have a role to play in this version of Abel’s Theorem, which Kleiman calls
Abel’s Relations Theorem
in
[
29
]
. We will see below that the genus is indeed a key player.

Abel then states a second theorem:

”
One can always express the sum of a given number of functions,
”
which are each multiplied by a rational number, and whose vari-
”
ables are arbitrary, by a similar sum of a given number of func-
”
tions, whose variables are algebraic functions of the variables of
”
the given functions“
[
1
, p. 146]

The “given number” of functions in this quote is the number of relations in the previous theorem, which as we will see in (
6.4
) is the genus. There is also a slight omission, for Abel forgot to say that the “similar sum” also involves a logarithmic and algebraic function. This is included when he states the result later in the memoir
[
1
, (119) on p. 188]
). In
[
29
]
, Kleiman calls this
Abel’s Addition Theorem
.

The key insight of Abel’s Addition Theorem is that a sum of Abelian integrals equals a logarithmic and algebraic function plus a given number, usually more than one, of Abelian integrals whose variables depend algebraically on the variables of the original integrals. As just noted, the given number of integrals is the genus.

6.2.
Comments on Abel’s Theorems

The details of Abel’s Relations and Addition Theorems are described by Houzel
[
24
, Section 5]
, and their implications for algebraic geometry in the 19th and 20th centuries are discussed by Kleiman
[
29
, Sections 4–7]
. Our goals here are more modest: we will (1) indicate how the sum

(6.2)

v=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}

studied earlier in these notes leads to a special case of Abel’s Relations Theorem and (2) say a few words about how the genus enters the picture.

In (
6.2
), the
(x_{i},y_{i})
are the solutions of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y,\underline{a})=0
with
dx_{i}\neq 0
and hence are algebraic functions of
\underline{a}=a_{1},a_{2},\dots
. Recall that the first result proved in
[
1
]
states that
v
is a rational and logarithmic function of
\underline{a}
.

Following Abel
[
1
, p. 170]
, let
\alpha
denote the number of indeterminates
\underline{a}=a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\alpha}
that appear in
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
. We will assume that
F_{0}(x)=1
, which guarantees that
a_{1},\dots,a_{\alpha}
are algebraically independent. Abel argues
[
1
, p. 170]
that
x_{1},\dots,x_{\mu}
can be numbered so that
x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}
are algebraically independent. This implies that
a_{1},\dots,a_{\alpha}
,
x_{\alpha+1},\dots,x_{\mu}
, and
y_{1},\dots,y_{\mu}
are algebraic functions of
x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}
. Now write (
6.2
) in the form

(6.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=v-\!\sum_{i=\alpha+1}^{\mu}\int\!f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}.

In this equation,
v
is a rational and logarithmic function of
\underline{a}
and hence an algebraic and logarithmic function of
x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}
. There are
\mu-\alpha
integrals on the right-hand side. Abel assures us that this number is “trés-remarquable”
[
1
, p. 172]
.

Abel’s enthusiasm for
\mu-\alpha
is due in part to its relation to the number
\gamma
from (
1.6
). More precisely, when
F_{0}(x)
is constant, he proves that

\mu-\alpha\geq\gamma.

He goes on to claim that equality can occur when
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
is chosen carefully (see
[
1
, pp. 176–177]
).
Several pages later, Abel
[
1
, (105) on p. 180]
concludes that

(6.4)

\mu-\alpha=\gamma

when all coefficients of the carefully chosen
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
are indeterminates (more on this below). By Theorem
5.8
,
\mu-\alpha=\gamma
is the genus in many cases.

The full story is very complicated. More details can be found in Sylow
[
5
, Vol. 2, pp. 296–300]
, Brill and Noether
[
9
, pp. 215–222]
, and Houzel
[
24
, pp. 92–96]
. Kleiman’s article
[
29
]
discusses these issues from the point of view of both classical and modern algebraic geometry, while Edwards
[
19
, Chapter 9]
, drawing on work of Dedekind and Weber
[
16
]
, takes a more constructive and algebraic point of view. In particular, Essay 9.9 of
[
19
]
shows that
\mu-\alpha
equals the genus for an explicitly described choice of
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
.

For us, a link between
\mu-\alpha
and the genus
g
of the Riemann surface
S
of
\chi(x,y)=0
can be described as follows (omitting many details). Let
D
be a divisor of degree
\mu\geq 2g-1
on
S
. As usual,
L(D)
is the vector space of rational functions
\varphi(x,y)
on
S
such that
\mathrm{div}(\varphi(x,y))+D\geq 0
, and the corresponding complete linear system of divisors is the projective space
|D|
of
L(D)
. Set
\ell(D)=\dim L(D)=1+\alpha
, so that
\alpha=\dim|D|
. Fix a basis
\varphi_{0}(x,y),\dots,\varphi_{\alpha}(x,y)
of
L(D)
and define

\theta(x,y,\underline{a})=a_{0}\varphi_{0}(x,y)+\cdots+a_{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(x,y).

For a generic choice of
\underline{a}
, the solutions
(x_{i},y_{i})
of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y,\underline{a})=0
give an effective divisor of degree
\mu
on
S
in the complete linear system
|D|
of dimension
\alpha
. This is why the number of indeterminates in
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
is written
1+\alpha
, not
\alpha
.

By the Riemann-Roch Theorem (see, for example,
[
21
, §8.6]
), we have

\deg(D)-g+1=\ell(D)-\ell(K-D),

where
K
is a canonical divisor on
S
. But
\deg(D)=\mu
and
\ell(D)=1+\alpha
, and
\mu\geq 2g-1
guarantees that
\ell(K-D)=0
. Hence

\mu-g+1=(1+\alpha)-0,\ \ \text{so that}\ \ \mu-\alpha=g.

This argument gives one way of seeing why Abel needed to make a careful choice of
\theta(x,y,\underline{a})
. It also justisfies his claim that
\mu-\alpha
is “very remarkable”.

When
\mu-\alpha
equals the genus, we get a version of Abel’s Relations Theorem as follows. In (
6.3
), impose
\mu-\alpha
conditions by requiring that
x_{\alpha+1},\dots,x_{\mu}
be constant. Since
x_{\alpha+1},\dots,x_{\mu}
are algebraic functions of
x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}
, this imposes
\mu-\alpha
algebraic relations on
x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}
, and when these conditions are satisfied,
dx_{\alpha+1}=\cdots=dx_{\mu}=0
. Then (
6.3
) simplifies to

\int\!f(x_{1},y_{1})\,dx_{1}+\cdots+\int\!f(x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})\,dx_{\alpha}=v,

where
v
is an algebraic and logarithmic function of
x_{1},\dots,x_{\alpha}
. The number of conditions is
\mu-\alpha
, which is the genus in this situation. Hence we have a special case of Abel’s Relations Theorem!

A.1.
The Case of Rational Functions

The integrals
\int\!f(x,y)\,dx
in Abel’s memoir can be complicated because
y
is usually an algebraic function of
x
. When
y
is not present, we have the known case of an integral of a rational function. Abel’s formulas still apply and reveal an unexpected connection to partial fractions.

We begin by applying Abel’s formula for
dv
to a rational function.

Theorem A.1
.

For a rational function
\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{K}(x)
and a new variable
a
, we have

(A.1)

\varphi(a)=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{x=\beta_{j}}\Big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\Big),

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
are the poles of
\varphi(x)
in
\overline{\mathbb{K}}
.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
3.9
to
\chi(x,y)=y-x
and
\theta(x,y,a)=y-a
, so
\underline{a}=a
. The root of
\chi(x,y)
is
y^{(1)}=x
, and the solution of
\chi(x,y)=\theta(x,y,a)=0
is
(x_{1},y_{1})=(a,a)
. Thus

dv=\varphi(x_{1})\,dx_{1}=\varphi(a)\,da.

We also have
F(x)=x-a
and
F_{0}(x)=1
. Following Abel
[
1
, (201) on p. 203]
, set

\varphi(x)=\frac{f(x)}{f_{2}(x)},

so that the poles of
\varphi(x)
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)
. Note that
\chi^{\prime}(y)=1
and
\delta=\frac{d}{da}\,da
.

The definition of
S(x)
given in (
3.3
) reduces to

(A.2)

S(x)=\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(x,y^{(1)})}{\theta(x,y^{(1)})}=\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\frac{d}{da}(x-a)\,da}{x-a}=\varphi(x)\frac{-\hskip 1.0ptda}{x-a}=\frac{\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0ptda}{a-x}.

Theorem
3.9
implies that if
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\alpha}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)
, then

\varphi(a)\,da=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt(S(x))-\text{\large$\varPi$}(S(x))=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\frac{\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0ptda}{a-x}\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0ptda}{a-x}\Big).

Canceling
da
, we get (
A.1
).
∎

Now comes a surprise: (
A.1
) is an elegant way of writing the partial fraction decomposition of
\varphi(a)
. Here is the precise result.

Proposition A.2
.

Let

\varphi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{A_{j}^{(k)}}{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}}+G(x)

be the partial fraction decomposition of
\varphi(x)
, where the
A_{j}^{(k)}
are constants,
G(x)
is a polynomial, and
\nu_{j}
is the order of
\beta_{j}
as a pole of
\varphi(x)
. Then

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{res}_{x=\beta_{j}}\Big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{A_{j}^{(k)}}{(a-\beta_{j})^{k}}

(A.4)

\displaystyle\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\Big)

\displaystyle=-G(a).

Thus (
A.1
) computes the partial fraction decomposition of
\varphi(a)
.

Remark A.3
.

The formulas (
A.3
) and (
A.4
) linking residues and partial fractions are well known (see, for example,
[
11
, 1.2.4]
). What is new here is the recognition of their relevance to Abel’s formulas in the special case considered in Theorem
A.1
.

Proof.

We first compute
\mathrm{res}_{x=\beta_{j}}\big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\big)
. In the partial fraction decomposition of
\varphi(x)
, isolate the
j
th summand and expand everything else in terms of
x-\beta_{j}
to obtain

(A.5)

\varphi(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{A_{j}^{(k)}}{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}}+b_{0}+b_{1}(x-\beta_{j})+\cdots.

We also have the expansion

\frac{1}{a-x}=\frac{1}{(a-\beta_{j})\big(1-\frac{x-\beta_{j}}{a-\beta_{j}}\big)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-\beta_{j})^{n}}{(a-\beta_{j})^{n+1}}.

Some easy algebra implies that
\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}
has the expansion

\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}=\cdots+\frac{1}{x-\beta_{j}}\Big(\frac{A^{(1)}_{j}}{a-\beta_{j}}+\frac{A^{(2)}_{j}}{(a-\beta_{j})^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{A^{(\nu_{j})}_{j}}{(a-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}}\Big)+\cdots.

Since
\mathrm{res}_{x=\beta_{j}}\big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\big)
is the coefficient of
\frac{1}{x-\beta_{j}}
, (
A.3
) follows immediately.

For
\text{\large$\varPi$}\big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{z-x}\big)
, expand
\varphi(x)
in terms of descending powers of
\frac{1}{x}
to obtain

(A.6)

\varphi(x)=\underbrace{c_{m}x^{m}+c_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\cdots+c_{0}}_{\displaystyle G(x)}+\frac{c_{-1}}{x}+\frac{c_{-2}}{x^{2}}+\cdots.

Note also that

\frac{1}{a-x}=\frac{-1}{x}\frac{1}{1-\frac{a}{x}}=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{n}}{x^{n+1}}.

Then multiplying out
\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}
gives the expansion

\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}=\cdots+\frac{1}{x}\big(-c_{m}a^{m}-\cdots-c_{1}a-c_{0}\big)+\cdots.

Since
\text{\large$\varPi$}\big(\frac{\varphi(x)}{a-x}\big)
is the coefficient of
\frac{1}{x}
, (
A.4
) follows easily.
∎

We can now compute
\int\!\varphi(a)\,da
using Theorem

.

Theorem A.4
.

(A.7)

\int\!\varphi(a)\,da=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{x=\beta_{j}}(\varphi(x)\log_{\beta_{j}}(a-x)-\text{\large$\varPi$}(\varphi(x)\log_{\infty}(a-x))+C,

where
\log_{\beta_{j}}(a-x)
and
\log_{\infty}(a-x)
are from Definition
4.1
.

Proof.

By (
A.2
), we obtain

S(x)=\frac{\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0ptda}{a-x}=\varphi(x)\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta(a-x)}{a-x},

which implies

\widehat{S}_{\beta_{j}}(x)=\varphi(x)\log_{\beta_{j}}(a-x)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{S}_{\infty}(x)=\varphi(x)\log_{\infty}(a-x)

by the formulas given in Section
4.3
. Then (
A.7
) follows from Theorem
4.3
.
∎

Remark A.5
.

Abel’s version of (
A.7
) is equation (201) on
[
1
, p. 203]
, which states

(A.8)

\int\!\frac{fx_{1}\hskip 0.5pt.\hskip 0.5ptdx_{1}}{f_{2}x_{1}}=C-\text{\large$\varPi$}\frac{fx}{f_{2}x}\log(x-x_{1})+\sum\nu\frac{d^{\nu-1}}{d\beta^{\nu-1}}\bigg(\frac{f\beta}{f_{2}^{(\nu)}\beta}\log(\beta-x_{1})\bigg).

Similar to (
1.3
) and (

), Abel’s formula is not quite correct, as will be explained in Sections

and
A.3
.
After stating (
A.8
), Abel notes that his formula “gives, as one can see, the integral of every rational differential.”

Not surprisingly, (
A.7
) computes the integral of partial fraction decomposition of
\varphi(a)
.
To see why, we begin with
\log_{\beta_{j}}(a-x)
. Since

, Definition
4.1

\log_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt(a-x)=\log(a-\beta_{j})-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}}{k(a-\beta_{j})^{k}}.

Combining this with the expansion of
\varphi(x)
given in (

), one computes without difficulty that

\mathrm{res}_{x=\beta_{j}}(\varphi(x)\log_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt(a-x))=A^{(1)}_{j}\log(a-\beta_{j})-\frac{A^{(2)}_{j}}{a-\beta_{j}}-\cdots-\frac{A^{(\nu_{j})}_{j}}{(\nu_{j}-1)(a-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}-1}},

which is easily seen to be an antiderivative of

Similarly,

Combining this with (

\text{\large$\varPi$}(\varphi(x)\log_{\infty}(a-x))=-\frac{c_{m}a^{m+1}}{m+1}-\cdots-\frac{c_{1}a^{2}}{2}-c_{0}a+c_{-1}\log(-1),

which is clearly an antiderivative of

.

Remark A.6
.

We can summarize our discussion of

Proof of Theorem
A.7
.

We begin with

f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=-\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}(S(x))=-\mathrm{res}_{x_{i}}\Big(\frac{R(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\Big)=-\frac{R(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}.

The first equality is Lemma

, the second uses (
A.9
), and the third follows from (
3.13
) since
\theta_{1}(x_{i})=f_{2}(x_{i})F_{0}(x_{i})\neq 0
. Thus

(A.11)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}.

This is equation (22) of
[
1
, p. 153]
. If you take the proof of (

Now divide

to get polynomial differential forms

and

\frac{R(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)}=R_{2}(x)+\frac{R_{3}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)},\ \ \deg(R_{3}(x))<\deg(\theta_{1}(x)).

Then (

) becomes

(A.12)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{2}(x_{i})}{F^{\prime}(x_{i})}-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{3}(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}.

By Lemma
A.9
, the first sum on the right-hand side is

(A.13)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{2}(x_{i})}{F^{\prime}(x_{i})}=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{R_{2}(x)}{F(x)}\Big)=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{R_{2}(x)}{F(x)}+\frac{R_{3}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\Big)=\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{R(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\Big).

The second equality follows from
\deg(R_{3}(x))<\deg(\theta_{1}(x))
and
0<\deg(F(x))
.

For the second sum on the right of (
A.12
), observe that by Lemma
A.10
,

\displaystyle\frac{R_{3}(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(x_{i})}\frac{R_{3}(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(x_{i})}\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(\beta)}{(x_{i}-\beta)\theta_{1}(\beta)}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(\beta)}{\theta_{1}(\beta)}\cdot\frac{1}{(x_{i}-\beta)F^{\prime}(x_{i})}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}.

When we add this up for

, the quantity inside the large parentheses is

\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(\beta)}{\theta_{1}(\beta)}\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{1}{(x_{i}-\beta)F^{\prime}(x_{i})}.

However, we have a classic partial fraction decomposition known to Abel:

(A.14)

\frac{1}{F(\beta)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{1}{(\beta-x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{1}{(x_{i}-\beta)F^{\prime}(x_{i})}.

This holds because
F(x)
has simple roots by assumption (
3.6
). It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{3}(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(\beta)}{\theta_{1}(\beta)F(\beta)}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{dx^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(x-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\bigg)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}}.

The second line follows from the first by replacing the variable
\beta
with the variable
x
. However, in this formula, we can replace
R_{3}(x)
with
R(x)=R_{3}(x)+\theta_{1}(x)R_{2}(x)
because

\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{dx^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(x-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}\theta_{1}(x)R_{2}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\bigg)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}}\!=\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{dx^{\nu_{j}-1}}(x-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}\frac{R_{2}(x)}{F(x)}\bigg)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}}\!=0

since
\beta_{j}
is not a root of
F(x)
. Thus

(A.15)

\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{3}(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}=-\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{dx^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(x-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\bigg)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}}.

We are now done, since

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}f(x_{i},y_{i})\,dx_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{2}(x_{i})}{F^{\prime}(x_{i})}-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{R_{3}(x_{i})}{\theta_{1}(x_{i})F^{\prime}(x_{i})}

\displaystyle=-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\frac{R(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\Big)+\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}{-}1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{dx^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(x{-}\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)F(x)}\bigg)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}},

where the first line is (
A.12
) and the second line follows from (
A.13
) and (

).

Proof of Lemmma

For us, this is an easy consequence of (

Here is what Abel says next

… it follows that

The upshot is that Abel has proved that

We now turn to Lemma
A.10
, which again uses partial fractions.

Proof of Lemma
A.10
.

Since
\deg(R_{3}(x))<\deg(\theta_{1}(x))
, we have a partial fraction expansion

\frac{R_{3}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)}=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{A_{j}^{(k)}}{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}},

where it is well known that

A_{j}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-k)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-k}p(x)}{dx^{\nu_{j}-k}}\bigg)\Big|_{x=\beta_{j}},\ p(x)=(x-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}\frac{R_{3}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)}

(see, for example,
[
37
, (7-84) on p. 175]
). Following Abel
[
1
, p. 156]
, we switch from
x
to
\beta
and write

(A.16)

A_{j}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-k)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-k}p(\beta)}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-k}}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}},\ p(\beta)=(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}\frac{R_{3}(\beta)}{\theta_{1}(\beta)}.

Abel then sets

q(\beta)=\frac{1}{x-\beta}

and observes that

\frac{1}{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}}=\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{k-1}q(\beta)}{d\beta^{k-1}}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{A_{j}^{(k)}}{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-k)!}\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-k}p(\beta)}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-k}}\cdot\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\frac{d^{k-1}q(\beta)}{d\beta^{k-1}}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\binom{\nu_{j}-1}{k-1}\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-k}p(\beta)}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-k}}\cdot\frac{d^{k-1}q(\beta)}{d\beta^{k-1}}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}.

By the generalized product rule, the quantity inside the large parentheses is

\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}p(\beta)q(\beta)=\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(\beta)}{(x-\beta)\theta_{1}(\beta)}.

Putting everything together, Abel obtains the desired formula

\frac{R_{3}(x)}{\theta_{1}(x)}=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\nu_{j}}\frac{A_{j}^{(k)}}{(x-\beta_{j})^{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}-1)!}\bigg(\frac{d^{\nu_{j}-1}}{d\beta^{\nu_{j}-1}}\frac{(\beta-\beta_{j})^{\nu_{j}}R_{3}(\beta)}{(x-\beta)\theta_{1}(\beta)}\bigg)\Big|_{\beta=\beta_{j}}.\qed

A.4.
Boole and Rowe

Abel’s Paris memoir contains a large number of powerful ideas that were studied after its publication in 1841. In particular, the papers by Boole
[
8
]
in 1857 and Rowe
[
34
]
in 1881 considered Abel’s formulas for
dv
and
v
. Their main contribution was to simplify his proofs by judicious use of residues.

The key feature of Boole’s 1857 paper
[
8
]
is the symbolic operator
\Theta
, which he introduces as follows:

As respects the methods and processes which will be employed in this paper, the only peculiarity to which it seems necessary to direct attention, is the introduction of a symbol [the
\Theta
just mentioned], differing in interpretation only by the addition of one element, from that which Cauchy has employed in his ‘Calculus of Residues’.
[
8
, pp. 745–746]

Boole’s use of a symbol like
\Theta
is not surprising since he was one of the creators of symbolic logic. He was a firm believer in the power of symbolic operators.

The definition of
\Theta
involves residues, including residues at
\infty
. Section
A.2
gave Abel’s definition of
\varPi
as the coefficient of
\frac{1}{x}
is an expansion of descending powers of
x
. In
[
8
]
, Boole uses
C_{\frac{1}{x}}
in place of
\varPi
. This is the “addition of one element” in the above quote. Here is a modern version of Boole’s definition of
\Theta

[
8
, p. 752]
.

Definition A.13
.

Let
\varphi(x)
and
f(x)
be rational functions in
\mathbb{L}(x)
, where
\mathbb{L}
is algebraically closed. Then

\Theta[\varphi(x)]f(x)\ =\!\!\!\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{L}\text{ pole of }\varphi(x)}\!\!\!\mathrm{res}_{\beta}(\varphi(x)f(x))\ -\ C_{\frac{1}{x}}(\varphi(x)f(x)).

Boole then proved some basic properties of
\Theta
. In particular, on
[
8
, p. 755]
, he showed that if

\varphi(x)
a rational function and
f(x)
is a polynomial, then

(A.21)

\Theta[\varphi(x)]f(x)=0.

When applied to the constant polynomial
f(x)=1
, this implies the Global Residue Theorem for
\varphi(x)
. Not surprisingly, Boole’s proof used partial fractions, similar to the Serre’s proof of (
3.14
) in
[
35
, Lemma 3 on p. 21]
.

Boole considered some special cases of Abel’s formulas, but the general case had to wait for the 1881 paper
[
34
]
of Rowe. The symbol
\Theta
enabled Rowe to restate Abel’s formula (
3.2
) more concisely. Using
f(x,y)=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y)}
, note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)}\cdot F_{0}(x)\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{f_{2}(x)\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}.

Now recall the version of Abel’s formula for
dv
stated in (
3.2
):

dv=\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha}\mathrm{res}_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-1.0pt\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big)-\text{\large$\varPi$}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}f(x,y^{(\ell)})\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big),

where the
\beta_{j}
are the roots of
f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)
, i.e,, the poles of
\frac{1}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)}
. By the definition of
\Theta
, it follows that (
3.2
) is equivalent to the equation

(A.22)

dv=\Theta\Big[\frac{1}{f_{2}(x)F_{0}(x)}\Big]\Big(\!F_{0}(x)\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{f_{1}(x,y^{(\ell)})}{\chi^{\prime}(y^{(\ell)})}\hskip 1.0pt\frac{\delta\theta(y^{(\ell)})}{\theta(y^{(\ell)})}\Big).

Rowe assumes (
3.6
) and gives a complete proof of (
A.22
) on p. 720 of
[
8
]
. His proof does not use the Global Residue Theorem, but it is definitely in the background. As noted in Section
4.5
, Forsyth
[
20
, pp. 579–586]
discusses Abel’s formula for
v
. His argument follows Rowe (including the use of
\Theta
) but takes place in a purely analytic context.

The papers of Boole and Rowe were the inspiration for these notes.
