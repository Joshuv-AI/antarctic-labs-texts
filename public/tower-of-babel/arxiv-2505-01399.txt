Dynamic Robot Tool Use with Vision Language Models
Noah Trupin, Zixing WangAhmed H. Qureshi Purdue University {ntrupin, wang5389, ahqureshi}@purdue.edu

Abstract
Tool use enhances a robot’s task capabilities. Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have equipped robots with sophisticated cognitive capabilities for tool-use applications. However, existing methodologies focus on basic quasi-static tool manipulations or high-level tool selection while neglecting the critical aspect of task-appropriate tool grasping. To address this limitation, we introduce i nverse T ool- U se P lanning (iTUP), a novel VLM-driven framework that enables grounded fine-grained planning for versatile robotic tool use. Through an integrated pipeline of VLM-based tool and contact point grounding, position-velocity trajectory planning, and physics-informed grasp generation and selection, iTUP demonstrates versatility across (1) quasi-static and more challenging (2) dynamic and (3) cluster tool-use tasks. To ensure robust planning, our framework integrates stable task-appropriate grasping by reasoning over semantic affordances and physical constraints. We evaluate iTUP and baselines on a comprehensive range of realistic tool use tasks including precision hammering, object scooping, and cluster sweeping. Experimental results demonstrate that iTUP ensures a thorough grounding of cognition and planning for challenging robot tool use across diverse environments.

Introduction

Tool use represents an advanced skill that expands a robot’s task capabilities, enabling autonomous systems to transcend their inherent physical limitations and tackle complex challenges. Past research in this area has demonstrated impressive progress through various prototypes and demonstrations. For example, robots have retrieved distant objects by manipulating a long stick
[
1
,
2
]
and employed a brush to sweep away scattered clusters
[
3
]
.

We recognize two major challenges for generalizable and robust robot tool use: (1)
Fine-grounded Open-vocabulary Cognition
and (2)
Task-appropriate Grasping for Dynamic Tool Use
. Regarding the first challenge, cognition skills play a vital role among the core cognition, perception, and manipulation components of tool use
[
4
]
. Effective cognition enables robots to understand contextual tool affordances and select appropriate objects as tools to complete assigned tasks. However, most existing work suffers from an inability to generalize. Current approaches rely on large-scale datasets and abstractions (such as keypoints, flows, and geometric features) to enable affordance estimation
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, making them less dependent on specific visual features. Despite these efforts, there remains a significant gap in achieving true open-vocabulary generalization, where robots can reason about and utilize novel tools without extensive prior training. Concerning the second challenge, most existing work studies quasi-static or minimal torque tasks, creating an incomplete representation of real-world tool manipulation. For instance, previous attempts
[
6
,
10
,
2
]
involving hammering tasks never consider the torque incurred during hammer-nail interaction. This simplified setup fails to address critical challenges in realistic scenarios, such as tool slippage or displacement due to unstable grasps during dynamic interactions, raising an urgent need for frameworks that consider more realistic tool use factors, especially in dynamic tool manipulation.

Recent advances in VLMs have equipped robots with powerful semantic reasoning capabilities
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
, opening an avenue to open-vocabulary robot tool use. Some pioneering studies
[
10
,
2
]
have demonstrated the ability of VLMs to generalize and understand tool affordances. However, the improvements brought by these frameworks focus on object and part selection, with more fine-grained and manipulation-related reasoning missing.

To remedy these challenges, we propose inverse Tool Use Planning (iTUP), a VLM-driven framework that bridges high-level semantic grounding with fine-grained physics-informed planning for dynamic robot tool use tasks. Our approach handles both dynamic and quasi-static tool manipulation requirements, offering a unified solution across interaction contexts as Fig.
1
shows. iTUP consists of a VLM module, a position-velocity planner, and a grasp quality evaluation network, SDG-Net. These modules conduct inverse planning for tool use tasks: beginning with tool-object interactions, proceeding to manipulation motions, and concluding with tool grasp poses. This inverse planning approach ensures that physics constraints, which originate from tool-object interactions, inform each stage of the planning process. We summarize the main contributions of this work as follows:

•

We studied dynamic robot tool use scenarios to facilitate realistic applications.

•

We propose iTUP, the first unified framework that couples VLM-based open-vocabulary cognition with physics-aware inverse planning.

•

We evaluated our framework and modules in real-world and simulation environments, demonstrating improved tool use cognition and manipulation performance over existing baselines.

Methods

. We study three regimes of tool-use tasks: (1) quasi-static, (2) dynamic, and (3) clustered, each characterized by distinct physical interactions and dynamics.
Quasi-static

dynamic

VLM-driven Hierarchical Tool Grounding

Humans operate on an intrinsic understanding of physical properties when using tools. In imitation of this, we apply a two-level granularity tool grounding framework to enable open-vocabulary tool grounding. This framework leverages chain-of-thought prompting (Appx.
E

Coarse Grounding

Position-velocity Trajectory Planning

Given tool contact point

4.3
Real-World Evaluation of iTUP

We conduct a series of tool use tasks including hammer nail, sweep toys, and knock over tower (10 trials for each) to evaluate the performance of iTUP and other baselines. The results reported in Table

We attribute iTUP’s performance to its integration of VLM cognition and SDG-Net physical grounding, enabling a fine-grained understanding of the objects present in the scene and the task at hand. For example, in the coarse grounding phase of the “Hammer nail” task, iTUP identifies the correct tool and object (the hammer and nail) and adorns them with physical context. Later, in the fine grounding phase, it recognizes the need to grasp the hammer by the handle and generate a trajectory from the hammer’s head to the top of the nail, aligning normals as necessary for the task. The “Knock over tower” task contains minimal forces and does not constitute a classic dynamic scenario; however, iTUP’s success showcases its open-vocabulary cognition and improvisation, identifying an object with which to complete the task and planning the interaction in this context.

4.4
Ablation Studies

We employ a series of ablation studies to determine (1) the impact of SDG-Net on physical grounding, (2) the significance of the VLM backbone, and (3) the need for two-level granularity tool grounding.

SDG-Net for stable dynamic grasping
.
We perform an ablation of SDG-Net to quantify its contribution of physical grounding in iTUP. Instead, we select the grasp with the highest static score and disregard both the context provided by the VLM and the forces from the generated trajectory. The results in Tab.
2
reveal a significant decrease in performance, with a success rate of
56.67\%
across tasks, falling short of the baseline. Fig.
4
demonstrates a failure to select an adequate grasp for the given context. As such, the physical grounding provided by SDG-Net proves invaluable in robust, grounded planning.

Understanding without VLMs
.
We remove the VLM component, instead relying on bare Set-of-Mark prompting
[
36
]
with trajectory synthesis based on tool and object centroids and a naive directional choice. We lose task context but maintain physical grounding through SDG-Net. However, the results in Tab.
2
show iTUP struggles with task completion without the context and reasoning from the VLM, underscoring the need for robust cognition and contextual grounding in planning for dynamic tool use.

Effect of two-level granularity on perception
.
We eliminate the two-level granularity framework in favor of allowing the VLM to select values from the initial coarse segmentation of the scene. Results in Tab.
2
reveal a decline in performance, with an emphasis on tasks requiring fine-grained reasoning such as “Hammer nail.” As iTUP injects physical context using the VLM’s real-world knowledge, ablation of the two-level granularity framework results in an additional loss of context.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce iTUP, which enables open-vocabulary and robust robot tool use. We evaluate the performance of iTUP in both real-world and simulator, the empirical results suggest strong open-vocabulary generalization ability and stable grasp pose generation for dynamic and quasi-static robot tool use tasks. In the future, we believe a promising research direction is using Vision-language-action models to effectively ground and manipulate tools in an end-to-end behavior, improving the efficiency and reducing the compounding error in-between different modules.

6
Limitations

Despite compensating for previous works’ lack of contextual and physical grounding and enabling effective planning for dynamic and cluster tool use, iTUP suffers from a few limitations addressable through future work in physical modeling, VLMs, and motion planning. Our physical grounding module uses three penalty metrics: torque, slip, and alignment. While an improvement over previous work
[
3
,
28
,
27
]
, the integration of a complete dynamics model could provide more thorough grounding through additional metrics present in complex tool use scenarios. Furthermore, VLMs operate on two-dimensional scene representations; as such, they lack genuine grounding in the three-dimensional world, affecting their ability to perform the spatial reasoning required for more complex tasks
[
10
]
. While our physical grounding module compensates for this, improved spatial awareness in VLMs would boost tool-object contact selection and trajectory synthesis in pre-physical grounding stages. Related to this, we find our pipeline over-reliant on VLM-provided contextual grounding: if the VLM provides nonsensical results, SDG-Net cannot determine a grasp that still completes the task. Finally, our trajectory planner relies on target points and alignment normals to synthesize an appropriate trajectory without regard for potential collisions. Future work could provide enhanced, collision-free trajectories using the outputs from iTUP’s contextual grounding.

Appendix B
Task Regime Dynamics

We provide here the mathematical formulations corresponding to the three task regimes discussed in Section
3.1
.

Quasi-static tool use.

In the quasi-static regime, inertial effects are negligible. The system satisfies approximate force and torque balance conditions:

\sum F_{\text{ext}}+F_{\text{grasp}}\approx 0,\quad\sum\tau_{\text{ext}}+\tau_{\text{grasp}}\approx 0,

where
F_{\text{ext}}
and
\tau_{\text{ext}}
represent external forces and torques, and
F_{\text{grasp}}
and
\tau_{\text{grasp}}
denote grasp forces and torques.

Dynamic tool use.

In the dynamic regime, inertial effects are significant, and the system must obey Newton-Euler equations. Let
m
be the tool mass,
a
the linear acceleration,
I
the inertia tensor, and
\omega
the angular velocity:

\sum F_{\text{ext}}+F_{\text{grasp}}=ma,\quad\sum\tau_{\text{ext}}+\tau_{\text{grasp}}=I\dot{\omega}+\omega\times I\omega.

Clustered tool use.

In the clustered regime, the tool interacts with multiple objects simultaneously. Let the set of objects be indexed by
i
, with each object contributing external forces
F_{\text{ext},i}
and torques
\tau_{\text{ext},i}
. The overall dynamics are:

\sum_{i\in\text{objects}}F_{\text{ext},i}+F_{\text{grasp}}=ma,\quad\sum_{i\in\text{objects}}\tau_{\text{ext},i}+\tau_{\text{grasp}}=I\dot{\omega}+\omega\times I\omega.

Appendix C
SDG-Net Architecture

SDG-Net incorporates convolutional
[
58
]
and multilayer perceptron modules to handle multimodal inputs. Given point cloud tool representation
X
, grasp candidate set
G=\{g_{i}\}
, tool contact point
c_{\text{tool}}
, and estimated interaction force
F
, we define our learnable convolutional
[
58
]
point cloud encoder
{z_{p}=\phi_{p}(X;\theta_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}}}
, multilayer perceptron (MLP) grasp encoder
{z_{g,i}=\phi_{g}(g_{i};\theta_{g})\in\mathbb{R}^{g_{i}}}
, and final MLP module
m_{i}=\phi_{m}(z_{i};\theta_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For each candidate
i
, we invoke the encoders and form the concatenated feature
{z_{i}=[z_{p};z_{g,i};c_{t};F]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}+d_{g}+6}}
. We then pass
z_{i}
through the final MLP to retrieve
{m_{i}=[\hat{\tau}_{i};\hat{s}_{i};\hat{\alpha}_{i}]^{\top}}
, which we interpret as the predicted torque penalty
\hat{\tau}_{i}
, slip penalty
\hat{s}_{i}
, and normal alignment penalty
\hat{\alpha}_{i}
. With fixed hyper-parameter scalar weights
(w_{\tau},w_{s},w_{\alpha})
, the final penalty for each grasp becomes
{\hat{P}_{i}=w_{\tau}\hat{\tau}_{i}+w_{s}\hat{s}_{i}+w_{\alpha}\hat{\alpha}_{i}}
. This penalty serves as the metric for grasp scoring and selection.

Appendix D
Data Generation for SDG-Net

We describe the detailed steps of our data generation pipeline used for training SDG-Net. We decouple our data generation process from real-world experiments to enable rapid dataset composition and network training and validation. Physical understanding of an object under our framework relies on the visual geometry and estimated physical properties from VLM module
\Phi_{1}
, which allows us to create and use synthetic objects in training. We utilize the trimesh library to generate multi-body closed meshes and NVIDIA Isaac Sim to adorn them with realistic physical properties such as mass (for which we assume a uniform density) and texture.

We then select random tool-object pairs from the generated dataset, sample a grasp

and

E.2.1
Self-Prompting GPT-4o

Feeding our prompts into GPT-4o and asking for rewritten prompts that “GPT-4o would follow and execute optimally with minimal refusals” yielded an increase in valid output production. Our original prompts resulted in proper output structure and correct selection in

E.2.2
System Instruction Ablation

We tried prompting GPT-4o with and without a descriptive system prompt, keeping all other inputs (scenes and prompts) constant. We found that without a system prompt GPT-4o refused to assist with our requests in
26.67\%
of tasks. The most common cause of refusal (occurring 7 out of 8 times) involved denial to analyze an image, with GPT-4o citing the task as outside its capabilities. The other refusal yielded no explanation. After adding a system message detailing a personality, capability set, and set of constraints for the model, refusals dropped to
0\%
. In addition, the rate of correct outputs increased to
96.67\%
