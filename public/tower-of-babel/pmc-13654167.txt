The impact of hearing loss on tinnitus-related brain reorganization: evidence from multimodal resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Ling Wang, Jiancheng Zhang, Yu Zhang, Yan Huang, Zhaodi Wang, Xuejing Li, Han Lv, Pengfei Zhao, Zhenghan Yang, Zhenchang Wang, Qian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether hearing loss fundamentally alters tinnitus-related brain reorganization remains unclear. To address this issue, this study compared features from multimodal resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) between tinnitus patients with hearing loss (TIN + HL) and those without hearing loss (TIN − HL).MethodsA total of 38 patients with tinnitus (23 TIN + HL and 15 TIN − HL) and 28 healthy controls (HCs) underwent rs-fMRI. Amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), regional homogeneity (ReHo), and bilateral auditory cortex (AC)-based functional connectivity (FC) were compared among groups via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc t tests. Correlations with clinical characteristics were examined via partial correlation.ResultsCompared with the HC group, both tinnitus groups exhibited decreased ALFF in the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), along with increased ALFF and decreased ReHo in the left middle occipital gyrus (MOG) [cluster-level family-wise error (FWE)-corrected P<0.05]. Additionally, the TIN + HL group showed decreased ALFF in the bilateral caudate nucleus (CN) and decreased ReHo in the right parahippocampal gyrus (PHG) and middle frontal gyrus (MFG), while the TIN − HL group exhibited decreased ReHo in the right rectus gyrus. Compared with the HC group, both tinnitus groups showed reduced FC between the right AC and the left superior temporal gyrus (STG), with more pronounced reduction in the TIN − HL group (P<0.05, Bonferroni correction). The ALFF in left MOG negatively correlated with the depression scores in the tinnitus groups (r=−0.513; P=0.001).ConclusionsHearing loss may differentially shape tinnitus-related brain reorganization. Although both subtypes shared common alterations in limbic and visual association regions, TIN + HL appears to be characterized by disruptions in sensory gating and cognitive control circuits, whereas TIN − HL may involve emotion-regulation network alterations and more pronounced auditory network disconnection. These findings provide a neuroimaging basis for subtype-specific treatment.

Introduction

Tinnitus, defined as the perception of sound in the absence of an external source, is a heterogeneous disorder affecting more than 740 million adults globally, with more than 120 million perceiving it as a severe problem (1). It frequently co-occurs with anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, and even an elevated risk of suicide (2), and approximately 10–20% of patients experience symptom severity sufficient to markedly reduce their quality of life (3). Despite an array of interventions being available, none of them are curative (4). These treatment challenges may be due to a limited understanding of the complex and heterogeneous etiologies of tinnitus, which hinders the development of personalized treatment strategies.

Because tinnitus is the most common pathology associated with hearing loss, it is commonly assumed that tinnitus is caused by peripheral hearing loss followed by variations in the central auditory pathways (4). However, tinnitus can also occur clinically in individuals with normal hearing. It is estimated that approximately 4.7% to 46% of children with normal audiograms may have tinnitus (4,5). Notably, clinical observations and epidemiological data indicate that tinnitus patients without hearing loss (TIN − HL) often exhibit more pronounced psychological or emotional disturbances (4), suggesting that the underlying neural mechanisms may fundamentally differ from those in tinnitus patients with hearing loss (TIN + HL). In recent years, neuroimaging studies have identified hearing loss as a trigger for maladaptive plasticity in the central auditory system, which includes tonotopic map reorganization, reduced inhibitory neurotransmission, and enhanced cross-modal inputs—changes widely considered to key contributors to the emergence of tinnitus. Conversely, in the absence of hearing loss, tinnitus may arise more from aberrant interactions between limbic and auditory networks (4,9). This suggests that the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying tinnitus may differ between the conditions with and without hearing loss—with each potentially involving distinct neural pathways—highlighting the need for a direct comparison between these two subgroups.

A few studies have revealed distinct patterns of brain reorganization between TIN + HL and TIN − HL (10-14). Regarding structure, voxel- and surface-based morphometry studies have identified differences between these subtypes in the limbic-related regions such as the parahippocampal gyrus (PHG) and insula (11,14), providing valuable insights into the neural mechanisms of TIN + HL and TIN − HL. However, structural alterations alone cannot capture the differences in neuronal activity that underlie the persistent perceptual experience of tinnitus in these two subgroups. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) offers a noninvasive tool for investigating spontaneous brain activity and has been widely used to examine the functional alterations in patients with tinnitus (15). However, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies on TIN + HL and TIN − HL have only focused on global connectivity or conducted seed-based analyses of subcortical regions such as the thalamus (10,13). Despite being the primary locus of plasticity related to both tinnitus and hearing loss, the auditory cortex (AC) remains under-investigated, and the differences in its functional network between the TIN + HL and TIN − HL subtypes have yet to be systematically characterized. Moreover, beyond connectivity-level analyses, potential differences in local neuronal activity—such as the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) and regional homogeneity (ReHo)—remain unexamined. These local metrics can effectively capture fundamental properties of spontaneous brain activity in the resting state (15) and provide critical insights into subgroup-specific neural reorganization mechanisms.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically investigate brain functional reorganization in TIN − HL and TIN + HL through the use of multimodal rs-fMRI analyses. ALFF, ReHo, and seed-based functional connectivity (FC) were assessed to capture local neural oscillations, regional synchronization, and AC network interactions, respectively. By investigating the shared and distinct patterns of brain reorganization in TIN − HL and TIN + HL, we sought to clarify the impact of hearing loss on tinnitus-related brain plasticity and to provide a neuroimaging-related foundation for personalized treatment based on tinnitus subtype. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1069/rc).

Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional case–control study was conducted at Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2017‐P2‐134‐01) and has been registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (No. NCT03764826). All participants were recruited into the study only after they had submitted written informed consent forms.

The inclusion criteria for participants with tinnitus were as follows: (I) right-handedness; (II) a subjective clinical diagnosis of tinnitus with no identifiable organic cause; (III) persistent tinnitus lasting 6 to 48 months; (IV) tinnitus frequency between 250 and 8,000 Hz; and (V) ability of the patient to cooperate with the clinician and complete magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) pulsatile tinnitus; (II) tinnitus symptoms resulting from otologic disorders, Ménière disease, surgical intervention, or trauma; (III) comorbid neurological or severe psychiatric conditions; and (IV) any contraindications to MRI. Healthy controls (HCs) were recruited and matched to patients with tinnitus in terms of age, gender, and handedness. All HCs had normal hearing (pure-tone average ≤25 dB hearing level across 0.25–8 kHz bilaterally) and no history of neurological or psychiatric illnesses, drug or alcohol abuse, or contraindications to MRI.

Clinical assessment

Pure-tone audiometry was administered by a licensed audiologist in accordance with standard clinical protocols. Air conduction thresholds were measured from 250 to 8,000 Hz. To maximize the sensitivity for the detection of subtle cochlear damage often associated with tinnitus (including the high-frequency range), hearing loss was defined as a threshold >25 dB hearing level at any single frequency in either ear. This criterion was selected due to the following: (I) early cochlear damage, particularly that associated with noise exposure or tinnitus, often presents as isolated threshold elevations at high frequencies before affecting the speech‑frequency average (16); and (II) using a single‑frequency threshold minimizes the risk of misclassifying individuals with subtle, localized cochlear dysfunction as TIN‑HL, thereby enhancing the biological contrast between the TIN + HL and TIN‑HL subgroups. For descriptive purposes, average hearing thresholds were calculated separately for low frequencies (500, 1,000, and 2,000 Hz) and high frequencies (4,000, 6,000, and 8,000 Hz) for each ear. The severity of hearing loss was determined by the higher value among these four average thresholds (12).

In addition, all participants underwent tinnitus matching and completed the Tinnitus Handicap Inventory (THI) (17). Levels of anxiety and depression were evaluated with the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) (18) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) (19), respectively.

MRI data acquisition

Imaging data were acquired with a 3.0-T MRI scanner (Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel phased-array head coil. Foam padding was used to restrict head motion, and earplugs were provided to reduce scanner noise. Axial fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences were first obtained to exclude visible brain abnormalities. For rs-fMRI, participants were asked to remain awake with eyes closed and to stay relaxed. Functional images were obtained with a gradient-echo single-shot echo-planar imaging sequence under the following parameters: repetition time/echo time =2,000/30 ms, field of view =224×224 mm2, flip angle =90°, matrix size =64×64, bandwidth =2,368 Hz/Px, slice thickness =3.5 mm, gap =1 mm, and voxel size =3.5×3.5×4.2 mm3. The rs-fMRI scan lasted 8.06 minutes and yielded 240 volumes.

Functional image processing

Preprocessing of the rs-fMRI data was conducted with Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI; http://www.rfmri.org/dpabi). The processing steps were as follows: (I) removal of the first 10 volumes to ensure signal stabilization; (II) slice timing correction to adjust for interleaved slice acquisition; (III) rigid-body motion correction (participants with translation >3.0 mm or rotation >3.0° were excluded); (IV) spatial normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template and resampling to 3×3×3 mm3; (V) spatial smoothing with a 6-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel; (VI) nuisance regression implemented with 26 covariates (including white-matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals) and 24 motion parameters to reduce physiological and motion-related artifacts; and (VII) temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) and linear detrending. ALFF, ReHo, and seed-based FC were then calculated.

Calculation and statistical analysis of ALFF and ReHo

ALFF was computed from the preprocessed data (steps 1–7). Subsequently, the time series of each voxel was transformed into the frequency domain via fast Fourier transform to obtain the power spectrum, and the square root of the power spectrum was averaged across 0.01–0.08 Hz to obtain the ALFF value. Each voxel’s ALFF was then standardized by dividing by the global mean ALFF. For ReHo, preprocessing included steps 1 to 4, 6, and 7. ReHo was quantified according to the Kendall coefficient of concordance (KCC), reflecting the temporal synchronization between a voxel and its 26 nearest neighbors. Individual ReHo maps were standardized by dividing each voxel’s KCC by the global mean KCC value, followed by spatial smoothing (as in step 5).

Intergroup differences in ALFF and ReHo were assessed via a general linear model in Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM12) based on MATLAB 2013b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), with age and gender serving as covariates. Following this, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed, and significance was set at P <0.05 after cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction.

Calculation and statistical analysis of FC

Following preprocessing steps 1 to 7, seed-based FC analysis was conducted with Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF) version 4.3 (http://www.restfmri.net/forum/DPARSF). Seeds were defined as bilateral AC encompassing Heschl’s gyrus and the superior temporal gyrus (STG) according to the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas (http://www.gin.cnrs.fr/AAL) (20). This anatomical definition, covering the primary (Heschl’s gyrus) and secondary auditory areas (STG) (21), has been widely used to represent the whole AC in seed-based FC analyses (22). Given that tinnitus-related alterations have been documented in both the primary and secondary auditory cortices (2), this combined region of Heschl’s gyrus and STG was selected—as opposed to restricting the seed to a single subregion—to capture the full extent of auditory network dysfunction. For each seed, the reference time course was obtained as the average of signals across all voxels within the region. Finally, correlation analyses were conducted between the seeds and the remaining brain voxels, and the resulting correlation coefficients were converted to z values via Fisher r-to-z transformation.

Intergroup differences in FC between the three groups were evaluated within the general linear model in SPM12, with age and gender serving as covariates (cluster-level FWE- corrected P<0.05). For clusters showing significant between-group differences, mean FC values were extracted with DPABI (http://www.rfmri.org/DPABI). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc t tests with Bonferroni correction (P<0.05) were then applied to compare groups, with age and gender being the covariates (23).

Correlation analyses

Finally, the average ALFF, ReHo, and FC values in brain regions with significant between-group differences were extracted via DPABI. To determine the correlations between the values of brain MRI features and the clinical characteristics (including the THI score, SAS score, and SDS score) in patients with tinnitus, partial correlation analyses were calculated with SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) after age and gender were controlled for. To correct for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied, with the corrected significance threshold calculated as 0.05 divided by the total number of comparisons.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In this study, 15 TIN − HL (7 males; age 39.00±14.127 years), 23 TIN + HL (11 males, 40.52±12.143 years), and 28 HCs (13 males; age 39.46±10.668 years) were included. All patients experienced tinnitus symptoms with a duration ranging from 6 to 48 months. No significant differences were found between the three groups in terms of age (one-way ANOVA, P=0.919) or gender (chi-squared, P=0.995). Furthermore, there were no significant differences between the TIN + HL and TIN‑HL groups in terms of THI score (two-sample t-test, P=0.165), SAS score (Mann-Whitney U test, P=0.686), SDS score (two-sample t-test, P=0.890), or the lateralization of tinnitus (chi-squared, P=0.889). Abnormal brain functional changes in the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups

Compared with the HC group, the TIN + HL group had significantly decreased ALFF in the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and caudate nucleus (CN) but increased ALFF in the left middle occipital gyrus (MOG); meanwhile, the TIN − HL group had significantly decreased ALFF in the bilateral ACC and increased ALFF in the left MOG (Figure 1 and Table 2). Compared with the HC group, the TIN + HL group had significantly decreased ReHo in the right PHG, middle frontal gyrus (MFG), rectus gyrus, and the left MOG; meanwhile, the TIN − HL group significantly decreased ReHo in the right rectus gyrus and the left MOG (Figure 2 and Table 2). Direct comparison between the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups revealed no significant differences in ALFF or ReHo.

Correlation analyses

A total of 11 brain regions showed significant between-group differences. Measurements from these regions were correlated with three clinical scales (THI, SAS, and SDS), yielding 33 comparisons. After Bonferroni correction (corrected threshold: P<0.05/33=0.0015), there was a statistically significant, moderate negative correlation between the ALFF value in the left MOG and SDS scores among patients with tinnitus (r=−0.513; P=0.001; Figure 4) but no significant associations for other factors. Moreover, no significant correlations were found between other brain alterations and clinical characteristics.

Discussion

In this study, we employed multimodal rs-fMRI to compare TIN + HL and TIN − HL, aiming to determine if hearing status modulates tinnitus-related brain alteration. Both the TIN + HL and TIN − HL subgroups had abnormalities in the ACC and MOG, suggesting the presence of core mechanisms operating independently of peripheral hearing status. However, the TIN + HL group showed disruptions in the striatal-limbic-prefrontal circuits associated with sensory gating and cognitive control, whereas the TIN − HL group exhibited rectus gyrus dysfunction and more pronounced auditory network disconnection. These distinct patterns may offer a neuroimaging basis for understanding the role of hearing loss in tinnitus-related neural reorganization and could inform subtype-specific therapeutic strategies.

Tinnitus-related dysfunction independent of hearing status

Our study revealed several alterations shared by both the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups, suggesting tinnitus-related neural reorganization that may extend beyond peripheral auditory pathology. Compared with the HC group, both tinnitus groups exhibited decreased ALFF in the bilateral ACC, along with increased ALFF and decreased ReHo in the left MOG. The ACC is a core limbic region implicated in emotional processing and network connectivity among patients with tinnitus (4). According to the noise cancellation model, the ACC exerts inhibitory control over auditory pathways to suppress phantom percepts, and its dysfunction may allow tinnitus signals to reach conscious awareness (24). Consistent with this, studies have reported abnormalities in the ACC of patients with tinnitus regardless of hearing status (4,24).

Notably, both the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups demonstrated increased ALFF alongside decreased ReHo in the left MOG, which may suggest a state of desynchronized hyperexcitability, in which heightened activity lacks coordinated patterning. The MOG is primarily responsible for visual information processing. Patients with tinnitus often exhibit abnormalities in the visual network, reflecting underlying cross-modal plasticity (25-28). The presence of this alteration in both the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups suggests the possibility that such cross-modal changes do not merely compensate for hearing loss and could also constitute a core feature of tinnitus-related sensory processing.

Taken together, these shared alterations in the ACC and MOG suggest that tinnitus, independent of hearing status, may involve impaired top-down modulation of auditory perception and heightened cross-modal plasticity, potentially reflecting core mechanisms that extend beyond peripheral auditory pathology. Furthermore, these resting-state metrics may serve as sensitive biomarkers for tracking tinnitus-related brain reorganization across different etiologies (29).

Hearing status as a modulator of divergent neural pathways in tinnitus

Beyond these common alterations, our findings revealed marked differences between the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups, suggesting that the presence or absence of hearing loss may direct tinnitus-related plasticity along divergent neural pathways. Compared with the HC group, the TIN + HL group exhibited decreased ALFF in the bilateral CN and decreased ReHo in the right PHG and MFG. As a central striatal region critical for sensory gating, the CN has been proposed to be a gatekeeper that filters auditory signals in the noise-cancellation system (30). Reduced ALFF in the CN of patients in the TIN + HL group may reflect impaired gating, which would allow aberrant auditory signals to reach awareness. The PHG is a limbic region critical for emotional contextualization and memory encoding, and reduced ReHo in the PHG may contribute to tinnitus persistence in TIN + HL through disrupted contextual gating (31,32), a function distinct from direct emotional regulation (33,34). The MFG is part of the prefrontal cortex and is typically involved in higher-order cognition (35-37), and reduced ReHo in the MFG may reflect impaired cognitive control over tinnitus perception. It should be noted, however, that the subgroup-specific patterns described below are based on each subgroup’s deviation from the HC group rather than on direct statistical differences between the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups, as direct comparisons for ALFF and ReHo did not yield significant differences.

Several of the abnormalities in the TIN + HL group were not observed in the TIN − HL group, which is consistent with the notion that the presence of hearing loss may impose a distinct pathophysiological load on the extended circuitry. Instead, TIN − HL had a decreased ReHo in the right rectus gyrus compared with the HCs, a change not observed in TIN + HL. The rectus gyrus, a prefrontal region implicated in emotional regulation (38,39), may modulate emotional responses in a top-down fashion via limbic connections (38)—a function that may differ from the contextual role of the PHG (33,34). Although we did not observe significant differences in anxiety or depression scores between the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups in our cohort, clinical observations and epidemiological data have shown that individuals with TIN − HL often exhibit more pronounced psychological or emotional disturbances (4,26). Reduced ReHo in the rectus gyrus could serve as a neural substrate for this clinical phenomenon, raising the possibility that impaired direct emotional regulation—rather than the disruption in tinnitus signal processing seen in TIN + HL—may underlie the greater affective burden in this subgroup. However, this interpretation remains speculative and should be validated in future studies. It is important to emphasize that the subgroup-specific patterns described above represent different deviation patterns from those of HCs rather than statistically confirmed differences between the two tinnitus subgroups. Notably, direct comparisons between the TIN + HL and TIN − HL groups revealed no significant differences in ALFF or ReHo, indicating that the observed differential patterns primarily reflect within-subgroup deviations from the HC group rather than robust intersubgroup differences. These results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Additionally, both the TIN − HL and TIN + HL groups exhibited reduced FC between the right AC and left STG as compared to the HC group, with a significantly greater reduction in the TIN − HL group. The STG serves as a core region of the AC and is critically involved in the perception and maintenance of tinnitus. Reduced FC between the right AC and contralateral auditory association areas could disrupt integration within the auditory network, potentially contributing to phantom percepts. Interestingly, this reduction was more pronounced in the TIN − HL group, which may seem counterintuitive, as hearing loss is generally expected to cause greater network disruption. This finding may be interpreted as evidence of divergent mechanisms: in TIN − HL, tinnitus likely stems from intrinsic central dysconnectivity, whereas in TIN + HL, relatively preserved connectivity may reflect compensatory plasticity triggered by peripheral deafferentation, with hearing loss driving both maladaptive and adaptive reorganization.

Correlation analyses

This study identified a weak but significant negative correlation between the ALFF values in the left MOG and the SDS scores across all patients with tinnitus, suggesting the possibility that enhanced neural activity in the MOG may serve a protective effect against depressive symptoms in patients with tinnitus. This negative correlation, together with the observed pattern (increased ALFF and decreased ReHo), suggests that cross-modal processing could potentially help mitigate emotional burden among individuals with tinnitus. Effective recruitment of visual regions to compensate for abnormal auditory processing may be associated with reduced affective burden in patients with tinnitus. However, the correlation coefficient was relatively low, which may be due to the relatively small sample size in our study.

Limitations

Several limitations to this study should be acknowledged. First, the design was cross-sectional, and longitudinal studies are needed to validate our findings. Second, the small size of the TIN − HL subgroup (n=15) might have limited the statistical power to detect subtle subgroup-specific alterations, increasing the risk of type II errors; these findings should be considered preliminary and require replication in larger cohorts. Third, our sensitive hearing loss criterion (>25 dB hearing loss at any single frequency), while enhancing the biological contrast between subgroups, might have included patients with mild isolated high-frequency elevations into the TIN + HL group, potentially limiting generalizability to pure-tone average–based definitions. Fourth, we could not exclude undetected hidden hearing loss in TIN − HL, as extended high-frequency audiometry and distortion-product otoacoustic emission were not performed. Fifth, the cross‑modal interactions indicated by our MOG results need to be further examined with specific experimental tasks for audiovisual integration.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that hearing status may modulate the neural reorganization associated with tinnitus. TIN + HL exhibit functional abnormalities in circuits associated with sensory gating, memory, and cognitive control, whereas TIN − HL show selective alterations in emotion-regulatory regions and more pronounced auditory network disruptions. These distinct neural signatures may help explain the greater emotional distress in TIN − HL despite their intact peripheral hearing. Our results highlight the importance of considering hearing status in tinnitus research and clinical practice and suggest that optimal therapeutic strategies may differ between subtypes—specifically, a focus on sensory and memory circuits in TIN + HL and emotion-related networks in TIN − HL. Future studies incorporating longitudinal imaging, task-based fMRI, and multimodal auditory assessments will be essential to validating these subgroup-specific neural mechanisms and to facilitating translation of these imaging findings into clinical practice.
