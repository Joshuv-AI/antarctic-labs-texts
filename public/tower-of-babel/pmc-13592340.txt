Fetal Copy Number Variant Detection in Pregnancies with Ultrasound Soft Markers: A Maternal Age-Stratified Retrospective Study
Authors: Shuxian Huang, Lifang Lin, Lingna She, Lina Liu, Heming Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrenatal ultrasound soft markers are common indicators for fetal genetic screening, while the correlation between maternal age and fetal copy number variant (CNV) detection remains controversial. This study aimed to analyze CNV characteristics in fetuses with ultrasound soft marker abnormalities and explore the influence of maternal age on CNV detection.MethodsA total of 468 fetuses with abnormal ultrasound soft markers were enrolled and divided into <35 years (n=390) and ≥35 years (n=78) groups based on maternal age. Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was performed for the enrolled fetuses. Fetal pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) CNVs and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were detected and statistically analyzed.ResultsThe overall detection rates of P/LP CNVs and VUS were 4.1% and 9.0%, respectively. Notably, the P/LP CNVs detection rate was significantly higher in women aged <35 years (4.9%) than in those aged ≥35 years (0%, p=0.047). The CNVs detection rate was 14.0% in fetuses with a single soft marker abnormality and 7.5% in those with ≥2 abnormal soft markers; the difference did not reach statistical significance (p=0.138). No significant inter-group difference was found in VUS detection rate and CNV detection rates of different types or numbers of soft markers (all p>0.05). In cases with single ultrasound soft marker abnormality, The CNV detection rate was the highest in cases with pyelic separation (n=19), reaching 26.3%; echogenic bowel (n=14) ranked second with a detection rate of 21.4%.ConclusionAs a single‑center retrospective study with underpowered subgroup analyses, our findings suggest that younger pregnant women with ultrasonic soft‑marker abnormalities may still carry risk of fetal pathogenic CNVs. CMA should be considered in pregnancies with ultrasonic soft marker abnormalities after individualized counseling, regardless of maternal age.Keywords: chromosomal microarray analysis, copy number variant, maternal age, prenatal diagnosis, ultrasound soft marker

Introduction

In prenatal diagnosis, ultrasound screening is routinely used to preliminarily assess fetal physical condition and detect potential abnormalities.1,2 Prenatal ultrasound soft markers refer to transient and non-specific subtle abnormal imaging manifestations on prenatal ultrasound, without obvious anatomical organic malformations in the fetus.3 These markers include increased nuchal translucency (NT) thickness, nasal bone hypoplasia, echogenic intracardiac focus, choroid plexus cysts, short long bones, pyelic separation, ventriculomegaly, echogenic bowel, and single umbilical artery, among others.4 Most of these markers do not have clear pathological significance and may resolve spontaneously with advancing gestational age.5 However, they are potentially associated with fetal chromosomal aneuploidies, genomic copy number variants (CNVs), and long-term neurodevelopmental abnormalities, serving as crucial ultrasound clues for prenatal genetic risk screening.3 At present, prenatal clinical practice has regarded ultrasound soft markers as an important auxiliary basis for fetal genetic evaluation.6

CNV refers to deletions, duplications, insertions, or complex multi-copy alterations of DNA fragments longer than 1 kilobase (kb) in the genome.7 Widely distributed across the entire human genome, CNV represents a major form of genomic structural variation.8 CNVs can affect embryonic development and human physiological functions through multiple molecular mechanisms, including altering gene dosage, disrupting the intact structure of genes, and regulating the expression of adjacent genes.9,10 They are important genetic causes of hereditary diseases, fetal congenital malformations, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, and a variety of complex disorders.11,12 Pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) CNVs are genetic causes of fetal developmental abnormalities, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and neonatal congenital diseases.13,14 By performing whole-genome scanning, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) is capable of identifying CNVs within small chromosomal fragments at a high resolution.15,16 With the widespread application of CMA in prenatal diagnosis, an increasing number of subtle fetal CNVs have been identified, greatly improving the detection rate of submicroscopic chromosomal abnormalities that cannot be detected by conventional karyotyping.17

At present, most existing research primarily evaluates the overall correlation between ultrasound soft markers and fetal CNV abnormalities.18,19 Few studies systematically distinguish isolated single soft marker anomalies from multiple soft marker abnormalities, despite their distinct clinical risk implications: multiple soft marker presentations generally confer higher overall chromosomal aberration risk,20,21 while isolated soft markers remain ambiguous in genetic risk stratification across different maternal age cohorts. Additionally, prior studies focusing on maternal age-related genomic risk largely regard advanced maternal age (≥35 years) as the dominant risk factor for fetal genetic aberrations,21,22 with inconsistent conclusions regarding pathogenic CNV prevalence in younger pregnant women presenting ultrasound soft markers.23,24 Stratified comparisons of P/LP CNV detection rates between women aged <35 and ≥35 years within soft marker cohorts remain insufficient, and many previous cohorts lack balanced sample distribution across age subgroups, limiting robust clinical inference.19,25 Furthermore, few studies have comprehensively stratified CNV detection outcomes by both maternal age and the number of abnormal ultrasound soft markers, nor quantified the varying diagnostic yield of distinct soft marker subtypes across age groups.19,25 This study aimed to analyze CNV detection in fetuses with abnormal ultrasonic soft markers. Stratified analyses stratified by maternal age, type of ultrasonic soft markers and the count of abnormal markers were conducted to explore the impact of maternal age on CNVs among these fetuses.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort

This retrospective observational study consecutively enrolled a total of 468 pregnant women who received prenatal genetic testing due to fetal ultrasonographic soft marker abnormalities in Meizhou People’s Hospital from October 2022 to December 2025. All enrolled participants underwent karyotype analysis and CMA with complete clinical and ultrasonic data. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) singleton pregnancy; (2) presence of one or more fetal ultrasound soft marker abnormalities during prenatal ultrasound screening; (3) complete gestational and clinical baseline information; and (4) available fetal genetic detection results. Exclusion criteria: (1) the pregnant woman has a family history of chromosomal abnormalities; (2) fetuses structural malformations, fetal growth restriction, or abnormal amniotic fluid volume; (3) cases confirmed to have chromosomal aneuploidies based on karyotyping; and (4) enrolled pregnant individuals had clear exposure to teratogenic agents including ionizing radiation and chemical toxicants during gestation. All procedures of this research were reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Meizhou People’s Hospital. The flowchart of this study.

All enrolled fetuses underwent a complete detailed anatomical ultrasound scan performed by certified senior maternal-fetal medicine specialists to comprehensively evaluate organ development and exclude definite fetal structural malformations. The soft marker presentations were categorized into two groups for stratified analysis: isolated single soft marker abnormality (only one type of ultrasound soft marker identified, without additional minor sonographic aberrations or structural defects) and multiple soft marker abnormalities (two or more distinct soft markers detected simultaneously).

Sample Collection and Processing

Prenatal samples were collected in accordance with standard prenatal sampling protocols, including amniotic fluid, villus, and umbilical cord blood specimens. Sampling gestational age was categorized into three subgroups: first trimester (≤13 weeks), second trimester (14–28 weeks), and third trimester (>28 weeks). All samples were strictly stored, transported, and processed in a standardized laboratory environment to ensure DNA integrity and avoid contamination. Genomic DNA was extracted from fetal specimens using commercial DNA extraction kits, and DNA concentration and purity were detected to meet the requirements of subsequent microarray detection.

CNV Detection and Classification

Genomic DNA was isolated from fetal specimens and digested with the NspI restriction enzyme to generate short DNA fragments. Following biotin-labeled amplification, the DNA samples were fully blended with hybridization solution and denatured. The processed samples were then loaded onto the Affymetrix Cytoscan 750k chip for hybridization, washing, and staining. Subsequent chip scanning was performed to acquire raw data, which was imported into GenomeStudio software for systematic analysis and result interpretation.

All obtained results were aligned with international genomic variation databases. The pathogenicity of identified gene-related CNVs was assessed based on multiple authoritative databases, including the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen), Database of Genomic Variation and Phenotype in Humans using Ensembl Resources (DECIPHER), Database of Genomic Variants (DGV), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), and PubMed literature database. In accordance with the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG), CNVs were classified into five categories of clinical significance: pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variants of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign (LB), and benign (B) CNVs.26,27

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software. Count data were expressed as case numbers and percentages. The differences in the detection rates of P/LP CNVs and VUS between different maternal age groups, different types, and different numbers of ultrasonographic soft markers were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No prospective sample size power calculation was performed prior to participant enrollment, given the retrospective nature of this clinical cohort. Multiple exploratory subgroup comparisons stratified by maternal age, soft marker category and number of abnormal soft markers were conducted in this study, and correction for multiple testing was not applied. All subgroup comparisons are regarded as exploratory analyses. We attempted multivariable regression adjusting for soft marker type, number of soft markers, gestational age at sampling, and adverse pregnancy history, but model convergence failed due to the limited total number of P/LP CNV positive cases and zero positive events in the advanced maternal age subgroup.

Regarding the gestational age at sample collection, most samples were obtained during the second trimester: 349 cases (74.6%) were sampled at 14–28 weeks of gestation, followed by 70 cases (15.0%) at ≤13 weeks, and 49 cases (10.5%) at >28 weeks. For fetal sample types, amniotic fluid specimens accounted for the vast majority of samples (398 cases, 85.0%), followed by villus samples (69 cases, 14.7%), with only one case (0.2%) of umbilical cord blood sample collected.

Overall Detection Status of CNVs

In this study, P/LP CNVs were identified in 19 fetuses (4.1%), and VUS were detected in 42 fetuses (9.0%). No P/LP CNVs or VUS were found in the remaining 407 fetuses (87.0%) (Figure 2). We further characterized the spectrum of detected P/LP CNVs. The identified pathogenic variants mainly involved classic microdeletion/microduplication syndromes including 15q11.2 deletion syndrome, and X-linked pathogenic copy number alterations. The sizes of these pathogenic fragments ranged from 112 Kb to 46.15 Mb, and each variant contained multiple core dosage-sensitive genes closely associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, congenital malformations and intellectual disability (Supplementary Table 1).

For single ultrasound soft marker abnormalities, subgroup analysis showed no statistically significant differences in the combined detection rates of P/LP CNVs and VUS between the two maternal age groups for all marker types (all p>0.05). Typical single markers including increased NT thickness (14.8% vs 13.0%, p=0.822), ventriculomegaly (11.6% vs 14.3%, p=0.834), and nasal bone hypoplasia (9.4% vs 12.5%, p=0.786) exhibited comparable variant detection rates regardless of maternal age. In addition, no significant age-based differences were observed in cases with choroid plexus cyst, short long bones, pyelic separation, and other rare soft marker abnormalities (all p>0.05).

In terms of the number of abnormal ultrasonic items, the detection rate of P/LP CNVs and VUS in cases with single ultrasonic abnormality was 14.8% in the <35 years group and 8.5% in the ≥35 years group, with no statistical difference (p=0.158). For multiple ultrasonic abnormalities, the variant detection rate was 6.5% in the younger maternal age group, while no variants were detected in the advanced age group, and the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.490).

Discussion

Ultrasonic soft markers are subtle non-structural fetal abnormalities identified during routine prenatal ultrasound, serving as critical warning signs for potential submicroscopic chromosomal defects and fetal CNVs.19,28 Conventional karyotype can only detect macroscopic chromosomal aneuploidies and large structural rearrangements, whereas CMA enables genome-wide detection of microdeletions and microduplications, greatly improving the diagnostic yield of fetal genetic variations in high-risk pregnancies with soft marker anomalies.17,29,30 In clinical practice, ultrasound soft markers are frequently encountered in prenatal screening, yet their genetic significance and optimal prenatal diagnostic strategies remain inconsistent across different populations.30,31 The present study systematically analyzed fetal P/LP CNVs and VUS in a cohort of 468 pregnant women with fetal ultrasonic soft marker abnormalities, with a particular focus on maternal age stratification, aiming to provide refined clinical evidence for individualized prenatal genetic counseling.

In this study, the overall detection rates of fetal P/LP CNVs and VUS were 4.1% and 9.0%, respectively, which aligns with previous studies focusing on prenatal soft marker populations.30,32,33 Of course, there are also certain differences from the results of some studies.20,34 The vast majority of cases (87.0%) presented no detectable CNV abnormalities, confirming that isolated ultrasonic soft markers are generally benign phenotypic variants with low overall pathogenic genetic risk.28,35 Among all ultrasound soft marker subtypes, increased NT thickness, ventriculomegaly, nasal bone hypoplasia, and choroid plexus cyst were the most prevalent anomalies,15,30,36 while pyelic separation yielded the highest CNV detection rate at 26.3% followed by echogenic bowel with a detection rate of 21.4%. These findings support the consensus that these ultrasonic markers are more closely correlated with fetal submicroscopic genetic disorders and should be regarded as key indicators triggering further genetic assessment in prenatal clinical practice. The research conducted by Pan et al revealed that the detection rates of P/LP CNVs were high for thickened nuchal fold, single umbilical artery, mild ventriculomegaly, and nasal bone dysplasia.37 Lu et al reported that CNV was mainly observed in cases of increased NT thickness.38

The most compelling finding of the current study is the distinct difference in P/LP CNV prevalence between different maternal age groups. Traditionally, advanced maternal age (≥35 years) has been universally recognized as the dominant risk factor for fetal chromosomal abnormalities, while younger pregnant women are generally considered low-risk populations in prenatal genetic evaluation.39 Clinically, genetic testing is often selectively recommended for advanced-age pregnancies, whereas soft marker anomalies in younger women are frequently underestimated. Larroya et al suggested that the presence of pathogenic CNVs is not associated with paternal age or maternal age.40 Our stratified analysis demonstrated that the fetal P/LP CNVs detection rate in women aged <35 years reached 4.9%, while no pathogenic CNVs were identified in the advanced maternal age group, and this difference achieved statistical significance (p=0.047). These results indicate that young pregnant women are also at risk of fetal CNV in the presence of abnormal ultrasonic soft markers; therefore, CMA testing may be considered for this group of pregnant women. Of note, the between‑group difference in P/LP CNV detection yielded a borderline p value of 0.047. Coupled with the limited sample size of the advanced‑maternal‑age subgroup, this observation should be regarded as suggestive rather than definitive. It is worth emphasising that, in contrast to chromosomal aneuploidies strongly linked to advancing maternal age,41,42 CNVs represent structural genomic rearrangements whose aetiology is not primarily dependent on maternal age.43,44 Therefore, further multicenter and large-sample studies are required to validate these findings.

In this study, no significant inter-group difference was observed in VUS detection rates between younger and advanced-age pregnant women (9.2% vs 7.7%, p>0.05). As VUS represent genetic variants with unconfirmed clinical pathogenicity, they cannot directly guide pregnancy outcome judgment or clinical intervention.45 The consistent VUS distribution across age groups suggests that the elevated pathogenic CNV risk in younger pregnant women is a specific clinical phenomenon rather than a random variation of uncertain genetic alterations.46 Moreover, further subgroup analysis stratified by the type and number of ultrasound soft markers revealed no significant age-related differences in CNV detection for either single or multiple soft marker abnormalities. These results imply that fetal CNV occurrence associated with ultrasound soft markers is modulated by multifactorial interactions, including genetic polymorphism, embryonic development status, and environmental factors, rather than being simply determined by maternal age.

This study provides valuable clinical implications for prenatal genetic counseling and diagnostic strategy optimization. Current clinical guidelines tend to prioritize advanced maternal age as the major indication for invasive genetic testing, potentially leading to insufficient attention and undertesting for younger pregnant women with isolated soft marker abnormalities. Based on our findings, we strongly recommend that CMA detection should be routinely offered to all pregnant individuals with fetal ultrasound soft marker anomalies, regardless of maternal age. Universal genetic screening for such high-risk ultrasound phenotypes can effectively avoid missed diagnosis of pathogenic CNVs in young populations, thereby improving the accuracy of prenatal genetic risk assessment and enabling more precise individualized pregnancy counseling.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, it should also be noted that all participants in this study were referred for invasive prenatal diagnosis owing to fetal ultrasound soft‑marker abnormalities, which introduces a selection bias. The CNV detection rates observed here should be extrapolated to the general obstetric population with great caution selection bias and limit the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, this study only focused on the detection rate of fetal CNV variants and lacked long-term follow-up data on neonatal developmental outcomes, making it impossible to further validate the prognostic value of detected variants. Third, this study has several statistical limitations that deserve acknowledgment. Of note, this higher detection rate of P/LP CNVs among women aged < 35 years was observed specifically within our cohort of pregnancies with fetal ultrasound soft‑marker abnormalities. Given the absence of multivariable adjustment in the present study, we cannot conclude that younger maternal age itself acts as an independent risk factor for pathogenic CNVs; this represents an observed association rather than proven causation. In addition, the zero-rate of P/LP CNVs among the advanced‑maternal‑age subgroup (n = 78) should be interpreted with great caution, as it is likely caused by limited sample size and low statistical power rather than a true absence of pathogenic CNV risk in this population. Moreover, multiple uncorrected subgroup comparisons across soft marker subtypes may increase the risk of type I error; all subgroup findings should be considered exploratory and require external validation in larger cohorts. Future large-sample, multicenter prospective studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to validate our findings, further clarify the age-related genetic risk discrepancies in soft marker pregnancies, and establish more standardized prenatal diagnostic protocols for this specific high-risk population.

Conclusion

This study explored fetal CNV features in pregnancies with ultrasound soft marker abnormalities. Notably, younger pregnant women (<35 years) exhibited a significantly higher rate of fetal pathogenic CNVs than advanced-age cases, while VUS detection was comparable between groups. Clinically, abnormal soft markers indicate a potential risk of CNV, which differs from maternal age-related chromosomal aneuploidy, regardless of maternal age. CMA should be considered in pregnancies with ultrasound soft marker abnormalities after individualized counseling, regardless of maternal age, particularly when high-risk marker types are present.
