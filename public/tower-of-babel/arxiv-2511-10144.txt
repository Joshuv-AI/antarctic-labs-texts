The maximum diameter of 2 2 -dimensional simplicial complexes
Olaf Parczyk, Silas Rathke, Tibor Szabó

Abstract
We study a problem of Santos about the largest possible diameter of a d d -dimensional (abstract) simplicial complex on n n vertices. For dimension 2 2 , we determine the exact value of the maximum for every n n using an explicit construction. We also come across a tantalizing open problem about the packing of squares of Hamilton cycles in the complete graph and obtain an infinite sequence of tight explicit constructions.

A

If the maximum size of a set in

The

The

Let

\Omega\!\left(n^{\frac{2d+2}{3}}\right)\leq H_{s}(n,d)\leq\frac{1}{d}\binom{n}{d}.

(1)

Subsequently, Criado and Santos
[
8
]
gave an explicit construction of simplicial

-complexes whose diameter matched the order of magnitude of the upper bound for every fixed

and an infinite sequence of

.
Criado and Newman
[

]
used the Lovász Local Lemma to establish the existence of a construction for any fixed

and all

Most recently Bohman and Newman

\left(\frac{1}{d}-(\log n)^{-\varepsilon}\right)\binom{n}{d}\leq H_{s}(n,d)\,,

(2)

where

and

The upper bound in (

between any two vertices in the dual graph, each vertex (corresponding to a
(d+1)
-set) contains

sets of size

that are not contained in any previously visited vertex as this would create a shortcut. Hence, if

H_{s}(n,d)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{1}{d}\binom{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{d}\right\rfloor.

(3)

The main result of this paper is a matching lower bound for

for every

Theorem 1.1
.

For

H_{s}(n,2)=\begin{cases}\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right\rfloor&n\neq 6\\
5=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{2}\binom{6}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\right\rfloor-1&n=6.\end{cases}

-complexes that have this diameter. It is worth noting that the previously best known lower bound

For example, any ordering of the vertex set provides such a sequence, and the pairs appearing at distance at most two in the sequence form the square

But this is only a linear number of pairs however, while in an optimal trail-square we are aiming to cover (almost) all the pairs.
Our initial approach to this problem was to create a large family of pairwise edge-disjoint squares of Hamilton paths and “glue” them up into a trail-square with the introduction of a new vertex or two at each connection.
How much these new vertices can be reused at more than one connection might depend on the ends of the squares of Hamilton paths being “well-distributed”.
To gain flexibility in that regard, a decomposition of the edges of the complete graph into squares of Hamilton cycles proves to be quite handy, since each can then be “cut” anywhere to obtain a family of pairwise edge-disjoint squares of Hamilton paths with a good variety of endings.
This leads us to the following natural

For which positive integers

and, thus, this will be a circular simplicial complex.

Proposition 2.4

Let

By Observation

Therefore, the existence of a generating sequence will already be sufficient to prove Theorem

for

.
We note that from a generating sequence we could also directly construct the simplicial complex, but we think that this intermediate step simplifies the arguments and is also helpful for the reader in the remainder of this section to understand the constructions.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

By construction, the circular simplicial complex can only contain edges whose residue appears in the generating sequence.
Let
\{u,v\}\in\binom{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}{2}
be an edge whose residue is covered by the generating sequence.
By
(iii)
of Definition
2.2
, there is a unique

such that
u-v
or
v-u
is
a_{\ell}
,
a_{\ell}+a_{\ell+1}
and
\ell\not\in I
, or
a_{\ell-1}+a_{\ell}+a_{\ell+1}
and
\ell\in I
.
W.l.o.g. we can assume that it is

.

Let us first consider the case that

.
The simplicial complex contains the edges

\{x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1}\},\{x_{m+\ell},x_{m+\ell+1}\},\dots,\{x_{(n-1)m+\ell},x_{(n-1)m+\ell+1}\}.

By definition of

, each of these edges has the same residue as the edge
\{u,v\}
.
Since there are just
n
edges with this residue, it is enough to show that these edges are pairwise distinct.
Then we know that
\{u,v\}
must be among these edges.

Suppose there are distinct

\{x_{s_{1}m+\ell},x_{s_{1}m+\ell+1}\}=\{x_{s_{2}m+\ell},x_{s_{2}m+\ell+1}\}

First, we will show that

and
x_{s_{1}m+\ell+1}=x_{s_{2}m+\ell+1}

x_{s_{1}m+\ell}=x_{s_{2}m+\ell+1}

x_{s_{1}m+\ell+1}-x_{s_{1}m+\ell}=a_{\ell}=x_{s_{2}m+\ell+1}-x_{s_{2}m+\ell}

which is a contradiction since

Thus, we can conclude

of Definition

Thus,

Hence,

must appear in the simplicial complex.

The case that

which are all distinct and have endpoints which differ by

The final case

2.4
The Construction for
n=4k+4

It is not possible to find a generating sequence with no missing residues when
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, because for the former we need the number of non-zero elements in
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
to be divisible by 4.
Instead, we will give an optimal construction for the case

by extending a generating sequence for
n^{\prime}=4k+1
.
Note that when
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
,

is even, and, hence, we again have one edge to spare.

We let the vertex set be

where

are three distinct symbols.
By the previous section, we can find a generating sequence for

and get a circular simplicial complex that covers all edges of
\binom{\mathbb{Z}/n^{\prime}\mathbb{Z}}{2}
.
But then the edges left uncovered form a

and a triangle on the smaller side
(i.e. the part of the bipartition of size 3)
. This would be a linear loss compared to the upper bound and there would be no “space” to extend the simplicial complex significantly since every new triangle must intersect
\{a,b,c\}
in two vertices.
Therefore, we will instead find a generating sequence that misses two residues

and

except for two Hamilton cycles.
Now the remaining edges form a
K_{n^{\prime},3}
together with two cycles of length
n^{\prime}
on the larger side
(i.e. the part of the bipartition of size
n^{\prime}
)
and a triangle on the smaller side. As it turns out, this already provides enough “wiggle room” to extend the circular simplicial complex to cover all edges but one.
For this, we need to be able to cut the circular complex in an appropriate way. The generating sequence for which this is possible is provided by the next lemma.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Lemma 3.1
.

If

p\equiv 5\mod 8
is a prime, then
\ord_{p}(2)
is divisible by 4.

Proof.

This is a well-known fact. If
p\equiv 5\mod 8
, the Second Supplement to Law of Quadratic Reciprocity
[
15
, Proposition 5.1.3]
implies that

2^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\equiv-1\mod p\,.

Hence,
\ord_{p}(2)
does not divide
the even number
\frac{p-1}{2}=:2\ell
, yet by Lagrange’s Theorem, it does divide
p-1=4\ell
. Thus,
\ord_{p}(2)
must be
divisible by 4.
∎

Note that Lemma
3.1
is not a characterization of those primes for which
\ord_{p}(2)
is divisible by four and there are more, e.g. 17.
We are not aware of any other easy criteria that guarantee this, let alone a full characterization.

The idea in the proof of Theorem
1.3
is that the pairs
(x,2x)
form cycles divisible by four, i.e. we can find a matching which then forms our square of Hamilton cycles.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We show that there are
\ell=\tfrac{p-1}{4}
elements
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
such that the squares of Hamilton cycles which are given by the vertex ordering

\big(0,a_{i},2a_{i},3a_{i},\dots,(p-1)a_{i}\big)

for
i\in[\ell]
are pairwise edge-disjoint and, therefore, partition the edges of
K_{p}
.

Consider the normal (multiplicative) subgroup
H=\left\langle 2\right\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
. It partitions
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
into left cosets
a\cdot H
with
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
and each coset has a size of
\left\lvert a\cdot H\right\rvert=\left\lvert H\right\rvert=\ord_{p}(2)
which is divisible by 4. Notice that
2^{\ord_{p}(2)/2}=-1

in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
because
2^{\ord_{p}(2)/2}
is a root of
X^{2}-1
, but it cannot be 1 by the minimality of
\ord_{p}(2)
. Thus, for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
and
k\in\{0,\dots,\ord_{p}(2)/2-1\}
, we get

a\cdot 2^{k+\ord_{p}(2)/2}=-a\cdot 2^{k}.

Let
R=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
be a set of representatives of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}/H
. Then

\left\{a\cdot 2^{k},-a\cdot 2^{k}|a\in R,0\leq k\leq\ord_{p}(2)/2-1\right\}

contains each element of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
exactly once.

Let’s view the vertices of
K_{p}
as elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Given an
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ast}
and a
0\leq k\leq\ord_{p}(2)/2-1
, we define the square of a Hamilton cycle
H_{a,k}
via the vertex ordering

\big(0\cdot a\cdot 2^{k},1\cdot a\cdot 2^{k},2\cdot a\cdot 2^{k},\dots,(p-1)\cdot a\cdot 2^{k}\big).

As
a\cdot 2^{k}
is coprime to
p
, every vertex appears in this ordering exactly once. Thus,
H_{a,k}
contains all edges
uv
with

u-v\in\big\{a\cdot 2^{k},-a\cdot 2^{k},a\cdot 2^{k+1},-a\cdot 2^{k+1}\big\}.

Therefore the collection

\big\{H_{a,k}|a\in R,0\leq k\leq\ord_{p}(2)/2-1,2|k\big\}

contains each edge exactly once. Here we used that
\ord_{p}(2)
is not only even but also divisible by 4.
∎

4
Concluding remarks

Packing squares of Hamilton cycles.

Question
1.2
belongs to the extensive field of packing problems, where pairwise disjoint embeddings of some graph
H
into some host graph
G
are sought for, ideally covering each edge of
G
exactly once. Manifestations of this general problem have long been studied, for a wide range of graphs
H
and host graphs
G
, as well as for hypergraphs.
In the classic examples, one usually tries to decompose the edge set of the complete graph
G=K_{n}
into copies of a fixed graph
H
(solved by Wilson’s Theorem, which is a special case of the existence of designs
[
12
,
19
]
), into trees of certain size (e.g. the conjectures of Ringel and Gyárfás, the former recently being solved
[
18
,
23
]
), or into
2
-regular graphs (e.g. the Oberwolfach problem
[
11
]
).
There are also far-reaching results for more general families of graphs (e.g.
[
1
]
, see also the survey
[
13
]
).

For many of these problems, the “immediate” divisibility conditions are conjectured/proved to be sufficient for the perfect decomposition in question when
n
is sufficiently large
[
11
, Conjecture 1.4]
. For the square of a Hamilton cycle, this means that the degrees should be divisible by
4
, i.e.
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

In terms of small values, for all primes up to
p\leq 61
,
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, Theorem
1.3
applies. It is not difficult to check that it is impossible to partition
K_{9}
into two copies of
C_{9}^{2}
. Computer-aided searches of the second author together with Sebastian Lüderssen and Fabien Nießen
[
22
]
found examples of decompositions for
n=21,25,33,45,49,57,65,69
, which leaves
n=73
as the smallest unresolved case.
Furthermore, in Appendix
C
, we also describe a construction of an optimal packing of squares of Hamilton cycles into
K_{105}
, representing a relatively large non-prime order
n
.
We believe this to be sufficient evidence to conjecture that
n=9
is the only exception.

Conjecture 4.1
.

For any
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
,
n\not=9
, there is a decomposition of
E(K_{n})
into copies of
C_{n}^{2}
.

This is of course a strengthening of the special case of the conjecture from
[
11
]
for squares of Hamilton cycles, yet its main goal is not really to identify all the exceptional small
n
for which a decomposition does not exist. It is rather to inspire novel ways of constructing the decomposition explicitly.
The anticipated proof technique for
[
11
, Conjecture 1.4]
is the powerful method of iterative absorption (see
[
12
]
). By its nature, this will only serve as a proof of existence.
Even though for an infinite sequence of
n
an explicit construction is superior to proof of existence, explicit constructions for design-type questions are extremely rare to come by.
Finding an explicit construction for a decomposition of
E(K_{n})
into copies of
C_{n}^{2}
, even just for a new infinite sequence of
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
(besides the one in Theorem
1.3
), would be highly interesting.

Dimension
d\geq 3
.

We have determined precisely for every
n
the maximal diameter
H_{s}(n,2)
of a
2
-dimensional simplicial complex on
n

Using the “partition/cut/glue” approach mentioned in the introduction we are able to prove the following improved lower bound for

for

.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any
d\geq 3
, we have

\left(\frac{1}{d}-n^{-1/2+o(1)}\right)\binom{n}{d}\leq H_{s}(n,d).

This improves for arbitrary

the error term of Bohman and Newman
[
4
]
from inverse logarithmic to inverse polynomial.
The strategy is to first partition “most” edges of the complete
d
-uniform hypergraph into a limited number of so-called
desired cycles
(essentially squares of tight cycles).
We then cut these into
desired paths
and due to our flexibility about where we cut, we obtain a good variety of vertex sequences at the ends.
Finally, we glue these pieces up into the promised
desired trail
sequence, which gives a simplicial
d
-complex with large diameter.
The detailed proof of this argument appears in a separate manuscript
[
24
]
, where we also conjecture that the trivial upper bound is tight for every
d\geq 3
and large enough

.

Conjecture 4.3
.

For every
d\geq 3
, we have

H_{s}(n,d)=\left\lfloor\frac{1}{d}\cdot\binom{n}{d}-\frac{d+1}{d}\right\rfloor

for all

.

History of the problem and pseudo-manifolds.

The question of the maximum possible diameter of simplicial
d
-complexes originated from the Hirsch conjecture, which was disproved by Santos
[
25
]
. The polynomial Hirsch conjecture however remains open: Is there a polynomial
p(n,d)
such that any
d
-dimensional polytope with
n
facets has diameter at most
p(n,d)
? If the answer is “no”, then this also implies that there is no polynomial pivot rule for the simplex algorithm.
To approach this question,
Santos
[
26
]
considered the more general concept of
simplicial
d
-complexes instead of polytopes and established a superpolynomial lower bound.
Criado and Santos also studied the problem for the more restrictive concept of

d
-dimensional pseudo-manifolds without boundary
. These are simplicial
d
-complexes in which every ridge (sets of size
d-1
) is contained in exactly two facets (sets of size
d
). This is in contrast to a shortest path in simplicial
d
-complexes, where this number is
at most
two.
Analogous to
H_{s}(n,d)
let
H_{pm}(n,d)
be the maximum diameter of a
d
-dimensional pseudo-manifold.
Criado and Santos
[
8
]
showed that any
(d-1)
-dimensional simplicial complex with
n
vertices can be turned into a
(d-1)
-dimensional pseudo-manifold without boundary with
2n
vertices and diameter at least two larger.
Again, Criado and Newman
[
7
]
and subsequently Bohman and Newman
[
4
]
improved the constructions and currently the best known bounds from
[
4
]
are

\left(\frac{2}{(d+1)(d+1)!}-(\log n)^{-\varepsilon}\right)n^{d}\leq H_{pm}(n,d)\leq\frac{2n^{d}}{(d+1)(d+1)!}\,.

In our language, for
d=2
the object of interest is a “trail-cube” or the third power of paths.
It would be interesting to see if our approach also can be generalized to this setting, but already Theorem
1.3
does not extend naturally to the third power of a Hamilton cycle.

Relation to
2
-radius sequences and harmonious colourings.

While finalizing our manuscript we learned about a closely related set of problems that goes back even further than the definition of
H_{s}(n,d)
.
For two integers
n\geq k
a sequence of elements from
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
is called a
k
-radius sequence
if every pair from
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
appears at least once at distance at most
k
in that sequence.
Let
f_{k}(n)
be the length of a shortest
k
-radius sequence.
Note that an easy double-counting lower bound is
f_{k}(n)\geq\lceil\tfrac{1}{k}\binom{n}{2}+\tfrac{k+1}{2}\rceil
.
This concept was introduced by Jaromczyk and Lonc
[
17
]
20 years ago, motivated by a problem from transmission of data sets, and has since then been studied from different angles (see
[
5
]
and the references therein), for example, the general asymptotically optimal bound
f_{k}(n)=\tfrac{1}{2k}n^{2}+\mathcal{O}(n^{1+\varepsilon})
was obtained by Jarmoczyk, Lonc, and Truszczyński
[
16
]
.

A
2
-radius sequence in which every pair appears exactly once at distance two gives an optimal simplicial complex because as a
labels
sequence together with the all zero
layout
it gives a good
(\textsc{labels},\textsc{layout})
pair.
This is only possible when
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and if it exists implies that
f_{2}(n)=H_{s}(n,2)-3
in this case.
Notably, Bondy, Lonc, and Rzążewski
[
5
]
precisely determined
f_{2}(n)
for all but few residues classes of
n
(see
[
5
,
6
, Corollary 4.11]
) and thereby also determine
H_{s}(n,2)
for any
n\equiv 1\pmod{4}
such that
n\not=9
,
n\not\equiv 21\pmod{24}
, and
n\not\equiv 7665\pmod{8760}
.
The other way round our generating sequence for
n\equiv 5\pmod{8}
does not use turns (c.f. Lemma
2.5
) and, hence, our result determines
f_{2}(n)
for these
n
, which solves the case
n\equiv 21\pmod{24}
(and with
[
5
, Lemma 2.1]
also the open cases
n\equiv 18,19,20\pmod{24}
of
[
5
, Corollary 4.11]
).
Interestingly, they use the concept of a
2
-additive sequence, which very closely resembles our definition of a generating sequence, and find these to construct the optimal
2
-radius sequences.
When
n\not\equiv 1\pmod{4}
then there is no direct connection between the two concepts, because an optimal
2
-additive sequence will repeat some pairs within distance two and an optimal simplicial complex will need to have turns.
There also is a notion of a related
2
-perfect sequence, which was known in the literature before, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Another concept related to our
d>2
case was investigated by Dębski, Lonc, and Rzążewski
[
9
]
.
They study
harmonious
colorings of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
H
, which is a coloring of the vertices of
H
such that each edge receives
k
different colors and every
k
-subset of colors is used at most once.
The
harmonious number
of a
k
-uniform hypergraph
H
is the minimum number of colors in a harmonious coloring of
H
.
Note that the maximum number
N
such that the harmonious chromatic number of the desired path on
N
vertices is at most
n
is a lower bound for
H_{s}(n,d)+d+1
.
Indeed, identifying vertices with the same color gives a desired trail of length
N
with only
n
vertices.

A class of hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
is called
fragmentable
if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
c>0
such that for any
H\in\mathcal{H}
without isolated vertices there is a set
S\subseteq V(H)
of size
\varepsilon v(H)
such that
H-S
only has components of size at most
c
.

Theorem 4.4

(Dębski, Lonc, and Rzążewski
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a fragmentable class of
k
-uniform hypergraphs of bounded maximum degree. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an
m_{0}
such that for any
H\in\mathcal{H}
with
m\geq m_{0}
edges the harmonious number is at most
(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt[k]{k!m}
.

This result is asymptotically optimal and can be applied to the family of desired paths, which is a fragmentable family of
d
-uniform hypergraphs, where each member with
N
vertices has
dN-\binom{d+1}{2}
edges and maximum degree bounded by
d^{2}
.
Hence, the theorem gives the upper bound
(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt[d]{d!dN-d!\binom{d+1}{2}}
for the harmonious number and, with the observation above, implies
H_{s}(n,d)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\tfrac{1}{d}\binom{n}{d}
.
This asymptotically determines
H_{s}(n,d)
, just as Bohman and Newman
[
4
]
do, but without an explicit error term.

. The proof is using iterative absorption, hence the construction is not explicit.
