Orthopaedic surgeons’ perceptions of individual feedback from a national arthroplasty quality register
Authors: Per Jolbäck, Maziar Mohaddes, Fredrika Sundberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsOur aim was to explore Swedish orthopaedic surgeons’ and trainees’ perceptions of receiving individual surgeon feedback following total hip arthroplasty (THA) from a national arthroplasty quality register.MethodsA qualitative study with a phenomenographic approach was conducted. Informants were strategically selected to maximize variation in perceptions, based on hospital type (according to the national arthroplasty register classification) and level of surgical experience (trainee or years since specialist certification). A total of 15 orthopaedic specialists and four trainees from 15 hospitals in Sweden were interviewed. Data were analyzed using the established seven-step phenomenographic analysis procedure.ResultsFour qualitatively distinct perceptions of individual surgeon feedback from a national quality register were identified: 1) progression in the profession; 2) exposure to inaccurate criticism; 3) potential impairment of patient benefit; and 4) does not contribute to enhanced feedback for surgeons. Feedback was perceived as a means of professional development but was also considered to carry a risk of misinterpretation and unfair criticism if results were used inappropriately. Participants further expressed concern that surgeon-specific outcome reporting could shift the focus from patient benefit to performance metrics, potentially influencing patient selection and clinical decision-making. Finally, some informants questioned the need for a national feedback programme, citing existing local and organizational mechanisms that already provide meaningful feedback.ConclusionSwedish orthopaedic surgeons and trainees hold diverse perceptions of individual surgeon feedback from a national arthroplasty quality register. Awareness of these perceptions is important when refining or designing future surgeon-specific feedback programmes. Development and refining of feedback systems should occur in close collaboration with orthopaedic surgeons to ensure clinical relevance and acceptance.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(9):1137–1144.

Introduction

Constructive feedback is a fundamental component of medical education; however, there is no universally accepted definition of feedback within the medical education literature. Van de Ridder et al1 defined feedback as “specific information about the comparison between a trainee’s observed performance and a standard, given with the intent to improve the trainee’s performance”. According to this definition, a trainee may be any individual engaged in a medical learning context. Through feedback, the trainee develops the knowledge, skills, and professional attitudes required to become an excellent practitioner, depending on clinical exposure and case mix.

Archer2 proposed a complementary definition, describing feedback as information about previous performance that is used to promote positive and desirable development. The importance of feedback in medical training and professional development has been well established; without feedback, errors may remain uncorrected and effective performance may not be reinforced. Feedback provides essential information about performance and thereby creates the conditions necessary for improvement.3 Furthermore, previous research suggests that perceptions of feedback may differ according to the surgeon’s level of clinical experience.4

In 2013, the UK NHS began publishing individual surgeon outcomes across ten specialties, with the aim of enabling patients to make informed choices and thereby enhance the quality of care.5 Proponents of publicly reported surgeon-specific outcomes argue that such transparency empowers patients, supports informed decision-making, and may contribute to improved clinical outcomes. In contrast, critics contend that public reporting may discourage surgeons from operating on high-risk patients and increase the risk of data manipulation.6 Feedback programmes providing individual orthopaedic surgeons with their own arthroplasty outcomes from national or regional quality registers have already been implemented in several countries.7-10 The purpose of these programmes is to offer surgeons structured information about their clinical practice and to promote continuous quality improvement. Previous studies exploring orthopaedic surgeons’ experiences of receiving their own patient-reported outcome measures have shown mixed responses, ranging from enhanced self-reflection to little or no impact on clinical behaviour.11

In Sweden, there is no national standardized follow-up programme after total hip arthroplasty (THA). Follow-up practices vary considerably between hospitals and individual surgeons, ranging from no routine postoperative visits to structured follow-up of all patients. However, all orthopaedic hospitals report data to the Swedish Hip Arthroplasty Register (SHAR), which provides hospital-level feedback on reoperations, adverse events, and patient-reported outcomes.12 Since the completion of this study, SHAR and the Swedish Knee Arthroplasty Register have merged to form the Swedish Arthroplasty Register.

Despite this national reporting system, there is currently no programme in Sweden that provides individual surgeons with feedback on their own THA outcomes. Moreover, knowledge about Swedish orthopaedic surgeons’ perceptions of surgeon-specific feedback programmes remains limited. The aim was therefore to explore Swedish orthopaedic surgeons’ and trainees’ perceptions of being provided with individual surgeon feedback following THAs from a national arthroplasty quality register.

Methods

A qualitative study was conducted using the phenomenographic approach. A purposive sampling strategy was employed, supported by a strategic selection matrix designed to recruit 24 informants to maximize variation in perceptions. The matrix (Table I) assumed that understanding of the phenomenon could vary according to hospital type, years of experience in orthopaedics, and sex. In cases where multiple individuals expressed interest within the same category of the matrix, a secondary selection was made based on hospital location to ensure geographical representation.

Hospitals were categorized according to the national arthroplasty register’s classification:12 rural, county, university or regional, and private hospitals. Surgeons were further categorized by level of experience (orthopaedic trainee or years since specialist certification), consistent with a previously published approach.13

All orthopaedic surgeons and trainees at the 74 departments reporting primary THAs to the SHAR were invited to participate.12 An information letter and an expression-of-interest form were distributed via the heads of the orthopaedic departments to eligible surgeons. Responses were returned directly to the first author by post or email. Interested individuals were subsequently contacted by telephone and provided with additional information about the study. Those who agreed to participate scheduled an interview at a time and location of their choosing. Written informed consent was obtained prior to each interview. A total of 35 surgeons responded to the invitation. Of these, 19 were recruited to the study: 15 orthopaedic specialists and four trainees from 15 hospitals across Sweden (Table I). Among the specialists, both male and female surgeons were represented, with varying lengths of clinical experience.

Detailed demographic information, including age, sex distribution within specific hospitals, and exact workplace, is not presented due to ethical considerations and the risk of identifying individuals in departments with few participating surgeons.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted by the first author using a semistructured interview guide. Two pilot interviews, not included in the study, were conducted to test the guide, resulting in minor revisions. The opening question in each interview was: “In your opinion, what is a reliable quality register?” Interviews were conducted over a four-month period. All were face-to-face, at a location chosen by the informant: 14 at the informant’s hospital of employment, two at external locations, and three via Skype (Skype Communications, Luxembourg). The mean interview duration was 24 minutes (20 to 34).

All interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed verbatim in Word (Microsoft, USA), and pseudonymized. Transcripts were reviewed individually by the first author and an external reviewer to ensure quality prior to analysis. Transcripts were not returned to informants for verification or comment.

Statistical analysis

In accordance with phenomenographic methodology, the data were analyzed following the seven-step procedure for phenomenographic studies (Table II).14,15 Analysis began with repeated readings of the interview transcripts to gain an overall understanding of the material and the meaning of participants’ statements. The next step involved identifying statements reflecting different perceptions, with attention paid to qualitative similarities and differences in the data. Through comparison, distinct perceptions became apparent, and the characteristic features of each conception were identified in contrast to other perceptions.

The analysis was iterative, moving between the full transcript text and condensed summaries, and between preliminary descriptions and the original data, to ensure that less dominant perceptions were also captured. Once preliminary descriptive categories were defined, a repetitive process was conducted with the coauthors. Following these discussions, the first author returned to the transcripts, recategorized the data, and refined the descriptive categories until consensus was reached with the external reviewer.

All transcripts were kept in Swedish throughout the analysis. Quotations were translated into English by a professional translator to preserve nuance. Finally, the significance of each descriptive category was assessed by considering the frequency of perceptions, their sources, and the aspects emphasized by the informants as particularly important17,18 (Table III).

An ethical approval for this study has been obtained from the Regional Ethical Review Board in Gothenburg (DNR 1057 to 18) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2013.19

Results

Four perceptions of understanding the phenomenon of individual surgeon feedback from a national quality register were revealed in the analysis of the collected data: 1) progression in the profession; 2) exposing the surgeons to inaccurate criticism; 3) potential impairment of patient benefit; and 4) not contributing to enhanced feedback to the surgeons. Various ways of understanding individual surgeon feedback from a national arthroplasty quality register, formulated as descriptions, in categories, are presented in Figure 1.

The two perceptions, progression in the profession and exposing the surgeons to inaccurate criticism, were described by many of the informants, while the other two, potential impairment of patient benefit and not contributing to enhanced feedback to the surgeons, were described by a limited number of informants (Table III).

Progression in the profession

The central focal point in this category was how to become more skilled orthopaedic surgeons by acquiring knowledge of the outcomes of the surgeries they have performed and, using this knowledge, also improve the utility for the patients. The informants expressed their need for information about their own practice, if their skills and expertise are accepted, or whether they have to modify deficient components in their medical practice (quotation 1). In the past, clinical follow-up visits have provided surgeons with this information relating to their skills and expertise or deficiencies directly from patients, but, due to slimmed-down hospital organizations, these follow-up visits have been discontinued (quotation 2). So, there is a lack of information provided to the surgeon about her/his own practice from the surgeon’s point of view. The need for new learning (quotation 3), if surgeons are to modify deficient components in their practice, is also outlined. These first three (quotations 1 to 3) descriptions of the phenomenon outlined the fact that a programme of individual surgeon feedback from a national quality register may provide surgeons with information on their own practice, and this could enhance patient utility following the surgery.

Improved compliance and accuracy (uotation 4) are also highlighted. Enhanced accuracy was described in terms such as “working a little harder on every problem before” and being “more thoughtful” in clinical decision-making. This suggests a behavioural change associated with awareness that individual results are being evaluated and compared with those of peers. Such awareness may encourage greater diligence, stricter adherence to guidelines, and increased attention to detail in both surgical technique and perioperative management. Moreover, surgeon-specific results and improved compliance were perceived as an opportunity to encourage surgeons to follow recommendations and guidelines more effectively. Enforcing a change in practice (quotation 5) is also expressed as a perception of the phenomenon and is outlined as the feedback perhaps forcing unwilling surgeons to change their practice. One presumption when it comes to realizing this transformation is that the unwilling surgeon is exposed in public to the views expressed by the informants.

Quotation 1: “…even if it emerges that I am by far the worst or that I have the poorest results among patients, this is information I want, something the department can act on, steps can be taken in the form of training or dual-surgeon operating or something, choosing slightly more hand-picked cases, I think there are huge benefits with potential…”

Quotation 2: “I’m in favour of it because it would give me the opportunity to see whether there’s something I need to change or improve, especially now when so much feedback has been taken away, we don’t have any follow-up visits, we have very poor control of how things actually work out for patients, so…”

Quotation 3: “…making it clear that there is a need for training or a practice that is incorrect or strange or deviating…”

Quotation 4: “…it makes you think about things in more detail…you don’t make mistakes, or perhaps you reduce short cuts…so that’s a positive effect…very definitely, a positive effect…I think it would make us more diligent in adhering to routines, working a little harder on each problem before…”

Quotation 5: “…open results might make anyone who has simply not wanted to change, even though there is scientific evidence to prove the opposite, feel that they actually need to change because this is no longer good enough, you can’t cover it up if it is there for everyone to see…”

Exposing the surgeons to inaccurate criticism

The central point in this descriptive category concerned the perceived consequences of a deficient result for the surgeon, such as fear of media exposure, misinterpretation of data, and reduced wellbeing. Furthermore, participants emphasized that attributing outcomes solely to the individual surgeon, without acknowledging the contributions of the broader healthcare team, may be problematic.

The informants expressed a fear that the collected data could be misinterpreted or misused, together with the risk of being accused of being a poorly performing surgeon depending on the case mix or, in the worst-case scenario, being portrayed as “Doctor Death” in the tabloids (quotation 6). Fear that the collected data could be misinterpreted or misused was the most frequently mentioned and frequently expressed perception during the interviews. The individual feedback must be kept confidential from the public or media to minimize the risk of the data not being presented in their correct context. These perceptions, fear of being exposed in the media and the misinterpretation or misuse of the data, appear to be essential to almost all the surgeons in the study, regardless of hospital level or time in the profession.

Diminished wellbeing was also described as a potential consequence for surgeons receiving unfavourable results within the feedback programme (quotation 7). An unfavourable result may negatively affect the surgeon’s self-confidence and sense of professional autonomy. Conversely, high-performing results may also adversely affect wellbeing, as patients may specifically request these surgeons as their operating surgeon instead of those with less favourable results. This impairment in wellbeing depending on unfavourable results was expressed in the interviews as something surgeons have experienced in their practice and need to master prior to the individual feedback programme. Otherwise, they must try to find advice and help to handle the response to a reduction in wellbeing (quotation 8). This descriptive category also highlights the risk of discrimination against surgeons with unfavourable results and emphasizes that surgical outcomes are not solely determined by the surgeon. Such discrimination may occur across various aspects of employment (quotation 9), including recruitment, case allocation, promotion, and access to professional development, and may also affect collegial trust and professional reputation. Moreover, even though surgical outcomes depend on the wider team involved in patient care, the contributions of this team may be overlooked (quotation 10).

Quotation 6: “…and so it looked as though it was Doctor Death and that’s exactly the risk if there are no shades of difference and it isn’t put in context.”

Quotation 7: “…feedback is great, but you also need to be aware of the way feedback is given. The figures can be quite powerful and can impact individual surgeons quite heavily in the way they feel, so you need to be aware that it’s good to have figures, but they need to be awarded in the right way.”

Quotation 8: “To get things on an even keel, not psychosocially, we need to have learned to handle surgeons. If we haven’t done that, we need to look for help ourselves.”

Quotation 9: “…intracollegially it can be both good and bad. Well, what can I say about a collegial atmosphere; it can be used in everything from salary negotiations to almost pure blackmail or…”

Quotation 10: “…as far as orthopaedics are concerned, it isn’t simply a question of an operation. It also focuses heavily on what happens outside the surgical ward and one thing that could be improved is the registration of complications, which is difficult, because some infections perhaps need to be managed in different places like the GP’s clinic, the Emergency Room, if we’re talking about infections, wound-related complications, or perhaps luxations/disclocations that aren’t always reported…”

Potential impairment of patient benefit

The central focus of this descriptive category concerned the consequences of an individual surgeon feedback programme for high-risk patients, as well as the potential for poorer outcomes resulting from the stress generated by the programme. One identified risk was that some surgeons could deselect patients with a higher risk of adverse surgical outcomes, regardless of the potential benefits of surgery for the individual patient, thereby effectively distorting the data. Such deselection would occur because high-risk patients could negatively influence individual surgeons’ outcome statistics. Consequently, the focus could shift from the clinical benefits of surgery to the numerical results, creating a risk that patients who could benefit substantially from surgery are denied treatment (quotation 11). In addition, some surgeons could experience impaired performance due to the stress associated with adhering to an individual surgeon feedback programme, potentially leading to poorer outcomes (quotation 12).

Quotation 11: “…I am a bit worried about registration as it may result in us focusing…excluding patients who might otherwise have been given a prosthesis, but we exclude them to protect ourselves or…”

Quotation 12: “We send masses of documents to patients who simply sign and say it’s OK if they get an infection, which it naturally isn’t, but you are simply watching your own back. We’ve already started doing this, we write some things and perform certain examinations just to protect ourselves, as there are more and more complaints and this is naturally not the right way to do things, even if I obviously think we should be skilled and maintain a certain standard. That isn’t what I mean, but I think that we can sometimes do a poorer job under the stress that this could cause.”

Does not contribute to enhanced feedback for surgeons

The central point in this final descriptive category was that an individual surgeon feedback programme based on a national quality register was perceived as not contributing to enhanced feedback for surgeons. Participants emphasized that relevant and meaningful feedback is already provided through existing mechanisms and organizational structures within the healthcare system. These include educational supervision during residency training and departmental radiology rounds, where orthopaedic surgeons and trainees attend to review pre- and postoperative radiographs and discuss the technical aspects of surgery in a collegial setting (quotation 13). During these sessions, surgeons receive direct feedback on their surgical technique.

In addition, annual reports from national quality registers and intracollegial feedback (quotation 14) were described as established sources of performance evaluation. Informants also noted that locally designed follow-up registers have already been implemented in some orthopaedic departments, providing surgeons with feedback on their individual results (quotation 15). Consequently, the perceived need for a national programme delivering surgeon-specific feedback was considered limited.

Quotation 13: “…so individual feedback…we actually have it all the time…in our residents’ training…you get feedback during training…on every radiograph round, a prosthesis is shown and someone is there to comment…how did you do this…OK…what happened, so you actually get feedback the whole time…that’s one of the strengths in the way we organize the care where I work…unlike other countries, we have an radiograph round where everyone sits and judges everyone…and there is not that much prestige…”

Quotation 14: “But we have the radiograph round where you can get feedback, both positive and negative, of course, depending on how you interpret it, but I think there’s incredible added value in terms of potential improvements, in the way things can be improved the whole time, if you have colleagues who can explain things pedagogically…”

Quotation 15: “Well, we have that, so we get it. I’ve heard about it and I’m really proud of it and I think we are very good.”

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore orthopaedic surgeons’ and trainees’ perceptions of receiving individual surgeon feedback from a national arthroplasty quality register. The qualitative design enabled a nuanced and in-depth understanding of these perceptions. Through systematic analysis of participants’ own descriptions and conceptions, the study provides empirically grounded insights into the phenomenon, rather than relying on anecdotal assumptions.

The findings reveal four distinct ways in which orthopaedic surgeons and trainees in Sweden perceive individual surgeon feedback from such a register. The results in this study indicate that individual surgeon feedback from a national quality register is a complex phenomenon and can be seen from many aspects. Most of the informants stated that a programme of individual surgeon feedback may provide surgeons with information and increase their awareness of their own deficiencies. Moreover, reflecting, understanding, and working actively on these deficiencies in their practice might then generate enhanced patient utility. “When surgeons are aware of their outcomes, they can improve them to provide better care to patients” is concluded by Carlsson et al18 in a study investigating the effects of surgeon variation on functional and oncological outcomes for patients undergoing radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer. In contrast to this perception of enhanced patient utility, a perception was expressed that individual surgeon feedback (i.e. registration of the surgeon) may impair patient utility, followed by a shift in focus from what is the best for the patient to what is preferable for the surgeon to avoid unfavourable results. Swedish orthopaedic surgeons and trainees doubt whether individual surgeon feedback would benefit patients. However, the distorsion of data and the deselection of patients for surgery have not been demonstrated following the introduction of surgeon-specific feedback in colorectal cancer surgery.6

Some informants mentioned that individual surgeon feedback could lead to improved compliance and greater accuracy in clinical practice. This improvement may reflect behavioural changes triggered by awareness of being assessed, a phenomenon consistent with the Hawthorne effect. Such an effect suggests that surgeons modify their practice when they know their performance is being monitored, which could enhance adherence to guidelines or attention to technical details.20

Participants highlighted the risk of reduced wellbeing among surgeons receiving unfavourable performance results. Interestingly, even surgeons achieving high-standard results were reported to experience potential declines in wellbeing. This risk is linked to the expectation that patients may specifically request these high-performing surgeons, creating increased workload and a stressful working environment. Similarly, negative feedback provided to trainees in the operating theatre has been shown to adversely affect their wellbeing, with some trainees describing intentions to leave the profession because of such feedback.21 Perceptions of feedback can differ between surgical staff and residents.4 The way surgeons respond to feedback and how they manage it can also have broader implications for surgeons in general. Some may be motivated by negative feedback, while others may find it discouraging. These differences in response are likely influenced by prior experiences with feedback, individual emotional traits, and generational factors.22 There is also a contradiction regarding the perceived need for individual surgeon feedback from a national quality register. On one hand, it could help bridge gaps in knowledge not addressed during routine clinical follow-up visits. On the other hand, some participants noted that surgeons already receive substantial feedback through existing mechanisms and structures within healthcare. These opposing perceptions may be partially explained by differences in how arthroplasty care is organized across hospitals. Some orthopaedic departments continue to hold clinical follow-up visits, radiology rounds, and other activities that provide direct feedback to operating surgeons, while others lack these structures, narrowing the feedback available. Another contributing factor may be the presence of local follow-up registers in some Swedish orthopaedic departments, which provide surgeons with individual feedback. Variations in departmental organization and the availability of local feedback systems may therefore underlie the differing perceptions of the need for a national surgeon-specific feedback programme.

The perceptions of “progression in the profession” and “exposing the surgeons to inaccurate criticism” are partly linked. Most of the informants expressed a positive attitude towards individual surgeon feedback from a national quality register in terms of learning, education, and being provided with information on their own practice. Regardless of this positive attitude, there is a deep understanding and respect among the informants that it may expose the surgeons to inaccurate criticism if the information is detached from its context or made publicly available to patients and the media. This fear of the media and the possibility that that information is detached from its context is similar to the findings reported by Hildon et al.23 Most informants stated that information on individual surgeons’ results should serve as a tool for surgeons to gain credibility within the Swedish orthopaedic community. However, some informants suggested that this information could be provided to the head of the department or the surgeon responsible for the arthroplasty section, enabling them to offer support and guidance if the results are negative.

The general finding in our study is similar to the results of a study by Boyce et al,11 exploring orthopaedic surgeons’ experience of receiving patient-reported outcomes at surgeon level, where the experience varied from promoted self-reflection to the information having no impact on surgeon behaviour. However, our findings are also similar when it comes to the perception and criticism of individual surgeon feedback in general, despite the surgical speciality area.

One of the strengths in our study is the data-generation method. It has been suggested that semistructured interviews, one informant at each point, are the correct method for data generation in phenomenographic research24 with a few entry questions and a following dialogue depending on the informants’ answers. Semistructured interviews empower the informants to act more openly; think aloud, speak, or pause. Another strength is the geographical spread of informants, where the informants were recruited from 15 hospitals, and the reduction in the bias of capturing the perceptions of the surgeons at one hospital rather than individual surgeons’ perceptions. To strengthen the trustworthiness of the study, several procedures were implemented: an external reviewer to ensure the quality of the transcript before analysis and all the quotations being translated by a professional translator in order not to risk the loss of nuances in translation.

One limitation of the study is the uneven distribution of informants across hospital types, particularly the limited inclusion of informants from university, regional, and private hospitals. This may reflect the relatively low proportion of such hospitals in Sweden — 12% each for university/regional and private hospitals — compared with 37% of rural hospitals and 38% of county hospitals, which employ a larger number of orthopaedic surgeons. Limited interest in participating may also have contributed, particularly in university or regional hospitals with comparatively low annual surgical volumes (approximately 10% of the Swedish THA annual production12 performed at these hospitals). These hospitals may also treat a more complex case mix or perform more technically demanding total hip arthroplasties compared with rural and county hospitals and may therefore already have established outpatient follow-up programmes. As a result, surgeons at university, regional, and private hospitals may not have considered the need for an additional feedback programme. A second limitation of this study is that we did not reach the prespecified target of 24 informants, which was intended to maximize variation in perceptions. However, according to Larsson and Holmström, 12 interviews are generally sufficient in a phenomenographic study, and empirical evidence suggests that even studies with fewer than 20 interviews can yield meaningful results, with no new perceptions typically emerging after ten to 12 interviews.25 However, we do not feel that this lack of informants will interfere with the trustworthiness of the study.

The recruitment method for the study, informants actively communicating their interest in participating, may have threatened the trustworthiness of the study. We can speculate about whether there is bias in the recruitment of informants where surgeons with a positive attitude to surgeon-specific feedback were more eager to participate than surgeons with a negative attitude.

Further research is needed to explore the nature of feedback at individual surgeon level to achieve a better understanding and usefulness of the feedback from a quality register from a surgeon’s perspective as well as from the patient’s perceptive.

In conclusion, orthopaedic surgeons and trainees in Sweden hold multiple perceptions regarding the value of individual surgeon feedback from a national arthroplasty quality register. Identifying these perceptions may deepen understanding and inform and guide the refinement and future development of feedback programmes in total hip arthroplasties. Such programmes should be developed in close collaboration with orthopaedic surgeons to ensure clinical relevance, practical applicability, and professional acceptance.

Take home message

- Surgeon-specific feedback from national arthroplasty registers may support professional development and quality improvement by helping surgeons identify opportunities to refine their practice.

- However, feedback should be confidential, appropriately risk-adjusted, and interpreted within the context of case mix and team-based care to avoid inaccurate criticism, adverse effects on surgeon wellbeing, and unintended avoidance of high-risk patients.

- Feedback programmes should therefore be developed collaboratively with surgeons and linked to constructive support and education
