Cost-effectiveness of intermittent preventive therapy and insecticide-treated bed nets for malaria prevention among pregnant women in Jos North, Plateau State, Nigeria
Authors: Lenz Nwachinemere Okoro, Ayuba Ibrahim Zoakah, Pokop Bupwatda, Ebuka Louis Anyamene, Naya Gadzama Bulus, Fwangshak D Kumbak, Mirembe Christine Sabano, Kaweesi Abdulrahim Mukisa, Jonathan Mawutor Gmanyami, Michael Yaw Amoakoh, Isaac Isiko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health concern, particularly among pregnant women in endemic regions. Intermittent preventive therapy using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) and insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) is widely recommended for malaria prevention in pregnancy. However, the cost-effectiveness of these interventions, particularly in low-resource settings, needs further evaluation to inform policy and resource allocation. This study aimed to assess the cost-effectiveness of IPTp-SP and ITNs as malaria prevention strategies among pregnant women attending antenatal care (ANC) services in Jos North, Plateau State, Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study design was used to conduct a cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) comparing IPTp-SP alone to a combined IPTp-SP/ITN intervention. A total of 1000 pregnant women accessing ANC services from 2019 to 2020 in five primary healthcare facilities were included. Cost data were collected from a provider’s perspective using a cost valuation sheet adapted from the WHO CHOICE tool. Health outcomes were measured in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost, with the primary outcomes being the prevalence of low birth weight (LBW) and maternal anaemia at 36–40 weeks of gestation. Data were analysed using STATA version 16, and an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted using Monte Carlo simulations.ResultsThe total DALYs lost were higher in the IPTp-SP-only group (101.53 DALYs) than in the IPTp-SP/ITN group (85.9 DALYs), yielding an incremental effect of 15.63 DALYs averted. The total cost of the intervention was higher for IPTp-SP/ITN (US$231.98) compared to IPTp-SP alone (US$131.79), with an incremental cost of US$100.19. The ICER was US$2.12 per DALY averted, indicating that IPTp-SP/ITN is a cost-effective intervention. The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) indicates that the probability of IPTp-SP/ITN being more cost-effective than IPTp-SP only at a WTP set at 150 US$/DALY is approximately 60%, suggesting that cost-effectiveness improves at higher WTP thresholds.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that IPTp-SP/ITN is a cost-effective intervention for preventing malaria during pregnancy. However, its cost-effectiveness is influenced by integration into ANC services, adherence to ITN use, and funding mechanisms. Policymakers should consider optimizing funding and service delivery models to improve intervention efficiency and sustainability. Further research incorporating household-level costs and long-term economic impacts is recommended.Keywords: Cost-effectiveness, Malaria prevention, Intermittent preventive therapy, Insecticide-treated nets, Pregnant women, Nigeria

Background

The control of malaria requires expanded coverage and access to effective malaria control interventions, such as insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), intermittent preventive treatment (IPT), prompt diagnostic testing and appropriate treatment [1, 2]. Decisions on how to scale up the coverage of these interventions need to be based on evidence of program effectiveness, equity, and cost-effectiveness [3]. Malaria, an endemic disease in most tropical countries, has remained a major public health problem in this part of the world and has devastating impacts on vulnerable groups of people. Malaria is a mosquito-borne disease that affects humans and other animals, caused by parasitic protozoans of the genus Plasmodium. It is transmitted through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito [4, 5]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) latest report, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 malaria deaths in 2023 [5], with the African region disproportionately accounting for 95% of malaria cases and 96% of malaria deaths globally [6]. IPTp currently involves giving at least three treatment courses of SP to pregnant women from the second trimester onwards, at least one month apart. [7, 8] IPT with three or more doses of SP given during the second and third trimesters of gestation is efficacious in reducing the incidence of placental malaria, LBW, and severe maternal anaemia during pregnancy [7–9]. Increasing the effectiveness of malaria preventive interventions during pregnancy would not only require the availability of safe and efficacious drugs for IPTp but also improve ANC clinic attendance and the quality of ANC services. However, new strategies for improving effectiveness are likely to entail extra costs to the health system. Consequently, the economic evaluation of intermittent preventive therapy using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) should also include the estimation of the cut-off levels of the intervention costs beyond which the strategy ceases to be cost-effective under different epidemiological conditions, levels of efficacy and factors that may limit effectiveness [10, 11].

Economic evaluation of health programmes such as malaria control programmes entails comparing the cost and consequences of specific health interventions, as this serves as one of the vital approaches that can assist with resource allocation to ensure that more cost-effective interventional methods are employed among competing alternative interventions [12]. In economic evaluation, the impact of health problems can be assessed using a variety of health measures, such as the number of cases of illness, the number of deaths due to illness, the number of potential years of life lost due to illness or, in monetary terms, the cost of health problems, which can be expressed as the monetary value of resources spent or lost because of the health problem [12–14].

Paradoxically, although IPTp-SP has been recommended for more than a decade, there is still little and incomplete information on the economic evaluation of this strategy, especially as it relates to local settings [15]. Many published economic evaluations of IPTp-SP have used indicators of infant morbidity or mortality, such as LBW, maternal anaemia, and placental malaria, as surrogate indicators of maternal morbidity and mortality to calculate disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [16–20]. This study, therefore, assessed the cost-effectiveness of ITN and IPTp malaria interventions during pregnancy, using the Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) between the IPTp-SP/ITN and IPTp-SP intervention groups.

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Primary Health Care facilities in Jos North Local Government Area (LGA), comprising 20 political wards in Plateau State, Nigeria. The LGA shares boundaries to the west with Bassa LGA, to the north with Toro LGA of Bauchi state, and to the east with Jos East LGA and Jos South LGA southwards. Jos is an administrative and cosmopolitan city comprising diverse ethnic groups, including the Anaguta, Berom, Irigwe, Mwaghavul, Ngas, and Rukuba. Civil service, farming, and small-scale businesses are the predominant occupations, and the majority of the population identifies with the Christian or Islamic faiths. As of 2018, the coverage for IPTp-SP and ITN uptake among pregnant women accessing ANC in PHC facilities in JoS North LGA was 69% and 15%, respectively [24]. These IPTp-SP and ITN services are supported by the U.S. President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI), which channels support through the ANC platform to strengthen malaria prevention among pregnant women in PHC facilities [21]. In the Jos North LGA, there are 32 PHC facilities; three tertiary health institutions, which include the Plateau State Specialist Hospital, Jos University Teaching Hospital, and Bingham University Teaching Hospital; and over 40 private health institutions, including Our Lady of Apostles Hospital and the Faith Alive Foundation [22–24].

Study design and study population

This comparative cross-sectional study was conducted among pregnant women at 13–36 weeks of gestational age who accessed ANC at the selected PHCs in Jos North LGA, Plateau State, between 2019 and 2020. The study compared pregnant women who received IPTp-SP alone with those who used both IPTp-SP and ITN during pregnancy. An existing malaria intervention using the ITN and IPTp-SP intervention packages is already being delivered via ANC services in PHC facilities in Plateau State, Nigeria [25]. An incremental cost-effectiveness analysis (ICEA) was performed comparing the group of pregnant women who used a combined package of IPTp-SP/ITN with the group that used IPTp-SP only. This study involved 1000 pregnant women accessing antenatal care (ANC) at a selected Primary Health Care (PHC) facility in Jos North LGA. Five PHC facilities were purposively selected out of the 32 PHCs in Jos North LGA, based on service readiness and ANC client volume. Participants were: 13–36 weeks pregnant, HIV negative, and provided informed consent. They were divided into two groups based on those receiving Intermittent Preventive Treatment in pregnancy with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) alone and those using both IPTp-SP and ITNs during pregnancy. Participants were prospectively followed until delivery, and relevant birth outcomes, including low birth weight (LBW) and maternal haemoglobin at 36–40 weeks of gestation, were recorded.

Selection of subjects for comparison

Study respondents in the IPTp-SP/ITN and IPTp-SP groups (500 respondents in each study group) were selected for comparison based on their behavioural and consumption preferences and practices. The respondents in the IPTp-SP/ITN group had taken at least two doses of SP under direct observation and always slept under their ITN each night during their index pregnancies. Respondents in the IPTp-SP group were those who had received at least two doses of SP under direct observation, regardless of whether they owned or did not own an ITN, but had never slept under an ITN at night during their index pregnancies. ITN usage data were obtained through structured self-reported questionnaires administered during ANC visits, which included detailed recall questions on daily ITN use during pregnancy.

Cost valuation sheet

Cost data were collected as primary data from a provider’s perspective using a cost valuation sheet adapted from the WHO CHOICE tool. The costing focused on direct, recurrent costs only, such as staff time, drug procurement, and ITN logistics. Indirect and capital costs were excluded. Cost information was collected at the facility level using interviews with facility personnel, record reviews, and a structured costing guide. The costing covered the intervention implementation period from 2019 to 2020. Five PHC facilities were purposively selected for the costing component based on high ANC client volume, readiness to deliver malaria-in-pregnancy interventions, and consistent availability of routine data. These facilities also represented a mix of service delivery contexts to reflect cost variation across facility types. Disaggregating cost data by individual facility, rather than aggregating at the ward or LGA level, enabled a more accurate reflection of the operational and financial diversity influencing malaria intervention delivery at the point of care. This was used alongside a costing interview guide and a sheet for the identification of cost items to be valued (both were adapted from the WHO CHOICE tool for CEA) [26]. In all participating facilities, ITNs were provided as a standard intervention to every pregnant woman during her first ANC visit, regardless of existing household net ownership. The nets distributed were long-lasting insecticidal nets (PermaNet® 2.0) supplied through the U.S. President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI) programme. The distribution process was well defined to ensure consistency across facilities: each pregnant woman received an ITN at her first ANC contact, accompanied by a brief, structured counselling session on correct hanging, use, and maintenance. Facility staff demonstrated net hanging techniques, provided tips for prolonging net lifespan, and addressed any misconceptions about use. ITN logistics/distribution included storage at the facility level, careful transportation from state-level depots to individual facilities, and allocation of dedicated staff time for stock management, record-keeping, and delivery of counselling on utilization of ITNs. For the IPTp-SP–only arm, all costs directly related to ITN procurement, distribution, and associated activities were excluded to ensure accurate estimation of the incremental costs attributable to the ITN component.

Collection and measurement of costs

Costs were collected from a provider’s perspective, and only costs of intervention borne at the public health facility level were included. Financial cost data were collected, and the economic value of the cost items for the intervention packages was derived to account for the opportunity cost of the next best alternative forgone. The ANC service delivery platform was used as a proxy for estimating the cost of interventions, as IPTp-SP administration and ITN distribution services are delivered as components of ANC services at the respective health facilities.

Measurement of the effects of IPTp and ITN interventions

The main health outcomes (for DALY estimation) measured were the prevalence of LBW and the prevalence of maternal anaemia at 36–40 weeks of gestation among enrolled pregnant women [27]. The health outcomes of 1000 pregnant women were studied. The health outcomes were transformed into DALYs lost. The (untreated) disability weights for LBW and associated disabilities and maternal anaemia were 0.291 and 0.012, respectively. At the same time, the life expectancies of male and female newborn babies in Nigeria were 53.4 and 55.6 years, respectively [25, 26, 30]. An average life expectancy of 54.5 years for both sexes and a discount rate of 3% for future life years were used to calculate the DALYs lost per LBW baby and per episode of maternal anaemia, respectively.

Total DALYs lost were calculated for the reference population of women in the study by multiplying the DALYs lost per LBW baby and maternal anaemia episode by the number of adverse health outcomes for the reference population, using the prevalence obtained in this study.

The total costs and effects, measured in DALYs lost, for each intervention group were used to calculate the incremental costs and effects. The ICERs of the IPTp-SP/ITN versus IPTp-SP-only intervention packages were calculated by subtracting the total cost of the IPTp-SP intervention group from the total cost of the IPTp-SP/ITN-only arm for the numerator and subtracting the total DALYs lost in the IPTp-SP/ITN arm from the total DALYs lost in the IPTp-SP-only arm for the denominator. This ICER was then interpreted as the incremental cost per DALY averted.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for cleaning and coding and then exported into software for statistics and data science (STATA) version 16 for analysis. Cost and DALY parameters were analysed for uncertainty to assess the robustness of the baseline ICERs. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was used to determine the influence of multivariate uncertainty in health outcomes and cost per intervention on the ICER. Probabilistic CEA was performed using a Monte Carlo simulation with the @Risk (version 5.0) add-in tool in Microsoft Excel 2016. Ten thousand iterations were performed in the simulations, and the results were presented using the ICER scatterplot. A threshold analysis of the cost-effectiveness of IPTp-SP/ITN compared to that of IPTp-SP was performed to estimate the cut-off points beyond which the intervention is no longer cost-effective. The mean probabilistic results from the PSA were presented at a 95% confidence interval. The threshold level of the ICERs used to define the cost-effective IPTp-SP/ITN intervention package was $ 150 per DALY averted, while $ 25 per DALY averted was the threshold used to define the intervention as highly cost-effective. These threshold figures were based on the set thresholds used in a previous study by the World Bank, which employed the World Bank’s definitions of acceptable cost-effective thresholds [28]. The Cost-Effectiveness Acceptability Curve (CEAC) analysis evaluated how cost-effective the combined IPTp-SP/ITN intervention is compared to IPTp-SP only at various willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds.

Table 4 presents the results of the probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), which evaluates the uncertainty in cost-effectiveness estimates for the IPTp-SP/ITN intervention compared to IPTp-SP alone. By incorporating a range of possible values, this analysis provides a more comprehensive assessment of how cost and health outcomes might vary under different conditions. Regarding cost, the IPTp-SP/ITN intervention remains more expensive than IPTp-SP alone. The base case results indicate that the total cost of IPTp-SP/ITN was US$759.8, while the cost of IPTp-SP-only was US$425.7. However, PSA results show that cost variations exist, with IPTp-SP/ITN averaging US$756.9 (95% CI 753.9–759.9) and IPTp-SP-only at US$425.2 (95% CI 423.6–426.8). Although the costs of IPTp-SP/ITN are consistently higher, the overall difference remains relatively stable, highlighting the potential affordability of the combined intervention. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which measures the additional cost required to avert one DALY, is a key finding in Table 4. The base case result indicates an ICER of US$7.2 per DALY averted, meaning that for every additional US$7.2 spent on IPTp-SP/ITN, one DALY is prevented. However, the PSA results suggest that this intervention may be even more cost-effective under different conditions, with an adjusted ICER of US$2.12 per DALY averted (95% CI 1.74–2.55). This implies that in most scenarios, IPTp-SP/ITN remains an economically favourable option for malaria prevention among pregnant women.

The other slopes in Fig. 1a reflect the ceiling ratios set at specific WTPs below which IPTp-SP/ITN compared to IPTp-SP is considered to be cost-effective and above which the converse applies. The ICER (7.2 US$/DALY) from this study, as shown in Fig. 1a, is lower than the set WTPs at 25 US$/DALY and 150 US$/DALY, which indicates IPTp-SP/ITN being more cost-effective than IPTp-SP only.

Discussion

The findings from this study demonstrate the relative cost-effectiveness of intermittent preventive therapy using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) in combination with insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) compared to IPTp-SP alone for malaria prevention among pregnant women in Jos North, Plateau State, Nigeria. The analysis shows that while the total cost of the intervention was higher for the IPTp-SP/ITN group (US$231.98) compared to IPTp-SP only (US$131.79), the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of US$2.12 per disability-adjusted life year (DALY) averted indicates that the combined intervention is a more efficient strategy in reducing the burden of malaria-related complications in pregnancy. This aligns with previous studies highlighting the benefits of combining ITNs with chemoprophylaxis in improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes [15, 19].

The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve (CEAC) shows that the probability of IPTp-SP/ITN being more cost-effective than IPTp-SP only at WTP set at 150 US$/DALY is about 60%. This suggests that, while effective, the intervention's cost-effectiveness remains limited within lower willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds, emphasizing the need for more cost-efficient delivery strategies, particularly within low-resource settings [28, 29]. Previous research in Mozambique and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) supports the conclusion that combining ITNs with IPTp-SP is cost-effective when compliance with ITN use is high and interventions are well-integrated into antenatal care (ANC) services [15, 19].

The results from the probabilistic sensitivity analysis further reinforce the robustness of these findings. Monte Carlo simulations revealed that the IPTp-SP/ITN intervention had a higher probability of being cost-effective when the WTP threshold was increased. However, the ICER scatterplot showed that a proportion of iterations fell into the northwest quadrant, indicating scenarios where IPTp-SP alone was more cost-effective than IPTp-SP/ITN. This highlights the importance of contextual factors such as adherence to ITN use, the effectiveness of ANC service integration, and external funding mechanisms in determining the overall cost-effectiveness of malaria prevention interventions [19, 28].

A key challenge in implementing IPTp-SP/ITN interventions remains the fragmented nature of funding and service delivery. While IPTp-SP is often provided free of charge in many settings under the President’s Malaria Initiative (PMI), ITN distribution relies on separate funding streams, leading to potential inconsistencies in access and utilization [33, 36]. The Mozambican study, which demonstrated higher cost-effectiveness probabilities for IPTp-SP/ITN, benefited from a societal perspective that accounted for household cost savings and externalities, whereas this study adopted a provider perspective, potentially underestimating the broader economic benefits of the intervention [15, 29].

Moreover, the study did not assess long-term economic consequences, such as the impact of preventing low birth weight (LBW) on future health expenditures and productivity losses. Previous studies suggest that the economic burden of LBW extends beyond immediate healthcare costs, influencing long-term developmental outcomes and increasing susceptibility to non-communicable diseases later in life [16, 17]. Future research should incorporate modelling techniques to capture these long-term economic implications and provide a more comprehensive cost-effectiveness analysis.

Another limitation of this study is its exclusion of household-level costs and indirect economic benefits. Studies in Uganda and the DRC have shown that accounting for broader societal costs, such as transportation, caregiver burden, and productivity losses, can significantly alter cost-effectiveness outcomes [19, 28, 32]. This suggests that a more inclusive analytical approach, incorporating both health system and household perspectives, is needed to fully evaluate the economic impact of IPTp-SP/ITN interventions in Nigeria.

Furthermore, the results indicate that while IPTp-SP/ITN is more effective in reducing maternal anaemia and low birth weight, challenges related to adherence, accessibility, and implementation costs must be addressed to maximize its benefits [31]. A significant factor influencing cost-effectiveness is the efficiency of ANC service delivery. Studies have shown that integrating malaria interventions within ANC services leads to improved health outcomes and cost savings [29, 34]. However, ensuring uniform access to IPTp-SP and ITNs across all health facilities remains a major public health challenge. The findings from this study underscore the need for stronger health system support, improved logistics, and community engagement to enhance intervention uptake and sustainability.

Another critical issue is the potential variability in malaria transmission dynamics across different regions. The effectiveness of IPTp-SP/ITN may differ in high and low transmission areas, affecting cost-effectiveness estimates. Studies have reported that in low-transmission areas, IPTp-SP alone may be sufficient, whereas in high-transmission regions, ITN use significantly enhances malaria prevention efforts [15, 28, 35]. Policymakers should therefore consider tailoring malaria prevention strategies based on local epidemiological contexts to ensure optimal resource allocation.

Additionally, community engagement and health education play a crucial role in improving intervention uptake. Previous research has highlighted that misconceptions about IPTp-SP and ITNs, as well as cultural beliefs, can influence adherence rates [20, 25]. Targeted awareness campaigns, along with behavioural change interventions, could enhance compliance and improve the overall cost-effectiveness of the intervention.

Despite these limitations, the findings highlight critical policy implications. The cost-effectiveness of IPTp-SP/ITN could be improved by integrating ANC services under a unified funding mechanism, ensuring consistent ITN distribution, and enhancing community-based interventions to increase ITN utilization. Additionally, targeted subsidies or financial incentives for pregnant women could further improve adherence and maximize health benefits.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that IPTp-SP/ITN is a cost-effective strategy for malaria prevention among pregnant women in Jos North, Plateau State, Nigeria. However, its cost-effectiveness is contingent upon higher WTP thresholds and effective integration into ANC services. Policymakers should consider optimizing funding mechanisms and enhancing service delivery models to improve intervention efficiency. Future research should explore long-term economic impacts and incorporate broader cost perspectives to strengthen policy recommendations for malaria prevention in pregnancy.
