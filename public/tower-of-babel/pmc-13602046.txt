Gastric Acid Suppression in Peritoneal Dialysis: An Old Question Revisited With a New Study Design

In the article by Fan et al.,1 the authors examine the association between gastric acid suppression (GAS) and peritonitis in patients with peritoneal dialysis (PD). Peritonitis remains one of the leading causes of hospitalization, technique failure, and mortality for patients with PD.2 As such, PD programs devote considerable effort to technique training, exit-site care, constipation prevention, and antibiotic prophylaxis during procedures.2

GAS, with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs), is commonly prescribed in the general population and the PD population. Observational studies have shown conflicting results, but the prevailing interpretation has been that the main risk of peritonitis with GAS in patients with PD lies with H2RAs rather than PPIs.3,4 This is reflected in the 2022 International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis peritonitis guidelines, which caution against H2RAs for peritonitis risk, but not against PPIs.2

The results of these past studies have been counterintuitive. A biologically plausible mechanism links GAS and the risk of peritonitis through mechanisms, such as dysbiosis of the gut microbiome, along with overpopulation of pathogenic bacteria and subsequent translocation across the intestinal barrier.5 Despite the more profound GAS by PPIs, past studies have implicated H2RAs more strongly.3,4 This paradox is the rationale for the study by Fan et al.1

One potential limitation of past observational studies has been immortal time bias. If patients are classified on the basis of whether they received PPI at any point during the follow-up period, time preceding PPI initiation poses an issue.1 Those who initiate PPI therapy late in the study period therefore have preceding unexposed time that is attributed to PPI exposure. This event-free pre-exposure period can make PPI use appear less harmful than it actually is.

This study by Fan et al.1 addresses the issue of immortal time bias through their study design. Rather than performing another conventional retrospective cohort analysis, the authors used a sequential target trial emulation which redesigns their analysis of retrospective data to mimic a hypothetical prospective randomized clinical trial. Instead of asking, “who received GAS at any point in the follow up period?,” they asked a more meaningful question—among patients eligible for GAS today, what is the risk of peritonitis or death if they initiate GAS today compared with no initiation?

To accomplish this, each month in the retrospective data was treated as the beginning of a new emulated trial. Eligibility and treatment initiation were reassessed monthly, with each new month serving as a new “time zero.” This aligned exposure with the beginning of follow-up and reduced time-zero misalignment and immortal time bias. At the beginning of each month, patients were followed in an intention-to-treat framework to determine whether GAS was associated with subsequent peritonitis and death. The authors also used inverse probability of treatment weighting and doubly robust adjustment to address confounding by indication, because patients who receive GAS may be sicker than those who do not.

This study included 11,693 individuals, which contributed to 240,431 person-trials. PPI initiation was associated with a higher risk of peritonitis (hazard ratio: 1.53; 95% confidence interval: 1.31–1.80), all-cause mortality (hazard ratio: 1.55; 95% confidence interval: 1.43–1.67), and peritonitis-related mortality (hazard ratio: 1.39; 95% confidence interval: 1.05–1.58). H2RAs were also associated with an increased risk of peritonitis and all-cause mortality, although the magnitude of associations was smaller. The authors also performed a head-to-head analysis of PPIs and H2RAs, and there was no statistically significant difference in peritonitis between the 2, although peritonitis-specific mortality was higher with PPIs.

The main contribution of this study is that it uses target trial emulation and propensity weighting to address 2 fundamentally different methodological problems with retrospective observational studies. Target trial emulation improves the timing and structure of the comparison by aligning treatment initiation with the beginning of follow-up between exposure groups, thereby reducing immortal time bias. Inverse probability of treatment weighting on the basis of propensity scores and doubly robust adjustment addresses confounding by indication because patients prescribed PPIs are more likely to have gastrointestinal bleeding, peptic ulcer disease, corticosteroid exposure, recent hospitalization, or other comorbidities that could contribute to the risk of peritonitis and death. These 2 complementary approaches strengthen the study design by addressing distinct sources of bias.

Despite the strength of the study design used in this article, the authors note that residual confounding still exists. Unmeasured variables, such as socioeconomic indicators, could plausibly influence peritonitis and mortality, although an extensively used data set was used for the study. Second, the outcomes of peritonitis and death were identified through diagnostic codes, which could introduce misclassification of the outcomes. In addition, non-GAS initiators may be healthier than noninitiators as well as the fact that confounding by indication still exists as underlying gastrointestinal illness may independently contribute to adverse outcomes. The authors addressed this potential bias through multiple statistical methods, though. Lastly, there were no peritoneal fluid cultures available in the data set which prevented evaluating differences in causative organisms among the study groups. Nonetheless, the authors note these limitations, and more importantly, used well-considered statistical handling of the data to construct the most realistic view of reality from the data available.

This study is important because it raises the possibility that previous studies, using less rigorous methods, may have underestimated the association between PPI use and peritonitis. Nevertheless, the findings should not be used to support widespread deprescribing of PPIs because they are appropriate for those with clear indications. Furthermore, because PPIs and H2RAs were both associated with an increased risk of peritonitis compared with no GAS, this study does not support routine switching from one class to another to reduce peritonitis risk. What this study does support is reassessment of GAS in patients with PD and an ongoing clear indication for their continued use. Future studies will be needed to clarify the association of GAS and peritonitis in patients PD, but this study represents an important step forward.
