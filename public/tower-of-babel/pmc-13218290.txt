A case series of 21 adult cows with acute infectious bronchopneumonia: clinical, laboratory, and thoracic ultrasonographic findings
Authors: Nicolas Masset, Maud Rouault, Romane Gindrey, Claire Marget, Sébastien Assié, Vincent Herry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute infectious bronchopneumonia is widely reported in adult cattle, yet most descriptions focus on postmortem findings.Hypothesis/ObjectivesTo describe signalment, clinical, hematological, and ultrasonographic findings in affected cows, and to identify pathogens and clinical findings associated with outcome.AnimalsTwenty-one cows from 11 farms.MethodsProspective case series. All cows underwent clinical examination, thoracic ultrasonography, bronchoalveolar lavage for culture and PCR, and CBC. Thoracic ultrasonography was repeated to assess lesion dynamics after antibiotic treatment. Outcomes were classified as favorable (clinical recovery and maintained milk production) or unfavorable (death or reduced milk production leading to premature drying-off and early culling). Descriptive statistics were computed, and univariate analyses were performed to identify variables associated with outcome.ResultsMean age at inclusion was 47 months (range, 24-104). Cases occurred mainly in winter (10/21) and during the early postpartum period (10/21); 17/21 were dairy cows. Typical clinical signs associated with acute respiratory disease (coughing, wheezes, and crackles) were rarely observed. Thoracic ultrasonography revealed frequent consolidations (17/21) and pleural effusion (13/21), with lesion size decreasing over time. Mannheimia haemolytica and Pasteurella multocida were identified by PCR in bronchoalveolar lavage samples in 13/21 and 18/21 cases, respectively. Unfavorable outcomes (11/21) were associated with nasal discharge, left shift on CBC, reduced ruminal contractions, higher respiratory rate on day 0, and deeper consolidation lesions on days 0 and 5.Conclusions and clinical importanceUltrasonography is a valuable cow-side tool that supports diagnosis and provides key information on the clinical course of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in adult cows.Keywords: adult, bovine respiratory disease, cattle, diagnosis, pneumonia, thoracic ultrasonography

Introduction

The bovine respiratory disease (BRD) complex encompasses a wide range of etiologies and pathogenic processes.1 Among lower respiratory tract disorders, infectious bronchopneumonia, caused by airborne pulmonary infections with viruses and bacteria, is a major health concern that leads to relevant economic losses in livestock production systems.2 It primarily affects young cattle across various production types, including beef and feedlot calves, pre- and postweaning dairy replacement heifers, and veal calves.3–6

In contrast, the incidence of infectious bronchopneumonia is considerably lower in adult cattle.7 However, in recent years, veterinarians have reported an increasing number of cases, sometimes as outbreaks with associated death.8 This trend has been supported by several necropsy studies, which report a considerable proportion of cases, and in some studies an apparent increase over time, of infectious bronchopneumonia, particularly due to Mannheimia haemolytica (Mh).8–10

However, the clinical diagnosis of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in adult cattle remains challenging.11 In most studies, there is no clear definition of clinical deviation from normal, and no validation of diagnostic combinations of clinical signs that can be reliably applied. Many diagnostic criteria are extrapolated from knowledge of the disease in young cattle.11 In this context, thoracic ultrasonography (TUS) represents a promising tool, as it allows direct visualization of pulmonary lesions and may enhance diagnostic accuracy. However, its performance and applicability in adult cattle for diagnosing infectious bronchopneumonia remain insufficiently documented, in contrast to calves, in which its use has been extensively studied.12–15 Studies addressing its use in adult cattle, in which body size restricts ultrasonographic examination,16 remain scarce,17–20 and none have specifically investigated its role in the diagnosis and monitoring of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in these animals.

The main objective of this study was to describe the signalment, clinical presentation and ancillary diagnostic findings (TUS, hematology and microbiological analysis on bronchoalveolar lavage [BAL] samples), treatments and follow-up examinations in cows over 2 years of age diagnosed with acute infectious bronchopneumonia. A secondary objective was to identify potential factors associated with an unfavorable outcome (ie, death, reduced milk production leading to early drying off and early culling). Our hypotheses were that (1) cows diagnosed with acute infectious bronchopneumonia would exhibit consistent clinical, ultrasonographic, hematologic, and microbiological patterns that could be characterized through a descriptive approach; and (2) specific clinical, ultrasonographic, or laboratory findings would be associated with an unfavorable outcome. In a subset with available blood cultures, we explored whether bacteremia occurs and whether it is associated with unfavorable outcomes.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in a private practice in France between 2022 and 2023 in compliance with the French regulations on experimental animal care under the authorization of Oniris Ethics Committee for Clinical and Epidemiological Veterinary Research (Approval number, CERVO-2022-19-V, Approval date, October 8, 2022).

Study design and animals

Eligible animals for this study were cows over 2 years of age, with a duration of clinical signs ≤ 5 days, and without prior antibiotic treatment. At the herd level, only farms free from bovine herpesvirus type 1 (BoHV-1), bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV), and Mycobacterium bovis were eligible. Cases were prospectively recruited by 2 experienced field practitioners over a 1-year period during routine farm visits for animals reported sick by the farmers. Each cow initially underwent a standardized clinical examination, followed systematically by reticular and thoracic ultrasonography. All cows that met the clinical criteria (rectal temperature ≥ 39.0°C and tachypnea ≥ 36 breaths per minute) and exhibited ultrasonographic abnormalities (consolidation ≥ 1 cm in depth, pleural effusion, ≥ 3 B-line artifacts within a single intercostal space, or a combination of these findings) were enrolled in the study. Cows that were normothermic at the time of inclusion but had previously been detected as febrile (≥39.0°C) by the farmer and treated only with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) before initial clinical examination were included in the study.

Cases with signs of respiratory disease consistent with reticuloperitonitis (abdominal effusion and modifications in the amplitude and frequency of reticular contractions on ultrasonography), embolic pneumonia (diagnosed by multifocal small (1-2 cm) lung consolidations across all thoracic fields on TUS), or parasitic bronchitis due to Dictyocaulus viviparus (diagnosed by fecal analysis using the Baermann sedimentation technique, in a grazing context, and with supportive clinical findings such as spontaneous coughing) were excluded.

To avoid overrepresentation of a single farm and potential clustering effects, as well as for practical reasons of workload and feasibility, a maximum of 3 cases per farm were included.

Procedures

At inclusion

Signalment, history, and clinical examination

For each case, the following historical information was collected: breed, date of birth, and parity. The case history included physiological stage (eg, lactation or dry period), date of last calving, reason for veterinary consultation, consultation date (defined as day 0), onset of clinical signs (duration between the onset of clinical signs observed by the farmer and day 0), milk yield before disease onset (dairy cows only), and any treatments administered before inclusion (ie, NSAIDs). All cows underwent a complete clinical examination, including a general assessment (body condition score)21; demeanor (normal or depressed); hydration status (evaluated using an 8-point scale based on skin tent duration, degree of eyeball recession, and mucous membrane moisture extrapolated from dehydration scoring systems developed for calves)22,23; mucous membrane color, a cardiac examination (heart rate and auscultation including assessment of heart rhythm and the presence or absence of murmurs), and a digestive examination (rumen fill score24; ruminal contraction frequency, considered normal when 3 contractions occurred within 2 min; fecal consistency).25 A thorough respiratory examination was also performed, documenting nasal discharge (presence and type; serous, mucous, muco-purulent, or purulent), cough (presence and character; spontaneous, induced), expiratory grunting, active expiration, nasal flaring, and open-mouth breathing. Respiratory rate was measured, and bilateral thoracic auscultation was performed to evaluate lung sounds and detect any adventitious sounds (crackles, wheezes, or pleural friction rubs).

Thoracic ultrasonography

On day 0, a bilateral TUS was performed from the 4th to the 10th intercostal space (ICS). The lung field was clipped on both sides and sprayed with 70% alcohol before probe application. Two different ultrasound machines were used in this study: an Easi-Scan:Go Curve device (IMV Imaging, France) equipped with a 3-8.5 MHz broadband curved linear probe, and a MyLab One Vet device (Esaote, Italy) equipped with a 2.5-6.6 MHz sectorial probe. The ultrasound imaging depth was individually adjusted for each case, ranging from 8 to 20 cm depending on the extent of the lesions. The following parameters were recorded: the maximal depth of consolidation lesions as described earlier,26 the maximal depth of pleural effusion, and the maximal number of B-lines within a single intercostal space, by ICS (4-10) and dorsoventral extension (the thorax was divided into 3 equal parts, dorsal, middle, or ventral). For each cow, lung consolidation was scored using a 5-point graduated scale based on the maximal depth of consolidation lesion observed across the entire lung examination field: score 0 corresponding to no lung consolidation or consolidation less than 1 cm in depth; score 1 to consolidation between 1 and 3 cm; score 2 to consolidation between 3 and 5 cm; score 3 to consolidation between 5 and 10 cm; and score 4 to consolidation more than 10 cm. A superficial lesion less than 1 cm in depth was scored as 0 in order to avoid any misclassification with pleural thickening and/or pleural irregularity.12 This scoring system for consolidation was adapted for adult cows from published classifications commonly used in calves, with modifications to account for deeper consolidations, by extending the thresholds commonly used in calves (1 and 3 cm).27 Similarly, pleural effusion was scored using a 5-point scale based on the maximal depth of the pleural effusion across the entire lung examination field: score 0 corresponding to no effusion; score 1 to effusion less than 1 cm; score 2 to effusion between 1 and 3 cm; score 3 to effusion between 3 and 5 cm; and score 4 to effusion greater than 5 cm in extension. This scoring system for pleural effusion was newly created for the purposes of this study, as no suitable published classification was available. All TUS examinations were performed by 2 experienced specialist practitioners (the first and last authors).

Blood analysis

On day 0, blood samples were collected by coccygeal venipuncture into EDTA-coated Vacutainer tubes for CBC analysis. Samples were gently mixed, stored at 4-8°C, and analyzed within 2 h using the Procyte Dx Hematology Analyzer (IDEXX Laboratories, USA). Results were verified by manual differential counts of 100 leukocytes on stained blood smears. Band neutrophils were identified according to standard morphological criteria (nonsegmented, horseshoe-shaped nucleus). A left shift was defined as band neutrophils representing more than 3% of the total neutrophil count.28

Hemocultures were performed in a subset of 10 cows (the first 10 cows included in the study whenever sampling was technically feasible) to investigate a possible bacteremia associated with acute infectious bronchopneumonia. Blood was aseptically taken with sterile gloves using a 18-gauge needle and a 30 mL syringe from the jugular vein, after clipping, scrubbing (chlorhexidine digluconate 4%, HydeaClean, Savetis, France) and disinfection (70% alcohol, Savetis, France).29 Two blood culture bottles (BacT/ALERT SA/SN, bioMérieux, France) were filled using new sterile needles after disinfection of the lid with 70% alcohol.29 Samples were transported to the clinic and stored at ambient temperature, then shipped to an external laboratory on the same day or the following day, always at ambient temperature. Bacterial identification was performed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry after 48 h incubation in culture bottles and spreading onto a nonselective media.

Bronchoalveolar lavage and microbiological analysis

On day 0, a non-endoscopic, unguarded BAL was performed using the same technique as described in calves,30 with a sterile 3-m silicone balloon catheter. The procedure was performed in standing animals without sedation. After cleaning the nostril with 70% alcohol, the catheter was inserted medioventrally into the nasal cavity and advanced until reaching a bronchus. A volume of 120 mL sterile 0.9% NaCl was instilled into the lungs and immediately aspirated (at least half of the instilled volume was recovered). Samples were transported at ambient temperature to the clinic, then stored and shipped to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions.

For bacteriological analysis, BAL samples were inoculated onto Columbia agar with 5% sheep blood and chocolate agar for the isolation of Pasteurellaceae and incubated for 24-48 h (for up to 72 h) at 37°C in 5% CO₂. Identification of isolates was achieved using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (VITEK MS system and the SARAMIS database, bioMérieux, France). The M. haemolytica strains were serotyped. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted by the disk diffusion method according to standardized protocols. In parallel, real-time PCR (RT-PCR) analyses were conducted using a commercial kit (VetMAX Ruminant Respiratory Screening kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, France) to detect bovine respiratory syncytial virus (BRSV), bovine parainfluenza virus type 3 (BPI3V), bovine coronavirus (BCoV), Pasteurella multocida (Pm), Mh, Histophilus somni (Hs), and Mycoplasmopsis bovis (Mb).

Treatment

On day 0, both dairy and beef cows received an intravenous injection of a combination of oxytetracycline (10 mg/kg, Oxytetracycline 10%, Vetoquinol, France) and tylosin (10 mg/kg, Pharmasin 200 mg/mL, Huvepharma, Belgium), along with a NSAID (carprofen, 1.4 mg/kg, intravenous; Rimadyl 50 mg/mL, Zoetis, France, or flunixin, 2.2 mg/kg, intravenous; Genixine 50 mg/mL, Ceva, France, or ketoprofen, 3 mg/kg, intravenous; Ketofen 10%, Ceva, France, or tolfenamic acid, 2 mg/kg, intravenous; Tolfine, Vetoquinol, France) unless already administered by the farmer. Farmers continued the treatment intramuscularly for 4 additional days. All treatments were used in compliance with French and European regulations on antimicrobial use. Pharmasin is registered for intravenous administration in cattle, in addition to intramuscular use, for both dairy and beef animals. The combination used was selected to take advantage of its documented synergistic effect,31 aiming to enhance therapeutic efficacy while remaining within the category of non-critically important antimicrobials. Indeed, although tetracyclines are considered first-line antimicrobials for the treatment of bovine pneumonia, recent antimicrobial resistance reports have shown high resistance rates among Pm (52%) and Mh (22%) isolates.32

During follow-up

Clinical and TUS follow-ups were performed on day 5 using the same procedures as during the initial examination. If signs of pneumonia persisted (clinical signs, TUS lesions, or both), antibiotic treatment was extended for another 5 days, and new clinical and TUS examinations were conducted on day 10.

Outcomes

Follow-up examinations were carried out by phone on day 30 and in December 2023 by a single investigator. This corresponded to a minimum follow-up period of 8 months for the last included case and up to 20 months for the first included case. The owners of the cows were asked about the outcome of their cows. Animals were classified into 2 groups. The unfavorable outcome group included cows that had died, exhibited low milk production that resulted in premature dry-off and/or early culling. The favorable outcome group included cows that recovered clinically with a recovery of milk production and continued their lactation and productive career normally within the herd. Milk production was evaluated at a single time point by comparing the yield before disease onset with the yield recorded on day 30 after inclusion. A decrease of up to 20% was tolerated for the favorable outcome group to account for normal day-to-day variability and the expected decline associated with advancing days in milk.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive statistical analysis was performed. Regardless of their distributions, quantitative variables were summarized by means, SDs, medians, and ranges, and qualitative variables were characterized by frequency of occurrence. For descriptive purposes, we assessed the between-TUS dynamics of lung consolidation and pleural effusion, defined as the change in maximal consolidation depth and the change in maximal effusion depth between successive examinations. To evaluate the impact of performing unilateral TUS, agreement between left- and right-side consolidations and pleural effusion on day 0 was assessed using the Cohen’s test, and the prevalence-adjusted and bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK) was calculated to account for prevalence bias if necessary. Kappa agreement was considered poor if < 0; slight if 0 ≤ ≤ 0.20; fair if 0.21 ≤ ≤ 0.40; moderate if 0.41 ≤ ≤ 0.60; substantial if 0.61 ≤ ≤ 0.80; and almost perfect if > 0.80.33

Cases were grouped by outcome as described previously, and univariate analyses were performed to identify associated factors (historical, clinical and ultrasonographic findings at days 0, 5, and 10, laboratory results). Fisher’s exact test was used for binary (breed, depressed demeanor, nasal flaring, nasal discharge, expiratory dyspnea, decreased and increased of intensity of breathing sound, wheezes, crackling sounds, audible expiratory grunt, ptyalism, poor ruminal score, decreased ruminal contractions, immature neutrophils, Mh PCR, and Pm PCR) and categorical variables (farm, parity, mucosal color, abnormal feces, and dorsoventral extension). Student’s t-test or the nonparametric Wilcoxon test were used for continuous variables depending on distribution (days in milk, age, onset of clinical signs before inclusion, rectal temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, PCV, leukocytes and segmented neutrophils at inclusion, maximal depth of consolidation, and maximal depth of pleural effusion). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.R-project.org). The significance threshold used was 0.05.

Microbiology on BAL samples

Table 6 summarizes the results of microbiological analyses on BAL samples. Bacteria isolated by culture were Mh (33%, 7/21), Pm (10%, 2/21), Bibersteinia trehalosi (5%, 1/21), Bacillus spp. (5%, 1/21), and Proteus mirabilis (5%, 1/21). Five BAL samples were contaminated and 5 yielded no bacterial growth. Serotyping was performed on 5 Mh isolates, all belonging to the serotype A6. Bacteria detected by RT-PCR were Pm (86%, 18/21), Mh (62%, 13/21), Mb (14%, 3/21), and Hs (14%, 3/21). Bovine respiratory syncytial virus was detected in only one sample (5%, 1/21), while neither BPI3V nor BCoV was detected.

Temporal evolution of TUS results

One cow died between visits 1 and 2. Two and 6 cows were, respectively, not examined at visit 2 and visit 3 due to farmers’ refusal or unavailability. One cow was not examined at visit 2 due to subcutaneous emphysema but examined at visit 3. Five cows had no lesion at visit 2 and were not examined at visit 3 and considered recovered. The between-TUS dynamics of lung consolidation and pleural effusion are shown in Figures 3 and 4, respectively, and display a progressive decrease over time for both parameters. Overall, the cows with the most important lesions of consolidation on day 10 were those that initially had the most relevant lesions.

Outcomes

A total of 10 cows had a favorable outcome, whereas 11 cows experienced an unfavorable outcome, including 1 cow that died and 10 cows that showed decreased milk production leading to premature dry-off and early culling. Based on univariate statistical analysis, cases exhibiting nasal discharge of any type (serous, mucous, muco-purulent, or purulent) (P = .012), decreased ruminal contraction frequency (P = .004), or a left shift in the blood smear (P = .035) at inclusion were more likely to have an unfavorable outcome compared with those without nasal discharge, with normal ruminal contraction frequency or without a left shift. In addition, cows with favorable outcomes showed a significantly lower dorsoventral extension of consolidation lesions on day 5 (P = .002) than cows with unfavorable outcomes. Cows with favorable outcomes had significantly lower respiratory rates on day 0 (P = .009) and smaller consolidation lesions on day 0 (P = .002) and day 5 (P = .002) compared with those with unfavorable outcomes (Figure 5).

Discussion

Our study shows that in adult cows with acute infectious bronchopneumonia, the most common clinical findings, in addition to hyperthermia and tachypnea, are a poor ruminal filling score, modifications (increased or decreased) in the intensity of breathing sounds on thoracic auscultation, nasal discharge, and decreased ruminal contraction frequency. Thoracic ultrasound is a valuable diagnostic tool for the diagnosis of this disease in adult cows. Negative outcomes are associated with the presence of nasal discharge, decreased ruminal contraction frequency, a left shift on the blood smear, a higher respiratory rate on day 0, and deeper consolidation lesions on days 0 and 5. Although several studies and case reports have described clinical or thoracic imaging findings in adult cattle, these typically involved very small numbers of animals and often focused on pleuropneumonia secondary to reticuloperitonitis19,20 or on chronic cases17 rather than on acute infectious bronchopneumonia. However, the clinical signs observed in acute infectious bronchopneumonia may differ from those reported in these cases. Moreover, unlike calves or young beef cattle, adult cows often present with subtle respiratory signs, making clinical diagnosis of acute infectious bronchopneumonia more challenging. Indeed, in half of the cases, farmers did not identify any respiratory warning signs. In our study sample, coughing and adventitious sounds at auscultation such as crackles or wheezes were rarely observed, in contrast with reports in calves.15,34 In addition, early manifestations were often nonspecific and included digestive signs such as anorexia and cessation of rumination, as well as decreased milk production. These differences hinder timely diagnosis and highlight the limitations of relying on clinical examination alone.

The use of TUS to confirm the diagnosis of acute infectious bronchopneumonia or to establish a prognosis is well established in young cattle, but remains poorly documented in adult cows.12,35 In adult cattle, only one study has described TUS findings in cases of chronic suppurative pneumonia.17 In that study, bilateral lesions extending more than 10-15 cm above the level of the olecranon were associated with poor prognosis.17 In our study sample, cows with less extensive consolidation lesions at inclusion and on day 5 had a more favorable outcome than those with deeper lesions. This might be explained by the fact that cows with shallow consolidation lesions are likely at an earlier stage of the disease and therefore are more likely to respond to treatment.36 Indeed, the pathogenesis of infectious bronchopneumonia involves initial lung lesions developing in the cranial lobes following airborne bacterial infection, with potential subsequent extension to the more caudal lobes.37 Furthermore, consolidation lesions located in ICS 1-3 are not detectable by TUS due to limited access caused by the forelimb musculature.35,38

Focused or unilateral thoracic ultrasound approaches have been proposed to optimize field-based diagnostics, reducing examination time while maintaining acceptable agreement with more complete protocols.14,39 Agreement regarding the distribution of consolidation lesions or pleural effusion between both sides ranged from fair to substantial (the wide 95% CIs indicate substantial uncertainty about the true level of agreement, due to the small sample size), supporting the recommendation to perform a bilateral thoracic examination as part of the diagnostic work-up in individual cases. These observations are consistent with other studies, which reported kappa values for agreement on the presence of consolidation between both sides were fair (κ = 0.33) in preweaned dairy calves12 and substantial (κ = 0.62) in young beef cattle.40

Most cases (15/21) occurred during the autumn or winter seasons, and nearly half of the cases (10/21) were observed within the first 2 months postpartum, suggesting that these periods may represent increased risk. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting a higher prevalence of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in cows during autumn and winter.9,10,41 Low temperatures, increased relative humidity, and rapid changes in climatic conditions have been identified as risk factors for BRD.41–44 Recent calving has also been suggested as a risk factor.10 Indeed, many cows in the postpartum period experience an exacerbation of inflammation, which impairs their immune function and increases their susceptibility to disease.45

Mannheimia haemolytica was identified by culture as the most prevalent pathogen responsible for acute infectious bronchopneumonia in our adult cattle study sample, which is consistent with observations from necropsy-based studies.8–10 Mannheimia haemolytica was detected by PCR in 61.9% of BAL samples. This finding is consistent with previous necropsy-based studies, in which Mh was identified in lung samples in 69.2% of cases in the study by Dorso et al. and in 60.4% of cases in the study by Burrows et al.9,10 These comparable results across studies reinforce the predominant role of Mh in the etiology of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in adult cattle, highlighting its importance as a primary bacterial pathogen in this population. All Mh isolates belonged to serotype A6, and none exhibited antimicrobial resistance. These observations are consistent with those of Het Lam et al., who reported that isolates from cows with acute fibrinous pleuropneumonia were predominantly serotypes A1 or A6, with few antimicrobial resistance genes detected.41

Several factors might explain the 5 negative cultures obtained. First, the bacterial load could be very low in some cases, which can limit culture sensitivity.46 Bronchoalveolar lavage procedures were performed blindly, which might have resulted in sampling from bronchi or lung areas that were not affected by the infectious process.47 This sampling variability could contribute to negative or low-yield culture results despite the presence of clinical or ultrasonographic evidence of pneumonia, highlighting the value of molecular diagnostics when conventional culture methods lack sensitivity. Second, the culture media and incubation conditions used were not suitable for the isolation of Mycoplasma spp.48: 2 BAL samples were negative for culture but positive for Mb by PCR.

In our study, PCR analysis revealed a higher detection rate of Pm compared with Mh, in contrast to the results obtained from conventional bacterial culture. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors. First, Pm is known to grow more slowly and may be overgrown by faster-growing contaminants during culture, potentially leading to false-negative results. Second, contamination of non-endoscopic, unguarded BAL samples by upper respiratory tract microbiota is a well-documented issue that can affect both culture-based and molecular diagnostics. In particular, studies have shown that Pm is more sensitive to the presence of contaminant flora than Mh, as microbial overgrowth can inhibit its recovery in culture.49 In contrast, PCR remains sensitive for detecting target bacterial DNA, regardless of competitive growth dynamics.

Bibersteinia trehalosi was isolated in one case of acute infectious bronchopneumonia with an unfavorable outcome, with the strain showing resistance to tetracyclines. Although B. trehalosi is more commonly associated with respiratory disease in sheep, it has also been identified as an emerging opportunistic pathogen in cattle. A case report by Cortese et al. described peracute to acute fatal pneumonia outbreaks in feedlot cattle attributed to B. trehalosi, emphasizing its potential pathogenicity in bovines. Although rare, B. trehalosi should be considered in acute respiratory cases with poor prognosis.50

Neither viruses nor Mb appeared to play a primary pathogenic role in cases of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in our study sample. Indeed, BRSV was detected only once, in association with Pm, and Mb was detected 3 times (once alone, once in combination with Mh, and once with both Mh and Pm). Neither BPI3V nor BCoV was detected. In the study by Dorso et al., BRSV was isolated in only 1 out of 26 cases, BPI3V was not detected, BCoV was found in 8 out of 26 cases, and Mb in 3 out of 26 cases.9 In another study, BRSV was identified in 8 out of 55 cases and Mb in 6 out of 24 cases.10 Our study, which included only acute cases of infectious bronchopneumonia, tends to confirm the findings of the 2 previous necropsy studies suggesting that viral involvement, similar to Mycoplasma, is not an obligatory step in adult cattle. This contrasts with the established role of viral infections in calves, where viruses are considered primary initiators of bovine respiratory disease that predispose animals to secondary bacterial infections.51

Blood cultures were mainly negative in our cases or yielded results considered to be contaminants (Enterococcus spp. and Bacillus spp.). However, Bacillus spp. was isolated from both blood culture and BAL sample from the same animal, suggesting possible bacteremia. These findings indicate that the primary bacterial agents identified in the lungs, namely, Mh and Pm, were not associated with bacteremia in the studied animals. Although certain strains of Mh52 and Pm53 have been reported to cause bacteremia, our results support the hypothesis that these pathogens remain localized to the respiratory tract in adult cattle with acute infectious bronchopneumonia, without evidence of systemic dissemination.

The inclusion of only herds free from BoHV-1, BVDV, and M. bovis may limit the external validity of our findings, as such herds are not representative of the general cattle population. This criterion was nevertheless chosen to avoid confounding factors and ensure that acute bronchopneumonia cases were not misclassified.

Due to the study design and the absence of a control group, it is not possible to determine whether all consolidations observed were exclusively associated with the acute disease episode, which represents a limitation of our study. Indeed, a slaughterhouse investigation reported that pulmonary consolidations may also be present in apparently healthy culled cows: among 1461 slaughtered animals, severe lesions (>50% consolidation of any single lung lobe) and mild lesions (<50% consolidation of any single lung lobe) were identified in 10.3% and 23.5% of cows, respectively.54 Although it cannot be ruled out that a proportion of these animals had been clinically affected at some point, these findings indicate that lung consolidations may persist as sequelae of previous pneumonia episodes. However, among the 14 cows in our study with consolidation lesions on day 0 and with follow-up examinations on day 5 and/or day 10, dynamic changes (either progression or regression) in pulmonary consolidations were observed in all cases, suggesting that these lesions were indeed active. Chronic pulmonary lesions could theoretically be reactivated after a stressful event such as calving, but this appears unlikely in our study. Indeed, Trueperella pyogenes, a common bacterial isolate in chronic pneumonia in cattle,17 was never isolated in our study sample.

In conclusion, this study provides recent insights into the clinical, ultrasonographic, and outcome characteristics of acute infectious bronchopneumonia in a series of 21 adult cows. Several variables were associated with outcomes in our study sample.
