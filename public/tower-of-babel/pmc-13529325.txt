Plants fix carbon and convert it into sugars. These sugars need to be transported from source tissues, mainly leaves, to sink tissues, such as stems, roots, seeds, and reproductive organs. The phloem functions as the specialized vascular tissue for long-distance transport of sugars. Maintaining phloem flow is key for carbohydrate partitioning: if it fails, carbohydrates accumulate in leaves, while sink tissues become deprived of energy and building blocks. McCubbin et al. (2026) identified a member of the GT14 glycosyltransferase family, linking cell wall integrity and carbohydrate transport in maize leaves.

From sugar accumulation to a cell wall gene

Sucrose is the most abundant sugar transported by the phloem. According to the pressure-flow hypothesis proposed by Ernst Münch (Münch, 1930; De Schepper et al., 2013; Knoblauch et al., 2016), high sugar concentrations in source tissues draw water from the xylem into the phloem. This generates a pressure gradient that drives flow movement toward sink tissues. This pressure can reach values in the MPa range (higher than that of a car tyre whose pressure is in the kPa range); therefore, phloem cell walls must combine strength, elasticity, and integrity to maintain active transport (McCubbin and Braun, 2021).

Several genes have been previously implicated in sugar transport. Mutating them often causes diverse phenotypes, including decreased growth, chlorosis, and reduced fertility (Bezrutczyk et al., 2018; Julius et al., 2018; Tran et al., 2019). In addition, carbohydrate accumulation in leaves is commonly observed. However, apart from sugar transporters, in most cases, the precise mechanistic link between the mutated gene and phloem transport remains unclear. McCubbin and colleagues approached this problem using a forward genetic screen in maize. They examined a collection of ethyl methanesulfonate mutants and searched for plants with reduced height, impaired reproductive development, chlorosis, and anthocyanin accumulation. They identified three mutants, named carbohydrate partitioning defective 7, 48, and 49 (cpd7, cpd48, and cpd49). Further studies revealed that the photosynthetic assimilation rate, transpiration, and stomatal conductance were reduced in the mutants compared with wild-type plants. Consistent with their chlorosis and reduced gas exchange, the mutants exhibit accumulation of starch, sucrose, glucose, and fructose, and deficient sucrose transport in mature leaves. The authors also measured leaf turgor pressure, reasoning that carbohydrate accumulation could alter plant–water relationships, and found that mutant leaves showed increased pressure relative to the wild type.

The mutants produce and accumulate carbohydrates in leaves but fail to transport them. Which gene is affected, and how could it influence carbohydrate transport? Genetic analysis showed that all three mutants were allelic caused by mutations in a gene, named Cpd7. Cpd7 belongs to the GT14 glycosyltransferase family. This finding was unexpected in the context of phloem transport since in Arabidopsis, GT14 enzymes catalyse the transfer of glucuronate to β-1,3-galactooligosaccharides and β-1,6-galactooligosaccharides present in type II arabinogalactans proteins (AGPs), and have been linked to cell elongation and seed mucilage formation (Knoch et al., 2013; Ajayi et al., 2022). However, this is the first report connecting a GT14 enzyme to carbohydrate transport. All three mutations cluster within a predicted catalytic domain that is conserved among related proteins, suggesting that they are loss-of-function mutants.

The authors then asked whether Cpd7 might affect the localization or abundance of AGPs. Cpd7 localized to punctate structures that overlapped with the Golgi apparatus but not with the trans-Golgi network, consistent with a role in polysaccharide modification. However, immunofluorescence using Yariv reagents to target the conserved AGP galactan backbone structure and monoclonal antibodies against specific AGP epitopes did not reveal obvious differences between wild-type and mutant leaf samples.

This result raises a central question. If AGPs are not mislocalized or depleted, what explains the strong carbohydrate transport phenotype of cpd7 mutants? The answer came from cell wall composition analysis. Cell wall isolates from cpd7 mutants showed broad changes in hemicellulose composition, including reduced terminal arabinosylation, increased xylan branching, and a transient increase in xylose. These changes suggest that Cpd7-dependent modification of AGPs may influence interactions among multiple wall components. Furthermore, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis detected reduced cellulose levels in cpd7 leaves compared with the wild type (Box 1). In mature leaves, FTIR and brightfield imaging also revealed increased lignin signal in the phloem. No major abnormalities in vasculature morphology or other ectopic deposits were detected.

The sucrose export route

The transport of molecules in leaves occurs through a network of veins (Scarpella, 2024). These veins contain three major tissue systems: the xylem, which transports water and minerals; the phloem, which transports carbohydrates and other photoassimilates; and the bundle sheath, a surrounding layer that provides support. Phloem flow is directed towards the basal part of leaves for long-distance export (Fig.1A).

In maize leaves (Fig. 1B), sucrose is synthesized in the mesophyll cells, and moves symplastically via plasmodesmata into the bundle sheath cells or further into vascular parenchyma cells (Tran et al., 2019). It then requires the action of efflux and influx transporters to travel to the phloem apoplast and into the companion cell–sieve element complex (Braun, 2022). Sieve elements are enucleated cells that form long tubes for long-distance transport. Companion cells provide metabolic and structural support needed for sieve element function.

Symplastic transport through plasmodesmata is therefore essential for proper sucrose trafficking across the vascular network. Plasmodesmata permeability is controlled by the deposition and degradation of callose at the plasmodesmatal neck region, which can restrict passage through symplastic channels (Wu et al., 2018). For this reason, it is not unexpected that several mutations affecting sugar transport are associated with ectopic callose deposition in the phloem. In the present study (McCubbin et al., 2026), however, the authors did not find evidence of ectopic callose deposition in cpd7 mutant maize leaves. This shows that callose accumulation is not a universal feature of defective long-distance sugar transport. Instead, the authors detected ectopic lignin deposition in mutant phloem cell walls by observing cell wall autofluorescence, FTIR spectroscopy, and phloroglucinol staining. This lignin signal was absent from wild-type leaves. Moreover, the cell wall defect was not limited to lignification. FTIR spectroscopy and fluorescence microscopy showed reduced cellulose levels, localized to the bundle sheath and phloem in mature leaves, and to the phloem of all vein types in immature leaves.

These findings support the idea that altered cell wall composition compromises efficient phloem transport, although the possibility that defective transport also sends feedback signals to cell wall biosynthesis mechanisms remains to be tested.

When the wall controls the flow: cell wall integrity as a regulator of carbon partitioning

The study by McCubbin and colleagues places the integrity of cell walls at the centre of carbohydrate partitioning in maize. This is important because cell wall metabolism and sucrose transport are often treated as separate processes. Yet to function properly, the phloem needs to adjust the mechanical characteristics of its cell walls, enabling sap flow. Cpd7 may represent a general principle: enzymes that make apparently subtle modifications to wall-associated glycans can have large physiological effects when those modifications affect the mechanical or structural properties of tissues. In this case, a GT14 glycosyltransferase appears to influence cellulose deposition and phloem wall integrity, with consequences for carbon allocation.

This is not the first example in maize where cell wall defects affect carbohydrate transport. The COBRA-like gene Brittle Stalk 2-like3 (Bk2l3), which regulates cellulose deposition, is also required for proper phloem transport (Julius et al., 2021). Mutations in Bk2l3 show phenotypes like those of cpd7, including chlorosis and sucrose accumulation in leaves. However, the compensatory wall response differs: Bk2l3 mutants show ectopic callose deposition in the phloem, whereas cpd7 mutants show increased lignification (Box 1). This difference is intriguing. Both mutants appear to face a similar physiological problem, defective carbohydrate transport, but they reinforce the wall using different materials. This opens up the possibility that the cell wall integrity response in not a single uniform pathway. Instead, in may depend on the nature of the wall defect, the affected cell type, or the developmental stage.

Differences in cell wall composition in grasses and other plant species may be relevant (Zhang et al., 2024; Fuertes-Rabanal et al., 2025). Type I walls, found in most flowering plants and gymnosperms, are rich in pectin and xyloglucan. By contrast, type II walls in grasses contain less pectin and are enriched in glucuronoarabinoxylan and mixed-linkage glucans. This compositional difference is likely to influence how cellulose interacts with matrix polysaccharides and glycoproteins, and may explain why defects in cellulose-associated pathways have particularly strong effects on phloem function in grasses. These raise the hypothesis that the cpd7 phenotype is not unique to maize but may be a broader and previously overlooked link between cellulose-related wall architecture and sugar transport in grasses.

A key future direction will be to determine whether Cpd7 affects phloem transport through altered cell wall composition, integrity signalling, or mechanics. Although cpd7 mutants show reduced cellulose and increased lignin, it remains unclear whether these changes compromise the mechanical support required for pressure-driven phloem flow.

Another important question is whether cell wall integrity sensors participate in this response. In Arabidopsis, cellulose biosynthesis defects activate cell wall integrity signalling pathways and can lead to ectopic lignin and callose deposition (Desprez et al., 2002; Hématy et al., 2007). Sensors such as THESEUS1, FERONIA, WALL ASSOCIATED KINASES, and mechanosensitive ion channels have been implicated in monitoring cell wall status (Wolf, 2017; Vaahtera et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2021). However, downstream signalling events remain incompletely understood. The maize genome contains three homologues of FERONIA, but no clear homologues of THESEUS1. This raises the possibility that grasses use a partly different cell wall monitoring system. One hypothesis is that diversification of FERONIA-like receptors or other sensors in grasses could contribute to the connection between wall integrity and carbohydrate transport. However, this idea needs to be carefully tested, especially because rice contains many FERONIA-like receptors without a clear link to phloem transport.

Developmental timing is another open question. The strongest carbohydrate transport defects in cpd7 are observed in transition and mature leaf stages. This suggests that young leaves may either be less dependent on Cpd7 or may compensate more efficiently.

By linking a glycosyltransferase, cellulose deposition, and phloem transport, McCubbin and colleagues reveal that the sucrose highway depends not only on transporters and osmotic gradients, but also on the construction and maintenance of the walls that support the flow. Understanding this connection could potentially pave the way to help improve carbon allocation in crops. If phloem function can be made more robust under developmental or environmental stress, plants may distribute resources more efficiently to sink tissues. In a changing climate, such knowledge could contribute to crop resilience and food security.
