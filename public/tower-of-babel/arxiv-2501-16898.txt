The spin-orbital Kitaev model: from kagome spin ice to classical fractons
Weslei B. Fontana, Fabrizio G. Oliviero, Rodrigo G. Pereira, Willian M. H. Natori

Abstract
We study an exactly solvable spin-orbital model that can be regarded as a classical analogue of the celebrated Kitaev honeycomb model and describes interactions between Rydberg atoms on the ruby lattice. We leverage its local and nonlocal symmetries to determine the exact partition function and the static structure factor. A mapping between S = 3 / 2 S=3/2 models on the honeycomb lattice and kagome spin Hamiltonians allows us to interpret the thermodynamic properties in terms of a classical kagome spin ice. Partially lifting the symmetries associated with line operators, we obtain a model characterized by immobile excitations, called classical fractons, and a ground state degeneracy that increases exponentially with the length of the system. We formulate a continuum theory that reveals the underlying gauge structure and conserved charges. Extensions of our theory to other lattices and higher-spin systems are suggested.



I
Introduction

The key tasks of condensed matter physics are to describe, classify, and predict emergent phenomena of quantum systems in the thermodynamic limit
(
Moessner and Moore 2021
)
. An ongoing trend in this branch of physics is the characterization of phases lying outside the Landau-Ginzburg-Wilson paradigm, as is often the case for frustrated magnets
(
Balents 2010
;
Lacroix et al. 2011
)
.
It has long been hypothesized that these correlated insulators can host quantum spin liquids (QSLs)
(
Savary and Balents 2017
;
Knolle and Moessner 2019
)
, whose highly entangled ground states and fractionalized excitations are described by effective gauge theories. The first proposals of QSLs were motivated by the effects of geometric frustration, as exemplified by Anderson’s resonant valence bond (RVB) state on lattices with triangular plaquettes
(
Anderson 1973
)
. Later research demonstrated that QSLs turn up as ground states of a class of exactly solvable models displaying exchange frustration, whereby bond-dependent anisotropic interactions enhance quantum fluctuations. In this context, the most studied Hamiltonian is the
Kitaev honeycomb model (KHM)
(
Kitaev 2006
)
, which harbors Majorana
fermion matter excitations coupled to a static
Z_{2}
gauge field.
Several candidates for QSLs have been synthesized and characterized
over the past decades, raising hopes that this long-sought state will soon
be found in solid state platforms
(
Norman 2016
;
Winter et al. 2017
;
Zhou et al. 2017
;
Hermanns et al. 2018
;
Takagi et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
.

Figure 1:
(a) Rydberg atoms arranged on a ruby lattice. White
(black) dots correspond to atoms in the ground (excited) state. In
this figure,
\langle n_{i}\rangle=1/4
. (b) Any state on the ruby lattice following the specification above can be mapped onto a dimer covering on the kagome lattice. Alternatively, it can also be represented in terms of a spin ice state, as indicated by the arrows.

Fractonic spin liquids (FSLs) form a distinct class of phases that can also be found in exactly solvable models and described by exotic quantum field theories
(
Nandkishore and Hermele 2019
)
. FSLs are characterized by a robust subextensive ground state degeneracy and immobile excitations called fractons, which hinder thermal equilibrium at low temperatures
(
Chamon 2005
)
. In type-I FSLs, fractons can form composite excitations that are mobile only within a lattice submanifold. The restricted mobility of FSL excitations in real space is reflected on the corresponding field theories, which typically involve tensor gauge fields and dipole conservation laws
(
Pretko 2017a
;
Pretko 2017b
;
Slagle and Kim 2017
;
Slagle et al. 2019
;
Rudelius et al. 2021
;
Seiberg and Shao 2020
;
Seiberg and Shao 2021a
;
Burnell et al. 2022
;
Fontana et al. 2021
;
Fontana et al. 2022
;
Fontana and Pereira 2023
;
Luo et al. 2022
;
Delfino et al. 2023
;
Macêdo and Pereira 2024
)
.

While fracton order is restricted to exist only in three dimensions
(
Aasen et al. 2020
;
Haah 2021a
;
Haah 2021b
)
, similar physics can emerge in lower dimensions in the form of subsystem protected topological order (SSPT) or spontaneous subsystem symmetry breaking phases (SSSB)
Devakul et al. 2018
;
You et al. 2018
;
San Miguel et al. 2021
;
Stephen et al. 2019
. Such phases share many similarities with FSLs, such as a geometry-dependent ground state degeneracy and mobility restrictions of the excitations, but differ in the structure of entanglement. In fact, fracton-like behavior has even been identified in classical models constructed from classical spins and Ising variables in two dimensions
Yan 2019
;
Benton and Moessner 2021
;
Myerson-Jain et al. 2022
;
Placke et al. 2024
or canonically conjugate variables with dynamics described by Hamilton’s equations
Prakash et al. 2024
. Here we shall use the term
classical fractons
to refer to these models.

Quantum simulators based on Rydberg atom arrays
(
Browaeys and Lahaye 2020
)
provide potential platforms for both QSLs and FSLs. Rydberg atoms trapped on the ruby lattice are already capable of simulating the RVB spin liquid on the kagome lattice
(
Semeghini et al. 2021
)
. Geometry ensures the isomorphism between excited Rydberg atoms (Fig.
1
) and singlets that occupy kagome lattice bonds
(
Semeghini et al. 2021
;
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)
.
The low-energy cold-atom states are univocally mapped onto kagome dimer coverings, which resonate among themselves under tunable transverse fields. The measurements are then compared with the expectation values predicted for a kagome quantum dimer model (KQDM), a projection
of the usual Heisenberg model onto the covering set
(
Zeng and Elser 1995
)
that is also exactly solvable
(
Misguich et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
)
.
Alternatively, the KQDM is translated into a honeycomb lattice model of exchange interactions among
S=3/2
spins subject to longitudinal and transverse fields
(
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)
. The zero-field model hosts a dimer liquid corresponding to an equal weight superposition of kagome
dimer coverings. The transverse fields introduce electric, magnetic, or fermionic anyon fluctuations, which drive the dimer liquid to different types of QSLs. In particular, both the spin-1/2 KHM and the kagome RVB occur in this model, thus providing a unified framework for two paradigmatic liquid phases.

In this paper, we study the exchange-frustrated model formulated in Ref.
(
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)
in the form of a (classical) spin-orbital Kitaev model (SOKM). This rewriting elucidates both local and nonlocal symmetries, allowing us to uncover the extensive ground state degeneracy associated with the kagome dimer liquid, as well as the exact partition function of the SOKM. Analyzing the nonlocal symmetries, we identify a mechanism to lift the ground state degeneracy leading to a subextensive zero-point entropy. We also discuss the classical fractons and other excitations with restricted mobility that arise in this model. Finally, we study the continuum limit of this classical fracton model using previously developed techniques for
\Gamma
-matrix models
(
Fontana et al. 2021
;
Fontana et al. 2022
)
, illuminating its conservation laws by analogy with Chern-Simons-like theories.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
presents several exact
results on the SOKM, connecting the latter with the well-established physics of frustrated phases on the kagome lattice. In Sec.
II.1
, we review the theoretical models that describe quantum simulations of Rydberg atoms on the ruby lattice in the blockade limit with a
fixed density of excited atoms
(
Semeghini et al. 2021
;
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)
. Section
II.2

lists the SOKM symmetries which are then applied to determine its ground state manifold. This analysis automatically leads to classical analogues of Lieb’s theorem for gauge fluxes
(
Lieb 1994
)
and topological order in gapped
Z_{2}
QSLs
(
Wen 2007
)
.
The symmetries also determine the level degeneracies, leading to a closed form for the partition function that recovers general features of spin ice thermodynamics
(
Ramirez et al. 1999
;
Wills et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
;
Möller and Moessner 2009
)
. We use these results to compute the exact static structure factor in Section
II.3
, which presents the expected
Z_{2}
spin ice character of the ground states. In Sec.
III
, we first introduce a sublattice-dependent rotation that maps the ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic models and simplifies the notation in the remainder of the paper. We then construct a perturbation that does not commute with local plaquette operators but with strings in two distinct directions. This perturbation lifts the extensive zero-point entropy while also inducing classical fractons. We show that this model projected on the subspace of SOKM ground states is similar to the square-lattice fracton model studied in Ref.
(
Yan 2019
)
. In Sec.
IV
, we construct an effective field theory for the SOKM. Using this framework, we recover the main features of the lattice model and relate the ground state degeneracy to the algebra of line operators defined in the continuum limit. Finally, we present our conclusions and outlook in Sec.
V
. The appendix contains explicit expressions for the matrix representations of spin-orbital operators used in this work.

II
The Classical Spin-Orbital Kitaev Model

II.1
Equivalence between the Kagome Dimer Model and the Spin-orbital Kitaev
model

We briefly review the minimal model for Rydberg atoms arranged on a ruby lattice [see Fig.
1
(a)] as proposed in Ref.
(
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)
, both for completeness and to set the notation. The simulations in Ref.
Semeghini et al. 2021
were performed using 219
{}^{87}\text{Rb}
ions trapped with optical tweezers, displaying two relevant states at each site: the atomic ground state
\left|g\right\rangle
and one Rydberg state
\left|r\right\rangle
. The energy difference between
\left|r\right\rangle
and
\left|g\right\rangle
will be called
\hbar\omega_{rg}
. Each atom is modeled by a hard-core boson located at a site
i
that is described by
n_{i}=0
or
n_{i}=1
if the atom is in the ground or excited Rydberg state, respectively.

An external incoming laser with frequency
\omega_{l}
is necessary to populate the Rydberg states and serves to define two additional parameters: the Rabi frequency
\Omega
, and the detuning
\delta
. The Rabi frequency is related with transitions between two states induced by an electromagnetic potential and defined by
\Omega=\left\langle r\left|\textbf{r}\cdot\textbf{E}\right|g\right\rangle
, in which
E
is the electric field amplitude of the laser. Repulsive interactions between Rydberg atoms have the form
V_{0}/r^{6}

Browaeys and Lahaye 2020
;
Saffman et al. 2010
. The competition between the exciting laser field and the Rydberg mutual repulsion defines a blockade radius
R_{b}=(V_{0}/\Omega)^{1/6}
, i.e., given a Rydberg state, no other atom can be excited within a radius
r<R_{b}
. The lattice parameter is chosen to ensure that the six nearest neighbors of a given atom are within
R_{b}
, thus enforcing a constraint to the Rydberg state arrangements. Finally, the detuning
\delta=\omega_{l}-\omega_{rg}
of the incoming laser can favor the occupation of Rydberg states, when positive, and hinder it, when negative.

The classical part of the Hamiltonian describing the aforementioned experimental setup is
(
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)

H_{\text{diag}}=U\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_{\triangle}}n_{i}n_{j}+V\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_{\square}}n_{i}n_{j}-\delta\sum_{i}n_{i}.

(1)

Here,
U
is the Rydberg blockade repulsion within the lattice triangles and
\langle ij\rangle_{\triangle}
corresponds to their bonds. The
V
parameter is the interaction between Rydberg states on sites
i
and
j
outside the blockade radius but within nearest-neighbor triangles. Finally, the longitudinal field models the detuning.
H_{\text{diag}}
is readily converted into an Ising model via
n_{i}=\left(1+s_{i}^{z}\right)/2
. Quantum fluctuations can be introduced by the Rabi frequency
\Omega
or by including possible hoppings of Rydberg states within elementary triangles, which are modeled by

\displaystyle H_{\text{fl}}

\displaystyle=\Omega\sum_{i}s_{i}^{x}+t^{\prime}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_{\triangle}}\left(s_{i}^{+}s_{j}^{-}+s_{i}^{-}s_{j}^{+}\right)

\displaystyle+it^{\prime\prime}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_{\triangle}}\left(s_{i}^{+}s_{j}^{-}-s_{i}^{-}s_{j}^{+}\right),

(2)

in which we followed Ref.
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
and separate the real and imaginary parts of the hopping.

tends to maximize the distance between

Misguich et al. 2003
)
, states that minimize the interaction energy of

in the sector

1
(b). It is also possible to set a correspondence between dimer coverings and spin-ice states formed by triangles in two-in-one-out and three-out arrow configurations
(
Zeng and Elser 1995
;
Misguich et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
)
; see Fig.

distinct dimer coverings on the kagome lattice
(
Misguich et al. 2002
;

, in which

is the number of unit cells, implying an extensive zero-point entropy. Quantum fluctuations related with the Rabi frequency introduce resonances among the coverings, allowing the simulation of RVB physics
(
Semeghini et al. 2021
)
. An exhaustive classification of anyon fluctuations was already performed in Ref.

)

The four allowed configurations in each triangle define spin-3/2 states
placed on a honeycomb lattice. The associated observables are more
conveniently written in terms of pseudospin (

) and pseudo-orbitals

\displaystyle=\left\{\tau_{i}^{\alpha},\tau_{i}^{\beta}\right\}=2\delta^{\alpha\beta},

\displaystyle\left[\sigma_{i}^{\alpha},\sigma_{j}^{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=2i\delta_{ij}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}\sigma_{i}^{\gamma},

\displaystyle\left[\tau_{i}^{\alpha},\tau_{j}^{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=2i\delta_{ij}\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}\tau_{i}^{\gamma},

\displaystyle\left[\sigma_{i}^{\alpha},\tau_{j}^{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

\displaystyle H_{\text{diag}}

\displaystyle=H_{\text{SOKM}}+H_{l},

\displaystyle=J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle_{\gamma}}A_{i}^{\gamma}A_{j}^{\gamma},

(8)

II.2
Symmetries, Exact Spectrum and Partition Function of the Spin-Orbital
Kitaev Model

The main properties of the SOKM follow from its local symmetries. Assuming periodic boundary conditions, we can identify the ground state manifold starting from the reference ground
state
\left|\phi_{0}\right\rangle
shown in Fig.
4
(a). In analogy to Eq. (
8
), we define the plaquette operators

W_{p}^{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}^{z}\sigma_{2}^{x}\sigma_{3}^{y}\sigma_{4}^{z}\sigma_{5}^{x}\sigma_{6}^{y}.

(9)

Such plaquette operators still commute with Eq. (
7
) while locally changing
\left|a^{x},a^{y},a^{z}\right\rangle
of sites pertaining to
p
. This observation allows the construction of the following ground state family:

\left|\psi_{0}\left(\left\{S_{p}\right\}\right)\right\rangle=\prod_{p=1}^{N}\left(W_{p}^{\sigma}\right)^{S_{p}}\left|\phi_{0}\right\rangle,

(10)

in which
S_{p}=0,1
; see Fig.
4
(b) for a minimal
example. We could also define plaquette operators
W_{p}^{\tau}
using the pseudo-orbital operators, but the relation
W_{p}^{\sigma}W_{p}^{\tau}=W_{p}^{A}
implies that the states generated by applying
W_{p}^{\tau}
differ from Eq. (
10
) only by a global phase factor, thus being physically equivalent.
To count the number of states defined by Eq. (
10
), let us recall that the
W_{p}^{\sigma}
operators are not independent in periodic boundary conditions, but must satisfy
\prod_{p}W_{p}^{\sigma}=i^{N_{\text{sites}}}
due to the identity
\sigma^{x}\sigma^{y}\sigma^{z}=i
. We take the number of plaquettes
N
to be even, so that the product of these operators is set to
+1
. The
N-1
independent plaquettes then define
2^{N-1}
distinct ground states.

In addition to the local symmetries, the SOKM also commutes with nonlocal
operators defined over noncontractible strings. Consider, for example,

V_{l_{xy}}^{\sigma}=\prod_{i\in l_{xy}}\sigma_{i}^{z},

(11)

which flips
\left\{a_{i}^{x}\right\}
and
\left\{a_{i}^{y}\right\}

along an
xy
zigzag chain labeled by
l_{xy}
; see Fig.
4
(c). A new ground state
\left|\Phi_{0}\right\rangle

can be written as follows:

\left|\Phi_{0}\left(\left\{S_{l_{xy}}\right\}\right)\right\rangle=\prod_{l_{xy}=1}^{L_{xy}}\left(V_{l_{xy}}^{\sigma}\right)^{S_{l_{xy}}}\left|\phi_{0}\right\rangle,

(12)

in which
S_{l_{xy}}=0,1
and
L_{xy}
is the number of
xy
chains.
An even number of
V_{l_{xy}}^{\sigma}
corresponds to the application
of
W_{p}^{\sigma}
operators around the honeycomb lattice following
a set of
xy
chains, but an odd number of
V_{l_{xy}}^{\sigma}

cannot be generated using this operation. This even-odd distinction
classifies the ground states by the
parity of the string operators connecting it to the reference ground
state
\left|\phi_{0}\right\rangle
. Since the lattice is two dimensional,
we can repeat this discussion for another set of noncontractible loops, say, the ones along
yz
chains labeled by
l_{yz}=1,\dots,L_{yz}
. As a consequence, the parity of the number of noncontractible strings on a torus defines
2^{2}=4
ground state sectors, in each of which we can generate the states in Eq. (
10
). The resulting ground state degeneracy is
4\times 2^{N-1}=2^{N+1}
, for
N=L_{xy}L_{yz}
unit cells. Clearly, this classical analogue of
Z_{2}
topological degeneracy
(
Wen 2007
)

is essential to recover the aforementioned degeneracy related to kagome dimer coverings
(
Misguich et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
)
. Note also that all states in the ground state manifold obey the relation
A_{i}^{\gamma}+A_{j}^{\gamma}=0
for every bond
\langle ij\rangle_{\gamma}
. Summing over all bonds, we obtain
\sum_{i,\gamma}A_{i}^{\gamma}=0
. As a consequence, the longitudinal field term
H_{l}
in Eq. (
6
) has vanishing projection in the ground state sector. Therefore, the ground state degeneracy is robust against small changes in the detuning that generate a weak longitudinal field,
|h_{l}|\ll J
. Only for
0<h_{l}\sim J
do we expect a transition to a polarized phase where
a_{i}^{\gamma}=-1

\forall i,\gamma
.

We can also use the symmetries to calculate the partition function of the SOKM. Figure
4
(d) represents
a minimally excited state of energy
4J
, obtained by applying a local operator
\sigma_{i}^{\gamma}
on a ground state. This operator preserves the eigenvalue
a_{i}^{\gamma}
while flipping the other two eigenvalues,
a_{i}^{\alpha},a_{i}^{\beta}
, thus changing the interaction energy on the
\alpha,\beta
bonds. This excited state contains two visons
(
Misguich et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
)
associated with
W_{p}^{A}=-1
, equivalent to a
\pi
flux through the plaquette. Note that visons are always created or annihilated in pairs. Hence, the spectrum reads

E_{n}=-3NJ+4nJ,\qquad n\geq 0.

(13)

Visons can be separated by arbitrary distances without an energy cost by applying
\sigma_{j}^{\gamma}
along open strings. Combining the distinct ways of distributing the
2n
unsatisfied
bonds with the degeneracy arising from the symmetry operators, we obtain the degeneracy of each energy level:

g\left(E_{n}\right)=2^{N+1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}3N\\
2n\end{array}\right),

(14)

whose summation recovers the Hilbert space dimension. Using Eqs.
(
13
) and (
14
), we obtain the partition function

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}g\left(E_{n}\right)e^{-\beta E_{n}}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{N}e^{3NJ\beta}\left[\left(1+e^{-2\beta J}\right)^{3N}+\left(1-e^{-2\beta J}\right)^{3N}\right].

The partition function allows us to compute the thermodynamics of the SOKM,
which displays the qualitative behavior expected for spin ice
(
Ramirez et al. 1999
;
Wills et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
;
Möller and Moessner 2009
)
.
The free energy
F=-\frac{1}{\beta}\ln Z
has the usual high-temperature limit given by

\displaystyle F\left(T\rightarrow\infty\right)

\displaystyle=-3NJ-2Nk_{B}T\ln 4,

(16)

which recovers the expected entropy per site of
s_{\infty}=k_{B}\ln 4
.
On the other hand, the low-temperature limit
T\rightarrow 0
yields

\displaystyle F\left(T\rightarrow 0\right)

\displaystyle=-3NJ-\left(N+1\right)k_{B}T\ln 2,

(17)

which reflects the
2^{N+1}
-fold ground state degeneracy and leads
to an entropy per site of
s_{0}=\frac{1}{2}k_{B}\ln 2
in the thermodynamic limit. Such residual entropy is a defining characteristic of spin ice systems and can be experimentally estimated using specific heat integration
(
Ramirez et al. 1999
;
Wills et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
;
Möller and Moessner 2009
)
.
From the exact partition function, we can also calculate the specific heat and entropy per site at arbitrary temperatures. The result is shown in Fig.
5
(a), where the dashed lines highlight the low- and high-temperature limits. Notice that the SOKM does not exhibit a phase transition but rather a crossover connecting the spin-ice and the paramagnetic regimes.

We end this section by comparing our work with other classical Kitaev-like models in which the thermodynamics is also exactly computed
Mizoguchi 2022
;
Mizoguchi 2024
. These models are defined on the decorated honeycomb lattice and rely on the conservation of certain components of the spin operators that live on the bonds of the honeycomb lattice. This allows one to map the interacting problem onto a set of noninteracting spins under position-dependent magnetic fields. The partition functions in these models are then given by an exponentially large degeneracy factor multiplied by single-particle partition functions. This should be contrasted with our model, where the conserved quantities are plaquette and closed-loop operators that keep the interactions always active. The partition function in Eq. (
15
) is then different from those obtained in mean-field theories, being better understood in terms of a spin ice phase.

We now turn to the calculation of the static structure factor

S\left(\mathbf{q},T\right)=\frac{1}{N}\text{Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}\left(T\right)\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{i,j}A_{i}^{\alpha}A_{j}^{\beta}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}\right)}\right],

(18)

in which
\hat{\rho}(T)=Z^{-1}\sum_{\psi}\exp(-\beta E_{\psi})\left|\psi\right\rangle\left\langle\psi\right|
is the thermal density matrix defined over all eigenstates
\left|\psi\right\rangle
. We are particularly
interested in the low-temperature limit, where the correlation function
simplifies to

\lim_{T\rightarrow 0}S\left(\mathbf{q},T\right)=\sum_{\underset{i,j}{\psi_{0},\alpha,\beta,}}\frac{\left\langle\psi_{0}\left|A_{i}^{\alpha}A_{j}^{\beta}\right|\psi_{0}\right\rangle}{2^{N+1}N}e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}\right)},

(19)

in which
\psi_{0}
runs over the ground state manifold. It is then convenient to define

\left|0\right\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{N+1}}}\sum_{\psi_{0}}\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle.

(20)

Since

\displaystyle A_{i}^{\alpha}A_{j}^{\beta}\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{\alpha}a_{j}^{\beta}\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle,\,a_{i}^{\alpha},a_{j}^{\beta}=\pm 1,

\displaystyle\left\langle\psi_{0}^{\prime}|\psi_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{\psi_{0}^{\prime},\psi_{0}},

(21)

we can write the
T\rightarrow 0
correlation as

S\left(\mathbf{q}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\underset{i,j}{\alpha,\beta,}}\left\langle 0\left|A_{i}^{\alpha}A_{j}^{\beta}\right|0\right\rangle e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\left(\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}\right)}.

(22)

The
\left|0\right\rangle
state is the honeycomb representation of the kagome dimer liquid discussed in Refs.
(
Misguich et al. 2002
;
Misguich et al. 2003
)
, which allows us to assert the ultrashort correlation functions

\left\langle 0\left|A_{i}^{\alpha}A_{j}^{\beta}\right|0\right\rangle=\delta^{\alpha\beta}\times\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i=j,\\
-1,&\text{if }i,j\in\left\langle ij\right\rangle_{\alpha}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(23)

The first two equations are explained by the antiferromagnetic Ising
couplings of
Z_{2}
local states. On the kagome lattice, the last equation reflects the independence of dimer states for triangles that do not share a vertex. This is the classical analogue of the KHM correlation function, which is known to vanish beyond nearest neighbors from analytical arguments
(
Baskaran et al. 2007
;
Baskaran et al. 2008
)
. Substituting Eq. (
23
) in
Eq. (
22
), we find

\displaystyle S\left(\mathbf{q}\right)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{\gamma}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}}{2}\right),

(24)

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{z}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\left(0,-1\right)
,

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{x,y}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
are the nearest-neighbor vectors of the honeycomb lattice.

The structure factor
S\left(\mathbf{q}\right)
plotted in Fig.

5
(b) displays no signature of conventional long-range order,
as expected from a classical spin liquid. The momentum dependence is commensurate with the lattice periodicity and consistent with the
Z_{3}
rotational invariance of the model. The correlations
are weak in the neighborhood of the
\Gamma
point, remaining relatively
small for all points inside the first Brillouin zone. The spectral
weight is maximal at some reciprocal lattice points and remains large
along the edges connecting them. Such a pattern is reminiscent of
the spin ice structure factor, with the key difference that there
are no pinch point singularities in the SOKM due to the ultrashort
correlations.

IV
Effective field theory

The study of fractons initially emerged through exactly solvable lattice spin models
Chamon 2005
;
Vijay et al. 2015
, but has since been extended to include effective field theory approaches
Nandkishore and Hermele 2019
. One motivation for this type of approach is to shed light on robust, universal properties of the fractonic phase of matter that persist beyond the solvable models. Unlike topological quantum field theories that describe topologically ordered states such as gapped QSLs, effective field theories for fractonic systems must retain certain geometric features and discrete symmetries of the lattice; see, e.g. Ref.
Slagle and Kim 2017
. In fact, physical properties associated with the low-energy sector, such as the ground state degeneracy, can depend on microscopic details related to the lattice regularization. Although the continuum limit may be more subtle in these cases
Seiberg and Shao 2020
;
Seiberg and Shao 2021a
;
Seiberg and Shao 2021b
;
Gorantla et al. 2023
, it is possible to derive an effective field theory starting from the lattice model by representing the physical spin operators as exponentials of fields that obey canonical commutation relations
Fontana et al. 2021
. This representation naturally introduces an emergent gauge structure. From the perspective of the gauge theory, the ground state degeneracy and mobility constraints of excitations in the fractonic phase follow from subsystem conservation laws that can be inferred by analyzing the gauge invariance of the effective action. In particular, rigid Wilson lines constructed from the gauge fields play a central role in encoding these properties.

In this section, we construct and analyze an effective field theory that captures the main properties of the lattice model discussed in Secs.
II
and
III
, notably the existence of conserved line operators and the associated ground state degeneracy.

We start by rewriting the SOKM Hamiltonian as

H_{\rm SOKM}=-J\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}}O({\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}}),

(33)

where
\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}
are the positions of the centers of the
\gamma
bonds and
O(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})=A^{\gamma}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma})A^{\gamma}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma})
. Here we use the notation
A_{j}^{\gamma}=A^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}_{j})
for the operators that act on site
j
, at position
\mathbf{r}_{j}
in the honeycomb lattice. For fixed
\gamma
, the set
\{\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}\}
forms a triangular lattice. Crucially,
O(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})
are mutually commuting operators that square to the identity. Thus, the SOKM belongs to the class of frustration-free models whose ground state subspace is a stabilizer code. This property is required to apply the procedure described in Refs.
Fontana et al. 2021
;
Fontana and Pereira 2023
, as we shall do in the following. The first step is to introduce a new basis of operators:

\Gamma^{1}=\sigma^{x}\tau^{x},\;\Gamma^{2}=\sigma^{y}\tau^{x},\;\Gamma^{3}=\sigma^{z}\tau^{x},\;\Gamma^{4}=\sigma^{0}\tau^{y},

(34)

where
\sigma^{0}
denotes the identity matrix in pseudospin space. We also define
\Gamma^{5}=\Gamma^{1}\Gamma^{2}\Gamma^{3}\Gamma^{4}
. These operators obey
\{\Gamma^{I},\Gamma^{J}\}=2\delta_{IJ}
for
I,J\in\{1,\dots,5\}
. The operators
A^{\gamma}
can be written as

A^{x}=\Gamma^{1},\quad A^{y}=i\Gamma^{1}\Gamma^{3}\Gamma^{4},\quad A^{z}=-i\Gamma^{3}\Gamma^{4}.

(35)

Next, we represent the operators
\Gamma^{I}
using four bosonic fields. At each site, we introduce the fields
\theta_{n}(\mathbf{r}_{j})
, with
n\in\{1,\dots,4\}
, which obey the commutation relation

[\theta_{m}(\mathbf{r}),\theta_{n}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})]=i\pi\delta_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}(K^{-1})_{mn},

(36)

where
K
is the antisymmetric matrix

K=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&1&1&1\\
-1&0&1&1\\
-1&-1&0&1\\
-1&-1&-1&0\end{array}\right).

(37)

Following Refs.
Fontana et al. 2021
;
Fontana and Pereira 2023
, we use the “bosonization” map

\Gamma^{I}_{j}\sim\exp[it^{I}_{m}K_{mn}\theta_{n}(\mathbf{r}_{j})],

(38)

with an implicit sum over repeated indices
m,n\in\{1,\dots,4\}
. The vectors
t^{I}_{m}
are chosen so that

\displaystyle t^{I}_{m}K_{mn}t^{J}_{n}=\begin{cases}0\text{ (mod 2)},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ I=J,\\
1\text{ (mod 2)},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{if}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ I\neq J,\end{cases}

(39)

and
\sum_{I=1}^{5}t_{m}^{I}=0
. A convenient choice is

\displaystyle t_{m}^{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{Im},\quad\text{for }I=1,\dots,3,

\displaystyle t^{4}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta_{m4}.

(40)

We write the local operators of the SOKM as

A^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}_{j})\sim\cos[\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}_{j})],

(41)

where
\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})=v_{m}^{\gamma}K_{mn}\theta_{n}(\mathbf{r})
, with

\displaystyle v_{m}^{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{m}^{1},

\displaystyle v_{m}^{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t_{m}^{1}+t_{m}^{3}+t_{m}^{4},

\displaystyle v_{m}^{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t_{m}^{3}-t_{m}^{4}.

(42)

The four-component vectors
v_{n}^{\gamma}
are linearly dependent and satisfy

v^{\gamma}_{n}K_{mn}v^{\gamma^{\prime}}_{n}=0.

(43)

We then have
[\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}),\theta^{\gamma^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})]=0
. This commutation relation ensures that the operators
A^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})
defined in Eq. (
41
) commute among themselves, as happens in the original lattice model.

In the bosonic representation, the SOKM becomes

\displaystyle H_{\rm SOKM}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{J}{2}\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}}\cos[2\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})]

(44)

\displaystyle-\frac{J}{2}\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}}\cos[\Delta_{\gamma}\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})],

where

\displaystyle\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\theta^{\gamma}_{\rm e}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}/2)+\theta^{\gamma}_{\rm o}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}/2)}{2},

(45)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\gamma}\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta^{\gamma}_{\rm e}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}/2)-\theta^{\gamma}_{\rm o}(\mathbf{R}_{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}/2).

(46)

Here we have added the subindex e,o to indicate that the that fields act on positions that belong to the even or odd sublattices of the honeycomb lattice, respectively. At this point, we assume that we can take the continuum limit with fields that vary smoothly on the lattice scale. Similar assumptions have been made in the derivation of effective field theories for fractonic models such as the X-cube
Slagle and Kim 2017
and the Chamon code
Fontana et al. 2021
. Although our system has ultrashort-range correlations—as usual for stabilizer codes—this continuum limit can be justified a posteriori by showing that the resulting field theory reproduces key properties of the solvable lattice model. In this approximation, we replace
\Delta_{\gamma}\theta^{\gamma}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}a_{0}\partial_{\gamma}\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})
, where
a_{0}
is the lattice spacing (set to
a_{0}=1
in previous sections) and
\partial_{\gamma}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla
are spatial derivatives in the direction of the unit vectors
\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\gamma}=\sqrt{3}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\gamma}/a_{0}
. Expanding the second term in Eq. (
44
) to leading order in the derivatives, we obtain the effective Hamiltonian in the continuum limit

H_{\rm c}=\sum_{\gamma}\int d^{2}r\,\left[\frac{u}{2}(\partial_{\gamma}\theta^{\gamma})^{2}-g\cos(2\theta^{\gamma})\right],

(47)

where
u\sim J
and
g\sim J/a_{0}^{2}
. This Hamiltonian respects the
Z_{3}
symmetry that takes
\gamma\mapsto\gamma+1
(mod 3) in real and internal space, thus retaining the discrete rotational symmetry of the microscopic model. The cosine term imposes the constraint that the physical values of the phase fields are
\theta^{\gamma}=0,\pi
(mod
2\pi
), corresponding to the eigenvalues
a^{\gamma}=\pm 1
of
A^{\gamma}
. If this term is irrelevant, we can treat
\theta^{\gamma}
as continuous functions and focus on the effects of the first term in Eq. (
47
).

We can reformulate the effective field theory as a gauge theory. Let us consider the effective action

S=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{3}x\,\left(\frac{K_{mn}}{4\pi}\mathcal{A}_{m}\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{n}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}\right),

(48)

where
\mathcal{M}
is a spacetime manifold,
\mathcal{A}_{n}(\mathbf{r})=\theta_{n}(\mathbf{r})/a_{0}
are rescaled fields with dimension of inverse length, with the linear combinations
\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})=v_{m}^{\gamma}K_{mn}\mathcal{A}_{n}(\mathbf{r})
, and
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\gamma}
are Lagrange multipliers enforcing the constraint
\partial_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}=0
in the ground state subspace. This action is reminiscent of the Chern-Simons-like theories studied in Refs.
Fontana et al. 2021
;
Fontana et al. 2022
;
Fontana and Pereira 2023
;
Delfino et al. 2023
. In fact, using Eq. (
43
) we verify that the action is invariant under the U(1) gauge transformation

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}+\sum_{\gamma}v_{m}^{\gamma}\,\partial_{\gamma}G^{\gamma},

(49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\gamma}+\partial_{t}G^{\gamma},

(50)

where
G^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r},t)
are arbitrary dimensionless functions. Explicitly, the gauge transformation acts on the fields as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}+\partial_{1}G^{x}-\partial_{2}G^{y},

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2},

(52)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{3}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{3}+\partial_{2}G^{y}-\partial_{3}G^{z},

(53)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{4}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{4}-\partial_{2}G^{y}+\partial_{3}G^{z}.

(54)

Defining the linear combinations
\mathcal{A}_{\pm}=(\mathcal{A}_{3}\pm\mathcal{A}_{4})/2
, we note that
\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\mathcal{A}_{+}
are gauge invariant. In order for
e^{i\mathcal{A}_{n}(\mathbf{r})}
to describe physical operators for all components
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, the gauge functions must be restricted to the form

G^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})=2\pi n^{\gamma}(\bar{x}_{\gamma})\,x_{\gamma}+f^{\gamma}(\bar{x}_{\gamma}),

(55)

where
x_{\gamma}=\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\gamma}
is the coordinate in the direction parallel to the
\gamma
bond,
\bar{x}_{\gamma}
is the coordinate in the perpendicular direction,
n^{\gamma}(\bar{x}_{\gamma})
are integer functions, and
f^{\gamma}(\bar{x}_{\gamma})
are arbitrary real functions. This type of behavior is common in higher-rank gauge theories, where it stems from the structure of higher derivatives and allows for discontinuous configurations in the low-energy sector
Seiberg and Shao 2021a
;
Seiberg and Shao 2021b
;
Rudelius et al. 2021
;
Gorantla et al. 2023
.

The equations of motion derived from the action in Eq. (
48
) lead to the conditions of vanishing “electric” and “magnetic” fields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{n}-\sum_{\gamma}v_{n}^{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\gamma}=0,

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle K_{mn}D^{\gamma}_{m}\mathcal{A}_{n}=0,

(57)

where
D^{\gamma}_{m}=v_{m}^{\gamma}\partial_{\gamma}
. From Eq. (
56
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{+}=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{x}-\partial_{2}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{y},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{A}_{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{y}-\partial_{3}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{z}.

(58)

The conservation of
\mathcal{A}_{2}(\mathbf{r})
and
\mathcal{A}_{+}(\mathbf{r})
has a simple physical interpretation. In this classical theory, the Hamiltonian is a function of the mutually commuting fields
\theta^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r})
. These fields are linearly dependent and can be written in terms of only two independent components. For the parametrization in Eqs. (
40
) and (
42
), we have
\theta^{x}=\theta_{2}+2\theta_{+}
,
\theta^{y}=-\theta_{2}
, and
\theta^{z}=-2\theta_{+}
. Thus, the conservation of
\theta^{\gamma}
implies that
\mathcal{A}_{2}
and
\mathcal{A}_{+}
become conserved quantities in the gauge theory.

The theory also contains local operators that involve the fields
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{-}=(\theta_{3}-\theta_{4})/2
and act nontrivially on the eigenvalues of
A^{\gamma}\sim\cos(\theta^{\gamma})
. For instance, we have

\sigma_{j}^{z}=-i\Gamma^{1}_{j}\Gamma^{2}_{j}\sim e^{-i\theta_{1}},

(59)

where we omit the factors that depend on
\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{+}
, which only give unimportant phases when acting on a ground state. The operator
e^{-i\theta_{1}}
anticommutes with
\cos(\theta^{x,y})
on the same site, but commutes with
\cos(\theta^{z})
. We can also check that
e^{-i\theta_{1}}
commutes with the terms
\cos(2\theta^{\gamma})
in the effective Hamiltonian in Eq. (
47
). Similarly,
\sigma_{j}^{x}\sim e^{-i(2\theta_{1}+\theta_{-})}
anticommutes with
\cos(\theta^{y,z})
, but commutes with
\cos(\theta^{x})
. Once again, these relations confirm that the mapping in Eq. (
41
) reproduces the behavior observed in the lattice model.

We can now identify the line operators in the effective field theory. Consider the model defined on a torus with
L_{yz}
unit cells in the direction of
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}
and
L_{xy}
unit cells in the direction of
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\hat{\mathbf{y}}
. Note that
\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}=\bar{x}_{3}
and
\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2}=\bar{x}_{1}
. The line operator associated with applying
\sigma_{j}^{z}
along an
xy
chain that winds around the torus has the form

V^{\sigma}_{l_{xy}}\sim\prod_{k=0}^{L_{yz}-1}e^{-i\theta_{1}(\mathbf{r}+ka_{0}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1})}e^{-i\theta_{1}(\mathbf{r}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1}+ka_{0}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1})},

(60)

where
\mathbf{r}
belongs to the odd sublattice. Note that each line in the direction of
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1}
has length
\ell_{1}=L_{yz}a_{0}
and can be labeled by
(\mathbf{r}+ka_{0}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{1})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{3}=x_{3}
. Thus, in the continuum limit,

V^{\sigma}_{l_{xy}}\to\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3})\sim e^{-i\oint d\bar{x}_{3}(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\rm o}+\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\rm e})}.

(61)

Here we have restored the indices
\nu=\text{e},\text{o}
for even and odd sublattices, with the convention
\mathcal{A}_{n}=(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\rm o}+\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\rm e})/2
. Similarly, the line operator acting on a
yz
line with length
\ell_{2}=L_{xy}a_{0}
is given in the continuum limit by

V^{\sigma}_{l_{yz}}\to\mathcal{V}_{x}(x_{1})\sim e^{-i\oint d\bar{x}_{1}(2\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\rm o}+2\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\rm e}+\mathcal{A}_{-}^{\rm o}+\mathcal{A}_{-}^{\rm e})}.

(62)

Using the commutation relation

[\mathcal{A}^{\nu}_{m}(\mathbf{r}),\mathcal{A}_{n}^{\nu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})]=i\delta_{\nu\nu^{\prime}}(K^{-1})_{mn}\delta(\bar{x}_{1}-\bar{x}_{1}^{\prime})\delta(\bar{x}_{3}-\bar{x}_{3}^{\prime}),

(63)

we verify that

[\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3}),\mathcal{V}_{x}(x_{1})]=0,\qquad\forall\,x_{1},x_{3}.

(64)

Importantly, line operators running in different directions commute with each other because their crossing contains two sites, one in each sublattice. To see that they also commute with the Hamiltonian, we need to invoke the lattice regularization and interpret the spatial derivative in Eq. (
47
) as
\partial_{\gamma}\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}=\sqrt{3}(\mathcal{A}^{\gamma,\rm e}-\mathcal{A}^{\gamma,\rm o})/a_{0}
, noting that conjugation by a line operator shifts
\mathcal{A}^{\gamma,\nu}
by the same amount for both sublattices.

In addition to the line operators
V_{l}^{\sigma}
, the SOKM has conserved quantities associated with contractible loops because the lines along which we apply the
\sigma_{j}^{\gamma}
operators can make
120^{\circ}
turns without creating any excitations.
The perturbation in Eq. (
29
) breaks the conservation of the line operators
V^{\sigma}_{l_{xz}}
and associates an energy cost with the corners between the
l_{xy}
and
l_{yz}
lines. As a result, contractible loops are excluded from the ground state sector, and the ground states are generated by rigid line operators acting upon the reference state with uniform
A_{j}^{\gamma}
. From the perspective of the effective field theory, the perturbation generates terms that break the
Z_{3}
symmetry but still commute with
\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3})
and
\mathcal{V}_{x}(x_{1})
.

The ground state degeneracy is associated with the nontrivial action of the line operators on the local conserved quantities of the classical model. For instance,

\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3})e^{ia_{0}\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}=e^{i\pi a_{0}\delta(x_{3}-x_{3}^{\prime})}e^{ia_{0}\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3}).

(65)

Note that, when written in terms of
\mathcal{A}_{n}(\mathbf{r})
, the local conserved quantities involve the short-distance scale
a_{0}
, and the correct anticommutation relation requires the regularization
a_{0}\delta(x_{3}-x_{3}^{\prime})\to 1
for
x_{3}\to x_{3}^{\prime}
, i.e., when the line operator
\mathcal{V}_{xy}(x_{3})
crosses the point
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
. It is convenient to use the local conserved quantities to define another set of line operators:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{x}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-i\oint d\bar{x}_{1}\mathcal{A}^{x,\rm e}},

(66)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{z}(x_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-i\oint d\bar{x}_{3}\mathcal{A}^{z,\rm e}},

(67)

which act on only one sublattice. We then obtain the nontrivial algebra

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{x}(x_{1})\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3})=-\mathcal{V}_{z}(x_{3})\mathcal{U}_{x}(x_{1}),

(68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{z}(x_{3})\mathcal{V}_{x}(x_{1})=-\mathcal{V}_{x}(x_{1})\mathcal{U}_{z}(x_{3}).

(69)

Equations (
68
) and (
69
) imply that the ground state degeneracy scales exponentially with the total number of lines, as
2^{L_{xy}+L_{yz}}
. As usual in effective field theories for fractons
Seiberg and Shao 2020
;
Seiberg and Shao 2021a
;
Fontana et al. 2021
;
Fontana et al. 2022
;
Fontana and Pereira 2023
;
Pretko 2017a
;
Pretko 2017b
;
Slagle and Kim 2017
;
Slagle et al. 2019
;
Burnell et al. 2022
;
Rudelius et al. 2021
, this counting depends on a lattice regularization since the number of lines is given by the lengths of the cycles divided by the short-distance scale,
L_{yz}=\ell_{1}/a_{0}
and
L_{xy}=\ell_{2}/a_{0}
.

V
Conclusion and Outlook

We unveiled the spectrum, level degeneracy, and partition function
of the classical SOKM, which is the parent Hamiltonian describing
quantum simulations of Rydberg atoms placed on the ruby lattice
(
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
)
.
The mapping between
S=3/2
honeycomb models and spin models on the
kagome lattice allows an understanding of the SOKM entropy and specific
heat in terms of the kagome spin ice. We developed a low-energy field theory
using methods to study
\Gamma
-matrix fracton models. We identified the underlying
gauge transformations and showed that the continuum theory recovers the ground state degeneracy of the lattice model. In addition to providing exact results for the SOKM, our symmetry analysis guided the proposal of a perturbation that keeps the model classical but induces immobile fractonic excitations. We expect that our work will guide further research on
realistic implementations of FSLs and possible pathways to connect them
with QSLs. Another interesting line of inquiry concerns the adaptability
of other simulators for kagome spin ice phases
(
Tanaka et al. 2006
;
Lopez-Bezanilla et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2012
)

to simulate the fracton model discussed here.

Our work indicates that classical integrable spin models with stable
FSLs can be formulated from exactly solvable models with Majorana
QSL ground states. In quantum models, the extensive
Z_{2}
symmetries
can be exactly mapped onto a static
Z_{2}
gauge field, thus allowing
their formulation in terms of free fermions. The symmetries in the
classical counterparts are responsible for their exponentially large
ground state degeneracy; being selective on how to break such symmetries
allows us to introduce fractons. This procedure can be immediately
applied to investigate extensions of the SOKM on other three-coordinated
lattices that are known to host Kitaev spin liquids
(
Yao and Kivelson 2007
;
O’Brien et al. 2016
;
Peri et al. 2020
;
Cassella et al. 2023
)

to verify the effect of lattice topology on the fracton properties.
In particular, the SOKM is exactly solvable in three-coordinated hyperbolic
lattices
(
Dusel et al. 2024
;
Lenggenhager et al. 2024
)
, so that we expect that
the corresponding fracton theory satisfies key properties of the AdS/CFT
correspondence
(
Yan 2019
)
. The Rydberg atoms can be arranged in
all these lattices using a recently formulated protocol with programmable
tweezer arrays
(
Julià-Farré et al. 2024
)
. As a further step, just as
the KHM principles can be extended to formulate exactly solvable
models with spin
S>1/2

(
Nussinov and Ortiz 2009
;
Chulliparambil et al. 2020
;
Chulliparambil et al. 2021
)
,
the SOKM and its fracton model are expected to be extended to more
general
\Gamma
-matrix models.

The simplicity of the fracton model indicates that it can be efficiently simulated using Monte Carlo algorithms
(
Placke et al. 2024
)
. Developing such
numerical methods are useful to study the SOKM with classical symmetry-breaking
perturbations, most notably the longitudinal field
h_{l}
.
An interesting question concerns the possible induction of magnetization
plateaus through these fields akin to those observed in kagome
spin ice
(
Moessner and Sondhi 2003
)
. These numerical methods can also uncover
static and dynamic correlation functions, thus providing phase signatures
in a larger range of experimental parameters.

Finally, we point out that the spin-orbital representation used throughout this paper can also aid the discussion of quantum fluctuations. The simplest example occurs for the case
\Omega=t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime}=0
in Eq. (
2
), which was called
f
-anyon fluctuations in Ref.
Verresen and Vishwanath 2022
. This term is translated into the spin-orbital notation by an out-of-plane external field
H_{Z}=-\Omega\sum_{i}(\tau^{x}_{i}+\tau^{y}_{i}+\tau^{z}_{i})
. This result connects our study on quantum simulations with those on the Kitaev
Sun and Chen 2009
;
Sørensen et al. 2023a
;
Sørensen et al. 2023b
;
Richards and Sørensen 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
and Yao-Lee
Seifert et al. 2020
;
Chulliparambil et al. 2021
models under out-of-plane external fields. The rich phenomenology related to those quantum fluctuations will be the subject of future research.

Note
- As we concluded this manuscript, we came across an independent work studying the thermodynamics and static structure factor of the SOKM using the Monte Carlo method
(
Yan and Pohle 2025
)
. Our results are entirely consistent with their numerical simulations.
