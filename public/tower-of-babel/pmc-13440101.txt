Immune cell subsets linking insomnia trajectories and incident cognitive impairment: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Liantian Chen, Jinjing Fu, Xueqing Jia, Liming Zhang, Xinli Jin, Shuhui Wu, Bin Zhu, Kaili Sun, Duoduo Fu, Yanyan Wang, Zuyun Liu, Shimin Li, Jingyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to examine the associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment, as well as to evaluate whether immune cell subsets mediate these associations.MethodsA total of 6,316 participants aged 50 years or older from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) were included in this prospective cohort study. Insomnia symptoms, including severe insomnia, difficulty initiating sleep (DIS), difficulty maintaining sleep (DMS), early morning awakening (EMA), and nonrestorative sleep (NRS), were assessed using the Jenkins Sleep Questionnaire. Longitudinal insomnia trajectories were identified from three examination waves during 2010-2016 (waves 10, 12 and 13) using latent class trajectory modeling. Incident cognitive impairment was defined according to the Langa-Weir cognitive status classification during 2016-2022 (waves 13-16). Data on immune cell subsets were obtained through flow cytometry of peripheral blood samples collected in the 2016 HRS Venous Blood Substudy. Cox proportional hazards models and mediation analyses were performed to assess associations.ResultsTrajectories of DIS, EMA, and NRS were significantly associated with increased risk of incident cognitive impairment. Compared to participants with persistently good trajectories, those with worsening trajectories demonstrated significantly elevated risks of cognitive impairment, with hazard ratios of 1.37 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.16, 1.61) for DIS; 1.26 (95% CI: 1.07, 1.48) for EMA; and 1.16 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.35) for NRS. Mediation analysis revealed seven specific immune cell subsets as mediators of the associations of insomnia trajectories (DIS, EMA, and NRS) with the risk of incident cognitive impairment: CD4 + T cells (central memory, CM), CD4 + T cells (Naive), CD4 + T cells (Effector Memory, Tem), CD8 + T cells, CD8 + T cells (CM), CD8 + T cells (TemRA) and NK Cells (CD56LO), accounting for 1.16% to 8.21% of the total effect. Sex-stratified analysis revealed distinct mediation patterns: CD4 + T cells (Tem) and CD8 + T cells mediated the associations in males, whereas CD4 + T cells (CM), CD8 + T cells (CM) mediated the associations in females.ConclusionsThese findings reveal the associations of dynamic insomnia trajectories with cognitive health, and potential immune mechanisms, suggesting that maintaining healthy sleep patterns may mitigate neurodegenerative progression.

Background

Cognitive impairment, which disproportionately affects older adults, is a major public health concern in ageing societies [1, 2]. Its burden is expected to increase substantially as populations age, imposing considerable clinical, caregiving, and socioeconomic costs [3, 4]. Although disease-modifying therapies for cognitive decline have recently advanced, their applicability remains limited, highlighting the continued importance of prevention [3, 5]. Accordingly, identifying modifiable risk factors remains an urgent public health priority for safeguarding cognitive health in ageing societies [6, 7].

Among lifestyle-related factors, insomnia warrants particular attention because it is prevalent among older adults and can be modified through scalable, evidence-based treatments [8, 9]. Emerging evidence indicates that insomnia serves as a significant risk factor for the development of various chronic diseases, particularly neurodegenerative diseases [10–15]. Insomnia may exert its effects early in the neurocognitive disease process through plausible biological pathways, including sleep fragmentation, circadian dysregulation, and downstream neuroimmune alterations, thereby potentially influencing the transition from normal cognition to cognitive impairment [16–19]. Nonetheless, current research examining the association between insomnia and cognitive impairment faces several critical gaps. First, the evidence linking specific insomnia symptoms to cognitive impairment is inconsistent, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about their distinct effects on cognitive health [20]. Second, the impact of changes in chronic insomnia patterns on the risk of incident cognitive impairment has yet to be sufficiently elucidated [11]. Although a recent study has examined the association between trajectories of overall insomnia symptom burden and subsequent cognitive decline [21], evidence remains limited regarding the distinct information conveyed by changes in individual insomnia symptoms. Third, accumulating evidence suggests that insomnia is associated with immune dysregulation, including alterations in specific immune cell subsets [17, 22–25]. In parallel, dysregulation of immune cell subsets has been linked with cognitive impairment and related pathology [26–29]. Compared with conventional inflammatory markers, immune cell subsets may capture more granular and potentially targetable immunological features that point to specific immune pathways (e.g., immunosenescence [24], T-cell exhaustion [28], and regulatory imbalance [30]) and reflect immune remodelling. However, it remains unclear whether specific immune cell subsets mediate the insomnia-cognitive impairment association.

Therefore, leveraging longitudinal data on various insomnia symptoms and immune cell subsets from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), this study aims to: (1) examine the prospective associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment, and (2) delineate the mediating effects of distinct immune cell subsets in these associations. The findings may help identify potential neuroprotective targets and facilitate the development of preventive strategies to mitigate cognitive impairment.

Methods

Study participants

The HRS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey of U.S. adults aged 50 and older, comprising approximately 20,000 community-dwelling participants and conducted biennially in survey waves [31, 32]. Questionnaire data, including demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics, were collected through face-to-face or telephone interviews. Mortality information was ascertained through linkage to administrative death records from the National Death Index and the Social Security Administration, as provided by the HRS data products (https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/data-products/restricted-data). In 2016, all HRS participants who completed the interview and did not respond via proxy were invited to consent to provide peripheral blood samples (Venous Blood Study of HRS, VBS). A total of 9,934 participants provided blood samples and underwent flow cytometry assays. Detailed information regarding the cohort profile is available elsewhere [33, 34].

The HRS was approved by the Institute for Social Research and Survey Research Centre of the University of Michigan (IRB Protocol: HUM00061128). All participants or their guardians provided written informed consent at the time of data collection. The datasets are released to researchers in an anonymized form to ensure participant confidentiality. Participants are informed of data sharing and linkage procedures and retain the opportunity to opt out where applicable. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

This longitudinal study leveraged data from six waves of HRS data spanning from wave 10 (2010) to wave 16 (2022). Insomnia trajectories were assessed across waves 10, 12, and 13 (2010-2016) with wave 13 (2016) designated as the baseline (wave 11 was omitted due to substantial missing data on insomnia). Participants were followed through 2022 to determine cognitive health status, as shown in Fig. 1. Briefly, of the 20,912 participants surveyed in 2016, we excluded those meeting the following criteria: (1) missing insomnia data in any of the three assessment waves (N = 6,047); (2) non-participation in the VBS or missing >80% of immune cell subset measurements (N = 6,564); (3) missing covariate information (N = 147); or (4) diagnosed with cognitive impairment (N = 1,838). Assessments of insomnia symptoms and identification of insomnia trajectories

Insomnia symptoms were assessed using a modified version of the Jenkins Sleep Questionnaire [35]. As detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1, participants reported on four aspects of sleep disturbance: difficulty initiating sleep (DIS), difficulty maintaining sleep (DMS), waking up too early and being unable to fall back asleep (early morning awakening, EMA), and the frequency of feeling rested upon waking in the morning. Response options were “rarely or never”, “sometimes”, and “most of the time”. In this study, the original fourth question was rephrased to assess the frequency of not feeling rested upon waking (nonrestorative sleep, NRS). Participants who reported experiencing any of the four symptoms “sometimes” or “most of the time” were classified as having the corresponding subtype of insomnia. Those who reported experiencing any symptom “most of the time” were classified as having severe insomnia at each wave (2010, 2012 and 2016).

We used Latent Class Trajectory Modelling (LCTM) to identify insomnia trajectories through the R package lcmm (version 1.2.0). LCTM is a specialized form of finite mixture modelling used to identify latent classes of individuals who exhibit similar longitudinal patterns over time or with age. Our model employed a second-order polynomial and calculated the posterior probabilities for each trajectory, allowing us to classify participants based on their distinct longitudinal patterns of insomnia symptom change [36]. Each participant was assigned to the trajectory corresponding to their highest posterior probability. The optimal number of trajectories was determined based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion, with additional criteria requiring that the posterior probability for each class exceed 0.70 and that each class comprise at least 2% of the total sample [37]. To enhance interpretability, each trajectory was labelled according to its graphical patterns of the model (Fig. 2). Assessment of incident cognitive impairment

We assessed cognitive status using the Langa-Weir cognitive status classification based on HRS data (Additional file 1: Table S2). A composite self-respondent cognitive health score (0-27 points) was calculated by summing scores from multiple cognitive domains, including word recall (0-20 points), working memory (0-5 points), and mental processing (0-2 points) [38, 39]. Missing values were imputed by HRS researchers [40].

In this study, cognitive impairment was defined as a composite score of ≤ 11, while dementia was defined as a subgroup with a composite score of ≤6 [6]. Time-to-event was measured from the baseline (2016) to the earliest occurrence of any of the following: incident cognitive impairment, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of follow-up in wave 16 (2022). We used the RAND variable RwIWEND to define the interview end date for each follow-up wave and RADDATE to define the date of death.

Assessment of immune cell subsets

Data on immune cell subsets were obtained via flow cytometry at baseline (2016). A multiparameter flow cytometry assay was used to measure counts and percentages for the distribution of 24 distinct immune cell subsets (reporting a total of 48 measurements) from approximately 9,300 participants [41–44]. Detailed information about the cell subsets is provided in Additional file 1: Table S3. Specifically, one vial of cryopreserved mononuclear cell sample, containing approximately 4 million cells, was thawed and incubated at 37°C in RPMI media for 1 hour. The cells were then centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 10 minutes at room temperature. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in 1X PBS and stained with a mixture of two antibody panels. The samples were kept on ice until analysis. All flow cytometry measurements were conducted using either an LSRII or Fortessa X20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Diego, CA). Additional details on sample collection and data processing can be found on the HRS website (https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/publications/biblio/9065 and https://hrsdata.isr.umich.edu/data-products/vbs-2016-flow-cytometry). To maximize data availability, remaining missing values in specific immune cell subset measures were imputed using the predictive mean matching algorithm in the mice R package.

Covariates

Drawing on previous research [6], we included the following covariates from the HRS questionnaires: age, sex, marital status, race/ethnicity, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, depressive symptoms and chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, lung disease, heart disease, and stroke). Detailed definitions and assessment methods for each covariate are presented in Additional file 1: Supplementary Text: Method of Covariates [45, 46].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized for the overall sample and stratified by severe insomnia trajectory groups and individual insomnia symptom trajectory groups. Categorical variables were presented using counts (percentages). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Intergroup differences in baseline characteristics across severe insomnia trajectory groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and Chi-squared (χ2) tests for categorical variables. Frequencies of incident cognitive impairment and the changes across waves were calculated and visualized using a Sankey diagram (Additional file 1: Figure S2).

Associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment

Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to examine the prospective associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment, with the persistently good trajectory group serving as the reference group. The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Three models were constructed for analysis: Crude model was unadjusted; Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 was additionally adjusted for marital status, race/ethnicity, physical activity, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, depressive symptoms and chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, lung disease, heart disease, and stroke) based on model 1. After assessing adherence to the proportional hazards assumption by using Schoenfeld residuals method against time, no violations of this assumption were observed.

Mediation analysis

Immune cell counts were first natural log-transformed; and both immune cell counts and percentages were then standardized as z‐scores. To investigate whether immune cell subsets mediated the associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment, we conducted the following analysis in addition to the Cox proportional hazards regression models described above. First, multivariable linear regression models were employed to assess the associations between insomnia trajectories and immune cell subsets, reporting regression coefficient (β) with 95% CIs. Next, Cox proportional hazards models were applied to examine the associations between immune cell subsets and the risk of incident cognitive impairment, reporting HRs with 95% CIs. All models were adjusted for age and sex. A Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. Finally, mediation analysis was performed for immune cell subsets that were significantly associated with both insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment, using the R package mediation (version 4.5.0) with 1,000 bootstrap simulations. The mediation proportions and their corresponding 95% CIs were then reported.

Sex-specific analysis

Given the observed differences in immune cell profiles and insomnia between males and females, we conducted sex‐stratified analysis. Specifically, we repeated all association and mediation analysis separately for males and females to examine whether the mediating effects of immune cell subsets on the association between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment differed by sex.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we compared the baseline characteristics of participants excluded due to missing insomnia symptoms data across waves 10, 12, and 13 with those of the analytical cohort. Second, for associations of insomnia trajectories with incident cognitive impairment, we performed additional analysis as follows: (1) we further adjusted for baseline insomnia symptoms (i.e., DIS, DMS, EMA, NRS) based on Model 2; (2) we considered death as a competing risk and applied a Fine–Gray competing risk regression model to examine the associations between insomnia trajectories and incident cognitive impairment, reporting subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) with 95% CIs; (3) we excluded participants who developed incident cognitive impairment within two years of baseline (N = 619) to address possible reverse causation; (4) we additionally adjusted for HRS personal sampling weight (RAND variable: RwWTRESP) based on Model 2 to account for the survey design and differential nonresponse; (5) we examined the association between insomnia trajectories and cognitive health status (normal/cognitive impairment) at wave 16 (2022) using logistic regression; (6) we examined associations between baseline insomnia symptoms and incident cognitive impairment, to assess whether insomnia trajectories provide additional information beyond baseline symptoms regarding cognitive health. Third, given the potential influence of hypertension and diabetes on immune cell profiles, we conducted an additional mediation analysis by further adjusting for hypertension and diabetes based on Model 1. Finally, we repeated the mediation analysis using incident dementia as the outcome, which was defined as a score of ≤ 6 according to the Langa-Weir cognitive status classification [11].

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.1.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As shown in Fig. 2 and Additional file 2: Table S5, we identified five distinct trajectories each for severe insomnia, DIS, and DMS; six trajectories for EMA; and three trajectories for NRS. For DIS, EMA and NRS, the largest proportion of participants followed the persistently good trajectory (34.47%, 32.63%, and 77.76%, respectively). For DMS, the most common trajectory was the persistently poor trajectory (37.87%), and for severe insomnia, the most common trajectory was the improving trajectory (37.78%).

Prospective associations of insomnia trajectories with the risk of incident cognitive impairment

Over a median follow-up of 5.74 years, 1,463 participants (23.16%) developed incident cognitive impairment. The associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment are presented in Additional file 2: Table S5. Our analysis revealed that trajectories for each insomnia symptom were significantly associated with incident cognitive impairment in the crude model. These associations remained significant for trajectories of DIS, EMA, and NRS in the full adjusted Model 2 (Fig. 2). For instance, compared with participants with a persistently good trajectory, those with a worsening trajectory had higher risks of incident cognitive impairment, with HRs of 1.37 (95% CI: 1.16, 1.61) for DIS, 1.26 (95% CI: 1.07, 1.48) for EMA, and 1.16 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.35) for NRS. Notably, improving trajectories were also associated with a higher risk of incident cognitive impairment for DIS (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.00, 1.43) and EMA (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.08, 1.45). In contrast, an improving trajectory of severe insomnia showed a trend toward a lower risk of incident cognitive impairment, although the association did not reach statistical significance (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.74, 1.02; P = 0.076).

Mediation analysis of immune cell subsets in the associations between insomnia trajectories and the risk of incident cognitive impairment

Among the 48 analysed immune cell subset measurements, 19 demonstrated significant associations with the risk of incident cognitive impairment after adjusting for age and sex (Additional file 2: Table S6). Of these, 11 measurements were positively associated and eight were negatively associated with incident cognitive impairment. After Bonferroni correction for multiple testing, three measurements (percentage of CD4 + T cells (central memory [CM]), CD4 + T cells (Naïve) and NK Cells (CD56HI)) remained significant (P < 0.05/48). We then examined the associations between insomnia trajectories and immune cell subsets (Additional file 2: Table S7) and identified the overlapping subsets that were nominally significant association with both insomnia trajectories and incident cognitive impairment. As shown in Fig. 3A, a lower percentage of CD4 + T cells (Naive) was associated with unfavourable insomnia trajectories and with a higher risk of incident cognitive impairment. Sensitive analysis

First, compared with participants excluded due to missing insomnia symptoms data, the analytical sample was older and had a higher incidence of cognitive impairment, while distributions of sex, BMI, and alcohol consumptions were broadly similar. (Additional file 2: Table S13). Second, results for the associations between insomnia trajectories and incident cognitive impairment were broadly consistent across several sensitivity analyses, including: (1) further adjustment for baseline insomnia symptoms; (2) Fine–Gray competing risk regression accounting for all-cause mortality; (3) exclusion of participants who developed incident cognitive impairment within 2 years after baseline; (4) additional adjustment using HRS personal sampling weight; and (5) logistic regression of insomnia trajectories with cognitive status (normal/cognitive impairment) at Wave 16 (Additional file 2: Tables S14-17). Third, analyses of baseline insomnia symptoms showed that only severe insomnia and EMA were associated with a higher risk of incident cognitive impairment, with HRs of 1.29 (95% CI: 1.16, 1.44) and 1.13 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.26), respectively (Additional file 2: Table S18). Additionally, mediation analyses further adjusted for hypertension and diabetes based on Model 1 yielded results consistent with our main analysis (Additional file 2: Tables S6-S7). Finally, when incident dementia was used as the outcome, trajectories of DIS, DMS, and EMA were significantly associated with incident dementia. Twelve immune cell subset measurements were significant in unadjusted models; however, none remained significant after covariate adjustment. Mediation analyses were conducted only in unadjusted models, in which six immune cell subsets mediated the association between DMS trajectories and incident dementia, with proportions mediated ranging from 18.07% to 32.12% (Additional file 2: Tables S19-S21).

Discussion

In this large-scale prospective study, we identified distinct trajectories of insomnia symptoms and examined their associations with the risk of incident cognitive impairment. Participants with trajectories characterized by unfavourable insomnia symptom patterns (i.e., persistently poor, good-poor-good or worsening trajectories) had a higher risk of developing cognitive impairment, highlighting these subgroups as potential candidates for targeted intervention and close monitoring. Mediation analysis further revealed seven immune cell subsets that significantly mediated the insomnia trajectories-cognitive impairment associations, with stratified analysis suggesting sex-specific mediation patterns. Collectively, by integrating longitudinal insomnia trajectories with immune cell subsets, our findings extend existing evidence and suggest sex-specific mediation patterns involving immune cell subsets in the association of insomnia trajectories with incident cognitive impairment, providing preliminary evidence for future mechanistic investigations of sleep-related cognitive decline.

Recent research has focused on the long-term impact of sleep disorders on cognitive health [6, 21, 47]. Our study identified distinct trajectory patterns among various insomnia symptoms, indicating heterogeneous symptom dynamics over time. Huang et al. [21] modelled trajectories using a composite insomnia score and reported that all unfavourable trajectories were significantly associated with cognitive decline. In contrast, by examining symptom-specific trajectories, our findings substantiated that unfavourable trajectories of DIS, EMA, and NRS were associated with elevated risks of incident cognitive impairment, whereas trajectories of DMS were not. These findings emphasize the value of analysing individual insomnia symptom trajectories rather than relying solely on a composite burden score. Notably, the improving trajectory of EMA was associated with elevated risk of incident cognitive impairment, with a hazard ratio comparable to that of the worsening trajectory. A potential explanation is that early and sustained sleep disturbances may have caused irreversible brain damage (e.g., amyloid-β deposition), which could continue to affect cognition even if symptoms later improve [48]. In addition, an improving trajectory of severe insomnia showed a trend toward a lower risk of incident cognitive impairment. This may suggest that reductions in persistent, high-frequency insomnia, even partial improvements, could be beneficial for cognitive health [21].

Moreover, analysis of baseline insomnia symptoms yielded different findings, showing that baseline severe insomnia and EMA were associated with a higher risk of incident cognitive impairment. In contrast, trajectory-based analyses provided additional longitudinal information that was not captured cross-sectionally; specifically, a worsening trajectory of NRS was associated with a higher risk of incident cognitive impairment, highlighting the value of symptom-specific, long-term management of insomnia. Previous studies have reported conflicting findings [19, 20, 49, 50]. Sindi et al. reported that only EMA was significantly linked to an increased risk of dementia, whereas other insomnia components demonstrated no such association [51]. By contrast, Jaussent et al. reported NRS to be a risk factor for cognitive decline and DMS to be protective [20]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in study populations, heterogeneity in the instruments used to assess sleep and cognitive health, and the presence of potential unmeasured confounders. Further research is warranted to clarify the distinct contributions of specific insomnia symptoms to the development of cognitive impairment.

Notably, to our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine the mediating role of specific immune cell subsets in associations between insomnia trajectories and risk of incident cognitive impairment. Our findings suggest immune-related mediation patterns involving T cell markers, including lower levels of CD4 + T cells (Naive) and higher levels of CD4 + T cells (CM) and CD8 + T cells (TemRA). These results extend existing evidence by providing preliminary evidence of immune-related mediation patterns in the links between insomnia trajectories and cognitive impairment. By highlighting immune-related features that may be involved in these associations, our findings may help generate hypotheses for future studies. Accumulating evidence indicates that sleep plays an important role in maintaining immune homeostasis and adaptive immune function [52], whereas sleep disturbances, such as insomnia, may be associated with shifts in peripheral immune profiles and inflammatory activity [53]. Chronic insomnia has been linked to systemic inflammation dysregulation, including alterations in cytokine signalling and neuroendocrine rhythms, which collectively may modulate immune cell proliferation and differentiation [54, 55]. Importantly, cognitive impairment is closely associated with adaptive immune remodelling, characterized by a progressive transition of T cell phenotypes from naive to memory and terminally differentiated subtypes [30, 56–58]. Such immune dysregulation has been proposed as a contributor to neurodegenerative processes and may be detectable even during the preclinical stage before overt cognitive symptoms [59, 60]. In this aberrant immune response, vascular inflammation and increased blood–brain barrier permeability may facilitate leukocyte trafficking into the central nervous system, potentially amplifying neuroinflammation and neuronal injury, ultimately leading to neuronal damage and synaptic loss [61–64]. Furthermore, persistent antigen exposure accelerates immunosenescence in naive T cells, disrupting T cell subset equilibrium and precipitating immune dysfunction [58]. Overall, these findings not only underscore the promise of immunomodulatory strategies for cognitive impairment prevention but also provide clinically relevant insights for personalized preventive interventions targeting sleep-related immune dysregulation. Furthermore, they call for future prospective and interventional studies to clarify the biological mechanisms among sleep, immunity, and cognitive health.

We conducted sex-stratified mediation analysis to investigate the divergent roles of immune cell subsets in the association between insomnia trajectories and incident cognitive impairment. Notably, we identified sex-specific mediation patterns: in males, CD4 + T cells (Tem) and CD8 + T cells significantly mediated the association between insomnia trajectories and incident cognitive impairment, whereas in females, CD4 + T cells (CM) and CD8 + T cells (CM) were identified as significant mediators. Previous studies have highlighted sex differences in immune cell composition and function, as well as sex-dimorphic patterns of immune aging [65–67]. These differences are often attributed to sex hormones and age-related changes in immune regulation, including thymic involution, which may become more pronounced with aging [66, 67]. Understanding these sex-specific differences in immune profiles may provide valuable insights into how immune function contributes to sex-based differences in cognitive healthspan [28, 53, 57, 68]. Overall, our findings provide preliminary evidence that insomnia trajectories may be associated with incident cognitive impairment in older adults, with exploratory evidence of sex-specific immune-mediated patterns, highlighting the value of integrating sleep trajectory profiling with sex-specific immune status assessments to inform future risk stratification and prevention research.

The main strengths of our study include the use of symptom-specific insomnia trajectories beyond a conventional composite burden score, the provision of additional biological insight through mediation analyses of immune cell subsets, and extensive sensitivity analysis. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, assessment of key factors, including insomnia symptoms, the diagnosis of cognitive impairment, and other covariates, relied on self-reported HRS interviews, which may be susceptible to recall biases and misclassification. Future studies should incorporate gold-standard diagnostic criteria for cognitive impairment and objective device-based measurements of sleep. Second, the analysis was restricted to participants who completed the 2016 VBS substudy, did not respond by proxy, and had no missing insomnia data, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Third, data on immune cell subsets were only available from the 2016 HRS survey wave, precluding the evaluation of longitudinal changes in immune markers over follow-up. Additionally, future studies should include a more comprehensive range of T cell phenotypes, including T helper and regulatory T cells, to better understand these associations. Fourth, although participants were followed through 2022, longer follow-up is needed to further reduce the potential for reverse causality. Fifth, participants in the HRS may not be fully representative of other demographic populations, emphasizing the need for validation in larger and more diverse cohorts. Sixth, although we applied a multiple-comparison correction to main association analyses, the mediation results remain exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, although we adjusted for major confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured or unknown factors cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates the associations between dynamic changes in insomnia symptoms and cognitive health, suggesting that maintaining healthy sleep patterns may slow neurodegenerative progression by modulating sex-specific immune cell subsets. This work advocates for personalized, continuous sleep monitoring and management strategies to preserve and safeguard cognitive health in middle-aged and older adults.
