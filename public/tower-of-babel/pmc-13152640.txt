Experimental and computational analysis of SERCA inhibition by naphthoquinone derivatives
Authors: Sophie Cheng, Sahil Kumar, Sydni Sobota, Aubrei Drennan, Nick Johnson, Sarah Maleh, Dina Almahmodi, Stefan Paula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNaphthoquinones are small aromatic compounds with diverse biological activities and established applications in medicine and industry. Previous studies have identified a limited number of naphthoquinones that inhibit the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA), an ion-translocating enzyme that plays a central role in cellular calcium homeostasis. To further explore naphthoquinone-mediated SERCA inhibition and its molecular determinants, we evaluated the inhibitory potencies of 13 commercially available naphthoquinones using enzyme activity assays with SERCA1a, enabling the establishment of structure-activity relationships (SARs). The most potent compounds inhibited SERCA at low micromolar concentrations and were characterized by the presence of a hydroxy or nitro substituent at the C5 position. In contrast, electronegative substituents at the C2 position produced mixed effects on potency whereas increased steric bulk reduced or eliminated inhibitory potency. Possible inhibition mechanisms were considered, including covalent modification of cysteine residues and reversible binding to defined sites on the enzyme, with the latter being more consistent with the observed SARs. Molecular simulations of naphthoquinone binding to SERCA further supported this model and provided insight into potential ligand-protein interactions within the ATP-binding pocket.Keywords: Calcium homeostasis, Calcium ion transport, ATPase activity assay, Enzyme inhibition, Computational docking, Protein surface screen, SERCA inhibition

Introduction

Naphthoquinones are a family of small aromatic compounds derived from the naphthalene scaffold and characterized by two condensed benzene rings bearing quinone functionalities (Table 1). The most common representatives are 1,2-naphthoquinone and 1,4-naphthoquinone, which differ in the positioning of the two carbonyl groups at the aromatic system. Naphthoquinones occur widely in nature, particularly in plants where they are found in roots, bark, and leaves and are thought to serve signaling functions and contribute to defense against pathogens.1,2 In addition to their long-standing use as dye pigments3-5 (e.g., lawsone, juglone, shikonin), naphthoquinones display a broad range of biological activities, including antimicrobial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and anticancer effects.6-11 Some of these properties have been attributed to antioxidant effects mediated by redox cycling and radical scavenging, which may contribute to cancer-preventive activity. Other effects involve more specific interactions with cellular signaling pathways, resulting in selective toxicity toward cancer cells compared to healthy cells.12 For example, several synthetic naphthoquinone derivatives exhibit selective cytotoxicity against human carcinoma cell lines. One such compound, 2-chloro-3-(methylphenylamino)-1,4-naphthoquinone, displays in vitro IC50 values of 4.3 μg/mL and 10.7 μg/mL against human leukemia (MOLT-3) and breast cancer (MDA-MB-231) cells, respectively. Similarly, the natural product shikonin inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasiveness of HCCLM3 cells and promotes apoptosis.12,13 Another example is the well-known anti-cancer drug doxorubicin, a potent topoisomerase inhibitor that has also shown anti-malarial properties.14 As structural components of the vitamin K family, naphthoquinones also play essential roles in blood clotting, vascular function, and bone health. Hydrophobic naphthoquinone derivatives such as ubiquinone, plastoquinone, and menaquinone reside within mitochondrial and thylakoid membranes where they perform essential electron transfer functions in oxidative phosphorylation and photosynthesis.15

Among the many reported biological activities of naphthoquinones is their ability to inhibit the sarco/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA). SERCA is a transmembrane enzyme that plays a central role in cellular calcium homeostasis by coupling ATP hydrolysis to the transport of calcium ions from the cytosol into the endoplasmic or sarcoplasmic reticulum.16-22 These organelles serve as intracellular calcium stores with calcium concentrations that are orders of magnitude higher than those in the cytosol. Rapid calcium release from these reservoirs leads to transient increases in cytosolic calcium levels, which trigger a range of physiologically important processes, including muscle contraction. Inhibition of SERCA disrupts the refilling of calcium stores while calcium release through ion channels such as ryanodine receptors can continue, potentially leading to sustained elevations in cytosolic calcium. Prolonged calcium dysregulation can be cytotoxic and may ultimately trigger apoptosis. This property has been exploited in the development of prodrugs based on the potent SERCA inhibitor thapsigargin that selectively deliver SERCA inhibitors to prostate cancer cells.23-26 Although the potency of thapsigargin remains unmatched, similar prodrug strategies may be applicable to other inhibitor scaffolds, offering alternatives in cases where bioavailability, toxicity, synthetic accessibility, or drug resistance pose challenges. At the same time, the cytotoxic effects associated with SERCA inhibition highlight the potential toxicological risks of compounds that interfere with calcium homeostasis.

Despite these observations, our current understanding of SERCA inhibition by naphthoquinones remains limited. Aside from a virtual screening study that identified a single naphthoquinone as a SERCA inhibitor,27 early work by Floreani and coworkers reported inotropic effects of several naphthoquinones approximately three decades ago (Table 1).28,29 These effects were attributed to SERCA inhibition, supported by enzyme activity assays showing that menadione, plumbagin, and juglone inhibited SERCA, whereas a dimethylated analog was inactive. Because some inhibitory effects occurred with a delay and required incubation of SERCA with the inhibitor, the authors proposed that naphthoquinones inhibit SERCA primarily through covalent modification of cysteine residues via a Michael addition reaction.30 This interpretation was supported by the observation that the inhibitory effects of menadione could be attenuated in the presence of reduced glutathione, a tripeptide capable of restoring cysteine residues to their reduced state. Furthermore, the inability to reverse inhibition of two of the compounds by dilution was interpreted as evidence for irreversible inhibition. While these findings favored a covalent mechanism over classical reversible inhibition involving binding to a defined site on the enzyme, the study was limited to a small number of active compounds, complicating a broader generalization.

To investigate naphthoquinone-mediated SERCA inhibition in greater detail, we systematically examined thirteen naphthoquinone analogs using SERCA activity assays. The compound set was limited to commercially available materials and included commonly used naphthoquinones such as lawsone, menadione, juglone, plumbagin, naphtharazin, dichlone, quinoclamine, and lawsone methyl ether. These compounds featured different substitution patterns in positions 2, 3, 5, and 8 on the naphthoquinone scaffold. Analysis of the resulting structure-activity relationships (SARs) identified structural features in those specific positions that contribute to SERCA inhibition. The results were evaluated in the context of two plausible inhibition mechanisms: covalent modification of cysteine residues and inhibition through binding to a specific inhibitory site on SERCA. The latter possibility was explored further using molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. In addition to providing insight into potential mechanisms of naphthoquinone-mediated SERCA inhibition, our study identifies structural features that may be relevant when assessing the biological activity and toxicological profile of naphthoquinones.

Results and discussion

Observed trends in inhibitory potencies

Ten of the 13 tested naphthoquinones displayed inhibitory activity against the ATP hydrolysis activity of SERCA, with potencies spanning approximately two orders of magnitude (Table 2, Fig. 1, and Fig. 1S, Supplementary Materials). The observed potency of juglone (7) was in excellent agreement with the previously reported value of 11 μM.28

Comparison of the parent compound 1 with mono-substituted derivatives (2–6) revealed a pronounced dependence on the nature of the substituent at position 2. Introduction of a hydroxyl group (lawsone, 2) or a chlorine atom (5) enhanced inhibitory potency, whereas substitution with bromine at the same position (6) reduced activity. In contrast, a methyl group (menadione, 3) or a methoxy group (lawsone methyl ether, 4) at position 2 resulted in inactive compounds. Although menadione has previously been described as a weak SERCA inhibitor, this effect was reported only after extended preincubation with the enzyme, which was not part of the experimental protocol used here. The trend in declining potencies seen in response to increased steric bulk in position 2 in compounds 5, 6, 8 and 4 (chlorine, bromine, methyl, methoxy) point to size limitations within the enzyme's binding pocket. The same trend is observed in the activities of 7 and 8, where the removal of the methyl group in position 2 increases potency by a factor of about 25.

Substitution at position 5 also had a marked effect on activity. Introduction of a hydroxyl group at this position significantly increased potency, as illustrated by comparison of compound pairs 1 and 7 (juglone) as well as 3 and 8 (plumbagin). Notably, addition of a second hydroxyl group at position 8 (naphtharazin, 9) led to reduced potency relative to juglone (7), an observation that is unlikely due to minor impurities potentially present in this particular compound (see section 4.1.).

Additional substitutions further highlighted the sensitivity of SERCA inhibition to both substituent identity and position. Introduction of an amino group at position 3 of compound 5 (quinoclamine, 11) resulted in complete loss of activity. By comparison, introduction of a second chlorine atom at position 3 (compound 10 compared to 5) had little effect, whereas the further addition of a nitro group at position 5 of compound 10 yielded the highly potent inhibitor 12. Consistent with these trends, the multi-substituted compound 13, bearing chlorine atoms at positions 2 and 3 and hydroxyl groups at positions 5 and 8, also exhibited high inhibitory potency which exceeded that of its non-chlorinated parent compound naphtharazin (9) by almost 15-fold.

Taken together, these comparisons suggest that substituents at positions 2 and 5 play key roles in determining SERCA inhibitory potency, with steric constraints at position 2 and potential hydrogen-bonding capability at position 5 emerging as important determinants of activity.

Potential inhibition mechanism by cysteine modification

A study by Floreani and coworkers published in the 1990s evaluated the inhibition of SERCA by compounds 3, 7, 8, and 2,3-dimethylnaphthoquinone.28 In that study, compound 7 was identified as the most potent inhibitor, followed by 8 and 3, whereas 2,3-dimethylnaphthoquinone was inactive (Table 1). The authors further reported that compounds 3 and 8 required preincubation with SERCA to exhibit inhibitory activity.

Based on these observations, the authors proposed an inhibition mechanism involving irreversible covalent modification of SERCA via Michael addition of the naphthoquinone moiety to cysteine residues. Support for this mechanism was drawn from the observation that methyl substitution at positions 2 reduced or abolished inhibitory activity, which was attributed to increased electron density in the aromatic ring that would disfavor a nucleophilic attack by a cysteine sulfur atom. However, this interpretation was based on a limited set of observations and did not fully account for all experimental findings. In particular, the reversibility of SERCA inhibition by compound 3, but not by the other two active compounds is difficult to reconcile with a strictly irreversible covalent mechanism. In addition, covalent modification does not readily explain the pronounced influence of a hydroxy group at position 5, which appears critical for SERCA inhibition despite being remote from the putative Michael addition site.

The inhibitory SAR trends observed in the present study are likewise inconsistent with an irreversible, Michael addition-based mechanism for several reasons. First, compounds 10, 12, and 13 exhibit high inhibitory potencies while bearing substituents at both positions 2 and 3, even though such a substitution pattern would be expected to sterically and electronically hinder a nucleophilic attack by a cysteine residue. Second, substituents located on the second ring (positions 5 and 8) exert a pronounced influence on inhibitory potency, despite being unlikely to affect a Michael addition at positions 2 or 3. Finally, the observation that an electron-withdrawing bromine atom at position 2 (compound 6) reduces potency relative to the parent compound 1 further argues against an irreversible covalent inhibition mechanism.

Potential mechanism by reversible competitive inhibition

As SERCA inhibition via covalent modification of cysteine residues appears unlikely, we next considered the possibility that naphthoquinones interact with SERCA as classical, reversible inhibitors in a fashion similar to the structurally related hydroquinones.31-33 The only SERCA inhibitor with a known binding site that bears some structural resemblance to naphthoquinone is tert-butylhydroquinone (BHQ) which binds to the E2 conformation of the enzyme. In comparison to BHQ, attempts to dock naphthoquinones into the BHQ site were unsatisfactory in that they yielded lower docking scores and no discernible hydrogen bonds (see Fig. S2, Supplementary Materials). Because none of the other SERCA inhibitors for which enzyme-inhibitor crystal structures are available bear substantial resemblance to naphthoquinones, we employed molecular modeling approaches to identify plausible binding sites. Using juglone (7) as a representative high-potency inhibitor, cavity-focused docking was performed with the CB-Dock web server,34,35 using X-ray crystal structures of SERCA in three distinct conformations: E1, E2, and an inhibitor-bound E2 state (PDB codes 1VFP, 3B9B, and 2AGV, respectively). This strategy was chosen to cover a broad range of possibilities, as the preferred conformational state for naphthoquinone binding is currently unknown.

According to the Vina scoring function employed by CB-Dock,36 the highest-affinity binding pose of juglone was consistently observed in the ATP-binding pocket of the E1 conformation (Fig. 2). The same site was also ranked highest, albeit with a slightly lower score, for the E2 conformation and ranked second for the inhibitor-bound E2 state, yielding a strong consensus across conformations. No other SERCA domain showed a comparable consensus when considering the five top-ranked poses for all three conformations. Together, these results suggest that juglone, and by extension structurally related naphthoquinones, likely inhibit SERCA by occupying the ATP-binding pocket. This observation is consistent with, but does not demonstrate, a competitive inhibition mechanism. Superposition of docked juglone with SERCA complexed with an ATP analog (Fig. 3A) revealed substantial overlap between the aromatic ring systems of both ligands, further supporting competition for the same region of the binding pocket.

MD simulations of the juglone/SERCA (E1) complex identified ligand-protein interactions relevant for binding. It should be noted that these simulations were not intended to yield quantitative binding Free Energies but to assess the stability and thus plausibility of docking-predicted binding poses. The system equilibrated quickly (Fig. 3B) and during the production phase of the simulations, both carbonyl groups of juglone formed hydrogen bonds with Gly516 and Arg560, while the first aromatic ring engaged in π-π interactions with Phe487 (Fig. 3A and C). Overall, a good complementarity between the polarity profile of juglone and that of the ATP-binding pocket was observed (Fig. 3D). A juglone-specific interaction likely shared with compounds 8, 9, and 13 - all of which are active inhibitors - was a hydrogen bond between the oxygen atom at position 5 and Arg560, providing a plausible explanation for the importance of a hydroxyl group at this position. It is plausible that one of the oxygen atoms of the nitro group in compound 12 can engage in a similar interaction, accounting for its high potency.

The entrance to the ATP-binding pocket is relatively narrow and includes Arg489, whose positive charge may facilitate favorable interactions with electronegative oxygen and chlorine substituents present in active compounds such as 2, 5, 10, 12, and 13 (Fig. 3D). In contrast, the reduced potency of the brominated compound 6 is likely attributable to steric clashes arising from the larger size of the bromine atom at the pocket entrance. In addition, the inactivity of compound 11 may result from electrostatic incompatibility, as the protonated amino group at position 3 confers a net positive charge at physiological pH. While definitive assignment of a competitive mechanism will require ATP-dependence kinetic studies, the convergence of modeling and SAR trends arguably favors an ATP-competitive mode of inhibition over irreversible covalent mechanisms. Finally, the modeling results do not indicate an opportunity for the formation of covalent bonds as there were no Cys residues observed in close proximity to the ligand, a requirement for an irreversible inhibition mechanism.

Conclusions

We have determined the inhibitory potencies of a series of commercially available naphthoquinones against the ATPase activity of SERCA. The observed SARs and molecular modeling results are most consistent with a competitive inhibition mechanism in which naphthoquinones bind at or near the ATP-binding site of SERCA. Modeling studies identified key intermolecular interactions involving both keto groups, one of the aromatic rings, and substituents at position 5 on the second ring with residues in the ATP-binding pocket of SERCA. Whereas the convergence of SAR trends and molecular modeling is compatible with a competitive inhibition mechanism, additional kinetic experiments will be required to experimentally confirm the nature of this inhibition. Our findings provide a framework for assessing the potential biological impact of individual naphthoquinones and may also assist in the rational design of novel SERCA inhibitors based on the naphthoquinone scaffold.

Experimental

Test compounds

Compounds 1–4, 7, and 10 (Table 2) were purchased from Fisher Scientific, compounds 5, 11–13 from TCI America, compounds 8 and 9 from Sigma Aldrich, and compound 6 from Avantor. All compounds were used without further purification and were of 98% or higher purity, except for compound 9, which was of technical grade with an unspecified purity level, as supplied by the manufacturer.

SERCA activity assays

SERCA1a microsomes were prepared from rabbit hind-leg skeletal muscle (kindly provided by the Chen-Izu group, University of California, Davis) following a previously described protocol and stored at −80 °C until use.33,37 The auxiliary enzymes, pyruvate kinase (PK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). All other chemicals required for the assay were purchased from Fisher Scientific or Avantor.

For measurement of SERCA-catalyzed ATP hydrolysis, 225 μL samples were prepared in 96-well plates, containing approximately 1 μg/mL total protein in assay buffer composed of 5 mM MgCl2, 100 mM KCl, 7 μM CaCl2, 2 μM EGTA, 4.5 μM calcimycin, 1.5 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0), 47 U/mL PK, and 57 U/mL LDH. Test compounds were added from 10 mM stock solutions in DMSO and serially diluted in water to yield 10 concentrations spaced evenly of a logarithmic scale from 560 nM to 110 μM. Reactions were initiated by adding ATP to a final concentration of 0.22 mM. SERCA-catalyzed ATP hydrolysis, coupled to NADH oxidation via PK and LDH, was monitored for approximately 10 min using a Biotek Synergy HTX microplate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) operating at 340 nm, recording between 10 and 15 data points per assay. Reaction rates were determined by linear regression of absorbance-versus-time traces. Dose-response data were fit to a three-parameter logistic equation using SigmaPlot (version 14.5, Systat Software) to determine potencies in the form of IC50-values, the concentration at which the maximum rate was reduced by half.38 Reported values of these exploratory trials represent the average and standard deviation of at least three independent repeats. Control experiments with vehicle (DMSO) indicated negligible solvent effects over the tested concentration range.

Molecular modeling, docking, and MD simulations

The three-dimensional structure of the potent inhibitor juglone was modeled using the molecular modeling suite MOE (Molecular Operating Environment, version 2024.06; Chemical Computing Group, Montreal, Canada). The conformational energy of the ligand was minimized by molecular mechanics using the MMFF94x force field.39 High-resolution X-ray crystal structures of SERCA in the E1 (PDB ID: 1VFP), E2 (3B9B), and an inhibitor-bound E2-like conformation (2AGV) were obtained from the Protein Data Bank and processed using MOE's Structure Preparation tool to remove non-protein components, add hydrogen atoms, and optimize atom positions by molecular mechanics using the AMBER force field.31,40,41

To identify putative binding sites for juglone, the three SERCA models were submitted to the online server CB-Dock, which employs surface screening to identify concave pockets prior to molecular docking using the AutoDock Vina scoring function to rank prospective binding sites and poses.34,35 Docking poses were evaluated based on their Vina scores and their consistency with experimentally observed SAR relationships.

The highest-ranked juglone/SERCA pose was used as the starting point for MD simulations performed in MOE. Simulations employed an implicit solvent model at 300 K, with a time step of 2 fs and a sampling interval of 10 ps. Systems were gradually heated to the target temperature over 500 ps, followed by a 10 ns production phase. Positional restraints implemented via MOE's QuickPrep tool were applied to limit large deviations of atoms within the binding pocket, while receptor atoms located more than 5 Å from the ligand were held fixed to prevent unrealistic movements and reduce computational cost. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) trajectories were subsequently calculated using VMD (Visual Molecular Dynamics, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign).42,43
