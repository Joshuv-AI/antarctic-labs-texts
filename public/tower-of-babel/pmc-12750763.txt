Mobile endoscopy in rural South Africa: outcomes of a decentralised outreach programme to improve gastrointestinal healthcare access
Authors: Michael Deal, Joseph Karam, Shannon Rychener, Dania Hailat, Aleeza Masood, Sydney Bertram, Mike Mallah, Hugo Stark
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy is a valuable diagnostic tool for identifying upper and lower GI pathologies yet is rarely available in resource-limited settings. This project evaluates the impact of a mobile endoscopy outreach effort implemented across five district hospitals in the Western Cape of South Africa.MethodsA mobile endoscopy outreach programme was created and implemented at five district hospitals within the Western Cape of South Africa. Health records of patients who presented for endoscopy across these five hospitals were then retrospectively reviewed. Descriptive statistics were used as part of data analysis.ResultsA total of 515 procedures (486 oesophagogastroduodenoscopies (94%), 28 colonoscopies (5%) and one proctoscopy (0.2%)) were performed at five district hospitals between January and November of 2024 (323 female (63%), 192 male (37%); mean (SD) age 56 (14) years). The most common pathologies identified across all sites included gastritis (76%), hiatal hernia (70%) and oesophagitis (69%). For all patients, the average distance travelled to their nearest district hospital (Beaufort West, Oudtshoorn, Riversdale, Mossel Bay or Knysna) was 13.6 km (SD (26.6)), compared with the average distance they would have needed to travel to the regional referral centre (mean (SD) 102.1 (66.8) km) in George, South Africa, for the same endoscopy services.ConclusionImplementing a mobile, cost-conscious endoscopy outreach programme can offer diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities to patients in international resource-limited settings, while also alleviating resource strain on regional referral centres. This approach can improve healthcare access and can be replicated in other resource-limited settings.Keywords: EPIDEMIOLOGY, ENDOSCOPY, GASTRIC AND DUODENAL ULCERS, OESOPHAGEAL DISEASE

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy is an important diagnostic tool for identifying and managing GI diseases, such as oesophagitis, gastritis and gastric cancer. However, in resource-limited settings such as rural South Africa, access to endoscopy services is limited, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment.

As of December 2019, South Africa had a specialist surgical workforce density of 10.5 per 100 000 people, which is well below the target of 20 per 100 000 set by the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery.1 In contrast to South Africa, other upper-middle-income countries like Peru (41.77 per 100 000), Mauritius (34.57 per 100 000) and Colombia (20.33 per 100 000) have significantly higher densities of specialists.2 Moreover, the density of gastroenterologists in South Africa is very low, with only 0.1 gastroenterologists per 100 000 people highlighting the need for increased availability of trained endoscopists.1

In addition to the disparity of trained endoscopists, the average wait time for endoscopy across South Africa is 9.25 weeks.3 South Africa has one of the highest global rates of Helicobacter pylori infection with an estimated 77.6% infection rate, compared with an estimated 30%–50% infection rate in high-income countries.4 Given that H. pylori is a causative agent in gastritis, peptic ulcer disease and gastric cancer, interventions and programming that improve detection, timeliness of treatment and decrease wait times are of utmost importance.5

To address similar gaps in healthcare access, previous studies have highlighted the feasibility and utility of creating a mobile endoscopy outreach programme. In rural India, for example, a mobile endoscopy unit screened over 700 individuals, referred patients to secondary and tertiary care facilities as needed, provided lifestyle counselling and identified major barriers to healthcare access, such as transportation.6 Prior to this effort, a similar programme in India had been created that performed over 32 000 endoscopic procedures, highlighting the scalability of such programmes.7 Creating similar programmes in other low- and middle-income countries could be of similar benefit.

The lack of access to timely GI endoscopy services in South Africa represents a major gap in healthcare delivery. With a high burden of GI diseases, particularly in rural areas, addressing this gap is crucial to improving health outcomes for the rural South African population. Therefore, we created a sustainable and reproducible mobile endoscopy outreach programme within the Western Cape of South Africa.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was completed as part of a global surgery partnership between the Medical University of South Carolina in Charleston, South Carolina and the George Regional Hospital (GRH) in the Western Cape of South Africa. It was reviewed by the Medical University of South Carolina Institutional Review Board (study ID: Pro00144755) and determined to be exempt from further review. This report follows the SQUIRE 2.0 reporting standards for quality improvement studies8 9 (online supplemental file 1). The SQUIRE reporting checklist was used when editing this manuscript.8 9 All patients included in this study provided informed consent to endoscopy and the use of their protected health information in research efforts.

Mobile endoscopy programme and endoscopy technique

Members of the clinical team would travel by car with endoscopy equipment to the participating district hospitals. These hospitals were located in Beaufort West, Oudtshoorn, Riversdale, Mossel Bay and Knysna, all of which are in the Western Cape of South Africa. Each district site would be visited as part of the outreach effort based on the number of new referrals for endoscopy. Typically, this would result in two to four visits to each site per month.

For upper endoscopy, the equipment that was brought to each hospital included a monitor (STORZ TELE PACK X GI TP200) and compatible flexible endoscope. Patients were seated in the upright position and were consented for the procedure. A topical anaesthetic (Xylocaine Pump Spray 10%) was used to obtain oropharyngeal anaesthesia. No sedative agents were administered during upper endoscopy. Standard upper endoscopy was then performed, including direct visualisation of the pharynx, proximal to distal oesophagus, stomach (including views with retroflexion) and the first two portions of the duodenum. Biopsies were performed if needed. Following completion of the endoscopy, the scope was withdrawn and meticulously sterilised by trained hospital staff members. While the scope was sterilised, the endoscopist discussed the results of the endoscopy along with any planned interventions with the patient.

As part of the mobile outreach effort, colonoscopies were also completed. These were similarly completed with a compatible flexible colonoscope in the standard fashion, including a careful inspection of colonic mucosa, visualisation of the ileocaecal valve and appendiceal orifice, caecal intubation and a minimum withdrawal time of 6 min.10 Low doses of propofol were administered throughout the procedure for sedation, and vitals were monitored continuously. Following the procedure, patients were closely monitored in the recovery area prior to discharge home.

One proctoscopy was also performed. With the patient in left lateral decubitus position, a digital rectal exam was performed, and findings were noted. Then, a lubricated proctoscope was gently inserted and advanced under direct vision with inspection of the mucosa during withdrawal.

The travelling clinical team included a board-certified general surgeon and general surgery trainee. All procedures were performed by the general surgeon or by the trainee under supervision.

Map generation

A map of the Western Cape, South Africa, was generated to visualise the geographic distribution of the mobile endoscopy outreach locations. A blank municipal boundary map of the Western Cape was obtained from Wikimedia Commons (Map of the Western Cape with Municipalities Blank) and downloaded in SVG format for further modification in Adobe Illustrator. Locations of hospitals visited by the mobile endoscopy outreach programme were collected and geocoded using QGIS.

Travel distance calculations

To assess the travel distance savings provided by the mobile endoscopy programme, we used QGIS (V.3.38.2) with OpenStreetMap road network data to calculate the shortest distances between patient locations, the five hospitals included in the outreach programme and the regional referral centre, GRH). For patient-specific distance calculations, patients’ listed addresses were used to measure two distances: (1) a standard route, representing the shortest distance to GRH, and (2) a mobile endoscopy route, representing the shortest distance to hospitals included in the outreach effort.

Data collection and analysis

Patients who underwent endoscopy as part of the outreach effort between January 2024 and November 2024 at participating hospitals were included in a retrospective chart review. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (V.10.4.1). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient demographics, chief complaints and endoscopic findings. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means±SD or medians with ranges, as appropriate.

Results

Patient demographics and exclusion criteria

A total of 518 procedures were performed between January and November of 2024 at five district hospitals as part of the mobile endoscopy outreach programme. Three procedures were excluded from analysis due to patient age (less than 18 years). 486 oesophagogastroduodenoscopies (OGDs) (94%), 28 colonoscopies (5%) and one proctoscopy (0.2%) were performed during the study period (figure 1). The study population included 323 females (63%) and 192 males (37%) (mean (SD) age 56 (14) years).

The 515 adult procedures represent procedures performed on 495 unique patients. Of the 495 patients included in our analysis, 465 underwent OGD. During the study period, 15 of the 495 underwent follow-up OGD, and two underwent follow-up colonoscopy. Of the 15 patients who underwent a second OGD, two had a third OGD, and one underwent a second follow-up colonoscopy. 22 patients underwent colonoscopy at their initial visit, four underwent both colonoscopy and OGD and one underwent proctoscopy (online supplemental figure 1).

Epidemiological data

Among patients undergoing OGD, the most common chief complaints were epigastric pain (20.3%), heartburn (17.9%), dysphagia (7.2%), haematemesis (6.0%) and iron deficiency anaemia (5.8%) (figure 3a). The most frequently diagnosed pathologies included gastritis (76.1%), hiatal hernia (70.2%), unspecified oesophagitis (69.1%), duodenitis (19.1%) and acute gastric ulcers (7.0%) (figure 3b). Less common, but clinically significant findings included 34 patients (6.9%) with gastric ulcers, 15 patients (3.1%) with duodenal ulcers and 17 patients (3.5%) with malignancies. Among those diagnosed with malignancies, 12 patients (2.5%) had oesophageal cancer, and five patients (1.0%) had gastric cancer. Seven of these patients were referred to GRH for further management, such as stenting or surgery.

(c) Depiction of observed combinations of clinical findings with the classic GERD phenotype (gastritis, oesophagitis and hiatal hernia) being the most common. OGD, oesophagogastroduodenoscopy; GERD, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.

Patients often experienced a combination of findings. The most common combination was that of gastritis, oesophagitis and hiatal hernia, which is the classic phenotype for long-term untreated gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Pan-GI inflammation (combination of gastritis, oesophagitis and duodenitis) and another GERD phenotype (oesophagitis+hiatal hernia) were the next most common combination of findings (figure 3c). Of note, 18 patients had no pathologic findings on OGD despite their chief complaints and referral for endoscopy.

Regarding management, proton pump inhibitors were prescribed to 87.2% of patients, and 13.3% were scheduled for follow-up at a local site. Additionally, patient counselling was documented for smoking cessation (18.7%), weight loss (10.3%) and other lifestyle modifications (3.5%).

Among patients undergoing colonoscopy, the most common chief complaints were referral for screening (39.3%), rectal or anal bleeding (28.5%), generalised abdominal pain (3.6%) and unintentional weight loss (3.6%). The most frequently identified pathologies included anal haemorrhoids (21.4%), colonic diverticulosis (14.3%) and ulcerative colitis localised to the rectum (10.7%). Following colonoscopy, 25% of patients were referred for local follow-up, and 14.3% were scheduled for a repeat colonoscopy. Additionally, 14.3% of patients received counselling on weight loss, while 10.7% were counselled on smoking cessation.

Epidemiological data for all patients and by outreach hospital location can be found in online supplemental appendix 2.

Geographical reach of mobile endoscopy outreach programme

GRH is the regional referral centre located in George, South Africa, a rural community of the Western Cape. The mobile endoscopy outreach programme, based at GRH, aims to reduce patient travel distances by offering services at five locations: Beaufort West, Riversdale, Oudtshoorn, Knysna and Mossel Bay (figure 4a). For example, Beaufort West Hospital, which is the furthest location travelled to, is located 240 km from George, saving patients (n=76) in surrounding areas an average of 221 km of travel, each, to receive endoscopy services (figure 4b).

The red marker is the location of George Hospital; green markers signify locations where mobile endoscopy took place. (b) Mean reduction in travel distance for patients seen at five outreach locations as compared with travelling to GRH. Beaufort West, the location furthest from George, saw the greatest reduction in patient travel distance reflecting the impact in the most geographical rural location. (c) Travel reduction for patients who did not live in towns visited by the mobile endoscopy programme. GRH, George Regional Hospital.

Of the 515 Patients included in our study, 270 were local to the towns with hospitals visited as part of the mobile endoscopy outreach programme. We were interested in evaluating the geographical reach of the outreach effort to rural regions that were outside the towns it travelled to. As expected, we found that patients receiving care at the most rural location—Beaufort West Hospital—who did not live in Beaufort West had the greatest reduction in their mean travel distance to their endoscopy appointments compared with the distance that they would have needed to travel to reach GRH (figure 4c). 18 patients accounted for 39 of the endoscopies performed over the study period. This subset of patients collectively avoided travelling 3266 km, reducing their travel distances to follow-up appointments from an average of 95.25 km to 11.5 km. The travel burden for patients was greatest at the two locations furthest from GRH, Beaufort West and Riversdale.

Discussion

Here, we highlight the meaningful impact of a mobile endoscopy outreach programme in improving access to GI diagnostic services in the Western Cape of South Africa. The study demonstrates that cost-conscious mobile endoscopy outreach efforts can effectively help bridge the gap in healthcare accessibility by reducing travel distances, alleviating patient burden and expediting diagnosis and treatment.

Additionally, our data confirm that without the mobile outreach programme, patients had to travel an average of 221 km to access endoscopy services at GRH, significantly delaying diagnosis and treatment. In a large-scale study of all paediatric admissions in the Western Cape of South Africa, GRH received patients from a mean distance of 30.3 km (13 452 patients over a 5-year period). Furthermore, among the rural hospitals in the Western Cape, GRH had the largest number of admissions, reflecting the breadth of services offered.11

This outreach effort identified a high prevalence of GI pathologies, including gastritis (76.1%), hiatal hernia (70.2%) and oesophagitis (69.1%). Importantly, malignancies were detected in 3.5% of patients, underscoring the programme’s role in early cancer detection. Given South Africa’s high prevalence of H. pylori infection and gastric cancer ranking among the leading causes of death in the Western Cape, timely screening and intervention are critical. The mobile outreach model aligns with similar programmes in resource-limited settings, such as India, where mobile endoscopy improved early detection of GI conditions, facilitated risk factor counselling and demonstrated scalability.6 7

In a retrospective endoscopic-histologic analysis at the Nelson Mandela Academic Hospital in the Eastern Cape province, H. pylori infection was present in 54.5% of patients and 95% of the 156 patients included were diagnosed with chronic gastritis. There was also a 16% incidence of PUD and 6.4% rate of gastric malignancies in that tertiary-care cohort.12 Rates observed in our study were comparable, with a 13.9% incidence of peptic ulcer disease when acute and chronic gastric ulcers were combined. By contrast, only 3.5% of our patients were diagnosed with malignancy—likely reflecting earlier detection through decentralised outreach.

Patients undergoing multiple endoscopies through this outreach effort collectively avoided travelling over 3200 km, illustrating its role in mitigating the travel burden. This is particularly relevant for patients at the most remote locations, such as Beaufort West, where alternative access to care would have necessitated long-distance travel. By reducing geographic barriers, the programme enhances continuity of care, which can facilitate timely follow-up evaluations, earlier interventions and improve patient outcomes.

The success of this mobile endoscopy outreach programme supports its potential expansion within South Africa and other low- and middle-income countries facing similar healthcare disparities but was not without limitations. First, patient discomfort during unsedated upper endoscopies can limit procedural time and in turn reduce the ability to detect small or subtle abnormal lesions as well as reduce the ability to perform therapeutic interventions. Second, we recognise that the study was only performed in one context and therefore may limit generalisability. Additionally, patient outcomes—such as resolution of symptoms, completion of referrals and treatment of malignancy—were not tracked in this retrospective review and could be the subject of future study. Future directions should also include a cost-effectiveness analysis of such programmes prior to implementation in other low- and middle-income countries.

Conclusion

This mobile endoscopy outreach programme at GRH demonstrates a viable strategy for addressing disparities in GI healthcare access in rural South Africa. By significantly reducing patient travel distances, expediting diagnoses and facilitating early intervention, this model could be considered for broader implementation in similar resource-constrained settings. Scaling up mobile endoscopy services could help mitigate the burden of GI diseases and improve overall health outcomes in underserved communities.
