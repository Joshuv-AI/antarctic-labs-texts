Rational surfaces on low degree hypersurfaces
Tim Browning, Shuntaro Yamagishi

Abstract
. We use function field analytic number theory to establish the irreducibility and dimension of the moduli space that parameterises morphisms ℙ 2 → X \mathbb{P}^{2}\to X of fixed degree, for an arbitrary smooth hypersurface X X of sufficiently small degree.

14H10 (11P55, 14G05, 14J26, 14J70)

1.
Introduction

The study of rational curves on hypersurfaces has led to major advances in algebraic geometry, with deep connections to arithmetic geometry, enumerative geometry and moduli theory. In contrast, comparatively little is known about
rational surfaces
contained in hypersurfaces. In this paper we investigate the moduli space of degree
e
morphisms
\mathbb{P}^{2}\to X
, where
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is a smooth hypersurface of degree
d\geqslant 2
defined over an algebraically closed field
K
whose characteristic exceeds
d
if it is positive. Our main result establishes that this moduli space is irreducible and has the expected dimension when
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
e
, extending techniques originally developed for the study of rational curves.

The geometry of the moduli space
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
of degree
e
rational curves on
X

has been extensively studied.
The irreducibility and dimension of
\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
have been established by Kim and Pandharipande
[
15
, Cor. 1]
when
d=2
, and by Coskun and Starr
[
9
]
when
d=3
. For general hypersurfaces
X
of degree
d
, the strongest result is due to Riedl and Yang
[
19
]
, who use a version of Bend-and-Break to prove that

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is irreducible and has the expected dimension
\mu_{1}(e)=e(n-d)+n-5
, provided that
n\geqslant d+3
.
Recent work of Bilu and Browning
[
4
, Cor. 1.4]

achieves the same conclusion for any smooth hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d
, provided that

n>(d-1)2^{d}
, improving on work of Browning and Sawin
[
6
, Thm. 1.1]
who
required
n>(2d-1)2^{d-1}
. The latter result is proved using analytic number theory over function fields and, in turn, builds on an approach
employed by Browning and Vishe
[
7
]
. The idea is to study
the moduli space of morphisms

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)
, whose expected dimension is
\mu_{1}(e)+3
, since

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
is obtained from
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)

on taking the quotient by
\mathrm{PGL}_{2}
.
To compute the dimension of
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)
, it suffices to work over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
>d
, estimate the cardinality

\#\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{1},X)(\mathbb{F}_{q})
using analytic number theory, and then compare it with the statement of the Lang–Weil estimate. The application of function field analytic number theory to the geometry
of the moduli spaces of degree
e
morphisms to
X

has been
implemented in a range of contexts, provided that
n
is sufficiently large in terms of
d
and
e
. These applications include:

•

The dimension of the singular locus of

\mathscr{M}_{0,0}(X,e)
(Browning–Sawin
[
6
]
);

•

The geometry of
\mathscr{M}_{0,m}(X,e)
when
X
is a complete intersection cut out by more than one equation (Browning–Vishe–Yamagishi
[
8
]
);

•

The geometry of the moduli space

\mathscr{M}_{g,0}(X,e)

of degree
e
morphisms from a smooth
projective curve of genus
g
to
X
(Hase-Liu
[
13
]
);

•

The singularities of
\mathscr{M}_{g,0}(X,e)
(Glas–Hase-Liu
[
11
]
).

While the geometry of rational curves on hypersurfaces is now well understood in many regimes, the analogous study of rational surfaces is largely undeveloped.
As described by Lang
[
17
]
,
a result of Tsen shows that
X
always contains a rational surface
if
n>d^{2}
.
By contrast,
when
X
is very general and
n>\max\{20,d\}
, for a suitable range of

e
compared to
d
and
n
, it follows from work of
Beheshti and Riedl
[
1
]
that there are no
rational surfaces in
X
that are ruled by low-degree rational curves.

Let
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)

denote the moduli space
of degree
e
morphisms
g:\mathbb{P}^{2}\to X
.
Such a morphism is given by

g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n}),

where
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in K[u,v,w]
are ternary forms of degree
e
,
with no common zero in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
, such that
f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
vanishes identically.
Since the number of monomials of degree
D
in three variables is
\binom{D+2}{2}
,
we may regard
g
as a point in
\mathbb{P}^{n\binom{e+2}{2}-1}
and
the morphisms of degree
e
on
X
are parameterised by

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
, which is an open subvariety of

\mathbb{P}^{n\binom{e+2}{2}-1}
cut out by a system of
\binom{de+2}{2}
equations of degree
d
.
This shows that
either
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
is empty or else it has dimension at least
\mu(e)
,
where

(1.1)

\mu(e)=n\binom{e+2}{2}-\binom{de+2}{2}-1

is the
expected dimension
of
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
.

Although it does not address
the geometry of
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
, the most relevant work in this direction is due to Starr
[
22
]
,
who focuses on the moduli space

F_{2}(e,X)
of degree
e

Veronese surfaces
contained in
X
. These are rational surfaces
embedded in
X
via morphisms induced by complete linear systems of degree
e
homogeneous forms.
(In fact, Starr’s work treats the more general case of degree
e
Veronese
r
-folds, for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, but in this discussion we restrict our attention to the case
r=2
.)
Note that when
e=1
, we recover the classical Fano variety of planes
F_{2}(X)=F_{2}(1,X)
in
X
, which is obtained from
\operatorname{Mor}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
on taking the quotient by
\mathrm{PGL}_{3}
.
It follows from
[
22
, Thm. 1.3]
that
F_{2}(e,X)
has the expected dimension for a sufficiently general hypersurface

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d
, provided that

(1.2)

n\geqslant\frac{(de+2)(de+1)}{(e+2)(e+1)}+(e+2)(e+1).

Similarly, the irreducibility of
F_{2}(e,X)
can be deduced from
[
22
, Cor. 1.5]
and
[
22
, Thm. 1.6(i)]

under the more stringent condition that

(1.3)

n\geqslant n_{e}+\frac{1}{n_{e}+1}\binom{n_{e}+d}{n_{e}},

where
n_{e}=\frac{1}{2}e(e+3)
.
For fixed
d
, Stirling’s formula shows that the right hand side is asymptotic to
e^{2(d-1)}/(\exp(d)2^{d-1}d!)+\mathbf{1}_{d=2}e^{2}/2
, as
e\to\infty
.

More is known about the Fano variety of planes
F_{2}(X)
. According to work of
Hochster and Laksov
[
14
]
,
for a general hypersurface

X
, the variety
F_{2}(X)
is irreducible and has the expected dimension
\mu(1)-8=3n-\binom{d+2}{2}-9
, whenever this quantity is positive. In
fact, it follows from
[
10
, Thm. 6.28]
that
F_{2}(X)
is non-empty if
d\geqslant 3
and
3(n-3)-\binom{d+2}{2}\geqslant 0
.

Remark 1.1
.

We can give simple conditions under which

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
is non-empty, for any hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
of degree
d
.
Suppose first that

d\geqslant 3
and

n\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\binom{d+2}{2}+3
.
Then
F_{2}(X)\neq\emptyset
by
[
10
, Thm. 6.28]
, whence
\operatorname{Mor}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)\neq\emptyset
. But then, on composing any morphism in
\operatorname{Mor}_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
with one from
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathbb{P}^{2})
, we obtain a morphism in
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
.
If
d=2
and
n\geqslant 6

then

F_{2}(X)\neq\emptyset
by
[
12
, Thm. 22.13]
, whence
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
is non-empty if
n\geqslant 6
.

We are now ready to state our main result, which for the first time
establishes the irreducibility and dimension of
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
in suitable regimes,
for arbitrary smooth hypersurfaces
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
.
In the case
e=1
we also record an
easy consequence for the Fano variety of planes.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
e\geqslant 1
be integers and let
K
be an algebraically closed field whose characteristic is
0
or exceeds
d
.
Let

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth hypersurface of degree
d
defined over
K
such that

n>2^{d}(d-1)(de+1).

Then
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)

is irreducible and has dimension
\mu(e)
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and let
K
be an algebraically closed field whose characteristic is
0
or exceeds
d
.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth hypersurface of degree
d
defined over
K
such that
n>2^{d}(d^{2}-1).

Then
F_{2}(X)
is irreducible and has dimension
3n-\binom{d+2}{2}-9
.

It is worth emphasising that the dependence of
n
on
e
is linear
in Theorem
1.2
, compared to the polynomial dependence on
e
that occurs in
Starr’s result for the moduli space of degree
e
Veronese surfaces in a sufficiently general hypersurface
X
,
with the constraints in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).
In Proposition
3.2
we shall prove
that

\dim\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)>\mu(e)
when
K=\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
and
p
is sufficiently small compared to
d
and
e
, so that some lower bound on the characteristic is necessary when it is positive.

Let us now describe the main ideas of the proof. By spreading out,
we may reduce the problem to estimating the quantity

(1.4)

N(e)=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{t}}=(\mathbf{t}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{t}_{e})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]^{n(e+1)}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{t}_{s}|<q^{s+1}\text{ for all $0\leqslant s\leqslant e$}\\
F_{j}(\underline{\mathbf{t}})=0\text{ for all $0\leqslant j\leqslant de$}\end{array}\right\},

where
F_{j}(\underline{\mathbf{t}})
are the degree
d
forms with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
defined in (
3.3
).
For fixed integers
N,R\geqslant 1
,
consider the general problem of
estimating the quantity

\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]^{N}:\begin{array}[]{l}|x_{i}|<q^{e+\delta_{i}}\text{ for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant N$}\\
G_{j}(\mathbf{x})=0\text{ for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant R$}\end{array}\right\},

where
G_{j}(\mathbf{x})
are degree
d
forms with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{N}

are real numbers.
This is exactly the setting of the function field analogue
[
18
]

of a classical result by Birch
[
2
]
. The latter is capable of providing an asymptotic formula for this counting function (as
e\to\infty
) when

|\delta_{1}|,\dots,|\delta_{N}|
are sufficiently small with respect to
e
,
provided that
N-B\geqslant 2^{d-1}(d-1)R(R+1)
, where
B
is the dimension of the
Birch singular locus

(1.5)

\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{N}:\textnormal{rank}(\textnormal{Jac}_{\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{x}))<R\right\}.

In fact, it follows from
[
8
]
that an asymptotic formula is also possible when
N-B
grows only linearly in
R
.
In our setting, we have
N=n(e+1)
and
R=de+1
, both of which depend on
e
.
Two major obstacles obstruct a direct application of
[
18
]
or
[
8
]
. First, the side lengths of the boxes vary wildly in our setting, ranging from
q
to
q^{e+1}
. Second, we have
B\geqslant en

in our setting,
since it turns out that
the variety (
1.5
) contains all vectors

\underline{\mathbf{t}}\in\mathbb{A}^{n(e+1)}
with
\mathbf{t}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the function field analogue of the unit interval. Then,
as described in Section
5
,
the starting point of the circle method
is the identity

N(e)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{de+1}}S(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\alpha},

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{de})
and

S(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is the exponential sum defined in (
5.1
).
For any choice of
j\in\{0,\dots,de\}
we can isolate the portion of the phase function involving
\alpha_{j}
via an expression of the form

|S(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|^{2^{d-1}}=\prod_{j=0}^{de}|S(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|^{\frac{2^{d-1}}{de+1}}\leqslant\prod_{j=0}^{de}|T_{j}(\alpha_{j})|^{\frac{1}{de+1}},

where
T_{j}(\alpha_{j})
is an exponential sum whose phase function depends only on
\alpha_{j}
, and is independent of
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{j-1},

\alpha_{j+1},\dots,\alpha_{de}
.
This part of the argument exploits the specific bihomogeneous structure of the forms
F_{j}
and in the case
e=1
is reminiscent of arguments used by Brandes
[
3
]
to study linear spaces on hypersurfaces.
This decoupling allows us to factor the integral as

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{de+1}}|S(\boldsymbol{\alpha})|\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\leqslant\prod_{j=0}^{de}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|T_{j}(\alpha_{j})|^{\frac{1}{(de+1)2^{d-1}}}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{j},

which reduces the problem to the hypersurface case.

One advantage of this approach is that
the dimension of the Birch singular locus (
1.5
) of the resulting system is essentially optimal, as we shall discuss further in Remark
5.2
.
A second advantage is that it eliminates the issue of the
lopsidedness
of the box.
The latter causes serious technical issues, and even a difference of
q
in side lengths
introduces inefficiencies into the argument that result in excess factors of
q
.
In our approach, Weyl differencing is applied in such a way that the problem is essentially reduced to
studying an exponential sum with the phase function given by a bihomogeneous form
G(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})
, where the summation is over
|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P_{1}}
and
|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P_{2}}
.
This topic has been addressed by Schindler
[
20
]
over number fields, and we present
a function field version that significantly extends her ideas in Section
4
.

We believe that the main ideas of this paper, or a suitable generalisation, have the potential to be useful in studying

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{r},X)
for
r>2
, as well as in obtaining an upper bound of the correct order of magnitude for the number of integral points on suitable complete intersections where the circle method is not directly applicable.

.

.

. Finally, in Section

.

2.
Preliminary facts about function fields

In this section, we collect together some basic notation and facts concerning the function field
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{F}_{q}(u)
, where
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field of characteristic
p
.

Notation

We shall need absolute value

|a/b|=q^{\deg a-\deg b},

for any
a/b\in\mathbb{K}^{*}
. We extend these definitions to
\mathbb{K}
by taking
|0|=0.

Let
\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
be the completion of
\mathbb{K}
with respect to
|\cdot|
.
We can extend the absolute value to get a non-archimedean
absolute value

|\cdot|:\mathbb{K}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}

given by
|\alpha|=q^{\ord\alpha}
, where
\ord\alpha
is the largest
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
a_{i}\neq 0
in the
representation
\alpha=\sum_{i\leqslant M}a_{i}u^{i}
.
In this context we adopt the convention
\ord 0=-\infty
and
|0|=0
.
We extend this to vectors by setting

|\mathbf{x}|=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|,

for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}^{n}
, noting that it satisfies the ultrametric inequality

|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}|\leqslant\max\{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|\},

for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}^{n}
.

We may identify
\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
with the set

\mathbb{F}_{q}((u^{-1}))=\left\{\sum_{i\leqslant M}a_{i}u^{i}:\text{for $a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$ and some $M\in\mathbb{Z}$}\right\}

and put

\mathbb{T}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}:|\alpha|<1\}=\left\{\sum_{i\leqslant-1}a_{i}u^{i}:\text{for $a_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}$}\right\}.

Since
\mathbb{T}
is a locally compact
additive subgroup of
\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
it possesses a unique Haar measure
\mathrm{d}\alpha
, which is normalised so that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{d}\alpha=1.

We can extend
\mathrm{d}\alpha
to a (unique) translation-invariant measure on
\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
in
such a way that

\int_{\{\alpha\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}:|\alpha|<q^{M}\}}\mathrm{d}\alpha=q^{M},

for any
M\in\mathbb{Z}
.
These measures also extend to
\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
\mathbb{K}_{\infty}^{n}
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
we define
\|\alpha\|=|\{\alpha\}|
, where
\{\alpha\}\in\mathbb{T}
is the
fractional part
of
\alpha
.
Given any finite set
S
we shall interchangeably write
\#S
or
|S|
to denote its cardinality.

Characters

There is a non-trivial additive character
e_{q}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
defined
for each
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
by taking

e_{q}(a)=\exp(2\pi i\tr(a)/p)
, where
\tr:\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p}
denotes the
trace map.
This character induces a non-trivial (unitary) additive character
\psi:\mathbb{K}_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
by defining
\psi(\alpha)=e_{q}(a_{-1})
for any

\alpha=\sum_{i\leqslant M}a_{i}u^{i}
in
\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
.
We have the basic orthogonality property

(2.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in\mathscr{O}\\
|b|<q^{N}\end{subarray}}\psi(\gamma b)=\begin{cases}q^{N}&\text{if $|\gamma|<q^{-N}$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{T}
and any integer
N\geqslant 0
, as proved in
[
16
, Lemma 7]
.
We also have

(2.2)

\int_{\{\alpha\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}:|\alpha|<q^{M}\}}\psi(\alpha\gamma)\mathrm{d}\alpha=\begin{cases}q^{M}&\text{ if $|\gamma|<q^{-M}$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}
and
M\in\mathbb{Z}
, as proved in
[
16
, Lemma 1(f)]
.

Counting in function fields

Given a variety
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}(u)}
, we define

\delta(X)=\deg X_{1}+\cdots+\deg X_{s},

where
X=\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant s}X_{i}
is the decomposition of
X
into its geometrically irreducible components. Note that
\delta(X)=\deg X
if
X
is equidimensional and the Bézout inequality
\delta(X\cap Y)\leqslant\delta(X)\delta(Y)
always holds. We write

X(J)=X\cap\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]^{n}:|\mathbf{x}|<q^{J}\},

for any
J\in\mathbb{N}
.
The following result extends to
\mathbb{F}_{q}(u)
a familiar finite field counting result that was recorded in
[
8
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be a variety defined over
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}(u)}
. Then

\#X(J)\leqslant\delta(X)q^{J\dim X},

for any
J\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
r=\dim X
. When
r=0
then
X
consists of at most
\deg X=\delta(X)
points and the lemma is trivial.
Assume that
r\geqslant 1
and let

Z
be an irreducible component of
X
, with
\dim Z\geqslant 1
.
We shall show that there is an index
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
such that
the intersection of
Z
with the hyperplane
H_{y}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}:x_{i}=y\}
satisfies

\dim(Z\cap H_{y})<\dim Z,

for any
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
such that
|y|<q^{J}
. Suppose that the opposite is true, so that there exist
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
such that

|y_{1}|,\dots,|y_{n}|<q^{J}
and

Z\subset\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}:x_{j}=y_{j}\},

for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
.
However, this means that
Z=\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\dim Z\geqslant 1
.
Since
Z\cap H_{y}
has dimension at most
\dim Z-1
and
\delta(Z\cap H_{y})\leqslant\deg Z
, the induction hypothesis yields

\#Z(J)\leqslant\sum_{|y|<q^{J}}\#(Z\cap H_{y})(J)\leqslant\sum_{|y|<q^{J}}(\deg Z)q^{J(\dim Z-1)}\leqslant(\deg Z)q^{J\dim Z}.

Therefore, if we denote by
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{s}
the irreducible components of
X
, we obtain

\#X(J)\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{s}\#Z_{i}(J)\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{s}(\deg Z_{i})q^{J\dim Z_{i}}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{s}(\deg Z_{i})q^{Jr}.

The result follows since
\delta(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\deg Z_{i}
.
∎

Weyl differencing

In this section, we recollect results from
[
21
, Sect. 11]
in the
\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
-setting. Since these results
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
can be deduced in essentially the same manner, provided
p>d
, we omit the details.
Given a box
\mathscr{Z}\subset\mathbb{K}_{\infty}^{N}
, Schmidt introduces the
sets
\mathscr{Z}^{D}=\mathscr{Z}-\mathscr{Z}=\{\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}^{\prime}:\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Z}\}
and

\mathscr{Z}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t})=\bigcap_{\epsilon_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\bigcap_{\epsilon_{t}\in\{0,1\}}(\mathscr{Z}-\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{z}_{1}-\cdots-\epsilon_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}),

for any given
\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}^{N}
.
In our function field setting, the ultrametric inequality implies that in fact

\mathscr{Z}^{D}=\mathscr{Z}
and

\mathscr{Z}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t})=\mathscr{Z}
, provided that

\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathscr{Z}
.
We may now record the following lemma, which is the
\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
-analogue of
[
21
, Lemma 11.1]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathscr{P}\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}[z_{1},\dots,z_{N}]
be a non-constant polynomial,
let
\mathscr{Z}\subset\mathbb{K}_{\infty}^{N}
be a box and let

S=\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathscr{Z}}\psi(\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{z})).

For any
t\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

|S|^{2^{t-1}}\leqslant|\mathscr{Z}|^{2^{t-1}-t}\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{1}\in\mathscr{Z}}\dots\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{t-1}\in\mathscr{Z}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{t}\in\mathscr{Z}}\psi(\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t}))\right|,

where

\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t})=\sum_{\epsilon_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{\epsilon_{t}\in\{0,1\}}\,(-1)^{\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t}}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{z}_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}).

It follows from
[
21
, Lemma 11.2(a)]
that if
t>\deg\mathscr{P}
, then

(2.3)

\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t})\equiv 0;

i.e. it is the zero polynomial. Furthermore, it follows from
[
21
, Lemma 11.4(A)]
that if
t=\deg\mathscr{P}
,
then
\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t})
is the unique symmetric multilinear form associated to
\mathscr{P}^{[t]}(\mathbf{z})
, the degree
t
component of
\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{z})
; i.e.
\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{t})
satisfies

(2.4)

\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{z},\ldots,\mathbf{z})=(-1)^{t}t!\mathscr{P}^{[t]}(\mathbf{z}).

3.
Reduction to a counting problem

We revisit the argument of
[
7
]
to establish the irreducibility
and dimension of the moduli space
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
.
For
d\geqslant 2
and
e\geqslant 1
, let

K
be a field whose characteristic is
0
or exceeds
d
.
Let

X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a smooth hypersurface of degree
d
defined
by a form

f\in K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.
The argument in Section
1
shows that

\dim\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)\geqslant\mu(e),

where
\mu(e)
is given by
(
1.1
).
Thus it suffices to show that

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)

is irreducible with
\dim\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)\leqslant\mu(e)
.
Spreading out to a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, with characteristic
p>d
, it suffices to do this
when everything is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We henceforth fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of
characteristic
p>d
and we suppose that
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a non-singular degree
d
form defined over it.
Let us write

(3.1)

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{d}},

with coefficients
c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
which are symmetric in the indices.
We also denote

(3.2)

\Gamma_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}x_{1,i_{1}}\dots x_{d,i_{d}}.

The cone over
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
is
the space of tuples of (not necessarily homogeneous)
polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u,v]

of degree at most
e
, at least one of degree exactly
e
, with no common zero
shared by
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, nor by the homogeneous degree
e
parts
of
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, and
such that
f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=0
.
Any degree
e
polynomial
g_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u,v]
takes the shape

g_{i}(u,v)=g_{i,0}v^{e}+g_{i,1}v^{e-1}+\cdots+g_{i,e},

where
g_{i,s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
with
\deg g_{i,s}\leqslant s
, for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant e
.
Then

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{g})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}g_{i_{1}}(u,v)\dots g_{i_{d}}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\left(\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{e}g_{i_{1},s_{1}}v^{e-s_{1}}\right)\dots\left(\sum_{s_{d}=0}^{e}g_{i_{d},s_{d}}v^{e-s_{d}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{de}v^{de-j}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant s_{1},\dots,s_{d}\leqslant e\\
s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}=j\end{subarray}}g_{i_{1},s_{1}}\dots g_{i_{d},s_{d}}.

But then it follows that

f(\mathbf{g})=\sum_{j=0}^{de}v^{de-j}F_{j}(\underline{\mathbf{g}}),

where

(3.3)

F_{j}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant s_{1},\dots,s_{d}\leqslant e\\
s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}=j\end{subarray}}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}=1}^{n}c_{i_{1},\dots,i_{d}}g_{i_{1},s_{1}}\dots g_{i_{d},s_{d}},

for
0\leqslant j\leqslant de
, and where

\underline{\mathbf{g}}=(\mathbf{g}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{g}_{e})

with
\mathbf{g}_{s}=(g_{1,s},\dots,g_{n,s})
for all
0\leqslant s\leqslant e
.
Note that when
e=1
, the same forms appear in work of Brandes
[
3
]
over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Let
M(q,e)
be
the space of tuples of (not necessarily homogeneous)
polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u,v]

of degree at most
e
, at least one of the degrees exactly
e
, with no common zero
shared by
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, nor by the homogeneous degree
e
parts
of
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, and
such that
f(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=0
.
Then we have

\#\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\frac{\#M(q,e)}{q-1}

and
the expected dimension of
M(q,e)
is
\widehat{\mu}(e)
, where

(3.4)

\widehat{\mu}(e)=n\binom{e+2}{2}-\binom{de+2}{2}.

Appealing to the Lang–Weil estimate, as in the proof of

[
7
, Eq. (3.3)]
,
in order to deduce that

\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)

is irreducible and has the expected dimension it will be enough to show that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}q^{-\ell\widehat{\mu}(e)}\#M(q^{\ell},e)\leqslant 1.

To this end, on dropping some of the conditions in the definition of

M(q,e)
, it will suffice to
establish the existence of

\delta>0
such that

(3.5)

N(e)\leqslant q^{\widehat{\mu}(e)}+O(q^{\widehat{\mu}(e)-\delta}),

where
the implied constant is independent of
q
and

\displaystyle N(e)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u,v]^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}\deg g_{1},\dots,\deg g_{n}\leqslant e\\
f(\mathbf{g})=0\\
\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\#\left\{\underline{\mathbf{g}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]^{n(e+1)}:\begin{array}[]{l}|\mathbf{g}_{s}|<q^{s+1}\text{ for all $0\leqslant s\leqslant e$}\\
F_{j}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})=0\text{ for all $0\leqslant j\leqslant de$}\end{array}\right\},

which is exactly the quantity that was defined in (
1.4
).
We shall prove the following result in Section
5
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 2
and
e\geqslant 1
be integers.
Let
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a non-singular degree
d
form defined over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p>d
. Suppose that
n>2^{d}(d-1)(de+1)
.
Then there exists
\delta>0
such that (
3.5
) holds,
where the implied constant depends on
d,n
and
e
, but is independent of
q
.

We conclude this section by showing that the statement of Theorem
1.2
does not hold when
K
is a field of small characteristic.
The following result draws inspiration from
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
K=\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
for a prime
p
and let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be the Fermat hypersurface

x_{1}^{d}+\dots+x_{n}^{d}=0.

Assume that
e\in\mathbb{N}
and
d=(e+1)!p+1
. Then
X
is smooth and

\dim\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)>\mu(e).

Proof.

Firstly, the Fermat hypersurface
is smooth over
K
if and only if
p\nmid d
. For the second part, we take
f(\mathbf{x})=x_{1}^{d}+\dots+x_{n}^{d}
and note that
(
3.3
) becomes

F_{j}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant s_{1},\dots,s_{d}\leqslant e\\
s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}=j\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i,s_{1}}\dots g_{i,s_{d}},

for
0\leqslant j\leqslant de
, where we recall that

g_{i,s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]
satisfies
\deg g_{i,s}\leqslant s
, for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
and
0\leqslant s\leqslant e
.
Our assumption on
d
implies that
d>e+1
.
For
j=e+1
we obtain

\displaystyle F_{e+1}(\underline{\mathbf{g}})=\penalty\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant s_{1},\dots,s_{d}\leqslant e\\
s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}=e+1\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i,s_{1}}\dots g_{i,s_{d}}

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\sum_{er_{e}+\cdots+2r_{2}+r_{1}=e+1}\binom{d}{r_{e}}\binom{d-r_{e}}{r_{e-1}}\dots\binom{d-(r_{e}+\dots+r_{2})}{r_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i,e}^{r_{e}}\dots g_{i,1}^{r_{1}}g_{i,0}^{d-(e+1)}.

Suppose
r_{e}=\cdots=r_{2}=0

in the summation. Then
r_{1}=e+1
and the summand becomes

\binom{d}{e+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i,1}^{e+1}g_{i,0}^{d-(e+1)}.

Since
e+1\geqslant 2
, we see that if
d=(e+1)!p+1
then the binomial coefficient
\binom{d}{e+1}
is divisible by
p
.
On the other hand, if
r_{e}+\dots+r_{2}\geqslant 1
, then one of the binomial coefficients

\binom{d-(r_{e}+\dots+r_{j})}{r_{j-1}}=\frac{(d-(r_{e}+\dots+r_{j}))\cdots(d-(r_{e}+\dots+r_{j})-r_{j-1}+1)}{r_{j-1}!}

must contain
d-1
in the numerator,
for some
j\in\{2,\dots,e+1\}
.
Thus, since
r_{j-1}\leqslant e+1
, this binomial coefficient is divisible by
p
.
We have therefore shown that
F_{e+1}
vanishes in
K
and so
the space
\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)
is cut out by at most
\binom{de+2}{2}-1
equations in

n\binom{e+2}{2}
variables, whence
\dim\operatorname{Mor}_{e}(\mathbb{P}^{2},X)>\mu(e).

∎

4.
Weyl differencing and a mean value estimate

Let
d\geqslant 2
be an integer and let

\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of characteristic
p>d
.
Let
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
d_{1}\geqslant 0
and
d_{2}\geqslant 1
, with
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
.
Let

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and let
F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

be a degree
d
polynomial of the form

F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=G(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})+\mathfrak{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

where
G(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(d_{1},d_{2})
and

\mathfrak{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{K}_{\infty}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
are such that every term of
\mathfrak{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
has degree in
\mathbf{y}
strictly less than
d_{2}
and every term of
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
has degree in
\mathbf{x}
strictly less than
d_{1}
. (Note that this means that the degree in
\mathbf{y}
can be strictly greater than
d_{2}
in
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.)

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
and integers
1\leqslant P_{1}\leqslant P_{2}
, we define

T(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathscr{X}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathscr{Y}}\psi(\alpha F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})),

where

\mathscr{X}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]^{n}:|\mathbf{x}|<q^{P_{1}}\}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathscr{Y}=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[u]^{n}:|\mathbf{y}|<q^{P_{2}}\}.

We shall set
P_{1}=P_{2}
and
G(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})=G(\mathbf{y})
if
d_{1}=0
.
Let
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d_{1}})
and
\mathbf{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d_{2}})
. Then we may write

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{n}\cdots\sum_{j_{d_{1}}=1}^{n}\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{n}\cdots\sum_{k_{d_{2}}=1}^{n}G_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d_{1}}}y_{k_{1}}\cdots y_{k_{d_{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{j}\leqslant n}\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{k}\leqslant n}G_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d_{1}}}y_{k_{1}}\cdots y_{k_{d_{2}}},

where
G_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
is symmetric in
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{d_{1}})
and also in
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d_{2}})
.

4.1.
Weyl differencing

In this section, we make use of the results
introduced in Section
2
, inspired by work of Schindler
[
20
]
. However, we
remark that one additional step of Weyl differencing is required,
due to the fact that the degree
d
component of the polynomial we consider is not necessarily bihomogeneous.
First, by Hölder’s inequality we obtain

(4.1)

|T(\alpha)|^{2^{d_{2}}}\leqslant|\mathscr{X}|^{2^{d_{2}}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathscr{X}\end{subarray}}|T_{\mathbf{x}}({\alpha})|^{2^{d_{2}}},

where

T_{\mathbf{x}}(\alpha)=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathscr{Y}}\psi(\alpha F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})).

We proceed by using Lemma
2.2
to bound
|T_{\mathbf{x}}(\alpha)|^{2^{d_{2}}}
.

Let

(4.2)

\mathscr{P}(\mathbf{y})=\alpha F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

and

\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{y})=\alpha G(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{y})=\alpha\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{k}\leqslant n}\left(\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{j}\leqslant n}G_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d_{1}}}\right)y_{k_{1}}\cdots y_{k_{d_{2}}}.

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
we denote

\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t})=\sum_{\epsilon_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{\epsilon_{t}\in\{0,1\}}\,(-1)^{\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t}}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{y}_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}),

and we set
\mathscr{P}_{0}\equiv 0
.
In particular, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{t}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t})=\penalty\

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t-1})

\displaystyle-\sum_{\epsilon_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{\epsilon_{t-1}\in\{0,1\}}\,(-1)^{\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t-1}}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{y}_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t-1}\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\mathbf{y}_{t})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t-1})-\mathscr{P}_{t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t-2},\mathbf{y}_{t-1}+\mathbf{y}_{t})

\displaystyle+\mathscr{P}_{t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t-2},\mathbf{y}_{t}),

for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
. But then, on taking
t=d_{2}+1
, we may deduce

(4.3)

\begin{split}\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})&-\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}}+\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})\\
&=\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}+1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})-\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}}).\end{split}

Turning to the application of Lemma
2.2
,
it follows that

(4.4)

|T_{\mathbf{x}}({\alpha})|^{2^{d_{2}-1}}\leqslant|\mathscr{Y}|^{2^{d_{2}-1}-d_{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\cdots\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathscr{Y}}\psi(\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1},\mathbf{z}))\right|.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle|T_{\mathbf{x}}({\alpha})|^{2^{d_{2}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\mathscr{Y}|^{2^{d_{2}}-d_{2}-1}\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\cdots\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathscr{Y}}\psi(\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1},\mathbf{z}))\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\mathscr{Y}|^{2^{d_{2}}-d_{2}-1}\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\cdots\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1}\in\mathscr{Y}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{Y}}\psi(\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1},\mathbf{z})-\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}-1},\mathbf{z}^{\prime})).

Writing
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}_{d_{1}}
and
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}}+\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1}
, and
recalling (
4.3
), it follows that

(4.5)

|T_{\mathbf{x}}({\alpha})|^{2^{d_{2}}}\leqslant|\mathscr{Y}|^{2^{d_{2}}-d_{2}-1}\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\cdots\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\psi(\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}+1}(\underline{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})-\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})),

where
\underline{\mathbf{y}}=(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}})
.

Let

\mathscr{F}_{d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{\epsilon_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{\epsilon_{d_{2}}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{d_{2}}}\mathscr{F}(\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{y}_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{d_{2}}\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}})

and

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},t}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{t})=\sum_{\epsilon_{1}\in\{0,1\}}\cdots\sum_{\epsilon_{t}\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{\epsilon_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t}}\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{x},\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{y}_{1}+\cdots+\epsilon_{t}\mathbf{y}_{t}),

for
d_{2}\leqslant t\leqslant d_{2}+1
. We recall that the degree of
\mathfrak{f}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
in
\mathbf{y}
is strictly less than
d_{2}
.
Therefore, on making use of (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we obtain

\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})=\mathscr{F}_{d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})+\alpha\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})

and

\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}+1}(\underline{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})=\alpha\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}+1}(\underline{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1}).

Moreover, the polynomial
\mathscr{F}_{d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})
is the unique symmetric multilinear form associated to
\mathscr{F}(\mathbf{y})
.
In particular, it follows that

(4.6)

\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})=(-1)^{d_{2}}d_{2}!\alpha\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{k}\leqslant n}\left(\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{j}\leqslant n}G_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d_{1}}}\right)y_{1,k_{1}}\cdots y_{d_{2},k_{d_{2}}}+\alpha\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}}).

Moreover, since
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a polynomial of degree in
\mathbf{x}
strictly less than
d_{1}
, it follows that so are

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}+1}(\underline{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})
.

We substitute the inequality (
4.5
) into (
4.1
), and we interchange the order of summation moving
the sum over
\mathbf{x}
inside the sums over
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1}
.
Coupled with an application of Hölder’s inequality, this yields

(4.7)

\begin{split}|T(\alpha)|^{2^{d_{1}+d_{2}-1}}\leqslant\penalty\ &|\mathscr{Y}|^{2^{d_{1}+d_{2}-1}-d_{2}-1}|\mathscr{X}|^{2^{d_{1}+d_{2}-1}-2^{d_{1}-1}}\\
&\times\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{1}\in\mathscr{Y}}\cdots\sum_{\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1}\in\mathscr{Y}}|\mathfrak{T}_{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}(\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1};\alpha)|^{2^{d_{1}-1}},\end{split}

where

\mathfrak{T}_{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}(\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1};\alpha)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathscr{X}}\psi\left(\alpha\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}+1}(\underline{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1})-\alpha\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{j}\leqslant n}\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{j}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{d_{1}}}-\alpha\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{x},d_{2}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})\right)

and

\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{j}}(\underline{\mathbf{y}})=(-1)^{d_{2}}d_{2}!\sum_{1\leqslant\mathbf{k}\leqslant n}G_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}}y_{1,k_{1}}\cdots y_{{d_{2}},k_{d_{2}}}.

We now apply the same differencing process as before to
|\mathfrak{T}_{\underline{\mathbf{y}}}(\mathbf{y}_{d_{2}+1};\alpha)|^{2^{d_{1}-1}}
, but using

Lemma 4.7

Let

Proof.

First suppose that

it follows
from (

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose

\sigma_{G}\varrho>2(d-1)

and

P_{1}\geqslant\frac{d_{1}P_{1}+d_{2}P_{2}+d-1}{2(d-1)}.

Then there exits

such that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|E(\alpha)|^{\varrho}\mathrm{d}\alpha\leqslant E(0)^{\varrho}q^{-d_{1}P_{1}-d_{2}P_{2}+d-1}\left(1+(d-1)^{n\varrho}\frac{q^{-\delta}}{1-q^{-\delta}}\right).

Proof.

First we have the trivial bound

\int_{\mathfrak{M}(1)}|E(\alpha)|^{\varrho}\mathrm{d}\alpha\leqslant E(0)^{\varrho}q^{-d_{1}P_{1}-d_{2}P_{2}+d-1}.

Therefore, by combining this estimate with Lemma

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|E(\alpha)|^{\varrho}\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{M}(1)}|E(\alpha)|^{\varrho}\mathrm{d}\alpha+\sum_{J\geqslant 1}I_{J}(\varrho)

\displaystyle\leqslant E(0)^{\varrho}q^{-d_{1}P_{1}-d_{2}P_{2}+d-1}\left(1+(d-1)^{n\varrho}\frac{q^{-\delta}}{1-q^{-\delta}}\right),

as required.

Remark 5.2
.

We now give a non-rigorous explanation of why our choice of the bihomogeneous structure
is essentially optimal, in terms of the dimension of the Birch singular locus (
1.5
), among all possible choices of different Weyl differencing processes.
Let us choose subsets
\mathfrak{I}_{i}\subset\{0,\dots,e\}
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
.
We can modify the selection of the differencing process in Section
4.1

by differencing with respect to
\mathbf{t}_{s}
with
s\in\mathfrak{I}_{1}
in the first round, then with
s\in\mathfrak{I}_{2}
in the second round,
and so on, with the effect that we only pick out the terms whose monomials are of the form

t_{s_{1},i_{1}}\dots t_{s_{d},i_{d}},

with

(s_{\sigma(1)},\dots,s_{\sigma(d)})\in\mathfrak{I}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathfrak{I}_{d}

for some permutation
\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(d)\}=\{1,\dots,d\}
.
The usual Weyl differencing process corresponds to taking
\mathfrak{I}_{i}=\{0,\dots,e\}
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
,
while when
r<d
our choice in Section
5
corresponds to

\mathfrak{I}_{i}=\begin{cases}\{\ell\}&\text{ for $1\leqslant i\leqslant d-r$,}\\
\{\ell-1\}&\text{ for $d-r<i\leqslant d$}.\end{cases}

We may observe that every
\alpha_{j}
remaining in the resulting exponential sum corresponds to

(5.6)

j\in\{s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}:s_{1}\in\mathfrak{I}_{1},\dots,s_{d}\in\mathfrak{I}_{d}\}.

In particular, if we denote by
j_{0}
the smallest such
j
, then

j_{0}=s_{1}^{\prime}+\dots+s_{d}^{\prime},

where
s_{i}^{\prime}=\min_{s\in\mathfrak{I}_{i}}s
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, is the unique representation of
j_{0}
in the set (
5.6
).
This means that in the Weyl differencing argument behind Lemma
4.6
, after intersecting with the relevant diagonal,
the row corresponding to
j_{0}
in the Jacobian of the resulting system of forms corresponds to the first partial derivatives of

c\Gamma_{f}(\mathbf{t}_{s^{\prime}_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{t}_{s^{\prime}_{d}}),

for some
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
By multilinearity, it is readily seen that the Birch singular locus (
1.5
) of any system of forms that includes
c\Gamma_{f}(\mathbf{t}_{s^{\prime}_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{t}_{s^{\prime}_{d}})

has codimension at most
n
in the ambient space. Thus it appears that
the Weyl differencing process results in the codimension of the Birch singular locus being at most
n
, no matter how we choose the sets

\mathfrak{I}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{I}_{d}
.
