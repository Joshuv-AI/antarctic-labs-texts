FBXO17 regulates the malignant progression of colorectal cancer via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway
Authors: Enyan Tang, Yijie Xiao, Xiaohong Qian, Shufei Li, Bin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide, often presenting without apparent early symptoms, and most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage with a poor prognosis. F-box protein 17 (FBXO17) is up-regulated in various tumors and may represent a potential prognostic marker in CRC. This study sought to elucidate FBXO17’s specific role in CRC and the underlying mechanism.MethodsReal-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), together with Western blot, was applied to determine FBXO17 expression in CRC cell lines. FBXO17-silenced and FBXO17-overexpressing CRC cells were established using a lentiviral system and liposome transfection technology, respectively. Cell counting kit-8, Transwell, and wound healing assays, as well as Western blot, were used to assess CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, and to examine Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), Fe2+, malondialdehyde (MDA), oxidized glutathione, and reduced glutathione, along with GPX4 and PTGS2 protein expression, were determined to assess CRC cell ferroptosis.ResultsCompared with human normal colon cells, human CRC cells exhibited generally high FBXO17 expression. Silencing FBXO17 led to decreased CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, attenuated Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, and enhanced ferroptosis, whereas overexpression of FBXO17 reversed these effects by enhancing CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and suppressing ferroptosis. Furthermore, treatment with ferrostatin-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor) or CHIR-99021 (Wnt/β-catenin pathway agonist) enhanced the proliferation, invasion, and migration of FBXO17-silenced CRC cells while attenuating ferroptosis.ConclusionsSilencing FBXO17 can promote CRC cell ferroptosis but inhibit proliferation, invasion, and migration through suppression of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), F-box protein 17 (FBXO17), Wnt/β-catenin, ferroptosis

Highlight box

Key findings

• F-box protein 17 (FBXO17) is abnormally overexpressed in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells.

• Silencing FBXO17 inhibits CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, suppresses Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, and promotes ferroptosis.

• Treatment with ferrostatin-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor) or CHIR-99021 (a Wnt/β-catenin pathway agonist) restores the malignant phenotypes of FBXO17-silenced CRC cells and attenuates ferroptosis.

What is known and what is new?

• F-box proteins regulate cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis. FBXO17 is highly expressed in several cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer, glioma, and breast cancer, and has been reported to modulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been shown to be involved in its regulation.

• This study demonstrates that FBXO17 is overexpressed in CRC cells and promotes their malignant progression. Mechanistically, FBXO17 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and suppresses ferroptosis, thereby enhancing CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• FBXO17 may serve as a prospective therapeutic target for CRC.

• Targeting FBXO17, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, or ferroptosis could represent effective strategies for CRC treatment.

• Further studies are warranted to validate these findings in in vivo models and clinical samples.

Introduction

As a malignant tumor that usually develops in the gastrointestinal tract, colorectal cancer (CRC) frequently presents without apparent early symptoms. Established risk factors for CRC include aging, unhealthy eating habits, cigarette smoking, physical inactivity, and obesity (1). Globally, CRC represents the fifth leading contributor to cancer mortality, with its incidence showing a continuous upward trend, particularly among younger adults (2). Data from the United States have shown that (3) CRC usually progresses to the late stage, which suggests a pessimistic outlook for the future trends of the disease. Moreover, global disparities in CRC stage distribution have been reported, highlighting the necessity of developing targeted strategies to facilitate early diagnosis and improve disease management (4). Accordingly, current guidelines from the US Preventive Services Task Force support routine screening of average-risk, asymptomatic adults aged 45 to 75 years (5).

The onset and progression of CRC are closely correlated with aberrant alterations in multiple signaling pathways (6), among which the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has received particular attention. This pathway is highly conserved and is initiated by a multiprotein signalosome complex, playing a crucial part in embryonic development, organogenesis, as well as tissue homeostasis (7). It is driven by the cascade transduction of β-catenin. Following activation, there is accumulation of β-catenin in the nucleus, which subsequently binds to T cell factor/lymphoid enhancer-binding factor transcription factors, thereby activating downstream target genes, including axis inhibition protein (AXIN), Cyclin D1, and c-Myc (7). Current research reports that an aberrantly activated Wnt/β-catenin pathway is in close association with elevated CRC incidence, heightened malignancy, unfavorable prognosis, as well as increased cancer-associated mortality (8). Accordingly, targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has gained increasing attention as a potential therapeutic strategy for CRC (9).

At present, accumulating evidence highlights the essential role of ferroptosis, a specialized form of regulated cell death marked by iron-dependent lipid peroxide accumulation, in modulating cancer progression (10). Studies (11-14) have identified ferroptosis as an important regulator of CRC progression and the development of other cancers. Notably, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis (15); however, the underlying mechanism of this regulation in CRC remains unclear.

F-box protein 17 (FBXO17) functions within the ubiquitin-proteasome system and participates in various processes, including apoptosis, proliferation, cell cycle regulation, as well as tumor progression (16). A previous study has also suggested that FBXO17 regulates tumor development by modulating the Wnt pathway (17). Moreover, clinical evidence suggests that FBXO17 is highly expressed in high-risk CRC populations, with its aberrant overexpression being linked to poor prognosis (18). Nevertheless, the precise role and molecular mechanisms underlying FBXO17’s involvement in CRC remain incompletely characterized. Therefore, we sought to elucidate in the current research FBXO17’s biological functions in CRC using in vitro cellular models. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0704/rc).

Methods

Cell culture

The indicated human CRC cell lines (HT29, HCT-116, SW480, LoVo, and SW620) and normal colonic epithelial cell lines (FHC and CCD841) were obtained respectively from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA). All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, A4192101, Gibco, Grand Island, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (1009158, Gibco) at 37 ℃ under an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Transfection and treatment of cells

The FBXO17 siRNA lentiviral vector and FBXO17 overexpression lentiviral vector were from Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). We inoculated cells into a 6-well plate and transfected lentivirus-mediated vectors (si-NC, si-FBXO17, oe-NC and oe-FBXO17) with Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) based on the provided instructions. After a 6-hour incubation at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2, complete culture medium was then used.

Both ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, HY-100579) and CHIR-99021 (a Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway agonist, HY-10182) were obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, USA) and dissolved in DMSO. Cells from the si-FBXO17 group underwent 24 h of treatment with either Fer-1 (1 μM) or CHIR-99021 (10 μM).

Real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Isolation of cellular total RNA was performed with the use of Trizol reagent (B610409, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), followed by quantification of RNA concentration. Subsequently, synthesis of cDNA from the extracted RNA was carried out using a reverse transcription kit (B532431, Sangon Biotech). With β-actin serving as the reference gene, we performed qPCR for the cDNA with RT-qPCR instrument (CFX-96 TOUCH, BIO-RAD, Hercules, USA), and calculated the relative expression of FBXO17. Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. provided the primers. FBXO17: 5'-TGG GGA AGA TTG GGA AAG GC-3' (forward), 5'-TCG CCC ATT GGC TAC ATC TC-3' (reverse). β-actin: 5'-CTG GAA CGG TGA AGG TGA CA-3' (forward), 5'-CGG CCA CAT TGT GAA CTT TG-3' (reverse).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates prior to the indicated treatments. Next, following the addition of CCK-8 solution (10 µL, E606335, Sangon Biotech) to each well, the plate was subjected to 1 h of incubation at 37 ℃. Subsequently, we determined the absorbance (at 450 nm) through a preheated microplate analyzer (MK-3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and cell viability was assessed.

Wound healing assay

Horizontal reference lines were marked on the bottom of the 6-well plate at 1-cm intervals using a marker pen. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at the desired density and incubated overnight until a confluent monolayer was formed, as confirmed through microscopic examination. A scratch was made on the cell monolayer using a sterile 10-µL pipette tip held perpendicular to the pre-drawn reference line on the plate. After gently washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times, the cells were subjected to 24 h of culture in serum-free DMEM. Images were subsequently captured for observation.

Transwell invasion assay

The prepared cell culture medium and Transwell chamber (354480, Corning, New York, USA) covered with Matrigel were incubated at 37 ℃. We added complete culture medium (600 μL) to the lower chamber, and serum-free cell suspension (150 μL, 2×105/mL) to the upper chamber for 24 h of incubation. The Transwell chamber was subsequently removed, and the liquid in the upper chamber was sucked out and then transferred to a new 24-well plate. Following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for half an hour, the cells subsequently underwent 20 min of staining with 1% crystal violet staining solution (E607309, Sangon Biotech). The dye solution was rinsed with running water. Finally, we put the washed chamber under a microscope and took photos.

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

2',7'-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (S0033M, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was diluted with serum-free DMEM and utilized for detecting intracellular ROS. Following the indicated treatments, we collected the cells and resuspended them in diluted DCFH-DA (10 μM) prior to 20 min of incubation at 37 ℃. During the process incubation, we gently inverted the tubes every 3–5 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed with serum-free DMEM for three times. Lastly, we observed the fluorescence intensity using a fluorescence microscope (Axio Observer A1, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Detection of Fe2+

Following the indicated treatments, we washed the cells with serum-free medium three times. Subsequently, cells were cultured with FerroOrange working solution (1 μM, F374, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) diluted in serum-free DMEM, at 37 ℃ for half an hour following the provided instructions. After that, a fluorescence microscope was used to capture images of the cells for observation.

Detection of malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), and oxidized glutathione (GSSG). Detection kits for MDA (D799762), GSH (D799614), and GSSG (D799616) were all bought from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Cells were processed as per the provided protocol, followed by collection of the culture supernatants. After preheating the microplate reader, we detected MDA (532 nm), GSH (412 nm), and GSSG (412 nm) through standardized procedures. At last, a standard curve was drawn and the corresponding concentration was calculated.

Western blot

Extraction of total proteins from all experimental groups was performed using RIPA Lysis Buffer II (C510006, Sangon Biotech), followed by protein quantification with the use of the BCA Protein Assay Kit (C503021, Sangon Biotech). The extracted proteins underwent separation by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel (C631100, Sangon Biotech) and were subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (F619537, Sangon Biotech), which was subjected to 15 min of blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST at room temperature and an subsequent overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with the following listed primary antibodies: FBXO17 (1:5,000, 68246-1-Ig), PTGS2 (1:2,000, 66351-1-Ig), GPX4 (1:2,000, 67763-1-Ig), β-catenin (1:10,000, 66379-1-Ig), GSK3β (1:10,000, 67329-1-Ig), p-GSK3β (Ser9) (1:5,000, 67558-1-Ig), c-MYC (1:5,000, 67447-1-Ig), AXIN2 (1:5,000, 67458-1-Ig), Cyclin D1 (1:3,000, 60186-1-Ig), and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:50,000, 60004-1-Ig). The PVDF membrane was next subjected to 2 h of incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) (1:10,000, SA00001-1) at room temperature. The protein imprint was developed by ECL luminescence reagent (C500044, Sangon Biotech) and a chemiluminescence gel imaging system (FluorChem Q, Proteinsimple, San Jose, USA). All antibodies were sourced from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Wuhan, China).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.0) was employed for performing statistical analysis and preparing graphical presentation. Three independent cell culture wells were included in each experimental group as biological replicates (not technical replicates, n=3). Data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation and tested for normality prior to statistical analysis. Between-group comparisons were performed using an unpaired t-test. One-way ANOVA was conducted to determine statistical significance, followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test for multiple pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as a P value below 0.05.

Results

FBXO17 is abnormally overexpressed in CRC cells

To determine FBXO17 expression in CRC, two normal human colon cell lines as well as five CRC cell lines were selected, and FBXO17 expression was assessed at both the messenger RNA (mRNA) and protein levels. Relative to normal human colon cells, CRC cells exhibited significantly elevated FBXO17 expression at the mRNA level (all P<0.001, Figure 1A) and protein level (all P<0.01, Figure 1B,1C). Among the examined CRC cell lines, SW480 cells exhibited the highest FBXO17 expression and were therefore selected for subsequent experiments.

FBXO17 is abnormally overexpressed in CRC cells. (A) The mRNA expression of FBXO17 determined through RT-qPCR. (B,C) The protein expression of FBXO17 assessed though western blot. n=3. CRC, colorectal cancer; GAPDH, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase; mRNA, messenger RNA; RT-qPCR, real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction.

FBXO17 affects CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To elucidate FBXO17’s role in the function of CRC cells, si-FBXO17 and oe-FBXO17 SW480 cell models were established, respectively. We evaluated FBXO17 expression (Figure 2A,2B) along with cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. As illustrated in Figure 2, the si-FBXO17 group exhibited significantly lower levels of proliferation (P<0.01, Figure 2C), migration (P<0.001, Figure 2D), and invasion (P<0.001, Figure 2E) than the si-NC group. In comparison to the oe-NC group, the aforementioned proliferation, migration, and invasion in the oe-FBXO17 group were significantly enhanced (P<0.001). Overall, it was demonstrated that FBXO17 promotes CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

FBXO17 affects ferroptosis and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in CRC cells

Targeting ferroptosis with ubiquitin system enzymes represents a valuable approach for cancer therapy (19), and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has emerged as a critical modulator of ferroptosis. In the current research, we investigated FBXO17’s role in ferroptosis and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in SW480 cells. si-FBXO17 and oe-FBXO17 cell models were established. Relative to the si-NC group, the si-FBXO17 group exhibited markedly higher ROS, Fe2+, and MDA levels (P<0.01, Figure 3A-3C), and a lower GSH/GSSG ratio (P<0.05, Figure 3D). Additionally, GPX4, β-catenin, c-Myc, AXIN2, and Cyclin D1 protein expression, along with the p-GSK3β/GSK3β ratio, decreased obviously, whereas GSK3β and PTGS2 expression increased (P<0.05, Figure 3E,3F). The oe-FBXO17 group demonstrated significantly lower levels of ROS, Fe2+, MDA, and PTGS2 protein (P<0.001), a markedly higher GPX4 protein level (P<0.01), and a notably higher GSH/GSSG ratio (P<0.001) relative to the oe-NC group. Meanwhile, significant increases were observed in β-catenin, c-Myc, AXIN2, and Cyclin D1 protein expression, as well as the p-GSK3β/GSK3β ratio (P<0.01), whereas a decrease was demonstrated in GSK3β expression (P<0.01). Our observations indicated that silencing FBXO17 markedly inhibited Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation while promoting ferroptosis of SW480 cells, while overexpressing FBXO17 had the opposite effects.

FBXO17 affects CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via regulation of ferroptosis

For clarifying whether FBXO17 influences CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via modulation of ferroptosis, si-FBXO17 SW480 cells were subjected to treatment with Fer-1 (a ferroptosis inhibitor). Relative to the si-NC group, the si-FBXO17 group exhibited notably suppressed ferroptosis, accompanied by attenuated proliferation, migration, and invasion (P<0.01, Figure 4). Relative to the si-FBXO17 group, the si-FBXO17 + Fer-1 group demonstrated markedly decreased levels of ROS, Fe2+, and MDA in SW480 cells (P<0.001, Figure 4A-4C), notably increased GSH/GSSG ratio (P<0.05, Figure 4D), decreased PTGS2 protein expression (P<0.001, Figure 4E), increased GPX4 protein expression (P<0.05, Figure 4E), along with notably higher rates of proliferation, migration, and invasion (P<0.05, Figure 4F-4H). Therefore, it was concluded that FBXO17 enhances CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via regulation of ferroptosis.

FBXO17 regulates ferroptosis through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to affect CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To further determine whether FBXO17 regulates ferroptosis through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to affect CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, si-FBXO17 SW480 cells were treated with CHIR-99021 (a specific agonist of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway). Consistent with the aforementioned results, FBXO17 silencing induced ferroptosis and suppressed the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells. Compared with the si-FBXO17 group, the si-FBXO17 + CHIR-99021 group exhibited significantly decreased ROS, MDA, and Fe2+ levels (P<0.01, Figure 5A-5C) and an increased GSH/GSSG ratio (P<0.05, Figure 5D). Concurrently, β-catenin, c-MYC, AXIN2, Cyclin D1, and GPX4 protein expression, along with the p-GSK3β/GSK3β ratio, were substantially elevated, whereas PTGS2 expression was markedly reduced (P<0.01, Figure 5E,5F). Moreover, cell proliferation, migration, and invasion rates were notably restored (P<0.01, Figure 5G-5J). Therefore, these findings demonstrate that FBXO17 regulates CRC cell ferroptosis through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thus influencing cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

Discussion

Despite considerable progress in CRC treatment, the management of metastatic CRC continues to represent a major clinical challenge. Drugs targeting malignant characteristics of cancer cells, like proliferation and metastasis, contribute to targeted therapy and have a crucial role in CRC treatment (20). F-box protein, a substrate recognition subunit of the SKP1-cullin 1-F-box protein E3 ligase complex, function in phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitination and participate in modulating the proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, angiogenesis, as well as metastasis of various cells. Targeting F-box proteins and their associated signal pathways has broad prospects in cancer treatment (21,22).

FBXO17, a newly associated F-box protein, is substantially expressed in multiple cancers (23-25), including non-small cell lung cancer, glioma, and breast cancer, and it influences the progression of cancers via modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. A bioinformatics report (18) proved that FBXO17 is closely related to overall survival in CRC and is highly expressed in high-risk populations with poor prognosis. However, FBXO17’s role in CRC has not been revealed. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation was performed to elucidate FBXO17’s exact role in CRC. Our study showed that FBXO17 knockdown significantly suppressed CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, inhibited Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, and enhanced ferroptosis. On the contrary, FBXO17 overexpression enhanced CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, promoted Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, and attenuated ferroptosis. Overall, it was indicated that FBXO17 enhances CRC progression, potentially via over-activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and suppression of ferroptosis.

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been widely reported as a therapeutic target of CRC (9). Currently, numerous genes and drugs targeting this pathway have been identified for CRC treatment. Moreover, accumulating evidence has established Wnt/β-catenin signaling as an important modulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in CRC (26). Runt-related transcription factor 1 is abnormally elevated in CRC, and it enhances CRC metastasis through activation of EMT as well as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (27). NLRP12, belonging to the NOD-like receptor family, is a cytoplasmic pattern recognition receptor that inhibits the phosphorylation of GSK3β by interacting with serine-threonine protein kinase 38, thereby suppressing Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation as well as CRC progression (28). Replication factor C4 enhances EMT in CRC cells via modulation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation (29). Liquidambar acid, a pentacyclic triterpenoid isolated from Liquidambar formosana Hance, can suppress the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and CRC by targeting TNF receptor-related factor 2 (30). Cryptopropine, an alkaloid extracted from the roots of Cryptolepis sanguinolenta, which is a native shrub in Africa, inhibits the malignant behavior of CRC cells through suppression of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation mediated by Wnt3a (31).

Targeting ferroptosis is a crucial strategy in treating cancers, including drug-resistant cancers (32). In melanoma (33), targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway promotes ferroptosis and concurrently enhances the efficacy of immunotherapy. In gastric cancer (12), the β-catenin/TCF4 transcription complex binds to the GPX4 promoter and activates its expression, thus leading to suppression of ferroptosis. Ferroptosis is modulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (15). The findings of the current research fully verify this mechanism in CRC. In this study, the proliferation, migration, and invasion of FBXO17-silenced CRC cells were substantially improved following treatment with Fer-1. Moreover, when FBXO17-silenced CRC cells were treated with CHIR-99021, there was a marked enhancement of cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, along with suppression of ferroptosis. These observations suggest that silencing FBXO17 facilitates ferroptosis by suppressing Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, thus inhibiting CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Previous studies have demonstrated that FBXO17 targets GSK3β for proteasomal degradation (34). As a core kinase of the β-catenin destruction complex, GSK3β degradation or inactivation leads to nuclear translocation, β-catenin stabilization, as well as subsequent activation of downstream target gene transcription. Collectively, it is suggested that FBXO17 modulates CRC cell ferroptosis via the GSK3β-regulated Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified FBXO17, an E3 ubiquitin system enzyme, as a prospective therapeutic target for CRC. Importantly, FBXO17 was shown to regulate ferroptosis in CRC cells via the GSK3β-modulated Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thus affecting CRC progression. The findings of the current study further underscore the prospect of targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and ferroptosis as effective strategies for CRC treatment.
