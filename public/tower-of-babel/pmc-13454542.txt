Mental Health Literacy and Stigma Among Community Pharmacists in Jordan: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Rana K. Abu‐Farha, Noor Saleh, Karem H. Alzoubi, Aya Nawasreh, Muna Barakat, Fahmi Y. Al‐Ashwal, Faten Odeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThis study aimed to assess the mental health literacy (MHL) of community pharmacists in Jordan and identify factors influencing stigma towards mental health disorders (MHDs).MethodsA cross sectional survey design was used among an adequate sample of community pharmacists in Jordan; a pre‐designed questionnaire was shared using Google Forms via social media. A multi‐section survey queried knowledge based upon MHL as well as measures of stigma (The Opening Minds Scale for Health Care Providers (OMS‐HC‐15) related to MHDs. Data were analyzed using SPSS® software, and linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with stigma toward MHDs.ResultsA total of 268 pharmacists participated in the study. The median age was 28 years (IQR = 5.0), and 36.2% (n = 97) reported receiving formal mental health training. Additionally, 81.7% (n = 219) expressed willingness to provide mental health support in their pharmacy practice. The median MHL score was 5.0 out of 9 (IQR = 4.0). Pharmacists generally reported low to moderate confidence in managing MHDs. The median OMS‐HC‐15 stigma score was 47.0 (IQR = 6.0), indicating moderate levels of stigma. The most commonly reported barriers to providing mental health care were lack of specialized training (76.1%), insufficient private space for confidential discussions (75.0%), lack of reimbursement for mental health services (74.6%), insufficient communication with other healthcare professionals (70.5%), and time constraints (69.4%). Multiple linear regression analysis showed that higher MHL scores were significantly associated with lower stigma scores (B = −0.927, p < 0.001).ConclusionCommunity pharmacists in Jordan have expressed a desire to provide mental health care but experience challenges because of a lack of training and stigma. Enhancing the education of Community Pharmacists regarding mental health will allow them to take on a greater role in caring for patients with mental health needs.

Plain Language Title

Understanding Mental Health Knowledge and Stigma Among Pharmacists in Jordan.

Plain Language Summary

This research looked at the level of knowledge and stigma that community pharmacists in Jordan had regarding mental health and how this may impact their ability to provide support for patients who suffer from a mental health disorder. The purpose of this study was to determine whether or not the lack of knowledge impacted the attitudes of community pharmacists toward patients with mental health disorders.

Data were collected from a sample of 268 community pharmacists, most of whom were relatively new to the field (average age 28). Although more than half of all pharmacists reported being able to assist patients who suffer from mental health disorders (81.7%) their level of understanding of mental health issues varied significantly between them. Most of the pharmacists who participated in this study felt that they could assist patients who have mental health disorders, but not with full confidence in their ability to do so.

Pharmacists participating in the study demonstrated significant amounts of stigma toward individuals with mental health disorders. The relationship was found to be inversely related so that the higher a pharmacist's level of education related to mental health disorders, the lower level of stigma exhibited to individuals with a mental health disorder. Other barriers to providing care were found such as lack of formal training (64%), time constraints (56%), and the absence of a private location in which to consult with clients about their mental health status (51.2%). It can be concluded that community pharmacists in Jordan want to provide assistance with mental health issue but require additional education and/or training in order to do so.

Introduction

Mental health disorders (MHDs) account for 10% of the global disease burden, contributing to both fatal and non‐fatal conditions with significant impacts on the lives of individuals [1]. MHDs affect millions of people worldwide, posing a major challenge to public health [2]. In many countries, there is a widespread stigma surrounding mental health, compounded by limited understanding and awareness about MHDs [3, 4]. Such stigma can significantly hinder the quality of care available to individuals with MHDs, as attitudes towards mental health problems affect the delivery of care.

The stigma surrounding MHDs and lack of knowledge regarding mental health are still major obstacles toward seeking proper management for MHDs in Jordan and in the region [5, 6, 7, 8]. Enhancing mental health literacy (MHL) among healthcare providers might reduce the existing stigma, promote effective communication with patients and contribute toward improved mental health status [9, 10]. Identifying pharmacists' awareness and attitude toward MHDs may contribute to defining future knowledge gaps, intervention strategies and training programs.

Mental health services are primarily provided in primary care settings, community health programs and specialized health centers. However, negative attitudes toward MHDs and stigmatizing beliefs still lead to limited health care utilization, treatment adherence and poor health outcome of individuals with MHDs [11]. Within the professional sector, knowledge gaps and lack of experience in mental health care could lead to discriminatory attitudes toward individuals with MHDs. Previous literature revealed that increasing MHL have been correlated with an increase in empathy, understanding, and reduction of stigma toward MHDs [12].

Pharmacists are readily available and accessible healthcare providers [13, 14]. Their potential in mental health care includes managing and dispensing medications, counseling patients about medication use, detecting symptoms of mental illness early and follow up with treatment compliance [15, 16, 17]. Training in advanced psychiatric pharmacy practice or continuing professional development education could also improve their service in mental health care [9, 10]. Their roles can be hindered if they possess low MHL and have negative perceptions towards MHDs due to stigma [18]. Therefore, the study aimed to assess MHL, experiences, stigma, and perceived barriers regarding mental health care among community pharmacists in Jordan, and to identify factors associated with stigma towards MHDs.

Methods

Data Collection

This cross‐sectional study was conducted using a structured questionnaire designed to measure MHL and stigma related to MHD patients among community pharmacists in Jordan. The sample population of community pharmacists that were selected was registered with the Jordanian Pharmacist's Association (JPA), which is the national professional body for pharmacists in Jordan, and were actively working in the community pharmacists. Data was collected between 18th December and 31st December 2024 by administering a survey on Google forms which was distributed through social media (Facebook, WhatsApp, and Twitter) as well as through pharmacists' professional network. Convenience sampling combined with snowball sampling was utilized.

Questionnaire Development and Validation

The design of the questionnaire used in this study was carried out based on a review of relevant literature available [19, 20]. The questions were intended to represent various aspects of MHL. These aspects included symptom recognition, common psychiatric diagnoses and their management, treatment options, and general information pertaining to MHDs. The items were designed to relate to community pharmacy practice, to measure baseline rather than expertise in clinical issues, and to represent critical concepts for pharmacy practice with respect to patients with mental disorders. The questionnaire was drafted in English as it is the primary language used in pharmacy education at the academic institutions in Jordan. The form was divided into six sections: 1.Demographics: Data collected included age, gender, level of education, years of experience, type and location of the pharmacy, working shifts, and the daily number of patients handled.2.Mental Health Experience: Explored pharmacists' experiences with mental health, including formal training, participation in workshops, personal experience with patients having mental health issues, and willingness to give mental health support.3.Mental Health Literacy (MHL): Assessed pharmacists' knowledge of all aspects of MHDs such as symptoms, diagnosis, and treatments, giving a score according to number of correct answers: each correct answer earned one (1) point, with a maximum possible score of nine (9) points.4.Confidence in Managing MHDs: Determining confidence in managing MHDs using a 5‐point Likert scale from “very confident” to “not confident at all.”5.Stigma Assessment: The Opening Minds Scale for Health Care Providers (OMS‐HC‐15) was used as a self‐reporting tool to assess health care providers' attitude towards persons with mental illness, especially for evaluating anti‐stigma interventions [21]. The tool has 15 questions that use a 5‐point rating scale with 1 indicating strongly disagree to 5 indicating strongly agree, with higher scores indicating more stigmatizing attitudes. The total score ranged from 15 (least stigmatizing) to 75 (most stigmatizing) according to the validated instrument. Certain items (10, 11, 12, 13, and 15) were reverse‐coded to ensure accurate scoring. Permission to use the scale was obtained beforehand.6.Barriers to Providing Effective Mental Health Care: This section of the study utilized a 5‐point Likert scale (strongly disagree) to (strongly agree). It includes time constraints, communication challenges with other healthcare professionals, lack of specialized training, and insufficient private space for confidential discussions.

Before distribution, the questionnaire was pilot‐tested on five community pharmacists to assess clarity of wording, readability, and face validity. This pilot phase was used exclusively for questionnaire refinement, and no psychometric validation was conducted. Data from the pilot test were excluded from the final analysis.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size for this study was determined based on the guidelines provided by Green (1991), who recommended a minimum of 50 + 8 m participants for multiple linear regression analysis, where m represents the number of predictors [22]. Given that nine predictors were included in the final model, the minimum required sample size was 122 participants. This number was aimed at ensuring adequate statistical power to assess the relationships between the predictors and the outcomes reliably.

Ethical Considerations and Informed Consent

The Research and Ethics Committee at Applied Science Private University approved the study (Approval number 2024‐PHA‐42, approved in 16th December 2024). Before starting the questionnaire, participants were informed about the voluntary nature of their participation as well as the purposes of the study. All participants gave their consent electronically prior to starting the survey and they were made aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time. To protect participant identity and confidentiality, no individual participant identifiers were collected during the study.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using the SPSS version 22, developed by SPSS® Inc., Chicago, IL, USA. Medians and interquartile ranges were used for continuous variables. The Shapiro‐Wilk test was used to test for the normality of the distribution of continuous variables. Data were considered to be of normal distribution if p‐value was >0.05. The descriptive statistics for the categorical variables were presented using frequency and percentage. To analyse which factors affected pharmacist's stigma scores, univariate and multiple linear regression were used. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis at p < 0.25 were included into the model using the enter method for the multivariate linear regression analysis [23]. Pearson correlation was used to assess the independence of variables, with values below.9 indicating no multicollinearity among the independent variables in the regression analysis. The level of significance was set at p value ≤ .05, and all statistical tests were two‐sided.

Results

This study enrolled 268 participants, with a median age of 28 years (IQR = 5.0). Females made up 76.5% (n = 205) of the sample. The majority held bachelor's degrees (BPharm and Pharm D) (n = 246, 91.8%), and the median years of experience was 5 years (IQR = 5.0). Most participants worked in independent community pharmacies (n = 155, 57.8%), and most of the pharmacies were located in the central region of the country (n = 148, 55.2%). Regarding pharmacy location, 57.1% (n = 153) worked in side street pharmacies, and 46.6% (n = 125) served fewer than 50 patients per day. These details are summarized in Table 1.

Table 3 highlights pharmacists' MHL about MHDs (n = 268). Many pharmacists correctly identified persistent sadness as a common symptom of depression (n = 160, 59.7%), and bipolar disorder as characterized by both manic and depressive episodes (n = 150, 56.0%). However, fewer pharmacists (n = 116, 43.3%) correctly identified delusions as a primary feature of schizophrenia, and 39.9% (n = 107) recognized that increased energy and focus are not typically associated with substance use disorders. Furthermore, only 32.1% (n = 86) correctly stated that grief is not considered MHD. In terms of treatment knowledge, 72.8% (n = 195) identified antidepressants as the primary medication for depression, while 47.4% (n = 127) recognized psychotherapy as a common non‐pharmacologic treatment, and 49.6% (n = 133) correctly identified electroconvulsive therapy as a treatment for severe depression unresponsive to medication. Overall, pharmacists' knowledge of mental health conditions showed variability, with some areas of strong understanding, particularly regarding antidepressant use and antipsychotic medications. The median MHL score was 5.0 out of 9, with an IQR of 4.0.

Regarding attitudes towards mental illness, 55.2% (n = 148) of pharmacists agreed that they are more comfortable helping individuals with physical illnesses than those with mental illnesses. Additionally, 57.1% (n = 153) agreed that there is little they can do to help people with mental illness. Conversely, 39.6% (n = 106) agreed that people with mental illness don't try hard enough to get better. When it comes to disclosure and help‐seeking, 61.9% (n = 166) of pharmacists agreed they would not disclose their own mental health treatment to colleagues. Moreover, 56.7% (n = 152) agreed that they would feel weak if they couldn't manage their mental illness independently.

In terms of social distance, 64.3% (n = 179) of pharmacists agreed they would be willing to work with a colleague who has a managed mental illness. Similarly, 66.8% (n = 179) agreed that employers should hire individuals with managed mental illness if they are the best candidates for the job. However, 21.8% (n = 58) agreed that someone with a mental illness, even if managed, should not work with children. Finally, 88.4% (n = 237) agreed that they would not mind living next to someone with a mental illness.

The barriers to providing mental health care as perceived by community pharmacists are outlined in Table 6. A significant proportion of pharmacists, 76.1% (n = 204), felt inadequately trained to address complex MHDs. Time constraints were found to be a major barrier with 69.4% (n = 186) confirming that this impacts their effectiveness in dealing with mental health issues. In addition, 70.5% (n = 189) of pharmacists highlighted insufficient communication with other healthcare professionals regarding patients' mental health needs. Findings also showed that lack of private areas in pharmacies to discuss mental health issues with patients in confidence (n = 201; 75.0%) and lack of funding, in relation to mental health services not being reimbursed, were identified as challenges by 74.6% (n = 200) of these practitioners.

Discussion

This study examined community pharmacy practitioners' MHL, stigma‐related attitudes, perceived confidence in managing mental health conditions, and perceived barriers to mental health care. The findings show that while community pharmacists are generally supportive of providing assistance to the mental health community and individuals with mental disorders, there is considerable variability with respect to the practitioners' perception of their ability to help patients with these disorders and the resources available to them. Of particular note is that only a small percentage of participants had completed formal educational training in mental health care, indicating a significant need for more comprehensive training. This aligns with the results of previous studies that indicate significant gaps in MHL among pharmacists [24, 25].

Specifically, our findings indicate differences in community pharmacists' knowledge of MHDs across domains, with highest rate of identification of symptoms of depression and bipolar disorder, while gaps were observed in recognizing features of schizophrenia and in understanding substance use disorders and grief. Moreover, most pharmacists in our study identified antidepressants as the primary treatment for depression, while fewer identified psychotherapy as a non‐pharmacological treatment option and electroconvulsive therapy as a treatment for cases unresponsive to pharmacological therapy. These patterns suggest variability in MHL across different conditions and aspects of care. This may be influenced by contextual factors in Jordan, where mental health and psychiatric pharmacy education is not consistently delivered as a standalone core component across all pharmacy schools, with variability in curriculum depth between universities. In addition, while PharmD programs typically include more extensive experiential and clinical training compared with BPharm programs, structured exposure specifically to mental health practice settings remains limited. This may further contribute to gaps in applied knowledge and confidence in managing mental health disorders among community pharmacists.

Nevertheless, despite some existing gaps in the areas of training and knowledge, a great majority of the pharmacists (81.7%) reported willingness to provide mental health servicesas part of their pharmacy work. This alligns with a study done in Qatar, which found that although pharmacists had positive attitudes about depression care, actual clinical participation in many central elements such as screening for depression, following up with depressed patients, and managing antidepressant therapy was still low [26]. Similarly, our findings are consistent with previous studies reporting gaps in MHL and confidence among pharmacists, highlighting the need for targeted educational and training interventions to better prepare pharmacists for mental health care provision [24, 27].

The level of pharmacists' confidence in taking care of psychiatric issues was found to be between minimal and moderate, with few being fully confident in their capacity. This clearly shows the immense requirement for focused training and support in preparing pharmacists in this critical area. Similar views have been reported in the literature within the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, whereby the pharmacists did not feel confident owing to inadequate formal education and patient‐centered communication skills [19, 24]. Such gaps would be addressed by providing interventions in education and training in order to increase the perception and readiness of the pharmacists to manage mental health treatments [28, 29, 30]. This might also reflect lack of formal continuing professional development in the mental health area for community pharmacists, and inconsistent levels of clinical experience with psychiatric presentations encountered in general practice which can have a cumulative impact on confidence. Notably, a previous study among PharmD graduates found that the psychiatric rotation was a great learning experience that substantially improved the graduates' readiness and confidence in providing mental health services [31].

Most pharmacists in our study felt that mental health care is an important area, though they presently feel uncomfortable when talking to patients about mental health issues. In agreement, Batarseh et al. (2022) found that lack of training makes pharmacists feel that they are incompetent to communicate about mental health issues [19]. The social stigma surrounding mental health disorders is a major hindrance to seeking care, as reported in studies from the MENA region, Australia, and the United Kingdom [19, 30, 32]. Previous research showed that direct interaction between pharmacists and patients having schizophrenia can reduce this stigma effectively. This strategy was most effective for younger pharmacists and those with little experience, as it made them more confident and empathetic when communicating with patients [33]. Notedly, our study shows that increased knowledge about mental health disorders can reduce stigma among community pharmacists, underpinning the influence of education on increased knowledge and positive attitudes. Thus, such a nationwide educational and training program would help to improve the standard of mental health care services provided by community pharmacists.

Participants reported barriers to delivering mental health care that correspond to similar research conducted globally. In this light, a major barrier reported by many pharmacists in our survey was insufficient training in managing complex mental health issues, a barrier that has also been highlighted in previous studies [24, 34]. Time constraints were a second major barrier, which prevented pharmacists from adequately supporting their patients. Similar findings have been documented in other research studies looking at pharmaceutical care and patient consultations [35]. Moreover, a lack of effective communication with other healthcare professionals and a lack of a secluded area for consultations in community pharmacies made it difficult to provide effective mental health care [24, 26, 36]. Cost‐related factors, like a lack of reimbursement for mental health care delivery, further highlighted the need for systemic policy reforms [37]. Similarly, results of a study conducted in the United Arab Emirates indicate that a majority of community pharmacies do not feel adequately prepared to manage complex MHDs. These feelings have arisen from inadequate training, lack of time, and insufficient collaboration with other health care professionals [38].

A number of approaches have been suggested in the existing literature to overcome the barriers to providing mental health supports [34, 39]. For example, tailored training programs have been seen to positively improve the confidence levels and competence of pharmacists [34], as well as improving mental health services [40]. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of utilizing collaborative or “team‐based” models of healthcare. For example, including pharmacists in a multidisciplinary team of healthcare professionals would improve collaboration and help to increase positive patient outcomes [41]. Concerns regarding patient confidentiality would be sufficiently addressed by changes in pharmacy design to incorporate private consultation spaces [42]. Furthermore, addressing financial constraints requires policy reforms to recognize and expand pharmacists' roles in mental health with reimbursement for mental health services. The Royal Pharmaceutical Society (RPS) has emphasized the importance of advocating for policy changes and structural support as an important next step toward achieving sustainable improvement in access to mental health care services [37]. Implementation of these strategies will improve the capacity of community pharmacists to deliver effective mental health care; an action that would not only benefit the patient but also the overall health system.

There are a few limitations to this study. First, there is a potential risk of selection bias due to the use of electronic methods for recruitment. This could have implications for the representativeness of this study's results, especially for those pharmacists less inclined to deal with technology. As a cross‐sectional study design, it also fails to capture changes in MHL and perceptions over a period of time. In addition, self‐report measures were utilized which could have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias, especially for the items regarding attitudes toward mental illness and perceived self‐efficacy in treating mental health problems. Furthermore, a large number of pharmacists stated they had never known or had experience with anyone with a mental health problem. This could represent an underdiagnosis of mental illness, failure of others to disclose their problems, or stigma associated with mental illness. This may have contributed to their views on mental health conditions. Also, one of the items, which measured willingness to support mental health treatment may also have led to social desirability bias, with participants inclined to answer favorably. In addition, the knowledge assessment in the present study tested basic mental health literacy concepts as opposed to advanced clinical knowledge and lacked a comparison with other or external groups. Lastly, convenience and Snowball sampling were utilized, therefore the total number of pharmacists surveyed could not be computed and hence the response rate cannot be evaluated, also, this the non‐probability sampling approach may limit the generalizability of the findings to all community pharmacists in Jordan.

Future actions can be taken through educational and training interventions to increase MHL and decrease stigma toward individuals with MHDs among community pharmacists. Potential interventions may involve increasing mental health content in the pharmacy curriculum, providing more continuing professional development and Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) training, increasing experiential learning in mental health settings, and encouraging interprofessional and stigma reduction education.

The findings from this study may inform future research, including comparative studies across settings, intervention studies evaluating educational and training programs, and longitudinal studies examining changes in mental health literacy and stigma over time. The results may also support policymakers, educators, and healthcare professionals in developing strategies to enhance mental health literacy and reduce stigma within community pharmacy practice.

Conclusion

This study highlights the variability in pharmacists' MHL, confidence, and stigma. Therefore, targeted educational/training opportunities focused on skill development and stigma reduction are needed. Future research should examine how structured educational/training programs specifically designed for pharmacists influence their ability to serve as key sources of mental health support.
