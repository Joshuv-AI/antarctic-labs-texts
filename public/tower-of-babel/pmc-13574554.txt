Socioeconomic Disparities in Post-Discharge Suicide Risk Among Individuals With Schizophrenia: A Population-Based Cohort Study in Korea
Authors: Kyoung-Hoon Kim, Minha Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between socioeconomic status (SES) and suicide in individuals with schizophrenia is complex and not fully understood. This population-based cohort study examined the association between SES and post-discharge suicide risk among individuals with schizophrenia in South Korea.MethodsUsing data from the National Health Insurance Claims Database, as well as national mortality records, this study included 17,565 adults newly hospitalized for schizophrenia between 2017 and 2018. SES was proxied by insurance type—health insurance (HI) versus medical aid (MA). The association between insurance type and suicide risk was examined using Cox’s proportional hazards model.ResultsDuring the follow-up period, patients receiving MA—indicating lower SES—had a significantly higher risk of suicide (hazard ratio, 1.135; 95% confidence interval, 1.096–1.175), compared with those with HI, even after adjusting for age, sex, comorbidities, and outpatient follow-up within seven days post-discharge. While early outpatient visits were associated with reduced suicide risk, this effect was notably lower among MA recipients.ConclusionThese findings highlight persistent disparities in suicide outcomes within a universal healthcare system and underscore the need for targeted post-discharge interventions addressing structural barriers faced by socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals with schizophrenia.

INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a chronic and severe mental disorder associated with a significantly elevated risk of suicide, compared with that in the general population.1 Previous studies have reported that individuals with schizophrenia are approximately 10 times more likely to die by suicide.234 Notably, suicide risk increases sharply during the period immediately following hospital discharge. In a recently published South Korean study, individuals with schizophrenia exhibited a 66.8-fold higher suicide mortality after hospital discharge, compared with the general population, thereby highlighting this group’s extreme vulnerability.5 Similarly, psychotic disorders have been identified as the strongest diagnostic predictor of post-discharge suicide in children and adolescents.6 This post-discharge phase is crucial due to the interplay of multiple factors, including residual symptoms, social isolation, and discontinuity of care.78 Therefore, effective follow-up management during this critical period is essential.

One key strategy for reducing post-discharge suicide rates is promoting early outpatient follow-up after hospital discharge. Early outpatient visits help maintain continuity of care, prevent rehospitalization, and provide ongoing opportunities to monitor patients’ mental health status, thereby contributing to suicide-risk reduction.910 Several studies have demonstrated that patients who receive outpatient care within 7 or 30 days after discharge exhibit lower suicide rates, compared with those who do not.1112 However, outpatient care after discharge has been shown to be closely associated with patients’ socioeconomic status (SES).1314 Low SES may hinder timely and appropriate outpatient follow-up by limiting access to healthcare, disrupting treatment continuity, and increasing exposure to stigma. These structural barriers can ultimately contribute to a heightened risk of suicide.

The relationship between SES and suicide in individuals with schizophrenia is complex and not fully understood. While some studies have reported a significant association between low SES and increased suicide risk.1415 Others have suggested that individuals with higher SES may also exhibit elevated suicide rates.1617 According to Chen et al.,18 the mortality rate among individuals with schizophrenia was 3.9 per 1,000 person-years in the low-income group, whereas it was 2.9 per 1,000 person-years among those with high income levels. However, in a Swedish study on individuals with schizophrenia, having a secondary-school or higher level of education was associated with a significantly increased risk of suicide (with an odds ratio of 2.9), compared with that among people with only primary education.17 Accordingly, this study aims to analyze differences in suicide rates among individuals with schizophrenia, based on SES, focusing on the post-discharge period. Further, it seeks to examine how SES influences suicide risk through the mediating role of outpatient service utilization following hospital discharge.

METHODS

Data source and study population

This study obtained data from the National Health Insurance Claims Database (NHICD), which is maintained by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA). Since establishing universal health coverage in 1987, South Korea has mandated enrollment in the National Health Insurance (NHI) system for all citizens. The HIRA reviews medical claims submitted by healthcare providers and stores the finalized data in the NHICD. The NHICD contains comprehensive information on all healthcare services provided and patients’ characteristics such as age, sex, diagnoses, type of health insurance (HI), procedures, and cost.19 To obtain information on causes of death, the NHICD was merged with the National Death and Cause Database (NDCD), maintained by Statistics Korea, utilizing unique patient identifiers for data linkage.

The study population included all individuals aged 18 years or older who were newly hospitalized with a primary diagnosis of schizophrenia (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] codes F20.x–F29.x) in January 1, 2017–December 31, 2018. Patients were considered newly admitted if they had no record of hospitalization during the preceding three years. Post-discharge outpatient visits were treated as a key mediating factor in examining the association between SES and suicide risk. Accordingly, patients who died from any cause on the day of discharge or within seven days thereafter were excluded from the study population, as they would not have had the opportunity to receive the post-discharge outpatient follow-up that is central to this mediation analysis. Consequently, the final cohort consisted of 17,565 patients.

Outcome measure and variables

The outcome of interest in this study was death by suicide following discharge from psychiatric inpatient care. Suicide cases were identified using the NDCD, based on ICD-10 codes X60–X84 as the underlying cause of death. Patients were followed until December 31, 2021, for the occurrence of suicide.

HI type, classified into HI and medical aid (MA), was considered a proxy measure of individual-level SES. South Korea mandates universal enrollment in the NHI system, which covers approximately 97% of the population; the remaining 3% comprises beneficiaries of the MA program, a public assistance system designed to protect socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals. Control variables included age, sex, receipt of outpatient care within seven days after discharge, and comorbid conditions such as ischemic heart disease (ICD-10: I20.x–I25.x), cerebrovascular disease (ICD-10: I60.x–I69.x), cancer (ICD-10: C00.x–C97.x), and alcohol use disorder (ICD-10: F10.x), which are known to be associated with suicide risk.2021 These comorbid conditions were measured by following healthcare service for the previous three years based on the index admission, which was defined as the first admission during the study period. Receipt of outpatient care within seven days after discharge was defined as having at least one outpatient claim within this period with a primary or secondary diagnosis of schizophrenia (ICD-10: F20.x–F29.x).

Statistical analysis

To analyze patients’ baseline characteristics by HI type, we conducted descriptive statistics, including mean and frequency analyses. The t tests and χ2 tests were performed to compare the differences between HI type and patients’ general characteristics as well as comorbidities. Using hierarchical modeling, in model 1, Cox’s proportional hazards model was performed adjusting for sex and age. In model 2, comorbidities were additionally included in the model, while, in model 3, receipt of outpatient care within seven days after discharge was added. At each step, adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, while the association between insurance type and suicide risk was examined. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Kongju National University (KNU_IRB_2025_073). The need for informed consent was waived because anonymized data were used.

DISCUSSION

This population-based cohort study investigated the association between individual-level SES, as proxied by HI type, and suicide risk among individuals with schizophrenia following hospital discharge. The results demonstrated that MA beneficiaries—representing the lower SES cohort—exhibited a significantly higher risk of suicide, compared with HI beneficiaries, even after adjusting for demographic factors, comorbidities, and early outpatient follow-up. These results highlight persistent disparities in suicide among individuals with schizophrenia, despite Korea’s universal health coverage system.

Individuals with schizophrenia who received outpatient follow-up care within seven days post-discharge exhibited a 13.7% lower risk of suicide. This finding reinforces existing evidence indicating the importance of continuity of care during the high-risk transition period immediately following hospitalization.111222 However, despite the benefits of outpatient follow-up, disparities in access to outpatient care were evident. Although MA beneficiaries face fewer financial barriers to healthcare access, compared with HI beneficiaries,23 their early outpatient care rate (26.6%) was significantly lower than that of HI beneficiaries (41.7%). Barriers such as low awareness of the need for continuous care and systemic inadequacies in the delivery of community-based mental health services may disproportionately affect MA beneficiaries, thereby making it difficult for them to receive timely follow-up care.102425 These findings suggest that inequities in healthcare utilization, even in the early period following hospital discharge, may mediate the association between SES and suicide risk.

The elevated suicide risk in the MA group remained statistically significant even after accounting for outpatient follow-up care and comorbidities, indicating that other factors such as social isolation, hopelessness, and stigma likely contributed to this disparity.26 Previous studies have reported that lower income, unemployment, and limited educational attainment are associated with higher suicide risk among individuals with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders.272829 A United States study reported that Medicare recipients with schizophrenia exhibited a 4.5-fold higher prevalence of suicide, compared with the general population.14 In contrast, a population-based study in Scotland found that the incidence of suicide was notably high among individuals with severe mental illness (SMI) residing in both the most affluent and most deprived areas, suggesting that suicide risk in individuals with SMI may not be confined to lower SES groups.30 Taken together, these studies emphasize that suicide in schizophrenia is a pervasive and critical issue across diverse healthcare systems and socioeconomic contexts, warranting targeted prevention strategies beyond simple economic indicators.

It is important to note that neighborhood deprivation, while related to socioeconomic conditions, represents a multidimensional area-level construct that does not necessarily reflect individual-level income or financial status. Meanwhile, a Swedish study examining the association between neighborhood deprivation and suicide risk found no significant difference in suicide risk among individuals with schizophrenia living in areas of low neighborhood deprivation (HR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.81–1.34), compared with individuals in areas of high neighborhood deprivation.31 This discrepancy may reflect differences in how SES is operationalized, with our study employing an individual-level proxy (insurance type) that may more directly capture personal financial hardship and healthcare access barriers.

Despite the reduced financial burden for MA beneficiaries in South Korea, socioeconomic disparities in suicide risk may persist due to non-economic barriers that limit effective access to mental health care. Individuals with low SES often face challenges such as stigma surrounding psychiatric conditions, limited mental health literacy, inadequate awareness of available services, and insufficient social or family support, all of which can delay or prevent timely engagement with outpatient care. These structural and psychosocial constraints may partly explain why higher suicide risk is observed among low-SES groups, even within a universal healthcare system. Furthermore, although widely adopted in research using NHICD, insurance type is a single-dimensional indicator and cannot capture the broader spectrum of socioeconomic disadvantage including education, employment status, and psychosocial resources that may influence both healthcare access and suicide risk. Future studies should incorporate multidimensional SES measures and relevant psychosocial variables to more comprehensively examine socioeconomic inequalities in post-discharge suicide risk.

Another potential explanation for the socioeconomic disparities observed in this study relates to structural differences in reimbursement between MA beneficiaries and HI enrollees in South Korea. Although MA patients face minimal direct copayments due to government assistance, providers receive substantially lower reimbursement rates for services delivered to MA beneficiaries compared to HI patients. This differential payment structure may create financial disincentives for hospitals and outpatient clinics, potentially limiting the availability of psychiatric beds, constraining admission decisions, or reducing the intensity and continuity of outpatient follow-up for MA patients. Such systemic barriers could inadvertently contribute to poorer care coordination, delayed treatment, and ultimately a higher risk of adverse outcomes, including suicide, among individuals with low SES.

The significant association between cancer comorbidity and suicide risk in this population further supports the theory of compounding vulnerability. Individuals with dual burdens of mental illness and life-threatening physical conditions may experience intensified hopelessness, treatment fatigue, and diminished social support, thereby increasing their risk for suicide. These results underscore the importance of integrated care approaches that address both psychiatric and physical health needs. Although no difference in suicide risk was observed between schizophrenia patients with and without comorbid alcohol use disorder in the present study, alcohol use disorder is widely acknowledged as a significant contributor to elevated suicide risk in patients with schizophrenia.32 In a meta-analysis reported by Mulligan et al.,21 alcohol use was associated with suicide completion (HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.00–1.74) in individuals with schizophrenia, and suggested that clinicians should be encouraged to routinely assess patients’ alcohol use.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, HI type as a proxy for SES may not fully capture the complexity of socioeconomic disadvantage. Although MA beneficiaries face minimal direct financial burden, they may experience structural disadvantages due to differential reimbursement and associated discrimination, which can affect the availability, quality, and continuity of psychiatric care. At the same time, individuals in the lowest SES strata within the HI system may paradoxically encounter more limited access to medical services than MA patients. Because insurance type reflects only one component of socioeconomic vulnerability, our study may not fully capture broader structural and psychosocial determinants of suicide risk. Future research incorporating multidimensional SES indicators and detailed measures of healthcare access is needed to more comprehensively interpret these disparities. Second, this study lacked data on important psychosocial variables such as family support and medication adherence, which have been associated with suicide. Future research should incorporate more granular SES indicators and psychosocial variables to further elucidate the complex mechanisms underlying these disparities. Third, the proportion of MA beneficiaries is markedly higher among individuals with SMI in South Korea; thus, the relatively large share of MA patients in this cohort likely reflects the socioeconomic distribution of psychiatric inpatients rather than misclassification. Forth, as the NHICD is a secondary data source intended for claims and reimbursement, diagnostic validity of schizophrenia must be verified. However, the validation of diagnoses was not feasible in the present study due to the use of encrypted patient and hospital identifiers. Although the consistency of diagnosis between electronic medical records of hospitals and the NHICD was high for mental illness,33 the possibility of disease misclassification exists. Lastly, early post-discharge outpatient visits are widely recommended as a key intervention to reduce suicide risk after hospital discharge. Although no consensus exists on the optimal timing, shorter intervals (e.g., within 7 days) are emphasized in clinical guidelines.34 Because suicide risk is highest immediately after discharge, adopting a 30-day follow-up window would result in the exclusion of a larger proportion of patients who die within that period, potentially underestimating the true association. Future research should evaluate whether the impact of outpatient care differs by timing.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that individuals with schizophrenia who are MA beneficiaries face significantly higher suicide risks following hospital discharge, despite receiving care within South Korea’s universal health coverage system. This study highlights significant disparities in suicide risk among individuals with schizophrenia, based on SES. While early outpatient follow-up after discharge appears protective, it does not fully offset the increased risk faced by socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals. These findings underscore the need for equity-focused interventions to prevent suicide among vulnerable populations.
