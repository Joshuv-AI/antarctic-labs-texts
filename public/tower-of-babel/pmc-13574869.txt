The effects of coronoid fracture fixation on functional outcomes and complication rates following terrible triad injuries of the elbow
Authors: Tamara Babasiz, Jan Philipp Hockmann, Marc Weber, Nadine Ott, Tim Leschinger, Lars Peter Müller, Valentin Rausch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManagement of the coronoid fracture in terrible triad injuries (TTI) of the elbow remains controversial. We compared outcomes of TTI patients with versus without coronoid fixation.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 105 TTI patients; 59 patients with no fixation (group 1) and 46 patients with coronoid fixation (group 2). Complications, revisions, range of motion (ROM) and functional scores (Mayo Elbow Performance Score [MEPS], Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand [QuickDASH], Subjective Elbow Score [SES]) were assessed. Stratified analyses using t-tests and Fisher's exact tests with Cohen's d were performed according to O'Driscoll (OD) subtype (type 1 tip, type 2 anteromedial facet, and type 3 basal).ResultsBaseline OD distribution differed substantially between groups 1 and 2 (type 1: 79.7% vs. 34.8%, respectively; type 2: 18.6% vs. 58.7%, respectively; P<0.001). Follow-up timing was comparable between the two groups (first, 4.4 vs. 4.1 months; final, 3.0 vs. 2.8 years). In the whole cohort, group 2 patients showed greater flexion (P=0.030), pronation and supination (both P<0.001), and better MEPS (P=0.032), QuickDASH (P=0.027) and SES (P=0.044) than group 1 patients. Stiffness (22.0% vs. 2.2%, P=0.003) and revision surgery rates (30.5% vs. 10.9%, P=0.018) were significantly higher in group 1 than in group 2. Within OD type 2, fixation was associated with significantly greater flexion (P=0.001), pronation (P=0.005), and supination (P<0.001), and lower rates of nonunion (9% vs. 50%, P=0.046) and posttraumatic osteoarthritis (5% vs. 50%, P=0.025) than non-fixation.ConclusionsOD type 2 fractures repaired with coronoid fixation showed greater ROM and lower complication rates than these types of fractures repaired without coronoid fixation. Within OD type 1, coronoid fixation did not show any significant benefits compared to no fixation.Level of evidenceIII, retrospective cohort study.Keywords: Elbow injuries, Terrible triad injury, Coronoid fracture, Postoperative complications, Treatment outcome

INTRODUCTION

The elbow is the second most commonly dislocated joint in adults, with 10%–20% of dislocations associated with fractures [1,2]. Terrible triad injuries (TTI) of the elbow represent a particularly complex and challenging injury pattern, characterized by the combination of elbow dislocation, radial head fracture, and fracture of the coronoid process (CP) [3]. These injuries are often accompanied by significant soft tissue damage, most notably involving the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL). The typical mechanism of injury involves a fall onto an outstretched hand, with the forearm in supination and a valgus force applied to the elbow [4]. The radial head plays a key role in maintaining valgus and external rotational stability, whereas the CP acts as a buttress against varus stress and internal rotation of the forearm [5-7]. Additionally, the CP is essential in resisting varus and posteriorly directed forces, contributing substantially to overall elbow stability [4,8].

The primary goal in the surgical management of TTI is to reestablish joint stability, restore functional range of motion (ROM), and minimize the risk of long-term complications such as stiffness, instability, redislocation and posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Standard surgical approaches typically involve fixation or replacement of the radial head followed by LUCL repair [9]. However, the indication for coronoid fracture fixation and the impact of this fixation on clinical outcomes remain topics of ongoing debate. While some authors advocate for surgical fixation of the CP to improve joint stability and facilitate early mobilization for better functional outcomes regardless of the size of the fragment [10,11], others suggest that smaller fragments may not require fixation if the radial head and LUCL are adequately repaired [12]. Because the O'Driscoll (OD) classification reflects both anatomical location and biomechanical relevance of the coronoid fragment [13], differences in fracture subtype may independently influence postoperative function and complication risk. In practice, the surgeon-determined decision whether to fix the coronoid is often made intraoperatively, based on the residual stability of the ulnohumeral joint after radial head and lateral ligament reconstruction [14]. Despite appropriate surgical treatment, elbow stiffness, defined as loss of extension >30° or flexion <120°, has been reported in up to 27% of patients following treatment for TTI [15,16]. Its etiology is multifactorial and includes heterotopic ossification, soft-tissue injury, prolonged postoperative immobilization and the extent of surgical dissection, in addition to the stability achieved by bony and ligamentous reconstruction [17-19].

Although several studies have emphasized the biomechanical importance of the CP in elbow stability [20,21], there remains a lack of clinical data assessing whether surgical fixation of the CP translates into improved patient outcomes in TTI. Therefore, we investigated postoperative complications, revision rates, ROM, and objective and subjective elbow function scores in two retrospective cohorts of patients with TTI, managed with and without coronoid fixation at a single tertiary center. Because the two cohorts differed substantially at baseline in OD subtype distribution, an expected consequence of treatment selection driven by fracture morphology and intraoperative stability, the principal analysis was stratified by OD subtype, with the aim of clarifying whether the association between coronoid fixation and outcome differed across fracture patterns. The relationship between OD fracture severity and functional outcomes was additionally examined.

METHODS

This study was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Cologne (No. 25-1123). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

A single-center retrospective study was conducted on patients diagnosed with TTI of the elbow who were treated at our institution between January 2020 and July 2024. Inclusion criteria were adult patients aged ≥18 years treated surgically at our institution between January 2020 and July 2024; confirmed terrible triad injury, defined as elbow dislocation with concomitant fracture of both the radial head and the CP, documented on preoperative imaging and confirmed intraoperatively; and availability of first postoperative clinical follow-up with documented ROM. Patients with incomplete operative or clinical records precluding reliable assessment of surgical treatment or outcomes were excluded from the analysis. Patients were considered lost to final follow-up if they could not be reached after repeated contact attempts, declined re-evaluation, or had relocated. Reasons for attrition were recorded.

The study population was divided into two groups based on intraoperative management of the coronoid fracture. Group 1 comprised patients in whom the coronoid fracture was not surgically treated. Group 2 comprised patients who underwent coronoid fracture fixation using one of the following techniques: open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) or suture augmentation. Coronoid fractures were classified according to the OD classification [13], and radial head fractures according to the Mason classification [22,23]. Demographic data (age and sex) and injury-related variables were collected.

Classification of coronoid fractures was performed based on computed tomography (CT) imaging by two independent experienced residents specialized in elbow surgery (TB and JPH). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa. In case of disagreement, a senior elbow surgeon (VR) reviewed the case and rendered the final decision.

The decision to fix or not fix the coronoid fracture was made by two senior elbow surgeons based on a consistent clinical approach. Preoperatively, fracture morphology was assessed on CT imaging (fragment size, displacement, OD subtype) together with the clinical examination findings. Intraoperatively, after radial head and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) reconstruction, elbow stability was routinely tested under fluoroscopy through the full arc of flexion–extension and under varus and valgus stress. Coronoid fixation was performed when (1) the fragment was of sufficient size and displacement to allow stable fixation by ORIF or suture augmentation, or (2) residual instability or incongruity of the ulnohumeral joint was observed on intraoperative fluoroscopic stress testing despite radial head and LCL reconstruction. Coronoid fixation was not performed when the fragment was small, and the elbow was stable and congruent throughout the full ROM after radial head and LCL reconstruction. Within group 2, the choice between ORIF and suture augmentation was based on intraoperative assessment of the fragment: ORIF (screw or plate fixation) was performed for larger, displaced fragments amenable to stable internal fixation, whereas suture augmentation was reserved for small or comminuted fragments not suitable for internal fixation. The same two surgeons performed or supervised all cases.

The timing from injury to surgery varied on an individual basis depending on the condition of the soft tissue and patient factors. The standard surgical approach was a lateral Kocher approach, with an additional medial approach used in selected cases when required for fragment access or medial-sided instability. The radial head was managed individually: ORIF was performed when stable internal fixation appeared feasible, whereas radial head arthroplasty was chosen when the fracture pattern was not amenable to reliable reconstruction. The LUCL was repaired whenever torn, using transosseous sutures or suture anchors. The medial collateral ligament (MCL) was not routinely addressed; MCL repair was performed only in cases of persistent instability after bony reconstruction and LUCL repair.

Clinical outcomes included complication rates and the need for revision surgery. Complications recorded included postoperative stiffness (defined as loss of extension >30° or flexion <120° [15,16]), instability, redislocation, nonunion, and posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Revision surgery was defined as any unplanned reoperation on the affected elbow during the follow-up period. Indications for revision comprised arthrolysis for postoperative stiffness, stabilization procedures for persistent instability or redislocation, refixation or bone grafting for nonunion, and joint-preserving or reconstructive procedures for symptomatic posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Functional outcomes were evaluated by measuring ROM at the first follow-up, specifically flexion, extension, pronation, and supination.

Furthermore, functional outcomes were assessed at final follow-up using the Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS), the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) questionnaire, and the Subjective Elbow Score (SES) [24]. Additionally, statistical analyses were performed to assess whether fracture severity was significantly associated with functional out-comes.

Follow-up time points were defined retrospectively: first follow-up corresponded to the earliest postoperative clinic visit at which ROM was documented, and final follow-up corresponded to the most recent assessment of functional outcome scores (MEPS, QuickDASH, and SES), obtained by telephone interview.

Postoperative protocol included immobilization with a splint only during the first few postoperative days, followed by a hinged elbow brace allowing progressive ROM. Active-assisted mobilization was initiated on the first postoperative day under physiotherapist supervision. Standardized clinical follow-up was performed at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months postoperatively, with additional visits as clinically indicated.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Because groups 1 and 2 differed substantially in baseline OD distribution, the principal analysis was a stratified comparison within three biomechanically homogeneous strata: type 1 (tip, 1.1 + 1.2), type 2 (anteromedial facet, 2.1 + 2.2), and type 3 (basal, 3.1). Within each stratum, continuous outcomes were compared with Student's t-tests and binary outcomes with Fisher's exact tests; Cohen's d and 95% CIs of the mean difference are reported as effect sizes. The type 3 stratum (n=4) was too small for inferential testing and results for this group are reported descriptively. Unadjusted between-group comparisons across the whole cohort are reported descriptively for transparency. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess associations between O’Driscoll fracture severity and functional outcomes. No significant correlations were found for MEPS (Spearman’s ρ=−0.12, P=0.342), QuickDASH (ρ=0.11, P=0.982), or Subjective Elbow Score (ρ=−0.15, P=0.233). Alpha was set to 0.05. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis, without imputation. Patients with missing values for a given outcome were excluded from the respective analysis. The resulting sample sizes are reported for each outcome.

The minimum detectable effect size (Cohen's d at 80% power, P=0.05) was 0.71 for whole-cohort final-follow-up scores (n=32 per group) and 0.56 for ROM (59 in group 1 vs. 46 in group 2). Within OD strata, minimum detectable d rose to 1.01 (type 1 final follow-up) and 1.37 (type 2 final follow-up) for patient-reported outcomes, and 0.81 (type 1 final follow-up) and 1.11 (type 2 final follow-up) for ROM. The range-of-motion findings within OD type 2 achieved high observed power (flexion 0.93, pronation 0.84, supination 0.99), supporting the robustness of these significant results. In contrast, patient-reported outcomes within OD type 2 had low observed power (0.14–0.34) despite medium-to-large observed effect sizes, and all whole-cohort complication endpoints had an observed power below 0.70 (stiffness 0.57, revision 0.35). These endpoints are therefore underpowered, and non-significant findings for them should not be interpreted as evidence of no difference between groups.

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the outcomes of TTI of the elbow based on whether the coronoid fracture was surgically addressed. Stratification by OD subtype revealed a coherent pattern: coronoid fixation showed clear benefits in patients with OD type 2 fractures, where fixation was associated with significantly greater flexion, pronation, supination, and lower rates of nonunion and posttraumatic osteoarthritis, with large effect sizes (d=1.17–1.64), than in patients with this fracture type that did not undergo CP fixation. Within OD type 1 tip fractures, ROM outcomes favored fixation but without statistical significance. The unadjusted between-group differences originally reported were therefore partly driven by the baseline imbalance, but within the biomechanically critical OD type 2 subgroup, the association of CP fixation with improved function and fewer complications remained robust.

Previous studies have consistently shown that the CP plays a crucial role in resisting varus and posteromedial rotatory forces, especially when the LCL complex is compromised [4,25,26]. Several key anatomical structures that contribute to elbow stability attach to the CP. These include the anterior bundle of the MCL, the LCL complex, the anterior joint capsule, and the brachialis muscle. These structures are frequently compromised in complex elbow injuries involving coronoid fractures [27]. Previous research has supported a standardized surgical protocol for TTI, which includes radial head repair or replacement, LCL reconstruction, and coronoid fracture fixation, to restore elbow stability and function [9,11,14]. However, some authors have argued that in OD type 1 and 2 fractures, coronoid fixation may not be necessary if the radial head and LCL complex are adequately addressed and intraoperative stability is restored [12]. In our study, type 2.1 and 2.2 fractures were more frequent in group 2 (58.7%) than group 1 (18.6%), whereas type 1.1 tip fractures, often considered less biomechanically significant, were substantially more common in group 1 than group 2 (44.1% vs. 8.7%, respectively). Groups 1 and 2 differed substantially at baseline in terms of OD subtype distribution (type 2: 18.6% in group 1 vs. 58.7% in group 2; type 1: 79.7% in group 1 vs. 34.8% in group 2, P<0.001). The stratified analysis addressed this directly. The large effect sizes for ROM within OD type 2 patients who received CP fixation are biomechanically consistent with the established role of the anteromedial facet in resisting varus and posteromedial rotatory forces. The absence of significant differences within OD type 1 fractures between patients who received CP fixation versus those who did not, despite consistent directional trends, supports the view of Papatheodorou et al. [12] that smaller tip fragments may not universally require fixation if radial head and LCL integrity are restored. Morrey and An [28] have shown that maintaining at least 50% of the coronoid height is essential for preserving sagittal stability of the humeroulnar joint. The significantly better ROM observed in group 2 than group 1, particularly in pronation, supination, and flexion, may also be related to improved congruity and stability of the proximal radioulnar joint following coronoid reconstruction. This aligns with previous studies that have reported that early mobilization after stable fixation can reduce the incidence of arthrofibrosis and elbow stiffness [17,18]. Sardelli et al. [29] demonstrated that a maximum flexion arc of 130°±7° and a maximum pronation-supination arc of 103°±34° are necessary for sufficient elbow joint function in everyday life. Of note, CP fixation could in principle contribute to stiffness through additional soft-tissue dissection, heterotopic ossification, or hardware impingement. The opposite pattern observed in our cohort is most plausibly explained by the fact that stable coronoid reconstruction permits earlier and more aggressive postoperative mobilization, whereas residual micro-instability in unfixed cases may prompt protective immobilization and contribute to secondary arthrofibrosis. However, because the indication for coronoid fixation was not protocolized but determined intraoperatively, the poorer outcomes in the non-fixation group may equally reflect unmeasured differences in injury severity or surgeon-driven selection rather than residual instability per se. The observed associations are therefore compatible with both mechanisms and cannot be used to distinguish between them.

In addition to higher rates of elbow stiffness and revision surgery, we also observed a greater incidence of postoperative instability, redislocation, and nonunion in the non-fixation group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. These complications are of particular clinical relevance, as they reflect underlying joint instability and impaired osseous healing, both of which are likely consequences of leaving the coronoid fracture untreated. Deficiency of the CP may compromise the elbow’s resistance to subluxation, even when the radial head and lateral ligament complex are addressed [4]. Importantly, intraoperative stability was systematically assessed under fluoroscopy in both groups after radial head and LCL reconstruction (see Methods), and group 1 patients were by definition those deemed intraoperatively stable without coronoid fixation. Within the OD type 2 subgroup, these patients nevertheless showed significantly worse ROM and higher rates of nonunion and posttraumatic osteoarthritis than fixated patients. This pattern suggests that apparent intraoperative stability may not be a sufficient surgical endpoint in OD type 2 anteromedial facet fractures, and that residual micro-instability or biomechanical compromise may persist despite satisfactory fluoroscopic stress testing. This observation is potentially clinically relevant and warrants further investigation in prospective studies [30]. Furthermore, the higher occurrence of nonunion in this group may be attributed to inadequate mechanical stability at the fracture site, which is known to impair fracture healing.

Moreover, in previous studies, it has been shown that stable reconstruction of the CP leads to favorable clinical outcomes, reflected by good to excellent MEPS [14,31]. In accordance, in the present study, patients who underwent coronoid fixation achieved significantly better functional scores at a mean follow-up of 3 years than those who did not undergo fixation. The higher MEPS and SES, along with lower QuickDASH values in the fixation group, indicated not only greater objective elbow performance but also better patient-perceived function and less disability in the fixation group. Supporting this, a previous study that investigated patients with varus posteromedial rotatory instability reported excellent functional outcomes following combined treatment with internal brace augmentation of the collateral ligament and ORIF or reconstruction of the coronoid fragment. In that study, the mean MEPS was 91.2 and the mean QuickDASH score was 11.4, underscoring the importance of coronoid integrity in restoring joint stability and function [32]. While generally favorable outcomes were achieved in both groups in our study, the statistically and clinically meaningful differences observed suggest that coronoid fixation may enhance joint stability, allow earlier and greater ROM, and ultimately improve functional recovery. The clinical meaningfulness of the observed differences deserves explicit consideration. Established MCIDs are approximately 12 points for MEPS and 10 points for QuickDASH. Observed between-group differences in patient-reported scores remained below these thresholds in both the whole cohort and within OD type 2, indicating no clearly decisive clinical difference. In contrast, the ROM differences between group 1 and 2 patients with OD type 2 fractures exceeded the Sardelli thresholds [29] for activities of daily living and are clinically meaningful, as are the differences in nonunion and posttraumatic osteoarthritis between those patients who received fixation and those who did not within this stratum.

Our correlation analysis did not reveal a statistically significant association between fracture severity according to OD classification and functional outcome scores. This finding indicates that the extent of coronoid involvement alone may not be the decisive factor affecting postoperative function. Specifically, within the OD type 1 stratum, patients without coronoid fixation showed a trend toward poorer function, reflected by higher QuickDASH and lower MEPS and SES scores, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. In accordance with previous studies [12], our data suggest that adequately stabilized fractures can allow favorable recovery, whereas even small untreated fractures may compromise biomechanics and increase the risk of complications.

This study has several limitations. The principal limitation of this study is confounding by indication. The decision to fix the coronoid was driven by fracture pattern, resulting in a highly significant baseline imbalance in OD subtype (P<0.001). We addressed this through stratified analysis within biomechanically homogeneous strata, but stratification accounts only for the stratifying variable; Mason radial head classification, sex and soft-tissue injury severity could not be incorporated. Stratum-specific sample sizes were small (e.g., 16 OD type 1 fractures in group 2; 11 OD type 2 fractures in group 1), reducing power for patient-reported outcomes despite medium-to-large effect sizes. The type 3 stratum (n=4) could not be analyzed inferentially. Residual confounding can therefore not be excluded, and findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Second, the sample size was adequate for detecting large effects in high-frequency endpoints (e.g., ROM in OD type 2, observed power 0.84–0.99), but was underpowered for patient-reported outcomes within the stratified subgroups (observed power 0.14–0.34) and for all complication endpoints (observed power ≤0.69); non-significant differences in these outcomes should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of absence of effect. Third, the majority of coronoid fixations were performed using ORIF, with only a few cases treated using suture augmentation, limiting the ability to compare potential differences in outcomes between fixation techniques. Another limitation is the absence of a prospectively defined, written protocol for coronoid fixation. Decisions based on combined preoperative CT-based assessment with intraoperative fluoroscopic stability testing were made by two senior surgeons, but the criteria were not formally codified. The threshold for fixation in borderline cases was therefore surgeon-dependent and cannot be reconstructed retrospectively. In addition, loss to final follow-up was 39.0%, although no systematic differences in baseline characteristics between retained and lost patients were detected. Fixation techniques in group 2 were heterogeneous (plate, screw, suture augmentation), precluding technique-specific comparisons. Further potential confounders such as operative time, rehabilitation compliance, and comorbidities were not available in the anonymized dataset. Moreover, multiple between-group comparisons were performed without correction for multiplicity; individual P-values should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Finally, although validated outcome measures were used, some degree of reporting bias, particularly in patient-reported outcomes, cannot be ruled out.

CONCLUSIONS

In this retrospective cohort, stratification by OD subtype revealed that the benefit of coronoid fixation was concentrated in OD type 2 anteromedial facet fractures, with significantly greater ROM and lower rates of nonunion and posttraumatic osteoarthritis. Within OD type 1 tip fractures, no significant differences were found between patients who received CP fixation and those who did not. Given the retrospective design of the study and the presence of residual confounding, the study findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Prospective controlled studies are needed to confirm stratum-specific effects and refine fixation indications across OD subtypes.
