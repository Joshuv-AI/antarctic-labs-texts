Cohort profile: the Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study: a large longitudinal study of children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment and their caregivers
Authors: Børge Sivertsen, Mari Hysing, Gertrud Sofie Hafstad, Kaia Kjørstad, Viktor Schønning, Vivian Woodfin, Sofie Øvstebø Næss, Skjalg Eirik Vervik, Kristin Stokke, Stian Tobiassen, John Are Jonassen, Leif Edvard Aarø, Aida Babaii, Øystein Vedaa, Anders Dovran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChildhood maltreatment is common and associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, yet few large, well-designed longitudinal studies follow children and adolescents with substantiated maltreatment over time. The Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study was established to recruit and follow children and adolescents, together with their non-offending caregiver, to identify key risk and resilience factors and understand long-term health and functioning.ParticipantsThe cohort invites children and adolescents aged 5–18 years with substantiated maltreatment, including physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, neglect or exposure to domestic violence. Participants attend the Stine Sofie Centre, a national learning and coping centre for families affected by maltreatment in Norway. At intake, both the child/adolescent and the non-offending caregiver are invited to participate. Participants are followed at regular intervals after enrolment, typically at 2-year intervals, and, with consent, survey data can be linked to national health, education, employment and social welfare registries. As of June 2026, 733 caregivers and 325 adolescents aged 12–18 years had completed baseline questionnaires. Recruitment is ongoing and will continue until the cohort reaches approximately 1500 participating children and adolescents.Findings to dateData collection includes information on children and adolescents’ maltreatment type, mental and somatic health, behavioural and neurodevelopmental symptoms, self-harm and suicidality, sleep, social relationships, school functioning, health behaviours and service use. Caregiver data cover mental and physical health, self-harm and suicidality, interpersonal victimisation across the life course, socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, parenting practices, family dynamics and relationship quality and perceived service needs. Published analyses have documented substantial mental health and sleep difficulties, impaired school functioning and high levels of non-suicidal self-harm among children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment. Other studies have shown considerable variation in service use and coordination, and that emotional, relational and practical barriers may hinder disclosure. Analyses of caregiver data have also highlighted both relational strengths and areas of difficulty in parenting, as well as socioeconomic vulnerability among mothers with cumulative exposure to interpersonal violence across childhood and adulthood.Future plansContinued follow-up will enable detailed investigation of long-term outcomes after substantiated childhood maltreatment, including trajectories of mental health, education, work participation and service use. The cohort will be used to identify key risk and protective factors, including intergenerational patterns of adversity and resilience. Planned linkages to national registries will provide comprehensive life-course data, and additional data collection within the cohort through qualitative interviews is intended to offer deeper insight into the lived experiences of children and caregivers. Findings will inform prevention efforts and contribute to improved service delivery and coordination for maltreated children and their families.

Introduction

Childhood maltreatment, including physical, emotional and sexual abuse, neglect and exposure to domestic violence, is a major global public health concern with well-documented long-term consequences for health, development and well-being. A meta-analysis showed high worldwide prevalence of maltreatment,1 and the WHO highlights maltreatment as a pervasive threat to child health and development.2

Childhood maltreatment is consistently associated with elevated risks of mental health problems, behavioural difficulties, impaired social functioning, poorer school outcomes and sleep disturbances among children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment.3–6 Increasing attention is also directed toward the broader family context, as many caregivers, particularly mothers, have their own histories of early-life maltreatment or interpersonal victimisation, which may contribute to intergenerational patterns of vulnerability.7 Understanding how caregiver experiences and family circumstances intersect with child development is, therefore, essential.

Despite substantial evidence, most existing studies rely on retrospective or cross-sectional data. There is a marked lack of prospective, family-based cohorts that follow children and adolescents with substantiated maltreatment, together with their caregivers, over time, using repeated assessments across multiple domains and linkage to administrative registries. Such designs are essential to identify developmental pathways, risk and protective mechanisms, and potential targets for prevention and intervention.8

The Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study was established to address these gaps.9 It is a longitudinal, family-based cohort following children and adolescents exposed to substantiated maltreatment, together with their non-offending caregiver(s). The study includes repeated assessments with multiple informants on child and caregiver health, functioning and life circumstances, with consent-based linkage to national registries covering health, education, employment and welfare. This cohort profile describes the study rationale, recruitment and design, the measures collected and the opportunities for future research on developmental and intergenerational trajectories following childhood maltreatment.

Cohort description

Study governance and funding

The Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study is conducted as an ongoing collaboration between Stine Sofie’s Foundation (SSF) and the Norwegian Institute of Public Health (NIPH), with additional scientific contributions from the University of Bergen and the Norwegian Centre for Violence and Traumatic Stress Studies.

The cohort was established in 2021 and was initially funded and operated by SSF, with support from Gjensidigestiftelsen, during the period 2021–2024. From 2025 to 2028, the continued operation and further development of the cohort are funded through the Triple-S initiative, supported by Sparebankstiftelsen DNB, Gjensidigestiftelsen and Sparebankstiftelsen Sparebanken Sør, all major Norwegian charitable foundations. The funders have no role in the analysis or interpretation of the data, preparation of manuscripts or decisions regarding publication.

Setting and recruitment

The Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study recruits participants through the Stine Sofie Centre (SSC), a national learning and coping centre for children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment. The centre serves as the recruitment arena for the cohort but is not responsible for study governance or data handling.

Families from all regions of Norway are referred to the centre through child welfare services, specialist mental health services, primary care and support organisations. During their stay, families participate in a structured, week-long psychoeducational programme designed to strengthen coping skills and improve family functioning. Participation in the programme is independent of involvement in the research study.

Recruitment to the cohort has taken place through two pathways. The first pathway was historical recruitment, in which families who had attended the SSC between 2016 and 2020 were invited to participate after the cohort had been established. The second and ongoing pathway is centre-based recruitment, which began in 2021 and is integrated into routine SSC procedures. In this pathway, eligible families are invited while attending the centre. Apart from the timing of invitation and consent, recruitment procedures and questionnaires were identical across pathways, and participants are treated as part of a single cohort.

All eligible families are informed about the study by trained staff, and electronic informed consent is obtained from caregivers and from adolescents aged 12 years and older, in line with Norwegian regulations.10 Following approval from the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics, adolescents between the ages of 12 and 16 may themselves consent to research using health information in specifically designated research projects, including studies concerning violence, neglect or other forms of maltreatment by parents, others with parental responsibility or close relations.

Eligibility criteria

To be eligible for participation, the child must have a substantiated history of maltreatment, including physical, emotional or sexual abuse, neglect or exposure to domestic violence, as documented or reported through child welfare services or other services involved with the family. The accompanying caregiver must be a non-offending parent or legal guardian. Foster parents are not considered eligible study informants unless they hold full legal guardianship under Norwegian law. Eligible children must be aged 5 years or older at enrolment, and both children and caregivers must have sufficient proficiency in Norwegian to complete online questionnaires. No other exclusion criteria are applied. The lower age limit aligns with the target population and programme structure of the SSC, which is designed for school-aged children and adolescents with their caregivers. This age range also supports the feasibility of developmentally appropriate survey assessments, including caregiver proxy reports for younger children and child self-report from adolescence.

In this cohort, the term ‘non-offending caregiver’ refers to the accompanying parent or legal guardian who, based on referral information and the centre’s intake procedures, is not identified as responsible for the maltreatment that led to referral. This designation is used for research eligibility and informant selection and does not constitute an independent legal assessment by the research team. In cases involving neglect or complex family circumstances, eligibility is assessed according to whether the accompanying caregiver can provide informed consent and caregiver-reported information, and is not identified as the offending caregiver in the referral context. Children without such an eligible accompanying caregiver are not included in the survey component of the cohort.

Follow-up

Participants are invited to complete follow-up questionnaires approximately every 2 years after baseline completion. For each wave, participants receive an electronic invitation around the scheduled follow-up time, followed by automated reminders and, where needed, telephone follow-up from cohort staff. The questionnaire remains open for a defined completion period of 2 months, with extensions allowed in selected cases when this is necessary to support participation. Age-specific questionnaire modules are assigned according to the participant’s age at the time of invitation or completion, and the exact date of completion and participant age are recorded. This allows analyses to define exposure and outcome measures in relation to the participant’s age at assessment. The repeated prospective design reduces reliance on long-term recall, although self-reported information may still be affected by recall and reporting bias.

Data collection procedures

Data are collected through online questionnaires completed separately by children and caregivers. With informed consent, survey data may be linked to national registries to provide long-term information on health, education, employment and social welfare. All data are collected digitally and stored in the Services for Sensitive Data (TSD) at the University of Oslo, a secure, International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 27001-certified platform for handling sensitive personal data. Access to the TSD platform is strictly regulated, and all data processing complies with national ethical and data protection requirements.

In Norway, linkage between survey data and national registries is enabled by the unique personal identification number assigned to all residents. Linkage is deterministic and does not rely on probabilistic matching or propensity score methods. Registry linkage requires ethical approval, a valid legal basis under Norwegian and European data protection regulations, and approvals or data access agreements from the relevant registry owners and data controllers. Depending on the registry, data are prepared by the registry owner or an authorised linkage unit and transferred to a secure analysis environment. Directly identifying information is handled separately from research variables, and analytical datasets are de-identified or pseudonymised before they are made available to approved researchers. Participant retention is supported through automated reminder systems, flexible completion windows and formats adapted for use on mobile devices. Cohort staff also follow up non-responders by telephone, offering assistance with questionnaire completion if preferred and making several contact attempts in accordance with ethical approval. To encourage continued participation, families are entered into an iPad lottery every 6 months, and caregivers receive a 300 Norwegian krone gift card on completion of every follow-up wave.

What has been measured

Data collection in the Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study covers a broad range of domains relevant to child development, functioning and family context following experiences of maltreatment. Adolescents and caregivers complete separate online questionnaires at baseline and at each follow-up, with parallel items included where appropriate to capture both perspectives. Measures were selected to provide developmentally appropriate, repeated assessments of mental and somatic health, school and daily functioning, maltreatment exposure, family relationships and service use.

To maximise comparability with national reference data, key constructs and instruments were harmonised with major Norwegian population-based studies, including:

The study provides population-level estimates of physical, emotional and sexual abuse, neglect and exposure to domestic violence among Norwegian youth.11

For children, repeated measures include detailed information on maltreatment exposure type, mental health and behavioural symptoms, somatic issues, sleep patterns and sleep problems, self-harm and suicidality, school functioning, peer relationships, social support and help-seeking. Additional questions address perceived safety, exposure to violence in home and peer contexts and experiences with support services. Content is adapted to the developmental stage of the respondent.

Caregivers provide repeated information on their own mental and physical health, sleep, self-harm and suicidality, socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle factors, service use, parenting practices, perceived family functioning and household circumstances. They also report lifetime interpersonal victimisation, including exposure to childhood maltreatment, intimate partner violence and other traumatic events, enabling examination of intergenerational patterns of adversity and resilience. These caregiver-focused domains and instruments correspond to the content summarised in table 2.

Across informants, the study includes a wide range of potential mediators, moderators and contextual factors, including social relationships, coping strategies, daily functioning, parenting dynamics and broader family context. Standardised and validated instruments are used wherever possible to ensure measurement consistency across developmental periods and follow-up waves.

With consent, self-report data can be supplemented with linkage to national health, education, employment and social welfare registries. Registry linkages will be conducted through secure national infrastructures in accordance with Norwegian data protection regulations. These registries, outlined in table 3, enable long-term follow-up of healthcare use, diagnoses, medication, school progression, labour-market participation and welfare receipt. Together, the survey measures and registry linkages create a comprehensive, multi-informant longitudinal resource for examining developmental pathways, service trajectories and intergenerational mechanisms of risk and resilience among children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment and their caregivers.

Findings to date

Using baseline data collected over several years at the SSC, several analyses from the Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study have already been published. The findings demonstrate a wide range of difficulties faced by children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment and their caregivers across mental health, sleep, school functioning, self-harm, disclosure, service engagement and parenting. Collectively, these studies highlight a substantial cumulative burden and underline the importance of multivictimisation, caregiver functioning and system-level factors in shaping developmental outcomes.

Mental health and overall functioning

A comparative study using caregiver-reported and adolescent-reported data showed that children and adolescents exposed to substantiated maltreatment had substantially higher levels of emotional, behavioural and social difficulties than peers in the general population.15 Using the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ16), large effect sizes were observed for total difficulties (d=1.28), emotional problems (d=1.19), hyperactivity problems (d=0.88), peer problems (d=0.86) and the impact score (d=0.93). A moderate-to-large effect size was observed for conduct problems (d=0.68), while the effect size for prosocial behaviour was small (d=−0.22). A dose-response association was also observed between cumulative maltreatment exposure and SDQ total difficulties, with higher exposure associated with greater overall symptom burden.

Sleep and circadian functioning

Sleep has emerged as another major domain of vulnerability. In a matched-control study of adolescents aged 16–19 years, those exposed to maltreatment had longer sleep onset latency than their peers in the general population (1 hour 15 min vs 47 min), longer wake after sleep onset (34 vs 15 min), lower weekday sleep efficiency (77.8% vs 85.0%) and lower weekend sleep efficiency (81.8% vs 88.9%).17 Mental health problems statistically mediated the associations between maltreatment exposure and both sleep onset latency and wake after sleep onset; however, these mediation findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mediation.

School functioning

A study of school functioning among children aged 6–12 years showed consistent challenges across academic and classroom-related domains.18 In this study, based on parent-reported data from 192 children, 15.6% of children were reported to perform very poorly in at least one subject. Approximately 1 in 4 children (27.1%) received special education, and 1 in 10 (10.0%) had high absenteeism. Multivictimisation, defined as exposure to three or more maltreatment types, was associated with poorer school performance (OR 3.15, 95% CI 1.42 to 7.03) and higher school absence (OR 3.31, 95% CI 1.26 to 8.68), but not with special education (OR 1.95, 95% CI 0.99 to 3.85). Children with very poor performance in at least one subject were also more likely to have a decision for special education (OR 19.1, 95% CI 7.16 to 51.15).

Healthcare service use and coordination

A study of caregiver-reported service use found that most families had recent contact with multiple health and support services, including general practitioners, child and adolescent mental health services and school health services.19 In this study of 457 children and adolescents aged 5–18 years, caregivers reported moderate satisfaction with services overall (mean 6.2 out of 10, SD 3.2), but lower satisfaction with service coordination (mean 4.7, SD 3.2). Only 53% of caregivers reported that their child received help according to their needs. Low satisfaction with services, defined as 3 or lower on a 0–10 scale, was associated with fewer appointments during the past year (mean 8.4 vs 12.4, p=0.002), and not receiving needed help was associated with lower satisfaction with service coordination (mean 2.86 vs 6.35, p<0.001).

Self-harm and suicidality among adolescents

Among adolescents aged 12–18 years exposed to maltreatment, levels of non-suicidal self-harm (NSSH) were high.20 In analyses of 308 adolescents aged 12–18 years, 52.8% reported a history of NSSH and 35.8% reported 10 or more episodes. Girls were more likely than boys to report NSSH (59.3% vs 25.0%), although boys reported an earlier age of onset. One in six participants reported familial self-harm or suicide attempts. NSSH prevalence increased with the number of maltreatment types, from 42.4% among those exposed to one type to 61.0% among those exposed to four or more types. Ethnic Norwegian adolescents had higher self-harm prevalence than adolescents with immigrant backgrounds.

Disclosure and barriers to seeking help

A study examining adolescents’ reasons for not disclosing maltreatment exposure found that the most frequently reported barrier was a perceived lack of need to tell anyone.21 The study included 200 adolescents aged 12–18 years. Of these, 82.4% reported that they had an adult they trusted and could disclose abuse to. Sexual abuse by adults and physical abuse by adults in the home were the most frequently disclosed abuse types (81.1% and 80.4%, respectively), while online sexual abuse was the least frequently disclosed, with 67.3% reporting that they had disclosed it. The most common reason for non-disclosure was not feeling a need to disclose, reported by 36.1% of respondents. These findings underscore that non-disclosure often reflects complex cognitive and relational processes rather than the absence of harm and suggest that many adolescents exposed to maltreatment may remain unsupported despite significant needs.

Parenting practices and parent–child relationship

A study of maternal parenting practices and the parent–child relationship among mothers of children aged 5–11 years with maltreatment experiences included 228 mothers from the Triple-S cohort.22 Parenting strategies were assessed using the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire, while the parent–child relationship was assessed using the Child–Parent Relationship Scale. The study found no significant differences between mothers with and without personal maltreatment histories in positive parenting, inconsistent discipline or parent–child closeness. Both groups reported positive parenting strategies and close relationships with their children. However, mothers with personal maltreatment histories reported significantly higher parent–child conflict than mothers without such histories (p=0.009). These findings suggest that, alongside substantial adversity, many caregivers report important relational strengths, while elevated conflict may represent a relevant target for family-centred support.

Caregiver victimisation and socioeconomic vulnerability

A study of lifetime exposure to interpersonal violence and socioeconomic status among mothers of children with substantiated maltreatment histories included 488 mothers from the Triple-S cohort.23 Childhood and adulthood victimisation were both common, and mothers exposed in both childhood and adulthood showed the highest levels of socioeconomic vulnerability. Compared with non-victimised mothers, those exposed in both childhood and adulthood had higher odds of financial hardship (adjusted OR 3.75, 95% CI 2.04 to 6.88) and no paid employment (adjusted OR 3.39, 95% CI 1.89 to 5.99). Educational attainment did not differ significantly across victimisation groups. These findings suggest that caregiver trauma histories and socioeconomic strain are interrelated and may represent important contextual factors when supporting families affected by childhood maltreatment.

Together, these findings document a substantial cumulative burden affecting children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment, and their caregivers, across multiple domains. They underscore the value of long-term, multi-informant, family-based cohort data such as the Triple-S Cohort Study for understanding developmental trajectories, identifying vulnerable subgroups and informing prevention and service development.

Strengths and limitations of this cohort

An important strength of the Triple-S Cohort Study is its prospective, longitudinal design. This design enables the establishment of temporal sequences, examination of stability and change across development, identification of sensitive developmental periods and assessment of how risk and protective factors evolve over time. Compared with retrospective designs, recall bias is substantially reduced. The use of repeated panel data further enhances opportunities to explore potential causal relationships, which is critical for understanding developmental pathways following childhood maltreatment and for informing targeted interventions and services.

Another major strength of the cohort is the inclusion of children and adolescents with substantiated maltreatment, documented through child welfare or related services. This provides a clearly defined exposure and reduces misclassification, a frequent challenge in studies that rely solely on retrospective self-report. The family-based design, with repeated assessments from both children and their non-offending caregivers, enables analyses of developmental trajectories as well as intergenerational patterns of adversity and resilience. The breadth of measurement represents an additional strength, offering detailed, multi-informant information across child, caregiver and family domains.

A further strength is the use of standardised and validated instruments aligned with major Norwegian population-based studies, including UEVO, the Bergen Child Study/youth@hordaland study and MoBa. This harmonisation allows direct comparison with general population samples and substantially strengthens the interpretability and external relevance of findings. In addition, linkage to national health, education, employment and welfare registries provides objective and comprehensive long-term follow-up, supporting analyses of service trajectories, educational attainment and socioeconomic participation that cannot be captured through questionnaire data alone, and helping to reduce attrition-related bias in adult outcomes.

The cohort also has several limitations. Recruitment occurs through a specialised national learning and coping centre, meaning that participants represent children and families who have already been identified by services and referred for structured support. This may limit representativeness, as children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment who never come to the attention of authorities or who receive no formal services are unlikely to be included. Some demographic groups, such as non-Norwegian speakers or families with limited digital access, may also be under-represented at follow-up due to reliance on online questionnaires. Finally, caregiver participation ends when the child turns 18 years, which restricts the ability to examine caregiving dynamics into adulthood. The cohort does not include children younger than 5 years, which is an important limitation because maltreatment is common in infancy and early childhood, and developmental consequences may differ for children exposed at younger ages. In addition, detailed statutory child protection records are not currently linked to the cohort. Such data would enable triangulation of self-reported maltreatment with administrative information on reporting, substantiation, services, placement history and out-of-home care. Where feasible, future linkage to child protection data may help address this limitation, subject to ethical approval, legal basis, data availability and agreements with relevant data controllers. Repeated follow-up reduces reliance on retrospective recall, but the timing of questionnaire completion may vary within each wave. Analyses of age-specific outcomes will therefore need to account for exact age and date of completion. Although several procedures are used to support retention, including electronic reminders, flexible completion procedures, mobile-adapted questionnaires, telephone follow-up and participant incentives, attrition may still introduce bias. Retention may vary by age, family circumstances, symptom burden, digital access and language proficiency. Future longitudinal analyses will examine patterns of non-response and attrition and apply appropriate statistical methods to account for missing data where possible.

Taken together, these strengths and limitations position the Triple-S Cohort Study as a unique, large-scale, prospective and multi-informant resource for studying developmental pathways following childhood maltreatment, while acknowledging the structural constraints inherent in recruitment through specialised services.

Future plans

Ongoing follow-up will enable detailed examination of developmental consequences of substantiated maltreatment from childhood into adulthood, including changes in mental and somatic health, functioning and educational and occupational outcomes. The family-based design provides opportunities to investigate intergenerational patterns of risk and resilience, including how caregiver trauma histories, socioeconomic factors and service use may shape children’s trajectories. Planned linkages with national health, education, employment and welfare registries will allow long-term follow-up through objective indicators across the life course. Additional expansions may include qualitative interviews with adolescents, caregivers and key service providers, aimed at deepening our understanding of service use, service pathways and processes related to disclosure that cannot be fully captured through quantitative measures and registry data alone.

Administrative child protection system data are not currently included among the planned registry linkages. Such data would provide important complementary information on reporting, substantiation, maltreatment type, child protection measures, placement history and out-of-home care, and would allow triangulation of self-reported maltreatment with administrative records. Future linkage to child protection system data is therefore a priority, subject to ethical approval, legal basis, data availability and agreements with relevant data controllers.

Collaboration

The Norwegian Triple-S Cohort Study welcomes collaboration with national and international researchers. The NIPH regulates access to data and requires approval from the appropriate ethical committees and data controllers. Researchers interested in collaboration or data access may contact the study team to discuss project proposals, data availability and conditions for use. All data sharing follows Norwegian data protection regulations, and registry-linked datasets can only be accessed through secure analysis environments.
