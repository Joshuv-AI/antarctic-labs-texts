An assessment of the effect of external window shading on heating and cooling loads in buildings in Iraq
Authors: Bashir Eskander Kareem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
External shading devices are critical for improving the energy performance of buildings, particularly in hot climates. Effective design must allow beneficial solar gains during winter while minimizing heat ingress in summer. This study systematically evaluates overhangs, left fins, and right fins on windows with varying orientations in Erbil, Iraq, using the hourly analysis program to quantify solar radiation reduction and corresponding energy savings. Results indicate that overhangs are most effective for south facing windows, left fins for east facing windows, and right fins for west facing windows, whereas north facing windows have minimal effect from shading. Peak solar radiation occurs in summer, yielding energy savings of 0% for north facing, 19.21% for south facing, 25.19% for east facing, and 16.93% for west facing windows. The study recommends overhangs for south facing, right fins for west facing, and left fins for east facing windows, highlighting the importance of orientation specific shading strategies to reduce cooling loads and enhance overall building energy performance.

Introduction

Energy loss or gain in buildings arises from a combination of external and internal sources. Externally, components such as walls, roofs, windows, and floors significantly influence energy transfer and overall thermal performance. Internally, factors including partitions, ceilings, occupants, lighting, equipment, and ventilation systems also contribute to energy dynamics, affecting both consumption and heat dissipation within the structure1. Understanding these interactions is essential for designing energy-efficient buildings that minimize unnecessary energy loss and optimize energy use. Heat transfer through windows, via radiation, conduction, and convection, plays a critical role in building energy performance. Minimizing unwanted heat transfer enhances energy efficiency and maintains indoor thermal comfort. External shading for windows and skylights plays a vital role in managing heat gain and improving a building’s energy efficiency2. During the summer, effective shading can significantly reduce heat gain from solar radiation, thereby lowering the cooling load. However, during winter, allowing solar radiation to enter is beneficial, as it reduces heating requirements and enhances indoor air quality. Optimal shading design strikes a balance by preventing excessive heat gain in summer while permitting beneficial sunlight and warmth in winter, contributing to year-round energy efficiency3. Outdoor temperature and solar radiation are key factors influencing a building’s heating and cooling loads, affecting energy efficiency and indoor thermal comfort. Solar radiation significantly impacts cooling loads, particularly during the summer months4. Windows and shading systems strongly influence building energy use and occupant comfort. A study in Tehran showed that optimized shading parameters, such as angle and depth, can significantly improve both energy efficiency and visual-thermal comfort5. In hot desert climates, improper window shading increases cooling loads and glare, while integrating daylighting systems can reduce energy use and improve comfort6. In hot climates such as Jordan, large glazed windows without shading increase cooling loads and glare. Experimental and simulation studies have shown that shading devices, particularly diagonal fins and egg-crate systems, can effectively lower indoor temperatures and improve visual comfort compared to unshaded or vertically shaded windows7. In Erbil, July is the warmest month, with an average high temperature of 42.1 °C and an average low of 27.8 °C. In contrast, January experiences the lowest average high temperature of 11.9 °C8. The intense summer heat leads to substantial interior warming, increasing cooling demands and requiring more energy to be expended by air conditioning systems. This highlights the importance of considering solar radiation in energy management strategies to minimize cooling loads and enhance efficiency. This study aims to reduce heating loads in winter by allowing solar radiation to enter the building and blocking it during the summer, taking advantage of the higher solar altitude angle in summer and the lower angle in winter as shown in Fig. 1. By utilizing natural solar heat during colder months, the approach reduces heating demands, while preventing excessive heat gain in warmer months helps lower cooling requirements. This strategy seeks to optimize energy efficiency, enhance indoor thermal comfort, and minimize overall energy consumption throughout the year9.

Experimental studies are time-consuming and costly, making numerical simulations a more efficient approach. In this study, the HAP software is used to estimate heating and cooling loads for various external shading scenarios. The results indicate that the optimal position for using an Iwan is facing south. Additionally, incorporating an Iwan in different climate types temperate & humid, hot & humid, cold & mountainous, and hot & dry can lead to significant reductions in building energy consumption, with savings of 32%, 26%, 14%, and 29%, respectively. The greatest reduction in thermal and cooling loads occurs with the application of an Iwan on the south side of the building. Therefore, the ideal orientation for an Iwan is south-facing, with the most significant benefits in hot and humid climates, followed by temperate and humid, hot and dry, and cold and mountainous climates11. A well-integrated shading device has a significant positive impact on school buildings, often surpassing other sustainable strategies. This research aims to evaluate several architectural shading devices for educational buildings, specifically in classrooms illuminated by natural daylight from one side in semi-arid climates. These devices not only enhance daylight penetration but also improve indoor thermal comfort and reduce energy consumption by minimizing the need for cooling and artificial lighting. The study focuses on identifying the optimal solar shading devices to enhance the daylight performance of school buildings12. Among the four types of window glass materials studied, laterite buildings with grey glass windows were found to be the most energy-efficient, with the least heat gain. The study also revealed that a south orientation is optimal for minimizing heat gain. These findings are valuable for designing energy-efficient residential and commercial buildings across four different climatic zones in India13. Shading is necessary not only during overheated periods but also when conditions are comfortable. Allowing solar gain during comfortable periods can lead to excess heat, causing discomfort. Therefore, the lower limit of comfort is used to determine when shading should be applied to prevent overheating. Commercial buildings in the UAE often require significant energy to maintain thermal comfort, with highly glazed buildings still being constructed despite their environmental impacts. To mitigate these effects, implementing shading techniques can provide a more sustainable urban design, reduce the heat island effect, and minimize energy consumption14. The impact of external shading on heat gain through windows has been studied, with results indicating that overhangs and side fins are more effective than advanced glazing, regardless of the window’s orientation15. A numerical study investigated the effects of window-to-wall area ratio, window position, and external shading on the heating load of south-facing walls. The results demonstrated that horizontal and vertical shading could reduce total building loads by 3% and 1.83%, respectively16. Despite the proven effectiveness of shading devices in reducing solar radiation and building energy loads, there is a lack of comprehensive studies addressing their performance in hot and dry regions such as Iraq. While HAP enables accurate evaluation of shading effectiveness, practical limitations including economic feasibility, material costs, and local construction practices remain underexplored. Therefore, there is a need to systematically investigate external shading devices under different scenarios in hot climates to identify strategies that optimize energy efficiency and indoor thermal comfort for residential buildings while accounting for these practical constraints.

Methodology

This study employed the Hourly Analysis Program (HAP, Carrier™) to calculate heating and cooling loads for individual building zones and to evaluate the performance of external shading devices. HAP is a widely used and validated tool for assessing thermal loads, HVAC system design, and building energy consumption. It performs detailed hour by hour simulations over an entire year (8760 h), applying the ASHRAE endorsed transfer function method to estimate thermal loads and identify peak design conditions. Building heating and cooling demands are influenced by orientation, building type, and temporal factors such as time of day and season, all of which are accounted for in the simulations. For this study, a typical residential building in Erbil, Iraq, was modeled with specified characteristics including window to wall ratio, insulation properties, and occupancy schedules. Three external shading configurations overhangs, left fins, and right fins were applied to windows with varying orientations. HAP provided hourly solar radiation, heating and cooling loads, and energy savings estimates for each shading scenario, supporting the optimization of shading dimensions and orientation specific designs to minimize cooling demand and enhance overall building energy performance.

Initial and boundary conditions

The study focuses on a building located in Erbil, Iraq (latitude 36.1901°N, longitude 43.9930°E), designed with full thermal insulation except for the window. This configuration enables a focused assessment of external shading effects on solar radiation gain through the window throughout the year. The window measures 3.0 m in length and 1.5 m in height and is composed of double-layered glass, each layer 6 mm thick, separated by a 13 mm air gap.

External shading window scenarios

In this study, four external shading scenarios were evaluated. The first three scenarios involved individual shading devices: a left fin, a right fin, and an overhang. The fourth scenario examined the combined effect of all three shading types. The projection length of the shading devices ranged from 0 to 2.5 m to assess the impact of extended shading on solar radiation gain through the window. Results and discussion

The room is designed to study the effect of external shading in reducing solar energy in summer and allowing solar energy to enter the room during the winter. Only a single window is exposed to solar radiation, and the other walls are assumed to be adjacent wall partitions. The window orientation varies for east, west, north, and south to study the effect of shading for each orientation individually. Three types of external shading overhang, left fin, and right fin have been tested for each orientation, and the optimum shading type for each window orientation has been selected. Table 1 shows that shading effectiveness varies significantly with season and orientation. Overhangs are most effective for reducing solar gains on the south orientation, especially in April (45%) and October (29%), while left fins provide substantial reductions on the east orientation, particularly in January (49.4%). Conversely, right fins are most effective for the west orientation, achieving up to 37.9% reduction in January. Combined shading (overhang + fins) consistently delivers the highest overall reductions, with peak effectiveness on the south and east orientations, highlighting the importance of integrated shading strategies for year round performance.

Conclusion

This study examines the impact of external shading devices overhangs, right fins, and left fins on solar radiation through windows across different orientations (south, north, east, and west) in Erbil, Iraq, for the months of January, April, July, and October. The results indicate that overhangs are most effective for south-facing windows, whereas north-facing windows receive minimal solar gain and require no shading. Right fins are effective in reducing summer solar radiation while allowing winter gains for west-facing windows, and left fins perform best for east-facing windows. Shading length is a critical factor for balancing summer shading with winter solar access. The study optimizes shading dimensions and estimates energy savings by quantifying the reduction in solar heat gain achieved with external shading devices.
