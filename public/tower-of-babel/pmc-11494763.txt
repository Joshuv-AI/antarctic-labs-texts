Surgical treatment of cesarean scar pregnancy based on the three-category system: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Ruyue Ma, Shuang Chen, Weihua Xu, Ruirui Zhang, Yu Zheng, Jianing Wang, Lei Zhang, Rujun Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCesarean scar pregnancy (CSP), a distinct form of ectopic pregnancy, presents challenges in effective management. It is categorized into three subtypes according to the location of placental implantation and the thickness of the myometrium at the uterine scar. Nevertheless, the optimal choice of treatment modalities for these subtypes remains largely unexamined.MethodsIn this retrospective analysis, we investigated the cases of 130 patients diagnosed with CSP who underwent diverse treatment approaches, namely ultrasound-guided dilation and curettage (D&C), hysteroscopic surgery alone or in combination with laparoscopic surgery (HCoLC), or uterine artery embolization (UAE) followed by curettage. Clinical data were meticulously retrieved from medical records and follow-up data, and a comparative analysis of relevant indicators was carried out across the different CSP subtypes.ResultsFrom January 2017 to December 2021, 35 patients underwent D&C, 85 underwent HCoLC, and 10 received UAE as a pretreatment. In the D&C group, the success rates for Type I and Type II CSP were 64.29% (18/28) and 14.28% (1/7), respectively. Significant differences were observed between the success and failure groups in terms of gestational sac size and clinical classification. Compared to Type I CSP, Type II CSP exhibited significantly longer surgical durations and higher hospitalization costs (P < 0.05). Three patients classified as Type III underwent simultaneous hysteroscopic evacuation of cesarean scar pregnancy and laparoscopic repair of the cesarean scar defect, achieving a 100% success rate in their initial treatment. HCoLC showed no significant differences in surgical duration and hospitalization costs but had higher success rates and shorter hospital stays compared to the D&C and UAE groups (P < 0.05). The UAE group had significantly longer surgical durations, higher hospitalization costs, and a higher incidence of postoperative complications (P < 0.05). However, these factors did not result in improved surgical success rates.ConclusionThe classification of CSP and the measurement of gestational sac are of crucial importance in determining the most appropriate surgical intervention strategy. For patients diagnosed with Type I CSP, D&C and hysteroscopy are reliable treatment choices. In cases with larger gestational sacs or Type II CSP, hysteroscopy, either alone or in combination with laparoscopy, is regarded as a reliable and effective treatment approach. In patients with type III CSP, lesion excision with uterine repair is the recommended treatment.Keywords: Cesarean scar pregnancy, Hysteroscopy, Laparoscopy, Uterine artery embolization, Uterine scar repair

Introduction

Cesarean scar pregnancy (CSP) is a distinctive form of ectopic pregnancy characterized by the implantation of a fertilized egg within the scar tissue of the uterine wall following a previous cesarean delivery. The incidence of CSP has been steadily rising in recent years, largely due to the global increase in cesarean delivery rates [1]. In China, the prevalence of CSP has notably increased due to policy changes and advancements in diagnosis [2]. Cesarean Scar Pregnancy now accounts for 6.1% of ectopic pregnancies following a previous cesarean delivery [3–5]. As the pregnancy progresses, there are significant risks of severe hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and abnormal placental implantation, which can lead to life-threatening complications. Therefore, timely termination of the pregnancy is essential for the well-being of patients with CSP following a confirmed diagnosis.

Many factors predispose to CSP, including the number of births, number of previous cesarean deliveries, elective versus emergency cesarean sections, in vitro fertilization pregnancies, breech presentation, previous gynecological surgeries, suturing techniques, smoking early in pregnancy, and maternal age greater than 35 years [6, 7]. Early symptoms of CSP may go unnoticed or resemble those of a threatened abortion, including vaginal bleeding and mild lower abdominal discomfort [8]. The etiology of CSP is not completely understood but is believed to involve thinning or absence of basal decidua in the lower uterine scar region due to previous uterine surgery or intervention, which allows the trophoblast cells to invade the myometrium [9].The partial loss of myometrium and the uterine vascular system at the implantation site results in the pregnancy being supplied by deeper and larger arcuate and spiral arteries, exposing the trophoblast to high-pressure blood flow. Additionally, the loss of myometrium at the scar site impairs uterine contractility, leading to excessive blood loss during CSP [10].

The current spectrum of treatment options for CSP is diverse, encompassing expectant management, local administration of methotrexate (MTX), uterine artery embolization (UAE), various surgical interventions, or combinations thereof [11–14]. However, consensus on the optimal therapeutic approach for cesarean scar pregnancy remains elusive. Recently, Shandong University Qilu Hospital has introduced a patient classification system and corresponding direct surgical treatments, yielding promising outcomes [15]. Nevertheless, broader validation through multicenter studies is imperative. Thus, the quest for personalized treatments for CSP persists.

In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis to explore the prognosis and safety of various surgical treatment modalities for CSP and to identify the risk factors associated with treatment failure. Considering the etiology of CSP and the risk of recurrence, we emphasize the importance of repairing uterine scar defects during surgery. Our aim is to provide enhanced clinical guidance for managing this unique form of ectopic pregnancy.

Methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective analysis involving 130 patients who were diagnosed and treated at the Ninth People’s Hospital in Suzhou. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) absence of severe systemic diseases; (2) early pregnancy (less than 14 weeks); (3) absence of life-threatening bleeding before the operation; (4) availability of complete clinical data. The collected data encompassed patient age, gravidity, history of cesarean deliveries, interval since the last cesarean delivery, gestational age, clinical symptoms, diagnostic approaches, dimensions of the gestational sac, serum β-hCG levels, CSP classification, type of surgery, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, hospitalization costs, postoperative complications, treatment outcomes, and other relevant clinical particulars.

Diagnostic methods and criteria

Ultrasonography was the primary diagnostic modality for CSP, based on the following criteria [15]: (1) An empty uterine cavity and cervical canal are observed; (2) A gestational sac is present in the lower anterior uterine wall, which may or may not exhibit fetal cardiac activity; (3) There is a deficiency or thinning of the uterine muscle layer adjacent to the gestational sac and the bladder; (4) Doppler ultrasound reveals high-velocity, low-resistance blood flow around the gestational sac. In certain cases, patients diagnosed with CSP via transvaginal sonography (TVS) also received magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for further evaluation. MRI results were prioritized when categorizing CSP.

CSP types and treatment selection

According to the guidelines of Chinese Medical Association, CSP can be classified into three types based on diagnosis and treatment [16]. This classification is based on the location, shape, and blood flow characteristics of the gestational sac, with particular emphasis on the thickness of the myometrium at the uterine cesarean scar, which has been extensively detailed in prior research [17, 18]. Shandong University Qilu Hospital has developed a clinical classification system for CSP, informed by years of clinical experience [15]. In summary, Type I CSP signifies a scar area with a uterine muscle layer thickness greater than 0.3 centimeters; Type II CSP indicates a scar area with a uterine muscle layer thickness less than or equal to 0.3 centimeters but greater than 0.1 centimeters; Type III CSP denotes a scar area with a uterine muscle layer thickness less than or equal to 0.1 centimeters or muscle layer discontinuity, the presence of masses, conspicuous protrusion of the gestational sac into the bladder, or arteriovenous fistula formation. Currently, there is no universally accepted standard for treating CSP. The choice of treatment method depends on patient preferences, surgical indications and contraindications, as well as the expertise and recommendations of the clinician.

Ultrasound-guided dilation and curettage

The procedure was performed under strict sonographic surveillance. Gentle suction was utilized to meticulously remove ectopic tissue from the cesarean scar until the ultrasound confirmed a clear endometrial line devoid of any residual gestational sac. The retrieval of villous tissue from the aspirate served as an indicator of complete uterine evacuation. Post-evacuation hemorrhage was managed with a spectrum of interventions, including uterotonics, re-aspiration, Foley catheterization, or intrauterine balloon tamponade, UAE, and additional surgical measures when necessary. Patients were considered for discharge if their serum β-hCG levels had dropped by at least 50% from preoperative values within one day post-surgery, and if minimal vaginal bleeding was observed. Serum β-hCG levels were monitored weekly after discharge until they normalized. The efficacy of uterine cleansing was reassessed via ultrasound one month after the procedure.

Hysteroscopic surgery alone or combined with laparoscopic removal of lesions

The operation, performed by an experienced gynecologist, utilized epidural anesthesia for the patient. Standard disinfection was applied to the external genitalia, vagina, and cervix. A probe was used to assess the depth and orientation of the uterine cavity, followed by sequential cervical dilation with dilators. A hysteroscope was then inserted. Under hysteroscopic guidance, the ectopic pregnancy tissue and surrounding blood flow were directly visualized. Significant tissue portions were clamped with oval forceps, followed by negative pressure uterine suction. Subsequently, any remaining ectopic pregnancy tissue was excised with an electric ring down to the superficial muscle layer. Any bleeding sites were promptly cauterized to achieve hemostasis. A comprehensive examination of the uterine cavity was conducted to confirm the absence of residual tissue.

The procedure for laparoscopic uterine scar pregnancy repair closely resembled that of standard laparoscopic surgery. After general anesthesia and routine disinfection, small incisions were made above the umbilicus and on the left and right sides of the lower abdomen. Carbon dioxide (CO2) was introduced into the abdominal cavity to maintain pneumoperitoneum. The bladder peritoneum was opened to expose the bladder-uterine space and facilitate the exposure of the uterine isthmus. Intraoperatively, a hysteroscope with a light source was employed to precisely localize the cesarean scar pregnancy (CSP) lesion. An ultrasonic scalpel was used to completely remove the scarred area, including the pregnancy tissue. The uterine defect was then thoroughly irrigated, trimmed, and sutured. To control bleeding, a total of 6 units of pituitrin (0.2 U/mL) were injected into the myometrium. Due to potential complications such as transient myocardial ischemia, bradycardia, and cardiac arrest, strict monitoring was implemented during administration to ensure patient safety [19–21]. Finally, a hysteroscopic examination confirmed the complete removal of the lesion and proper incision alignment. The resected tissue was sent for pathological examination.

UAE pretreatment combined with curettage

Uterine artery embolization pretreatment was performed by an interventional surgeon. After receiving local anesthesia, a catheter was inserted through a puncture site below the inguinal crease. A Rosch hepatic catheter was used to selectively catheterize the uterine arteries, and gelatin foam sponge particles (900-1,200 μm) were used for embolization until blood flow was substantially obstructed. The catheter sheath was then withdrawn, local pressure was applied for hemostasis, and a pressure dressing was applied before the patient was returned to the ward. Potential complications, including acute pulmonary embolism and postoperative issues such as pelvic pain, fever, and venous thrombosis, were closely monitored [22, 23]. Dilatation and curettage or hysteroscopic examination was scheduled 1–3 days following UAE. Preoperatively, central venous access was established, and resuscitation equipment was prepared. Uterine curettage was conducted by a specialized gynecologist, who meticulously documented the procedure, intraoperative blood loss, and sent the extracted tissue for pathological examination.

Criteria for treatment success and failure

Treatment success criteria included: (1) A decrease in blood β-hCG levels to less than 50% of preoperative levels one day post-surgery and a return to normal nonpregnant levels within two weeks post-surgery. (2) Absence of gestational sac observed in the ultrasonic examination after treatment.

Treatment failure criteria included: (1) Excessive bleeding during or after the operation or difficulty achieving hemostasis, necessitating alternative surgical methods or uterine artery embolization. (2) The presence of an abnormal mass in the lower uterine cavity persisting after surgery, with observed blood flow signals on ultrasound, and the failure of plasma β-hCG levels to show a sustained decrease of at least 50%, mandating supplementary treatment with methotrexate (MTX) or mifepristone, or even necessitating reoperation.

Discharge criteria

Patients were considered for discharge based on: (1) Marked clinical improvement and the cessation of significant vaginal bleeding. (2) A serum β-hCG level decrease of at least 50% from pre-treatment values. (3) An absence of significant postoperative infectious signs, such as fever, local inflammation, or other clinical indications of infection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous data were presented as means ± standard deviation. A two-independent samples t-test was utilized for comparing two groups, while one-way ANOVA was employed for comparing three or more groups. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentages, and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was used for analysis, depending on appropriateness.

Results

The retrospective analysis included 130 CSP patients, with 91 (70%) identified as Type I, 36 (27.7%) as Type II, and 3 (2.3%) as Type III. Of these, 110 cases were treated successfully, while 20 experienced treatment failure. Notably, there were no cases of uterine rupture, hysterectomy, or other severe adverse events in the treatment failure group. Among the 35 patients who underwent D&C, 16 required alternative surgical interventions due to intraoperative or postoperative hemostatic issues or evidence of residual tissue on postoperative ultrasound. Of the 85 patients who underwent HCoLC, 52 had hysteroscopic treatment alone, and 33 had a combined approach. There were no significant differences in surgical success rates or postoperative β-hCG level reductions between these two groups. Within the HCoLC group, two patients were administered oral MTX due to postoperative ultrasound detection of abnormal masses and inadequate β-hCG level decline. Among the 10 patients who underwent UAE treatment, 8 had ultrasound-guided curettage, 1 had hysteroscopic surgery, and 1 had both hysteroscopic and laparoscopic assessments. In the UAE pretreatment group, two patients required additional surgery due to postoperative ultrasound evidence of abnormal masses. Among those who received UAE pretreatment, 5 developed postoperative fever, which responded to anti-infective treatment, and 2 reported postoperative numbness or intermittent pain in the puncture site’s lower limb. Discussion

The management of CSP presents a complex challenge for both patients and gynecologists, necessitating personalized treatment strategies. Identifying the most suitable treatment approach is crucial for achieving a favorable prognosis. Our study utilized established clinical criteria to classify 130 cases of CSP, highlighting a significant correlation between gestational sac size, CSP clinical classification, and the efficacy of different modalities of treatment. As the gestational sac expands, it contains an increasing number of chorionic villi and demonstrates heightened blood flow, thereby elevating the risk of significant bleeding and residual tissue post-procedure. Emerging evidence suggests that gestational sac size, scar thickness, and CSP type are pivotal risk factors for treatment failure and intraoperative bleeding [17, 24, 25].

This study observed a significantly higher failure rate in patients with type II CSP compared to those with type I CSP. This aligns with Sun et al.‘s prospective study, which involved 228 CSP patients and underscored scar thickness as a key determinant of treatment success or failure [26]. In instances where ultrasound-guided clearance was ineffective in 9 patients, hysteroscopy was conducted to eliminate residual lesions attributed to persistent inhomogeneous masses within the uterine cavity. For individuals with Type I and II CSP, hysteroscopy provides direct visual guidance, enabling the precise removal of pregnancy tissue via a combination of blunt dissection and electrosurgical excision, followed by an assessment for any remaining tissue [17]. A meta-analysis by Gonzalez et al. reported success rates of up to 88% for hysteroscopy alone and up to 96% for scar lesion excision with repair, in contrast to 55% for systemic MTX treatment and 76% for curettage alone [13].

The blood supply to the lower uterine segment is defined by three primary vascular pedicles: the uterine arteries superiorly, the cervical arteries mid-way, and the vaginal arteries inferiorly [27], with the anastomotic system outside the uterus potentially playing a critical role in hemostasis failure during CSP. This underscores the necessity of considering these vascular structures during surgical planning. This evidence suggests that surgical intervention, encompassing hysteroscopy, scar excision, and repair, may be more efficacious than conservative MTX treatment or simple curettage.

For Type II and III CSP, the risk of complications escalates due to the gestational sac’s proximity to the bladder and the rich vascularity at the CSP site. Potential complications include significant hemorrhage, uterine perforation, bladder injury, and persistent trophoblast. The preferred treatment strategies for these types of CSP involve laparoscopic excision, cesarean delivery, or vaginal excision, integrated with scar defect repair. These methods are deemed the most effective for managing such conditions [28, 29]. Especially for type III patients with thinner myometrial thickness at the incision site, conventional curettage may increase the risk of residual tissue and uterine perforation. Laparoscopic surgery is suitable for patients whose lesions extend beyond the uterus, as it enables the simultaneous removal of the lesion and uterine repair. Consequently, all type III cases in this study underwent hysteroscopic and laparoscopic resection and repair of scar tissue, resulting in successful outcomes. Consequently, all type III cases in this study underwent hysteroscopic resection of the lesion and laparoscopic repair of the scar tissue, achieving successful outcomes. A retrospective study indicates that hysteroscopic-assisted laparoscopy is successful in treating Type II CSP beyond 8 weeks of gestation [30]. According to Qin Tang et al., hysteroscopic scar pregnancy removal is a reliable and precise surgical approach, particularly for advanced Type III CSP. This method is minimally invasive, maintains fertility potential, and mitigates the risk of recurrent ectopic pregnancy [31].Furthermore, hysteroscopy-assisted laparoscopy not only resects CSP but also addresses cesarean delivery scar defects. While laparoscopy offers superior visualization and is less invasive than cesarean surgery, it necessitates specialized surgical expertise, and its long-term impact on fertility and CSP recurrence risk is yet to be fully understood. However, if laparoscopic resection of cesarean scar pregnancy and repair of the defect are employed as the primary treatment strategy for all patients, including types I and II, it may result in overtreatment. Consequently, it is postulated that type III cesarean scars could represent an optimal indication for such surgery, necessitating further investigation.

UAE combined with curettage and hysteroscopy is a recommended treatment method for CSP [8, 25, 32]. UAE significantly reduces the risk of significant bleeding by blocking blood flow and reducing trophoblast activity. However, it incurs higher costs and potential complications, such as lower limb numbness, endometrial damage, and ovarian dysfunction [32]. A recent prospective study showed that ultrasound, combined with the blood flow distribution around the gestational sac and gestational weeks, could predict intraoperative blood loss with a sensitivity of 93.8%, thereby minimizing the overuse of UAE [33]. Despite this, the success rate following UAE pretreatment was not significantly different from immediate clearance, with patients undergoing UAE pretreatment experiencing longer hospital stays, increased treatment costs, and a higher incidence of postoperative complications. Consequently, clinical research is now directed toward evaluating the risk of significant bleeding in direct surgery for CSP patients and decreasing the rate of prophylactic preoperative UAE. Some studies advocate for laparoscopic bilateral uterine artery ligation as an alternative to preoperative UAE in patients at high risk of bleeding [30].

In conclusion, timely diagnosis, intervention, and treatment initiation are paramount for managing CSP. Accurate staging prior to treatment is instrumental in selecting the most suitable therapeutic approach. Patients diagnosed with Type I and Type II CSP may not necessitate routine preoperative UAE and often achieve higher success rates with direct surgical procedures. For those with a gestational sac diameter exceeding 3 cm and Type II or higher CSP, treatment under the guidance of hysteroscopy or laparoscopy is advised. When local blood flow is substantial and the risk of intraoperative hemorrhage is elevated, it is imperative to adhere strictly to the established indications, potentially warranting preventive UAE. Ultrasound-monitored uterine clearance offers a less invasive alternative with a more expedited recovery period, particularly for patients with Type I CSP and some Type II CSP cases with smaller gestational sacs.

It is essential to emphasize the significance of classifying CSP in clinical diagnosis and treatment. However, this study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, a relatively small sample size, and the absence of a control group. The current body of research on the impact of CSP on fertility and the risk of recurrent ectopic pregnancy is sparse, and long-term follow-up data are insufficient. Consequently, additional research is imperative to substantiate these findings and to establish the most efficacious and secure treatment strategies for patients with CSP.
