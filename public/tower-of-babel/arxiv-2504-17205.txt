A NEW LOOK AT THE ODDS RATIO IN LOGISTIC REGRESSION
JOSÉ RAÚL MARTÍNEZ

Abstract
. The standard odds ratio of logistic regression is foundational but limited to individual explanatory variables. This work derives a multivariable odds ratio that applies to all the explanatory variables in all their combinations.



1.
Introduction

In a nutshell, this work is about extracting more information from the standard odds ratios of multivariable logistic regression models whose explanatory variables are binary and have no interactions. For such models, the standard odds ratios by themselves provide incomplete information because each applies only to an individual explanatory variable. In this work, I derive a generalized multivariable odds ratio that treats all the variables jointly in all their combinations. The resulting ensemble of odds ratios tells the complete odds-ratio story.

This document’s organization is as follows. Section
2
sets the stage by defining variables, notation, a general binary logistic regression model, and certain mathematical objects that I shall call “events.” Sections
3
–
5
contain the main results. In Section
3
, I derive, analyze, and interpret the known standard odds-ratio formula for individual variables with a view toward recasting it. Recasting is done in Section
4
, in which I derive and validate a new formula for the odds ratio of individual variables. In Section
5
, I derive a formula for a generalized multivariable odds ratio and show that the formula produces the entire ensemble of odds ratios. Section
7
contains conclusions.

3.
The Basic Odds Ratio for Individual Variables

3.1.
Defining the Odds Ratio

The odds ratio statistic is well known, is widely used in many areas in addition to logistic regression, and needs little introduction.
[
3
,
1
,
4
]
Szumilas
[
4
]
explains and defines the odds ratio particularly well:

Definition

(Odds Ratio
[
4
]
)
.

“An odds ratio (OR) is a statistic that quantifies the strength of the association between two events,
A
and
B
. The odds ratio is defined as the ratio of the odds of
A
in the
presence
of
B
and the odds of
A
in the
absence
of
B
.”

.

The “events”
A
and
B
in this definition are unrelated to the events introduced in Section
2.3
on page
2.3
.

3.2.
Interpreting the Odds Ratio

Expressed schematically, the definition is

\text{Odds Ratio}=\frac{\text{Odds }(\textsf{{A}}\text{ and }\textsf{{B}}\text%
{ are present})}{\text{Odds }(\textsf{{A}}\text{ is present and }\textsf{{B}}%
\text{ is absent})}.

(15)

It follows from (
15
) that odds ratio dynamics are determined solely by
B
because
A
does not change.

Events
A
and
B
are either related or not. If not, then
B
is immaterial and the ratio is
equal to one
. If related, their connection takes one of two forms. In one,
B
makes the ratio
greater than one
. In the other,
B
makes the ratio
less than one
. There are several ways for the odds ratio to be greater or less than one.

The odds of outcomes associated with
A
are: (1) enhanced when the odds ratio is greater than one, or (2) diminished when the odds ratio is less than one, or (3) unaffected when the odds ratio is equal to one.

I explain the connection between the logit’s variables
x_{n}
and
A
and
B
on page
3.4
.

3.3.
Deriving the Basic Odds Ratio Formula

In this section, I derive the known formula of the odds ratio of individual explanatory variables to establish a benchmark for new results. I shall call the benchmark odds ratio of individual variables the
basic odds ratio
and denote it
\mathscr{B}(\cdot)
.

To derive the basic odds ratio formula, I shall follow the algebraic approach in the literature (as in
[
1
]
, for example). The labels 0 and 1 will be treated as numbers. I shall derive the basic odds ratio for a single-variable logit—the usual textbook choice—and for a two-variable logit.

To begin, consider the following generic expression for the log-odds-ratio:

log-odds-ratio

\displaystyle=\log(\text{numerator}/\text{denominator}),

(16)

\displaystyle=\log(\text{numerator})-\log(\text{denominator}).

(17)

Thus, the log-odds-ratio is calculated for the variables in the numerator
relative
to the variables in the denominator. Examples in the literature (e.g.,
[
1
]
) invariably use the All-Zeros Event (
13
) in the denominator and I shall follow suit here. (But see the first remark in Section
3.5
.)

The single-variable logit is

\displaystyle\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(X_{1})\big{)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{1}.

(18)

Substituting
x_{1}=1
and
x_{1}=0
in (
18
) yields

\displaystyle\text{Numerator:}\quad\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=1)\big{)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1},

(19)

\displaystyle\text{Denominator:}\quad\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0)\big{)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}.

(20)

Subtracting (
20
) from (
19
) and substituting in (
16
) yields

\displaystyle\log\bigg{(}\frac{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=1)}{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0)}%
\bigg{)}=\beta_{1}.

(21)

Exponentiating (
21
) leads to the basic odds ratio of
x_{1}
, to wit,

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg{(}\log\bigg{(}\frac{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=1)}{\mathscr{O}(%
x_{1}=0)}\bigg{)}\Bigg{)}=\exp(\beta_{1}),

(22)

where “
\exp(\cdot)
” denotes the exponential function
\bm{\mathrm{e}}^{(\cdot)}
. The basic odds ratio (
22
) quantifies the odds ratio when
x_{1}
changes state from 0 to 1.

Turning to coefficient
\beta_{0}
, (
20
) shows that
\beta_{0}
is the

—not the log-odds-
ratio
—when all explanatory variables are equal to zero. Hence,

pertains to

not to an

; its treatment is explained in
[

]
. The numeric value of
\exp(\beta_{0})
is a default output of

Proceeding to the two-variable example, its logit is

\displaystyle\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(X_{2})\big{)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}x_{1}+\beta_{2}x_{2}.

(23)

I first obtain the expression for the basic odds ratio of
x_{1}

, then derive

. The evident pattern of these two derivations leads to the general benchmark basic odds ratio formula, denoted
\mathscr{B}(x_{n})

.

Moving on, observe that variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
combine in four ways, namely,

\displaystyle x_{1}=0,

\displaystyle\quad x_{2}=0,

(24)

\displaystyle x_{1}=0,

\displaystyle\quad x_{2}=1,

(25)

\displaystyle x_{1}=1,

\displaystyle\quad x_{2}=0,

(26)

\displaystyle x_{1}=1,

\displaystyle\quad x_{2}=1.

(27)

To define the numerator for

, notice that (

\displaystyle\text{Numerator:}\quad\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=1,x_{2}=0)\big%

\displaystyle\text{Denominator:}\quad\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0,x_{2}=0)%

(31)

Exponentiating (

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg{(}\log\bigg{(}\frac{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=1,x_{2}=0)}{%

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(x_{1})=\frac{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=1,x_{2}=0)}{\mathscr{O%

\displaystyle\text{Numerator:}\quad\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0,x_{2}=1)\big%

\displaystyle\text{Denominator:}\quad\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0,x_{2}=0)%

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}.

(35)

Subtracting (

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0,x_{2}=1)}{\mathscr{O}(x_{1}=0,x_{2}=0)%

(36)

The interpretation of (

The odds ratio was defined in Section

(page

).
Because

(1)

4.
Recasting the Basic Odds Ratio

4.1.
The Basic Odds Ratio as a Function of Events

In this section, I formulate the basic odds ratio as a function of the events defined in Section
2.3
(page
2.3
) and show that the new formulation yields an odds ratio formula that matches the benchmark basic odds ratio (
38
). The seeds of the events-based formulation are present in the derivation of (
38
) but the methodology used to obtain (
38
) is not conducive for those seeds to germinate. The events-based formulation provides insights about the odds ratio and leads to novel applications.

The following expression formulates the basic odds ratio of
x_{n}
as a function of two events, a
reference event
denoted
E_{r}
, and a
target event
denoted
E_{t}
:

\displaystyle B(x_{n},E_{r},E_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{O}(E_{t})}{\mathscr{O}(E_{r})},\textrm{where }E_{%
r}\neq E_{t},\text{ }n=1,2,\ldots,N,

(39)

and

\displaystyle E_{r}

\displaystyle=\textbf{\text{Reference Event}},

(40)

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\textbf{\text{Target Event}},

(41)

\displaystyle r,t

\displaystyle=\text{Event numbers};r,t=0,1,\ldots,2^{N}-1;r\neq t,

(42)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}(E_{r})

\displaystyle=\text{Odds of the reference event occurring},

(43)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}(E_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{Odds of the target event occurring}.

(44)

I use the symbol “
B(\cdot)
” in (
39
) to distinguish it from (
38
) even though both yield the same result.

Event
E_{r}
is the base event in which
x_{n}
is in state 0, hence the name “reference event.” Event
E_{t}
is named “target event” because it is the event in which
x_{n}
has transitioned to state 1. These names describe the
0\rightarrow 1
state transition (
E_{r}\to E_{t}
transition) of
x_{n}
. Thus, the basic odds ratio
B(\cdot)
is equal to the ratio of the odds of the target event relative to the odds of the reference event. As it should,
B(x_{n},E_{r},E_{t})
in (
39
) has the same general form as (
37
). The conditions
E_{r}\neq E_{t}
and
r\neq t
are the same and preclude
B(x_{n},E_{r},E_{t})
from being
identically
equal to one. Nevertheless, the odds ratio is equal to one when
E_{r}\neq E_{t}
and there is no association between
E_{r}
and
E_{t}
.

To use (
39
) to compute the basic odds ratio, I first rewrite (
6
) as

\displaystyle\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(X_{N})\big{)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{X}_{N},

(45)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}

\displaystyle=[\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{N}]=\text{Vector of }\beta%
\text{ coefficients},

(46)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{N}

\displaystyle=[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}],

(47)

\displaystyle=\text{Vector of numerical explanatory variables},

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{X}_{N}

\displaystyle=\text{Dot product of }\bm{\beta}\text{ and }\bm{X}_{N}.

(48)

The zeros and ones of the set
X_{N}
are treated as numbers in (
47
).

Substituting
E_{r}
and
E_{t}
for
\bm{X}_{N}
in (
45
) yields

\displaystyle\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(E_{r})\big{)}=\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}\text{%
\textbullet}\bm{E}_{r},

(49)

\displaystyle\log\big{(}\mathscr{O}(E_{t})\big{)}=\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}\text{%
\textbullet}\bm{E}_{t}.

(50)

Exponentiating (
49
) and (
50
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}(E_{r})

\displaystyle=\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{E_{r}}),

(51)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}(E_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp(\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{E_{t}}).

(52)

Then substituting (
51
) and (
52
) into (
39
) it follows that

\displaystyle B(x_{n},E_{r},E_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{O}(E_{t})}{\mathscr{O}(E_{r})}=\frac{\exp\big{(}%
\beta_{0}+\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{E}_{t}\big{)}}{\exp\big{(}\beta_{0}+%
\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{E}_{r}\big{)}},

(53)

and simplifying (
53
) yields

\displaystyle B(x_{n},E_{r},E_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{O}(E_{t})}{\mathscr{O}(E_{r})}=\exp\big{(}\beta_{%
0}+(\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{E}_{t})-\beta_{0}-(\bm{\beta}\text{%
\textbullet}\bm{E}_{r})\big{)},

(54)

whence it follows that

\displaystyle B(x_{n},E_{r},E_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp\big{(}\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}(\bm{E}_{t}-\bm{E}_{r})%
\big{)}.

(55)

When
E_{r}=E_{0}
, (
55
) becomes simpler as shown below.

\displaystyle B(x_{n},E_{0},E_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp\big{(}\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}(\bm{E}_{t}-\bm{E}_{0})%
\big{)}=\exp\big{(}\bm{\beta}\text{\textbullet}\bm{E}_{t}\big{)}

(56)

It remains to show that (
55
) reproduces the benchmark odds ratio formula (
38
). As explained next, reproducing the benchmark formula is achieved by selecting the appropriate events

and
E_{t}
.

4.2.
Attributes of the Basic Odds Ratio

The basic odds ratio and its event-based formula (
55
) have the following attributes:

(1)

The basic odds ratio quantifies the effect on the odds induced when the state of one individual variable transitions from 0 to 1 while the state of the other variables remains constant at either 0 or 1.

(2)

The basic odds ratio is context-free: The state of the other variables does not matter—their respective states can be either 0 or 1—as long as the states remain constant in the transition
E_{r}\rightarrow E_{t}
. Thus,
the basic odds ratio has the property that it separates one individual variable from the influence of the other variables.

(3)

The reference and target events
E_{r}
and
E_{t}
differ only in that the state of the individual variable of interest is equal to 0 in
E_{r}
and to 1 in
E_{t}
.

(4)

Several variable combinations can be used to calculate the basic odds ratio. The number of combinations increases with
N
.

(5)

It is both sufficient and convenient to always use the All-Zeros Event
E_{0}
as the reference event.

5.
The Group Odds Ratio: A Generalized

Multivariable Odds Ratio

5.1.
Deriving the Group Odds Ratio Formula

Thus far, I have dealt with the odds ratio of individual explanatory variables—standard fare in logistic regression. Such variables change state one variable at a time: the basic odds ratio (
55
) quantifies the effect of that single-variable state change. But what is the effect when a
subset of several variables
changes state from 0 to 1 simultaneously? To answer this question requires computing a different odds ratio, a
generalized multivariable odds ratio
, which I have named
Group Odds Ratio
. The Group Odds Ratio is calculated using a generalized version of (
55
), namely,

\displaystyle G(S_{k},E_{r},E_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathscr{O}(E_{t})}{\mathscr{O}(E_{r})}=\exp\big{(}\bm{%
\beta}\text{\textbullet}(\bm{E}_{t}-\bm{E}_{r})\big{)}.

(60)

In (
60
), the terms
E_{r},E_{t},\bm{\beta},\bm{E}_{t},\bm{E}_{r}
are the same as in (
55
); the term
S_{k}
will be defined shortly.

As shown in (
60
), the Group Odds Ratio consists of the exponential function whose exponent is a sum of beta coefficients. This is superficially the same as in (
55
), but in (
55
) the sum always lands on a single variable: All but one of the summands cancel out. In contrast, in (
60
) the sum of coefficients may have more than one component. For example, suppose that
N=3
and
\bm{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}]
. Ignoring
S_{k}
for the moment, suppose also that
\bm{E}_{t}-\bm{E}_{r}=[1,0,1]
. Then the Group Odds Ratio is equal to
\exp\big{(}[\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3}]\text{\textbullet}[1,0,1]\big{)}=%
\exp\big{(}\beta_{1}+\beta_{3}\big{)}
. But
\exp\big{(}\beta_{1}+\beta_{3}\big{)}=\exp(\beta_{1})\times\exp(\beta_{3})
, which is the product of the basic odds ratios
\exp(\beta_{1})
and
\exp(\beta_{3})
. Thus, the Group Odds Ratio can be expressed in two equivalent ways: (1) as the exponential function raised to a power that is equal to the sum of a subset of beta coefficients, or (2) as the product of the same subset of basic odds ratios. Either as a sum or as a product, and in keeping with their foundational property,
the basic odds ratios are the building blocks of the Group Odds Ratios
.

The generalization of (
55
) consists of replacing the single variable
x_{n}
in (
55
) with
S_{k}
in (
60
), where
S_{k}
is a set defined as

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=k\text{th subset of the {power set} of the set }X_{N}.

(61)

The
power set
is the
set of all subsets
of the set
X_{N}
and is denoted
\mathcal{P}(X_{N})
.
[
8
,
9
]
The power set
\mathcal{P}(X_{N})
contains
2^{N}
subsets, including the
empty set
, denoted
\emptyset
, and the set
X_{N}
itself. I numbered the subsets
S_{k}
the same way as the events, namely,
k=0,1,\ldots,2^{N}-1
, where
k=0
corresponds to the empty set. For example, the power set of the set
X_{2}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
has
2^{2}=4
subsets numbered
k=0,1,2,3
. The power set itself is
\mathcal{P}(X_{2})=\big{\{}S_{0}=\emptyset,S_{1}=\{x_{1}\},S_{2}=\{x_{2}\},S_{%
3}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\big{\}}
. I will show later that for
k=1,2,\ldots,2^{N}-1
there is a one-to-one correspondence between the subsets
S_{k}
and the target events
E_{t}
.

Changing
x_{n}
in (
55
) to
S_{k}
in (
60
)—a seemingly minor modification—makes all the difference because in (
60
) the subsets
S_{k}
encompass all
2^{N}
combinations of the variables
x_{n}
, thereby enabling calculating the odds ratio when several variables change state from 0 to 1 simultaneously. Therefore, (
60
)
contextualizes
the odd ratios of the multivariate subsets. Because the power set
\mathcal{P}(X_{N})
includes a subset for each of the
N
individual variables, it follows that (
60
)

subsumes
(
55

60
) suffices for computing all the odds ratios, be they basic odds ratios or Group Odds Ratios.

6.
Completing the Story

6.1.
Prologue

Every data set contains a story waiting to be told. The story is often elicited from the data via statistical analysis. For the binary type of data considered in this note, the analysis may use
logistic regression
, which endeavors to model the data and whose output is an
odds ratio
for each explanatory variable: I call such odds ratios
basic odds ratios
. This section shows how basic odds ratios come together to tell the story.

6.2.
Why the Group Odds Ratio?

Although the basic odds ratio is the fundamental statistic of logistic regression, in a multivariable setting basic odds ratios provide only partial information because they lack context. The basic odds ratio is exactly what it is designed for, namely, a context-free statistic for a single variable. The ability to isolate the effect of one variable from the combined effects of all other variables is powerful but presages a weakness: The basic odds ratio acts as if only one variable exists. But the other variables do exist. In fact,
all
the variables are always
realized jointly
, as described by the events (for example, as illustrated in Table
4
). The basic odds ratio’s contextual shortcomings become glaring when the logit model has three or more variables.

In contrast to the basic odds ratio, the Group Odds Ratio provides contextual information for subsets consisting of all the combinations of two or more variables, ending with the All-Ones-Event (see, for example, subsets
S_{3},S_{5},S_{6},S_{7}
in Table
5.1
). Only the All-Ones-Event provides full contextual information. Subsets containing at least two but fewer than
N
variables provide partially-contextual information; the odds ratio for those subsets is context-free with respect to the variables not in the subset. Contextual information increases as the subsets add variables.

Finally, the ensemble of Group Odds Ratios provides a framework for analyzing odds-ratio dynamics as the odds ratios vary across events. The ensemble of Group Odds Ratios (such as displayed in Table

7.
Conclusions

The odds ratio of
individual
explanatory variables—named “basic odds ratio” in this work—is the principal statistic of logistic regression. The basic odds ratio is foundational and purposefully devoid of context. It is a powerful statistic that separates the effect of one explanatory variable from the influence of all other variables.

The basic odds ratio answers the question:
What is the effect on the odds of a desired response occurring when the state of
exactly one
explanatory variable changes from state 0 to state 1 while the respective states of the other variables remain unchanged?

The purposeful lack of context limits the scope of each basic odds ratio to one explanatory variable. But in multivariate logistic regression all the variables always appear jointly in various combinations about which basic odds ratios provide no information. For this reason, basic odds ratios do not tell the full story of the odds ratios.

The complete odds-ratio story answers a more general question that goes beyond the basic odds ratio while encompassing it, namely: What is the effect on the odds of a desired response occurring when the state of a
subset
consisting of more than one explanatory variable changes
simultaneously
from state 0 to state 1 while the respective states of the variables not in the subset remain unchanged?

To answer the preceding question and complete the odds-ratio story requires contextual knowledge obtained by treating all the variables simultaneously. The generalized multivariable odds-ratio statistic named “Group Odds Ratio” derived in this work provides both the requisite contextual information and the computational methodology to calculate it. The complete odds-ratios story is told by the ensemble of Group Odds Ratios, which contains all the odds ratios including the basic odds ratios.
