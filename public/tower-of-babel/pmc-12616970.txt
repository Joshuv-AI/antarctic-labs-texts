Effects and dosage of exercise therapy on functional outcomes in chronic ankle instability: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yanhao Liu, Qipeng Song, Dongmei Wang, Qi Wang, Qingqing Song, Peixin Shen, Deepashini Harithasan, Devinder Kaur Ajit Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effects of exercise therapy on patient-oriented function among individuals with chronic ankle instability (CAI) and analyze the influence of exercise dosage characteristics on these outcomes.MethodsLiterature searches were conducted in multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, EBSCO (MEDLINE, SPORTDiscus), Physiotherapy Evidence Database, CNKI, WANFANG, and VIP, from the earliest available time until May 22, 2025. The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool was used to assess the risk of bias. Meta-analysis, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias analysis were conducted using RevMan 5.3.0 and Stata 14.0 software. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach was applied to evaluate the quality of evidence. Main outcomes were assessed using the Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT), Foot and Ankle Ability Measure-Activities of Daily Living (FAAM-A), Foot and Ankle Ability Measure-Sports (FAAM-S), Foot and Ankle Disability Index-Activities of Daily Living (FADI-A), and Foot and Ankle Disability Index-Sports (FADI-S).ResultsTwenty-six randomized controlled trials (1,032 participants) were included. Exercise therapy significantly improved patient-oriented function compared to non-trial intervention control group, as measured by CAIT (14 studies, MD = 4.59, 95% CI: 4.16–5.03, p < 0.001, I²=45.5%), FAAM-A (13 studies, MD = 7.71, 95% CI: 6.36–9.05, p < 0.001, I²=42.5%), FAAM-S (14 studies, MD = 11.86, 95% CI: 7.86–15.85, p < 0.001, I²=83.4%), FADI-A (2 studies, MD = 8.65, 95% CI: 3.11–14.19, p = 0.002, I²=0%), and FADI-S (3 studies, MD = 13.88, 95% CI: 11.97–15.78, p < 0.001, I²=0%). Subgroup analyses suggested that manual therapy, delivered 1–2 times per week for ≤ 4 weeks, yielded the greatest improvements in CAIT, whereas multimodal training, performed 1–2 times weekly over 5–8 weeks, demonstrated superior effects for FAAM-A and FAAM-S.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis provides very low to moderate certainty evidence that exercise therapy improves patient-oriented function among individuals with CAI compared to comparator controls. Manual therapy of shorter duration may optimize CAIT outcomes, while longer multimodal training programs appear most effective for enhancing FAAM-A and FAAM-S scores.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01404-y.Keywords: Ankle sprain, Exercise rehabilitation, Physical therapy, Self-reported outcome, CAIT, FAAM, FADI

Introduction

Ankle sprains are the most common lower-extremity musculoskeletal injuries [1], particularly in physically active populations [2, 3]. Following an initial ankle sprain, up to 40% of individuals develop chronic ankle instability (CAI) [4–6], a condition characterized by recurrent sprains, episodes of ankle ‘giving way,’ and persistent functional limitations [7–9]. CAI not only has a negative impact on physical activity and quality of life, but also brings long-term health challenges and causes huge economic burdens [10, 11].

Exercise therapy, recognized as the first-line treatment for CAI, is a fundamental rehabilitation approach that focuses on restoring sensorimotor function through active patient participation, assistive devices, and manual therapy techniques [12–14]. Specifically, an appropriate exercise dosage is essential for achieving optimal rehabilitation outcomes, as it influences the physiological adaptations, neuromuscular improvements, and overall functional recovery among individuals with CAI [15, 16]. Although numerous clinical trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of exercise therapy for CAI, considerable variability in exercise prescription persists [17]. The absence of standardized protocols further hampers the identification of best practices, especially given the heterogeneous clinical presentations observed among individuals with CAI.

In recent years, the concept of patient-oriented function has been increasingly emphasized in evidence-based practice [18]. It refers to a patient’s self-reported ability to perform activities of daily living and their perceived physical limitations caused by a health condition [19]. Among individuals with CAI, patient-oriented function is commonly assessed using validated self-reported instruments such as the Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) [20], the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) [21], and the Foot and Ankle Disability Index (FADI) [22]. Studies have shown that individuals with CAI exhibit significantly impaired patient-oriented function compared to healthy controls [19], largely due to deficits in muscle strength, proprioception, and dynamic balance [23].

To address this, the International Ankle Consortium strongly recommends consistently using validated, ankle-specific self-report tools to define inclusion criteria, assess functional status, and evaluate treatment effectiveness in CAI research [8]. As a result, patient-oriented function has become a key indicator of treatment success in many clinical trials. However, the effectiveness of exercise therapy in improving these outcomes remains inconsistent. A review by Kosik et al. (2017) [24] highlighted the wide range of therapeutic interventions used to enhance patient-oriented function in CAI, but the absence of a meta-analysis due to heterogeneity, and the lack of focus on studies with non-trial intervention control group, limited its conclusions and generalizability. Furthermore, no clear consensus exists regarding the optimal dosage parameters of exercise interventions, adding to the complexity of clinical decision-making.

Given these gaps, in this study, we aim to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of exercise therapy on patient-oriented function among individuals with CAI, with a specific focus on trials incorporating non-trial intervention control group. This review will contribute to clarifying the impact of exercise interventions on functional outcomes and support evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice.

Methodology

In this study, we reviewed and reported the findings in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses [25]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251017726) on March 23, 2025.

Data sources and searches

Searches were conducted in the following electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, EBSCO (MEDLINE, SPORTDiscus), Physiotherapy Evidence Database, CNKI, WANFANG, and VIP from the earliest available time until May 22, 2025. The literature data is retrieved in both Chinese and English. A combination of keywords was used to identify relevant articles: (1) population: ankle instability, ankle sprain; (2) intervention: exercise therapy, rehabilitation, balance training, strength training, proprioceptive training, neuromuscular training, manual therapy, home-based exercise; (3) outcomes: patient-oriented function (e.g., FAAM, FADI, CAIT); (4) study design: randomized controlled trial; and (5) Boolean combination: (1) AND (2) AND (3) AND (4). The detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Supplementary Tables 2–11.

Study selection

The reference lists of all included studies were also checked manually to identify other eligible trials. Two reviewers independently (Y.L.&Q.W.) screened search results. Any disagreements were resolved through a third reviewer (D.W.). As some Chinese trials are indexed in multiple international databases, particular care was taken to identify and remove cross-database duplicates during screening to ensure that each eligible study was included only once.

The participants, intervention, comparison, outcome measures, and study design were used to assess the eligibility of studies. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) participants: Individuals with CAI, defined according to the criteria recommended by the International Ankle Consortium [8]. (2) intervention: any type of training programs that consist of therapeutic exercises; (3) comparator controls (non-intervention, placebo/sham, or usual/standard care); (4) outcome measures: patient-oriented functional outcomes; (5) study design: randomized controlled trials. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) trials conducted in animals, in vitro, cadavers, simulators, or prosthesis; (2) if the characteristics of control group are different from that of experimental group or the control group included the healthy/nonaffected ankle in the same participant; (3) Case report studies or descriptive studies, or studies not published as peer-reviewed journal papers, including book chapters and conference abstracts; (4) Articles written in language other than English or Chinese.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers (Y.L.&Q.W.) extracted data of included studies. Descriptive data were extracted, including study characteristics, intervention descriptions, participant characteristics, and outcome measures. The main outcome is patient-oriented functional outcomes. The Meta-Analysis Accelerator tool was used to calculate the mean and SDs of pre- and post-intervention differences [26]. If mean or SD were missing, the reviewers estimated the value using the available data (e.g., p values, confidence interval [CI], SE, median, interquartile range). All disagreements were resolved through a third reviewer (D.W.).

Risk of bias assessment and quality evaluation

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool [27] by 2 independent reviewers (Y.L.&Q.W.), with disagreement resolved by a third reviewer (D.W.). The six domains used for the assessment of each study, included the randomization process, deviation from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result, and overall bias. Overall bias was rated as “low risk” if the study is rated as “low risk” in all domains, “some concerns” if there is at least one domain that is rated as “some concerns” but not “high risk” in any, and as “high risk” if there is at least 1 domain that is rated as “high risk” or multiple domains rated “some concerns”.

The quality of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE evaluation system (GRADE profiler Version 3.6), including study design limitations because of risk of bias, inconsistency of evidence, indirectness of evidence, imprecision of results, and publication bias [28]. Each unsatisfied item decreased the quality of evidence by 1 for each outcome. The quality of evidence for each outcome was consequently graded as high, moderate, low, or very low quality. All disagreements were resolved through a third reviewer.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan, version 5.3; Cochrane Collaboration) and Stata (version 14.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX). All outcomes were treated as continuous variables, and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Effect sizes of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, moderate, and large, respectively. Statistical significance was determined at p ≤ 0.05.

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A fixed-effects model (inverse variance method) was applied when heterogeneity was low (I²<50%), whereas a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) was used for higher heterogeneity (I²≥50%). Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to test the robustness of pooled estimates. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to intervention dosage to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

Publication bias was assessed qualitatively and quantitatively. Funnel plots were examined for asymmetry, and Egger’s regression test was used to detect small-study effects, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Risk of bias

The Cochrane Bias Risk Tool (RoB2) was used to conduct the assessment of risk of bias for 26 included studies. For the randomization process, 21 studies provided a detailed description of their randomization methods and were therefore rated as having a low risk of bias [29–37, 39, 42–44, 46–50, 52–54]. In contrast, five studies did not clearly report their randomization procedures, resulting in a high risk of bias [38, 40, 41, 45, 51]. In the domain of deviations from intended interventions, 18 studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias [30, 31, 33–38, 40, 41, 44, 45, 47–51, 54]. However, 8 studies raised some concerns as they included participants with bilateral CAI, which may influence the outcomes [29, 32, 39, 42, 43, 46, 52, 53]. In the domain of missing outcome data, all studies reported specific outcome data and were therefore assessed as having a low risk of bias [29–54]. In the domain of measurement of the outcome, 23 studies were assessed as having a low risk of bias [29–46, 48–50, 53, 54], 3 studies were deemed to have a high risk of bias because outcome assessors were not blinded [47, 51, 52]. In the domain of selection of the reported result, all included studies were rated as having a low risk of bias [29–54]. In total, 12 studies were judged to have a low risk of bias [30, 31, 33–37, 44, 48–50, 54]. Seven studies raised some concerns [29, 32, 39, 42, 43, 46, 53], primarily related to deviations from the intended interventions. Seven studies were rated as having a high risk of bias [38, 40, 41, 45, 47, 51, 52], with five due to shortcomings in the randomization process [38, 40, 41, 45, 51] and three due to issues in outcome measurement [47, 51, 52]. These results are summarized in Fig. 2.

Meta-analysis

In 15 studies, CAIT was analyzed, involving 610 individuals with CAI (Exercise therapy group: 352; Non-trial intervention control group: 258). The pooled results indicated a significant heterogeneity (p < 0.001, I²=84.2%). Sensitivity analysis was identified in the study by Cruz-Diaz et al. (2020)-b [35] as the primary source of heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 1). After excluding this study, the heterogeneity decreased significantly (p = 0.019, I²=45.5%). Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we synthesized evidence from 26 randomized controlled trials involving 1,032 participants with chronic ankle instability (CAI) to evaluate the effectiveness of exercise therapy on patient-oriented function and to explore the influence of exercise dosage. Overall, exercise interventions produced significant improvements across multiple validated functional outcomes, including CAIT, FAAM-A/S, and FADI-A/S. Subgroup analyses further highlighted potential dosage-response patterns: for CAIT, manual therapy delivered 1–2 times per week for up to 4 weeks demonstrated the most pronounced benefits, whereas for FAAM-A and FAAM-S, multimodal exercise programs delivered 1–2 times per week over 5–8 weeks were associated with greater functional gains. These findings suggest that both the type and dosage of intervention may differentially influence outcomes, highlighting the importance of tailoring rehabilitation protocols to specific functional targets in CAI management.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first review to specifically examine patient-oriented functional outcomes in relation to exercise dosage among individuals with CAI. Unlike previous reviews focused solely on whether exercise therapy is effective for individuals with CAI [10, 24, 55, 56], our study distinguishes between intervention types and evaluates how specific dosage influence patient-oriented outcomes. This approach allows clinicians to better determine which types and doses of exercise are most effective in improving function for individuals with CAI.

Our meta-analysis demonstrated a significant improvement in CAIT scores (MD = 4.59, p < 0.001). However, the certainty of this evidence was rated as moderate, primarily downgraded due to concerns regarding the high risk of bias in many of the included trials. These findings suggest that exercise therapy effectively enhances patient-oriented function among individuals with CAI. Exploratory subgroup analysis indicated that manual therapy delivered 1–2 times per week for up to 4 weeks may provide superior improvements in patient-oriented outcomes. This supports existing evidence that techniques such as joint and soft tissue mobilization can rapidly improve sensorimotor function by enhancing proprioceptive input, correcting joint mechanics, and modulating neuromuscular control [40, 47]. The short duration required to achieve meaningful improvements suggests that manual therapy may be particularly effective in producing early functional gains, making it a valuable component in the initial phase of CAI rehabilitation.

Beyond CAIT outcomes, this meta-analysis demonstrated significant improvements in FAAM-A (MD = 7.71, p < 0.001, moderate certainty) and FAAM-S (MD = 11.86, p < 0.001, very low certainty). The FAAM-A findings were supported by moderate certainty, downgraded primarily due to high risk of bias in most studies. In contrast, the FAAM-S results were rated as very low certainty, reflecting downgrades for high risk of bias, substantial statistical heterogeneity (I²=83.4%), and wide confidence intervals. Sensitivity analyses, which sequentially excluded individual studies, did not meaningfully reduce heterogeneity, indicating persistent variability likely attributable to differences in intervention dosage. Exploratory subgroup analysis further suggested that the type of intervention was the major contributor to this heterogeneity.

Notably, Seyedi et al. (2021) [50] was a key contributor to heterogeneity within the balance training subgroup, likely due to the use of a home-based balance program in contrast to the supervised rehabilitation settings adopted in other studies. This distinction may reflect differences in adherence, supervision quality, or training intensity, which can influence functional outcomes. Importantly, exploratory subgroup analysis indicated that multimodal training programs that integrate balance, strength, proprioception, and functional task components, administered 1–2 times per week for 5–8 weeks, were associated with greater improvements in both FAAM-A and FAAM-S scores. These findings suggest that longer-duration, supervised, and multifaceted interventions may be more effective in addressing the complex functional limitations experienced by individuals with CAI, particularly in sports-related activities, which typically require higher levels of sensorimotor integration and dynamic control.

Our analysis also showed that exercise therapy significantly improved FADI-A (MD = 8.65, p = 0.002) and FADI-S scores (MD = 13.88, p < 0.001), suggesting positive effects on both daily and sport-specific function among individuals with CAI. The certainty of evidence was rated as very low for FADI-A and low for FADI-S. These downgrades primarily reflected the high risk of bias across most contributing studies, the relatively small sample sizes, and the limited number of available trials. For FADI-A, the certainty was further reduced due to imprecision, as indicated by wide confidence intervals. Furthermore, due to the limited number of studies incorporating this outcome measure, we were unable to conduct subgroup analyses to examine the effects of intervention dosage. The small sample size and absence of dosage-response data limit the generalizability of these results. Consequently, further high-quality research utilizing the FADI as a primary outcome is needed to clarify the effectiveness of specific exercise interventions in improving foot and ankle function in this population.

This review highlights the importance of exercise dosage in guiding therapy for individuals with CAI. Exploratory subgroup analysis suggested that manual therapy delivered 1–2 times per week for up to 4 weeks may provide superior improvements in CAIT scores, whereas multimodal training performed 1–2 times weekly over 5–8 weeks appeared more beneficial for FAAM-A and FAAM-S. Notably, the observed improvements exceeded the established minimum clinically important differences (MCID) for CAIT (MD = 4.59; MCID = 3 points) [57] and FAAM-S (MD = 11.86; MCID = 9 points)[21], indicating that these gains are both statistically significant and clinically meaningful. For FAAM-A, the improvement (MD = 7.71) approached but did not fully reach the MCID threshold of 8 points[21], suggesting that while the effect is meaningful, its clinical significance may be borderline.

These results offer preliminary guidance for clinicians when designing individualized treatment plans. However, given the current limitations in the evidence base, these recommendations should be interpreted with caution and used as a reference rather than definitive guidelines for CAI rehabilitation.

Future research should focus on conducting more high-quality randomized controlled trials that adopt a standardized intervention protocol, employ rigorous blinding procedures, ensure adequate sample size, and include long-term follow-up assessments to enhance the certainty of evidence and provide more robust implications for future research and clinical practice.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across intervention types, outcome measures (e.g., FAAM-S), and supervision formats (clinic-based versus home-based), which may have influenced the pooled estimates. Second, many included RCTs were limited by small sample sizes, inadequate blinding, and subgroup analyses that were based on only a few studies, thereby reducing the robustness and reliability of the findings. Third, although the inclusion of Chinese databases enhanced the comprehensiveness of the search, it may have introduced language bias. Fourth, long-term follow-up data were generally lacking, which precludes firm conclusions about the sustainability of exercise-related benefits. Finally, the small number of studies employing FADI outcomes limited the feasibility of subgroup analyses and restricts the generalizability of those findings.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides very low to moderate certainty evidence that exercise therapy improves patient-oriented function among individuals with CAI compared to non-trial intervention control group, as assessed using validated instruments. Manual therapy delivered 1–2 times per week for up to 4 weeks appears most effective for CAIT outcomes, while multimodal training performed 1–2 times weekly over 5–8 weeks yields greater benefits for FAAM-A and FAAM-S. However, the certainty of evidence for FAAM-S was very low, and the limited number of FADI studies constrains meaningful interpretation. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution given the high heterogeneity and restricted evidence base. Further rigorously designed trials are required to confirm these results and to establish optimal exercise dosage parameters for CAI rehabilitation.
