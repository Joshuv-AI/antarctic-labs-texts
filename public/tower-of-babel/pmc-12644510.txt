Foliar application of sodium nitroprusside and chitosan induced antioxidant system and alleviated drought stress in spinach
Authors: Faezeh Khatami, Vahid Niknam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) is a leafy green vegetable known for nutrition and health benefits. However, it is highly susceptible to drought, compromising its growth, yield, and nutritional quality. To alleviate drought stress, application of elicitors is a recognized strategy. This work aimed at exploring the impact of chitosan (CS) and sodium nitroprusside (SNP) on the oxidative status and antioxidant response of spinach. The SNP (0 and 50 µM) and CS (0 and 30 mg L−1) were sprayed on the 3-leaved spinach seedlings under drought (0, 5 and 15% PEG 6000) in two steps, with 2-day intervals. The 21-day-old age seedlings were then harvested to evaluate their morpho-physio-molecular responses by different methods based on spectrophotometry, HPLC and qRT-PCR. The SNP and CS increased the activity of key antioxidant enzymes and the production of osmolytes (glycine betaine and proline), phytohormones, and radical scavengers (tocopherol). Co-application of SNP and CS caused a synergistic increase in tocopherol, NO, abscisic acid, methyl jasmonate and an antagonistic decrease in brassinosteroid, gibberellin, and auxin. The qRT-PCR analysis showed higher expression of glycine betaine biosynthesis genes (CMO and BADH) in the spinach treated with SNP and CS under drought. The glycine betaine content was highly correlated with oxidative status in the spinach. The foliar application of both elicitors mitigated adverse effects of drought and promoted the spinach growth in normal condition. Cross-talk between phytohormones, signaling molecules, and osmolytes regulated the antioxidant response, turned out significant changes in the spinach morphology under each treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25546-8.

Introduction

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) is a leafy green annual dicot herb of Chenopodiaceae family and Chenopodioideae subfamily, native to central and western Asia. It was first cultivated in Persia (Iran) over 2000 years ago, used by Chinese in the 6th century, and introduced to Spain and then Europe in the 11th century1. The leaves are alternate, simple, ovate to triangular, variable in size from 2 to 30 cm long and 1–15 cm broad, with larger leaves at the base of the plant and small leaves higher on the flowering stem2.

Spinach is beneficial to a safe and healthier life. It contains β-carotene (precursor of vitamin A), vitamin K, vitamin C, high folate, calcium, phosphorus, iron, sodium, potassium, and phenolic compounds3. Spinach is well-known as part of a nutritious diet in most regions of the world. It can support immune function, aid the digestive system, reduce the risks of high blood sugar, high blood pressure, and cancer, and prevent heart disease. Spinach is also good for the skin, hair, and eyesight1,3.

Global demand for spinach is growing over the years, mainly because of increased consumer demands for healthier food options4, while spinach yield and quality were decreased because of drought issues. Drought is the most perplexing and disastrous abiotic stress for plants worldwide1,65, particularly in Iran with approximately 88% arid and semi-arid regions5. Drought and water scarcity were also intensified with climate change along with industrialization and population growth over the past decades. There was a global interest in minimizing the adverse effects of drought on high-value crops such as spinach through efficient abiotic stress management practices.

Drought stress induce significant physio-molecular changes in spinach, compromising its growth, yield, and nutritional quality3,6. One of the unavoidable consequences of drought is increased reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and RNS) such as nitrogen monoxide (NO), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide radicals (O2•−), and hydroxyl radicals (OH•) in plant tissues, which affects normal cell functions by damaging lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids7,8, which in turn affects the morphological characteristics such as plant height and root development.

Antioxidant defense and osmoregulation are important mechanisms to increase the plant tolerance to oxidative damage under drought. An increase in the activity of antioxidant enzymes including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POX) and polyphenol Oxidase (PPO) as well as the contents of osmolytes (proline, glycine betaine, and soluble carbohydrates), lipid soluble organics (α-tocopherol and carotenoids), and phytohormones (abscisic acid and jasmonic acid, and NO) has been observed in plants under drought stress7,9. Lipid soluble antioxidants such as α-tocopherol were found as powerful free radical scavengers, shielded biological membranes of Lens culinaris Medik against oxidative damage under drought stress10.

Osmolytes like proline, soluble carbohydrates and glycine betaine are known as osmoregulatory agents11. They play three major roles: A metal chelator, an antioxidative defense molecule and a signaling agent. Glycine betaine was identified as one of the major organic osmolytes that accumulate in a variety of plant species such as sugar beet, spinach, barley, wheat and corn in response to environmental stresses. Meanwhile, tolerant genotypes accumulate more glycine betaine than sensitive genotypes under drought stress. Glycine betaine is an important osmolyte that accumulates in some plant species, while intracellular proline levels have been found to increase during drought stress in a wide range of plant species12. In higher plants, serine is synthesized into glycine betaine in the chloroplasts via choline and betaine aldehyde. Choline is oxidized to betaine via two-step irreversible reactions mediated by choline monooxygenase (CMO) and betaine aldehyde dehydrogenase (BADH)12.

Phytohormones play critical roles in improving the plants’ tolerance to adverse environmental conditions through synergetic or antagonist interactions. The most well-known phytohormones are auxin (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA), salicylic acid (SA), benzoic acid (BA), gibberellin (GA), and methyl jasmonate (MJ)13.

To alleviate drought stress in plant cells, exogenous application of chitosan (CS) and sodium nitroprusside (SNP) were found effective in inducing defense mechanisms14,15. Moreover, both CS and SNP were found to stimulate growth and development of plant species such as tomato, spinach, maize, and mungbean at normal conditions16. SNP, an a exogenous NO donor, is inexpensive compound and available in bulk with the formula Na2[Fe(CN)5NO].2H2O. NO is released from SNP mainly by the photolytic decomposition of pure solutions. NO is a small highly diffusible gas with a diffusion coefficient close to oxygen, and a relatively stable radical that has a central role in signaling pathways throughout the plant growth and development. NO is now recognized as an important signaling molecule involved in the regulation of many physiological functions, including cell proliferation, programmed cell death, expression of defense-related genes, stomatal closure, seed production, root development and tolerance to several different types of stress17. Chitosan is a natural biopolymer derived from chitin that is a primary component of cell walls in fungi and the exoskeletons of arthropods such as crustaceans. Chitin is a linear polysaccharide made up of N-acetyl-d-glucosamine monomers that are connected by β−1,4-glycosidic bonds. Chitosan and its derivatives were widely used in biotechnology due to environmental compatibility, non-toxicity and biodegradability14. Chitosan treatment of plants will create resistance against abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, heat and heavy metal toxicity18. CS treatment alleviates the adverse effects by boosting antioxidant enzyme activities and by increasing water absorption and retention capacities19. CS application was reported to compensate the negative effects of drought through NO and hydrogen peroxide based signaling pathways18,20. It is hypothesized that CS binds to specific cell receptors, activating important secondary messengers such as Ca2+, NO, ROS, H2O2 and phytohormones20. The inhibitory effects of CS on oxidative status depends on the concentration, molecular weight, and the degree of acetylation21.

Although the positive effects of both NO and CS in alleviating drought stress was understood in some plant species, the foliar co-application of them were not comprehensively scrutinized in spinach plants, to the best of our knowledge. The objective of the present research is to explore the synergistic effects of both CS and NO on the antioxidant defense mechanisms in spinach plants subjected to three different levels of drought induced by polyethylene glycol (PEG) treatment. The application of both NO and CS can activate distinct signaling pathways, leading to significant changes in the antioxidant enzymes and radical scavengers. Accordingly, we are interested in gaining insights into the impact of both treatments in separate and in combination at varying intensities of drought.

Results

Growth parameters of the spinach plants

The PEG-induced drought stress reduced both FW and DW of the shoots (Table 1). However, the SNP and CS application increased both. The non-stressed plants (PEG 0%) treated with SNP and CS produced the highest FW (2.6 g) and DW (1.0 g) of spinach shoots, while the lowest was observed in the plants treated with PEG 15%. The drought stress increased the FW and DW of roots and RL/SL. The application of SNP and CS significantly decreased the FW and DW of the spinach roots and RL/SL compared to the untreated plants. The maximum RL/SL ratio was observed in the plants treated with PEG 15%. On the other hand, the control plants (PEG 0%) treated with SNP and CS produced the lowest RL/SL ratio. The 3-week drought treatment decreased the RWC of spinach in relation to control (PEG 0%).

The lowest rate of RWC was observed at the 15% PEG concentration and the highest one in the control. The SNP and CS resulted in significant increase in the RWC under drought, and helped the plants to retain optimum water status. Chitosan treatment was more effective in holding RWC than SNP treatment. Co-application of CS and SNP caused no improvement and slightly less effective than separate application.

Treatment with PEG 15% significantly increased pH in spinach plants. The lowest level of acidity was observed in control plants (PEG 0%). The plants treated with 30 mg L−1 CS and 50 µM SNP showed higher acidity than control plants under the same level of PEG. Co-application of CS and SNP resulted in a significant decrease in pH or the highest acidity (Table 1).

Oxidative status in the spinach plants

The H2O2 and MDA contents showed a significant increase with PEG treatment concentrations (Fig. 1), consistent with significant increase in the antioxidant enzyme activities (Table 2). Both levels of H2O2 and MDA were upregulated significantly, with the highest value in PEG 15%. Both SNP and CS treatments caused a significant decrease in the MDA and H2O2 contents in the spinach plants. The lowest H2O2 and MDA were observed in control plants (PEG 0%) co-treated with SNP and CS (Fig. 1).

The protein contents showed a decreasing trend with an increase in the PEG concentration. There was about 2.5-fold reduction in the protein content at high PEG (15%) compared to the control and PEG 5%, while there was no significant difference between the control and PEG 5%. The plants treated with both SNP and CS had significantly higher protein levels by 12.12 and 11.18 mg g−1FW, respectively. The highest total protein content was observed in the SNP 50 under PEG 0% treatment (Table 2).

The antioxidant enzyme activities raised with PEG treatment (Table 2). The highest antioxidant enzyme activities were observed at PEG 15%, while the lowest activities were observed at PEG 0%. The co-application of the SNP and CS strongly induced all four enzymes in the treated plants under PEG 15% (Table 2). The SNP and CS-treatments led to a significant increase in the enzyme activities compared to the sole PEG-treated and control spinach plants. Accordingly, the highest antioxidant enzyme activity was observed in the plants co-treated with both CS (30 mg L−1) and SNP 50 under PEG 15%. The separate application of SNP and CS did not cause significant differences in the POX and SOD activities in the spinach plants under PEG 0 and 5%.

In addition to enzymatic antioxidant response, we observed significant changes in the non-enzymatic antioxidants including lipid-soluble and water-soluble organics as well as phytohormones. There were significant increases in tocopherol content with PEG and SNP treatments (Fig. 2). The highest increase in the tocopherol content were observed in the plants treated with both SNP and CS under PEG 15%. Comparing the means, it was found that the SNP has been more potent to tocopherol induction than drought. Interestingly, CS treatment had no significant effect on α-tocopherol production (Fig. 2).

The drought treatment significantly increased the contents of osmolytes including proline, soluble carbohydrates, and glycine betaine. The plant treatments with PEG, CS and SNP resulted in an increase in proline so that the maximum induction of proline was observed at the highest PEG 15% under CS and SNP treatment (Fig. 3a). The CS was more potent than SNP in inducing proline.

There was only a significant increase in the soluble carbohydrates of Spinach at PEG 15% (Fig. 3b). At PEG 0% and 5%, the content of soluble carbohydrates decreased upon treatment with 30 mg L−1 CS and 50 µM SNP. The co-application of CS and SNP under drought stress resulted in a significant decrease in carbohydrate content even at high PEG 15% (Fig. 3b).

There were significant increases in glycine betaine level with PEG, SNP and CS treatments (Fig. 3c). The highest increase in the glycine betaine was observed in the plants under PEG 15% and treated with both CS and SNP. Comparing the means, it was found that both CS and SNP had nearly similar effects on triggering glycine betaine production.

There were significant increases in NO accumulation with PEG, SNP and CS treatments (Table 3). The highest increase in the NO contents was observed in the plants under PEG 15% and treated with both CS and SNP. Indeed, the CS and SNP act synergistically in NO induction in the spinach plants at different PEG levels. Despite the role of SNP in providing both endogenous NO induction and exogenous NO supply, the CS was more potent than SNP in NO accumulation (Table 3).

Discussion

Drought stress cause significant changes in both internal structure and external form of the plants. Depending on the species, the intensity of the response and the harmful effects of stress can be different22. Drought is probably the most severe abiotic stress led to tissue dehydration because of imbalance between root water uptake and leaf transpiration. The root is the first plant organ to sense drought because of being in contact with soil to absorb water and nutrients. Upon drought, the root system is usually elongated to improve uptake of water from the soil, whereas the shoot growth is inhibited22. The reduced leaf area was reported as the main growth regulation in spinach plants under drought1. Consistently, we observed an increase in the RDW, RFW, and the RL/SL as well as a decrease in the SDW and SFW (Table 1). These changes in plant morphology may be due to the spinach adaptation to increase water uptake and mineral absorption by roots and to minimize the leaf transpiration by shoots23. These external changes can be attributed to the significant physio-molecular responses in the internal structure.

One of the primary consequences of drought is excessive accumulation of ROS (Fig. 1), which are generated in the cellular compartments because of respiration. Moderate ROS levels are required for basic biological processes, as they are rapidly detoxified by enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants naturally occurring in plants. Overproduction of ROS under drought may damage cellular proteins, DNA, and membrane lipids. Enhanced level of ROS induced membrane lipid peroxidation as determined by MDA (Fig. 1), a marker of the severity of oxidative stress. Damage to the cell membranes will result in ionic imbalance and may be one possible reason for alkalization of the cell sap upon drought stress (Table 1)24. The morphological changes were consistent with the cell sap pH and the RWC, Table 1. The higher root development led to higher assimilation of anions such as nitrates24, accompanied by sap alkalinity (Table 1). Besides, based on acid-growth theory24, the shoot cell wall expansion will negatively be regulated at higher alkalinity.

Spraying CS and SNP on the leaves were known to trigger defensive processes in the plants which lead to lower hydrogen peroxide and consequently less macromolecule degradation such as protein and carbohydrates25. Higher alkalinity and lower hydrogen peroxide led to impaired stomatal opening26 and less water loss. To keep balance, the root system will shrink to minimize water and mineral uptake, accompanied by lower RFW and RDW and no significant changes in the RWC (Table 1).

Lower ROS will increase total protein content (Table 2) and decrease the soluble carbohydrates (Fig. 3) in plants only treated with CS and SNP under no drought. These physiological changes will lead to higher biomass density accompanied by higher SFW and SDW (Table 1). In addition, foliar application of CS in early growth stage enhances key nitrogen metabolism activities and consequently the growth and development of different plants at normal conditions16, which is consistent with higher SFW and SDW of the CS-treated spinach plants under no PEG treatment, Table 1.

Supplementing the plants with low levels of NO can remove peroxyl radical and prevent further oxidative damage by converting the ROS into less reactive forms and also by inducing the expression of antioxidant genes and then increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes27. Thus, NO improve drought tolerance by preventing oxidative stress and eliminating free radicals, stomatal movement, and so retaining plant water28. Similarly, NO mitigated the deleterious effect of drought through enhancing the antioxidant enzyme activities and consequently ROS scavenging in plants29. In plant research, the most commonly deployed NO donors are sodium nitroprusside (SNP), S-nitrosopenicillamine (SNAP), S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) and diethylamine NONOate (DETA/NO). NO donor molecules are used in different ways, which can be mentioned as food supplements in the root growth environment, injection into the leaf apoplast, and spraying on the plant.

There are a number of different enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants that help higher plants to mitigate the deleterious effects of oxidative stress. The most important antioxidant enzymes include SOD, CAT, POX, and PPO. The SOD converts O2•− to H2O2 and O2. The CAT reduces H2O2 to 2 H2O. The POX involved in scavenging H2O2 in chloroplast and enhances plant growth and development. The PPO catalyzes the oxidation of phenolic compounds into highly reactive quinones7. Consistently, we observed a significant increase in these enzymes under oxidative stress imposed by drought, Table 2. The lack of significant change in total proteins at moderate drought, PEG 5% (Table 2), can be attributed to higher drought induced production of functional proteins such as antioxidant enzymes as well as regulatory proteins such as transcription factors30.

Similar to our findings (Table 2), the foliage-applied chitosan enhanced the enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses in different plant species such as spinach31. Chitosan and its derivatives were able to induce tolerance in maize under drought stress by increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes32. Additionally, accumulating evidence also indicates good correlations of NO and CS with other non-enzymatic antioxidants such as phytohormones and signaling molecules which are able to protect the plants through different means.

The alpha-tocopherol, a fat-soluble molecule, is able to protect membrane components from free radical damage by scavenging oxygen radicals and quenching singlet oxygen. It is also involved in neutralizing the noxious impacts of lipid peroxyl radicals and maintaining biological membrane integrity. As a result, an increase in alpha-tocopherol is consistent with accumulation of ROS, Fig. 1. The endogenous or exogenous applications of alpha-tocopherol reduce leaf transpiration, which helps conserve water and maintain normal cellular functions30.

Chitosan treatment of plants can be widely different in terms of method of application, dosage, and chemical structure. In literature, optimal concentration of chitosan varied significantly between different plant species and consistently resulted in different responses16. Consistently, the foliar application of chitosan (30 mg L−1) had no significant effect on the tocopherol content of the plants under drought, while co-application of SNP and chitosan synergistically increased the tocopherol content, Fig. 2. This synergistic effect can be attributed to chitosan-induced endogenous NO production and then higher-order assemblies in drought resistance of the spinach plants20.

Under drought stress, plant osmotic regulation for the reduction of osmotic potential is an important strategy for maintaining cell turgor pressure and stomatal conductance. The decrease of intracellular water, the decrease of cell volume and the increase of cell contents are three ways for osmotic regulation in plants. The most common osmolytes used for osmotic balance include proline, soluble sugars, and glycine betaine30. Osmolytes are low-molecular-weight, relatively non-toxic, and water-soluble organics which help the cells to protect cellular and molecular structures as well30. Moreover, proline has been shown to protect plant cells from oxidative damage through chelating singlet oxygen and hydroxyl radical33. Increased proline content in rice was reported under PEG 30%34. The changes in soluble carbohydrates strongly depends on the plant growth stage, drought intensity and duration22. We observed an increase in osmolytes with an increase in the severity of drought stress (Fig. 3). In agreement with our findings, the proline, soluble carbohydrates, and glycine betaine were significantly increased under drought stress in a variety of pistachio genotypes35. However, the moderate drought (PEG 5%) stress has no significant effect on soluble carbohydrates at early growth stage possibly because of the negative effects of drought on the photosynthetic system, agreed with previous findings36. Indeed, there was a decrease in the soluble sugar contents of the early-stage leaves in Lanzhou lily under drought stress which was attributed to the transfer of soluble sugars to the bulbs and the decrease of chlorophyll content and yield. Similarly, under chitosan and SNP treatment, NO is produced endogenously or supplied exogenously which is an important signaling component of ABA-induced stomatal closure37. Thus, a decrease in the photosynthetic carbon assimilation and soluble sugars is plausible.

The accumulation of glycine betaine has been proven as an osmoprotectant in sunflower, wheat, barley, pepper and Axonopus compressus under drought stress30. The foliar glycine betaine application considerably reduced the negative effects of drought stress by maintaining leaf water due to improved osmotic adjustment and enhanced photosynthesis, primarily due to a greater stomatal conductance and carboxylation efficiency. Furthermore, genetically engineered plants with genes synthesizing GB have been demonstrated to enhance tolerance to abiotic stresses38. The accumulation of glycine betaine in cells is known to protect organisms against drought stresses via positive effects on enzyme and membrane integrity and by osmoregulation. Glycine betaine may also help plants to reduce the adverse effects of various environmental stresses as intracellular signal transduction pathways. The capacity for osmotic adjustment was species-specific. For example, increased proline is a common adaptive response for a wide range of drought-stressed plants, whereas glycine betaine is produced by certain plant species subjected to drought such as sugar beet (Beta vulgaris), spinach (Spinacia oleracea), and barley (Hordeum vulgare)39. Transgenic rice plants that overexpressed a gene for CMO from spinach (Spinacia oleracea) indicated a negligible amount of glycine betaine and increased stress tolerance to drought. Successful GB metabolic engineering has been reported for several plant species such as Arabidopsis, rice, tobacco, Brassica, Persimmon, tomato, maize and potato could increase the resistance to drought stress40. As a result, there were significant increase in CMO and BADH gene expression in spinach plants under drought (Fig. 4). Indeed, betaine is synthesized in a two-step oxidation reaction. First, choline is oxygenated into betaine aldehyde by ferredoxin-dependent choline monooxygenase (CMO) and second, the NAD+-dependent betaine aldehyde dehydrogenase (BADH) catalyze the oxidation of betaine aldehyde into betaine30,40. Upon treatment with CS and SNP, the contents of glycine betaine were changed consistent with the oxidative status of the plants (Figs. 1 and 3c). As a result, the glycine betaine content is highly correlated with the level of hydrogen peroxide in the spinach plants.

Phytohormones with diverse chemical structures are signaling messengers regulating critical aspects of plant growth and development. Plant hormones regulate a wide variety of biological events in cells41. Phytohormones affect the redox state, pH, osmotic potential, signaling and stress-responsive enzymes. Some phytohormones are known to play as chemical positive signals under drought stress. For example, as soil moisture decreases, the content of NO, ABA and ethylene increases30, consistent with our findings (Table 3). ABA is an essential lipophilic signal molecule which is involved in drought stress64. It has been found that ABA synthesized in root cells under drought and increases plant survival by inducing quick responses such as stomatal closure and long-term responses such as extended growth inhibition, osmotic regulation, accumulation of cuticular wax, senescence, abscission and dormancy42. The increase in ABA is aligned with changes in other physiological parameters such as pH. Indeed, under drought stress, the pH of apoplast increase and will induce stomatal widening, while ABA avoids extrusion of H+ into the apoplast and so stop stomatal widening and excessive water loss24, agreed with our observations (Tables 1 and 3).

Jasmonic acid (JA), especially methyl jasmonate (MJ), has been reported to improve drought tolerance by accumulation of ethylene together with abscisic acid. Moreover, JA-mediates production of osmolytes which increase the efficiency of plant water absorption. Similarly, we observed a positive correlation between MJ, ABA, and osmolytes (Table 3; Fig. 3). It has been observed that JA is effective in reducing leaf water content loss through cuticle production and other surface tissues. It has been proven that most plants with enhanced drought resistance have a higher ability to produce JA. Also, JA pretreatment improves drought tolerance of plants by regulating the level of antioxidants. In addition, JA is known to be involved in the stomatal closure together with ABA and independently under drought stress. It was reported that abscisic acid cannot replace JA in closing stomata and in its absence, complete closure does not occur22.

Nitric oxide induces stomatal closure with an increase in the water potential of the guard cells against oxidative stress which in turn leads to the ABA production. Moreover, chitosan treatment was known to stimulate stomatal closure through ABA synthesis, enhancing antioxidant enzymes via NO and hydrogen peroxide signaling pathways, and inducing production of organic acids which are required for the osmotic adjustment, stress signaling, and energy metabolism under stress. As a result, their co-application will possibly contribute to higher-order assemblies in the production of signaling molecules such as ABA and MJ (Table 3).

Gibberellins, auxin, and brassinosteroid pay key roles in plant growth regulation through complex signal transduction pathways43. Both GA and IAA can promote internode elongation by regulating cell division, growth and differentiation which will lead to plant growth and development. Under drought stress, the plant cell expansion will be retarded via different signaling pathways and so there were significant decrease in these phytohormones, Table 4. Chitosan also triggers plant defense through intricate crosstalk with different phytohormones such as NO, ABA, MJ, and GA as well as ROS and antioxidant metabolic pathways44. Accordingly, antagonistic reduction of GA and AUX upon treatment with CS and SNP can be attributed to the activation of defense mechanism in the spinach plants.

Conclusion

Drought, even at low levels, significantly affected the morphological features of the spinach plants. The plants exposed to drought had lower shoot and higher root biomass. These changes can be attributed to the physio-molecular responses of the plant to the oxidative status. Indeed, there was a significant rise in ROS level, which was accompanied by significant changes in the antioxidants, phytohormones, and signaling agents. Foliage applied CS and SNP enhanced the plant tolerance to drought stress and promoted the plant growth to normal culture, possibly because of both enzymatic (CAT, POX, SOD, PPO) and non-enzymatic (proline, GB, soluble sugars, tocopherol) antioxidant induction. Indeed, the cross-talk between phytohormones and signaling molecules caused a synergistic increase in tocopherol, NO, ABA, and MJ and an antagonistic decrease in GA, IAA, BR, H2O2 of the spinach plants treated with both chitosan and SNP.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experimental conditions

Commercial Spinach seeds were purchased from Pakan Bazr-e-Esfahan Company (Esfahan, Iran). The seeds were sterilized with sodium hypochlorite (1%) for 5 min and then washed thoroughly by distilled water. The uniform sterilized seeds were germinated by placing them between two layers of filter paper in Petri dishes containing double distilled water. The dishes were kept in darkness at ambient temperature (25 °C) until the emergence of radicles (~ 72 h). The germinated seedlings were then transferred to pots containing peat and perlite (1:1) in a culture room in which illumination was set at a light intensity of ~ 200 µmol m−2 s−1 under 16/8 h photoperiod and 25/18°C day/night temperature. The plants at the three-leaf stage were exposed to three certain treatments: (1) CS foliar spray (0 and 30 mg l−1); (2) SNP foliar spray (0 and 50 µM); and (3) simultaneous addition of CS and SNP, under irrigation with three different polyethylene glycol (PEG) concentrations (0, 5 and 15%) after 1 d. These treatment levels were set based on literature review and preliminary experimental works. The samples were re-sprayed after 7 days as well. Drought was imposed by PEG treatment for three weeks. The plants aged 21-days old were then harvested in the vegetative growth stage. The harvested plants were washed with double distilled water and then used immediately for further analysis.

Growth parameters

The fresh weights of shoots and roots were weighed and the lengths of shoots and roots were measured. The samples were then dried in a forced air oven at 70 °C for 48 h to determine the dry weights. The turgid weight was estimated by adding fresh shoots (1 g) to 50 ml distilled water for 4 h. The relative water content (RWC) was determined by: RWC (%) = [(FW − DW)/(TW − DW)] ×100, where FW is the fresh weight, DW is the dry weight, and TW is the turgid weight45.

Physiological parameters

pH

The cell sap pH was measured in extracts obtained from 0.5 g macerated leaves in 5 ml double distilled water. After 2 h, the solution pH was measured by a pH meter (Metrohm, Switzerland)46.

Total protein and antioxidant enzyme activities

To measure total protein, 0.1 g fresh seedling was homogenized at 4 °C with a pestle and mortar in 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 6.8). The homogenates were centrifuged at 13,249×g and 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant was kept at − 70 °C and used for enzyme assays and protein determination according to47, using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as standard.

Catalase (CAT) activity

CAT (EC 1.11.1.6) activity was assayed by measuring the initial rate of disappearance of H2O2 according to48. The decrease in absorption was followed for 180 s. The reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), H2O2 (3%), and 10 µl enzyme extract. The CAT activity was expressed as units per mg protein.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD (EC 1.15.1.1) activity was estimated by monitoring the inhibition of photochemical reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium as described by49 in a reaction mixture containing 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 75 µM riboflavin, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 100 µl enzyme extract. The reaction mixture was irradiated for 16 min and the absorbance was read at 560 nm against the non-irradiated blank.

Peroxidase (POX) activity

POX (E.C.1.11.1.7) activity was measured according to the method of50. The reaction mixture contained 2 ml acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 4.8), 0.2 ml H2O2 (3%), 0.1 ml benzidine (40 mM), and 0.1 ml enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance was read at 530 nm.

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity

PPO (EC 1.10.3.1) activity was determined according to the method of51. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.5 ml sodium phosphate buffer (200 mM, pH 6.8), 0.2 ml pyrogallol (20 mM), and 20 µl enzyme extract at 40 °C.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and lipid peroxidation

The H2O2 content was determined according to method of52. The seedling material (0.1 g) was homogenized in an ice bath with 5 ml trichloroacetic acid (TCA) 0.1% w/v. The homogenate was centrifuged at 11,289×g for 15 min. A 0.5 ml aliquot of the supernatant was added to 0.5 ml potassium phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.0) and 1 ml KI (1 M). The supernatant’s absorbance was measured at 390 nm. The H2O2 content was calculated using a standard calibration curve method.

The lipid peroxidation was determined based on MDA content following the method of53. The plant seedling (0.1 g) from leaf was homogenized in 5 ml TCA 0.1% (w/v) and centrifuged at 7840×g for 20 min. To 1 ml aliquot of the supernatant, 4 ml thiobarbituric acid 0.5% in 20% TCA was added. The mixture was heated at 95 °C for 30 min and quickly cooled in an ice bath. After centrifugation at 7840×g for 15 min, the supernatant’s absorbance was read at 532 and subtracted from the value of nonspecific absorption at 600 nm. The MDA concentration was found using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM−1 cm−1.

Measurement of proline and soluble carbohydrates

The proline content was determined by the method of54. The fresh seedling material (0.1 g) was homogenized in 5 ml sulfosalicylic acid 3%. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,249×g for 20 min. The resulting solution (2 ml) was treated with 2 ml acid ninhydrin and 2 ml concentrated glacial acetic acid, shaken vigorously, and boiled at 100 °C for 1 h. The reaction mixture was extracted with 4 ml toluene, and the absorbance was read at 520 nm by UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-160, Tokyo, Japan). The proline content was determined using a standard curve method.

Soluble sugar content was measured using phenol sulfuric acid method55. The fresh leaves (0.1 g) were extracted with 3 ml de-ionized water. A 50 µl extract was then mixed with 450 µl water and 500 µl phenol solution (5%). Immediately thereafter, a 2.5 ml concentrated sulfuric acid was added and kept at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was read at 485 nm by a UV/Vis spectrophotometer (UV-Visible160, Shimadzu, Japan). The soluble carbohydrate content was then calculated by a standard curve method using glucose as standard and expressed in mg g−1 FW.

Measurement of nitric oxide (NO) content

The NO production was determined using Greiss reagent as described by56. The samples were ground in 3 ml cool acetic acid buffer (50 mM, pH 3.6, 4% zinc diacetate) by a mortar and pestle. The homogenates were centrifuged at 7840×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, a 0.1 g charcoal was added to the supernatant. After vortex and filtration, a 1 ml aliquot of the filtrate and 1 ml Greiss reagent containing 1% sulfanilamide solution in 3 M HCl and 0.1% N-1-naphthylethylene diamine dihydrochloride in water were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The sample’s absorbance was read at 540 nm. The NO content was calculated by a standard curve method using NaNO2 as standard.

Phytohormone production assay

The fresh seedlings (0.5 g) were extracted in liquid nitrogen and the powder was dissolved in 10 absolute MeOH in a Falcon tube covered with aluminum foil in an ice bath. The specimen was kept overnight at 4 °C. Prior to use, the extract was filtered by a sterile syringe filter (0.22 μm) and then injected into a HPLC system (Waters e2695) on a C18 column (Perfectsil Target ODS-3 (5 μm), 250 × 4.6 mm; MZ Analysentechnik, Germany). Phytohormones were eluted at a flow rate of 1 ml min−1 with a MeOH: Acidic water gradient (deionized water containing 0.67% acetic acid, pH 3.0). ABA, IAA, GA, BR, and MJ were detected at 220 nm, and were quantified by comparison of their retention times and peak area with genuine standards57.

Tocopherol

Tocopherol content was determined by the method of58. Approximately 0.02 g fresh material was homogenized in 3 ml ethanol. One ml ethanol extract was mixed with 0.2 ml bathophenanthroline in ethanol (0.2%) and the content of each tube was homogenized and vigorously mixed with 0.2 ml FeC13 solution in ethanol (1 mM) by a Vortex. After 1 min, the solution mixed by a 0.2 ml H3PO4 solution (1 mM) in ethanol. The solution absorbance was read at 534 nm using α-tocopherol as standard and expressed in µg g−1 FW.

Glycine betaine

The leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized with 20 ml de-ionized water. The samples were filtered and the filtrates were diluted 1:1 with 2 N H2SO4, kept in centrifuge tubes and cooled in ice bath for 1 h. The cold KI-I2 reagent was added and the reactants were gently stirred with a vortex mixer. The tubes were kept at 4 °C for 16 h and then centrifuged at 7840×g for 15 min. The periodide crystals were dissolved in 9 ml 1,2-dichloroethane. After 2 h, the absorbance was read at 365 nm59.

Analysis of gene expression in spinach plants

RNA extraction

The frozen leaves (100 mg) were ground in liquid nitrogen to a fine powder using a pestle and mortar. Then, high-quality RNA was successfully isolated by RNeasy Plant Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentration was found by reading absorbance at 230, 260 and 280 nm using the spectrophotometer. The RNA quality was assessed by fractionation on 1% agarose gel visualized by staining with ethidium bromide60.

cDNA synthesis

First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using 1 µg oligo dT (23) primer, M-MLV RT buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3, 75 mM KCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT), dNTPs, RNase inhibitor, and M-MLV RT enzyme in a final volume of 10 µl according to the manufacturer’s instructions (cDNA Synthesis Kit, Toyobo, Japan). The mixture was incubated in a SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 5 min incubation at 98 °C61.

qRT-PCR

The primers were designed from conserved regions of nucleotide sequences of Spinach oleracea L. on the GenBank database, NCBI. The online version of Primer 3 software (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/)62 was applied to produce amplicon sizes from 170- to 210-bp (Table 4) using ß-actin as an internal control. qRT-PCR was performed using the Thermal Cycler Dice® real time system (Takara Bio. Inc., USA). The reaction mixtures were composed of 1 µl cDNA as a template, 20 µM forward and reverse primers, and SsoAdvancedTM Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) in a final volume of 10 µl. The qRT-PCR reactions were conducted as follows: (1) One initial cycle at 95 °C for 30 s; (2) 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 95 °C for 15 s; (3) a final extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The threshold cycle (Ct) value was automatically detected for each reaction by the qRT-PCR system with default parameters. The relative level of gene expression was calculated by the delta Ct and the 2 − ΔΔCT method63.
