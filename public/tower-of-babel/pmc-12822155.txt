Role of APOC1 and NOP16 in the diagnosis of prostate cancer
Authors: Yi Yan, Songtao Shuang, Zheqi Fan, Zhuo Wei, Bei Li, Qin Yuan, Ying Lu, Chang Ye, Pan Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPCa (Prostate cancer) is the most prevalent urogenital malignancy among men. The genes APOC1(Apolipoprotein C1) and NOP16 (Nucleolar protein 16) have been associated with various types of cancer. This article aims to investigate the potential of APOC1 and NOP16 as preliminary proactive diagnostic markers for PCa.MethodsSearch for APOC1 and NOP16 using the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) and GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) databases. Immunohistochemical staining was employed to detect the expression of APOC1 and NOP16 in prostate tissue. The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was utilized to quantify the levels of APOC1 and NOP16 proteins. Spearman correlation coefficient and Pearson correlation coefficient were calculated to validate the association between APOC1 and NOP16. A binary logistic regression model was developed to analyze the factors that influence PCa. Predictive models for APOC1 and NOP16 and PSA, and their role in detecting PCa, were established through the construction of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsAPOC1 and NOP16 are up-regulated in PCa tissues, and their expressions are related with the clinical stage of PCa(n = 51). Furthermore, compared with individuals affected by benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), the expression levels of APOC1 and NOP16 in the serum of individuals affected by PCa are higher(n = 61). Moreover, in the tissues and serum of the same PCa patient, APOC1 and NOP16 are positively correlated(n = 32). APOC1 and NOP16 are independent influencing factors for PCa(n = 50). APOC1 and NOP16 showed predictive potential for PCa (n = 50, APOC1 AUC = 0.729, NOP16 AUC = 0.777).ConclusionsAPOC1 and NOP16 are anticipated to function as biomarkers for the early proactive diagnosis of PCa.Keywords: Prostate cancer, APOC1, NOP16, Early diagnosis

Introduction

PCa is an epithelial malignant tumor that occurs in the prostate. It ranks first in the incidence of male genitourinary tumors and continues to rise [1–3]. At present, clinical detection mainly relies on a combination of multiple methods, including prostate biopsy, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) test, a digital rectal exam (DRE), and multi-parametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) [4–6]. However, the substantial increase in the detection rate of clinically indolent low-risk PCa has gradually revealed the limitations of traditional surveillance systems [7]. These limitations pertain to early detection sensitivity, risks associated with invasive procedures, and issues related to overdiagnosis [8–10]. In today’s world, many studies are increasingly focusing on improving the detection and treatment of PCa through precision medicine and minimally invasive methods [11]. Consequently, there is a growing trend in the field of urologic oncology to identify improved and less invasive methods for diagnosing PCa, as well as to develop high-sensitivity, low-trauma molecular diagnostic techniques.

Apolipoprotein C1 (APOC1), as the apolipoprotein member with the smallest molecular weight (6.6 kDa), plays a core role in the regulation of lipid metabolism, it specifically binds to phosphatidylinositol modification sites and participates in the assembly of extremely low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and the reverse transport of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol [12, 13]. In recent years, a growing body of evidence has indicated that APOC1 is associated with the onset and progression of various cancers, including breast cancer, renal clear cell carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, and lung cancer [14–17]. Notably, it has been found that down-regulation of APOC1 expression can arrest the cycle of PCa cells at G2/M phase and promote cell apoptosis [18].

Nucleolar protein 16 (NOP16) is the core component of large ribonucleoprotein particles, which is located in the nucleolar region [19, 20]. Research demonstrates that NOP16 holds a pivotal regulatory function in cancer emergence and advancement [19–24]. For instance, in a breast cancer model, the knockdown of NOP16 through RNA interference significantly hinders the proliferation of malignant cells. In PCa, the downregulation of carboxy-terminal binding protein 2 (CtBP2) leads to a reduction in the levels of c-Myc and its direct transcriptional target NOP16, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of PCa cells [22, 25].

These findings suggest that NOP16 and APOC1 play a pivotal role in various cancers. However, their effects on biological behaviors such as the initiation, progression, propagation, invasion, and metastatic dissemination of PCa, along with the specific molecular mechanisms involved, remain poorly understood. Therefore, this paper aims to provide novel insights for detection and treatment strategies as well as a theoretical foundation for proactive surveillance of PCa by investigating the relationship between APOC1, NOP16, and PCa.

Methods

TCGA and GEPIA database

After conducting a comprehensive review of the literature, we decided to explore APOC1 and NOP16. They were first searched in TCGA and GEPIA databases to obtain boxplots of APOC1 and NOP16 transcript levels in PCa and normal prostate. Secondly, the relationship between the transcriptional levels of APOC1 and NOP16 and the disease-free survival rate of patients with PCa was investigated.

Tissue samples and serum samples

Inclusion: (a) PCa/BPH confirmed by pathology; (b) complete case information.

Inclusion: (a) PCa/BPH confirmed by pathology; (b) complete case information.

Exclusion: (a) concomitant primary tumors at other sites; (b) unwilling/unable to cooperate.

Exclusion: (a) concomitant primary tumors at other sites; (b) unwilling/unable to cooperate.

Prostate tissue specimens from prostate surgeries performed in our hospital from 2022 to 2024 were collected through continuous enrollment. Serum samples of PCa patients who were hospitalized in our hospital from 2023 to 2024 were collected. Tissue samples and serum samples were from different patients. The collected serum samples were stored in a refrigerator at −80 ° C by number for subsequent studies.

Consent was obtained from all patients or their families and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiaogan Central Hospital for all materials used in this study. (Ethics batch number: KY-2025010501)

Immunohistochemistry

Firstly, the paraffin-embedded tissue is sectioned, dewaxed (xylene), and hydrated. Then antigen retrieval was performed to expose the masked antigenic epitopes. Subsequently, the block was dropped in, and a specific primary antibody was added (usually incubated overnight at 4℃) to bind to the target antigen. After thorough washing, add the secondary antibody and incubate at room temperature to bind to the primary antibody. After thorough washing again, add DAB for color development. Finally, hematoxylin was counterstained, washed, blued, dehydrated, sealed, and the localization and expression of the specific antigen were observed under a light microscope.

APOC1 primary antibody (Concentration: 1:150, ab198288, Abcam, USA), NOP16 primary antibody (Concentration: 1:100, sc-81866, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). The intensity scores of the two staining in prostate tissue were 0 (negative), 1 (weak brown), 2 (medium brown), and 3 (strong brown). The staining degree scores were 0 (≤ 5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (25–50%), and 3 (> 50%). Multiply the two fractions (intensity and proportion) to obtain the final score. The final expression classification is as follows: a score of 0–3 is considered a negative expression, and a score of 4–9 is considered a positive expression. The immunohistochemical scoring findings of this time were independently evaluated by two pathologists, respectively. The staining intensity was observed at a magnification of X40 and the staining ratio at a magnification of X100. The number of fields of view under the microscope was 22 mm.

ELISA

We performed an evaluation of APOC1 and NOP16 levels in serum samples sourced from individuals affected by BPH and those suffering from PCa by employing specific APOC1 and NOP16 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (APOC1: DM4971, NOP16: DM7061, Dumabio, China), absorbance (OD value) was determined at a wavelength of 450 nm using Micro-plate Reader (Kehua Biotech ST-360, China). The APOC1 and NOP16 ELISA kits employ the double antibody sandwich method. The standard curve was drawn with the gradient dilution standard concentration as the horizontal axis and the corresponding OD value as the vertical axis, and then the sample concentration was calculated using the OD value of the sample.

Statistical analysis

Employing SPSS version 27.0.0, statistical analysis ensued. The independent sample t-test facilitated comparisons among groups featuring measurement data. Comparison between count data sets using the chi-square test. The comparison between groups of grade data is conducted using the non-parametric rank sum test. Spearman correlation coefficient and Pearson correlation coefficient were used for association analysis. Univariate and multivariate analyses of the influencing factors of PCa were conducted using the binary Logistic regression model. GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 was used for plotting. The ROC curve was drawn to establish a PCa prediction model. All the statistical results in the text are marked with corresponding statistics and confidence intervals. All tests were two-tailed, P < 0.05 served as an indicator of statistical significance.

Results

APOC1 and NOP16 are overexpressed genes in PCa

We discovered through the TCGA database that, in comparison to normal prostate tissues, both APOC1 and NOP16 were significantly upregulated in PCa tissues (Fig. 1a, b). Furthermore, survival analysis indicated that individuals with elevated levels of APOC1 and NOP16 exhibited lower disease-free survival rates compared to those with reduced levels of these markers (Fig. 1c, d).

Transcriptional levels of APOC1 (a) and NOP16 (b) in PCa (T) and normal prostate (N) were shown in the TCGA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). There is an association between the transcriptional levels of APOC1(c) and NOP16(d) and the disease-free survival rate of individuals affected by PCa

APOC1 and NOP16 are highly expressed in PCa tissues

To verify the expression of APOC1 and NOP16 in PCa tissues, 61 prostate tissue specimens preserved in our hospital were collected, including 51 cases of PCa and 10 cases of BPH. In the subgroup of PCa, there were 25 cases in the GS (Gleason score) 6–7 group and 26 cases in the GS 8–10 group. According to TNM staging, there were 15 individuals affected by stage I-II cancer and 36 individuals affected by stage III-IV cancer. 16 patients with PCa were randomly selected and classified according to cancer tissues and adjacent non-cancerous tissues.

We found that the expressions of APOC1 (P < 0.01, t = 5.337, 95%CI:2.322–5.109) and NOP16 (P < 0.01, t = 4.813, 95%CI:1.510–3.659) were significantly higher in PCa tissues compared with BPH tissues. (Fig. 2a, b). Subsequently, among the 16 randomly selected individuals affected by PCa, we found that the expression of both APOC1 (P < 0.01, t = 4.922, 95%CI:1.682–4.068) and NOP16 (P < 0.01, t = 5.412, 95%CI:1.673–3.702) was higher in cancer tissues than in adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 2c, d). Finally, we analyzed the differences in the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 in 51 PCa tissues with different GS and TNM stages. The findings showed that APOC1(P < 0.05, t = 2.253, 95%CI:0.139–2.433) and NOP16(P < 0.05, t = 2.979, 95%CI:0.399–2.051) levels were higher in GS 8–10 PCa tissues than GS 6–7 PCa tissues (Fig. 2e, f). The expression levels of APOC1 (P < 0.01, t = 3.386, 95%CI: 0.815–3.196) and NOP16 (P < 0.01, t = 5.629, 95%CI: 1.382–2.918) were higher in stage III-IV PCa tissues than in stage I-II tissues (Fig. 2 g, h). APOC1 and NOP16 are highly expressed in the serum of individuals affected by PCa

Based on the previous research, we tested and analyzed the serum of 32 PCa patients and 18 BPH patients. In the PCa subgroup, there were 15 cases with GS 6–7 points and 17 cases with 8–10 points. According to the TNM staging, it included 14 patients in stages I to II and 18 patients in stages III to IV.

The findings showed that the expressions of APOC1(P < 0.01, t = 2.845, 95%CI:2.029–11.80) and NOP16(P < 0.01, t = 3.355, 95%CI:1.470–5.865) in the serum of PCa patients significantly increased compared with individuals affected by BPH (Fig. 4a, b). In addition, individuals affected by GS 8–10 PCa had higher serum levels of APOC1(P < 0.05, t = 2.426, 95%CI:0.933–10.86) and NOP16(P < 0.05, t = 2.628, 95%CI:0.768–6.125) than those with GS 6–7 PCa (Fig. 4c, d). Individuals affected by stage III-IV PCa had higher serum APOC1(P < 0.05, t = 3.665, 95%CI:3.607–12.68) and NOP16(P < 0.05, t = 2.320, 95%CI:0.375–5.878) expression levels than individuals affected by stage I-II PCa (Fig. 4e, f).

The expression levels of APOC1 and NOP16 in the tissues and serum of the same PCa patient were positively correlated

Firstly, the expression levels of APOC1 and NOP16 were analyzed in 51 cases of PCa tissue samples using the Spearman correlation coefficient (Fig. 5a, P < 0.01, r = 0.398, 95%CI:0.129–0.612), and then utilizing Pearson correlation analysis, we investigated the relationship between APOC1 and NOP16 expressions in serum samples obtained from 32 PCa patients (Fig. 5b, P < 0.01, r = 0.5625, 95%CI:0.266–0.762). Our findings unveiled a positive association between the expression levels of APOC1 and NOP16 in these samples.

Association of APOC1 and NOP16 expression with general clinicopathological features

The associations of APOC1 and NOP16 expressions with various clinicopathological features, including age, family history, smoking history, GS, PSA, and TNM stage, were analyzed in 51 individuals affected by PCa (Tables 1 and 2). The results showed that APOC1 and NOP16 expressions were correlated with age, GS, TNM stage, and PSA, independent of smoking history and family history. The expression levels of APOC1 and NOP16 in the tissues of patients with ≥ 65 years old, GS 8–10 and stage III-IV PCa were significantly higher than those of patients with < 65 years old, GS 6–7 and stage I-II PCa.

The expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 are independent influencing factors of PCa

To investigate the relationship between the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 and PCa, we used Elisa samples. This analysis included 32 samples of PCa and 18 samples of BPH for both univariate and multivariate analyses (Table 3). After conducting univariate analyses on the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16, PSA, age, family history, and smoking history, multivariate analyses were performed on the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 based on the principle of stepwise regression analysis. The results of stepwise regression analysis indicated that the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 were independent influencing factors of PCa.

APOC1 and NOP16 diagnostic value in PCa

ROC curves were plotted using the contents of serum APOC1 and NOP16 proteins and serum PSA (Fig. 6a, b, c, d), the findings showed that: The area under the ROC curve (AUC) of APOC1 serum was 0.729(P < 0.01, 95%CI:0.583–0.875). When the Youden index was 0.368, the optimal cutoff value of APOC1 protein content was 42.9351 (pg/ml), and the sensitivity and specificity achieved were 71.90% (95%CI:0.529–0.862) and 67.70% (95%CI:0.385–0.882). The positive predictive value was 0.793 (95%CI:0.597–0.925), and the negative predictive value was 0.571 (95%CI:0.343–0.777).

The ROC curve was plotted using the protein contents of serum APOC1(a) and NOP16(b), serum PSA(c), and the combined indicators of APOC1 and NOP16(d)

NOP16 serum demonstrated an AUC of 0.777 (P < 0.01, 95%CI:0.650–0.904). With a Youden index of 0.441, the ideal cutoff value for NOP16 protein content was established at 13.3900 (pg/ml), resulting in sensitivity and specificity scores of 71.90% (95%CI:0.529–0.862) and 72.20% (95%CI:0.465–0.903). The positive predictive value was 0.821 (95%CI:0.632–0.931), and the negative predictive value was 0.591 (95%CI:0.366–0.789).

The AUC of PSA serum was 0.808 (P < 0.01, 95%CI:0.682–0.935). When the Youden index was 0.646, the cut-off value of PSA was 14.4250 (pg/ml), and the sensitivity and specificity were 81.30% (95%CI:0.634–0.922) and 83.30% (95%CI:0.586–0.964). The positive predictive value was 0.897 (95%CI:0.731–0.974), and the negative predictive value was 0.714 (95%CI:0.476–0.885).

Most importantly, when the AUC of the predictive probability of APOC1 combined with NOP16 was 0.806 and the Youden index was 0.614, the sensitivity and specificity were 78.10% and 83.30%, respectively (P < 0.01, 95%CI:0.683–0.929). This result indicates that both and their combined indicators have certain predictive potential for PCa.

Discussion

As a significant concern for men’s health, the incidence of PCa continues to increase, posing considerable challenges for clinicians [1, 26, 27]. PSA, mpMRI, digital rectal examination and needle biopsy are the core surveillance methods for PCa at present [4, 5, 8, 9]. Currently, emerging biomarkers are demonstrating significant advantages. The application of these biomarkers can enhance the sensitivity of early PCa detection, reduce complications associated with invasive procedures, and simultaneously lower the overdetection rate of low-risk indolent cancers [4, 5, 8, 9, 28, 29]. Consequently, the discovery of biomarkers will assist clinicians in making decisions that are more beneficial to patients [30].

APOC1 and NOP16 are frequently upregulated in a variety of malignant tumors. Owing to their functional diversity, they can contribute to tumorigenesis and progression through multiple mechanisms. Notably, Hui Ren et al. demonstrated that APOC1 expression is elevated in breast cancer [17]. Furthermore, Liwen Ren found, APOC1 is a kind of immune biomarker; it can regulate macrophage polarization, eventually leading to tumor metastasis [16]. Wenmin Xiong et al. found that knocking down NOP16 to reduce its expression could inhibit a series of biological behaviors of nasopharyngeal carcinoma cells; Notably, the RhoA/PI3K/Akt/c-Myc and IKK/IKB/NF-κB signaling pathways can be inhibited by knocking out NOP16 [24]. In addition, NOP16 significantly enhances the invasive ability of liver metastasis from colorectal cancer by regulating the lipid metabolism reprogramming of cancer-associated fibroblasts in the tumor microenvironment [21]. However, at present, there are relatively few studies on the expression of APOC1 and NOP16 in PCa. For instance, Wei-peng Su et al. knocked down the expression of APOC1 in PCa cell lines through RNA interference technology, verifying that APOC1 promotes the proliferation of PCa cells in vitro. Changwen Zhang et al. found that down-regulating the expression of CtBP2 could reduce the expression levels of c-Myc and its direct transcription target NOP16, but neither has been demonstrated at the clinical level [18, 25]. APOC1 and NOP16 are at different locations in the cell, but the relationship between them and their association with PCa has not been reported in the literature. Therefore, investigating the expression patterns of APOC1 and NOP16 in PCa, and their relationship with clinical parameters such as stage and GS, as well as the correlation between the two genes, has important potential clinical implications.

In this study, we first conducted a search of the TCGA database and concluded that APOC1 and NOP16 are associated with PCa. Subsequently, we examined the expression levels of APOC1 and NOP16 in PCa tissues, adjacent non-cancerous tissues, and BPH tissues from different individual PCa patients using immunohistochemical staining. We observed elevated expressions of both APOC1 and NOP16 in PCa tissues, which were particularly pronounced in GS 8–10 and III-IV tumors. Furthermore, a positive correlation was identified between the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 within the same PCa tissue samples. Secondly, by analyzing the relationship between the expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 and various clinical characteristics of PCa patients, we found that their expression levels were associated with GS, PSA, TNM stage, and age; however, no significant correlations were observed concerning family history or smoking history. This is the same as the result proved by the TCGA database and Wei-peng Su, Changwen Zhang, et al. [18, 25]. These findings suggest that APOC1 and NOP16 hold potential as novel biomarkers for the early diagnosis of PCa.

It is well known that the early detection, diagnosis and treatment of any disease are of vital importance, which can effectively control the development and deterioration of the disease [18, 25]. To this end, we quantified the serum concentrations of APOC1 and NOP16 in patients with BPH or PCa using ELISA. The results demonstrated that both proteins were significantly elevated in PCa patients compared to those with BPH. Moreover, this upregulation was more pronounced in patients with GS 8–10 or III-IV disease. Further analysis revealed a positive correlation between serum APOC1 and NOP16 levels, and identified both as independent factors in PCa. Subsequently, by drawing the ROC curve, it was concluded that APOC1 (AUC = 0.729), NOP16 (AUC = 0.777), and the combination of APOC1 and NOP16 (AUC = 0.806) have certain predictive potential for PCa. Of course, we also plotted the ROC curve of PSA to verify its significance in the diagnosis of PCa (AUC = 0.808). When the content of APOC1 protein in the serum exceeds 42.9351 (pg/ml) and the content of NOP16 protein exceeds 13.3900 (pg/ml), medical attention should be sought promptly. This is the first report demonstrating elevated expressions of APOC1 and NOP16 in serum samples from patients with PCa. Previously, Weipeng Su and Changwen Zhang et al. [14, 21] verified the relationship between APOC1 and NOP16 and PCa through in vitro experiments. Our findings collectively demonstrated that APOC1 and NOP16 were consistently upregulated in both tissue and serum samples from PCa patients, and that their expression levels were positively correlated. These results strongly support the potential of APOC1 and NOP16 as promising biomarkers for the early diagnosis of PCa.

Because PCa has no typical symptoms or almost no symptoms in its initial proactive stage, many PCa patients are already in the advanced or metastatic stage when they are first diagnosed, which also indirectly leads to a poor prognosis of the disease [31, 32]. Data indicates that APOC1 and NOP16 have great potential in the early diagnosis of PCa. For patients presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms secondary to BPH, serum testing for APOC1 and NOP16 could serve as valuable diagnostic triage. A positive test would warrant more definitive investigations, enabling the proactive identification of underlying PCa. This stratified approach holds the potential to improve early detection rates while minimizing unnecessary invasive procedures in patients with negative results.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, in the TNM staging group for immunohistochemistry, the majority of patients had stage III-IV PCa (ratio 2.4:1), which may lead to a loss of statistical power. Secondly, the sample size was insufficient due to the following reasons: serum samples from patients with prostate diseases could not be collected in a timely manner; there was a risk of damage during collection and preservation; and some patients had missing information. This limits the generalizability of the results. The small sample size also resulted in an overly wide confidence interval for PSA in the logistic regression analysis (P < 0.01, OR = 21.667, 95%CI: 4.717–99.527), suggesting an unstable model. Moreover, when PSA, APOC1, and NOP16 were included in a multivariate model, APOC1 and NOP16 were not statistically significant, and the confidence interval for PSA became even wider (95%CI: 3.349–101.720). We therefore considered this multivariate model unstable and included only APOC1 and NOP16 in the final multivariate analysis. Furthermore, the existing data cannot explain the regulatory mechanisms of APOC1 and NOP16 in PCa. To address these issues, we will conduct in vitro experiments for further verification and continue to increase the sample size.

Conclusion

APOC1 and NOP16 are overexpressed in PCa and exhibit positive correlation, suggesting potential utility as complementary diagnostic biomarkers. This will provide a reference for subsequent studies of PCa diagnosis.
