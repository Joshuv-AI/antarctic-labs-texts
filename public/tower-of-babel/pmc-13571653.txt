Levocarnitine attenuates diabetic seminal vesicle fibrosis in association with improved lipid metabolism and reduced ER stress
Authors: Pengfei Liu, Yi Yu, Junjie Wu, Lei Wang, Fan Yao, Xueqin Chen, Kerong Wu, Xuejun Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySeminal vesicle integrity supports semen composition and sperm function, whereas type 2 diabetes mellitus can induce atrophy and fibrosis in this organ. Using a high-fat diet/streptozotocin-induced model in male mice and primary mouse seminal vesicle epithelial cells exposed to high glucose, we assessed fibrotic remodeling, lipid metabolism, PPARα/PGC-1α signaling, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and apoptosis through histological, biochemical, and lipidomic analyses. Diabetic seminal vesicles showed atrophy and fibrosis together with altered lipid profiles, free fatty acid accumulation, reduced PPARα/PGC-1α expression, and increased IRE1-associated stress and apoptosis. Levocarnitine attenuated these changes in vivo and reduced profibrotic responses in vitro. PPARα overexpression reproduced several protective effects, whereas IRE1 inhibition reduced stress-associated injury without restoring PPARα/PGC-1α signaling. These findings identify metabolic dysregulation and endoplasmic reticulum stress as associated features of diabetic seminal vesicle remodeling and support further evaluation of levocarnitine for diabetes-associated reproductive tract injury.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and progressive β cell dysfunction, accounting for approximately 90% of diabetes cases worldwide.1 Its rising prevalence among younger populations has rendered its complications a pressing public health concern.2 In addition to its well-recognized cardiovascular, renal, and neurological sequelae, T2DM has also been closely linked to male reproductive dysfunction. Nearly half of diabetic males exhibit compromised semen quality, erectile dysfunction, and structural abnormalities of reproductive organs.3 Importantly, these manifestations should not be viewed as a uniform, testis-centered phenotype, as accessory reproductive organs such as the seminal vesicle (SV) may also undergo diabetes-related pathological remodeling.

The SV is a major accessory gland of the male reproductive system and plays an essential role in maintaining semen quality and sperm function through the secretion of fructose, prostaglandins, and other bioactive components.4 Despite its physiological importance, the SV remains considerably less studied than the testis or epididymis in the context of diabetes-associated reproductive injury. Previous studies have reported SV atrophy, histopathological remodeling, and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition in diabetic males, indicating that fibrosis may represent an important pathological feature of diabetic SV injury.5,6 These changes contribute to male reproductive dysfunction by impairing seminal fluid secretion and altering its biochemical composition.7 However, the molecular mechanisms that drive SV fibrosis under diabetic conditions remain poorly defined.

Among the mechanisms implicated in diabetic tissue remodeling, lipid metabolic dysregulation, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress are of particular interest.8,9 The ER is a pivotal organelle involved in protein folding, lipid biosynthesis, and calcium homeostasis.10 Under diabetic conditions, metabolic overload, lipotoxicity, and oxidative stress can disrupt ER homeostasis and trigger persistent activation of the unfolded protein response, eventually promoting apoptosis and tissue remodeling.11,12 In parallel, disturbed lipid metabolism has been recognized as a key contributor to diabetic organ damage, particularly in tissues with high metabolic demand. Altered fatty acid handling may lead to intracellular lipid accumulation, exacerbate cellular stress responses, and facilitate fibrotic progression. Although the interplay between lipid metabolic disturbance and ER stress has been reported in several diabetic complications, whether these processes contribute to SV fibrosis in T2DM remains unknown.

Levocarnitine (LC) is a naturally occurring quaternary ammonium compound that is essential for fatty acid transport and mitochondrial β-oxidation.13 Beyond its canonical metabolic role, LC has been reported to exert antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective effects, and to modulate metabolic stress responses including ER stress in several disease settings.14,15,16,17 Importantly, LC may also influence systemic metabolic and endocrine status, including glucose-lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and reproductive hormonal milieu. In the field of male reproduction, LC has been used as an adjunctive therapy to improve sperm quality, alleviate reproductive tract inflammation, and enhance fertility.18,19 These properties make LC an attractive candidate for the treatment of diabetes-associated reproductive tract injury. However, its potential role in diabetic SV fibrosis has not yet been investigated.

Therefore, in the present study, we investigated whether T2DM-associated SV fibrosis is accompanied by lipid metabolic dysregulation and ER stress-associated apoptosis and whether LC mitigates these pathological alterations. To address this question, we performed mechanistic studies in a high-fat diet (HFD)/streptozotocin-induced mouse model of T2DM and in primary mouse seminal vesicle epithelial cells (SVECs) exposed to high glucose (HG). By integrating histological, biochemical, and lipidomic analyses, this study aimed to clarify the pathogenic basis of diabetic SV fibrosis and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of LC in this underexplored form of male reproductive tract injury.

Results

LC improves systemic metabolic parameters in HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice

To investigate the effects of LC in vivo, we established a T2DM mouse model using HFD feeding combined with low-dose streptozotocin administration (Figure 1A). Compared with the control (CTRL) group, diabetic mice developed marked metabolic abnormalities, including reduced body weight, elevated fasting blood glucose (FBG), and moderately increased insulin levels, consistent with a T2DM phenotype characterized by insulin resistance and progressive β cell dysfunction (Figure 1B). LC treatment significantly improved these metabolic abnormalities, as reflected by partial recovery of body weight, decreased FBG and improved circulating insulin levels. Moreover, LC treatment increased testosterone levels and improved sperm quality, supporting a beneficial effect on diabetes-associated reproductive impairment. LC attenuates seminal vesicle atrophy and fibrotic remodeling in diabetic mice

We next assessed whether LC could alleviate diabetes-induced structural injury in the SV. Diabetic mice exhibited significant reductions in SV volume, weight, and organ index compared with CTRLs, confirming marked glandular atrophy (Figures 2A, 2B, S1A, and S1B). Histological analysis further showed a reduced number of glandular ducts, decreased epithelial height, and pronounced collagen accumulation in diabetic SV tissues, indicating severe fibrotic remodeling (Figure 2C). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated increased deposition of fibronectin, collagen I, and collagen III, predominantly in the periglandular stromal region, and these findings were further supported by western blot analysis of fibrosis-related proteins (Figures 2D–2G). ECM remodeling is a dynamic physiological process that underpins tissue homeostasis, injury repair, and adaptation to physiological changes. This balance is precisely regulated by a cascade of profibrotic and antifibrotic factors. SV tissues from diabetic mice exhibited significant upregulation of connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) together with marked downregulation of matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3) (Figures 2H and 2I), indicating a shift toward profibrotic ECM remodeling. Moreover, expression of the profibrotic factor tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP1) was increased in diabetic SV tissues. In contrast, TIMP3, another member of the TIMP family, showed no significant change in expression under diabetic conditions (Figures 2H and 2J), suggesting distinct roles for individual TIMP family members in diabetes-associated SV fibrosis. In addition, α-SMA expression showed an increasing trend in diabetic SV tissues, indicative of myofibroblast activation, although this change did not reach statistical significance (Figures 2K and 2L). Notably, LC treatment improved these remodeling-related changes. Together, these results indicate that LC attenuates SV atrophy and fibrotic remodeling in diabetic mice, at least in part in association with improved ECM homeostasis.

LC partially restores lipid metabolic homeostasis and PPAR pathway in diabetic seminal vesicles

Given the established role of LC in fatty acid metabolism, we next performed lipidomic and molecular analyses to characterize lipid metabolic alterations in SV tissues from diabetic mice. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) demonstrated clear separation among the CTRL, T2DM, and T2DM + LC groups, indicating distinct lipid metabolic profiles across groups (Figures 3A and S2). Quantitative lipidomic analysis further revealed that total lipid content was increased in diabetic SV tissues (Figure 3B). Free fatty acids (FFAs) were also significantly elevated in the T2DM group, whereas LC treatment reduced these changes (Figure 3C), suggesting alleviation of lipid accumulation and lipotoxic stress. Lipids with variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 and raw p < 0.05 were regarded as candidate differential lipids for exploratory visualization, whereas lipids additionally meeting false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered FDR-significant differential lipids (Table S1). Differential lipid analysis identified 345 differentially accumulated lipids (DALs) between the CTRL and T2DM groups and 47 DALs between the T2DM and T2DM + LC groups (Figures 3D and 3E). Venn diagram analysis further identified 18 common DALs that were altered under diabetic conditions and reversed by LC treatment (Figure 3F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the reversed lipid subset provided an exploratory overview of lipid pathways potentially affected by diabetes and LC treatment. Given the limited number of reversed lipid species, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive pathway evidence. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that diabetes-associated lipid metabolic alterations were mainly related to ether lipid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and ferroptosis-associated pathways (Figure 3G). Although the PPAR signaling pathway was not among the top significantly enriched pathways, the prominent accumulation of FFAs and the known role of LC in fatty acid transport and oxidation led us to examine PPAR signaling as a hypothesis-driven metabolic axis. PPARα is a key transcriptional regulator of fatty acid uptake, transport, and peroxisomal β-oxidation, making it highly relevant to the FFA-dominant lipidomic phenotype observed in diabetic SV. Therefore, PPARα was prioritized as a candidate metabolic node closely aligned with both the observed lipid disturbance and LC pharmacology. Western blot analysis revealed significant downregulation of PPARα expression in diabetic SV tissues, whereas no notable difference was observed in PPARγ expression. In parallel, expression of PGC-1α, a critical coactivator of PPARα, was also significantly downregulated in the T2DM group. Importantly, LC treatment restored the expression of both PPARα and PGC-1α (Figures 3H and 3I). Together, these three-group lipidomic data indicate that diabetic SV tissues exhibit broad lipid metabolic alterations and that a subset of these diabetes-associated lipid changes is partially reversed by LC treatment. Because CTRL+LC samples were not included in the lipidomics analysis, these data do not determine whether LC alters the basal lipidome of non-diabetic SV tissue. However, protein-level analysis showed that LC alone did not markedly alter PPARα/PGC-1α-related signaling in non-diabetic SV tissues, whereas it partially restored their diabetes-associated suppression.

LC suppresses ER stress and apoptosis in diabetic seminal vesicles

We next investigated whether ER stress contributes to diabetic SV injury in vivo. Expression of the ER stress markers GRP78 and CHOP was significantly increased in SV tissues from diabetic mice (Figures 4A–4D). To compare the three canonical unfolded protein response (UPR) branches, we examined p-IRE1α, p-PERK, and ATF6 expression in SV tissues. p-IRE1α showed the most prominent increase in the T2DM group and was reduced by LC treatment, whereas p-PERK and ATF6 were not robustly altered (Figures 4E and 4F). These findings indicate preferential activation of the IRE1α branch under diabetic conditions. Furthermore, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells in the diabetic SV tissues, which contributes to the progression of SV fibrosis (Figures 4I and 4J). Consistently, the Bcl-2/Bax ratio was decreased, indicating enhanced activation of apoptosis-associated signaling (Figures 4G and 4H). LC treatment significantly reduced GRP78, CHOP, and p-IRE1α expression, decreased TUNEL-positive cells and restored the Bcl-2/Bax ratio. High glucose induces PPARα/PGC-1α dysregulation, ER stress, and profibrotic remodeling in primary SVECs

Given the pivotal role of SVECs in the synthesis and secretion of SV fluid and the direct compromise of this function by diabetes-induced atrophy and fibrosis, we chose SVECs as our in vitro model to explore the underlying pathogenic mechanisms. We first isolated primary SVECs from 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice using a differential adhesion method (Figure 5A). Cell identity and purity were assessed by immunofluorescence staining before experimental stimulation. The isolated cells showed strong pan-cytokeratin positivity (>97%), whereas vimentin-positive cells were rarely detected (Figures 5B and S3). We next examined whether HG stimulation promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in SVECs. Immunofluorescence staining for key EMT markers following treatment with varying glucose concentrations revealed a dose-dependent reduction in E-cadherin (an epithelial marker) alongside a concurrent increase in N-cadherin (a mesenchymal marker) (Figures 5C–5F). These findings suggest that HG induces EMT-like phenotypic changes in SVECs. Consistent with the elevated fibrosis levels and alterations in associated pathways observed in the SVs of diabetic mice, we evaluated the expression of key proteins involved in ECM remodeling, PPAR signaling, and ER stress in HG-stimulated SVECs. As shown in Figures 5G–5N, HG treatment significantly upregulated the expression of the ECM components collagen I and α-SMA, as well as the ER stress markers GRP78, CHOP, and p-IRE1α, while downregulating the PPAR pathway regulators PGC-1α and PPARα. Similarly, the expression of these proteins demonstrated a time-dependent trend under HG conditions (Figures S4A and S4B). To assess whether increased osmolarity contributed to the HG-induced SVEC phenotype, a high-mannitol osmotic control was included. Mannitol treatment did not significantly affect collagen I, GRP78, or PGC-1α expression compared with normal glucose (NG), whereas HG significantly increased collagen I and GRP78 expression and reduced PGC-1α expression (Figures S5A–S5D). These findings suggest that the HG-associated profibrotic response, ER stress activation and PGC-1α suppression were not attributable to increased osmolarity alone. Collectively, these in vitro findings recapitulate key pathological changes observed in diabetic SV tissues and support the contribution of SVECs to diabetes-induced SV fibrosis. LC restores PPARα/PGC-1α signaling and attenuates ER stress-associated profibrotic responses in HG-treated SVECs

We next investigated whether LC could protect SVECs from HG-induced injury by restoring PPARα/PGC-1α-related metabolic signaling. SVECs were exposed to HG (30 mM) to mimic the diabetic microenvironment and were subsequently treated with different concentrations of LC. In this dose-response experiment, LC reduced the HG-induced upregulation of fibrosis-related proteins in a concentration-dependent manner, with 1 mM producing the most consistent and pronounced inhibitory effect (Figures 6A and 6B). Therefore, 1 mM LC was selected for subsequent in vitro mechanistic experiments. In addition, LC restored the HG-induced suppression of PPARα/PGC-1α-related signaling in SVECs, consistent with its effects in diabetic mouse SV tissues (Figures 6C and 6D). LC also markedly suppressed HG-triggered ER stress and apoptosis-associated changes, as evidenced by reduced expression of GRP78, CHOP, and p-IRE1α (Figures 6E and 6F). To further distinguish the effect of LC from selective ER stress inhibition, HG-stimulated SVECs were treated with 4μ8C, an IRE1 inhibitor. Although 4μ8C effectively suppressed ER stress-related protein expression and apoptosis-associated changes, it did not restore impaired PPARα/PGC-1α-related signaling under HG conditions (Figures 6E and 6F). PPARα overexpression restores PPARα/PGC-1α signaling and ameliorates ER stress-associated profibrotic changes in HG-treated SVECs

To further clarify whether restoration of PPARα-associated metabolic signaling contributes to the protective effects of LC, we overexpressed PPARα in SVECs and evaluated its effects under HG conditions. To confirm the successful establishment of the PPARα overexpression model, PPARα protein expression was examined by western blotting before subsequent functional experiments. PPARα protein levels were markedly increased in SVECs transfected with the PPARα-overexpressing plasmid compared with the mock and empty vector controls, confirming effective PPARα overexpression (Figures 7A and 7B). Under HG conditions, western blot analysis revealed a marked downregulation of both PPARα and its coactivator PGC-1α. Importantly, transfection with the PPARα plasmid effectively reversed this HG-induced suppression (Figures 7C and 7D). PPARα overexpression also significantly reduced the HG-induced expression of fibrosis-related proteins, including collagen I and α-SMA (Figures 7E and 7F), indicating attenuation of profibrotic remodeling. PPARα overexpression also suppressed HG-triggered ER stress and apoptosis-associated changes, as evidenced by reduced expression of GRP78, CHOP, and p-IRE1α, together with an increased Bcl-2/Bax ratio (Figures 7G–7J). Notably, the effects of PPARα overexpression were consistent with the inhibitory outcomes observed with LC treatment, not only reinforcing the pivotal role of PPARα in counteracting HG-induced profibrotic responses but also suggesting that restoration of PPARα-associated metabolic signaling is an important component of the protective effects of LC. It is worth noting that reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in SVECs were significantly elevated after HG administration, which could be suppressed by LC treatment (Figures 7K and 7L). Discussion

The mechanisms underlying diabetes-induced SV fibrosis remain largely elusive. In the present study, we show that T2DM induces marked structural and molecular injury in the SV, characterized by glandular atrophy, ECM accumulation, and fibrotic remodeling. By integrating findings from a diabetic mouse model and primary SVECs, we further demonstrate that these pathological changes are accompanied by disturbed lipid metabolic homeostasis, impaired PPARα/PGC-1α-related signaling, ER stress, and apoptosis. Importantly, LC attenuated diabetic SV injury in both in vivo and in vitro models. Collectively, these findings suggest that diabetic SV fibrosis is associated with metabolic dysregulation and ER stress-associated apoptosis and that LC may exert protective effects through simultaneous improvement of lipid metabolic homeostasis and suppression of ER stress responses.

The SV is an essential accessory gland of the male reproductive tract, and its secretory products are critical for semen composition and sperm function, thereby contributing to male reproductive capacity. In line with evidence that metabolic disorders can disrupt somatic cell support within male germinal niches,20 diabetic SV injury may represent a post-testicular impairment of epithelial support, whereby SVECs shift from maintaining the seminal microenvironment toward lipid dysregulation, ER stress, and profibrotic remodeling. Although the detrimental effects of diabetes on male reproductive health have been increasingly recognized, the SV has received considerably less attention than the testis or epididymis. Previous clinical studies have identified the presence of SV atrophy in diabetic patients.5,21 In the present study, diabetic mouse SV tissues exhibited clear histopathological evidence of fibrosis, including collagen deposition, increased expression of ECM-related proteins, and glandular atrophy. These structural abnormalities were accompanied by impaired semen quality in diabetic mice, supporting the potential contribution of SV injury to diabetes-associated reproductive dysfunction. LC treatment substantially improved SV morphology, reduced fibrotic remodeling, and partially restored sperm quality, suggesting that preservation of SV structure may contribute to improvement of the reproductive microenvironment under diabetic conditions. In addition, our in vitro data showed that HG promoted collagen I and α-SMA expression, together with EMT changes in primary SVECs, indicating that epithelial cells themselves may actively participate in establishing a profibrotic microenvironment in the diabetic SV.

A central finding of this study is that diabetic SV fibrosis is accompanied by disturbed lipid metabolic homeostasis. The SV is mainly composed of pseudostratified columnar epithelium, which secretes multiple components of seminal plasma, including fructose, prostaglandins, and antioxidants, thereby supporting sperm function and the seminal microenvironment.4 Due to their high secretory activity, efficient fatty acid utilization may be particularly important for maintaining SV epithelial homeostasis. Microscopic evidence further indicates that SVECs contain abundant mitochondria.22,23 This metabolic characteristic is similarly observed in other high-energy-consuming cells, such as cardiomyocytes, skeletal muscle cells, and renal cells.24 Our lipidomic data showed increased total lipid content and marked accumulation of FFAs in diabetic SV tissues, together with broad alterations in the lipid profile. Moreover, the expression of PPARα and its coactivator PGC-1α was reduced in diabetic SV tissues and in HG-treated SVECs, and both were restored by LC treatment. These findings support the presence of impaired PPARα/PGC-1α-related metabolic signaling in diabetic SV injury. Although direct measurements of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) flux were not performed in the present study, the observed accumulation of FFAs and suppression of PPARα/PGC-1α suggest impaired fatty acid handling in diabetic SV fibrosis. Impaired fatty acid handling may lead to excessive intracellular lipid accumulation and contribute to fibrotic progression by promoting lipotoxicity. Interestingly, in a study of liver fibrosis, the FAO-related enzyme CPT1A was upregulated in hepatic stellate cells from fibrotic patients and animal models, thereby promoting FAO and exacerbating liver fibrosis.25 This pattern differs from the evidence of disturbed lipid metabolic homeostasis observed in SV tissues in the present study, a discrepancy likely attributable to intrinsic metabolic heterogeneity across distinct cell types. Collectively, these results suggest that FAO may undergo tissue-specific metabolic reprogramming during fibrotic progression.

Another important finding of this study is the close association between lipid metabolic dysregulation and ER stress in diabetic SV injury. The ER is a central organelle for protein folding, lipid synthesis, and calcium homeostasis, and is particularly vulnerable to metabolic overload. Although sustained elevation of FFAs is known to induce ER stress,26 the phenotype of lipid metabolic disorders in diabetic SV remains unreported. In SV tissues from diabetic mice, expression of the ER stress markers GRP78 and CHOP was increased. Similar changes were reproduced in HG-treated SVECs, together with increased p-IRE1α, suggesting prominent activation of the IRE1α-associated branch of the unfolded protein response. At the same time, diabetic SV tissues and HG-treated SVECs showed enhanced apoptosis-associated signaling, including a reduced Bcl-2/Bax ratio and increased TUNEL-positive cells in vivo. These findings support the involvement of ER stress-associated apoptosis in the pathogenesis of diabetic SV injury. Importantly, LC markedly reduced ER stress markers and apoptosis-related changes in both models, indicating that attenuation of ER stress is a major component of its protective effect. In addition to apoptosis, ER proteostasis may also be relevant to diabetic SV injury. SVECs are highly secretory epithelial cells responsible for producing multiple components of seminal plasma, which imposes substantial demand on ER-dependent protein folding, lipid biosynthesis, and calcium homeostasis. Under diabetic conditions, lipid metabolic disturbance and FFA accumulation may increase ER proteostatic burden and promote maladaptive UPR activation.

The mechanistic relationship between impaired PPARα-associated metabolic signaling and ER stress was further supported by our intervention experiments in SVECs. LC restored PPARα/PGC-1α expression under HG conditions and simultaneously attenuated ER stress, apoptosis-associated changes and profibrotic responses. By contrast, selective inhibition of IRE1 with 4μ8C reduced ER stress-related and apoptosis-related changes but failed to restore impaired PPARα/PGC-1α signaling. Although 4μ8C is commonly used as an inhibitor of IRE1α RNase activity, we acknowledge that potential off-target or compensatory effects cannot be fully excluded.27 Further studies using genetic inhibition of IRE1α and direct measurement of XBP1 splicing or regulated IRE1-dependent decay (RIDD) activity will be needed to validate this mechanism. Moreover, direct overexpression of PPARα alleviated HG-induced profibrotic remodeling and suppressed ER stress-associated changes, producing effects broadly comparable to those observed with LC treatment. Taken together, these observations suggest that disruption of PPARα-associated metabolic signaling may occur upstream of, or in parallel with, ER stress activation in HG-treated SVECs. Therefore, LC may alleviate diabetic SV fibrosis by restoring PPARα/PGC-1α-associated fatty acid metabolic regulation, reducing lipotoxic stress, and attenuating ER stress-associated apoptosis. LC has recently been shown to simultaneously activate fatty acid β-oxidation and alleviate HFD-induced ER stress, supporting our speculation.28 However, because the ER itself participates in lipid homeostasis by regulating enzymes involved in lipid synthesis and modification,26 reciprocal regulation between lipid metabolic disturbance and ER stress may also exist. In addition to regulating lipid metabolism, LC may also attenuate organ fibrosis through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.29 Furthermore, LC administration has been demonstrated to ameliorate insulin resistance and enhance testosterone secretion in an obese rat model, which aligns with our findings.30

An important consideration in interpreting the in vivo protective effects of LC is the potential contribution of systemic metabolic improvement. In the present study, LC partially improved FBG, insulin-related metabolic indices, and serum testosterone levels in diabetic mice. Improved glycemic control may reduce glucose toxicity, oxidative stress, advanced glycation-related injury, and ER stress activation in SV tissues, whereas improved insulin sensitivity may alleviate systemic metabolic inflammation and lipid overload.31,32 In addition, testosterone is a key trophic factor for maintaining SV structure and secretory function.33 Therefore, recovery of circulating testosterone may contribute to the attenuation of SV atrophy and remodeling. Thus, the beneficial effects of LC on diabetic SV injury in vivo should be interpreted as the combined result of systemic metabolic improvement and local tissue-level protection. Nevertheless, the protective effects observed in HG-treated primary SVECs support an additional cell-autonomous effect of LC, independent of circulating insulin or testosterone. These findings suggest that restoration of PPARα/PGC-1α-related metabolic regulation and suppression of ER stress may represent local mechanisms contributing to LC-mediated SV protection, while systemic metabolic correction remains an important contextual factor.

Nevertheless, diabetic SV fibrosis is unlikely to be explained solely by SVEC-intrinsic PPARα-associated lipid dysregulation and ER stress-associated apoptosis. T2DM-related chronic inflammation may promote tissue remodeling through inflammatory mediators and profibrotic pathways such as TGF-β/Smad signaling. Recent SV-related studies showed that lysyl oxidase (LOX) promotes diabetic SV atrophy and fibrosis through TGF-β1 signaling, accompanied by CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration, whereas pyroptosis has also been implicated in diabetic SV injury.5,21 In addition, evidence from infectious epididymo-orchitis suggests that S100a4+ macrophages may acquire a myofibroblast-like phenotype and contribute to collagen I and fibronectin production, highlighting a potential role for epithelial-immune crosstalk in male reproductive tract fibrosis.34 Thus, systemic inflammation, TGF-β1 signaling, inflammatory cell death, and immune-mediated remodeling may represent parallel or convergent mechanisms contributing to diabetic SV fibrosis, although their relative contributions remain to be defined.

The possible involvement of ferroptosis in SV fibrosis was also considered in the present study. Previous studies have shown that inhibition of ferroptosis can reduce liver fibrosis and renal fibrosis caused by diabetes.35,36 Our lipidomics enrichment analysis hinted at potential involvement of ferroptosis in diabetic SV. While we observed elevated ROS and MDA levels in HG-treated SVECs, which were attenuated by LC, these markers are not exclusive to ferroptosis. Future studies are warranted to definitively test ferroptosis involvement, for example, by measuring GPX4 activity, SLC7A11 expression, ACSL4, lipid peroxidation-specific markers such as 4-HNE, and by performing rescue experiments with ferroptosis inhibitors.37 Nevertheless, the reduction in ROS/MDA by LC aligns with its known antioxidant properties and further supports its pleiotropic protective effects.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that T2DM promotes SV fibrosis in association with disrupted lipid metabolic homeostasis, impaired PPARα/PGC-1α-related signaling, and activation of ER stress-associated apoptosis. LC ameliorates SV fibrosis in diabetic mice and is associated with improved lipid metabolic profiles, increased PPARα/PGC-1α expression, and reduced ER stress. These findings provide new insight into the pathogenesis of diabetic SV injury and support LC as a candidate intervention worthy of further investigation.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the energy metabolism pattern of SVECs still needs to be further explored, including direct assessment of FAO, glycolysis-related enzyme activity, and mitochondrial function. Second, because primary SVECs have limited proliferative capacity and may be vulnerable to combined genetic manipulation and high-glucose stress, we did not establish a stable PPARα-deficient SVEC model for LC rescue experiments in the present study. Future studies using optimized PPARα knockdown, pharmacological PPARα blockade, or SV-specific PPARα knockout models are needed to determine whether LC-mediated protection depends on PPARα. Third, although sperm quality and testosterone were evaluated, SV-specific functional outcomes, such as seminal plasma composition, fructose secretion, SV-derived secretory proteins, ejaculatory parameters, or fertility/mating outcomes, were not assessed. Therefore, the present data cannot establish that LC-mediated SV rescue directly contributes to reproductive functional recovery.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals and ethical statement

Male C57BL/6 mice (5-week old; 24–28 g; specific pathogen-free [SPF]) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All mice were housed in pathogen-free facilities under a 12–12 h light-dark cycle (lights on at 7 a.m.) and permitted ad libitum access to food and water. At least 3 mice were analyzed for each data point. All animal operations were approved and supervised by the Laboratory Animal Center (DZYJSKT202507160001) and complied with AAALAC animal welfare requirements.

Randomization and blinding

Animals were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence by an investigator not involved in the subsequent experiments or analyses. Group allocation was concealed using coded animal and sample identifiers. Histological scoring and image quantification were performed on coded slides/images by investigators blinded to treatment group. The allocation code was disclosed only after completion of all scoring and quantitative analyses.

Mouse models of T2DM and experimental grouping

To establish a T2DM mouse model, 40 mice were randomly assigned to either the control (CTRL) group or the T2DM model group. To evaluate the potential therapeutic effect of LC on SV fibrosis in diabetic mice, the animals were further subdivided into four groups (n = 10/group): CTRL group, T2DM group, T2DM + LC group and CTRL+LC group. Mice in the CTRL and CTRL+LC groups were fed a standard chow diet and received citrate buffer injections. In contrast, mice in the T2DM and T2DM + LC groups were maintained on a high-fat diet (HFD) for five weeks. The HFD used for T2DM induction provided 60% kcal from fat, 20% kcal from carbohydrate and 20% kcal from protein, with an energy density of approximately 5.24 kcal/g. The mice were subsequently administered intraperitoneal injections of streptozotocin (STZ) (S110910, Aladdin) at a dosage of 50 mg/kg/day for five consecutive days, with the STZ dissolved in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5). One week after STZ administration, fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured after overnight fasting and mice with FBG ≥11.1 mmol/L were considered successful T2DM models. Subsequently, mice in the T2DM + LC and CTRL+LC groups received oral LC (100 mg/kg/day), while the remaining two groups received saline. The dose of LC was selected based on previous mouse studies in which 100 mg/kg/day LC administered by oral gavage restored high-fat diet-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and metabolic abnormalities. To support translational relevance, the human equivalent dose (HED) of LC was estimated using the FDA-recommended body-surface-area conversion method; 100 mg/kg/day in mice corresponds to 486 mg/day for a 60-kg adult, which is below the lower end of established oral therapeutic doses in humans. Body weight and FBG were monitored biweekly. Ten weeks after model establishment, all mice were euthanized for histological and biochemical analyses.

Isolation and culture of primary seminal vesicle epithelial cells

The isolation protocol was adapted from previous studies with modifications.38 Briefly, freshly harvested SV tissues were minced using sterile ophthalmic scissors into approximately 1 mm3 fragments. Tissue digestion was performed in DMEM/F12 medium containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type I and 1 mg/mL hyaluronidase at 37°C for 60 min with constant shaking (150 rpm). The cell suspension was filtered through an 80 μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in complete culture medium consisting of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 1% epithelial cell growth supplement (ECGS). The cell suspension was seeded onto rat tail collagen type I-coated (50 μg/cm2, Corning #354236) culture dishes and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To selectively enrich SVECs, differential plating was employed. After 8 h of initial seeding, the medium containing non-adherent epithelial cells was carefully transferred to new rat tail collagen-coated dishes, while adherent stromal cells (primarily fibroblasts) were discarded. The culture medium was replaced every 48 h with fresh complete medium until cells reached 70–80% confluence.

Primary epithelial cell identity was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining with pan-cytokeratin antibody (1:1000, 26411-1-AP, Proteintech), showing >97% purity in the final culture. To assess possible fibroblast contamination, vimentin immunofluorescence was performed before experimental stimulation and only rare vimentin-positive cells were observed. These purified epithelial cells were then used for subsequent experiments.

High glucose stimulation and osmotic control

Primary SVECs were cultured under normal glucose conditions (NG; 5.5 mM D-glucose), high mannitol osmotic control conditions (HM; 5.5 mM D-glucose plus 24.5 mM D-mannitol), or high glucose conditions (HG; 30 mM D-glucose). Cells were treated for 72 h, after which collagen I, GRP78 and PGC-1α expression levels were assessed by Western blot.

Method details

Evaluation of diabetic mice

After fasting overnight, FBG and fasting insulin (FINS) levels were measured using a glucometer (Sinocare, Changsha, China) and a commercial insulin ELISA kit (D721159, BBI), respectively. Insulin sensitivity was calculated as follows: Insulin sensitivity index = 14.1/[FBG × FINS] × 100. Additionally, HOMA-IR was determined using the formula: HOMA-IR = FBG × FINS/14.1.

Seminal vesicle weight and organ index assessment

The bilateral seminal vesicles were dissected, cleaned of surrounding adipose and connective tissues and weighed immediately. The SV organ index was calculated using the following formula: SV organ index (%) = bilateral SV wet weight (g)/body weight (g) × 100.

Lipid extraction and LC-MS/MS analysis

Quantitative lipidomic profiling was performed using a UPLC-MS/MS-based platform on SV samples from three groups: CTRL, T2DM and T2DM + LC (n = 10 per group). Briefly, approximately 20 mg of frozen mouse SV tissue was thawed on ice and homogenized with a steel bead at 30 Hz for 20 s. Lipids were extracted by adding 1 mL of MTBE: MeOH = 3:1, v/v, extraction solvent containing an internal standard mixture. After vortexing for 15 min, 200 μL of ultrapure water was added to induce phase separation. The samples were vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. A 200 μL aliquot of the upper organic phase was collected and evaporated to dryness. The dried lipid extract was reconstituted in 200 μL of ACN: IPA = 1:1, v/v, vortexed, centrifuged and the supernatant was used for LC-MS/MS analysis. Lipid extracts were analyzed using an ExionLC AD UPLC system coupled to an SCIEX QTRAP 6500+ tandem mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Thermo Accucore C30 column. Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile/water = 60/40, v/v, containing 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate, and mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile/isopropanol = 10/90, v/v, containing 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 45°C and the injection volume was 2 μL. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes using multiple reaction monitoring.

Lipid identification, quantification and quality control

Lipid species were annotated using the Metware in-house lipid database, MWDB, based on retention time and precursor/product ion-pair information. Quantification was performed using the internal standard method. Chromatographic peaks were integrated and corrected and lipid contents were calculated according to the following equation: X = 0.001×R×c×F×V/m, where X represents lipid content, R represents the analyte/internal standard peak area ratio, F represents the correction factor for different lipid classes, c represents the internal standard concentration, V represents the extraction volume and m represents the tissue weight. Lipid abundance data were therefore normalized to internal standards and tissue weight before downstream analysis. Pooled QC samples were prepared by mixing equal aliquots from all study samples and were periodically injected during the analytical sequence, generally after every 10 study samples, to monitor analytical stability. Instrument performance and data reproducibility were assessed using QC total ion current overlap, Pearson correlation analysis among QC samples and QC coefficient-of-variation distribution. No evident batch effect was observed based on these QC assessments; therefore, no additional computational batch-correction algorithm was applied beyond internal standard correction, tissue-weight normalization and peak integration correction.

Lipidomics data processing and analysis

Data were log2-transformed and mean-centered prior to OPLS-DA performed with the MetaboAnalystR package in R (www.r-project.org). Metabolites with VIP (variable importance in projection) scores >1 and Student’s t test p values <0.05 were considered significant. A permutation test (200 iterations) validated model robustness against overfitting. Significant metabolites were annotated against the KEGG Compound database and mapped to KEGG pathways. Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) assessed pathway significance using a hypergeometric test.

Analysis of serum testosterone

To minimize the influence of diurnal variation on circulating testosterone levels, blood samples were collected between 08:00 and 10:00 a.m. under a fixed 12-h light/dark cycle. Blood samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C to obtain serum. Serum concentrations of testosterone were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit (JL25196, Jonlnbio) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocols.

Sperm collection and analysis

The caudal epididymis was dissected from each mouse and immersed in 200 μL PBS buffer. The tissue was carefully minced using sterile ophthalmic scissors and incubated at 37°C for 15 min to allow sperm release. A 10 μL aliquot of the resulting sperm suspension was placed on a glass slide and covered with a coverslip. Sperm count and motility were assessed using light microscopy at 200× magnification.

Histopathological analysis

SV tissues were excised from mice and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 h. After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Histopathological evaluation was performed using seminal vesicle tissues from 3 mice per group. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a descending ethanol series, stained with hematoxylin, differentiated, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral resin. H&E-stained sections were used to evaluate general tissue morphology, including glandular architecture, epithelial integrity, ductal structure and stromal remodeling. Collagen deposition and fibrotic remodeling were assessed using a commercial Masson’s trichrome staining kit (G1340, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained sequentially with the reagents provided in the kit, differentiated, dehydrated, cleared and mounted. Histopathological changes were evaluated descriptively based on representative microscopic images.

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells in SV tissues were detected using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay. Following standard dewaxing and rehydration procedures, tissue sections were treated with proteinase K (20 μg/mL) for 15 min at 37°C for permeabilization. Sections were then incubated with 50 μL of TUNEL reaction mixture for 60 min at 37°C. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 5 μg/mL) for 5 min at room temperature.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 (when required) and blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. After primary antibody incubation overnight at 4°C, cells were treated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1 μg/mL) for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (NIB610-FL, Nexcope), with three random fields captured per sample for quantitative analysis.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent dewaxing and rehydration through graded alcohols. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C for 20 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibody at 4°C overnight, followed by corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Following PBS washes, immunoreactivity was developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Stained sections were imaged under bright-field microscopy (Eclipse 80i, Nikon), with three random fields captured per sample at 200× magnification.

Western blot

Seminal vesicle tissue and cell samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce/Thermo Scientific). Aliquots containing 10–40 μg protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Germany). After blocking with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Secondary antibodies included HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (LF101, 1:5000, Epizyme) and HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (LF102, 1:5000, Epizyme). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software. For Western blot analyses, all reported n values represent biological replicates rather than technical replicates. Each biological replicate was generated from an independently collected or prepared sample and subjected to the indicated experimental treatment. Technical repeats were not counted as independent n values for statistical analysis.

Intracellular MDA and ROS assays

After incubating the well-cultured SVECs with high glucose (HG; 30 mM) and LC (1 mM) for 24 h, the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using MDA assay kits (S0131, Beyotime) and ROS assay kits (S0033, Beyotime), respectively, while the protein concentration of the cell samples was determined using a BCA protein assay kit.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s or Brown–Forsythe test where appropriate. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used when the data met parametric assumptions. For comparisons among more than two independent groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test when all pairwise comparisons were relevant. Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used when multiple treatment groups were compared with a predefined control group. For data that did not meet the assumptions of parametric testing, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test was used, as appropriate. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
