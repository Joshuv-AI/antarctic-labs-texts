Self-sensing of load and deformation in concrete using intrinsic capacitance and resistance
Authors: Murat Ozturk, Asiye Akis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the electromechanical behavior and self-sensing performance of plain and reinforced concrete specimens under flexural loading through real-time electrical capacitance and resistance measurements. The primary objective is to assess the feasibility of using these electrical parameters as indicators of stress and deformation without relying on conductive fillers and external or embedded sensors. Concrete and reinforced concrete beams were prepared with standard mix proportions, and electrical measurements were conducted during three-point bending tests using an LCR meter operating at 2 kHz. Experimental results reveal a strong linear correlation between mechanical load and fractional changes in both electrical capacitance and resistance for unreinforced specimens, with R² values of 0.99 and 0.98, respectively. The peak fractional changes in these specimens reached 3.13% and 3.01%, confirming their sensitivity to stress-induced microstructural changes. In reinforced concrete specimens, which exhibited ductile behavior due to steel reinforcement, maximum changes in capacitance and resistance were observed as 8.89% ± 0.13 and 10.62% ± 1.48, respectively. Notably, capacitance demonstrated higher correlation with structural deflection (R² = 0.97 ± 0.02), whereas resistance was more responsive to crack propagation but exhibited greater signal fluctuation. These findings establish capacitance as a stable indicator for elastic deformation and resistance as a damage-sensitive signal. The proposed method enables continuous, non-destructive damage detection and deformation tracking in both plain and reinforced concrete, making it suitable for integration into future innovative infrastructure systems.

Introduction

Smart structural materials are broadly defined as materials capable of sensing their own state or condition1. This sensing functionality is typically enabled by establishing a correlation between the material’s electrical properties (such as resistance or capacitance) and changes in mechanical, thermal, or environmental parameters—including stress2–4, strain5–7, temperature8–10, and moisture content11–13. In essence, variations occurring within the structure are monitored through measurable changes in its electrical behavior, making these materials self-sensing and potentially autonomous in performance evaluation.

Initially developed for aerospace14, automotive15, and manufacturing industries16, smart materials have increasingly found relevance in civil engineering applications, particularly in the construction of safer, more resilient, and cost-effective infrastructure systems17. The integration of sensing functionality into structural materials eliminates the need for discrete sensor devices, thereby enabling real-time condition monitoring, enhancing structural health management, and reducing maintenance demands throughout the service life of a structure.

Among the various smart functionalities, self-stress sensing—the ability of a material to detect internal mechanical stress—is particularly valuable. Within the elastic range, stress and strain are linearly related; therefore, measuring either allows for the estimation of the other18. This capability facilitates the detection of micro-level deformations, which are essential for early-stage damage detection and timely preventive maintenance19. Particularly in critical infrastructures such as bridges, tunnels, dams, and residential buildings, such early warning mechanisms can play a pivotal role in avoiding catastrophic failure and extending service life.

Traditionally, structural sensing is achieved through attached or embedded sensors20. However, external sensors are often vulnerable to environmental degradation and may require regular maintenance, while embedded sensors can compromise the integrity of the host material and are difficult to repair or replace. To overcome these limitations, recent research has focused on transforming the structural material itself into a self-sensing system. This is achieved by correlating the material’s inherent electrical response to mechanical changes, without the need for discrete sensing devices21.

Self-sensing behavior in materials is generally characterized using two primary electrical measurement strategies: resistance-based and capacitance-based sensing22. Resistance-based sensing typically requires the introduction of electrically conductive fillers to reduce the otherwise insulating nature of cementitious materials. These conductive networks allow the material to exhibit piezoresistive behavior, where deformation alters the electrical resistance23. Capacitance-based sensing, on the other hand, can function without the need for conductive additives, which presents significant advantages in terms of material simplicity, cost, and retrofitting feasibility24. Beyond these two primary methods, other electro-mechanical sensing techniques—such as piezoelectric25–29, fiber optic30, and electromagnetic sensors11,31–36—have also been applied in concrete and reinforced concrete to monitor strain, cracks, moisture, and internal defects.

The sensing capability of a structural material depends on how external influences (e.g., mechanical load, environmental effects) impact its electrical properties. In this context, reversible changes in electrical characteristics correspond to self-stress sensing, while irreversible changes are indicative of deformation sensing or damage accumulation37. Although resistance-based methods have been widely studied in the literature, capacitance-based self-sensing approaches remain relatively underexplored. Notably, most capacitance-based studies have focused on cement paste systems38–40, which consist solely of cement and water. However, in practical applications, concrete—a composite of cement, water, fine and coarse aggregates—is the standard construction material. Therefore, expanding self-sensing research from paste to concrete systems is both necessary and timely.

Moreover, in reinforced concrete structures, which combine concrete with embedded steel reinforcement, the application of capacitance-based sensing holds significant promise. The interaction between the concrete matrix and steel bars offers new pathways for electrical response that can be harnessed for advanced structural monitoring. Yet, the potential of this approach remains largely unexplored. Addressing this gap, the present study investigates the self-stress and deformation sensing behavior of both concrete and reinforced concrete prism specimens, focusing on the correlation between applied mechanical loads and changes in electrical resistance and capacitance under quasi-static loading conditions. This dual-mode sensing approach, applied to both plain and reinforced concrete, represents a novel contribution by demonstrating how intrinsic electrical properties can serve as effective indicators of both load and damage evolution, without the need for conductive additives or external sensors.

Through this approach, the study aims to evaluate and distinguish the electrical signatures corresponding to elastic deformation and microstructural damage, thereby contributing to the development of smart, multifunctional building materials that can perform real-time structural assessments without the need for conductive fillers or embedded electronic devices.

Materials and methods

Concrete mixtures are produced using Portland cement (CEM I 42.5R), tap water, and both fine and coarse aggregates. The fine aggregate possesses a bulk density of 2.7 g/cm³ and a maximum grain size of 4 mm, while the coarse aggregate has a density of 2.65 g/cm³ and a maximum particle size of 10 mm. These components are proportioned by weight in a ratio of 1:3:5 (cement: fine aggregate: coarse aggregate), with the fine and coarse aggregates used in equal measure. For the reinforced concrete samples, a standard concrete cover of 20 mm is maintained. During the preparation process, the dry constituents are first mixed in a pan mixer at a controlled speed. Water is then gradually incorporated, and mixing continues until a uniform and workable consistency is reached. The fresh mixture is cast into molds measuring 100 × 100 × 300 mm, which have been lubricated to facilitate demolding. For reinforced specimens, steel reinforcement is installed before casting. This reinforcement includes two 8 mm diameter bars in the tension zone, two 8 mm bars in the compression zone, and stirrups of the same diameter arranged at 70 mm intervals. This configuration is chosen to prevent brittle failure and replicate common reinforcement conditions, without investigating the influence of stirrup quantity. The dimensions of the concrete prism specimens are not selected based on a specific standard but rather chosen for their practicality in specimen preparation and compatibility with the test setup. Since the primary objective of this study is to investigate the self-sensing behavior of concrete and reinforced concrete, rather than to characterize their mechanical performance, these dimensions are deemed sufficient to evaluate the electrical response under controlled flexural loading. After casting, the samples are demolded 24 h later and cured by spraying water once daily for a period of 15 days. While full immersion curing for 28 days is the standard approach in laboratory settings, this method was modified to reflect more realistic curing conditions observed in field applications, where continuous immersion is rarely feasible. Instead, water spraying is commonly employed on construction sites to maintain surface moisture.

Upon completion of the curing period, aluminum strips measuring 0.024 mm × 30 mm × 300 mm are applied to the tensile faces of the reinforced concrete specimens, serving as electrodes for electrical testing. Each strip maintains 100 mm contact with the surface to ensure reliable signal transmission, while the remaining 200 mm serves as a connector for measurement devices. The strips are affixed using a 1 mm thick double-sided adhesive. The double-sided adhesive tape also serves as a dielectric film. Since LCR meters are designed to measure the capacitance of dielectric materials rather than conductive ones, the use of a dielectric layer becomes necessary38. Although the electrical conductivity of the tested concrete is low and the use of such a film is not strictly required, it was applied to ensure stable and consistent electrode contact throughout the measurements. Electrode stability is verified through repeated baseline capacitance readings, which showed a standard deviation limited to ± 0.5 pF. Although specific impedance calibration of the LCR-6000 was not performed during this study, the instrument has been previously validated using reference ceramic capacitors. Capacitive measurements are conducted at an applied AC voltage of 1.000 V and a fixed frequency of 2 kHz, which is selected based on the dielectric characteristics of cementitious materials. The frequency of 2 kHz is chosen for its accessibility and alignment with previous studies4,37,41. While gigahertz frequencies are suited to radio and microwave propagation analyses, the present work centers on capacitive sensing rather than radar-based applications. Furthermore, a low excitation voltage of 1.000 V is employed to ensure that the specimens remain free from heating, as higher voltages may cause temperature rise through the Joule heating mechanism. The tests use a resistor-capacitor series configuration, which is preferred due to its precision in capturing interactions between resistance and capacitance, especially where variations in electrical properties are subtle.

Mechanical testing involves a three-point bending setup with a support span of 250 mm. The samples are loaded at a constant rate of 0.5 kN/min using a hydraulic testing machine operating under displacement control. The machine’s integrated step-control system captures displacement data. Load and deformation values are logged concurrently, while real-time electrical readings of resistance and capacitance are obtained using an LCR meter. This dual approach enables the continuous monitoring of both the structural response and electrical behavior, thereby supporting the evaluation of self-sensing capabilities in both plain and reinforced concrete specimens. While the absence of direct crack width or quantity measurements is a limitation of this study, the three-point bending configuration introduces a predictable tensile stress field that leads to crack initiation and propagation along a known path. This allows the observed changes in resistance and capacitance to be indirectly—but reliably—correlated with the progression of microcracking. The details of the capacitance measurements are thoroughly described in the References37,42,43. Figure 3 shows the self-sensing measurement setup.

Results and discussion

Figure 4 illustrates the load-deflection behavior of both plain concrete and reinforced concrete specimens under three-point bending conditions. For the plain concrete, a linear load-deflection relationship is observed until an abrupt fracture occurs at a maximum load of 12.67 ± 0.58 kN. This sudden failure indicates that the specimen exhibits a brittle failure mode with minimal post-peak deformation capacity44. In contrast, the reinforced concrete specimen demonstrates a notably different response under identical loading conditions. A substantially higher peak load of 37.72 ± 1.23 kN is recorded, which is attributed to the mechanical contribution of the internal steel reinforcement45. The gradual decline in load following the peak implies a redistribution of stresses and progressive microcrack development rather than catastrophic failure46.

Figure 5 presents the evolution of fractional changes in capacitance and electrical resistance for the plain concrete specimen as the applied load increases. Initially, both parameters follow a relatively stable trajectory, indicating structural integrity. As loading progresses, a proportional rise in both capacitance and resistance is observed, corresponding to the accumulation of microstructural damage41. At the point of fracture, a sudden drop in the load is mirrored by sharp discontinuities in the electrical measurements. The maximum fractional change in capacitance reaches 3.13 ± 0.24%, while the resistance shows a slightly lower change of 3.01 ± 0.64%. The near-identical magnitude of both responses suggests that either can serve as a reliable indicator of stress-induced damage in the early stages of development.

In Fig. 6 (a-b), the electrical response of the reinforced concrete specimen is shown under the same mechanical loading protocol. The resistance and capacitance both change in response to applied stress, but their behaviors diverge post-peak. Capacitance continues to decrease gradually even after the peak load, reflecting ongoing deformation and the progressive formation of cracks and voids within the matrix. This sustained response indicates that capacitance is more sensitive to irreversible structural changes such as crack propagation42. On the other hand, resistance shows a peak corresponding closely to the mechanical load maximum and subsequently declines as the load decreases. This pattern suggests that resistance more closely tracks stress variations, whereas capacitance better correlates with strain or deflection. At the maximum load, the fractional change in capacitance is measured at 8.89 ± 0.13%, whereas resistance increases by 10.62 ± 1.48%. Despite the larger resistance change, capacitance provides a more stable trend throughout the loading history, enhancing its utility for long-term monitoring of structural performance.

The relationship between electrical properties and mechanical deformation is further clarified in Fig. 6 (c-d). This figure shows a nearly linear relationship between mid-span deflection and the fractional change in capacitance. Resistance, while still responsive, exhibits greater fluctuation and nonlinearity, especially after the yield point.

Figure 7 focuses on the linear relationship between applied load and the electrical responses in plain concrete. It should be noted that Fig. 7 reflects the behavior of plain concrete, which undergoes brittle failure shortly after reaching peak load. The continued increase in capacitance following peak load is attributed to ongoing structural deformation and widening of internal cracks, despite the reduction in applied force. This response differs from that of reinforced concrete (as shown in Fig. 6), where reinforcement delays complete structural failure and limits post-peak deflection. Therefore, the observed capacitance trends are consistent with the respective failure mechanisms. The R² value for the load-capacitance correlation is calculated as 0.99, indicating near-perfect linearity and minimal signal deviation. The load-resistance correlation is slightly lower at R² = 0.98, but still indicates a strong association. These trends reflect that in unreinforced specimens, where the structural complexity is lower, both capacitance and resistance effectively capture the progression of mechanical stress. The changes in capacitance are attributed to increases in electrode spacing and microcrack-induced dielectric changes43, while resistance increases are likely caused by disruption of the electrical conduction paths due to crack growth47.

In reinforced concrete, shown in Fig. 8 (a-b), the load-capacitance relationship becomes less linear as a result of the complex interaction between the concrete matrix and the steel reinforcement. The capacitance R² value is recorded as 0.93 ± 0.05, while resistance correlation improves slightly to 0.95 ± 0.07. The deviation from linearity in capacitance measurements is attributed to the layered response of the composite system, where cracks form and close in different zones at different load levels. Despite these complexities, the results indicate that both parameters continue to exhibit strong predictive capability.

Finally, Figs. 8(c) and 8 (d) illustrate the correlation between deflection and variations in electrical parameters. The capacitance-deflection curve exhibits an R² of 0.97 ± 0.02, indicating excellent agreement and supporting the premise that capacitance is a robust indicator of physical deformation in reinforced systems. The resistance-deflection curve, however, reveals an R² of 0.89 ± 0.09, suggesting that resistance is more susceptible to fluctuations and less consistent in capturing cumulative strain. The greater stability and linearity of capacitance measurements establish this parameter as a preferred tool for continuous structural health monitoring, particularly in detecting gradual deformations and localized damage.

The observed variations in capacitance and electrical resistance under mechanical loading originate from the fundamental electromechanical interactions occurring within the concrete matrix. In the case of capacitance, the concrete acts as a dielectric medium situated between embedded or surface-mounted conductive electrodes40,48. As flexural stress is applied, internal microstructural changes—particularly crack initiation, pore redistribution, and localized deformation—lead to alterations in the effective dielectric constant and the geometry of the capacitor system. Specifically, tensile stress induces an increase in the spacing between electrodes, especially across developing cracks, which reduces the capacitance due to its inverse proportionality to electrode separation. Simultaneously, microcrack formation alters the dielectric permittivity by disrupting the uniformity of the matrix, further contributing to capacitive fluctuations. These changes are reversible in the elastic regime and can thus be directly correlated with strain evolution.

For resistance, the mechanism involves disruption of the conductive pathways within the composite structure49. Concrete inherently possesses limited ionic conductivity due to its pore solution, and any increase in crack density or width directly impedes the flow of electric current, thereby elevating the material’s overall resistance50. In other words, despite the inherently low intrinsic conductivity of concrete, ionic transport through the pore solution represents the primary conduction mechanism. This ionic connectivity, however, becomes increasingly disrupted as cracks form and propagate within the matrix, thereby hindering charge transfer. The presence of reinforcement or conductive additives can introduce additional complexity; for instance, steel reinforcement may create partial bridging paths for current, while crack propagation interrupts these paths, resulting in spikes in resistance. In reinforced specimens, as tensile cracks widen and propagate along the tensile zone, the resistance increases due to the disconnection of continuous conduction routes and the elongation of current pathways.

In summary, the lower fluctuation observed in capacitance measurements compared to resistance can be attributed to the fundamentally different sensing mechanisms involved. Resistance responds sharply to localized events such as microcrack initiation or disruptions in ionic pathways, leading to abrupt and irregular changes in the signal. Capacitance, on the other hand, is mainly governed by uniform geometric deformation and variations in the material’s dielectric properties under load. Consequently, the capacitance signal evolves more smoothly, capturing global strain behavior rather than isolated damage, and thus exhibits significantly less fluctuation during the loading process.

Together, these phenomena reflect the dual sensitivity of capacitance and resistance to structural and microstructural evolution under load. While resistance provides sharp, load-sensitive feedback primarily tied to discontinuities in electrical conduction, capacitance offers a more stable and gradual response to strain and deformation. The combined use of both metrics enables a comprehensive sensing strategy that captures both instantaneous load effects and cumulative structural damage, reinforcing the viability of self-sensing concrete systems for real-time structural health monitoring. Additionally, although long-term performance evaluation is beyond the scope of this study, no signal drift or instability is observed during the full duration of testing. The stability of both resistance and capacitance signals under gradually increasing load indicates that the sensing response is reliable within the tested time frame. This provides a strong foundation for the technique’s short-term applicability. Future research may focus on extended-duration and cyclic loading scenarios to systematically evaluate long-term durability, repeatability, and the potential influence of cumulative damage on sensing accuracy.

Conclusion

This study investigates the self-sensing capabilities of both plain and reinforced concrete specimens under flexural loading using capacitance and resistance measurements. A comprehensive experimental program is carried out to explore the electrical responses of cementitious materials to mechanical deformation, with a particular focus on correlating structural behavior with real-time electrical signal variations. The results demonstrate that capacitance and resistance values serve as effective indicators of stress and deformation within cement-based composites. In plain concrete specimens, a linear correlation is observed between the applied load and fractional changes in both capacitance and resistance up to the point of brittle failure. In reinforced concrete specimens, the electrical responses reflect the more ductile and progressive nature of damage evolution, with capacitance continuing to change beyond peak load, while resistance tracks the load curve more directly.

Capacitance exhibits superior stability and linearity in relation to deflection, making it a reliable parameter for detecting cumulative strain and structural integrity. Conversely, resistance shows higher sensitivity to sudden changes in load but is more susceptible to signal fluctuation, particularly in the post-peak regime. These complementary characteristics suggest that combining both electrical parameters can enhance the accuracy and robustness of self-sensing systems in civil infrastructure. The underlying electromechanical mechanisms responsible for these observations are rooted in alterations to the dielectric property and disruption of conductive paths caused by microcracking and deformation. Capacitance changes are primarily attributed to variations in electrode separation and dielectric permittivity, while resistance increases are driven by elongation and discontinuity of conductive pathways.

Ultimately, this work validates the feasibility of employing capacitive and resistive sensing techniques for real-time, non-destructive monitoring of concrete and reinforced concrete elements. Electrical measurements can be particularly valuable in large-scale structures such as bridges and dams, where visual inspection is limited or impractical. By integrating these measurements with remote data acquisition systems, critical information can be collected and transmitted to centralized monitoring platforms. This enables the continuous assessment of damage evolution, supporting timely decisions regarding maintenance, repair, or even structural decommissioning. The findings emphasize the potential for developing self-sensing structural components that enable early detection of damage, improving maintenance strategies, extending service life, and enhancing the safety and resilience of built environments. Future research should investigate the integration of these sensing systems into comprehensive structural health monitoring frameworks and evaluate their long-term performance under various environmental and cyclic loading conditions, including the effects of temperature and humidity fluctuations.
