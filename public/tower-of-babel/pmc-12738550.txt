Advanced prediction of combustion phasing in methane homogeneous engines via in-cylinder ion current profiling and a novel ionic kinetics framework
Authors: Elaheh Neshat, Amin Motevali Imami, Damon Honnery
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a reduced ionic chemical kinetics mechanism for predicting combustion phasing in methane-fueled homogeneous charge compression ignition (HCCI) engines. Starting from the detailed GRI-Mech 3.0 mechanism, a reduced scheme with 22 species and 48 reactions was developed. Ionic reactions were then added, forming a comprehensive mechanism with 27 species and 54 reactions. The mechanism was integrated into a multi-zone combustion model and validated against experimental data from a CFR engine under four operating conditions. Results show that the mechanism accurately predicts the start of combustion (SOC), with a maximum error below 0.09%. In-cylinder pressure and temperature profiles closely match experimental data. The model also captures the behavior of key radicals and ions, including H₃O⁺, OH⁻, and O₂⁻. Exhaust emissions such as CO and CO₂ are predicted with relative errors under 12%, while UHC predictions show moderate discrepancies. Notably, electrons are fully consumed during combustion, while some ions remain in the exhaust. The proposed mechanism offers a reliable and computationally efficient tool for combustion diagnostics and control, with potential applications in low-temperature and fuel-flexible engine technologies.Keywords: Ion current, Chemical kinetics mechanism, HCCI combustion, MethaneSubject terms: Engineering, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Natural gas is one of the most important types of fossil energy source is widely used as fuel in combustion and reforming processes1,2. The most important component of natural gas is methane, due to having only one carbon in its chemical structure, it leads to the production of less carbon pollutant during combustion processes3,4. Due to its high calorific value, easy transportability, and numerous available sources, today this fuel is used as the main fuel in many combustion processes5,6. Combustion is a chemical process based on various reactions. During these reactions, many different species are produced and significant energy is released. For combustion modeling, existing reactions and species involved in them should be identified and accurate chemical kinetic mechanisms should be developed7,8.

Several chemical kinetic mechanisms have been developed to model the methane combustion process9. GRI mechanism is the most important chemical kinetic mechanism developed for the simulation of methane and natural gas combustion10. In many studies, this mechanism has been used as the main mechanism, and its accuracy in predicting numerous combustion phenomena has been proven11–13. The latest version of this mechanism, GRI Mech.3, contains 53 species and 325 reactions. In this mechanism, NOx related reactions are also considered. Hughes et al. proposed a chemical kinetic mechanism consisting of 351 reactions and 37 species. The mechanism was named as Leeds mechanism and experimentally tested for various operational conditions and its accuracy was compared with previous mechanisms including GRI, Chevalier, and Konnov mechanisms14. These two mechanisms (GRI mech. And Leeds), unlike other mechanisms, have been developed only for modeling natural gas combustion. Other mechanisms are generally designed to simulate the combustion of hydrocarbons15–20.

Several reduced mechanisms have been developed using GRI mech. to model methane combustion. Frenklach and Kazakov used the GRI-2.11 mechanism as a reference to introduce a reduced skeletal mechanism with 84 reactions and 19 species for methane combustion (DRM-19)21. Fotovat and Rahimpour reduced the GRI 3.0 mechanism by the direct relation graph with error propagation followed by the full species sensitivity analysis. This approach reduced the number of species from 53 to 2322.

With the advancement of combustion technologies and fuel blending strategies, numerous chemical kinetic mechanisms have been developed simulating the combustion behavior of natural gas-based fuel blends, enabling better prediction of ignition characteristics, pollutant formation, and overall engine performance. In the technical literature, there are various mechanisms for the blended fuel of methane and n-heptane11, the blended fuel of hydrogen and methane23, the blended fuel of natural gas and C1 to C7 alkanes24, the blended fuel of methane and diesel25, the blended fuel of methane and syngas26, CO2 and methane27, etc.

Although at the beginning of the development of chemical kinetics mechanisms, it seemed that the combustion chamber is a pool of radicals and species, but with the conducting more studies, it became clear that ions and electrons are also formed during hydrocarbons combustion. Therefore, new researches aimed at simulate the formation of ions during hydrocarbons combustion and their role in combustion characteristics28–30. The first ionic chemical kinetics mechanism to simulate ion current during methane combustion was presented by Pederson and Brown. The proposed mechanism had 5 positive ions, but no negative ions were considered in it31. Prager et al. modeled the ion chemistry methane – air flames. 11 ions and 69 ionic reactions were considered. In this mechanism, the method of ions formation from radicals is not known, and no connection between ions and stable species or radicals has been reported. In addition, the presented mechanism has not provided acceptable accuracy in comparison with the experimental data32. Chen et al. studied on ion chemistry during methane rich combustion according to the results H3O+ was the most important ion in slightly rich flames and C3H3+ was the most significant in very rich flames33. Knyazkov et al. studied on cations formation in near-stoichiometric burner-stabilized methane/oxygen/argon flames at atmospheric pressure. They revised the previously published mechanisms for cations formation and presented new paths. The results showed that the accuracy of the mechanism is improved using new cation base reactions34. A reduced mechanism for the prediction of methane/air flames impinged by external electric fields was presented by Renzo and Cuenot. This mechanism was composed of 26 species and 134 reactions. Thus, the mechanism guaranteed a significant reduction of the computational cost without penalties in the accuracy of electrified methane/air flames calculations35. López-Cámara et al. presented a reduced chemical kinetic model for methane-air combustion which included minor excited species and charged species. The mechanism included 45 species and 216 reaction and was validated for a two-dimensional flame geometry by comparison with detailed chemistry simulation results for species location and peak values, and experimental chemiluminescence location36. Cherepanov et al. improved ion chemistry in flames considering C3H3+ reaction pathways. They validated the mechanism against mass spectrometric measurements in non-sooting premixed flames of small aliphatic hydrocarbons. They also experimentally detected many CxHy+ cations not included in their mechanism and discussed the possible reaction pathways leading to CxHy+ production37.

According to previous studies, the onset of ignition has been shown to coincide with the initiation of the ion current within combustion chambers. The main objective of this study is to develop a reduced ionic chemical kinetics mechanism capable of predicting the ignition timing during homogeneous methane combustion. To achieve this, the HCCI combustion chamber is modeled as a homogeneous reactor. Using the GRI-Mech mechanism as a baseline, the scheme is first reduced, and then supplemented with relevant ionic reactions and charged species to construct the final mechanism.

Methodology

In the present study at first the previously published multi zone model is coupled to the semi-detailed chemical kinetics mechanism and is validated against experimental data. Then the validated cases are used to specify the important reactions at the start of combustion (SOC), at the time that 50% of cumulative heat is released (CA50) and at the end of combustion (EOC) process (when the 90% of cumulative heat is released). The new reduced chemical kinetics mechanism is created using the specified important reactions and the species participating in them. At the next step the reduced chemical kinetics is coupled to multi zone model and is validated against experimental data. Finally the ions and related reactions are added to the chemical kinetics mechanism and the applicability of ionic chemical kinetics mechanism in prediction of SOC of the methane fueled HCCI engine is examined.

Multi zone model

Figure 1 illustrates the zone configuration in the developed multi-zone model, which consists of four distinct types of zones: crevice zone, boundary layer zone, outer zones, and core zone.

The zone configuration of multi zone model.

The crevice zone maintains a constant volume, equivalent to 3% of the clearance volume. This zone does not engage in heat transfer with other zones or the walls, resulting in a stable temperature that matches the wall temperature. Mass transfer occurs bidirectionally between the boundary layer zone and the crevice zone. The crevice zone refers to the narrow regions between piston rings and the cylinder wall. During the compression stroke, a portion of the in-cylinder gas escapes into this zone due to elevated pressure. Upon expansion, these trapped gases are released back into the combustion chamber. Because of the lower temperature in the crevice zone and limited flame penetration, hydrocarbons present in this region may not undergo complete combustion. As a result, they can either oxidize partially to form CO or remain unburned, contributing to unburned hydrocarbon (UHC) emissions. Therefore, incorporating the crevice zone into the multi-zone model and assigning a realistic volume to it plays a significant role in accurately predicting exhaust emissions. This inclusion helps capture the local conditions that affect post-combustion pollutant formation and strengthens the model’s environmental relevance. The boundary layer zone is the closest zone to the wall, forming a hollow cylindrical structure that remains in contact with the cylinder wall, cylinder head, and piston head. Its thickness varies proportionally with the cylinder height near the cylinder head and piston head, while maintaining a similar proportion relative to the bore near the cylinder wall. Consequently, its thickness fluctuates over time in the axial direction but remains constant in the radial direction. This zone interfaces with all other zones, facilitating both mass and heat transfer in multiple directions. Additionally, convective heat transfer occurs between this zone and all walls of the combustion chamber. The multi-zone model integrates a chemical kinetic mechanism to simulate combustion chemistry. Ionic chemical kinetics mechanism

A detailed mechanism consisting of 53 species and 325 reactions has been implemented to develop the reduced mechanism10. One of the key approaches to reducing mechanisms is the identification and elimination of unessential reactions. Several methods have been proposed in the literature for this purpose, including: Comparing progress rates and Selecting reactions with lower relative progress rates compared to the basic reaction40, Sorting by progress rate: Prioritizing reactions based on their progress rates and selecting those with the lowest values41 Applying threshold criteria: Defining a user-specified threshold and selecting reactions with progress rates below this limit42 Among these methods, sorting reactions based on their progress rate has proven to be the most effective and reliable43. Using this approach, species involved in unessential reactions are removed from the mechanism. Various studies have employed this strategy for mechanism reduction44–46.

In this study, a reduced ionic mechanism has been developed to predict HCCI engine performance. The process began by selecting different cases, followed by identifying essential reactions based on their progress rates at three critical combustion stages: the start of combustion, CA50, and end of combustion. To ensure an accurate reduced ionic mechanism, an optimal number of essential reactions must be selected carefully, avoiding the elimination of significant combustion pathways. Several reduced mechanisms have been explored, incorporating different numbers of essential reactions—ranging from 20 to 50 reactions (specifically, 20, 30, 35, 40, 45, and 50). The accuracy of each mechanism has been assessed using experimental data. Ultimately, the mechanism with the lowest number of reactions that still yields accurate results is presented as the optimal chemical kinetics mechanism. By choosing important reactions, the species that participated in these reactions were also selected and finally a mechanism with 48 reactions and 22 species was formed. Results

Four different engine operating conditions are selected and used to validate the accuracy of the multi zone model and reduced ionic chemical kinetics mechanism. The numerical in-cylinder pressure and engine exhaust emissions are compared with the experimental data. Experimental data was obtained from University of Alberta engine research facility. All experiments were performed using a Waukesha CFR (Cooperative Fuel Research) single cylinder research engine coupled to a DC motoring dynamometer. The engine was maintained at two constant speeds of 700 and 800 rpm and ran with an open throttle. The intake system included a 2.4 kW heater with a PID temperature controller to preheat intake air when required. Two types of fuel injectors, one for the n-heptane and the other for natural gas (NG), were located upstream of the intake valve to facilitate proper mixing. Air and fuel flow are crucial measurements for a precise determination of air/fuel ratio. A TSI model 4235–01-01 air mass flow meter was installed upstream of the engine before the pulsation damping barrel. Omega FMA21 series flow meters were used to measure the gaseous fuel mass flow rate. Two Max Machinery positive displacement flow meters, model 213–311, were used in the liquid fuel supply line. The control module of an AFS Sparrow-II engine was utilized to regulate the injection rate of each fuel separately or as a blend. External uncooled exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) line was connected to the intake after the heater and before the fuel injectors and was controlled by a manual butterfly valve. Intake gas was analyzed for the EGR fraction determination. A Vetronix PXA-1100 portable gas analyzer, which is capable of measuring different gases, was used in the intake system and EGR connection to the intake plenum to determine the carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration in the intake mixture. The lubricating oil was maintained at a constant temperature using a heater. A BEI rotary incremental encoder with a resolution of 0.1 crank angle degree (CAD) was used to monitor engine rotational speed and coordinate the pressure trace with respect to crank position. Cylinder pressure was measured with a Kistler 6043A, water-cooled pressure transducer in combination with a Kistler 507 charge amplifier. The pressure transducer was mounted in the cylinder wall close to the cylinder head. Experimental data were acquired using a personal computer running custom Labview software and using three high sampling rate NI PCI-MIO-16E1 data acquisition cards51,52.

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is another significant radical in the combustion process. Similar to hydroperoxyl (HO₂), it forms during initial reactions such as hydrogen abstraction and eventually converts to water. Additionally, H₂O₂ can be produced in termination reactions involving HO₂ and hydroxyl (OH) radicals. If the chamber temperature is sufficiently high, the radicals generated through these pathways readily transform into water. However, under low-temperature conditions, H₂O₂ may persist and remain as part of the exhaust mixture. The OH radical, likewise, is formed through hydrogen abstraction from the fuel molecule or via reactions between atomic hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O). It too ultimately transforms into water by the end of combustion. Inside the combustion chamber, OH, HO₂, and H₂O₂ radicals share similar formation and consumption pathways. All three emerge from initial and chain propagation reactions and eventually convert into water through termination steps. These radicals can interconvert during various intermediate reaction. Figure 8 visualizes the interactions among these radicals. As shown, Reaction R7 initiates earlier than the others, while Reaction R13 exhibits the highest progress rate, leading to the conversion of OH radicals into water. In contrast, Reactions R16, R21, and R22 demonstrate considerably lower rates compared to the other reactions.

Progress rates of OH, HO2, and H2O2 related reactions.

The results show that this modeling approach enhances the predictive capability for ignition timing and combustion phasing, thereby contributing to the design and control of advanced low-temperature combustion systems. By incorporating ionic reaction pathways into the kinetic scheme, the model facilitates improved diagnosis of combustion events through simulation of ion current signals. Such integration enables the potential deployment of ion current–based control strategies, linking chemical kinetics to practical feedback systems for real-time engine management. In contrast to conventional single-zone or global models, the spatial resolution of the proposed framework captures in-cylinder reactivity variations, providing valuable insights into combustion stability, heat release uniformity, and emission formation. These capabilities are critical for broadening the operational limits of HCCI engines and mitigating issues such as knock and misfire. Comparative analyses between the GRI-Mech mechanism and the ionic mechanism further demonstrate the trade-off between computational load and diagnostic precision, offering guidance for mechanism selection in engine-specific optimization. Moreover, the simulation results support early-stage design decisions without reliance on extensive experimental campaigns, particularly for unconventional fuel candidates. The proposed model is adaptable to alternative fuel compositions, making it a flexible tool for future research on fuel-agnostic HCCI operation. This aligns with global objectives toward decarbonization and supports the development of clean combustion technologies.
