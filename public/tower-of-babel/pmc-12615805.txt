Mixed granulocytic airway inflammation impairs aversive learning and alters neuroimmune biomarkers in mice
Authors: Pamela Maria Araujo Morera, Jose Ivo Araújo Beserra-Filho, Beatriz Soares-Silva, Rafael Herling Lambertucci, Vitoria Caroline de Queiroz, Nathalia Montouro Pinheiro-Menegasso, Rafael Cossi da Silva, Vitor Ponci, Maria Aparecida Oliveira, Wothan Tavares-de-Lima, João Henrique Ghilardi Lago, Alessandra Mussi Ribeiro, Carla Maximo Prado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Asthma is characterized by different phenotypes of airway inflammation, granulocytic cells may be recruited, both eosinophils and neutrophils. This pathology has been associated with behavioral alterations and neuroinflammation. The aim of the present study was to evaluate changes in behavior and neuroinflammation induced by mixed granulocytic chronic airway inflammation in male mice. Mice received injections of Saline (Control group) or ovalbumin (OVA group) plus Complete Freund’s Adjuvant administered subcutaneously on day 0 followed by OVA nasal instillations on days 21, 22 and 23. On the 24 and 25rd, animals were evaluated in the plus-maze discriminative avoidance task to evaluate anxiety levels and motor activity concomitantly to the memory. Then, bronchoalveolar lavage (BALF), lung and brain structures were collected to perform ELISA to evaluate cytokines. In the brain, oxidative stress markers were also evaluated. OVA-challenged mice showed increased neutrophils and eosinophils recovered in BALF and the TNF-α levels quantified in the lung. OVA challenge increased TNF-α levels in cortex and hypothalamus, as well as I1-β and IL-6 levels in cortex and hippocampus. Moreover, OVA-challenged mice showed a deficit in aversive learning. Taken together our main results showed that mixed granulocytic chronic airway inflammation triggered neuroimmunomodulation, marked by increased pro-inflammatory cytokines in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus and hypothalamus, and behavior changes, notably learning deficit.

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease characterized by chronic inflammation of the airways and lung parenchyma. Allergic asthma is the most common phenotype, and the recruitment of neutrophils contributes to the severity and progression of this disease1,2. It is estimated that 300 million people are affected by asthma worldwide, causing approximately 1,000 deaths per day3.

Evidence reports that mental disorders such as anxiety and depression are comorbidities observed in asthmatic patients4–6. One hypothesis is that cellular recruitment and production of interleukins due to lung inflammatory response may induce neuroinflammation and behavioral alterations. These changes were demonstrated in animal models of eosinophilic asthma7,8, moreover increased gene expression of inflammatory cytokines in the hypothalamus9, prefrontal cortex and hippocampal formation10,11 were also observed in different experimental allergy models, including asthma in male mice.

One possible explanation for the behavioral alterations in asthmatic patients could be related to the oxidative stress process12,13. Using a murine model of allergic asthma, an increase in the oxidative stress associated with a reduction of the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) in the prefrontal cortex and cerebral cortex was observed10,14.

Although previous studies clearly suggest that asthma is associated with neuroinflammation, they have primarily focused on Th2-phenotype models of asthma (Type-2 asthma), in which eosinophils are the main effector cells. However, both clinical and experimental evidence indicate a pivotal role for neutrophils in asthma severity, which characterizes the Th1 asthma phenotype (Non-Type 2 asthma)15. In addition, this phenotype of asthma is glucocorticoid-resistant16,17 and experimental data also indicated that failure of release/secretion of endogenous glucocorticoid drive the Type 2 to Non-Type 2 asthma phenotype in male mice18. Noteworthy, endogenous glucocorticoids mediate the stress response and maintain many brain functions, consequently these hormones are associated with several stress-related neuropsychiatric disorders such as depression and cognitive deficits19. Besides this, the relationship between asthma, social stress and behavior changes is well documented in male and female8,20,21. Given the role of neutrophils in the Th1 asthma phenotype, we hypothesized that this model of granulocytic asthma can show different behavioral alterations and neuroinflammation. In view of this, we evaluated behavioral parameters, neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in male mice submitted to the mixed granulocytic asthma model.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks; 20–25 g) were obtained from the Center for the Development of Experimental Models for Biology and Medicine (CEDEME) and kept in groups of two or three per cage in the animal facility of the Bioscience Department of the Federal University of São Paulo (UNIFESP), under controlled conditions of ventilation, temperature (23 ± 1 °C) and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle (lights on 7:00 a.m.), with water and food ad libitum. This project was approved by the Animal Use Ethics Committee (CEUA; protocol nº 4889090418) and this study was conducted in accordance with the Brazilian law for the use of animals in research (Law number 11.794) and according with ARRIVE guidelines. All efforts were made to minimize animal pain, suffering or discomfort.

Experimental model of asthma with predominance of neutrophils

In this study male mice were used to avoid the influence of female hormones on lung inflammation22,23. Animals were distributed in two experimental groups (n = 12 for each) as follows: Control group: Mice that received saline solution associated with Freund’s complete adjuvant (CFA) and subsequently received saline solution intranasally (i.n.); OVA group: Mice sensitized with ovalbumin (OVA) plus CFA and thereafter were OVA-challenged. Groups of mice that received a subcutaneous injection of 30 µg of OVA grade V (Sigma-Aldrich São Paulo, Brazil) in 25 µL of saline solution emulsified with 75 µL of CFA (Sigma-Aldrich Brazil, Day 0). The OVA-challenge was conducted on day 21, 22 and 23 with i.n. instillation (20 ul) containing 10 µg of OVA Grade II (Sigma-Aldrich Brazil). The control group consisted of mice receiving saline solutions emulsified with CFA and challenged with saline solution as performed in the OVA group of mice. Part of the animals were used for ELISA measurements and the other part for oxidative stress evaluation. All of them were submitted to a behavior test.

Behavioral assessment

Locomotor activity, learning, aversive memory, and anxiety-like behavior were assessed in all animals 24 and 48 h after the final OVA challenge (day 24 and 25 respectively). To this end, animals were subjected to a modified version of the conventional elevated plus-maze, known as the plus-maze discriminative avoidance task (PMDAT)24. The apparatus was constructed of wood, painted black, and consisted of two enclosed arms (28.5 cm × 7 cm × 15 cm) opposite two open arms (28.5 cm × 7 cm), all connected by a central platform (10 cm × 10 cm). One of the enclosed arms, designated as the aversive arm (AV), was equipped with a 100 W lamp (1500 lx) and a small speaker emitting an 80 dB noise from beneath the arm. The other enclosed arm served as the non-aversive (NAV) arm.

On day 24 (24 h after the last OVA challenge), the animals underwent a 10-minute training session to allow habituation to the apparatus. During training, each mouse was placed in the central area facing the NAV arm. Upon entering the AV arm, the aversive stimuli (light and sound) were immediately activated and remained on until the animal exited the arm. On day 25 (24 h after training session), a 10-minute test session was conducted in the absence of aversive stimuli (Fig. 1). After each session, the apparatus was cleaned with a 10% ethanol solution to eliminate olfactory cues. All sessions were recorded using a digital camera positioned above the maze, and behavioral parameters were analyzed using video-tracking software (Any-maze, Stoelting, USA).

Schematic representation of the experimental protocol for sensitization challenge, behavioral assessment and euthanasia.

In the training session, learning analysis was based on the percentage of time spent in the enclosed arms (%TAV: time spent in the AV/time spent in both NAV) and total time spent in the AV versus NAV. In the test session, aversive memory retention was assessed by %TAV and AV versus NAV25. Anxiety-like-behavior was evaluated by the total time spent in the open arms (%TOA: time spent in open arms/time spent in all arms). Moreover, locomotor activity was evaluated by the total distance traveled in the apparatus during the training session.

Sample collection

On day 25, the animals were anesthetized with ketamine (120 mg/kg) and xylazine (12 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally. All animals were euthanized approximately one hour after the completion of the behavioral task (test session) on day 25, ensuring an adequate interval between testing and tissue collection across groups. Then, the animals were submitted to euthanasia by exsanguination, through the vena cava section. The bronchoalveolar lavage and peripheral blood were collected. Afterwards, lung and brain samples (prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, hypothalamus, and cerebellum) were collected (Fig. 1).

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

For the collection of BALF from 6 animals per group, a plastic cannula coupled to a syringe (0.5 ml) was inserted via tracheotomy and PBS was infused, three consecutive times (total volume: 1.5 mL). The recovered volume (approximately 80%) was centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C and the cell pellets were suspended in 300 µL of PBS containing 1% of BSA. Afterwards, 90 µL of the suspension was collected and 10 µL of violet crystal was added. The total cell count was performed using a Neubauer hemocytometer (40x magnification). For the differential cell count, 100 µL of the cell button was resuspended and cytocentrifuged at 300 xg for 10 min at 4 °C to produce slides stained by the Diff-Quick technique, using hemocytological criteria. Thus, 300 cells were counted in an optical microscope (at x1000) and differentiated into neutrophils, eosinophils, lymphocytes, and macrophages26.

Quantification of total protein

Lung tissue and brain structures of 6 animals per group (cortex, hippocampus, hypothalamus) were removed and individually homogenized in extraction buffer containing SDS (10%), Tris (100 mM pH 7.4), EDTA (10mM), sodium pyrophosphate (10 mM), sodium fluoride (100 mM), sodium vanadate (10 mM)] with a Polytron PTA 20 S generator (model PT 10/35, Brinkmann Instruments, Inc., Westbury, NY). The samples were centrifuged at 1000×g, 4◦C, 15 min and the protein concentration was determined using Bradford assay.

Determination of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6

Cytokines levels were quantified by ELISA using Duo-Set Kit from R&D Research and Development (San Diego, CA, USA) in aliquots of samples obtained from lung and brain structures homogenates following the manufacturer’s instructions in a spectrophotometer (Epoch - Bioteck, Vermont, USA) and by the GEN 5.1.1.1 program using a wavelength of 450 nm26.

Determination of total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

Total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in samples from selected brain structures was determined by monitoring the reduction of cytochrome C (0.15 g/L) by superoxide radicals generated via the xanthine–xanthine oxidase system27. Initially, the appropriate volumes of buffer and xanthine oxidase were calibrated to achieve an absorbance of approximately 0.025 at 550 nm, which served as the blank control. Subsequently, 10 µL of the sample, 185 µL of solution A (composed of cytochrome C, EDTA, and xanthine in phosphate buffer), and the determined amount of the blank solution (50 mM sodium phosphate buffer containing xanthine oxidase) were added.

Determination of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity

The activity of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) in brain tissue samples was determined according to the method described by Flohé and Günzler28, which measures the rate of NADPH oxidation coupled to the reduction of tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BuOOH). The assay was conducted spectrophotometrically at 340 nm using a SpectraMax M2 microplate reader.

Each 200 µL reaction mixture contained 143 mM sodium phosphate buffer with 6.3 mM EDTA (pH 7.5), 0.25 mM sodium azide (NaN₃), 1 mM reduced glutathione (GSH), 0.12 mM NADPH, 0.25 U/mL glutathione reductase (GR), and 5 µL of tissue homogenate. After a pre-incubation at 37 °C for 30 s, the reaction was initiated by the addition of 5 µL of tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BuOOH).

The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm was monitored continuously for 5 min. A blank containing all components except the sample was used to correct non-enzymatic NADPH oxidation. Enzyme activity was expressed as nanomoles of t-BuOOH reduced per minute per milligram of protein, using the molar extinction coefficient of NADPH at 340 nm (ε = 6.22 mM⁻¹·cm⁻¹)28.

Determination of nitrite concentration

Analysis of nitrite levels in the selected brain areas were conducted using a previously described method29 in which nitrite reacts with sulfanilamide under acidic conditions, and the reaction product reacts with N-chloridrate (l-naphtyl) ethylenediamine (NED) that generates a compound with an intense red colour. The assay was performed incubating 100 µL of sample and equal volume of Griess reagent (sulfanilamide (1%), N-chloridrate (l-naphtyl) ethylenediamine (0.1%) and phosphoric acid (2.5%) in a plastic microplate (96 wells) for 10 min at 25 °C. The levels of nitrite were quantified by spectrometry (Molecular Devices –Espectra Max 190) at a wavelength of 550 nm using a standard curve (g with a sodium nitrate curve in µM from 0,78 to 100). Data are expressed as µM/ of nitrite/mg protein. Absorbance readings were performed by a spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using the GraphPad Prism. (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). We assumed that a sample containing six (n = 6) mice is sufficient to quantify the changes found in the model of asthma as used herein, notably an increase in inflammatory cells recruited to the lung. We performed the calculation power of each parameter quantified in the endpoint (total cells and neutrophils) using Sigma Plot and both have a power test > 80%. These parameters were chosen since the asthma model used in the present study is a mixed granulocyte phenotype where neutrophils significantly increased30 Then, the Grubbs test was applied and when identified the outliers were excluded. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normality. Independent Student t-test was carried out to evaluate OVA treatment-effect. Regarding the behavior test, previous studies from Dr Ribeiro’s group show that a minimum of 8–10 animals per group is necessary31,32. In addition, dependent t-test (paired samples) was used to evaluate behavioral alterations in the PMDAT task. The results presented in the manuscript reflect the data after outlier removal. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and the significant level (α) was set at 0.05.

Data represented in Fig. 2 show that relative to the control group, the frontal cortex of mice submitted to OVA-challenge increased the levels of TNF-α (2 A), IL-1β (2B) and IL-6 (2 C). In contrast, OVA-challenge did not modify the levels of these cytokines in the cerebellum (Fig. 2J–L).

Behavior and mixed asthma: locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior

In the training session, OVA-challenged mice had an increase in the percentage of time spent in the AV (p < 0.05, Fig. 4A) in comparison to the control group. No differences were found in time spent in the AV between control and OVA groups on test sessions (Fig. 4B). Once exposed to a test session, mice of the control group spent less time exploring the AV (Fig. 4C), indicating retrieval memory. On the other hand, OVA-challenged animals did not discriminate between enclosed arms (Fig. 4C), suggesting impairment in learning. Furthermore, OVA-challenge did not interfere with the locomotor activity (total distance travelled) and anxiety-like behavior (Table 2).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that male mice subject to experimental mixed asthma developed neuroinflammation in the frontal cortex, hippocampus and hypothalamus, accompanied by oxidative stress imbalance and impaired learning performance. These findings add to growing evidence that asthma is not only a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways but also has systemic consequences, including effects on the central nervous system. By employing a mixed granulocytic asthma model, we provide evidence that neutrophilic airway inflammation – often associated with severe, corticosteroid-resistance asthma16,33 – can trigger central immune alterations that may underlie cognitive dysfunction observed in asthmatic patients5,34.

Asthma is a heterogenous disease. Classical allergic asthma is driven by eosinophilic inflammation and is typically modeled through sensitization with aluminum hydroxide adjuvant, generating a Th2-high response (Type 2 asthma)26,35,36. In contrast, severe asthma often involves a mixed eosinophilic/neutrophilic phenotype, with neutrophils recruited to the airways alongside mast cells, macrophages and lymphocytes33,37. This phenotype is generally corticosteroid-resistant and associated with poor symptom control and greater vulnerability to stressors16,17,38. In the present model, sensitization with Freund´s complex adjuvant followed by OVA challenge promote a Th2-low profile (Non-Type 2 asthma)30,39, characterized by increased neutrophils in BALF, consistent with the mixed asthma endotype. Such models are particularly relevant for studying comorbidities, including behavioral and cognitive dysfunction, that are prevalent in patients with severe asthma4,5.

Our neurochemical data demonstrated increased IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α levels in the prefrontal cortex, IL-1β and IL-6 in the hippocampus, and TNF-α and IL-6 levels in the hypothalamus following OVA challenge. These results are in line with previous studies using allergic asthma models which have reported central increases in proinflammatory cytokines11. This suggests that lung inflammation, regardless of eosinophilic or neutrophilic predominance, can drive neuroinflammatory responses. The specific brain regions affected in our study – prefrontal cortex, hippocampus and hypothalamus – are central to cognition, memory and stress regulation9,11,40, supporting the idea that neuroinflammation contribute directly to the behavioral deficit observed. Of note, no significant changes were detected in the cerebellum, suggesting preferential involvement of limbic structures on the lung-brain axis. This selectively reinforces the hypothesis that neuroinflammation in asthma models targets circuits underlying learning and memory rather than motor coordination8.

Although IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6 are not canonical Th1 markers, they are also mechanistically relevant to the Th1/neutrophilic axis in asthma16,41. IL-1 β and IL-6 are characteristic cytokines of neutrophilic asthma. which is typically a non-type 2 asthma16. In line of this, increased IL-6 and IL-8 levels have been detected in plasma and sputum samples from patients with severe asthma, suggesting that these cytokines contribute to disease pathogenesis42. TNF-α is frequently elevated in severe asthma serving as a marker disease severity43. Experimental models of neutrophilic asthma have consistently shown that elevated IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 are features of the non-eosinophilic/Th1-high endotype, linked to neutrophilic influx and steroid resistance41,44.

Several mechanisms may account for the observed neuroinflammation. First, peripheral inflammation may activate afferent pathways such as vagus nerve45,46, transmitting signals from the lung to the brain8,10. Second, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, a known consequence of systemic inflammation, could facilitate entry of the cytokines or peripheral immune mediators into the CNS47. Mast cell derived histamine, and other inflammatory mediators are capable of modulating BBB permeability, which may explain central immune activation after antigen challenge8,48. Third, circulating cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-a may act on CNS endothelial or glial cells, amplifying neuroinflammatory cascades40,46,49. Together, these mechanics support the existence of a lung-brain axis through which peripheral immune events shape central outcomes50.

The mechanisms linking peripheral inflammation to central neuroimmune alterations are not restricted to severe asthma. Pathways such as vagal activation, BBB disruption, and cytokine signaling into the CNS have also been described in classical models of allergic (type-2) asthma, indicating that they represent generalizable routes of lung-brain communication14,51. However, the predominance of neutrophilic influx in our model suggests that the intensity and qualitative profile of neuroinflammation may vary across asthma endotypes. In particular, severe or mixed phenotypes may be more susceptible to persistence or amplified CNS involvement due to their chronicity and corticosteroid resistance. To date, no studies have specifically investigated neuroinflammation in non-type 2 asthma. This gap highlights the need for future work to determine whether different inflammatory endotypes differentially shape the extent and consequences of lung-brain interactions.

Oxidative stress is another key pathway linking asthma to CNS changes52–54. We found region-specific modulation of SOD activity: decreased in the hippocampus, increased in the hypothalamus, and unchanged in the prefrontal cortex. These findings highlight the differential vulnerability of brain regions to redox balance. The hippocampus, with high metabolic demand is consistent with its central role in memory processing53,55. Interestingly, GPx activity remained unaltered across regions, suggesting that compensatory antioxidant pathways were not sufficient to counterbalance hippocampal vulnerability. The rection in nitrite/nitrate levels in prefrontal cortex and hippocampus may reflect either a compensatory mechanism to avoid peroxynitrite overproduction56 or a depletion of nitric oxide availability57,58, both can impair synaptic function. Previous reports have described increased oxidative stress markers in both eosinophilic and neutrophilic asthma59,60, reinforcing the notion that oxidative imbalance is a core feature of asthma-associated CNS dysfunction14,34,53.

It is important to consider that limitation of oxidative stress measurements concerns relatively low statistical power observed in some oxidative stress analyses. While nitrite levels in the cortex reached an adequate power (> 0.90), several other measures, particularly GPx and SOD activity in regions such as the cerebellum and hypothalamus, presented low power values (≤ 0.26). This limitation suggests that our study may have been underpowered to detect subtle differences in antioxidant enzyme activity across brain regions. Therefore, results regarding oxidative stress parameters should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes or complementary assays are warranted to confirm and expand these findings.

Behavioral results further support this interpretation. Using the PMDAT, we demonstrated impaired learning in OVA-sensitized mice, while locomotion and anxiety-like behaviors remained unaffected. This distinction is essential: while classical elevated plus maze paradigms primarily assess anxiety, the PMDAT allows simultaneous evaluation of learning/memory acquisition24. OVA- exposed mice failed to discriminate between aversive and non-aversive arms during the retention session, indicating impaired aversive memory61–64. Importantly, their exploration of open arms and total distance travelled (Table 2) did not differ from controls, suggesting intact anxiety-baseline and motor performance. Thus, the observed deficits can be attributed specifically to impairing learning rather than to confounding effects of anxiety or motor alterations. This conclusion is consistent with evidence linking central cytokine upregulation, particularly in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, to memory deficits in inflammatory conditions49,65,66.

Previous studies reinforce these findings. Mokhtaru-Zaer aet al.67, reported spatial learning impairments in the Morris water maze in an allergic asthma model, while other groups have described anxiety and depressive-like behavioral in models with different inflammatory profiles or behavioral paradigms8,20,21. The discrepance across studies likely reflects both model-specific inflammatory features and the behavioral teste employed. Notably, our results emphasize learning impairment without concomitant anxiety alterations, a distinction that highlights the cognitive consequences of neuroinflammation in asthma.

The choice to use only male was deliberate, to avoid variability introduced by hormonal fluctuation during the estrous cycle. Female hormones are known to modulate both pulmonary inflammation and neuroinflammatory responses, as shown in preclinical and clinical studies22,23,68. While this approach reduced variability, it represents a limitation as male and females may exhibit distinct inflammatory profiles, cytokine expression patterns, and behavioral outcomes. Sex differences in asthma severity, prevalence of comorbidities, and responsiveness to treatment are well documented69,70. Future studies should include female cohorts and consider estrous cycle monitoring to fully elucidate sex-specific mechanism on the lung-brain axis.

Conclusion

Taken together our findings provide evidence that mixed granulocytic asthma promotes central-cytokine upregulation, oxidative stress imbalance and learning impairment in male mice (Fig. 5). The results highlight the importance of considering CNS consequences in asthma pathophysiology, particular in severe phenotypes where neutrophilic inflammation predominates. They also underscore the relevance of the lung-brain axis as a pathway linking peripheral inflammation to cognitive comorbidities. From a translational perspective, identifying neuroinflammation and oxidative stress as potential mediators of memory deficits in asthma may open new avenues for therapeutic intervention, targeting not only respiratory symptoms but also cognitive health.
