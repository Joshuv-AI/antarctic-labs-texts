Best practices for respiratory function training in perioperative patients with lung cancer: A scoping review and evidence synthesis
Authors: Husheng Li, Yuying Shi, Guojiang Wei, Jiaqi Wang, Jie Cheng, Ling Ge, Xiaoxin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify, evaluate, and synthesize the best available evidence for respiratory function training (RFT) in perioperative patients with lung cancer, with the aim of providing guidance for clinical practice and facilitating evidence-based decision-making.MethodsA dual design of scoping review and evidence synthesis was adopted. We comprehensively searched 9 Chinese and English academic databases, and 13 clinical guideline and professional society platforms, from the inception of each database to February 28, 2026. A team of five researchers participated in literature screening, methodological quality appraisal, and evidence extraction according to predefined criteria.ResultsThe initial search yielded 7483 records, of which 31 were ultimately included, comprising 5 clinical practice guidelines, 15 systematic reviews and meta-analyses, 3 expert consensus reports, 1 clinical decision document, 3 evidence syntheses, and 4 randomized controlled trials. A total of 28 evidence items were extracted and categorized into 7 dimensions: assessment and preparation, multidisciplinary team (MDT), training principles, training modalities, supervision and management, outcome monitoring, and safety precautions.ConclusionsThis study synthesizes 28 best evidence items of perioperative RFT for patients with lung cancer, establishes a whole-cycle management framework, addresses evidence fragmentation, and provides a standardized evidence-based foundation for clinical protocol development.Review registrationThis study was registered at the Fudan University Center for Evidence-based Nursing (Registration No. ES20269701) and PROSPERO (Registration No. CRD420251161582).Keywords: Lung cancer, Perioperative period, Respiratory function training, Best practices, Evidence-based nursing, Scoping review

Introduction

Lung cancer represents a paramount public health challenge, ranking as the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-associated mortality worldwide.1,2 For patients with early-stage or locally advanced lung cancer, surgical resection remains the gold standard for curative-intent management, given its unparalleled potential to confer long-term survival.3 However, perioperative management quality is a critical prognostic factor with significant unmet needs: 15%–45% of patients with lung cancer develop postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs).1,4 These complications prolong average hospital stays by 3–7 days, increase health care expenditures by 2–3 times, and reduce 1-year survival rates by up to 20%, making them a major barrier to optimizing lung cancer surgical outcomes.5,6

Under this substantial disease burden, respiratory function training (RFT), as a core non-pharmacological auxiliary measure, plays a significant role in airway management during the perioperative period of lung resection.7 Its main purpose is to optimize ventilation, clear airway secretions, improve gas exchange, and reduce respiratory workload. Surgical trauma, parenchymal lung resection, and perioperative sedation and analgesia consistently impair respiratory mechanics, driving respiratory muscle weakness, reduced lung volumes, and impaired mucociliary clearance.8 By re-establishing physiological breathing patterns, enhancing respiratory muscle strength and endurance, and improving cardiopulmonary function, perioperative RFT reverses these deficits to expand preoperative respiratory reserve, enhance surgical tolerance, and reduce PPC incidence.9,10 Beyond its primary PPC-preventive role, RFT is a widely validated non-pharmacological analgesic strategy.11,12 The American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) and European Respiratory Society (ERS) have formally validated RFT’s clinical utility in lung cancer perioperative care, cementing its position as a core evidence-based component of pulmonary rehabilitation frameworks.13

Despite consensus regarding the benefits of RFT, its clinical implementation in patients with lung cancer is hindered by universal challenges that compromise real-world impact. The implementation of RFT currently remains predominantly experience-based, typically administered by nurses or allied health professionals, with a lack of standardized protocols. Critical parameters, such as training modalities, frequency, and duration, exhibit substantial variability across institutions and regions. Furthermore, relevant evidence is fragmented across single-center studies, small-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and regional guidelines, lacking a comprehensive synthesis of research data to establish evidence-based best practices. A systematic appraisal of the existing literature identifies a critical gap: the absence of a dedicated, broadly applicable evidence-based RFT program tailored to perioperative patients with lung cancer. This creates an evidence-practice disconnect that erodes care consistency across settings.

This gap is further underscored by key health care priorities centered on evidence-based, patient-centered care. The World Health Organization (WHO) Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Noncommunicable Diseases (2013–2030) prioritizes the optimization of long-term cancer survivorship outcomes via high-quality perioperative care, with the International Council of Nurses (ICN) concurrently advocating for specialized, protocol-guided nursing interventions to mitigate health inequities. These imperatives highlight the urgency of systematically synthesizing evidence on perioperative RFT, positioning it not merely as a clinical necessity but as a strategic priority for narrowing disparities in lung cancer care.

To address this gap, this study aims to: (1) systematically identify and appraise evidence on RFT for perioperative patients with lung cancer; (2) synthesize best practices for the safe and effective implementation of RFT; and (3) propose a framework for developing broadly applicable, standardized RFT protocols. By synthesizing aggregated evidence, this study seeks to fill the current research gap, guide clinical decision-making across diverse health care settings, and ultimately contribute to reducing the incidence of PPCs, improving patient-centered outcomes, and aligning lung cancer perioperative care with established best practices.

Methods

Methodological framework and reporting standards

Within the nursing field, the development of a standardized international framework for reporting evidence synthesis remains an ongoing process, with no fully unified protocols yet established to guide consistent practice. To fill this void, the Center for Evidence-Based Nursing at Fudan University has adopted the methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and tailored it to address the specific needs of synthesizing heterogeneous evidence, particularly within Chinese-language research contexts where localized methodological adaptations are often necessary.

This study adopts a dual design of scoping review and evidence synthesis, with its implementation strictly adhering to the JBI methodological framework. For transparent and standardized reporting, the study also follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).14 These two methodological pillars work in tandem to ensure the rigor of the research process and the clarity of result dissemination.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of evidence

The inclusion criteria for this study were defined as follows: (1) Studies focused on adult perioperative patients with lung cancer; (2) Publications presenting recommendations, findings, or evidence related to RFT for the aforementioned patient group; (3) Study designs encompassing clinical practice guidelines, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, evidence syntheses, best practice statements, expert consensus reports, and RCTs, all of which meet the requirement for providing credible, actionable information on perioperative RFT; (4) Publications available in either Chinese or English, ensuring accessibility to both domestic and international research evidence.

The exclusion criteria were established as follows: (1) Non-primary evidence materials such as guideline interpretations, study protocols, or research proposals; (2) Duplicate publications, incomplete research reports, or studies for which full-text access was unavailable; (3) Studies published over a decade ago, except for those that offer fundamental evidence or remain clinically relevant to current perioperative lung cancer management; (4) Studies classified as low quality following a strict methodological evaluation.

Literature screening

Literature screening was conducted independently by two researchers, and their respective screening results underwent cross-verification to ensure accuracy. When discrepancies emerged between the two researchers’ judgments, a third researcher with relevant expertise was invited to mediate and facilitate a consensus-based resolution.

The screening workflow followed a three-step sequential process: First, all retrieved literature was imported into Zotero 7.0.27 and the platform’s built-in deduplication function was utilized to eliminate duplicate entries, ensuring no redundant studies were included in subsequent steps. Second, a preliminary screening of article titles was performed to exclude studies that were manifestly irrelevant to the core research focus, which helped narrow down the literature pool efficiently. Third, the remaining articles underwent in-depth review: their abstracts were first assessed to further filter out non-conforming studies, and those that passed the abstract review then had their full texts examined thoroughly. Ultimately, eligible studies were selected strictly in accordance with the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, ensuring the rigor and relevance of the literature included.

Quality evaluation of the literature

Based on the type of included literature, appropriate quality assessment tools were selected to ensure targeted and rigorous evaluation. Specifically, the methodological quality of clinical practice guidelines was evaluated via the AGREE II (Appraisal of Guidelines for Research & Evaluation II).16 For systematic reviews and meta-analyses, the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews was used.17 As for expert consensus reports, the JBI tool specifically developed for evaluating expert opinions and consensus documents was utilized.18 Additionally, the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool19 was applied to assess the methodological quality of the RCTs.

For clinical decision-making documents and evidence syntheses, we performed a dedicated methodological quality appraisal using the Criteria for Appraisal of Synthesis Evidence (CASE), a validated tool specifically designed for evaluating the quality of these types of documents. Meanwhile, we verified the credibility of their source institutions and traced the methodological quality of the original high-quality studies cited in these documents for cross-validation. This approach not only maintained consistency in methodological quality assessment across all included literature types but also ensured the rigor and reliability of the included evidence, balancing stringency with practicality.

All literature quality assessments were carried out independently by two researchers with training in evidence-based practice; any discrepancies arising during the assessment process were addressed by discussion with a third researcher. To further improve the consistency and validity of the assessment results, guideline appraisals were conducted collaboratively by four researchers, ensuring comprehensive and uniform judgment across all evaluated guidelines.

Evidence extraction and synthesis

Prior to formal evidence extraction and synthesis, all investigators completed standardized certified training in JBI evidence synthesis methodology, scoping review reporting standards, and use of pre-specified critical appraisal tools. Two independent master’s-level investigators with specialized experience in perioperative respiratory rehabilitation and evidence-based nursing performed all evidence extraction and synthesis. Pre-piloting of the extraction protocol in 10% of included studies showed excellent inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s kappa = 0.87). Discrepancies were first resolved via bilateral discussion, with unresolved disagreements arbitrated by a senior JBI-certified evidence-based methodology expert to maintain methodological rigor.

Evidence synthesis was conducted in strict accordance with three pre-specified, methodologically sound core principles: (1) For evidence with consistent conclusions across multiple included sources, priority was given to the version with the most professional, concise, and precise standardized expression, aligned with international clinical nursing and respiratory rehabilitation terminology norms; (2) For evidence with partially overlapping or complementary content from multiple sources, we integrated the content into a single, cohesive, clinically actionable evidence entry without altering the original meaning and core conclusions of the source literature; (3) For inconsistencies in evidence content across sources, we prioritized evidence with the highest methodological quality (graded via our pre-specified critical appraisal tools), followed by the most recently published evidence from authoritative peer-reviewed journals.

Evidence grading was performed using the 2014 version of the JBI Center for Evidence-Based Health care framework,20 which categorizes all included evidence into five levels (Level 1 to Level 5). Among these, Level 1 is defined as the highest-quality evidence and Level 5 as the lowest. To further evaluate the clinical applicability of the evidence, the FAME criteria (i.e., Feasibility, Appropriateness, Meaningfulness, and Effectiveness) were applied. These criteria were applied to determine the strength of recommendations, which were categorized into two grades: Grade A (strong recommendation) and Grade B (weak recommendation). The specific evaluation criteria are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

In line with the PRISMA-ScR, a pre-designed data charting form was utilized to extract relevant variables from each included evidence source. The extracted data items covered eight key dimensions: (1) bibliographic information; (2) evidence type; (3) study population characteristics and specific clinical conditions; (4) intervention type and core characteristics; (5) delivery setting; (6) primary recommendations, findings, or evidence; (7) evidence level assigned based on the JBI hierarchy; and (8) results of the FAME criteria. This standardized, structured extraction process not only ensured consistency in data collection across different evidence sources but also laid the foundation for synthesizing findings across the eight aforementioned dimensions, supporting the systematic analysis of RFT evidence for perioperative patients with lung cancer.

Discussion

This scoping review and evidence synthesis systematically collates current evidence on RFT for perioperative patients with lung cancer, summarizes 28 distinct items of best evidence, and identifies 7 core evidence-based pillars for guiding clinical practice: dynamic assessment and comprehensive preparation, MDT collaboration, adherence to training principles, patient-centered selection of training modalities, perioperative whole-period management, strengthened outcome monitoring, and clear safety precautions. All evidence items were graded using the 2014 JBI Evidence Grading Framework, with recommendation strength determined via the FAME criteria. The implementation of these evidence items is supported by core facilitators including the low-threshold nurse-led MDT model and equipment-free basic RFT modalities, while key barriers include resource disparities across clinical settings and patient adherence limitations. These findings not only align with the existing consensus on perioperative pulmonary rehabilitation but also address the unique challenges within clinical practice settings, and thereby provide actionable guidance to bridge the evidence-practice gap.

Dynamic assessment and comprehensive preparation: the foundation for effective implementation

Clinical evidence supports that patients undergoing lung resection should be assessed preoperatively by health care professionals, to enable timely monitoring of their physical status, disease progression, and risk of PPCs. Prompt intervention for identified abnormalities is critical to ensuring the safety and smooth progression of both surgery and subsequent pulmonary rehabilitation. Expert consensus reports explicitly state that pulmonary rehabilitation incorporating RFT should adhere to the principle of “Starting with Assessment, Ending with Assessment”.40 This emphasizes that assessment should be integrated into the entire perioperative RFT. Health care professionals are therefore advised to conduct dynamic assessments combining objective indicator evaluations with patient subjective feedback, thereby establishing a robust foundation for effective RFT.

This study further revealed that forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO), which are key indicators employed in preoperative pulmonary function assessment, serve to jointly characterize expiratory airflow dynamics and gas exchange efficiency. These parameters not only quantify the extent of preoperative pulmonary function impairment but also enable the assessment of patients’ tolerance for surgery and perioperative pulmonary rehabilitation regimens, thereby providing objective evidence to guide clinical decision-making.

Beyond preoperative assessment, comprehensive preoperative preparation serves as another cornerstone for effective pulmonary rehabilitation. Clinical evidence identifies two critical interventions during this preparatory phase: smoking cessation and health education.21,23,45,49 These measures significantly improve patients’ responsiveness to perioperative RFT by optimizing their baseline physiological status and mitigating modifiable risk factors, thus reducing potential barriers to achieving optimal rehabilitation outcomes.

MDT: the provider of scientific guidance for personalized protocols

Evidence indicates that RFT is frequently underprioritized in clinical management, characterized by insufficient engagement of specialized personnel and a lack of adequate guidance. A critical gap in current practice is the absence of a dedicated MDT model tailored specifically to RFT. To address this gap, any MDT established for RFT should be based on the framework of well-developed pulmonary rehabilitation teams, which typically comprise nursing practitioners, anesthetists, thoracic surgeons, respiratory physiotherapists, rehabilitation specialists, and clinical nutritionists.21

The expertise of each team member collectively underpins the scientific design of personalized RFT protocols. Anesthetists leverage their expertise in perioperative respiratory risk assessment to adjust RFT intensity for patients at high surgical risk. Respiratory physiotherapists design technically precise exercise modules based on patients’ individual lung function levels. Rehabilitation specialists optimize long-term RFT adherence via lifestyle-tailored strategies, while clinical nutritionists boost exercise tolerance by addressing unique nutritional needs and optimizing baseline physiology. Crucially, nurses serve as the cornerstone and core coordination hub of these MDTs, taking on the primary responsibility of guiding and monitoring patients’ RFT protocols, cross-specialty communication coordination, and patient training adherence management. Their close, continuous clinical contact with perioperative patients with lung cancer equips them with unique insights: they can observe real-time details of RFT implementation to capture individual nuances, promptly identify training anomalies specific to each patient, coordinate targeted MDT interventions, address patient training resistance, and integrate targeted encouragement into their guidance to sustain consistent daily respiratory exercises.27

It is therefore recommended to establish nurse-led RFT MDTs. This structure not only fully leverages nurses’ strengths in assessment, communication, and coordination but also integrates the specialized expertise of other team members. By synthesizing these diverse perspectives, the MDT can develop flexible, personalized RFT protocols that align with patients’ evolving physiological needs and clinical status. Ultimately, this ensures the safe and effective implementation of perioperative RFT, enabling the MDT to fulfill its core role as a provider of scientific and personalized care guidance.

Adherence to training principles: a prerequisite for safety and efficiency

Evidence synthesis indicates that RFT for perioperative patients with lung cancer must adhere to three core principles: overload, reversibility, and specificity. These foundational tenets directly determine the safety and efficacy of the training.

Specifically, perioperative RFT is an incremental process, as the human body gradually reaches maximum exercise potential through a cycle of response and adaptation. This necessitates initiating training at a tolerable low intensity, with gradual increments in both exercise load and duration only when patients remain free of fatigue.50 This progressive approach aligns with the overload principle, preventing sudden physiological stress that could compromise perioperative safety.51

Regarding the reversibility principle, while the optimal duration of perioperative RFT for patients with lung cancer remains undefined in current literature, it is widely accepted that the expected optimal functional state is transient and reversible. Without consistent, regular training, improvements in respiratory function are likely to diminish over time, which highlights the critical need for sustained patient compliance to maintain training-induced benefits.

In terms of the specificity principle, although respiratory muscle training is recognized as an effective component of respiratory rehabilitation, inspiratory and expiratory muscle training differ in their mechanisms of action. Notably, studies exploring the combined application of these two modalities in patients with lung cancer remain scarce.

Collectively, these findings emphasize that adherence to training principles is not merely a procedural requirement but a prerequisite for translating RFT into tangible clinical outcomes. Therefore, health care professionals should prioritize patient education on these principles to enhance patients’ understanding of RFT, motivate their active engagement in training, and ultimately facilitate disease recovery.

Rational selection of modalities based on patient needs: a key to individualized efficacy

Existing evidence reveals a wide range of RFT modalities, each with its own advantages and limitations. Unassisted techniques such as pursed-lip breathing and abdominal breathing require no additional equipment, making them cost-effective and practical, but they fail to provide intuitive measurements of patients’ respiratory parameters during training. In contrast, incentive spirometers feature simple operation and real-time visual feedback, which can facilitate active self-monitoring by patients. Currently, no evidence demonstrates that any single training modality is significantly superior to others, nor that combined modalities are more effective than monotherapy.26 Therefore, during the translation of evidence into clinical practice, full consideration should be given to patients’ preferences, needs, abilities, and economic conditions to develop individualized training protocols, thereby improving training adherence.

Severe postoperative pain frequently impairs deep breathing and effective coughing in lung cancer surgery patients, while also restricting mobility. Thus, appropriate pain management constitutes an indispensable prerequisite for effective pulmonary rehabilitation. Pharmacological analgesia remains the core strategy, yet physical therapy interventions offer crucial supplementary support. Research evidence indicates that for patients undergoing thoracotomy, applying ice packs prior to or during coughing and deep breathing exercises effectively alleviates exacerbated incisional pain triggered by these postoperative physical therapy measures.52 This underscores that incorporating targeted analgesia into RFT programs constitutes not merely supportive care, but a critical component in ensuring full patient participation in training.

Evidence further emphasizes the need for a multimodal strategy in RFT for perioperative patients with lung cancer, encompassing three core components: early mobilization, exercise training, and multidisciplinary coordination.45 Among these components, early mobilization and exercise training are widely regarded as foundational cornerstones of effective RFT.21 A Systematic review of multiple RCTs has verified that preoperative rehabilitation combining respiratory training with exercise training effectively reduces postoperative pulmonary complications PPCs associated with lung cancer surgery and shortens hospital stays.45,53

Despite progress, critical gaps persist. The absence of unified standards or systematic best practices to guide individualized exercise selection in clinical practice creates significant uncertainty in decision-making. Consequently, future research should focus on developing scientifically sound, personalized exercise rehabilitation programs tailored to patients’ actual conditions.

Perioperative whole-period management: the driver for effective execution

Beyond the rational selection of RFT modalities, whole-period perioperative management exerts a significant impact on the efficacy of RFT. Regarding the timing of RFT implementation, evidence consistently recommends initiating training preoperatively. A randomized single-blind controlled study confirmed that preoperative RFT for patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) improved their preoperative physical status and prevented postoperative functional decline. However, the optimal specific timing for initiating preoperative RFT remains unclear.54 Some studies recommend commencing RFT at least 2 weeks prior to surgery.55 Yet, given regional disparities within current health care systems and the rapid adoption of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols in cardiothoracic surgery, this recommendation proves challenging to implement universally in clinical practice.

Notably, patients’ willingness to engage in postoperative RFT is often compromised by postoperative weakness, fatigue, and incision-related pain. These factors are key barriers that demand targeted intervention within the perioperative whole-period management framework. Therefore, health care professionals should re-instruct patients to perform deep breathing and forceful coughing under adequate analgesia immediately after they awaken from postoperative anesthesia.56 This intervention not only facilitates the drainage of sputum and oozing blood from the lungs but also shortens the indwelling time of drainage tubes, ultimately reducing the risk of pulmonary infections. Such postoperative guidance, paired with preoperative training planning, forms an integrated whole-period management approach that ensures the continuous and effective execution of RFT.

Strengthened outcome monitoring: an enabler for enhancing adherence

Evidence collectively indicates that supervisory management is vital for improving adherence to RFT among perioperative patients with lung cancer. Specifically, effective supervision facilitates a virtuous cycle of guidance, feedback, adjustment, execution, and re-evaluation throughout the training process, thereby further boosting adherence. As highlighted in expert consensus reports, sound adherence serves as the cornerstone for the smooth delivery of RFT, and the effectiveness of such perioperative training directly influences patients’ prognosis.38

Nevertheless, suboptimal outcomes of RFT often occur. This is attributed to two key factors: first, the physical and psychological impacts of lung cancer and its treatment on patients; second, the gradual weakening of patients’ commitment to sustained exercise over time under conventional health education.56

Against this backdrop, this study puts forward targeted recommendations. During hospitalization, medical staff should work with family members to jointly monitor patients’ adherence to RFT and track their physiological recovery. After discharge, telemedicine and mobile technologies should be used for ongoing supervision and follow-up, with the goal of enhancing patients’ self-management abilities in respiratory training.

Clear safety precautions: a premise for ensuring safety

Establishing evidence-based safety precautions constitutes the foundational prerequisite for implementing perioperative RFT in patients with lung cancer. While RFT is a key intervention for postoperative care, it inherently carries procedure-related risks—particularly in the perioperative setting, where patients exhibit significantly heightened physiological vulnerability. Therefore, the principle of safety prioritization must be integrated into the entire workflow of RFT: ranging from preoperative patient eligibility assessment and screening to the development and implementation of personalized training protocols tailored to patients’ functional status and extending to longitudinal monitoring during training sessions and postoperative follow-up.

Health care professionals bear the primary responsibility of providing patients with safety education on RFT. This education should not only cover the clinical necessity and mechanistic benefits of the intervention but also include explicit, actionable safety protocols. A critical component of this education is emphasizing that patients must immediately cease RFT and promptly notify the health care team if any subjective discomfort occurs, such as chest tightness, dyspnea, or dizziness. To further standardize risk management, the implementation of RFT must adhere to predefined, evidence-based physiological parameters. Specifically, training cessation is mandated when two or more abnormal physiological indicators are observed concurrently.

Even when low-intensity RFT is deemed clinically safe, vigilant real-time clinical observation and regular interval-based respiratory function assessments remain indispensable. These measures ensure the timely identification of subclinical adverse responses, enable prompt adjustments to the training protocol, and thereby protect patients from preventable adverse events.

Research limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, most high-level evidence included is derived from literature concentrated in specific geographic regions, and variations in local health care delivery models, clinical practice norms, patient demographic characteristics, and compliance patterns across different practice settings may limit the generalizability and practical utility of the synthesized evidence when applied to diverse clinical contexts. Second, perioperative RFT encompasses diverse modalities, and substantial heterogeneity in intervention protocols across included studies persists, which leaves the identification of an optimal modality unresolved.

For future research, more robust study designs are warranted to address these gaps. Specifically, studies should focus on: (1) identifying the optimal perioperative RFT modality for patients with lung cancer; (2) optimizing key parameters such as training frequency, intensity, and duration; and (3) refining implementation protocols to meet clinical practice demands; and (4) conducting multi-center regional validation studies across diverse Asia–Pacific health care settings to further optimize the localized adaptation of the synthesized evidence. Such endeavors are expected to maximize the clinical benefits of RFT for perioperative patients with lung cancer, thereby advancing evidence-based care in this population.

Conclusions

Based on current studies and evidence synthesis principles, this study summarized 28 pieces of best evidence for RFT in perioperative patients with lung cancer from 7 aspects: assessment and preparation, MDT, training principles, training modalities, supervision and management, outcome monitoring, and safety precautions. These evidence items integrate key components of perioperative RFT to achieve whole-period management. They provide an evidence-based basis for clinical practice to establish standardized protocols and carry out personalized RFT in perioperative patients with lung cancer.
