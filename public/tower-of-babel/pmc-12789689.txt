Influenza vaccination post-COVID-19 expands vaccine-specific effector CD4 T-cells and Tregs under positive influence of host trained innate immunity
Authors: Hima Bindhu, Srabanti Rakshit, Vasista Adiga, Asma Ahmed, Nirutha Chetan Kumar, Maria Macrina, Himanshu Tripathi, Francesca Vanni, Emanuele Montomoli, Sweety Samal, Sneha Raj, Preeti Vishwakarma, George D. Souza, Shruthi Sasidharan, Mary Dias, Timothy Tree, Annapurna Vyakarnam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SARS-CoV-2 immunity and innate immune training may influence influenza vaccine immunogenicity. We investigated this in India. Adult volunteers with hybrid SARS-CoV-2 immunity were administered FluarixTM Tetra (GlaxoSmithKlein) 2022/2023 NH Vaccine in 2022. Significant induction of hemagglutinin inhibition-specific antibodies and polyfunctional central memory CD4+ T-cells (TCM) were observed 1-week post-vaccination with variable induction of CD8+T-cell and innate effectors. Vaccination also expanded Flu-specific regulatory T-cells (Treg), which negatively correlated with CD4 responses, highlighting vaccine immunogenicity may be subject to Treg dampening. FluarixTM did not boost SARS-CoV-2 immunity. However, SARS-CoV-2-specific T-cell responses correlated positively with vaccine-induced T-cell responses. We evaluated trained immunity post-COVID-19 as a potential regulatory mechanism linking SARS-CoV-2 and heterologous vaccine immunogenicity. We observed, elevated frequencies of basal bacterial Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced IL-6+IL1β+HLA-DR+CD14+CD16- frequencies post-COVID-19 correlated positively with vaccine-induced Fluarix-specific CD4 T-cell frequencies. Our study highlights a potential positive role for COVID-19-driven immune imprinting on heterologous vaccine immunogenicity in a post-COVID-19 era.

Introduction

Influenza is a major global health concern with India reporting 19872 H1N1 (influenza A) cases in 2024, which included 324 registered deaths1. Vaccination is recommended for infants, children (~5 years), elderly (>65 years) and people with comorbidities2. Vaccine effectiveness (VE) is linked to pre-existing immunity to circulating strains due to prior exposure or vaccination3,4, with the World Health Organisation recommending yearly vaccination. The protective efficacy and immunogenicity of inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV) and live attenuated influenza vaccines (LAIV) have been documented through clinical trials involving infants, children5 and adults/elderly6. VE of LAIV versus IIV in children revealed both vaccines to be protective against H3N2, while the efficacy of LAIV against the H1N1pdm09 strain was compromised5. Hence, vaccines currently recommended for use in India primarily include inactivated vaccines, and LAIV use is restricted.

The primary immune correlate of IIV is a serum hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titre of 1:40 and is associated with 50–70% protection against clinical infection in adults7. Successful control of influenza is governed by the induction of antibody (Ab) responses in conjunction with antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells8–13. Antigen-specific CD4+ T-cells can be helpers and immune effectors, compensating for high immune escape due to antigenic variation in neutralising Ab-inducing B-cell epitopes10–12. Depletion of CD4+ T-cells attenuates the generation of Hemagglutinin (HA)-specific Abs and impacts the magnitude, quality and persistence of Flu-specific Ab responses10. CD4+ T-cell effectors expressing IFNγ, IL-2 and TNF can indirectly contribute to protective immunity by promoting effector functions of other cell types11. Moreover, granzyme B and perforin expressing IFNγ+CD4+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) can kill in an MHC-II restricted manner and thereby offer protection12. A single mutation in an influenza-virus CD4+ T-cell epitope was shown to influence immunogenicity and protective immunity in an animal challenge study, further demonstrating their critical importance13. Likewise, influenza-specific CD8+ CTLs producing IFNγ and TNF are critical in the elimination of virus-infected cells via perforin-mediated granule exocytosis7,9.

Studies in humans and mouse models highlight the additional role of CD4+ regulatory T-cells (Treg), which have the potential to dampen effector responses14–16. The frequency of Treg cells is significantly increased following secondary influenza virus infection, and their depletion promotes viral clearance14. HA-specific CD137+CTLA-4+FOXP3+ Treg cells induced after primary influenza vaccination are expanded upon repeated annual vaccination and negatively correlate with seroconversion and HA antibody titres15. Circulating CD127loCD25hiFOXP3+ Tregs are elevated in elderly vaccine non-responders compared to responders17. In contrast to their immune dampening role, Tregs, along with T follicular helper cells (Tfh)18, have been shown to boost the germinal centre (GC) B cell response and limit tissue destruction by promoting tissue repair19. Memory regulatory T-cells, or mTreg, generated post influenza infection in mice when adoptively transferred home to the lungs and contribute towards the reduction of influenza-associated pathology in infected animals20. In Lymphocytic Choriomeningitis Virus (LCMV) infection, Treg-mediated protection is associated with IL-10-linked mechanisms21. Similarly, Treg cells have also been demonstrated to be critical for differentiation of effector CD8 to the memory phenotype22, help in the expansion of memory CD8+21 and low-avidity CD8+ T-cells in LCMV infection23. Taken together, Treg cells can play critical roles in viral infection and vaccination; understanding their role is therefore critical to the design of effective vaccines. In this study, we addressed an important knowledge gap in the context of Flu-vaccine immunogenicity in India: we assessed if influenza vaccination induced both effectors and Tregs and if the induced Tregs had the potential to dampen vaccine immunogenicity.

Immune imprinting can shape immunity to influenza, with emerging data highlighting a similar phenomenon in the context of SARS-CoV-224. It has been demonstrated that prior exposure to the Influenza A virus regulates subsequent SARS-CoV-2 Spike-specific T-cell responses25. In addition, influenza vaccine responses can be influenced by symptomatic COVID-19, especially in male vaccinees26. However, whether pre-existing SARS-CoV-2 hybrid immunity can shape influenza vaccine-induced responses in Flu vaccine-naïve subjects is not known. We addressed this specific question in India, where yearly Flu vaccination rates are low, thus enabling assessment of the immunogenicity of primary influenza vaccination in young adults. This study provides the first in-depth analysis of the breadth and quality of Influenza haemagglutinin-specific Ab and T-cell responses, with a specific focus on the quality of Flu-specific cellular responses induced by primary vaccination in Indian subjects with pre-existing ancestral SARS-CoV-2-specific immunity, with wider implications for current and next generation Flu-vaccine immunogenicity in the post-COVID-19 era.

FluarixTM vaccination boosts influenza-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses

Vaccine-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses were measured by multiparameter flow cytometry using FluarixTM vaccine itself as an in vitro stimulus and a measure of T-cell vaccine responses specific to all four vaccine strains. Figure. 2A shows the gating strategy for viability and lineage markers, and Fig. 2B shows representative staining of IFNγ, IL-2 and TNF after antigen stimulation at T0 and T1. Figure. 2C shows the frequencies of CD4+ T-cells over time, expressing IFNγ or IL-2 or TNF using Boolean gating (Total cytokine frequency), confirming significant expansion of FluarixTM-specific CD4+ T-cell frequencies over time, with only two of the eighteen donors tested, lacking vaccine-induced responses above baseline at T1 (Fig. 2C). Fold change calculation of antigen-specific CD4+ T-cell frequencies post-vaccination relative to T0 reveals the highest response at T1 (median fold change: 5.575, range: 0.5128–71.00), falling to 3.875-fold at T2 and 1.917 at T3 (Fig. 2C). In contrast, expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T-cells (Fig. 2D) post-vaccination was lower in magnitude, with significant induction above baseline only evident at T1 with several donors (10 of 19 tested) lacking vaccine-induced responses above baseline (Fig. 2D). Additional enumeration of IAV (Ann Arbour/H2N2) nucleoprotein (NP) (IBV (NP) (H2N2) specific T-cells (non HA T-cells) to assess if vaccine-induced T-cell expansion could potentially be influenced by seasonal Flu exposure, showed variable expansion (Fig. 2G) with the proportion of NP-specific CD4 and CD8 T-cell responders ranging from 20–40% (see Supplementary Fig. 2A, C), suggesting that the expansion of HA-specific CD4+ T-cells at T1 is primarily due to vaccine-induced memory recall responses and to a lesser extent a consequence of seasonal influenza strain exposure. Taken together, these data highlight that the Flu vaccine is immunogenic in terms of the induction of antigen-specific Ab and CD4+ and, to a lesser extent, CD8+ T-cell response, with CD4+ T-cell frequencies and Ab titres showing a significant positive correlation (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Vaccine-specific CD4+ T-cells are qualitatively superior to CD8+ T-cells

Two approaches were used to probe the quality of vaccine-induced FluarixTM-specific T-cell responses. First, flow cytometry data shown in Fig. 2 were subjected to SPICE analysis to assess polyfunctionality in terms of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells expressing different combinations of IFNγ, IL-2, TNF and IL-17A + F. Cellular subsets that were induced at T1, T2 and T3 relative to T0 at a statistically significant level are highlighted as boxes shaded pink, with the P value. Figure. 3A shows expansion of 3+ (IFNγ+IL-2+TNF+) and 2+ (IFNγ+IL-2+; IL-2+TNF+; IFNγ+TNF+) CD4+ T-cells at T1 and T2 with IFNγ+IL-2+ double-positive cells persisting at T3. Significant induction of single IL-2+ and single IFNγ+ cells was also observed. Polyfunctional double-positive (IFNγ+IL-2+, IL-2+TNF+, IFNγ+TNF+) CD4+ T-cells were also noted specific for all four vaccine strains at T1 and T2 (Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast, the vaccine-induced CD8+ T-cell response lacked polyfunctionality to FluarixTM stimulation, with only single IFNγ+ or single IL-2+ cells significantly induced (Fig. 3B). Evidence for varied expansion of innate lymphocyte subsets by vaccination

Several innate lymphocyte subsets are associated with influenza immunity27. We therefore explored gamma delta (γδ) TCR+ lymphocytes and NKT-cells, which express ab TCR with restricted diversity, as well as conventional CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells using a gating strategy shown in Fig. 6A. A proportion of samples showed expansion of cytokine-expressing γδ and NKT -cells above baseline. This did not reach statistical significance when all samples were considered for γδ T -cell subset (Fig. 6B). However, significant induction of IL-2 expressing NKT cells was noted at T1 relative to T0 (Fig. 6B). IFNγ+ CD56dim NK cells recognised for high granzyme expression and superior cytolytic function, were also expanded at T1 in many vaccinees with no significant changes to pre-existing high frequencies of CD56bright NK cells over time (Fig. 6C). Dampening of FluarixTM-induced CD4+ T-cell responses by Treg cells

CD4+ Regulatory T-cells (Treg) are known to rapidly expand in response to influenza infection and vaccination and can dampen Flu-specific cellular and humoral immunity14–23,28. Therefore, we assessed the frequencies of both total circulating Tregs and Fluarix-specific Tregs over time to understand their impact on FluarixTM-induced CD4+ T-cell responses. Frequencies of circulating total Treg (irrespective of antigen-specificity) defined as CD4+CD45RA-CD127loCD25hiFOXP3+ (representative gating strategy shown in Supplementary Fig. 6A) were measured and were observed to remain unchanged over time (Supplementary Fig. 6B). In contrast, Fluarix-specific Treg were expanded concomitant with vaccine-induced effector subsets (Fig. 7). We used a well-documented antigen-induced AIM expression ICS assay to track antigen-specific Tregs with suppression potential29,30. Specifically, we assessed frequencies of Fluarix-induced memory (CD45RA-) CD4 T-cells that co-express CD69 and CD137, lack expression of CD154 with low to negligible expression of cytokines: IFNγ, IL-2, TNF and IL-17A + F31. This data was available as part of our ICS assay and flow cytometry panel deployed to measure Fluarix-specific CD4 and CD8 T-cell frequencies, as shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5. See also Supplementary Table 1 for Flow panels used. We analysed this data in OMIQ for the presence of Fluarix-specific CD45RA-CD69+CD137+CD154- cells within the total CD4 T-cell compartment, using a recognised gating strategy (Fig. 7A). The frequency of these AIM+ T-cells in Fluarix-stimulated samples after background (no stimulus control) subtraction was enumerated. Using this assay, we demonstrate that (i) there is no evidence of Fluarix-specific Treg cells at T0 (Fig. 7B); (ii) the maximum frequencies of Fluarix antigen-specific Tregs was observed at T1 with frequencies falling sharply at T2, except for one donor (Fig. 7B); (iii) induction of Fluarix-specific CD4 T-cells at T1 was observed only in a proportion of vaccinees, i.e. 9 out of 18 (Fig. 7B); (iv) our data shows that Fluarix-specific Tregs (CD4+CD45RA-CD69+CD137+CD154- T-cells) are IFNγ/IL-2/TNF/IL-17A + F−/lo compared to the counterpart CD4 effector cell population, particularly those co-expressing CD154 and CD137 (Fig. 7C), consistent with literature using the AIM assay to enumerate Treg31 and (v) a significant negative correlation was observed between frequencies of Fluarix-specific Tregs and vaccine-induced CD4, but not CD8, effector responses at T1. The most significant correlation was observed between Treg frequencies at T1 and CD4 effector frequencies at T2 and T3, suggesting that induced Tregs at T1 perhaps have greater impact in dampening the persistence, rather than initial expansion, of vaccine-induced effectors (Fig. 7D). Discussion

This study provides the first in-depth characterisation of the immunogenicity of a currently used Flu vaccine, FluarixTM, in young Indian adults receiving this vaccine for the first time post-COVID-19 pandemic. Whilst confirming the vaccine to be immunogenic, as previously reported38,39, this study highlights several novel aspects of vaccine immunity: (i) primary vaccination rapidly expands pre-existing Flu-specific Ab and T-cell memory responses, rather than triggering de novo naïve cells, as demonstrated by the quick peaking of responses at 7-days post-vaccination, (ii) specific Ab, T-cell responses and additionally innate Flu antigen triggered NKT lymphocytes are boosted, highlighting significant breadth of vaccine immunogenicity, (iii) in-depth probing of the quality of effector T-cell responses highlights expansion of distinct memory cellular phenotypes including CD4+ T-cell central memory polyfunctional effectors and to a lesser extent Granzyme+ TEMRA CD8+ T cells and virus-specific single positive IFNγ+, TNF+ or IL-2+ effector CD8+ T cells, (iv) vaccine-induced specific T-cell frequency can potentially be dampened by concomitant expansion of antigen-specific Tregs, and (v) vaccine induced cellular responses may be influenced by pre-existing hybrid cellular immunity to SARS-CoV-2 and the HCoV-HKU1 virus, potentially through elevated monocyte TI, thereby highlighting the importance of immune imprinting influencing vaccine immunogenicity. Together, this manuscript provides the first in-depth novel insight on a current influenza vaccine in India, post-COVID-19.

Increasing evidence highlights protective immunity against influenza to include specific T-cells, neutralising Ab and innate lymphocytes. Given the increasing importance of CD4+ T-cells in anti-Flu immunity8–13, it is encouraging that a single dose of FluarixTM is a potent inducer of HA-specific CD4+ T-cells and to a lesser extent cytolytic CD8+ T-cells consistent with an inactivated vaccine preferentially activating CD4+ responses, unlike live-attenuated vaccines which efficiently induce both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells40. Polyfunctional CD4+ T-cells expressing multiple cytokines, co-stimulatory molecules and with cytolytic potential are an important correlate of vaccine efficacy38. Our data confirm that FluarixTM induces distinct subsets of 3+, 2+ CD4+ T-cells expressing combinations of IFNγ, IL-2, TNF, AIM and granzyme with double-positive cells persisting for 6 months, highlighting that vaccine efficacy could potentially last for this period. Beyond adaptive cells, vaccination-induced specific IL-2 expressing NKT-cells together with IFNγ+CD56dim NK cells at 7 days. Distinct subsets of IFNγ and perforin-expressing γδ T-cells have been shown to persist after virus clearance and rapidly expand upon subsequent influenza vaccination in the elderly41. NKT-cells besides directly lysing infected cells can restrict the influenza virus by enhancing the cytolytic ability of NK and CD8+ T-cells, by dampening the suppressive capacity of myeloid-derived suppressor cells and by reducing immune pathology and preventing lung damage by removing inflammatory monocytes through IL-22 secretion42. Activated NKT and NK cells have been shown to potentiate pre-existing immunity from vaccination42,43. Expansion of innate lymphocytes highlights a significant breadth of immunity induced by FluarixTM vaccination.

FOXP3+ Tregs have been shown to expand after flu vaccination and infection19,44,45. In our study too, we report concomitant induction of Fluarix-specific T-cell effectors and Tregs that peak at T1. Ag-specific Treg cells that expand post/during influenza infection/active disease can have varying impacts: they can suppress effector T-cell responses14–16,46; regulate excessive inflammation45; promote Tfh and GC B cell responses by absorbing excessive IL-2 which inhibits Tfh differentiation19 and aid in lung tissue repair post-infection47. However, expansion of Ag-specific Tregs post-flu vaccination has been largely noted to dampen cellular and humoral immune responses14,15. A reduction in the suppressive capacity of T-bet+ Treg cells improves influenza vaccination outcomes in mice, further demonstrating the negative impact of Tregs on flu vaccine-induced immunity48. Also, engineered influenza vaccines where Treg stimulatory epitopes have been removed show improved survival and lower morbidity in a humanised mouse model of influenza infection49. This is being actively pursued as a strategy for improving the immunogenicity of future flu vaccines50.

We report a distinct hierarchy of Flu-specific homotypic Ab and T-cell responses following vaccination. Robust responses to IAV H1N1, IBV Austria and Phuket strains and a comparatively muted T-cell response to the IAV H3N2 virus were induced, a reflection potentially of exposure to circulating IAV HIN1 and IBV but not IAV H3N2 strains during our study (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, immune imprinting can impose positive and negative effects on vaccine immunogenicity51. Thus, immunity to the first childhood influenza virus infection leaves profound effects on subsequent infection and vaccination52,53 and development of original antigenic sin54, a phenomenon in which the immune system preferentially boosts pre-existing immunity over responses against novel antigenic epitopes. IAV H3N2 viruses are rapidly evolving, which can negatively impact VE, with vaccination selectively inducing responses against conserved epitopes55. In contrast, IAV (H1N1) pdm09 virus and IBV viruses being slow evolving and less genetically diverse, immune printing can positively influence VE, whereby vaccination can significantly boost T-cell responses to the dominant highly conserved epitopes in the haemagglutinin (HA) stem region as well as new epitopes in the vaccine56,57.

Our study provides a fresh perspective on how immune imprinting by SARS-CoV-2 may influence FluarixTM immunogenicity. The concept of cross-protection between Influenza and SARS-CoV-2 has been previously highlighted25: Influenza vaccination reduced COVID-19 incidence and severity in the elderly25. However, clinical trials testing co-administration of seasonal Flu and COVID-19 vaccines show conflicting results58–61. Some reports show no impact59,60; others show dampening of anti-SARS-CoV-2 -Spike neutralising Ab titres60,61. In contrast, Barouch et al.62 showed prior influenza vaccination or concurrent administration of Flu (FluarixTM or Fluzone) and COVID-19 bivalent mRNA vaccines to increase the magnitude and durability of SARS-CoV-2 IgG1 responses to the XBB.1.5 strain, whilst maintaining Flu-vaccine induced responses. Our study demonstrates no evidence for concomitant expansion of SARS-CoV-2 or the HCoV-HKU1 virus-specific T-cells by FluarixTM vaccination. However, we provide evidence of a strong positive correlation between pre-existing hybrid T-cell immunity to ancestral SARS-CoV-2 and the magnitude of vaccine-induced Fluarix-specific T-cell responses. This relationship cannot be readily explained by clonal expansion of cross-reactive SARS-CoV-2 and Influenza-specific ab+ T-cells by FluarixTM vaccination, as FluarixTM only expanded Influenza, but not SARS-CoV-2-specific T-cell frequencies (Fig. 8). Whilst the mechanisms of how pathogens shape immune memory is an active research area63,64, we propose, based on our previous work on BCG34,35, and others, showing microbes, including SARS-CoV-236,37, to enhance innate immune training, that trained immunity could be one mechanism linking SARS-CoV-2 and FluarixTM- induced influenza T-cell responses. TI promotes innate and adaptive responses34–37. Therefore, if SARS-CoV-2 exposure and/or infection enhances TI36,37, this has the potential to enhance adaptive responses to both homologous and heterologous vaccines, similar to BCG-induced TI enhancing adaptive responses to a heterologous COVID vaccine, through epigenetic immune regulation35. Our data on elevated LPS responses in post- compared to pre -COVID19 pandemic samples provides evidence in support. Given the global health challenges of both Influenza and the continuous emergence of novel SARS-CoV-2 variants, our work highlighting a positive role for immune imprinting by SARS-CoV-2 on FluarixTM T-cell vaccine immunogenicity has broad implications for current and next-generation Influenza vaccines in a post-COVID-19 era, warranting further understanding.

Limitations of study is (i) As an experimental medicine, hypothesis driven, proof of concept study, our findings are limited by cohort size (ii) in the trained immunity assay analysis, sample numbers from the Flu study cohort (N = 6) were limited by the availability of frozen PBMC in our repository (iii) defining NK cells could be improved by use of CD1d tetramers and (iv) we accept that a comparative analysis of Fluarix vaccine immunogenicity in pre- versus post COVID19-pandemic volunteers would provide compelling evidence for the impact of the pandemic on vaccine immunogenicity -this is not feasible as we had not conducted a study with a similar design, with banked samples, prior pandemic that could be simultaneously tested.

Methods

Study design

A proof-of-concept study was conducted in healthy adult volunteers to test the immunogenicity of seasonal flu vaccine- FluarixTM Tetra Inactivated Influenza Vaccine (Split Virion IP) 2022/2023 NH manufactured by GlaxoSmithKline Pharmaceuticals LTD. The study enroled 43 healthy adults, aged 18 to 74, who consented to participate (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Excel file 1). Blood samples were collected at baseline (T0, before vaccination) and at 1-week (T1), 4-weeks (T2) and 6-months (T3) post-vaccination from October 2022 to September 2023. Participants who had a history of flu vaccination before recruitment were excluded from analysis. Baseline data included age, sex, medical history and vaccination history to COVID-19 and flu vaccines (Table 1 and Supplementary Excel file 1). Immune assays were performed on 18 participants across all time points, depending on sample availability. Participants who missed blood collection were considered dropouts and excluded from immune analysis. The pre-pandemic samples were obtained from a separate study34,35 conducted among healthy volunteers, with collection taking place between November 2019 and January 2020. Clinical details, including age and sex, are summarised in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Excel file 1 (Sheet 3).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated using density gradient centrifugation as described previously34,35. Isolated PBMC were re-suspended at 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×106 cells/mL in cryopreservation medium (90%FBS and 10%DMSO (Sigma) and incubated first at −80 °C overnight in a freezing container- Mr. FrostyTM (Nalgene, Rochester, New York, USA) and then stored in liquid nitrogen until further analysis.

Production of influenza viruses used for HAI testing and HA titre assessment

The influenza viruses (A/Victoria/2570/2019 (IAV H1N1), A/Darwin/9/2021 (IBV H3N2), B/Austria/1359417/2021 (IBV Austria), B/Phuket/3073/2013 (IBV Phuket)) used for HAI testing were provided from NIBSC (National Institute for Biological Standards and Control).

The viral growth was performed in T175 tissue-culture flasks pre-seeded with Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) (ATCC #CCL-34) cells at a concentration of 1.3 × 10⁵ cells/mL in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM) supplemented with 0.5% FBS. After an 18–24-h incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂, the flasks reached ~90% confluency and were washed twice with sterile Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS, Euroclone), without calcium and magnesium.

The inoculation was carried out using Influenza virus diluted in viral growth medium consisting of Gibco™ OptiPRO™ serum-free medium supplemented with 1% L-glutamine, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 2 µg/mL tosyl phenylalanyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK)-treated trypsin. Each flask was infected at multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10-4. The virus was allowed to adsorb onto the cell monolayer for 1 h at 37 °C for Influenza A strains and at 35 °C for Influenza B strains in 5% CO₂.

After adsorption, 45 mL of viral growth medium was added to each flask. The flasks were then incubated over days at 37 °C for Influenza A strains and at 35 °C for Influenza B strains, in 5% CO₂.

Each flask was monitored daily until 70–80% Cytopathic Effect (CPE) was observed. The supernatant was then harvested and centrifuged at 266 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was collected, gently homogenised, aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Before testing, the viral HA titre of each strain was assessed in a 96-well V bottom plate by incubating the propagated virus with turkey RBC solution at 0.5% for 60 min. The HA titre was expressed as Haemagglutinant Units (HAU), as indicated by the WHO Manual, where one unit of hemagglutination is contained in the end-point dilution of the HA titration. For HI testing, the propagated virus was then diluted in a DPBS solution to obtain a final concentration of 160 HAU/ml. A back-titration assay was performed to assess the proper dilution of the virus.

Flu-specific antibody responses: HAI assay

HAI measurement was performed by VisMederi Srl according to internal procedures and was carried out as previously published39,65,66. Briefly, serum samples, positive and negative controls, were treated in a ratio of 1:3 with receptor-destroying enzyme from Vibrio cholerae (RDE) (Denka Seiken Co.), incubated overnight at 37 °C, then heat-inactivated for 1 h at 56 °C. Serum samples could contain no-specific agglutinin, which can bind red blood cells (RBC) giving false negative. Serum could be treated with RBCs to deplete sera from this not-specific signal. RDE-treated sera are incubated 1–2 h at 4 °C with a 12.5% RBC solution. Sera were centrifuged then the adsorbed serum is removed. After this preliminary treatment, each serum sample was serially diluted two-fold and tested in duplicate.

The same amount of virus solution (4 HAU/25 μl) was added to each dilution, then plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Following incubation, a solution of 0.5%turkey red blood cells was added to each well, and plates were incubated again for 1 h at RT. If present, specific antibodies against viral HA interfere with the capability of HA itself to bind surface glycoproteins of erythrocytes, thus agglutinating red blood cells. Therefore, inhibition of the hemagglutination phenomenon results in a change in the appearance of the well. The read-out of each plate is performed twice by two operators by the naked eye, distinguishing between the presence or not of hemagglutination. The HAI titre was calculated as the reciprocal value of the highest serum dilution where hemagglutination is inhibited. Correctness of virus dilution to 4 HAU/25 μl was checked out for each test through a dedicated back titration plate. A serum sample with an HAI titre≥10 was considered positive, while a serum sample with an HAI titre≥40 was considered as protected.

SARS-CoV-2 binding assay

Plasma isolated from heparinized whole blood was used to measure SARS-CoV-2 Spike, RBD or nucleocapsid-specific IgG using an in-house ELISA34. Briefly, a half area high-binding ELISA microplate (Corning) was coated with either Spike, RBD monomer, or nucleocapsid at a final concentration of 3 μg/mL and left overnight at 4 °C. Following a wash with PBS-T (PBS (Gibco) with 0.05% Tween-20 (Sigma)), the wells are blocked using 5% milk (HiMedia) in PBS-T for 1 h. Positive control plasma (pooled SARS-CoV-2/COVID RT-PCR positive plasma; 1:25), negative control plasma (pooled RT-PCR SARS-CoV-2/COVID RT-PCR negative plasma; 1:25), monoclonal antibodies—CR3009 (2 μg/mL) and CR3022 (0.2 μg/mL) and blank (only 5%milk in PBS-T) was added along with the test plasma (diluted 1:25 with milk) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Secondary antibody IgG (Goat-anti-human-Fc-Ap; Jackson) was added after the wells were washed with PBS-T and incubated for 1 h at RT. Wells are washed with PBS-T, AP substrate (1 AP tablet (Sigma) in 5 mL Alkaline phosphate stain buffer) and read at 405 nm. Background subtracted data is shown.

Antigen stimulation for measuring T-cell effector responses

An optimised stimulation was used as described previously34,35,66,67. Briefly, thawed PBMC were seeded in 96-well round-bottom plates (Falcon) at 1 × 106 cells/well in complete RPMI, which comprised of RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), glutamine (Thermo Scientific) and antibiotics (Thermo Scientific). After 2 h of rest, cells were either cultured without stimuli (no stimulation background control) or stimulated with the following antigens: FluarixTM (GlaxoSmithKline Pharmaceuticals LTD) diluted 1:100; PepMix™ Influenza A (HA/H1N1/Victoria/2019) (IAV H1N1), PepMix™ Influenza A (HA/H3N2/Darwin/2021) (IBV H3N2), PepMix™ Influenza B (HA/H0N0/Austria/2021) (IBV Austria), PepMix™ Influenza B (HA/H0N0/Phuket/2013) (IBV Phuket), PepMix™ Influenza A (NP/Ann Arbour (H2N2) IBV (NP) (H2N2), all sourced from JPT peptide technologies; PepTivator® SARS-CoV-2 Prot_S, (Miltenyi Biotec); PepMix™ HCoV-HKU1 (Spike Glycoprotein)- (Common cold virus) (JPT peptide technologies). All stimuli were added at a pre-titrated final concentration of 0.5 μg/mL. Costimulatory antibodies CD28/49d (BD Biosciences) at 0.5 μg/mL were added to all stimulation conditions, including negative control and incubated for 20 h at 37 °C. Phytohemagglutinin (PHA, Remel) at final concentration of 4 μg/mL was included as a positive control. Brefeldin A and Monensin (BioLegend) were added for the last 18 h of stimulation.

Flow cytometry: Intracytoplasmic Cytokine Staining (ICS) assay

After 20 h of antigenic stimulation (see above), cells were stained first with viability dye AviD (Thermo Scientific) followed by staining with 50 µl pre Fix/Prem (referring to staining before fixing and permeabilization of cells) (MasterMix column, Supplementary Table 1, T cell effector cytokine panel) cocktail prepared in PBS + 2%FBS for 30 min at RT in the dark. Next, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 1X FACS lysis buffer (BD Biosciences) for 10 min at RT. After washing, cells were permeabilised by addition of 200 μl of 1X Perm wash solution (BD Biosciences) and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Cells were pelleted and stained with a 50 µl post Fix/Prem (referring to staining after fixing and permeabilization of cells) (MasterMix column, Supplementary Table 1, T cell effector cytokine panel) cocktail containing antibodies against intracellular (IC) markers for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, cells were washed and re-suspended for flow cytometry analysis. Cell fluorescence data were acquired using the 5-laser, 18-parameter BD FACSAria™ Fusion flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), operated with BD FACSDiva™ software (version 8.0.1) as described34,35,66,67. All flow cytometry data were analysed using FlowJo 10.9.0 (BD Biosciences). The gating strategy was as follows: singlets were identified by plotting FSC-H versus FSC-A to exclude doublets. From the singlet population, AviD+ and dump lineage markers (CD14 and CD19) were excluded. The remaining cells were then gated on FSC-A versus SSC-A to identify the lymphocyte population. Non-specific antibody aggregates were removed (gated out) from the lymphocytes using the APC versus BV650 channels, all subsequent downstream analysis was performed on the complementary, ‘NOT gated’ population. After the exclusion of non-specific cells, CD3+ and CD3- cells was gated. CD4+, CD8+, NKT and γδ T-cells were gated within the CD3+ population. To avoid double-counting, NKT and γδ T-cells were gated to exclude any CD4 or CD8 cells. The CD3- population was then used to identify NK cells based on CD56 expression. Finally, effector cytokines, activation-induced markers (AIM) and memory subsets were analysed within each of these immune cell subsets. A representative gating strategy is provided in Fig. 2A.

Effector cytokine gates were set based on no stimulation control (Supplementary Fig. 10) and strictly maintained across all samples. Antigen-specific cytokine frequencies in adaptive and innate immune subsets were plotted after background subtraction (no stimulation negative control of the matched sample). Background subtraction was performed using Pestle Version 2. The frequencies of PHA-induced cytokines (positive control), after background subtraction, did not show a significant difference across time points (Supplementary Fig. 10). Polyfunctionality of CD4+ and CD8+ cells expressing combinations of IFNγ, IL-2, TNF and IL-17A + F (acquired from FlowJo- combination gates) was analysed with SPICE version 6.1 software34,35,66,67.

OMIQ data analysis of antigen-specific T-cell responses

UMAP, FlowSOM analysis was performed using OMIQ data analysis software (www.omiq.ai) as described previously34,66. Briefly, FCS files were uploaded to OMIQ platform along with metadata information. Cells were sequentially gated into singlets based on FSC-A vs FSC-H >live cells >CD3+ and CD3-. CD3+ cells are further classified into CD4+ and CD8+ cells. CD4+ and CD8+ cells from all-time points were digitally concatenated and randomly subsampled to a total of 2 million and 0.8 million cells, respectively, where each time point has equal numbers of cells. UMAP and FlowSOM were performed on the subsampled CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells. Volcano plots were generated with the integrated edgeR tool. AIM+ Treg cells (CD45RA-CD69+CD154-CD137+) were identified by sequentially gating on CD4+, then CD45RA-, followed by total CD137+ and finally CD69+CD154- (Fig. 7A). Gating thresholds were determined using unstimulated and FMO controls.

Measurement of FluarixTM induced T-cell proliferation

Archived PBMC from T0, T1, T2 and T3 were thawed and labelled with CFSE as described earlier67. Briefly, cells were counted and labelled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as follows: 2 million cells resuspended in 1 ml of PBS + 1% FBS were incubated with 10 mM CFSE (Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, Oregon, USA) at 37 °C for 7 min in the dark. CFSE uptake was stopped by the addition of 5 ml ice-cold complete RPMI media. Cells were pelleted, washed, seeded at 200,000/well in 96-well U-bottom plates (BD Falcon) and cultured for 5 days in a total volume of 200 ml complete RPMI with either FluarixTM at a dilution of 1:100 or 1 µg/ml of SARS-CoV-2 peptide pool (Miltenyi Biotec). Cells cultured without stimuli served as the negative control. Co-stimulatory anti-CD28/49d at a concentration of 0.5 µg/ml was added to all culture conditions, including negative control. On the 6th day, CFSE dilution was measured by flow cytometry. Representative gating to enumerate CFSElow CD4 T cells is shown in (Supplementary Fig. 9A).

ICS assay for measurement of PAMP responses

Archived PBMC from N = 12 pre-pandemic recruited in 2019–2020 (Supplementary Table 2), and N = 6 post-pandemic T0 samples from the Flu-study (Table 1), were utilised. Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed and seeded at 1 × 106 cells/well in 96-well flat-bottom plates (Eppendorf) in 200 μL of complete RPMI and stimulated with 1 ng/ml (gram negative bacterial lipopolysaccharide) LPS (Sigma) or without any stimulus (no PAMP control, unstimulated) for 2 h. Brefeldin A was added after 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed and stained with a panel of antibodies (Supplementary Table 1, innate panel) and data acquired and analysed as previously described34,35,66,67. The gating strategy involved sequentially identifying singlets (FSC-A vs FSC-H), live cells and then the monocyte population (CD14 + CD3-CD56-CD16-HLA-DR+). Effector cytokines were then gated within monocytes. A representative gating strategy is shown in Fig. 9A and representative monocyte effector staining shown in Fig. 9B.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed on data exported from FlowJo. Graphical representations and statistical tests were performed in Prism version 10.1.1 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). Nonparametric tests were used for all comparisons. Statistical significance was assessed by Friedman test along with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. P values are indicated as an asterisk using the following criteria: *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, ****P ≤ 0.0001.
