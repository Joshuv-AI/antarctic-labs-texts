Treatment patterns in homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia: Undertreatment and delayed adoption of low-density lipoprotein receptor–independent therapies
Authors: Dana J Murdock, Li Wang, Jing Gu, Robert J Sanchez, Richard Godin, Eric J Brandt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHomozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH) is an ultrarare disease characterized by extremely high levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) dysfunction, making most LDLR function–dependent lipid-lowering therapies (LLTs) less effective. For HoFH, LDLR-independent LLTs are usually required to meaningfully reduce LDL-C levels and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk.ObjectiveTo examine treatment patterns and pathways among people with HoFH.MethodsA retrospective HoFH cohort was formed using closed claims for evinacumab and lomitapide from the Komodo Healthcare Map® and confirmed diagnoses from myRARE®. myRARE data were de-identified and linked to the Komodo Healthcare Map. LLTs and apheresis at the index date (the last day of the most recent enrollment period) were used to describe contemporary treatment patterns among individuals with HoFH. A reverse Sankey plot described how LLT use evolved over time.ResultsIn the overall cohort, 15.9% (42/264) did not have LLT claims during the 6 months before and including the index date. At the index date, 42.8% (113/264) of individuals were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs only, including statins, ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors, bempedoic acid, and bile acid sequestrants. Only 38.3% (101/264) of participants were dispensed LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapy (evinacumab/lomitapide). Nineteen individuals (7.2%) used apheresis with or without other LLTs. Over the 3 years before the index date, LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapy use increased (17.1% to 43.2%); use of LDLR-dependent LLTs decreased slightly (49.2% to 40.5%).ConclusionIn the United States, individuals with HoFH are currently undertreated, with significant delays in the adoption of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies.Keywords: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, Lipid-lowering therapy, Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Treatment patterns

Introduction

Homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH) is an ultrarare condition that impacts approximately 1 in 160,000 to 360,000 individuals worldwide, with an estimated 1000 to 2000 individuals in the United States [[1], [2], [3]]. It is the most severe form of familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) [4] and is characterized by markedly elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [2]. HoFH results in low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) dysfunction, which impairs clearance of LDL-C from the circulation [5]. Consequently, LDL-C concentrations in people with HoFH are typically dangerously high (often >400 mg/dL), placing them at significant risk for premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), cardiac events, and early mortality [6,7].

Early diagnosis and timely initiation of lipid-lowering therapies (LLTs) are essential to prevent premature ASCVD and mortality in individuals with HoFH [4,8]. The most recent US National Lipid Association (NLA) expert clinical consensus recommends LDL-C goals of <55 mg/dL for secondary prevention of ASCVD and <70 mg/dL for primary prevention of ASCVD among adults with FH, with even lower goals for those with a particularly high risk of ASCVD [3]. However, severely elevated baseline LDL-C concentrations and hyporesponsiveness to many LLTs makes achieving these goals in HoFH extremely challenging [3]. Standard first-line pharmacological LLTs, including statins, ezetimibe, and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9is), used alone or in combination, provide limited LDL-C reduction in HoFH [[9], [10], [11]], largely because they act through the LDLR pathway, which is severely impaired in this population [12,13]. As a result, the vast majority of people with HoFH exhibit an inadequate response to the triple combination of high-intensity statin, ezetimibe, and PCSK9is, and therefore will need one or more of the pharmacological therapies or procedures that work independently of the LDLR pathway, such as evinacumab, lomitapide, and lipoprotein apheresis [3].

Despite the availability of effective LDLR-independent therapies, prior studies suggest that people with HoFH remain substantially undertreated in real-world practice, especially with LDLR-independent therapies, and experience considerable delays in treatment escalation [[14], [15], [16]]. In the present study, we aimed to use large‑scale, real-world claims data to further characterize the contemporary LLT patterns of individuals with HoFH in the United States, and to describe the treatment pathway among those receiving LLTs.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study used administrative claims data collected from the Komodo Healthcare Map® claims database (data available from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2024) and information from the myRARE® patient support program sponsored by Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc. (data available from February 11, 2011, to December 1, 2024). The Komodo Healthcare Map contains the medical and pharmacy claims of over 330 million patients in the United States, with claims from over 150 private insurers, inclusive of patients with commercial, individual, state-based exchanges, Medicare Advantage, and Medicaid managed‑care insurance coverage. myRARE is a United States-based patient support program that provides financial support and resources to help eligible individuals access commercially available evinacumab.

At the time of the study, there was no International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis code specifically for HoFH, and thus identification of individuals with HoFH from the Komodo Healthcare Map claims database alone was not possible. Therefore, we included individuals in this study who: 1) had at least one dispensation for evinacumab or lomitapide (United States Food and Drug Administration–approved LDLR-independent LLTs solely for HoFH [17,18]) based on closed claims in the database (January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2024); or 2) were enrolled in the myRARE program. Individuals enrolled in myRARE are required to have a clinically or genetically confirmed diagnosis of HoFH. Each individual enrolled in the myRARE program was de-identified and tokenized using Datavant (Datavant, Inc., San Francisco, CA), a privacy-preserving record-linkage software. The unique tokens generated for each de-identified individual were used to link them with their claims data in the Komodo Healthcare Map. Additionally, at least 6 months of continuous medical data and pharmacy enrollment in Komodo Healthcare Map claims data were required for inclusion in the study. The most recent enrollment period was used to examine treatment patterns; the last day of this period was the index date. The 6-month period before the index date was considered the baseline period, and treatments before the most recent therapy were described.

Assessments

Baseline demographic characteristics (such as age, sex, and race/ethnicity), payer type, and clinical characteristics were sourced from the Komodo Healthcare Map. Clinical characteristics were identified using at least one ICD-10 code (Supplementary ICD-10 codes), including the presence of ASCVD and subtype (cerebrovascular disease, other coronary heart disease, peripheral artery disease, acute coronary syndrome), hypertension, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, FH diagnosis, and family history of FH. Data on genetic diagnoses for HoFH and xanthoma presentation were available only for individuals who were included in myRARE.

Use of pharmacological LLTs such as statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9is, lomitapide, evinacumab, bempedoic acid, and bile acid sequestrants (BAS), based on claims during the 6-month baseline period, was evaluated and determined using relevant National Drug Code (NDC) codes (Supplementary NDC codes). Apheresis use during the 14 days prior to the index date was determined using relevant Current Procedural Terminology or Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes. For baseline assessment, individuals were categorized into three groups based on the pharmacological LLTs they were dispensed, defined using the following hierarchy: 1) LDLR-independent pharmacological LLTs (lomitapide or evinacumab); 2) LDLR-dependent LLTs (statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9is, bempedoic acid, or BAS); and 3) no LLT. Apheresis was evaluated separately and was not mutually exclusive with pharmacological treatment categories.

Data analysis

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (mean [standard deviation; SD] and median [25th to 75th percentile] for continuous variables, and number and frequency for categorical variables).

LLT agents, combinations, and procedures at the index date were used to describe contemporary treatment patterns among individuals with HoFH. Furthermore, four treatment categories in order from low to high were defined as no treatment, LDLR-dependent LLTs, LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies, and apheresis, and individuals were categorized into these groups based on the highest category of LLT prescribed. The proportion of individuals belonging to each category was reported. For example, if a person was dispensed lomitapide and a statin, they were categorized into the LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies group; if a person was dispensed evinacumab and underwent apheresis, they were categorized into the apheresis group.

A reverse Sankey plot was developed to describe how LLT use evolved over time for the study cohort. Starting from the index date of the most recent enrollment period, each person was traced back to the first day of the last continuous enrollment period or 3 years before the index date, whichever occurred first. Categories of LLTs were determined every 3 months during this period. Individuals were categorized into no treatment, LDLR-dependent LLTs, LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapies, and apheresis, as described above. Subgroup analyses were performed for individuals aged <18 years versus those aged ≥18 years, and individuals with a genetic diagnosis of HoFH versus those with a clinical diagnosis of HoFH, and a separate reverse Sankey plot was generated for each subgroup. This study used de-identified claims data and was exempt from institutional review board review.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As of December 2024, 338 individuals with HoFH were enrolled in myRARE. Additionally, 712 individuals in the Komodo Healthcare Map claims database were dispensed evinacumab or lomitapide from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2024. From these two sources, 525 unique individuals with HoFH were identified during the study period, of whom 264 had at least 6 months of continuous enrollment in medical and pharmacy plans and were included in the study population.

On the index date, the overall mean (SD) age was 49 (19) years (Table 1). Most individuals (89.8%) in the overall population were aged ≥18 years and 65.5% were female. Of the individuals who reported race and ethnicity (220/264; 83.3%), most were non-Hispanic White (60.0%). Commercial payers were the most frequently reported insurance type (49.8%). Five of the 264 individuals (1.9%) had chronic kidney disease and 87 (33.0%) had any type of ASCVD. In addition, 65 (24.6%) had hypertension and 38 (14.4%) had diabetes. Nearly half of the individuals had an ICD-10 code related to a diagnosis of FH (123/264; 46.6%); 35 (13.3%) had a documented family history of FH. Among those individuals with information available in myRARE, 35.9% (37/103) reported a confirmed genetic diagnosis for HoFH and 22.4% (24/107) reported the presence of xanthomas.

Most individuals were dispensed one (27.3%) or two (26.9%) LLTs during the most recent 6-month period of enrollment; 20.8% were dispensed three LLTs and 9.1% were dispensed four or five LLTs. Nineteen (7.2%) individuals reported apheresis use within 14 days of the index date (Table 1). A total of 42 individuals (15.9%) did not have any claims for LLTs. Among those who were dispensed pharmacological LLTs (222/264), 121 were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs only and 101 were dispensed LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies (with or without LDLR-dependent LLTs). Baseline demographic characteristics between all three groups (LDLR-dependent LLTs, LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies, and no LLT) were generally similar, although the group without any claims for LLT had a higher proportion of patients with Medicaid coverage and a lower proportion with commercial insurance coverage compared with the other two groups (Table 1). The no LLT group also had fewer diagnoses of ASCVD, hypertension, and diabetes compared with the LDLR-dependent LLT and LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapy groups. Most of the individuals (81.2%) in the LDLR-independent pharmacotherapy group had a documented FH diagnosis, compared with only 30.6% in the LDLR‑dependent LLT group and 9.5% in the no LLT group. In the group of LDLR‑dependent LLTs, 26.5% of individuals were on three or more LLTs, while in the group of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies 46.6% of individuals were on three or more LLTs, with 4% on five LLTs (Table 1).

Contemporary treatment patterns

Of the individuals who were dispensed LLTs (222/264), many different combinations were observed (Fig. 1A). PCSK9is were the most frequently dispensed LLT (114/264; 43.2%), with 22 individuals (8.3%) dispensed PCSK9i monotherapy and 92 (34.8%) dispensed PCSK9is in combination with other LLTs. This was followed by ezetimibe (96/264; 36.4%) and high-intensity statins (85/264; 32.2%). Seventy-four (28.0%) of 264 individuals were dispensed evinacumab, 30 (11.4%) were dispensed lomitapide, and 19 (7.2%) reported apheresis use.

As shown in Fig. 1B, 113/264 individuals (42.8%) were dispensed LDLR‑dependent LLTs only, i.e., one or multiple of the following agents: statins; ezetimibe; PCSK9is; bempedoic acid; or BAS. Only 64/264 (24.2%) of participants were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs plus LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies (i.e., evinacumab and lomitapide). Additionally, 26/264 (9.8%) participants were dispensed LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies only. Eight individuals reported apheresis use in addition to dispensations of LDLR-dependent LLTs and LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies, four underwent apheresis only, four underwent apheresis plus were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs, and three reported apheresis use plus LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies.

Treatment pathways

As shown in the reverse Sankey plot (Fig. 2A), adoption of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies gradually increased from 17.1% to 43.2% for the individuals with available data during the 3 years before the index date. The proportion of individuals receiving only LDLR-dependent LLTs decreased from 49.2% to 40.5%, while those receiving no treatment decreased from 27.1% to 12.5%. The use of apheresis remained low, decreasing from 6.6% to 3.8%. Comparing adoption of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies on the index date versus 1 year before the index date, 8% (43.2% vs. 35.3%) more participants were dispensed LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies.

The subgroup analysis for participants aged ≥18 years at the index date (n = 237) showed a similar treatment pathway trend to the overall cohort (Fig. 2B). Among the subgroup of those aged <18 years on the index date (n = 27), the proportion on LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies was higher than in the overall population (63.0% vs 43.2%, respectively) (Fig. 2C). This increased from 17.6% for those individuals with available data 3 years before the index date. Accordingly, over the 3‑year period, there was a substantial decrease (41.2% to 14.8%) in the proportion of individuals who were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs only among the age <18 years subgroup. In this subgroup, the proportion of those who were untreated was 22.2% on the index date versus 35.3% at 3 years before the index date.

Among the subgroup of individuals who were known to have a genetic confirmation for HoFH at the index date (n = 37), the proportion dispensed LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapies was 43.2%; this increased from 12.5% for those who had available data 3 years before that date (Fig. 2D). The proportion of those dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs decreased from 58.3% at 3 years before index to 40.5% on the index date. The proportion of untreated individuals also decreased from 20.8% to 8.1% at these timepoints.

Among the subgroup of those who were known to have a clinical diagnosis of HoFH, adoption of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies was 25.8% at the index date (17/66), which was substantially lower than in the overall cohort (43.2%; 114/264) and in the genetically confirmed subgroup (43.2%; 16/37) (Fig. 2E). Most individuals with a clinical diagnosis (60.6%) were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs only, and this proportion remained stable over the 3-year period. For all subgroups, apheresis use was under 10% across the 3-year period.

Discussion

In this study, we found that a large portion of individuals with HoFH remain substantially undertreated. According to the claims database, over 10% of those studied were not dispensed any LLTs during the most recent enrollment period of 6 months before the index date. Among treated individuals, the majority were receiving LDLR-dependent LLTs only, which are known to have a substantially diminished effect among those with HoFH, a disease of LDLR dysfunction, compared with heterozygous FH [3]. Over half of the population were dispensed only one or two LLTs, while <40% were on LDLR-independent pharmacotherapy, namely evinacumab or lomitapide, and only 7% underwent apheresis.

These findings are consistent with data from another retrospective study that showed that more than half of participants with HoFH studied were treated with only one or two concurrent LLTs [15], and existing data from the CASCADE FH Registry which reported that only 23% and 15% of adults with HoFH were on lomitapide and evinacumab, respectively, at their most recent follow-up visit [14]. Although the use of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies gradually increased over the 3 years prior to index in this study, likely due to recent guidelines and approvals of LLTs, the adoption rate remained slow, with only an additional 8% of individuals being dispensed LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapies in the most recent year of this study. Further research has also highlighted significant delays in treatment escalation to advanced therapies. One study involving interviews with United States physicians and individuals with HoFH reported an average of approximately 10 months to escalate from a statin to a PCSK9i, and a further ∼22 months to escalate from a statin to evinacumab [16].

As suggested by the 2026 NLA expert clinical consensus [3], an inadequate response to triple combination therapy (high-intensity statin, ezetimibe, and PCSK9is) is likely in most, if not all, individuals with HoFH and treatment intensification using at least one LDLR-independent pharmacological LLT, including evinacumab or lomitapide, or apheresis will be required. In addition, the consensus recommends that the initiation of LDLR-independent therapies should be considered after only one or two doses of PCSK9is, which equates to approximately 1–2 months [3]. The low and delayed adoption of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies observed in this study therefore underscores a substantial gap in current clinical practice. As a result, the HoFH population rarely achieves guideline-recommended LDL-C goals and remains at markedly elevated risk for cardiovascular events and early mortality, as demonstrated by other retrospective studies [15,19].

Several differences were identified when comparing those who were not dispensed any LLTs with those who were dispensed LDLR-dependent LLTs alone or LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies. Untreated individuals had less commercial insurance coverage but used Medicaid more frequently, suggesting lower socioeconomic status and highlighting the potential contribution of access barriers to undertreatment. Additionally, individuals without any LLT dispensation appeared to have fewer documented comorbidities, whereas those dispensed LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies were more likely to have a diagnosis of FH. This pattern may reflect that only the individuals with the most severe HoFH have been formally diagnosed and aggressively treated, as a confirmed FH diagnosis is usually required for insurance approval of specialty therapies. Alternatively, the lower comorbidity burden among untreated individuals may be an artifact of limited healthcare access, resulting in underdiagnosis of FH and other comorbid conditions.

Of the individuals dispensed LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies, a marked proportion were receiving evinacumab compared with lomitapide, potentially due to insurer coverage, provider awareness, the requirements for Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy certification for lomitapide [20], or a documented favorable benefit–risk profile of evinacumab [17,18]. Despite the effectiveness of lipoprotein apheresis as an LDLR-independent therapy recommended for HoFH and real-world data showing its effect in delayed onset of ASCVD [3,19], reported apheresis use in this study remained extremely low. This may be due to limitations including its scarce availability and the time-intensive nature of the procedure, which has been reported to adversely impact quality of life [21,22]; these factors may be especially apparent now that newer and more convenient pharmacological LDLR-independent LLTs are available.

Based on the subgroup analyses, the subgroup aged <18 years appeared to have higher dispensation of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapy than the overall cohort; however, a substantial proportion remained untreated with any LLT. It is crucial for pediatricians and parents to understand that LLTs are generally well‑tolerated in children and adolescents [3]. A current expert clinical consensus recommend that, once a person has been diagnosed with HoFH, LLT should be started as soon as possible to prevent premature cardiovascular events and mortality [3]. In addition, results from the subgroup analyses of genetically versus clinically diagnosed individuals indicated that genetic confirmation seems to be helpful in prompting the initiation of LDLR‑independent pharmacotherapy, as a higher proportion of individuals who were clinically diagnosed were dispensed standard LDLR-dependent LLTs only compared with those with a genetic diagnosis. However, initiation of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapy remained slow even among those with genetically confirmed HoFH.

LDLR function is a key determinant of response to LDLR-dependent therapies in HoFH, with markedly attenuated efficacy in individuals with null/null genotypes and low rates of LDL-C goal attainment, underscoring the need for LDLR-independent therapies [23]. Functional LDLR genotyping may therefore provide a framework for precision medicine in HoFH at diagnosis by enabling prediction of response to LDLR-dependent therapies, and support earlier initiation of LDLR-independent therapy, particularly for individuals with null/null genotypes in whom disease burden is high and LDL-C goal attainment remains limited.

This study is subject to certain limitations. Claims-based data have the potential for diagnostic or procedural coding errors, as well as incomplete or missing data. Major pharmacy chains offer generic prescriptions, which include statins that are widely available for a very low monthly cost. If individuals take advantage of generic drug discount programs and pay for these prescriptions outside of their insurance plans, these transactions may not be noted in the dispensations via the claims database, which may lead to systematic underestimation of statin use [24]. The inclusion of a small subset of individuals aged <18 years limits the generalizability of findings to the pediatric population. Additionally, individuals with HoFH were selected based on the dispensation of lomitapide or evinacumab, or enrollment in myRARE. Given these criteria, the potential for survival bias and care navigation bias should be considered. The cohort may overrepresent individuals with greater healthcare and specialist access, longer disease duration, or milder LDLR dysfunction, reflecting a best-case scenario of individuals with HoFH. As a result, undertreatment may be underestimated in this study. In addition, no LDL-C level data are presented in this paper, so it is unclear how treatment changes may relate to the achievement or lack of achievement of LDL-C goals.

Conclusion

Individuals with HoFH in current United States clinical practice remain substantially undertreated, with significant delays in the adoption of LDLR-independent pharmacotherapies and significant underutilization of lipoprotein apheresis. In alignment with the 2026 NLA expert clinical consensus recommendations [3], early initiation of LDLR-independent therapies is essential to help those affected achieve guideline‑recommended LDL-C goals and reduce their risk of cardiovascular events and mortality.
