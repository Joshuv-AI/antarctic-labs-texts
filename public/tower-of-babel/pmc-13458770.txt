An interpretable surrogate modeling framework for rice husk ash concrete using copula-based virtual sampling
Authors: Van Hiep Huynh, Hung La, Thi My Dung Huynh, Tan Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study proposes an interpretable and variance-aware sensitivity analysis framework for analyzing the compressive strength of rice husk ash concrete under correlated input conditions, using statistically consistent virtual sampling and surrogate-based sensitivity analysis. A Gaussian copula is used to construct a virtual design space that preserves empirical marginal distributions and inter-variable dependencies. An Extreme Gradient Boosting surrogate optimized using the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy achieves high predictive accuracy on unseen data (mean R2 = 0.974). Global variance attribution is performed using a Sobol-inspired, permutation-based sensitivity measure designed for correlated inputs, enabling robust sensitivity analysis of black-box models without relying on the independence assumption. Cement, Water, and Age are identified as dominant contributors, with notable interaction effects between Cement–Water and Coarse aggregate–Superplasticizer. These findings are further supported by SHAP analysis and partial dependence plots. External validation using independently published experimental data and deployment in a web-accessible environment demonstrate the framework’s reliability and practical relevance for sustainable concrete mix design. Overall, the proposed framework offers a practical and interpretable tool for supporting robust and sustainability-oriented concrete mix design under correlated input conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55368-1.

Introduction

The need for sustainable development in the construction industry has become increasingly urgent in light of rising environmental concerns, resource limitations, and the intensifying effects of climate change1. Among the most significant contributors to global carbon emissions is cement production, which accounts for approximately 7–8% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions worldwide2. In response, considerable attention has been directed toward the incorporation of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) into concrete mixtures as a viable strategy to reduce clinker usage and mitigate environmental impacts3,4.

Rice husk ash (RHA), a silica-rich agricultural byproduct, has emerged as a promising SCM due to its pozzolanic reactivity and abundance in rice-producing countries5. When properly processed and integrated into concrete, RHA can improve workability, durability, and long-term strength while reducing reliance on Portland cement6. However, the inclusion of RHA also introduces greater complexity into the mix design process. The mechanical performance of RHA-based concrete is influenced by a multitude of interrelated variables—such as cement and water content, aggregate proportions, admixture dosage, and curing conditions—that interact in nonlinear and often non-intuitive ways7.

Conventional empirical models and regression-based approaches are limited in their ability to capture these intricate relationships8. They typically assume linearity, input independence, or narrow design domains, which fail to reflect the real-world complexity of concrete behavior. As a result, predictive accuracy is often compromised, and more importantly, the derived models offer little interpretability for engineering decision-making under multivariate interactions9.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has been increasingly adopted to model concrete properties due to its capacity to learn complex nonlinear mappings from data10–15. However, most ML applications in this field suffer from two key limitations. First, they often treat input variables as independently distributed and rely on uniform or random sampling methods that can produce unrealistic mix combinations16–20—particularly problematic in SCM-blended systems where variables such as water-to-cement ratio, aggregate gradation, and admixture dosage are strongly correlated. Second, while many ML models achieve high predictive accuracy, they typically operate as black boxes, offering little insight into which variables govern model output or how design changes affect performance21,22.

These limitations also directly impact global sensitivity and interpretability analyses. Variance-based methods such as Sobol sensitivity analysis23–25, and its widely adopted Monte Carlo implementation by Saltelli23,24, have been extensively used to rank input importance and interaction effects. However, classical Sobol–Saltelli frameworks inherently rely on assumptions of input independence and structured resampling schemes26,27. When applied to machine learning surrogate models trained on correlated experimental data, these assumptions may lead to unrealistic sample configurations, ambiguous variance attribution, and potentially misleading sensitivity rankings28. More fundamentally, the variance decomposition in classical Sobol analysis is defined under an independence-based probability measure; when this assumption is violated, the resulting sensitivity indices no longer correspond to the true contribution of inputs under the actual joint distribution, thereby undermining their physical interpretability. Consequently, the direct application of traditional Sobol or Saltelli methods becomes problematic in practical engineering–ML settings where correlated inputs and black-box models are unavoidable.

Recent studies have increasingly recognized the limitations of classical sensitivity analysis and machine learning frameworks when input variables exhibit statistical dependence. In particular, dependence-aware global sensitivity approaches have been developed to improve variance attribution under correlated inputs, overcoming the restrictive independence assumption commonly associated with classical Sobol decomposition29–31. In parallel, uncertainty- and variability-oriented machine learning methods have also been increasingly applied in cement-based material research to improve predictive robustness and support more reliable mix design decisions under heterogeneous experimental conditions32–34. These developments provide an important theoretical basis for the present study and further motivate the need for an integrated, dependence-aware interpretability framework for rice husk ash concrete.

Despite these recent advances, the growing use of machine learning in sustainable concrete design, existing studies have largely focused on either surrogate prediction models complemented by explainable artificial intelligence techniques35–37 or statistical sampling techniques38,39 in isolation, with limited efforts to integrate these components within a unified framework. In contrast, this study combines dependency-preserving copula-based virtual sampling, CMA-ES-optimized surrogate modeling40,41, and global interpretability analysis into a statistically consistent pipeline for RHA concrete. The key contribution lies in this coherent methodological integration, which supports realistic design-space exploration and a more reliable interpretation of variable interactions under correlated mix conditions. As such, the framework extends beyond a purely predictive tool and instead provides model-based insights that may assist in guiding robust and sustainable mix design decisions.

To overcome these limitations and bridge the identified gap, this study proposes an integrated and statistically consistent framework that combines dependency-preserving data generation, robust surrogate modeling42, and multi-faceted interpretability into a unified pipeline tailored for RHA concrete43,44. The framework is underpinned by a copula-based virtual laboratory45, which enables the generation of synthetic input samples that preserve both the empirical distributions and inter-variable dependencies observed in experimental mix design datasets26,27. Unlike traditional Monte Carlo or grid-based sampling methods46,47, the use of copulas ensures that the synthetic design space is statistically realistic and free from implausible combinations, thereby providing a coherent foundation for subsequent sensitivity analysis under the true joint input distribution.

Built upon this enriched input domain, the study develops a surrogate model using Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB)—a widely adopted, high-performance tree-based learning algorithm—whose hyperparameters are automatically optimized via the Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES). This coupling provides a flexible yet stable modeling structure capable of learning complex interactions among input features in a stable and data-efficient manner.

Beyond predictive modeling, the framework is designed with interpretability and engineering transparency as core objectives. To that end, the study introduces a permutation-based, variance-oriented sensitivity analysis framework inspired by Sobol-type decomposition, but specifically designed for correlated-input settings. The proposed approach performs variance attribution under a dependence-preserving sampling space constructed via copula-based virtual data generation. As a result, the derived sensitivity measures are defined with respect to the joint input distribution, which differs fundamentally from the independence-based probability space underlying classical Sobol analysis. In addition, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) are applied to the copula-sampled dataset to provide both global and local insights into model behavior. These tools allow for the quantification and visualization of input effects, interaction strengths, and nonlinear response patterns—essential capabilities for engineers seeking to optimize mix proportions in a reliable and informed manner.

This study is positioned at the interface of sustainable concrete engineering and interpretable machine learning. In contrast to existing data-driven approaches that either rely on unrealistic input sampling or apply sensitivity analysis under restrictive independence assumptions, the proposed methodology directly addresses the practical challenges of variance attribution for black-box surrogate models trained on correlated experimental data. The main contributions of this study can be summarized as follows:(i)Copula-based synthetic data generation that preserves both marginal distributions and inter-variable dependencies,(ii)Evolutionary-optimized surrogate modeling to ensure reliable emulation of complex material behavior, and(iii)Permutation-based, dependence-aware variance attribution mechanism that enables meaningful sensitivity analysis without invoking independence assumptions or ANOVA-based decomposition constraints.

Building on our previous copula-based inverse design framework for RHA concrete48, the present study extends the methodology beyond predictive optimization toward a more comprehensive interpretability-driven analysis framework. By unifying statistically realistic sampling, robust surrogate modeling, and multi-level interpretability, the proposed pipeline enables sensitivity and interaction analyses to be performed directly within a physically plausible and dependency-preserving design space. This allows dominant drivers, critical interaction mechanisms, and design-relevant thresholds to be identified in a transparent and reproducible manner.

Benchmark dataset for rice husk ash concrete

The predictive reliability of any ML framework depends fundamentally on the quality, diversity, and representativeness of the dataset used for model development. In concrete mix design, where material combinations and proportions vary widely, generalization—the ability of a model to maintain accuracy when applied to unseen mixtures—is an essential performance criterion.

The dataset employed in this study is a benchmark compilation by Hamidian et al.44, assembled from multiple independent experimental investigations49–89. It comprises 1,150 experimental records for RHA concrete, each containing seven input variables—cement content, rice husk ash content, coarse aggregate content, fine aggregate content, water content, superplasticizer dosage, and curing age (days)—and one target output, the measured compressive strength (MPa).

This dataset provides a broad and representative coverage of mix design conditions, enabling the exploration of diverse binder compositions, aggregate gradations, water-to-binder ratios, and curing regimes. Such diversity ensures that the subsequent modeling framework can capture both linear and nonlinear effects while preserving the realistic dependencies among variables during synthetic data generation. Prior to model training and copula-based sampling, the dataset underwent systematic preprocessing, including unit consistency checks, removal of anomalous values, and feature scaling where necessary, ensuring that both empirical and generated datasets reflect physically feasible and practically relevant concrete mixtures.

It should be noted that the benchmark dataset was compiled from multiple independent experimental studies, and therefore may inherently contain variability arising from differences in raw material sources, specimen preparation procedures, curing environments, and testing protocols. Such cross-study heterogeneity may introduce measurement noise and partially contribute to the observed prediction residuals, particularly during external validation.

Dependence-aware permutation-based first-order and total-effect measures

In this study, we address the challenge of variance-based sensitivity analysis for black-box surrogate models under correlated inputs. Rather than directly estimating classical Sobol indices, which are defined under ANOVA-based independence assumptions23,24, we formulate a dependence-aware permutation framework that quantifies first-order and total-effect contributions on a copula-preserving virtual dataset. This formulation is specifically intended for practical engineering settings in which input variables are statistically dependent and classical structured resampling schemes are no longer appropriate.

To better emulate realistic concrete mix designs, we construct a virtual experimental dataset by sampling from the empirical marginal distributions of each input while preserving their dependencies via a copula-based approach. The sampling procedure is described in detail in Section "Sampling procedure". First-order and total-effect measures are then computed through variable-wise permutation and empirical variance comparison, without requiring structured Sobol sampling matrices or conditional integration94,95.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{f}$$\end{document} be a trained surrogate model and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{y} = \widehat{f}\left( X \right) \in R^{N}$$\end{document} be its predictions on the sampled dataset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \in R^{N \times d}$$\end{document}. For a given variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}$$\end{document}, we define \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{y}_{perm\left( i \right)} = \widehat{f}\left( {X_{perm\left( i \right)} } \right)$$\end{document} as the predictions after permuting the i-th column of X while keeping other columns fixed.

The first-order index is approximated as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{S1}_{{}}^{i} = 1 - \frac{{{\mathrm{Var}}\left( {\widehat{y}_{perm\left( i \right)} } \right)}}{{{\mathrm{Var}}\left( {\widehat{y}} \right)}}.$$\end{document}

This measures the proportion of variance preserved after breaking the dependency structure of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}$$\end{document}, i.e., if permuting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}$$\end{document} causes little change, it implies a low main effect.

The total-effect index is approximated as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{ST}_{{}}^{i} = \frac{{{\mathrm{Var}}\left( {\widehat{y} - \widehat{y}_{perm\left( i \right)} } \right)}}{{{\mathrm{Var}}\left( {\widehat{y}} \right)}}$$\end{document}which measures the total perturbation caused by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{i}$$\end{document}, including both its direct and interaction effects. All variances are computed empirically over the sampled dataset.

Global model interpretability using PDP

To better understand the overall behavior of the optimized surrogate model in the high-dimensional input space, this study employs a widely used global interpretability method known as Partial Dependence Plots, originally introduced by Friedman96. PDP visualizes the marginal impact of one or two input features on the model’s prediction, while integrating out the influence of other variables. These analyses are conducted on a large-scale, high-fidelity virtual dataset that was generated to preserve the true input distributions and inter-variable correlations, thus serving as a reliable “virtual laboratory” for global explanation.

This approach is particularly advantageous when applied to the sampled dataset, as it enables smoother visualization of global trends across the input space, including regions that are sparsely covered or underrepresented in the original experimental data.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{f}$$\end{document} denote the trained surrogate model and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X = \left( {X_{1} ,X_{2} ,...,X_{d} } \right) \in R^{d}$$\end{document} be the input vector. The partial dependence function for a subset of features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \subseteq \left\{ {1,...,d} \right\}$$\end{document} is defined as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PD_{S} \left( {x_{S} } \right) = {\mathrm{E}}_{{X_{ - S} }} \left[ {\widehat{f}\left( {x_{S} ,X_{ - S} } \right)} \right] = \int {\widehat{f}\left( {x_{S} ,X_{ - S} } \right)p} \left( {X_{ - S} } \right)dX_{ - S} ,$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{ - S}$$\end{document} denotes all features not in S, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p\left( {X_{ - S} } \right)$$\end{document} is their marginal distribution. While the classical PDP formulation assumes feature independence, applying it to a synthetic dataset that preserves the original joint distribution helps reduce the occurrence of implausible input combinations. In this study, PDP is employed on the copula-based virtual dataset to visualize smooth marginal trends, which are interpreted as qualitative indications of input–output relationships rather than exact isolated effects.

Result and discussion

Performance of surrogate models

Comparison of surrogate models for selection

To identify the most suitable surrogate model for subsequent advanced analysis techniques, this section compares the performance of five regression models optimized using CMA-ES93: XGB91, Light Gradient Boosting Machine97 (LGBM), Support Vector Regression98 (SVR), Random Forest99 (RF), and Decision Tree100 (DT). Each model is trained and evaluated over 30 randomized train–test splits to ensure robust comparison. Table 1 summarizes their average performance across key metrics, while Fig. 2 visualizes the distribution of results for each model.Fig. 2Violin plots of R2, RMSE, and MAE distributions for different surrogate models on training and testing sets.

Violin plots of R2, RMSE, and MAE distributions for different surrogate models on training and testing sets.

In addition, an ablation study is conducted by including an XGB model tuned using random search (XGB–RS) under the same hyperparameter space and evaluation budget. This allows for a controlled comparison to isolate the effect of the CMA-ES optimization strategy on model performance.

As shown in Table 1, the XGB–CMA-ES model achieves the best overall performance, with the highest test R2 (0.9735), the lowest test RMSE (3.8433 MPa), and the lowest test MAE (2.6181 MPa). Compared to its ablation counterpart (XGB–RS), XGB–CMA-ES consistently delivers improved accuracy (ΔR2 ≈ +0.004, ΔRMSE ≈ –0.28 MPa, ΔMAE ≈ –0.21 MPa), demonstrating the effectiveness of CMA-ES in navigating the hyperparameter space beyond random exploration. LGBM–CMA-ES ranks third, performing closely behind XGB in all metrics. SVR, RF, and DT show a clear drop in accuracy and increased error magnitudes, indicating weaker generalization. The results highlight that tree-based ensemble models, especially gradient boosting, are more capable of capturing the nonlinear interactions present in the dataset compared to shallow learners like SVR or DT.

Figure 2 presents violin plots of the R2, RMSE, and MAE distributions across 30 runs for both training and testing sets. The XGB–CMA-ES model not only achieves the highest central performance but also exhibits minimal variability, as reflected by its narrow and symmetric distribution. Notably, compared to XGB–RS, the CMA-ES–optimized variant shows slightly tighter distributions and reduced dispersion on the test set, indicating improved robustness to data partitioning. In contrast, models like SVR and RF show wider and more dispersed distributions, particularly on the test set, indicating higher sensitivity to data partitioning. These distributional insights further confirm the stability and reliability of the XGB-based surrogate model for downstream tasks.

Based on both average performance metrics and distributional consistency, the XGB–CMA-ES model is selected as the optimal surrogate for subsequent analysis. Its high accuracy and low variance across repeated runs establish a strong foundation for trustable interpretation and design applications.

While this variance-based analysis does not explicitly model predictive uncertainty, it provides a principled framework to explain how output variability arises in the surrogate model. As such, it enhances model interpretability by revealing the relative contributions and interactions of input variables in complex data-driven systems.

To further examine these findings, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis is employed as a complementary, game-theoretic approach to feature attribution. Global interpretability (PDP)

One-dimensional partial dependence plots

To investigate the marginal influence of each input variable on the predicted compressive strength, Fig. 20 presents the one-dimensional partial dependence plots (1D PDPs) derived from the XGB–CMA-ES surrogate model. These plots are computed over the copula-sampled synthetic dataset, offering a global perspective on how each input affects the output across its empirical range while accounting for all other variables in expectation. The results reveal distinct non-linear behaviors and variable-specific trends that support physical interpretability and domain-specific design insight.Fig. 20One-dimensional partial dependence plots showing the marginal effect of each input variable on predicted compressive strength.

One-dimensional partial dependence plots showing the marginal effect of each input variable on predicted compressive strength.

The PDP for Water content (Fig. 20a) displays a clearly negative trend, wherein strength gradually decreases from approximately 60 MPa to below 47 MPa as water dosage increases from 130 to 250 kg/m3. The most significant reduction is observed beyond 180 kg/m3, aligning with the well-known Abrams’ law. This sharp decline corresponds to increased porosity and diluted cementitious paste, confirming the model’s sensitivity to excessive water in mix design. The total magnitude of change (~13 MPa) reinforces water as a dominant variable influencing strength reduction.

In contrast, the PDP for Cement content (Fig. 20b) shows a strong positive effect on strength, particularly in the range of 300 to 500 kg/m3. Within this interval, compressive strength rises sharply from 36 MPa to over 60 MPa. However, beyond 500 kg/m3, the response begins to plateau, indicating diminishing returns where additional cement no longer contributes proportionally to strength gain. This saturation behavior reflects the upper efficiency limit of binder content, where the formation of additional calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) becomes constrained by available water and space within the microstructure.

The Cement–Water interaction can be mechanistically interpreted through the governing role of the water-to-binder ratio. While sufficient water promotes hydration and C–S–H formation, excess water increases capillary porosity after setting, reducing the gel–space ratio and weakening the hardened matrix. This trade-off explains the strong interaction effect observed between cement and water in controlling compressive strength101,102.

The Fine aggregate content (Fig. 20c) reveals a non-linear, unimodal response with a maximum strength of approximately 55 MPa occurring near 650–700 kg/m3. Outside this optimal window, especially beyond 800 kg/m3, the strength declines to values near 40 MPa. This inverted-U pattern suggests an optimal packing density for fine aggregates, beyond which excessive fines likely increase water demand and impair mix workability.

The plot for Coarse aggregate (Fig. 20d) exhibits a more abrupt, stepwise pattern. Strength increases sharply from ~63 MPa to over 70 MPa between 800–900 kg/m3, followed by a pronounced drop to below 55 MPa beyond 1100 kg/m3. This behavior suggests the presence of a critical transition zone in aggregate skeleton formation. At optimal proportions, coarse aggregates enhance load transfer and reduce paste demand; however, excessive coarse content disrupts particle packing and weakens interfacial transition zones, resulting in reduced strength.

The response of Rice husk ash (RHA) content (Fig. 20e) is generally positive, with compressive strength increasing from 52 MPa to above 62 MPa as RHA rises from 0 to 170 kg/m3. Some minor fluctuations are noted between 40–100 kg/m3, likely due to interaction effects. This improvement reflects the dual role of RHA as both a pozzolanic material and a micro-filler. The pozzolanic reaction consumes calcium hydroxide to form additional C–S–H gel, while the fine particle size enhances matrix densification, collectively contributing to strength development103–105.

The PDP for Age (Fig. 20f) follows a classic logarithmic growth trend. Strength increases rapidly during the early curing period (up to ~56 days), followed by stabilization beyond 90 days. The total strength improvement spans over 30 MPa, making Age the most impactful variable in terms of absolute gain. Despite this dominant trend, previous sensitivity analysis indicated a first-order index near zero (S1 = 0), suggesting that the effect of Age is strongly contingent on interaction with other features, particularly cement and water.

Finally, the PDP for Superplasticizer (Fig. 20g) exhibits a non-monotonic, U-shaped curve. A peak strength of ~54 MPa is observed around 8–10 kg/m3, after which performance declines below 46 MPa as the dosage exceeds 25 kg/m3. This behavior reflects the dual effect of superplasticizers: moderate dosages improve particle dispersion and reduce water demand, enhancing hydration efficiency, whereas excessive dosages may induce segregation or retard hydration kinetics, ultimately impairing strength.

Overall, the 1D PDPs provide smooth and interpretable summaries of marginal relationships between each input and the model prediction. Variables such as Age, Cement, and Water exhibit the largest magnitudes of change, while others like Superplasticizer and Coarse aggregate show non-linear effects indicative of threshold or saturation behaviors. These findings offer valuable support for mix design strategies aimed at achieving target strength levels while managing the underlying sensitivity of the response.

External validation against published literature

To further evaluate the robustness and external validity of the proposed modeling framework, an independent validation was conducted against published experimental data on rice husk ash (RHA) concrete reported in the literature106.

In contrast to the present study, where coarse and fine aggregates are treated as separate input variables during model development, the reference dataset in106 aggregates both fractions into a single “aggregate” variable. To ensure a fair and consistent comparison, the optimized XGB–CMA-ES model was retrained using a harmonized training dataset in which coarse and fine aggregates were combined into a unified aggregate feature. It should be noted that the aggregation of fine and coarse aggregates was performed solely for compatibility with the external dataset and does not reflect the structure of the proposed framework. This simplification may reduce the model’s ability to capture aggregate gradation effects and partially explain the observed reduction in predictive accuracy. The retrained model was then evaluated on the external dataset, and residual-based diagnostics were employed to assess prediction bias, error structure, and generalization behavior, as illustrated in Fig. 23.Fig. 23Residual diagnostics for external validation of the proposed model against published RHA concrete data: (a) residuals versus predicted compressive strength, and (b) distribution of residuals.

Residual diagnostics for external validation of the proposed model against published RHA concrete data: (a) residuals versus predicted compressive strength, and (b) distribution of residuals.

Figure 23 reports the external validation of the proposed framework against published RHA concrete data106. On the unseen validation dataset, the retrained XGB–CMA-ES model yields R2 = 0.84, RMSE = 7.2 MPa, and MAE = 4.4 MPa, indicating a slight but reasonable decrease in predictive accuracy under cross-study heterogeneity. The observed reduction in predictive performance can also be attributed to cross-study heterogeneity, including differences in experimental conditions, material sources, and input distributions. In addition, the necessary feature harmonization (including aggregate aggregation) may reduce input granularity and limit the model’s ability to fully capture underlying physical variability.

The residual plot reveals a non-uniform error structure across the strength range: (i) at low predicted strengths (~10–25 MPa), residuals are predominantly positive, suggesting systematic underestimation; (ii) within the mid-range (~25–45 MPa), a dense cluster appears near zero, indicating comparatively better agreement for a substantial portion of samples; (iii) around ~50–70 MPa, residuals spread markedly with many negative values, implying overestimation for part of this regime; and (iv) at higher predicted strengths (~70–85 MPa), residuals return to a narrow band around zero, indicating relatively stable predictions even at elevated compressive strengths. Despite differences in input representation between datasets, these results demonstrate reasonable generalization capability of the proposed XGB–CMA-ES framework when applied to external, unseen experimental data.

Conclusion

This study presents an integrated framework for interpretable and variance-based modeling of RHA concrete, combining a copula-based virtual laboratory with an XGB surrogate model optimized via CMA-ES. The proposed pipeline enables dependency-preserving input sampling, accurate prediction, and interpretable analysis within a statistically consistent synthetic design space. The surrogate model demonstrates strong generalization performance (mean R2 = 0.9989 for training and 0.9735 for testing across 30 trials), providing a reliable basis for downstream analysis.

A key contribution lies in the application of permutation-based, dependence-aware sensitivity analysis, which enables variance attribution and interaction diagnosis directly within correlated input spaces. The results identify Cement, Water, and Age as dominant factors governing compressive strength variability, while highlighting strong interaction effects—particularly involving Superplasticizer and Coarse aggregate—indicating that mix variables must be optimized jointly. Complementary SHAP analysis and partial dependence plots further reveal consistent variable rankings and key nonlinear thresholds, including rapid strength degradation when water exceeds ~180 kg/m3, diminishing returns beyond ~400–450 kg/m3 of cement, and an optimal superplasticizer range of ~8–12 kg/m3. In addition, coarse aggregate contents above ~1000 kg/m3 are associated with reduced strength, while fine aggregate stabilizes near ~650–700 kg/m3, providing insights that may assist in guiding mix design decisions and identifying stable and robust mix configurations.

The framework is further supported by external validation on independent experimental data, demonstrating its robustness under cross-dataset variability. In addition, its deployment as a web-based decision support tool enhances practical applicability by enabling transparent and reproducible evaluation of mix designs.

Despite its strengths, the proposed framework has several limitations. The Gaussian copula models dependence through a Gaussian latent correlation structure and may not capture nonlinear, asymmetric, or tail-dependent relationships among mix variables. The permutation-based sensitivity analysis provides a dependence-aware, approximate interpretation of variance contributions, offering a practical alternative for correlated input spaces, although it does not constitute an exact variance decomposition under classical independence assumptions. The framework does not perform true probabilistic uncertainty quantification, as predictive uncertainty is not explicitly modeled. In addition, the results depend on the specific dataset used for training and copula fitting, which may limit generalizability. While quantitative metrics are used to assess distributional and correlation fidelity, systematic comparison across multiple copula families has not been conducted.

Future work will address these limitations by exploring more flexible dependence models, such as vine copulas, and performing systematic model comparison. The framework will be extended to incorporate rigorous probabilistic uncertainty quantification and validated on independent and out-of-distribution datasets to assess robustness and transferability. Further extensions to other supplementary cementitious material systems and practical decision-support tools will also be pursued.
