Redox-Mediated Reversible C–C Bond Cleavage in Aqueous Electrochemical Systems
Authors: Jong-Hwa Shon, Ruozhu Feng, Ying Chen, Peter S. Rice, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Redox-active organic molecules provide a versatile platform for applications such as organocatalysis, electrocatalysis, and energy storage; however, parasitic reactions can yield competing bond-forming pathways that trap the molecule in electrochemically inaccessible states, passivating the redox electrochemical reversibility. Reductive radical coupling exemplifies this challenge. Although carbonyl reduction to an alcohol is a standard chemically reversible process, electrochemical reduction can induce radical coupling that leads to C–C bond formation, effectively diverting the reaction from the canonical two-electron, two-proton pathway and from electrochemical reversibility. Here, we demonstrate an electron-abstraction mechanism in which the mediator activates chemically reversible, yet electrochemically irreversible C–C bond scission with an estimated apparent rate constant of 3.54 × 10–6 M–1 s–1 under the tested conditions, which supports the restoration of “net” electrochemical reversibility and bypasses the large kinetic activation barrier in electrochemical systems. A flow battery configuration is used to illustrate how this mediating strategy enhances energy efficiency in electrochemical systems by bypassing high-kinetic barrier reaction pathways. Spectroscopic analyses directly capture both C–C bond formation and dissociation, while density functional theory calculations suggest an electron-abstraction mechanism for C–C bond cleavage. The demonstrated mediated, reversible C–C bond cleavage highlights a mechanistic proof-of-concept for overcoming high kinetic activation barriers in otherwise sluggish chemical transformations under electrochemical conditions.

Introduction

Redox-active organic molecules underpin a wide range of technologies, including organocatalysis, − electrocatalysis, , and electrochemical energy storage, − owing to their structural tunability and rich redox chemistry. These redox-active compounds are particularly attractive for flow batteries due to their natural abundance, structural tunability, and compatibility with scalable synthetic methods. However, under electrochemical conditions, these molecules are often susceptible to parasitic reactions that generate competing bond-forming pathways, trapping the active species in electrochemically inaccessible states and thereby degrading redox reversibility. Such parasitic reactions are frequently associated with catalyst degradation or loss of energy efficiency in electrochemical systems. In the context of energy storage, these observed parasitic reactions reduce storage capacity through irreversible losses, such as electrochemical aromatic hydrogeneration and electrochemical deoxygenation. Reductive radical coupling is a prototypical example of this challenge. Although carbonyl reduction to an alcohol is chemically reversible, electrochemical reduction can generate radical intermediates that undergo rapid C–C bond formation, , diverting the reaction from the canonical two-electron, two-proton pathway and effectively passivating electrochemical reversibility (a wide potential gap between electrochemical reduction and oxidation). Subsequent electrochemical reoxidation of the formed pinacol requires overcoming a substantial kinetic activation barrier. These limitations highlight a fundamental challenge in electrochemical systems: maintaining functional electrochemical reversibility in redox-active organic molecules.

To date, research on the design of redox-active organic molecules for flow batteries has largely emphasized thermodynamic control, with molecular engineering employed to selectively promote desired reaction pathways. In contrast, our work introduces a distinct kinetic approach to salvaging electrochemically irreversible C–C bonds. Using the well-established electrochemical carbonyl reduction as a model reaction in a flow battery configuration, we present a redox-mediated electron-abstracting mechanism that offers a potential approach for activating otherwise inaccessible bond-cleavage processes. Specifically, the pinacol dimerformed via reductive radical coupling during electrochemical carbonyl reductionbecomes electrochemically irreversible. Reoxidation of the species would require a substantial kinetic activation barrier under electrochemical conditions. A suitable redox mediator can activate such chemically reversible, yet electrochemically irreversible, C–C bond scission, thereby restoring “net” electrochemical reversibility and bypassing the large kinetic activation barrier in electrochemical systems. Combined computational and spectroscopic studies suggest that the reversible formation and cleavage of the pinacol C–C bond proceed via an electron-abstraction mechanisma process typically inaccessible under aqueous galvanostatic conditions due to high activation barriers. This kinetic approach offers insights into overcoming the electrochemical kinetic activation barrier in electrochemical systems with sluggish chemical transformations.

Method

Chemicals and Materials

2,7-Dihydroxy-9-fluorenone (DHFL, 98%), sodium ferrocyanide (Na4Fe­(CN)6·10H2O, 98%), potassium ferrocyanide (K4[Fe­(CN)6]·3H2O, 99%), potassium ferricyanide (K3Fe­(CN)6, 99%), and sodium hydroxide were obtained from chemical vendors (Aldrich, Fisher Scientific, Ambeed). 2,7-Disulfonate-4-carboxylate-9-fluorenone (27S4CFL) and 4-carboxylate-7-sulfonate-9-fluorenone (4C7SFL) were prepared using previously reported methods. Nafion membrane (Nafion NR212, Dupont), carbon papers (Freudenberg HC23), and ELAT carbon cloth electrodes were obtained from vendors.

Electrolyte Preparation

Both posolyte and negolyte were prepared with nitrogen-purged deionized distilled water in the nitrogen purge box. All chemicals were weighed at the benchtop in each conical tube, and the tubes were transferred to the purge box. Then, water was added to the conical tube to achieve the exact volume for molarity.

Flow Cell Assembly

The flow cell is composed of stacked layers of a collector, a bipolar plate with a frame, two pieces of carbon paper, and a piece of ELAT carbon cloth on each side, with an active area of 2 × 5 cm2 of active area. The Nafion membrane is positioned in the middle of each side. Supporting carbon electrodes were thermally treated at 400 °C for 8 h, and the membrane was immersed in 1 M NaOH solution for 12 h before assembly. The cell was assembled and tested in a nitrogen-purging box.

NMR Measurement

NMR spectra were either collected on a 500 MHz Oxford NMR spectrometer at 25 °C (the system consists of an Oxford AS500 magnet connected to an Agilent Technologies console) or collected on a 400 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker 400 MHz Avance III NMR with NanoBay console, equipped with a SampleCase autosampler using IconNMR automation. Probe: BBFO 5 mm SmartProbe). Chemical shifts were reported in ppm with the solvent resonance as the internal standard (DMSO, δ = 2.50; D2O, δ = 4.70; CDCl3, δ = 7.26).

EPR Measurement

All EPR measurements were conducted on a Bruker ELEXSYS E580 spectrometer at 298 K. For static EPR measurements of solutions, samples were prepared inside a glovebox immediately prior to analysis to minimize exposure to air and moisture. The electrolyte solution was drawn into a glass capillary (VitroTubes, ID 0.8 mm, OD 1 mm) using a Hamilton syringe connected via NanoTight fittings (Valco Instruments Co. Inc.). The capillary was sealed at both ends with Critoseal (Leica Microsystems) and placed inside a 4 mm EPR tube, which was also sealed inside the glovebox.

Typical spectral parameters included: microwave frequency = 9.857 GHz, modulation frequency = 100 kHz, sweep time = 10.49 s, time constant = 5.12 ms, field modulation amplitude = 0.05 G, sweep width = 26 G, and microwave power = 0.0025 mW. Absolute spin concentrations were quantified using calibration curves of the spin standard TEMPO (2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxyl) over a concentration range of 0.01–100 mM.

Electrochemical Measurements

The cyclic voltammetry tests were performed on the benchtop with a nitrogen flow atmosphere introduced into the vial. The system is composed of three electrodes: a glassy carbon electrode for the working electrode, a graphite rod for the counter electrode, and a Hg/HgO electrode for the reference electrode. The galvanostatic charging–discharging was performed using an Arbin instrument.

Due to the high oxygen sensitivity of the electrochemically generated radical anion intermediates in alkaline aqueous media, all cyclic voltammetry (CV), EPR spectroscopy, and flow-cell operational evaluations were strictly performed inside a nitrogen-filled purge box. All aqueous electrolytes were rigorously degassed via high-purity N2 purging for at least 30 min prior to each electrochemical measurement to ensure the complete exclusion of oxygen-induced degradation pathways.

Computational Methods

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using ORCA version 6.0.1. Geometry optimization and single-point energies used the hybrid PBE0 (25% HF) exchange-correlation functional. Dispersion was included using Grimme’s D4 scheme, , and the minimally augmented triple-ζ basis set with one set of polarization functions (ma-def2-TZVP) was used for all atoms. The aqueous solvent environment was accounted for using the universal solvation model (SMD) for water with the Dynamic Radii Adjustment for Continuum Solvation (DRACO) approach. Harmonic frequency analysis verified minima and provided zero-point vibrational energy and entropic corrections, allowing for the extraction of Gibbs free energies at 300 K. Stepwise and concerted proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps were calculated for the entire two-electron/two-proton square scheme; an example is shown in Figure S14 for 27S4CFL. pK a’s values were calculated using an isodesmic reaction scheme , with hydroquinone used as the reference acid/base. Redox potentials were calculated relative to the standard hydrogen electrode ( ESHEo=4.28V) . The free energy for the concerted PCET steps was calculated according to the standard thermodynamic cycle expression: GPCETo= (G(FL)+G(H+(aq))+G(e−))−(G(FL−H)) . pH effects were incorporated a posteriori using the expression μ­(H+) = μ0(H+) – (kTlog­(10))­pH. Calculated pathway energies were further refined using DLPNO–CCSD­(T)/cc-pVTZ single-point calculations and compared with the PBE0-D4/ma-def2-TZVP results. Both levels of theory identified the same minimum-energy pathway, Figure S21. The energy required to form and cleave the carbon–carbon bond in the dimer species was estimated by performing a relaxed scan of the C–C bond distance from 1.5 Å to 6.0 Å with a step size of <0.05 Å.

Results and Discussion

Reductive Radical Coupling Reaction of Dihydroxyfluorenone in Alkaline Media

Reductive radical coupling from fluorenones has been reported by multiple research groups. ,,, Among the fluorenone family, 2,7-dihydroxy-9-fluorenone (DHFL) was used to exemplify this electrochemically irreversible parasitic reaction, deviating from the two-electron, two-proton ketone-alcohol conversion (Scheme a). The NMR and EPR experiments were used to confirm the product speciation upon charging (reduction). In the 1H spectra of the charged DHFL (Figure S2), the broader signals at 6.8–7.1 ppm exhibit a smaller diffusion coefficient (2.8 × 10–10 m2/s) estimated from pulse field gradient (PFG) NMR than the sharp aromatic peaks (3.5 × 10–10 m2/s), suggesting the presence of higher-molecular-weight species, which are consistent with the formation of the DHFL pinacol dimer ((DHFL-OH)2). These broad features are mirrored in the 13C spectra, where the bridging carbon (C9) gives a sharp resonance at 87 ppm (Figure a). The peak at 74 ppm was assigned to the C9 carbon of fluorenol (DHFL-OH). In the corresponding 1H NMR, the proton resonance near ∼5 ppm, corresponding to the C9 position, was utilized to estimate the fluorenol concentration based on the integration methods detailed in the Supporting Information. Separately, EPR spectroscopy identifies a radical species distinct from the UV-excited 27S4CFL anion radical (Figure S5), which we tentatively assigned to the DHFL-O•– (phenoxide) radical anion. The broadened 13C resonances of DHFL relative to DHFL–OH in the same solution, and to pristine DHFL (Figure a) suggest chemical exchange between the radical and closed-shell species. However, this radical’s steady concentration over 15 h suggests a dynamic equilibrium with the pinacol species (Figure b). Quantitative 13C NMR, calibrated to 100 mM DHFL, yields an estimated ∼40 mM pinacol dimer and ∼15 mM DHFL-OH after 5 h of the fully charged state (within the bounds of experimental uncertainty). Combining the integration from NMR and EPR, three major species were tentatively identified at the 5-h detection time: pinacol dimer ((DHFL-OH)2, estimated at 80–90%), fluorenol (DHFL-OH, 10–20%), and less than 0.2% of the DHFL radical anion. Time-series 1H and 13C NMR measurements were performed to track the molecular speciation dynamics of the charged DHFL solution. Over time (Figures S3 and S4), the intensity of these dimer ((DHFL-OH)2) signals decreases, while two new sets of peaks emerge: one (relatively broad) matches DHFL (carbonyl resonance at 200.90 ppm, Figure a), and the other corresponds to DHFL-OH, with diagnostic 13C resonances at 74 ppm in 13C at C9 (Figure a) and 5.25 ppm in 1H NMR (Figure S3). This persistent radical signal likely originates from one-electron-reduced DHFL that did not undergo dimerization. Electrochemical behavior is investigated with cyclic voltammetry (CV) and a galvanic cell to illustrate how this reductive radical coupling impacts an electrochemical system. The CV reveals a reduction peak (peak 1c) that initiates at approximately −1.11 V vs Hg/HgO (Figure d blue trace), corresponding to a one-electron reduction of the ketone (Scheme a). The minor oxidation peak (peak 1a) at −1.02 V vs Hg/HgO is proposed to correspond to the observed radical phenoxide oxidation (Figure S1). The small peak intensity observed correlates with the low radical concentration measured by EPR. The broader oxidation peak observed at approximately +0.25 V vs Hg/HgO (peak 3a) is suggested to arise from the oxidation of phenolic hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring, rather than the oxidation of the pinacol dimer. In contrast, as shown in Figure d orange trace, and Figure S1, the oxidation peak 2a intensity increases markedly near +0.13 V vs Hg/HgO only when the charged DHFL electrolyte is used, compared with the uncharged DHFL electrolyte. Quantitative analysis in prior discussions indicates that the charged DHFL electrolyte contains approximately 80–90% (DHFL-OH)2. The selective enhancement of peak 2a under these conditions, therefore, supports its assignment to the oxidation of (DHFL-OH)2, with an oxidation potential of (DHFL-OH)2 (E pinacol, ox = +0.1 V vs Hg/HgO).

Correspondingly, the observed electrochemical behavior alters the voltage profile and the charge-storage capacity of a galvanic cell. Pairing with the ferro-/ferricyanide redox couple, the DHFL displays a voltage plateau near 1.3 V (Figure e) and rises gradually to a cutoff voltage of 1.7 V as the active DHFL is fully consumed during chargingbehavior consistent with its reduction potential observed in the CV shown in Figure d (dark blue trace). The measured capacity corresponds to a one-electron reduction of the ketone, supporting the hypothesis of the reductive radical coupling that leads to the formation of the pinacol structure. The pinacol dimer exhibits oxidation activity, despite being considered electrochemically irreversible but chemically reversible. The oxidation reaction requires relatively high energy activation, leading to a pronounced voltage drop during discharge and a system energy efficiency (EE) of ∼27% (Figure d). Less than 90% Coulombic efficiency (CE) is obtained due to the overlap of the phenol oxidation (peak 3a) at +0.3 V vs Hg/HgO and the pinacol oxidation (peak 2a) at +0.1 V vs Hg/HgO. This overlap introduces competition between the two electrochemical pathways at the electrode, which can promote irreversible degradation of DHFL into electrochemically inactive species. Extended discharge at this potential results in a measurable decline in capacity in subsequent cycles (Figures S1 and S6).

In summary, DHFL redox exemplifies a chemically reversible, yet electrochemically irreversible, process. Rapid reductive radical coupling promotes C–C bond formation to a pinacol structure and limits it to the 1-electron utilization of each ketone moiety. In addition to this 50% reduction in electron-accepting capability, the electrochemical irreversibility renders the material unsuitable for energy storage.

Redox-Mediated Reversible C–C Bond Activation

To overcome the large activation energy barrier associated with the direct electrochemical oxidation of organic pinacol species, we introduce a redox-mediated strategy that enables indirect, homogeneous chemical oxidation, as depicted in Scheme b. As a representative mediator, 27S4CFL is used to validate the proposed mediating strategy. As reported in our previous work, 27S4CFL does not dimerize due to its bulky structure and exhibits high stability of its radical anion, which is stabilized by electron-withdrawing substituents. In situ 1H, 13C NMR, and EPR measurements were conducted to monitor the evolution of molecular and radical species after introducing 27S4CFL into the charged DHFL solution (consisting of an estimated 80–90% pinacol). (Figures a–c, S7, and S8). Diagnostic 1H signals at 7.6–8.0, 6.9–7.2, 6.3–6.5, 5.5, and 5.2 ppm correspond to 27S4CFL-OH, the pinacol-27S4CFL cluster, DHFL/DHFL-OH (aromatic region), 27S4CFL-OH (benzylic proton), and DHFL-OH (benzylic proton), respectively (Figure a). The initial low apparent concentration of 27S4CFL observed by NMR can be attributed to transient clustering with the pinacol dimer, which may broaden and shift its 1H NMR signals, and, more importantly, to the rapid exchange with radicals being generated, further broadening the 27S4CFL signal to baseline. Comparison of 13C spectra of the mixture to those of 27S4CFL and DHFL standards (Figure S7) suggests that after 12 h, the solution contains only 27S4CFL-OH and DHFL, with no detectable (DHFL-OH)2, 27S4CFL, or DHFL-OH remaining (Figure b).

The decomposition of pinacol proceeds more than ten times faster in the presence of 27S4CFL, with a half-time of ∼3 h (pinacol decomposition 16.7 times faster, Figure b). Simultaneously, EPR detects a radical species identical to the UV-excited 27S4CFL anion radical (Figure S5), whose concentration rises rapidly to ∼5 mM within 30 min (Figure S8; Figure c). These observations are consistent with a bimolecular reaction pathway eq : 3 (DHFL‐OH)2+27S4CFL⇄27S4CFL‐OH+DHFL+27S4CFL‐O•− where DHFL-OH acts as a short-lived intermediate that decays over time. Although a full kinetic model remains to be developed, the key finding is that 27S4CFL accelerates the regeneration of DHFL from pinacol (C–C bond cleavage) by more than an order of magnitude and rapidly generates 27S4CFL-O• radicals. Such rapid electron transfer from pinacol to 27S4CFL supports the viability of the proposed mediating process.

The time-dependent radical concentration profile obtained from EPR measurements enabled the estimation of the initial radical formation rate. Since the radical anion of 27S4CFL forms through a bimolecular reaction between 27S4CFL and the pinacol dimer (DHFL–OH)2, the reaction is modeled under the assumption of second-order kinetics. From the slope of the initial concentration–time curve, the observed rate of radical formation is 4.43 × 10–6 M/min, and the estimated apparent rate constant is 3.54 × 10–6 M–1 s–1. The apparent rate constant, however, represents a composite value rather than the intrinsic radical generation rate, since the 27S4CFL radical anion can readily undergo disproportionation to yield nonradical species. Accordingly, direct comparison with rate constants reported for other redox mediators, such as TEMPO in alcohol oxidation, is not meaningful without separately quantifying the disproportionation rate of the 27S4CFL radical and considering the overall mass balance and experimental uncertainties.

The accumulation of the 27S4CFL radical anion persisted for approximately 5 h, decaying to baseline as shown in Figure S8. This behavior reflects the limited radical stability previously observed for 27S4CFL in our earlier study. The radical species undergoes disproportionation or gradual chemical oxidation in the presence of trace O2 to form 27S4CFL and 27S4CFL-OH, as indicated by the growth of 1H NMR resonances at 7.6–8.0 ppm, corresponding to 27S4CFL in the mixture of 27S4CFL and charged DHFL (Figure S8).

The observed redox-mediated reversible C–C bond activation is validated in a flow battery cell. DHFL electrolyte was first charged to its full one-electron theoretical capacity (reaching a state of approximately 80–90% pinacol in the electrolyte), after which 27S4CFL was added to the pinacol electrolyte, and then the mixed electrolyte discharge voltage profile was recorded (Figure d). The observed discharging voltage increased to 1.1 V with the presence of 27S4CFL in Figure d (orange curve)approximately 0.7 V higher than that of DHFL alone (Figure e)and matched the discharge plateau of 27S4CFL itself. This instant voltage enhancement is consistent with the above-demonstrated redox-mediated C–C bond activation to bypass the electrochemical oxidation kinetic barrier, in which the pinacol is chemically oxidized by 27S4CFL, forming the discharged DHFL and the charged 27S4CFL radical anion (eq ).

The proposed redox-mediated C–C bond activation can be further demonstrated by employing other suitable redox mediators (Figure d). 2,6-Dihydroxyanthraquinone (DHAQ), a well-known redox couple in alkaline aqueous media, delivers a discharge voltage approximately 100 mV higher than that of 27S4CFL under identical conditions, but with lower pinacol dimer utilization (58%). In contrast, fluorenone bearing a single sulfonate group, 4-sulfonated-7-carboxyfluorenone (4S7CFL), activates less than 10% of charged DHFL during discharge. This limited performance likely arises from the time-dependent nature of the redox-mediating step, as confirmed by time-controlled experiments presented in Figure S10. On the basis of this specific observation (Figure d), it is suggested that the ideal mediator needs to have sufficient thermodynamic driving force for electron abstraction from pinacol, while not having too positive a redox potential itself. DHAQ exhibits an onset reduction potential approximately 100 mV more negative than that of 247S4CFL. Consequently, this results in about 100 mV less thermodynamic driving force for electron abstraction, which may lead to lower utilization during the mediating process under the same current density measurement, despite observing a discharge approximately 100 mV higher.

Figure illustrates the effectiveness of the proposed mediating strategy by comparing flow cell performance before and after the introduction of the 27S4CFL redox mediator. Without the mediator, the energy efficiency was below 40%, whereas the addition of 27S4CFL increased the energy and voltage efficiencies to approximately 80% (Figure a), with the discharge voltage reaching 1.1 V (Figure b). The instant voltage and energy efficiency enhancement supports the feasibility of bypassing the electrochemical oxidation kinetic barrier, achieving a “net” electrochemical reversible conversion under the tested conditions. Over 200 cycles, no significant capacity fading was observed for the sum capacity from DHFL and 27S4CFL, suggesting a relatively stable regeneration of both redox species (Figure c). Redox-Mediated Electron Abstraction Mechanism

To elucidate the reaction mechanism underlying the observed redox-mediating process, we performed DFT free energy calculations for all electron transfer (ET), proton transfer (PT), and concerted proton–electron transfer (PCET) steps, as shown in the thermodynamic square scheme in Figures S14–S20. We first computed the pK a values of the two phenol-like hydroxyl groups in DHFL. Under experimental alkaline conditions (pH = 14), both hydroxyl groups are predicted to be fully deprotonated, with ΔG deprotonation values between −0.29 eV and −0.40 eV. These protonation states strongly affect the redox potential and PCET sequence. As shown in Figure a, the electrochemical reduction of a DHFL monomer to the corresponding DHFL-OH is predicted to proceed through an ET–PCET–PT sequence, with the first two steps being endergonic and the final PT exoergic. The first ET step or the concerted PCET step is proposed as potentially rate-limiting, each requiring ∼1.30 eV within the accuracy of the computed energy profile. The initial protonation preferentially occurs at the benzylic carbon (pK a = 26.4) compared with the ketone oxygen (pK a = 17.3). The second PT is downhill by 0.210 eV, completing the DHFL-OH alcohol formation without forming a dimer. As a comparison, two DHFL monomers were arranged in a π–π stacked configuration, and the electrochemical reduction process was simulated (Figure b). The overall sequence remains ET–PCET–PT, but the first ET step becomes ∼0.1 eV more positive in the dimer complex. This ET, followed by successive PCET events, is suggested to facilitate C–C bond formation to yield the pinacol dimer, which has a C–C bond association free energy ΔG assoc. = −0.160 eV. The subsequent PT (ΔG PT = −0.196 eV) completes the one-electron-per-carbonyl-moiety redox process, corresponding to the pinacol dimer, which has a more negative ΔG assoc. = −0.848 eV. This analysis is consistent with the hypothesis that pinacol dimer formation is favored thermodynamically, with the formation of the pinacol structure effectively passivating the system and preventing the regular 2-electron redox process with the C–C bond formed.

To understand how substitution affects C–C bond cleavage and formation, energy profiles as a function of the C–C bond length are calculated for DHFL and 27S4CFL dimers (Figure c). The C–C bond cleavage barrier is slightly lower for 27S4CFL (1.15 eV) than for DHFL (1.28 eV), suggesting weaker dimer stability. However, C–C bond formation in 27S4CFL requires 2.06 eVmuch higher than the 0.44 eV in DHFLdue to electrostatic repulsion introduced by the charged sulfonate substituents. Hence, 27S4CFL dimers are predicted to be both easier to break and significantly harder to form, providing a plausible explanation for the experimentally observed suppression of dimerization in 27S4CFL and successful two-electron utilization per ketone moiety. In contrast, DHFL forms a dimer more readily (0.44 eV), suppressing the second consecutive electron reduction and resulting in one-electron reduction per carbonyl moiety with the C–C bond formed.

Having established the intrinsic behavior baselines for DHFL and 27S4CFL, we next analyzed the electron-abstraction process between 27S4CFL and the DHFL pinacol (Figure c–e, eq . The pinacol dimer arises via electrochemical reductive radical–radical coupling, whereas the C–C bond cleavage pathway occurs through chemical oxidation in aqueous media. Although the pinacol rearrangement is common under acid-catalyzed or thermal conditions, , in our alkaline system, we postulate that 27S4CFL promotes C–C bond cleavage through an electron-abstraction mechanism rather than a proton-coupled oxidation due to the strong acidity (pK a = 3.22, Figure S16) of the 27S4CFL radical alcohol (eq ), in clear contrast to the TEMPO-mediated electrochemical alcohol oxidation previously reported. In this mechanism, a single-electron transfer from the pinacol to 27S4CFL (pure electron abstractor) is accompanied by a proton transfer from the pinacol-OH to the solution hydroxide. Three mechanistic pathways were evaluated (Figure d). The pinacol dimer contains two distinct hydroxyl sitesphenolic and benzylicwith different acidities. At high pH, the phenolic group is fully deprotonated, as suggested by DFT (Figure S17), making oxidation start from the phenoxide state. Given the pK a of pinacol alcohol is calculated at 17.26 and the first PT requires 0.2 eV (Figure d), the oxidation starting with the PT step is suggested to be less energetically favorable. DFT analysis indicates that for pH > 10.2, the stepwise ET-PT (ΔG = −0.297 eV) is slightly less favorable than the concerted PCET pathway (ΔG = −0.330 eV), suggesting that a concerted process may be energetically competitive or slightly favored under these conditions, though both pathways could remain accessible given the small energy difference (Figure d). C–C bond cleavage occurs after the second PT step, with a d­(C–C) extending to ∼3.7 Å; the final ET event then regenerates 2 equiv of DHFL. Minor pH variations alter the ET/PT sequence, but the underlying core mechanism remains supported by the model (Figures S17–S18).

Building on the above discussion, we summarize the overall redox mechanism during the electrochemical process in a flow cell in Figure . During charging at approximately 1.1–1.4 V, pairing with ferricyanide, both DHFL and 27S4CFL are electrochemically reduced at the electrode surface to form their respective reduced species. Consistent with the measured utilization of 27S4CFL (ca. 90% based on its theoretical capacity) when evaluated solely, a small fraction of uncharged 27S4CFL remains at the end of the charging step voltage cutoff. Two key homogeneous electron-transfer (ET) processes occur concurrently in solution. A comproportionation reaction between uncharged 27S4CFL and its reduced form (27S4CFL-OH) generates a 27S4CFL radical anion, which is subsequently oxidized at the electrode with facile kinetics. The other intermolecular ET between 27S4CFL and the (DHFL-OH)2 pinacol dimer results in C–C bond cleavage to form DHFL, along with the generation of a 27S4CFL radical anion. These radicals are reoxidized at the electrode surface during the battery discharge process, closing the redox-mediation loop and recovering 27S4CFL for the next mediating cycle.

Proposed mechanism of coupled electrochemical and chemical mediating processes in the electrochemical system.

Conclusion

This work demonstrates that kinetically inaccessible C–C bonds formed during electrochemical reactions can be reversibly cleaved in water through redox-mediated electron abstraction. Although pinacol formation during carbonyl reduction is chemically reversible, it is typically regarded as electrochemically irreversible because direct oxidation requires overcoming a large kinetic barrier. By introducing a suitable redox mediator, this kinetic barrier can be bypassed through a homogeneous electron-abstraction pathway that enables net electrochemical reversibility. Spectroscopic measurements and DFT simulations suggest that this process proceeds through a concerted PCET-type C–C bond cleavage mechanism. When implemented in an electrochemical energy storage system, this new chemistry manifests as a 2-fold improvement in energy efficiency under the investigated conditions by avoiding a high-overpotential anodic oxidation pathway. More broadly, this redox-mediated strategy presents a promising approach to accessing chemically reversible but electrochemically inaccessible states, potentially expanding the scope of bond activation reactions that can be realized under electrochemical conditions.
