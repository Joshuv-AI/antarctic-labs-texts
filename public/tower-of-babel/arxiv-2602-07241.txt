Extremal Graphs for the Lights Out Problem Thanks: Princeton University, Princeton, NJ, USA Thanks: E-mail addresses: {jc3530, sergio.cris, adivoux, varunsiva}@princeton.edu
Julien Codsi, Sergio Cristancho, Alexander Divoux, Varun Sivashankar

Abstract
. Lights Out is a game played on a graph G G where every vertex has a light bulb that is either on or off, and pressing a vertex v v toggles the state of every vertex in the closed neighborhood of v v . The goal is to find a subset of vertices S S such that pressing every vertex in S S results in all light bulbs being turned off. We study the extremal graphs for which pressing every vertex is the unique solution to the lights out problem given an initial configuration of all lights on. We show that a graph is extremal if and only if it is even and has an odd number of matchings. Furthermore, there is a bijection between the set of labeled n n -vertex extremal graphs and the set of symmetric invertible matrices of size n − 2 n-2 over 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} . We prove that any even graph with no cycle of length 0 ( mod 3 ) 0\pmod{3} must be extremal. We also demonstrate operations that build larger extremal graphs from smaller ones. Along the way, we prove using the polynomial method that in any even graph, the number of matchings of a fixed size covering an odd subset of vertices is even.



1.
Introduction

Lights Out is a game played on a graph
G
. Each vertex represents a light that is either on or off. Pressing a vertex switches the state of that vertex and all of its neighbors: lights that were on are turned off, and lights that were off are turned on. The objective of the game is to turn all lights off. Pressing a vertex twice has no effect and the vertices can be pressed in any order to achieve the same state, so a sequence of presses is determined entirely by the set of vertices pressed exactly once. Loops and parallel edges do not affect the Lights Out game, so we assume that all graphs are simple unless otherwise stated.

The Lights Out game was first studied by Pelletier
[
7
]
as Merlin’s Magic Square, from the Merlin game console distributed by Parker Brothers in 1978, which was played on a grid graph with 9 vertices. Sutner
[
9
]

[
10
]
observed that the Lights Out game, or as he called it, the
\sigma
-game, is closely related to the study of additive cellular automata on graphs. The first instance of the name Lights Out in the literature is due to Anderson and Feil
[
1
]
after the electronic game by Tiger Electronics from 1995.

For an arbitrary initial configuration of lights, one may ask whether there is a set of vertices
S
whose presses turn every light off. In terms of linear algebra, given the adjacency matrix
A_{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n\times n}
and the indicator vector of the initial configuration
p\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, this is equivalent to determining whether the system
(A_{G}+I_{n})x=p
admits a solution over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Sutner
[
9
, Thm. 3.1]
proves that an initial configuration
p
is solvable if and only if
p
belongs to the orthogonal complement of the kernel of the matrix
A_{G}+I_{n}
. This question has been also been studied for specific families of graphs. For example, Anderson and Feil
[
1
]
determined the solvable initial configurations for certain grid graphs. For arbitrary graphs, Berman, Borer and Hungerbühler
[
2
]
gave a linear algebra criterion to determine when a particular state can be achieved from another. It is worth noting that finding a minimal size solution to an arbitrary initial configuration is an NP-complete problem
[
8
, Thm. 3.2]
.

In this paper, we focus on the natural setting where all the lights are initially on; the rest of the paper will always assume this initial configuration. Sutner
[
9
, Thm. 3.2]
proves that this initial configuration always has a solution. This result is equivalent to a theorem of Gallai on partitioning the vertices of a graph into two sets whose induced subgraphs are both even, see for instance
[
3
]
. In fact, Gallai’s theorem is equivalent to the assertion that the diagonal of any symmetric matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
belongs to its image. Additionally, the solvability of the all lights on configuration can observed in
[
2
, Thm. 5]
, which states that any configuration can be inverted. Recently, Mirzaei
[
6
]
also gives a proof of this fact using only combinatorial arguments.

We study the class of extremal graphs for the Lights Out game. Given a graph
G
, pressing a subset of vertices
S\subseteq V(G)
turns all the lights off precisely when
|N[v]\cap S|
is odd for every
v\in V(G)
. Call such a subset
S
a
hitting set
. For brevity, we refer to the parity of
|N(v)\cap S|
as the parity of
v
when
S
is clear from context. A hitting set
S
of
G
is
proper
if
S
is a proper subset of
V(G)
. We say that
G
is
extremal
if it has no proper hitting set. In other words, a graph
G
is extremal if hitting every vertex of
G
is the unique solution to the Lights Out game on
G
. For a graph
G
, let
m(G)
denote the total number of matchings in
G
(including the empty matching).

The paper is organized as follows.

•

In

•

In

•

2.
Characterization by parity of matchings

The goal of this section is to provide an equivalent definition of extremal graphs that is entirely independent of the Lights Out game, thereby further motivating the study of this family.

Let
G
be a graph with
n
vertices, let
A_{G}
be its adjacency matrix, and set
M_{G}=A_{G}+I_{n}
. A first observation is that
G
is extremal if and only if
M_{G}\bf{1}_{n}=\bf{1}_{n}
and
M_{G}
is invertible over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. With this perspective, we prove the following characterization.

Theorem 2.1
.

A graph
G
is extremal if and only if
G
is even and has an odd number of matchings.

Proof.

Consider the matrix
M_{G}=A_{G}+I_{n}
with coordinates
x_{v,w}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}
labeled by
v,w\in V(G)
. We aim to compute

\det(M_{G})=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\prod_{v\in V(G)}x_{v,\sigma(v)}.

Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
, and let
\sigma=c_{1}\dots c_{k}
be its unique decomposition into disjoint cyclic permutations of length
\geq 2
. If
c=(v_{1}\dots v_{\ell})
is a cyclic permutation, let
\overline{c}=(v_{\ell}\dots v_{1})
be the reversed cyclic permutation, and define
\overline{\sigma}=\overline{c_{1}}\dots\overline{c_{k}}
. Note that
\overline{c}=c
if and only if
c
has length
2
, hence
\overline{\sigma}=\sigma
if and only if
\sigma
is composed of cycles of length
\leq 2
.

Now, the term
\prod_{v\in V(G)}x_{v,\sigma(v)}
is equal to 1 if and only if each
c_{i}
forms a cycle in
G
(here single edges in
G
are considered cycles of length 2). Furthermore, the term corresponding to
\sigma
is equal to 1 if and only if the term corresponding to
\overline{\sigma}
is equal to 1. Therefore

\det(M_{G})=\sum\limits_{\overline{\sigma}=\sigma}\prod_{v\in V(G)}x_{v,\sigma(v)}+\sum\limits_{\overline{\sigma}\neq\sigma}\prod_{v\in V(G)}x_{v,\sigma(v)}=\sum\limits_{\overline{\sigma}=\sigma}\prod_{v\in V(G)}x_{v,\sigma(v)},

since the summation over all
\sigma\neq\overline{\sigma}
must be zero by our previous observation.

Finally, if
\sigma
is composed of cycles of length
\leq 2
and its corresponding term is equal to 1, then its cycles correspond to non-adjacent edges in
G
, i.e.
\sigma
defines a unique matching
\mu
in
G
. Conversely, every matching
\mu
in
G
uniquely determines such a permutation
\sigma
. Consequently,

\det(M_{G})=m(G)\pmod{2}.

As
M_{G}\bf{1}_{n}=\bf{1}_{n}
implies the graph is even, the theorem statement follows.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The proof of the previous theorem shows that a graph has a unique solution to the Lights Out game if and only if it has an odd number of matchings. The even condition guarantees that hitting all the vertices is a solution.

3.
Counting extremal graphs

A natural starting point in the study of extremal graphs is to determine how many such graphs exist on
n
vertices. We answer this question exactly.
Precisely, in this section, we count the number of labeled extremal graphs on the vertex set
[n]
. To do so, we make use of the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
5
]

Let
\bm{S}^{n}
denote the set of
n\times n
symmetric invertible matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Then the cardinality
|\bm{S}^{n}|
is given by

|\bm{S}^{n}|=2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor(n+1)/2\right\rfloor}\left(1-2^{\,1-2i}\right).

Recall that
G
is extremal if and only if
A_{G}=M_{G}+I_{n}
where
M_{G}
is a symmetric invertible matrix with diagonal
\diag(M)=\mathbf{1}_{n}
and
M_{G}\bf{1}_{n}=\bf{1}_{n}
. Therefore, we need to determine the cardinality of the following set:

\bm{E}^{n}=\{M\in\bm{S}^{n}\colon M\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n},\diag(M)=\mathbf{1}_{n}\}.

Theorem 3.2
.

There is a bijection from the set of labeled extremal graphs on
n
vertices to the set of
(n-2)\times(n-2)
symmetric matrices
\bm{S}^{n-2}
. In particular,

|\bm{E}^{n}|=2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{\left\lfloor(n-1)/2\right\rfloor}\left(1-2^{\,1-2i}\right).

Remark 3.3
.

Since the number of even labeled graphs on
n
vertices is
2^{\binom{n-1}{2}}
, the proportion of even graphs that are extremal tends to
\sim 0.419
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{B}^{n-1}=\{B\in\bm{S}^{n-1}\colon B\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=\diag(B)\}
. We construct a bijection
\Phi\colon\bm{E}^{n}\to\bm{S}^{n-2}
by constructing two bijections
\Phi_{1}:\bm{E}^{n}\to\bm{B}^{n-1}
and
\Phi_{2}:\bm{B}^{n-1}\to\bm{S}^{n-2}
.

Let us define
\Phi_{1}
. Let
M\in\bm{E}^{n}
and write

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&r^{T}\\
\hline\cr r&N\end{array}\right),

where
r\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-1}
and
N\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
. The condition
M\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
implies that
r^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=0
and
r+N\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=\mathbf{1}_{n-1}
.
A standard Gaussian reduction leads to

LAL^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&0\\
\hline\cr 0&N+rr^{T}\end{array}\right)\text{, where }L=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&0\\
\hline\cr r&I_{n-1}\end{array}\right).

The matrix
B=N+rr^{T}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\det(B)=1
,

(2)

\diag(B)=\diag(N)+r=\mathbf{1}_{n-1}+r
,

(3)

B\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=(N+rr^{T})\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=(\mathbf{1}_{n-1}+r)+0=\mathbf{1}_{n-1}+r
,

and thus
B\in\mathcal{B}^{n-1}
. The map
\Phi_{1}:M\mapsto B
is a bijection, as
r
is uniquely determined as
r=\diag(B)+\mathbf{1}_{n-1}
.

Now let us define
\Phi_{2}
. Let
B\in\mathcal{B}^{n-1}
and write

B=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\alpha&y^{T}\\
\hline\cr y&X\end{array}\right),

where
X\in\bm{S}^{n-2}
. The condition
B\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=\diag(B)
implies that
y+X\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=\diag(X)
. We apply the transformation

P=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}1&\mathbf{1}_{n-2}^{T}\\
\hline\cr 0&I\end{array}\right)

to
B
to get

B^{\prime}=PBP^{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\gamma&(y+X\mathbf{1}_{n-2})^{T}\\
\hline\cr y+X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}&X\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\gamma&\diag(X)^{T}\\
\hline\cr\diag(X)&X\end{array}\right)

for some scalar
\gamma
. We claim
X
is invertible. Suppose for contradiction that
\det(X)=0
. Then there exists a non-zero
u
such that
Xu=0
. For any
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n-2}
we have
v^{T}Xv=\diag(X)^{T}v
since
X
is symmetric. Consequently,
\diag(X)^{T}u=u^{T}Xu=0
. The vector
(0,u^{T})^{T}
then lies in the kernel of
B^{\prime}
, contradicting
\det(B^{\prime})=\det(B)=1
. We define
\Phi_{2}:\bm{B}^{n-1}\to\bm{S}^{n-2}
by
B\mapsto X
.

Let us prove
\Phi_{2}
is a bijection by exhibiting an inverse. Given
X\in\bm{S}^{n-2}
, we can uniquely reconstruct
B
. Let
\gamma=1+\diag(X)^{T}X^{-1}\diag(X)
and let

B^{\prime}\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\gamma&\diag(X)^{T}\\
\hline\cr\diag(X)&X\end{array}\right),

where our choice of
\gamma
makes
B^{\prime}
invertible. Now consider

B\coloneqq P^{-1}B^{\prime}P^{-T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\alpha&y^{T}\\
\hline\cr y&X\end{array}\right),

where
\alpha=\gamma+\mathbf{1}_{n-2}^{T}X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}
and
y=\diag(X)+X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}
. By construction,
B
is symmetric and our choice of
\gamma
ensured that
B
is invertible. It remains to show that the defined
B
satisfies the condition
B\mathbf{1}_{n-1}=\diag(B)
. Observe that
y+X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}=\diag(X)+X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}+X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}=\diag(X)
, so
(B\mathbf{1}_{n-1})_{i}=\diag(B)_{i}
for
i\geq 2
. Lastly, we need to show that
\alpha+y^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n-2}=\alpha
, so it suffices to show that
y^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n-2}=\diag(X)^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n-2}+\mathbf{1}_{n-2}^{T}X\mathbf{1}_{n-2}=0
, but this holds since
X
is symmetric. Therefore,
B\in\bm{B}^{n-1}
. This defines an inverse to
\Phi_{2}
, hence it is a bijection.
∎

In the remainder of this paper, we establish several structural properties of extremal graphs.

4.
(0\bmod 3)
-cycle free graphs

In this section, we establish a sufficient condition for an even graph to be extremal by restricting the length of its cycles. We begin by classifying when a cycle, the simplest even graph, is extremal.

Theorem 4.1
.

A cycle is extremal if and only if it does not have length divisible by
3
.

Proof.

Let us denote the vertices of
C_{k}
by
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
such that
v_{i}v_{j}\in E(C_{k})
if
i-j=\pm 1\pmod{k}
.
Note that for any hitting set
S
,

(1)

S
needs to be dominating,

(2)

if
v_{i},v_{i+2}\in S
then
v_{i+1}\in S
(where the indices are modulo
k
), and

(3)

if
v_{i},v_{i+1}\in S
then
v_{i+2}\in S
(where the indices are modulo
k
).

If
|S|\neq k
, then (3) implies that
S
is a stable set. Moreover, (1) and (2) imply that if
v_{j}\notin S
then
|N(v_{j})\cap S|=1
. By combining these remarks and centering balls of radius one at the vertices in
S
, there must exist a packing of balls that is a cover of
C_{k}
. Since each balls cover exactly
3
vertices, we must have that
k
is divisible by
3
. If
k
is divisible by
3
then
S=\left\{v_{2},v_{5},\dots,v_{k-1}\right\}
is a hitting set.
∎

Theorem

4.1
seems to indicate that cycles with length divisible by
3
are at the crux of what can make an even graph non-extremal. The following theorem captures this idea.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
G
is an even graph with no cycle of length divisible by
3
, then
G
is extremal.

To prove Theorem
4.2
, we will make use of the following result.

Lemma 4.3
.

[
4
, Corollary 2]

Let
G
be an even graph whose edges are colored red and blue so that every vertex is incident with an odd number of edges of each color. Then
G
has an alternating cycle.

Proof of
Theorem

4.2
.

Assume for contradiction that
G
is not extremal. Then there exists a proper hitting set
S\subsetneq V(G)
of
G
. Let
T=V(G)\setminus S
be the nonempty set of vertices which are not hit. As
G
is an even graph and
S
is a hitting set for
G
, for any
s\in S
we have
|N(s)\cap S|
is even, and hence
|N(s)\cap T|=\deg(s)-|N(s)\cap S|
is also even. Conversely, for any
t\in T
we have
|N(t)\cap S|
is odd, and hence
|N(t)\cap T|=\deg(t)-|N(t)\cap S|
is also odd.

Let
H^{\prime}
be the multigraph obtained from
G
by adding a clique of new edges on
N(s)\cap T
for every
s\in S
, and let
H=H^{\prime}\setminus S
. Color an edge
e\in E(H)
red if
e\in E(H^{\prime})\setminus E(G)
and blue if
e\in E(H^{\prime})\cap E(G)
. Consider some arbitrary
v\in V(H)
. Since
v\in V(H)=T
, we have that
|N_{G}(v)\cap S|
is odd and
|N_{G}(s)\cap T|
is even for every
s\in N_{G}(v)\cap S
. Therefore,
v
is incident with an odd number

\sum_{s\in N_{G}(v)\cap S}(|N_{G}(s)\cap T|-1)

of red edges in
H
. Furthermore,
v
is incident with an odd number
|N_{G}(v)\cap T|
of blue edges in
H
. Therefore,
H
is an even graph whose edges are colored red and blue so that every vertex is incident with an odd number of edges of each color, so by
Lemma

4.3

H
has an alternating red/blue cycle. Among all such cycles, choose
C
to be the shortest.

Let
C=v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k},v_{1}
for some even
k\geq 2
, and assume without loss of generality that
v_{1}v_{2}
is a red edge. By the definition of
H
, for any red edge
v_{i}v_{i+1}\in E(C)
,
i\in[k]
, there exists some
s\in S
such that
v_{i},v_{i+1}\in N_{G}(s)\cap T
. On the other hand, for any
s\in S
, at most one red edge of
C
has both endpoints in
N_{G}(s)\cap T
. To see this, assume for contradiction that there exists some
s\in S
and distinct
i,j\in[k]
such that
v_{i}v_{i+1}
and
v_{j}v_{j+1}
are both red edges in
C
and
v_{i},v_{i+1},v_{j},v_{j+1}\in N_{G}(s)\cap T
. Assume without loss of generality that
i<j
. Since
C
is an alternating cycle,

, and since

is a clique,

. But now the cycle
C^{\prime}=v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{i},v_{j+1},\dots,v_{k},v_{1}
is a shorter alternating cycle in

, contradicting the minimality of

. Therefore, for any red edge
v_{i}v_{i+1}\in E(C)
,
i\in[k]
, there exists a unique
s_{i,i+1}\in S
such that
v_{i},v_{i+1}\in N_{G}(s_{i,i+1})\cap T
. Furthermore, by the definition of
H
, any blue edge in
C
corresponds to a unique edge of
G[T]
.

Lifting
C
back to

, we have that

C^{\prime\prime}=v_{1},s_{1,2},v_{2},v_{3},s_{3,4},v_{4},\dots,v_{k-1},s_{k-1,k},v_{k}v_{1}

is a cycle of length

in

, contradicting that

has no cycle of length divisible by

.

5.
Operations

To better understand graphs whose extremality does not follow from
Theorem

4.2
, we explore ways to build extremal graphs from smaller ones. In this section, we show that the family of extremal graphs is closed under several simple operations.

Clearly, the disjoint union of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is extremal if and only if both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are extremal. The following lemma shows that the
1
-join of two extremal graphs remains extremal.

Lemma 5.1

(
1
-join)
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be extremal graphs, and let
x\in V(G_{1})
and
y\in V(G_{2})
. Then the graph
G
obtained by identifying
x
and
y
is extremal.

Proof.

Let
v\in V(G)
be the vertex corresponding to the identification of
x
and
y
. Assume for sake of contradiction that
G
is not extremal, and let
S\subsetneq V(G)
be a proper hitting set of
G
. Let
a=|N(v)\cap S\cap V(G_{1})|
and
b=|N(v)\cap S\cap V(G_{2})|
.

Case 1:
v\notin S
. Since
S
is a hitting set of
G
, we have that
a+b
is odd. Assume without loss of generality that
a
is odd and
b
is even. Then
S\setminus(V(G_{2})\setminus\{v\})
is a proper hitting set of
G_{1}
, contradicting that
G_{1}
is extremal.

Case 2:
v\in S
. Since
S
is a hitting set of
G
, we have that
a+b
is even. First assume both
a
and
b
are even. Since
S
is a proper hitting set of
G
, there exists some
u\in V(G)\setminus S
. Assume without loss of generality that
u\in V(G_{1})
. Then
S\setminus(V(G_{2})\setminus\{v\})
is a proper hitting set of
G_{1}
, contradicting that
G_{1}
is extremal. Now assume both
a
and
b
are odd. Let
H=G[S\cap V(G_{1})]
. Then
\deg_{H}(v)=a
is odd and
\deg_{H}(w)
is even for any

. Therefore,

is a graph with exactly one odd-degree vertex, a contradiction.
∎

By the lemma above, taking the
1
-sum of two extremal graphs produces an extremal graph with a cut-vertex. Conversely, it is also true that any extremal graph with a cut-vertex
v
is the
1
-join of two extremal graphs at

.

Lemma 5.2

(Cut-vertex)
.

Let
G
be an extremal graph with a cut-vertex
v
. Let
A,B\subseteq V(G)
such that
A\cup B=V(G)
,
A\cap B=\{v\}
, and there are no edges from
A\setminus B
to
B\setminus A
. Then
G[A]
and
G[B]
are extremal.

We deduce Lemma
5.2
from a corollary of the following result on the parity of matchings in even graphs. For a matching
M
, let
V(M)
be the set of endpoints of the edges of
M
. The size of a matching is the number of edges in it. For a graph
G
and
A\subseteq V(G)
, we say that a matching
M

covers

A
if
A\subseteq V(M)
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
G
be an even graph and
S\subseteq V(G)
with
|S|
odd. Then for any
k
, the number of size-
k
matchings covering
S
is even.

Proof.

Let
G=(V,E)
, and work in the ring

, it must be that
[t^{k}x^{V}]\partial(PQ)=0

\partial(PQ)

\partial(PQ)=(\partial P)Q+P(\partial Q)=P(\partial Q).

Since

\partial Q=Q\sum_{w\in T}(1+x_{w})=Q(|T|+\sum_{w\in T}x_{w})

\partial(PQ)=PQ(|T|+\sum_{w\in T}x_{w})

U\not\ni w\end{subarray}}c_{U}

is the number of size-

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in T}[t^{k}x^{V\setminus\{w\}}](PQ)

It follows that

Corollary 5.4
.

For any even graph
G
and any

, the number of matchings covering

is even.

We now use Corollary

to deduce Lemma

. For a graph
G
and a vertex

, let

denote the number of matchings in

covering
v
.

Proof of Lemma

.

Let
G_{1}=G[A]
and
G_{2}=G[B]
. Suppose for contradiction that, without loss of generality,
G_{1}
is not extremal. Since
G
is an even graph, it must be that
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are even graphs. Indeed, for
i\in[2]
and
u\in V(G_{i})\setminus\{v\}
, we have
\deg_{G_{i}}(u)=\deg_{G}(u)
is even. This further implies that
\deg_{G_{i}}(v)
is even, as otherwise
G_{i}
would have exactly one vertex of odd degree, a contradiction.

As
G
is extremal, by Theorem

we have
m(G)
is odd. Any matching in
G
either covers
v
via an edge in
G_{1}
, covers
v
via an edge in
G_{2}
, or does not cover
v
. Since there are no edges from
A\setminus B
to
B\setminus A
,

\displaystyle m(G)

\displaystyle=m(G_{1}\setminus v)\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus v)+m(G_{1},v)\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus v)+m(G_{1}\setminus v)\cdot m(G_{2},v)

\displaystyle=m(G_{2}\setminus v)[m(G_{1}\setminus v)+m(G_{1},v)]+m(G_{1}\setminus v)\cdot m(G_{2},v)

\displaystyle=m(G_{2}\setminus v)\cdot m(G_{1})+m(G_{1}\setminus v)\cdot m(G_{2},v).

Since
G_{1}
is not extremal,
m(G_{1})
is even, so
m(G)
has the same parity as
m(G_{1}\setminus v)\cdot m(G_{2},v)
. However, by Corollary

we have
m(G_{2},v)
is even, so
m(G)
must also be even, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 5.5

(Triple subdivision)
.

A graph

is extremal if and only if for any edge

Proof.

In the following, all sums will be taken modulo

.

x\hbox{-}a\hbox{-}b\hbox{-}c\hbox{-}y

G

For
I\subseteq\{xa,ab,bc,cy\}
, we define
K_{I}
as the number of matching

such that

. We have that

\displaystyle m(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle=K_{\varnothing}+K_{xa}+K_{xa,bc}+K_{cy}+K_{ab,cy}+K_{ab}+K_{bc}+K_{xa,cy}

\displaystyle=K_{\varnothing}+K_{xa}+K_{xa}+K_{cy}+K_{cy}+K_{ab}+K_{ab}+K_{xa,cy}

\displaystyle m(G^{\prime})

\displaystyle=K_{\varnothing}+K_{xa,cy}

\displaystyle=m(G-e)+m(G,e)

Lemma 5.6

(Even completion)
.

Let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be graphs. Let
X_{1}\subseteq V(G_{1})
and
X_{2}\subseteq V(G_{2})
be sets of even size. Let
G
be the completion of
G_{1},G_{2}
along
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
.
Then
G
is extremal if and only if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are extremal.

Proof.

Work modulo
2
throughout. Clearly
G
is even if and only if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are even. Let
H
be the bipartite subgraph of
G
with
V(H)=X_{1}\cup X_{2}
and
E(H)=E(X_{1},X_{2})
(the edges added in the completion operation).
Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of matchings of
H
. Since there are no edges from
V(G_{1})
to
V(G_{2})
except those in
E(H)
,

m(G)=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}m(G_{1}\setminus V(M))\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus V(M)).

We will group the terms of this summation into three: the empty matching, matchings of size exactly one, and matchings of size at least two. The latter two will be shown to contribute zero to the sum. We have:

m(G)=m(G_{1})\cdot m(G_{2})+\sum_{u\in X_{1},v\in X_{2}}m(G_{1}\setminus u)\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus v)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}\\
|M|\geq 2\end{subarray}}m(G_{1}\setminus V(M))\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus V(M)).

By
Corollary

5.4
, for any
u\in X_{1}
, we have
m(G_{1}\setminus u)=m(G_{1})-m(G_{1},u)=m(G_{1})
. Similarly, for any
v\in X_{2}
, we have
m(G_{2}\setminus v)=m(G_{2})
. Therefore,

m(G)=m(G_{1})\cdot m(G_{2})+|E(X_{1},X_{2})|\cdot m(G_{1})\cdot m(G_{2})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}\\
|M|\geq 2\end{subarray}}m(G_{1}\setminus V(M))\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus V(M)).

Since
|X_{1}|
and
|X_{2}|
are even,
|E(X_{1},X_{2})|=0
, so this simplifies to

m(G)=m(G_{1})\cdot m(G_{2})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}\\
|M|\geq 2\end{subarray}}m(G_{1}\setminus V(M))\cdot m(G_{2}\setminus V(M)).

Consider some arbitrary

be sets of size

. Then the number of matchings

with

is exactly

, which is
0

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}\\

m(G_{2})=1

is extremal if and only if
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are extremal.

Lemma 5.7

(Sun cycle)
.

Let
G
be a graph and let
S=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}\subseteq V(G)
. Let
G^{\prime}
be formed from
G
by applying the sun cycle operation using
S
. Then
G
is extremal if and only if
G^{\prime}
is extremal.

Proof.

(
\implies
) Suppose
G
is extremal. Suppose by contradiction that
G^{\prime}
admits a proper hitting set
S^{\prime}\subseteq V(G^{\prime})
. We claim that
S=S^{\prime}\setminus\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}\subseteq V(G)
is a hitting set for
G
.

Consider any
v_{i}
in
G^{\prime}
and suppose
v_{i}
is hit. Consider the pair of vertices
\{v_{i-1},w_{i-1}\}
. To ensure that
w_{i-1}
is toggled, it must be the case that either both or neither of them are hit. The same is true for the pair
\{w_{i},v_{i+1}\}
. On reversing the sun-cycle operation to obtain
G
, it is easy to see that the parity of
v_{i}
is preserved.

Now suppose
v_{i}
is not hit. To ensure that
w_{i-1}
and
w_{i}
are toggled, it must be the case that exactly one of
\{v_{i-1},w_{i-1}\}
and exactly one of
\{w_{i},v_{i+1}\}
has been hit. Reversing the sun-cycle operation to obtain
G
, the parity of
v
is again preserved.

So it follows that
S
is a hitting set for
G
. Since
G
is extremal, it must be the case that
S=V(G)
. Since
S^{\prime}
is a hitting set for
G^{\prime}
and each
w_{i}
has exactly two hit neighbors in
V(G)
, it must be the case that
w_{i}
is also hit. It follows that
S^{\prime}=V(G^{\prime})
, a contradiction.

(
\impliedby
) Suppose
G^{\prime}
is extremal. Suppose for contradiction that
G
admits a proper hitting set
S
. Then set
S^{\prime}=S\cup\{w_{i}\colon v_{i},v_{i+1}\in S\}
. By similar parity arguments as the previous direction,
S^{\prime}
is a hitting set for
G^{\prime}
, which implies that
S^{\prime}=V(G^{\prime})
and in turn forces
S=V(G)
, a contradiction.
∎
