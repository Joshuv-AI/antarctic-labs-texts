VExUS versus CVP for assessing venous congestion: oasis or mirage?
Authors: Xiang Si, Daiyin Cao, Christopher Lai, Sheldon Magder, Xavier Monnet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Both venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) and central venous pressure (CVP) can assess venous congestion from different perspectives. CVP provides continuous and simple monitoring of the risk of congestion and is easily repeatable in patients with a central venous catheter. In contrast, VExUS offers an intermittent, organ-level evaluation of established congestion and may help identify the venous waterfall phenomenon. Furthermore, assessment of pulmonary congestion should not be overlooked.

Introduction

The Venous Excess Ultrasound (VExUS) has emerged as a promising tool, gaining considerable attention for patients at risk of fluid overload [1, 2]. Could VExUS be the missing piece of the puzzle, serving as a new gold standard for assessing venous congestion? Does it give much more clinical utility than central venous pressure (CVP)?

What does VExUS measure?

VExUS is an integrated ultrasound assessment of the splanchnic venous system, assessing the inferior vena cava (IVC), hepatic, portal, and intrarenal veins, and providing an overall “grade” of venous congestion. In post-cardiac surgery patients, VExUS predicted post-operative acute kidney injury (AKI) more accurately than CVP [3]. The authors rightly discussed how VExUS estimates venous congestion without requiring a central venous catheter and eliminates errors in CVP measurement and interpretation.

Subsequent studies suggested the value of VExUS to guide fluid strategy in shock. Increased VExUS grades [4] and portal vein pulsatility [5] are associated with cumulative fluid balance. Both abnormal portal and renal venous flow reliably predict successful fluid removal [6, 7]. Combined with fluid responsiveness prediction, VExUS helps determine whether to administer a fluid bolus (Fig. 1). While elevated VExUS may exist in patients who will increase their cardiac output with a fluid bolus (fluid responders) [8, 9], our group recently showed that volume expansion worsened venous congestion, as estimated by VExUS, in only 5% of fluid responders but in 73% of non-responders [9].Fig. 1Conceptual integration of the Frank–Starling curve (in red), venous return curve (in blue), and venous congestion curve (in green), illustrating how volume expansion simultaneously affects venous return, cardiac output, and venous congestion. What are we overlooking about VExUS?

First, assessing VExUS is time-consuming and requires specialised skills and dedicated training. In contrast, CVP is fast, continuous and simple. Practical challenges (e.g., image acquisition quality, hepatic flow distortion due to tricuspid regurgitation, altered pulsatility in portal hypertension, difficulty in obtaining intrarenal venous waveforms) [10] also impair its routine feasibility. Interpretation is further complicated by respiratory variability, ECG dependence [11], and cardiac arrhythmias.

Second, the weights assigned to these thresholds defining the VExUS grades are problematic. For example, an enlarged IVC is highly specific for organ-level venous congestion, but its sensitivity is poor, as over half of patients with an IVC < 2 cm still present with hepatic, portal, or renal venous congestion [9]. Thus, overweighing the IVC in the VExUS grading system can lead to underestimation of organ congestion. This may explain why isolated portal or renal congestion outperforms VExUS in predicting successful fluid removal [6, 7]. A more granular estimate could be obtained by quantifying VExUS not in four grades, but by assigning a point to each observed anomaly [9].

Third, VExUS only assesses right-sided congestion. While this is relevant for assessing the risk of fluid overload in the kidney and liver, pulmonary congestion should not be overlooked, as in left ventricular failure or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). In the study cited above [9], in fluid non-responders with ARDS, volume expansion increased lung water more than in non-responders without ARDS, while the fluid-induced increase in VExUS was similar [9].

CVP should not be forgotten!

Let’s put the question clearly: rather than VExUS, shouldn’t we consider CVP to assess venous congestion? Among its many advantages [12, 13], its measurement is much simpler, requiring only consideration of the transducer position relative to the right atrium, is easily repeatable, and is accessible to all patients with a central venous catheter. It is accurate and detects small changes. Observational studies showed that elevated CVP [14] or, even better, a low mean arterial pressure minus CVP gradient [15], is associated with AKI during shock. In septic patients, CVP predicts the occurrence of AKI while VExUS does not [16]. During volume expansion, it detects congestion due to a lack of preload reserve as well as VExUS [9]. So, isn’t CVP the gold standard for measuring and monitoring venous congestion?

VExUS versus CVP? maybe “side by side”!

VExUS and CVP may act side by side (Table 1) because they do not have the same meaning. However, due to the venous waterfall phenomenon, CVP is not always the downstream pressure of organ perfusion. While this phenomenon is absent at the renal level, it is significant in the splanchnic region, particularly the liver. In cases of intra-abdominal hypertension, an abdominal vascular zone II condition may develop. Ultrasound examination could capture characteristics of venous flow in these organs, indicating a waterfall phenomenon [17], for which the key is abdominal decompression to tame this “elephant in the room” [18], whereas CVP would be unhelpful for detecting such a phenomenon.

In other words, assessing CVP alone is like measuring the height of a reservoir without knowing the upstream riverbank flow, whereas assessing VExUS alone is like inspecting the flooded riverbanks without heeding the alarms already raised by the downstream reservoir.

So… VExUS: oasis or mirage?

VExUS is grounded in physiology, visually intuitive, and shows the hemodynamic features of the venous flow, possibly detecting specific signs of the waterfall phenomenon. Future research will better define this value and develop ways to overcome its current limitations. Automated analysis could facilitate its measurement and detect the Doppler abnormalities that suggest a waterfall effect. The value of summing the points for each abnormality, rather than using grades, also needs further investigation.

In practice: VExUS or CVP?

The hemodynamic monitoring strategy in patients with shock should be multimodal [1]. VExUS tells us how much damage has happened when we do not respect CVP, and CVP tells us what could happen if no action is taken. CVP should be measured in all patients in shock with a central venous catheter. Done with all due care [19], it continuously monitors the risk of venous congestion. It can be easily repeated, for example, before and after a fluid bolus. VExUS is a valuable intermittent complement to information, particularly when hepatic congestion is suspected. In patients with ARDS, estimating pulmonary congestion using lung water or pulmonary artery occlusion pressure should not be overlooked.
