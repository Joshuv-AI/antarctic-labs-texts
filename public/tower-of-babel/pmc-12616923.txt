Cellular osmoregulation enhances porcine embryo development by restoring zygotic genome activation via metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk
Authors: Xiangyuan Zhao, Lixiang Liu, Xiaoyu Chu, Ying Zhang, Yu Tang, Jing Shao, Bingfeng Fan, Yifeng Yang, Baozeng Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn vitro embryo culture is essential for human assisted reproduction and livestock breeding, yet its efficiency remains limited owing to developmental arrest triggered by suboptimal media composition and environmental stressors. Preimplantation embryos are highly sensitive to a minor increase in osmolarity under organic osmolyte deficiency, which disrupts cell volume homeostasis to cause developmental block. However, the osmosensing mechanisms and the causal link between volume dysregulation and developmental arrest remain undefined. Elucidating these mechanisms will identify targeted osmoregulatory interventions to enhance in vitro culture efficiency.MethodsThis study established a porcine two-cell embryo developmental arrest model under physiological-range hyperosmotic stress (330 mOsm) and organic osmolyte deficiency, which disrupts cell volume homeostasis. Through single-embryo RNA-seq, Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), H3K4me3/H3K27ac/H3K9me3/m6A/BrdU immunofluorescence, mitochondrial assays (MitoTracker Red and reactive oxygen species (ROS) staining), and metabolic analysis (pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity by Western blotting, fatty acid oxidation by FAOBlue staining), we identified hyperosmosis-induced developmental impairments. Rescue experiments via organic osmolyte supplementation, PDH modulation, and epigenetic interventions further defined the molecular basis of embryonic arrest.ResultsHere, we reveal that physiological-range hyperosmolarity in the absence of organic osmolytes disrupts cell volume homeostasis in porcine two-cell embryos, triggering developmental arrest at the S phase of the four-cell stage. This arrest coincides with aberrant maternal-to-zygotic transition, characterized by impaired maternal transcript degradation, compromised zygotic genome activation (ZGA), and coordinated dysregulation of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA transcription. Mechanistically, arrested embryos exhibit disrupted metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk, including PDH inactivation via S293 p-PDH accumulation that blocks pyruvate-to-acetyl-coenzyme A (CoA) conversion, fatty acid β-oxidation inhibition, alongside elevated mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), increased ROS accumulation, and reduced H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications. Critically, while pharmacological modulation of H3K4me3/H3K27ac fails to rescue developmental defects, restoring volume homeostasis with organic osmolytes (e.g., glycine/betaine) or reactivating PDH via dichloroacetate (DCA) treatment completely reverses hyperosmotic stress-induced developmental arrest.ConclusionsThese findings identify that mitochondria in porcine preimplantation embryos act as osmotic stress sensors. Under conditions of extracellular organic osmolyte deficiency and elevated osmolarity, they drive metabolic reprogramming and nuclear epigenetic dysregulation, ultimately disrupting mitochondrial–nuclear communication, compromising ZGA, and inducing developmental arrest. These findings provide mechanistic insights for optimizing in vitro culture systems in reproductive technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00826-3.Keywords: Embryo culture in vitro, Zygotic genome activation, Osmoregulation, PDH, Metabolic–epigenetic crosstalk

Introduction

Infertility, the third most common human disease, affects approximately 15% of reproductive-age couples [1]. Its treatment increasingly relies on the ability to culture preimplantation (PI) embryos in vitro for extended periods while ensuring their health [2]. It is well established that culturing embryos in suboptimal conditions negatively impacts development and pregnancy, often leading to treatment failure or even diseases in offspring [2]. Previous studies, including our own, have shown that mammalian PI embryos are very sensitive to cell size perturbations and that cell volume dysregulation plays a major role in the developmental arrest that occurs in early embryo culture in vitro [3–5]. PI embryos possess the same mechanisms as somatic cells to mediate the initial acute recovery upon increased extracellular osmolarity through a rapid import of inorganic ions primarily via functionally coupled Na+/H+ and HCO3−/Cl− exchangers [3, 4]. Such accumulation of inorganic ions can be disruptive since, above a certain level, the increased ionic strength disrupts cellular biochemistry and macromolecular functions and alters membrane potential. To mitigate this, PI embryos rely on unique mechanisms of cell volume control not employed by other cell types. Through the GLYT1 and SIT1 transporters, PI embryos accumulate uncharged organic osmolytes glycine and betaine to replace a portion of the intracellular inorganic ions but still maintain intracellular osmolarity to restore cell volume and prevent intracellular ionic strength from rising to detrimental levels [3, 4]. When fertilized eggs are cultured in media lacking organic osmolytes, even physiological osmolality can lead to cell volume perturbation and developmental arrest in PI embryos [3–5]. This species-specific developmental block typically occurs at the two-cell stage in mice [4–7], the eight-cell stage in bovines [4, 8], the four- to eight-cell stage in humans [4, 9], and the four-cell stage in pigs [10, 11]. A critical characteristic of media that eliminates the developmental arrest of PI embryos is the reduction of osmolarity to well below physiological osmolarity or the supplementation of organic osmolytes. Although this cross-species conserved developmental checkpoint has been observed, its molecular basis remains unclear. Owing to ethical constraints and restricted availability of human embryos, as well as the significant disparities between murine and human embryos, it is necessary to select porcine embryos that are more similar to human embryos in developmental dynamics, ZGA regulation, and early lineage differentiation than mouse embryos [12, 13] to reveal the regulatory mechanisms of human embryonic development. This study investigates porcine PI embryos to determine how cell volume dysregulation induces developmental arrest in both human and porcine in vitro cultured embryos, aiming to advance therapeutic strategies for human infertility and enhance porcine breeding efficiency.

Mitochondria are maternally inherited semi-autonomous organelles that require coordinated expression of both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes (mtDNA) genes to regulate energy metabolism, redox balance, epigenetic modifications of histone and DNA, stress responses, and cellular homeostasis [14–18]. The mtDNA, a circular double-stranded genome of approximately 16.5 kilobases (Kb), encodes only 13 polypeptide genes, 22 tRNAs for translation, and 2 ribosomal RNAs [19]. The other mitochondrial proteins, estimated to be around 1500, are nuclear genome-coded and imported into the mitochondria posttranslationally [17]. Critically, mitochondrial-to-nuclear (mito-nuclear) communication regulates cell fate by transmitting metabolites or stress signals that directly or indirectly influence epigenetic machinery [20]. Serving as substrates or cofactors for epigenetic modifiers, mitochondrial metabolites, such as α-ketoglutarate essential for protein and DNA demethylation, and acetyl-CoA required for protein acetylation [21], are generated via PDH-mediated conversion of pyruvate in the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [22]. Stage-specific epigenetic marks, such as H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, are conserved hallmarks of transcriptional activity and open chromatin, whereas H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 are widely used as markers of inactive chromatin and repressive transcription [23–28], to demarcate distinct genomic elements [26], and to regulate the expression of developmental genes during PI embryo development in mammals [25].

Epigenetic dynamics are indispensable for ZGA during the maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) [28–31]. Timely clearance of maternal mRNAs and proteins enables ZGA [31–33], where failure induces developmental arrest evidenced by porcine PI embryos failing to develop beyond the four-cell stage upon ZGA inhibition [34]. Notably, compatibility between the mitochondria and the nucleus is essential for stress adaptation [14–18], particularly under hyperosmotic conditions where mitochondria act as direct sensors to trigger metabolic remodeling for cell survival [14, 35]. However, whether the dysregulated mito-nuclear communication during ZGA in porcine embryos is involved in the hyperosmolarity-induced developmental block and, if so, its underlying mechanisms are largely unknown.

Here, we reveal that the drastically altered transcriptome and developmental arrest during ZGA are triggered in porcine PI embryos upon a minor increase in osmolarity in the absence of organic osmolytes. Moreover, we discovered that hyperosmotic stress triggers a cascade of cellular perturbations, including metabolic reprogramming, compromised histone epigenetic remodeling, and oxidative damage. Notably, we identify that supplementation with organic osmolytes, such as glycine and betaine, or pharmacological activation of PDH, rather than modulating the activity of epigenetic modifiers, effectively rescues embryos from hypertonicity-induced abnormalities. Our findings constitute the first evidence that embryonic volume homeostasis serves as a critical prerequisite for bidirectional mitochondria–nucleus communication in porcine PI embryos, which in turn ensures proper histone modifications, redox balance, timely ZGA progression, and healthy embryonic development. This work provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying the developmental arrest of mammalian PI embryos under osmotic or other stress responses, which allows us to improve the efficiency of embryo culture and cryopreservation by optimizing the osmolarity, organic osmolyte content, and appropriate energy substances of the culture medium, thereby more effectively treating human infertility, and improving porcine reproductive efficiency.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and media

All reagents and chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless stated otherwise in Supplementary Table S1.

MI and MII medium were used for maturation of porcine oocytes, and modified Tris-buffered medium (mTBM) was used for in vitro fertilization of porcine oocytes, as previously described in our laboratory [36]. The MI medium was formulated by supplementing TCM-199 (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with 10% porcine follicular fluid (PFF), 10 IU/mL luteinizing hormone (LH), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), 10 IU/mL follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), 100 IU/mL insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS), 50 μg/mL streptomycin sulfate, and 75 μg/mL potassium penicillin G. In contrast, the MII medium contains identical components to the MI-medium except for the omission of LH and FSH. The NCSU-23 medium for porcine embryo culture has an osmolarity of 288 ± 5 mOsM, which was prepared according to previous research [37] with bovine serum albumin (BSA) replaced with 0.1% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (hereinafter referred to as 288 medium). The hypertonic organic osmolytes-supplemented NCSU-23 medium (hereinafter referred to as 330 medium) was prepared by adding an appropriate amount of d-(+)-raffinose, which is not metabolized nor transported by mammalian cells [38], to NCSU-23 medium to adjust the osmolarity to 330 ± 5 mOsM. Meanwhile, the isotonic and hypertonic organic osmolytes-free NCSU-23 medium (hereinafter referred to as 288(–) and 330(–) medium, respectively) was formulated by removing l-glutamine, taurine, and hypotaurine from the NCSU-23 medium, followed by supplementation with d-(+)-raffinose to adjust the osmolarity to 288 ± 5 mOsM and 330 ± 5 mOsM, respectively. Glycine (10 mM) [11] and betaine (0.5 mM) [39] were directly supplemented to the medium, followed by d-(+)-raffinose addition to adjust the final osmolarity when specified. The osmolarity of the media was confirmed by osmometer (Wescor, UT, USA) measurements. The pH of all media was 7.2–7.4. These media had been pre-equilibrated with 5% CO2 in air at 38.5 °C for at least 12 h before use.

Oocyte collection and culture

Oocyte collection and in vitro maturation (IVM) were performed according to previously established methods in our laboratory [36, 40–42]. Briefly, porcine ovaries were obtained from prepubertal gilts at a local slaughterhouse and transported to our laboratory in saline at 37 °C. Cumulus–oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from antral follicles (3–7 mm in diameter) with an 18-gauge needle. Oocytes with at least three layers of cumulus cells and evenly granulated cytoplasm were selected for experiments. For IVM of oocytes, every 50 COCs were sequentially cultured in 500 μl MI and MII medium in a four-well Petri dish (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) for 22 h each at 38.5 °C with a saturated humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

In vitro fertilization and preimplantation embryo culture

For in vitro fertilization (IVF) of oocytes, groups of 15 cumulus-free oocytes exhibiting, first polar bodies (MII oocytes) were transferred into a 60 μl mTBM droplet covered with paraffin oil at 38.5 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator until spermatozoa were added for fertilization. For sperm preparation, frozen-ejaculated boar spermatozoa were thawed at 37 °C and washed three times by centrifugation at 700 × g for 3 min in mTBM solution. The sperm pellet was then resuspended in mTBM and incubated for 30 min at 38.5 °C. Sperm suspension (15 μl) was added to the oocyte-containing droplet to give a final sperm concentration of 1 × 105 cells/ml for 6 h. After removing the sperm attached to the zona pellucida, a group of ten fertilized eggs was transferred into 30 μl embryo culture medium with different osmolarities under paraffin oil at 38.5 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 for preimplantation embryo culture in vitro. The percentages of two-cell embryos, four-cell embryos, eight-cell embryos, morulae, and blastocysts were recorded at 32, 56, 80, 104, and 148 h post-insemination (hpi), respectively.

Embryonic size measurements

As previously reported [43], early two-cell embryos were transferred from 288 medium to 330(–) medium and imaged at 0, 2, 20, 40, and 60 min using a confocal microscope (Nikon C2, Japan) with a 40× objective and differential interference contrast (DIC) optics. Between imaging intervals, embryos were maintained in an incubator (38.5 °C, 5% CO₂). For each embryo, the major and minor diameters of both blastomeres were measured and converted to microns using a stage micrometer calibrated under identical settings. The relative projected area per blastomere was calculated as the product of its two diameters, and the total projected area for each embryo was reported as the sum of both blastomeres’ areas. This area-based metric served as a volume surrogate to avoid errors from estimating the z-axis dimension perpendicular to the focal plane. When discussing volume changes, we assumed proportionality to the calculated relative areas raised to the 3/2 power. Three independent replicates per treatment yielded pooled data of 20 embryos per time point.

Single-embryo RNA-seq and annotation

Preparation of cDNA from individual embryos was carried out following the previously reported Smart-seq2 protocol [44]. Briefly, a single embryo was placed into a tube with 2 μl cell lysis buffer using a mouth pipette after its zona pellucida had been removed by pronase treatment. First-strand cDNA was reverse-synthesized using Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen), template-switching oligonucleotides (TSO, 5′-AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTACATrGrG + G-3′) and oligo(dT) (5′-AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTACT30VN-3′). The reverse transcription reaction was processed at 42 °C for 90 min, followed by 11 cycles at 50 °C for 2 min, 42 °C for 2 min, and final enzyme inactivation at 75 °C for 15 min. The PCR preamplification was carried out using KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems) and IS PCR primers (5′-AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGT- 3′). The reaction was incubated at 98 °C for 3 min, followed by 18 cycles at 98 °C for 20 s, 67 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 6 min, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR product was sheared randomly by ultrasonic waves for Illumina library preparation, including DNA fragmentation, end-repair, 3′ end poly(A) tailing, adapter ligation, PCR amplification, and library validation. Qualified libraries were loaded on the Illumina HiSeq platform for PE150 sequencing. Low-quality reads were removed to obtain clean data for downstream analysis. The reference genomes (Sscrofa 11.1) and the annotation file were downloaded from the ENSEMBL database (http://www.ensembl.org/index.html). Clean reads from individual embryos were aligned to the reference genome for gene annotation using HISAT2 (version 2.0.1) software. Aligned read counts and fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments (FPKM) calculations for each gene were performed with HTSeq (version 0.6.1) software.

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression analysis between embryos was determined using the DESeq2 R package as previously described [45]. The Benjamini and Hochberg method was used to adjust the p-value and control the false discovery rate. Genes with corrected p-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change) |≥ 1 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The volcano plots were generated with the Enhanced Volcano R package. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed by the clusterProfiler R package, and hypergeometric tests were performed with default parameters to adjust p-values. All GO terms/KEGG pathways with padj ≤ 0.05 were considered enriched with DEGs.

Single-embryo real-time quantitative PCR

cDNA was extracted and amplified from individual embryos following previously published methods [46]. The real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on the AriaMx Real-Time PCR System (Gene Company Limited, Hong Kong, China) using the FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (Rox) kit (Roche, Germany). The sequences of oligonucleotide primers were designed using OligoPerfect software (Invitrogen Carlsbad, CA, USA) and are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The cycling parameters of PCR were as follows: 1 cycle at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 50 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, and 60˚C for 60 s. The Hprt gene was used as a housekeeping gene. The relative amount of RNA was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCT method [47].

Assessment of early apoptosis

Early apoptosis of embryos was assessed using the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit as previously described [36] with minor changes. Briefly, embryos were washed with NCSU-23 and resuspended in 100 μl binding buffer. Then, 5 μl Annexin V-FITC and 5 μl PI were sequentially added to the suspension and incubated at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. After washing with 400 μl binding buffer, the embryos were observed on a Nikon C2 plus Si confocal microscope within 1 h. Two-cell embryos cultured in 288 medium with 1 mM H2O2 at 37 °C for 1.5 h before annexin V staining served as a positive control, as previously described [5]. Embryos without Annexin V-FITC and PI staining were considered nonapoptotic embryos. Embryos with Annexin V staining only on the cell membrane without PI staining on the nucleus indicated that the embryos were at an early stage of apoptosis. In addition, embryos co-stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI indicated that these embryos had undergone cell death.

BrdU incorporation assay

Cellular DNA replication was assessed by immunofluorescence staining for BrdU as previously described [5] with minor modifications. As described in the “Results” section, early embryos were synchronized to the metaphase of the two-cell embryo by treatment with 0.1 μg/ml nocodazole, then 1 mM BrdU was added to different culture media for more than 24 h to examine BrdU incorporation into nascent DNA strands in cells. Immediately after the zona pellucida was removed by brief exposure to acid Tyrode’s (pH 2.5), embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min and incubated in permeabilization solution (2% Triton X-100 in PBS) overnight at 37 °C. DNA was denatured with 2 N HCl for 30 min and neutralized in 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.5) for 10 min. After thorough washing with washing buffer (0.1% Tween-20, 0.01%Triton X-100 in PBS), embryos were blocked for 2 h with washing buffer containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated with a monoclonal anti-BrdU antibody (1:100) overnight at 4 °C. Embryos were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:500) in the dark for 1 h after the primary antibody was thoroughly washed. Following thorough washing, DNA was counterstained with 10 μg/ml propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min. Embryos were mounted on glass slides with the Prolong Antifade mounting medium and imaged within 1 week using a Nikon C2 plus Si confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Assessment of mitochondrial function

MitoTracker Red CMXRos was used to label the functional mitochondria, and the ROS Assay Kit was used to detect intracellular ROS production according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, embryos were incubated in NCSU-23 medium supplemented with 10 μM DCFH-DA (ROS staining) for 20 min or 500 nM MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Mitochondrial staining) for 30 min at 38.5 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After three washes of 2 min each in PBS-PVA, embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min. Then embryos were placed on a glass slide using the ProLong Diamond Antifade mounting medium with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and imaged with a confocal laser scanning microscope under the same parameters.

H3K4me3, H3K27ac, H3K9me3, and m6A epigenetic modification detection

Embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 30 min and permeabilized in permeabilizing solution at 37 °C overnight. After thorough washing, embryos were blocked for 1 h with washing buffer containing 3% FBS and incubated with the H3K4me3 (1:200), H3K27ac (1:200), H3K9me3 (1:100), or m6A (1:100) antibody overnight at 4 °C. After washing, embryos were labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark. Embryos were mounted on microscope slides using the ProLong Diamond Antifade mounting medium with DAPI and imaged within 1 week with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2, Japan) under the same parameters.

Western blotting

To detect the expression of p-PDH (S293), translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20), and β-actin, a pool of 100 embryos was lysed in 10 μl 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer (130 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 10% β-mercaptoethanol, 298 μM bromophenol blue, 20% glycerol and 5% SDS) and boiled for 10 min, then stored at −80 °C until use. The total proteins were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) with a 4% stacking gel and a 10% separating gel at 90 V, 0.5 h, and 120 V, 2.5 h, respectively, and then electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membrane for 2.5 h, 200 mA, at 4 °C. Membranes were blocked in TBST buffer (10 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20, pH 7.4) containing 5% skimmed milk (blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was probed with either rabbit polyclonal anti-p-PDH (S293) antibody (diluted 1:1000) or rabbit polyclonal anti-TOMM20 antibody (diluted 1:2000) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. After three washes of 10 min each in TBST buffer, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG diluted 1:5000 in blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were washed three times in TBST buffer and processed for the detection of HRP using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system. Protein loading was verified by the detection of β-actin as follows. After ECL detection, the membrane was washed in stripping buffer (62.5 mM Tris, 100 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 20% SDS, pH 6.7) for 30 min at 50–55 °C and then incubated with mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin antibody (diluted 1:5000) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (diluted 1:5000) in a blocking buffer for 1 h at room temperature, and HRP was detected as described above. All experiments were repeated three times starting with independently collected samples. Band densities were quantified by AlphaEaseFC 4.0 software. Individual band intensities were normalized by those of β-actin.

Drug treatment

The H3K4me3 demethylase KDM5A inhibitor CPI-455 and the H3K27ac deacetylase SIRT1 inhibitor Ex-527 were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution at 20 mM and 10 mM, respectively, and stored at −80 ℃. In contrast, DCA, a pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) inhibitor, was dissolved directly in media. The working solution of CPI-455 (20 μM) [48, 49] and Ex-527 (10 μM) [50] was diluted in culture media just before use. To explore whether CPI-455 or/and Ex-527 can improve embryonic development via correcting abnormal histone epigenetic modifications caused by hyperosmotic conditions, early two-cell embryos were treated with these inhibitors as described in the text. To determine whether restoring PDH activity via DCA improves embryo development under hyperosmotic stress, early two-cell embryos were incubated in media containing 0–20 mM DCA for durations outlined in the text. The resulting embryos were collected at designated time points to quantify developmental progression and cellular homeostasis.

Assessment of fatty acid β-oxidation

Fatty acid β-oxidation levels were assessed using the FAOBlue fluorescent probe (Funakoshi, cat. no. FDV-0033) according to the procedure described previously with minor modifications [51]. Briefly, 20 μM FAOBlue was added to the respective culture media and co-incubated with embryos at 38.5 °C under 5% CO₂ for 30 min. Immediately after thorough washing, samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2, Japan) with excitation at 405 nm and emission at 460 nm under the same parameters. Fluorescence intensity was then analyzed.

Data analysis

The intensity of each fluorescent staining was quantified using ImageJ (version 1.4d; National Institutes of Health, USA). Quantitative data were graphed and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). Each experiment was repeated at least three times, and the specific number of embryos tested in the experiment is stated in the text. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error on the mean (SEM) of independent experiments. For independent treatment groups, means were compared using Student’s two-tailed t-test (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey multiple-comparison test (three or more groups). Differences at p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Physiological osmolarity triggers four-cell arrest in porcine embryos via cell volume dysregulation in the absence of organic osmolytes

To investigate whether elevated external osmolarity, approximating the in vivo osmolarity of oviductal fluid, induces developmental arrest in early porcine embryos and whether this arrest can be mitigated by the addition of organic osmolytes, early two-cell embryos (28 hpi) were cultured under two osmotic conditions: 288 mOsM and 330 mOsM, with or without supplementation of 10 mM glycine and 0.5 mM betaine, which were found to be the most effective organic osmolytes used by embryos [39, 52], as illustrated in Fig. 1A. In 288 medium (control group), 64.4 ± 0.9% (n = 160, N = 7) of embryos progressed to the eight-cell stage or beyond. In contrast, 90.25 ± 3.09% of embryos (n = 118, N = 7) arrested at or before the four-cell stage, with only 9.75 ± 3.09% (n = 118, N = 7) advancing to the eight-cell stage in the 330(–) medium (hypertonic group). Notably, when early two-cell embryos were exposed to hyperosmotic conditions (330(–)) for 8 h and subsequently transferred to 330(–) medium supplemented with glycine and betaine (referred to as 330(–)⸱8h → 330(–) + Gly + Bet medium, rescue group), 55.71 ± 1.29% (n = 104, N = 4) of embryos successfully overcame the four-cell block and developed to the eight-cell stage or beyond (Fig. 1B). To determine whether the developmental arrest of four-cell embryos in 330(–) medium is attributed to the deprivation of intrinsic organic osmolytes, including l-glutamine, taurine, and hypotaurine, in NCSU-23 medium, we assessed the impact of their deprivation on embryonic development under isotonic (288 mOsM) and hyperosmotic (330 mOsM) conditions by comparing 288, 288(–), 330, and 330(–) media groups. Specifically, the 288 medium yielded the highest developmental rate at 56.78 ± 1.90% of embryos beyond the four-cell stage (n = 55, N = 3), significantly exceeding all other groups. Meanwhile, the 288(–) medium achieved 26.65 ± 6.65% (n = 22, N = 3), which still surpassed both hyperosmotic groups of 330(–) medium at 8.70 ± 3.10% (n = 35, N = 3) and 330 medium at 17.15 ± 2.85% (n = 29, N = 3), with no significant difference between 330(–) and 330 media. Consequently, these organic osmolytes deprivation significantly impaired development under isotonic conditions but not hyperosmotic conditions, demonstrating that hyperosmotic stress (330 mOsM) is the primary factor compromising development, while deprivation synergistically exacerbates damage only in isotonic environments (Fig. 1C). To determine whether subtle increases in extracellular osmolarity affect cell volume regulation, we quantified the transcript levels of key genes of mechanosensitive genes that are linked to cell volume homeostasis using RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 1D, four-cell embryos cultured from the early two-cell stage in 288 medium exhibited significantly elevated expression of WNK lysine deficient protein kinase 1 (Wnk1), piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (Piezo1), Piezo2, catenin beta 1 (β-catenin), Yes1-associated transcriptional regulator (Yap1), and macrophage stimulating 2 (Mst2) compared with those in 330(–) medium. Conversely, serine/threonine kinase 39 (Stk39 or Spak), nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (Nfat5 or TonEBP), and macrophage stimulating 1 (Mst1) gene showed significantly reduced expression. Furthermore, embryonic size measurements revealed that early two-cell embryos exhibited a significant reduction in volume after transfer from 288 mOsM medium to 330(–) medium (Fig. 1E). These findings imply that elevated osmolarity in the absence of organic osmolytes disrupts cell volume homeostasis and underscore the critical role of organic osmolyte accumulation in counterbalancing external osmotic pressure to maintain normal cell volume regulation, thereby facilitating normal progression through early embryonic development in porcine embryos.

Disturbed cell volume homeostasis causes abnormalities of Z-decay and ZGA in porcine four-cell embryos

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying developmental arrest induced by physiological osmolarity in the absence of external organic osmolytes, a detailed comparative transcriptomic analysis was conducted at a single-embryo resolution between four-cell embryos cultured in 288 medium and 330(–) medium. The results demonstrated high reproducibility, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.96 to 0.99 for control groups and 0.70 to 0.97 for hypertonic groups, based on quintuplicate samples from each condition (Supplementary Fig. S1A). From a total of 31,908 assembled genes, a library of 1035 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was identified. Among these, 9778 genes were annotated, with 182 genes significantly downregulated and 853 genes significantly upregulated (p-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)|≥ 1) in arrested four-cell embryos compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. S1B). To assess whether these DEGs are linked to disruptions in Z-decay and ZGA, key processes occurring between the two-cell and four-cell stages, the down- and upregulated DEGs were mapped to Z-decay [53], minor ZGA [53], and major ZGA [54] gene sets derived from publicly available porcine PI embryo transcriptome data. Among the 182 downregulated DEGs in arrested four-cell embryos, 28 genes (15.4%) overlapped with the Z-decay gene set (n = 528), 23 genes (12.6%) overlapped with the minor ZGA set (n = 2384), and 1 gene (0.5%) overlapped with the major ZGA set (n = 1397) (Fig. 1F). Conversely, among the 853 upregulated DEGs in arrested four-cell embryos, 8 genes (0.9%) overlapped with the Z-decay gene set, 65 (7.6%) genes overlapped with the minor ZGA gene set, and 212 genes (24.9%) overlapped with the major ZGA gene set (Fig. 1G). These findings suggest that disruptions in cell volume homeostasis may contribute to aberrant Z-decay and ZGA processes in porcine PI embryos. This hypothesis was further supported by RT-qPCR analysis, which revealed that the expression levels of Z-decay marker genes Gdf9 [29, 55] and Btg4 [56] were significantly elevated in arrested four-cell embryos compared with controls (Fig. 1H). In contrast, the expression of ZGA marker genes Cdk1 [57] and H47 [5] was markedly reduced in arrested embryos (Fig. 1I). Additionally, other Z-decay marker genes, including Rnf114 [58], Nlrp5 [59], Tut7 [56], and Tut4 [56], exhibited significantly decreased expression levels in arrested embryos compared with controls (Fig. 1H). Conversely, the ZGA marker gene Zscan4 [60] showed a significant increase in expression in arrested embryos (Fig. 1I). These results collectively indicate that disturbances in cell volume homeostasis disrupt the regulation of Z-decay and ZGA, leading to developmental arrest in porcine PI embryos.

Cell volume dysregulation induced by hyperosmotic stress impairs mitochondrial homeostasis in porcine embryos

To investigate the mechanisms by which elevated osmolarity in the absence of organic osmolytes impedes Z-decay and ZGA in porcine four-cell embryos, we performed GO terms and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses on DEGs between control and hypertonic groups. Both analyses revealed consistent enrichment patterns in upregulated DEGs of arrested embryos, predominantly associated with (a) cellular metabolism; (b) mitochondrial function; (c) transcription, translation, and the processing of mRNA and protein; (d) apoptosis; (e) cysteine and methionine metabolism (Fig. 2A, B). Given the prominence of mitochondrial pathways in these enrichments, we further investigated the impact of hyperosmotic stress on mitochondrial biogenesis and function in porcine embryos by comparing the transcript levels of mitochondrial components and regulators between control and hypertonic groups using single-embryo RNA-seq data, followed by RT-qPCR verification. Strikingly, 10 out of 13 mtDNA-encoded genes, including Nd1–Nd6, Cox2, Cox3, Cytb, and Atp6, as well as key regulators of mtDNA transcription, such as mitochondrial RNA polymerase (Polrmt), mitochondrial transcription factor A (Tfam), and transcription factors Tfb1m, exhibited significant differential expression between the two groups (Fig. 2C, D). Additionally, nuclear DNA-encoded mitochondrial components and regulators, including inner membrane mitochondrial protein (Immt), mitochondrial import inner membrane translocase subunit TIM16 (Pam16), heat shock protein 60 (Hsp60), and PTEN-induced kinase 1 (Pink1), also showed marked expression differences (Fig. 2C, D). These findings suggest that hyperosmotic stress disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis and function in porcine embryos, potentially contributing to the observed developmental arrest.

Assessment of MMP using MitoTracker Red CMXRos staining revealed a significant increase in MMP in embryos exposed to hyperosmotic stress compared with the control group (Fig. 2E, F). Concurrently, ROS levels were markedly elevated in hypertonic-treated embryos relative to controls (Fig. 2G, H). The transcript levels of key regulators of cellular antioxidant responses, including the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and GPX6, were significantly elevated in hyperosmotic-treated embryos (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, PDH, a key enzyme linking glycolysis to the TCA cycle through the irreversible conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, is inactivated by phosphorylation at serine residue S293 [14, 61].TOMM20, a critical subunit of the mitochondrial outer membrane translocase complex, facilitates the recognition and import of mitochondrial proteins from the cytosol into mitochondria [62] and interacts with PARKIN to regulate mitophagy, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality [63]. Our findings demonstrated that phosphorylation of the nuclear-encoded mitochondrial enzyme PDH (S293) was significantly upregulated in four-cell embryos arrested under hyperosmotic conditions compared with controls, while protein levels of the mitochondrial quality marker TOMM20 remained unchanged (Fig. 2J, K). Moreover, genes regulating fatty acid and amino acid metabolism were differentially expressed in four-cell embryos cultured in 288 versus 330(–) medium, as revealed by single-embryo transcriptomics (Supplementary Fig. S2A, B). These results suggest that hyperosmotic stress induces PDH inactivation, disrupts the balance between aerobic glycolysis and the TCA cycle, and drives metabolic reprogramming in four-cell embryos. Given that excessive ROS exerts pathological effects through apoptosis induction [64] and that hyperosmotic stress typically triggers cell cycle arrest followed by apoptosis [65, 66], we assessed early apoptosis in four-cell embryos using Annexin-V staining. Intriguingly, hypertonic treatment did not induce apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Collectively, these findings suggest that porcine PI embryos adapt to hyperosmotic stress-induced cellular damage through PDH inactivation-mediated metabolic reprogramming, circumventing activation of the apoptotic cascade.

Hyperosmotic stress triggers metabolic remodeling and stress adaptation in porcine four-cell embryos

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying MZT failure under hypertonic stress, we further performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on downregulated transcripts in arrested four-cell embryos. Among the 13 significantly enriched GO terms, 7 terms were related to the cytoskeleton, including 6 terms related to spindle assembly and 1 term linked to microfilament assembly (Fig. 3A). KEGG analysis identified the Fanconi anemia pathway (Fig. 3B), which plays a critical role in repairing DNA interstrand crosslinks, stabilizing DNA replication forks, and regulating cytokinesis [67]. These findings suggest that hypertonic treatment induces developmental arrest at or before the G2/M transition in four-cell embryos. Consistent with this, BrdU immunofluorescence revealed robust DNA replication in control four-cell embryos (n = 25, N = 3), whereas hypertonic-treated embryos exhibited weak and partial DNA staining (n = 24, N = 3), indicating impaired DNA synthesis (Fig. 3C, D). These findings indicate that hyperosmotic stress induces S-phase (DNA synthesis phase) arrest in porcine four-cell embryos under conditions devoid of organic osmolytes. Furthermore, among the nine significantly enriched KEGG pathways identified, six pathways were involved in cell metabolism, including metabolic pathways, sphingolipid metabolism, type I diabetes mellitus, cysteine and methionine metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, as well as valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (Fig. 3B). In parallel with this, transcripts associated with fatty acid metabolism, such as carbonyl reductase 4 (Cbr4), alcohol dehydrogenase 5 (Adh5), and diacylglycerol kinase kappa (Dgkk), were significantly downregulated in arrested four-cell embryos (Fig. 3E). FAOBlue staining also revealed that the levels of fatty acid β-oxidation were downregulated in hypertonic-treated embryos (Fig. 3F, G). Furthermore, amino acid metabolic-associated transcripts, including glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate transaminase 2 (Gfpt2), glucosamine-phosphate N-acetyltransferase 1 (Gnpnat1), methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (Mtap), cysteine dioxygenase type 1 (Cdo1), isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase (Ivd), dihydrolipoamide branched chain transacylase E2 (Dbt), alcohol dehydrogenase 5 (Adh5), and glutathione peroxidase 8 (Gpx8), exhibited marked dysregulation (Fig. 3H). These findings further support the occurrence of metabolic reprogramming in hyperosmotic-treated porcine embryos (Fig. 3A, B, E–H). Previous studies confirm that hypertonic stress triggers endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and unfolded protein response (UPR) [68, 69], as well as the integrated stress response (ISR) [70] in somatic cells or embryos. As shown in Fig. 3A, transcripts levels of ER stress and UPR sensors/effectors, including activating transcription factor 6 (Atf6), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (Ire-1), heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 5 (Hspa5), protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 15A (Ppp1r15a, also known as Gadd34), and glucose-regulated protein 94 (Grp94 or Hsp90b1), were significantly elevated in hypertonic-treated embryos compared with controls (Fig. 3I, J). Similarly, ISR effectors such as activating transcription factor 4 (Atf4) and C/EBP homologous protein (Chop) were upregulated, whereas the ISR effector eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit alpha (eIF2α) exhibited an opposite expression trend in hypertonic-treated embryos (Fig. 3I, J). These observations suggest that the activation of UPR and ISR pro-survival pathways may delay cell death, potentially allowing for recovery once the stress is alleviated in embryos.

Hyperosmotic stress triggers epigenetic reprogramming in porcine four-cell embryos

Growing evidence in reproductive sciences underscores the significant influence of environmental factors and cellular metabolism on epigenetic regulation [71]. As shown in Fig. 3A, B, biological processes related to transferase activity, acyl group transfer, and the function of mitochondria and peroxisomes may be impaired or dysregulated in embryos cultured under hyperosmotic conditions. To validate whether hypertonic stress affects epigenetic modifications in porcine embryos, we analyzed single-embryo RNA-seq data and performed RT-qPCR validation to compare the transcript levels of enzymes regulating DNA methylation, histone methylation, and histone acetylation between control and hypertonic-treated four-cell embryos. As shown in Fig. 4A, B, significant differences were observed in the transcript levels of DNA methyltransferases (Dnmt1o and Dnmt3a), DNA demethylase (Tet3), histone demethylases (Kdm1b, Kdm4c, Kdm5a, Kdm5c, Kdm6b, and Kdm7a), and histone deacetylases (Sirt1 and Sirt3) between the two groups. Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the levels of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications were significantly higher in control four-cell embryos compared with hypertonic-treated embryos (Fig. 4C–F), while no significant differences were observed in H3K9me3 and mRNA m6A modifications between the groups (Fig. 4G–J). Collectively, elevated osmolarity in the absence of organic osmolytes disrupts H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications in porcine four-cell embryos.

Epigenetic interventions do not reverse mitochondrial dysfunction in hyperosmotic-stressed embryos

To determine whether epigenetic reprogramming is a cause or consequence of hyperosmotic stress-induced metabolic dysfunction, we investigated whether pharmacological correction of epigenetic modifications could rescue mitochondrial metabolic defects. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, early two-cell embryos were subjected to hyperosmotic treatment in 330(–) medium with or without supplementation of CPI-455 (an inhibitor of H3K4me3 demethylase KDM5A) or/and Ex-527 (an inhibitor of H3K27ac deacetylase SIRT1) for 8 h, followed by transfer to 288 medium. Comparative analysis revealed that CPI-455 treatment fully restored H3K4me3 levels in four-cell embryos, effectively counteracting the hyperosmotic stress effects (Fig. 5B, C). In contrast, Ex-527 treatment only partially reversed H3K27ac reduction caused by hyperosmotic stress (Fig. 5D, E). Notably, neither inhibitor alone nor their combination significantly mitigated the detrimental effect of hyperosmolarity on mitochondrial activity (Fig. 5F, G), ROS levels (Fig. 5H, I), or embryonic development (Fig. 5J). These results establish that epigenetic alterations occur secondary to hyperosmotic stress-driven metabolic reprogramming in four-cell embryos.

Organic osmolytes mitigate hyperosmotic stress damage via the metabolic–epigenetic axis in porcine embryos

Single-embryo RNA-seq and RT-qPCR validation indicated differential expression of glycine/serine and betaine metabolic genes in arrested versus control four-cell embryos (Fig. 6A, B). Specifically, hyperosmotic-arrested embryos showed upregulated expression of glycine transport (SLC6A9), glycine metabolism (Gldc and Gcsh), serine metabolism (Psat1 and Psph), serine–glycine interconversion (Shmt2), and betaine transport (Sit1), with Dld as the sole exception. Crucially, Dld, a core component of the glycine cleavage system, was nearly silenced in arrested embryos, indicating disruption of glycine catabolism. These findings suggest that compensatory elevation of glycine and betaine levels may be required to mitigate hyperosmotic stress damage in developmentally arrested embryos. To investigate whether organic osmolytes could alleviate the effects of hyperosmolarity on the mitochondrial defects and epigenetic modification alterations, embryos were treated according to the experimental protocol depicted in Fig. 1A. Compared with the control and hyperosmotic groups, addition of glycine and betaine partially reversed the hyperosmotic stress-induced alterations in MMP (Fig. 6C, D), ROS levels (Fig. 6E, F), H3K4me3 (Fig. 6G, H), and H3K27ac (Fig. 6I, J) modifications. However, this intervention only restored embryo progression to the eight-cell stage, with 11.46 ± 1.30% (n = 104, N = 4) reaching the morula stage and none forming blastocyst (Fig. 7B). We then assessed whether osmolyte addition could fully rescue hyperosmotic stress-impaired developmental competence using protocols in Fig. 7A. Treatment of two-cell embryos with hyperosmotic stress in 330(–) medium for 8 h, followed by transfer to either 330(–) or 330 medium supplemented with glycine and betaine for rescue, yielded similar development rates to the morula stage (11.46 ± 1.30%, n = 104, N = 4; and 11.94 ± 1.03%, n = 75, N = 4; respectively) (Fig. 7B). No significant difference in the rescue effect was observed between these two groups when compared with the control and hypertonic groups (Fig. 7B). In contrast, both transferring the embryos to 288 medium after the hypertonic exposure of two-cell embryos in 330(–) medium for 8 h (8.01 ± 0.70%, n = 112, N = 4) and culturing in 330 medium supplemented with glycine and betaine (6.25 ± 6.25%, n = 43, N = 4) partially restored their developmental competency to the blastocyst stage (Fig. 7B). Crucially, organic osmolyte supplementation during hypertonic stress to prevent cell volume dysregulation before rescue in 288 medium maximized rescue efficacy. Specifically, two-cell embryos treated in 330 medium supplemented with glycine and betaine for 8 h before transfer to 288 medium exhibited a higher blastocyst rate (26.83 ± 1.55%, n = 105, N = 4) than controls (18.45 ± 1.44%, n = 160, N = 7) (Fig. 7B). These results imply that organic osmolytes prevent hypertonicity-induced cell volume dysregulation, thereby mitigating developmental impairment.

Time-dependent organic osmolytes reverse hyperosmotic stress-induced embryonic damage

To determine the maximum hypertonic exposure duration that two-cell embryos could tolerate while maintaining blastocyst development under optimal rescue conditions, we further evaluated 15 h and 22 h treatments. Results indicated that 15 h was the critical threshold for reversible developmental recovery, spanning most of the two-cell stage (Fig. 8B). To determine whether organic osmolytes in hypertonic medium can rescue hyperosmotic stress-induced developmental impairment by reversing metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming, we assessed multiple rescue protocols illustrated in Fig. 8A. As anticipated, culturing early two-cell embryos in 330 medium supplemented with glycine and betaine for 15 h or 22 h followed by transfer to 288 medium fully reversed the effects of hyperosmotic stress on MMP (Fig. 8C, D), ROS levels (Fig. 8E, F), H3K4me3 (Fig. 8G, H), and H3K27ac (Fig. 8I, J) modifications, and blastocyst development rates (Fig. 8B), restoring values to control levels (Fig. 8 B-J). In contrast, embryos treated in 330(–) medium for 15 h before transfer to 288 medium showed persistent stress on MMP, ROS levels, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac modifications, and the capacity of embryos to develop to the morula stage, compared with both the control group and the optimal rescue group (Fig. 8B–J). These results demonstrate that addition of organic osmolytes to optimize cell volume regulation and homeostasis can effectively rescue the effects of hyperosmotic stress on embryonic development by normalizing metabolic and epigenetic remodeling.

PDH-mediated metabolic rewiring rescues embryonic dysfunction from hyperosmotic stress

To evaluate whether PDH activation by DCA treatment rescues hyperosmotic stress-impaired embryonic development, early two-cell embryos were exposed to 0–20 mM DCA at 288 or 330 mOsM for 15 h. Embryos were then transferred to 288 mOsM medium for continued culture and developmental assessment. Our data revealed that 1 mM DCA optimally restored developmental competence, outperforming 0.5, 2, 5, 10, and 20 mM concentrations (data not shown). As shown in Fig. 9A, the rescue group with 1 mM DCA added to the 330(–) medium (15.30 ± 2.49%; n = 70, N = 4) achieved a blastocyst development rate comparable to that of the 288 medium (12.91 ± 2.37%; n = 53, N = 4) and the rescue group with 1 mM DCA added to the 330 medium (11.02 ± 3.39%; n = 68, N = 4) (P > 0.05), while being significantly higher than the 330(–) medium (3.07 ± 1.78%; n = 58, N = 4) and the 330 medium groups (1.56 ± 1.56%; n = 62, N = 4) (P < 0.05). Additionally, no significant difference in blastocyst development rate was observed between the 330(–) medium and the 330 medium groups (P > 0.05). As expected, addition of 1 mM DCA to 330(–) medium significantly suppressed hyperosmotic stress-induced increase in MMP (Fig. 9B, C) and ROS generation (Fig. 9D, E), reversed declines in H3K4me3 (Fig. 9F, G) and H3K27ac (Fig. 9H, I) modification levels, and restored all these parameters to levels comparable to the 288 medium group (Fig. 9A–I).

Discussion

Metabolic reprogramming under hyperosmotic stress triggers epigenetic dysregulation

Hyperosmotic stress triggers metabolic reprogramming in porcine PI embryos, characterized by PDK-mediated PDH inactivation and fatty acid oxidation suppression. This metabolic adaptation directly depletes H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications, resulting in impaired Z-decay and ZGA failure. Mounting evidence establishes epigenetics as a critical molecular interface connecting mitochondrial metabolism with the regulation of gene expression, thereby exerting significant influence on embryonic development and arrest [17, 18, 23, 71–74]. Functioning as molecular rheostats, dynamic fluctuations in mitochondrial metabolite concentrations modulate the activity of epigenetic regulators, thereby orchestrating the upregulation or downregulation of transcriptional programs to maintain cellular homeostasis or adapt to environmental stressors [17, 18, 23, 71–75]. This regulatory paradigm is exemplified in HeLa and Raw264.7 cells, where hyperosmotic stress triggers a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to aerobic glycolysis via PDK-mediated PDH inactivation and concomitant inhibition of fatty acid oxidation. This metabolic reprogramming is essential for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) maintenance and cell survival [14]. Crucially, while compounds such as l-glutamine and taurine support basal metabolism, their removal alone does not recapitulate hyperosmotic stress-induced arrest in this study. Instead, hyperosmotic stress directly disrupts mitochondrial–nuclear communication, depleting metabolites essential for epigenetic machinery and driving developmental failure in porcine PI embryos.

Mitochondrial PDH, rather than nuclear PDH, determines embryonic fate under hyperosmotic stress

Key TCA cycle enzymes, including PDH, undergo pyruvate-dependent nuclear translocation from mitochondria, enabling localized synthesis of acetyl-CoA within the nucleus in murine embryos during ZGA. This compartmentalization directly regulates histone acetylation modifications essential for ZGA progression. Crucially, pyruvate deprivation blocks enzyme translocation, causing loss of H3K4Ac and H3K27ac, and subsequent ZGA arrest. This regulatory mechanism appears evolutionarily conserved, with analogous phenomena documented in human embryos at the four- to eight-cell stage [22]. Moreover, nuclear PDH drives S-phase entry for cell-cycle progression [76]. Since PDK inhibits mitochondrial PDH but is absent in the nucleus [76, 77], PDK inhibition selectively enhances mitochondrial PDH activity without affecting nuclear PDH. In this study, DCA intervention, by inhibiting PDK, successfully restored hyperosmotic stress-induced reduction in H3K4me3 and H3K27ac levels, and rescued the S-phase developmental arrest in four-cell embryos. This demonstrates that mitochondrial PDH-derived acetyl-CoA or its TCA cycle metabolites participate in histone methylation and acetylation modifications, as well as embryonic development, which likely underpins metabolic–epigenetic coordination in porcine preimplantation embryos under hyperosmotic stress.

Organic osmolytes sustain embryo volume homeostasis to mitigate stress damage

Cell volume homeostasis is fundamental for mitochondrial function, redox balance, and epigenetic fidelity in early PI embryos, ultimately determining developmental competence. Organic osmolytes, such as glycine and betaine, play a pivotal role in this process by counteracting osmotic injury. For instance, glycine supplementation during stage-specific osmolarity adjustment (290 mOsM for the first 22 h and 320 mOsM thereafter) enhances porcine oocyte maturation and embryonic development by improving mitochondrial function and reducing ROS levels [78]. In vitrified porcine and mouse oocytes, glycine protects against osmotic swelling/shrinkage damage during freeze–thaw cycles [36, 79]. Moreover, betaine, a key metabolite absent in in vitro culture, enhances sheep oocyte competence by regulating osmotic pressure and promoting methylation-dependent epigenetic modification to support early embryo development [80]. In this study, without exogenous organic osmolytes, mild increases in extracellular osmolarity disrupt cell volume homeostasis, triggering mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, aberrant histone epigenetic modifications, and altered global gene expression. This culminates in developmental arrest in porcine embryos during ZGA. While glycine and betaine may concurrently serve as substrates in energy metabolism, exerting biological functions by providing energy or metabolic intermediates, their primary role here is as osmoprotectants against volume dysregulation.

Mitochondria as osmosensors: coupling volume perturbations to metabolic–epigenetic signaling

While volume-sensing mechanisms in embryos remain unclear [43], hyperosmotic stress perturbs cell membrane tension, ionic strength, and macromolecular crowding [81, 82], suggesting that intracellular osmosensors in mammalian PI embryos are likely underestimated. Our data propose that mitochondria act as primary osmosensors, detecting volume perturbations to trigger metabolic remodeling via regulation of PDH phosphorylation at S293. This phosphorylation is a key inactivation switch of PDH that uncouples glycolysis from the TCA cycle by suppressing pyruvate-to-acetyl-CoA conversion. Consequently, altered mitochondrial metabolites disrupt histone modification patterns, nuclear genome expression, mitochondrial biogenesis, and embryonic cell cycle progression.

Conclusions

Even minor increases in osmolarity within the physiological range disrupt cell volume homeostasis and impair the developmental progression of early porcine two-cell embryos beyond the four-cell stage in the absence of organic osmolytes. Compared with controls, arrested four-cell embryos exhibited profound transcriptional and metabolic alterations, characterized by incomplete Z-decay and defective ZGA, dysregulated mtDNA transcripts, reduced levels of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modifications, and diminished PDH and fatty acid β-oxidation activities, alongside elevated MMP and increased ROS levels. Supplementation with organic osmolytes such as glycine and betaine or DCA treatment to activate PDH, but not inhibitors targeting the H3K4me3 demethylase KDM5A and/or the H3K27ac deacetylase SIRT1, fully rescued these defects and restored embryonic development under hyperosmotic stress. These findings suggest that hyperosmolarity-induced cell volume dysregulation disrupts the bidirectional crosstalk between mitochondria and the nucleus, exacerbating aberrant histone epigenetic modifications, dysregulated nuclear gene and mtDNA expression, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Collectively, cell volume homeostasis is a critical prerequisite for the healthy development of mammalian PI embryos (Fig. 10).

Mechanistic model illustrating early embryonic arrest induced by cell volume dysregulation. Under conditions of organic osmolyte deficiency, mitochondrial sensors in early embryos detect extracellular hyperosmotic stress, triggering metabolic reprogramming by inhibiting the activities of PDH and fatty acid β-oxidation. This metabolic shift results in ROS accumulation, establishing a pathological cascade. The consequent elevation in ROS levels and dysregulation of metabolic intermediates disrupt mitochondrial–nuclear crosstalk, ultimately manifesting as: (i) aberrant histone modifications, particularly in H3K4me3 and H3K27ac marks; (ii) substantial transcriptomic alterations in both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes; and (iii) failure of zygotic genome activation (ZGA), collectively contributing to embryonic developmental arrest
