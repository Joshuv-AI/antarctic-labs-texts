Postpartum-specific anxiety among working mothers in relation to maternity leave perceptions: a cross-sectional study from Turkey
Authors: Melike Punduk Yilmaz, Iclal Ilknur Ozdemir, Gunay Cukurlu, Selma Dagci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPostpartum anxiety is common among working mothers, and statutory maternity-leave provisions may not align with maternal recovery needs. This study examined whether working mothers’ subjective perceptions of maternity-leave adequacy were associated with their postpartum-specific anxiety levels, beyond what would be expected from objective leave duration and demographic factors alone. A descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted with 287 employed mothers in the postpartum period (defined as up to 12 months after delivery) in Turkey between March and July 2024. Data were collected through an online questionnaire that included sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, maternity-leave perceptions, and the 47-item Turkish version of the Postpartum-Specific Anxiety Scale (PSAS), which is treated as a unidimensional measure with scores ranging from 47 to 188. Statistical analyses included chi-square tests, the Mann–Whitney U test, the Kruskal–Wallis H test, and Spearman’s rank-order correlation, with categorical anxiety classified as low (≤ 73), moderate (74–100), and high (≥ 101). The mean PSAS score was 92.10 (SD = 23.86), and 81.2% of respondents fell into the moderate or high anxiety category. Three-quarters of participants (75.3%) rated the 16-week maternity leave as insufficient, and 69.7% indicated that it was inadequate for postpartum recovery. Perceiving maternity leave as insufficient was significantly associated with higher anxiety category, χ2(4) = 10.92, p = .028, and the clearest categorical association emerged between the intention to extend leave and anxiety, χ2(4) = 21.37, p < .001. On the continuous PSAS score, sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics were largely non-significant, with the exception of gestational health problems, Mann–Whitney U = 3819.5, p = .038. The distribution of anxiety categories differed according to maternity-leave perceptions; however, the pattern was not strictly monotonic, and effect sizes (Cramer’s V) for the leave-perception items ranged from trivial to small. The clearest categorical association was observed for the intention to extend maternity leave, and findings should therefore be interpreted as suggestive of a meaningful, but bivariate and modest, link between subjective leave appraisal and postpartum anxiety. The gap between statutory provisions and lived maternal experience supports further investigation of policy and workplace measures that may extend beyond minimum leave durations, including phased return-to-work models, paid lactation breaks, and routine screening for postpartum anxiety in occupational and primary care settings.Keywords: Postpartum anxiety, Maternity leave, Working mothers, Maternal mental health, PSAS, TurkeySubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

The transition to motherhood is a critical life stage that, in contemporary economies, increasingly overlaps with sustained labour-force participation. As women’s employment rates have risen worldwide, mothers face tensions between professional responsibilities and the physical and psychological demands of the postpartum period. Maternity-leave policies have evolved from employment benefits into public-health instruments that influence maternal wellbeing, infant development, and family functioning during this sensitive window.

Postpartum anxiety affects an estimated 13% to 40% of new mothers, depending on assessment timing and instruments1,2. Unlike generalised anxiety, postpartum-specific anxiety centres on infant care, maternal competence, and newborn safety, and when excessive it can impair maternal functioning, disrupt mother–infant bonding, and compromise maternal and child outcomes3. Despite its prevalence, the relationship between maternity-leave adequacy and postpartum anxiety remains underexplored, particularly in settings where statutory provisions may not match maternal needs.

Maternity-leave policies vary substantially across national contexts. The International Labour Organization recommends a minimum of 14 weeks of paid leave4, and Turkey provides 16 weeks divided into eight weeks before and eight weeks after delivery under Social Security Law No. 55105. Shorter leave durations have been linked to elevated depressive symptoms, stress, and poorer maternal health, with a U-shaped dose–response curve reaching its minimum around six months postpartum6,7. For women in precarious employment or unsupportive workplaces, concerns about premature return and the management of work and childcare demands are particularly salient8.

Conservation of Resources (COR) theory offers a useful framework for examining why perceived inadequacy of maternity leave, rather than the objective duration alone, may predict postpartum anxiety9. COR theory posits that psychological stress arises when valued resources are threatened, lost, or insufficiently replenished after major investments. Childbirth and early infant care involve substantial depletion of physical, cognitive, and emotional resources, and the period of paid leave functions as a resource that supports recovery and replenishment. When mothers appraise the available leave as insufficient for these demands, the resulting resource imbalance is expected to produce anxiety symptoms, independent of demographic background. This perspective aligns with role-strain accounts of work–family conflict and emphasises subjective appraisal as a proximal mediator between policy provisions and mental-health outcomes.

Studies that explicitly examine working mothers’ perceptions of their leave, whether they consider it sufficient, whether its duration creates stress, and how these appraisals relate to anxiety, remain scarce, especially in non-Western and high-inflation contexts. Accordingly, the present study addresses the following research question: among employed mothers in the Turkish postpartum population, do subjective perceptions of maternity-leave adequacy and the intention to extend leave show stronger associations with postpartum-specific anxiety than standard sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics? By centring maternal appraisal within a COR-informed framework, this work aims to clarify how policy provisions translate into lived experience and mental-health outcomes, with implications for policy design, workplace practice, and clinical screening.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive, cross-sectional design was used to examine postpartum-specific anxiety among employed mothers, with particular emphasis on subjective perceptions of maternity-leave adequacy.

Study population and sample

The target population comprised employed women in the postpartum period, aged 18 years and older. The sample size was calculated a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.710. For a chi-square analysis with 90% power, an effect size of w = 0.30, alpha of 0.05, and 4 degrees of freedom, the minimum required sample size was 267. Anticipating a 10% attrition rate, 293 participants were targeted. A final sample of 287 participants provided valid responses for the primary outcome (PSAS) analysis. A small number of participants did not respond to individual items in the sociodemographic, obstetric, or leave-perception sections; these are reported as “No response” in the descriptive tables, and the corresponding analyses are based on the available cases for each variable. Convenience sampling was used, with data collected between March and July 2024 through an online survey platform.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were the following: being at least 18 years of age; being within the first 12 months after delivery (i.e., the postpartum period was operationalised as 0 to 12 months postpartum, in line with the validation window of the Turkish PSAS); having been employed in the public or private sector before childbirth; literacy in Turkish; and capacity to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were the following: psychiatric or neurological diagnoses likely to affect cognitive functioning, language barriers, and incomplete or internally inconsistent survey responses. Six participants were excluded for incomplete or inconsistent responses, yielding a final analytic sample of 287.

Data collection measures

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics form

A researcher-developed questionnaire was constructed on the basis of previous national and international literature7,11,12. The form contained two sections: ten items addressing sociodemographic factors (age, education, occupation, economic status, social-security coverage, and employment sector) and thirteen items addressing obstetric characteristics and maternity-leave perceptions (pregnancy history, complications, and views on maternity-leave adequacy and work obligations).

Postpartum-specific anxiety scale (PSAS)

The PSAS was originally developed by Fallon et al.3 to measure postpartum-specific anxiety. The original English scale contains 51 items distributed across four subscales (maternal competence and attachment, infant safety and welfare, practical infant care, and psychosocial adjustment). Duran13 validated the Turkish version and, on the basis of low factor loadings, recommended removing four items (items 1, 2, 15, and 46). The validation analyses also indicated that the four-factor structure observed in the original English version did not replicate in the Turkish data; many items loaded across different subscales. Accordingly, Duran13 recommended a 47-item unidimensional structure for the Turkish PSAS. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from Never (1) to Almost always (4), and total scores range from 47 to 188, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. Cut-off scores established in the Turkish validation were ≤ 73 (low anxiety), 74–100 (moderate anxiety), and ≥ 101 (high anxiety). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.96 in the original development study3, 0.91 in the Turkish validation13, and 0.96 in the present sample.

Data collection procedure

The survey was conducted between March and July 2024 using Google Forms. The questionnaire link was distributed through social-media channels (mother–baby communities and working-mothers’ groups) and email lists. Participants received information about the study purpose, voluntary participation, and confidentiality through an online information sheet, and they provided informed consent electronically. Survey completion required approximately 15 to 20 min. IP controls were applied to prevent duplicate responses. Six surveys were excluded for incompleteness or internal inconsistency, leaving 287 valid responses for analysis.

Two recognised sources of bias inherent in online convenience sampling were addressed. To attenuate digital-literacy bias, the link was distributed across multiple platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, and Telegram groups) and forwarded through workplace and professional networks reaching mothers in non-academic occupations, with response times indicating that the survey was completed by participants with heterogeneous digital fluency. To mitigate self-selection bias, the recruitment text used neutral wording about employed mothers’ experiences during the postpartum period rather than emphasising anxiety or dissatisfaction. Despite these procedures, residual selection toward digitally connected and relatively educated mothers cannot be excluded, and this is acknowledged in the Limitations section.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0. Normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test together with skewness and kurtosis statistics. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges) were computed. Chi-square (χ2) tests examined associations between categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) and the Kruskal–Wallis H test (three or more groups). Spearman’s rank-order correlation assessed monotonic associations between continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p <.05 with 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was granted by the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No.: E-10840098-202.3.02–1626; Date: 26 February 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Online informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data were stored on password-protected computers accessible only to the research team. Permission to use the Turkish version of the PSAS was obtained from Dr. Duran via email correspondence.

Results

The study included 287 employed mothers in the postpartum period. The mean age was 29.39 years (SD = 5.11; range 19–47). Regarding educational attainment, 28.9% held an associate degree, 28.2% had completed high school, 27.2% held a bachelor’s degree, 9.4% had completed primary or secondary school, and 6.3% held a postgraduate degree.

On economic status, 44.6% of participants reported income matching expenditure, 43.9% reported income exceeding expenditure, and 11.5% reported income below expenditure. The vast majority of participants (98.3%) held social-security coverage. Concerning employment, 70.7% worked in the private sector, 25.4% in the public sector, and 3.8% in other arrangements (e.g., self-employed, home-based work) (see Table 1).

Regarding maternity-leave perceptions, 75.3% of participants considered the postpartum maternity leave to be insufficient, 16.4% were undecided, and 8.0% considered it adequate. When asked whether the leave duration was sufficient for postpartum recovery, 69.7% disagreed, 16.7% were undecided, and 12.9% considered it sufficient.

Restricted leave was perceived as a source of personal stress by 63.4% of participants; 21.6% were undecided, and 15.0% disagreed. Half of the participants (50.9%) indicated that they were considering extending their maternity leave, 35.5% were undecided, and 13.2% were not. With regard to work obligation, recorded dichotomously in the dataset, 63.8% of participants reported feeling obliged to work, and 36.2% did not. Associations between sociodemographic and obstetric variables and anxiety were examined at two complementary levels: the categorical anxiety classification (low/moderate/high) with chi-square tests, and the continuous PSAS score with Mann–Whitney U tests, Kruskal–Wallis H tests, and Spearman’s rank-order correlation. In the categorical comparisons, statistically significant associations were detected for age group, χ2(6) = 14.48, p =.025; educational level, χ2(6) = 16.69, p =.011; economic status, χ2(4) = 12.59, p =.014; and employment sector, χ2(4) = 12.83, p =.012. However, several cells had low expected frequencies (minimum 2.07 for sector), and the Kruskal–Wallis tests on the continuous PSAS score did not yield significant differences for any of these variables (age, H = 5.49, p =.139; education, H = 3.61, p =.307; economic status, H = 4.77, p =.092; sector, H = 4.02, p =.134). This pattern suggests that the categorical significance was driven in part by sparse cells rather than by genuine group differences in the underlying continuous score.

The number of births showed a borderline but non-significant association with anxiety category, χ2(6) = 10.93, p =.091, and the continuous comparison was likewise non-significant, Spearman’s ρ = 0.05, p =.415. No significant associations were observed between PSAS score and pregestational health problems, U = 3052.0, p =.575; planned pregnancy, U = 6554.0, p =.262; or feeling obliged to work, U = 9598.5, p =.903. By contrast, participants who experienced gestational health problems had significantly higher PSAS scores than those who did not, U = 3819.5, p =.038 (medians 95.0 vs. 87.0). The significant difference for social-security coverage, U = 1108.0, p =.029, should be interpreted with caution given highly unbalanced group sizes (Yes: n = 282; No: n = 5). Spearman’s correlations between the PSAS score and age, number of pregnancies, number of births, and number of miscarriages were weak and non-significant (ρ = −0.04, 0.06, 0.05, and 0.06, respectively; all ps > 0.28) (see Table 6).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between working mothers’ maternity-leave perceptions and postpartum-specific anxiety in a Turkish sample. The majority of participants (81.2%) fell into the moderate or high anxiety category, with a mean PSAS score (M = 92.10) indicating substantial distress. These elevated anxiety levels co-occurred with widespread perceptions that the statutory 16-week maternity leave was insufficient (75.3%) and inadequate for postpartum recovery (69.7%); however, the categorical association between leave-insufficiency perception and anxiety was not strictly monotonic across response options, and the corresponding effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.138) is in the small range, so the relationship should be interpreted cautiously and only as a bivariate categorical signal.

Recent evidence from Sweden by Heshmati et al15., using national registry data from more than 210,000 first-time mothers, has shown that more generous parental-leave benefits are associated with reduced maternal postpartum mental-health problems even after preconception mental health and labour-market attachment are accounted for. A systematic review by the same group16 similarly concluded that more generous parental-leave policies are associated with improved maternal mental-health outcomes across high-income countries. These findings align with the present observation that mothers who perceived their leave as insufficient reported higher anxiety levels.

The high prevalence of distress in this sample exceeds rates reported in recent international syntheses. Dennis et al1. found pooled postpartum anxiety prevalence ranging from 13% to 40%, depending on assessment timing and instruments. The elevated rates observed in this study may reflect the specific pressures faced by working mothers managing return-to-work concerns during a high-inflation, high-cost-of-living period in Turkey. Zanardo et al17. likewise reported that employment-related issues intensified anxiety symptoms in Italian mothers, indicating that work–recovery tensions are widespread and that their expression is shaped by local policy environments and workplace cultures.

International validations of the PSAS provide a useful benchmark for the present mean, although direct comparisons require methodological caution because the 47-item Turkish form13 cannot be compared on raw total scores with the 51-item English3, Spanish18, and Italian19 versions. To enable a fair cross-version benchmark, the present sample mean is most appropriately expressed as a mean item score (total/number of items) or as a percentage of the maximum possible score on the same scale. The mean item score in the present sample was 1.96 (on the 1–4 response scale), and the mean total represented approximately 49.0% of the maximum possible score (92.10/188). When standardised in this way, the present sample appears in the higher end of the distributions reported in available international PSAS validations, suggesting that working mothers in Turkey experience comparatively elevated postpartum-specific anxiety, although the absence of standardised cross-sample comparisons in the original validation papers means that this benchmark should be regarded as approximate. The Turkish validation by Duran13 further documented that the unidimensional 47-item structure performs reliably in this population, supporting the use of total scores rather than subscale scores for within-sample analyses.

The observation that 75.3% of participants considered 16 weeks of maternity leave insufficient, despite Turkey exceeding the ILO minimum recommendation4, challenges assumptions about what constitutes adequate leave. Aitken et al6. reported progressive maternal mental-health benefits with longer leave duration, with substantial gains observed beyond most statutory thresholds. Consistent with this, Dagher et al7. identified a U-shaped relationship between leave duration and postpartum depressive symptoms, with the minimum at six months postpartum, indicating that 12- and 16-week leave durations may leave many mothers exposed to elevated symptom levels at the point of return to work.

Hecht et al20., drawing on Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) data from New York City and State, reported that unpaid leave was associated with significantly higher odds of postpartum depressive symptoms compared with at least some paid leave, adjusted OR = 1.41, 95% CI [1.04, 1.93], after adjusting for leave duration and selected covariates. Their findings highlight that not only duration but also payment status and perceived adequacy contribute to mental-health outcomes, an observation that resonates with our finding that subjective perceptions of insufficiency correlated with anxiety independently of demographic characteristics.

Interpreted through Conservation of Resources theory9, the bivariate categorical association between perceived leave insufficiency and anxiety, χ2(4) = 10.92, p =.028, indicates that when the principal resource for postpartum recovery (paid time away from work) is appraised as inadequate, mothers experience the resource-loss spiral that COR theory predicts will yield psychological strain. The markedly significant association between the intention to extend leave and anxiety, χ2(4) = 21.37, p <.001, can be read as a behavioural expression of attempted resource protection: mothers who recognise unmet recovery needs seek to acquire additional resource (time). Yan and Xu21 similarly demonstrated that perceived social support, a subjective appraisal, was a stronger predictor of postpartum anxiety than objective measures, with self-esteem mediating this association. Yet individual cognition should not be over-interpreted in isolation: 82.6% of participants reported awareness of at least one person in their social environment who had been compelled to leave employment because of insufficient leave, indicating that subjective perceptions reflect tangible social and economic consequences.

Courtin et al22., exploiting a 1984 Danish policy reform that extended paid parental leave from 14 to 20 weeks, used a regression-discontinuity design on national registry data to show that the additional six weeks reduced the long-term risk of psychiatric diagnoses among mothers. Their findings reinforce the principle that incremental extensions of leave beyond minimum statutory requirements may yield measurable mental-health benefits.

The lack of a significant association between feeling obliged to work and the anxiety category (p =.670) was, on first inspection, unexpected. Contextual factors specific to Turkey during the study period merit consideration. Annual consumer-price inflation in Turkey was officially recorded at 53.9% in 2023 and 44.4% in December 202423, with food inflation considerably higher; in this environment, employment is functionally non-discretionary for the majority of mothers, and a high prevalence of work-obligation endorsement (63.8%) likely compresses between-group variance. Meeussen and Van Laar24reported that perceived obligation interacts with career commitment, financial necessity, and workplace flexibility, and that obligation produces distress mainly when it conflicts with salient values; in a high-inflation context with limited household alternatives, the perceived obligation may be normalised rather than experienced as conflict. Hidalgo-Padilla et al25., in a systematic review of 23 studies on maternity-leave policies and postpartum depression, similarly observed that the relationship between employment-related attitudes and mental health is moderated by workplace support, financial security during leave, and cultural norms around maternal employment.

The absence of significant associations on the continuous PSAS score for principal sociodemographic and obstetric variables warrants careful interpretation. Although chi-square tests on the categorical anxiety classification reached significance for age, education, economic status, and employment sector, these results were not corroborated by Kruskal–Wallis tests on the continuous score and were partly driven by sparse cells. The exception was gestational health problems, which were significantly associated with higher PSAS scores. The salience of maternity-leave concerns in this sample therefore appears to outweigh several other demographic and obstetric influences, suggesting a relatively universal vulnerability across demographic groups and structural challenges for working mothers even when individual circumstances are otherwise favourable.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationship between maternity-leave perceptions and anxiety; longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal dynamics. Second, online data collection and convenience sampling may introduce digital-literacy and self-selection biases, despite mitigation procedures that included multi-platform distribution and neutral recruitment language. Third, focusing exclusively on anxiety leaves depression and other mental-health conditions unmeasured, providing a partial picture of maternal mental health. Fourth, several unmeasured confounders may have influenced the observed associations, including objective workplace support, childcare arrangements, partner involvement, and maternal–infant temperament; infant temperament in particular has been associated with maternal anxiety in earlier work and should be incorporated into future designs. Fifth, all data were self-reported and may be subject to recall and social-desirability biases. Finally, the Turkish PSAS is treated as unidimensional, which precludes subscale-level inferences and may obscure heterogeneous symptom profiles.

Implications for practice and policy

The findings have implications for several stakeholders. For policymakers, the observation that 75.3% of participants considered 16 weeks of leave insufficient and that 50.9% indicated a wish to extend it suggests that a graduated reform path may be more acceptable and feasible than a single large extension. Specifically, a phased return-to-work model could be considered, in which the statutory 16 weeks of fully paid leave is followed by a four-month transitional phase featuring reduced working hours (for example, a 50% schedule that increases incrementally to 75% and then to full hours over successive months), guaranteed remote-work options where role characteristics permit, protected daily breastfeeding and pumping breaks, prohibition of business travel and overtime during the transitional phase, and a guaranteed right to return to the same or an equivalent position with no penalty to seniority or compensation. Such a model is consistent with the dose–response findings of Aitken et al6., Dagher et al7., and Courtin et al22. and aligns with COR theory in providing graduated resource replenishment rather than an abrupt return to full work demands.

For employers, the findings argue for genuinely supportive workplace cultures, including phased return-to-work agreements, manager training on perinatal mental health, accessible employee assistance programmes, and dedicated lactation facilities. For healthcare providers, systematic postpartum anxiety screening (using validated instruments such as the PSAS or the PSAS Research Short-Form) should be implemented during the routine postpartum care pathway and during occupational-health consultations at the time of return to work. Mental-health professionals should develop interventions that address both individual coping strategies and the structural limitations within which mothers operate, validating maternal experiences while advocating for system-level change.

Conclusion

Among employed mothers in the Turkish postpartum population, the distribution of postpartum-specific anxiety categories differed according to subjective perceptions of maternity-leave adequacy, with 81.2% of participants in the moderate or high anxiety categories and 75.3% rating the 16-week leave as insufficient. The clearest categorical association was observed for the intention to extend maternity leave, although the underlying response-level patterns were not strictly monotonic and effect sizes for the leave-perception items were in the trivial-to-small range. Findings should therefore be read as suggestive of a modest, bivariate link between maternal appraisal of leave adequacy and anxiety classification rather than as evidence of a strong, gradient association. Even within these analytical limits, the gap between statutory provisions and lived maternal experience signals a potential misalignment between current policy and maternal recovery needs and supports further evaluation of policy and workplace measures, including phased return-to-work models, paid lactation breaks, and routine postpartum anxiety screening. Failure to address maternal perceptions of leave adequacy may carry long-term public-health costs, including persistent maternal mental-health morbidity, compromised mother–infant bonding, attenuated long-term labour-force participation, and intergenerational transmission of mental-health risk; conversely, modest, evidence-based reforms aligned with maternal appraisal may yield measurable gains in maternal, infant, and family wellbeing. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs, adjusted regression modelling with relevant covariates, qualitative inquiry into mothers’ and families’ perspectives on leave adequacy, and evaluate the impact of phased return-to-work and policy-extension interventions on postpartum anxiety trajectories.
