An intelligent OCR-LLM prescription review system for inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drugs
Authors: Rong Ma, Kai Xu, Mingming Zhang, Xiangpeng Li, Xingrong Ma, Junyu Wang, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to construct an intelligent prescription review system for inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drugs based on deep learning-based optical character recognition (OCR) and a local large language model (LLM) and evaluate its review performance and closed-loop management value. This study adopted a combined system development and retrospective validation design. Prescription images were first processed using OCR for text recognition and layout reconstruction. A local LLM was then applied to convert unstructured prescription text into standardized JavaScript Object Notation-formatted data. Subsequently, a locally developed rule base for narcotic and psychotropic drugs was used to evaluate prescription completeness, diagnosis-based dosage limits, medication instructions, physician signatures, and residual drug handling for injectable formulations. Using pharmacists’ manual review results as the reference standard, 500 inpatient prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs were retrospectively included. System performance was evaluated in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, F1 score, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient. The system successfully implemented prescription image recognition, text structuring, rule-based validation, signature verification, residual drug calculation, and triggers for manual pharmacist review. In the retrospective validation, compared with pharmacists’ manual review, the system identified 144 true positives, 16 false positives, 334 true negatives, and 6 false negatives. The overall accuracy was 95.6%, sensitivity was 96.0%, specificity was 95.4%, positive predictive value was 90.0%, negative predictive value was 98.2%, F1 score was 0.929, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient was 0.897. The proposed OCR-LLM-based intelligent prescription review system for inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drugs demonstrates high accuracy and strong consistency. It can serve as a front-end screening and decision-support tool for pharmacists and provides technical support for standardized, traceable, and closed-loop management of specially controlled medications.

1. Introduction

Narcotic drugs and psychotropic drugs are essential agents in clinical analgesic therapy; however, they are associated with risks of addiction and dependence. Therefore, the prescription management of such drugs is not only critical for patient medication safety but also closely related to the full-process quality control of specially regulated medications within healthcare institutions.[1] In inpatient settings, prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs must simultaneously meet requirements for complete patient identification, clear diagnosis, compliant dosage, traceable physician signatures, and verifiable residual quantities of injectable formulations. Any missing or incomplete documentation may increase management risks.[2,3]

At present, prescription review of narcotic and psychotropic drugs in inpatient pharmacies still largely relies on manual assessment by pharmacists.[4,5] Although manual review offers professional flexibility, it is susceptible to variability in experience, workload pressure, and fatigue. In the context of increasing prescription volume, nonstandardized paper prescription formats, difficulty in standardizing handwritten signatures, and complex review rules, these factors may reduce review efficiency and increase the risk of missed detections.[6–10]

Clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) have been widely applied to reduce prescribing errors and medication-related adverse drug events.[11] Previous studies have shown that the implementation of computerized physician order entry (CPOE) and CDSS systems can reduce inpatient medication errors or preventable adverse drug events; however, substantial heterogeneity exists among studies. Moreover, electronic prescribing systems themselves may introduce new issues, such as data entry errors, duplicate prescriptions, technology-induced errors, and alert fatigue.[10,12] In addition, drug–drug interaction alerts are frequently overridden or ignored in CPOE/CDSS systems, and evidence regarding their improvement of clinically meaningful patient outcomes remains limited.[13,14] Therefore, intelligent systems for prescription review of narcotic and psychotropic drugs should not only possess robust risk detection capabilities but also emphasize rule interpretability, false-positive control, human-in-the-loop verification, and closed-loop traceability.

In recent years, deep learning-based optical character recognition (OCR) technology has enabled automated extraction of textual information from paper prescriptions, providing machine-readable text together with confidence scores and spatial coordinates for subsequent processing.[15] However, inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drug prescriptions often contain complex layouts, handwritten signatures, and irregular formatting, making accurate structured information extraction challenging. Previous studies have shown that incorporating layout-aware information and spatial relationships can improve document understanding and structured extraction from complex medical documents.[8,9] More recently, large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in structured information extraction and clinical text understanding.[16,17] Nevertheless, concerns regarding hallucination, inconsistent outputs, and limited generalizability indicate that LLMs should function as assistive tools rather than fully autonomous decision-makers.[18,19] Therefore, integrating OCR, LLM-based structured parsing, deterministic rule-based reasoning, and pharmacist verification may provide a more reliable strategy for intelligent prescription review.

Based on these considerations and the clinical needs of inpatient narcotic and psychotropic prescription review, this study developed an intelligent review system integrating OCR-based image recognition, prescription layout reconstruction, local LLM-based structured parsing, pharmacological rule-based reasoning, physician signature verification, and residual calculation for injectable formulations. The system adopts a human–machine collaborative workflow consisting of “image recognition – layout reconstruction – structured parsing – rule-based review – manual review triggering – closed-loop traceability.” A retrospective validation was conducted using 500 real inpatient prescriptions to evaluate the system’s performance in prescription compliance checking and pharmacist-assisted decision-making. We hypothesized that the proposed hybrid framework would achieve high agreement with pharmacist review while maintaining high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for identifying prescription noncompliance, thereby providing a practical and reliable solution for intelligent prescription management in healthcare institutions.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and prescription sources

This study was a single-center investigation combining system development and retrospective validation. In the system development phase, an intelligent prescription review workflow for inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drugs was established, comprising functional modules including prescription image recognition, text layout reconstruction, structured field extraction, rule-based validation, physician signature verification, injectable residual volume calculation, and manual review triggering. In the validation phase, real-world prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs archived in the inpatient pharmacy of a tertiary hospital were retrospectively collected. Pharmacists’ manual review results were used as the reference standard to evaluate the system’s performance in prescription compliance assessment and risk identification.

A total of 500 consecutive prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs were retrospectively collected during the study period and included in the validation set. These prescriptions were derived from paper prescriptions, scanned documents, or image-based prescription records generated during the use of narcotic drugs, first-class psychotropic drugs, or second-class psychotropic drugs in hospitalized patients. All prescription images were processed within the hospital’s local network environment for recognition and review. Prior to data analysis and result export, all data were de-identified, and directly identifiable patient information, including names, inpatient numbers, and identification numbers, was removed or masked. The sample size represented all eligible prescriptions collected during the study period and was therefore not determined by a formal sample size calculation.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: prescriptions for narcotic drugs or psychotropic drugs for inpatient use; prescription images with sufficient quality for basic text recognition; prescriptions containing essential reviewable information, such as drug name, specification, dosage, quantity, route of administration, or frequency; and availability of pharmacist manual review results as the final adjudication. The exclusion criteria were as follows: images that were severely blurred, incomplete, or lacking key fields necessary for interpretation; prescriptions not involving narcotic or psychotropic drugs; duplicate prescriptions; and prescriptions without documented pharmacist review or a determinable final review outcome.

2.2. Reference standard establishment and manual review

Pharmacists’ manual review results were used as the reference standard for evaluating system performance. The content of the manual review included completeness of the prescription header and footer information, patient identity information, clinical diagnosis, drug name and specification, prescribed quantity, route of administration, dosing frequency, duration of medication, physician signature, potential inappropriate drug combinations, and documentation of residual volumes of injectable formulations.

Each prescription was ultimately classified as either “compliant” or “noncompliant.” A prescription was defined as “noncompliant” if any of the following issues were present: missing critical information, abnormal identification number format, mismatch between diagnosis and prescribing limits, inappropriate dosing frequency for sustained- or controlled-release formulations, overdosing beyond regulatory limits, potentially inappropriate drug combinations, abnormal physician signature, or incomplete documentation of injectable residual drug tracking. Prescriptions without any identifiable issues during manual review were classified as “compliant.” In cases of disagreement during manual review, a senior pharmacist adjudicated the final classification based on institutional policies for narcotic and psychotropic drug management and prescription review standards, thereby establishing the final reference standard.

2.3. System architecture and workflow

The system was deployed using a hospital-based local private architecture and consisted of 5 main modules: prescription image input, OCR-based recognition and layout reconstruction, local LLM-based structured parsing, narcotic and psychotropic drug rule-based validation, and review report output.[20] The prescription image input module was responsible for receiving scanned or photographed prescription images. The OCR module, implemented using PaddleOCR (an open-source OCR toolkit), performed text detection, text recognition, confidence score estimation, and extraction of bounding box coordinates. It was encapsulated as an OCR microservice via FastAPI. The layout reconstruction module restored spatial relationships among prescription fields based on coordinate information. The local LLM module converted OCR-generated text into standardized JavaScript Object Notation (JSON)-formatted data. The complete prompt template used for structured information extraction and the corresponding JSON Schema (JSON structure validation) defining the expected output are provided in Appendix S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. The rule-based validation module compared structured fields against a locally constructed rule base for narcotic and psychotropic drugs and generated structured review reports.

The structured information extraction module was implemented using a locally deployed Qwen3.5-9B (Alibaba Group Specific, Tongyi Lab) LLM running on the Ollama (Ollama Inc.) inference framework in a Windows-based environment. The model was quantized using the Q4_K_M format. During inference, the temperature was set to 0.1, while top-p and top-k sampling were disabled to improve the determinism of structured information extraction. The system was deployed on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5080 GPU (16 GB VRAM; NVIDIA Corporation, ASUS, PNY, Gainward, INNO3D, and other licensed add‑in board partners). All inference was performed entirely within the hospital’s local network without using external cloud-based application programming interfaces (APIs), thereby protecting patient privacy and ensuring secure local deployment.

The system workflow proceeded as follows: after prescription image upload or import, the system sequentially performed OCR recognition, spatial layout reconstruction, LLM-based field extraction, JSON schema validation, and rule-base verification, and finally generated a review outcome of “pass,” “fail,” or “requires manual review.” All prescription images, OCR outputs, and LLM-generated results were processed within the hospital’s internal network environment without reliance on external public LLM APIs, thereby reducing the risks of patient privacy leakage and external transmission of sensitive healthcare data.

2.4. OCR recognition, layout reconstruction, and LLM-based structured parsing

Paper-based prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs are often characterized by dispersed fields, left–right column layouts, table-line interference, scanning angle deviations, cross-line medication entries, and mixtures of printed text with handwritten signatures. To reduce field misalignment caused by the default reading order of OCR outputs, a spatial layout reconstruction method was introduced after OCR processing. The system extracted recognized text content, confidence scores, and bounding box coordinates for each text instance. A 2-dimensional sorting strategy was applied based on vertical and horizontal coordinates. Adjacent text boxes were evaluated for row grouping according to vertical coordinate differences; when the vertical distance exceeded a predefined threshold, a line break was automatically inserted. If the average OCR confidence was below 0.75, the prescription was excluded from the fully automated workflow and instead flagged for “manual review.” The threshold of 0.75 was determined empirically during pilot system development based on repeated observations of OCR recognition performance. Prescriptions with an average OCR confidence below this threshold were found to have an increased likelihood of recognition errors that could adversely affect downstream structured information extraction. Therefore, these prescriptions were automatically routed for manual review to achieve a practical balance between automation efficiency and review reliability.

The local LLM received the OCR-recognized and layout-reconstructed prescription text and extracted structured fields under a predefined prompt constraint. Extracted fields included prescription number, department, inpatient number, patient age, sex, identification number, clinical diagnosis, drug name, specification, quantity, single dose, route of administration, dosing frequency, physician signature, and prescription date. The output was standardized into a JSON format. To mitigate the risk of model hallucination, the prompt explicitly required the model to extract information strictly based on content explicitly present in the OCR text. The model was prohibited from inferring or fabricating patient information, diagnoses, dosages, or review conclusions. Fields that could not be confidently identified were output as “unrecognized” or “pending manual confirmation.” The final review decision was determined by the local rule base and pharmacist verification, rather than being independently generated by the LLM. Sensitive information, including local server paths, account information, hospital-specific interfaces, physician signature databases, and identifiable prescription data, was removed before disclosure.

2.5. Narcotic and psychotropic drug rule base and manual review triggering

In this study, a local rule base for narcotic and psychotropic drug review was constructed based on national regulations for the management of narcotic drugs and psychotropic drugs, institutional policies for controlled drug management, prescription review standards, and clinical pharmacy expertise. The rule base mainly included completeness review of prescription header and footer information, including department, inpatient number, patient demographic information, identification number, diagnosis, prescription date, and physician signature; diagnosis-based dosage limit review, in which prescription quantities were assessed according to clinical scenarios such as general inpatient use, discharge medication, cancer pain, or malignant tumor-related conditions; medication regimen and dosage review, focusing on consistency among dosage form, specification, single dose, dosing frequency, and duration of therapy; potential inappropriate drug combination review, including risks of duplicate analgesia, overlapping sedation, or irrational combination therapy; physician signature verification based on signature similarity, physician identification number, and authorization status to assess prescription traceability; and residual volume calculation for injectable formulations based on drug specification, prescribed quantity, and actual usage to estimate residual or discarded medication volume.

When any of the following conditions occurred – low OCR confidence, missing or conflicting key fields, JSON structuring failure, a mismatch between diagnosis and dosage limit rules, unreliable physician signature recognition, inability to calculate injectable residual volume, potential inappropriate drug combination risks, or scenarios not covered by the rule base – the system automatically triggered manual review. Therefore, the system was positioned as a prescreening and decision-support tool prior to pharmacist review, and all prescriptions classified as “fail” or “requires manual review” were ultimately adjudicated by pharmacists.

2.6. System performance evaluation and statistical analysis

Using pharmacists’ manual review results as the reference standard, system outputs were categorized as true positives, false positives, true negatives, and false negatives. True positives were defined as prescriptions judged as noncompliant by both manual review and the system. False positives were defined as prescriptions judged as compliant by manual review but noncompliant by the system. True negatives were defined as prescriptions judged as compliant by both manual review and the system. False negatives were defined as prescriptions judged as noncompliant by manual review but compliant by the system.

Evaluation metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, F1 score, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient (Cohen’s kappa). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp.) or R software (The R Foundation and the R Development Core Team, Ross Ihaka and Robert Gentleman, University of Auckland, New Zealand). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Cohen’s kappa was used to assess agreement between system outputs and manual review results. As this study was a single-system retrospective validation, no control group for review time comparison was established; therefore, no statistical comparison of review time between manual and system-based review was performed.

2.7. Data security, ethics, and exploratory comparison

The prescription data used in this study were derived from historical records of narcotic and psychotropic drug management and prescription review in the hospital, without any intervention in patient treatment. All prescription images and structured data were de-identified prior to analysis, and no directly identifiable patient information was included. The system was deployed within the hospital’s internal network, and prescription images, OCR text, and LLM outputs were not transmitted to external public network platforms. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (Approval No. QYFY WZLL 50407). Because this retrospective study used anonymized historical prescription review records and involved no patient intervention, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

To explore the feasibility of the OCR-LLM system compared with a traditional rule-based engine, an exploratory comparative analysis was conducted using the same set of 500 prescriptions. The traditional rule engine performed prescription review based on predefined keyword matching, fixed field rules, and simple deterministic logic. In contrast, the OCR-LLM system performed review through OCR-based layout reconstruction, LLM-based structured parsing, and a local rule base. Comparison metrics included diagnostic extraction accuracy, correctness of medication regimen compliance assessment, average system processing time, and reduction rate in pharmacist manual review workload. This analysis was exploratory in nature and was not considered a primary endpoint of the study.

3. Results

3.1. Construction of the intelligent review system and implementation of a closed-loop workflow

This study successfully developed an intelligent prescription review system for inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drugs based on deep learning-based OCR and a local LLM. The system implemented a complete closed-loop workflow, including prescription image upload, OCR-based recognition, confidence assessment, spatial layout reconstruction, LLM-based structured parsing, JSON schema validation, narcotic and psychotropic drug rule-based validation, review report generation, manual review triggering, and prescription intervention feedback.

The system first received prescription images and invoked a PaddleOCR microservice encapsulated via FastAPI to perform text recognition and confidence estimation. When the average OCR confidence reached a predefined threshold, the system proceeded to spatial text reconstruction and LLM-based structured parsing. When OCR confidence was insufficient, the prescription was directly labeled as a “low-quality image” and routed to the manual review queue. Subsequently, the system performed JSON schema validation on the LLM output. If structured parsing failed, 1 retry with an optimized prompt was allowed; if parsing still failed, the output was marked as a structural parsing failure and sent to manual review. Structured prescription information that passed validation was then processed by the local narcotic and psychotropic drug rule-based validation module, which generated a structured review outcome of “pass,” “fail,” or “requires review.” For prescriptions classified as “fail” or “requires review,” pharmacists could communicate with prescribing physicians and provide revision suggestions. After physician confirmation, prescriptions could be updated and resubmitted for review. The complete system workflow is shown in Figure 1.

3.2. Implementation of functional modules and structured output

The system was capable of extracting prescription-level information, patient demographics, diagnostic information, and medication items from inpatient narcotic and psychotropic prescription images and converting them into standardized JSON data for subsequent rule-based validation. Through spatial layout reconstruction, the correspondence among drug name, specification, quantity, dosage, route of administration, and dosing frequency was effectively restored, thereby reducing the risk of field misalignment caused by complex prescription layouts. Based on the embedded rule base, the system performed validation of prescription header completeness, identification number format correctness, consistency between diagnosis and dosage limits, dosing frequency appropriateness for sustained- or controlled-release formulations, potentially inappropriate analgesic combinations, physician signature validity, and residual volume assessment for injectable formulations and generated structured review outputs. For injectable drugs, the system further calculated residual or discarded volumes, providing documentation support for empty ampoule recovery, residual drug disposal, and traceability of specially regulated medications. Prescriptions with low OCR confidence, missing fields, structured parsing failure, unreliable signature verification, or conditions not covered by the rule base were automatically flagged for manual review. A qualitative review of the misclassified cases indicated that most errors occurred during the early stage of system development and were primarily attributable to failures in LLM-based structured parsing rather than OCR recognition. In these cases, the OCR module correctly recognized the prescription text, whereas the LLM failed to accurately organize the extracted information into the predefined structured format, leading to subsequent rule-based validation errors. These issues were substantially reduced through iterative prompt optimization and parameter tuning. Owing to hardware limitations of the local deployment environment, a larger-parameter LLM could not be adopted, and system performance was primarily improved through prompt engineering and inference parameter optimization.

In terms of processing time, the traditional rule-based engine required an average of 1.2 seconds per prescription, whereas the OCR-LLM system required 3.5 seconds. The increased processing time of the proposed system was likely attributable to additional computational steps, including prescription image recognition, spatial layout reconstruction, LLM-based structured parsing, and rule-based reasoning. 4. Discussion

Overall, this study developed an intelligent system for inpatient prescription review of narcotic and psychotropic drugs and conducted an initial validation using real-world prescription data. Compared with conventional prescription review systems, the proposed system is not limited to OCR recognition or LLM-based question answering. Instead, it is designed around the key risk points in narcotic and psychotropic drug management, establishing an end-to-end closed-loop workflow covering prescription image recognition, text structuring, rule-based validation, signature verification, residual volume calculation, and manual review. The retrospective validation demonstrated an overall accuracy of 95.6% and a high level of agreement with pharmacists’ manual review, suggesting its potential as a front-end screening tool to support pharmacists’ decision-making.

Prescription review for narcotic and psychotropic drugs differs fundamentally from general rational drug use evaluation. In addition to drug name, dosage, and frequency, it involves requirements related to patient identification, diagnostic justification, quantity limits, prescription completeness, traceability of physician signatures, and documentation of residual volumes for injectable formulations. Accordingly, it encompasses not only medication safety but also regulatory compliance and administrative control. Previous studies have shown that CPOE systems and CDSSs can reduce prescription-related risks to some extent. However, traditional rule-based systems still face limitations, including insufficient rule coverage, high false-positive rates, alert fatigue, and limited adaptability to complex clinical scenarios.[21] A study by Lee et al on opioid prescriptions at pediatric discharge also demonstrated that prescribing or administration documentation errors in controlled medications are not uncommon.[3] These findings indicate that prescription review for narcotic and psychotropic drugs requires a more stringent and robust verification framework than general prescription screening.

CPOE systems and CDSSs have been widely implemented to reduce medication errors. An Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality rapid review reported that medication-related CDSSs have moderate-strength evidence for reducing medication errors and low-strength evidence for preventing adverse drug events; however, substantial heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of clinical settings, CDSS types, and outcome measures.[21] At the same time, a prominent limitation of traditional CDSSs is alert fatigue and a high rate of false-positive alerts. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Felisberto et al demonstrated that drug–drug interaction alerts are frequently overridden or ignored by clinicians, indicating that rule-based pop-up alerts alone are insufficient to ensure clinical safety.[13] Similarly, Olakotan and Yusof reported that inappropriate or low-value alerts may disrupt clinical workflow, leading healthcare professionals to ignore, disable, or bypass these notifications.[22] These findings suggest that intelligent prescription review systems should not only focus on detecting potential problems but also reduce low-value alerts, improve interpretability, and appropriately escalate uncertain cases for pharmacist verification.

The results of this study are consistent with the above-mentioned literature. The system generated 16 false-positive cases and 6 false-negative cases, indicating that automated tools cannot fully replace human judgment in the review of high-risk medication prescriptions. The false positives may be attributed to unstable recognition of prescription field positions by OCR, overly sensitive triggering of diagnostic keywords, conservative thresholds in signature similarity matching, or insufficient coverage of special prescribing scenarios in the rule base. The false negatives may be associated with image quality issues, deviations in drug name recognition, complex expressions of dosage regimens, or incomplete structuring of risk-related information within prescriptions. Because all prescriptions included in this study followed a standardized printed format, failures related to highly heterogeneous document layouts or handwritten prescriptions were uncommon. In line with previous CDSS studies, improperly managed false positives may increase pharmacists’ review workload and contribute to alert fatigue, whereas false negatives are directly associated with missed detection of high-risk prescriptions. Therefore, system optimization should not rely solely on overall accuracy metrics; instead, it is necessary to further perform a stratified analysis of misclassification causes and adjust rule thresholds and review strategies according to clinical risk levels.

Information extraction from paper-based or image-based prescriptions is a key technical component that distinguishes the present study from traditional electronic order-based CDSS systems. In inpatient settings, prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs often exhibit variations in layout, inconsistent scanning quality, misaligned fields, interference from table lines, and mixed handwritten signatures. In recent years, document intelligence research has increasingly emphasized “layout-aware” modeling rather than relying solely on plain text recognition. Singh and Middleton demonstrated that, in tabular documents, combining OCR, spatial structure recognition, and post-processing error correction can significantly improve the structural reconstruction of complex formatted records.[8] Tu and Hsu also highlighted that, due to the diversity of layouts and formats in clinical diagnostic documents, automated information extraction must jointly consider textual content and document spatial structure.[9] In the present study, text box coordinate sorting and layout-aware spatial reconstruction were introduced after OCR processing to address field misalignment issues commonly observed in paper prescriptions.

The application of LLMs in medical text understanding, information extraction, and clinical decision support is rapidly expanding; however, their role in pharmaceutical safety contexts must be carefully defined. Sahoo et al summarized the opportunities and risks of biomedical LLMs, noting their strong potential in medical natural language processing while also emphasizing persistent challenges, including hallucinations, limited interpretability, data bias, and insufficient clinical validation.[19] Although LLM-based CDSSs can function as a “copilot” for pharmacists and improve the detection of serious medication-related problems, LLMs alone should not be regarded as independent and fully reliable prescription screening agents.[16] A systematic review by Liu et al on retrieval-augmented generation-based LLMs further indicated that retrieval enhancement can improve performance in medical applications; however, such systems still require validation in specific clinical scenarios and deep integration with electronic medical records or local knowledge bases.[23] Accordingly, in this study, the LLM was not used to directly generate final prescription review decisions. Instead, it was constrained to structured parsing of OCR-extracted text, while the final judgment was made by a local rule base and pharmacist review. This design better aligns with the safety boundaries required for high-risk medication management.

Local deployment is also a key feature of this study. Prescriptions for narcotic and psychotropic drugs contain sensitive information, including patient identity, diagnosis, medication, and physician signatures. Reliance on public LLM APIs may therefore pose risks related to data leakage and privacy compliance. A 2026 study by Wada et al demonstrated that, after systematic optimization, local LLMs can achieve performance comparable to cloud-based models in medical privacy information extraction tasks while maintaining data sovereignty and on-premises processing capabilities. In this study, an in-hospital OCR microservice and a local LLM inference framework were adopted, enabling prescription images, OCR-generated text, and structured outputs to be processed entirely within the hospital’s local area network. This approach provides a feasible pathway for privacy-preserving artificial intelligence (AI) applications in the context of specially controlled drugs.[20]

In addition, this study highlights the closed-loop management characteristics of narcotic and psychotropic drugs. Conventional prescription review systems mainly focus on drug name, dosage, frequency, and drug–drug interactions, whereas the management of narcotic and psychotropic drugs also emphasizes verifiable physician signatures, traceable accountability, and recordable residual drug handling for injectable formulations. The present system incorporates physician signature verification and injectable residual volume calculation into the review workflow, thereby extending prescription review from simple rational drug use assessment to full-process supervision of specially controlled medications. In particular, the calculation of discarded injectable volume can provide digital evidence for empty ampoule collection and residual drug destruction, demonstrating strong practical management value.

In exploratory comparisons, the OCR-LLM-based system outperformed the traditional rule-based engine in diagnostic information extraction and medication order compliance assessment, suggesting advantages in processing unstructured text and complex semantics. However, its average processing time was longer than that of the traditional rule-based engine, which may be attributed to additional steps such as image recognition, layout reconstruction, LLM-based parsing, and rule-based reasoning. Although the average processing time of the OCR-LLM system (3.5 seconds per prescription) was longer than that of the traditional rule-based engine, the absolute processing time remained short and may be unlikely to affect routine inpatient pharmacy workflows. Given that the system performs OCR recognition, layout reconstruction, LLM-based structured parsing, and rule-based validation within a single automated workflow, the modest increase in processing time represents an acceptable trade-off for improved structured information extraction and reduced manual review workload in routine inpatient pharmacy practice. Therefore, the results of this study should not be interpreted as indicating that the OCR-LLM system is superior in all efficiency-related metrics. Instead, it should be understood as providing complementary value in the review of complex paper-based prescriptions. The actual efficiency gains of the system still require further evaluation in real-world workflows, including metrics such as pharmacist review time, alert acceptance rate, prescription correction rate, and missed detection rate.

From a clinical evaluation perspective, this study remains at the stage of system development and single-center retrospective validation. The DECIDE-AI guideline emphasizes that early-stage evaluation of AI-based decision support systems should not only report model performance but also describe the clinical workflow, human–system interaction, failure modes, human–AI collaboration patterns, and safety monitoring mechanisms.[24] Compared with these requirements, although the present study has established a trigger mechanism for manual pharmacist review and formed a preliminary closed-loop workflow, a prospective real-world workflow study has not yet been conducted. In addition, formal evaluations of user acceptance, pharmacist satisfaction, and trust in the system were not performed in the present study. Future prospective implementation studies incorporating structured usability assessments and end-user feedback will be valuable for further evaluating the practical applicability and clinical acceptance of the proposed system. In addition, this study was conducted in a single center, and the applicability of the system across other hospitals and different management workflows remains to be further validated. Moreover, subgroup analyses were not performed because this study included a relatively homogeneous dataset of inpatient controlled-drug prescriptions with a unified prescription format. Future studies including more diverse datasets are warranted. Future work should also include robustness evaluation under different OCR confidence thresholds, calibration of confidence estimates, and systematic analysis of error propagation across the OCR-LLM pipeline to further improve system reliability and generalizability. Future studies should compare 3 modes – manual review, system-based automated prescreening, and pharmacist-assisted review guided by the system – in terms of review efficiency, intervention quality, pharmacist workload, and patient safety outcomes.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study constructed an intelligent prescription review system for inpatient narcotic and psychotropic drugs based on deep learning-based OCR and a local LLM. Retrospective validation demonstrated that the system achieved high review accuracy and consistency. The system can serve as a front-end screening and clinical decision-support tool for pharmacists and provides a feasible solution for intelligent prescription review and closed-loop supervision of specially controlled medications.
