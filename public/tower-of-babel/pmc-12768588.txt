Identification of Poor Prognostic Markers in Triple-Negative Breast Cancer Using Whole Exome Sequencing
Authors: Seungju Lee, Hyun Yul Kim, Youn Joo Jung, Seok-Kyung Kang, Miri Ryu, Meehyun Lee, Sun Min Lee, Seung Hwan Oh, Jieon Lee, Seongdo Jeong, Junho Kang, Jee Yeon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a heterogeneous and aggressive subtype of breast cancer associated with poor clinical outcomes. Although programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression has emerged as both a prognostic and predictive biomarker, its utility remains limited, especially in PD-L1-negative tumors. The identification of additional molecular markers is crucial for improving prognostic stratification and guiding treatment strategies.MethodsFormalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues from 38 patients with TNBC were analyzed. PD-L1 expression was assessed using immunohistochemistry and categorized as positive or negative. Whole-exome sequencing was performed, and somatic variants were analyzed using Maftools. Mutational signatures were compared with the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer reference profiles. Survival analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic significance of the identified variants.ResultsMutational landscape analysis revealed that C>T and G>A transitions were the most frequent base substitutions. PD-L1-negative tumors exhibited a predominance of single-base substitution (SBS) 5, whereas PD-L1-positive tumors resembled SBS6, reflecting potential differences in the underlying mutational processes. Comparative analysis identified 12 PD-L1-negative-specific and seven PD-L1-positive-specific variants. Among PD-L1-negative tumors, mutations in ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L were significantly associated with worse overall survival.ConclusionOur findings highlight distinct mutational profiles and prognostic variants according to PD-L1 status in TNBC. ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L variants may serve as potential prognostic markers for PD-L1-negative tumors. These results underscore the value of incorporating genomic information to refine the prognostic stratification of TNBC.Keywords: Exome Sequencing, Mutation, Prognosis, Triple Negative Breast Neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subtype of breast cancer with a poor prognosis [1-4]. With advancements in neoadjuvant chemotherapy, chemotherapy before surgery has become the standard practice for patients with TNBC [3,5]. Moreover, assessing whether pathological complete remission is achieved through surgery following neoadjuvant chemotherapy has proven useful in predicting patient prognosis [3,5,6]. Additionally, the Impassion130 and KEYNOTE-522 trials demonstrated that a combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting programmed death protein 1 (PD-1) and chemotherapy significantly prolonged progression-free survival compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with PD-L1-positive TNBC [7-10]. Cancer cells express programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), which interacts with the PD-1 receptor on T-cell surfaces. This interaction inhibits T-cell function, suppresses the immune response, and enables cancer cells to escape immune detection. Immune checkpoint inhibitors block the PD-1 receptor, thereby reactivating T cells and allowing them to target cancer cells again [10-12]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors can induce a robust immune response; however, they may also cause immune-related adverse events, such as pneumonitis and colitis [13,14]. Therefore, it is imperative to select patient groups most likely to benefit from this treatment. Next-generation sequencing has been used to identify several mutations in breast cancer. Hoda et al. [15] attempted to identify specific genetic mutations in patients with PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative TNBC using next-generation sequencing; however, no notable genetic mutations were reported. This retrospective study aimed to assess genetic variants that influence PD-L1 expression using whole-exome sequencing.

METHODS

Patients and samples

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded primary tumor tissues (n = 38 samples) were collected from 38 patients diagnosed with TNBC who underwent treatment at the Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital between 2016 and 2020. PD-L1 (SP142) immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed with 4-μm-thick whole slides. IHC staining was conducted using a Benchmark Ultra System (Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, USA), with antibody detection facilitated by the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (Ventana Medical Systems), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. PD-L1-positive cases were defined as those showing PD-L1 expression on stromal tumor-infiltrating immune cells comprising ≥ 1% of the tumor area. Stromal tumor-infiltrating immune cells were quantified in all cases. Among the 38 samples, 22 were primary tumor tissues with PD-L1 positivity, and 16 were primary tumor tissues with PD-L1 negativity.

Evaluations included histology, tumor grade, tumor size, lymph node status, type of surgery, chemotherapy status, and radiation therapy. Allred scoring was employed to assess hormone receptor status, with scores ranging from three to eight classified as positive. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression was assessed by IHC, and the results were categorized on a scale of 0, 1+, 2+, and 3+. In this study, TNBC was defined as a tumor with a score of zero for both hormone receptors and HER2.

Owing to the retrospective design of this study, we were unable to obtain blood samples or matched nontumor tissues from these patients. Therefore, we excluded potential germline mutations by filtering variants based on their frequencies in a healthy population. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital (04-2023-014). Specimens and clinical data were obtained from the Institutional Biobank Project (OF-2023-8) in accordance with the approved research protocol. This study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Whole exome sequencing

Hematoxylin and eosin slides were prepared for all samples, and the tumor content was evaluated by a pathologist. A genomic DNA (gDNA) library was constructed from 50 ng of input gDNA using the Twist Library Preparation EF Kit 2.0 (96 samples; PN 104207). Full-length combinatorial dual-index TruSeq-compatible Y adapters (Illumina, San Diego, USA) were used according to the Twist Bioscience Library Protocol. DNA was quantified using PicoGreen (Invitrogen, Waltham, USA), and DNA quality was assessed using the TapeStation gDNA Screentape (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, USA). Fifty nanograms of gDNA, diluted with EB Buffer, was sheared to a target peak size of 200 bp using the fragmentation enzyme. DNA fragmentation was followed by end repair and A-tailing, which involved the enzymatic addition of an “A” tail to the 3′ ends. The Twist UDI index adapters were ligated into the fragments. After evaluating ligation efficiency, adapter-ligated DNA fragments were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The final purified product was quantified using a TapeStation DNA Screentape D1000 (Agilent Technologies, Inc.) and fluorescence-based PicoGreen assay. For exome capture, each hybridization reaction required 1,500 ng of indexed libraries prepared by pooling equimolar amounts from eight individual libraries. The mixture was combined with a hybridization mix, Twist exome 2.0 probe, blocker solution, Universal Blocker, and Hybridization Enhancer according to the Twist library preparation protocol. Captured DNA was washed thoroughly and amplified. The final purified product was quantified by quantitative PCR following the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA Library Quantification Kits for Illumina Sequencing Platforms; Illumina) and qualified using a TapeStation DNA Screentape D1000 (Agilent Technologies, Inc.). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina), achieving an average coverage depth of 100×.

Somatic variant calling and annotation

Somatic variants were identified using the DNA analysis pipeline of Genome Analysis Toolkit version 4 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/) [16]. Since matched normal samples were not available, variants were called in tumor-only mode. To reduce the risk of residual germline variants, we applied population-based filtering using the germline-resource option, referencing af-only gnomAD.vcf.gz and dbSNP population allele frequencies, and retained only variants with a population minor allele frequency below 1%. Variants were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: 1) presence of three or more mismatched bases within variant reads, 2) classification as sequencing artifacts, 3) frequent occurrence of error-prone reads across different clones, 4) depth of coverage < 10 in tumor samples, or 5) an average base quality score < 20. These steps were performed to minimize germline carryover and increase the reliability of somatic variant calls in the absence of matched normal cells.

Analysis of somatic variants

Annotated variant data were analyzed using the R package “maftools” [17]. Comparative variant profiling between PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative groups was visualized using the Oncoplot function. Co-occurring or mutually exclusive variants between the two groups were identified using the somaticInteraction function, which applies Fisher’s exact test to determine statistically significant co-occurrence or mutual exclusivity (p < 0.05). To identify group-specific variants, the mafCompare function was used to compare variant frequencies between the PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative groups. Statistical significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact test, and variants with p < 0.05 were considered group-specific. To investigate mutational signatures, a variant matrix was generated using the “BSgenome.Hsapiens.NCBI.GRCh38” package, and non-negative matrix factorization was performed. The best-fit model was determined based on the lowest cophenetic correlation coefficient. The identified signatures were compared with Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) database signatures by calculating cosine similarity. Survival analysis was performed to assess the prognostic impact of the group-specific variants. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression models were conducted using the survival and survminer packages in R. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival were evaluated. Statistical significance was determined using the log-rank test, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Clinical and pathological characteristics between the PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative groups were compared using the independent two-sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on normality, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The clinical and pathological characteristics of the enrolled patients are presented in Table 1. All patients were diagnosed with TNBC. Regarding tumor grade, the majority of the PD-L1-positive group had grade 3 tumors (21 patients, 95.5%), whereas nine patients (56.3%) in the PD-L1-negative group had grade 3 tumors (p = 0.005). PD-L1 (SP142) expression in TNBC is associated with an increase in stromal tumor-infiltrating immune cells. The patients underwent chemotherapy with doxorubicin–cyclophosphamide and paclitaxel regimens. In the PD-L1-positive group, one patient received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, whereas four patients in the PD-L1-negative group underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy. In the PD-L1-positive group, one patient (4.5%) declined adjuvant chemotherapy owing to advanced age, whereas one patient (6.3%) in the PD-L1-negative group also refused adjuvant chemotherapy. The median follow-up duration was 80 months. In the PD-L1-positive group, local recurrence was observed in seven patients during follow-up. In the PD-L1-negative group, four cases of local recurrence, three cases of lung metastasis, one case of bone metastasis, and one case of brain metastasis were identified.

Landscape of somatic variants in TNBC

We performed comprehensive variant analysis across all tumor samples to characterize the somatic variant profiles of patients with TNBC. In the analysis of single nucleotide variant patterns, C>T and G>A transitions were the most frequently observed substitutions (Figure 1A), consistent with the mutational processes commonly reported in cancers. The number of somatic variants per sample varied considerably, ranging from 81 to 637, with a median of 300.5 (Figure 1B). The most frequently observed variants were in TP53, MUC5B, and CDK11A (Figure 1C). TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene, consistent with its established role as a key driver of TNBC. Additionally, recurrent variants were observed in genes, such as MUC5B, CDK11A, BARD1, and OBSCN, which are implicated in tumorigenesis and genomic instability. These findings demonstrate extensive mutational heterogeneity among the TNBC tumors in our cohort. All observed variants are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Genomic and clinical differences according to PD-L1 status in TNBC

Given the established association between PD-L1 expression and clinical outcomes in TNBC, we first examined whether PD-L1 status influenced survival in our cohort. In the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Figure 2A), patients in the PD-L1-negative group exhibited significantly worse OS than that of patients in the PD-L1-positive group (p = 0.010). This finding is consistent with previous reports highlighting the prognostic relevance of PD-L1 expression in TNBC. Multivariate Cox regression analysis incorporating clinical variables, such as age, body mass index, and N stage (Figure 2B) confirmed that PD-L1 status remained the only significant predictor of survival, whereas other clinical factors showed no statistically significant association. Subsequently, we explored differences in the underlying mutational processes according to PD-L1 status. Mutational signature analysis with cosine similarity against COSMIC reference signatures confirmed that PD-L1-negative tumors showed the greatest similarity to single-base substitution (SBS) 5 (cosine similarity = 0.838), whereas PD-L1-positive tumors were most similar to SBS6 (cosine similarity = 0.864) (Figure 2C). These findings support distinct mutational processes between the two subgroups. The detailed results of the cosine similarity analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 2. SBS5 is an age-related signature observed in multiple cancer types, whereas SBS6 is associated with defects in DNA mismatch repair. This finding suggests potential differences in the mutagenic mechanisms between PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative tumors. To further characterize genomic differences, we identified group-specific variants using comparative analysis. Twelve genes were specifically mutated in the PD-L1-negative group, whereas seven genes were uniquely altered in the PD-L1-positive group (Figure 2D). Among the PD-L1-negative-specific variants, notable genes included KIAA1549L, previously implicated in cancer invasion and metastasis; EXOC7, associated with cell motility and cytoskeletal organization; ANGPTL5, linked to tumor angiogenesis and microenvironment remodeling; and ERBB3 (HER3), a key member of the epidermal growth factor receptor family involved in tumor growth, immune evasion, and therapeutic resistance. These findings highlight distinct genomic alterations that may underlie the poor prognosis observed in patients with PD-L1-negative TNBC.

Identification of prognostic variants in PD-L1-negative TNBC

Based on our previous findings, we aimed to determine whether group-specific variants observed according to PD-L1 status were associated with clinical outcomes. Our previous analysis indicated that PD-L1 negativity is a significant clinical marker of poor prognosis in TNBC. Therefore, we focused on the 12 somatic variants uniquely identified in the PD-L1-negative group and evaluated their prognostic significance using survival analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that mutations in TRDN, ANGPTL5, KIAA1549L, and EXOC7 were significantly associated with reduced OS (Figure 3). Notably, patients harboring mutations in these genes exhibited markedly lower survival rates than those of patients without mutations (TRDN, p = 0.011; ANGPTL5, p < 0.001; KIAA1549L, p < 0.001; EXOC7, p = 0.031). These results suggest that specific somatic variants enriched in PD-L1-negative tumors contribute to poor clinical outcomes and highlight their potential roles in the aggressive behavior of this TNBC subgroup.

Impact of prognostic variants in PD-L1-negative TNBC

To further assess the prognostic significance of the identified somatic variants, we stratified patients into four groups based on PD-L1 expression status (positive vs. negative) and variant status (wild type vs. variant) and performed survival analysis accordingly. The analysis demonstrated that mutations in ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L were associated with significantly worse OS, even within the PD-L1-negative subgroup (Figure 4). By contrast, variants in TRDN and EXOC7 did not show statistically significant differences in survival among patients with PD-L1-negative TNBC. Considering that TNBC itself is linked to poor clinical outcomes and that PD-L1 negativity further exacerbates prognosis, our findings suggest that the presence of variants in ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L may contribute to an even more aggressive clinical course in PD-L1-negative TNBC.

DISCUSSION

TNBC comprises various molecular subtypes that complicate treatment selection and limit the effectiveness of current therapies. Although treatment options, such as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, are available, chemotherapy remains the mainstay. Despite recent advances, effective targeted therapies for TNBC remain limited [18]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as promising treatments, particularly for patients with PD-L1-positive TNBC [19]. By contrast, patients with PD-L1-negative TNBC continue to have limited treatment options and are often associated with worse outcomes. To improve risk classification and support personalized treatment strategies, it is important to identify additional molecular markers that can complement PD-L1 status. In this study, we analyzed the mutational profiles of TNBC according to PD-L1 expression status to identify somatic variants that may help predict patient outcomes, particularly in the PD-L1-negative group.

Given the prognostic differences observed between PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative tumors in our TNBC cohort, we hypothesized that each subgroup harbors distinct underlying mutational processes. To investigate this, we performed mutational signature analysis and identified the COSMIC signatures with the highest cosine similarity for each tumor. The PD-L1-negative group showed the greatest similarity to SBS5, a clock-like signature previously associated with age and defects in ERCC-related DNA repair. Although patients in the PD-L1-negative group were, on average, 2.5 years older than those in the PD-L1-positive group, this difference was not statistically significant, and no ERCC mutations were detected. By contrast, the PD-L1-positive group showed the highest similarity to SBS6, a signature commonly linked to mismatch repair deficiency and microsatellite instability. While we did not directly assess microsatellite instability or mismatch repair status, SBS6 has also been associated with elevated tumor mutational burden, and PD-L1-positive tumors in our study exhibited a modest, though not statistically significant, increase in the mutation count per sample. Although both SBS5 and SBS6 have been reported in TNBC [20], our findings revealed that these signatures were differentially distributed according to PD-L1 expression. This suggests that TNBC subgroups defined by PD-L1 status may arise through distinct mutational processes or DNA repair deficiencies, reflecting the underlying molecular heterogeneity of the disease.

Previous analyses showed that PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative tumors have different mutational signatures. These results suggest that TNBC consists of distinct molecular subgroups. To examine whether these differences were clinically relevant, we analyzed the somatic variants and their associations with patient outcomes. In the TNBC cohort, we identified four genes that were significantly associated with OS. Patients with mutations in ANGPTL5, KIAA1549L, EXOC7, or TRDN had shorter survival times than those of patients without mutations. These genes showed prognostic value regardless of PD-L1 status. We evaluated whether the same variants were associated with outcomes in PD-L1-negative tumors. Among the four genes, ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L remained significantly associated with a poor prognosis in this subgroup. These results suggest that while some variants may predict outcomes in all patients with TNBC, certain genes, such as ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L, may further stratify risk within the PD-L1-negative group. Genomic information may help refine prognosis beyond PD-L1 status alone. Although ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L have not been well characterized in TNBC, previous studies have suggested their potential involvement in cancer-related processes. ANGPTL5 is a member of the angiopoietin-like protein family and is involved in lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, and inflammation [21]. It has also been implicated in hematopoietic stem cell expansion, indicating its possible involvement in cellular proliferation. In non-small cell lung cancer, the co-expression of ANGPTL5 with immunoglobulin-like transcript 4 has been linked to poor OS, suggesting a potential role in tumor progression in certain contexts [22]. Although the role of ANGPTL5 in breast cancer has not been extensively studied, recent multiomics studies have identified ANGPTL5 as a candidate driver gene in TNBC [23]. This study integrated data on DNA methylation, copy number alterations, and somatic mutations, highlighting ANGPTL5 as a gene that shows recurrent genomic alterations in TNBC. These findings suggest that ANGPTL5 may contribute to the development and progression of TNBC, although the precise biological mechanisms remain unclear. They also raise the possibility that ANGPTL5 influences tumor progression through mechanisms related to proliferation or immune modulation. KIAA1549L is a gene with largely uncharacterized functions. However, emerging evidence suggests its potential involvement in cancer [24]. Notably, KIAA1549L has been identified as a fusion partner of PAX5 in pediatric B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia, implying a role in leukemogenesis [25]. Additionally, data from The Human Protein Atlas indicate that high expression of KIAA1549L is associated with an unfavorable prognosis in pancreatic cancer, suggesting its potential role as a prognostic biomarker. In the context of breast cancer, KIAA1549L has been included in the KIAA family of genes exhibiting genetic variants associated with breast cancer risk, although its biological significance remains unclear [26]. Although the precise biological functions of KIAA1549L are yet to be fully elucidated, these findings imply its possible role in tumor development and progression. Further studies are required to elucidate its specific functions in cancer, including its potential involvement in TNBC.

Despite the insights gained from this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, particularly after stratification according to PD-L1 status and mutational subgroups. This may have limited the statistical power to detect associations with greater confidence. Second, although we identified ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L as potential prognostic markers, their functional roles in TNBC remain largely unknown. Our findings are based on statistical associations, and further biological studies are required to elucidate their mechanistic relevance. Third, the lack of an external validation cohort limited the generalizability of our results, as all analyses were performed using data from a single institution. Further studies involving independent cohorts are necessary to validate and strengthen the clinical applicability of these findings. Fourth, survival analyses of group-specific variants were based on a small set of prespecified candidates, and multiple testing corrections were not applied. Accordingly, these results should be considered exploratory, interpreted with caution, and validated using independent cohorts. Finally, PD-L1 status was assessed by IHC, a method that may vary depending on antibody clones, scoring criteria, and interpretation. This technical variability could influence subgroup classification and should be considered in clinical translation.

In summary, our study suggests that TNBC is not a single disease but consists of distinct molecular subtypes that may differ in clinical behavior. We observed that PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-negative tumors showed different mutational signatures, indicating potential differences in the underlying genomic processes. Several somatic variants were associated with worse prognosis across the cohort. Among these, ANGPTL5 and KIAA1549L showed prognostic significance, even within the PD-L1-negative subgroup. These results suggest that the selected mutations may provide additional information for identifying high-risk patients beyond PD-L1 expression alone. Although our findings require further validation in larger independent cohorts, they highlight the potential value of incorporating genomic markers in the prognostic assessment of TNBC. This approach may help refine risk stratification and support personalized treatment strategies in clinical practice.
