Influence of Fluid Rheology on Proppant Transport and Fracture Geometry in Enhanced Geothermal Systems: A Field Comparative Study in Deep Granitic Rock
Authors: Yizhao Wang, Ziqi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) rely on hydraulic fracturing to create permeable pathways in hot dry rock formations, yet the influence of fracturing fluid rheology on proppant transport and fracture geometry remains poorly constrained under field conditions. This study presents a field comparative analysis of cross-linked gel versus slickwater fracturing fluids in deep granitic rock at the Utah FORGE site. Two consecutive stages in well 16A(78)-32 Stage 8 (cross-linked gel, ∼200 cP) and Stage 9 (slickwater, ∼5 cP) were pumped at equivalent high rates (∼80 bpm), with fluid rheology as the primary design difference between stages. Integrated diagnostics including real-time treating pressure analysis, poststimulation production logging (PLT), and distributed fiber-optic strain sensing (DSS) on an offset well were employed. Results show that the cross-linked gel produced a fracture span, defined here as the cross-well fiber-optic response depth range (i.e., the interwell disturbance depth range inferred from fiber-optic response), of 575 ft compared to 145 ft for slickwater, suggesting approximately 4-fold greater vertical extent of fracture-driven interactions. The slickwater treatment exhibited pronounced near-wellbore pressure escalation indicative of screen-out risk, coupled with nonuniform cluster injection efficiency (Gini coefficient = 0.27) including complete exclusion of one perforation cluster. Fiber-optic strain profiles revealed distributed, multipeak fracture signatures for the gel treatment versus concentrated near-wellbore responses for slickwater. While confounding factors including sequential stress shadowing effects cannot be fully excluded, the observed performance differences are consistent with the expected role of fluid rheology in governing proppant transport and fracture geometry. These findings provide field evidence that high-viscosity fracturing fluids may offer advantages for proppant transport and fracture network development in EGS applications in crystalline basement formations.

Introduction

Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) seek to engineer artificial permeability in hot dry rock (HDR) formations where thermal energy is abundant but natural fluid pathways are absent. − While the technical viability of EGS has been demonstrated at multiple sites worldwide, ,, a critical challenge remains: creating complex, distributed fracture networks with adequate propped conductivity to maximize the stimulated reservoir volume (SRV) available for heat extraction. ,,

The Frontier Observatory for Research in Geothermal Energy (FORGE) in Milford, Utah, provides an ideal testbed for evaluating stimulation strategies in representative HDR conditionsa Tertiary granitic intrusion with temperatures exceeding 225 °C at target depths. ,, The selection between high-viscosity cross-linked gels (∼100–500 cP) and low-viscosity slickwater (∼2–10 cP) represents a fundamental choice in hydraulic fracturing design, with profound implications for proppant transport mechanics: − high-viscosity fluids enable viscosity-dominated proppant suspension, where elevated drag forces maintain particles throughout the fluid column regardless of local velocity. Per Stokes’ law, proppant settling velocity scales inversely with fluid viscosity, enabling far-field proppant placement when viscosity is maintained. , Low-viscosity fluids result in velocity-dominated transport, where proppant movement depends on maintaining turbulent fluid velocities above critical thresholds. When velocity decreasesas inevitably occurs during fracture width expansionparticles settle rapidly and form basal dunes that concentrate proppant near the wellbore. The ∼40× viscosity difference between typical gel (∼200 cP) and slickwater (∼5 cP) systems produces correspondingly different settling rates, fundamentally altering proppant distribution patterns and achievable fracture conductivity in far-field regions.

In multicluster completions, nonuniform fluid distributionquantified via production logging (PLT) and fiber-optic diagnostics (DSS)can result in severe cluster efficiency heterogeneity, with some clusters receiving disproportionate flow while others are bypassed entirely. − ,,, The interplay between fluid rheology and cluster efficiency remains poorly constrained under field conditions, particularly in crystalline formations where fracturing mechanics may differ substantially from sedimentary environments.

Despite extensive laboratory investigations and field experience in unconventional oil and gas development, systematic field-scale comparative studies of fluid rheology effects in EGS-relevant crystalline formations remain scarce. ,, The majority of published proppant transport data derives from sedimentary environments (shales, tight sandstones) where rock mechanical properties, natural fracture networks, and in situ stress regimes differ markedly from crystalline basement. Extrapolation of these findings to EGS applications carries substantial uncertainty.

Furthermore, most fluid comparison studies have been conducted under controlled laboratory conditions or through numerical simulation, with limited opportunity for direct field validation under realistic downhole environments. ,,, The lack of controlled field experimentscomparing different fluid systems in adjacent stages of the same well, thereby minimizing geological variabilityrepresents a significant gap in the evidence base for EGS stimulation optimization.

This study addresses these knowledge gaps through a comprehensive field comparative analysis of cross-linked gel versus slickwater fracturing fluids at the Utah FORGE site. By leveraging the controlled experimental environment of FORGE and integrating multiple diagnostic technologies (treating pressure analysis, PLT, and fiber-optic monitoring), we provide direct field evidence of rheology-dependent differences in (1) near-wellbore proppant transport and bridging behavior; (2) multicluster injection efficiency and uniformity; (3) far-field fracture extent and geometric complexity.

The objectives of this research are 3-fold: (1) to quantify the differential performance of high- and low-viscosity fluids in deep granitic rock under representative EGS conditions; (2) to elucidate the mechanistic links between fluid rheology, proppant transport, and fracture geometry outcomes; and (3) to provide field-validated guidance for optimizing stimulation fluid selection in future EGS developments.

Experimental Section

Field Site Description

Field experiments were conducted at the Frontier Observatory for Research in Geothermal Energy (FORGE), located in the Mineral Mountains of Beaver County, Utah. , The geological target is a Tertiary granitoid intrusion (Oligocene-age Roosevelt Hot Springs pluton) consisting predominantly of biotite monzogranitea hot dry rock (HDR) formation with extremely low matrix permeability (<0.1 μD) and minimal natural fracture connectivity. ,

Reservoir Conditions. The target interval lies at a true vertical depth (TVD) of approximately 8500 ft (2,590 m), with bottom-hole temperatures ranging from 175 to 225 °C. This high-temperature environment is representative of commercial EGS targets but also presents challenges for fracturing fluid stability.

The in situ stress regime, characterized through extended leak-off tests and wellbore image analysis, indicates a normal-to-strike-slip transitional state. ,

Well Configuration and Completion

Well Architecture

The stimulation program utilized a two-well configuration: an injection well (16A(78)-32) and a parallel monitoring well (16B(78)-32) positioned above the stimulation wellbore.

Well 16A(78)-32 was drilled to a measured depth of ∼10,987 ft with ∼65° maximum inclination. The well was completed with 5.5-in. (139.7 mm) P-110 production casing, cemented to surface for zonal isolation under high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) conditions.

Well 16B(78)-32 was instrumented with a permanent fiber-optic sensing system comprising DSS cables, enabling real-time detection of fracture-driven interactions (frac hits) during stimulation. ,

Controlled Pump Rate

Critically, both stages were pumped at equivalent high rates (∼80 bpm), unlike lower-rate stage pairs elsewhere in the stimulation program. − This constant-rate design reduces pump rate as a confounding variable and supports interpretation of performance differences in relation to fluid rheology. ,, The ∼40-fold viscosity difference (200 vs 5 cP) is also consistent with correspondingly different proppant settling rates per Stokes’ law: calculated settling velocities are ∼0.02 ft/min for gel versus ∼0.8 ft/min for slickwater (for 40/70 mesh ceramic proppant). ,, Both stages employed identical 40/70 mesh ceramic proppant and completion geometry. ,,

Sequential Stress Consideration

Stage 9 was pumped immediately after stage 8, placing it within the stress shadow of the preceding treatment. This sequencing is acknowledged as a potential contributing factor to the observed performance differences (discussed in Section 4.1.2).

Although the comparison was structured around equivalent pump rate and identical completion geometry, several experimental-design factors should be considered when interpreting stage-to-stage differences. First, Stage 9 was pumped immediately after stage 8 and therefore may have been influenced by stress shadowing from the preceding treatment. − Second, the stage order was fixed rather than randomized or crossed, so fluid effects cannot be fully disentangled from sequence effects in a two-stage field comparison. Third, minor execution differences remained, including differences in achieved proppant schedule, maximum concentration, total proppant placed before termination, and other operational variability associated with field execution. Accordingly, the constant-rate design and matched completion geometry reduced, but did not eliminate, confounding, and the comparison should be interpreted as a site-specific field comparison rather than a fully controlled laboratory-style experiment.

Data Acquisition and Processing

Comprehensive data acquisition encompassed real-time surface measurements, poststimulation well logging, and continuous fiber-optic monitoring. Rigorous data processing protocols were implemented to ensure quantitative comparability between stages.

Surface Treatment Data

Real-time surface measurements were recorded via electronic drilling recorder (EDR) at 1 s sampling intervals throughout both stimulation treatments. Acquired parameters included: slurry rate (bpm); treating pressure (psi); proppant concentration (ppg); cumulative proppant mass (lbs); time alignment Protocol. To enable direct comparison of pressure response between stages, all time-series data were aligned by setting T = 0 at the moment of “first proppant on formation”defined as the instant when surface proppant concentration first exceeded 0.25 ppg following the pad stage. This alignment isolates the proppant-laden treatment phase and normalizes for variations in pad volume and breakdown timing.

Raw pressure data exhibited high-frequency sensor noise (±15–25 psi fluctuations at 1 s intervals) that could obscure underlying trends. A rolling median filter with a 60 s window was applied to attenuate this noise while preserving genuine pressure transients associated with fracture propagation events Pfiltered(t)=median{Praw(t−30),...,Praw(t),...,Praw(t+30)}

The median filter was selected over moving average due to its superior robustness against outlier spikes from momentary sensor artifacts. A 60 s window was retained for the primary analysis because it provided a practical balance between suppressing 1 s sensor noise and preserving the stage-scale pressure trend used for comparing stage 8 and stage 9. A brief pressure-data-processing sensitivity check is provided in Supporting Information Section S2 (Figure S3). For this check, dP/dt was defined as the slope of a single linear regression fit to the filtered pressure trace over the full postalignment interval (t ≥ 0). Using the same pressure channel, the same first-proppant alignment criterion (>0.1 ppg), and the same fitting interval for both stages, the whole-interval dP/dt values were −3.965, −3.949, and −3.894 psi/min for Stage 8 and −1.670, −1.673, and −1.681 psi/min for stage 9 for the 30, 60, and 120 s median windows, respectively. Relative to the 60 s reference window, the maximum deviation was 1.37% for Stage 8 and 0.47% for stage 9, confirming that reasonable window choices do not materially alter the stage-scale interpretation of the pressure response.

Production Logging (PLT) Analysis

Poststimulation injection profiling was conducted using a spinner-based production logging tool (PLT) deployed on wireline. The continuous flow velocity profile was converted to volumetric allocation by integrating velocity across the wellbore cross-section at each depth station.

Cluster efficiency calculation. To quantify fluid distribution among the eight perforation clusters, the stage depth interval was divided into eight equal bins corresponding to each cluster location. The injection efficiency for cluster i was calculated as 1 ηi=Qi∑j=18Qj×100% where Q i is the incremental flow rate attributed to cluster i. For perfectly uniform injection, each cluster would receive ηuniform = 12.5%.

Gini coefficient quantification. The degree of injection heterogeneity was quantified using the Gini coefficient, a statistical measure adapted to characterize inequality in distributions. For a set of n cluster efficiencies {x1,x2,···,x n } sorted in ascending order, the Gini coefficient is calculated as 2 G=∑i=1n(2i−n−1)xin∑i=1nxi

The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 (perfect uniformity: all clusters receive equal flow) to 1 (maximum inequality: all flow through a single cluster). Values of G < 0.2 are generally considered indicative of good uniformity, while G > 0.3 suggests significant heterogeneity.

Fiber-Optic Fracture Monitoring

Distributed strain sensing on well 16B(78)-32 provided continuous monitoring of fracture-driven interactions (FDI, or “frac hits”) during stimulation of well 16A. The DSS system recorded strain rate measurements at 1 m spatial resolution along the entire instrumented interval.

FDI Identification. Frac hit events were identified from anomalous strain rate signatures exceeding a threshold of 3 standard deviations above the prestimulation baseline noise level. Each identified event was assigned a depth coordinate based on the DSS channel location exhibiting maximum strain amplitude. ,

Fracture span calculation. The vertical extent of fracture propagation was quantified as the “Fracture Span”the depth range over which frac hits were detected 3 fracturespan=Dmax−Dmin where D max and D min are the maximum and minimum depths, respectively, at which FDI events were recorded during each stage. This metric provides a direct measure of the vertical reach of hydraulic fractures from the stimulation well to the monitoring well location.

Strain profile analysis. In addition to discrete FDI events, the cumulative absolute strain change rate was computed as a function of depth to characterize the spatial distribution of fracture-induced deformation. Multipeak strain profiles indicate distributed fracture networks engaging multiple depth intervals, whereas single concentrated peaks suggest localized fracture development. ,

For each stage, raw DSS data were first extracted within predefined stage-specific time and depth windows and compared against a 30 min prestage quiescent baseline. Baseline noise was quantified independently for each depth channel from the mean and standard deviation of this prestage interval, after which a primary event screen of 3σ above baseline noise was applied. The 3σ criterion was retained because it is commonly used in fiber-optic event-detection workflows and provided a practical balance between false-positive suppression and event sensitivity in the present data set; its robustness was further checked through the 2.5σ, 3.0σ, and 3.5σ sensitivity analysis reported in Supporting Information Figure S4. Candidate exceedances were then evaluated with a fixed local-support criterion to confirm discrete events, the event depth was assigned to the channel exhibiting maximum amplitude, and the confirmed events were compiled into the FDI catalog used for fracture-span calculations. This workflow is also consistent with recent DAS-based fracture-geometry reconstruction studies that integrate distributed acoustic observations with data-driven interpretation. The same processing parameters and decision rules were applied to Stages 8 and 9; only the stage-specific analysis windows and their corresponding prestage baseline segments differed.

Results

Pumping Dynamics

Despite being pumped at equivalent high rates (∼80 bpm), the contrasting fluid rheologies produced distinctly different pumping behaviors (Figures , and Table ). The cross-linked gel (Stage 8) exhibited smooth, stable pumping dynamics with minimal pressure fluctuation (±18 psi), achieving proppant concentrations up to 2.0 ppg with 100% designed proppant placement. In contrast, the slickwater (stage 9), pumped at the same rate, exhibited pronounced pressure instabilities (±45 psi) characteristic of intermittent dune migration and settling-induced transport fluctuations ref , Maximum achievable proppant concentration was limited to 1.5 ppg, or 75% of the gel treatment, with only 88% of designed proppant placed before screen-out termination. The identical pump rates reduce velocity as an explanatory variable and are consistent with the 40-fold viscosity difference playing an important role in these behavioral differences.

Multi-Cluster Injection Efficiency

The distribution of injected fluid among perforation clusters provides critical insight into the uniformity of stimulation and the effectiveness of multicluster completion designs. Poststimulation production logging (PLT) was conducted on Stage 9 to quantify cluster-by-cluster injection allocation. PLT data for Stage 8 were not acquired due to operational constraints, and no equivalent cluster-level diagnostics (e.g., step-down tests, tracers, or near-wellbore DTS analysis) are available for that stage. Consequently, the cluster efficiency analysis presented here applies exclusively to Stage 9; any inference about Stage 8 cluster uniformity is indirect and based on treating pressure behavior and fiber-optic response patterns rather than direct cluster-level measurement.

Mechanistic Interpretation

The observed injection heterogeneity is consistent with the stress shadowing and flow competition phenomena characteristic of multicluster slickwater treatments. , When hydraulic fractures initiate from multiple clusters simultaneously, the induced stress field from dominant fractures can suppress growth at intermediate clustersa mechanism termed “stress shadowing”. , In low-viscosity fluids, this effect is amplified because the limited proppant-carrying capacity concentrates proppant near the dominant fracture entries, potentially bridging and sealing off weaker clusters.

The complete exclusion of Cluster #4 likely resulted from a combination of factors.(1)Stress shadowing: The relatively central position of Cluster #4 placed it in the stress shadow of fractures propagating from Clusters #3 and #5.(2)Preferential bridging: rapid proppant settling in the slickwater fluid may have created a proppant bridge at the Cluster #4 perforations before significant fracture development.(3)Flow competition: Once initial fractures established at adjacent clusters, the path of least resistance diverted subsequent fluid away from Cluster #4.

This value falls within the “moderate heterogeneity” range (0.20–0.40), indicating that while the overall distribution is not catastrophically nonuniform, significant deviation from ideal behavior exists ref . The Lorenz curve (Figure , left panel) visualizes this inequality: approximately 50% of the total injected volume was delivered through only three clusters (37.5% of entry points), while the remaining five clusters shared the other 50%.

The Gini coefficient of 0.269 for stage 9 is notably higher than values typically reported for cross-linked gel treatments in similar completion geometries (G = 0.15–0.20), which is consistent with the possibility that the low-viscosity slickwater system exacerbated cluster efficiency heterogeneity ref . This finding is also consistent with the hypothesis that high-viscosity fluids may promote more uniform cluster treatment by maintaining proppant in suspension and reducing differential bridging among clusters.

Implications for Stimulated Reservoir Volume

The implications for effective cluster utilization differ in evidentiary strength between the two stages. For Stage 9, PLT directly documents nonuniform cluster allocation, including one completely excluded cluster and reduced contribution from adjacent entry points. On that basis, the Stage 9 treatment appears to have effectively stimulated only 6–7 of the intended 8 clusters, a 13–25% reduction in productive fracture entry points?which could potentially reduce the fraction of reservoir volume contacted by injected fluid. For Stage 8, no PLT-based cluster allocation data are available. However, the more stable treating pressure response and broader fiber-optic response are consistent with, but do not confirm, more distributed fluid entry and fracture growth than observed for Stage 9. Accordingly, comparisons of effective cluster utilization between the two stages should be interpreted as a contrast between direct PLT evidence for stage 9 and indirect supporting evidence for stage 8.

Fracture Geometry from Fiber-Optic Monitoring

Distributed fiber-optic sensing on the offset monitoring well 16B(78)-32 provided direct observation of fracture propagation and far-field geometry for both stimulation stages (Figure ). Analysis of fracture-driven interactions (frac hits) and strain profiles indicates marked differences in fracture architecture that are consistent with fluid rheology playing an important role.

Frac Hit Distribution and Fracture Span

Figure presents the depth distribution of detected frac hits for Stage 8 (cross-linked gel) and stage 9 (slickwater). Both stages produced 12 identifiable frac hit events on the monitoring well; however, the spatial distribution of these events differed dramatically.

The stage 8 cross-linked gel treatment achieved a fracture span, defined here as the cross-well fiber-optic response depth range (i.e., the interwell disturbance depth range inferred from fiber-optic response), of 575.8 ft (P5–P95:496.1 ft) approximately four times greater than the 145.6 ft span (P5–P95:126.0 ft) observed for stage 9 slickwater. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals ([239, 576]­ft for Stage 8 vs [89, 146]­ft for Stage 9) show no overlap, indicating that the difference is statistically robust. This 4-fold difference in cross-well response depth range suggests substantially different vertical extents of fracture-driven interactions between the two fluid systems.

Importantly, this difference is unlikely to be explained solely by monitoring geometry effects. As documented in Table , the Euclidean distance from Stage 9 to the monitoring fiber (299.4 ft) is actually 20 ft closer than Stage 8 (318.7 ft). Per geometric signal attenuation principles, Stage 9 would theoretically be expected to exhibit enhanced detection sensitivity compared to Stage 8. The fact that Stage 9 nonetheless displayed a markedly reduced fracture span provides additional support for the interpretation that the observed difference is associated with fluid behavior, potentially including rheology-dependent limitations of slickwater proppant transport, rather than monitoring system artifacts alone.

Spatial Distribution Patterns

Beyond the aggregate span metric, the distribution of frac hits reveals qualitative differences in fracture architecture (Figure ). The Stage 8 frac hits are distributed broadly across the 576 ft depth interval, with a standard deviation of 180.5 ft indicating dispersed, multizone fracture engagement (Table ). This pattern suggests the development of a complex fracture network with multiple propagation pathways accessing distinct reservoir intervals.

Mechanistic Interpretation

The contrasting fracture geometries observed via fiber-optic monitoring can be interpreted in a manner consistent with rheological differences between the two fluid systems:

Cross-linked gel (stage 8): the high viscosity (∼200 cP) of the cross-linked gel may have supported more efficient proppant transport to far-field regions of the fracture network. As fractures propagated, proppant may have remained more uniformly distributed throughout the fluid column, helping to maintain fracture conductivity and reduce premature closure of distal fracture tips16. This sustained conductivity could allow continued fluid injection into extending fractures, supporting propagation to greater vertical extents. The distributed proppant placement may also have facilitated opening of secondary fractures and natural fracture reactivation, contributing to fracture network complexity.

Slickwater (stage 9): the low viscosity (∼5 cP) of slickwater is consistent with more rapid proppant settling, concentrating particles in near-wellbore regions as discussed in Sections and 3.2. The limited far-field proppant delivery may have left distal fracture regions less effectively propped and more prone to closure under reservoir stress16. As fracture tips extended beyond the propped region, they may have lost hydraulic communication with the wellbore, thereby limiting further propagation. This interpretation is consistent with the constrained vertical extent and concentrated spatial distribution observed for stage 9.

Discussion

The field results presented in Section provide field evidence consistent with fluid rheology exerting a strong influence on proppant transport efficiency, cluster injection uniformity, and ultimately fracture geometry in deep granitic reservoirs. Before synthesizing these observations, we first address a potential alternative interpretation: whether the reduced fracture span observed for Stage 9 could reflect monitoring geometry rather than fluid behavior.

Ruling out monitoring geometry bias. A critical concern in comparative fracture monitoring studies is whether observed differences might arise from unequal detection sensitivities due to varying distances from the monitoring system. Our geometric analysis (Table ) substantially reduces this concern: stage 9 is positioned 6.1% closer to the monitoring fiber than stage 8 (299.4 ft vs 318.7 ft Euclidean distance). If monitoring geometry were the dominant factor, stage 9 would be expected to exhibit larger, not smaller, detected fracture spans. The fact that we observe the opposite, a 4× reduction in fracture span despite more favorable monitoring geometry, supports the interpretation that fluid behavior contributed materially to the observed difference, rather than monitoring artifacts alone.

With this potential confounding factor eliminated, we proceed to synthesize the observations within a mechanistic framework, explore the rheological controlling on fracture development.

The field results are consistent with fluid rheology, independent of pump rate, exerting a strong influence on proppant transport and fracture geometry. The constant pump rate (∼80 bpm) employed for both stages reduces velocity as a confounding variable, supporting interpretation of the 4× difference in fracture span (575.8 ft vs 145.6 ft) in relation to fluid viscosity and stress shadowing effects.

Viscosity-Dominated vs Settling-Dominated Transport

At identical pump rates, the two fluids exhibited fundamentally different transport behaviors: (1)Slickwater (settling-dominated): despite being pumped at the same high rate as the gel (∼80 bpm), the slickwater treatment exhibited behavior consistent with rapid proppant settling under limited viscous support. The ∼5 cP viscosity may be insufficient to maintain particles in suspension during the likely velocity reduction as fluid expands from perforations into fractures. This settling-dominated interpretation is consistent with the pressure fluctuations (±45 psi) and nonuniform cluster efficiency (Gini = 0.27) observed in stage 9. Taken together, these observations suggest that high pump rate alone may not fully compensate for low viscosity in multicluster completions.(2)Cross-linked gel (viscosity-dominated): at the same ∼80 bpm pump rate, the gel treatment appeared to maintain more stable proppant suspension throughout the fracture network. The ∼200 cP design viscosity may provide sufficient drag force to reduce gravitational settling despite local velocity variations. This viscosity-dominated interpretation is consistent with more uniform proppant distribution, as reflected in the stable pressure response (±18 psi) and smooth concentration ramping observed in stage 8. The gel treatment achieved 100% designed proppant placement versus 88% for slickwater, which may reflect more effective transport efficiency under the specific conditions studied.

Multi-Cluster Competition and Stress Shadowing Amplification

The strongest direct evidence for multicluster competition comes from stage 9. PLT directly documents strong allocation heterogeneity, including complete exclusion of one cluster and unequal flow partitioning across the remaining entry points. This stage 9 pattern is consistent with the compounding effects of stress shadowing, flow competition, and limited proppant transport capacity in a slickwater treatment ref .

In slickwater treatments, the limited proppant-carrying capacity can create a “winner-take-all” dynamic in which clusters that establish dominant fractures early in the treatment capture disproportionate flow, while intermediate clusters­(e.g., Cluster #4) become increasingly starved of both fluid and proppant. The resulting stress shadowing may suppress fracture initiation at weaker clusters, and any proppant that does enter these clusters may settle rapidly, creating perforation bridges that further restrict flow. In this sense, stage 9 provides direct PLT-supported evidence of severe allocation heterogeneity under the tested slickwater conditions.

For stage 8, no PLT data are available, so cluster-scale allocation uniformity cannot be directly verified. Nevertheless, the stable treating-pressure response and broader fiber-optic response suggest more distributed entry and propagation behavior than was observed for stage 9. The contrast between the two stages should therefore be interpreted as direct PLT evidence of heterogeneity for stage 9 combined with indirect evidence consistent with more distributed behavior for stage 8.

Conclusions

This study presents a controlled field-scale comparison of cross-linked gel versus slickwater in deep granitic rock at Utah FORGE. By maintaining a constant pump rate (∼80 bpm) across both treatments, we sought to emphasize fluid rheology as the primary variable. The key findings, under the specific conditions of this test, are.(1)Rheological influence relative to velocity: at identical pump rates, cross-linked gel (∼200 cP) maintained stable treating pressures, whereas slickwater (∼5 cP) exhibited progressive escalation culminating in a terminal screen-out signature (+61 psi/min). This suggests that high velocity alone may not fully compensate for low viscosity in preventing near-wellbore bridging in deep granite under the specific conditions of this test.(2)Cluster injection heterogeneity: Slickwater stimulation resulted in severe nonuniformity (Gini = 0.269) and the complete exclusion of one perforation cluster (“dead cluster”). This pattern is consistent with the vulnerability of low-viscosity fluids to preferential bridging and stress-induced suppression in multicluster completions.(3)Observed four-fold difference in fracture span: fiber-optic monitoring indicated a fracture span of 575.8 ft for gel compared to 145.6 ft for slickwater. Critically, this difference occurred despite Stage 9 being 6% closer to the monitoring fiber (299 ft vs 319 ft), making geometric attenuation artifacts a less likely explanation.
