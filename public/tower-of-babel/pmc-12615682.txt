Therapeutic efficacy of rehmannioside A on 5×FAD mice in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Yunrong Lai, Hengbo Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), characterized by Aβ plaques and cognitive decline, remains a significant therapeutic challenge due to limited efficacy of current pharmacotherapies, while Rehmannioside A (ReA) has shown promising neuroprotective effects against neurodegenerative diseases. This research focuses on investigating the therapeutic effects of ReA on 5×FAD mice, emphasizing its potential application in AD treatment. The 5×FAD mice were divided into experimental groups and treated with ReA at varied dosages or donepezil for a twelve-week continuous treatment period. A series of evaluation methods, including behavioral tests, histopathological analysis, Western blotting, and measurement of oxidative and inflammatory markers, were implemented. The findings demonstrated that optimal ReA doses significantly improved learning, memory, and cognitive functions in 5×FAD mice, reduced Aβ plaque accumulation in the hippocampus, decreased microglial cell counts, increased PSD 95 and synapsin-1 protein expression, mitigated oxidative stress and inflammation, and showed no harmful effects on liver or kidney function. ReA’s diverse biological activities suggest its potential in reducing neural damage. More extensive studies are needed to fully understand its molecular mechanisms in AD, which could lead to the development of innovative and effective treatments for this severe neurodegenerative condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23434-9.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, is primarily characterized by progressive memory impairment1. The increasing global geriatric population has led to a worrying rise in AD prevalence, with recent estimates indicating that more than 55 million people worldwide currently live with AD 2. While current pharmacotherapeutic interventions have limited therapeutic efficacy and significant adverse effects3. This therapeutic impasse highlights the urgent need for novel neuroprotective agents4.

The pathological characteristic of AD shows through abnormal amyloid-β (Aβ) peptide accumulation, which comes from sequential β- and γ-secretase-mediated proteolytic processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP)5,6. Under pathological conditions, abnormal APP cleavage results in excessive Aβ production, forming cytotoxic extracellular plaques. Preclinical studies show that soluble Aβ oligomers from AD-affected cortical tissues and APP-transfected neuronal models cause structural synaptic remodeling and quantitative reduction, ultimately leading to cognitive decline7,8. These pathogenic processes establish Aβ aggregation as a central diagnostic biomarker, emphasizing its key role in AD pathophysiology9,10. In addition to Aβ deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction are key contributors to cognitive decline11.

Rehmannioside A (ReA), a bioactive component of Rehmanniae Radix, shows neuroprotective effects by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing neuroinflammation, and promoting synaptic protein expression. In models of vascular dementia (VD), ReA activates Nrf2 signaling and inhibits NF-κB-mediated cytokine release12,13. These mechanisms are directly relevant to AD, in which oxidative and inflammatory pathways play a central role.

Although ReA has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in VD, its role in amyloid-driven AD pathology remains unexplored. To clarify ReA’s therapeutic potential in Aβ pathology, we used 5×FAD murine models, which show progressive cognitive decline by five months - an ideal model for studying AD pathogenesis. This study investigates the potential of ReA as a novel therapeutic candidate for AD.

Methods

Animal subjects

The Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China) provided the 5×FAD mice. All mice were kept at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, following natural light/dark conditions, with unrestricted access to food and water. At 4 weeks of age after weaning, the animals were given ear identification tags and had tail sampling for genetic analysis. The experimental groups included wild-type (WT) controls and 5×FAD transgenic cohorts. The Experimental Animal Ethics Committee at Jiangxi University of Chinese Medicine (IACUC-IUCM-20240710[0015]) and the US National Research Council’s “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals,” approved all experimental procedures related to the animals. All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines.

Genotypic characterization

Tail snips were used to extract genomic DNA for PCR - based genotyping. The following primer pair was utilized for amplification: Forward: 5′-AATAGAGAACGGCAGGAGCA-3′, Reverse: 5′-GCCATGAGGGCACTAATCAT-3′. The thermal cycling parameters were as follows: Initial denaturation was at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 62 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 35 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The electrophoretic separation on 1% agarose gels was analyzed by the Tanon Gel Imaging System (Shanghai).

Experimental animal grouping and drug administration

Mice were categorized into Wild type (WT) and 5×FAD genotypes based on agarose gel electrophoresis analysis of PCR products, and then were separately caged. All mice used in this study were male and each group contains 8 animals. The dosing concentrations were based on our preliminary studies and previous studies14,15. Rehmannioside A (purity > 98%, determined by HPLC) was obtained from Shanghai yuanye Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The experimental groups were established as follows:

WT group: Equal - volume distilled water was daily gavaged to them;

5×FAD group: Equal - volume distilled water was daily gavaged to them

ReA low - dose group: 5 mg/kg was administered via oral gavage to the mice in this group;

ReA medium - dose group: 15 mg/kg was administered via oral gavage to the mice in this group;

ReA high - dose group: 45 mg/kg was administered via oral gavage to the mice in this group;

Positive control group: Donepezil (5 mg/kg/day) was received via oral gavage by the mice in this group.

Randomization was performed using a simple random allocation method, in which each mouse was given a unique identification code and group assignment was determined by a random number table to minimize selection bias. Once - daily gavage administration was received by the mice for 12 consecutive weeks, and continued dosing was maintained throughout the behavioral testing period. Behavioral assessments were carried out during the final week of administration following established phenotyping protocols, and the experimental timeline was illustrated in Fig. 1A. All genotyping procedures strictly adhered to the 3R principles and conformed to institutional animal care guidelines.

Behavioral assessment

On 24-week-old animals, cognitive evaluations were conducted through three standardized paradigms. Behavioral tests were conducted during the light cycle between 08:00–16:00. Tracking software was used to assist in the analysis, and data were manually scored by two independent researchers.

Novel object recognition test

Twenty - four hours before formal testing, the square arena (50 × 50 × 40 cm) was where the subjects were acclimated. In phase 1 (a 10 - min trial), two indistinguishable objects were placed diagonally for free exploration. In phase 2 (24 h later), one familiar object was replaced by a novel object along the same diagonal. In the Novel Object Recognition (NOR) test, mice were not subjected to a habituation phase. During the training phase, mice were allowed to freely explore the objects for 10 min. In the testing phase, mice were exposed to the same environment 24 h later and allowed to explore for 7 min. The exploration behavior was video - recorded during the 7 - min test session, and the nose - point proximity (2–3 cm) to the novel object was quantified during the final 5 - minute interval16. Video-tracking software (Taimeng Technologies, Chengdu) was used to assist in the analysis of mouse behavior during the NOR test.

Y - maze test

The animals were led to the central junction of a Y - shaped apparatus (arm specifications: 30 cm length × 6 cm width × 15 cm barrier height). The arm designations (A/B/C) were assigned systematically, and the arm entry sequences were recorded through video - tracking technology (Taimeng Technologies, Chengdu). Continuous 7 - minute monitoring allowed for the calculation of spontaneous alternation behavior, expressed as the alternation frequency relative to the total arm entries17.

Morris water test

Over a consecutive 5 - day period, the animals had daily training sessions in the spatial navigation task. During acquisition trials, the escape platform was kept submerged with spatial cues placed strategically, and each trial started from different quadrants. Subjects that didn’t find the platform within the allotted time were given guidance followed by a 30 - second platform familiarization period. On day 6 during the probe trial, spatial memory parameters such as swim velocity, initial platform crossing latency, preference for the original platform quadrant, and total platform crossings were quantified after the platform was removed18.

Histopathological analysis

After achieving a surgical plane of anesthesia, an overdose injection of 10% pentobarbital sodium (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) was administered to euthanize the animals. Following euthanasia, the animals were perfused with normal saline, and then immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at 4 °C overnight for tissue fixation. Subsequently, 30% sucrose infiltration was carried out for cryopreservation, after which coronal cryosections (30 μm thickness) were prepared. Immunostaining was performed using primary antibodies: Mouse anti - Aβ (1:1000; sc − 398595) and Rabbit anti - IBA1 (1:1000; 019–19741). Secondary detection made use of Alexa Fluor 594 - conjugated goat anti - mouse (1:1000, Invitrogen) or anti - rabbit (1:1000, Invitrogen) antibodies.

Western blotting

Hippocampal specimens were homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Through incubation at 95 °C for 10 min, denaturation was achieved, followed by electrophoresis on 8–12% SDS - PAGE gels. The transferred proteins (0.22 μm nitrocellulose membranes, Millipore) were blocked with 5% non - fat dry milk and probed sequentially with primary antibodies: PSD95 (1:1000; CST, Boston, USA), Synapsin − 1 (1:1000; CST, Boston, USA), Aβ (1:500; Covance), and GAPDH (1:1000, CST). After washing, the membranes were subjected to either anti - mouse or anti - rabbit secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. The bands were visualized and quantified by using Image J software, with the protein expression of each sample being normalized to GAPDH.

Oxidative and inflammatory factor quantification

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the enzymatic colorimetric test was utilized to detect oxidative and inflammatory factors. Briefly, to determine oxidative and inflammatory levels, hippocampal tissue samples were taken from the mice. The levels of malondialdehyde (MDA; nmol/mg), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; U/mg), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD; U/mg), TNF-α (pg/mg), IL-1β (pg/mg), and IL-6 (pg/mg) were measured with a kit from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Serum biochemical analysis

Blood samples of mice were collected. Using a biochemical analyzer, liver function indices such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT; U/L) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST; U/L) were detected. Meanwhile, renal function indices such as creatinine (Cr; mg/dL) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN; mg/dL) were also detected. When collecting blood samples from mice, after blood collection, the blood was immediately put into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes (without adding anticoagulants or preservatives). The blood was left to stand at room temperature for 30–60 min and then centrifuged at a relative centrifugal force (RCF) of 14,000 g for 10–15 min. The separated serum was aspirated with a pipette and transferred to a clean centrifuge tube. The serum sample could be centrifuged at 14,000 g for another 2–3 min to separate any remaining red blood cells. The separated serum was transferred to a clean centrifuge tube and detected according to the instructions of the kit.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between groups were carried out using one - way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism 8.0. For the Morris Water Maze escape latency data, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

The impacts of rea on the behavioral tests of 5×FAD mice

To assess the effect of ReA on the memory cognitive ability of 5×FAD mice, at three months of age, Rehmannioside A or Donepezil (ReA/DP) was administered to the mice via continuous gavage for three months, and the impact of the drug on the behavioral aspects of the mice was examined using the object recognition test, Y-maze test, and Morris water maze test. As all experimental mice were male, sex-based differences were not evaluated in this study.

The behavioral outcomes of the water maze indicated that during the positioning and navigation training, the 5×FAD group exhibited longer escape latencies compared to the WT group. Treatment with ReA, particularly at medium and high doses, significantly shortened the escape latency in 5×FAD mice (Fig. 1B). During the spatial probe trial, 5×FAD mice spent less time in the target quadrant and showed fewer platform crossings than WT mice. Medium and high doses of ReA significantly increased the time spent in the target quadrant and the number of platform crossings (P < 0.01, Fig. 1C-F). The low dose of ReA showed a less pronounced improvement.

The results of the Y-maze test showed that the 5×FAD mice administered low, medium, and high doses of ReA showed a significant improvement in working memory compared to the untreated 5×FAD group mice (P < 0.01, Fig. 1G). Collectively, the behavioral results showed that moderate amounts of ReA were effective in improving the learning memory ability and the cognitive impairment.

The results of the new object recognition test showed that the 5×FAD mice given the medium- and high-dose group of ReA had a significant change in preference for fresh objects compared to the 5×FAD group mice (P < 0.01), while the low-dose group of ReA had no significant improvement in the preference for new objects in the 5×FAD mice (Fig. 1H).

ReA reduces hippocampal Aβ expression levels in 5×FAD mice

Aβ plaque load and Iba1-positive cells were quantified as the percentage of area covered in the hippocampal CA1 and DG regions using ImageJ software. The staining results showed that the Aβ plaques in the hippocampal region of the brain were significantly more in the 5×FAD group than in the WT group; after the administration of medium and high doses of ReA, the Aβ plaques in the CA1 and dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampal region of 5×FAD mice were significantly reduced (P < 0.01, Fig. 2). We compared the number of Iba1 - positive microglia cells that reflect the neuroinflammation state in the hippocampus. The results indicated that the 5×FAD mice given medium and high - dose groups of ReA were effective in reducing the number of microglia cells in the 5×FAD mice compared to the 5×FAD group mice, in both the CA1 region and the DG (Fig. 2). Medium- and high-dose ReA treatment reduced plaque density by ~ 38% and ~ 45%, respectively (p < 0.01). Effect of rea on oxidative stress and inflammatory in the brain tissue of 5×FAD mice

The results showed that the activities of the antioxidant enzymes SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT were significantly increased, while the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly decreased after treatment with medium to high doses of ReA (Fig. 4A–F). ReA treatment significantly increased antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD + 28%, GSH-Px + 34%, CAT + 31%) while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (TNF-α -22%, IL-1β -25%, IL-6 -27%, all p < 0.01). Cytokines were quantified in hippocampal tissue homogenates and expressed in pg/mg protein. It is clear that ReA had a more beneficial antioxidant effect and alleviated the oxidative stress in the hippocampus of 5×FAD mice.

Safety evaluation

In this research, serum levels of ALT, AST, Cr, and BUN were measured to evaluate liver and kidney function in different groups of 5×FAD mice (Fig. 5). The findings showed no significant differences in these parameters among the experimental groups, suggesting that ReA did not cause any remarkable adverse effects on hepatic or renal function within the tested dose range, thus demonstrating its good safety profile.

Discussion

This study explored the effects of ReA on the behavior, Aβ expression, oxidative stress, and inflammation levels in the hippocampal region of 5×FAD mice through experiments. The results indicated that appropriate amounts of ReA could effectively enhance the learning and memory abilities and cognitive impairments of 5×FAD mice, reduce Aβ plaque deposition in the hippocampal region, lower the number of microglial cells, raise the expression levels of PSD 95 and synapsin-1 proteins, alleviate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, and had no evident adverse effects on liver and kidney functions within the tested dose range, showing good safety. Importantly, ReA administration showed no adverse effects on hepatic or renal function within the tested dose range. This favorable safety profile may provide a translational advantage over some existing AD therapeutics10.

AD presents as a progressive degenerative neurological disorder characterized by cerebral neurodegeneration19. A key neuropathological signature shows through Aβ peptide aggregation within cortical areas, correlating with predominant cognitive decline as the main clinical manifestation20,21. Contemporary therapeutic models prioritize Aβ-targeted pharmacotherapeutics22. Despite immunotherapeutic approaches showing efficacy in Aβ plaque clearance, clinical studies reveal insufficient alleviation of cognitive deficits across AD populations23. Complementary medicinal systems offer economically viable alternatives with favorable therapeutic indices, such as lithopanax methyl derivatives showing significant nootropic effects in experimental AD models with negligible adverse event profiles24,25. Our findings position ReA among other plant-derived compounds with reported benefits in AD models, such as curcumin26, resveratrol27, and ginsenosides28. The novelty of ReA lies in its specific efficacy in a robust amyloidosis model (5×FAD) and its demonstrated multi-target action on Aβ, inflammation, and oxidative stress concurrently.

ReA demonstrates multifaceted biological activities including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neurotrophic capabilities, indicating therapeutic potential in reducing neural injury29,30. Its pathophysiological mechanisms in AD are still not fully characterized31,32. Integrative analysis of behavioral measures validates ReA’s cognitive-enhancing properties in transgenic AD murine models. Moreover, this study shows that ReA might exert its beneficial effects by modulating multiple signaling pathways related to synaptic plasticity, Aβ clearance, and neuroinflammation. Further comprehensive studies are necessary to accurately define the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying ReA’s action in the context of AD, which could potentially lead to the development of new and more effective therapeutic strategies for this devastating neurodegenerative disorder.

Through bidirectional interactions with Aβ homeostasis, oxidative stress mechanisms amplify AD pathogenesis33. Specifically, the generation of ROS is stimulated by Aβ elevation34, while oxidative byproducts in turn facilitate APP hyperexpression and subsequent Aβ plaque formation35. The observed upregulation of hippocampal antioxidant enzymes (SOD, GSH-Px) and concomitant reduction in lipid peroxidation (MDA) indicate that ReA can restore redox equilibrium, a critical factor in mitigating the oxidative damage that exacerbates Aβ toxicity and neuronal death in AD 36. Similarly, the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and microglial activation suggests that ReA dampens the chronic neuroinflammatory response, a key driver of disease progression37,38. Treatment with ReA exerted concerted beneficial effects by decreasing hippocampal Aβ deposition and microglial activation indices, accompanied by selective suppression of pro - inflammatory mediators (IL − 1β, IL − 6, TNF - α). These findings suggest ReA as a modulator of neuroinflammatory pathways and amyloid clearance mechanisms.

The multifaceted effects of ReA observed in this study suggest it may act on core AD pathogenic pathways. The reduction in oxidative stress is consistent with potential activation of the Nrf2 antioxidant signaling pathway, as previously suggested39. The concurrent mitigation of neuroinflammation points to a possible inhibition of the NF-κB pathway40. Furthermore, the restoration of synaptic protein levels (PSD-95, synapsin-1) invites speculation about the modulation of synaptic plasticity pathways, such as CREB-BDNF signaling, which warrants future investigation.

Limitations of the study

While this study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of ReA in a 5×FAD AD model, it has several limitations. First, the study was conducted exclusively in male mice; future investigations should include female subjects to evaluate potential sex-dependent effects. Second, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying ReA’s benefits (e.g., Caspase-3, Bax/Bcl-2), such as its direct molecular targets and its effects on specific pathways like apoptosis, remain to be fully elucidated. Third, the pharmacokinetic profile of ReA and its long-term efficacy were not assessed. These aspects represent important directions for future research.

In conclusion, the current study comprehensively explored the effects of ReA on 5×FAD mice, uncovering its remarkable multifaceted therapeutic potential in Alzheimer’s disease. ReA showed efficacy in improving cognitive impairments, reducing Aβ plaque deposition, modulating neuroinflammatory responses, and restoring redox balance. These findings not only highlight ReA’s potential as a new therapeutic agent but also emphasize the importance of further in-depth research to fully clarify its underlying molecular mechanisms.
