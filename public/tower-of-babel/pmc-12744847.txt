Community and bioethicists’ perspectives on iPSC research with biobanked samples collected using broad consent
Authors: P.A. Ikhane, T. Yusuf, O. Adeyemo, T.O. Ogundiran, S.N. Adebamowo, C.A. Adebamowo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryUsing anonymized, biobanked samples for induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) creates new and unresolved ethical dilemmas. To elucidate the issues, we studied the perspectives of community members and bioethicists involved in the collection of the Yoruba Resident in Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI) HapMap samples. We found support for broad consent, commercial use of samples, more benefit sharing, sustained engagement of the community and local researchers, particularly for novel research, where a long time has elapsed between samples’ collection and new research projects, and in the oversight of biobanked samples. Broad consent was durable when coupled with sustained community engagement, transparent governance, and practical mechanisms for reciprocal benefit.

Introduction

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are adult cells, such as skin or blood cells, that have been genetically reprogrammed to an embryonic-like pluripotent state, enabling them to differentiate into any cell type in the body. Their discovery has created unique opportunities to research and model the development of tissues, organs, and diseases and the molecular impacts of genomic variations for regenerative and personalized medicine (Hamazaki et al., 2017; Karagiannis et al., 2019; Kleiman and Engle 2021; Mallapaty 2025) To date, most iPSC research has taken place in developed countries leading to disparities in iPSC research (Figure 1). Cells, including those from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), stored in biorepositories are readily available resources for creating iPSC and can contribute to global iPSC research, thereby reducing the disparities. Cells derived from the International Haplotype Mapping (HapMap) Project are one of banked samples available for iPSC research. The HapMap Project, a global collaboration launched in 2002 to develop a haplotype map of the human genome, describes common patterns of genetic variation from different populations (International HapMap Consortium 2003). In the first phase, samples were collected from the Yoruba Resident in Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI), because of the strong relationship between the community and the research team. Samples were also collected from Utah Residents with Northern and Western European Ancestry, Han Chinese in Beijing, and Japanese in Tokyo. Subsequent phases of the HapMap collected samples from other parts of the world. The resulting publicly available resource profoundly impacted genomics research by enabling genome-wide association studies and accelerating medical discoveries and therapeutic developments, enabling precision medicine and personalized healthcare, and advancing population genetics and evolutionary biology (Manolio et al., 2008; Manolio and Collins 2009; International HapMap Consortium, Altshuler et al., 2010).

The YRI samples were donated anonymously, immortalized, and stored at the Corriell Institute, NJ, USA, from where researchers obtain them for research. These and similar samples are a potential resource for iPSCs (International HapMap Consortium 2003; Manolio et al., 2008). They have the advantage that they have undergone deep genotypic and phenotypic characterization, which supports rational cell line selection (Kilpinen et al., 2017; Merkle et al., 2022; Pantazis et al., 2022). The YRI samples are particularly valuable because of their ancestral geographical origin, which would improve diversity in genomic and iPSC research and accelerate discoveries (Ghosh et al., 2022; Moore et al., 2022). However, iPSC technology was not envisaged and had not developed at the time these samples were collected. This raises ethical and social questions, including the determination of a suitable consent framework if they are to be used for iPSCs, the ethical regulation of their use in research, ensuring that such use aligns with the preferences of the donors or their communities, and establishing mechanisms for sharing benefits from commercial products derived from such research (Lomax et al., 2015; Moradi et al., 2019; Heyder et al., 2020).

During the implementation of the HapMap project, there was extensive community engagement and establishment of a Community Advisory Board (CAB). The HapMap samples were collected anonymously using broad consent (International HapMap Consortium 2003; Manolio et al., 2008; Lowenthal et al., 2012). The community and donors were informed that the samples would not be used for reproductive cloning and that the risks of future studies were unlikely to be different in kind from the risks associated with the HapMap project itself (Rotimi et al., 2007).

The YRI CAB has remained functional since its establishment in 2002 to date with support from the Nigerian PI of the YRI sampling, receiving and disseminating reports on the users and uses of the YRI samples stored at the Corriell Institute, NJ (Rotimi et al., 2007; Lowenthal et al., 2012). Similarly, the strong relationships between the community members and the local researchers have been maintained, and all the researchers and bioethicists involved in the YRI HapMap project have remained directly or indirectly in touch with each other and with the community while remaining engaged in global discussions on ethical issues surrounding genomics technologies in LMICs. This unique set of circumstances provided us with an opportunity to study the knowledge, attitudes, and recommendations of community members and bioethicists who were involved in the collection, banking, and distribution of the YRI samples to the use of these samples for iPSC research. We therefore conducted key informant interviews with CAB members and bioethicists and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with randomly selected community members as described in supplemental methods. The outcome of our study would inform genomic researchers about community and bioethicists’ perspectives on the scope of broad consent, sharing of benefits, commercialization, and property rights when using anonymously donated and biobanked samples from LMICs for research using novel technologies such as iPSC.

Perspectives of community members and bioethicists on iPSC research using biobanked samples

The YRI CAB and community members stated that they lacked adequate knowledge of iPSC research. They expressed trust in the local principal investigator (LPI) of the HapMap study to ensure that their preferences for the permissible use of the YRI samples were upheld and that they would benefit from any commercialization or profit derived from research using their samples. They predicated this trust on the long history of interactions between members of the community and the investigators in contrast with researchers that they do not know and who do not know them. They reported that since the end of the HapMap Project in their community, they have been regularly informed about the use of their samples via quarterly reports from the Coriell Institute, discussions with the community, and responses to their questions about the reports.

Three main themes and several subthemes emerged from our analysis of the interviews and discussions. These are: (1) permission for use of YRI samples for iPSC research, (2) commercial use of samples and benefit sharing, and (3) trust building in community-based research. Excerpts from the interviews and discussions are in Table S1.

Permission for the use of YRI samples for iPSC research

Adequacy of HapMap consent for creating iPSCs using YRI samples

All the CAB members, most of the FGD participants, and some of the bioethicists stated that they believe the broad consent obtained at the time of collecting the YRI samples is sufficient for the use of the samples for iPSCs because they trust the local research team and their ability to ensure protection of the community’s interests (Table S1 section 1a). CAB members believed that the LPI would ensure that the samples are used in the best ethical and scientific manner (Table S1 section 1b). However, some community members and bioethicists stated that the broad consent is insufficient to allow the use of the YRI samples for iPSC research (Table S1 section 1c).

The main reason why broad consent was considered insufficient is the long duration between the samples’ collection and use for iPSCs. Community members preferred that the community be re-contacted before the YRI samples are used for iPSCs (Table S1 section 1d). This position was supported by one of the bioethicists who suggested that it is necessary to consult the community on this issue (Table S1 section 1d).

Hierarchy of permission for the use of YRI samples for iPSC research

Regarding who should be consulted for permission to use the YRI samples given that the samples were donated anonymously, all participants said members of the donor community including members of the CAB, where the CAB still exists, should be consulted (Table S1 section 1e). Where there is no functioning CAB, participants recommended that community leaders should be consulted (Table S1 Section 1f). Most participants strongly objected to consulting religious leaders and preferred non-religious affiliated leadership structures (Table S1 section 1g). One community member however agreed with consulting religious leaders (Table S1 section 1h).

Community members preferred local community leaders over institutional or national ethics committees for ethical oversight of the samples if the CAB no longer existed or could not be contacted because they believe that the ethics committees may lack sufficient knowledge about the research and the communities (Table S1 section 1i). A CAB member noted that the CAB always takes account of the opinion of the community and would recommend that the researchers do the same (Table S1 section 1j).

Types of iPSC research that can be conducted on the YRI samples

Most of the CAB and community members and the bioethicists supported the use of YRI samples for iPSC research that may lead to the discovery of treatment for diseases, including use in the development of tissue organoids (Table S1 section 1k). The possibility of using the YRI samples for reproductive cloning research was a concern for the study participants except a community member who had no objection (Table S1 section 1l). Some were surprised at the possibility but expressed support because of scientific progress and its potential benefits. Most community members supported the use of the samples for any type of research that improves health, as long as such use does not harm the donors or their community (Table S1 section 1m).

The CAB and community members preferred that the YRI samples and iPSCs derived from them should be shared only with researchers with integrity who would use the samples and iPSCs strictly for stated purposes. They recommended that such use should be aimed at improving global health and that samples should never be sold (Table S1 section 1n). Most CAB and community members did not want the samples to be shared with researchers known to engage in unconventional research.

One CAB member expressed surprise that other researchers different from those engaged in the community would have access to the samples, even though this was mentioned in the consent form. The CAB member stated after some moments of reflection that the community places the responsibility for verifying the ethical and scientific credentials of such users on the local researchers. Most community members supported this opinion.

Commercial use of samples and benefit sharing

Making profit from commercial research use of samples by companies

Most CAB and community members had positive attitude toward companies making profits from using the YRI samples because they believe that investors deserve to obtain profits from research. Such profits will generate funding for future studies. They said researchers should properly articulate the potential commercial use of biospecimens in the consent process so that the donors and members of their communities are aware. Most CAB members supported sharing samples with commercial entities who may make profits from using the samples because they viewed health research as closely linked to progress in finding solutions to various diseases and health problems (Table S1 section 2a).

Fair sharing of benefits and risks

Community members and bioethicists supported sharing benefits with donor communities. They considered that this is fair returns for the contributions that sample donors and their communities make to research. A bioethicist advocated that considerations should be given to the culture, values, and roles of donor communities when considering benefit sharing (Table S1 section 2b).

Among several ways suggested for commercial entities to share profits with donor communities are the provision of medications to donor communities and building infrastructure. A bioethicist opined that operationalizing benefit sharing including profits with donor communities may be challenging (Table S1 section 2c).

Mechanism for benefit sharing

Some CAB and community members recommended the involvement of government or third parties in guaranteeing the delivery of the benefits by commercial entities. In addition to the government, a community member suggested the World Health Organization as a potential third party that could be involved in ensuring delivery of benefits to donor communities. A CAB member further stated that any mechanism for benefit sharing with donor communities would be effective only if a sharing plan was agreed upon at the start of the research (Table S1 section 2d).

Community engagement in research

Approaching communities for research

CAB and community members suggested that researchers should start engaging by contacting community leaders because the leaders are charged with decision-making on behalf of the community and can give permission for conduct of research (Table S1 section 2e). If community leaders cannot be reached, CAB and community members suggested asking residents for help with contacting them (Table S1 section 2e).

For communities without leaders, most CAB members suggested that researchers can contact groups like the property owners association in the community (Table S1 section 2f). Most community members suggested that researchers can also contact residents directly, while the CAB and some community members suggested other approaches including using other researchers known to the community as intermediaries. This will reassure the community about the researchers’ intentions, and they can learn about the risks and benefits of the research from the researcher they are familiar with.

Researchers’ disposition in approaching the community

CAB, community members, and a bioethicist commented on the need for researchers to be open and honest with the community about their research (Table S1 section 2g). Study participants affirmed that transparency and the willingness to understand communities’ customs and practices would also build trust affirmed study participants. CAB and community members want researchers to understand that building trust is a multi-step process, which must continue during the research and afterward (Table S1 section 2h).

Building the trust and confidence of the community in the researcher

Community members indicated that knowing researchers’ identities is important for building trust (Table S1 section 2i). A CAB member recalled how easy it was for the community to approve the HapMap Project in the community because they already knew the local HapMap researchers. The CAB was also successful in building the community’s trust during the HapMap Project because the members were respected community leaders (Table S1 section 2j).

Once confidence and trust have been built, most CAB members and community members said regular feedback to the community about the research being conducted with their samples helped to maintain the trust and confidence of the community. They further noted that such reports give the community an opportunity to assess the work of the research team (Table S1 section 2k).

Key insights from the qualitative study of CAB, community members, and bioethicists

The CAB, community members, and bioethicists we studied unequivocally supported the use of broad consent for future unspecified use research (FUUR). However, they recommended that researchers should re-engage with the community for any new studies on banked samples, particularly when such studies involve novel technologies such as iPSC and when a long time has elapsed between original consent and new research projects on banked samples. Community members supported sample sharing, but they want the researchers they know and worked with to remain engaged with biobanks and participate in reviewing requesting scientists and projects, to protect their interests. They supported the use of the samples by commercial entities with potential to make profits. However, they want fair sharing of benefits from any commercial use of the samples. Most of the participants were still against reproductive cloning, though a few were willing to accept it if it yields health benefits for humankind.

The use of broad consent in genomics and biobanking research remains controversial (Maseme et al., 2024). While broad consent may cover many foreseeable research uses, it should offer research participants a range of choices regarding the future use of their samples (Grady et al., 2015; Fisher and Layman 2018). Such choice is critical because the risk-benefit or risk-knowledge calculus that underpinned the original consent may change over time, with new knowledge, new research methods, changes in participants’ and communities concerns, preferences, and attitudes, or changes in normative requirements (Lowenthal et al., 2012; Lomax et al., 2015; Wiertz and Boldt 2022; McGuire and Beskow 2010; Tindana and de Vries 2016; Mikkelsen et al., 2019; Lee 2021; Gefenas et al., 2022). Most of our study participants viewed the HapMap consent as broad enough to cover iPSC research, while a few felt that it was not. Some of the bioethicists in our study argued that the emergence of iPSC research after the collection of the YRI samples raised new ethical considerations that were not explicitly discussed, envisaged, or covered by the HapMap consent.

A major factor influencing the attitude of community members to sufficiency of the HapMap’s broad consent for iPSC research is the duration of time between when the HapMap samples were collected and the proposed use of the samples for iPSCs, which was more than two decades. The community members advocated that given this long duration, researchers should re-consent participants where possible or use alternative means of ensuring that the proposed studies are aligned with the wishes of the samples’ donors and their communities. The association between the efficacy of broad consent and the elapsed time from sample donation to the proposed use of the samples is a novel finding in our study. With the proliferation of biobanks throughout the world and increasing duration of samples’ storage, elapsed time of storage may become an important consideration in whether donors or their communities need to be re-consented before FUUR with banked samples.

The community members in our study disapproved of selling the YRI samples, and they supported their use by commercial entities provided that the outcomes benefit the global community and are consistent with the consent that they gave. They acknowledged the role of private investment in advancing medical research but emphasized that benefits should be shared equitably (Bedeker et al., 2022). No explicit benefit-sharing agreement was concluded at the time the community participated in the HapMap Project, and the consent explicitly stated that there would be no additional payments if the samples were used to create commercial products. The ethical principles and implementation strategies for benefit sharing from commercialization in genomic research are not well developed (Nicol et al., 2001; Marshall et al., 2022). This lack of principles and mechanisms for benefit sharing may increase the risks of exploitation, particularly for LMIC populations where there is limited knowledge, resources, tradition, and legal framework for commercialization of research (Mwaka et al., 2022). To address this issue, our study participants urged the use of transparent benefit-sharing agreements (Ravinetto and Dierickx 2018). Participants suggested that the benefits can include subsidies of the cost of the products of the research for community members, investments in the communities, training of local researchers, and infrastructure development. They suggested that third parties could serve as guarantors of benefit-sharing agreements, and they emphasized that such agreements should be in place before commencement of research (Yakubu et al., 2023). Some of the bioethicists, while agreeing with benefit sharing in principle, raised concerns about the feasibility of its implementation. Nevertheless, they urged the scientific community to develop policies and mechanisms to ensure fair benefit sharing in commercial research.

Several strategies including research exemptions, meta-consent models that include re-contacting donors, consent for ethical governance, direct engagement of donors’ communities, and partnering with research ethics authorities, governments, or independent third parties have been suggested for ethical oversight of research using biobanked specimens (Aalto-Setälä et al., 2009; Lovell-Badge et al., 2021; Mummery and Anthony 2021; Fabbri et al., 2023). Research exemptions may not sufficiently account for the risks associated with new research or donor communities’ preferences. Re-contacting donors is very expensive and inefficient (Miller and Wertheimer 2007; Lowenthal et al., 2012; Heyder et al., 2020). It’s also not practicable for anonymous samples or where participants can no longer be identified (Heyder et al., 2020). Local institutions and local or national research ethics authorities may lack sufficient scientific or historical knowledge about the original research and sample donors, and they may lack sufficient resources or interest in providing ethical oversight. Directly engaging donor communities may allow decisions on the use of banked samples to be influenced by and reflect the shared history and culture of the donors. This is particularly useful where the sample donors are anonymous. However, this approach may expose communities to the risk of group/dignitary harm (Aalto-Setälä et al., 2009). Obtaining consent for samples’ governance may be useful for future samples’ collections but would not be applicable to samples already collected without applying this mechanism. Re-engaging donor communities through the community leaders, CABs, and community representatives was the most preferred options by the participants in our study (Borthwick et al., 2023). There was strong opposition to the involvement of religious leaders because of the diversity of religious affiliation and the need to avoid religious conflicts.

While there has been a lot of debate about data and samples’ sharing, very little is known about the attitudes of donor communities regarding who should be able to access their samples, and how and what the samples can be used for (Dixon-Woods et al., 2017). The community members in our study emphasized the importance of integrity of the researchers who access their samples ensuring that the researchers’ methods are conventional. They were surprised to learn that the samples could be used by researchers that they or the HapMap researchers who worked with their community do not know. Because the community members lack knowledge, access, power, information, and resources to monitor the use of their samples and the researchers to whom the samples are distributed, they strongly urged for continued engagement of the researchers and institutions that worked with them during sample collection in all decisions about who receives their samples and what the samples are used for in future. They recommended this because they know and trust these individuals and institutions, and they believe that they can hold these researchers accountable. They reiterated that trust in the researchers and the institutions that they know is the basis for their support for the sufficiency of the HapMap’s broad consent for use of their samples for iPSC research.

There was general support for iPSC research that is aimed at developing treatments for diseases and improving healthcare, including the development of tissue organoids for global populations. Most of the CAB and community members in our study strongly opposed using iPSCs derived from the YRI samples for reproductive cloning citing religious and moral concerns; however, a community member and a CAB member had no objection to reproductive cloning. While expressing surprise about the possibility to such research, they were willing to support it if it would bring health benefits. This finding is consistent with the general attitude to the use of iPSCs in reproductive cloning research found in other studies (Moradi et al., 2019). This attitude is based on concerns about the health and safety risks of the procedure, loss of individuality, confusion about parenthood and impact on family structures, religious objections to what amounts to replication of life in contrast to its reproduction, the moral status of the iPSC-derived embryo, and potential misuse of the technology (Hagelin 2004; Xue and Shang 2022; Aremu et al., 2024; Le Goff, Jeffries Hein et al., 2024). Despite this, studies from different parts of the world show that 6%–15% of populations support the use of iPSCs for reproductive cloning. Nevertheless, even in the most liberal societies, the support tends to be for therapeutic or basic science research applications (Hendriks et al., 2015; Akatsuka et al., 2021).

Trust emerged as the most important moral philosophical principle underpinning individuals’ and communities’ engagement with research in general, with the HapMap project, subsequent banking of donor samples, and their attitude to future unspecified use research. Similar to the findings from other studies, our study participants said they often lack sufficient knowledge, access, power, and information to adequately evaluate the risks and benefits of research, or to hold researchers accountable for promises made, or to follow up on issues that may arise after research participation (McCaughey et al., 2016; Bukini et al., 2020). This is particularly true of complex genomics research projects using biobanked samples (Lowenthal et al., 2012). Participants therefore rely on trust in the institutions and the researchers conducting the research, as well as the communications they receive about the research before deciding to participate (Bukini et al., 2020; Orzechowski et al., 2021). The community members in our study indicated that trust in the researchers who engaged them for the HapMap project would similarly influence their attitude to allow the use of the YRI samples for iPSC research. While trust is widely recognized as a fundamental factor in research relationships and the willingness to participate in research, it is difficult to measure directly because it encompasses complex, context-dependent factors, including communication, cultural alignment, and historical experiences with researchers and research institutions (Buchan 2009; Bauer and Freitag 2017). Indicators such as engagement quality, respect, transparency, and evidence of community benefits are often used as proxies to gauge trust, though these lack standardization (Lansing et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Our study has several implications for global genomic researchers and bioethicists. Our findings highlight the value of strong relationships/collaborations between researchers and communities where they conduct research in contrast to “extractive research” relationships. Further, the community involved in the YRI sample collection had derived benefits from previous research in their community, including return of research results, help with navigating the healthcare system, and treatments for “simple” ailments within the community by the researchers. Therefore, they trusted the researchers and the new HapMap research project when it was introduced. The expectation by the community that the local researchers would remain involved in decision-making about their samples, helping to verify the science for which the samples are being requested, the integrity of the requestors/scientists, the outcomes of the research, and the potential for benefit sharing, is a reflection of this trust. However, this is novel and different from existing biobanking and sample-sharing mechanisms and practices. It has implications for global biobanking practices and genomic research.

Our study has some limitations. We evaluated the perspectives of only the YRI community, and these may be different from that of the other communities that contributed samples to the HapMap Project or indeed other biobanks, thereby limiting the generalizability of our results. However, we did this because of the unique set of circumstances that made our study possible. We used qualitative methods, which are vulnerable to subjective biases. In this study in particular, the history of collaboration between the local researchers and the community may have encouraged participants to give socially desirable responses. The researchers’ personal experiences, preconceptions, and interpretations may also inadvertently influence the collection and analysis of data, leading to findings that may reflect individual perspectives rather than objective realities.

Our study highlights the complex ethical, cultural and practical considerations involved in using biobanked samples, particularly from LMICs, for novel research such as iPSC. We found support for broad consent and community re-engagement, particularly with increasing duration of time between samples’ collection and new projects. Our participants support commercialization of research but wanted more benefit sharing. Trust was the most important moral philosophical principle underpinning the attitude of participants research in general, collection, storage, and distribution of YRI samples and use of those samples in future unspecified use research.
