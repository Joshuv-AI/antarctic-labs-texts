Markov chains, AR linear models, and regular variation
Piotr Dyszewski, Tamara Mika

Abstract
. We investigate multivariate regular variation in the context of time-homogeneous Markov chains on general vector spaces and in random coefficient linear models. In the first part, we show that the regular variation of the stationary distribution can be derived from that of the innovations, provided that the chain satisfies a certain monotonicity condition with respect to a gauge function. In the second part, we study random linear models with random coefficients defined by an explicit iterative scheme. We prove that the precise structure of the underlying chain affects the form of the associated spectral measure.



1.
Introduction

Regular variation is a key concept in the analysis of heavy-tailed random phenomena,
which arise in areas such as finance, insurance or meteorology.
Originally, the theory was developed for
univariate functions and random variables in
\mathbb{R}
, see
e.g. Bingham et al.
[
2
]
and Resnick
[
23
]
.
Over the years, the framework has been extended to random
vectors
[
6
,
22
]
. This, in turn, quickly
led to the study of regular variation in function spaces
[
21
,
5
]
.
Important contributions in this direction include Hult and Lindskog
[
11
]
,
who proposed the
M_{0}
-convergence to study measures on metric spaces with scalar multiplication.
Building on these ideas, Meinguet and Segers
[
15
]
and
Segers et al.
[
25
]
provided a systematic treatment of
regularly varying time-series in Banach spaces,
extending classical results and introducing new aspects such as polar
decompositions in star-shaped metric spaces.
For a more in-depth introduction to the topic of regular variation we refer to
[
14
,
17
]
.

A theoretical study of heavy-tailed phenomena requires an investigation of time-series models,
which are very often a Markov chain or a function of such.
A useful tool in studying the extreme behaviour of Markov chains is
the so-called
tail chain
, which arises under
a regularity assumption on the transition kernel
[
24
]

that heuristically means
that the Markov chain in question is asymptotically multiplicative.
One typically works with a stationary version of a Markov chain.
In this direction the analysis of Markov kernels was first proposed by Smith
[
26
]

in the univariate case and by Perfekt
[
19
,
20
]
in the multivariate case.
The first purpose
of the present article is to treat the non-stationary case and later pivot it
to the description of regular variation of the stationary measure. These types of
reasoning are known in the literature only on the real line
[
8
,
18
,
4
]

or for very particular Markov chain in the multivariate setting
[
7
]
.

In the second part of this paper, following
[
12
]
,
we study a class of random coefficient linear models.
This corresponds to random vectors of the form

(1.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}X_{j}Z_{j},

where
\{Z_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed (iid)
random vectors, and
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a matrix-valued Markov
chain that is predictable with respect to the natural filtration of
\{Z_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
The motivation for studying the tail asymptotics of the random vector (
1.1
)
lies in its ability to capture extreme events in complex stochastic systems.
The main examples include random coefficient linear models, stationary solutions to the
random difference equation, and stochastic integrals, see
[
12
, Section 2]
.
When the innovations
(Z_{j})
follow heavy-tailed distributions,
the large values of (
1.1
) are typically driven by a few exceptionally
large realizations of the noise variables,
amplified or dampened through the random coefficients
(Z_{j})
.
Understanding this mechanism is crucial in applications where joint extremes play a
central role, such as financial markets experiencing simultaneous crashes,
insurance portfolios exposed to catastrophic losses,
or telecommunication networks subject to heavy traffic bursts.
The asymptotic analysis of tails provides a description of the dependence
structure between different components of (
1.1
) in extreme regimes.

We extend this framework to a broader setting within the context of Banach algebras.
Under these generalised conditions, we show that the random series (
1.1
)
exhibits a regularly varying distribution.
Furthermore, we provide a detailed characterization of its associated spectral measure.

The article is organised as follows.
Section
2
introduces the concept of multivariate
regular variation and outlines the main results of the paper.
Section
3
illustrates these findings in the case of the random
difference equation.
Finally, Section
4
presents all proofs,
beginning with an equivalent reformulation of multivariate regular variation into a more
tractable form.

2.
Main results

In this section, we provide a concise overview of multivariate regular variation
and summarize the main contributions of the paper.
While our primary motivation comes from finite-dimensional random vectors,
we develop and prove the results in the more general framework of
separable Banach spaces.
Some of our results also remain valid in the broader context of star-shaped metric spaces,
but to keep the presentation concise, we restrict attention to the setting of vector spaces.

2.1.
Multivariate regular variation

Throughout this article, let
(\mathcal{V},\|\cdot\|)
denote a separable
Banach space over the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
.
The central focus of this manuscript is the concept of
regular variation for
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vectors,
that is, random elements taking values in
\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A distribution of a
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vector

{\bf X}
is called
regularly varying
if

\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{{\bf X}}{\|{\bf X}\|}\in{\rm d}\theta\>,\>\frac{\|{\bf X}\|}{t}\in{\rm d}r\>\middle|\|{\bf X}\|>t\right]\to\mathbb{P}\left[\Theta\in{\rm d}\theta\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Z\in{\rm d}r\right]

weakly as
t\to\infty
, where
\Theta
is a random variable taking values in

\mathcal{S}=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}\>:\>\|{\bf x}\|=1\right\},

and
Z
is a random variable with a pure Pareto distribution given by

\mathbb{P}[Z>t]=t^{-\alpha},\qquad t\geq 1,

for some
\alpha>0
. In this case, we write that
{\bf X}
is
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta)

or simply
{\bf X}\in{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta)
.

Note that the conditions in Definition
2.1
imply that
for any positive constant
c
, as
x\to\infty

(2.1)

\frac{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}\|>cx]}{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}\|>x]}\to c^{-\alpha}.

This shows that the tails of
\|{\bf X}\|
are regularly varying with index
-\alpha
.

Loosely speaking, the distribution of a
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vector
{\bf X}

is said to be regularly varying if the random variable
\|{\bf X}\|
has a regularly varying tail,
and the direction
{\bf X}/\|{\bf X}\|
is asymptotically independent of the radial component,
conditioned on
\|{\bf X}\|
being large.

By
[
25
, Proposition 3.1]
,
regular variation in the sense of Definition
2.1
is equivalent
to regular variation in the sense of Hult and Lindskog
[
11
]
,
formulated in terms of
M_{0}
-convergence.
We will return to this relation in more detail in Section
4
.

2.2.
Markov chains

We now present a detailed framework for our results
on the regular variation of
\mathcal{V}
-valued Markov chains.
Throughout, let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{V})
denote the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
on
\mathcal{V}
, i.e., the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all open subsets of
\mathcal{V}
.

Recall that a function
{\rm P}\colon\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{V})\to[0,1]

is called a transition function if,
for any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
, the map
A\mapsto{\rm P}({\bf x},A)
is a
probability measure on
\mathcal{V}
,
and for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{V})
, the map
{\bf x}\mapsto{\rm P}({\bf x},A)
is Borel measurable.

Consider a time-homogeneous Markov chain

\{{\bf X}_{n}^{{\bf x}}:n\in\mathbb{N},\,{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}\}
on
\mathcal{V}

with transition function
{\rm P}
.
That is a family of random elements in
\mathcal{V}

on a common probability space, satisfying the following conditions:

1. For any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,
\mathbb{P}[{\bf X}_{0}^{{\bf x}}={\bf x}]=1
.

2. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,
the regular conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{n+1}^{{\bf x}}

given
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{{\bf x}}=\sigma({\bf X}_{j}^{{\bf x}}:j\in\mathbb{N},\,j\leq n)

has the form

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf X}_{n+1}^{{\bf x}}\in{\rm d}{\bf y}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\bf x}\right]={\rm P}\!\left({\bf X}_{n}^{{\bf x}},{\rm d}{\bf y}\right).

Our goal is to analyse the impact of heavy-tailed innovations on the extremal
behaviour of the sequence
\{{\bf X}_{n}^{{\bf x}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
particularly in scenarios where the principle of one big jump applies.

Assumption 2.2
.

The following four conditions are satisfied:

Innovations:

For any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}}{\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|}\in{\rm d}\theta\>,\>\frac{\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|}{t}\in{\rm d}r\>\middle|\>\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|>t\right]\to\mathbb{P}\left[\Theta({\bf x})\in{\rm d}\theta\right]\mathbb{P}\left[Z\in{\rm d}r\right]

weakly as
t\to\infty
, where
\Theta({\bf x})

takes values in
\mathcal{S}
,
and
Z
follows a pure Pareto distribution with parameter
\alpha
.

Asymptotic stability:

For any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,
there exists a random element
{\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x}
of
\mathcal{V}
such that

\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{t}{\bf X}_{1}^{t{\bf x}}\in{\rm d}{\bf y}\right]\to\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x}\in{\rm d}{\bf y}\right]

weakly as
t\to\infty
, uniformly in
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
on bounded sets.
That is, for any continuous and bounded function

f\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
,
and for any
R>0
, as
t\to\infty
,

\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|\leq R}\left|\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(t^{-1}{\bf X}_{1}^{t{\bf x}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[f({\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x})\right]\right|\to 0.

Comparability:

There exists a reference point
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}

such that,
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|>t\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf X}_{1}^{{\bf x}_{0}}\|>t\right]}=R({\bf x}),

where
R\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,+\infty)
is a locally bounded function.

Single jumps:

For all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf X}_{2}^{\bf x}\|>t,\>\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|\in(M,t/M)\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf X}_{1}^{{\bf x}_{0}}\|>t\right]}=0,

where
{\bf x}_{0}
is the reference point in

\mathcal{V}
specified in the previous condition.

The second condition in Assumption
2.2
implies that the innovations in the Markov
chain under consideration are heavy-tailed.
The first condition quantifies the impact of large jumps on the future evolution of the process,
aligning with the condition proposed by
[
24
]
in
the context of the real line.
The final condition ensures that the sequence
\{{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

adheres to the principle of a single big jump.
Notably, this framework reveals a Markovian structure in the extremes of
{\bf X}^{\bf x}
.
To describe this, let
\{{\bf Z}_{n}^{\bf x}\>:\>n\in\mathbb{N},\>x\in\mathcal{V}\}

be a Markov chain with transition function
\mathbb{P}[{\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x}\in\cdot]
, where

{\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x}
is the random element of
\mathcal{V}
appearing in the second condition
in Assumption
2.2
.
Note that
\{{\bf Z}_{n}^{\bf x}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is homogeneous in
{\bf x}
.
Indeed, for any
c>0
and any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
by definition
one must have that
{\bf Z}_{1}^{c{\bf x}}
and
c{\bf Z}_{1}^{{\bf x}}
have the same law.
This by Markov property implies that
the processes
\{{\bf Z}_{n}^{c{\bf x}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and

\{c{\bf Z}_{n}^{{\bf x}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
have the same law.

Let also
\kappa\colon\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{S}
be given via

\kappa({\bf x})={\bf x}/\|{\bf x}\|
, where

\mathcal{B}=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}\>:\>\|{\bf x}\|\leq 1\right\}.

Our first result asserts regular variation of

{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}
’s. We note that in contrast to the
literature
[
1
,
13
]
we study the extremes of

{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}
conditioned on
\|{\bf X}_{n}^{{\bf x}}\|
being large rather than
\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let the Assumption
2.2
be in force. For any

n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
the law of
{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}

is regularly varying. More precisely

{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\in{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta_{n}^{\bf x})
,
where

\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{n}^{\bf x}\in\cdot]=C_{n}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[R({\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x})\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\Theta({\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x})}\in\>\kappa^{-1}(\cdot)\>\right\}}\left\|{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\Theta({\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x})}\right\|^{\alpha}\right]

and

C_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[R({\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x})\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\left\|{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\Theta({\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x})}\right\|\geq 1\right\}}\left\|{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\Theta({\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x})}\right\|^{\alpha}\right].

The processes
\{{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

\{{\bf Z}_{n}^{{\bf x}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\Theta({\bf y})\}_{{\bf y}\in\mathcal{V}}

are all independent in the
representation above.

Our second result addresses the regular variation of the stationary distribution of

{\bf X}
, denoted by a probability measure
\pi
on
\mathcal{V}
, which satisfies

\pi(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{V}}{\rm P}({\bf x},\cdot)\>\pi({\rm d}{\bf x}).

Additionally, we require that, for any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,
the distributions of
\{{\bf X}_{n}^{{\bf x}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge weakly to
\pi
.
To guarantee this, we impose the following classical conditions.

Assumption 2.4
.

Assume that there exists a function
V\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,+\infty)

satisfying the following two conditions:

(i)

There exist constants
\kappa>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

\mathbb{E}[V({\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x})]\leq\gamma V({\bf x})+\kappa

for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
.

(ii)

There exists a constant
\epsilon>0

and a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{V}
such that

(2.3)

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{C}}{\rm P}({\bf x},\cdot)\geq\epsilon\nu(\cdot),

where
\mathcal{C}=\{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}\>:\>V({\bf x})\leq R\}

for some
R>2\kappa/(1-\gamma)
.

Assumption
2.4
guarantees that the stationary distribution
\pi
exists,
is unique, and that
{\bf X}^{\bf x}
converges geometrically fast to
\pi
.
To formalize this, we introduce the
V
-weighted norm:

\|\phi\|_{V}=\sup_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{|\phi({\bf x})|}{1+V({\bf x})}.

Using this notation, we state the following result.

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.4
holds.
Then, the transition function
{\rm P}
has a unique stationary distribution
\pi
.
Moreover, there exist constants
\rho\in(0,1)
and
C>0
such that

\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\phi\left({\bf X}_{n}^{(\cdot)}\right)\right]-\int\phi\>{\rm d}\pi\right\|_{V}\leq C\rho^{n}\left\|\phi(\cdot)-\int\phi\>{\rm d}\pi\right\|_{V}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any function

\phi\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\|\phi\|_{V}<\infty
.

This theorem originates from
[
10
]
.
For a more contemporary treatment, see
[
16
, Section 15]
.
The specific version presented here is taken from
[
9
]
.

Moving towards the regular variation of
\pi
,
recall that for a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{V}
,
the gauge function
\rho_{A}\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,+\infty]
is defined as

\rho_{A}({\bf x})=\inf\{r\in\mathbb{R}\>:\>r>0,\>r{\bf x}\in A\}.

Here, and throughout the article, the infimum of the empty set is taken to be
+\infty
.

We will proceed under an additional assumption.

Assumption 2.6
.

Monotonicity:

There exists a convex,
closed set
K\subseteq\mathcal{V}
such that
0\in\operatorname{int}(K)

and that if
s<t
, then for every

{\bf x}\in\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{s\}]
and
{\bf y}\in\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{t\}]
,

{\rm P}({\bf y},rK^{c})\leq{\rm P}({\bf x},rK^{c})

for any
r>0
.

Contractivity:

The following holds:

\rho_{*}=\sup_{\|\theta\|=1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\bf Z}_{1}^{\theta}\right\|^{\alpha}\right]<1.

Note that the monotonicity assumption can be conveniently reformulated as follows:
if
s<t
, then for any probability measures
\mu
and
\nu

supported on
\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{s\}]
and
\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{t\}]
, respectively,

(2.4)

\int{\rm P}({\bf z},rK^{c})\,\nu({\rm d}{\bf z})\leq\int{\rm P}({\bf z},rK^{c})\,\mu({\rm d}{\bf z})

for any
r>0
.

Since the set
\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{s\}]
behaves like
\tfrac{1}{s}\,\partial K^{c}
,
this condition can be interpreted as follows: the further the Markov chain starts from
0
,
the higher the probability that it will remain away from
0
after one step.

The second condition in Assumption
2.6

is of a more technical nature and is needed to control
\pi(tK^{c})
.
To see this, let
\eta_{{\bf x}}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}[{\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x}\in\cdot]

denote the transition function of
{\bf Z}
.
The homogeneity of
{\bf Z}
implies that

\eta_{c{\bf x}}({\rm d}{\bf y})=\eta_{{\bf x}}(c^{-1}{\rm d}{\bf y})

for any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
and positive
c
, and thus

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\|{\bf Z}_{n}^{\bf x}\|^{\alpha}\,\right|\,{\bf Z}_{n-1}^{\bf x}\right]=\|{\bf Z}_{n-1}^{\bf x}\|^{\alpha}\int\|y\|^{\alpha}\,\eta_{\frac{{\bf Z}_{n-1}^{\bf x}}{\|{\bf Z}_{n-1}^{\bf x}\|}}({\rm d}{\bf y}).

Under the aforementioned contractivity assumption, this yields the bound

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\|{\bf Z}_{n}^{\bf x}\|^{\alpha}\,\right|\,{\bf Z}_{n-1}^{\bf x}\right]\leq\rho_{*}\|{\bf Z}_{n-1}^{\bf x}\|^{\alpha}.

This in turn implies

(2.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\bf Z}_{n}^{\theta}\right\|^{\alpha}\right]\leq\left(\rho_{*}\right)^{n},\qquad\theta\in\mathcal{S}.

Since
K
is assumed to contain an open neighbourhood of the origin of
\mathcal{V}
,
there exists such
\epsilon>0
, depending on
K
, such that
\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf x})\geq\epsilon\|{\bf x}\|^{-1}
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
.
Thus, for all
\theta\in\mathcal{S}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{K^{c}}\!\left({\bf Z}_{n}^{\theta}\right)^{-\alpha}\right]\leq\left(\rho_{*}\right)^{n}/\epsilon^{\alpha}.

In particular, there always exists
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.6)

\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\rho_{K^{c}}\!\left({\bf Z}_{k_{0}}^{\theta}\right)^{-\alpha}\right]<1.

We will use this condition to control
\pi(tK^{c})
in the proof of our main result.
Denote by
K_{0}
the largest cone containing
K
,

K_{0}=\bigcap_{\epsilon>0}\epsilon K.

Note that
K_{0}=\{0\}
if
K
is bounded.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let the assumptions
2.2
,
2.4
and
2.6
be in force.
Assume additionally that

(2.7)

\inf_{\|{\bf x}\|\geq t}V({\bf x})\geq Ct^{\alpha}

for some constant
C>0
as
t\geq 1
and that
\pi(K_{0}^{c})>0
.
Then the stationary distribution of
\{{\bf X}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
conditioned on
K_{0}^{c}
is regularly varying.
More precisely
\pi(\cdot|K_{0}^{c})
is
\mathrm{Rv}(\alpha,\Theta_{\pi})
with

\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{\pi}\in\cdot]=C_{\infty}^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}\left[R({\bf x})\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{\bf Z}_{j}^{\Theta({\bf x})}\in\>\kappa^{-1}(\cdot)\cap K_{0}\>\right\}}\left\|{\bf Z}_{j}^{\Theta({\bf x})}\right\|^{\alpha}\right]\pi({\rm d}{\bf x}),

where

C_{\infty}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}\left[R({\bf x})\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\left\|{\bf Z}_{j}^{\Theta({\bf x})}\right\|\geq 1,\>{\bf Z}_{j}^{\Theta({\bf x})}\in K_{0}\right\}}\left\|{\bf Z}_{j}^{\Theta({\bf x})}\right\|^{\alpha}\right]\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})

and the processes
\{{\bf Z}_{j}^{{\bf x}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\Theta({\bf y})\}_{{\bf y}\in\mathcal{V}}

are independent in the representation above.

2.3.
Random coefficient linear models

We now turn to the study of random coefficient linear models.
We first outline the general framework and subsequently present our results
in two distinct scenarios.

2.3.1.
A general setting

Random coefficient linear models are typically defined in a finite-dimensional space.
However, our arguments allow us to consider a vector space of any dimension.
Since the random series in question involve multiplication,
one is naturally led to the setting of Banach algebras.
In what follows, let
\mathcal{A}
be a Banach algebra.

We aim to study random series of the form

{\bf S}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\bf X}_{j}{\bf N}_{j},

where the
{\bf X}_{j}
’s and
{\bf N}_{j}
’s are random elements of
\mathcal{A}
.
We will always assume that the
{\bf N}_{j}
’s are iid with a generic
copy denoted by
{\bf N}
, such that
{\bf N}\in\mathrm{Rv}(\Theta_{\bf N},\alpha)
.

We would like to examine how the dependence structure between the

{\bf X}_{j}
’s and
{\bf N}_{j}
’s affects the behaviour of
{\bf S}
.
As we will soon see, even small differences can lead to significant
consequences in the asymptotic behaviour of
{\bf S}
. To show that we will consider two scenarios.

Predictable case:

For some filtration
\mathcal{F}_{j}
,
we have
{\bf X}_{j+1}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
,
{\bf N}_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
,
and
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is independent of
\sigma({\bf N}_{j+1},{\bf N}_{j+2},\ldots)
.

Adaptable case:

For some filtration
\mathcal{F}_{j}
,
we have
{\bf X}_{j},{\bf N}_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{j}

is independent of
\sigma({\bf N}_{j+1},{\bf N}_{j+2},\ldots)
.

As a matter of fact, in order to accommodate the long-lasting effects
of large innovations coming from
\{{\bf N}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
,
we will impose even stronger conditions than the two presented above.
We will always assume that
\{{\bf X}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Markov
chain defined by the iteration of a deterministic
function
\psi\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
satisfying the following condition.

Assumption 2.8
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
{\bf m},{\bf n}\in\mathcal{A}
,

(2.8)

\|\psi({\bf m},{\bf n})\|\leq C\|{\bf m}\|(\|{\bf n}\|+1).

Furthermore, suppose that there exist functions

\chi,\zeta\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}

such that for every choice of

{\bf v},{\bf m},{\bf n},{\bf u}\in\mathcal{A}

(2.9)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\psi(t\cdot{\bf v},{\bf m})=\chi({\bf v},{\bf m})\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-1}\psi({\bf n},\ t\cdot{\bf u})=\zeta({\bf n},{\bf u}),

and
\chi
is continuous with respect to the second variable,
while
\zeta
is continuous with respect to the first variable.

As far as the distribution of the innovations is concerned,
we will work under the following assumption.

Assumption 2.9
.

The law of
{\bf N}
is regularly varying
\mathrm{Rv}(\alpha,\Theta_{\bf N})
and

\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf N}_{1}\|\cdot\|{\bf N}_{2}\|>t,\>\|{\bf N}_{1}\|\in(M,t/M)]}{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf N}\|>t]}=0.

2.3.2.
The adaptable case

We will now specify our setting in the adaptable case. We aim to study

{\bf S}_{\bf M}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j},

where
\{{\bf N}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of iid

\mathcal{A}
-valued random vectors, and
\{{\bf M}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

is a sequence of
\mathcal{A}
-valued random vectors
admitting the recursive formula

{\bf M}_{j+1}=\psi({\bf M}_{j},{\bf N}_{j+1}),\qquad j\in\mathbb{N},

with
{\bf M}_{0}={\bf m}\in\mathcal{A}
deterministic,
and some deterministic function
\psi\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}

satisfying Assumption
2.8
.

Consider a function
\psi^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\psi^{*}({\bf n})=\sup_{{\bf m}\neq 0}\left(\frac{1}{\|{\bf m}\|}\|\psi({\bf m},{\bf n})\|\right).

Note that by Assumption
2.8
,
\psi^{*}({\bf n})\leq C(\|{\bf n}\|+1)
.
In what follows, we assume that

(2.10)

\mathbb{E}\left[\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{1})^{\alpha/2}\right]<1.

This ensures that

\Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Theta_{\bf N}\zeta({\bf M}_{j-1},\Theta_{\bf N})\|^{\alpha/2}\right]<\infty.

To state our next result in a most compact form consider a probability measure

\mathbb{P}_{\bf S}
given by

\frac{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\bf S}}{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}}=\Lambda^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{j}}\zeta({\bf M}_{j-1},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{j}})\|^{\alpha/2}.

Let
T_{\bf S}
be a random, positive integer distributed according to

\mathbb{P}_{\bf S}\left[T_{\bf S}=k\>\middle|\>\{{\bf M}_{j},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\right]=\frac{\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}\zeta({\bf M}_{k-1},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})\|^{\alpha/2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{j}}\zeta({\bf M}_{j-1},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{j}})\|^{\alpha/2}}.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let the Assumptions
2.8
and
2.9
be in force.
If additionally (
2.10
) holds then
the law of
{\bf S}_{\bf M}
is
{\rm Rv}(\alpha/2,\Theta_{{\bf S}_{\bf M}})
with

\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{{\bf S}_{\bf M}}\in\cdot]=\mathbb{P}_{{\bf S}}\left[\frac{\Theta_{{\bf N}_{T_{\bf S}}}\zeta\left({\bf M}_{T_{\bf S}},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{T_{\bf S}}}\right)}{\left\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{T_{\bf S}}}\zeta\left({\bf M}_{T_{\bf S}},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{T_{\bf S}}}\right)\right\|}\in\cdot\right].

2.4.
The predictable case

In the predictable case, we will study the series of the form

{\bf R}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j},

where, as previously,
\{{\bf N}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are iid
\mathcal{A}
-valued random vectors, and
\{{\bf H}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Markov chain given by iterations of
\psi
of the form

{\bf H}_{j+1}=\psi({\bf H}_{j},{\bf N}_{j}),\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

We will need to assume that

(2.11)

\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]<1.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
consider a Markov chain
\{{\bf U}^{k}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
given via
{\bf U}^{k}_{0}=\zeta({\bf H}_{k},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})

and for
j\geq 0
,

{\bf U}_{j+1}^{k}=\chi\left({\bf U}_{j}^{k},{\bf N}_{j+k+1}\right).

Theorem 2.11
.

Let the Assumptions
2.8
and
2.9
be in force.
If additionally (
2.11
) holds then the law of
{\bf R}
is regularly varying
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta_{\bf R})
where

\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{\bf R}\in\cdot]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\|\Gamma_{k}\|^{\alpha}}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\Gamma_{j}\|^{\alpha}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\frac{\Gamma_{k}}{\|\Gamma_{k}\|}\in\cdot\right\}}\right],

where

\Gamma_{k}={\bf H}_{k}\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\bf U}_{j}^{k}{\bf N}_{k+j}.

3.
Random difference equation

We illustrate our results using Markov chains defined explicitly by the random difference equation

(3.1)

{\bf Y}_{n+1}^{\bf y}={\bf A}_{n}{\bf Y}_{n}^{\bf y}+{\bf B}_{n},\qquad{\bf Y}_{0}^{\bf y}={\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

where
\{({\bf A}_{n},{\bf B}_{n})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed
pairs of
d\times d
matrices and
d
-dimensional random vectors. Markov chains given
via (
3.1
) and its numerous modifications attracted a fair amount of
attention among the probabilistic and statistical community. We refer the reader
to a monograph
[
3
]
for a detailed discussion and an introduction
to the topic. Recasting Assumption
2.2
for (
3.1
) yields
the following setup. In what follows we treat
({\bf A}_{n},{\bf B}_{n})
as a random element
of
\mathbb{R}^{d(d+1)}
.

Assumption 3.1
.

The following two conditions are satisfied:

Innovations:

The random vector
({\bf A}_{1},{\bf B}_{1})
has a regularly
varying distribution.

Single jumps:

For all
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf A}_{2}{\bf A}_{1}{\bf y}+{\bf A}_{2}{\bf B}_{1}\|>t,\>\|{\bf A}_{1}{\bf y}+{\bf B}_{1}\|\in(M,t/M)\right]}{\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf A}_{1}{\bf y}+{\bf B}_{1}\|>t\right]}=0.

By Proposition
2.3
, for any
{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

{\bf Y}_{n}^{{\bf y}}
has a regularly varying distribution with parameter
\alpha
.

Assumption 3.2
.

The following two conditions are satisfied:

(i)

The distribution of
{\bf A}
is such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bf A}_{1}\|^{\alpha}\right]<1
.

(ii)

The law of
({\bf A}_{1},{\bf B}_{1})
has an absolutely continuous
component with the density bounded away form zero on a open neighbourhood
of zero.

Under Assumption
3.2
the Markov chain is ergodic. We note in passing
that the above conditions are not optimal for the random difference equation.
They do however reflect the general conditions imposed in Subection
2.2
.
If we denote by
{\bf Y}_{\infty}
the random vector distributed according to the stationary distribution
of (
3.1
) then

{\bf Y}_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\bf A}_{1}{\bf Y}_{\infty}+{\bf B}_{1}\qquad{\bf Y}_{\infty}\mbox{ independent from }({\bf A}_{1},{\bf B}_{1}).

Assumption 3.3
.

There exists a deterministic
{\bf y}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that almost surely

{\bf y}_{0}
is a eigenvector of
{\bf A}_{1}^{T}
corresponding to a non-negative eigenvalue.

Consider

K=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>:\>\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle\leq 1\right\}.

Then

\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle^{-1},&\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle>0,\\
\infty,&\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle\leq 0.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, if
{\bf x}\in\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{s\}]
and
{\bf y}\in\rho_{K^{c}}^{-1}[\{t\}]

for
s<t
then
\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle\geq\langle{\bf y},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle
.
Denote by
\Lambda
the (random) eigenvalue of
{\bf A}_{1}
corresponding to
{\bf y}_{0}
.
Write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf A}_{1}{\bf y}+{\bf B}_{1}\in rK^{c}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\left\langle{\bf y},{\bf A}_{1}^{T}{\bf y}_{0}\right\rangle+\langle{\bf B}_{1},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle>r\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\Lambda\left\langle{\bf y},{\bf y}_{0}\right\rangle+\langle{\bf B}_{1},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle>r\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\Lambda\left\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\right\rangle+\langle{\bf B}_{1},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle>r\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf A}_{1}{\bf x}+{\bf B}_{1}\in rK^{c}\right].

Our regular variation result concerns the behaviour of the stationary distribution
on

K_{0}=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\>:\>\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}_{0}\rangle\leq 0\right\}.

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose that the Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3

hold. Then the stationary distribution of (
3.1
) conditioned
on
K_{0}^{c}
is regularly varying.

We note that this approach can be easily adapted to
a number of variants of the random difference equation not necessarily having an explicit
representation.

There is one more way to analyse the behaviour of
{\bf Y}
. Namely using our results on random
coefficient linear models.
Note that for
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\displaystyle{\bf Y}_{n}^{{\bf y}}

\displaystyle={\bf A}_{n}\cdots{\bf A}_{1}{\bf y}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf A}_{n}\cdots{\bf A}_{k+1}{\bf B}_{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}{\bf A}_{1}\cdots{\bf A}_{n}{\bf y}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}{\bf A}_{1}\cdots{\bf A}_{k}{\bf B}_{k+1}.

Therefore, provided that the latter is convergent in
\mathcal{V}
, the stationary
distribution can be represented as the distribution of an infinite series

(3.2)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\bf A}_{1}\cdots{\bf A}_{k}{\bf B}_{k+1}.

We are therefore in the setting of Subsection
2.4
with

{\bf N}_{j}=({\bf A}_{j},{\bf B}_{j})
and

{\bf H}_{n+1}=\psi({\bf H}_{n},{\bf N}_{n})={\bf H}_{n}{\bf A}_{n}
.
Thus
\chi({\bf v},({\bf a},{\bf b}))={\bf v}{\bf a}
,
\zeta({\bf n},({\bf a},{\bf b}))={\bf n}{\bf a}

and
\psi^{*}({\bf a},{\bf b})=\|{\bf a}\|
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let the Assumption
2.9
be satisfied and let
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf A}_{1}\|^{\alpha}]<1
.
Then the series (
3.2
) is absolutely convergent
and defines a regularly varying random vector.

4.
Proofs

In this final section, we present the proofs of our main results.
We first establish the results for general Markov chains,
specifically Proposition
2.3
and Theorem
2.7
.
The proof of Theorem
2.10
is provided at the end of this section.

We begin, however, with a brief recap of some fundamental concepts in multivariate regular variation.

4.1.
Multivariate regular variation

We begin by reformulating the weak convergence condition of probability measures,
as stated in the definition of multivariate regular variation,
into an equivalent condition involving the convergence of Radon measures.
Our main results will be established using the
M_{0}
-convergence approach.

Note that if the distribution of a random vector
{\bf X}

is regularly varying in the sense of Definition
2.1
,
then, by (
2.1
), we know that the tails of
\|{\bf X}\|

are regularly varying with index
-\alpha
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}\|>t]=t^{-\alpha}L(t),

for some slowly varying function
L
. Recall that
L
is slowly varying if it satisfies

L(ct)/L(t)\to 1

as
t\to\infty
, for any positive constant
c
.

By the asymptotic inversion of regularly varying functions
[
2
, Theorem 1.5.12]
,
there exists a function
a\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
a_{t}
is regularly varying with index
1/\alpha
and satisfies

(4.1)

t\,\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}\|>a_{t}]\to 1.

If
f\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is sufficiently well-behaved,
then, under Definition
2.1
,

t\,\mathbb{E}\left[f\!\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right]\;\to\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[f\!\left(s\,\Theta\right)\right]\alpha s^{-\alpha-1}\,{\rm d}s

as
t\to\infty
, where
\Theta
is the random vector appearing
in Definition
2.1
.
The right-hand side is just the integral of
f
with respect to
\mu
, given by

(4.2)

\mu(A)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left[s\,\Theta\in A\right]\alpha s^{-\alpha-1}\,{\rm d}s.

Note that
\mu
is normalized in the sense that
\mu(\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{B})=1
,
where, here and throughout,

\mathcal{B}=\left\{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}:\|{\bf x}\|\leq 1\right\}.

Additionally,
\mu
satisfies the homogeneity property

(4.3)

\mu(sA)=s^{-\alpha}\mu(A),

for all
s>0
and all Borel sets
A\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.

Our last limit relation implies that the sequence of
Radon measures
t\,\mathbb{P}[{\bf X}\in a_{t}\,{\rm d}{\bf x}]
converges to
\mu({\rm d}{\bf x})
.
To proceed, we need to specify the space of test functions required to carry out our analysis.

Denote by
M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
the set of all Borel measures on

\mathcal{V}_{0}=\mathcal{V}\setminus\{0\}

that are finite on the sets
\epsilon\mathcal{B}^{c}
for any
\epsilon>0
.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}
denote the set of all continuous functions

f\colon\mathcal{V}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}

for which there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
f
vanishes on
\epsilon\mathcal{B}
.

Following Hult and Lindskog
[
11
]
, we say that,
for
\mu_{n},\mu\in M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mu_{n}\to\mu\quad\text{in }M_{0}(\mathcal{V})

if

\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\;\to\;\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\quad\text{for any }f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}.

Note that if
\mathcal{V}
is finite-dimensional and thus
\sigma
-compact,
then
M_{0}
-convergence is equivalent to vague convergence.

Our next lemma asserts that regular variation conditions
can be expressed in terms of
M_{0}
-convergence.

For future purposes, it will be useful to narrow the class of test functions.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
denote the class of all bounded, positive, continuous functions

f\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
0\notin\operatorname{supp}(f)
and

f
is uniformly continuous on the sets
M\mathcal{B}
for all
M>0
.

We will verify that the classes
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}

are sufficiently rich to test regular variation in the sense
of Definition
2.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
{\bf X}
be a
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vector.
The following three conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The law of
{\bf X}
is
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta)
.

(ii)

For
\mu
defined by (
4.2
), and
any regularly varying function
a
satisfying (
4.1
),

t\,\mathbb{P}\!\left[a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\in\cdot\right]\;\to\;\mu(\cdot)\qquad\text{in }M_{0}(\mathcal{V}).

(iii)

For all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
,

(4.4)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,t\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[f\!\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right]=\int f({\bf x})\,\mu({\rm d}{\bf x}),

where
\mu
is defined by (
4.2
), and

a
is any regularly varying function satisfying (
4.1
).

Proof.

The equivalence of (i) and (ii) is established in
[
25
, Proposition 3.1]
.
Therefore, we will show only the equivalence of (i) and (iii).

Let
{\bf X}\in{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta)
.
Take
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
and
\epsilon>0
such that

f({\bf x})=0
for
\|{\bf x}\|\leq\epsilon
.
Define the function
g\colon(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,\infty)
by

g(r,\theta)=f(\epsilon r\theta).

The function
g
is bounded and continuous. Therefore,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[f\!\left(\epsilon t^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\;\middle|\;\|{\bf X}\|>t\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\!\left[f(\epsilon r\Theta)\right]\alpha r^{-\alpha-1}\,{\rm d}r.

Rewriting the expectation on the left-hand side,
taking the limit along
a_{t}
, and utilizing the regular variation of
a_{t}
,
we simplify the expression to yield (
4.4
).

Suppose now that (
4.4
) is satisfied for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
.
Consider the class
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
of all functions

g\colon(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}\to[0,\infty)

that are bounded, continuous with respect to the product topology,
and uniformly continuous on all sets
[1,M]\times\mathcal{S}
with support contained in

[1,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}
.

For any
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, the function

f({\bf x})=g(\|{\bf x}\|,{\bf x}/\|{\bf x}\|)

belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
. Therefore,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[g\!\left(t^{-1}\|{\bf X}\|,{\bf X}/\|{\bf X}\|\right)\;\middle|\;\|{\bf X}\|>t\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[g(Z,\Theta)\right]

for all
g\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, where
Z
has pure Pareto distribution.
As the class
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
contains all uniformly continuous functions with support contained in
[1,\infty)\times\mathcal{S}
,
this secures the claim.
∎

The last lemma motivates the following equivalent definition of
multivariate regular variation.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mu
be a measure on
\mathcal{V}_{0}
such that

\mu(\mathcal{V}_{0}\setminus\mathcal{B})=1
and (
4.3
) holds.
A random vector
{\bf X}
is said to have a regularly varying distribution
with index
\alpha>0
and measure
\mu
if there exists
a function
a\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
regularly varying with index
1/\alpha
, such that

t\mathbb{P}[a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\in\cdot]\to\mu(\cdot)\qquad\mbox{in $M_{0}(\mathcal{V})$.}

For brevity, we write
{\bf X}\in{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\mu)
.

Lemma
4.1
asserts that Definition
2.1
and Definition
4.2
are
equivalent.

It is worth noting that if the dimension of
\mathcal{V}
is finite,
multivariate regular variation can be characterized using vague convergence.
This requires that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int h({\bf x})\>\mu_{t}({\rm d}{\bf x})=\int h({\bf x})\>\mu({\rm d}{\bf x}),

for any continuous, non-negative function
h

with compact support in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
.
However, this notion does not work well in infinite-dimensional spaces.
This limitation becomes apparent in the second part of the proof of Lemma
4.1
,
where the constructed function
f
cannot have compact support
unless the domain is locally compact—a property that infinite-dimensional spaces lack.

4.2.
Markov chains

Recall that we consider a time-homogeneous Markov chain

(\{{\bf X}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}},\{\mathbb{P}_{\bf x}\}_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}})

on
\mathcal{V}
with transition function
{\rm P}
.

We begin by reformulating our assumptions in terms of
M_{0}
-convergence.
Specifically, we restate Assumption
2.2
.

Assumption 4.3
.

There exist
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
and a positive function

a\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
regularly varying with index
1/\alpha
, such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}_{1}^{{\bf x}_{0}}\|>a_{t}]=1,

and the following four conditions hold:

Innovations:

There exists a family of measures

\{\nu_{\bf x}\}_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}}
in
M_{0}(\mathcal{V}_{0})
such that, for every
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(4.5)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t{\rm P}({\bf x},a_{t}\cdot)=\nu_{\bf x}(\cdot)

in
M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
.

Asymptotic stability:

There exists a family

\{\eta_{\bf x}\}_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}}
of probability measures
in
M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
such that, for any
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(4.6)

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm P}(t{\bf x},t\cdot)=\eta_{\bf x}(\cdot)

in
M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
, uniformly on bounded sets.
That is, for every
R>0
and every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|\leq R}\left|\int f({\bf y}){\rm P}(t{\bf x},t{\rm d}{\bf y})-\int f({\bf y})\eta_{\bf x}({\rm d}{\bf y})\right|=0.

Comparability:

There exists a locally bounded function

R\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,+\infty)
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{{\rm P}({\bf x},t\mathcal{B}^{c})}{{\rm P}({\bf x}_{0},t\mathcal{B}^{c})}=R({\bf x}).

Single jumps:

For every
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,

(4.7)

\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\big[\|{\bf X}_{2}^{\bf x}\|\geq a_{t},\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|\in(M,a_{t}/M)\big]=0.

We note in passing that the limiting random variables and
measures in Assumptions
2.2
and
4.3
are related via

\nu_{\bf x}(\cdot)=R({\bf x})\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[s\Theta({\bf x})\in\cdot]\alpha s^{-\alpha-1}\,{\rm d}s\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta_{\bf x}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}[{\bf Z}_{1}^{\bf x}\in\cdot].

We prove Proposition
2.3
by applying the following lemma iteratively
n
times.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that the
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vector

{\bf X}
is
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\mu_{\bf X})
for some measure
\mu_{\bf X}
.
Denote

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}\|>a_{t}]=C_{\bf X}.

Consider a
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vector
{\bf Y}
with distribution given by

\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf Y}\in\cdot\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}({\bf X},\cdot)\right].

If Assumption
4.3
is satisfied,
then
{\bf Y}
is
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\mu_{\bf Y})
, where

C_{\bf Y}\mu_{\bf Y}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}[\nu_{{\bf X}}(\cdot)]+C_{\bf X}\int\eta_{\bf y}(\cdot)\mu_{\bf X}({\rm d}{\bf y}),

and

C_{\bf Y}=\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf Y}\|>a_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\nu_{{\bf X}}(\{\|{\bf x}\|>1\})]+C_{\bf X}\int\eta_{\bf y}(\{\|{\bf x}\|>1\})\mu_{\bf X}({\rm d}{\bf y}).

Proof.

For
M\geq 1
, let
\varphi_{M}\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,1]

be any continuous function such that

\varphi_{M}({\bf y})=\begin{cases}1,&\|{\bf y}\|\leq M,\\
0,&\|{\bf y}\|\geq 2M.\end{cases}

Similarly, let
\psi_{M}\colon\mathcal{V}\to[0,1]

be any continuous function such that

\psi_{M}({\bf y})=\begin{cases}1,&\|{\bf y}\|\geq 1/M,\\
0,&\|{\bf y}\|\leq 1/(2M).\end{cases}

To prove that
{\bf Y}
is regularly varying,
take
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
and write

t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf Y}\right)\right]=t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf Y}\right)\varphi_{M}\left({\bf X}\right)\right]+\\
t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf Y}\right)\left(1-\varphi_{M}\left({\bf X}\right)\right)\left(1-\psi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right)\right]+\\
t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf Y}\right)\psi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right]=:A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3},

for sufficiently large
t
.

The first term can be reformulated as

A_{1}=\mathbb{E}\left[t\int f({\bf y}){\rm P}({\bf X},a_{t}{\rm d}{\bf y})\varphi_{M}({\bf X})\right].

Using (
4.5
), the comparability assumption,
and the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\lim_{t\to\infty}A_{1}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int f({\bf y})\nu_{{\bf X}}({\rm d}{\bf y})\varphi_{M}({\bf X})\right].

Applying the dominated convergence theorem once again, we conclude that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}A_{1}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int f({\bf y})\nu_{{\bf X}}({\rm d}{\bf y})\right].

The second part of the decomposition,
A_{2}
,
is negligible as
M\to\infty
and
t\to\infty
.
This follows from the fact that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

\{\|{\bf x}\|<\epsilon\}\cap\operatorname{supp}(f)=\emptyset
.

Since
f
is non-negative and bounded above by some constant
C>0
, we have

A_{2}\leq t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf Y}\right){\mathbf{1}}_{\{M<\|{\bf X}\|<a_{t}/M\}}\right]\leq Ct\mathbb{P}\left[a_{t}^{-1}\|{\bf Y}\|>\epsilon,M<\|{\bf X}\|<a_{t}/M\right].

Using (
4.7
), it follows that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}A_{2}=0.

The third part can be rewritten as

A_{3}=\mathbb{E}\left[t\int f({\bf y}){\rm P}({\bf X},a_{t}{\rm d}{\bf y})\psi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right].

Define

G_{t}({\bf x})=\int f({\bf y}){\rm P}(a_{t}{\bf x},a_{t}{\rm d}{\bf y}).

Using (
4.6
), we have

G_{\infty}({\bf x}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}G_{t}({\bf x})=\int f({\bf y})\eta_{\bf x}({\rm d}{\bf y}),

where the convergence is almost uniform in
{\bf x}
.
With this notation, we can express
A_{3}
as

A_{3}=\mathbb{E}\left[tG_{t}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\left(1-\varphi_{M}(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X})\right)\psi_{M}(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X})\right]\\
+\mathbb{E}\left[t\left(G_{t}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)-G_{\infty}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right)\varphi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\psi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right]\\
+\mathbb{E}\left[tG_{\infty}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\varphi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\psi_{M}\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}\right)\right]=:B_{1}+B_{2}+B_{3}.

The contribution of
B_{1}
in
A_{3}
is negligible.
Indeed, since
\eta_{\bf x}
are probability measures and
f
is bounded, we have

\sup_{t>0}\sup_{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}}G_{t}({\bf x})<\infty.

In view of
\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}\|\geq Ma_{t}]\sim C_{\bf X}M^{-\alpha}t^{-1}
, the last expression yields

\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}B_{1}=0.

The term
B_{2}
can be handled similarly.
We have

B_{2}\leq\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|\leq 2M}\left|G_{t}({\bf x})-G_{\infty}({\bf x})\right|\>t\>\mathbb{P}(\|{\bf X}\|\geq a_{t}/M).

Using the fact that for all
M>0

\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|\leq M}\left|G_{t}({\bf x})-G_{\infty}({\bf x})\right|=0

we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}B_{2}\leq C_{{\bf X}}M^{-\alpha}\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{\|{\bf x}\|\leq 2M}\left|G_{t}({\bf x})-G_{\infty}({\bf x})\right|=0.

It remains to analyse
B_{3}
. By the regular variation of
{\bf X}
, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}B_{3}=C_{\bf X}\int G_{\infty}({\bf y})\varphi_{M}\left({\bf y}\right)\psi_{M}\left({\bf y}\right)\mu_{\bf X}({\rm d}{\bf y}).

As
M\to\infty
, the right-hand side converges to

C_{\bf X}\int G_{\infty}({\bf y})\mu_{\bf X}({\rm d}{\bf y})=C_{\bf X}\int\int f({\bf x})\eta_{\bf y}({\rm d}{\bf x})\mu_{\bf X}({\rm d}{\bf y}).

This completes the proof and secures our claim.
∎

With Lemma
4.4
established, we are now ready to prove our first result.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Fix
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
. Then, by (
4.5
),
{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}
is regularly varying since,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\in\cdot\right]=\nu_{\bf x}(\cdot)

in
M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
. Thus,

C_{1}=\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf X}_{1}^{\bf x}\|\geq a_{t}\right]=\nu_{\bf x}(\mathcal{B}^{c}).

Put
C_{1}\mu_{{\bf X}_{1}}=\nu_{\bf x}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n>1
, we apply Lemma
4.4
to obtain

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\in\cdot\right]=\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n}}(\cdot)

in
M_{0}(\mathcal{V})
, where

C_{n}\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n}}(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}[\nu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n-1}}(\cdot)]+C_{n-1}\int\eta_{\bf y}(\cdot)\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n-1}}({\rm d}{\bf y}),

with

C_{n}=\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\|>a_{t}]=\\
\mathbb{E}[\nu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n-1}}(\{\|{\bf x}\|>1\})]+C_{n-1}\int\eta_{\bf y}(\{\|{\bf x}\|>1\})\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n-1}}({\rm d}{\bf y}).

The recursive relation for
\{C_{n}\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

can be solved as

C_{n}\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n}}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bf x}\left[\int\mathbb{P}[{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\bf y}\in\cdot]\nu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{j}}({\rm d}{\bf y})\right].

To analyse the radial component of
\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n}}
,
recall the map
\kappa\colon\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{S}
,
defined as
\kappa({\bf x})={\bf x}/\|{\bf x}\|
.
Then

\mu_{{\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n}}\circ\kappa^{-1}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{n}({\bf x})\in\cdot],

so

C_{n}\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{n}({\bf x})\in\cdot]=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\int\mathbb{P}[{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\bf y}\in\kappa^{-1}(\cdot)]\nu_{{\bf X}_{j}^{\bf x}}({\rm d}{\bf y})\right].

Expressing
\nu_{\bf x}
in terms of
\Theta({\bf x})
and using the fact that for every
n

{\bf Z}^{c{\bf y}}_{n}

has the same law as
c{\bf Z}^{\bf y}_{n}
gives that for any
j
,

\int\mathbb{P}[{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\bf y}\in\kappa^{-1}(\cdot)]\nu_{{\bf x}}({\rm d}{\bf y})=\\
R({\bf x})\int_{\mathcal{S}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\kappa({\bf Z}^{r\theta}_{n-j-1})\in\cdot\>,\>\|{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{r\theta}\|\geq 1]\alpha r^{-\alpha-1}{\rm d}r\mathbb{P}[\Theta({\bf x})\in{\rm d}\theta]=\\
R({\bf x})\int_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\left\{\kappa({\bf Z}^{\theta}_{n-j-1})\in\cdot\right\}}\|{\bf Z}_{n-j-1}^{\theta}\|^{\alpha}]\mathbb{P}[\Theta({\bf x})\in{\rm d}\theta].

Plugging this into the formula of the law of
\Theta_{n}({\bf x})
secures the claim.
∎

]
.

We begin with a simple lemma, showing that that it suffices to verify the regular variation of
\pi
on
K
instead of
K_{0}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that there exists a measure
\mu_{\pi}
on
\mathcal{V}_{0}
satisfying condition (
4.3
), and such that for all
g\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
,
supp(f)\subseteq K^{c}
, the following limit holds

(4.8)

t\int g(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x})\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\to\int g({\bf x})\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

Then
\pi(\cdot|K_{0}^{c})
is regularly varying with index
\alpha
. More precisely for every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})=\int f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

Proof.

First note that by approximating
{\mathbf{1}}_{{\bf x}\in K^{c}}
by continuous bounded functions and using (
4.8
) we obtain that for every
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}

(4.9)

t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}\in K^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\to\int f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K^{c}\}}\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

The same limit holds for any
h\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
such that
supp(h)\subseteq{\varepsilon}K^{c}
, by applying (
4.9
) to
f({\bf x})=h({\varepsilon}{\bf x})
.
Take
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
. Since
{\varepsilon}K_{0}\subseteq K
for every
{\varepsilon}>0
, and the because
{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}
if and only if
a{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}
, we have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\geq\liminf_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}\in{\varepsilon}K^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\\
=\int f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in{\varepsilon}K^{c}\}}\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

Taking
{\varepsilon}\to 0
and applying the monotone convergence theorem to the functions
f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in{\varepsilon}K\}}
we obtain

\liminf_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\geq\int f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{x\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

For the upper bound, observe that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\pi(Ma_{t}\mathcal{B}^{c})\leq\limsup_{t\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf X}_{n}\|\geq a_{t}M]=CM^{-\alpha}

for some
C>0
.
Take
M>0
and split the limit into two parts

t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\\
\leq t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}\|{\bf x}\|\leq M\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})+t\|f\|_{\infty}\pi(a_{t}M\mathcal{B}^{c}).

In the first part, there exists
{\varepsilon}_{M}>0
, depending on
M
and
f
, such that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}\|{\bf x}\|\leq M\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\leq\\
\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}\in{\varepsilon}_{M}K^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})=\int f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in{\varepsilon}_{M}K^{c}\}}\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

Thus

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\int f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})\leq\int f({\bf x}){\mathbf{1}}_{\{{\bf x}\in K_{0}^{c}\}}\mu_{\pi}({\rm d}{\bf x})+C\|f\|_{\infty}M^{-\alpha}

and taking
M\to\infty
secures the claim.
∎

We now state and prove a lemma closely following
[
8
, Lemma 3]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
k_{0}
be an integer large enough for (
2.6
) holds.
There exists a random vector
{\bf W}
such that

t\mathbb{P}[{\bf W}\notin a_{t}K]\to c

for some positive
c>0
such that for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}[{\bf W}\in tK^{c}]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}^{k_{0}}({\bf W},tK^{c})\right],

where
{\rm P}^{k_{0}}
stands for the
k_{0}
step transition kernel of
{\rm P}
.

Proof.

By considering
k_{0}
-step Markov chain
\{{\bf X}_{k_{0}n}^{\bf x}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to ease the notation
we can assume without loss that
k_{0}=1
.
Consider any
\mathcal{V}
-valued random vector
{\bf Y}
which is
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\mu_{\bf Y})
such that

t\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf Y}\|>a_{t}]\to C_{\bf Y}>0

and
\mu_{\bf Y}(K)=0
.
Then, according to Lemma
4.4

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}({\bf Y},a_{t}K^{c})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\nu_{{\bf Y}}\left(K^{c}\right)\right]+C_{\bf Y}\int\eta_{\bf x}(K^{c})\mu_{\bf Y}({\rm d}{\bf x}).

Assumption
2.6
allows to control the second term. Indeed, since
{\bf Y}
is
also
{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\Theta_{\bf Y})
then

\int\eta_{\bf x}(K^{c})\mu_{\bf Y}({\rm d}{\bf x})=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\eta_{r\theta}(K^{c})\alpha r^{-\alpha-1}{\rm d}r\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{\bf Y}\in{\rm d}\theta].

Now note that for any
c>0
and

{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\eta_{c{\bf x}}({\rm d}{\bf y})=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm P}(tc{\bf x},t{\rm d}{\bf y})=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\rm P}(t{\bf x},tc^{-1}{\rm d}{\bf y})=\eta_{{\bf x}}(c^{-1}{\rm d}{\bf y}).

and thus we can continue the last display
with

\int_{\mathcal{S}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\eta_{\theta}(r^{-1}K^{c})\alpha r^{-\alpha-1}{\rm d}r\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{\bf Y}\in{\rm d}\theta].

The inner integral is bounded by

\int_{\mathcal{V}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}1_{\{{\bf x}\in r^{-1}K^{c}\}}\alpha r^{-\alpha-1}{\rm d}r\eta_{\theta}({\rm d}{\bf x})\leq\int_{\mathcal{V}}\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf x})^{-\alpha}\eta_{\theta}({\rm d}{\bf x})\leq\rho_{*}.

This yields

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}({\bf Y},a_{t}K^{c})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\nu_{{\bf Y}}\left(K^{c}\right)\right]+C_{\bf Y}\rho_{*}.

Since
\rho^{*}<1
we can without loss, by increasing
C_{\bf Y}
and keeping the expectation
bounded, assume that the right hand side of the last display is strictly smaller than
C_{\bf Y}
.
This means that for some
t_{0}>0
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}({\bf Y},a_{t}K^{c})\right]<\mathbb{P}[{\bf Y}\in a_{t}K^{c}]

for all
t>t_{0}
.
Define
{\bf W}
to be distributed as
{\bf Y}
conditioned on

{\bf Y}\in a_{t_{0}}K^{c}
. We will
check that such
{\bf W}
satisfies the claim of the lemma. For

t>t_{0}
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}({\bf W},a_{t}K^{c})\right]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm P}\left({\bf Y},a_{t}K^{c}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{P}[{\bf Y}\in a_{t_{0}}K^{c}]}<\frac{\mathbb{P}[{\bf Y}\in a_{t}K^{c}]}{\mathbb{P}[{\bf Y}\in a_{t_{0}}K^{c}]}=\mathbb{P}[{\bf W}\in a_{t}K^{c}].

For
t\leq t_{0}
we have, by convexity of
K
,

\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf W}\in a_{t}K^{c}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[\left.{\bf Y}\in a_{t}K^{c}\right|{\bf Y}\in a_{t_{0}}K^{c}\right]=1

so the claimed inequality holds trivially.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

We first establish that

(4.10)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\pi(a_{t}K^{c})<\infty.

To achieve this we will approach
\pi
with an appropriate choice of the initial condition.
Namely we will consider
our
k_{0}
-step Markov chain with
the law of
{\bf W}
the random vector from Lemma
4.6
, as the initial distribution.
More precisely, let
{\bf X}^{\bf W}=\{{\bf X}_{k_{0}n}^{\bf W}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
{\bf X}_{0}^{\bf W}={\bf W}
.
We next show monotonicity of
{\bf X}^{\bf W}
in an appropriate sense.
To verify the mentioned monotonicity, note that

\left\{{\bf W}\in tK^{c}\right\}=\left\{\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf W})\leq 1/t\right\}=\left\{1/\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf W})\geq t\right\}

Using the monotonicity condition from Assumption
2.6

we see that the sequence of random variables
1/\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{n}^{\bf W})

is stochastically decreasing. Indeed, the claim of the aforementioned Lemma
4.6

gives

\mathbb{P}\left[1/\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{0}^{\bf W}\right)\geq t\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf X}_{0}^{\bf W}\in tK^{c}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[{\bf X}_{k_{0}}^{\bf W}\in tK^{c}\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[1/\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{k_{0}}^{\bf W}\right)\geq t\right].

From here we argue inductively. If for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
1/\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{k_{0}(n-1)}^{\bf W})
is stochastically
grater than
1/\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{k_{0}n}^{\bf W})
then there exists a coupling

(X,Y)
of the random vector

(\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{(n-1)k_{0}}^{\bf W}),\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}))
such that
X\leq Y
.
Let
\nu(\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}),\cdot)
be the regular conditional distribution
of
{\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}
given
\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W})
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.{\rm P}^{k_{0}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W},tK^{c}\right)\right|\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\right)\right]=\int{\rm P}^{k_{0}}({\bf z},tK^{c})\nu(\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\right),{\rm d}{\bf z}).

Write
\mu
for the regular conditional distribution of
{\bf X}_{(n-1)k_{0}}^{\bf W}
given

\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{(n-1)k_{0}}^{\bf W})
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.{\rm P}^{k_{0}}\left({\bf X}_{(n-1)k_{0}}^{\bf W},tK^{c}\right)\right|\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{(n-1)k_{0}}^{\bf W}\right)\right]=\int{\rm P}^{k_{0}}({\bf z},tK^{c})\mu(\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{(n-1)k_{0}}^{\bf W}),{\rm d}{\bf z}).

Using the monotonicity condition we can write

\mathbb{P}\left[1/\rho_{K^{c}}({\bf X}_{(n+1)k_{0}}^{\bf W})\geq t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left.{\rm P}^{k_{0}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W},tK^{c}\right)\right|\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\right)\right]\right]=\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\int{\rm P}^{k_{0}}({\bf z},tK^{c})\nu(\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\right),{\rm d}{\bf z})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int{\rm P}^{k_{0}}({\bf z},tK^{c})\nu(Y,{\rm d}{\bf z})\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int{\rm P}^{k_{0}}({\bf z},tK^{c})\mu(X,{\rm d}{\bf z})\right]=\mathbb{P}_{\bf x}[1/\rho_{K^{c}}\left({\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\right)\geq t].

Thus the sequence
\left\{\mathbb{P}[{\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\in tK^{c}]\right\}
is non-decreasing and (
4.10
) follows since

t\pi(a_{t}K^{c})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}[{\bf X}_{nk_{0}}^{\bf W}\in a_{t}K^{c}]\leq t\mathbb{P}[{\bf W}\in a_{t}K^{c}].

From here we go back to the Markov chain started at some fixed
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{V}
.
For
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
vanishing in
K
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
write

t\int f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}\right)\pi({\rm d}{\bf y})=\\
t\left(\int f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}\right)\pi({\rm d}{\bf y})-\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\right)\right]\right)+t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\right)\right].

The first term is bounded by

Ct\rho^{n}(1+V({\bf x}))\left\|f(a_{t}^{-1}\cdot)-\int f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}\right)\pi({\rm d}{\bf y})\right\|_{V}

one only needs to argue that the term involving
\|\cdot\|_{V}
is
O(1/t)
.
The integral is easily manageable using (
4.10
), while
f(a_{t}^{-1}\cdot)

can be controlled using (
2.7
).
Now analyse the second term by first taking
t\to\infty
followed by
n\to\infty
.
We have

(4.11)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf X}_{n}^{\bf x}\right)\right]=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\nu_{{\bf X}_{n-j-1}^{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\right].

We now argue that the sum converges as
n\to\infty
.
Recall that there exists
\epsilon
such that
\epsilon\mathcal{B}\subseteq K
. Then
for non-negative integers
n
and
j<n
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\nu_{{\bf X}_{n-j-1}^{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}C\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y}\|\geq\epsilon]\nu_{{\bf X}_{n-j-1}^{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\right]\\
=C\epsilon^{-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{\bf Z}_{j}^{\Theta({\bf X}^{\bf x}_{n-j-1})}\right\|^{\alpha}\right]\leq C\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left(\rho_{*}\right)^{j}.

This allows to control the terms in the sum (
4.11
) for big values of
j
.
If
j
is fixed then one just need to note that

{\bf x}\mapsto F_{j}({\bf x})=\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\>\nu_{\bf x}({\rm d}{\bf y})

satisfies
\|F_{j}\|_{V}<\infty
. Thus for fixed
j
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\nu_{{\bf X}_{n-j-1}^{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F_{j}\left({\bf X}_{n-j-1}^{\bf x}\right)\right]\to\int_{\mathcal{V}}F_{j}({\bf y})\pi({\rm d}{\bf y}).

The last two remarks allow us to conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\nu_{{\bf X}_{n-j-1}^{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\right]=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\nu_{{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\pi({\rm d}{\bf x}).

This yields

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\int f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf y}\right)\pi({\rm d}{\bf y})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}[f({\bf Z}_{j}^{\bf y})]\nu_{{\bf x}}({\rm d}y)\pi({\rm d}{\bf x})

for
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
that vanishes in
K
.
Which, by Lemma
4.5

establishes multivariate regular variation of
\pi
.
To characterize the distribution of
\Theta_{\pi}
use the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
2.3
.
∎

4.3.1.
Adaptable case

Recall that
\{{\bf N}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

\mathcal{A}
-valued random vectors
with generic copy denoted
by
{\bf N}

{\rm Rv}(\alpha,\mu^{\bf N})

\{{\bf M}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathcal{A}
-valued Markov chain
admitting recursive formula

{\bf M}_{j+1}=\psi({\bf M}_{j},{\bf N}_{j+1}),\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}

for some deterministic function
\psi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we now define

{\bf S}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let the Assumptions
2.8
and
2.9
hold.
Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the law of
{\bf S}_{n}

is regularly varying with index
\alpha/2
.

Proof.

Let
a_{t}
be such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf N}\|>a_{t}\right]=1.

We find the limit as
t
tends to infinity of

(4.12)

t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf S}_{n}\right)\right]

for any
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
.
Let
\epsilon>0
be sufficiently small so that

\epsilon\mathcal{B}\cap{\rm supp}(f)=\emptyset
.
If
\|{\bf S}_{n}\|\geq\epsilon a_{t}^{2}
then there has to
exist an index
j\leq n
such that

\|{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|\geq\epsilon a_{t}^{2}/n
. Let
A_{k}
denote the event that the first
j

satisfying the last condition is equal to
k
.
Then

t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf S}_{n}\right)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf S}_{n}\right)\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\right].

We analyse the sum term by term. Fix
k\leq n
. Since

\|{\bf M}_{k}{\bf N}_{k}\|\leq\|{\bf M}_{k}\|\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\leq C(\|{\bf N}_{k}\|+1)^{2}\|{\bf M}_{k-1}\|\\
\leq(\|{\bf N}_{k}\|+1)^{2}C\|{\bf M}_{0}\|\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(C(\|{\bf N}_{j}\|+1)\right)

we can use the convolution condition imposed on the distribution of
\|{\bf N}\|
to get

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{k}\right]=o(1/t)+\\
\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\geq\sqrt{\epsilon a_{t}^{2}/n},\>\|{\bf M}_{k}{\bf N}_{k}\|\geq\epsilon a_{t}^{2}/n,\>\forall j<k\>\|{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|<\epsilon a_{t}^{2}/n\right]

and further

\mathbb{P}\left[A_{k}\right]=o(1/t)+\\
\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\geq\sqrt{\epsilon a_{t}^{2}/n},\>na_{t}^{-2}\|{\bf M}_{k}{\bf N}_{k}\|\in[\epsilon,1/\epsilon),\>\forall j\leq n,\>j\neq k\>\|{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|<\epsilon a_{t}^{2}/n\right].

Since on the last event
\sum_{k\neq j\leq n}\|{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|\leq\epsilon a_{t}^{2}

we can use uniform continuity of
f
on
\epsilon^{-1}n\mathcal{B}
to infer that

t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf S}_{n}\right)1_{A_{k}}\right]=t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf M}_{k}{\bf N}_{k}\right)1_{A_{k}}\right]+\Delta(t,\epsilon),

where

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\limsup_{t\to\infty}\Delta(t,\epsilon)=0.

In view of
{\bf M}_{k}{\bf N}_{k}=\psi({\bf M}_{k-1},{\bf N}_{k}){\bf N}_{k}
we recall that by (
2.9
)
we have that as
\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\to\infty

\psi({\bf M}_{k-1},{\bf N}_{k})=\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\zeta\left({\bf M}_{k-1},\|{\bf N}_{k}\|^{-1}{\bf N}_{k}\right)+o(\|{\bf N}_{k}\|).

Thus

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf M}_{k}{\bf N}_{k}\right){\mathbf{1}}_{A_{k}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[f\right(Y^{2}\Theta_{\bf N}\zeta({\bf M}_{k-1},\Theta_{\bf N}))]

and in conclusion

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-2}{\bf S}_{n}\right)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(Y^{2}\Theta_{\bf N}\zeta({\bf M}_{k-1},\Theta_{\bf N})\right)\right]

and as a consequence

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf S}_{n}\|>a_{t}^{2}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Theta_{\bf N}\zeta({\bf M}_{k-1},\Theta_{\bf N})\|^{\alpha/2}\right]

and

\mathbb{P}\left[\Theta_{S_{n}}\in\cdot\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\frac{\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}\zeta(M_{k},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})}{\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}\zeta(M_{k},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})\|}\in\cdot\right\}}\frac{\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}\zeta(M_{k},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})\|^{\alpha/2}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}\zeta(M_{j},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})\|^{\alpha/2}}\right].

∎

Before we present the proof of Theorem
2.10
let us state a simple Lemma that
{\bf S}_{\bf M}
is well defined.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let (
2.10
) be satisfied. If
\mathbb{E}[\log\|{\bf N}\|]<\infty
then
the series defining
{\bf S}_{\bf M}
converges in
\mathcal{A}
almost surely.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is complete it is sufficient to check that almost surely

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|{\bf M}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|<\infty.

We have

(4.13)

\|{\bf M}_{j}\|=\|\psi({\bf M}_{j-1},{\bf N}_{j})\|\leq\|{\bf M}_{j-1}\|\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{j})\leq\|{\bf M}_{0}\|\prod_{k=1}^{j}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{k}).

Under (
2.10
) the product on the left hand side converges to
0
exponentially fast.
The integrability assumption ensures that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|{\bf N}_{n}\|\leq 0\>a.s.

A simple ratio test ensures that almost surely

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|{\bf N}_{j}\|\prod_{k=0}^{j}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{k})<\infty

securing the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

The argument follows the simple idea of approximating
{\bf S}_{\bf M}
via
{\bf S}_{n}
for large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
To achieve that it is sufficient to control the tails of

{\bf S}_{n,{\bf M}}={\bf S}_{\bf M}-{\bf S}_{n}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}{\bf N}_{j}{\bf M}_{j}.

If we show that

(4.14)

\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf S}_{n,{\bf M}}\|>{\varepsilon}a_{t}^{2}\right]=0

then using the uniform continuity of
f
and the fact that

\lim_{{\varepsilon}\to 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf S}_{n}\|\geq{\varepsilon}^{-1}a_{t}^{-2}\right]=0

the main result will follow.

Write

\|{\bf S}_{n,{\bf M}}\|\leq\|{\bf M}_{n}\|\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\|{\bf N}_{j}\|\prod_{k=n+1}^{j}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{k}).

By the main result of
[
8
]
applied to

S^{*}_{n}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\|{\bf N}_{j}\|\prod_{k=n+1}^{j}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{k})

we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(S^{*}_{n}>{\varepsilon}a_{t})=\frac{{\varepsilon}^{-\alpha/2}}{1-\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha/2}]}

and

\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf S}_{n,{\bf M}}\|>{\varepsilon}a_{t}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{{\varepsilon}^{-\alpha/2}\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf M}_{n}\|^{\alpha/2}]}{1-\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha/2}]}.

By the estimate (
4.13
) and (
2.10
) we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bf M}_{n}\|^{\alpha/2}\right]=0.

This secures (
4.14
) and proves the result.
∎

4.3.2.
Predictable case

We finally move to the proof of our results for auto regressive linear models in the second case.
The predictable and adaptable cases bear some similarities. Thus we will only focus on the
parts of the argument where this cases differ.

Recall that
\{{\bf H}_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathcal{A}
-valued Markov chain
admitting recursive formula

{\bf H}_{j+1}=\psi({\bf H}_{j},{\bf N}_{j}),\qquad j\in\mathbb{N}

for some deterministic function
\psi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

{\bf R}_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let the Assumptions
2.8
and
2.9
hold.
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
the law of
{\bf R}_{n}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let again
a_{t}
be such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}t\mathbb{P}\left[\|{\bf N}\|>a_{t}\right]=1.

Fix
f\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{u}
and take
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small
so that
\epsilon\mathcal{B}\cap{\rm supp}(f)=\emptyset
.
Thus if

f(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf R}_{n})
is positive
there has to exist
j\leq n
such that

\|{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|>\epsilon a_{t}/n
. Since

\|{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|\leq\|{\bf H}_{0}\|\left(\|{\bf N}_{0}\|+1\right)\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(C(\|{\bf N}_{j}\|+1)\right),

up to asymptotic equivalence, there exists
j_{0}
such that

\|{\bf N}_{j_{0}}\|
is of order
a_{t}
. More precisely there exists

\delta=\delta(\epsilon,C,n)>0
such that

t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf R}_{n}\right)\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf R}_{n}\right){\mathbf{1}}_{A_{k}}\right]+o(1)

where

A_{k}=\left\{\|{\bf N}_{k}\|>\delta a_{t},\>\max_{j\leq k-1}\|{\bf N}_{j}\|\leq\delta a_{t}\right\}.

Fix
k\leq n
. On the event
A_{k}
, since
\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
, then

{\bf H}_{k+1}=\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\zeta\left({\bf H}_{k},\frac{{\bf N}_{k}}{\|{\bf N}_{k}\|}\right)+o(\|{\bf N}_{k}\|).

Still, on
A_{k}
,
since
\chi
is homogeneous

{\bf H}_{k+2}=\psi({\bf H}_{k+1},{\bf N}_{k+1})=\|{\bf H}_{k+1}\|\chi\left(\|{\bf H}_{k+1}\|^{-1}{\bf H}_{k+1},{\bf N}_{k+1}\right)+o(\|{\bf H}_{k+1}\|)\\
=\chi\left({\bf H}_{k+1},{\bf N}_{k+1}\right)+o(\|{\bf H}_{k+1}\|)

it transpires that conditioned on
\|{\bf N}_{k}\|\to\infty

the random vector

\|{\bf N}_{k}\|^{-1}({\bf H}_{k+1},\ldots,{\bf H}_{n})

converges
to a finite dimensional distribution of a Markov chain
\{{\bf U}_{j}^{k}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
given via

{\bf U}^{k}_{n+1}=\chi({\bf U}^{k}_{n},{\bf N}_{n+k+1})

started at

\zeta\left({\bf H}_{k},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}\right).

Thus

t\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(a_{t}^{-1}{\bf R}_{n}\right)\right]\to\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\zeta({\bf H}_{k},\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}})}\left[f\left(Y{\bf H}_{k}\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}+Y\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}{\bf U}^{k}_{j}{\bf N}_{k+j}\right)\right]\right].

Let

\Gamma^{n}_{k}={\bf H}_{k}\Theta_{{\bf N}_{k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}{\bf U}^{k}_{j}{\bf N}_{k+j},

then

\mathbb{P}[\Theta_{n}\in\cdot]=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{\frac{\Gamma^{n}_{k}}{\left\|\Gamma^{n}_{k}\right\|}\in\cdot\right\}}\frac{\left\|\Gamma^{n}_{k}\right\|^{\alpha}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\Gamma_{j}^{n}\|^{\alpha}}\right].

∎

We now move to the study of

{\bf R}_{\infty}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

We again approximate the infinite sum via finite one. Denote

{\bf R}_{n,\infty}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}.

As for the adaptable case we have

(4.15)

\|{\bf H}_{j}{\bf N}_{j}\|\leq\|{\bf H}_{j-1}\|\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{j-1})\|{\bf N}_{j}\|=\|{\bf H}_{j-l}\|\|{\bf N}_{j}\|\prod_{k=j-l}^{j-1}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{k}).

Therefore, we have a simple bound

\|{\bf R}_{n,\infty}\|\leq^{(d)}\|{\bf H}_{n+1}\|R^{*},

where

R^{*}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|{\bf N}_{j}\|\prod_{k=0}^{j-1}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{k})

and
{\bf H}_{n+1}
and
R^{*}
are independent.
Thus we want to control tails of
R^{*}
and
\|{\bf H}_{n+1}\|
separately.

The crucial feature of
R^{*}
is that it is a solution to a stochastic fixed-point equation

R^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\psi^{*}({\bf N})R^{*}+\|{\bf N}\|,

where
R^{*}
and
{\bf N}
are independent on the right hand side.
Consider

\tilde{N}=\begin{cases}\psi^{*}({\bf N}),&{\bf N}<\beta,\\
C(\|{\bf N}\|+1),&{\bf N}>\beta,\end{cases}

and choose
\beta>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{N})^{\alpha}]<1
.
Then for
\tilde{R}
defined as

\tilde{R}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\tilde{N}\tilde{R}+\|{\bf N}\|,

where
\tilde{R}
is independent of
{\bf N}
,
we have
R^{*}\leq^{(d)}\tilde{R}
.
Note that since
\psi^{*}({\bf N})\geq 0

and for each
r\in\mathbb{R}

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\tilde{N}r+\|{\bf N}\|>t]}{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf N}\|>t]}\leq(1+(1+C)r)^{\alpha},

we can apply the approach of
[
4
]
to control the tails of
\tilde{R}
and get

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\tilde{R}>t]}{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf N}\|>t]}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[(1+(1+C)\tilde{R})^{\alpha}]}{1-\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]}<\infty.

The same bound applies to
R^{*}
.

The tails of
{\bf H}_{n}
are easily manageable using the estimate

\|{\bf H}_{n}\|\leq\|{\bf H}_{0}\|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{j}).

Since
\psi^{*}({\bf N}_{j})\leq C(1+\|{\bf N}_{j}\|)
by the Assumption
2.9
we therefore have

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf H}_{n}\|>t]}{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf N}\|>t]}\leq nC^{-\alpha}\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]^{n}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf H}_{n}\|^{\alpha}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf H}_{0}\|^{\alpha}]\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]^{n}
the Assumption
2.9
constitutes

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf R}_{n,\infty}\|>t]}{\mathbb{P}[\|{\bf N}\|>t]}\leq\\
nC^{-\alpha}\mathbb{E}[(R^{*})^{\alpha}]\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]^{n}+\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf H}_{0}\|^{\alpha}]\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}[(1+(1+C)R^{*})^{\alpha}]}{1-\mathbb{E}[\psi^{*}({\bf N})^{\alpha}]}.

Since the right hand side of the above display tends to zero as
n
tends to infinity, the claim is secured.
∎
