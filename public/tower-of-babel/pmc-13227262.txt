Growth on corn salad leaves and polystyrene as well as the temperature influence the transcriptome of Listeria monocytogenes NCTC 10887
Authors: Tim Hoffmann, Agnes Weiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractListeria monocytogenes is an important food-borne pathogen which is frequently linked to ready-to-eat vegetables. In this work, the aim was to investigate by RNAseq how the colonization of corn salad leaves and the commonly used packaging material polystyrene impacts on the transcriptome of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 at 4 °C and 22 °C. The strongest transcriptional differences occurred on corn salad at 4 °C versus polystyrene at 4 °C, yielding 2629 differentially expressed genes (91.6% of protein-coding genes), indicating a habitat driven shift under cold-chain conditions. Corn salad promoted regulation of virulence related genes. The prfA gene was upregulated on corn salad compared to polystyrene at both temperatures (log2FC 2.43 at 22 °C and 4.16 at 4 °C), accompanied by increased hly (2.70), inlA (4.46), and inlB (2.10) expression at 4 °C. Stress/proteostasis gene expression was elevated on corn salad, including higher transcripts of clpP (3.34–3.51), clpE (5.58–6.89), and clpB (2.08–3.74), consistent with a stressed yet persistence-prone state on leaf surfaces. This indicates that L. monocytogenes persisting on leafy greens can remain transcriptionally primed for host interaction at 4 °C, implying that refrigeration alone may not mitigate risk on ready-to-eat produce, underlining the importance of pre-harvest hygiene.Keywords: Gene regulation, Virulence, Persistence, Listeria monocytogenes

Introduction

Besides Salmonella spp., Campylobacter spp., and enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes is a major food-borne pathogen and a serious threat in the food industry (Robert Koch-Institut (RKI), 2024; European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), 2024; European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) & European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2024). Listeriosis is mainly conveyed via meat (Matle et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2017), fish (Zakrzewski et al., 2023, 2024; Wieczorek et al., 2020; Jamali et al., 2015), and dairy products (U.S. Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 2022). The latest “European One Health Zoonoses Report” for 2023 shows that listeriosis cases reached their highest annual incidence since 2007 with 2952 confirmed cases in Europe and a hospitalization rate of 96.5% (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) & European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), 2024). Almost 20% of the reported cases had a fatal outcome (European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) & European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), 2024). This development has not gone unnoticed by politics. In 2024, the EU adopted the regulation (EU) 2024/2895, which extends food safety requirements by stipulating that L. monocytogenes must not be detectable in ready-to-eat (RTE) foods at the end of their shelf life, provided these foods support the growth of L. monocytogenes (European Union, 2024).

A growing attention over the past years has been drawn to listeriosis caused by the consumption of leafy vegetables, especially in the form of RTE salads (Kłapeć and Dutkiewicz, 2024; Bernardo et al., 2021; Ziegler et al., 2019). While L. monocytogenes is ubiquitously present in the environment and possible contamination pathways into the food processing environment are known (Osek et al., 2022; Jordan et al., 2018; Khan et al., 2016), little is known about how L. monocytogenes attaches to the vegetables' surface or how survival of this pathogen in this hostile environment is accomplished. Nevertheless, there has already been research on how growing on other food surfaces impacts on the transcriptome of different L. monocytogenes strains. It was found that when growing on a RTE meat matrix (turkey slices), L. monocytogenes F2365 upregulated its metabolic energy, fatty acid, and phospholipid pathways, among others (Bae et al., 2011). Contrarily, no significant impact was found on the regulation of virulence genes (Bae et al., 2011). The growth of L. monocytogenes H7858 on cold smoked salmon led to higher transcript levels of genes regulated by the transcriptional regulator PrfA, which is considered to be one of the main virulence regulators, when compared to growth in modified brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Tang et al., 2015). Additionally, co-cultivating L. monocytogenes 6179 with Brevibacterium S111 and Psychrobacter L7, typically found on the rind of cheese, led to an increase in the transcription of virulence factors such as Hly, Lap, and a homolog of lmo0514 (Anast and Schmitz-Esser, 2020), which is involved during murine infection (García-del Portillo et al., 2011). Plant related changes in the transcriptome were observed when L. monocytogenes LS670 grew on cantaloupe (Kang et al., 2019). Here, stress and virulence-related genes such as hly, actA, plcA, plcB as well as opuCABCD, gbuABC, betL, and bilEAB were upregulated (Kang et al., 2019) and it was also found that the transcription level of the Listeria cellulose binding protein gene (lcp) was upregulated on lettuce leaves (Bae et al., 2013). Work by Alsharif et al. (2025) shows that the extract of leafy vegetables like iceberg lettuce, spinach, rocket and also mixed salads promoted biofilm formation and virulence (Alsharif et al., 2025). When growing on the leaves' surface in contaminated RTE salads, L. monocytogenes may be exposed to the extracts of damaged leaves, leaking out. This may lead to an enhanced persistence and virulence on the salads’ surface during its shelf life, which poses a risk for the consumer, as the work of Alsharif et al. (2025) suggests.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of the surfaces – corn salad vs. polystyrene– on the transcriptome of L. monocytogenes strain NCTC 10887 incubated as static biofilms. For this purpose, corn salad (Valerianella locusta) was selected as a model plant organism because it has previously been used in studies investigating colonization by L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887, Salmonella enterica and enterohemorrhagic E. coli (Hoffmann and Weiss, 2025; Elpers et al., 2020; Bufe et al., 2019; Eiβenberger et al., 2018). In addition, the German Federal Office for Consumer Protection and Food Safety reported the detection of L. monocytogenes in packaged corn salad (German Federal Office for Consumer Protection and Food Safety, 2022). Polystyrene is a widely used packaging material in the food industry. The corn salad leaves as a habitat for L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 are mainly characterized by nutrient availability in the form of leaf extract released by damaged leaves, the cuticle of the leaves (composed of cutin, waxes, long chained fatty acids, alcohols, alkanes, esters, triterpenoids, and polysaccharides (Fernández et al., 2016; Grzegorzewski et al., 2010). Their exact composition is not fully analyzed yet and competition with the autochthonous microbiota of the corn salad, described in more detail by Griese and Weiss (2025), is hypothesized. Polystyrene on the other hand is much less complex, characterized by being relatively inert, nonpolar, hydrophobic, and (in this study) sterile. Additionally, a temperature dependent differential gene expression analysis of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887, serovar 1/2a, on both surfaces was performed, as the strain was incubated at 22 °C, which corresponds to ambient temperatures typically found in the food processing environment, and at 4 °C, the usual temperature at which food is stored.

Materials and methods

Cultivation of Listeria monocytogenes

L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 (serovar 1/2a, isolated from chinchilla) was cultivated in BHI broth (Cat. No. 53286, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) at temperatures depending on the experiment with shaking at 180 rpm, if not stated otherwise. For determination of the recovery rate, Palcam agar (Art. No. X919, Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) was used in addition to BHI agar (Cat. No. 70138, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Incubation of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 on corn salad and polystyrene

Incubation of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 on corn salad and sample preparation

L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 was grown in BHI broth at 37 °C on a shaker for 16 h. After incubation, a 200 mL suspension of BHI broth containing 5.0 × 10e7 cfu/mL was created in a flask and incubated at 37 °C, 130 rpm, for 16 h. After incubation, the 200 mL suspension was pelleted by centrifugation (Eppendorf, Centrifuge 5804 R) in sterile 50 mL tubes (Cat. No. 227 261, Greiner Bio-one, Munich, Germany) at 3230 × g at RT for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS (Cat. No. 0780-10L, VWR Chemicals, Solon, Ohio, USA) buffer and centrifuged again under the same conditions to wash away any remaining BHI broth. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS buffer, the viable count was adjusted to 2.0 × 10e11 cfu/mL and the suspension was transferred into a sterile Erlenmeyer flask. The concentrated cell suspension was used to moisten 25 g of commercially available corn salad (Contrada scorziello anc., Eboli, Italy), which was performed by immersing each corn salad leaf individually into the cell suspension using sterile tweezers. Immersed leaves were incubated for 24 h at 4 °C and 22 °C under sterile conditions. After incubation, the leaves were placed in a sterile plastic bag (T256.2, Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) containing 50 mL sterile PBS buffer. The following protocol for pulsifying corn salad to harvest cells was derived from Griese and Weiss (2025). Briefly, the bag was placed in a Pulsifier® (Microgen Bioproducts Ltd., Eastleigh, Great Britain) and the corn salad was washed by high frequency pulses for 30 s, resulting in the bacteria being suspended in the PBS buffer. The cell suspension was transferred in a sterile plastic bag containing a lateral fleece insert (EP17.1, Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany) to filter any major debris. The filtered cell suspension was transferred into a sterile 50 mL tube and pelleted by centrifugation at 3230 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet was used for RNA extraction as described in 2.3.

Incubation of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 on polystyrene and sample preparation

For incubation of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 on polystyrene, cells were prepared as described in 2.2.1 until the step of fully washing away the BHI broth with PBS buffer. For final resuspension in PBS, the cell count was adjusted to 1.0 × 10e9 cfu/mL. 10 mL of this cell suspension together with 10 mL sterile PBS buffer were then transferred into sterile polystyrene petri dishes (Cat. No. 633180, Greiner Bio-One International GmbH, Frickenhausen, Germany), which served as the polystyrene surface investigated in this study. This was performed 20 times, accounting for one biological replicate. After 24 h of incubation at 4 °C and 22 °C, the supernatant was discarded and a cell scraper (Cat. No. C5981, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to scrape the attached cells off the Petri dish and transfer them into a 50 mL tube. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 3234 × g at 4 °C for 10 min and the supernatants were discarded. For each condition tested, two biological replicates were created, each consisting of 20 petri dishes. The subsequent procedure for RNA extraction is described in 2.3.

Whole RNA extraction of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887

The L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 cell pellets acquired in 2.2.1 and 2.2.2 were resuspended in 1 mL Trizol®. Resuspended cells were transferred into ZR BashingBead Lysis Tubes (0.1 & 0.5 mm beads) (Cat. No. S6012-50, Zymo Research, Freiburg, Germany) and shook over 10 min at 2700 rpm. Afterwards, RNA extraction was performed as described in Direct-zol RNA Miniprep (w/Trizol) Kit (Cat. No. R2051, Zymo Research, Freiburg, Germany). RNA was eluted with 50 μL ultrapure water and stored at −80 °C.

Library construction, sequencing, and data analysis

The RNA extracted in 2.3 was sent to Novogene GmbH (Munich, Germany) for RNA sequencing. Altogether, four comparisons were performed: the same matrix (Vloc = corn salad or Poly = polystyrene) at two different temperatures (Warm = 22 °C and Cold = 4 °C) and the same temperature for the two different matrices. This results in the comparison groups VlocWarmvsPolyWarm, VlocColdvsPolyCold, PolyWarmvsPolyCold and VlocWarmvsVlocCold. Total RNA was cleaned from rRNA and precipitated with ethanol. Quantification and size distribution detection were performed with Qubit and real-time PCR. The quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on a NovaSeq X Plus Series (PE150) platform, according to effective library concentration and data amount required. The raw reads of fastq format were cleaned with fastp software by removing reads containing adapter, poly-N, and low-quality reads from raw data. Q20, Q30 as well as the GC content were calculated. The clean reads were aligned to the genome of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 (BioSample ID: SAMN53131052) using the software bowtie2 (v. 2.5.4). The gene expression level was quantified using the featureCounts (v. 2.0.6) software, where this software counted the number of reads mapped to each gene. The fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) of each gene was calculated based on the gene and read count mapped to each gene. The differential expression analysis (DEA) was performed with the DESeq2 R package (v. 1.42.0). The p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p-value (padj) < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed. The overall distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was visualized using volcano plots. The designation of genes, functional annotation, and categorization of DEGs from L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 were analyzed based on the information available in the databases “Clusters of Orthologous Groups of Proteins” (COGs), “Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes”, “UniProt” and “Integrated Microbial Genomes & Microbioms”. Only genes with ∣log 2 (FoldChange) ∣ > 2 were considered for differential gene expression analysis (DGA), if not stated otherwise. Genes with an adjusted p-value >0.05 were not considered for DGA. All protein coding DEGs were clustered by their respective function according to COGs database (last update August 2025), where a total of 2232 genomes from 2103 bacterial species is used to group proteins into 26 functional groups. To cluster the DEGs in this study, after cutting every non-significantly expressed gene, DEGs where then again filtered by their respective log2FoldChange, where a value between −2 and 2 was considered as negligible and therefore discarded as well. The criteria for filtering were chosen according to similar RNAseq studies in the past (Alio et al., 2023; Gędas et al., 2025). The remaining genes were then divided in up- and downregulated genes.

Determination of the bacterial biomass for RNA extraction

To determine the biomass used for RNA extraction, acquired through pulsification of inoculated corn salad leaves (see 2.2.1 and 2.2.2), the viable count of the autochthonous microbiota and L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 growing on corn salad was identified. For that, six times 25 g of commercially available corn salad leaves (Contrada scorziello anc., Eboli, Italy), which account for two times three biological replicates, were prepared as stated in 2.2.1. All replicates were inoculated with a L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 cell suspension in sterile PBS buffer. After immersion in PBS buffer, the biological replicates were transferred into sterile beakers separately, of which three were incubated at 4 °C and three at 22 °C, respectively, for 24 h. After pulsification (described in 2.1), 1 mL of the cell suspension was added to 9 mL of sterile PBS buffer and a decimal dilution series up to 10e-7 was created. Then, 100 μL of the 10e-5, 10e-6, and 10e-7 dilutions were plated in duplicate on BHI and Palcam agar, which is selective for Listeria spp., and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The colonies were counted and the viable counts were calculated. To determine the viable cell count of the autochthonous microbiota, the procedure was the same, except for the leaves were not immersed in a suspension of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 cells, but therefore in sterile PBS buffer. Plated decimal dilution steps were 10e-3, 10e-4 and 10e-5.

Results

To determine the biomass used for RNA extraction, the viable count of all cells washed from the corn salad leaves was calculated and also the amount of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 cells. For that, cells were plated on unselective BHI agar and to select for L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887, cells were also plated on Palcam agar. The evaluation of the viable count acquired in 2.5 (see Table S1) on BHI agar shows that at 22 °C 2.6 × 10e11 ± 13.36% cfu/25g corn salad were washed from the inoculated leaves and used for RNA extraction. The cells plated on Palcam agar led to a viable count of 2.7 × 10e11 ± 24.26 % cfu/25g corn salad. At 4 °C the viable cell count acquired from BHI agar is 2.2 × 10e11 ± 7.14 % cfu/25g corn salad and on Palcam agar 1.8 × 10e11 ± 9.61 % cfu/25g corn salad. Determination of the cell count of the autochthonous microbiota on the corn salad leaves led to viable counts of 1.5 × 10e9 ± 26.40 % cfu/25g corn salad after incubation at 22 °C and 5.7 × 10e8 ± 47.64 % cfu/25g corn salad at 4 °C. As metabolic processes, virulence, and colonization behavior of L. monocytogenes is highly influenced and regulated by temperature, the transcriptome of this bacterium was compared at room temperature (22 °C) and the food storage temperature of 4 °C. As the nutrient availability modulates the growth of L. monocytogenes and other chemical compounds may also impact its colonization behavior, which is why the transcriptome of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 growing on the corn salad leaves was furthermore compared to the growth on polystyrene, which is a commonly used packaging material L. monocytogenes may come in contact with, when being attached to commercially available corn salad. The raw data generated is available at the GEO repository of NCBI (Accession number: GSE324553).

For RNAseq analysis, DEGs with a padj value > 0.05 were not considered in this work.

Bioinformatic analysis also revealed “novel genes”. Novel genes are gene predictions made by comparing the generated reads with the reference genome using Rockhopper (McClure et al., 2013). By that, novel gene transcripts were predicted, which are considered potential protein coding by the RNAseq service provider. However, since the novel genes are considered only potentially protein coding and no scientifically sound statement can be made about them, they were cut. The unfiltered raw data plotted as a volcano plot is shown in Fig. S1. The results after cutting all insignificant genes, not regulated genes, sRNAs and novel genes are shown in Table 1. Across comparisons, the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was similar, with 2872–2968 total DEGs (or 3030–3126 when including novel genes) in each condition. The fraction of statistically significant DEGs (padj < 0.05) differed strongly between conditions: VlocColdvsPolyCold showed the highest count (2461 significant DEGs), whereas VlocWarmvsVlocCold showed the fewest (104 significant DEGs). Applying the additional fold-change filter (|log2FoldChange| > 2), the number of regulated genes was highest in VlocColdvsPolyCold (1,635) and lowest in VlocWarmvsVlocCold (30). In VlocWarmvsPolyWarm, regulated DEGs were nearly balanced between up- and downregulation (311 up vs. 323 down). In VlocColdvsPolyCold, downregulated genes exceeded upregulated genes (870 down vs. 765 up), whereas in PolyWarmvsPolyCold the regulated set was dominated by upregulated genes (363 up vs. 34 down). Overall, VlocColdvsPolyCold had both the largest significant and regulated DEG counts, while VlocWarmvsVlocCold displayed comparatively few differentially expressed genes under the applied thresholds.

After filtering all DEGs as described, it became clear that the surface on which L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 grows was more impactful on the total amount of DEGs than the exposed temperature (see Fig. 1). Growing on corn salad compared to polystyrene results in more DEGs (1621 and 634 DEGs, Fig. 1B and A) than growing on the same surface at the different exposition temperatures of 22 °C and 4 °C, which results in 396 and 30 DEGs (Fig. 1D and C). Clustering shows that among all incubation conditions the largest group of DEGs is functionally unknown, except for VlocWarmvsVlocCold (Fig. 1C), where the largest groups are “Inorganic ion transport and metabolism” for the upregulated genes and “Carbohydrate transport and metabolism” for downregulated genes (see Fig. 1). Besides these, when incubated on corn salad, most of the upregulated genes are “Transcription” related, compared to when incubated on polystyrene, independent from temperature. On corn salad at 22 °C the second most upregulated genes are related to “Coenzyme transport and metabolism” (15.0%) (Fig. 1C) and on polystyrene, 15.6% of all upregulated genes are related to “Translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis” (Fig. 1D). At both temperatures tested, among the downregulated DEGs the “Carbohydrate transport and metabolism” is the second largest cluster when L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 grows on corn salad compared to when it grows on polystyrene (Fig. 1A and B).

The genome of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 encodes a multitude of different virulence factors and virulence-associated genes. These genes were identified via the “VirulenceFinder 2.0” software (Software version: 2.0.5, last updated at 31.01.2024), by uploading the genome of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 into the tool, setting the “%ID threshold” to 90% and “minimum gene length” to 60%. Identified virulence factors and their log2FoldChange in the corresponding incubation condition are depicted in Table 2. No virulence-associated DEGs meeting the applied thresholds were detected for VlocWarmvsVlocCold. In contrast, prfA was upregulated on corn salad relative to polystyrene under both temperature conditions (VlocWarmvsPolyWarm: log2FoldChange 2.43; VlocColdvsPolyCold: log2FoldChange 4.16). In line with this, the PrfA-associated genes hly, inlA, and inlB were also increased on corn salad, where they were significant (hly: 2.70 on VlocColdvsPolyCold; inlA: 4.46 on VlocColdvsPolyCold; inlB: 2.10 in VlocColdvsPolyCold). Stress and proteostasis related genes showed elevated transcript levels on corn salad as well, with clpP increased in both corn salad comparisons (3.34 and 3.51) and in the temperature comparison on polystyrene (5.57), and clpE and clpB are also upregulated on corn salad (e.g., clpE: 5.58 and 6.89; clpB: 2.08 and 3.74). For biofilm-associated factors, flaA was upregulated in VlocColdvsPolyCold (4.32) and also in PolyWarmvsPolyCold (6.05). Overall, the most consistent pattern was higher expression of prfA and selected PrfA linked virulence genes together with increased Clp protease/chaperone transcripts on corn salad compared to polystyrene, with particularly strong effects in the cold corn salad comparison.

The top regulated genes of each condition can be found in Fig. 2. Among the top upregulated genes on VlocWarmvsPolyWarm (Fig. 2A), the copper sensing and uptake related proteins lmo1852, lmo1853, and lm1854 are to be found, with a log2FoldChange of 7.81, 6.86, and 7.66, respectively. Additionally, among the highest upregulated genes lmo2592 and lmo2593 are to be found (Fig. 2A), with log2FoldChanges of 6.71 and 6.68, while lmo2594 is downregulated with a log2FoldChange of −2.93. Ribosome-associated protein genes rplF, rplJ, rplV, rplX, and rpsS, all encoding ribosomal subunits, are among the most downregulated genes, with log2FoldChanges ranging from −6.63 to −8.21 (Fig. 2A). In contrast to that, in PolyWarmvsPolycold almost every ribosome subunit encoding gene (comprising rplABCEFJKLMNOPQRSTVWXY, rpmABCD, rpmE2, rpmF1, rpmG2, rpmHI, and rpsBCDEHIKLMOSPQRSTU, rpsZ1, rpsA1) is upregulated, with log2FoldChanges ranging from 6.42 to 2.03 (see Table S2). The gene encoding the polyisoprenoid-binding protein Ycel is strongly upregulated (log2FoldChange of 6.5 and 10.31, see Fig. 2A and B) on corn salad in comparison to polystyrene, independent of the temperature. This is also true for lmo2568, which remains uncharacterized to this point (Fig. 2A and B). The gene encoding the hypothetical protein lmo0647 is highly upregulated on polystyrene at 22 °C and on corn salad at 4 °C, both relative to polystyrene at 4 °C (Fig. 2B and D). Discussion

The determination of the biomass used for RNA extraction shows that L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 makes up for almost all cells washed from the corn salad leaves, with cell count determined by selective cultivation on Palcam agar even exceeding the cell count determined by unselective cultivation on BHI agar at 22 °C. This is surprising in a sense that we expected unselective cultivation leading to a higher cell count, as L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 in addition to the autochthonous microbiota is able to grow on BHI agar, whereas on Palcam agar only L. monocytogenes should be able to grow. However, the standard deviation calculated for Palcam agar at 22 °C is comparably high, so that the contribution of the autochthonous microbiota may fall within the range of experimental variation. The cell counts after incubation at 4 °C is overall smaller than at 22 °C, which was to be expected, since the lower temperatures inhibit the overall growth. Looking at the viable cell count for the autochthonous microbiota, we can show, that no naturally occurring L. monocytogenes cells could be detected by plating on Palcam agar.

Overall, three of the four conditions investigated show many significant DEGs (padj < 0.05), so the dataset contains a clear signal to work with. Even after applying the stricter |log2FoldChange| > 2 cutoff, there are still plenty of regulated genes (up to 1635) to analyze, meaning the regulatory differences are not only significant but also large enough to be analyzed. The low number of significant and regulated genes in VlocWarmvsVlocCold could also serve as a baseline, suggesting that strong differences mainly appear where expected rather than across all contrasts.

When evaluating the regulation of virulence factors L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 encodes, it becomes clear, that a multitude of virulence factors are differentially expressed in the incubation conditions presented in this study, except for VlocWarmvsVlocCold, where no DEG met the requirements of exceeding a |log2FoldChange| of >2 or a padj value < 0.05 to be considered significant.

A key role in the virulence of L. monocytogenes plays the positive regulatory factor A (PrfA). In the past, microarray analysis of Marr et al. (2006) suggested that 201 genes were regulated by PrfA, impacting not only virulence but also the stress response (Kazmierczak et al., 2006) and persistence (Price et al., 2018; Lemon et al., 2010). Interestingly, in this study prfA is upregulated temperature independent on corn salad in comparison to polystyrene, suggesting that growth on this surface promotes prfA transcription and thereby L. monocytogenes virulence and persistence, posing a potential risk for the consumer of contaminated corn salad. This is underlined by the fact that virulence factors like hly, inlA, and inlB, which are classified as group I virulence factors of L. monocytogenes and regulated by PrfA (Ollinger et al., 2008; Marr et al., 2006), are upregulated as well. This, however, does not come without a cost for L. monocytogenes. It has been reported that activation of PrfA leads to carbon nutrition/metabolism interference (Bruno and Freitag, 2010; Marr et al., 2006). This trend can also be observed in the results of this study, with the largest portion of genes (apart from “Function unknown”) associated with carbon metabolism being downregulated under conditions where prfA is upregulated. In this context, Bruno and Freitag (2010) suggest that activation of prfA leads to a lifestyle beneficial for being host-associated rather than environmental survival. This concern is supported by the fact that the proteases ClpP, ClpE, and ClpB are all upregulated on corn salad (see Table S2). The Clp proteases are considered to perpetuate protein homeostasis in prokaryotes, however, in L. monocytogenes, as well as other intracellular pathogens like Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the Clp proteases are crucial for the intracellular life style (Illigmann et al., 2021) and a knock-out of clpP, clpC, and clpE leads to impairment in virulence (Balogh et al., 2017; Nair et al., 1999, 2000). The elevated transcription levels of virulence-associated genes of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 suggest that cells persisting on corn salad can be primed for host interaction and may retain their pathogenic potential at the point of consumption. This stresses the importance of minimizing pre-harvest contamination and enforcing robust post-harvest hygiene and processing controls to reduce consumer risk. For the food industry, this data highlights corn salad as a relevant matrix where monitoring and sanitation strategies should be optimized to limit survival and transmission.

L. monocytogenes is known to be able to form biofilms as a form of persistence state, where it is able to withstand a multitude of different stresses, including dehydration, sanitizing agents or physical delocalization through washing. Work by Hoffmann and Weiss (2025) already showed that L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 forms biofilm in association with corn salad, however, genetic regulation still needs to be elucidated. Genes known to be involved in biofilm formation are flaA (Lemon et al., 2007), fliI (Alonso et al., 2014), virAB (Jiang et al., 2021), actA (Travier et al., 2013), and prfA, among others. Apart from the majority of the flagellar assembly machinery, (i.e., flgK, NCTC10587_000715, NCTC10587_000714, NCTC10587_000713, NCTC10587_000712, flgC, fliF, NCTC10587_000707, NCTC10587_000706) all of these genes are upregulated on corn salad (see Table S1), supporting the observations by Hoffmann and Weiss (2025) that biofilm is formed on the surface of the corn salad leaves.

Additionally, the gene encoding the cellulose binding protein NCTC10587_001709 (celBCA homolog) is among the highest upregulated genes on corn salad and polystyrene, indicating that L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 is able to sense the cellulose of the corn salad's cell walls and other polysaccharides or the polystyrene, for both of these investigated surfaces are rich in or based on carbohydrate.

The limited access to nutrients on the surface of the plant leaf in addition to the competition posed by the autochthonous microbiota makes room for the assumption that L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 is colonizing the corn salad leaves under stressful conditions. The GTP- sensing transcriptional repressor CodY is reported to be involved in the regulation of stationary growth phase and virulence genes in Gram-positive bacteria (Sonenshein, 2005), i.e. L. monocytogenes (Lobel and Herskovits, 2016) and is also upregulated on corn salad at 4 °C vs. corn salad at 22 °C. In pathogenic Gram-positive bacteria, the expression of several virulence factors is regulated by CodY in response to nutrient availability (Stenz et al., 2011). This connection supports the assumption of limited access to nutrients on the leaf surface. L. monocytogenes seems to encounter acidic stress when growing on corn salad leaves, as the gene encoding the regulatory protein RbsR is upregulated on both, corn salad at 22 °C and 4 °C compared to polystyrene. This protein is considered to be a sensor protein (Guerreiro et al., 2022) and leads to the activation of the σβ stressosome. The activation of the σβ stressosome is a process which is thought to promote general stress response and, alarmingly, favoring host invasion by activating the alternative sigma factor SigB (Meireles et al., 2024; Dessaux et al., 2021), which is the case for VlocColdvsPolyCold. This contrasts with the widespread reliance on refrigeration at ∼4 °C as a primary preservation strategy in commercial food distribution and highlights that cold-chain conditions alone may not reliably mitigate L. monocytogenes risk on ready-to-eat produce.

The expression of copper transport and copper efflux related genes (such as lmo1852, lmo1854, and lmo1853), is probably the result of copper stress, due to encountering leaking fluids from damaged corn salad leaves. Lmo1852, an ortholog to the copper chaperone CopZ, transports Cu+ cations to the CopA ATPase for copper efflux out of the cell, to cope with copper toxicity (Corbett et al., 2011). Interestingly, there is a link between the activation of copper efflux as a resistance mechanism and the infection process of L. monocytogenes (Ladomersky and Petris, 2015). White et al. (2009) showed that murine macrophages increase copper uptake and that copper-dependent mechanisms contribute to antimicrobial activity against invading pathogens. Encountering high copper concentration through chloroplast-associated copper proteins may trigger an adaptive response with potential relevance for conditions encountered during host infection. However, because copper concentrations on corn salad and macrophage survival were not assessed here, a direct link between plant-associated copper exposure and enhanced survival during host infection remains speculative and requires further experimental validation.

Conclusion

In this study, the transcriptomes of L. monocytogenes NCTC 10887 on corn salad leaves and on polystyrene at 22 °C and 4 °C were compared. The surface of attachment produced larger expression differences than the temperature, with the strongest response in VlocColdvsPolyCold and the weakest in VlocWarmvsVlocCold. On corn salad, prfA was upregulated relative to polystyrene at both temperatures, and hly, inlA, and inlB expressions were increased in the cold surface comparison. These findings indicate that cells persisting on corn salad, in particular under refrigeration temperatures, can retain, and in part increase, transcription of genes linked to host interaction. This implies that cold chain storage alone may not reliably reduce consumer risk if contamination occurs. Therefore, minimizing contamination at production level, and applying robust post-harvest hygiene and processing measures, are critical to reduce the likelihood that contaminated ready-to-eat corn salad carries the risk of infection to the consumer.
