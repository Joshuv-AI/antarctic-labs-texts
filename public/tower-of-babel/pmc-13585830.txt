Comparison of clinical outcomes of cabozantinib plus nivolumab and lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab in patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Kazutaka Nakamura, Kazuhiko Yoshida, Yuki Kobari, Yuki Nemoto, Hiroki Ishihara, Hironori Fukuda, Junpei Iizuka, Hiroaki Shimmura, Hiroshi Kobayashi, Yasunobu Hashimoto, Tsunenori Kondo, Toshio Takagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the treatment outcomes and safety of cabozantinib plus nivolumab (C + N) and lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab (L + P) for patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC).MethodsThis multicenter retrospective analysis included 92 patients with mRCC treated with either C + N or L + P as first-line therapy between April 2018 and August 2024. Progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were compared. Multivariable analysis was performed to identify independent prognostic factors for PFS.ResultsFifty-three patients received C + N and 39 received L + P. PFS (24.1 months vs. not reached, P = 0.725) and OS (46.7 months vs. not reached, P = 0.912) were not significantly different between the C + N and L + P groups. Over a median follow-up duration of 13.9 months, 31 patients experienced disease progression and 12 died. ORR was higher in the C + N group than in the L + P group (79% vs. 49%, P = 0.002), whereas DCR (100% vs. 95%, P = 0.096) and grade ≥ 3 TRAEs (47% vs. 36%, P = 0.280) were comparable. In the multivariable analysis, the treatment regimen (C + N as a reference) was not significantly associated with PFS (hazard ratio: 0.76, P = 0.476). Favorable/intermediate International mRCC Database Consortium risk was identified as an independent prognostic factor for PFS.ConclusionTreatment with C + N and L + P resulted in comparable PFS, OS, and safety profiles in patients with mRCC. Both regimens can be used to treat mRCC based on individual characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11255-026-05174-5.Keywords: Cabozantinib, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Lenvatinib, Nivolumab, Pembrolizumab, Renal cell carcinoma

Introduction

The therapeutic landscape for metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) has evolved dramatically with the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [1–4]. Combination regimens integrating these agents, such as cabozantinib plus nivolumab (C + N) and lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab (L + P), have demonstrated significant clinical benefits in large phase III trials [1, 2]. In the CheckMate 9ER trial, C + N showed progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and objective response rate (ORR) superior to those of sunitinib [1]. Similarly, the CLEAR trial showed that L + P significantly improved PFS, OS, and ORR compared with sunitinib, albeit with high rates of grade ≥ 3 adverse events [2]. Based on these findings, both combinations have received regulatory approval and are recommended as standard first-line therapies in major international treatment guidelines, including those from the European Association of Urology [5] and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network in the United States [6].

Although previous retrospective analyses have compared ICI-TKI combinations with dual immunotherapy [7–11], direct comparative studies between different ICI-TKI regimens remain scarce. Consequently, evidence regarding their relative efficacy and safety in real-world practice is limited, and understanding their comparative performance is essential for optimizing treatment selection.

Therefore, we performed a retrospective comparative analysis of the clinical performance of C + N and L + P as first-line therapies for mRCC in real-world settings.

Methods

Patient selection

We retrospectively evaluated 110 patients with advanced RCC (including metastatic and locally advanced RCC) treated with C + N or L + P as first-line therapy at our institution (Tokyo Women’s Medical University) and affiliated institutions (Tokiwakai Jyoban Hospital, Saiseikai Kawaguchi General Hospital, Saiseikai Kazo General Hospital, and Tokyo Women’s Medical University Adachi Medical Center) between April 2018 and August 2024. After excluding 18 patients based on the following criteria, 92 patients were included in the final analysis: Patients (1) with insufficient post-treatment clinical information, (2) who did not undergo imaging after initiation, and (3) without metastatic sites were excluded from the study. Histology was not restricted to this real-world cohort; patients with clear-cell and non-clear-cell subtypes were eligible.

Treatment regimens and evaluation method

Nivolumab (240 mg every 2 weeks or 480 mg every 4 weeks) or pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks or 400 mg every 6 weeks) was administered intravenously. Although dose modification of ICIs was not allowed, dosing intervals were adjusted based on the patient’s condition and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Cabozantinib was administered orally at a standard starting dose of 40 mg once daily. The first dose reduction was to 20 mg once daily, and the second dose reduction was to 20 mg every alternate day. If 20 mg was not tolerated every alternate day, cabozantinib was discontinued. Lenvatinib was administered at a dose of 20 mg once daily (full dose). The one-step reduction was 14 mg/day, and the two-step reduction was 10 mg/day. In selected patients, the initial dosing was determined through shared decision-making between the patient and the treating physician, taking into account the patient’s clinical background. On-treatment dose reductions or temporary interruptions were performed at the discretion of the treating physician, as clinically indicated. Following the initiation of these therapies, computed tomography of the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis, with or without contrast enhancement, was performed. Follow-up computed tomography was performed at regular intervals of 4–12 weeks, depending on the patient’s condition. These therapies were administered until radiographic or clinical disease progression or intolerable adverse events were observed. Both regimens were used across all International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) risk categories. Treatment selection was made through discussions between the treating physician and the patient; no standardized institutional criteria were applied. Deferred cytoreductive nephrectomy (CN) was considered in selected patients at the treating physician’s discretion. Indications and timing varied according to clinical judgment, and no standardized institutional protocol for CN was in place.

Outcome assessment

PFS, OS, ORR, and disease control rate (DCR) were evaluated. OS was calculated from the initiation of each therapy until death from any cause. Patients who were lost to follow-up were censored at the time of their last contact. PFS was calculated from drug initiation to disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. In addition, subgroup analyses of PFS and OS were performed for clear cell carcinoma and non–clear cell carcinoma (excluding cases with unknown histology), with further evaluations according to the prior history of previous surgery for renal tumor. Additional subgroup analyses stratified by disease onset (synchronous vs. metachronous) and IMDC risk category (favorable/intermediate vs. poor) are provided in the Supplementary Information. ORRs and DCRs were calculated based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [12]. If at least one target lesion was present, overall response was assessed according to RECIST version 1.1 regardless of the presence of non-target lesions. Responses of non-measurable lesions such as bone metastases were not included in the calculation of ORR, whereas non-CR/non-PD was considered equivalent to stable disease and included in the calculation of DCR. Tumor shrinking rate was calculated by measuring the diameters of target lesions according to RECIST version 1.1. In cases with non-measurable lesions these were excluded from the calculation of the percentage change in tumor size. Clinical outcomes following these regimens were also documented. TRAEs were evaluated using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Adverse events related to TKIs and immune-related adverse events related to ICIs were evaluated collectively and were not analyzed separately. The clinical courses of patients who underwent treatment discontinuation due to AEs were documented individually. In addition, multivariate analysis was performed to identify the factors independently associated with PFS. Radiological responses were assessed according to RECIST version 1.1 by the treating physicians at each institution, and the consistency of the recorded data, including patient clinical information, was subsequently verified by the site principal investigators and data managers.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Survival was calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between the groups were compared using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable analyses based on the Cox proportional hazards model were used to identify the risk factors for PFS. For multivariable analysis, variables were selected based on clinical relevance and univariable P-values (< 0.10), the use of a more liberal threshold for univariable screening was intended to prevent the omission of potentially important clinical factors for these regimens, while the final number of covariates was strictly limited to ensure model stability, owing to the small number of events. Notably, univariable and multivariable analyses were not performed for OS due to the limited number of events, which precluded statistically reliable modeling. The treatment regimen was included regardless of its P-value because of its clinical importance. Risks are expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed using JMP (version 17; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 92 patients were evaluated, comprising 53 patients treated with C+N (58%) and 39 patients treated with L+P (42%). The baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 69 years (range, 34–89 years), and 59% of the patients were aged >65 years. Most of the patients were male (74%). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, histological subtype, metastatic sites, or number of metastasis sites. The IMDC risk classification was 21% favorable, 51% intermediate, and 28% poor in the overall population. There was a slightly higher proportion of patients with an IMDC classification of favorable risk in the C+N group (C+N vs. L+P: 23% vs. 18%), whereas patients with poor risk were common in the L+P group (C+N vs. L+P: 21% vs. 38%). However, these differences were not statistically significant (P=0.176). Additionally, previous surgery for primary renal tumors before systemic therapy was more common in the C+N group than in the L+P group (74% vs. 62%, respectively), although this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.220).

Survival according to the therapies

After a median follow-up duration of 14.6 months in the C + N group and 16.4 months in the L + P group (P = 0.997) (Table 1), 31 patients (34%) experienced disease progression, and 12 patients (13%) died from cancer or other causes (Table 2). Over a median follow-up period of 13.9 months, PFS was not significantly different between the two groups (C + N vs. L + P: 24.1 months [95% CI 14.1–27.9] vs. not reached [NR; 95% CI: 14.6–not estimable {NE}], P = 0.725) (Fig. 1a). Similarly, OS was not significantly different between these groups (46.7 [95% CI 27.1 − 46.7] for C + N vs. N.R. [95% CI N.E. − N.E.] for L + P, respectively, P = 0.912) (Fig. 1b). The 6-month PFS rates were 91% in the C + N group and 82% in the L + P group, while the 12-month PFS rates were 77% and 76%, respectively. Regarding OS, the 6-month rates were 96% in the C + N group and 97% in the L + P group, with the 12-month OS rates were 96% and 87%, respectively. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed specifically for patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). In this subgroup, the median PFS was not significant different between these regimens (26.2 [95% CI 19.9 − 28.4] vs. N.R. [95% CI 14.6 − N.E.], P = 0.808) (Fig. 2a). Similarly, OS showed no significant difference between the groups (46.7 [95% CI 27.1 − 46.7] vs. N.R. [95% CI N.E. − N.E.], P = 0.465) (Fig. 2b). In addition, among patients with non–clear cell carcinoma (non-ccRCC), median PFS was also comparable between the two regimens (13.8 [95% CI 4.9 − N.E.] vs. 13.2 [95% CI 3.3 − N.E.], P = 0.772) (Fig. 2c), and no significant difference in OS was observed (N.R. [95% CI 22.8 − N.E.] vs. N.R. [95% CI 5.0 − N.E.], P = 0.336) (Fig. 2d). In addition, among patients with a prior history of nephrectomy or partial nephrectomy, there were no significant differences in PFS (26.2 [95% CI 16.5 − N.E.] vs. 17.4 [95% CI 13.1 − N.E.], P = 0.653) (Fig. 3a) or OS (46.7 [95% CI N.E. − N.E.] vs. N.R. [95% CI N.E. − N.E.], P = 0.890) (Fig. 3b). In patients with an intact primary renal tumor, PFS (14.1 [95% CI 7.2 − 27.9] vs. N.R. [95% CI 5.0 − N.E.], P = 0.524) (Fig. 3c) and OS (N.R. [95% CI 14.3 − N.E.] vs. N.R. [95% CI 6.9 − N.E.], P = 0.708) (Fig. 3d) did not differ significantly between the regimens.

Tumor responses according to the therapies and outcome after the therapies

ORRs were significantly higher in the C + N group than in the L + P group (79% vs. 49%, P = 0.002). In contrast, the DCRs were not significantly different between the groups (100% vs. 95%, P = 0.096). The median tumor shrinking rate at best response was −39% in the C + N group and −25% in the L + P group, showing no significance between the regimens (P = 0.061). Deferred CN was performed more frequently in the L + P group than in the C + N group (6% vs. 21%, P = 0.030). Regarding clinical status after the first-line regimen, ongoing treatment was recorded for 64% of patients in both groups (P = 0.996). Death during the first regimen (4% vs. 0%, P = 0.135), transition to a second-line regimen (24% vs. 21%, P = 0.649), and Best Supportive Care (8% vs. 15%, P = 0.236) were also comparable between the groups. Overall, the distribution of outcomes after the first regimen did not differ significantly (P = 0.301). (Table 2).

TRAEs and safety

The incidence of any-grade TRAEs (C + N vs. L + P: 100% vs. 97%, P = 0.241) or grade ≥ 3 TRAEs (C + N vs. L + P: 47% vs. 36%, P = 0.280) was not significantly different between the groups (Table 3). Overall, there was no significant difference in the incidence of TRAEs between the C + N and L + P groups. Table S1 (Online resource), summarizes the treatment intensity and modifications for each regimen. The distribution of initial TKI dosing differed between regimens (P = 0.014). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in subsequent TKI dose modifications, permanent discontinuation of TKIs or ICIs, or treatment interruptions due to AEs. Furthermore, the specific profiles of TRAEs leading to these dose-modifications or interruptions were analyzed descriptively, as summarized in Table S2 (Online Resource). Regarding the clinical trends for TKI dose reduction, the C + N group showed a higher frequency of modifications due to liver dysfunction (50%) and palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia (62%) compared to the L + P group (14% and 20%, respectively). Due to the limited and varied number of individual AE cases, formal statistical comparisons were not performed for these descriptive findings.

Subgroup analyses

In the univariable analysis, the treatment regimen (C + N vs. L + P) was not significantly associated with PFS (HR: 0.88 [95% CI 0.43–1.81], P = 0.725). In the multivariable analysis for PFS, after adjusting for IMDC risk group, histology and treatment regimen, favorable or intermediate IMDC risk was significantly associated with prolonged PFS (HR: 0.39 [95% CI 0.16–0.90], P = 0.029) (Table 4). Multivariable analysis for OS was not performed because of the limited number of OS events.

Discussion

In this retrospective multicenter Japanese cohort, C + N and L + P showed no significant differences in PFS, OS, or TRAE incidence, with consistent findings across histology-stratified subgroup analyses. While comparative studies specifically focusing on non-ccRCC have been reported [13], this is one of the first direct comparative studies of C + N and L + P in a real-world setting that includes both ccRCC and non-ccRCC histologies. This broader inclusion reflects routine clinical practice, whereas pivotal phase III trials primarily enrolled patients with a clear-cell component [1, 2].

Our findings align with those of cross-trial comparisons, which have shown substantial survival benefits of both regimens over TKI monotherapy controls in randomized phase III trials [1, 2]. The median PFS of 24.1 months for C + N in our cohort was longer than that reported in the CheckMate 9ER trial (median PFS: 16.6 months) [1]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient selection or clinical practice patterns, as our cohort included slightly older patients but had few patients with poor-risk disease or liver metastases, which are known adverse prognostic features [14, 15]. Similarly, the ORR for the C + N group was 79%, which was higher than the 56% reported in the latest extended follow-up trial [16]. In other real-world studies, such as the GUARDIAN study in Europe, the ORR was approximately 44%, and the median PFS was 18 months [17]. The ARON-1 study reported an ORR of 59% and a median PFS of 27.6 months for C + N, further supporting its efficacy in real-world populations [18].

The L + P group also showed favorable outcomes. Although the median PFS was not reached during the follow-up period, it was likely to approach or exceed 20 months, which is consistent with the 23.9 months reported in the CLEAR trial [2]. Only 10% of the patients in that trial were classified as having IMDC poor risk, which may have contributed to the relatively favorable PFS and ORRs [2]. The OS data are still immature, and the fact that the median OS in the L + P group was not reached is consistent with the 4-year follow-up of the CLEAR study [19]. Several real-world studies have evaluated the efficacy of L + P, reporting a time to treatment failure of 6.2 months in one European cohort [20], and PFS of 19 months in patients with IMDC intermediate or poor risk [21]. A Japanese study reported an ORR of 66% despite including a high proportion of patients with IMDC poor risk [22]. Additionally, the ARON-1 study, which included 26% of patients with IMDC poor risk, showed a median PFS of 25.6 months and an ORR of 59%, highlighting the favorable performance of L + P even among those with adverse prognostic features [23]. These observations reinforce the utility of both regimens in routine clinical practice for diverse patient populations.

We observed a higher ORR with C + N than with L + P (79% vs. 49%), which differs from pivotal trials in which L + P achieved a higher ORR than C + N (CLEAR vs. CheckMate 9ER) [1, 2]. Cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given differences in patient characteristics and study settings. First, the L + P group included a high proportion of patients with IMDC poor risk (38% vs. 12% in the CLEAR trial) [2] who are known to have low response rates regardless of treatment [24, 25]. In our multivariable analysis, IMDC poor risk was identified as an independent predictor of short PFS, reinforcing its prognostic value even in the context of modern ICI-TKI combination therapy. Second, intact primary renal tumors were relatively common in our cohort, particularly in the L + P group. In the CLEAR trial, 74% of patients had undergone prior nephrectomy, indicating that the majority did not have an intact primary renal tumor at treatment initiation. Similarly, in the CheckMate 9ER trial, 69% of patients in the C + N arm underwent nephrectomy. In our cohort, previous surgery for renal tumors was more frequent in the C + N group than in the L + P group (74% vs. 62%, respectively). As primary renal tumors are often minimally responsive to systemic therapy [26], this difference in tumor burden may have contributed to the lower observed ORR in the L + P group than in the CLEAR study cohort, and conversely, may explain the higher ORR observed in our C + N group than in the CheckMate 9ER study cohort. Third, outcomes of deferred CN were not available beyond the number of procedures performed. Deferred CN was more common in the L + P group, where intact primary tumors were frequent. Because deferred CN is often timed after an initial response of metastatic lesions, performing surgery before reaching the maximum response may have influenced the observed ORR, particularly in the L + P group.

While ICI-TKI combinations offer efficacy superior to that of TKI monotherapy, they also raise concerns about toxicity, warranting careful evaluation of their safety profiles [27]. No statistically significant differences in the overall safety profiles and grade ≥ 3 severe adverse events were observed between the L + P and C + N groups. These rates were lower than those reported in pivotal trials [1, 2] and real-world data [17, 21–23], possibly because of retrospective reporting or flexible dose adjustments in clinical practice. Liver dysfunction associated with C + N has also been reported in our affiliated institutions, and such events may be relatively frequent in Japanese patients [28]. The rate of permanent ICI discontinuation was comparable between the groups, with no significant differences. Overall, both regimens were well tolerated, with appropriate monitoring and dose modifications, suggesting comparable safety and feasibility in real-world Japanese patients.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. First, the treatment selection was non-randomized, introducing a potential selection bias. Although the baseline characteristics were generally balanced, differences in the IMDC risk, tumor burden, and nephrectomy rates may have influenced the outcomes. While the multivariate analysis was adjusted for known confounders, residual confounding factors could not be ruled out. Second, the modest sample size (n = 92) and median follow-up duration (13.9 months) limited the statistical power, particularly for detecting differences in OS or late PFS divergences. Third, the absence of a blinded independent central review for imaging and safety adjudication may have introduced assessment bias; tumor responses were determined per RECIST v1.1 by site investigators, and adverse events were graded using CTCAE v5.0. Fourth, low-grade or subjective TRAEs may have been underreported, and quality-of-life data were not available. Fifth, we used the median treatment duration as a surrogate for drug exposure and tolerability, as the retrospective design and individualized dose adjustments in real-world practice precluded a strict evaluation of the relative dose intensity (RDI). Finally, because this was a single-region cohort study, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. The validation of external cohorts is warranted.

In this real-world retrospective study, C + N and L + P demonstrated similar efficacy and safety as first-line treatments for mRCC. These findings support the use of either regimen based on individual patient characteristics.
