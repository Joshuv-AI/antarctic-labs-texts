Supporting community integration and wellbeing after relocation among older adults living alone: a study protocol for the welcome project in Japan
Authors: Mari Yamashita, Ayako Edahiro, Mariko Nishi, Mutsuko Okamura, Yukiko Kugimiya, Hana Sakurai, Nobuko Nagano, Kae Ito, Tsuyoshi Okamura

Background

Japan’s population is rapidly aging, and the number of single-person households comprising adults aged 65 and older is steadily increasing [1]. Many older adults are relocating from familiar neighbourhoods owing to aging housing, declining health, or economic challenges [2]. In some cases, they move to be closer to their adult children or relatives while continuing to live independently. In Japan, this type of move is known as yobiyose—a family-initiated relocation intended to enable adult children to provide nearby support and care [3]. In other cases, older adults relocate in search of housing that is more economically or physically manageable. However, whether yobiyose or self-initiated, such relocations often involve significant psychosocial challenges. Relocated older adults frequently face difficulties rebuilding social ties and adjusting to unfamiliar communities. Relocation in later life has been associated with increased risks of depression, cognitive decline, and reduced quality of life (QoL), particularly when the move is involuntary or poorly supported [4–7]. Although Japan is developing a comprehensive support system based on the philosophy of Aging in Place—enabling older adults to continue living in their communities—many existing services rely on individuals to initiate contact [8]. Consequently, older adults with limited ability to seek out support are at high risk of missing early opportunities to connect with their new communities and may become socially isolated.

However, research specifically focusing on practical support and the adaptation after relocation remains limited. Little is known about older adults’ actual experiences or the effectiveness of the interventions designed to support them.

This study’s purpose is to examine the feasibility and effectiveness of a community-based support program called the Welcome Project. This intervention facilitates early engagement with newly relocated older adults in a large metropolitan apartment complex. As part of the program, professionals from the local support centre (e.g., public health nurses) visit new residents shortly after their move, offering to accompany them when greeting neighbours and participating in local activities, depending on their preferences. This early intervention aims to prevent social isolation and enhance psychological well-being by fostering connections with the local community.

The study will evaluate changes in subjective quality of life, frequency of interpersonal interactions, and attitudes toward the community within the first three months after relocation. The findings will contribute to the development of support systems for socially vulnerable populations, such as older adults living alone, and offer practical insights for municipalities, public housing authorities, and community-based professionals.

Methods

Study design

This research consists of a quasi-experimental intervention study designed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a practical support program—the Welcome Project—targeting older adults living alone who have recently relocated to a large rental apartment complex in a major Japanese city. The study is not a randomized controlled trial, but rather a non-randomized controlled trial, in which participants are assigned to either the intervention or control group based on their own preferences (i.e., self-selection).

The study adopts a superiority trial framework, aiming to determine whether the intervention group demonstrates superior outcomes compared to the control group. Although the two groups are naturally formed based on participant preference, the allocation is not randomized, and the allocation ratio is not 1:1, as a higher number of participants are expected to opt into the intervention group.

This study protocol has been developed in accordance with the SPIRIT 2013 statement [9] and the TIDieR checklist [10], which are provided as Additional Files 1 and 2.

Study setting

The study will be conducted primarily in District A of Takashimadaira Danchi, located in Itabashi Ward, Tokyo. This large public rental housing complex, constructed in the 1970 s, has a high aging population and continues to see an influx of older adults living alone. In this area, the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology (TMIG) has established a community-based support hub known as CoCo-Kara Station. This centre, operated by TMIG, is open to all local residents and staffed by a multidisciplinary team, including public health nurses, clinical psychologists, social workers, physicians, and researchers, who provide individualized support services. The intervention for this study will be implemented at CoCo-Kara Station from July 2025 to March 2026.

The target participants are older adults aged 65 years and over, living alone, who have recently relocated to District A. Based on the number of residents who moved into the area between April 2024 and March 2025, approximately 80 such individuals are expected to relocate during the study period. We anticipate that around 75% will both consent and complete follow-up assessments (i.e., return both baseline and follow-up questionnaires), yielding approximately 60 participants for analysis. An a priori power calculation using G*Power (version 3.1) indicated that, under the assumption of a 3-point mean difference on the CASP-19, a standard deviation of 9, and a pre–post correlation of 0.60, the study would have approximately 83% power with 60 completers at a two-sided α of 0.05. Participants will receive an explanation of the study and a survey form at the time of their move. Those who provide informed consent will be enrolled. There are no specific exclusion criteria. Participants will be divided into two groups based on their expressed preference regarding the intervention: those who opt to receive support from the Welcome Project will constitute the intervention group, while those who decline support will form the control group.

Intervention

The Welcome Project is a practical support program designed to facilitate social integration and community adjustment following relocation. At the time of move-in, participants are provided with a flyer and a self-administered questionnaire by the housing caretaker. The flyer outlines the Welcome Project, includes the contact information for the researchers, and provides a map of CoCo-Kara Station. The questionnaire asks whether the participant is interested in joining the program. Those who express interest are contacted by the research team for further explanation and to schedule participation.

Support is provided by trained professionals, including public health nurses, psychologists, and social workers. Prior to implementation, all providers receive training on the program’s purpose, effective communication strategies, and safety protocols for accompanying residents. Intervention components

Accompaniment to greet neighbours

In Japan, new residents traditionally visit neighbouring households—typically those above, below, and on either side—with small gifts (e.g., soba noodles) to introduce themselves. However, this custom has declined, particularly among single-person households. In this intervention, public health nurses or other professionals accompany the participant with a small towel gift, facilitating neighbourly introductions and initial social connections.

Community gathering and consultation

Participants are also invited to a monthly 60-minute gathering held at CoCo-Kara Station, where they can meet other new residents and engage in informal interactions. In addition, long-term volunteer residents are available to offer advice on everyday matters in the community, such as where to find local activities or services.

Delivery and adaptation of the intervention

Generally, the greeting and accompaniment support is offered once within two weeks of the move-in. Upon request, additional support may be provided, such as information about local events, individual consultation regarding daily concerns, or referrals to community gathering opportunities. Any participant who receives one or more intervention components is considered part of the intervention group.

Support content and delivery are flexibly adapted based on each participant’s preferences and physical or mental health condition. For example, if neighbours are unavailable for accompaniment to greet neighbours, the activity may be postponed. If a participant has limited mobility and cannot attend community gathering and consultation, alternative forms of social connection—such as phone introductions or individual home visits—may be arranged. These decisions are made collaboratively with the participant.

Discontinuation or modification criteria

Intervention components may be modified or discontinued based on the participant’s request, physical or mental health condition, or other practical constraints. For example, if a participant experiences distress, discomfort, or deterioration in health, the support may be paused or terminated in mutual agreement with the provider. All such decisions will be documented using the fidelity checklist and reviewed during regular debriefing meetings.

Relevant concomitant care and interventions

Participants are free to access other health, welfare, or community-based support services during the study period, including regular visits to primary care physicians or social support programs, as these are considered part of standard community care.

Fidelity monitoring and feedback

To ensure consistency and fidelity in implementation, providers complete a structured checklist after each session, recording key aspects such as whether the project was explained, participant preferences, and intervention status. Additionally, quarterly debriefing meetings are held with implementers, researchers, and public housing managers from the Urban Renaissance Agency (UR) to review challenges, share insights, and refine intervention procedures.

Measurement

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study is psychological well-being. To assess this, we use the Japanese version of the CASP-19 [11], a 19-item scale comprising four subscales: Control, Autonomy, Self-realization, and Pleasure.

Other measurements

In addition to the primary outcome, the questionnaire includes items on sociodemographic attributes, mental health indicators, social functioning, and relocation-related experiences. Program satisfaction will be assessed only in the intervention group. Sociodemographic attributes include gender, age, marital status, perceived economic status, current employment, longest-held occupation, years of education, and medical history. Mental health indicators include psychological well-being (WHO-5-J) [12], loneliness (3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale) [13], depressive symptoms (2-item screening) [14], and personality traits (Japanese version of 10-item personality inventory) [15]. Social functioning is assessed using measures such as the frequency of interpersonal interactions and outings, size of the social network (Abbreviated version of the Lubben Social Network Scale) [16], neighbourhood ties, attitudes toward the community [17], social capital, frequency of participation in social activities, and frequency of eating meals alone. Relocation-related questions include the number of previous moves, presence of anxiety or concerns requiring consultation, and whether the participant has a regular physician and/or dentist. For participants in the intervention group, subjective evaluations of the program are collected using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire-8 [18] to assess satisfaction.

All data are collected using standardized, self-administered questionnaires. Data entry will be performed by trained personnel, and a subset of entries will be double-checked to ensure accuracy. Instruments used in the study have been validated in previous research, and references to their reliability and validity are provided.

Retention and follow-up

To promote participant retention, the follow-up questionnaire is designed to be brief and easy to complete, and includes a stamped return envelope. If participants discontinue or deviate from the intervention, they will still be asked to complete the follow-up questionnaire, and data on primary and secondary outcomes will be collected regardless of participation status.

Data management

All questionnaire responses will be entered into a password-protected spreadsheet by trained research staff. Each participant is assigned a unique ID code, and no personally identifiable information is included in the analysis dataset.

A subset of the data will be double-entered to check for accuracy, and simple range and logic checks will be performed.

The electronic data will be stored in a secure, password-protected cloud storage system (Dropbox), which is accessible only to authorized research team members.

Paper-based questionnaires will be stored separately in a locked cabinet at the research site.

No formal data management manual has been developed, but data handling procedures have been shared among team members and reviewed internally.

Statistical analysis

Using CASP-19 scores at baseline and follow-up as the dependent variable, we will examine differences in outcomes between the two groups: participants who received either the greeting accompaniment or the community gathering and consultation component of the Welcome Project (intervention group), and those who did not participate in the Welcome Project at all (control group), using a linear mixed-effects model. Secondary outcomes will also be compared between the two groups using the same modelling approach. To account for potential baseline differences between the groups, we will calculate propensity scores based on key confounding variables (e.g., age, gender, mental health status, indicators of social interaction) and include them as covariates in the model to adjust for confounding effects. All participants who complete both the baseline and follow-up assessments will be included in the primary analysis.

Participants who deviate from the intervention protocol (e.g., incomplete participation in the Welcome Project) will still be included in the intervention group under an intention-to-treat approach. Missing outcome data will be handled using multiple imputation under the assumption of missing at random (MAR), if the proportion of missing data exceeds 5%.

Ethics and dissemination

No data monitoring committee has been established, as this study involves a minimal-risk, non-invasive community-based intervention. Interim analyses are not planned due to the short duration and low complexity of the trial. Although serious harms are not anticipated, any psychological distress or participant complaints will be recorded and reviewed by the research team. No external auditing is planned; study implementation and data quality will be monitored internally through regular team discussions. As this is a minimal-risk, non-invasive study, no ancillary or post-trial care or compensation for harm is planned.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Review Committee of TMIG (approval number: R24-132). A self-administered, mail-based questionnaire will be used for data collection. At the beginning of the questionnaire, participants are provided with information about the study’s purpose and procedures, data privacy, and the voluntary nature of participation. A consent checkbox is included; participants are considered to have provided informed consent by checking this box. The study’s results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications, academic conference presentations, and reports submitted to relevant administrative agencies. Any major protocol amendments will be reported to the ethics committee and reflected in the trial registry. No biological specimens will be collected, and no ancillary studies are planned.

Discussion

This study investigates, within a quasi-experimental framework, the effectiveness of the Welcome Project—a practical support program designed to assist older adults living alone in adjusting to a new community following relocation. Although previous research has suggested that relocation can affect older adults’ health and psychological stability, there is a lack of empirical evidence regarding specific support strategies and their effectiveness in the early stages of post-relocation adjustment.

A distinctive feature of this study is its dual aim: to explore the psychological and social characteristics of older adults who relocate alone, and to present and evaluate a concrete intervention model. The primary outcome measure, the Japanese version of CASP-19, is a well-validated multidimensional scale that assesses subjective quality of life and has been widely used in international research. Given the limited accumulation of intervention studies on relocation in later life globally, this study provides novel empirical evidence in this area.

From a broader social and public policy perspective, the Welcome Project is implemented in collaboration with local resources widely available across Japan, such as municipal governments, UR, and community-based comprehensive support centres. This structure enhances the program’s potential for scalability and integration into governmental and community welfare systems. In particular, the early-intervention approach—targeting the narrow window of opportunity immediately after relocation—offers a promising model for preventing social isolation among older adults who are often underserved by conventional support systems.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is not a randomized controlled trial; participants self-selected into the intervention or control group based on their preferences, which may have introduced baseline differences between groups. Second, the content of the intervention was flexibly tailored to participants’ individual needs, which may limit the consistency and reproducibility of the support provided. Third, the evaluation period was limited to the first three months following relocation, and the sustainability of the intervention’s effects over time remains unknown.

Despite these limitations, this study represents an important effort to bridge field practice and research in addressing the real-world challenge of relocation—a major life transition for older adults. Future research should explore the generalizability and institutionalization of the support model through long-term follow-up studies and implementation research in other settings and regions.
