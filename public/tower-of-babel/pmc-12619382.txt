Evaluation of zonulin levels in pregnant women with hyperemesis gravidarum
Authors: Vefa Şakar, Gizem Işık Solmaz, Vildan Altahhan, Ömer Kadırhan, Sevda Yeleç, Şeyda Özdemir, Özhan Özdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHyperemesis gravidarum (HG) represents a serious pregnancy complication which affects 0.5-2% of pregnancies through severe nausea and vomiting that causes dehydration and weight loss and disrupts electrolyte levels. The exact causes of HG remain unknown but research suggests that hormonal shifts and genetic susceptibility and gastrointestinal problems all play a role. The research investigated serum zonulin levels in pregnant women with HG to determine their relationship with disease severity.MethodsThis case-control study included 44 pregnant women with HG aged 18–35 years with a BMI < 25 and 44 healthy pregnant women with similar demographic characteristics. Pregnant women with HG were divided into three groups based on PUQE scores: mild (n = 16), moderate (n = 19), and severe (n = 9). Serum zonulin levels were measured in all participants using the ELISA method, and their relationship with clinical parameters was investigated.ResultsZonulin levels were significantly lower in the HG group (33.7 ± 11.2 ng/ml) compared to the control group (46.8 ± 6.5 ng/ml) (p < 0.001). Zonulin levels decreased progressively with increasing severity of HG; they were 41.8 ± 5.0 ng/ml in the mild group, 35.2 ± 7.8 ng/ml in the moderate group, and 24.9 ± 9.2 ng/ml in the severe group (p = 0.002). A strong negative correlation was found between zonulin levels and HG severity (r=-0.72, p < 0.001), daily vomiting frequency (r=-0.65, p < 0.001), keton positivity (r=-0.58, p < 0.001), and electrolyte imbalance (r=-0.47, p = 0.002). ROC analysis showed a cut-off point of 40.2 ng/ml with 77.3% sensitivity and 84.1% specificity (AUC = 0.83).ConclusionThe low serum zonulin levels in pregnant women with HG and their negative correlation with disease severity suggest that alterations in intestinal barrier function may play an important role in the pathogenesis of HG. Zonulin may be used as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and severity assessment of HG.

Introduction

Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is a serious condition characterised by severe nausea and vomiting, weight loss, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalance that can persist until the 20th week of pregnancy [1]. The prevalence of HG during pregnancy ranges from 0.5% to 14% and it stands as a primary reason for hospital admissions during early pregnancy [2]. The gastrointestinal system undergoes multiple physiological changes during pregnancy which cause nausea and vomiting in 50–80% of pregnant women [3]. The scientific community has recently focused on understanding the role of gut microbiota together with gut permeability in pregnancy-related complications. Zonulin functions as a protein which controls intestinal permeability and its elevated levels link to inflammatory processes and metabolic changes [4]. Research shows that psychological stress damages intestinal barriers and disrupts gut microbiota which may lead to autoimmune disorders and other health issues [5]. Increased levels of estrogen and progesterone during pregnancy affect maternal metabolism by altering intestinal function and microbial composition [6]. The gut microbiome and barrier function experience changes through hormonal modifications (estrogen, progesterone, hCG) and immunological factors (Th1/Th2 balance) during pregnancy [7]. The catabolic stress condition of hyperemesis gravidarum triggers metabolic disorders in some patients which results in severe complications [8].

Research findings demonstrate that maternal microbiome changes and dysbiosis during pregnancy create intestinal barrier problems which lead to insulin resistance and inflammation that results in gestational diabetes mellitus complications [9]. The concentrations of zonulin and lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) were lower in preeclampsia patients than in normal pregnancies which indicates decreased intestinal permeability and modified immune system operation [10]. The presence of infections during pregnancy results in impaired barrier function because Plasmodium falciparum malaria in pregnant women leads to elevated gut-leak markers (sCD14, LBP) and systemic inflammation and increased preterm birth risk [11]. Research shows that pregnancy stress leads to disrupted tight junction proteins (Claudin, Occludin, ZO-1) and increased permeability and microbiota changes but estrogen maintains protective effects on barrier integrity [12]. The combination of Helicobacter pylori infection with metabolic syndrome-related mast cell activation leads to adverse outcomes in HG and preeclampsia and GDM through systemic inflammation and barrier dysfunction [13]. Research shows that plasma zonulin levels rise with increased adiposity and inflammation in non-pregnant individuals which demonstrates that metabolic stress acts as an independent factor for intestinal permeability changes [14]. The research shows that zonulin levels vary between different pregnancy disorders because they either increase or decrease based on the specific condition. The research fills an essential knowledge gap about zonulin in HG because previous studies have not examined this condition despite its major effects on both gastrointestinal and metabolic systems. The research fills an important knowledge gap by becoming the first investigation to measure zonulin levels in HG patients and their relationship to disease severity.

Studies investigating the relationship between hyperemesis gravidarum and intestinal permeability are limited in the literature. In particular, the role of zonulin levels in the pathogenesis of HG and its association with disease severity remain poorly understood [1, 15]. Although the potential use of zonulin as a non-invasive biomarker for assessing intestinal permeability has been explored in some pregnancy complications such as gestational diabetes [4], its value in HG has not yet been investigated. The limited availability of standardised protocols and assessment tools for managing HG can lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment [2]. The specific mechanisms by which changes in the gut microbiome and barrier function during pregnancy affect obstetric outcomes remain poorly understood [6]. In particular, further research is needed on the role of inflammatory and metabolic pathways in pregnancy complications [7]. The relationship between metabolic disorders triggered by catabolic stress conditions and HG has not yet been fully understood [8]. Although studies exist on the effects of probiotic and synbiotic supplements on serum zonulin levels [16], there is no data on the efficacy of these interventions in HG.

This study investigated whether serum zonulin levels which indicate intestinal permeability would be lower in pregnant women with HG while showing an inverse relationship with disease severity compared to the elevated zonulin levels found in other metabolic disorders in existing literature.

Method

Study population and sample

This case-control, analytical, and prospective study was conducted between April and July 2024 at the Gynaecology and Obstetrics Outpatient Clinic of Gülhane Training and Research Hospital. The sample size was determined to be at least 86 pregnant women, with at least 43 in each group, based on calculations with 80% power and 5% margin of error. The study included 44 pregnant women with hyperemesis gravidarum and 44 healthy controls (total 88 pregnant women) who were between 5 and 16 weeks of gestation, aged 18–35 years, nulliparous, and had a BMI < 25. The researchers chose nulliparous women for the study because previous pregnancies could affect both gastrointestinal symptoms and metabolic parameters and intestinal permeability. The researchers used a BMI restriction of less than 25 kg/m² to prevent obesity-related metabolic changes from affecting zonulin levels. The study’s internal validity improves through these criteria but the results become less applicable to women who have given birth before or who are older than 35 years or overweight or obese. The control group consisted of healthy pregnant women with similar demographic characteristics. Exclusion criteria included multiple pregnancy, pregnancy-related hypertension, gestational diabetes, chronic inflammatory bowel diseases, autoimmune diseases, liver or kidney failure, thyroid disorders, gastrointestinal diseases, antibiotic use in the past 3 months, and a history of medication use in the past 6 months that could affect intestinal permeability. The diagnosis of hyperemesis gravidarum was made according to the criteria of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists.

Study procedures

After obtaining written informed consent from all participants, demographic data and clinical characteristics were recorded. The PUQE (Pregnancy-Unique Quantification of Emesis and Nausea) score was used to determine the severity of hyperemesis gravidarum. According to this scoring system, patients were divided into three groups: mild (3–6 points), moderate (7–12 points), and severe (13–15 points).

Blood samples were collected in the morning (between 8:00–10:00 AM) after overnight fasting (minimum 8 h) and placed in red-capped or yellow-capped gel-filled biochemistry tubes. The samples were centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 15 min to separate the serum. The obtained serum samples were stored at −20 °C. Samples underwent 1 freeze-thaw cycle before analysis. After all samples were collected, serum zonulin levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit [Human Zonulin ELISA Kit, Catalog No: E-EL-H5560, Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Texas, USA; sensitivity: 0.47 ng/ml, measurement range: 0.78–50 ng/ml, intra-assay CV: <8%, inter-assay CV: <10%, lot number: L2024XXXX (exact number pending manufacturer confirmation)] in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. All samples were measured in single format.

The scientific community continues to debate how specific commercial zonulin ELISAs measure their targets. Multiple studies indicate that these commercial kits show reactivity to properdin and complement-related proteins instead of measuring pre-haptoglobin-2 which represents true zonulin. The assay continues to be used in pregnancy research because it is commercially available and manufacturers have validated its performance. The results from this assay should be understood as zonulin immunoreactivity measurements instead of precise zonulin protein concentrations.

The researchers collected blood samples from fasting patients who followed standardized collection procedures. The researchers excluded patients who needed immediate IV fluid treatment because of severe dehydration until they reached stable hemodynamic status. The researchers documented smoking status and recent infection history and medication use but these data were missing for some participants.

Intervention protocol

Groups were defined using specific criteria. Care was taken to ensure that the groups had similar demographic characteristics. The hyperemesis gravidarum group was divided into three subgroups using the PUQE score: mild (3–6 points), moderate (7–12 points), and severe (13–15 points). A total of 88 pregnant women were evaluated in the study; 44 of them were in the hyperemesis gravidarum group, and the other 44 formed the healthy control group. The severity distribution among patients in the hyperemesis gravidarum group was 16 patients with mild, 19 patients with moderate, and 9 patients with severe cases. The small number of participants in the severe group (n = 9) restricts the statistical power for subgroup-specific analysis. All patients received standard antenatal care procedures, and pregnancy follow-ups were conducted in accordance with routine protocols. No specific interventions were administered as part of the study protocol; the study was observational in nature.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS for Windows 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for intergroup comparisons. Due to unequal variances between groups for zonulin levels (Levene’s test p < 0.05), Welch’s ANOVA and Games-Howell post-hoc tests were also performed as sensitivity analyses. When significant differences were detected in the ANOVA test, Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons between groups. The subgroup analysis of HG severity requires caution because the small number of participants in the severe group (n = 9) makes the results exploratory in nature.

The relationship between zonulin levels and other parameters was evaluated using Pearson correlation test for variables with a normal distribution and Spearman correlation test for variables without a normal distribution. Multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing zonulin levels. The researcher checked the regression assumptions through three tests: Shapiro-Wilk test for normality of residuals and Breusch-Pagan test for homoscedasticity and VIF values for multicollinearity. The regression model was adjusted for gestational age as a continuous variable to account for the wide gestational age window (5–16 weeks).

The diagnostic value of zonulin levels was examined using ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals using the DeLong method. The Youden index was used to determine the optimal cutoff point. The Wilson score method produced sensitivity and specificity values with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. The statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Gülhane Training and Research Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study. Personal data were anonymised and coded for analysis to protect patient privacy. Data security was ensured on encrypted computers and in databases accessible only to the study team. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Results

The analysis of demographic and clinical characteristics between pregnant women with hyperemesis gravidarum and healthy pregnant women in our study revealed no significant statistical differences in age, BMI, height, current weight, pre-pregnancy weight and gestational age. The Shapiro-Wilk test results showed normal distribution for all demographic variables (p > 0.05) which validated the application of parametric tests for these statistical analyses. The study groups were comparable in terms of demographic characteristics, with no significant differences in age, BMI, height, weight, or gestational age between the HG and control groups. The serum zonulin levels of pregnant women with hyperemesis gravidarum (33.7 ± 11.2 ng/ml) were significantly lower than those of healthy pregnant women (46.8 ± 6.5 ng/ml). The difference in zonulin levels between groups was confirmed using both Student’s t-test (p < 0.001) and Mann-Whitney U test (p < 0.001), with the non-parametric test performed due to the unequal variances between groups (Levene’s test, p = 0.012). The notably wider standard deviation in the HG group (± 11.2 ng/ml) compared to controls (± 6.5 ng/ml) suggests heterogeneity within the HG population. No significant effect of age and BMI on zonulin levels was observed. The multivariate linear regression model explained 66% (R²=0.66) of zonulin level variation with an adjusted R²=0.64, because the model variables (HG severity, daily vomiting frequency, ketone positivity and electrolyte imbalance) explained two-thirds of the serum zonulin concentration differences among participants (Table 3).

According to the results of the ROC analysis, the diagnostic value of zonulin levels in determining hyperemesis gravidarum was found to be high (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.75–0.91, p < 0.001 vs. null hypothesis of AUC = 0.5). The optimal cutoff point of 40.2 ng/ml was determined using the Youden index method. A sensitivity of 77.3% (95% CI: 62.2–88.5) and a specificity of 84.1% (95% CI: 70.5–92.7) were obtained for a cutoff point of 40.2 ng/ml. The positive predictive value at this cutoff point was 82.9% (95% CI: 68.6–91.4), the negative predictive value was 78.7% (95% CI: 64.3–88.2), and the diagnostic accuracy was 80.7% (95% CI: 71.0–88.0) (Fig. 3). Discussion

This study evaluated serum zonulin concentrations in pregnant women experiencing hyperemesis gravidarum to establish their relationship with disease severity. The literature contains few studies about zonulin levels in pregnancy complications yet no research exists about zonulin measurements in hyperemesis gravidarum patients [15, 17]. Our study supported the initial prediction by showing that hyperemesis gravidarum patients had lower serum zonulin levels than healthy controls and that these levels decreased with worsening disease severity. The current commercial ELISA kits detect proteins other than true zonulin (pre-haptoglobin 2) because they identify properdin and complement-related proteins according to validation studies [18]. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as changes in “zonulin immunoreactivity” or “ELISA-detected intestinal permeability markers” rather than definitive zonulin protein measurements. The research findings introduce a fresh understanding of intestinal barrier dysfunction in hyperemesis gravidarum development while indicating that HG follows a different pathophysiological pattern than metabolic disorders which show elevated zonulin levels in the literature [17].

The research showed that pregnant women with hyperemesis gravidarum had lower zonulin levels (33.7 ± 11.2 ng/ml) than healthy pregnant women (46.8 ± 6.5 ng/ml). The study results match previous research on pregnancy complications because Mokkala et al. (2017) found zonulin levels in overweight pregnant women to vary between 25.3 ng/ml and 94.7 ng/ml based on metabolic condition [15]. The observed results were unexpected because they ran counter to the elevated zonulin levels documented in other metabolic disorders [14]. Mutluoglu and colleagues (2023) found that pregnant women with preeclampsia had lower plasma zonulin levels than healthy pregnant women [10]. This suggests that intestinal permeability may be affected by different mechanisms in some pathological processes that arise during pregnancy. The study by Güvey and colleagues showed that pregnant women with gestational diabetes and intrahepatic cholestasis and both conditions had elevated zonulin levels [19]. The research team developed multiple theoretical models to explain why zonulin immunoreactivity levels decrease in HG patients. The body might reduce intestinal permeability marker production through severe malnutrition and catabolic state as a protective response. The combination of persistent vomiting leads to acute mucosal damage which disrupts typical zonulin regulatory mechanisms. The hormonal environment of HG pregnancy produces distinct effects on intestinal barrier proteins which differ from those seen in other pregnancy-related complications. This difference suggests that malnutrition, weight loss, and catabolic processes observed in hyperemesis gravidarum may affect the gut microbiota and, consequently, gut barrier function. Ziętek and colleagues investigated the relationship between maternal serum short-chain fatty acids and metabolic parameters to understand how nutritional changes in hyperemesis gravidarum affect microbiota and intestinal barrier function [20]. Tzitiridou-Chatzopoulou and colleagues demonstrated that gut dysbiosis leads to various pregnancy complications while the low zonulin levels in hyperemesis gravidarum may result from microbiota changes and inflammatory responses that occur during the disease process [13].

Our study revealed a substantial negative relationship between the severity of hyperemesis gravidarum and zonulin concentrations. The zonulin values measured in the severe HG group (24.9 ± 9.2 ng/ml), moderate HG group (35.2 ± 7.8 ng/ml), and mild HG group (41.8 ± 5.0 ng/ml) and their relationship with disease severity (p = 0.002) indicate that intestinal barrier function undergoes significant changes during the HG process. The serum zonulin levels in different populations and conditions range from 0.00 to more than 100 ng/ml according to a systematic review of probiotics and zonulin. The inter-assay variability of zonulin measurements ranges from 5.9% to 10.6% [16]. The research by Liu and colleagues supports this finding because they detected elevated Clostridium and Candida bacteria in patients with hyperemesis gravidarum which indicates that gut microbiota changes might influence intestinal permeability [21]. The negative correlation between zonulin levels and electrolyte imbalance in our study (r=−0.47, p = 0.002) matches the results of Ajong and colleagues who studied electrolyte imbalances and their effects on pregnancy outcomes [22]. Adıgüzel and colleagues reported that in HG patients, inflammatory markers such as the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-lymphocyte ratio, and monocyte-lymphocyte ratio were significantly higher than those in the control group; this supports the possibility that the strong negative correlation observed in our study between zonulin and ketone positivity (r=−0.58, p < 0.001) may be associated with inflammatory processes [23]. These elevated inflammatory markers in HG support our finding of decreased zonulin levels, as chronic inflammation and metabolic stress in HG may paradoxically suppress intestinal permeability markers. The study found a significant negative relationship between zonulin levels and ketone detection (r=−0.58, p < 0.001) because worsening metabolic problems lead to decreased zonulin production. The observed relationship stands in contrast to other inflammatory diseases which show elevated zonulin levels. This study by Singh and colleagues shows that maternal microbiota alterations could lead to pregnancy complications which explains the negative correlation between zonulin levels and HG severity (r=−0.72, p < 0.001) and daily vomiting frequency (r=−0.65, p < 0.001) [9]. The severe vomiting and low oral consumption in HG patients results in significant changes to their gut microbiome structure through different mechanisms which include the depletion of beneficial bacteria that control zonulin regulation and changes in intestinal pH and electrolyte levels and physical damage to the intestinal lining from frequent vomiting. The negative relationship between zonulin levels and both HG severity (r=−0.72, p < 0.001) and daily vomiting frequency (r=−0.65, p < 0.001) indicates that persistent vomiting damages the gut epithelial barrier through mechanisms that differ from typical inflammatory responses by causing mucosal damage and pH changes and beneficial bacteria depletion which normally triggers zonulin production. The disease severity shows an inverse relationship with intestinal permeability markers because it increases instead of decreases.

In our study, the diagnostic value of zonulin levels in the diagnosis of hyperemesis gravidarum was evaluated using ROC analysis, and the AUC value was found to be 0.83 (95% CI: 0.75–0.91). This value indicates that zonulin has strong potential as a biomarker in the diagnosis of HG. The sensitivity and specificity values of 77.3% and 84.1%, respectively, for the cutoff point of 40.2 ng/ml are at a level that can be used in clinical practice. The high positive (82.9%) and negative (78.7%) predictive values and overall accuracy rate (80.7%) obtained in our study suggest that zonulin may be a valuable tool in the diagnosis of HG, particularly in the early identification of severe cases. The practical value of this biomarker needs evaluation against current medical prediction tools. The zonulin measurement at 40.2 ng/ml shows potential but additional research should conduct decision-curve analysis to establish its value when used in combination with basic clinical indicators like weight loss percentage and ketonuria presence and vomiting frequency. Unlike other pregnancy complications where zonulin levels increase, the decreased zonulin levels in HG represent a distinct pathophysiological pattern that warrants further investigation. Additionally, Liu and colleagues have noted that other biomarkers such as GDF15 may also be important in HG [21]. In a meta-analysis conducted by Jansen and colleagues, HG was shown to be associated with various adverse perinatal outcomes, and the use of biomarkers such as zonulin for early diagnosis may be clinically important [24]. The high positive (82.9%) and negative (78.7%) predictive values and overall accuracy rate (80.7%) obtained in our study suggest that zonulin may be a valuable tool in the diagnosis of HG, particularly in the early identification of severe cases.

Clinical implementation considerations

Zonulin measurement provides a potential tool for clinical HG patient management through risk assessment and treatment progress evaluation. The biomarker has multiple clinical applications including (1) High-risk patient detection through early pregnancy nausea and vomiting assessment with zonulin levels under 25 ng/ml for immediate hospitalization with aggressive IV fluid treatment and (2) Triage decisions based on zonulin levels between 25 and 40 ng/ml for outpatient oral antiemetic treatment and close monitoring and (3) Serial zonulin tests to monitor treatment effectiveness by showing increasing levels when metabolic status improves and (4) The combination of low zonulin with ketonuria and electrolyte disturbances helps identify patients who need thiamine supplementation for Wernicke’s encephalopathy prevention. The implementation of zonulin testing for clinical use requires cost-effectiveness studies to determine its value against current standard treatment approaches. The 24–48 h time needed for ELISA test results makes it less suitable for urgent clinical choices in clinical practice.

According to the results of our multivariate analysis, the most important factors influencing zonulin levels were HG severity (β=−0.64, p < 0.001), daily vomiting frequency (β=−0.44, p = 0.001), keton positivity (β=−0.39, p = 0.002), and electrolyte imbalance (β=−0.26, p = 0.028). The multivariate linear regression model demonstrates a 66% (R² = 0.66) ability to explain zonulin level variations which produces a clinically important result. The R² value of 0.66 shows that the model variables (HG severity and daily vomiting frequency and ketone positivity and electrolyte imbalance) explain 66% of the serum zonulin concentration differences between study participants. The statistical model demonstrates strong predictive power because its R² value of 0.66 indicates that clinical parameters effectively predict zonulin levels in HG patients. The unexplained 34% of variance in this study could stem from genetic elements and dietary influences before HG started and natural gut microbiota variations among participants. These results are consistent with the findings of Yadav and colleagues, who demonstrated that chronic stress during pregnancy disrupts the intestinal mucosal barrier by reducing the expression of tight junction proteins [25]. Similarly, Balci and colleagues observed gut microbiota dysbiosis in HG patients, reporting increased frequencies of Clostridium spp. and Candida spp., and decreased frequencies of Bifidobacterium spp. This dysbiosis may be associated with changes in zonulin levels and could play an important role in the pathophysiology of HG [26]. Wright and colleagues reported that intestinal barrier dysfunction during pregnancy increases plasma sCD14 and LBP concentrations, and these markers show a positive correlation with pro-inflammatory cytokines; this supports the interaction between changes in intestinal barrier function observed in HG and inflammatory processes [11]. According to the findings of Li and colleagues, chronic stress disrupts the intestinal mucosal barrier, reducing microbiota diversity and increasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This mechanism may explain the strong negative correlation we observed between zonulin levels and HG severity (r=−0.72, p < 0.001), vomiting frequency (r=−0.65, p < 0.001), and ketone positivity (r=−0.58, p < 0.001) in HG patients [12]. This study shows that zonulin levels decrease progressively as HG severity increases which creates a contradictory situation because intense metabolic stress and poor nutrition might activate a protective mechanism that reduces intestinal permeability markers rather than increasing them as seen in other stress conditions. The disease progression of other metabolic conditions leads to increased zonulin levels but HG shows an opposite pattern. The negative relationship between zonulin levels and HG severity (r=−0.72) and vomiting frequency (r=−0.65) and ketone positivity (r=−0.58) indicates HG has distinct pathophysiological processes that differ from other pregnancy complications when it comes to gut-brain axis changes during fasting and catabolic states. The extreme metabolic stress of HG leads to nutritional deficiencies and electrolyte imbalances which might reduce zonulin production or speed up its breakdown as a protective mechanism against severe metabolic stress instead of the typical inflammatory increase observed in other conditions. This paradoxical response may represent an adaptive mechanism to prevent excessive bacterial translocation during periods of severe metabolic compromise.

Study limitations and future directions

This study contains specific restrictions which need to be acknowledged. The study represents a single-center discovery-stage investigation that needs confirmation through independent multicenter cohort studies to achieve clinical readiness. The research took place at one facility with a small number of participants. The total number of 88 participants in our study provided enough statistical power for main analysis but the division of HG participants into severity levels created small subgroups especially in the severe HG group with only 9 participants. The small number of participants in the severe category weakens the reliability of subgroup analysis results and makes it more likely to miss actual effects. The large standard deviations between 5.0 and 9.2 ng/ml in the HG subgroups indicate individual differences that could reduce the applicability of our research results to specific severity levels. Research studies should work to obtain bigger participant numbers while making sure severe HG cases receive proper representation through at least 20–30 participants per subgroup to enhance statistical power and enable dependable subgroup evaluation. The uneven distribution of participants between mild (n = 16), moderate (n = 19) and severe (n = 9) groups in our study could have produced biased results when we analyzed the relationship between zonulin levels and disease severity. The research study focused on nulliparous women with BMI under 25 which restricts the ability to apply results to multiparous women and those who are overweight or obese during pregnancy because their intestinal permeability patterns could differ. The research needs to study diverse population groups to create reference values which apply to different population demographics. The commercial zonulin ELISA kits face a major issue because their specificity remains under dispute. The assays used in research studies detect properdin and complement C3 and other proteins instead of zonulin (pre-haptoglobin 2) according to mass spectrometry validation studies conducted by Ajamian et al. (2019). The absolute value measurements have limitations because of this restriction but the group-to-group comparisons remain valid for detecting intestinal permeability changes. The validation of research results should use lactulose/mannitol permeability tests and lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) and intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) as alternative markers. The measurement of zonulin levels at a single time point does not allow us to determine how these values change over the course of the disease. Future permeability markers would help to address these limitations and improve our understanding of zonulin’s role in HG pathophysiology. The practical utility of zonulin measurement in routine HG management needs to be multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal zonulin measurements and comprehensive metabolic profiling and independent intestinal established through cost-effectiveness analyses and clinical decision modeling.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the gut-brain axis and gut microbiota alterations may contribute to HG pathogenesis, as evidenced by lower serum zonulin immunoreactivity in HG patients that decreased with disease severity. The results need careful interpretation because commercial ELISA specificity has limitations and the study used a single-center design with a limited number of participants especially in severe HG (n = 9). The diagnostic potential of zonulin measurements for HG detection appears promising (AUC = 0.83) but clinical adoption needs validation through multiple centers with various patient groups (multiparous and BMI > 25) and comparison to established clinical markers and intestinal permeability tests and cost assessment. Research should verify these results by using LBP and I-FABP as permeability markers and determine if zonulin testing adds value to basic clinical indicators for HG treatment decisions.
