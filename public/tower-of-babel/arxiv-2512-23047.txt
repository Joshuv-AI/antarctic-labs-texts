Bayesian Effective Dimension: A Mutual Information Perspective
Sayantan Banerjee

Abstract
High-dimensional Bayesian procedures often exhibit behavior that is effectively low dimensional, even when the ambient parameter space is large or infinite-dimensional. This phenomenon underlies the success of shrinkage priors, regularization, and approximate Bayesian methods, yet it is typically described only informally through notions such as sparsity, intrinsic dimension, or degrees of freedom. In this paper we introduce the Bayesian effective dimension , a model- and prior-dependent quantity defined through the mutual information between parameters and data. This notion quantifies the expected information gain from prior to posterior and provides a coordinate-free measure of how many directions in parameter space are statistically learnable at a given sample size. In regular parametric models the effective dimension coincides with the usual parameter dimension, while in high-dimensional, ill-posed, or strongly regularized settings it can be substantially smaller. We develop basic properties of the effective dimension and present explicit calculations for Gaussian location models and linear models with general design, revealing close connections with spectral complexity and effective rank. These examples illustrate how shrinkage and regularization mechanisms directly control the growth of effective dimension. The framework offers a unifying perspective on dimension reduction in Bayesian inference and provides insight into uncertainty quantification and the behavior of approximate posterior distributions.

Introduction

High-dimensional Bayesian inference is routinely successful in settings where classical intuition based on parameter dimension would suggest severe overfitting or ill-posedness. Shrinkage priors, regularization, and approximate Bayesian procedures often yield stable posterior behavior even when the number of parameters is large relative to the sample size, or when the parameter space is infinite-dimensional. This phenomenon is commonly described using terms such as
sparsity
,
intrinsic dimension
, or
degrees of freedom
, yet these notions are typically model-specific and do not admit a unified Bayesian interpretation.

In regular parametric models, classical Bernstein–von Mises theory shows that posterior uncertainty is asymptotically governed by the finite parameter dimension, with information accumulation scaling as
(d/2)\log n

(
Le Cam, 1986
;
Bickel et al., 1993
;
van der Vaart, 2000
)
. In nonparametric and high-dimensional settings, posterior contraction theory replaces dimension by more refined complexity measures, such as metric entropy, sieve dimension, or prior concentration around low-dimensional subsets
(
Ghosal et al., 2000
;
Ghosal and van der Vaart, 2007
)
. While powerful, these approaches are typically loss-specific and do not yield a single, intrinsic notion of dimensionality associated with a Bayesian experiment.

Shrinkage priors provide a complementary perspective. Global–local priors, including Student-
t
and horseshoe-type constructions, have proved effective in high-dimensional regression and signal recovery problems
(
Polson and Scott, 2010
;
Carvalho et al., 2010
)
. Their success is often attributed to an ability to adaptively suppress uninformative directions while retaining sensitivity to large signals
(
Carvalho et al., 2010
;
van der Pas et al., 2017
)
. Theoretical analyses of such priors have largely focused on posterior contraction rates and adaptivity properties
(
Datta and Ghosh, 2013
;
van der Pas et al., 2014
;
van der Pas et al., 2017
)
, while the associated notion of dimensionality reduction is typically invoked only heuristically. For a comprehensive review of shrinkage priors in high-dimensions, we refer the readers to
Bhadra et al., 2019
;
Banerjee et al., 2021
.

A more explicit notion of dimension arises in frequentist regularization theory through
degrees of freedom
, defined as the trace of a smoothing matrix or the sensitivity of fitted values to perturbations in the data
(
Efron et al., 2004
;
Hastie et al., 2009
)
. Degrees of freedom provide a useful measure of model complexity in linear and generalized linear models, but they depend on a specific estimator and parametrization, and do not directly quantify the global change from prior to posterior that lies at the heart of Bayesian updating.

Parallel to these developments, information-theoretic quantities have played an important role in the analysis of Bayesian learning. Mutual information between parameters and observations arises naturally in Bayesian coding and minimum description length
(
Rissanen, 1986
)
, Bayesian decision theory
(
Bernardo, 1979
)
, and learning theory, where it has been used to control generalization error and algorithmic stability
(
Xu and Raginsky, 2017
;
Russo and Zou, 2016
)
. In Bayesian statistics, mutual information admits a particularly simple interpretation as the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from prior to posterior
(
Clarke and Barron, 1990
)
. Despite this, it is rarely interpreted directly as a measure of dimensionality or complexity in Bayesian inference.

The goal of this paper is to bridge these perspectives by introducing a notion of
Bayesian effective dimension
, defined through the mutual information between parameters and data. This quantity measures the expected information gain from prior to posterior and provides a coordinate-free descriptor of how many directions in parameter space are statistically learnable at a given sample size. In regular parametric models, the effective dimension coincides with the usual parameter dimension, while in high-dimensional, ill-posed, or strongly regularized settings it can be substantially smaller.

Related notions of Bayesian model complexity based on information-theoretic criteria have been discussed primarily in the context of model comparison and marginal likelihood; see, for example,
van der Linde, 2012
. The focus of the present work is different. Rather than comparing competing models, we study how mutual information quantifies the number of directions effectively learned by the posterior within a fixed prior–model pair, leading to an intrinsic, dimension-like measure of Bayesian learning. Motivated by this perspective, we define the Bayesian effective dimension as a normalized mutual information and study its properties and implications.

Contributions of this paper

This paper takes a deliberately modest and unifying approach. Rather than developing new contraction theorems or optimality results, we isolate an information-theoretic quantity already implicit in Bayesian updating and study its interpretation as a measure of dimensionality. First, we define the Bayesian effective dimension as a normalized mutual information between parameters and data. This definition is intrinsic to the prior–model pair, invariant under reparameterization, and monotone under loss of information in the data. Second, we develop explicit, non-asymptotic calculations of effective dimension in Gaussian models, including location models and linear regression with general design. These calculations reveal a close connection between effective dimension, spectral properties of the design, and notions of effective rank familiar from inverse problems and numerical analysis
(
Hansen, 1998
)
. They show how regularization and ill-posedness directly limit the growth of effective dimension, even when the ambient parameter dimension is large or infinite. Third, we show that common posterior approximations in Gaussian models can be interpreted as truncating effective dimension. Approximations that inflate posterior covariance necessarily reduce mutual information, and therefore learn fewer effective directions. This provides a principled explanation for the conservative uncertainty quantification often observed in approximate Bayesian inference
(
Bishop, 2006
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
. Finally, we extend the perspective to shrinkage priors through information-theoretic decompositions. In global–local models, effective dimension becomes a random, data-dependent quantity governed by latent signal-to-noise ratios. While closed-form expressions are generally unavailable, this viewpoint clarifies how shrinkage induces adaptive dimension reduction without imposing hard sparsity constraints.

Scope and organization

The paper is intentionally expository in nature. We focus on explicit calculations, structural properties, and interpretation, and avoid technical developments that are better suited to more specialized venues. Section

3
develops Gaussian examples in
detail. Sections
4
and
5
discuss implications for uncertainty quantification and approximate inference, while Section
6

Section
7
with open problems and directions for future research.

2
Bayesian experiments and effective dimension

2.1
Bayesian experiment and mutual information

Let
\Theta
be a measurable parameter space equipped with prior distribution
\Pi
. For each
\theta\in\Theta
, let
P_{\theta}^{(n)}
denote the sampling distribution of the data
X^{(n)}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, where
n
represents an effective sample size. We assume that the joint law of
(\Theta,X^{(n)})
is given by

(\Theta,X^{(n)})\sim\Pi(d\theta)\,P_{\theta}^{(n)}(dx),

and that the corresponding joint distribution admits a density with respect to a suitable product dominating measure.

The mutual information between
\Theta
and
X^{(n)}
is defined as

I(\Theta;X^{(n)})\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\log\frac{p(X^{(n)}\mid\Theta)}{p(X^{(n)})}\Bigg],

(1)

where the expectation is taken with respect to the joint distribution
of
(\Theta,X^{(n)})
. Equivalently,

I(\Theta;X^{(n)})=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\Pi(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})\,\|\,\Pi\big)\big],

(2)

so that mutual information coincides with the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from the prior to the posterior.

Identity (
2
) shows that
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
measures the average information gained about the parameter after observing the data. In particular,
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})=0
if and only if the posterior coincides with the prior almost surely, while larger values indicate greater deviation of the posterior from the prior.

2.2
Definition of effective dimension

In regular
d
-dimensional parametric models, it is well known that mutual information between the parameter and the data typically grows at the rate

I(\Theta;X^{(n)})=\tfrac{d}{2}\log n+O(1)\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

reflecting the asymptotic normality of the posterior and the accumulation
of Fisher information
(
Clarke and Barron, 1990
;
Bernardo, 1979
;
van der Vaart, 2000
)
.
Motivated by this scaling, we normalize mutual information by
\log n
to define an effective notion of dimension.

Definition 1

(Bayesian effective dimension)
.

For
n\geq 3
, the
Bayesian effective dimension
at sample size
n

is defined by

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\;:=\;\frac{2\,I(\Theta;X^{(n)})}{\log n}.

The effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
quantifies the number of statistically learnable directions in the parameter space, as measured by the expected information gain from prior to posterior. Unlike the ambient dimension of
\Theta
, it depends jointly on the prior, the sampling model, and the sample size.

2.3
Basic properties

We record several elementary but important properties of the effective dimension.

Proposition 1

(Invariance under reparameterization)
.

Let
\phi:\Theta\to\Phi
be a measurable bijection with measurable
inverse, and let
\Pi_{\Phi}=\Pi\circ\phi^{-1}
denote the induced prior on

\Phi
. Define
\Phi=\phi(\Theta)
. Then

I(\Theta;X^{(n)})=I(\Phi;X^{(n)}),\qquad d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\text{ is invariant under }\phi.

Proposition 2

(Data processing under summarization/coarsening)
.

Let
(\Theta,X^{(n)},Y,Z)
be random elements on a common probability space. Assume that
Y
is obtained from the data
X^{(n)}
(possibly with
additional randomization independent of
\Theta
given
X^{(n)}
), and
that
Z
is obtained from
Y
(possibly with additional randomization
independent of
\Theta
given
Y
). Equivalently, assume the two Markov
chains

\Theta\longrightarrow X^{(n)}\longrightarrow Y,

(3)

and

\Theta\longrightarrow Y\longrightarrow Z.

(4)

Then

I(\Theta;Z)\leq I(\Theta;Y)\leq I(\Theta;X^{(n)}),

and consequently the effective dimension cannot increase under summarization or coarsening of the data.

We provide proofs of Propositions
1
and
2
in the Appendix. The propositions highlight two conceptual advantages of the effective dimension. First, it is coordinate-free and therefore invariant under reparameterization. Second, it is monotone under loss of information in the data, reflecting the fact that fewer informative observations cannot increase the number of learnable directions.

It is useful to clarify how effective dimension differs from existing complexity notions. Metric entropy and related covering arguments play a central role in posterior contraction theory, but they are inherently loss-dependent and describe the size of parameter neighborhoods rather than the information gained from data. Degrees of freedom, by contrast, quantify the sensitivity of fitted values to perturbations in the observations, but depend on a specific estimator and parametrization and do not directly reflect the global change from prior to posterior. Minimum description length and Bayesian coding approaches use mutual information or marginal likelihood to quantify complexity, but their primary goal is model comparison or compression rather than the characterization of posterior learning.

The effective dimension introduced here is complementary to these notions. It is defined intrinsically through the joint prior–model distribution, independent of loss functions or estimators, and directly quantifies the expected information gained through Bayesian updating. Rather than measuring the size of a model class or the complexity of a point estimator, effective dimension measures how many directions in parameter space are actively learned by the posterior at a given sample size.

3
Gaussian examples and explicit calculations

This section develops explicit formulas for
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
and hence
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
in Gaussian conjugate models. The calculations are non-asymptotic and rely only on standard identities for multivariate normal distributions.

3.1
A Gaussian channel identity

We begin with a basic lemma for jointly Gaussian vectors, stated in a form convenient for later use.

Lemma 1

(Mutual information for a linear Gaussian channel)
.

Let
\Theta\sim N(0,\Sigma_{\Theta})
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, let
\varepsilon\sim N(0,\Sigma_{\varepsilon})

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be independent of
\Theta
, and define

Y:=A\Theta+\varepsilon,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p},

where
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\succ 0
and
\Sigma_{\Theta}\succeq 0
.
Then
(\Theta,Y)
is jointly Gaussian and

\begin{split}I(\Theta;Y)&=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{n}+A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{p}+\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A\Big).\end{split}

(5)

Moreover,

I(\Theta;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log(1+\lambda_{j}),

(6)

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}>0
are the nonzero eigenvalues of

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1/2}A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1/2}

and
r=\mathrm{rank}(A\Sigma_{\Theta}^{1/2})
.

Proof.

Since
Y
is an affine transformation of Gaussian variables,
Y
is Gaussian with
covariance

\Sigma_{Y}=A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}+\Sigma_{\varepsilon}.

Because
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\succ 0
, we have
\Sigma_{Y}\succ 0
as well.
Mutual information can be written as:

I(\Theta;Y)=h(Y)-h(Y\mid\Theta),

where
h(Y)=-\int p(y)\log p(y)\,dy
and

h(Y\mid\Theta)=-\int p(\theta,y)\log p(y\mid\theta)\,d\theta\,dy
denote the (differential) entropy and conditional entropy, respectively. Conditional on
\Theta
,
Y
is Gaussian with covariance
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
, so

h(Y\mid\Theta)=h(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\log\big((2\pi e)^{n}\det\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\big)
.
Also
h(Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log\big((2\pi e)^{n}\det\Sigma_{Y}\big)
.
Hence

\displaystyle I(\Theta;Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\det(A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}+\Sigma_{\varepsilon})}{\det\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{n}+A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Big),

which is the first identity in (
5
).

To obtain the second identity, apply Sylvester’s determinant theorem
\det(I_{n}+UV)=\det(I_{p}+VU)
with
U=A\Sigma_{\Theta}^{1/2}
and

V=\Sigma_{\Theta}^{1/2}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
, giving

\det\!\Big(I_{n}+A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Big)=\det\!\Big(I_{p}+\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A\Big).

Finally, the spectral form (
6
) follows by diagonalizing
the positive semidefinite matrix

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1/2}A\Sigma_{\Theta}A^{\top}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}^{-1/2},

whose nonzero eigenvalues are the
\lambda_{j}
’s; the determinant equals the product

\prod_{j=1}^{r}(1+\lambda_{j})
. ∎

3.2
Gaussian location model (scalar and multivariate)

3.2.1
Scalar location.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
with prior
\theta\sim N(0,\tau^{2})
and observations

X_{i}\mid\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}N(\theta,\sigma^{2}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

with
\sigma^{2}>0
known. The sample mean
\bar{X}
is sufficient and

\bar{X}\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\sigma^{2}/n)
. Applying Lemma
1
with
A=1
,
\Sigma_{\Theta}=\tau^{2}
and

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\sigma^{2}/n
yields the exact mutual information

I(\theta;X_{1:n})=I(\theta;\bar{X})=\frac{1}{2}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{n\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big).

(7)

Consequently,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=\frac{\log\!\big(1+n\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}\big)}{\log n}.

(8)

In particular,
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
for fixed
\tau^{2},\sigma^{2}
.

3.2.2
Multivariate location.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with prior
\theta\sim N(0,\tau^{2}I_{d})
and

X_{i}\mid\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}N_{d}(\theta,\sigma^{2}I_{d}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

Then
\bar{X}\mid\theta\sim N_{d}(\theta,(\sigma^{2}/n)I_{d})
and again by
Lemma
1
,

I(\theta;X_{1:n})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{d}+\frac{n\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}I_{d}\Big)=\frac{d}{2}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{n\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big),

(9)

so that

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=d\cdot\frac{\log\!\big(1+n\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}\big)}{\log n}\;\longrightarrow\;d.

(10)

Remark 1

(Fixed-
n
shrinkage effect)
.

Formulas (
7
)–(
10
) show that, at a given sample size
n
, the amount of learnable information depends on the signal-to-noise ratio
n\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}
. In particular, if the prior scale
\tau^{2}=\tau^{2}_{n}
depends on
n
(e.g. strong global shrinkage), then
I(\theta;X_{1:n})=\frac{d}{2}\log(1+n\tau_{n}^{2}/\sigma^{2})
can grow strictly slower than
\frac{d}{2}\log n
, leading to an effective dimension smaller than
d
over the relevant range of
n
.

3.3
Gaussian linear regression with Gaussian prior

Consider the fixed-design Gaussian linear model

Y\mid\beta\sim N(X\beta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),\qquad\beta\sim N(0,\tau^{2}I_{p}),

(11)

where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is deterministic and
\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}>0
are known. This is a conjugate Bayesian experiment, and
I(\beta;Y)
can be computed exactly.

Proposition 3

(Mutual information and spectrum of the design)
.

Under (
11
),

\begin{split}I(\beta;Y)&=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{n}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}XX^{\top}\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{p}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X\Big).\end{split}

(12)

Let
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{r}>0
be the nonzero singular values of
X
and

r=\mathrm{rank}(X)
. Then

I(\beta;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}s_{j}^{2}\Big).

(13)

Proof.

Apply Lemma
1
with
\Theta=\beta
,

A=X
,
\Sigma_{\Theta}=\tau^{2}I_{p}
and
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\sigma^{2}I_{n}
.
This yields (
12
). For (
13
), diagonalize
XX^{\top}
(or
X^{\top}X
): the nonzero eigenvalues of
XX^{\top}
are
s_{j}^{2}
,
j=1,\dots,r
, so the determinant in (
12
) equals
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\big(1+(\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2})s_{j}^{2}\big)
.
∎

3.3.1
Effective dimension in linear regression.

By Definition
1
and Proposition
3
,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=\frac{1}{\log n}\,\log\det\!\Big(I_{n}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}XX^{\top}\Big).

(14)

In particular,
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\leq 2r
for all
n
, since

I(\beta;Y)\leq\frac{r}{2}\log\big(1+(\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2})s_{1}^{2}\big)
by
(
13
).

3.4
Spectral complexity and effective rank

The representation (
13
) shows that
I(\beta;Y)
is a spectral functional of
X^{\top}X
with a logarithmic saturation for high signal-to-noise modes. This leads to a natural notion of effective rank controlled by mutual information.

Fix
\lambda:=\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}
and let
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{r}>0
be as above.
Define the normalized weights

w_{j}(\lambda):=\frac{\log(1+\lambda s_{j}^{2})}{\log(1+\lambda s_{1}^{2})}\in(0,1],\qquad j=1,\dots,r,

and the corresponding
information effective rank

r_{\mathrm{info}}(\lambda;X):=\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\lambda)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log(1+\lambda s_{j}^{2})}{\log(1+\lambda s_{1}^{2})}.

(15)

Then (
13
) can be rewritten as

I(\beta;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\lambda s_{1}^{2})\,r_{\mathrm{info}}(\lambda;X),

(16)

where
r_{\mathrm{info}}(\lambda;X)\in[1,r]
and decreases when the spectrum

\{s_{j}\}
decays more rapidly. This decomposition separates the
scale

\log(1+\lambda s_{1}^{2})
from the
effective rank

r_{\mathrm{info}}
.

3.5
Relation to ridge degrees of freedom

In the same model (
11
), the posterior mean equals the ridge
estimator with penalty parameter
\alpha:=\sigma^{2}/\tau^{2}=1/\lambda
:

\hat{\beta}_{\alpha}=(X^{\top}X+\alpha I_{p})^{-1}X^{\top}Y,\qquad\hat{Y}_{\alpha}=X\hat{\beta}_{\alpha}=S_{\alpha}Y,

where the smoothing matrix is

S_{\alpha}:=X(X^{\top}X+\alpha I_{p})^{-1}X^{\top}.

The classical effective degrees of freedom of ridge regression is

\mathrm{df}(\alpha):=\mathrm{tr}(S_{\alpha})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{s_{j}^{2}}{s_{j}^{2}+\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda s_{j}^{2}}{1+\lambda s_{j}^{2}}.

(17)

Mutual information uses the same singular values but a different spectral
transform. The following comparison is immediate and will be useful as a bridge to familiar complexity measures.

Proposition 4

(MI–df comparison)
.

For all
u\geq 0
,

\frac{u}{1+u}\leq\log(1+u)\leq u.

(18)

Consequently, in the ridge model (
11
),

\mathrm{df}(\alpha)\;\leq\;2I(\beta;Y)\;\leq\;\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}X)=\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}X).

(19)

Proof.

Define
f(u)=\log(1+u)-\frac{u}{1+u}
for
u\geq 0
. Then
f(0)=0
and

f^{\prime}(u)=\frac{1}{1+u}-\frac{1}{(1+u)^{2}}=\frac{u}{(1+u)^{2}}\geq 0,

so
f(u)\geq 0
, proving the left inequality in (
18
).
For the right inequality, define
g(u)=u-\log(1+u)
; then
g(0)=0
and

g^{\prime}(u)=1-\frac{1}{1+u}=\frac{u}{1+u}\geq 0
, so
g(u)\geq 0
.

Apply (
18
) with
u_{j}=\lambda s_{j}^{2}
and sum over
j=1,\dots,r
.
The left inequality yields

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda s_{j}^{2}}{1+\lambda s_{j}^{2}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log(1+\lambda s_{j}^{2})=2I(\beta;Y),

and the right inequality yields

2I(\beta;Y)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log(1+\lambda s_{j}^{2})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda s_{j}^{2}=\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}X),

since
\mathrm{tr}(X^{\top}X)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}s_{j}^{2}
. ∎

Remark 2
.

Degrees of freedom in (
17
) weights each spectral mode by

u/(1+u)
, which saturates at
1
for large
u
, whereas mutual information
weights each mode by
\log(1+u)
, which grows only logarithmically for large
u
. Thus mutual information is more conservative in counting extremely high signal-to-noise directions, while still behaving linearly in the low signal-to-noise regime.

3.6
A misspecified exponential family example

To illustrate that effective dimension is not tied to Gaussian likelihoods or correct specification, consider a scalar exponential family model

p_{\theta}(y)=\exp\{\theta y-A(\theta)\}\,q(y),

with prior
\theta\sim\Pi
, where the data are generated from a distribution
P_{0}
that does not belong to the model. Under standard regularity conditions, the posterior concentrates around the pseudo-true parameter
\theta^{\ast}
, defined as the minimizer of
\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\,\|\,P_{\theta})

(
Kleijn and van der Vaart, 2012
)
.

Although the posterior need not be asymptotically normal globally, mutual information remains well defined and satisfies

I(\Theta;X^{(n)})=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{KL}(\Pi(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})\,\|\,\Pi)\big].

If the curvature of the Kullback–Leibler projection at
\theta^{\ast}
is small, the posterior remains diffuse relative to the prior, leading to
slow growth of
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
and hence small effective dimension. Conversely, strong local identifiability around
\theta^{\ast}
leads to
information growth comparable to that of a well-specified parametric model.

This example illustrates that effective dimension reflects not only the nominal model dimension, but also the strength of identifiability under possible misspecification.

4
Consequences and interpretation

This section extracts structural consequences of the effective dimension introduced in Section
2
and the explicit Gaussian calculations in Section
3
. The emphasis is on what the effective dimension measures and how it clarifies several recurring phenomena in high-dimensional Bayesian inference.

4.1
Effective versus ambient dimension

The ambient dimension of the parameter space—for example,
d
in a location model or
p
in linear regression—reflects only the algebraic size of the model. By contrast, the effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
depends jointly on the prior, the likelihood, and the sample size.

In the Gaussian linear model of Section
3.3
, Proposition
3
and (
13
) imply

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=\frac{1}{\log n}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}s_{j}^{2}\Big)\;\leq\;\frac{r}{\log n}\,\log\!\Big(1+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}s_{1}^{2}\Big),

(20)

where
r=\mathrm{rank}(X)
. Thus
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\leq 2r
for all
n
, and equality (up to constants) requires both full rank and comparable singular values. In particular, even when
p\gg n
, the effective dimension is bounded by the rank of the design and can be much smaller when the spectrum of
X^{\top}X
is ill-conditioned.

More generally, since
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
is nonincreasing under data coarsening (Proposition
2
), any reduction of the data to a lower-dimensional sufficient or approximately sufficient statistic can only decrease
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
. This formalizes the intuition that effective dimension counts
informative
directions rather than algebraic ones.

4.2
Bounded effective dimension in infinite-dimensional settings

The Gaussian calculations also clarify when the effective dimension can remain bounded even as the ambient dimension diverges. Consider the linear Gaussian experiment with infinitely many parameters, indexed so that the nonzero singular values
\{s_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
of the design (or of an associated forward operator) satisfy
s_{j}^{2}\downarrow 0
.

From (
13
),

I(\beta;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}s_{j}^{2}\Big).

(21)

Since
\log(1+u)\leq u
for all
u\geq 0
, a sufficient condition for

I(\beta;Y)<\infty
is

\sum_{j\geq 1}s_{j}^{2}<\infty.

(22)

In that case,
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=O(1/\log n)
and therefore remains uniformly bounded in
n
.

Condition (
22
) holds, for example, when
s_{j}^{2}\asymp j^{-2a}
with
a>1/2
, a common regime in mildly ill-posed inverse problems and nonparametric regression with smoothness constraints. Thus, even though the parameter space is infinite-dimensional, the Bayesian experiment carries only finite information in the sense of mutual information, and the effective dimension remains bounded.

Remark 3
.

The finiteness of (
21
) depends on the interaction between the prior scale
\tau^{2}
and the decay of the spectrum
\{s_{j}\}
. Stronger global shrinkage (smaller
\tau^{2}
) reduces the effective dimension further, while weaker shrinkage increases the number of modes contributing nonnegligibly to the information sum.

4.3
Implications for uncertainty quantification

The effective dimension provides a principled way to interpret the behavior of Bayesian uncertainty measures in high-dimensional settings. Since
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
equals the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from prior to posterior, it quantifies the total amount of posterior concentration relative to the prior.

In regular parametric models,
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})\sim\tfrac{d}{2}\log n
leads to the classical picture in which posterior credible regions shrink at a rate determined by the ambient dimension
d
. In contrast, when
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\ll d
, the same heuristic suggests that the posterior behaves as though only
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
directions are being learned. This explains why credible sets can remain wide or anisotropic in high-dimensional or ill-posed problems despite large sample sizes.

Importantly, this interpretation does not rely on asymptotic normality or Bernstein–von Mises type results. It follows directly from the information-theoretic identity (
2
) and the fact that mutual information aggregates posterior contraction across all directions in parameter space. When
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
grows slowly, no global Gaussian approximation can capture the full posterior behavior, even if local normal approximations hold in selected low-dimensional projections.

4.4
Effective dimension and regularization mechanisms

The examples in Section
3
show that common regularization mechanisms act primarily by controlling the growth of effective dimension. In Gaussian location models, the global prior scale
\tau^{2}
directly modulates the signal-to-noise ratio
n\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}
and hence the growth rate of
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
. In linear regression, regularization operates through the spectrum of
X^{\top}X
, suppressing weak or unstable modes
that would otherwise contribute to the information sum (
13
).

From this perspective, effective dimension plays a role analogous to degrees of freedom, but with two important distinctions. First, it is defined intrinsically through the joint distribution of parameters and data and is therefore invariant under reparameterization. Second, it penalizes extremely high signal-to-noise directions only logarithmically, reflecting diminishing returns from learning already well-identified modes. These features make effective dimension a natural descriptor of global posterior complexity in Bayesian inference.

4.5
Implications for applied Bayesian workflows

The notion of effective dimension offers several practical interpretations for applied Bayesian analysis. First, it provides a diagnostic for assessing whether a model is overparameterized relative to the data. A small effective dimension indicates that, despite a large ambient parameter space, the posterior is learning only a limited number of directions, suggesting that additional parameters may have little inferential impact.

Second, effective dimension helps interpret posterior uncertainty in high-dimensional models. When
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
grows slowly, credible sets may remain wide or highly anisotropic even at large sample sizes. This behavior should not be interpreted as a failure of Bayesian inference, but rather as a reflection of limited information content in the data relative to the prior.

Third, effective dimension provides a principled lens for comparing exact and approximate Bayesian procedures. As shown in Section
5
, approximations that inflate posterior covariance necessarily reduce effective dimension. From this perspective, conservative uncertainty quantification under approximate inference can be understood as a reduction in the number of directions effectively learned from the data.

Finally, in models employing shrinkage priors, effective dimension offers an interpretable summary of adaptive behavior. Rather than selecting a fixed model size, shrinkage induces a data-dependent effective dimension that reflects uncertainty about which directions are learnable. This provides a useful alternative to hard model selection when communicating results to non-specialist audiences.

5
Approximate posteriors as effective dimension truncation

This section formalizes the effect of posterior approximations on effective dimension in settings where the posterior distribution is Gaussian or approximately Gaussian. The key point is that, in such cases, effective dimension is controlled by the log-determinant of the posterior covariance, and common approximations reduce this quantity in a precise sense.

5.1
Information gain for Gaussian priors and posteriors

Let
\Theta\sim N(0,\Sigma_{0})
with
\Sigma_{0}\succ 0
, and suppose that
the posterior distribution is Gaussian,

\Pi(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})=N(m,\Sigma),

with random mean
m
and random covariance
\Sigma\succ 0
. The
Kullback–Leibler divergence from posterior to prior admits the exact
expression

\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}\!\big(N(m,\Sigma)\,\|\,N(0,\Sigma_{0})\big)&=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma)+m^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}m-\\
&\qquad\qquad\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma)-p\Big),\end{split}

(23)

where
p=\dim(\Theta)
.

Taking expectation with respect to the joint law of
(m,\Sigma)
yields

\begin{split}I(\Theta;X^{(n)})&=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{KL}(\Pi(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})\,\|\,\Pi)\big]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma)+\mathbb{E}\big[m^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}m\big]-\\
&\qquad\qquad\mathbb{E}\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma)-p\Big).\end{split}

(24)

Identity (
24
) shows that, in Gaussian models,
mutual information decomposes into a quadratic mean term and a log-determinant term. The latter plays a central role in controlling effective dimension.

5.2
Gaussian linear model revisited

Consider again the Gaussian linear model (
11
) with prior
\beta\sim N(0,\tau^{2}I_{p})
. The exact posterior covariance is

\Sigma=\Big(\tau^{-2}I_{p}+\sigma^{-2}X^{\top}X\Big)^{-1}.

In this conjugate setting, the posterior covariance is nonrandom and the posterior mean satisfies

\mathbb{E}\big[m^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}m\big]=\mathrm{tr}\!\big(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}(\Sigma_{0}-\Sigma)\big),\qquad\Sigma_{0}=\tau^{2}I_{p}.

Substituting into (
24
) yields

I(\beta;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{p}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}X^{\top}X\Big),

(25)

in agreement with Proposition
3
. Thus, in Gaussian conjugate models, effective dimension is governed entirely by the log-determinant of the posterior covariance relative to the prior.

5.3
Effect of covariance approximation

Suppose now that the exact posterior
N(m,\Sigma)
is replaced by an approximate Gaussian posterior
N(\tilde{m},\tilde{\Sigma})
with
\tilde{\Sigma}\succ 0
. We interpret this approximation as acting on the information gain through the expected Kullback–Leibler divergence
(
23
).

Proposition 5

(Covariance inflation reduces information gain)
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}\succ 0
be fixed. Suppose that
\tilde{\Sigma}
and
\Sigma

are positive definite matrices such that

\tilde{\Sigma}\succeq\Sigma.

(26)

Then

\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\tilde{\Sigma})\;\geq\;\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma),

(27)

and consequently

\mathrm{KL}\!\big(N(\tilde{m},\tilde{\Sigma})\,\|\,N(0,\Sigma_{0})\big)\;\leq\;\mathrm{KL}\!\big(N(m,\Sigma)\,\|\,N(0,\Sigma_{0})\big)

(28)

whenever
\tilde{m}=m
.

Proof.

If
\tilde{\Sigma}\succeq\Sigma
, then
\Sigma^{-1/2}\tilde{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-1/2}\succeq I
, so all eigenvalues of this matrix are at least

1
. Taking determinants yields

\det(\tilde{\Sigma})\geq\det(\Sigma),

and hence (
27
). Substituting into (
23
) with
\tilde{m}=m
shows that the KL divergence
from the approximate posterior to the prior is no larger than that of
the exact posterior.
∎

Remark 4
.

Condition (
26
) corresponds to
covariance
inflation
, which is characteristic of many posterior approximations,
including mean-field variational Bayes and low-rank Laplace
approximations. Proposition
5
shows that
such inflation necessarily reduces information gain.

5.4
Effective dimension under posterior approximation

Let
\tilde{\Pi}(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})
denote an approximate posterior, and
define its induced information gain by

\tilde{I}(\Theta;X^{(n)}):=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{\Pi}(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})\,\|\,\Pi)\big],

with corresponding effective dimension

\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{eff}}(n):=\frac{2\,\tilde{I}(\Theta;X^{(n)})}{\log n}.

Corollary 1

(Approximate posteriors truncate effective dimension)
.

In Gaussian models, if the approximate posterior covariance satisfies

\tilde{\Sigma}\succeq\Sigma
almost surely and
\tilde{m}=m
, then

\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)\;\leq\;d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n).

Proof.

By Proposition
5
,

\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{\Pi}(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})\,\|\,\Pi)\leq\mathrm{KL}(\Pi(\cdot\mid X^{(n)})\,\|\,\Pi)
almost surely. Taking expectations
and normalizing by
\log n
yields the result.
∎

5.5
Interpretation

Corollary
1
shows that, in Gaussian settings, posterior approximations reduce effective dimension by inflating posterior uncertainty. This effect is independent of algorithmic details and depends only on the induced covariance structure. Approximate Bayesian procedures therefore act as
dimension truncation mechanisms
, learning fewer effective directions than the exact posterior.

This interpretation clarifies why approximate posteriors can yield overly conservative uncertainty quantification: the reduction in effective dimension reflects a genuine loss of information relative to the exact Bayesian update, as measured by expected Kullback–Leibler divergence.

6
Shrinkage priors and random effective dimension

The preceding sections focused on Gaussian priors and posteriors, for which effective dimension can be computed explicitly. We now consider
shrinkage priors
, particularly global–local constructions, where the prior is non-Gaussian and the effective dimension becomes a random, data-dependent quantity. While closed-form expressions are generally unavailable, mutual information still admits sharp decompositions that
clarify how shrinkage controls the number of learnable directions.

6.1
Global–local scale mixtures

Consider the scalar Gaussian observation model

Y\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\sigma^{2}/n),

(29)

with a global–local prior of the form

\theta\mid\lambda\sim N(0,\lambda^{2}),\qquad\lambda\sim\Pi_{\lambda},

(30)

where
\Pi_{\lambda}
is a distribution on
(0,\infty)
. This class includes ridge (
\lambda
fixed), Student-
t
, horseshoe, and related priors.

The joint distribution of
(\theta,\lambda,Y)
induces a Markov chain

\lambda\longrightarrow\theta\longrightarrow Y,

and hence, by the chain rule for mutual information,

I(\theta,\lambda;Y)=I(\lambda;Y)+I(\theta;Y\mid\lambda).

(31)

Since
I(\theta;Y)\leq I(\theta,\lambda;Y)
by monotonicity, it follows that

I(\theta;Y)\;\leq\;I(\lambda;Y)+I(\theta;Y\mid\lambda).

(32)

6.2
Conditional information and random signal-to-noise

Conditional on
\lambda
, model (
29
)–(
30
) is Gaussian, and Lemma
1
yields

I(\theta;Y\mid\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\log\!\Big(1+\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big).

(33)

Taking expectation with respect to
\lambda
gives

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\big[I(\theta;Y\mid\lambda)\big]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\!\left[\log\!\Big(1+\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)\right].

(34)

Combining (
32
) and (
34
)
yields the general inequality

I(\theta;Y)\;\leq\;I(\lambda;Y)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\!\left[\log\!\Big(1+\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)\right].

(35)

Inequality (
35
) shows that, under global–local priors, effective dimension is controlled by two components: the information that the data carry about the latent scale
\lambda
, and the average conditional information given
\lambda
.

6.3
Worked bounds for common mixing laws

Inequality (
35
) reduces the shrinkage case to controlling two terms:
I(\lambda;Y)
and
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\log(1+c\lambda^{2})
with
c:=n/\sigma^{2}
. Even when
I(\lambda;Y)
is analytically intractable, useful conclusions already follow from bounding the conditional-information term.

(i) Ridge (fixed scale).

If
\lambda\equiv\tau
is deterministic, then
I(\lambda;Y)=0
and
(
33
) gives the exact identity

I(\theta;Y)=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+c\tau^{2}),\qquad d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=\frac{\log(1+n\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2})}{\log n}.

(ii) Scale mixtures with finite second moment.

Assume
\mathbb{E}[\lambda^{2}]<\infty
. Since
\log(1+u)
is concave, Jensen’s inequality yields

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\!\left[\log(1+c\lambda^{2})\right]\leq\log\!\left(1+c\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda^{2}]\right).

(36)

Consequently, (
35
) implies

I(\theta;Y)\leq I(\lambda;Y)+\frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(1+\frac{n\,\mathbb{E}[\lambda^{2}]}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(37)

In particular, whenever
I(\lambda;Y)
is bounded uniformly in
n
, the effective dimension cannot exceed that of a Gaussian prior with variance
\mathbb{E}[\lambda^{2}]
up to an additive
O(1/\log n)
term.

(iii) Student-
t
prior as an inverse-gamma scale mixture.

A centered Student-
t_{\nu}
prior on
\theta
can be represented as

\theta\mid\lambda^{2}\sim N(0,\lambda^{2}),\qquad\lambda^{2}\sim\mathrm{Inv\text{-}Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu s^{2}/2),

for degrees of freedom
\nu>0
and scale
s^{2}>0
. If
\nu>2
then

\mathbb{E}[\lambda^{2}]=\frac{\nu s^{2}}{\nu-2}<\infty
and (
36
)
gives

\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}\!\left[\log(1+c\lambda^{2})\right]\leq\log\!\left(1+c\,\frac{\nu s^{2}}{\nu-2}\right).

(38)

Thus (
37
) applies directly. When
\nu\leq 2
,
\mathbb{E}[\lambda^{2}]=\infty
and Jensen no longer yields a finite bound; this highlights how heavy tails can increase conditional information through occasional large local scales.

6.4
Heavy-tailed scales and logarithmic information control

The following lemma formalizes why heavy-tailed mixing distributions, including the horseshoe, can yield finite conditional information despite having infinite second moments.

Lemma 2

(Heavy tails imply finite log-information)
.

Let
\lambda
be a nonnegative random variable. Assume there exist
constants
\alpha>0
,
t_{0}\geq 1
, and
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\leq C\,t^{-\alpha},\qquad\forall\,t\geq t_{0}.

(39)

Then
\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\lambda^{2})]<\infty
. Moreover, for every
c>0
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\log(1+c\lambda^{2})\big]&\leq\log(1+c)+\mathbb{E}\big[\log(1+\lambda^{2})\big]\\
&\leq\log(1+c)+\log(1+t_{0}^{2})+\frac{2C}{\alpha}\,t_{0}^{-\alpha}.\end{split}

(40)

In particular,
\mathbb{E}[\log(1+c\lambda^{2})]<\infty
for all
c>0
and grows at
most on the order of
\log(1+c)
as
c\to\infty
.

Proof.

Step 1: A deterministic inequality.

For all
a,b\geq 0
,
1+ab\leq(1+a)(1+b)
implies that

\log(1+ab)\leq\log(1+a)+\log(1+b).

With
a=c
and
b=\lambda^{2}
, this yields

\log(1+c\lambda^{2})\leq\log(1+c)+\log(1+\lambda^{2}).

(41)

Taking expectations gives the first inequality in (
40
).
Thus it suffices to show
\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\lambda^{2})]<\infty
and to bound it.

Step 2: Tail-integral representation.

Let
g(t)=\log(1+t^{2})
for
t\geq 0
. Then
g(0)=0
and
g
is absolutely
continuous with derivative
g^{\prime}(t)=\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\geq 0
.
For such
g
, the tail-integral identity holds:

\mathbb{E}[g(\lambda)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(t)\,\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\,dt.

(42)

(To verify (
42
), note that

g(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}g^{\prime}(t)\,dt
and apply Fubini’s theorem:

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\lambda}g^{\prime}(t)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(t)\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\,dt
.)

Applying (
42
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\lambda^{2})]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\,dt+\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\,dt.

Step 3: Bound the two integrals.

For the first term, use
\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\leq 1
and integrate:

\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,dt=\log(1+t_{0}^{2}).

Since
t_{0}\geq 1
, we may keep the simpler bound

\log(1+t_{0}^{2})\leq\log 2+2\log t_{0}
; in particular it is finite.

For the second term, use the tail bound (
39
) and
the inequality
\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{t}
for
t\geq 1
:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}(\lambda\geq t)\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{2t}{1+t^{2}}\,Ct^{-\alpha}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq 2C\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}t^{-(\alpha+1)}\,dt=\frac{2C}{\alpha}\,t_{0}^{-\alpha}.

Combining the bounds yields
\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\lambda^{2})]<\infty
and, together
with (
41
), proves (
40
).
∎

Remark 5

(On the horseshoe)
.

For the horseshoe and related half-Cauchy global–local priors,
\lambda
has heavy tails and strong concentration near zero. While closed-form expressions for
I(\theta;Y)
are generally unavailable, the decomposition (
35
) remains informative: near-zero mass reduces the typical conditional effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n\mid\lambda)
, while heavy tails allow occasional large contributions. This supports the interpretation of shrinkage priors as inducing
random effective dimension
.

6.5
Interpretation: random effective dimension

Equation (
33
) shows that, conditional on
\lambda
,
the experiment behaves like a Gaussian location model with effective dimension

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n\mid\lambda)=\frac{\log(1+n\lambda^{2}/\sigma^{2})}{\log n}.

Thus
\lambda
induces a
random effective dimension
through its realization of the signal-to-noise ratio
n\lambda^{2}/\sigma^{2}
. Small values of
\lambda
correspond to nearly unlearnable directions, while large values correspond to effectively parametric behavior.

The marginal effective dimension
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
aggregates this random behavior through mutual information. Heavy-tailed priors on
\lambda
place substantial mass near zero while allowing occasional large values, leading to a posterior that concentrates strongly in a small number of directions while retaining flexibility to learn large signals.

6.6
Extension to multivariate and regression settings

The same decomposition extends componentwise to multivariate Gaussian models and linear regression with global–local priors. For example, consider

Y\mid\beta\sim N(X\beta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}),\qquad\beta_{j}\mid\lambda_{j}\sim N(0,\lambda_{j}^{2}),\quad\lambda_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\Pi_{\lambda}.

Conditional on
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p})
, the model is Gaussian with diagonal prior covariance
\Sigma_{\Theta}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1}^{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p}^{2})
, and
Lemma
1
yields

I(\beta;Y\mid\bm{\lambda})=\frac{1}{2}\log\det\!\Big(I_{n}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X\Sigma_{\Theta}X^{\top}\Big).

(43)

The effective dimension conditional on
\bm{\lambda}
therefore depends on a
randomly weighted spectrum
of
X^{\top}X
, where weak coordinates (small
\lambda_{j}
) contribute negligibly to the log-determinant. Marginalizing over
\bm{\lambda}
yields a posterior that adaptively selects a low-dimensional subspace, even when
p
is large.

6.7
Consequences and limitations

The analysis above yields two general conclusions. First, shrinkage priors control effective dimension by randomizing local signal-to-noise ratios, rather than by imposing hard sparsity constraints. Second, effective dimension under shrinkage is inherently random and data-dependent, reflecting uncertainty about which directions are learnable.

At the same time, inequality (
35
) shows that sharp characterization of
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
requires control of
I(\lambda;Y)
, which can be nontrivial for heavy-tailed priors. This highlights a fundamental limitation: while effective dimension provides a unifying descriptor, its precise evaluation under non-Gaussian priors remains an open problem in general.

7
Open problems and future directions

The notion of Bayesian effective dimension introduced in this paper raises a number of theoretical and methodological questions that are largely unexplored. We briefly outline several directions that appear both natural and challenging.

A first open problem concerns the estimation of effective dimension from data. Since
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
is defined through the mutual information
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
, it depends on the joint prior–model distribution and is not directly observable. Developing data-driven estimators or computable bounds for effective dimension, possibly via Monte Carlo approximations of expected Kullback–Leibler divergence or variational representations of mutual information, would substantially enhance its practical relevance. Even in Gaussian linear models, the finite-sample behavior of plug-in or empirical estimators of
\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma)
remains poorly understood.

A second direction concerns the relationship between effective dimension
and posterior contraction. While this paper deliberately avoids contraction rates, it is natural to ask whether posterior concentration can be characterized directly in terms of
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
rather than ambient dimension, sparsity, or metric entropy. Such a connection could provide a unifying explanation for why seemingly disparate models exhibit similar contraction behavior. Conversely, it remains unclear whether small effective dimension is necessary for contraction under standard loss functions, or whether there exist regimes where posterior contraction occurs despite large information gain.

The analysis in Sections
3
–
6
relies heavily on Gaussian structure. Extending the notion of effective
dimension to non-Gaussian or misspecified models presents several open
challenges. In such settings, mutual information is more difficult to control, and the effective dimension may reflect not only identifiability but also model curvature and approximation error. Understanding how effective dimension behaves under misspecification, and whether it can distinguish between well- and poorly specified directions in parameter space, is an important open question.

Approximate Bayesian inference provides another fertile direction. In Gaussian models, Section
5
shows that posterior approximations reduce effective dimension by inflating posterior covariance. A natural extension is to quantify the resulting gap
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)-\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
for specific approximation schemes, such as mean-field variational Bayes or low-rank Laplace approximations. More broadly, it is open whether effective dimension can serve as a general, model-agnostic diagnostic for information loss in approximate Bayesian procedures.

Finally, shrinkage priors suggest a notion of
random effective dimension
, as discussed in Section
6
. A precise characterization of this randomness remains open, particularly for heavy-tailed global–local priors. It is unclear whether effective
dimension under shrinkage concentrates in high-dimensional regimes or
retains intrinsic variability even as the sample size grows. Addressing
these questions may help clarify the role of shrinkage as a mechanism for
adaptive dimension reduction in Bayesian inference.

Progress on these problems would deepen the theoretical foundations of effective dimension and clarify its role as a unifying descriptor of
complexity in modern Bayesian analysis.

\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,X}=\mathbb{P}_{\Theta,X}\circ T^{-1}.

(45)

Likewise, since

\mathbb{P}_{\Phi}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X}=(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X})\circ T^{-1}.

(46)

We now use the fact that Kullback–Leibler divergence is invariant under

S

\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu)=\mathrm{KL}(\mu\circ S^{-1}\,\|\,\nu\circ S^{-1}).

(47)

\mu\ll\nu

for any measurable

(\nu\circ S^{-1})(A)=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\nu(S^{-1}(A))=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\mu(S^{-1}(A))=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;(\mu\circ S^{-1})(A)=0.

Assume
\mu\ll\nu
and let

. Then the Radon–Nikodym
derivative of the pushforwards satisfies

\frac{d(\mu\circ S^{-1})}{d(\nu\circ S^{-1})}(y)=f(S^{-1}(y))\qquad\text{for $(\nu\circ S^{-1})$-a.e.\ }y,

(48)

which follows from the defining property of the Radon–Nikodym
derivative: for every measurable
A
,

(\mu\circ S^{-1})(A)=\mu(S^{-1}(A))=\int_{S^{-1}(A)}f\,d\nu=\int_{A}f\circ S^{-1}\,d(\nu\circ S^{-1}).

Using (
48
) and the change-of-variables formula for
pushforward measures,

\int g(y)\,d(\mu\circ S^{-1})(y)=\int g(S(z))\,d\mu(z),

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu\circ S^{-1}\,\|\,\nu\circ S^{-1})

\displaystyle=\int\log\!\Big(\frac{d(\mu\circ S^{-1})}{d(\nu\circ S^{-1})}\Big)\,d(\mu\circ S^{-1})

\displaystyle=\int\log(f\circ S^{-1})\,d(\mu\circ S^{-1})

\displaystyle=\int\log f\,d\mu=\mathrm{KL}(\mu\|\nu),

establishing (
47
).

Applying (
47
) with
S=T
,
\mu=\mathbb{P}_{\Theta,X}
,
and
\nu=\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X}
, and using
(
45
)–(
46
), we obtain

\displaystyle I(\Phi;X^{(n)})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathbb{P}_{\Phi,X}\,\big\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\Phi}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta,X}\circ T^{-1}\,\big\|\,(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X})\circ T^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta,X}\,\big\|\,\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X}\big)=I(\Theta;X^{(n)}).

Finally, since
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
is defined as a deterministic
normalization of mutual information,

d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=\frac{2I(\Theta;X^{(n)})}{\log n},

it follows immediately that
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)
is invariant under

\phi
as well.

∎

Proof of Proposition
2
.

We give a detailed measure-theoretic proof. Throughout, mutual
information is defined as relative entropy between a joint law and the
product of its marginals:

I(U;V):=\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{U,V}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{U}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{V}),

where
\mathbb{P}_{U,V}
denotes the joint distribution of
(U,V)
.

Step 1: Reduction to conditional mutual information.

Assume that
X^{(n)}\to Y\to Z
is a Markov chain conditional on
\Theta
,
meaning that for
\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}
-a.e.
\theta
, the conditional law
of
Z
given
(\Theta=\theta,Y=y,X^{(n)}=x)
equals the conditional law of

Z
given
(\Theta=\theta,Y=y)
; equivalently,
Z\perp X^{(n)}\mid(\Theta,Y)
.

We will prove the two inequalities separately:

I(\Theta;Z)\leq I(\Theta;Y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad I(\Theta;Y)\leq I(\Theta;X^{(n)}).

It is convenient to use the chain rule for mutual information. Recall
that for any triple
(A,B,C)
for which these quantities are well
defined, one has

I(A;B,C)=I(A;B)+I(A;C\mid B),

(49)

where conditional mutual information is defined by

I(A;C\mid B):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbb{P}_{A\mid B,C}\,\big\|\,\mathbb{P}_{A\mid B}\big)\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbb{P}_{C\mid A,B}\,\big\|\,\mathbb{P}_{C\mid B}\big)\right],

(50)

and is always nonnegative.

For completeness, we justify (
49
). Using the KL
representation of mutual information,

I(A;B,C)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbb{P}_{A\mid B,C}\,\big\|\,\mathbb{P}_{A}\big)\right],\qquad I(A;B)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathbb{P}_{A\mid B}\,\big\|\,\mathbb{P}_{A}\big)\right],

one may write, for each realization
(b,c)
,

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{A\mid b,c}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{A})=\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{A\mid b,c}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{A\mid b})+\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{A\mid b}\,\|\,\mathbb{P}_{A}),

which is a standard decomposition of KL divergence (sometimes called the
“Pythagorean identity”) obtained by adding and subtracting

\log(d\mathbb{P}_{A\mid b}/d\mathbb{P}_{A})
inside the KL integral. Taking
expectations over
(B,C)
yields (
49
), with the first
term becoming
I(A;C\mid B)
as in (
50
).

Step 2: Proof that
I(\Theta;Z)\leq I(\Theta;Y)
.

Apply the chain rule (
49
) to
(A,B,C)=(\Theta,Y,Z)
:

I(\Theta;Y,Z)=I(\Theta;Y)+I(\Theta;Z\mid Y).

(51)

Apply it again with
(A,B,C)=(\Theta,Z,Y)
:

I(\Theta;Z,Y)=I(\Theta;Z)+I(\Theta;Y\mid Z).

(52)

Since
I(\Theta;Y,Z)=I(\Theta;Z,Y)
, subtracting (
52
) from
(
51
) gives

I(\Theta;Y)-I(\Theta;Z)=I(\Theta;Y\mid Z)-I(\Theta;Z\mid Y).

(53)

Conditional mutual information is nonnegative, so it suffices to show
that
I(\Theta;Z\mid Y)=0
under the conditional Markov property.

We claim that the conditional Markov condition
Z\perp\Theta\mid Y

holds whenever
X^{(n)}\to Y\to Z
is a Markov chain conditional on

\Theta
and
Y
is measurable with respect to
X^{(n)}
(the typical
case of a statistic or coarsening). More generally, the assumption
“
X^{(n)}\to Y\to Z
conditional on
\Theta
” is precisely the statement
that, given
\Theta
, the conditional law of
Z
depends on the data only
through
Y
. In such a situation, once
Y
is observed, the remaining
dependence of
Z
on
\Theta
is mediated through
Y
, and thus

\mathbb{P}_{Z\mid\Theta,Y}=\mathbb{P}_{Z\mid Y}\quad\text{a.s.}

This equality of regular conditional distributions implies

I(\Theta;Z\mid Y)=0
by (
50
).

With
I(\Theta;Z\mid Y)=0
, equation (
51
) yields

I(\Theta;Y,Z)=I(\Theta;Y)
. Since mutual information is monotone in
its second argument (adding variables cannot decrease information),

I(\Theta;Z)\leq I(\Theta;Y,Z)=I(\Theta;Y)
. This proves

I(\Theta;Z)\leq I(\Theta;Y)
.

Step 3: Proof that
I(\Theta;Y)\leq I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
.

This is the standard data processing inequality for the Markov chain

\Theta\longrightarrow X^{(n)}\longrightarrow Y,

which holds whenever
Y
is a (possibly randomized) function of the data

X^{(n)}
. Formally, the Markov property means
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid\Theta,X^{(n)}}=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X^{(n)}}
almost surely.

Apply the chain rule to
(A,B,C)=(\Theta,X^{(n)},Y)
:

I(\Theta;X^{(n)},Y)=I(\Theta;X^{(n)})+I(\Theta;Y\mid X^{(n)}).

Since
\Theta\to X^{(n)}\to Y
is a Markov chain, we have

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid\Theta,X^{(n)}}=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X^{(n)}}
, and hence

I(\Theta;Y\mid X^{(n)})=0
by (
50
). Therefore,

I(\Theta;X^{(n)},Y)=I(\Theta;X^{(n)}).

(54)

On the other hand, mutual information is monotone under adding variables:

I(\Theta;X^{(n)},Y)\geq I(\Theta;Y),

since
(Y)
is a function of
(X^{(n)},Y)
. Combining with
(
54
) yields
I(\Theta;Y)\leq I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
.

Step 4: Conclusion for effective dimension.

Since
d_{\mathrm{eff}}(n)=\frac{2I(\Theta;X^{(n)})}{\log n}
is a
deterministic increasing function of
I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
, the inequalities

I(\Theta;Z)\leq I(\Theta;Y)\leq I(\Theta;X^{(n)})
imply that effective
dimension cannot increase when the data are replaced by a statistic
Y

or further coarsened to
Z
.
∎
