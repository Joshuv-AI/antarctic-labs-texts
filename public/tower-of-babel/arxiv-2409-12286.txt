Series expansions for SPDEs with symmetric α \alpha -stable Lévy noise
Raluca M. Balan, Juan J. Jiménez

Abstract
In this article, we examine a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) driven by a symmetric α \alpha -stable (S α \alpha S) Lévy noise, that is multiplied by a linear function σ ⁡ ( u ) = u \sigma(u)=u of the solution. The solution is interpreted in the mild sense. For this models, in the case of the Gaussian noise, the solution has an explicit Wiener chaos expansion, and is studied using tools from Malliavin calculus. These tools cannot be used for an infinite-variance Lévy noise. In this article, we provide sufficient conditions for the existence of a solution, and we give an explicit series expansion of this solution. To achieve this, we use the multiple stable integrals, which were developed in [ 30 , 31 ] , and originate from the LePage series representation of the noise. To give a meaning to the stochastic integral which appears in the definition of solution, we embed the space-time Lévy noise into a Lévy basis, and use the stochastic integration theory [ 9 , 8 ] with respect to this object, as in other studies of SPDEs with heavy-tailed noise: [ 12 , 13 , 14 ] . As applications, we consider the heat and wave equations with linear multiplicative noise, also called the parabolic/hyperbolic Anderson models.

Introduction

In this article, we study the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE):

\mathcal{L}u(t,x)=u(t,x)\dot{Z}(t,x),t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(1)

with constant initial condition
1
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a second-order pseudo-differential operator and

\dot{Z}
is the formal derivative associated with a
symmetric
\alpha
-stable (S
\alpha
S) random measure

Z
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with stability index
\alpha\in(0,2)
and control measure
m
given by:

m(B)=C_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm Leb}(B)\quad\mbox{where}\quad C_{\alpha}=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x^{\alpha}}dx\right)^{-1}.

(2)

This means that
Z=\{Z(B)\}_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}}
is a collection of random variables defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and indexed by the class
\mathcal{B}_{b}
of bounded Borel sets in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which has the following properties:

(i)
for any disjoint sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}
in
\mathcal{B}_{b}
,
Z(B_{1}),\ldots,Z(B_{n})
are independent;

(ii)
for any disjoint sets
(B_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
\mathcal{B}_{b}
with
\bigcup_{i\geq 1}B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
,

Z\Big(\bigcup_{i\geq 1}B_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i\geq 1}Z(B_{i})\quad\mbox{a.s.};

(iii)
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
,
Z(B)
has an
\alpha
-stable
S_{\alpha}(m(B)^{1/\alpha},0,0)
distribution, i.e.

\mathbb{E}[e^{iuZ(B)}]=e^{-m(B)|u|^{\alpha}}\quad\mbox{for any $u\in\mathbb{R}$}.

We refer the reader to Chapter 3 of
[
32
]
for more properties of
\alpha
-stable random measures.

More generally, if instead of (iii), one assumes that
Z(B)
has an infinitely divisible (ID) distribution, then
Z
is an
ID independently scattered random measure
, an object which was introduced and studied in
[
24
]
, and shares many properties with Lévy processes.

One way to construct a S
\alpha
S random measure
Z
is by setting: (see
[
2
]
)

Z(B)=\int_{B\times\{0<|z|\leq 1\}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{B\times\{|z|>1\}}zN(dt,dx,dz),

(3)

where
N
is a Poisson random measure
N
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
of intensity
\mu(dt,dx,dz)=dtdx\nu_{\alpha}(dz)
,
\mathbb{R}_{0}=\mathbb{R}\verb 2\2\{0\}
,
\widehat{N}(F)=N(F)-\mu(F)
is the compensated version of
N
, and

\nu_{\alpha}(dz)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha|z|^{-\alpha-1}1_{\{|z|>0\}}dz.

(4)

Another way is to use the
LePage series representation
:
for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
,

Z(B)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\frac{1}{\psi(T_{i},X_{i})}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})\quad\mbox{a.s.},

(5)

where
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables, i.e.

\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{i}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{i}=-1)=\frac{1}{2},

\{\Gamma_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}E_{j},i\geq 1\}
are the arrival times of a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
of intensity
1
(with
(E_{i})_{i\geq 1}
i.i.d. exponential random variables of mean
1
), and

\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. random variables on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with law
m_{\psi}(dt,dx)=\psi^{\alpha}(t,x)dtdx
,
where
\psi:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
is an arbitrary measurable function satisfying

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi^{\alpha}(t,x)dxdt=1.

(6)

The sequences
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
(E_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
are independent. Note that the series on the right-hand side of (
5
) converges a.s. since
W_{i}=\frac{1}{\psi(T_{i},X_{i})}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})
are i.i.d. random variables with
\mathbb{E}\left[W_{i}^{\alpha}\right]<\infty
(see Theorem 1.4.2 of
[
32
]
).

For simplicity, throughout this work we will use a weight function
\psi
of the form:

\psi(t,x)=T^{-1/\alpha}\phi(x)\quad\mbox{for all $(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

(7)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
is a measurable function such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi^{\alpha}(x)dx=1
.
Therefore,
(T_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. random variables with a uniform distribution on
[0,T]
,
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with density
\phi^{\alpha}(x)
, and
(T_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are independent. The LePage series representation (
5
) becomes: for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
,

Z(B)=T^{1/\alpha}\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\frac{1}{\phi(X_{i})}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})\quad\mbox{a.s.}

A (mild)
solution
of (
1
) is a predictable random field
u=\{u(t,x);t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
which satisfies the integral equation:

u(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)u(s,y)Z(ds,dy),

(8)

where
G
is the fundamental solution of the deterministic equation
\mathcal{L}u=\delta_{0}
.

To give a meaning to the right-hand side of equation (
8
), we need a stochastic integral which can be defined for
random integrands
. This case is not discussed in
[
32
]
or
[
24
]
. It turns out that the theory of stochastic integration with respect to
\alpha
-stable random measures developed by the first author in
[
2
]
(as a multi-dimensional extension of the theory of
[
17
]
) is too restrictive, and the best approach is to use the theory of stochastic integration with respect to
L^{0}
-random measures, which was introduced in
[
9
]
and was developed further in
[
22
,
23
,
8
,
15
]
.
This theory was used in the literature for other studies of SPDEs with heavy-tailed noise, such as
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. To implement this method, we need embed the S
\alpha
S random measure
Z
into a more general process
\Lambda
(called a
Lévy basis
), indexed by subsets of
\Omega\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This will unavoidably increase the technical level of the paper,
but the gain will be substantial.
At the same time, we need to preserve the series representation (
5
), to define the multiple integrals with respect to
Z
. This delicate technical issue is addressed in Section
2.3
below, where we give an explicit construction of a Lévy basis
\Lambda
which allows us to achieve both goals. This construction uses as the source of randomness the three sequences
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
to define a Poisson random measure
N
, which in turn is used to define
\Lambda
, via its canonical decomposition.
Specifically,
u(t,x)
is represented as a series of
random multilinear forms
that depend only on
(\epsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
, and
G
.

As examples, we consider the case of the heat operator
\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta
, for which

G_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2t}\right),

and the case of the wave operator
\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\Delta
in dimension
d\leq 2
, for which

\displaystyle G_{t}(x)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}1_{\{|x|<t\}}&\text{if $d=1,$}\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t^{2}-|x|^{2}}}1_{\{|x|<t\}}&\text{if $d=2.$}\end{cases}

(9)

Here
|\cdot|
is the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These examples are referred in the literature as the
parabolic Anderson model
(PAM), respectively
the hyperbolic Anderson model
(HAM).
By convention, we let
G_{t}(x)=0
if
t\leq 0
.

The existence of a solution of (PAM) driven by Lévy noise with
positive jumps was proved in
[
7
]
using a different method than in the present paper.
This method relies on the “continuum directed polymer” developed in
[
6
]
, and consists in solving the equation driven by the truncated noise with jumps that exceed a fixed value
a
, and then let
a\to 0
. A different truncation method was used in
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
for the
stochastic heat equation
(SHE) with general Lévy noise multiplied by a Lipschitz function
\sigma(u)
.
This method relies on first solving the equation driven by the noise truncated to the
region
\{(x,z);|z|<Kh(x)\}
for a suitable function
h(x)
, up to a stopping time
\tau_{K}
,
show that the solutions are consistent if
K<K^{\prime}
, and finally paste together all these solutions.
Unfortunately, this method does not yield uniqueness of the solution.
The same truncation method was used in
[
3
]
to show the existence of a
solution of the
stochastic wave equation
(SWE) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\leq 2
. The
uniqueness of this solution has been obtained in the recent article
[
20
]
using
the past light-cone property of the fundamental solution
G
of the wave equation.
In the case of (SHE) with general Lipschitz function
\sigma
, uniqueness of the solution remains an open problem.

In the present article, we introduce a different method, which is more robust,
does not require any truncation, and can be applied to a large class of SPDEs, being inspired
by the methodology used in the Gaussian case. We explain this method below.
Writing

u(s,y)=1+\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{s-r}(y-z)u(r,z)Z(dr,dz)

and inserting this into (
8
), we obtain:

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)Z(ds,dy)+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\left(\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{s-r}(y-z)u(r,z)Z(dr,dz)\right)Z(ds,dy).

Intuitively, it should be possible to iterate this procedure, and therefore obtain that the solution has the “stable chaos expansion”:

u(t,x)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}f_{n}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)Z(dt_{1},dx_{1})\ldots Z(dt_{n},dx_{n}),

(10)

where the kernel
f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
is given by:

f_{n}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)=G_{t-t_{n}}(x-x_{n})\ldots G_{t_{2}-t_{1}}(x_{2}-x_{1})1_{\{0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<t\}},

(11)

and the integral is interpreted as a “multiple stable integral”, which was
introduced and studied in
[
30
,
31
]
, using series representations
(originating from the LePage series representation (
5
)). The construction
and basic properties of the multiple stable integrals are recalled in Section
2.1
below.

Note that the first integral appearing in series (
10
) is
\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)Z(ds,dy)
, and this integral is well-defined if and only if

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)dyds<\infty.

(12)

In the case of the heat equation, (
12
) is equivalent to
\alpha<1+\frac{2}{d}
,
whereas in the case of the wave equation in dimension
d\leq 2
, (
12
) holds for all
\alpha\in(0,2)
.
In light of this, we introduce the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1.1
.

The fundamental solution
(t,x)\mapsto G_{t}(x)
of the operator
\mathcal{L}
is a jointly measurable
function on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which satisfies the following condition:

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}^{\alpha}(x)dxdt<\infty.

Note that Hypothesis
1.1
does not hold for the wave operator in dimension
d\geq 3
,
since the fundamental solution
is a distribution.

We expect that if the series (
10
) is well-defined, the corresponding partial sum sequence will coincide with the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of Picard’s iterations, defined by:
u_{0}(t,x)=1
,

u_{n+1}(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)u_{n}(s,y)Z(ds,dy),\quad n\geq 0.

(13)

This procedure is well-established in the case of equations with Gaussian noise or finite variance Lévy noise, using tools from Malliavin calculus (see e.g.
[
18
,
4
]
).
The goal of this article is to show that this method can be extended to the case of the S
\alpha
S noise, using the LePage series representations of the noise and of the associated multiple stable integrals.

Guided again by the intuition gained from the Gaussian framework,
we expect this solution to be unique. But uniqueness turns out to be a delicate
problem in our framework.
The fact that our noise may not have any moments forces us to work in the space
L^{0}
of
random variables equipped with the pseudo-norm
\|\cdot\|_{0}
(see (
22
) below).
Since the topology induced by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{0}}
is not locally convex,
we do not have access to the same techniques as in a Hilbert space, such as
L^{2}
,
nor do we have the Banach space structure of
L^{p}
for
p>0
.
As a consequence, proving the uniqueness of the solution remains an open problem.

We should also mention that stochastic evolution equations driven by a
cylindrical

\alpha
-stable Lévy noise

have been studied by several authors: see

[
11
,
21
]
and the references therein. Unlike the Gaussian case
(see
[
16
]
), a direct comparison of our results with the results obtained using
this framework is not available at the present time.

We are now ready to state the main result. But first, we need to introduce some notation and assumptions.
For any
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p>0
, we define:

K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x):=\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}f_{n}^{p}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha-p}(x_{k})d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{t},

(14)

with
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
and
T_{n}(t)=\{\boldsymbol{t}\in[0,t]^{n};t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}\}
.

Assumption 1.2
.

There exists
p\in(\alpha,2]
such that for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\right)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\Big)^{n/2}<\infty\ \text{a.s.}

(15)

Assumption 1.3
.

There exists
p\in(\alpha,2]
such that for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)\,dy\,ds\right)^{\frac{\alpha\wedge 1}{p}}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\Big)^{\frac{n(\alpha\wedge 1)}{p}}<\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(16)

The following theorem is the main result of the present article.

Theorem 1.4
.

Suppose that the fundamental solution
G
satisfies Hypothesis
1.1
.

(a) If Assumption
1.2
holds,
then the series on the right-hand side of (
10
) converges absolutely almost surely, for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(b) If Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
hold (with possibly different values
p
), then the process
\{u(t,x);t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
given by (
10
) is
a solution of equation (
1
).
Moreover, for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)|u(s,y)|^{\alpha}\,ds\,dy<\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(17)

and
u(t,x)
has representation:

u(t,x)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x)\quad\text{a.s.},

(18)

where
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
is the symmetrization of
f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
.

To verify Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
, we introduce the following condition on
\phi
:

Hypothesis 1.5
.

There exist some
c_{0}>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\frac{1}{\phi(x)}\leq c_{0}(1+|x|^{\delta})\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

(19)

Remark 1.6
.

An example of a function
\phi>0
which satisfies Hypothesis

is

\phi(x)=c\big(1_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}+|x|^{-\delta}1_{\{|x|>1\}}\big),

(20)

with

, and value

As an application of Theorem

Theorem 1.7
.

Background

2.1
Multiple stable integrals

In this section, we review the construction of the multiple stable integral with respect to the S
\alpha
S random measure
Z
, following
[
30
,
31
]
. The results contained in these references are stated for S
\alpha
S random measures on
\mathbb{R}
, but they can be easily extended to more general spaces, such as
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Before we begin, we recall that the stochastic integral
I_{1}(f)=\int fdZ
of a deterministic function
f
with respect to
Z
is constructed using the classical procedure, starting with simple functions, followed by approximation. This is explained in Chapter 3 of
[
32
]
. It turns out that the integral
I_{1}(f)
is well-defined for any function
f\in L^{\alpha}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the process
\{I_{1}(f);f\in L^{\alpha}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
has
\alpha
-stable finite dimensional distributions. In particular,
I_{1}(f)
has a
S_{\alpha}(\sigma_{f},0,0)
-distribution, where

\sigma_{f}=\left(\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{\alpha}m(dt,dx)\right)^{1/\alpha}.

This property is in fact valid even in the non-symmetric case, when
\nu_{\alpha}(z,\infty)=pz^{-\alpha}
and
\nu(-\infty,-z)=q(-z)^{-\alpha}
for all
z>0
, for some
p,q\geq 0
. From this perspective,
\alpha
-stable random measures are similar to isonormal Gaussian processes from Malliavin calculus, in which case the integrals
I_{1}(f)
live in the first Wiener chaos. This analogy is further enhanced by the fact that,

in the symmetric case
(when
\nu_{\alpha}
is given by (
4
)), it is possible to define a multiple integral with respect to
Z
, and this is what we will explain below.

Remark 2.1
.

If
\alpha\in(1,2)
, then
\int_{|z|>1}|z|\nu_{\alpha}(dz)<\infty
, and the Poisson representation (
3
) can be written as

Z(B)=\int_{B\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)
.
In view of this, one may try to define the multiple integral with respect to
Z
of a function
f:([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by:

I_{n}(f)=\int_{([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{n}}f(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n})z_{1}\ldots z_{n}\widehat{N}(dt_{1},dx_{1},dz_{1})\ldots\widehat{N}(dt_{n},dx_{n},dz_{n}).

This procedure works for finite-variance Lévy noise (see
[
4
]
), but fails for the infinite variance noise
Z
, since the multiple integral with respect to
\widehat{N}
is defined only for functions which are square-integrable with respect to the measure
\mu^{n}

(see Section 5.4 of
[
1
]
).

A different idea, which has been exploited successfully in
[
30
,
31
]
, is to start with a S
\alpha
S random measure

which has the LePage representation (
5
)
, and extend this representation to
multi-index series. We explain this procedure below. To simplify the writing, we let
E=[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{B}
be the class of Borel sets of

, and
\ell
be the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{A\in\mathcal{B};\ell(A)<\infty\}
.

The first step is the construction of the product stable measure
Z^{(n)}
.
For this, we recall that a
symmetric rectangle
of
E^{n}
is a set of the form

B=\bigcup_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}B_{\pi(1)}\times\ldots\times B_{\pi(n)}

for some disjoint sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}

\Sigma_{n}
is the set of all permutations of

Defining

Z^{(n)}(B):=n!Z(B_{1})\ldots Z(B_{n}),

and using the Le Page representation (
5
) for each

, we obtain that:

Z^{(n)}(B)=n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\psi^{-1}(T_{j_{k}},X_{j_{k}})1_{B}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}}).

(23)

The following theorem shows that this representation can be extended to more general sets.
First, we need to introduce some notation. Let
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{(s)}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the symmetric rectangles and

\mathcal{B}_{n,0}^{(s)}=\{B\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{(s)};\ell^{(n)}(B)<\infty\}
, where
\ell^{(n)}
is the Lebesgue measure on

.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any set
B\in\mathcal{B}_{n,0}^{(s)}
,
the series

S_{n}(B)=n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\psi^{-1}(T_{j_{k}},X_{j_{k}})1_{B}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}})

converges a.s. if and only if

\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-2/\alpha}\psi^{-2}(T_{j_{k}},X_{j_{k}})1_{B}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}})<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(24)

In this case, we let
Z^{(n)}(B):=S_{n}(B)
a.s.

We proceed now with the construction of the multiple stable integral. We recall some terminology.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
f:E^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be an arbitrary function. We say that:

a)
f
is
symmetric
if for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in E
and for any
\pi\in\Sigma_{n}
,

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)});

b)
f

vanishes on the diagonals
if
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
whenever
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\not=j
.

Lemma 2.4
.

A symmetric
\mathcal{B}^{n}
-measurable function

which vanishes on the diagonals is
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{(s)}
-measurable.

We say that
f:E^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
simple function
if it is the form
f=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}1_{B_{i}}
, for some
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and disjoint sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{n,0}^{(s)}
. If
Z^{(n)}(B_{i})
is well-defined for any

, we say that
f
is
n
-times integrable with respect to
Z
, and we let

I_{n}(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}Z^{(n)}(B_{i}).

Definition 2.5
.

Let
f:E^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathcal{B}^{n}
-measurable symmetric function which vanishes on the diagonals of
E^{n}
.
We say that
f
is
n
-times integrable with respect to
Z
if there exists a sequence
\{f^{(k)}\}_{k\geq 1}
of simple functions which are

-times integrable with respect to

(i)

converges to

in the measure
\ell^{(n)}
;

(ii) for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{(s)}
, the sequence
\{I_{n}(f^{(k)}1_{B})\}_{k\geq 1}
converges in probability to a limit denoted by
I_{n}(f1_{B})
.

In this case, we let
I(f1_{B})
be the limit in probability of
\{I(f^{(k)}1_{B})\}_{k\geq 1}
.

Recall that the
symmetrization
of a function

is defined by:

\widetilde{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}f(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)}).

A
\mathcal{B}^{n}
-measurable function

which vanishes on the diagonals is
n
-times integrable with respect to
Z
if its symmetrization
\widetilde{f}
is so; in this case, we let
I_{n}(f)=I_{n}(\widetilde{f})
.

We use the notation

I_{n}(f)=\int_{E^{n}}f(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},z_{n})Z(dt_{1},dx_{1})\ldots Z(dt_{n},dx_{n}),

and we say that

is the
multiple integral of order

of

with respect to

.

We now present a criterion for integrability.

Theorem 2.6
.

A symmetric
\mathcal{B}^{n}
-measurable function
f:E^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which vanishes on the diagonals is

n
-times integrable with respect to
Z
if and only if

\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-2/\alpha}\psi^{-2}(T_{j_{k}},X_{j_{k}})f^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}})<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(25)

In this case, the series

S_{n}(f):=n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\psi^{-1}(T_{j_{k}},X_{j_{k}})f(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}})

converges a.s. and
I_{n}(f)=S_{n}(f)
a.s.

A sufficient condition for (
25
) is

\int_{E^{n}}|f(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n})|^{\alpha}\left[\ln_{+}\frac{|f(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n})|}{\psi(t_{1},x_{1})\ldots\psi(t_{n},x_{n})}\right]^{n-1}dt_{1}dx_{1}\ldots dt_{n}dx_{n}<\infty.

Under this condition, the multiple integral has the following asymptotic tail behaviour:

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\lambda^{\alpha}}{(\ln\lambda)^{n-1}}\mathbb{P}(|I_{n}(f)|>\lambda)=n(n!)^{\alpha-2}\alpha^{n-1}\|f\|_{\alpha,n}^{\alpha},\quad\mbox{for any $n\geq 3$},

(26)

where
\|f\|_{\alpha,n}^{\alpha}=\int_{E^{n}}|f(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n})|^{\alpha}dt_{1}dx_{1}\ldots dt_{n}dx_{n}
. Relation (
26

under the additional condition:

\int_{E^{2}}|f(t_{1},x_{1},t_{2},x_{2})|^{\alpha}\ln_{+}\frac{|f(t_{1},x_{1},t_{2},x_{2})|}{\psi(t_{1},x_{1})\psi(t_{2},x_{2})}\ln_{+}\left|\ln\frac{|f(t_{1},x_{1},t_{2},x_{2})|}{\psi(t_{1},x_{1})\psi(t_{2},x_{2})}\right|dt_{1}dx_{1}dt_{2}dx_{2}<\infty.

This concludes our summary about multiple stable integrals.

2.2
Lévy bases and stochastic integration

In this section, we introduce the definition of a Lévy basis, and present the construction and main properties of the stochastic integral with respect to this object, following closely the notation and terminology of

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

Let
\mathcal{P}

\sigma

\Omega\times[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\sigma

Processes

We denote by

Definition 2.7
.

A
Lévy basis
is a mapping
\Lambda:\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}\to L^{0}
which satisfies the following:

(1)
\Lambda(\emptyset)=0
a.s.

(2) For any disjoint sets
(A_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
with
\bigcup_{i\geq 1}A_{i}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}

\Lambda\Big(\bigcup_{i\geq 1}A_{i}\Big)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\Lambda(A_{i})\quad\mbox{in}\quad L^{0}.

(3) For all
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
with
A\subseteq\Omega\times[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Lambda(A)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable.

(4) For all
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{t}

\Lambda\big(A\cap(F\times(t,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)=1_{F}\Lambda\big(A\cap(\Omega\times(t,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(27)

(5) For any disjoint sets
(B_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
\mathcal{B}_{b}

is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t}

(6) For any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}

has an infinitely divisible (ID) distribution.

(7) for any

and

\Lambda(\Omega\times\{t\}\times[-k,k]^{d})=0
a.s.

Intuitively, a Lévy basis is a generalization of a Lévy process. But for our purposes, it is important to realize that if
\Lambda

defined by

Z(B)=\Lambda(\Omega\times B)\quad\mbox{for all $B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}$ }

is an ID independently scattered random measure.

Similarly to the classical Lévy-Itô decomposition of a Lévy process, a Lévy basis has a canonical decomposition (see Theorem 3.2 of

]

\Lambda(A)=B(A)+\Lambda^{C}(A)+\int_{A\times\{|z|\leq 1\}}z\widehat{N}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{A\times\{|z|>1\}}zN(dt,dx,dz),

(i) a drift term, given by a deterministic signed measure

(ii) a continuous component, given by a Gaussian random measure

on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with variance measure

(iii) a pure-jump component, characterized by an underlying Poisson random measure

\mu

\widehat{N}

\Lambda

pure-jump

If

Proof.

(i) This follows from the following fact, stated on page 172 of

Definition 2.12
.

Let

a) A predictable process

For any

, a Lévy basis is an orthogonal
L^{0}
-random measure. We include the statement of this result below for a homogeneous Lévy basis, which is a generalization Theorem 2.7 of
[
24
]
, that corresponds to ID independently scattered random measures with deterministic integrands.

Theorem 2.14

(Theorem 4.1 of
[
15
]
for homogeneous Lévy bases)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a pure-jump homogeneous Lévy basis,
with canonical decomposition (
29
) (with
\Lambda^{C}=0
),
and
H
be
a predictable process. Then
H\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
if and only if

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}U(H(t,x))dxdt<\infty\ \mbox{a.s.}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}V_{0}(H(t,x))dxdt<\infty\ \mbox{a.s.},

(36)

where

U(y)=by+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\tau(yz)-y\tau(z)\big)\nu(dz)\quad\mbox{and}\quad V_{0}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|yz|^{2}\wedge 1)\nu(dz),

for any
y\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\tau(z)=z1_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}
.

In the particular case of a S
\alpha
S Lévy basis,

b=0
and
\nu=\nu_{\alpha}
is given by (
4
), so

U(y)=0
and
V_{0}(y)=\frac{2}{2-\alpha}|y|^{\alpha}
, which means that
condition (
36
) is equivalent to
H\in L^{\alpha}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
a.s. We state this result in the corollary below, noting that it gives a natural extension to random integrands of the integrability criterion which was mentioned at the beginning of Section
2.1
.

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
\Lambda
be a S
\alpha
S Lévy basis, and
H
be a predictable process. Then
H\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
if and only if

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|H(t,x)|^{\alpha}dxdt<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(37)

The following result shows that for elementary processes, the stochastic integral
I^{\Lambda}
coincides with the Itô integral (defined in the Walsh’ sense
[
34
]
). We include its proof since we could not find it in the literature.

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
X
be an elementary process of the form (
30
).
If
\Lambda
is a pure-jump homogeneous Lévy basis, and
Z
be given by (
28
),
then
X\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
and

I^{\Lambda}(X)=YZ\big((a,b]\times B\big)\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(38)

Proof.

Note that
U(X(t,x))=U(Y)1_{(a,b]}(t)1_{B}(x)
and
V_{0}(X(t,x))=V_{0}(Y)1_{(a,b]}(t)1_{B}(x)
. Hence, condition (
36
) holds, and

X\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
.

Let
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence of simple integrands given by Lemma
2.11
.(ii). More precisely, if
Y_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}a_{i,n}1_{F_{i,n}}
with
a_{i,n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
F_{i,n}\in\mathcal{F}_{a}
is the sequence of simple random variable such that
Y_{n}\to Y
and
|Y_{n}|\leq|Y|
for all

, then

S_{n}(t,x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}a_{i,n}1_{F_{i,n}}1_{(a,b]}(t)1_{B}(x).

Using (
31

I^{\Lambda}(S_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}a_{i,n}\Lambda(F_{i,n}\times(a,b]\times B)=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}a_{i,n}1_{F_{i,n}}Z\big((a,b]\times B\big)=Y_{n}Z\big((a,b]\times B\big).

Relation (
38
) follows letting

, since
I^{\Lambda}(S_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}I^{\Lambda}(X)
, by Theorem
2.13
.
∎

2.3
Construction of the noise

In this section, we give the construction of the noise.

Let
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,
(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
be the sequences mentioned in the introduction, defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Note that
\big(\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\big)_{i\geq 1}
are the points of a PRM on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
of intensity
\nu_{\alpha}
.
Therefore, using a procedure called “augmentation” (see Proposition 3.8 of
[
25
]
), the process

J_{\psi}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{(T_{i},X_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha})}

(39)

is a PRM on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
of intensity
m_{\psi}\times\nu_{\alpha}
. Consider the transformation
T_{\psi}(t,x,z)=(t,x,\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)})
. By Proposition 3.7 of
[
25
]
, the process

N_{\psi}=J_{\psi}\circ T_{\psi}^{-1}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{T_{\psi}(T_{i},X_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha})}=\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{(T_{i},X_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\psi^{-1}(T_{i},X_{i}))}

is also PRM on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
of intensity

(m_{\psi}\times\nu_{\alpha})\circ T_{\psi}^{-1}={\rm Leb}\times{\rm Leb}\times\nu_{\alpha}.

Now, using the PRM
N_{\psi}
, we define the homogeneous Lévy basis
\Lambda
on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\Lambda(A)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)z\widehat{N_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}1_{A}(t,x)zN_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz),

(40)

where
\widehat{N_{\psi}}
is the compensated version of
N_{\psi}
.

We let
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the filtration associated with
N_{\psi}
, i.e.

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left\{N_{\psi}([0,s]\times B\times F);s\in[0,t],B\in\mathcal{B}_{b},F\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(\mathbb{R}_{0})\right\}.

(41)

For any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{b}
, we define
Z(B)
by (
28
). Then
\Lambda
is a S
\alpha
S Lévy basis (see Definition
2.8
), and
Z
is a S
\alpha
S random measure (see Remark
2.9
). The next result shows that
Z
has the series representation (
5
).

Lemma 2.17
.

If
\Lambda
is the Lévy basis given by (
40
), then the process

Z
given by (
28
) has the LePage series representation (
5
).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be arbitrary. By the symmetry of
\nu_{\alpha}
,
\int_{B\times\{\varepsilon<|z|\leq 1\}}zdtdx\nu_{\alpha}(dz)=0
and hence,

\int_{B\times\{\varepsilon<|z|\leq 1\}}z\widehat{N_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)=\int_{B\times\{\varepsilon<|z|\leq 1\}}zN_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz),

and

Z(B)=\int_{B\times\{0<|z|<\varepsilon\}}z\widehat{N_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{B\times\{|z|>\varepsilon\}}zN_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)=:Z_{\varepsilon}(B)+S_{\varepsilon}(B).

Note that
Z_{\varepsilon}(B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\to}}0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, since
\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{\varepsilon}(B)|^{2}\right]=\int_{B\times\{\varepsilon<|z|\leq 1\}}z^{2}dtdx\nu_{\alpha}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, by the dominated convergence theorem.
Next, we prove that:

S_{\varepsilon}(B)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}S(B)\quad\mbox{as $\varepsilon\to 0$},

(42)

where
S(B)
is the sum on the right-hand side of (
5
). Note that

S(B)-S_{\varepsilon}(B)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\psi^{-1}(T_{i},X_{i})1_{\{\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\leq\varepsilon\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\}}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})=:\overline{S}_{\varepsilon}(B).

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\|\overline{S}_{\varepsilon}(B)\|_{0}=\mathbb{E}\big[\min\big(1,|\overline{S}_{\varepsilon}(B)|\big)\big]\leq\Big\{\mathbb{E}\big[\min\big(1,|\overline{S}_{\varepsilon}(B)|^{2}\big)\big]\Big\}^{1/2}.

Using the inequality
\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|X|)]\leq\min(1,\mathbb{E}|X|)
and the orthogonality of
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(1,|\overline{S}_{\varepsilon}(B)|^{2}\big)\,|\,(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\min\Big(1,\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-2/\alpha}\psi^{-2}(T_{i},X_{i})1_{\{\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\leq\varepsilon\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\}}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})\Big).

Taking expectation in the above inequality, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(1,|\overline{S}_{\varepsilon}(B)|^{2}\big)\Big]\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\min\Big(1,\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-2/\alpha}\psi^{-2}(T_{i},X_{i})1_{\{\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\leq\varepsilon\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\}}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})\Big)\Big].

The term on the right-hand side above converges to 0 as
\varepsilon\to 0
, by an application of the dominated convergence theorem, which is justified by the fact that

X:=\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-2/\alpha}\psi^{-2}(T_{i},X_{i})1_{\{\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\leq\psi(T_{i},X_{i})\}}1_{B}(T_{i},X_{i})=\int_{B\times\{|z|\leq 1\}}z^{2}N_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

since
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=\int_{B\times\{|z|\leq 1\}}z^{2}dtdx\nu_{\alpha}(dz)<\infty
.
∎

The following result gives an alternative representation for
\Lambda
. Its proof follows essentially using a change of measure, which allows us to pass not only from
N_{\psi}
to
J_{\psi}
, but also from
\widehat{N_{\psi}}
to
\widehat{J_{\psi}}
(although these are not measures), using the symmetry of
\nu_{\alpha}
.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
\Lambda
be the Lévy basis given by (
40
). For any
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Lambda(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq\psi(t,x)\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>\psi(t,x)\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz),

(43)

where
\widehat{J_{\psi}}
is the compensated version of
J_{\psi}
. Moreover, if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for any
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\frac{1}{\psi(T_{i},X_{i})}1_{A}(T_{i},X_{i}).

(45)

Proof.

We first prove (
43
).
Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be arbitrary. We write

\displaystyle\Lambda(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq\varepsilon\}}1_{A}(t,x)z\widehat{N_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\varepsilon<|z|\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)z\widehat{N_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}1_{A}(t,x)zN_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)=:T_{1}^{(\varepsilon)}+T_{2}^{(\varepsilon)}+T_{3},

and then we let
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
N_{\psi}=J_{\psi}\circ T_{\psi}^{-1}
,
using a change of measure, we have

T_{3}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>\psi(t,x)\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

By Itô’s isometry,
T_{1}^{(\varepsilon)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\to}}0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. For the second term,

\displaystyle T_{2}^{(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\varepsilon<|z|\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)zN_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)\quad\mbox{(by the symmetry of $\nu_{\alpha}$)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\varepsilon<\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)\quad\mbox{(since $N_{\psi}=J_{\psi}\circ T_{\psi}^{-1}$)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\varepsilon<\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)\quad\mbox{(by the symmetry of $\nu_{\alpha}$)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle L^{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)\quad\mbox{as $\varepsilon\to 0$}.

The last convergence holds by Itô’s isometry, using the fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq\varepsilon\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z^{2}}{\psi^{2}(t,x)}\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(t,x)\psi^{\alpha-2}(t,x)\left(\int_{\{\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq\varepsilon\}}z^{2}\nu_{\alpha}(dz)\right)dtdx\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}}{2-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(t,x)dtdx\right].

This proves (
43
).
Relation (
44
) follows directly from (
43
), since

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

To see this, note that by Fubini’s theorem and the symmetry of
\nu_{\alpha}
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{\frac{|z|}{\psi(t,x)}\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)z\psi^{\alpha-1}(t,x)dtdx\nu_{\alpha}(dz)=0.

To justify the application of Fubini’s theorem, we note that since
\alpha<1
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(t,x)\psi^{\alpha-1}(t,x)\left(\int_{\{|z|\leq\psi(t,x)\}}|z|\nu_{\alpha}(dz)\right)dxdt=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(t,x)dtdx<\infty.

Finally, (
45
) follows directly from (
44
), using definition (
39
) of
J_{\psi}
.
∎

Remark 2.19
.

When
\alpha\in(0,1)
, relation (
45
) extends the LePage series representation (
5
) to sets
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
. This relation will play an important role
in proving the recurrence relation (
13
); see the proof of Theorem
4.3
below.
We do not know if this representation holds for sets
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
, when
\alpha\in[1,2)
.

For integration purposes, we will use the following representation of
\Lambda
.

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
\Lambda
be the Lévy basis given by (
40
). For any
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
, we have:

\displaystyle\Lambda(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

(46)

Proof.

Consider the following (non-homogeneous) Lévy basis:

L_{\psi}(A)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}1_{A}(t,x)z\widehat{J_{\psi}}(dt,dx,dz)+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}1_{A}(t,x)zJ_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

(47)

This is an orthogonal
L^{0}
-random measure which has the canonical decomposition (3.2) of
[
15
]
, with respect to the truncation function
\tau(z)=z1_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}
, with
b=0
,
C=0
,
\mu=J_{\psi}
,
\nu(dt,dx,dz)=\psi^{\alpha}(t,x)dydx\nu_{\alpha}(dz)
, i.e.
A(dt,dx)=dtdx
and
K(t,x,dz)=\psi^{\alpha}(t,x)\nu_{\alpha}(dz)
. For this
L^{0}
-random measure, we have:

\displaystyle U(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=b(t,x)y+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\big(\tau(yz)-y\tau(z)\big)K(t,x,dz)=0

\displaystyle V_{0}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\big(|yz|^{2}\wedge 1\big)K(t,x,dz)=\frac{2}{2-\alpha}|y|^{\alpha}\psi^{\alpha}(t,x).

By Theorem 4.1 of
[
15
]
, if
H
is a predictable process, then

H\in L^{0}(L_{\psi})
if and only if
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi^{\alpha}(t,x)|H(t,x)|^{\alpha}dxdt<\infty
a.s.

(48)

In particular,
1_{A}\psi^{-1}\in L^{0}(L_{\psi})
,
for any
A\in\widetilde{P}_{b}
.

By Theorem 3.5 of
[
15
]
, we can define a null-spatial
L^{0}
-random measure
H\cdot L_{\psi}
by
(H\cdot L_{\psi})(A)=\int 1_{A}(t)H(t,x)L_{\psi}(dt,dx)
for suitable sets
A\in\mathcal{P}
, and this has canonical decomposition:

\displaystyle(H\cdot L_{\psi})(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}1_{A}(t)\tau(H(t,x)z)\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}1_{A}(t)\big(H(t,x)z-\tau(H(t,x)z)\big)J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

On the other hand, using the canonical decomposition (
47
) of
L_{\psi}
, we have:

\displaystyle(H\cdot L_{\psi})(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}1_{A}(t)H(t,x)\tau(z)\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}}1_{A}(t)H(t,x)\big(z-\tau(z)\big)J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

Combining these two expressions and writing them for

A=\overline{\Omega}:=\Omega\times[0,T]
, we obtain:

\displaystyle(H\cdot L_{\psi})(\overline{\Omega})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|H(t,x)z|\leq 1\}}H(t,x)z\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|H(t,x)z|>1\}}H(t,x)zJ_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}H(t,x)z\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}H(t,x)zJ_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

We apply this for
H=1_{A}\psi^{-1}
with
A\in\widetilde{P}_{b}
. The conclusion follows by (
43
).
∎

Finally, we give the following representation for the integral with respect to
\Lambda
.

Proposition 2.21
.

For any
H\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H(t,x)\Lambda(dt,dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}H(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}H(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

(49)

Proof.

By Proposition
2.20
, relation (
49
) holds for
H=1_{A}
with

, i.e.

\Lambda(A)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{A}(t,x)\frac{1}{\psi(t,x)}L_{\psi}(dt,dx),

(50)

where
L_{\psi}
is the Lévy basis given by (
47
).
By Corollary
2.15
,
H\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
is equivalent to
\int|H(t,x)|^{\alpha}dtdx<\infty
a.s., which in turn is equivalent to
H\psi^{-1}\in L^{0}(L_{\psi})
, by (

).

By Theorem 13.5 of
[
10
]
, there exists a sequence
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of simple integrands such that
S_{n}\to H
and
|S_{n}|\leq|H|
for all

. By Dominated Convergence Theorem (Theorem

),

I^{\Lambda}(S_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}I^{\Lambda}(H)\quad\mbox{and}\quad I^{L_{\psi}}(S_{n}\psi^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}I^{L_{\psi}}(H\psi^{-1}).

By (

I^{\Lambda}(S_{n})=I^{L_{\psi}}(S_{n}\psi^{-1})

The following result will be used in the proof of Lemma
4.7
below
(in the case

).

Proposition 2.22
.

Assume that
\alpha\in[1,2)
. For any
H\in L^{0}(\Lambda)
and

, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H(t,x)\Lambda(dt,dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq a\}}H(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>a\}}H(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

Proof.

In (
49
), we write the first term on the right hand-side as the sum of two integrals corresponding to sets
\{|z|\leq a\}
and
\{a<|z|\leq 1\}
. Notice that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{a<|z|\leq 1\}}H(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}\widehat{J}_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{a<|z|\leq 1\}}H(t,x)\frac{z}{\psi(t,x)}J_{\psi}(dt,dx,dz).

This is because
\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{a<|z|\leq 1\}}H(t,x)z\psi^{\alpha-1}(t,x)dxdt\nu_{\alpha}(dz)=0
, by Fubini’s theorem and the symmetry of

. To justify the application of Fubini’s theorem, we need to prove

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|H(t,x)|\psi^{\alpha-1}(t,x)\left(\int_{\{a<|z|\leq 1\}}|z|\nu_{\alpha}(dz)\right)dxdt<\infty,

which is equivalent to

. This last fact is clear when

, and can be proved using Hölder’s inequality with

, when

.
∎

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem

First, we show that under a condition weaker than the one given by Assumption

, the series on the right-hand side of (
10
) converges in probability. Then, we show that under Assumption
1.2
, this series converges absolutely almost surely. For the rest of the paper, we will denote this series by
u(t,x)
:

u(t,x):=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\big)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\big).

(51)

Recall that
f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
is the kernel given by (
11
) and
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
is its symmetrization. By Theorem
2.6
, the multiple stable integral

I_{n}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x))
is well-defined if and only if

h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x):=T^{\frac{2n}{\alpha}}(n!)^{2}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x)<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

and in this case, it has the LePage series representation:

I_{n}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x))=T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x)\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(52)

We have the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\varepsilon_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be i.i.d. with
\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{j}=1)=\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon_{j}=-1)=1/2
. Let
\{a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}};1\leq j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n},j_{i}\in\mathbb{N}\}
be an array of non-negative numbers. The series

\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\varepsilon_{j_{1}}\ldots\varepsilon_{j_{n}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\quad\mbox{converges in $L^{2}(\Omega)$}

if and only if

\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{2}<\infty
,
and in this case,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\varepsilon_{j_{1}}\ldots\varepsilon_{j_{n}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\right|^{2}\right]=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{2}.

Proof.

The terms of the series are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
since

\mathbb{E}\left[\varepsilon_{j_{1}}\ldots\varepsilon_{j_{n}}\varepsilon_{k_{1}}\ldots e_{k_{m}}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\},$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

If
\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{2}<\infty
, then by the generalized Khintchine inequality (Proposition 1 of
[
30
]
), the series

\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\varepsilon_{j_{1}}\ldots\varepsilon_{j_{n}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}
converges a.s. and in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for any
p\geq 0
.

Using Lemma
3.1
, we see that

h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}\Big[|I_{n}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x))|^{2}\left|\right.(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})\Big].

(53)

The following basic conditioning fact will be used several times in the sequel: if
X
and
Y
are independent random elements with values in measurable spaces
(E,\mathcal{E})
, respectively
(F,\mathcal{F})
, and
f:E\times F\to[0,\infty]
is a measurable function, then

\mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)|X=x]=\mathbb{E}[f(x,Y)].

(54)

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary. If

\mathcal{A}_{2}(t,x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.},

(55)

then the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x))
converges in probability.

Proof.

Recall that convergence in probability is equivalent with the convergence in
L^{0}
equipped with the norm
\|X\|_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|X|)]
.

Let
S_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}(\widetilde{f}_{k}(\cdot,t,x))
.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for any
n>m
,

\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|)]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|^{2})]\right)^{1/2}.

Conditioning on
(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|^{2})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|^{2})|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})]].

Using the inequality
\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|X|)]\leq\min(1,E|X|)
, followed by Lemma
3.1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|^{2})|(\Gamma_{i})=(\gamma_{i}),(T_{i})=(t_{i}),(X_{i})=(x_{i})]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(1,\left|\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}T^{k/\alpha}k!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j_{\ell}}\gamma_{j_{\ell}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(x_{j_{\ell}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(t_{j_{1}},x_{j_{1}},\ldots,t_{j_{k}},x_{j_{k}},t,x)\right|^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\min\left\{1,\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}T^{k/\alpha}k!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{j_{\ell}}\gamma_{j_{\ell}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(x_{j_{\ell}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(t_{j_{1}},x_{j_{1}},\ldots,t_{j_{k}},x_{j_{k}},t,x)\right|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad=\min\left\{1,\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}T^{2k/\alpha}(k!)^{2}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{k}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\gamma_{j_{\ell}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(x_{j_{\ell}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(t_{j_{1}},x_{j_{1}},\ldots,t_{j_{k}},x_{j_{k}},t,x)\right\}.

This means that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|^{2})|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})\Big]\leq\min\left\{1,\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}h_{k}^{(2)}(t,x)\right\}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|S_{n}-S_{m}|^{2})]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\min\Big(1,\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}h_{k}^{(2)}(t,x)\Big)\right].

By (
55
) and the dominated convergence theorem, the last term above converges to
0
as
n,m\to\infty
. This proves that
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{0}
.
∎

The following result gives a criterion for verifying (
55
). Recall that
K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)
is defined by (
14
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed. If there exists
p\in(\alpha,2]
such that

\sum_{n\geq 1}T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\Big)^{n}<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.},

(56)

then (
55
) holds, and consequently,
\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\big)
converges in probability.

Proof.

By the sub-additivity of the function
\varphi(x)=x^{p/2},x>0
, we have:

\Big(h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)\Big)^{p/2}\leq h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x),

(57)

where

h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x):=T^{pn/\alpha}(n!)^{p}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x).

Hence,

\mathcal{A}_{2}^{p/2}(t,x)\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\Big(h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)\Big)^{p/2}\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)=:\mathcal{A}_{p}(t,x).

We will prove that
\mathcal{A}_{p}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. For this, we apply Remark
B.1
to
X=(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
Y=\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
. We will show that:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{p}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(58)

By the independence between
(\Gamma_{i})_{i}
and
\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}_{i}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})\Big]=T^{pn/\alpha}(n!)^{p}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x)\Big].

For any
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}
fixed, we denote

A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x):=(n!)^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x)\right].

(59)

We now use the explicit expression of
A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)
, given by Lemma
3.5
below, which in particular, shows that
A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)
does not depend on
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle=T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\,n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\right)^{n},

(60)

where for the last inequality, we used the fact that for any
a_{j}>0
,

n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}a_{j_{1}}\ldots a_{j_{n}}\leq\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}a_{j}\Big)^{n}.

(61)

By the strong law of large numbers,
\Gamma_{j}/j\to 1
a.s., and hence
\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}<\infty
a.s. Finally, (
58
) follows by (
56
), since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}_{p}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\Big[h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})\Big]\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\right)^{n}.

∎

The following lemma gives the explicit expression for
A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
t\in[0,T]
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p>0
and
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}
, we have:

A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)=\frac{n!}{T^{n}}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x).

(62)

Proof.

By the definition of
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
,

\displaystyle A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})\Big(\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}f_{n}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},t,x)\Big)^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}\frac{f_{n}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},t,x)}{\phi(X_{j_{\pi(1)}})\ldots\phi(X_{j_{\pi(n)}})}\right)^{p}\right].

(63)

We now present two methods leading to the desired relation (
62
).

Method 1.
(due to G. Samorodnitsky)
Recall that
f_{n}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},t,x)
contains the indicator of

0<T_{j_{\pi(1)}}<\ldots<T_{j_{\pi(n)}}<t.

(64)

For fixed values
T_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}}\in[0,t]
, there is a unique permutation
\pi
for which (
64
) holds.
Therefore, the sum over all permutations above in fact contains
only one term
, all the other terms vanishing. The vector

(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}})
corresponding to that term
has the same distribution as
(T_{(1)},X_{1},\ldots,T_{(n)},X_{n})
, where
T_{(1)}<\ldots<T_{(n)}
are the order statistics of
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{-p}(X_{k})f_{n}^{p}(T_{(1)},X_{1},\ldots,T_{(n)},X_{n},t,x)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{T^{n}}\int_{T_{n}(T)}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}f_{n}^{p}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha-p}(x_{k})dx_{1}\ldots dx_{n}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{n},

using the fact that
(T_{(1)},\ldots,T_{(n)})
has a uniform distribution over the simplex
T_{n}(T)
. The desired relation follows since
f_{n}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)=0
if
t_{n}>t
.

Method 2.

From (
63
), since
(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}})
has density
T^{-n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha}(x_{k})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(t,x)=T^{-n}\int_{[0,T]^{n}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}\frac{f_{n}(t_{\pi(1)},x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,t_{\pi(n)},x_{\pi(n)},t,x)}{\phi(x_{\pi(1)})\ldots\phi(x_{\pi(n)})}\right)^{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha}(x_{k})d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle=T^{-n}\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma_{n}}\int_{0<t_{\rho(1)}<\ldots<t_{\rho(n)}<t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\left(\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}\frac{f_{n}(t_{\pi(1)},x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,t_{\pi(n)},x_{\pi(n)},t,x)}{\phi(x_{\pi(1)})\ldots\phi(x_{\pi(n)})}\right)^{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha}(x_{k})d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle=T^{-n}\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma_{n}}\int_{0<t_{\rho(1)}<\ldots<t_{\rho(n)}<t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\left(\frac{f_{n}(t_{\rho(1)},x_{\rho(1)},\ldots,t_{\rho(n)},x_{\rho(n)},t,x)}{\phi(x_{\rho(1)})\ldots\phi(x_{\rho(n)})}\right)^{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha}(x_{k})d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{t}

\displaystyle=T^{-n}n!\int_{0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<t}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\left(\frac{f_{n}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)}{\phi(x_{1})\ldots\phi(x_{n})}\right)^{p}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\phi^{\alpha}(x_{k})d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{t}.

∎

Proposition 3.6

4
The recurrence relation

In this section,
we prove that the partial sum sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
given by

u_{0}(t,x)=1\quad\mbox{and}\quad u_{n}(t,x)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)),\ n\geq 1

(67)

is indeed the Picard’s iteration sequence, i.e. it satisfies the recurrence relation (
13
).

By linearity, instead of proving (
13
), it is enough to show that for any
n\geq 0
,

I_{n+1}(f_{n+1}(\cdot,t,x))=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,s,y))Z(ds,dy).

where
f_{0}(s,y)=1
and
I_{0}(x)=x
. For this,
we define
the multiple integral process
:

X_{n}^{(t,x)}(s,y)=G_{t-s}(x-y)I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,s,y)),\quad s\in[0,T],y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For any
n\geq 0
,
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
fixed, we have to prove that:

(i) the multiple integral process is integrable wr.r.t.
Z
, i.e.

X_{n}^{(t,x)}\in L^{0}(Z);

(68)

(ii) the integral w.r.t.
Z
of the multiple integral process coincides with
I_{n+1}(f_{n+1}(\cdot,t,x))
:

I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})=I_{n+1}(f_{n+1}(\cdot,t,x)).

(69)

These facts will be proved separately in the following two sections.

4.1
Integrability of the muliple integral process

In this section, we give the proof of (
68
). By Corollary
2.15
, we need to check that

\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)|I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,s,y))|^{\alpha}dyds<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.},

(70)

provided that
X_{n}^{(t,x)}
is predictable. The next lemma addresses the issue of predictability.

Lemma 4.1
.

The process
\{I_{n}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x));t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}

Proof.

We only need to prove the first statement, since the map
(\omega,s,y)\mapsto G_{t-s}(x-y)
is clearly predictable. By LePage representation (
52
),
I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,t,x))=S_{n}(t,x)
a.s. where

\displaystyle S_{n}(t,x)

\displaystyle=T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x).

Hence, it is enough to prove that
S_{n}
has a predictable modification. For this, we proceed as in the proof of Lemma 6.2 of
[
12
]
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

S_{n}^{P}(\cdot,x)
be the
extended predictable projection
of
S_{n}(\cdot,x)
, given by Theorem I.2.28 of
[
19
]
, i.e.
S_{n}^{P}(\cdot,x)

is
\mathcal{P}
-measurable and
S_{n}^{P}(\cdot,x)=\mathbb{E}[S_{n}(t,x)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]

a.s. for all
t\in[0,T]
.
By Proposition 3 of
[
33
]
, there exists a predictable process
\widetilde{S}_{n}
such that

\widetilde{S}_{n}(t,x)=S_{n}^{P}(t,x)
a.s. for all
(t,x)
. Hence, for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\widetilde{S}_{n}(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[S_{n}(t,x)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}]=S_{n}(t,x)\quad\mbox{a.s.},

where the last equality is due to the fact that
S_{n}(t,x)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-}
-measurable, which is true because

is a function of the points of
N_{\psi}
situated in

is the filtration associated with

, given by (
41

∎

We continue now with the verification of (

).
This will be the consequence of the following more general result, which will be needed for the proof of Theorem

below.

Theorem 4.2
.

(72)

then
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)<\infty
a.s., and consequently
X_{n}^{(t,x)}\in L^{0}(Z)
.

Proof.

We only need to prove the first statement. The second statement is proved in the same way, dropping the sum over
n\geq 1
.

We consider first the case
\alpha\leq 1
.
We will prove that:

\mathcal{C}(t,x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

We apply Remark
B.2
with
X=(\Gamma_{i})
and
Y=\{(T_{i}),(X_{i}),(\varepsilon_{i})\}
. We will prove that:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(73)

We use the fact that

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})]|(\Gamma_{i})]
,
and we estimate separately the inner conditional expectation. By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\mathbb{E}[|I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,s,y))|^{\alpha}|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})]dyds

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\left(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\right)^{\alpha/2}dyds.

We now use the following form of Jensen’s inequality: if
(E,\mathcal{E},\mu)
is a finite measure space with
\mu(E)=a
, then for any measurable function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\int_{E}f^{p}d\mu\leq a^{1-p}\left(\int_{E}fd\mu\right)^{p}\quad\mbox{for any $p\in(0,1]$}.

(74)

We apply this inequality to the finite measure
\mu(ds,dy)=G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(s-y)1_{(0,t)}(s)dsdy
(whose total mass we denote
C_{t}
), and the exponent
p^{\prime}=\alpha/p
. We obtain:

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\left(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dyds\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\left(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\left(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}\Bigg|(\Gamma_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\mathbb{E}[\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\big)^{p/2}|(\Gamma_{i})]dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}},

using (
66
) (with exponent
p^{\prime}=\alpha/p
) for the last inequality. We now pass from

(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y))^{p/2}
to
h_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)
(using inequality (
57
)), and estimate

\mathbb{E}[h_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)|(\Gamma_{i})]
using (
60
). We get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}\big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\big)^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}.

The last series converges with probability
1
, due to (
16
). This proves (
73
).

Next, we consider the case
\alpha>1
.
We will prove that:

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t,x):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\big(\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)\big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

By the same application of Remark
B.2
as above, it suffices to prove that:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(75)

We use again double conditioning relation, and we estimate separately the inner conditional expectation. By Jensen’s inequality (
66
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)\big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})\Big]\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\mathbb{E}[|I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,s,y))|^{\alpha}|(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})]dyds\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\big)^{\alpha/2}dyds\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

Conditioning on
(\Gamma_{i})
, and using again the conditional Jensen’s inequality (
66
) to push the power
1/\alpha
outside the conditional expectation, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\big)^{\alpha/2}dyds\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\Bigg|(\Gamma_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\mathbb{E}[\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\big)^{\alpha/2}|(\Gamma_{i})]dyds\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

To arrive to the desired exponent
p/2
for
h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)
, we apply Jensen’s inequality
\mathbb{E}(|X||\mathcal{G})\leq\big(\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}|\mathcal{G}]\big)^{1/p}
for
p\geq 1
.
Combining this with bound (
57
), we obtain:

\mathbb{E}[\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\big)^{\alpha/2}|(\Gamma_{i})]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}[\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)\big)^{p/2}|(\Gamma_{i})]\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}\leq\left(\mathbb{E}[h_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)|(\Gamma_{i})]\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}.

Finally, we use (
60
) to estimate the last conditional expectation. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\left(\mathbb{E}[h_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)|(\Gamma_{i})]\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}dyds\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\Big)^{n/p}.

Relation (
75
) follows by (
16
).
∎

4.2
The integral of the multiple integral process:
\alpha\in(0,1)

In this section, we give the proof of (
69
) in the case
\alpha<1
. For this, an essential role is played by LePage representation (
45
) of
\Lambda(A)
for
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
, obtained in Section
2.3
.

We start with a preliminary result, which gives the series representation for
I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be fixed. Suppose that there exists

p\in(\alpha,1]
such that
K_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)<\infty
, and
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. Then

I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})=T^{1/\alpha}\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\frac{1}{\phi(X_{i})}G_{t-T_{i}}(x-X_{i})I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i})\big)\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(76)

Proof.

Condition
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. ensures that
X_{n}^{(t,x)}\in L^{0}(Z)
.

Step 1.
In this step, we prove that the series on the right hand-side of (
76
) converges absolutely a.s., i.e.

U:=\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{i})G_{t-T_{i}}(x-X_{i})\left|I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i})\big)\right|<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Since
p\leq 1
, by subadditivity,

U^{p}\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{i})G_{t-T_{i}}^{p}(x-X_{i})\left|I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i})\big)\right|^{p}=:U^{\prime}.

So it is enough to prove that
U^{\prime}<\infty
a.s.

Using the LePage representation (
52
), the subadditivity of the function
\varphi(x)=|x|^{p}
, and the fact that
\widetilde{f}_{n}(t_{1},x_{1},\ldots,t_{n},x_{n},t,x)=0
if
t_{i}=t
for some
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have:

\displaystyle|I_{n}(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i}))|^{p}\leq T^{pn/\alpha}(n!)\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{i},X_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
i\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})f_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},T_{i},X_{i}).

We multiply this inequality by
\Gamma_{i}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{i})G_{t-T_{i}}^{p}(x-X_{i})
, then we take the sum for all
i\geq 1
. We use the fact that
G_{t-T_{i}}^{p}(x-X_{i})f_{n}^{p}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i})=f_{n+1}^{p}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i},t,x)
. We obtain:

\displaystyle U^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
i\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad f_{n+1}^{p}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},T_{i},X_{i},t,x).

We now use the following fact: for any
a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}\geq 0
,

\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}a_{j_{\pi(1)},\ldots,j_{\pi(n)}}=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\geq 1\ {\rm distinct}}a_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}.

(77)

Hence,

\displaystyle U^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{i\geq 1}\Gamma_{i}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\geq 1\ {\rm distinct}\\
i\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\,\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad f_{n+1}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{i},X_{i},t,x)

\displaystyle=T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n+1}\geq 1\,{\rm distinct}}\,\,\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})f_{n+1}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n+1}},X_{j_{n+1}},t,x)

\displaystyle=T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n+1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n+1}}f_{n+1}^{p}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},X_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},t,x)

\displaystyle=:B_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x).

(78)

We prove that
B_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. For this, we apply Remark
B.2
with
X=(\Gamma_{i})
and
Y=\{(T_{i},X_{i})\}
. We will prove that

\mathbb{E}[B_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Note that
(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},X_{j_{\pi(n+1)}})
has density

T^{-(n+1)/\alpha}\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\phi^{\alpha}(x_{k})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{k}})f_{n+1}^{p}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},X_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},t,x)\right]=\frac{1}{T^{(n+1)/\alpha}}K_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[B_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]

\displaystyle=T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n+1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-p/\alpha}\cdot(n+1)!\frac{1}{T^{(n+1)/\alpha}}K_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq T^{pn/\alpha}\frac{1}{T^{(n+1)/\alpha}}K_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\right)^{n+1}<\infty\ \mbox{a.s.}

Step 2.
Since
X_{n}^{(t,x)}
is predictable, by Theorem 13.5 of
[
10
]
, there exists a sequence
(S_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of simple integrands such that
S_{k}\to X_{n}^{(t,x)}
as
k\to\infty
, and
|S_{k}|\leq|X_{n}^{(t,x)}|
for all
k
. Note that
X_{n}^{(t,x)}\in L^{p}(Z)
, since
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. By Theorem
2.13
,
I^{Z}(S_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})
as
k\to\infty
. This convergence is a.s., along a subsequence.

Since
S_{k}
is a linear combination of sets in
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
, by the LePage representation (
45
),

\displaystyle I^{Z}(S_{k})

\displaystyle=I^{\Lambda}(S_{k})=\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\frac{1}{\psi(T_{i},X_{i})}S_{k}(T_{i},X_{i}).

Relation (
76
) follows letting
k\to\infty
. On the right hand-side, we use the dominated convergence theorem, whose application is justified by
Step 1
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary. Suppose that there exists
p\in(\alpha,1]
such that
K_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. and
\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)<\infty
a.s. Then (
69
) holds.

Proof.

We use (
76
), in which we replace
I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i})\big)
by its LePage representation (
52
). Using the fact that
\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{i},X_{i})=0
if
i\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
, we have:

\displaystyle I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})

\displaystyle=T^{\frac{n+1}{\alpha}}\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{i})G_{t-T_{i}}(x-X_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
i\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}f_{n}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},T_{i},X_{i}).

We now use the fact that
G_{t-T_{i}}(x-X_{i})f_{n}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i})=f_{n+1}(\cdot,T_{i},X_{i},t,x)
, and we apply (
77
) two times. We obtain:

\displaystyle I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})=T^{\frac{n+1}{\alpha}}\sum_{i\geq 1}\varepsilon_{i}\Gamma_{i}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\geq 1\,{\rm distinct}\\
i\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad f_{n+1}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{i},X_{i},t,x)

\displaystyle\quad=T^{\frac{n+1}{\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{n+1}\geq 1\,{\rm distinct}\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})f_{n+1}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n+1}},X_{j_{n+1}},t,x)

\displaystyle\quad=T^{\frac{n+1}{\alpha}}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n+1}}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n+1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})f_{n+1}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},X_{j_{\pi(n+1)}},t,x)

\displaystyle\quad=T^{\frac{n+1}{\alpha}}(n+1)!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n+1}}\prod_{k=1}^{n+1}\varepsilon_{j_{k}}\Gamma_{j_{k}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{k}})\widetilde{f}_{n+1}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n+1}},X_{j_{n+1}},t,x)

\displaystyle\quad=I_{n+1}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n+1}(\cdot,t,x)\big).

∎

4.3
The integral of the multiple integral process:
\alpha\in[1,2)

In this section, we give the proof of (
69
) in the case
\alpha\geq 1
. The proof is significantly more involved than in the case
\alpha<1
, being the most technical part of the paper.
The reason is that in the case
\alpha\geq 1
, we are not able to prove a LePage representation for
\Lambda(A)
for all
A\in\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{b}
, as it was mentioned in Remark
2.19
.

We fix
k\geq 1
. In the LePage representation (
52
) of
I_{n}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,s,y)\big)
, in front of
\prod_{i=1}^{n}
, we introduce the factor
1=1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}>k^{-1}\}}+1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}
. Since
\Gamma_{j_{1}}^{-1/\alpha}>\ldots>\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}
, we see that

\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}>k^{-1}\}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}\{\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}>k^{-1}\}
,
We obtain the decomposition:

I_{n}\big(\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,s,y)\big)=\mathcal{J}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)+\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y),

(79)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)

\displaystyle=T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{i}}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}>k^{-1}\}}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},s,y),

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)

\displaystyle=T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{i}}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},s,y).

The following result shows that
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(t,x)
is asymptotically negligible in probability, when
k\to\infty
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume that
\alpha\in[1,2)
.
Let
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary. If there exists
p\in(\alpha,2]
such that
K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)<\infty
, then
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(t,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

We argue as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(t,x)\|_{L^{0}}=\mathbb{E}\big[\min\big(1,|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}(t,x)|\big)\big]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\big[\min\big(1,|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}(s,y)|^{2}\big)\big]\right)^{1/2}.

By Lemma
3.1
and the inequality

\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|X|)]\leq\min(1,\mathbb{E}|X|)
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\min\big(1,|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{k}(s,y)|^{2}\big)\,\big|\,(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})\big]\leq

\displaystyle\quad\min\Big(1,T^{2n/\alpha}(n!)^{2}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t,x)\Big).

The last expression converges to
0
as
k\to\infty
, by the dominated convergence theorem. To justify the application of this theorem, we bound the indicator above by
1
, which leads to
\min(1,h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x))
. To see that
h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)<\infty
a.s., we apply Remark
B.2
to
X=(\Gamma_{i})
and
Y=\{(T_{i}),(X_{i})\}
, noting that, by (
57
) and (
60
),

\mathbb{E}[\big(h_{n}^{(2)}(t,x)\big)^{p/2}|(\Gamma_{i})]\leq\mathbb{E}[h_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)|(\Gamma_{i})]\leq T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\right)^{n}<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Finally, another application of dominated convergence theorem shows that

\mathbb{E}[\min(1,|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(t,x)|^{2})]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}\big[\min\big(1,|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(t,x)|^{2}\big)\,|\,(\Gamma_{i}),(T_{i}),(X_{i})\big]\big]\to 0.

∎

Note that, if there exists
p\in(\alpha,2]
such that (
72
) holds, then the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
4.2
shows that

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)|\mathcal{J}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{\alpha}dyds<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

and

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{\alpha}dyds<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Choosing predictable modifications for the processes
Y_{n,k}^{(t,x)}(s,y)=G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{J}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)
and
Z_{n,k}^{(t,x)}(s,y)=G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)
, we infer that these processes are in
L^{0}(Z)
, by Corollary
2.15
.
By decomposition (
79
),

I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{J}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)Z(ds,dy)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)Z(ds,dy).

Hence, to prove that
I^{Z}(X_{n}^{(t,x)})=I_{n+1}\big(f_{n+1}(\cdot,t,x)\big)
, it suffices to show that the first term converges in probability to
I_{n+1}\big(f_{n+1}(\cdot,t,x)\big)
as
k\to\infty
, and the second one is negligible.
This will be achieved by the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.6
.

Assume that
\alpha\in[1,2)
and Hypothesis
1.1
holds.
Let
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary. If there exists
p\in(\alpha,2]
such that
K_{n+1}^{(p)}(t,x)<\infty
and (
72
) holds, then

A_{k}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)Z(ds,dy)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\quad\mbox{as $k\to\infty$}.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.21
, we have the decomposition:

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)\frac{z}{\psi(s,y)}\widehat{J}_{\psi}(ds,dy,dz)+

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|>1\}}G_{t-s}(x-y)\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)\frac{z}{\psi(s,y)}J_{\psi}(ds,dy,dz)=:T_{1}^{(k)}+T_{2}^{(k)}.

We treat separately the two terms.
Recall that
\psi(s,y)=T^{-1/\alpha}\phi(y)
.

Step 1.

First, we treat
T_{2}^{(k)}
.
Since
J_{\psi}
has points
\{(T_{j},X_{j},\varepsilon_{j}\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha})\}_{j\geq 1}
, we have

\displaystyle T_{2}^{(k)}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\varepsilon_{j}\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}>1\}}W_{n,j}^{(k)},

with

W_{n,j}^{(k)}:=T^{1/\alpha}G_{t-T_{j}}(x-X_{j})\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})\phi^{-1}(X_{j}).

(80)

Using the fact that
\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}\leq\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}
on the event
\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}>1\}
, we obtain:

\big|T_{2}^{(k)}\big|\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}>1\}}\big|W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big|\leq\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}\big|W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big|.

We use the following fact for any random variable
X
and sub-
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}
,

\|X\|_{L^{0}}\leq\big\|\mathbb{E}[|X|\,|\mathcal{G}]\big\|_{L^{0}}.

(81)

This leads to the following inequality:

\big\|T_{2}^{(k)}\big\|_{L^{0}}\leq\big\|\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}\big|W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big|\,\big\|_{L^{0}}\leq\Big\|\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big|\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]\Big\|_{L^{0}}.

(82)

We fix
j\geq 1
. By Hölder’s inequality for conditional expectation,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big|\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]\right)^{1/\alpha}.

Note that
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})
is a series depending on multi-indices
j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}
and each term in this series contains the factor
\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{j},X_{j})
, which vanishes if
j\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
. So, we can assume that
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})
is a function of the sequences
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1},(\Gamma_{i})_{i\geq 1},(T_{i})_{i\geq 1},(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.

We apply the basic conditioning fact (
54
) to
X=(\Gamma_{i})_{i}
and
Y=\{(\varepsilon_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\}
. Using the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{j,n}^{(k)}\big|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}=(\gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]=T\mathbb{E}\Big[G_{t-T_{j}}^{\alpha}(x-X_{j})\frac{1}{\phi^{\alpha}(X_{j})}

\displaystyle\Big|T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}1_{\{\gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{i}}\gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{j},X_{j})\Big|^{\alpha}\Big].

We use the fact that for independent random elements
X
and
Y
with values in measurable spaces
(E,\mathcal{E})
, respectively
(F,\mathcal{F})
, and a measurable function
f:E\times F\to[0,\infty]
,

\mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)]=\int_{E}\mathbb{E}[f(x,Y)]\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{E}f(x,Y)\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\right],

where
\mathbb{P}_{X}
denotes the law of
X
. We will use this fact with
X=(T_{j},X_{j})
(which has law
T^{-1}\phi^{\alpha}(y)dsdy
) and
Y=\{(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\geq 1},(T_{i},X_{i})_{i\not=j}\}
, to compute the previous expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{j,n}^{(k)}\big|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}=(\gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]=T\,\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\frac{1}{\phi^{\alpha}(y)}

\displaystyle\Big|T^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}n!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}1_{\{\gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{i}}\gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},s,y)\Big|^{\alpha}T^{-1}\phi^{\alpha}(y)dyds\Bigg].

Using again the basic conditioning fact (
54
) for the expectation above, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{j,n}^{(k)}\big|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}=(\gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]=\,\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\Big|T^{\frac{n}{\alpha}}n!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{i}}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},s,y)\Big|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}=(\gamma_{i})_{i}\right].

In summary, we have proved the following non-trivial fact:

A_{n,j}^{(k)}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|W_{j,n}^{(k)}\big|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)|\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{\alpha}dyds\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\right],

(83)

where

\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)=T^{n/\alpha}n!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{j_{i}}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-1/\alpha}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},s,y).

We continue with the estimation of
A_{n,j}^{(k)}
.
Using a double conditioning argument for the term on the right hand side of (
83
), we see that
A_{n,j}^{(k)}=\mathbb{E}\big[Q_{n,j}^{(k)}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\big]
, where

Q_{n,j}^{(k)}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\right]dyds.

Using Hölder’s inequality for conditional expectation, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{\alpha}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\right]\leq\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{2}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\right]\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=:\Big(h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

We obtain that:

\displaystyle Q_{n,j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\Big(h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}dyds

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\big(h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)\big)^{p/2}dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}},

where for the last line we applied Jensen’s inequality (
74
), exactly as before. Hence,

\displaystyle A_{n,j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\big(h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)\big)^{p/2}dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}\,\Big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)\mathbb{E}\big[\big(h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)\big)^{p/2}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}\big]dyds\right)^{\alpha/p}

(84)

where for the last line we used Jensen’s inequality (
66
), and we switched the
dyds
integral with the conditional expectation.

To continue the previous estimation, we need to evaluate
h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s,y)
, which is
in fact very similar to
h_{n}^{(2)}(s,y)
(see (
53
)):

\displaystyle h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s.y)

\displaystyle=T^{2n/\alpha}(n!)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},s,y).

We take power
p/2
, which we then move inside the sum, by sub-additivity. Dropping the restriction
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
, and recalling definition (
59
) of
A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(s,y)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big(h_{n,j}^{(k)}(s.y)\big)^{p/2}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})\big]

\displaystyle\leq T^{pn/\alpha}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(s,y)

\displaystyle=n!T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y),

where for the last line, we used the expression of
A_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}}^{(p)}(s,y)
given by Lemma
3.5
. Plugging this into (
84
), we obtain:

\displaystyle A_{n,j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\left(T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}n!\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}.

By the dominated convergence theorem, the first factor converges to
0
a.s. as
k\to\infty
. The second factor is a constant, that we denote
C_{t}^{\prime}
. Therefore
A_{n,j}^{(k)}\to 0
a.s. as
k\to\infty
. Moreover,
A_{n,j}^{(k)}\leq C_{t}^{1-\frac{\alpha}{p}}\big[T^{(p/\alpha-1)n}\big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\big)^{n}\big]^{\alpha/p}C_{t}^{\prime}
, due to inequality (
61
).
By another application of the dominated convergence theorem,

\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}A_{n,j}^{(k)}\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s.}\quad\mbox{as $k\to\infty$}

In particular, the previous sum converges in probability to
0
, as
k\to\infty
. Recalling inequality (
82
), we infer that
T_{2}^{(k)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
as
k\to\infty
, which concludes
Step 1
.

Step 2.

Next, we treat
T_{1}^{(k)}
.
Note that for any
(t_{0},x_{0})
fixed, the process

M_{t}^{(t_{0},x_{0})}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}G_{t_{0}-s}(x_{0}-y)\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)\frac{z}{\psi(s,y)}\widehat{J}_{\psi}(ds,dy,dz),\quad t\in[0,T],

is a local martingale. By Lenglart’s inequality, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|M_{t}^{(t_{0},x_{0})}|>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\{|z|\leq 1\}}G_{t_{0}-s}^{2}(x_{0}-y)|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(s,y)|^{2}\frac{z^{2}}{\psi^{2}(s,y)}J_{\psi}(ds,dy,dz)>\varepsilon\right).

We apply this inequality to
(t_{0},x_{0})=(t,x)
. Using the points of
J_{\psi}
, we obtain:

\mathbb{P}(|T_{1}^{(k)}|>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})|\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})|^{2}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}\leq 1\}}\frac{\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}}{T^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{2}(X_{j})}>\varepsilon\right).

We will show below that:

T_{3}^{(k)}:=\sum_{j\geq 1}G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})|\mathcal{R}_{n,j}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})|^{2}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}\leq 1\}}\frac{\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}}{T^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{2}(X_{j})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\longrightarrow}}0\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty.

(85)

It will follow that
\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|T_{k}^{(1)}|>\varepsilon)\leq\eta/\varepsilon^{2}
for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
. Letting
\eta\to 0
, we infer that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|T_{k}^{(1)}|>\varepsilon)=0
for any
\varepsilon>0
, i.e.
T_{k}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\to}}0
as
k\to\infty
.

It remains to prove (
85
).
We proceed as for
T_{1}^{(k)}
. Recalling definition (
80
) of
W_{n,j}^{(k)}
, we see that

T_{3}^{(k)}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}\leq 1\}}\big(W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big)^{2}.

We use again inequality (
81
),
with
\mathcal{G}
the
\sigma
-field generated by
(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}
.
We obtain:

\|T_{3}^{(k)}\|_{L^{0}}\leq\Big\|\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}\leq 1\}}B_{n,j}^{(k)}\Big\|_{L^{0}},

(86)

where

\displaystyle B_{n,j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\big[\big(W_{n,j}^{(k)}\big)^{2}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\big]

\displaystyle=T^{2/\alpha}G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})\phi^{-2}(X_{j})\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})\big|^{2}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\Big].

Similarly to (
53
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})\big|^{2}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=T^{\frac{2n}{\alpha}}(n!)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{j},X_{j})

\displaystyle\quad\leq h_{n}^{(2)}(T_{j},X_{j}),

where for the last line, we just bounded
1_{\{\Gamma_{j_{n}}^{-1/\alpha}\leq k^{-1}\}}
by
1
. Therefore,

B_{n,j}^{(k)}\leq T^{2/\alpha}G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})\phi^{-2}(X_{j})h_{n}^{(2)}(T_{j},X_{j}).

We will prove that

B_{n,j}^{(k)}\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s.}\quad\mbox{as $k\to\infty$, for any $j\geq 1$,}

(87)

and

S:=\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})\phi^{-2}(X_{j})h_{n}^{(2)}(T_{j},X_{j})<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

(88)

Then, by the dominated convergence theorem,
\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}1_{\{\Gamma_{j}^{-1/\alpha}\leq 1\}}B_{n,j}^{(k)}\to 0
a.s. as
k\to\infty
. Consequently, by (
86
),
\|T_{3}^{(k)}\|_{0}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. This proves (
85
).

We now prove (
87
).
We fix
j\geq 1
. It is enough to prove that

\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathcal{R}_{n}^{(k)}(T_{j},X_{j})\big|^{2}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},(T_{i})_{i},(X_{i})_{i}\Big]\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty.

This follows from the dominated convergence theorem, provided that we show that

\mathcal{M}_{2}:=(n!)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{j},X_{j})<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

For this, we proceed as in the proof of Proposition
3.4
. By sub-additivity,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}^{p/2}\leq(n!)^{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}\phi^{-p}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{j},X_{j})=:\mathcal{M}_{p}.

So, it is enough to prove that
\mathcal{M}_{p}<\infty
a.s. Note that
\mathcal{M}
is a measurable function of independent random elements
X=\{(\Gamma_{i})_{i},T_{j},X_{i}\}
and
Y=\{(T_{i})_{i\not=j},(X_{i})_{i\not=j}\}
. Using Lemma
B.1
, it suffices to prove that

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{M}_{p}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},T_{j},X_{j}\big]<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Using the basic conditioning fact (
54
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{M}_{p}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i}=(\gamma_{i})_{i},T_{j}=t_{j},X_{j}=x_{j}\big]

\displaystyle\quad=(n!)^{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi^{-1}(X_{j_{i}})\widetilde{f}_{n}^{p}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},t_{j},x_{j})\Big]

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}\cdot{\frac{n!}{T^{n}}}K_{n}^{(p)}(t_{j},x_{j}),

using Lemma
3.5
for the last line. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{M}_{p}\,\big|(\Gamma_{i})_{i},T_{j},X_{j}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{T^{n}}K_{n}^{(p)}(T_{j},X_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-p/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{T^{n}}K_{n}^{(p)}(T_{j},X_{i})\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\right)^{n}<\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Finally, we prove (
88
).
We denote by
\overline{f}_{n}^{(2)}(\cdot,t,x)
the symmetrization of
f_{n}^{(2)}(\cdot,t,x)
. Note that

\widetilde{f}_{n}^{2}(\cdot,t,x)\leq\frac{1}{n!}\overline{f}_{n}^{(2)}(\cdot,t,x)
. Therefore,

h_{n}^{(2)}(T_{j},X_{j})\leq T^{\frac{2n}{\alpha}}n!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})\overline{f}_{n}^{(2)}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n}},X_{j_{n}},T_{j},X_{j})

and

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\leq T^{\frac{2n}{\alpha}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j})G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n}\\
j\not\in\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\pi\in\Sigma_{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})f_{n}^{2}(T_{j_{\pi(1)}},X_{j_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,T_{j_{\pi(n)}},X_{j_{\pi(n)}},T_{j},X_{j})

\displaystyle=T^{\frac{2n}{\alpha}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j})G_{t-T_{j}}^{2}(x-X_{j})

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\geq 1\ {\rm distinct}\\

. Denoting

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\leq T^{\frac{2n}{\alpha}}\sum_{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{n+1}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\Gamma_{j_{i}}^{-2/\alpha}\phi^{-2}(X_{j_{i}})f_{n+1}^{2}(T_{j_{1}},X_{j_{1}},\ldots,T_{j_{n+1}},X_{j_{n+1}},t,x)

Lemma 4.7
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that Hypothesis
1.1
holds. If Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
hold (with possibly different values

(92)

Proof.

Step 1.
Denote

. In this step, we show that:

(93)

By Proposition
3.6

a.s., and so,

\displaystyle v_{n}^{(t,x)}(s,y)-v^{(t,x)}(s,y)

\displaystyle=G_{t-s}(x-y)\big(u_{n}(s,y)-u(s,y)\big)

\displaystyle=G_{t-s}(x-y)\sum_{k\geq n+1}I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,s,y))\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Notice that we have the following natural dominator:

|v_{n}^{(t,x)}(s,y)|\leq G_{t-s}(x-y)\sum_{k=1}^{n}|I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,s,y))|\leq G_{t-s}(x-y)\sum_{n\geq 1}|I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,s,y))|=:\overline{v}^{(t,x)}(s,y).

33

Hence,

\|\overline{v}^{(t,x)}\|_{L^{\alpha}([0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\left\|\sum_{n\geq 1}G_{t-*}(x-*)I_{n}\big(f_{n}(\cdot,*)\big)\right\|_{L^{\alpha}([0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}|\mathcal{I}_{n}(t,x)|^{\frac{1}{\alpha\vee 1}}.

By Theorem

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem

6.1
Heat equation

In this section, we show that Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
are satisfied
in the case of the heat equation.

We will use the following property of the heat kernel:

G_{t}^{p}(x)=\overline{K}_{p,d}\,t^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}G_{t/p}(x),\quad\mbox{with}\quad\overline{K}_{p,d}=(2\pi)^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}p^{-\frac{d}{2}}.

(95)

The following result is essentially contained in the proof of Theorem 3.1 of
[
13
]
. We include its proof since we need the explicit form of the constant
C_{\eta,p,d}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
G
is the fundamental solution of the heat equation,
then for any
\eta>0
and
0<p<1+\frac{2}{d}
,

\displaystyle I_{\eta,p}^{\rm heat}(t,x):=\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})(1+|x_{k}|^{\eta})d\boldsymbol{x}d\boldsymbol{t}\leq

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad C_{\eta,p,d}^{n}\left\{1+|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\right)\right\}\frac{t^{n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1)}}{\Gamma(n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1)+1)},

where
t_{n+1}=t
,
x_{n+1}=x
and

C_{\eta,p,d}=3\overline{K}_{p,d}(2^{\eta-1}\vee 1)d^{\eta}\Big[\Big(\frac{2}{p}\Big)^{\eta/2}\vee 1\Big]\Gamma\Big(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1\Big).

(96)

Proof.

Let
h(x)=1+|x|^{\eta}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By the generalized Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})h(x_{k})d\boldsymbol{x}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})h(x_{i})^{n}d\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k})h(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}x_{j})^{n}d\boldsymbol{x}\right)^{1/n}.

(97)

We estimate separately each of the integrals appearing in the product above. Using relation (
95
), we write

\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k})=\overline{K}_{p,d}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}f_{X_{k}}(x_{k}),

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
are independent random variables,
X_{k}\sim N_{d}(0,\frac{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}{p}I_{d})
, and
f_{X_{k}}
is the density of
X_{k}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k})h(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}x_{j})^{n}d\boldsymbol{x}=\overline{K}_{p,d}^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[h\big(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}\big)^{n}\Big].

Note that
h(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j})\leq(2^{\eta-1}\vee 1)(1+|x|^{\eta}+|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}|^{\eta})
, and hence

\mathbb{E}\Big[h\big(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}\big)^{n}\Big]\leq(2^{\eta-1}\vee 1)^{n}3^{n-1}\Big(1+|x|^{n\eta}+\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}\Big|^{n\eta}\Big).

Moreover,
|x|=\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}|x^{(\ell)}|^{2}}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}|x^{(\ell)}|
, for any

x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(d)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}\Big|^{n\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}^{(\ell)}\Big|\Big)^{n\eta}\Big]\leq d^{n\eta-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}^{(\ell)}\Big|^{n\eta}

\displaystyle\leq d^{n\eta}\max_{\ell=1,\ldots,d}\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}^{(\ell)}\Big|^{n\eta}.

Putting together these estimates, we infer that, for any
i=1,\ldots,n
fixed,

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k})h(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}x_{j})^{n}d\boldsymbol{x}\leq

(98)

\displaystyle\quad\Big(3\overline{K}_{p,d}(2^{\eta-1}\vee 1)d^{\eta}\Big)^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}\Big(1+|x|^{n\eta}+\max_{\ell=1,\ldots,d}\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}^{(\ell)}\Big|^{n\eta}\Big).

We will show that this can be bounded by a quantity not depending on
i
. Note that

\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}^{(\ell)}\sim N(0,\gamma_{i}),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\gamma_{i}:=\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}{p}=\frac{t-t_{i}}{p}.

Recall that if
Z\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
, then
\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}=(2\sigma^{2})^{p/2}\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{1+p}{2})
for any
p>0
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}\Big|\sum_{j=i}^{n}X_{j}^{(\ell)}\Big|^{n\eta}=(2\gamma_{i})^{n\eta/2}\pi^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\right)\leq\left(\frac{2t}{p}\right)^{n\eta/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\right).

Using this estimate in (
98
), we obtain the following bound, which does not depend on
i
:

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}G_{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}^{p}(x_{k})h(x-\sum_{j=i}^{n}x_{j})^{n}d\boldsymbol{x}\leq

\displaystyle\quad(C_{\eta,p,d}^{\prime})^{n}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}\left(1+|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2}\Gamma\Big(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\Big)\right),

where
C_{\eta,p,d}^{\prime}:=3\overline{K}_{p,d}(2^{\eta-1}\vee 1)d^{\eta}\big(\big(\frac{2}{p}\big)^{\eta/2}\vee 1\big)
.

Returning to (
97
), and integrating on
T_{n}(t)
we get:

\displaystyle I_{\eta,p}^{\rm heat}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\eta,p,d}^{\prime})^{n}\left(1+|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2}\Gamma\Big(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\Big)\right)\int_{T_{n}(t)}\prod_{k=1}^{n}(t_{k+1}-t_{k})^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}d\boldsymbol{t}.

The last integral is equal to
\frac{\Gamma(a+1)^{n}t^{n(a+1)}}{\Gamma(n(a+1)+1)}
, with
a:=\frac{d(1-p)}{2}>-1
. The conclusion follows.

∎

The following result gives an estimate for
K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)
in the case of the heat equation.

.

\phi
satisfies Hypothesis

.

(99)

To find upper and lower bounds for the Gamma functions appearing in the above estimate, we use Stirling’s formula. For any

,

\Gamma(an+1)\sim a^{an+1/2}(2\pi n)^{(1-a)/2}(n!)^{a}
, and hence

C_{a}^{-n}(n!)^{a}\leq\Gamma(an+1)\leq C_{a}^{n}(n!)^{a}\quad\mbox{for all $n\geq 1$},

where

(101)

where
C_{a,b}>1
is a constant depending on
a
and

.

The following result shows that Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
are satisfied in the case of the

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose that
\phi
satisfies Hypothesis
1.5
. If
\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta
is the heat operator and
\alpha<1+\frac{2}{d}
,
then (

) and (

) hold for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for any

such that

0<\delta(p-\alpha)<d(1-p)+2.

(102)

Proof.

We use the estimate for
K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)
given by Lemma
6.2
.
Recall that
\eta=\delta(p-\alpha)
and
C_{\eta,p,d}
is given by (
96
).

Step 1.
We first verify (
15
). By (
100
) and (
101
), there exist some constants
C_{d,p}>1
and
C_{\eta}>1
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\Gamma\Big(n\Big(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1\Big)+1\Big)\geq C_{d,p}^{-n}(n!)^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Gamma\Big(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\Big)\leq C_{\eta}^{n}(n!)^{\eta/2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)

\displaystyle\leq C^{n}\big(1+|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2}\big)\frac{t^{n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1)}}{(n!)^{\frac{d(1-p)-\eta}{2}+1}},

with

. It follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\Big(T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}K_{n}^{(p)}(t,x)\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\Big)^{n/2}\leq

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\sum_{n\geq 1}C^{n/2}\big(1+|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2}\big)^{1/2}\frac{T^{\frac{n}{2}(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+\frac{p}{\alpha})}}{(n!)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{d(1-p)-\eta}{2}+1)}}\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\Gamma_{j}^{-p/\alpha}\Big)^{n/2}.

The last series converges provided that

.
This proves (

).

Step 2.
Next, we verify (
16
). Using Lemma
6.2
, we have:

\displaystyle T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\leq c_{0}^{n(p-\alpha)}C_{\eta,p,d}^{n}\frac{T^{n(\frac{p}{\alpha}+\frac{d(1-p)}{2})}}{\Gamma(n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1)+1)}

We use Lemmas
C.1
and
C.2
to estimate the two integrals above. We get:

\displaystyle T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\leq c_{0}^{n(p-\alpha)}C_{\eta,p,d}^{n}\frac{T^{n(\frac{p}{\alpha}+\frac{d(1-p)}{2})}}{\Gamma(n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1)+1)}

(103)

we obtain:

\displaystyle T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\leq c_{0}^{n(p-\alpha)}C_{\eta,p,d}^{n}\frac{T^{n(\frac{p}{\alpha}+\frac{d(1-p)}{2})}}{\Gamma(n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1)+1)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad K_{\alpha,d}\left\{t^{\frac{d(1-\alpha)}{2}+1}\Big[1+C_{n\eta,\alpha,d}^{*}(|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2})\Big]+\Gamma\left(\frac{1+n\eta}{2}\right)t^{\frac{n\eta+d(1-\alpha)}{2}+1}\right\}.

Using inequalities (
100
) and (
101
), we obtain the estimates:

C_{n\eta,\alpha,d}^{*}\leq C^{n}(n!)^{\eta/2}
,

where

\displaystyle T^{(\frac{p}{\alpha}-1)n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t-s}^{\alpha}(x-y)K_{n}^{(p)}(s,y)dyds\leq c_{0}^{n(p-\alpha)}C_{\eta,p,d}^{n}\frac{T^{n(\frac{p}{\alpha}+\frac{d(1-p)}{2})}}{(n!)^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1}}C^{2n}

\displaystyle\quad=c_{0}^{n(p-\alpha)}C_{\eta,p,d}^{n}T^{n(\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+\frac{p}{\alpha})}C^{2n}K_{\alpha,d}\left\{\frac{t^{\frac{d(1-\alpha)}{2}+1}}{(n!)^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}+1}}+\frac{|x|^{n\eta}+t^{n\eta/2}+t^{\frac{n\eta+d(1-\alpha)}{2}+1}}{(n!)^{\frac{d(1-p)-\eta}{2}+1}}\right\}.

Using this estimate, it is not difficult to see that condition (

(due to condition (

∎

Appendix A
The stochastic integral with respect to
\widehat{N}

In this section, we recall some key components of the integration theory with respect to the compensated process
\widehat{N}
.

Let
N
be a PRM on the space
U=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}_{0}
of intensity
dtdx\nu(dz)
, where
\nu
is a Lévy measure on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\nu(\{0\})=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|z|^{2}\wedge 1)\nu(dz)<\infty
. For any Borel set
F
in
U
with
\mu(F)<\infty
, we let
\widehat{N}(F)=N(F)-\mu(F)
.

The stochastic integral with respect to the compensated process
\widehat{N}
is defined similarly to the Itô integral, as explained for instance in Chapter 4 of
[
1
]
.
More precisely, for any
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{0})
-measurable process
H
with
\mathbb{E}\int_{U}H^{2}d\mu<\infty
, the stochastic integral

I^{\widehat{N}}(H)=\int_{U}Xd\widehat{N}
is a zero mean random variable with

\mathbb{E}|I^{\widehat{N}}(H)|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\int_{U}X^{2}d\mu
, and
\{M_{t}=I^{\widehat{N}}(1_{[0,t]}H);t\geq 0\}
is a square-integrable martingale. The definition of the integral can be extended to processes
H
satisfying
\int_{U}|H|^{2}d\mu<\infty
a.s., and in this case
M
is a local martingale, which satisfies:

\mathbb{P}(|M_{t}|>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}H^{2}(s,x,z)\mu(dz,dx,dz)>\eta\right)

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
.
The process
M
has a càdlàg modification (denoted also by
M
), whose jump at time
s
is given by

\Delta M_{s}=\sum_{i\geq 1}H(T_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})1_{\{T_{i}=s\}}\quad\mbox{where $N=\sum_{i\geq 1}\delta_{(T_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})}$}.

By Lemma I.4.51 of
[
19
]
, the quadratic variation of
M
is

[M]_{t}=\sum_{s\in[0,t]}(\Delta M_{s})^{2}=\sum_{i\geq 1}H^{2}(T_{i},X_{i},Z_{i})1_{\{T_{i}\leq s\}}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}H^{2}(s,x,z)N(ds,dx,dz).

By the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality for càdlàg local martingales,

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{s\leq\tau}M_{s}^{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}[M]_{\tau},

for any stopping time
\tau
, i.e. the process
M_{t}^{*}=\sup_{s\leq t}M_{s}^{2}
is
L
-dominated by
[M]
(in the sense of Definition I.3.29 of
[
19
]
. By Lenglart’s inequality (Lemma I.3.30 of
[
19
]
),

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{s\leq t}|M_{s}|>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}H^{2}(s,x,z)N(dz,dx,dz)>\eta\right),

for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\eta>0
.

Appendix B
An application of Fubini’s theorem

In this section, we include an application of Fubini’s theorem which is used frequently in the article.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be independent random variables with values in measurable spaces
(E,\mathcal{E})
, respectively
(F,\mathcal{F})
, and
f:E\times F\to[0,\infty]
be a measurable function. Let
\mathbb{P}_{X}
be the law of
X
. If
f(x,Y)<\infty
a.s. for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-almost all
x\in E
, then
f(X,Y)<\infty
a.s. In particular, if
\mathbb{E}[f(x,Y)]<\infty
for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-almost all
x\in E
, then
f(X,Y)<\infty
a.s.

Proof.

We know that
\mathbb{P}(f(x,Y)<\infty)=1
for all
x\in N^{c}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{X}(N)=0
. By Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(f(X,Y)=\infty)

\displaystyle=\int_{E}\int_{F}1_{\{f(x,y)=\infty\}}\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\mathbb{P}_{Y}(dy)=\int_{N^{c}}\left(\int_{F}1_{\{f(x,y)=\infty\}}\mathbb{P}_{Y}(dy)\right)\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{N^{c}}\mathbb{P}(f(x,Y)=\infty)\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)=0.

∎

Remark B.2
.

Note that
h(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(x,Y)]<\infty
for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-almost all
x\in E
is equivalent to
h(X)<\infty
a.s. On the other hand,
h(X)=\mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)|X]
a.s., since
X
and
Y
are independent. So the criterion given by Lemma
B.1
can be stated as follows: for independent random variables
X
and
Y
,

if
\mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)|X]<\infty
a.s., then
f(X,Y)<\infty
a.s.

Here we use a generalized definition of the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[Z|\mathcal{G}]
of a random variable
Z
given a
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{G}
, for which
Z
does not have to be integrable.

(107)

Proof.

Let
X
be a random vector with a
N_{d}(0,(t/p)I_{d})

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}^{p}(x-y)|y|^{\gamma}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t}^{p}(y)|x-y|^{\gamma}dy=\overline{K}_{p,d}\,t^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G_{t/p}(y)|x-y|^{\gamma}dy

a
