Organoids: generation strategies, applications, and future challenges
Authors: Hao Yin, Guo-Qiang Zhu, Hai-Zhan Wang, Bin-Bin Yang, Zhen-Xing Wang, Hui Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organoids are microscopic 3D structures that resemble real organs, assembled in vitro from stem cells. Since the Dutch team of Hans Clevers successfully cultured intestinal stem cells to generate intestinal structures in 2009, organoid technology has developed rapidly, and culture protocols covering various organs such as the brain, liver, intestine, kidney, and bone have been established. These micro-organ models retain the cellular heterogeneity, tissue-specific structure, and genetic background of the original tissue. Compared to traditional two-dimensional culture, they provide a more physiologically relevant research platform. This article reviews organoid generation strategies, mainly dividing them into scaffold-free and scaffolded methods, and details specific generation protocols for brain, liver, intestine, kidney, and bone organoids. Furthermore, this article emphasizes innovations in bioengineering, such as organ-on-a-chip systems and 3D bioprinting technology. These technologies can enhance the maturity, vascularization, and reproducibility of organoids. The article also extensively discusses the biomedical applications of organoids, including in vitro disease models, infectious diseases, cancer, drug screening and toxicity testing, tissue engineering, and age-related diseases. Despite the significant potential of organoids, some challenges remain. Issues such as standardization, limited vascularization, ethical considerations, and scalability for industrial and clinical translation remain. Looking ahead, interdisciplinary efforts integrating stem cell biology, bioengineering, and computational methods promise to drive the development of organoid technology. This will make it a more robust and physiologically consistent model. Organoids hold the potential to become a cornerstone tool in biomedical research, bridging the gap between in vitro research and the clinical application of personalized medicine, drug development, and regenerative therapies.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Organoids are self-organizing three-dimensional (3D) multicellular structures derived from stem cells or tissue-resident progenitor cells. They can reproduce key structural and functional features of natural organs in vitro [1, 2]. These miniature organ models can serve as powerful research platforms. They have attracted widespread attention in the study of human development, disease modeling, drug screening, personalized medicine, and even in advancing regenerative therapies and exploring age-related biological processes [3–6].

In 2009, the Dutch team led by Hans Clevers pioneered the discovery that embedding a single adult intestinal stem cell into a three-dimensional extracellular matrix (ECM) could generate intestinal-like organoids with crypt-villous structures [7]. Since then, organoid technology has developed rapidly. Currently, various human organ generation schemes have been established, including brain [8], liver [9], intestine [10], kidney [5], lung [11], stomach [12], and pancreas [13], which utilize pluripotent stem cells (embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells) or adult tissue-specific stem cells [14]. Notably, organoids retain key cellular subtypes, tissue-specific structures, and genetic stability of their source tissues. Compared to traditional two-dimensional (2D) culture, they enable the generation of more physiologically relevant and patient-specific models [15, 16]. Therefore, organoids have become a cornerstone of in vitro human modeling [17–20]. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of organoid generation methods, their biomedical applications (including their emerging role in aging research), current limitations, and future directions in the rapidly evolving field of organoid research.

Organoid generation strategies

Organoid culture strategies are mainly divided into two categories: scaffold-free methods and scaffold-based methods (methods based on microcavity/micropore arrays combine physical constraints with self-organizing characteristics and can be regarded as a transitional strategy between the two) [21, 22] (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Scaffold-free methods

In scaffold-free systems, cells can be induced to aggregate and self-organize without the need for exogenous matrix. This is typically achieved by culturing cells under low-adhesion conditions, such as ultra-low-adhesion culture plates, suspension bioreactors, or hanging drop cultures [23, 24]. Under these conditions, cells secrete their own extracellular matrix (ECM), supporting three-dimensional tissue formation. Simultaneously, it can more closely mimic the natural microenvironment [25–27]. These systems offer numerous advantages, including ease of operation, no need for exogenous biomaterials, and reduced risk of immune responses or matrix-derived artifacts. Furthermore, scaffold-free methods are compatible with high-throughput spheroid production and can yield dense cell aggregates [28].

However, due to the lack of structural constraints, scaffold-free organoids often exhibit heterogeneity in size and shape [29]. They may also lack the spatial organization required to form complex organoid structures. In addition, larger organoids are prone to central necrosis because the transport of nutrients and oxygen depends entirely on passive diffusion.

Microcavity/microwell array-based methods

Based on the inherent self-organizing principle of scaffold-free methods, microcavity or micropore array platforms have emerged as a transformative “intermediate” strategy. They introduce artificial physical constraints to standardize the aggregation process. This technology effectively bridges the gap between the spontaneous self-assembly of scaffold-free methods and the guided morphogenesis provided by scaffolds. The core principle of this method is the use of microfabrication arrays containing hundreds to thousands of uniform, non-adhesive micropores. This method forces cells within each pore to aggregate, thereby forming a three-dimensional sphere or organoid precursor in each pore. The process yields highly uniform aggregates with controllable size and cell number. It directly addresses the batch-to-batch variation problem of traditional droplet covering or pendant drop methods. As a hybrid technology, it offers significant advantages in terms of high throughput and standardization [21]. It also provides precise size control, enabling researchers to customize models for specific applications [30]. Furthermore, it offers versatility and integration, further guiding organoid development and maturation [21]. By combining the biological principles of self-organization with precise engineering, the micropore array approach represents a key step towards reproducible, scalable, and quantitative organoid research.

Scaffold-based methods

In contrast, scaffold-based approaches involve embedding cells in three-dimensional hydrogels or extracellular matrix (ECM) mimicry materials. These materials provide mechanical support and biochemical signaling. The most widely used scaffold is Matrigel. It is a basement membrane extract derived from mouse tumors that provides adhesion ligands and growth-promoting signals, thereby promoting organoid morphogenesis [31, 32]. For example, when cultured in Matrigel containing specific growth factors, intestinal crypt-derived organoids can form well-polarized crypt-villi structures [10].

Scaffold-based systems better reproduce in vivo tissue structures and support long-term maintenance and passage [33, 34]. However, the dependence on Matrigel faces translational barriers due to its xenogeneic origin, uncertain composition, and batch-to-batch variability. Implantation of scaffold organoids may trigger an immune response. Therefore, biodegradable or synthetic matrices are often required [29]. To overcome these limitations, researchers are developing fully structured, chemically tunable hydrogels and decellularized extracellular matrix (ECM) scaffolds to create more reproducible and clinically translatable microenvironments [35]. In addition to hydrogels, other important categories of scaffold-based strategies are included. These categories support organoid generation through unique physical structures and biochemical properties. Synthetic polymer fiber scaffolds are typically fabricated using techniques such as electrospinning. They are made from biodegradable materials such as polycaprolactone (PCL) or polylactic-glycolic acid copolymer (PLGA). They mimic the fibrous morphology of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM), providing well-defined mechanical support and porosity, thereby facilitating cell migration and nutrient diffusion. These scaffolds have potential applications in tissue models that require specific mechanical strength or guided growth, such as muscle or nerve [36, 37]. Decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) scaffolds are derived from natural tissues or organs, with cellular components removed through physical and chemical methods. They retain organ-specific ECM composition, three-dimensional ultrastructure, and bioactive signals. These scaffolds can maximally reproduce the complex biochemical and physical signals of the natural microenvironment, providing a highly biomimetic “blueprint” for organoid development. They are particularly suitable for constructing organ models that require precise replication of complex in vivo structures, such as the liver or heart [38–40]. These non-hydrogel scaffolds expand the range of material choices for organoid culture, offering more options for addressing specific tissue engineering challenges (Table 1).

Ectoderm-derived organoids

Brain organoids derived from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) can reproduce certain aspects of early brain development. They can also self-organize into layered neural structures similar to the fetal cortex [44]. It is well known that hPSCs can spontaneously focus to form embryoids under suspension culture conditions, which can then be further directed to differentiate into neurons.

Supported by extracellular matrix (ECM) droplets, neuroectodermal buds can expand into three-dimensional neuroepithelial cysts. Finally, they can be cultured into brain organoids under suspension culture conditions. These brain organoids can develop radial glial layers, intermediate zones, and cortical plates containing neurons. It is similar to the developing brain in vivo. It provides a new perspective for studying neurodevelopmental processes and diseases unique to humans. For example, by culturing organoids derived from patients carrying gene mutations or infected with Zika virus, it is possible to better understand the areas where cortical growth is impaired. This allows for better simulation of microcephaly and the conduct of such studies [45, 46]. Recently, a type of organoid called assembloids has also been introduced. These are organoids that fuse different brain regions (or brain tissue with other tissues, such as the spinal cord or muscles). It can be used to study neural circuit connections and brain-body interactions [47]. Despite significant successes in brain organoid research, brain organoids remain relatively immature (usually stalled in the mid-fetal development stage). Furthermore, they lack key cell types such as microglia or blood vessels. Therefore, ongoing efforts are being made to extend their maturation period and increase their complexity [29, 45, 48].

Endoderm-derived organoids

Liver organoids can demonstrate the liver’s strong regenerative capacity. They also exhibit a dual lineage structure composed of hepatocytes and bile duct cells [49]. Liver organoids can be generated from adult liver stem/progenitor cells. For example, bile duct cell organoids derived from bile duct fragments and retaining the potential to differentiate into hepatocytes. They can also be differentiated from hPSCs [50]. Studies have shown that adult Lgr5⁺ stem cells isolated from human livers can form liver organoids when cultured in a three-dimensional matrix supplemented with specific cytokines such as Wnt, R-spondin, EGF, FGF, and Noggin [51]. These liver organoids typically form bile duct-like cystic structures or hepatocyte-like clusters. They can perform liver-specific functions such as albumin secretion and drug metabolism [52, 53]. To obtain more mature liver organoids, hPSC-induced liver organoids often need to be co-cultured with multiple cell types [9, 54]. For example, combining endoderm progenitor cells with endothelial cells and mesenchymal cells can generate vascularized liver organoids. Simultaneously, they possess the ability to develop into mature liver bud organoids after transplantation. Liver organoids have been successfully used to mimic hereditary liver diseases (such as α-1 antitrypsin deficiency and various metabolic disorders) and infectious liver diseases (such as hepatitis virus infection) [55, 56]. However, completely reproducing the complex structure of the adult liver (characterized by ordered lobules and a functional vascular network) remains an ongoing challenge [56, 57].

The gut was the first tissue to be successfully used to construct organoids. Since then, intestinal organoid technology has been highly optimized [41]. Researchers can use intestinal crypt stem cells or hPSCs to generate small intestinal and colonic organoids. These organoids contain crypt-like structures containing all major differentiated epithelial cell types, including enterocytes, goblet cells, Paneth cells, and enteroendocrine cells [58, 59]. Adult stem cell-derived intestinal organoids are typically cultured in Matrigel supplemented with essential growth factors such as Wnt agonists, R-spondin, and BMP inhibitors to maintain the stem cell microenvironment [41]. These organoids are highly stable. They can expand indefinitely. At the same time, they can retain the complex characteristics of intestinal epithelial mucus secretion and nutrient absorption [60]. Intestinal organoids have shown invaluable potential in mimicking hereditary diseases such as cystic fibrosis and intestinal pathogen infections. Furthermore, they can be co-cultured with components of the intestinal microenvironment, including symbiotic bacteria or immune cells, for studying host-microbe interactions. An inherent limitation of intestinal organoids is their formation as closed cystic structures. This restricts direct contact with the luminal side of the intestine [61–63]. To overcome this limitation, microengineering techniques have been employed. Intestinal on-chip organoid systems with perfusion capabilities or laser-assisted organoid anatomy techniques have been developed to mimic physiological processes. These systems enable peristalsis or directional flow of nutrients across the epithelial barrier [64–66].

Mesoderm-derived organoids

Kidney organoids derived from hPSCs have become important in vitro models for studying kidney development and kidney disease. Through directed differentiation protocols, hPSCs can differentiate to the mesodermal stage, generating cell clusters containing nephron progenitor cells. After culturing for approximately 20–30 days, these cells can self-organize into nephron-like structures composed of glomerular podocytes, proximal tubules, and distal tubules [67]. Morphologically, these kidney organoids are similar in size and developmental stage to early embryonic kidneys [5, 68]. They exhibit early kidney function, including expression of podocyte slit membrane proteins and limited filtration capacity. Despite these preliminary functions, their main limitation is the lack of a functional vascular network (avascularity). This limits their full maturation and organ-level physiological functions [69]. Although some endothelial cells may emerge during differentiation, they are unable to form well-perfused capillary networks. To address this angiogenesis defect, various strategies are currently being actively investigated, such as transplanting kidney organoids into animal models to induce host-derived blood vessel ingrowth. And the use of bioengineering methods, such as perfusion-based culture systems and pre-modeling of vascular lineage cells [70–72]. Despite these challenges, kidney organoids remain an important platform for studying human kidney development. They have been effectively used to mimic inherited kidney diseases such as polycystic kidney disease and to screen nephrotoxic compounds. Overall, they have great potential in mimicking kidney injury and regeneration [73, 74].

Bone organoids are three-dimensional models of bone tissue derived from stem cells (usually mesenchymal stem cells). They can reproduce key structural and functional features of natural bone [75, 76]. Advanced biomanufacturing technologies, such as 3D bioprinting, have been used to construct bone organoids. The principle is to precisely assemble cells in a supporting matrix to generate physiologically relevant microbone structures [75, 77]. These bone organoids can develop into mineralized extracellular matrix and bone-specific cell types (osteoblasts and embedded osteocytes). It provides a novel in vitro platform for bone biology research and regenerative medicine applications [76, 78, 79]. Mechanical stimulation is a key factor in the maturation of bone organoids. Bone is a mechanically sensitive tissue. Studies have shown that long-term cyclic mechanical loading of 3D bioprinted bone organoids can significantly improve their matrix mineral density, stiffness, and osteogenic differentiation capacity, including the formation of lacunae-canal networks containing osteocytes [77, 80]. Introducing this dynamic mechanical signal allows for the acquisition of bone organoids that more closely resemble the structure and function of mature bone tissue. Therefore, these mechanically mature bone organoids can serve as human-specific models for studying bone pathophysiology and testing bone-targeted therapies, thereby reducing reliance on animal models [18, 75, 81].

The emergence of bone organoid models has made it possible to study bone diseases in vitro where human-specific models were previously lacking. For example, osteoporotic bone organoids can be generated by enhancing osteoclast activity and simulating reduced mechanical loads, thus reproducing the characteristic bone loss and fragility of osteoporosis [82, 83]. These lab-cultured microskeletons can capture key features of age-related bone degeneration. This provides a platform for screening anti-osteoporosis drugs and studying disease mechanisms under controlled conditions [84, 85]. Similarly, bone tumor organoids (derived from primary bone cancers, such as osteosarcoma) retain the heterogeneous microenvironment of the original tumor. This allows researchers to study the progression of bone tumors in vitro and test targeted therapies. Therefore, the emergence of bone organoids has extended organoid-based disease modeling to the field of orthopedics, complementing traditional models and providing new insights into bone pathology [86–89].

In the field of orthopedic regenerative medicine, bone organoids are being explored as building blocks for repairing bone injuries and defects. Researchers have constructed “callus organoids” that mimic the transition of cartilage to bone during fracture healing. When implanted into larger bone defects, these organoids significantly accelerated new bone formation and improved healing outcomes [76, 80]. Similarly, osteogenic organoids tailored to specific patients have been developed to treat some challenging conditions, such as nonunion (e.g., congenital tibial pseudoarthrosis) and have successfully induced bone regeneration in cases where standard therapies have failed [80]. These pioneering studies demonstrate that bone organoid implants can actively promote bone repair. This strategy shows great promise for promoting fracture defect healing. In addition to direct tissue repair, bone organoids also provide a valuable in vitro platform for evaluating bone graft and implant materials before clinical application. Traditionally, the biocompatibility and efficacy of novel bone implants have been assessed in animal models. However, species differences can limit the predictive accuracy of such tests. In contrast, human bone organoids can be used to test implant materials under conditions that mimic humanized bone. This allows researchers to observe bone formation, biomaterial integration, and potential toxicity in a controlled environment [76, 80]. This approach enables more accurate preclinical evaluation of orthopedic implants while reducing reliance on animal testing, potentially simplifying the development of safer and more effective bone regeneration therapies. Future bone organoid models will integrate key features of natural bone, such as perfusable vascular networks, an active bone marrow microenvironment with hematopoietic function, and biomechanically responsive structures, to more accurately simulate the functional complexity of real bone tissue [75, 76, 90, 91]. Achieving these advances will enable bone organoids to support hematopoietic stem cells and undergo continuous remodeling, thereby greatly expanding their applications in basic bone biology research and the development of next-generation bone regeneration therapies [90, 91].

Bioengineering innovations: organoids-on-a-chip and bioprinting

To overcome the limitations of traditional organoid culture, researchers are increasingly integrating bioengineering methods, particularly through organoid-on-a-chip platforms [93]. These systems integrate microfluidic devices to provide continuous perfusion, enabling controlled delivery of nutrients and removal of metabolic waste. This approach more effectively mimics the dynamic in vivo environment compared to static culture [29, 94]. Microfluidic perfusion also helps alleviate core hypoxia, prolonging organoid survival and promoting the formation of more complex tissue structures. For example, brain and midbrain organoids cultured under flow conditions showed reduced necrosis and enhanced neural area development compared to static or shaker culture. In addition to improving nutrient supply, organoid-on-a-chip systems can also simulate mechanical signals crucial for epithelial maturation, such as fluid shear stress, pulsating pressure, or tissue stretching. This also includes applying periodic luminal flow to intestinal organoids to promote villus formation and digestive enzyme expression [95, 96] (Fig. 3).

A key advantage of microengineering systems is their ability to integrate multiple organoid types into interconnected chambers, forming multi-organoid circuits. These platforms enable the study of inter-organ communication. Such as immune-tumor interactions or liver-cardiac drug metabolism cascades. Co-culturing liver and heart organoids in a shared microfluidic loop allows the liver to metabolize candidate compounds while simultaneously monitoring cardiotoxicity in real time. Thus, multi-organoid platforms provide a more physiologically accurate systems pharmacology model. They have been used to study the pathogenesis of complex diseases and drug off-target effects [97].

Beyond microfluidics, 3D bioprinting and hydrogel engineering have further improved the structural fidelity of organoids. Bioprinting enables the precise deposition of cells and biomaterials into predetermined structures, facilitating the generation of spatially ordered organoids. For example, vascularized organoids with perfusion vascular networks have been successfully constructed by integrating stem cells and endothelial cells into lattice scaffolds through bioprinting. Furthermore, spatial patterning strategies—such as bioprinting vascular cells around the core of kidney tissue—have been successfully used to guide the self-organization of organoids. Simultaneously, advanced hydrogels are being developed to improve the reproducibility and functional maturity of organoids. For instance, synthetic polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based hydrogels with tunable stiffness, biodegradability, and biochemical ligands allow for precise control over the morphogenesis and recycling of organoids. Additionally, organ-specific decellularized extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogels derived from lung or liver tissue provide natural biochemical signals, further promoting organoid phenotypes that more closely resemble in vivo tissues. Overall, these bioengineering innovations, including microfluidic perfusion systems, multi-organ co-culture platforms, bioprinting technologies, and next-generation matrices, are driving the development of more mature, vascularized, and scalable organoid models. Organoids cultured in these systems exhibit enhanced functionality and reproducibility. They also provide standardized platforms. This is crucial for transitioning organoid research from academic exploration to clinical translation and drug development processes [98–100]. These improvements are attributed to a variety of engineering strategies, including the use of specific scaffold materials, biomechanical forces, micropatterning techniques, and 3D bioprinting, all of which are summarized in Fig. 4.

Applications of organoids in biomedicine

As shown in Fig. 5, organoids have been applied in multiple biomedical fields, including disease modeling, drug screening, infectious disease research, and spatial transcriptomics. Organoids have rapidly become an indispensable tool in modern biomedical research. The structure and function of human organs can be reproduced through in vitro experiments. Organoids have become an important bridge between traditional cell lines and animal models. They provide a more physiologically relevant platform for studying disease mechanisms, treatment responses, and tissue regeneration. Disease modeling in a dish

Due to species differences, many human diseases are difficult to replicate in animal models. Organoids are of particular value in mimicking these diseases. Patient-derived organoids, generated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or primary tissues, retain the unique genetic background and pathological features of the donor, thus enabling mechanistic studies under controlled in vitro conditions [109]. Organoids have been shown to play an important role in mimicking single-gene diseases affecting organ function. Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a prime example, caused by mutations in the CFTR gene. Intestinal organoids obtained from rectal biopsies of CF patients can be used for swelling assays. They can assess the response of CFTR channel activity to forskolin (cAMP stimulation) [110, 111]. This functional readout has been used to predict specific patient responses to CFTR modulators and to guide personalized treatment decisions. Similarly, kidney organoids carrying PKD1 or PKD2 mutations form fluid-filled cysts, providing an in vitro model of polycystic kidney disease (PKD) suitable for high-throughput drug screening [110, 112]. Brain organoids carrying mutations associated with microcephaly (such as CDK5RAP2 [29]) exhibit reduced proliferation of neural progenitor cells and reduced organoid volume, thus mimicking the key phenotype of the disease [42]. These disease-specific organoids can be used for mechanistic studies, such as transcriptome analysis, live-cell imaging, and drug response testing. However, these studies are difficult to perform in human patients and are generally difficult to replicate in traditional two-dimensional cell culture.

Infectious diseases

Organoids provide physiologically relevant models of human tissues that can be used to study host-pathogen interactions involving viruses, bacteria, and parasites. Compared to traditional immortalized cell lines, organoids can more accurately replicate tissue structures, cell diversity, and receptor expression, thereby revealing infection patterns that are typically not observable in standard in vitro systems [113].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, organoids derived from lung, airway, and intestinal tissues have been widely used to study the pathogenesis of SARS-CoV-2 [114]. Airway organoids, containing multiple respiratory epithelial cell types, have been used to map the cell tropism of the virus in the respiratory tract. They can also assess the susceptibility of different cell subtypes to emerging variants. Intestinal organoids have confirmed that SARS-CoV-2 can infect gastrointestinal epithelial cells, thus providing a mechanistic explanation for common gastrointestinal symptoms in COVID-19 patients [115]. These infected organoids can also serve as platforms for screening antiviral drugs and conducting preclinical testing of candidate therapeutics in human-related settings. In addition to SARS-CoV-2, organoids have been used to mimic infections of a variety of other human pathogens. For example, Helicobacter pylori colonization of gastric organoids has been used to study the pathogenesis of ulcers. Brain organoids infected with Zika virus reproduce selective exhaustion of neural progenitor cells, consistent with the known pathogenic mechanism by which the virus causes microcephaly in fetuses. Liver organoids have been used to study the entry, replication, and efficacy of antiviral drugs for hepatitis viruses [29, 116]. In summary, organoid-based infection models enable detailed analysis of host responses, especially when co-cultured with immune components, and provide a robust alternative to animal models. They enhance relevance to human biology while reducing reliance on animal experiments.

Cancer (tumor organoids)

Organoid technology has significantly advanced cancer research by enabling the in vitro culture of tumor tissue while maintaining its structural and genetic integrity. Patient-derived tumor organoids (also known as tumor-like organs) can be created from biopsy or surgical specimens using modified protocols. These protocols adapt standard organoid culture conditions to selectively support the growth of malignant cells. These tumor organoids retain key features of the original tumor, including histopathological structure, mutation profile, and intratumoral heterogeneity [117, 118].

Tumor organoids have been successfully constructed from various malignant tumors, such as colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, breast cancer, prostate cancer, and lung cancer. As personalized models or “living embodiments” of a patient’s cancer, they enable high-throughput drug testing, thus providing a basis for personalized treatment decisions. For example, in metastatic gastrointestinal tumors, researchers have used patient-derived organoids to screen for chemotherapy and targeted therapies. Their in vitro treatment responses are consistent with clinical outcomes [119, 120]. Furthermore, the ability of tumor organoids to preserve subclonal diversity significantly surpasses that of traditional two-dimensional cancer cell lines in the study of tumor evolution, drug resistance mechanisms, and metastasis [121].

Tumor organoids can also be co-cultured with immune components to evaluate responses to immunotherapy. They are also being incorporated into large-scale drug screening platforms to identify vulnerabilities in aggressive cancers. Organoids from genetically diverse patient cohorts can be combined with annotated clinical data to construct biobanks, which will facilitate the development of precision oncology resources [122, 123]. In conclusion, tumor organoids provide a physiologically realistic and genetically representative platform. They can be used to simulate the complexity of cancer, thereby accelerating mechanistic research and therapeutic discovery.

Drug screening and toxicity testing

Organoids are used as preclinical models to predict drug efficacy and toxicity. Their application is becoming increasingly widespread, overcoming the limitations of traditional two-dimensional cell lines and animal models in translational applications. The physiological relevance and human-specific structure of organoids make them a valuable tool for personalized medicine and large-scale drug screening. In the context of personalized treatment, patient-derived organoids support the “N = 1” testing strategy. Organoids can be constructed using tumor biopsies or rectal samples from patients. They are then exposed to a range of approved or investigational drugs to screen for effective treatments. This method has shown clinical value in colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and cystic fibrosis models. Their in vitro drug response is correlated with clinical outcomes, thus enabling precise functional therapy for each patient [124].

Beyond personalized applications, organoids can also serve as scalable high-throughput screening (HTS) platforms. Automated systems support the culture of organoids in multi-well plates or microfluidic arrays, enabling the parallel testing of thousands of compounds. Given the complexity of three-dimensional matrix culture, the development of detection methods requires innovation, such as organoid dissociation schemes and imaging-based functional assays. Meanwhile, proof-of-concept HTS studies have successfully identified neurotoxic substances in brain organoids and compounds that promote stem cell expansion in intestinal organoids [29]. By integrating organoid culture with automated liquid handling and high-content imaging techniques, the platform has supported medium-throughput screening in academic settings and has been extended to drug development to achieve higher-throughput screening capabilities. For example, membrane bioreactor platforms have been used to generate homogeneous kidney organoids for drug screening to assess nephrotoxicity and cyst formation [112].

The use of organoids in toxicology research is also becoming increasingly widespread. Many drug candidates have failed in late-stage trials due to unexpected organ-specific toxicities. Human liver organoids (including hepatocyte-cholangiocarcinocyte models) can be used to assess hepatotoxicity (such as steatosis and cholestasis). Heart organoids derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be used to detect arrhythmias and systolic dysfunction [125]. Importantly, organoids can reveal species-specific toxic effects that cannot be captured by animal models. This has prompted regulatory agencies and pharmaceutical companies to incorporate organoid systems into their “next-generation” safety screening processes. The Organoid Consortium is working to standardize experimental protocols and outcome metrics to support regulatory approval. Despite challenges in scalability and cost, organoid-based systems have demonstrated higher predictive value in detecting human-related toxicities. And it aligns with ethical initiatives to reduce animal testing under the 3R (replace, reduce, optimize) framework.

Tissue engineering and regenerative medicine

In addition to their applications in in vitro modeling, organoids are increasingly being studied as fundamental building blocks for tissue engineering and regenerative therapies. The long-term goal is to use organoids to repair or replace damaged organs. For example, transplanting liver organoids to treat liver failure or transplanting pancreatic islet organoids to treat diabetes. Although this vision is still in its early stages, several preclinical studies have shown encouraging results [126]. Organoid transplantation and implantation: Animal studies have shown that human organoids can be implanted, mature, and partially restore organ function after being transplanted into disease models. For example, transplanting human liver organoids into the livers of chronically injured mice has demonstrated that they can implant, differentiate into hepatocyte-like cells, secrete human serum albumin, and improve host liver function [127]. Similarly, implanting intestinal organoids into the intestines of damaged mice has resulted in the formation of sheets of human epithelial cells that support mucus secretion and nutrient absorption [128]. Notably, implanting human brain organoids into the brains of rodents has achieved integration with host blood vessels, extended neural development, and even partial integration into host neural circuits. This provides new insights into neural repair and integration strategies [129]. In kidney models, transplanted kidney organoids have become vascularized with the host blood vessels and have begun filtering plasma, producing small amounts of urine, although at levels not yet sufficient to sustain life [5, 130]. One study provided a compelling proof of concept by transplanting fetal-like human kidneys generated using organoid-based methods into nephrectomized rats. These transplanted kidneys performed some kidney function and prolonged the animals’ lifespan. In conclusion, these findings suggest that if organoids can be scaled up to the size and maturity of fetal or adult organs, they may one day provide a viable alternative or complement to organ transplantation in clinical practice [98].

Organoids as a technological model for studying aging

Organoid technology provides a unique and controllable three-dimensional modeling system derived from humans for aging research. This section focuses on how to utilize the technological characteristics of organoids, such as the preservation of epigenetic markers, gene editing, and microenvironment regulation, to simulate the aging process, generate age-related disease models, and evaluate anti-aging interventions.

Technical strategies for simulating aging processes

The core technological advantage of organoids lies in their ability to preserve age-related characteristics of donor tissues and to allow for artificial intervention to accelerate or slow down aging phenotypes. Organoids typically retain age-specific epigenetic characteristics of their source tissues, such as DNA methylation patterns. This keeps their “epigenetic clock” aligned with the donor’s age [131, 132]. This characteristic makes organoids a reliable technical model for studying age-related epigenetic changes in human tissues in vitro. Through gene editing (such as knocking out the WRN gene to simulate Werner syndrome) or environmental stress (such as regulating oxidative stress pathways) [133], researchers can controllably induce accelerated aging phenotypes in organoids, thereby elucidating mechanisms such as DNA damage and telomere loss. These operations highlight the value of organoids as a gene-modifiable platform in elucidating the causal relationships of aging. Standardized culture systems for organoids enable quantitative analysis of aging biomarkers (such as SA-β-galactosidase activity and p16/p21 expression) [134] and functional decline (such as decreased regenerative capacity), providing reproducible technical readouts for evaluating interventions [135].

Technical applications in modeling age-related diseases

Organoid technology integrates patient-specific cells, genetic engineering, and pathological microenvironment simulation, providing a revolutionary tool for studying age-related diseases. Brain organoids derived from patient-induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) carrying familial mutations (such as APP/PS1 mutations associated with Alzheimer’s disease and SNCA triploid mutations associated with Parkinson’s disease) can reproduce key pathological features (such as Aβ plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and α-synuclein aggregates) and their progression over “culture time” (simulating aging) in vitro [136–138]. More advanced technical strategies include exposing healthy brain organoids to patient-derived pathological protein aggregates (such as tau protein seeds) to simulate sporadic diseases [136]. Alternatively, they can be co-cultured with microglia to study neuroimmune interactions. Liver organoids can be used to construct models of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. This is achieved through exposure to fatty acids (mimicking metabolic stress) or by introducing common risk gene variants (such as PNPLA3) using CRISPR technology, thereby facilitating research on metabolic aging and screening for lipid-modulating drugs [139–141]. Similarly, by co-culturing osteoblasts with osteoclast precursors and applying mechanical unloading (mimicking microgravity), osteoporosis-like bone loss phenotypes can be induced in bone organoids. This will aid in drug testing and development [142, 143]. These examples demonstrate how organoid technology can translate complex age-related disease phenotypes into controllable in vitro experimental systems.

An in vitro platform for evaluating anti-aging interventions

The high-throughput potential and human-related properties of organoids make them an ideal technological platform for screening and evaluating anti-aging interventions. For example, treating intestinal organoids from aged donors with NAD+ precursors (such as NMN) allows for technical evaluation of their effects on intracellular NAD+ levels, stem cell activity, and aging markers, thus validating their in vitro regenerative potential [134, 144, 145]. Organoids are also used to test mTOR inhibitors (such as rapamycin) or culture conditions simulating caloric restriction to observe their activation of conserved longevity pathways (such as autophagy and AMPK/SIRT1) [146–148]. As previously mentioned, organoids can be used to test the efficacy of senescent cell scavengers such as Navitoclax or the combination of dasatinib and quercetin in clearing senescent cells [132] and improving tissue function [149–151]. Combined with automated and high-content imaging technologies, organoid platforms hold promise for high-throughput screening of large compound libraries, thereby accelerating the discovery of novel anti-aging molecules that can enhance the viability of senescent organoids. Current challenges include better simulating systemic factors of aging (such as circulating factors and immunosenescence) and maintaining the stability of the microenvironment during long-term culture. In the future, integration with multi-organ-on-a-chip technology and AI-assisted phenotypic analysis will further enhance the predictive efficacy of organoid models in aging research.

Regenerative medicine and clinical translation

Biomaterial integration for implantable constructs

To advance organoid-based therapies to clinical applications, researchers are combining organoids with biomaterial scaffolds to create implantable tissue patches or organ replacements [99]. Individual organoids are typically only millimeter in size and lack the mechanical integrity required for surgical manipulation or tissue replacement. Larger, more robust tissue structures can be created by seeding organoids onto biodegradable scaffolds or embedding them in inducible matrices, enabling them to interact with host tissues. For example, heart organoid pieces embedded in collagen scaffolds have been transplanted into infarcted mouse hearts, providing contractile cardiomyocytes and potentially secreting paracrine factors to improve cardiac function. Similarly, human lung organoids have been seeded into decellularized rodent lung matrices, preserving the structure of alveolar sacs and enabling organoid-derived cells to form airway and alveolar-like regions [152]. Bioprinting technology has further enabled the spatial localization of organoids in gel matrices that mimic organ geometry, such as printing kidney organoids into arrays that approximate kidney anatomy. Although these methods are still in the experimental stage, they represent an important step toward the goal of constructing functional organ tissues [153].

Clinical translation challenges

Despite encouraging progress, several key hurdles remain in the clinical translation of organoid-based regenerative therapies [99]. The most significant is vascularization: implanted organoids require rapid perfusion within minutes to prevent ischemic injury. This necessitates pre-vascularization of the organoids or co-transplantation with vascular grafts [154].

Immunocompatibility is also a major challenge. Autologous organoids derived from patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) exhibit ideal immunocompatibility. However, individual patient-specific preparation is costly and time-consuming. As an alternative, efforts are underway to develop universal donor cell lines. These genetically engineered cell lines are designed to evade the host’s immune response, enabling ready-to-use organoid therapies [155, 156]. Another major limitation is the scale of the required tissue. Millions of organoids may be needed to mimic the size and function of adult organs, requiring significant advancements in bioreactor technology and large-scale manufacturing processes [157]. Furthermore, ethical and regulatory issues must be carefully considered before initiating first-in-human organoid transplantation trials [158]. These issues include standardization of protocols, ensuring long-term safety, and defining efficacy endpoints. Nevertheless, combining organoids with tissue engineering remains a promising direction in regenerative medicine. Even partial restoration of tissue function, such as hormone production, metabolic activity, or barrier maintenance, can bring significant benefits to patients with end-stage organ failure. Several early-stage clinical trials using organoid-derived cell therapies are expected to begin in the near future, particularly in the areas of liver and gastrointestinal diseases [159–163].

Current challenges

Standardization and reproducibility

One of the most pressing challenges facing organoid research is the variability in culture outcomes. This variability exists not only within a single experiment but also between different laboratories [164]. Organoid formation is highly dependent on spontaneous self-organization. Even with the same source of starting cells, heterogeneous results in size, morphology, and cellular composition are often observed. Batch-to-batch variations in Matrigel components or culture medium additives further exacerbate this inconsistency. This variability undermines experimental reproducibility and complicates data comparisons between different studies, posing a significant obstacle to clinical and industrial applications [19].

To address this issue, researchers are developing chemically defined and highly controllable culture systems. Studies have shown that replacing Matrigel with fully synthetic hydrogels and implementing time-precise growth factor delivery protocols can reduce culture variability by providing consistent biochemical signals. For example, a recent study introduced UniMat [165], a membrane-based 3D culture platform capable of generating kidney organoids of uniform size and higher maturity on a large scale, thereby improving the reproducibility of assays [165]. Similarly, on-chip organoid systems offer inherent standardization advantages by applying the same flow rate, culture medium composition, and physical stimulation to all organoids in a controlled microenvironment. These platforms reduce the variability in gene expression and growth kinetics in parallel cultures. Currently, efforts are underway across various fields to establish standard operating procedures (SOPs) for organoid acquisition, quality control, and functional benchmarking [166, 167]. In 2020, the HCA|Organoid project, funded by the EU Horizon 2020, officially launched the construction of the Organoid Cell Atlas. The project has now published an integrated human neural organoid cell atlas and a human endoderm organoid cell atlas through the HCA Organoid Biology Network, providing benchmark resources for assessing organoid fidelity. In the future, the integration of bioprocessing engineering tools—such as real-time growth monitoring, automated feeding systems, and feedback-controlled bioreactors—will significantly improve the consistency of organoid production; while the further introduction of automation, robotics, and machine learning will push the reproducibility and rigor of organoid research to new heights.

Vascularization and size constraints

A major limitation of organoid systems is the lack of a vascular network. This restricts the diffusion of oxygen and nutrients. Consequently, most organoids cannot exceed 1–4 mm in diameter. Larger constructs develop necrotic cores due to diffusion limitations. This size limitation also restricts functional maturation, particularly for physiological processes that rely on vascular exchange, such as glomerular filtration in kidney organoids or gas exchange in alveolar models. Therefore, achieving vascularization is a key frontier area in organoid research [168, 169].

Several strategies to promote vascular integration are currently being investigated: (1) Endothelial cell co-culture: Co-culturing organoids with human endothelial cells, especially in the presence of angiogenic factors such as VEGF, can induce the formation of microvascular networks. After transplantation, these vascular networks can anastomose with the host vascular system—a phenomenon already demonstrated in brain and liver organoid studies—significantly improving graft survival and functional integration. (2) Fusion with vascular organoids: Pre-formed vascular organoids (self-assembled endothelial tubular structures) can be physically fused with other types of organoids, such as kidney organoids. This method enables vascular channels to infiltrate target tissues, thereby achieving perfusion. (3) Targeted angiogenesis: organoid differentiation schemes can be modified to induce vascular lineages while inducing parenchymal cells. For example, adding VEGF during brain organoid formation can drive partial differentiation into endothelial cells and parietal cells, thereby forming vascular-like structures within the organoid. (4) Organoid perfusion on a chip: Microfluidic chips simulate vascular flow by continuously perfusing culture medium. Such platforms support dynamic perfusion of nutrients, immune cells, and even erythrocytes by constructing endothelialized microchannel networks, thereby forming functional alternative vascular beds in vitro. (5) Three-dimensional bioprinting of vascular systems: Bioprinting technology can precisely place hollow channels in hydrogels. After endothelialization, organoid cells seeded in adjacent areas can directly benefit from spatial co-location with these perfusing channels. For example, bioprinted cardiac organoids have shown enhanced contractility and survival rate after being embedded in vascular meshes [170].

Despite some progress, achieving true organoid vascularization—that is, constructing a hierarchical network comprising arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules, and veins—remains a significant challenge. Current models primarily form capillary-sized vessels but lack upstream arterial and venous blood supply, likely due to a lack of hemodynamics and large-scale tissue signaling. Furthermore, tissue-engineered vessels often degenerate in the absence of physiological blood flow. Emerging solutions include utilizing bioreactors to provide pulsatile perfusion to mimic arterial blood pressure. Alternatively, in vivo maturation strategies can be employed, implanting organoids into the host microenvironment (such as the greater omentum or renal capsule) to allow the host vascular system to naturally remodel and perfuse the transplanted organ. In short, vascularization is a key bottleneck in advancing the development of functional, scalable organoids. While current organoids resemble fetal tissue, interdisciplinary innovations in tissue engineering and vascular biology are gradually bridging this gap. This brings us closer to fully vascularized, physiologically relevant organoid systems.

Ethical and regulatory considerations

Although organoids are generated in vitro, they are derived from human cells and their physiological complexity is constantly increasing. This raises significant ethical and regulatory challenges [171]. Brain organoids, in particular, have sparked intense debate. With advancements in differentiation protocols and increasing complexity of neural structures, concerns have arisen that they may possess primitive consciousness or the ability to sense harm. While most scientists believe that current brain organoids lack the sensory input and structural basis required for consciousness, their neuroethical implications are becoming increasingly urgent. Ethicists argue that if brain organoids exhibit characteristics of suggestive perception, such as EEG-like neural oscillations resembling patterns of consciousness, they should be given ethical consideration and protection rather than treated as disposable research material. Currently, there is discussion about whether thresholds should be set based on size, complexity, or electrophysiological activity to trigger specific regulatory measures. Proposed guidelines include limiting the time brain organoids can be cultured or genetically engineered to prevent them from integrating higher cognitive functions, for example, by knocking out genes associated with consciousness. Researchers are increasingly collaborating with bioethicists to establish a governance framework for brain organoid research [172]. In addition to the neurological aspects, informed consent and ownership are also critical issues. Organoids are typically derived from surgical or biopsy tissue from donors. Therefore, informed consent must cover the long-term fate of organoids, including the possibility of indefinite culture, biobank preservation, sharing, or commercial use. This raises questions about donor rights, privacy, and potential ownership. In particular, organoids that integrate immune or neural cells may retain donor-specific biological characteristics. Current consensus emphasizes the need for robust informed consent programs that clearly outline research objectives, data use, and intellectual property policies. Meanwhile, embryo-like organoids (e.g., blastocyst-like organoids that mimic early embryonic development) also raise new ethical and legal issues. Although these constructs are not embryos themselves, they may challenge existing regulatory definitions of embryo research, which is heavily restricted in many jurisdictions. Regulators are currently considering whether a new framework is needed to regulate such models. From a societal perspective, public perception will greatly influence the acceptance of organoid technology. Concerns about “playing God” or slippery slope effects (such as creating humanoid entities) highlight the need for transparency and responsible communication. Experts emphasize that organoids lack key features of complete organs (such as the vascular system and immune system) and cannot function autonomously. These are tools for research and treatment, not human prototypes. Finally, the issue of fairness cannot be ignored. If organoid-based personalized medicine proves effective, ensuring its widespread adoption is crucial to avoiding exacerbating health inequalities. The involvement of multiple stakeholders, including scientists, ethicists, policymakers, and the public, is essential. Several working groups, including the ISSCR Organoid Ethics Working Group, are developing recommendations on informed consent, biobanks, and the regulation of ethically sensitive applications.

Scaling up and industrialization

To transition organoids from experimental models to standardized tools for pharmaceutical and clinical applications, challenges related to scalability, automation, and cost must be addressed. Traditional organoid culture requires extensive manual manipulation, often involving steps such as individual embedding and culture medium changes. While suitable for research, these methods fall short for industrial applications, which demand high-throughput production of homogeneous and quality-controlled organoids for drug screening or therapeutic uses [173].

Bioreactor technology offers a scalable solution. Stirred tank and perfusion bioreactors can support large-scale cell culture and provide automated nutrient delivery and environmental control. Recent approaches have demonstrated the ability to generate hundreds to thousands of organoids in parallel. Automation has also extended to downstream processes. Robotic systems are now available that can perform organoid passage, culture medium changes, assays (such as imaging and immunostaining), and data acquisition with minimal human intervention. This reduces labor costs and improves reproducibility by minimizing operator variability. Large-scale quality control is another critical component. Industrial-scale production requires each organoid to meet established criteria, including size, viability, and marker expression. High-throughput analytical techniques such as flow cytometry and automated imaging are being used to assess batch-to-batch homogeneity. Furthermore, industry stakeholders are working to develop release criteria similar to those used in cell therapy. For example, pancreatic islet organoid products may require exhibiting a minimum insulin secretion threshold upon glucose stimulation. Upstream processes such as progenitor cell enrichment and protocol optimization are crucial to supporting this standardization. Cost factors are equally important. Culture media often contain expensive components such as Wnt agonists and recombinant growth factors, while extracellular matrices like Matrigel are also costly. To reduce costs, researchers are developing streamlined, serum-free, feeder-free culture medium formulations using small-molecule alternatives. As production scales up, bulk purchasing or in-house synthesis of reagents can further reduce costs. For drug applications, the cost per data point in organoid assays must be competitive with existing in vitro models, and they must demonstrate higher predictive value in efficacy or toxicity testing to justify their viability.

Despite current challenges, the momentum for organoid industrialization remains strong. Academic laboratories have scaled up intestinal organoid culture to hundreds of millions of cells, sufficient for transplantation research [90]. Commercial companies now offer organoid services ranging from generating patient-specific tumor organoids for personalized medicine testing to off-the-shelf “catalog” organoids (such as liver or heart organoids) for routine toxicology screening [174]. Looking ahead, pharmaceutical companies are likely to outsource organoid generation entirely, much like animal testing is often outsourced. This centralized model could increase standardization, reduce variability, and expedite regulatory approval processes. As regulators begin to develop expectations for organoid-based preclinical trials, the path to its widespread adoption in industry is becoming increasingly clear.

Outlook

Organoid technology is at the transformative intersection of stem cell biology, tissue engineering, and translational medicine. With continued technological advancements, current limitations are expected to be gradually overcome, leading to new applications and significant translational impacts in multiple fields [175]. Functional maturation is one of the major frontier areas. Future organoid models are expected to exhibit increasingly close capabilities to adult physiological characteristics. For example, brain organoids may develop more complex neural networks, mimicking the circuitry of a mature brain. Liver organoids are expected to exhibit a complete xenobiotic metabolic capacity comparable to adult liver tissue. Furthermore, integrating supporting cell types such as immune cells, endothelial cells, or matrix components into organoid systems will help construct complex “organoid ecosystems,” making them highly similar to natural tissue environments. The integration of high-resolution omics technologies, including single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, will further refine the characterization of organoids and identify their differences from in vivo tissues, thereby guiding iterative optimization of experimental protocols. In addition, machine learning methods can enhance standardization and predictive modeling capabilities, enabling accurate developmental trajectory analysis and robust quality control [176]. In the field of disease modeling, organoids will be increasingly used to study complex polygenic diseases and age-related diseases, as well as the fundamental mechanisms of aging [177]. For example, brain organoids have been used for long-term culture (in vitro “aging”) to mimic late-onset neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease. Combined with blood-brain barrier organoid models, these systems are well-suited for elucidating the underlying mechanisms of central nervous system inflammation and neurodegeneration. The emergence of co-culture systems that integrate immune cells with organoid models (called “immune organoids”) will further enhance our ability to study human-specific immune responses, the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy, and inflammatory pathology [178].

Furthermore, multi-organ organoid platforms, such as interconnected gut-liver, brain-gut, or maternal-fetal organoid systems, will facilitate the study of organ interactions and comorbidities under physiological and pathological conditions [179]. These integrated platforms hold promise for revealing new insights into complex diseases involving multiple organ systems, such as metabolic syndrome, cardiorenal syndrome, and neurogastrointestinal disorders. For example, organoids connecting the gut and liver may help elucidate the mechanistic basis of diseases like non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Brain-gut models may provide crucial insights into diseases like Parkinson’s disease, in which gastrointestinal dysfunction often precedes neurological symptoms. Such interconnected organoid platforms offer unprecedented potential for understanding systemic disease networks, significantly expanding their biomedical applications. Notably, with increasing focus on healthy aging, organoid systems are likely to play a key role in identifying and testing anti-aging interventions, from drugs clearing senescent cells to metabolic regulators. This will allow for testing directly in 3D human tissue, complementing traditional animal models in aging science research.

Organoids are expected to play an increasingly crucial role in drug discovery and preclinical testing. These technologies can also accelerate the development of anti-aging therapies, enabling researchers to evaluate the effects of interventions (such as senescent cell scavengers, NAD+ enhancers, or mTOR inhibitors) on the aging phenotypes of human tissues in vitro. It is expected that within the next decade, organoid-based testing methods (covering various tissue models such as liver, kidney, brain, and heart) will be routinely integrated into early drug evaluation processes to assess the efficacy and toxicity of drugs in humans [180]. Regulatory approval of validated organoid toxicity testing methods is expected to significantly reduce reliance on animal models, making drug development more closely aligned with human physiology. Furthermore, the concept of “in vitro clinical trials,” which utilizes patient-derived organoids for preclinical efficacy testing and patient stratification before clinical trial enrollment, is expected to revolutionize clinical trial design, improving trial efficiency and the personalization of treatment. In the field of regenerative medicine, there is cautious optimism regarding the translational potential of organoids as innovative cell therapies. Recent research directions may include dissociating organoids into cell suspensions for therapeutic infusion, or constructing transplantable grafts by integrating organoids with biomaterial scaffolds. In addition, organoids have shown great potential in xenotransplantation research. For example, human epithelial cells, endothelial cells, or vascular cells can be coated onto porcine scaffolds to construct hybrid organ replacements. Although achieving complete organ replacement remains a distant goal, its basic capabilities are being gradually established, laying a key foundation for future clinical applications [110, 181].

In summary, organoids represent a paradigm shift in our ability to mimic human biology and disease processes in vitro. With ongoing interdisciplinary innovation addressing key challenges such as standardization, vascularization, scalability, and ethical regulation, organoids hold the promise of reshaping not only biomedical research but also transforming clinical practice. From personalized drug screening to next-generation regenerative therapies, the organoid field is rapidly moving from proof-of-concept to tangible translational applications. Equally important, organoids open new avenues for aging science—enabling the analysis of the human aging process and the in vitro testing of anti-aging interventions—crucial for addressing the challenges of an aging population. Organoid research, emphasizing scientific rigor and ethical responsibility, is poised to become a cornerstone of 21st-century medicine.
