A scaffolding element rewires local 3D chromatin architecture during differentiation
Authors: Ivana Jerković, Marco Di Stefano, Hadrien Reboul, Michael F Szalay, Davide Normanno, Flora Paldi, Giorgio L Papadopoulos, Quentin Szabo, Frederic Bantignies, Giacomo Cavalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring differentiation chromatin and transcription are rewired to reflect the new cellular identity. However, what drives these changes and their mutual relationship remains unclear. Here, we use mouse ESC-to-NPC differentiation to study rewiring at a 3 Mb large Zfp608 locus. This large chromatin domain splits in half at the Zfp608 promoter, local chromatin gets littered with activating marks, compacts in 3D space and Zfp608 transcription is induced. We combine capture Hi-C (cHi-C), biophysical modelling, and 3-color 3D-FISH to investigate cis and trans elements driving this process. We find that transcription modulates both, local chromatin contacts and insulation at the domain split and we identify a genetic element with dual enhancer/architectural function essential for chromatin rewiring and loop formation, a scaffolding element. Together, these results demonstrate that a hierarchy of genetic elements in cis allows successful rewiring during differentiation and that multiple trans acting elements contribute to making this rewiring efficient.Subject terms: Gene regulation, Epigenomics, Gene expression, Functional genomics, Nuclear organization

Introduction

High cellular plasticity is a hallmark of pluripotent cells, allowing them to respond to developmental and differentiation cues. The molecular basis of this response is multifaceted and occurs concomitantly at the epigenomic, transcriptomic and chromatin conformation levels1–4. In particular, chromatin conformation changes during cell differentiation processes are well documented and encompass compartment switching, domain restructuring, and enhancer-promoter (E-P) contact rewiring2–7. Local contact rewiring (under 1 Mb) is often associated with specific gene-expression changes, highlighting the importance of chromatin contacts in regulating transcription3. However, even though many studies focused on the relationship between chromatin contacts and transcriptional response, they gave conflicting results indicating a context-dependent relationship8–19. In addition, most contacts are not functionally annotated; hence, their potential contribution to transcriptional response is not entirely known. Aside from activating and repressive loops, several recent studies described different types of chromatin contact regulators (facilitators, tethering elements and meta-loops) that flank cis-regulatory elements and might modulate the timing of E-P interactions and the robustness of transcriptional response7,20–24. Nevertheless, little is known about the mechanisms regulating specific 3D chromatin contacts, in particular in cis, leaving several unresolved questions about the hierarchical nature of these contacts and their importance for transcriptional regulation in steady state and differentiation7,20,22.

To study the mechanism and dynamics of these changes, we used an in vitro neuronal progenitor cell (NPC) differentiation system3,5–7. Here, the most striking chromatin reorganization happens at NPC-specific genes, like Zfp6083. Zfp608 gene function is relatively elusive. It is expressed in several cell types like thymocytes where it supresses T-cell maturation by silencing Rag1 and Rag2 in the developing foetus and in neuronal tissues where it directs neuronal migration in the embryonic cortical plate25–28. In both tissues, this is achieved through transcriptional antagonism between Zfp608 and Zfp609, orthologues of the Drosophila neuronal gene scribbler26–28. Any dysregulation of this balance in NPCs leads to Zfp608 misexpression and improper neuronal positioning, contributing to hallmark symptoms of Cornelia de Lange Syndrome (CdLS)26,29–31. This is likely achieved through physical interaction between ZFP609 and NIPBL which co-bind the Zfp608 promoter and its cis-regulatory elements, possibly via Cohesin-loading independent NIPBL function26. Here, we focused on the Zfp608 locus to study chromatin conformation rewiring during the mouse embryonic stem cell ESC-to-NPC differentiation and its connection to transcription. By combining molecular biology, biophysical modelling, and imaging with novel analyses in wildtype (wt) and eleven mutant cell lines in ESC and NPC states, we demonstrate that transcription directly contributes to the NPC-specific rewiring via 3D contact insulation and loop stabilization. Furthermore, we discover a genetic element with a dual role, functional and architectural, that is essential for contact rewiring and involves both CTCF-dependent insulation and loop-extrusion as well as CTCF-independent looping interactions.

Results

NPC differentiation rewires chromatin composition, transcription, and local chromatin contacts

Developmental genes activated in NPCs are typically large genes enriched in topologically associating domains (TADs) that contain few genes (see the NPC up genes in the 1-5 genes and 6-10 genes TAD categories in Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Zfp608 is ~103 kb long and occupies a TAD of ~3 Mb together with several other unexpressed and/or poorly annotated genes. This domain undergoes 2D and 3D chromatin rewiring during NPC differentiation, concomitantly with the activation of only one gene, Zfp608. These characteristics made the Zfp608 locus a convenient region to investigate 3D chromatin rearrangements, associated cis-regulatory elements (CREs) and their contribution to Zfp608 transcription. To this end, we differentiated wt ESCs into NPCs and characterized their chromatin composition (ChIP-seq of H3K27Ac, H3K4me3, H3K36me3, POL2, PAX6, CTCF, and RAD21), transcriptional status (RNA-seq), and local 3D chromatin conformation at the Zfp608 locus (cHi-C) in biological replicates (Fig. 1a–d, Supplementary Figs. 1c, d and 2a, b). NPC differentiation is accompanied by active histone mark deposition at the locus, Zfp608 transcriptional activation, and 3D chromatin contact rewiring (Fig. 1a–d, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Data 1 and 2)3,5. The Zfp608 chromatin domain splits into two upon NPC differentiation, due to a significant increase in insulation at the Zfp608 promoter (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Data 1 and 2). This boundary formation is concomitant with the emergence of multiple, specific chromatin contacts (loops) that appear mainly upstream to the gene promoter (Fig. 1e, f). These NPC-specific contacts include promoter-anchored loops (putative enhancer-promoter loops (E-P)) and contacts among elements covered by the H3K27Ac mark and bound by the PAX6 transcription factor (putative enhancer-enhancer loops (E-E)). Notably, apart from the promoter region and one putative enhancer region, which we named element A, we did not observe CTCF/RAD21 binding in any other loop anchor in this region (Fig. 1d). To examine the main NPC-specific putative E-P contacts and their importance to the establishment of the NPC-specific domain we selected two promoter-distal loop anchors based on their differential transcription factor (TF) occupancy and histone mark depositions. One loop anchor was a putative enhancer, element A and the other a loop anchor largely devoid of any marks or TFs, element B (Fig. 1d). According to the cHi-C maps, both of these two elements contact the Zfp608 promoter and other putative enhancers over long distances (560 kb and 826 kb, respectively), but are occupied by a distinct set of TFs and chromatin marks. In NPCs, element A harbours the active enhancer mark H3K27Ac, as well as PAX6, POL2, CTCF, and RAD21 binding, whereas element B only exhibited weak PAX6 occupancy and a very weak H3K27Ac signal (Fig. 1d). Altogether, these data demonstrated that Zfp608 transcriptional change is accompanied by chromatin conformation rewiring including changes at multiple putative regulatory elements, one of which, element A, could represent a putative enhancer.

Transcription attenuation weakens insulation and contacts at the Zfp608 promoter

To understand the interplay of 3D genome and transcription activity, we wanted to investigate how perturbing the Zfp608 transcription start site (TSS) and transcription strength might impact the local chromatin structure. To reduce transcription in this context, we annotated TSSs at Zfp608, and engineered two mutant cell lines: a proximal promoter deletion (Δprom) and a 3xSV40 polyA insertion (polyA) immediately downstream of the main TSS (Supplementary Fig. 3a). As for the wt case, we profiled the local 3D chromatin conformation (cHi-C), transcriptional status (RNA-seq) and chromatin composition (ChIP-seq of H3K27Ac, H3K4me3, H3K36me3, RNA POL2, PAX6, CTCF and RAD21) in both mutants in ESCs and NPCs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Figs. 1e, f and 4a-h). The two mutants differ substantially from one another in design. In the Δprom mutant, we deleted the proximal part of the promoter, the first two exons, and all but one poorly used upstream TSS (~4.3 kb), directly removing TF binding sites and histone marks at the promoter (Supplementary Fig. 3a). This deletion was designed so that the loop anchor would not be fully deleted while still disrupting TSS positioning. In the polyA mutant, the 3x SV40 polyA signal was inserted 78 bp downstream to the main TSS, leaving the TFs binding, histone marks, contact anchor, and the TSS position intact (Supplementary Fig. 3a). In both cases, we found modest changes in terms of Zfp608 transcription and no changes in terms of chromatin conformation, TF binding or histone marks in the ESC mutant lines compared to wt (Supplementary Figs. 1e, f and 2c, Supplementary Data 1 and 2). Interestingly, in the NPC state, Δprom and polyA mutants affected transcription differently. In the Δprom mutant, transcription starts upstream of the deleted TSS (at the alternative TSS) and transcribes the remaining part of the gene. In fact, the transcript appears more abundant in the mutant than in the wt and the transcript abounds even further from exon 4 to the end of the gene, likely due to the activation of a weak additional TSS in the 3′ part of the gene (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Overall, this causes mild but not significant overall transcriptional changes. In contrast, polyA insertion significantly reduces transcription (to ~25–50% (qPCR) and ~50% (RNA-seq)) of the NPC wt level over the entire gene body (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Figs. 2c and 4a, b). However, transcription is not completely lost, likely due to the presence of downstream TSSs, in particular at the exon 2 and at the 3′ end of the gene (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Notably, none of the transcriptional changes were mirrored by qualitative changes in TF occupancy or histone modifications at the Zfp608 locus. However, ChIP-seq quantification of normalized data revealed a trend in the polyA mutant, where H3K27Ac, POL2, and PAX6 were mildly increased at the promoter and across the entire locus (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Conversely, the main changes in histone mark and TF binding abundance in Δprom mutant stem from the loss of the signal due to the deletion. This deletion removed two ATAC peaks, the middle of the H3K4me4 and H3K27Ac domains, and one of two promoter-located CTCF sites, but no large changes in the overall domain signal were observed (Supplementary Figs. 3a and 4c–f).

Next, we analysed the NPC cHi-C maps, in particular the local chromatin contacts and insulation. We found that, despite different effects on transcription, both mutants significantly decreased insulation at the promoter and at the 3′ end of the Zfp608 gene (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 4d, e, orange arrows and asterisk). We noticed that insulation was also slightly reduced at elements A and B in the polyA mutant (Fig. 2b, orange arrows). In addition, we found ectopic contacts (present in the mutant) crossing the Zfp608 in Δprom but not in polyA, possibly due to lower insulation at the promoter in Δprom mutant (Fig. 2f, g (purple labelled contacts), Supplementary Figs. 4d (blue arrows), and 4g, h). Furthermore, promoter-anchored contacts significantly weakened in both mutants (Fig. 2f, g, orange labelled contacts). This effect was stronger in the Δprom mutant, consistent with the fact that the deletion overlaps with the loop anchor site (Supplementary Fig. 4d–h). Interestingly, promoter-unrelated (putative E-E) contacts were affected differently in the two mutants as they were reinforced in the Δprom but not in the polyA mutant (Fig. 2f, g purple and black labelled contacts).

Together, these findings suggest that both the changes in promoter histone marks and TF binding as well as the attenuated transcription may affect the establishment of the NPC-specific domain. In the Δprom mutant, attenuated contacts and insulation may result from the missing transcription factor binding and an altered transcription initiation site, while in polyA mutant, these effects are exclusively due to the decrease in transcription, which potentially destabilizes and/or weakens chromatin loops17. These results illustrate a complex relationship between chromatin structure and transcription at Zfp608, where the ability to switch between TSSs can compensate for mutations around the main TSS and prevent transcription impairment.

Chromatin contacts are hierarchically organized

Next, we characterized the role of the putative distal enhancers and their contribution to rewiring. In order to analyse long-distance loops involving the A and B elements that create a network of P-E and E-E contacts, we engineered three different cell lines where we deleted element A (ΔA), element B (ΔB), or both (ΔAB). We profiled them by cHi-C, RNA-seq, qPCR, and ChIP-seq in ESCs and NPCs (Fig. 3a–d, Supplementary Figs. 1g–i, 2d, 4i, j, 5 and 6a–d). In the ESC state, we found no major changes in Zfp608 expression, ChIP-seq or the cHi-C profiles in any of the mutant cell lines (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 1g–i, and Supplementary Data 1 and 2). Similarly, in NPCs Zfp608 transcription was minimally altered (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Figs. 2d and 4i, j). Since the Zfp608 region is littered with putative enhancers, which never completely lose interaction with the promoter, we postulated that there might be functional redundancy between individual enhancers, hence individual deletions might be insufficient to significantly affect transcription. To test this hypothesis, we further deleted putative enhancers C2 and C3, alone and in combination with element A, creating four new lines (ΔC2, ΔAC2, ΔC2C3, and ΔAC2C3). Then, we examined Zfp608 transcriptional status in the NPCs of all four mutants by qPCR (Fig. 3e) and chromatin conformation in ΔC2C3 and ΔAC2C3 lines by cHi-C (Supplementary Fig. 6e-l). We found that Zfp608 transcription is significantly reduced in all mutants but, most drastically, to ~50%, in ΔAC2C3, indicating that C2, C3 and A are bona fide Zfp608 NPC enhancers that are at least partially functionally redundant (Fig. 3e).

Secondly, we normalized and quantified H3K27Ac, POL2, and PAX6 signal in order to uncover any changes in chromatin composition. In three distal mutants ΔA, ΔB, and ΔAB, K27Ac and POL2 signals are generally slightly higher than in the NPC wt over the entire domain and especially around at the promoter region, but also more variable between replicates. Furthermore, in ΔB the H3K27Ac, POL2, and PAX6 signal at enhancer A is higher, perhaps indicative of some compensation process upon deletion of CREs (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 5a).

In addition, we produced RAD21 and CTCF ChIP-seq and examined available binding profiles of NIPBL and ZFP609, proteins involved in loop-extrusion and their partners26. As expected, NIPBL and ZFP609 co-bind at almost all putative CREs in the region. However, they co-bind with RAD21 and CTCF only at enhancer A, promoter and at the 3′ Zfp608 intron, suggesting that these three regions might act as Cohesin loading hotspots while other regions might have an independent function as suggested by other studies (Supplementary Fig. 5b)26.

Finally, we investigated local 3D chromatin changes of these mutants in the NPCs. cHi-C analysis showed that 3D genome organization was perturbed to varying degrees in all mutants. Strikingly, the ΔA mutant (~8.2 kb deletion) significantly affected not only the loops anchored in this element but also all other loops, including most promoter-anchored loops at the Zfp608 locus (Fig. 3a, f, and Supplementary Fig. 5c). In addition, this deletion significantly changed the insulation at element A but not at the Zfp608 promoter (Fig. 3b, asterisk above the insulation). The double deletion (ΔAB) affected local architecture similarly to the ΔA condition (Supplementary Fig. 5d–h). In contrast, the element B deletion (~7.4 kb deletion) caused a significant attenuation of the contacts anchored at element B but had no significant impact on insulation, nor on the element B-independent loops in the region (with the exception of mild strengthening of some A-anchored contacts where we also found slightly more H3K27Ac, POL2, and PAX6 signal) (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). Similarly, combinatorial deletion of more proximal enhancers ΔC2C3 (C2 ~ 6.9 kb, and C3 ~ 5.5 kb deletion) did not reduce the frequency of C2- or C3-unrelated contacts, but we did find insulation weakening at the promoter (Supplementary Fig. 6e–h). On the other hand, the triple ΔAC2C3 deletion induced stronger insulation weakening at the gene body, and a reduction in chromatin loop strength, although milder than in ΔA alone (Supplementary Fig. 6i–l). Unlike the ΔC2C3 mutant, the ΔAC2C3 and ΔA mutant showed that the B and Badj regions reduce contacts with their downstream regions (Supplementary Figs. 5c and 6k—blue color extending from B and Badj elements towards the gene body). These findings suggest that element A acts as an enhancer and may have a locus-wide master architectural role, bringing the other distal elements in 3D proximity. On the other hand, C2 and C3 seem to act more locally on chromatin flanking the Zfp608 promoter and favouring the establishment of promoter-proximal contacts.

Element A scaffolds the Zfp608 region upon differentiation

An important question is how can element A regulate global architecture of the Zfp608 locus in NPCs. We envisaged two hypotheses. Element A may either build a stable long-range contact hub, by directly interacting with multiple elements simultaneously, or it may function as a scaffolding element where individual frequent and dynamic contacts between two regulatory elements might increase the likelihood of further long-distance chromatin contacts, with the net results of bringing regulatory elements in 3D proximity via a web of individual, albeit unstable interactions. To discriminate between these two hypotheses, we performed 3-color 3D-FISH in ESC wt (a control of an inactive state with no loop), NPC wt, and NPC ΔA mutant to quantify simultaneously the distances between promoter (P), element A (A), and element B (B) in single cells. For this, we developed small, highly sensitive oligopaint probes (Methods). Specifically, we designed three 10 kb probes covering P, A, and B and a 15 kb probe for the element A in ΔA mutant condition (two 7.5 kb probes flanking each side of the deletion) (Fig. 4a) (Methods). Then, we analysed pairwise and triplet distances among P, A, and B elements (Fig. 4b, c) and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b).

We found that in wild-type NPCs all three pairwise distances (median: P-A = 394 nm, P-B = 620 nm, and A-B = 476 nm) are significantly shorter compared to the ESC wt condition (median: P-A = 489 nm, P-B = 676 nm, and A-B = 512 nm) indicating differentiation induced locus compaction (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Notably, the closest measured distance was P-A, despite A and B being much closer in genomic distance (P-A = 560 kb, P-B = 826 kb, and A-B = 266 kb) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Interestingly, this is also the case in ESC wt cells. To investigate how strong is this tendency in the two conditions, we calculated the ratios of median A-B and P-A distances (med(A-B)/med(P-A)) in wt ESC and NPC and found that the ratio is larger in NPCs (1.14) than in ESCs (1.05) indicating that, beyond a putative effect of global chromatin condensation, the contacts between P and A elements become particularly strong in NPCs (Supplementary Fig. 7a).

We, therefore, focused our analysis on the effect of ΔA mutation in the NPC state. We found that in the mutant all three pairwise distances were significantly increased (median: P-A = 439 nm, P-B = 644 nm, and A-B = 499 nm), with P-A being most strongly affected, indicating that all A-anchored contacts aren’t affected equally and that the element A facilitates the A-independent P-B contact (Fig. 4b). By varying the distance threshold to detect the percentage of contacts between pairs of loci, we found that in NPC wt, P and B are rarely closer than 200 nm (in <2.3% of cells), 300 nm (7.6%) or even 400 nm (17.6%). Conversely, P and A are found much more often closer than 200 nm (15.9%), 300 nm (35.3%) or 400 nm (51.9%), indicating that P and A interact either more frequently or stably (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7b).

Next, we quantified how often we find all three loci in the vicinity of one another (triplet) at varying distances (Fig. 4c). In comparison to expected, if P-A, P-B, and A-B are independent (<200 nm 0.02%, <300 nm 0.52%, and <400 nm 3.26%) the incidence of triple contacts in NPC wt is higher (<200 nm 0.4%, <300 nm 3.5%, and <400 nm 11.4%). This implies that 45.9% of the cells that present a P-B distance under 300 nm are also forming a triplet and this percentage increases to 64.7% for distances under 400 nm, respectively, strongly suggesting that the contact between element B and the promoter is facilitated by A-P proximity (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Consistent with pairwise analysis, the proportion of triplets decreased in ΔA mutant. Triplet frequencies remain unchanged at very short distances and start reducing between the wt and ΔA from the 300 nm distance threshold onward. The proportion of cells with triplets under 300 nm is reduced from 3.5% in wt to 2.8% in ΔA (p-val. = 0.6603, two proportions z test), from 11.4% in wt to 7.6% under the 400 nm threshold in ΔA (p-val. = 0.05659, two proportions z test) and from 18.2% in wt to 11.7% under 450 nm in ΔA (p-val. = 0.00604, two proportions z test). This trend continues with the same margin of difference up until the 650–700 nm distance, when it starts to decrease (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 7b). We note that the reduction of triplets in the ΔA mutant reflects changes in P-A and A-B distances, but also in P-B distances. Therefore, the deletion of element A leads to a significant increase in all the pairwise distances (P-A, A-B, and P-B), resulting in fewer triplets but not in their complete loss, which one would expect if element A behaved as an obligatory contact hub.

To further understand the relationship between the P, A, and B elements, we measured the area of the triangles made by the three elements and compared them between the wt and ΔA. We found that for all distances, the triangle area distribution is significantly higher in ΔA compared to the wt (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Even using the relatively high 400 nm threshold for triplet identification, we measured a significantly greater surface area in ΔA, showing that in ΔA not only the number of triplets decreases, but also that the remaining triplets drift further apart (Fig. 4d).

Finally, we set out to investigate how constrained in 3D space are the three loci in NPC wt versus the NPC ΔA condition. For this, we used a novel analytical approach where we optimally superimposed the three points on a single plane while preserving their distances. This was done by taking three loci from single cells and in silico connecting them to make triangles. The triangles’ centres of mass of were then stacked on top of one another while maintaining the topological position of the three loci (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Then, we calculated a 2D kernel density of the elements’ positions. Briefly, the same number of occurrences for each element was taken and their density was investigated over the equally sized 2D plane. The density (number of occurrences) of the element is shown as a set of values of each pixel on a 2D map (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). We found that, upon the deletion of element A, the chromatin immediately adjacent to A occupies a significantly larger area, indicating that it is less restricted in space (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 7f). Intriguingly, the same impact is seen on promoter and element B, showing that the mutual displacement of the three elements is restricted to a narrower region in NPC wt than in the NPC ΔA (Fig. 4e).

Altogether, these experiments suggest that there is a hierarchical relationship between contacts in this region. Element A acts as an architectural scaffold which, while contacting the promoter, also constrains the movements of other chromatin regions, thereby favouring long-range contacts that would otherwise happen less frequently. This constrained dynamic contributes to the efficient chromatin rewiring during differentiation.

The scaffolding element function mainly relies on CTCF-independent mechanisms

Given the scaffolding function of element A, we sought to investigate the mechanism driving this behaviour. The inspection of chromatin tracks revealed that element A was the only long-range enhancer in this region that displayed the presence of two proteins involved in or halting loop-extrusion, CTCF and RAD21 (cohesin) (Fig. 1d)32,33. To perturb halting of loop-extrusion at A, we engineered a new mutant cell line where we deleted a 22 bp CTCF motif (ΔCTCFA) aiming to abolish the CTCF binding and RAD21 accumulation without perturbing the binding of any other factor (Fig. 1d)32,33. Characterization of the ΔCTCFA mutant by cHi-C and ChIP-seq in ESC state revealed no overall architectural or TF occupancy changes (Supplementary Fig. 1j). Conversely, in the NPCs we observed a total loss of CTCF and RAD21 occupancy at element A, while the POL2, H3K27Ac, and PAX6 binding was only slightly decreased compared to wt (Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 5a). On the other hand, Zfp608 transcription very mildly increased in this mutant indicating that element A could be a slightly better enhancer without the CTCF motif (Supplementary Fig. 4j). Next, we examined if loss of CTCF binding changes DNA accessibility. To this end, we performed ATAC-seq in NPC wt and ΔCTCFA lines. We examined the tracks and quantified normalized ATAC-seq signal over the Zfp608 locus. We found that in NPC wt, all elements are accessible, with proximal elements, C2, C3, and A being most accessible (Fig. 5c, d). Upon CTCF motif deletion, accessibility at element A significantly decreased, suggesting that CTCF binding contributes to chromatin opening at this element. Surprisingly, we also found significantly lower accessibility at C2 and C3 elements located 413 kb and 344 kb away from A, respectively, suggesting that chromatin accessibility at these elements might be regulated through 3D contacts (Fig. 5c, d).

The cHi-C differential map (NPC ΔCTCFA vs. NPC ΔA) and insulation profiles revealed that, similarly to ΔA mutant, the insulation at element A is completely lost in ΔCTCFA, indicating that CTCF acts as the main loop-extrusion barrier at element A (Fig. 5e–g). The majority of A-anchored contacts (both putative E-P and E-E) were also significantly weakened in ΔCTCFA, consistent with a role of CTCF and cohesin in their formation (Fig. 5h). Nevertheless, the decrease in A-anchored contacts was much weaker than in the ΔA mutant and the A-independent contacts remained unperturbed (Fig. 5f and compare Fig. 3f with Fig. 5h), strongly suggesting that the function of A as a scaffolding element cannot be explained fully by CTCF dependent mechanisms. Consequently, we wondered what other mechanisms could drive the remaining loops. To this end, we used CistromeDB ChIP-seq database to investigate which TFs bind at enhancer A in NPCs or neural cell types34–36. We found several factors expressed in NPCs and with a known role in 3D contact formation that bind to this region. These include LDB1—a known self-association factor also used to force loops; LHX2 -a factor involved in the function of the so-called range-extender element at Shh; LHX3—a known co-factor of LDB1 in loop formation; NGN2—an NPC-specific loop driver, and BRD4—shown to associate with NIPBL to form loops (Supplementary Fig. 5b)11,12,19,37–41. These TFs do not bind abundantly at other CREs in the region (except C2). On the other hand, at element A, binding some of these factors (BRD4, LHX3, and NGN2) overlaps the CTCF motif, but they also bind at multiple other locations within this enhancer, suggesting that one or more of these factors might contribute to chromatin looping39,42,43.

Together, these data strongly suggest that in addition to CTCF-mediated halting of loop-extursion, CTCF-independent molecular interactions might play an important role during differentiation-driven chromatin structure rewiring at the Zfp608 locus.

Looping interactions are crucial for rewiring during differentiation

In order to test the contribution of epigenetic interactions for rewiring, we used an orthogonal approach involving modelling of the 3D Zfp608 chromatin architecture using a biophysical strategy based on Molecular Dynamics simulations of polymers (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 8a). We represented chromatin as a chain of spherical beads, each containing 5 kb of DNA, featuring general biophysical properties including connectivity, excluding volume, and bending rigidity (Methods). This model was used to test three biophysical mechanisms (loop-extrusion, compartment interactions, and looping interactions) which can contribute to spatial chromatin organization (Fig. 6a)44–46. We partitioned the Zfp608 region into active (A) and inactive (B) compartments from previously published ESC and NPC Hi-C data and modelled attractions within and between them with energy strengths EAA, EBB, and EAB, which favour the formation of compartments through microphase separation3,47–49. Simultaneously, we added attractive looping interactions of strength EL to model specific site-to-site contacts that were detected by cHi-C (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Data 6) (Methods). In the same framework, we applied the loop-extrusion process that can form local domains and loops and where extruders, such as the cohesin complex, load on chromatin at random positions and extrude loops in both directions until they unload or encounter a barrier32,33. The extruder activity was characterized by the density (Ne/Mb), lifetime (Sl) on chromatin, and extrusion speed. Each barrier was described by its position on chromatin, direction, permeability (p), and pausing time (tp) induced on incoming extruders50. To limit the number of parameters to explore, we fixed the extrusion speed to 1 kb/s and imposed that extruders can cross one another, consistent with recent works51–53. The barriers were positioned at the maxima of the experimentally determined insulation sites, and they could stop extruders coming from both directions with the same permeability, but they were set to induce a direction-dependent pausing time (tpL and tpR). To compare our models with the experimental data, we defined four metrics and tested more than 700 parameter sets, aiming to optimize these metrics (Methods)54,55. Similar to other studies, we found that more than one set of parameter values could similarly well describe the data. Hence, we visualized the metrics in a supervised-selected set of best parameter values (Supplementary Data 7)54,56.

Using extensive data-driven parameter screening (Supplementary Fig. 8b–f, and Supplementary Data 7) (Methods), we found that the optimized compartment-driven attractions were generally weak (0.00–0.02 kBT) and not compartment-specific (EAA = EBB = EAB). In fact, models generated using compartment-specific attractions resulted in cis-decay curves (average number of contacts vs. genomic distance) that could not match the corresponding cHi-C decay at around 2.4 Mb (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). Furthermore, compartment-driven attractions higher than 0.02 kBT resulted in suboptimal models due to the excessive compaction of the region. We also ruled out extruder densities Ne/Mb>8 and extruder lifetimes longer than 20 min, which induced an excessive compaction of the region between 100 kb and 1 Mb of genomic separation (Supplementary Fig. 8d). In the optimized parameter sets, ESC and NPC wt models resulted in two similar loop-extrusion-dependent left and right barriers (BL and BR) with high insulation strength (1-p = 0.70–0.50) that generated a 2.4 Mb domain at the centre of the region (Fig. 6b). The difference between the ESC and NPC state arose due to the internal barriers. In the NPCs, three novel, but weaker (1-p = 0.15–0.25) borders emerge between BL and BR at the enhancer A, the Zfp608 promoter and the 3′ end of the Zfp608 gene. Only combining these parameters with site-specific looping interactions (EL = 3.00 kBT) resulted in models that optimally recapitulated experimentally determined contact patterns in ESC and NPC. This result is compatible with the hypothesis that CTCF-independent molecular attractions are necessary to recover the chromatin organization observed at this locus in vivo (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 9a, b, Supplementary Data 7, and Supplementary Video 1).

We then identified the parameter changes that best explain the contact behaviour alterations in NPC mutant conditions compared to wt (Methods). The Δprom was best modelled by weakening the 3′ and Zfp608 promoter barriers by ~10% and by EL increasing from 3 to 4 kBT in order to strengthen the Zfp608 promoter-independent looping interactions (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9c). These models recapitulate the finding that a partial loss of the promoter can impact not only insulation and contacts anchored at the deleted site but also strengthens promoter-independent contacts. The polyA condition was best captured by the same weakening of the barriers at 3′ and the Zfp608 promoter in addition to a 5% weakening of the barrier at the A element and a 25% weakening of the promoter-anchored loops (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9d). These results agree that transcription and its regulatory factors associated at the Zfp608 promoter contribute to the strengthening of the insulation at the Zfp608 gene as well as to promoter-anchored looping interactions.

To account for the ΔA, ΔAB, and ΔB mutations, we removed the loop-extrusion (LE) barrier and the looping interactions involving the deleted elements (A and/or B) (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 8e–g). Notably, we found that these deletions also affected the insulation and the loops stemming from the Zfp608 gene: in ΔAB and ΔB the insulation strength at the promoter decreased by 10 and 5%, respectively, and in ΔA and ΔAB the looping interactions from the promoter had to be reduced by 25% (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 9e–g). Simulating the ΔCTCFA condition, we found that the LE barrier at element A still had a residual 10% insulation and the strength of the loops anchored in element A dropped by only 50%. We did not find any sizeable effect on the promoter for this mutant (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 9h). The mild effect of ΔA, ΔB, and ΔAB on promoter-associated barrier permeability and looping interactions, that is not found in the ΔCTCFA line, could therefore be due to binding of TFs other than CTCF at elements A and B.

These biophysical modelling results strongly suggested that loop-extrusion-independent molecular attractions could quantitatively explain chromatin conformation rewiring during differentiation. To better understand what might be driving these molecular attractions, we investigated the factors bound at the anchors of NPC-specific loops. As some of these elements are open and poised (harbouring H3K4me1 and H4K27Ac) already in ESC, we focused on PAX6, a master regulator of NPCs that is present at these sites and was proposed to play a role in NPC-specific contact formation (Fig. 1d)3,55,57,58. To this end, we used dCas9-VP64-p65-Hsf1 to ectopically overexpress Pax6 in ESCs (ESC Pax6+) and performed RNA-seq, PAX6 ChIP-seq, and cHi-C in this mutant (Supplementary Figs. 1k, 2e and 10). We found that expression of multiple genes was changed between ESC Pax6+ and wt, although the cells remained pluripotent (retained high Oct4, Nanog, Klf4, Sox2, Esrrb, and Myc) and lacked induction of expression of a majority of the neuronal markers (i.e., Nes, Ncam1, Msl1, Eno2, Foxa2, Dcx) in agreement with previous studies (Supplementary Figs. 2e and 10b, Supplementary Data 1 and 2)59. However, Pax6 overexpression did not induce expression of the Zfp608 gene (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Moreover, the analysis of the PAX6 binding pattern in ESC Pax6+ cells showed that more than 36% of the PAX6-binding sites in ESC Pax6+ cells are much weaker or absent in NPCs, and vice versa, but also that PAX6 in ESC Pax6+ cells binds some closed chromatin regions (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). Furthermore, PAX6 could not bind to any of its NPC-bound sites at the Zfp608 locus except at the 3′ end of the gene (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Finally, we examined the cHi-C contact pattern which did not reveal major changes between ESC Pax6+ and ESC wt cells, indicating that chromatin conformation rewiring did not occur upon Pax6 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 10a). These data show that PAX6 alone cannot act as a pioneer chromatin binding protein and cannot induce chromatin interactions at the Zfp608 locus in ESCs, suggesting that critical cofactors required for its recruitment to its NPC-specific target sites might be missing.

Discussion

In this work, we investigated chromatin rewiring during the ESC-to-NPC differentiation. Focusing on the Zfp608 locus, we identified a distinct genetic element (A) that directs this process by scaffolding the locus through a frequent promoter-element A loop driven by loop-extrusion as well as CTCF-independent chromatin looping. The CTCF motif at element A is responsible for stopping loop-extrusion and promoting contacts from A, but also to make the A, C2, and C3 elements more accessible. Other studies showed that CTCF depletion and conditional knock-out leads to global changes in accessibility, at and away from CTCF sites60–62. However, here, it is a single motif deletion that changes the accessibility of unrelated regions located hundreds of kbs away. This suggests that the accessibility at C2 and C3 elements might rely on their 3D contact with element A and on the presence of CTCF and/or other CTCF-associated factors (i.e., RAD21).

The scaffolding function of element A involves weak compartment-driven interactions, loop-extrusion and site- and cell-state-specific looping interactions. Since the anchors of distinct loops (especially the Zfp608 promoter and element A) are already marked by H3K4me1 and H3K27Ac in the ESC state, where loops are not formed, we speculate that these interactions are likely driven by general factors and/or by NPC-specific TFs. These may include PAX6, a master regulator of NPC development, and its cofactors57–59,63. Other TFs have been previously demonstrated to participate in a cell-type-specific looping and PAX6 has been found binding at the base of the NPC-specific loops38,39,55. Given that the human Pax6 overexpression can induce ESC-to-NPC differentiation, but murine Pax6 cannot, it is likely that there are inter-species differences for the PAX6 binding that need to be explored further59. We also noted locus-wide heavy binding of NGN2, a TF with an important role during central nervous system development, which has also been linked in facilitating loop formation in the developing mouse neocortex55. Furthermore, element A is bound by other proteins known for their role in loop formation. Beyond its role as a transcriptional and epigenetic regulator, BRD4 directs genome organization through its interaction with NIPBL, stabilizing its binding and aiding cohesin loading41. BRD4 binds relatively scarcely at the Zfp608 locus in cortical neurons, with the exception of strong binding at the gene promoter, 3′ region, and at element A, where it also co-binds with NIPBL and RAD21. Possibly, these regions might be used as cohesin loading hotspots, unlike other NIPBL-occupied sites that are not co-bound by BRD4 and RAD2142. The NIPBL non-RAD21 sites have been speculated to have a “genome architecture independent” role in gene regulation through its interaction with ZFP609, a ZFP608 antagonist26. At the Zfp608 locus, different NIPBL-occupied sites might therefore be involved both in chromatin organization and in independent functions, as indicated by other studies26,41,42. However, since RAD21 occupancy is lost in the ΔCTCFA mutant, cohesin loading cannot be the only mechanism responsible for the loops at element A. In addition to BRD4, another well-known looping factor, LDB1, binds at the Zfp608 promoter, C2, and at A, together with LHX2 and LHX3, factors known to collaborate with LDB1 in establishing chromatin contacts that might be instrumental in extending the reach of P-E loops19,37–40,64,65. In future work, it will be interesting to test which one of these factors might be acting through element A to scaffold the architecture of the Zfp608 locus.

One important observation is that the ΔA mutant induces weakening of most NPC-specific chromatin loops, including those that are not anchored at the element A. On the other hand, local conformation changes in ΔC2C3 and ΔAC2C3 mutants indicated that promoter contacts with proximal enhancers might compete with long-range promoter loops. In such cases, scaffolding elements, like A, might be necessary to bridge the promoter and other long-distance CREs. Relatively little is known about the mutual relations between different kinds of chromatin contacts in mammals. In Drosophila, one study found that so-called tethering elements form a subset of contacts early during early embryogenesis, before the establishment of topological domains. The emergence of these contacts fosters early, specific E-P loops that modulate the activation kinetics of developmental genes22. A second study found extremely long-range contacts in fly neurons that can bridge distant domains and contribute to neuronal gene expression7. Similarly, extremely long-range chromatin contacts were also observed in mammalian neurons, suggesting that the hierarchical aspect of chromatin contacts might be present in other species6. In mammals, earlier studies found CTCF-dependent mechanisms can provide transcriptional robustness during critical stages of development or to lead to transcriptional changes during cell differentiation20,66. Our results extend these observations and suggest that there are multiple, distinct hierarchical layers of E-P communication and that scaffolding elements, like A, might allow cells to regulate cell type- and stage-specific transcription relying on multiple mechanisms.

Mechanistically, element A scaffolds the region through its frequent contact with the Zfp608 promoter and other regulatory elements, globally constraining the contacts made within the locus in agreement with our cHi-C and 3D-FISH data. Similar constraining was reported in yeast by loop-extrusion factors67. In addition to this scaffolding role, element A is also a bona fide enhancer. However, intriguingly, A deletion alone does not change Zfp608 expression, it does so only in combination with deletions of C2 and C3 enhancers. Interestingly, the deletion of tethering elements in flies only causes a slight delay in transcriptional onset, but this is sufficient to cause morphological changes later in development22. Similarly, In mammals, tight temporal regulation is imperative during Hox gene activation, where a deletion or insertion of CTCF sites delays or speeds up the activation of Hox genes, respectively68,69. Therefore, while enhancer A is redundant with other enhancers in neural progenitor cells, it might be additionally required for regulation of the Zfp608 expression timing expression in different cell types, or for the appropriate response to other regulatory cues8,20,22.

Other than structural elements driving local rewiring, we also demonstrated that transcription itself can modulate both insulation and local contacts. This finding is in agreement with other studies where genes involved in thymocyte differentiation were ectopically activated in ESCs, and their activation impacted insulation as well as the intra-gene contacts70. However, only a subset of ectopically activated genes (Bcl6, Nfatc3) changed local contacts and insulation and the ectopic activation of Zfp608 in ESCs was unable to do so3,70. We suspect that this might be either due to the absence of NPC-specific TFs in ESCs or due to competition between the binding of dCas9 with TFs needed for chromatin rewiring in ESCs. However, we demonstrated thatin the polyA mutant in NPC,where there is no such binding obstruction at the promoter, transcription weakening is sufficient to diminish insulation and contacts. These data provide evidence for the role of transcription in establishing local chromatin conformation (insulation and contacts) upon native transcriptional induction. What remains to be understood is the relative contribution of RNA polymerase and its associated cofactors versus that of TF binding and histone marks at the promoter. Furthermore, CTCF/RAD21 binding at the Zfp608 promoter might contribute to regulating the frequency and/or the duration of promoter-anchored contacts in conjunction with transcription17,18. Future work will be required in order to test this possibility and to disentangle the respective roles of different components in the multi-layered regulation of chromatin architecture.

After thawing, cells were tested for viability with Trypan blue and 9 × 104 live NPCs were processed using the Active Motif ATAC-seq kit (Active Motif #53150) following manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were pooled and sent to BGI Tech Solutions (Hong Kong) for 100 bp paired-end sequencing. All experiments were performed in duplicates.

Oligopaint probe design and preparation

Oligopaint libraries were constructed following the procedures described by Beliveau et al.; see the Oligopaints website (https://oligopaints.hms.harvard.edu/) for further details. Libraries were synthesized by GenScript in the 12K Oligo pool format74. The coordinates (mm10), size, number, and density of probes and primers used for the libraries are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

Oligopaint libraries were designed using the mm10 Harvard “balance” BED files, which consist of 35–41-bp genomic sequences throughout the regions of interest. BED files can be retrieved from the Oligopaint Harvard website. Each library contains a universal primer pair followed by a specific primer pair (barcode without the “Sec” sequences) hooked to the genomic sequences (117–125 bp in total). Oligopaint libraries cover 10–15 kb of the genomic sequence of interest (i.e., the Zfp608 promoter, the A, and the B elements), representing 100–150 oligonucleotides distributed along each specific region. Oligopaint libraries were produced by emulsion PCR amplification from the oligonucleotide pool followed by a “two-step PCR” procedure and digestion by lambda exonuclease74. Here, to increase the sensitivity of the probes, we developed a “double-tail” strategy (dt). The first PCR (PCR1) produces a PCR fragment containing the genomic sequence flanked by two secondary oligonucleotide binding sites (Sec2/Sec1 or Sec4/Sec6 or Sec3/Sec5 binding site pairs) for signal amplification. The second PCR (PCR2) hooks a Phosphate atom at the 5′-end and a fluorophore (Alexa Fluor 488, ATTO 565 or ATTO 647) at the 5′-end on the opposite strand. The lambda exonuclease digests the PCR product from the 5′P, leading to the production of a pool of single-strand primary oligos bearing one fluorophore on their 5′-end. This probe can be used for FISH together with secondary oligos that are dual labelled at their 5′- and 3′-end, complementary to their binding sites (Sec2/Sec1 or Sec4/Sec6 or Sec3/Sec5) and bringing an additional 4 fluorophores per oligo, each oligo carrying five fluorophores in total (Alexa Fluor 488, ATTO 565 or ATTO 647 fluorophores). This new strategy allows us to produce reliable 10 kb Oligopaint probes with good confocal-based sensitivity. All oligonucleotides used for Oligopaint production were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. Oligonucleotide primer sequences (5′→3′) used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

FISH procedure

The FISH protocol is described in Szabo et al. and Reboul et al.75,76. Briefly, ESCs were grown directly on coverslips (170 ± 5 µm (ZEISS) in 6-well plates for 2 days. For NPCs, at the end of differentiation, aggregates were dissociated with Tryple (ThermoFisher #12604013) and resuspended at a concentration of 1.5 × 106 cells/ml in DDM media: (DMEM/F12 (ThermoFisher #31331-028), 500 µg/mL BSA (ThermoFisher #15260037), 1x non-essential amino acids (ThermofFisher #11140035), 1x Penicillin/Streptomycin (ThermoFisher #10378016), 1x sodium pyruvate (ThermoFisher #11360070), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (ThermoFisher #31350010) and 1x N2 supplement (ThermoFisher #17502048)). Then, the single cell suspension was plated onto the coverslips pre-coated with 33 µg/ml of poly-l-lysine in DPBS (SigmaAldrich #P8920) and 3 µg/mL of laminin in DPBS (SigmaAldrich #11243217001) in 6-well plates for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were washed once in PBS and fixed for 10 min in 4% PFA in PBS, rinsed with PBS, permeabilized for 10 min in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, rinsed with PBS, incubated for 10 min in 0.1 M of HCl and rinsed with 2× SSC/0.1% Tween 20 (2× SSCT). Cells were then incubated for 20 min in 50% formamide/2× SSCT at RT followed by 20 min in 50% formamide/2× SSCT at 60 °C. The 1–3 µM final concentration Oligopaint probe mixtures contained the same amount of their secondary oligos and 0.8 µl of RNase A (10 mg/mL) in 20 µl of FISH hybridization buffer (FHB: 50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2× SSC and salmon sperm DNA (0.5 mg/ml)). For good homogenization in FHB, probe mixtures were incubated on a thermomixer for 10 min at 80 °C at 1500 rpm and let cool down to RT before use. Probe mixtures were added directly to coverslips that were then sealed on glass slides with rubber cement (Fixogum; Marabu). Cellular DNA was co-denatured with the probe mixture for 3 min at 80 °C on a heating block immersed in a water bath and hybridization was performed overnight (16 to 20 h) at 42 °C in a dark and humid chamber. Cells were then washed for 15 min in 2× SSCT at 60 °C, 10 min in 2× SSCT at RT, 10 min in 0.2× SSC and twice in PBS. Cells were then incubated for 10 min in PBS with DAPI at RT (final concentration at 0.2 to 0.5 µg/ml) and washed twice for 5 min with PBS. Coverslips were mounted on slides with VECTASHIELD (CliniSciences) and sealed with nail polish.

Image acquisition

Confocal imaging was performed using a Confocal Zeiss LSM980 Airyscan II equipped with a x63/1.4 numerical aperture (NA) Plan Apochromat oil immersion objective. Diodes laser 405, 488, 561 and 639 nm were used for fluorophore excitations, leading to blue, green, red and far-red channels. Acquisitions were performed in 3D using the AiryScan Multiplex 4Y mode, with a pixel size of 40 nm and a z-axis step of 140 nm. 15–20 positions were scanned per FISH slide, and z-stacks were mounted in independent channels for analysis using the FIJI software77. Each experimental condition was analyzed in triplicate (from three independent NPC differentiations). Replicates were merged for data analysis, leading to 40–50 z-stacks per condition.

Image analysis

The 3D distance measurement in 3 colors was adapted from Szabo et al.75. 3D FISH analysis was conducted using the “Image Processing Toolbox” in MATLAB vR2023a. Briefly, channels were smoothed using a Gaussian filter (σ = 3 and 1 pixel for DAPI and FISH channels, respectively) and segmented in 3D using an adaptative threshold; FISH objects that were smaller than 900 voxels or located outside of the DAPI-segmented regions were discarded. 3D distances between the centroids of segmented FISH objects were calculated, and for the three objects (from the green, red and far-red channels), only the mutual nearest neighbours that were closer than 1.5 µm were considered. In each individual nucleus, the three distances were paired allowing for triangle area measurements and optimal superimposition and kernel density analysis.

Superimposition of FISH data by optimal superimposition

To examine the relative positions of the three loci (promoter, element A, and element B) in space, we devised a novel method inspired by geometric morphometrics. Since the three loci form a triangle in space, we performed an optimal superimposition to align the triangles, without scaling, over their barycenters using the R package “paleomorph” with default parameters. The triangle’s shape causes all its vertices to align within a 2D plane (Supplementary Fig. 7d). To enhance visualization, an orthogonal projection was performed using PCA of the optimal superimposition (Supplementary Fig. 7d)78. This process ensured that the original data’s positional identity and distances were maintained despite the alignment. Kernel density calculations were employed to examine the spatial occupancy of each locus. Comparison between conditions was conducted by evaluating the area at a specific kernel density across various density levels.

cHi-C data analysis

Generation of the cHi-C matrices

Capture HiC samples were analyzed using the TADbit pipeline88 (https://github.com/3DGenomes/tadbit) which was used to (i) check the quality of the FASTQ files; (ii) map the paired-end reads to the M. musculus reference genome (release mm10 downloaded from http://igenomes.illumina.com.s3-website-us-east-1.amazonaws.com/Mus_musculus/UCSC/mm10/Mus_musculus_UCSC_mm10.tar.gz) using bowtie2 v.2.3.5.1 (https://bowtie-bio.sourceforge.net/bowtie2/index.shtml) with command line “bowtie2 -x bowtie2Index -p 8 --reorder -k 1” accounting for the restriction-enzyme (DpnII) re-ligation-sites (fragment-based mapping)79; (iii) remove non-informative reads using the default TADbit filtering options88,89. Differences between replicates (2 per each of the 11 conditions) were measured using the Stratum-adjusted Correlation Coefficient (SCC) applying HiCRep v1.12. (https://github.com/TaoYang-dev/hicrep) on the capture region (chr18:52,439,203-57,439,739) using the get.scc function with parameters resol = 20 kb, lbr = 0 bp, ubr = 2 Mb, h = 1 which was previously trained using the htrain() on the NPC wild type replicates90. Using 1.0-SCC, as a measure of the similarity (0 - similar and 1 dissimilar) between replicates and hierarchical clustering analysis using hclust() function in R with Ward.D2 method allowed to distinguish and group together the replicates of the different conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1c) motivating us to merge the valid-pairs of different replicates in a unique dataset per each condition. The total numbers of sequenced reads, uniquely mapped reads, and valid read-pairs per sample are reported in Supplementary Data 8. The numbers of contacts within the capture region (cisContacts), between the capture region and chromosome 18 (transContacts), and between regions outside the capture region (outContacts) were measured with in-house bash scripts and reported in Supplementary Data 8. .cool files were generated using cooler v.0.9.1 (https://cooler.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html) from the cisContacts only at 100 bp resolution (command “cooler cload pairs --assembly mm10 --chrom1 1 --pos1 2 --chrom2 6 --pos2 12./scripts/chrom_sizes.txt:100”)91. .mcool files at various resolutions 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 40 kb were obtained and normalized via the Iterative Correction and Eigenvector decomposition algorithm (ICE) with default parameters (command “cooler zoomify -r 2000,5000,10000,15000,20000,40000 file.cool -o file.mcool --balance”)92. Applying the iterative correction normalization algorithm only to the capture region ensured a seamless convergence of the method for all samples. When comparing architectural features between different conditions, down-sampled datasets have been obtained by randomly selecting a number of cisContacts equal to the minimum within the compared datasets. Namely, ESC mutants were down-sampled to the ESC wt dataset (4,043,665 cis-contacts) and NPC mutants to the NPC wt dataset (6,596,808 cis-contacts). Map resolution of 5 kb for the entire cHi-C dataset (including down-sampled and single-replicate samples) has been defined as the smallest locus size such that 90% of loci in the entire capture region have at least 1000 contacts. The map resolution is meant to reflect the finest scale at which one can discern architectural features reliably73. Valid interactions were stored in a database using the “misha” R package (https://github.com/msauria/misha-package). The “shaman” R package [https://bitbucket.org/tanaylab/shaman] has been used for computing the cHi-C expected models with parameters grid_small = 0.5e6 bp and grid_high = 1e6 bp using the function shaman_shuffle_hic_track() and cHi-C scores (Figs. 1a, 2a, 3a and 5e, Supplementary Figs. 1d–k, 4d, 5d, 6a, e, i and 10a) with parameters k = 250 and k_exp = 500 using the function shaman_score_hic_mat(). The compared datasets were down-sampled to have the same number of valid pairs in the capture region and used as the observed and the expected models. For instance, in the differential cHi-C map of Fig. 5f the ΔCTCFA dataset was considered as the observed and the ΔA as the expected signal. The maps are represented with a color scale from −100 (blue—depleted contacts with reference to the expected model) to 100 (red—enriched contacts with reference to the expected model) showing the KNN (K=250 nearest neighbour) normalized score.

Each chain is initially organized in a rod-like folding featuring rosettes along the main axis, and placed in random positions inside a cubic simulation box of side 600σ with periodic boundary conditions54,99. The conformations after 1200 τLJ are next used as the initial conformations for the downstream simulations.

Biophysical modelling

Using the data generated in this work, we informed and parameterized three distinct mechanisms: compartment interactions, loop-extrusion, and site-specific looping shape the structural organization of the Zfp608 region32,46,50,107,108. In the following, we detail the parameters involved in each mechanism, the strategy to parameterize the models, and the downstream data analysis of the obtained structures.

Model parameters

Short-range interactions were used to test the attractions between the model regions, which correspond to A/B compartments as identified in Bonev et al. and the site-specific chromatin looping3. These interactions have been modelled using attractive Lennard-Jones potentials (see Eq. 2) with cutoff = 2.5σ, which allowed the inclusion of the attractive part of the Lennard-Jones potential. The strengths of compartments’ interactions (εAA, εBB, and εAB) were varied in the range of 0.00–1.40 kBT. To infer from the cHi-C data the strength of each enforced loop in the wt conditions, we assigned a rank ri (from 0 to Nloops−1) to each detected loop (see Section Analysis of looping interactions) by ordering them for decreasing number of total counts (we consider as loop anchors 10 kb-regions around the central nucleotide of the detected looping region). The attraction strength of the loop i, was defined as εL,i = (Nloops−ri)/NloopsεL, where εL was varied in the range 0.00-4.00 kBT.

The loop-extrusion process was regulated by two players (Fig. 6a): the extruders and the barriers. In our model, extruders bind on chromatin, start two-sided extrusion, and keep extruding until the end of their lifetime or until they reach the first or the last monomer in the chain. In any of these three cases, extruders are relocated to a random position on the polymer chain. Extruders can cross each other upon encounter and the extrusion speed was set to 1 kb/s (Fig. 6a)51,52,109. We tested various values of extruders’ density on chromatin, Ne = 4, 8, 16, and 32 extruders/Mb, and constant nominal extruders’ lifetime, Le = 5-24 min. Notably, due to the finite size of the chain and the effect of barriers, the effective lifetime (the time going from the binding to the unbinding of one extruder from the model chromatin in the simulation) can differ from the nominal one. Hence, we refer to and report the effective average lifetime of the simulation. The positions of loop-extrusion barriers have been determined by looking at the maxima of the insulation score (IS) profiles. The barriers could stop extruders from both directions with a probability (1-p), where p is the permeability of the barrier. As previously proposed, once one extruder engaged with a side of an active barrier, it was paused there for a pausing time tp, that is barrier and direction-specific, tpL (for extruders arriving from the left side of the barrier) and tpR (for extruders arriving from the right side of the barrier)50. During this pausing, the extruder could continue extruding in the unaffected direction (one-sided extrusion) until the extruder dissociates and re-associates elsewhere. Per each barrier, we tested several values of permeability in the range [0.00–1.00] and pausing time in the range [0-6 min]. The models’ particles assigned to A/B compartments, loop anchors, and loop-extrusion barriers in each condition are reported in Supplementary Data 6. All the set of parameters tested are reported in Supplementary Data 7 for each of the simulated conditions.

Model parameterization

To test each of the parameters’ sets, we performed MD simulations for the time needed to extrude ~500 Mb of chromatin in 50 independent replicates (Supplementary Video 1). Next, we computed the number of contacts per polymer bead of 5 kb within a distance threshold of 100 nm during the first half and the complete trajectories. From the models’ contact maps, we computed the number of contacts as a function of the genomic separation (P(s)), the normalized insulation score profile, and the number of contacts between the looping anchors. Given the considerable number of parameters to optimize, we decided to follow a supervised optimization strategy. Starting with the ESC and NPC wt conditions, we tested the compartment-driven attractions alone. These preliminary tests showed that the simulations with purely homotypic interactions (εAB = 0) or not-equal cis- and trans-attractions compartment attractions (εAA = εBB and εAA = εAB) don’t recover the cHi-C P(s) Supplementary Fig. 8a, b. Next, we tested the loop-extrusion process varying Ne and Le with εAA = εBB = εAB = 0 with no barriers and found constraints for Ne < 8 and Le ~ 20 min (Supplementary Fig. 8c). We, then, optimized the permeabilities and pausing-time at barriers with a supervised approach first varying the parameters for the barriers with higher insulation scores, BR and BL, that delimit the large central TAD in the region and then for all the others (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). Finally, we optimize the strength of the site-specific looping attractions, εL. This procedure resulted in a few sets of optimized parameters per condition, which differ for small adjustment of two or three parameters and resulted in very similar values of the optimized metrics. To model the NPC mutations, we perturbed the optimal parameters found in NPC wt. For the NPC Δprom and NPC polyA conditions, we tested an increase of the permeabilities at 3′ and promoter by 0.05, 0.10 and 0.15, an increase of looping strength εL = 4 kBT, and a tuning down of the promoter-anchored looping attractions. For NPC ΔA, we set the permeability and lifetimes at A, and the strength of A-anchored looping interactions to 0 and tested a tuning-down of the promoter-anchored looping interactions. For NPC ΔAB, in addition to the NPC ΔA perturbations, we also set the strength of B-anchored looping interactions to 0 and tuned the permeability at the promoter to 0.65 to account for the increased insulation at this point. For NPC ΔB, we set the strength of B-anchored looping interactions to 0 and tested a decrease of the permeability at the promoter and of the promoter-anchored looping interactions. For ΔCTCFA, we tested an increase of the permeability and a decrease of the looping interactions at A, and a decrease of the permeability at the promoter and 3′. To tune down the looping attractions anchored at the Promoter or A we introduced a weighting factor (LPf and LAf respectively) of 0.25, 0.50, or 0.75 to the attraction strength value.
