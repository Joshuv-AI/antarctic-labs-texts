Boosting buccal drug absorption: Mechanistic insights into bilosome-mediated delivery
Authors: Eleftheria Pantazoglou, Scarlett Zeiringer, Matteo Tollemeto, Nazanin Zanjanizadeh Ezazi, Zhongyang Zhang, Leticia Hosta-Rigau, Jette Jacobsen, Ramona Jeitler, Eva Roblegg, Line Hagner Nielsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Effective buccal drug delivery is limited by the barrier properties of the mucosa, necessitating innovative systems to enhance permeability without compromising tissue integrity. In this study, bilosomes composed of sodium glycodeoxycholate and phosphatidylcholine were evaluated as a nanoparticulate platform for buccal drug delivery. Their in vitro uptake was investigated using the TR146 buccal cell line. The bilosomes demonstrated stable physicochemical properties and no aggregation. Functional assays indicated that they transiently opened cell-cell junctions, promoting transport across the mucosal barrier while minimizing toxicity. Quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring confirmed specific interactions with barrier components, supported by observed modulation of desmosomal junctions and cellular uptake. Ex vivo studies using porcine buccal mucosa further showed concentration-dependent distribution. Collectively, these results suggest that bilosomes are a safe and effective platform for enhancing buccal drug absorption.

Introduction

Oral administration is the most widely used route for drug delivery. Nevertheless, its effectiveness can be hindered by factors such as changing pH and enzymatic degradation throughout the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.(Lou et al., 2023) The buccal route presents a promising alternative enabling drug absorption directly into systemic circulation via the mucosa. The buccal tissue is non-keratinized, maintaining a near-neutral pH of 6.5 and exhibits low enzymatic activity, characteristics that all help protecting drugs from degradation.(Kim and De Jesus, 2025; Malhotra et al., 2025) In the buccal epithelium, primarily desmosomes ensure cell-to-cell adhesion and provide essential mechanical strength, cohesion, and elasticity throughout the tissue, allowing resistance to constant movement and barrier protection.(Perl et al., 2024).

As an administration route, despite bypassing the first-pass effect of the GI-tract, buccal drug administration can still result in low bioavailability due to limited permeability of the stratified mucosa, its complex tissue architecture and rapid drug clearance by saliva.(Shojaei, 1998) Enhancing drug permeability across the buccal mucosa is necessary for improving bioavailability for most drugs; however, this must be achieved without compromising mucosal integrity. Therefore, it is essential to explore delivery systems that temporarily enhance permeability without injuring the mucosa due to their mode of action.

Nanoparticles have emerged as a versatile and effective strategy for enhancing transmucosal drug delivery, due to their ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs and facilitate controlled drug release thereby enhancing drug bioavailability.(Zhang et al., 2024) Nanoparticles have demonstrated to be a promising strategy for buccal drug delivery in various studies.(Sahatsapan et al., 2022),(Kraisit et al., 2021) This includes the work of Morales et al. showing that insulin-coated nanoparticles incorporated into Eudragit®-based buccal films enhanced permeation across a buccal tissue model, highlighting the role of polymer-nanoparticle interactions in optimizing mucosal delivery.(Morales et al., 2014).

For improving drug absorption through the buccal epithelial barrier several permeation enhancers such as surfactants, fatty acids and bile salts are often used, especially for peptide administration.(Macedo et al., 2020) Bile salts are frequently utilized permeation enhancers in the GI tract and have additionally gained attention for their potential to improve drug absorption through the buccal mucosa by interacting with mucosal lipids. The effects of 0.1 M di- and tri-hydroxy bile salts on ex vivo porcine buccal permeability using FITC as a model drug compound showed a 100- to 200-fold increase in permeability. Nevertheless, histological analyses showed notable structural changes in the epithelium after treatment, including cell loss and tissue separation.(Senel et al., 1994) Another recent approach combined mechanical deformation of the buccal mucosa with permeation enhancers (among them, one bile salt) using a suction patch inspired by octopus' suckers. In dog models, this method led to an increase in bioavailability of the peptide desmopressin by up to two orders of magnitude compared to commercial tablets.(Luo et al., 2023) Despite this encouraging results, the use of bile salts raises key safety concerns, including tissue irritation, cellular damage, and the rate of mucosal recovery.(Şenel and Hıncal, 2001) Moreover, the relationships between the chemical structure of bile salts, their irritation potential and permeation-enhancing efficacy are still not well understood.

This study used the concept of nanoparticles and the effects of bile salts as permeation enhancers to create bilosomes and to investigate their potential in delivering therapeutic compounds through the buccal mucosa. Bilosomes are lipid-based vesicular carriers incorporating bile salts within their phospholipid bilayer structure. They have demonstrated enhanced stability compared to conventional liposomes, owing to bile salts' capacity to protect the vesicles from enzymatic degradation and harsh pH conditions.(Shukla et al., 2008) Bilosomes have demonstrated the capacity to enhance mucosal permeation, positioning them as promising carriers for targeted mucosal drug delivery in physiological environments that are comparatively less harsh than the gastrointestinal tract. One study demonstrated that elastic (highly deformable) bilosomes prepared with various bile salt derivatives improved insulin transport across TR146 buccal epithelial cell layers.(Bashyal et al., 2018) Bilosomes showed high encapsulation efficiency, enhanced cellular uptake and permeability, with sodium glycodeoxycholate (SDGC)-loaded bilosomes showing the greatest enhancement on insulin. Although bilosomes have demonstrated potential in buccal drug delivery, their interactions with key biological barrier components, such as mucins and other components of the cell membrane, remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, the present study encompassed in vitro, and ex vivo models to clarify bilosome behavior and interactions at the buccal surface (Fig. 1) and investigated how bilosomes influenced epithelial junctions and modulated permeation pathways across the buccal barrier. Materials & methods

Lipoid S80 (lecithin phosphatidylcholine from soybean, 73–79 % pure) (LPC) was purchased from Lipoid GmbH (Ludwigshafen am Rhein, Germany) and cholesterol (CHOL) was from MP Biomedicals (Illkirch, France). Polyethylene glycol 2000 (PEG2000) and sodium glycodeoxycholate (GDC) and mucins from bovine submaxillary glands (Type I—S) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA), whereas Rhodamine B was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Eugene, USA). Methanol (MeOH) and chloroform were purchased from VWR Chemicals (Radnor, USA). Human buccal TR146 cells were obtained from Imperial Cancer Research Technology (London, United Kingdom). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), phosphate buffered saline (PBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin streptomycin (PEST), and 0.25 % trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (trypsin-EDTA) were all acquired from Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, Painsley, United Kingdom. Dynasore hydrate (purity of 100 %), chlorpromazine hydrochloride (purity of 98 %), genistein (purity of 99 %), 5-(N-Ethyl-N-isopropyl) amiloride (EIPA, purity of 100 %), and non-essential amino acid solution (NEAA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Munich, Germany). Alexa Fluor 488 Phalloidin (purity of 99 %) and Hoechst 33342 were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Vienna, Austria) and HyClone was from GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Logan, United States. Ultrapure water 18.2 MΩ·cm (Milli-Q®, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) was used for all experiments.

Bilosome synthesis and characterization

The bilosomes were prepared using a thin film hydration method. Specifically, the quantities listed in Table 1 were accurately weighed and dissolved in 12 mL of a 1:1 mixture of chloroform:MeOH. After ensuring complete dissolution of the components, nitrogen gas was introduced to the solution to facilitate solvent evaporation, resulting in the formation of a thin film. Following solvent evaporation, 10 mL of ultrapure water was added to rehydrate the film. In experiments where bilosomes were labelled, rhodamine-B (0.05 mg/mL) was dissolved in ultrapure water for rehydration. Physicochemical characterization of bilosomes

Bilosomes were synthesized and characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS) for their size and polydispersity index (PDI) using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK) in single-use disposable microcuvettes (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). The zeta (ζ)-potential was investigated by laser Doppler electrophoresis using the Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical). A volume of 700 μL of the sample was measured in folded capillary cells (Malvern Panalytical) with 10 runs in 3 measurements (N = 3, n = 10). Nanoparticles were prepared with varying amounts of cholesterol, and their size was measured. Size was also measured after incubation (37 °C for 1 h) with serum-free cell culture medium and with buffer solutions at different physiologically relevant pH values.

The nanoparticles' morphology was evaluated through cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) using a CryoTitan (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a field emission gun and autoloader and operated at 300 kV acceleration voltage in low-dose bright-field TEM mode. Samples for cryo-TEM were prepared by glow-discharging the grids (Quantifoil Cu grid with R 2/2 holey carbon films, Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH, part of the SPT Life Sciences group, Hatfield, PA, USA) in a Cressington 208 carbon coater for 40 s. Then, 3 μL of the bilosome solution was pipetted on the grid and blotted in a Vitrobot MARK IV (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at RT and 100 % humidity. The grid was blotted for 3 s (offset −3) and directly plunged and vitrified in liquid ethane. Cryo-TEM images were acquired with zero loss energy filtering mode (Gatan GIF 2002, 20 eV energy slit. Gatan, Inc., Walnut Creek, CA, USA) on a CCD camera (Gatan model 794. Gatan, Inc., Walnut Creek, CA, USA).

QCM-D analysis of bilosome interactions with biological barrier components

To investigate the interaction between bilosomes and components of biological barriers, two sets of experiments were conducted. First, experiments were performed using submaxillary mucin.(Ammam et al., 2024) Additionally, experiments were carried out with phosphatidylcholine, the primary component of epithelial cell membranes.(Kio et al., 2024) Quartz Crystal Microbalance with Dissipation (QCM-D) measurements were conducted using an E4 system from Q-Sense (Gothenburg, Sweden). This was equipped with gold-coated quartz crystals (for the mucin measurements) and silicon dioxide-coated quartz crystals (for the phosphatidylcholine measurements) having a fundamental frequency of 4.95 Hz (QSX301,Q-Sense, Gothenburg, Sweden) using an adapted protocol as previously described.(Pantazoglou et al., 2025) The sensors underwent a thorough cleaning process: first, they were rinsed with a solution of ultrapure water, NH3, and H2O2 in a 5:1:1 ratio, followed by washing with ultrapure water and ethanol 98 % v/v, and then dried using nitrogen gas. Experiments were performed at a controlled temperature of 37 °C with a flow rate of 50 μL/min and 100 μL/min for the submaxillary mucin (0.2 mg/mL) and the phosphatidylcholine (0.1 mg/mL), respectively. During the experiments, changes in frequency (Δf) and energy dissipation (ΔD) were simultaneously recorded at the third, fifth, seventh, ninth, 11th, and 13th overtones. Initially, ultrapure water was pumped through the flow cells to stabilize the Δf and ΔD signals. Next, the mucin dispersion or the phosphatidylcholine solution was introduced into the flow cells to allow the layer to deposit onto the sensor surface.(Frost and Svedhem, 2013) Once the Δf and ΔD signals stabilized again, ultrapure water was flushed through the system to remove any loosely bound components. Following this, the bilosome dispersion (0.2 mg/mL) was flushed through the system to investigate their interactions with the deposited layer. Finally, ultrapure water was flushed through the system once more to remove any loosely bound material, and this continued until the Δf and ΔD signals stabilized again. The experiment utilized three sensors, and the data presented focuses on the seventh harmonic overtone.

Culturing of TR146 cells

TR146 cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO₂ using DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS, 1 % PEST, and 1 % NEAA in T-25 flasks. The medium was changed every two to three days. After reaching confluency, the cells were sub-cultured once a week using 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA. Serum-free DMEM without phenol red was used for experimental procedures.

Cytotoxicity of bilosomes on TR146 cells

To explore the potential cytotoxic effects of bilosomes, cell viability and membrane integrity were assessed. For this, TR146 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Frickenhausen, Germany) using a seeding density of 3 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. After washing the cells with PBS, they were incubated with various bilosome concentrations (i.e., 10, 50, 100, 250, 500, 750, 1000 μg/mL related to the LPC concentration) in serum-free DMEM at 37 °C for 1 h (n = 6). All concentrations mentioned further in the manuscript are based on the LPC concentration. Changes in cell viability were evaluated by a CellTiter 96 Aqueous Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS, Promega Corporation, Madison, United States) according to the manufacturers' instructions. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm after 3 h of incubation using a microplate reader (CLARIOstarPlus, BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany). Untreated blank-corrected wells were used as control representing 100 % cell viability. Cell membrane integrity was tested using CytoTox-ONE Homogeneous Membrane Integrity Assay (Promega, Corporation, Madison, WI, USA), which determined the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release of the cells after 1 h incubation with bilosomes according to the manufacturers' instructions. Fluorescence measurements were performed at an excitation and an emission wavelength of 544 and 590 nm, respectively, with an UV–Vis Plate reader (CLARIOstarPlus, BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany). 100 % LDH release was triggered by adding 2 % of a lysis solution to untreated wells and served as positive control. In addition, all data were blank corrected. Measurements were performed twice while maintaining a constant incubation time of 1 h.

Uptake studies of bilosomes across TR146 cells

TR146 cells were seeded in 8-well chamber glass slides (Falcon®, Corning, Arizona, United States) using a seeding density of 2.5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 1 week. To assess whether active or passive transport mechanisms were involved in nanoparticle uptake, cells were incubated for 1 h with rhodamine B-labelled bilosomes diluted in serum-free DMEM at a final concentration of 10 and 50 μg/mL (related to the lipid concentration) at 37 °C (i.e., active transport, n = 6) and 4 °C (i.e., passive transport, n = 6). Untreated cells incubated at 37 °C for 1 h were used as control for the uptake studies (n = 6). The bilosomes were then removed, and the cells were incubated with fresh medium for 3 h.

In parallel, inhibitors that selectively inhibit active transport pathways were used according to Jeitler et al. to identify the precise uptake mechanism (i.e., caveolin- or clathrin-mediated, macropinocytosis).(Jeitler et al., 2022) TR146 cells were seeded in 8-well chamber glass slides (Falcon®, Corning, Arizona, United States) with a seeding density of 2.5 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 1 week. Visualization of cell–cell contacts upon bilosome incubation

To investigate the potential permeation-enhancing effects upon bilosome incubation, the intercellular connections, i.e., desmosomes, were visualized according to the protocol by Tetyczka et al.(Tetyczka et al., 2021) For this, TR146 cells were seeded in 24-well Transwell systems (1 × 105 cells/well) and incubated for 3 weeks. The transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER; World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA) was measured during the 3-week period to observe cell growth and membrane integrity. Following the 3-week period, cells were incubated with bilosomes at different concentrations (10 and 50 μg/mL) and for different incubation times (1, 3 and 4 h). Furthermore, 0.25 mM EDTA diluted with serum-free medium was added to the control wells and incubated for 1 h. These served as a control for cell destabilization. After a washing, fixation and blocking procedure according to the manufacturers' instructions, cells were labelled with the primary antibody (i.e., mouse anti-Desmoglein 3 Monoclonal Antibody, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted with PBS to a final concentration of 4 μg/mL for 3 h at room temperature. Mouse IgG1 kappa isotype control (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used as negative control and was treated the same as the primary antibody. Moreover, PBS was added to control wells, which were not treated with the primary antibody. After 3 h, cells were washed and incubated with the secondary antibody (i.e., 4 μg/mL diluted with PBS containing 0.2 % BSA) for 45 min (goat anti-mouse IgG1, Alexa Fluor 568, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Cell nuclei were counterstained using Hoechst 33342 (24 μg/mL). Images were acquired with CLSM. The desmosome staining was detected at 543 nm excitation wavelength using a LP 560 nm long pass detection for the red spectral region.

Ex vivo permeability studies of bilosomes using porcine buccal epithelium

The effects of bilosomes on ex vivo porcine buccal tissue were visualized. Cheeks from healthy experimental pigs (approximately 30 kg, Danish Landrace/Yorkshire/Duroc from the Department of Veterinary and Animal Sciences at University of Copenhagen, Denmark) were collected immediately after euthanization and kept in PBS on ice until use. Procedures were according to the authorization by Danish Veterinary and Food Administration (license number: 2020-15-0201-00610). The cheeks were trimmed using a scalpel and rinsed with PBS. To separate the epithelial layer from the underlying connective tissue, samples were subjected to heat treatment by incubation at 60 °C in a water bath for 4 min, after which the epithelial layer was gently peeled off. It was then mounted in 3D-printed custom-made permeable support devices. The bilosomes, in different concentrations with respect to lipid concentration (10, 50 and 500 μg/mL, n = 3), were pipetted (1 mL) onto the buccal epithelium in the donor compartment. The receptor volume was maintained at 1.5 mL, with PBS serving as the medium throughout the duration of the study. The experiment lasted 1 h, during which 200 μL samples were collected from the receptor compartment at specified time intervals (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 min). The compartment was replenished with 200 μL of fresh receptor fluid to maintain consistent conditions. After the experiment, the collected samples were analyzed using a Varioskan LUX Microplate Reader (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, USA). Following the experiment, the tissue used was directly imaged without any further posttreatments using Leica TCS SP8 Coherent anti-Stokes Raman Scattering (CARS) microscope. Raman signal at 2850 cm−1 was used to visualize the lipid-rich tissue, while two-photon excited fluorescent signals were detected from the rhodamine-B labelled bilosomes.

Results & discussion

Bilosome characterization

Bilosomes were successfully synthesized using cholesterol, LPC, GDC and PEG2000. Cholesterol was incorporated to improve the rigidity and stability of the vesicles, thereby maintaining their structural integrity.(Liu et al., 2000; Ohvo-Rekilä et al., 2002; Mannock et al., 2010) LPC served as the primary phospholipid component, enabling bilayer formation and promoting self-assembly into nanosized vesicles.(Li et al., 2015; Waghule et al., 2022) GDC, a bile salt, was included for its well-documented permeation-enhancing properties, which are anticipated to facilitate transport of the nanoparticles across the buccal epithelium.(Senel et al., 1994; Pavlović et al., 2018) PEG2000 was incorporated to provide steric stabilization, thus reducing vesicle–vesicle interactions and preventing aggregation.(Liu et al., 2003) Additionally, the presence of PEG2000 helps nanoparticles to avoid rapid clearance, making it a key component for drug delivery applications.(Tenchov et al., 2023) Collectively, these components were chosen to provide a balance between stability, permeability, and biocompatibility. The bilosomes' size and PDI were characterized by DLS. Increasing cholesterol concentration led to a reduction in nanoparticle size and variability, and a reduction in PDI (Fig. 2A). At 30 mg (2.50 mg/mL) cholesterol, bilosomes exhibited the smallest size (117.2 ± 1.1 nm) and lowest PDI (0.233 ± 0.006). Lowering cholesterol to 20 mg (1.67 mg/mL) increased both the size (123.6 ± 0.91 nm) and PDI (0.25 ± 0.009), with further reduction to 10 mg (0.83 mg/mL) yielding larger (146.3 ± 26.83 nm) and more polydisperse particles (PDI = 0.330 ± 0.006). These effects likely resulted from cholesterol-induced bilayer hydrophobicity, reducing surface free energy and particle size.(Bernsdorff et al., 1997) These findings are in accordance with previous literature, where it has been reported that an increasing cholesterol amount from 20 to 40 % led to about 20 and 27 % decrease in the vesicle size of Brij 72 and Span 60 niosomes.(Nowroozi et al., 2018).Fig. 2Bilosome characterization using DLS and cryo-TEM; A) Particle size (nm) (mean ± SD) and PDI (mean ± SD) and B) zeta potential (mV) for bilosomes fabricated using different cholesterol amounts. At 30 mg cholesterol, bilosomes exhibited a ζ-potential of −33.2 ± 2.60 mV, indicative of high colloidal stability (Fig. 2B). In comparison, formulations with 20 mg and 10 mg cholesterol showed less negative values (−23.2 ± 1.82 mV and − 28.1 ± 2.20 mV, respectively). The more negative ζ-potential at higher cholesterol content suggests enhanced electrostatic stabilization, likely due to improved bilayer packing and increased surface charge density. As ζ-potential exceeding ±30 mV are associated with stable dispersions, the 30 mg formulation was selected for further experiments.(Ge et al., 2019; Ahmed et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022).

Stability of the bilosomes was evaluated by monitoring size and PDI after incubation in buffer solutions of varying pH and serum-free cell culture medium (Fig. 2C). Cryo-TEM confirmed their bilayer morphology (Fig. 2D). In ultrapure water, bilosomes had a size of 160.8 ± 1.56 nm with a PDI of 0.286 ± 0.01. At physiological pH of 7.4, size increased to 182.7 ± 2.25 nm with improved consistency (PDI 0.197 ± 0.01). A gradual size increase was observed upon decreasing the pH from 6.2 to 5.3 (185.4–187 nm), with slight changes in PDI (0.207–0.209). At pH 4.5, particles remained stable (179 ± 0.84 nm, PDI 0.23 ± 0.01), but significant destabilization occurred at pH 3.5, where the size increased to 365.4 ± 8.21 nm and the PDI to 0.59 ± 0.01. The pH-dependent instability at very low pH likely results from bile salt protonation, reducing electrostatic repulsion and promoting particle aggregation. Additionally, buffer composition may contribute to observed size changes due to hydrodynamic effects.(Stepan et al., 2022) DLS analysis confirmed bilosome destabilization at pH 3.5, as evidenced by a shift and broadening of the size distribution curve (Fig. 2E, F). To assess compatibility with in vitro conditions, bilosomes were evaluated in serum-free medium (pH 7.2–7.4), where size increased to 177.5 ± 5.35 nm with a PDI of 0.24 ± 0.03. This slight increase suggests minor interactions with components of the serum-free medium, consistent with previous reports.(Meesaragandla et al., 2022) However, the extent of size change remained minimal, confirming that subsequent experiments in serum-free conditions would provide reliable and physiologically relevant results.

Interactions of bilosomes with biological barrier components

Mucoadhesion is essential for buccal drug delivery, enhancing residence time and drug permeation.(Salamat-Miller et al., 2005) To investigate bilosome interactions with mucosal components (i.e., submaxillary mucins and phosphatidylcholine, representative of mucus and epithelial membranes, respectively) QCM-D was used (Fig. 3A, B). Changes in frequency (Δf) and dissipation (ΔD) provided insights into mass adsorption and viscoelastic properties, revealing bilosome interactions with both physiological components. In both cases, an initial adsorption phase (Phase I) was used to deposit either mucin or phosphatidylcholine onto the sensor surface, followed by a washing step (Phase II) to remove loosely bound material. Upon exposure to the bilosome formulation (Phase III), noticeable changes in frequency and dissipation were observed for both surfaces, indicating mass uptake and structural modification of the adsorbed layers. For the mucin-coated sensor (Fig. 3A), the observed decrease in Δf and slight increase in ΔD suggest interaction between bilosomes and the mucin layer, leading to increased layer thickness. The net frequency shift upon bilosome addition was approximately 11 Hz, indicative of significant adsorption. However, after the final rinse with ultrapure water (Phase IV), the net shift decreased to 3 Hz, implying a relatively weak interaction, as some bilosomes were removed while a portion remained associated with the mucin layer. In contrast, the phosphatidylcholine-coated sensor (Fig. 3B) exhibited a larger net Δf shift of approximately 20 Hz following bilosome addition, along with a notable increase in ΔD, suggesting stronger interaction and structural reorganization. Importantly, the frequency shift persisted after the final washing step, indicating a stable and strong interaction between bilosomes and the phosphatidylcholine layer, with minimal removal during rinsing. As described in literature, bile salts can interact with various mucins, such as bovine submaxillary, porcine gastric, and rat intestinal mucins, potentially altering their structure and function, which could influence mucosal barrier properties. Furthermore, bile salts have been observed to interact with phosphatidylcholine vesicles, leading to their structural reorganization. (Martin et al., 1978; Hildebrand et al., 2002; Wiedmann et al., 2004) Our measurements indicate that bilosomes exhibit relatively weak interactions with the mucin layer, which may permit their diffusion through the mucus. Cytotoxicity of bilosomes on TR146 cells

Systems employing permeation enhancers are aiming at sufficiently disruption for epithelial barriers to enhance absorption, while minimizing both local and systemic toxicity.(Danielsen, 2021) As bile salts have been reported to cause injury to the epithelial cell membrane, it was necessary to assess the potential undesired cytotoxic effects of bilosomes.(Oumi and Yamamoto, 2000; Maillette de Buy et al., 2010) The selection of a suitable concentration for therapeutic applications is guided by cytotoxicity studies, which assist in identifying possible harmful effects on cells. Therefore, cell viability and membrane integrity were next investigated and compared to the untreated controls. The results showed significant decrease in cell viability after 1 h incubation for bilosomes at concentrations above 250 μg/mL (Fig. 4). This correlated with an increase in LDH release, suggesting cell membrane disruption. An initial bile salt-induced structural alteration, involving cristae remodeling and disintegration of the inner boundary membrane near the outer mitochondrial membrane, has been observed as an early damaging event of bile salts(Schulz et al., 2013). The highest studied concentration of 1000 μg/mL significantly decreased the cell viability to 17.1 ± 2.10 %. Our findings are consistent with previous reports, suggesting that such enhancers promote membrane gelation, thereby contributing to enhanced nanoparticle permeation and uptake.(Arvizo et al., 2010; Hansen et al., 2018; Jeitler et al., 2022) Interestingly, in this study, it was observed that in TR146 cells, higher concentrations of bilosomes led to a reduction or plateau in cellular uptake, when comparing 10 to 50 to 100 μg/mL, potentially due to the saturation of endocytic pathways.(Desai et al., 1997; Yin Win and Feng, 2005) This observation aligns with increased LDH release and decreased cell viability at higher concentrations from our cytotoxicity studies, suggesting that cellular stress responses may limit further nanoparticle internalization as a protective mechanism, while also contributing to increased cytotoxicity.(Augustine et al., 2020).

The buccal mucosa, here modeled by TR146 cells, is a non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with moderate barrier properties compared to intestinal epithelium.(Jacobsen et al., 1995) Unlike the intestinal epithelium, it contains no tight junctions; instead, its main permeability barrier resides in the upper third of the cell layers, where a lipid-rich domain is concentrated.(Nair et al., 2023) Notably, its cell-cell junctions are weaker than those in other biological barriers, such as the GI lining, making it more permissive to passive diffusion, particularly for nanoparticles that contain membrane-disrupting components like bile salts. Disruption of cell-cell integrity can compromise barrier function. Therefore, changes in levels of endogenous or exogenous factors that influence cell-cell junction protein expression or activity can have profound effects on the permeability of the epithelial barrier.(Hegyi et al., 2018) Several studies have demonstrated that cell-cell junctions' function is modulated by luminal bile acids, including sodium glycodeoxycholate, a conjugated secondary bile acid, which has been shown to alter epithelial permeability by affecting cell-cell junctions protein dynamics and signaling pathways involved in barrier regulation.(Brayden and Stuettgen, 2021).

The TR146 cell line, derived from human buccal carcinoma, is widely used as an in vitro model for studying buccal drug delivery.(Lin et al., 2020) Under normal physiological conditions, the membranes of TR146 cells carries a net negative surface charge, primarily due to the presence of negatively charged phospholipids and sialic acid residues on glycoproteins and glycolipids.(Jacobsen et al., 1995; Nielsen and Rassing, 2000) Although the bilosomes exhibit a net negative surface charge as shown by the z-potential measurements, the presence of PEG and bile salts in the bilosomes could modulate their interaction with TR146 cells. PEGylation can sterically shield the particles' surface charge, potentially reducing repulsion from the negatively charged cell membrane and stabilizing the particles in the extracellular environment.(Suk et al., 2016) Simultaneously, bile salts are known to fluidize cell membranes, promoting closer contact and possibly facilitating passive transcellular uptake. Their distinctive properties enable them to increase transcellular permeability by modifying the polarity of the lipid environment.(Chacko et al., 2020) Additionally, their mucolytic activity further aids in drug penetration.(Chakravarthy et al., 2024) These observations also align with the QCM-D experiments, where we showed that the bilosomes strongly interact with phosphatidylcholine, the main component of the lipid bilayer of the cell membrane. Together, these surface modifications may enable effective cellular interaction and uptake through passive mechanism, even in the context of electrostatic repulsion observed between similarly charged surfaces.(Foroozandeh and Aziz, 2018).

Loss of cell–cell contacts (desmosomes)

To further investigate the mode of action of bilosomes and their effect on cell-cell contacts of TR146 cells, their effect on desmosomes was visualized. Desmosomes are intercellular junctions that facilitate robust cell–cell adhesion, thereby ensuring tissue integrity in response to mechanical stress.(Müller et al., 2021) They are abundant in the buccal and are also found in TR146 cells.(Jacobsen et al., 1995) Therefore, an indication of opening of the desmosomes would imply the permeation of bilosomes through the cell-cell area. The cell control (not shown here) showed no red fluorescence, indicating intact intercellular junctions and a confluent cell layer. On the contrary, the positive control treated with EDTA, which led to complex formation with calcium ions and resulted in cell-junction opening, showed an increase in the red fluorescence (Fig. 7F). EDTA chelates calcium ions, which are essential for maintaining cell–cell adhesion, and is, therefore, anticipated to compromise barrier integrity.(Pell et al., 2021) Additionally, incubating the cells with pure 10 μg/mL of bile salt led to an increase in the red fluorescence but cell stress was also observed (Fig. 7C). Incubating the cells with 10 μg/mL or 50 μg/mL of bilosomes for 1 h (Fig. 7A and D) also led to red fluorescence, implying a loss of cell–cell contacts. In the case of 10 μg/mL, a time dependent effect could be observed, as an increase in red fluorescence was present after 3 h (Fig. 7B). In the case of 50 μg/mL, the fluorescence intensity was similar after 1 and 3 h (Fig. 7E). The permeability of bilosomes can be understood through two primary mechanisms. First, and relating to paracellular permeability, bile salts are known to enhance membrane fluidity. This increased fluidity can lead to a disruption of cell junctions between adjacent cells, thus increasing permeability through the paracellular space. Additionally, while not the primary mechanism, bile salts may reduce the concentration of calcium ions, which are crucial for maintaining desmosomes' integrity. This reduction further weakens the barrier between cells, contributing to enhanced permeability.(Chen et al., 2012) Another approach to understanding bilosome permeability is through transcellular permeability. Bile salts are amphipathic molecules, possessing both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions. This unique structure allows them to interact with and insert into lipid bilayers, facilitating membrane disruption. As a result, bile salts can increase permeability across the cell membrane, affecting the transcellular transport of substances.(Moghimipour et al., 2015) The hydrophobic regions of bile salts interact with the lipid components of cell membranes, while their hydrophilic regions engage with the aqueous environment. This dual nature allows bile salts to integrate into the plasma membranes of epithelial cells, behaving similarly to detergents.(Seddon et al., 2004) Once incorporated into the lipid bilayer, bile salts disrupt membrane structure by solubilizing membrane lipids, compromising the integrity of the cell membrane. This disruption leads to increased permeability, allowing substances to pass through the membrane that would normally be restricted. At high concentrations or following prolonged exposure, the damage to the membrane can become severe, resulting in cell lysis or necrosis (Martin et al., 1992; Perez and Briz, 2009). As described by Nicolazzo et al., at low concentrations, bile salts increase the presence of substances within the intercellular spaces, suggesting solubilization of intercellular lipids and enhanced diffusion via the paracellular pathway. At higher concentrations, substances are detected inside epithelial cells, indicating that bile salts may disrupt cell membrane lipids and increase permeability across cellular membranes.(Nicolazzo et al., 2005) The use of bilosomes, which incorporate bile salts in a controlled form, presents a promising strategy for enhancing epithelial permeability while mitigating the adverse effects associated with free bile salts. By harnessing the amphipathic nature of bile salts, these systems can promote transcellular transport by being taken up by TR146 cells and disrupting membrane integrity from the inside and/or within the plasma membrane. Additionally, bilosomes may also enhance paracellular permeability by transiently modulating cell-cell junctions, either through bile salt-mediated disruption or indirect effects such as binding to calcium ions, which reduces the availability of calcium needed to maintain desmosome stability. This temporary reduction in calcium-dependent adhesion can further facilitate the passage of substances between cells without permanently damaging the epithelial barrier. Ex vivo permeation of bilosomes across porcine buccal mucosa

To evaluate the permeation of bilosomes through ex vivo porcine buccal tissue, a qualitative assessment was performed using coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) microscopy (Garrett et al., 2012a, Garrett et al., 2012b; Lalatsa et al., 2015) After ex vivo permeation test, the tissue was directly examined using CARS, where a Raman shift of 2850 cm−1 was used to localize the lipid-rich buccal epithelium (in green), and a fluorescence signal indicating the rhodamine labelled bilosomes (in red). Rhodamine labelled bilosomes were observed permeating through the buccal epithelium, with increased permeation evident at higher concentrations. The initial concentrations of 50 and 100 μg/mL (Fig. 8A and B) were selected based on prior cytotoxicity data and their association with desmosome opening. To further investigate the concentration-dependent permeation effect, a higher concentration of 500 μg/mL (Fig. 8C), previously shown to be cytotoxic in TR146 cells, was also tested. Despite its cytotoxicity in vitro, this concentration was included due to the greater thickness and structural complexity of the ex vivo tissue, as well as a larger surface area that the bilosomes can be absorbed, as compared to the in vitro studies. CARS imaging revealed that increasing the applied bilosome concentration led to progressively greater accumulation of nanoparticles within the tissue, with the highest concentration (500 μg/mL) showing the most pronounced tissue localization. These qualitative observations suggested a concentration-dependent uptake pattern, which was further supported by quantitative analysis, as shown in Table 3. Intermediate time points in the ex vivo permeation study produced signals below the detection limit and were, therefore, not quantifiable. Only the cumulative percent recovery at 60 min is reported. The average rate of disappearance from the donor compartment and appearance in the receptor compartment was calculated as the total change divided by the experiment duration (1 h). For the receptor compartment, the rate equals the measured cumulative percent because t = 1 h. Tissue accumulation is reported as the final percent recovery at 60 min. At 50 μg/mL, approximately 54.9 % of the applied bilosomes were recovered in the tissue while 33.3 % remained in the donor compartment, and 11.9 % reached the receptor. At 100 μg/mL, tissue retention increased to 79.0 %, donor fraction decreased to 11.8 %, and receptor recovery was 9.2 %. Although, higher concentrations led to greater nanoparticle accumulation within the tissue, this did not translate into increased receptor flux. Instead, the tissue retained a larger portion of the applied dose, likely due to extensive cellular uptake by epithelial cells and adsorption within the intercellular spaces and extracellular matrix.

Previous literature has described and validated the ability of bilosomes to exhibit permeation through biological barriers (Aziz et al., 2019). The enhanced permeation of bilosomes through the buccal mucosa is attributed to the presence of bile salts in their composition, which increase vesicular deformability and facilitate penetration through cell–cell junctions.(El Zaafarany et al., 2010) Moreover, bile salts appear to enhance bilosome permeation through both paracellular and transcellular routes, as suggested by our uptake pathway studies. This effect may be attributed to the ability of bile salts to chelate calcium and other multivalent ions, which are essential for maintaining the structural integrity of cell-cell junctions such as desmosomes in the buccal epithelium. The removal of these ions promotes the loosening of intercellular connections, thereby increasing tissue permeability.(Gonzalez-Mariscal et al., 1990; Yin and Green, 2004) Additionally, bile salts can interact with membrane proteins, leading to their partial denaturation and unfolding, resulting in cellular swelling and reduced cohesion among epithelial cells.(Cremers et al., 2014) Although, this mechanism has been well characterized in keratinized tissues such as the stratum corneum, similar effects have been reported in buccal tissues, where bile salts have been shown to enhance drug permeation by disrupting epithelial integrity through both paracellular and transcellular pathways (Hanif et al., 2015). Consequently, although more nanoparticles are present within the tissue at higher applied concentrations, only a small fraction reaches the receptor compartment. Together, these results indicate that bilosomes efficiently penetrate and accumulate within the buccal epithelium, potentially acting as a tissue depot, while trans-tissue transport remains limited, suggesting that further optimization would be needed for systemic delivery.

Conclusions

In this study, bile salt-containing nanoparticles (bilosomes) derived from GDC were shown to enhance permeability across the buccal mucosa while maintaining a favorable cytotoxicity profile. Key findings include: (i) uptake of bilosomes by TR146 cells, (ii) modulation of cell-cell junctions, particularly desmosomes, (iii) high colloidal stability without aggregation or precipitation, and (iv) minimal cytotoxicity toward TR146 cells at concentrations up to 100 μg/mL, as supported by low LDH release. These results support our central hypothesis that incorporation of bile salts into nanocarriers enhances permeability, making bilosomes a promising and safe platform for mucosal drug delivery.

Compared with previous studies, which have primarily investigated bilosomes for oral and vaccine applications(Wilkhu et al., 2013; Aburahma, 2016), our work expands the scope of bilosomes to buccal delivery. While earlier work highlighted the stability and delivery potential of bile-salt vesicles(Aburahma, 2016; Wang et al., 2021), the present findings add new mechanistic insights by demonstrating that bilosomes can interact with biological interfaces and can modulate desmosomal junctions. Furthermore, although bilosome safety has been discussed broadly in the context of oral formulations(Zarenezhad et al., 2023), our data add evidence of biocompatibility in buccal epithelial cells, an area previously underexplored. Importantly, bilosomes accumulate in porcine buccal tissue in a concentration-dependent manner, highlighting efficient epithelial uptake while trans-tissue transport remains limited.

Overall, these findings highlight bilosomes as a novel buccal delivery system. By pairing stability with safe cell junction modulation, they present a dual innovation that strengthens their translational potential. Future work should focus on loading therapeutic macromolecules into bilosomes and evaluating in vivo pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Future studies could also explore whether bilosomes enhance permeation of co-administered drugs not directly incorporated into the vesicles. Additionally, mechanistic studies with advanced imaging and molecular tools may further clarify the pathways of mucosal interaction and guide optimization for specific drug classes.
