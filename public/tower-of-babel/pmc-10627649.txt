The effectiveness and safety of herbal medicine on suicidal behavior: A PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chan-Young Kwon, Boram Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Suicide is the leading cause of death worldwide. Herbal medicine (HM) has been reported to be related to clinical improvement of some risk factors for suicide including depression. This systematic review aimed to comprehensively investigate the effectiveness and safety of HM on suicidal behaviors.Methods:Fifteen electronic databases were searched to search relevant intervention studies, up to September 2022. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the modified Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. In the included studies, outcome on suicidal behavior were analyzed, and the effect sizes were presented as mean differences (MDs) or risk ratios (RRs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) through meta-analysis. The strength of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.Results:A total of 13 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included in this review. Two RCTs of HM in patients with suicidal behavior found significant benefits of HM as an adjunct to fluoxetine or cognitive therapy in improving symptoms of depression. In 11 RCTs of HM in patients with other conditions, there was no statistically significant difference between HM and antidepressants in cognitive disturbance (MD, 0.12; 95% CIs, −0.20 to 0.45), a subscale of the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD), and suicidal ideation (0.18; −0.16 to 0.53), an item of HAMD. The overall quality of the included studies was poor. The strength of evidence assessed by GRADE was low or very low.Conclusions:Though some of the studies reported significant benefits of HM in improving suicidal behavior in patients with depression, further clarification on some unsolved questions is needed in future well-designed clinical trials.

1. Introduction

Suicide is a major socioeconomic problem worldwide,[1] which has been further exacerbated by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.[2] According to an analysis of suicide mortality from 2000 to 2019 using the World Health Organization mortality database and the Global Burden of Disease Study, there were 759,028 suicide deaths in 2019. Suicide mortality has increased in 26 countries, including the United States, and disparities between countries have been observed.[3] A survey examining the prevalence of suicidal ideation worldwide in the context of COVID-19 found that 15.7% (range, 7.6%–24.9% by country) of the total sample reported having suicidal ideation in the past 2 weeks.[2] The survey also indicated that the youngest age group (range, 18–24 years) had the highest probability of having suicidal ideation compared to other age groups.[2]

Suicide is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon, primarily attributed to mental illnesses such as, but not limited to, depressive disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance use disorder.[4] As one of the major causes of suicide, depressive disorders contribute to suicidal behavior through complex mechanisms involving monoaminergic neurotransmitter systems, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) stress-response system, exogenous and endogenous stressors, genetics, and epigenetics.[5] In addition to diagnosed mental illnesses, adverse childhood experiences, feelings of hopelessness, and diathesis (the tendency to experience suicidal ideation and act on suicidal feelings) are also associated with suicidal behavior.[4] Furthermore, inequalities across countries,[6] regions,[7] and ethnicities[8] have also been identified as contributors to suicide mortality.

In East Asian countries such as China, Korea, and Japan, East Asian traditional medicines (EATMs) comprise an important portion of the medical system.[9] Two representative EATM modalities, acupuncture, and herbal medicine (HM), have been reported to have potentially beneficial clinical effects in major psychiatric disorders associated with suicide risk, including depressive disorders,[10,11] PTSD,[12,13] and substance use disorder.[14,15] Also, EATM modalities are often used in Western countries as a part of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM). According to a comprehensive survey of 21 European countries, 17.9% of the general population reported using CAM, including acupuncture.[16] Furthermore, up to 45% of conventional medicine professionals have reported using CAM.[17]

In addition to hopelessness, depression, and a lack of reasons to live, cultural factors play a significant role in predicting suicide attempts.[18] Therefore, effective suicide prevention strategies may include culturally relevant interventions. In this context, EATM may be considered a candidate for culturally relevant suicide prevention interventions, especially in East Asian countries. However, given the worldwide popularity of CAM, its potential impact is not limited to East Asian countries.

Despite accumulating evidence of the clinical benefits of HM in psychiatric disorders,[19] the possibility of using EATM modalities in the context of suicide has not yet been systematically investigated. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to comprehensively investigate the effectiveness and safety of HM on suicidal behaviors. The findings of this study may assist individuals at risk of suicide, clinicians managing suicide risk, and policymakers in establishing national suicide prevention strategies.

2. Methods

2.1. Study registration

The protocol for this systematic review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42022334384; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42022334384). This review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement (Supplementary 1, http://links.lww.com/MD/K574).[20]

2.2. Data sources and search strategy

Fifteen electronic bibliographic databases were comprehensively searched. The searched databases included 6 English databases (such as MEDLINE via PubMed, EMBASE via Elsevier, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature via EBSCO, Allied and Complementary Medicine Database via EBSCO, and PsycARTICLES via ProQuest), 3 Chinese databases (such as China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang data, and VIP Chinese Science and Technology Periodicals), 1 Japanese database (Citation Information by National Institute of Informatics), and 5 Korean databases (such as Oriental Medicine Advanced Searching Integrated System, Korean studies Information Service System, Korea Citation Index, Research Information Sharing Service, and Korean Medical database). To identify potentially missing literature, the reference lists of relevant review articles were reviewed. Additionally, a manual search was conducted using Google Scholar. The search terms used were “suicide” and “HM,” and were modified to match the language of each database. The search date was September 7,2022, and all studies published up to that date were screened and reviewed. The search strategies and results used in this database are presented in Supplementary 2, http://links.lww.com/MD/K574.

2.3. Inclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were based on the following criteria: population (P), intervention (I), comparison (C), outcomes (O), and study design (S). (1) P: No restrictions were placed on the population, including clinical and non-clinical populations, participants with or without suicidal behaviors, age, sex, or ethnicity. (2) I: Oral HM was allowed, including when used as adjuvant therapy. (3) C: No restrictions were imposed. (4) O: Any validated measure of suicidal ideation, such as the Beck scale for suicidal ideation,[21] was considered the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes included other measures of suicidal behaviors, such as suicidal ideation, attempts, or complete suicide, and the safety profile of the intervention. (5) S: Since suicidal behavior is a rare subject in clinical research, all types of intervention studies were included, such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), nonrandomized controlled trials (CCTs), and before-and-after studies. Observational studies, including cohort studies, case series, and case reports, were excluded. There were no restrictions on the language or publication format of the included studies.

2.4. Study selection

After removing duplicates from the initially searched articles, the selection for this review proceeded in 2 steps. First, the titles and abstracts of the articles were reviewed and evaluated for potential relevance. Articles whose potential relevance was acknowledged or unclear in this step progressed to the next step. Second, the full texts of the articles were carefully reviewed, and if an article was excluded in this step, the reason for the exclusion was recorded. Only studies that met the inclusion criteria were included in the systematic review. Management of the collected bibliographic information and study selection were conducted using EndNote software (version 20, Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA). The process was performed by 2 independent researchers (C.Y. Kwon and B. Lee), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

2.5. Data extraction

In the included studies, the following data were extracted using a predefined and pilot-tested extraction method in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA): the first author name, publication year, sample size and dropout rate, participant details, treatment intervention and comparison, duration of the intervention, outcome measures, adverse events, and information for assessing the risk of bias. Two independent researchers (C.Y. Kwon and B. Lee) performed the data extraction. Any missing or ambiguous data were requested via email from the corresponding author of the study.

2.6. Quality assessment

Although any type of interventional study was allowed, only RCTs, not CCTs or before-and-after studies, were included in this systematic review. Therefore, the methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool developed by the Cochrane group.[22] Using this tool, the methodological quality of the included RCTs was assessed in 5 domains, including bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of the outcome, and bias in the selection of reported results.[22] Each domain was evaluated as having a low risk of bias, a concern, or a high risk of bias. The process was performed by 2 independent researchers (C.Y. Kwon and B. Lee), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

2.7. Data synthesis and analysis

All the included studies were qualitatively analyzed. Although patients with and without suicidal behaviors were included in this systematic review, the 2 populations were distinguished in the analysis. Specifically, studies on patients with and without suicidal behavior were analyzed separately. If the same continuous or dichotomous variables were reported in 2 or more studies, a meta-analysis was conducted. The software RevMan 5.4 (the Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK) was used to conduct the meta-analysis. Meta-analyses of continuous or dichotomous variables were shown as mean differences (MDs) or risk ratios (RRs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To assess statistical heterogeneity in the meta-analysis, the χ² test and the I² statistic were used. In each meta-analysis, substantial heterogeneity was considered to exist if the I² values were >50%.[23] Regardless of the calculated statistical heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used, given that this systematic review was not limited to patient characteristics and potential clinical heterogeneity could exist. To explore the causes of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on the type of population (clinical or nonclinical) and the duration of the intervention. Also, a sensitivity analysis was performed by removing studies with a high risk of bias and numerically distant outliers to evaluate the robustness of the meta-analysis.

2.8. Assessment of reporting bias

As there were no meta-analyses with more than 10 studies, it was not possible to generate a funnel plot to assess publication bias.

2.9. Assessment of evidence quality

The certainty of evidence on meta-analysis findings was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.[24] After considering study limitations (risk of bias), inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias of each finding, the certainty of evidence was determined as “High,” “Moderate,” “Low,” and “Very Low.”

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

Out of the 12,227 articles that were initially retrieved from the electronic journals, 508 duplicates were excluded, and 9 more were added from other sources. The titles and abstracts of 11,728 articles were screened, resulting in the exclusion of 11,708 irrelevant articles. The full texts of the other 20 articles were reviewed to determine whether they met the inclusion criteria. As a result, 2 case reports,[25,26] 3 studies with no suicide-related outcomes,[27–29] 1 study using herbal injections,[30] and 1 study using the same HM in the treatment and control groups[31] were excluded. Finally, 13 articles[32–44] were included in this review (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of this review.

3.2. Characteristics of included studies

All studies included in this review[32–44] were published between 2005 and 2019 and were RCTs; no CCTs or before-after study designs were included. Of the included studies, 7[33–35,38,39,41,43] were published in journals; 5[32,37,40,42,44] were theses; and one[36] was a conference proceeding abstract. All included studies were conducted in China, with most[32–44] published there. With the exception of 2 3-arm RCTs,[34,40] the remaining studies[32,33,35–39,41–44] were 2-arm RCTs. The total number of participants across all studies was 1236 (range, 43–209), with a mean of 95.08 participants. Two studies[35,38] recruited participants at risk of suicide, with Liu et al[35] recruiting patients with depression following suicide attempts, and Li[38] recruiting patients with depression and suicidal ideation. The remaining studies[32–34,36,37,39–44] recruited patients with depression and reported suicidal behavior as an outcome but did not include suicidal behavior in their inclusion criteria. In total, 28 treatment or control groups were included, with 9 being the most common HM group (28.57%), followed by the fluoxetine group (n = 5; 17.86%), and the HM combined with fluoxetine group (n = 3; 10.71%). The average duration of the intervention across the studies was 41.43 days, with the exception of 2 studies,[36,40] while the rest[32–35,37–39,41–44] set the duration of intervention from 6 to 8 weeks (or 2 months) (n = 11; 84.62%). Similar to our protocol, studies involving patients with depression accompanying suicidal behaviors and others were analyzed separately. In 2 studies[35,38] involving patients with depression accompanying suicidal behavior, all outcomes were analyzed regardless of the type of outcome. In the remaining 12 studies[32–34,36,37,39–44] involving patients with depression, accompanying suicidal behavior was not mandatory, and only outcomes related to suicidal behavior were analyzed. One study used a tool developed to evaluate suicidal behavior, the Self-rating Idea of Suicide Scale (SIOSS).[45] Six studies[32,33,36,39–41] reported cognitive disturbance (i.e., guilt, suicide, and agitation) as a subscale of the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAMD),[46] 3 studies reported suicidal ideation as an item of the HAMD, and 1 study reported suicidal ideation as a subscale of the traditional Chinese medicine symptom scale (Table 1).

3.3. The methodological quality of included studies

There were no clear baseline imbalances between the treatment and control groups in any of the included studies. However, only 1 study[42] reported concealing the assignment order until the participants were recruited and assigned to the intervention. Therefore, only 1 study[42] was evaluated as having a low risk of bias arising from the randomization process. Two studies[32,36] with blinded participants and personnel were evaluated as having a low risk of bias due to deviations from intended interventions. However, the remaining studies were evaluated with concerns due to a lack of related information. Eight studies[32,34,35,38,41–44] were evaluated as having a low risk of bias due to missing outcome data by performing an intent-to-treat analysis or having no missing data. However, the remaining studies[33,36,37,39,40] performed per-protocol analysis and were evaluated as having some concerns or a high risk of bias, as missing data might depend on the true value. Three studies[32,36,41] with blinded outcome assessors were evaluated as having a low risk of bias in the measurement of the outcome, while the remaining studies[33–35,37–40,42–44] without relevant information were evaluated as having a high risk of bias. All studies were evaluated with some concerns regarding the selection of the reported results because the analytical intent, like the trial protocol or statistical analysis plan, was not available. Overall, 11 studies[33–40,42–44] were judged to have a high risk of bias, and 2 studies[32,41] were judged to have some concerns (Fig. 2).

3.5. Effectiveness of HM in patients with suicidal behavior (n = 2)

Two studies[35,38] of HM in patients with suicidal behavior commonly reported the total effective rate (TER), but it was not possible to perform a meta-analysis due to different study designs.

3.5.1. HM as add-on therapy (n = 2).

Liu et al[35] used HM combined with fluoxetine or fluoxetine alone for 20 days in 209 patients with depression after suicide attempts. The TER based on clinical symptoms was 97.12% (135 out of 139) in the treatment group and 82.86% (58 out of 70) in the control group, with a statistically significant difference (P < .05). Li[38] used HM combined with cognitive therapy or cognitive therapy alone for 6 weeks in 172 patients with depression and suicidal ideation. As a result, the total scores for HAMD in the treatment group were statistically significantly lower than those in the control group (11.86 ± 4.12 vs 15.26 ± 5.13; P < .01). Furthermore, the TER based on the reduction rate of HAMD was significantly higher in the treatment group (86.05 vs 69.76; P < .01).

3.6. Effectiveness of HM in patients with other conditions (n = 11)

3.6.1. HM as monotherapy (n = 8).

Eight studies[32–34,36,37,39,42,44] used HM as a monotherapy for patients with other conditions and reported outcomes related to suicidal behavior. According to the results of the meta-analysis, there was no statistically significant difference between HM and antidepressants in cognitive disturbance (MD, 0.12; 95% CIs, −0.20 to 0.45; I2 = 19%), a subscale of HAMD, and suicidal ideation (MD, 0.18; 95% CIs, −0.16 to 0.53; I2 = 74%), an item of HAMD (Fig. 3). Guo et al[39] used HM or antidepressants (such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs] and serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors) for 8 weeks in 180 patients with depression. The results showed that suicide ideation, an item on the traditional Chinese medicine symptom scale, was significantly higher in the treatment group than in the control group (3.55 ± 1.08 vs 2.98 ± 1.11; P < .01).

Meta-analyses comparing HMs and antidepressants (A) on cognitive disturbance as a subscale of HAMD and (B) on suicidal ideation as an item of HAMD. HAMD = Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression, HM = herbal medicine.

3.6.2. HM as add-on therapy (n = 3).

Three studies[40,41,43] used HM as an add-on therapy for patients with other conditions and reported outcomes related to suicidal behavior. The meta-analysis results showed no statistically significant difference between HM combined with antidepressants and antidepressants alone in terms of cognitive disturbance (MD, −0.37; 95% CIs, −0.89 to 0.16; I2 = 0%), which is a subscale of HAMD. Subgroup analysis based on the treatment period did not reveal any differences (Fig. 4). One study[43] used HM combined with fluvoxamine or fluvoxamine alone for 8 weeks in 88 patients with depression. The results showed that the total scale of SIOSS (4.76 ± 1.13 vs 6.87 ± 1.82), and its subscales, including despair (2.28 ± 0.56 vs 3.37 ± 0.85), optimism (0.67 ± 0.19 vs 0.94 ± 0.30), sleep (0.92 ± 0.26 vs 1.30 ± 0.35), and cover-up (1.03 ± 0.28 vs 1.52 ± 0.40) were significantly improved in the treatment group compared to the control group (all, P < .05).

Meta-analysis comparing HMs combined with antidepressants and antidepressants alone on cognitive disturbance as a subscale of HAMD. HAMD = Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression, HM = herbal medicine.

3.7. Safety of HM

Ten studies[32–34,36,37,39,41–44] reported safety data for HM. Among them, 8 studies[32–34,37,39,42–44] reported the number of patients with adverse reactions during the study period. According to the results of the meta-analysis, there was no statistically significant difference between HM or HM combined with antidepressants and antidepressants in the incidence of adverse events (RR, 0.55; 95% CIs, 0.28 to 1.07; I2 = 62%) (Fig. 5). In the study[36] comparing HM combined with maprotiline placebo and maprotiline combined with HM placebo, the Side Effects Rating scale was reported as a safety index, and the treatment group showed a statistically significant safety profile (3.53 ± 0.73 vs 5.94 ± 1.09; P < .05). In the study[41] comparing HM combined with SSRIs and SSRIs alone, the Asberg Rating Scale for Side Effects was reported, and there was no significant difference between the groups (1.94 ± 0.83 vs 2.11 ± 0.80; P = .377).

3.8. Evidence quality

The certainty of evidence for HM as monotherapy in patients with other conditions was “Low” to “Very Low.” The certainty of evidence for HM as add-on therapy was all “Low.” The individual study risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision downgraded the certainty of evidence for main findings (Table 3).

4. Discussion

4.1. Main findings of this review

This review aimed to investigate the effectiveness and safety of HM in addressing suicidal behaviors, including suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and completed suicide. After conducting a comprehensive search, 13 RCTs[32–44] were included in the analysis. Two RCTs[35,38] of HM in patients with suicidal behavior found significant benefits of HM as an adjunct to fluoxetine or cognitive therapy in improving symptoms of depression. However, suicidal behavior was not evaluated in these studies. In 11 RCTs[32–34,36,37,39–44] of HM in patients with other conditions, the SIOSS was the only validated suicidal behavior assessment tool (i.e., the primary outcome of this review). One study[43] reported that HM combined with fluvoxamine for 8 weeks compared to fluvoxamine alone for 8 weeks in patients with depression resulted in a significantly greater benefit on the total score of the SIOSS and its subscales, including despair, optimism, sleep, and cover-up. But in a meta-analysis of cognitive disturbance as a subscale of the HAMD (the secondary outcome of this review), no significant benefit was found for HM plus antidepressants compared to antidepressants alone. A comparison of HM and antidepressants showed that there was no significant difference between the interventions for cognitive disturbance, a subscale of the HAMD, and suicidal ideation, an item of the HAMD. There was no significant difference in terms of the incidence of adverse events between HM, HM combined with antidepressants, and antidepressants alone. The most frequently used herbs in the included studies were G uralensis Fisch. ex DC. and B falcatum L. The methodological quality of the included RCTs was poor, with 11 studies[33–40,42–44] rated as having a high risk of bias and the remaining 2 studies[32,41] having some concerns.

4.2. Interpretation of results

The included studies provided an overview of the current state of research on HM and suicidal behavior. Although the inclusion criteria did not restrict the population, all of the studies[32–44] focused on patients with depression. Suicidal behavior, however, is not limited to depressive disorders but is also observed in other mental conditions such as PTSD and insomnia[4,47] and non-clinical populations.[4] This suggests that studies on HM and suicidal behavior may be biased toward depressive disorders. This potential bias was also observed in a systematic review examining the effects of acupuncture, another well-known EATM modality, on suicidal behaviors. Although clinical studies have reported the potentially promising effects of HM on suicide-related mental illnesses such as PTSD,[48,49] insomnia,[50] and psychotic disorders,[51] suicidal behavior was not an outcome of interest in these studies. The findings of this review suggest that further clinical studies on the effects of HM on mental illnesses associated with suicidal behavior, in addition to depressive disorders, are needed.

In this review, a meta-analysis showed that there was no significant difference between HM and antidepressants as monotherapy for improving suicidal ideation in patients with depression. However, it should not be concluded that the effects of HM and antidepressants on suicidal ideation in patients with depression are equivalent. Therefore, further investigation may be needed to determine whether the insignificant difference between HM and an antidepressant as monotherapy for suicidal thoughts means equivalence by using an equivalence trial design. Moreover, the meta-analysis conducted in this review showed no significant benefit of HM as an add-on therapy to antidepressants for suicidal ideation as a subscale or item of the HAMD. However, a systematic review of 40 RCTs examining the efficacy and safety of HM on depression found that HM in combination with conventional Western medications significantly improved the total HAMD score compared to conventional Western medications alone (P < .00001).[11] It is possible that the number of studies included in the current systematic review was too small to detect a significant effect of HM on suicidal behavior, or that HM as an add-on therapy to antidepressants may not help reduce suicidal ideation in patients with depression. Herb-drug interactions can have both detrimental and beneficial effects,[52] which may be one factor that explains the insignificant add-on benefits found. So, there is no evidence to support the use of a combination of antidepressants and HM to reduce suicidal ideation in patients with depression.

The choice of antidepressants as controls may be inadequate when examining the effect of HM on the risk of suicidal behavior. However, the effects of antidepressants on suicide risk remain controversial. For example, a recent systematic review of observational studies identified associations between exposure to antidepressants and an increased risk of subsequent suicide.[53] However, some studies have reported that antidepressants do not have a significant effect on suicide rates[54] and may even have a protective effect on suicidal behaviors.[55] Therefore, evidence-based suicide prevention strategies, such as cognitive and dialectical behavior therapies, which have less controversy regarding their effectiveness in reducing suicidal behavior,[56] could potentially be better-controlled interventions.

The included studies in this review commonly used G uralensis Fisch. ex DC. and B falcatum L. as herbal components in HM. This is not surprising, as previous research on the effects of HM on depression found that these herbs were most commonly used for the treatment of depression.[11] G uralensis Fisch. ex DC. may be able to attenuate stress-induced behavioral and neurochemical impairments.[57] Licorice, one of its main ingredients, has a monoamine oxidase inhibitory effect that potentially mimics its antidepressant effects.[58] Another major component of this herb, liquiritin, exerts antidepressant effects by reducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation.[59] B falcatum L. has antidepressant effects associated with increased serum nerve growth factor and brain-derived neurotrophic factor,[60] relieving oxidative stress, regulating the HPA axis, and protecting neurons.[61] Furthermore, the antidepressant activity of this herb has been implicated in the central adrenergic mechanism[62] the serotonergic and noradrenergic systems.[63] G uralensis Fisch. ex DC. and B falcatum L. are also major components of Si-Ni-San, which is widely used for depression and has acute antidepressant activity mediated by the monoaminergic and neuroendocrine systems.[64] Since abnormalities in the serotonergic mechanism, dysregulation of the HPA axis, and neuroinflammation have been implicated in the neurobiology of suicidality,[5,65–67] further investigation is needed to determine the relationship between these underlying mechanisms and the potential anti-suicidal effects of HMs.

4.3. Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the number of studies included in the systematic review was insufficient, which contributed to the lack of studies in the meta-analysis, potentially contributing to the insignificance of the quantitative analysis. Secondly, except for one, most of the studies did not use validated tools to measure suicidal behavior.[43] Regarding the HAMD subscale analyzed in the meta-analysis, as it is not a validated assessment tool for evaluating suicidal ideation, there are inherent limitations in interpreting the results as a general effect of HM on suicidal behavior. Third, the overall quality of the included studies was poor. The strength of evidence assessed by GRADE was low or very low. None of the included studies was rated as having a low risk of bias using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. Because of this, a pre-planned sensitivity analysis based on the risk of bias was not possible with the protocol of this systematic review. The limitations in the methodological quality of the included studies indicate that the findings of the current review are likely to be influenced by later studies of higher quality. Finally, no more than ten studies were included in the meta-analysis, making it impossible to visually assess publication bias using funnel plot generation. Given that all included studies were published in a certain country (i.e., China), this could potentially indicate publication bias.[68] However, given the popular use of EATMs in East Asian countries, particularly in China, this deviation may be inevitable.

5. Conclusion

Some of the studies included in this review reported significant benefits of HM in improving suicidal behavior in patients with depression. However, the meta-analysis found no significant difference in the effect of HM as monotherapy or adjuvant therapy on suicidal behavior compared to antidepressants. This does not imply the equivalence of antidepressants and HM for suicidal behavior in patients with depression, and further clarification is needed in future well-designed clinical trials. Because of the effects of antidepressants on suicidal behavior, other control alternatives, like cognitive or dialectical behavior therapies, need to be considered. Additionally, the findings of this review highlight the need for further investigation of suicidal behavior in this field.
