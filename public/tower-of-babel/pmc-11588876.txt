Managing blood glucose control in the intensive care unit
Authors: Jan Gunst, Guillermo E. Umpierrez, Greet Van den Berghe
Published: 2024

Most critically ill patients develop insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, which are associated with mortality risk. However, the severity of hyperglycemia also reflects illness severity, highlighting the need for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to determine whether hyperglycemia directly impairs outcome [1]. We summarize RCT evidence and propose a practical framework for glucose control in intensive care unit (ICU) patients.

Landmark RCTs on tight glucose control

The interest in tight glucose control (TGC) sparked in 2001, after a RCT in surgical ICU patients in Leuven (N = 1548) showed significant morbidity/mortality benefit from lowering blood glucose into the normal range (80–110 mg/dL [4.4–6.1 mmol/L]) versus accepting hyperglycemia up to 215 mg/dL (11.9 mmol/L) [2]. Subsequently, the Leuven investigators confirmed benefits in medical (N = 1200) and pediatric ICU patients (N = 700) [3, 4]. Additionally, in children, 4-year neurocognitive outcomes improved [5]. Mechanistic studies attributed benefit to prevention of glucose toxicity rather than other insulin-mediated effects [1]. These data led many centers to swiftly adopt some form of glucose control as simple, cost-effective intervention. Yet, in 2009, the multicenter NICE-SUGAR RCT (N = 6104) reported increased mortality with TGC compared with maintaining blood glucose < 180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L), which was attributed to a 14-fold increase in hypoglycemia [6, 7].

The different results of TGC in Leuven and NICE-SUGAR fueled a long-standing debate regarding clinical implications. The opposing outcome effects have been attributed to methodological differences, including different TGC and nutrition protocols, and different control group targets [1]. NICE-SUGAR allowed the use of inaccurate glucometers, capillary and venous measurements (confounded by poor perfusion and intravenous glucose infusions, respectively), and insulin boluses. Together, these aspects may have induced prolonged/undetected hypoglycemia. In contrast, the Leuven TGC protocol included accurate glucose measurements on arterial blood, prevented/treated insulin-induced hypokalemia through simultaneous potassium monitoring, and only used continuous insulin infusion via a central venous catheter, which avoided large glucose fluctuations. However, the Leuven studies were conducted in the context of early supplementation of insufficient enteral nutrition by intravenous glucose and parenteral nutrition (PN), which may have exacerbated hyperglycemia to more harmful levels in the control group [8, 9]. In contrast, NICE-SUGAR patients received fewer parenteral calories in the acute phase, potentially increasing the risk of hypoglycemia with TGC.

Hence, efficacy and safety of TGC in the absence of early PN, combined with a protocol that strictly avoids hypoglycemia, remained unclear. To address this knowledge gap, the TGC-Fast multicenter RCT was conducted, in which 9230 ICU patients were randomized to accept hyperglycemia up to 215 mg/dL [11.9 mmol/L] or to TGC guided by the LOGIC-Insulin algorithm [10]. This computer algorithm had been validated previously through a multicenter RCT in patients not receiving early PN [11]. LOGIC-Insulin-guided TGC resulted in excellent glucose control and effectively avoided hypoglycemia, a finding later confirmed in the TGC-Fast RCT [10]. In TGC-Fast, TGC did not affect ICU dependency (primary endpoint) or mortality (safety endpoint), but it was associated with less severe acute kidney injury, lower need for renal replacement therapy, and decreased cholestatic liver dysfunction [10]. There were largely no subgroup differences for the primary and safety endpoints, except for a potential mortality benefit from TGC in neuro-ICU patients, which requires further study.

A recent meta-analysis, with individual patient data available for < 50% of eligible patients, suggested no benefit of TGC [12]. However, design and performance of included trials varied, with differences in measurement accuracy, insulin titration, nutrition strategy, blood glucose target, target attainment, and incidence of hypoglycemia [12]. Because of these confounders, subgroup analyses addressing the potential importance of a single confounder (often only available at the trial level) have limited added value.

Clinical implications: which target?

To extrapolate RCT findings to the bedside, the clinical context and individual study protocols must be considered.

Collectively, landmark RCTs have revealed the importance of avoiding severe hyperglycemia and iatrogenic hypoglycemia, and elucidated the confounding impact of early high glucose doses and early PN. Indeed, mortality harm by accepting hyperglycemia was only observed in such context evoking more severe hyperglycemia. In contrast, mortality harm by TGC was only observed when hypoglycemia was substantially increased. These findings support avoidance of early high glucose and PN, and suggest caution regarding liberal use of drugs aggravating insulin resistance, including corticosteroids, unless there is an evidence-based indication [1].

Once iatrogenic factors have been avoided, the optimal blood glucose target for ICU patients remains unclear but likely depends on available logistics (Fig. 1). TGC applied with accurate tools and a validated algorithm that prevent hypoglycemia and large glucose fluctuations may be appropriate to reduce morbidity [10]. Safe tight (80–110 mg/dL [4.4–6.1 mmol/L]) and intermediate (< 150–180 mg/dL [< 8.3–10 mmol/L]) blood glucose control requires a protocol that includes accurate and frequent measurements of blood glucose and avoids insulin boluses. Blood glucose is preferably measured using arterial blood and a blood gas analyzer, which yields a fast and accurate blood glucose measurement, and, simultaneously, a measurement of potassium (to act upon and prevent insulin-induced hypokalemia). Capillary blood glucose measurements are unreliable in ICU patients with poor tissue perfusion, and venous measurements may report falsely elevated blood glucose values during intravenous glucose infusion, increasing the risk of hypoglycemia. Short-acting insulin is preferably administered via continuous infusion through a dedicated lumen of a central venous line, to ensure continuous systemic availability and avoid large blood glucose fluctuations. When the arterial or central venous line is no longer needed, when the patient is being discharged to the ward, or when oral feeding is initiated, a more liberal approach to glucose control is advised, with insulin used only to maintain levels below 181–215 mg/dL [10–11.9 mmol/L]. Overly aggressive treatment of postprandial hyperglycemia may provoke hypoglycemia and large blood glucose fluctuations. In patients with known diabetes mellitus and in patients with persistent intravenous insulin need after resumption of oral feeding and at ICU discharge, transition to maintenance treatment (oral antidiabetics, subcutaneous insulin and/or other glucose-lowering treatments) should be performed in consultation with an endocrinologist to ensure long-term follow-up. Toward individualized glucose control?

Observational studies have suggested that ICU patients suffering from diabetes may benefit from higher targets because of adaptations to chronic hyperglycemia. However, the CONTROLING RCT (N = 2075) found no benefit by hemoglobin A1c-guided glucose control, although blood glucose considerably overlapped between groups [14]. In the LUCID RCT (N = 419), accepting severe hyperglycemia (180–252 mg/dL [10–14 mmol/L]) in ICU patients with type-2 diabetes reduced the risk of hypoglycemia (primary endpoint), but associated with 5% higher mortality [15], supporting prevention of severe hyperglycemia, also in diabetic ICU patients.

Strategies to improve quality and safety of glucose control

Any protocol for glucose control in ICU requires frequent and accurate measurements, which becomes particularly important with tighter targets. Regular potassium monitoring is also necessary to prevent insulin-induced hypokalemia. Although (near-)continuous glucose monitoring could reduce glucose variability, routine in-ICU use is off-label, and subcutaneous sensors lack accuracy for hemodynamically unstable patients [16].

Short-acting insulin is currently the preferred glucose-lowering drug in ICU. Theoretically, other drugs could be superior because insulin suppresses cellular repair processes that are important in critical illness [1]. For example, the potential benefits of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) -inhibitors, and the place of non-medicinal strategies that lower insulin needs, including alternative nutritional regimens and exercise, merit further study.

Take-home message

Current evidence supports avoiding iatrogenic factors that aggravate hyperglycemia, such as early-PN and liberal corticosteroid use, and does not support TGC, except with accurate tools and protocols that prevent iatrogenic hypoglycemia.
