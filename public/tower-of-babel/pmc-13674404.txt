H2FPEF Score and Charlson Comorbidity Index as Independent Predictors of Cardiovascular Outcomes in Elderly Patients Undergoing Geriatric Rehabilitation
Authors: Nicolas Dayer, Denise Auberson, Henri Lu, Elsa Costa, Hélène Krief, Sylvain Nguyen, Kristof Major, Wanda Bosshard, Roger Hullin, Pierre Monney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is difficult to diagnose in older adults. The H2FPEF score estimates the likelihood of HFpEF and has been associated with adverse prognosis, but its performance has not been evaluated in geriatric inpatients with a high comorbidity burden.HypothesisWe hypothesized that the H2FPEF score predicts adverse outcomes in geriatric inpatients independently of comorbidity burden.MethodsThis prospective cohort included consecutive patients admitted to a geriatric rehabilitation unit. All patients underwent functional and echocardiographic assessment at admission. The ability of the H2FPEF score, the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), and their combination to predict 1‐year all‐cause mortality (ACM) or cardiovascular rehospitalization (CR) was evaluated.ResultsAmong 302 patients (median age 83 years, 40% men), 24% had a H2FPEF score ≥ 6 indicating high HFpEF probability and 42% had a CCI > 3 indicating high comorbidity burden. The H2FPEF score correlated with CCI (β = 0.27, p < 0.01) and functional disability (lower Instrumental Activities of Daily Living score; β = −0.17, p = 0.003), but not with cognitive impairment. Both a high H2FPEF score and high CCI were associated with a higher risk of the combined outcome of 1‐year ACM or CR (adjusted HR 2.89 [95% CI 1.63–5.10], p < 0.001 and adjusted HR 2.66 [95% CI 1.67–4.21], p < 0.001, respectively), with no interaction between them (p = 0.63). Patients with both high‐risk scores had the highest risk (adjusted HR 4.66 [95% CI 2.54–8.57], p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn geriatric inpatients, a high H2FPEF score is common and independently identifies patients at increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes at 1 year.Keywords: Charlson comorbidity index, functional dependency, H2FPEF score, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, HFpEF, prognostic score

Introduction

The prevalence of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is high among older adults, accounting for at least two thirds of heart failure (HF) cases in the community [1, 2, 3, 4]. HFpEF diagnosis is based on the association of signs and symptoms of HF, a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50%, and on the objective evidence of cardiac structural or functional abnormality consistent with the presence of LV diastolic dysfunction and raised filling pressures [5]. To guide the clinician, relatively complex stepwise algorithms have been proposed, combining clinical and echocardiographic data, natriuretic peptide dosages and echocardiographic diastolic stress test results [6]. Simpler approaches include the HFpEF‐ABA score based on age, body mass index and the presence of atrial fibrillation (AF) [7], and the H2FPEF score, the score we chose to use for the present study as it also incorporates important echocardiographic data. It is a simple 9‐points score specifically developed to estimate the likelihood of HFpEF in patients with unexplained exertional dyspnea, a score ≥ 6 being associated with a > 90% probability (Supporting Information 1) [8]. In patients over 65 years, the score has demonstrated a sensitivity of 77% and a specificity of 97% [9]. Subsequent studies established H2FPEF score not only as a diagnostic tool, but also as a valuable metric to identify patients with more severe forms of HFpEF, to screen at‐risk patients for preclinical HFpEF, or to estimate the prognosis in terms of all‐cause mortality (ACM) or rehospitalization [10, 11].

Older adults admitted for a geriatric in‐patient rehabilitation program after surgery or acute medical hospitalization are at substantial risk of HFpEF due to older age and a high prevalence of comorbidities. Moreover, the symptom of dyspnea may not be specific for HF in such a population [12]. We therefore hypothesized that the systematic use of the H2FPEF score in patients with LVEF ≥ 50% attending an inpatient geriatric rehabilitation program should identify patients with high likelihood of HFpEF diagnosis and higher risk of cardiovascular endpoints, independently of the comorbidity load and the presence of a self‐reported symptom of dyspnea.

The first aim of this study was to assess the 1‐year prognosis in terms of a composite end‐point of ACM and rehospitalization for cardiovascular causes based on the H2FPEF score in a population of older (> 65 years) patients with LVEF ≥ 50% undergoing an inpatient rehabilitation program, as well as its relation with the comorbidity burden assessed with the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI). The second aim was to describe the prevalence of patients with an H2FPEF score ≥ 6 and to assess the relation of the H2FPEF score with indirect markers of HF (including dyspnea, echocardiographic evidence of elevated LV filling pressure and previous hospitalizations for HF) and with markers of comorbidity and functional impairment (including CCI, Basic Activities of Daily Living [BADL], Instrumental Activities of Daily Living [IADL] score and Functional Independence Measure [FIM] score).

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This observational, prospective, single‐center study was conducted at Lausanne University Hospital (Switzerland) from November 2021 to November 2022, approved by the Ethics Committee (CER‐VD 2021‐01693). All included patients provided written informed consent.

Participants and Data Collection

Consecutive patients aged > 65 years admitted for in‐patient geriatric rehabilitation after acute hospitalization, either for a medical condition (e.g., infection, HF, acute illness) or after a surgical intervention (most commonly non‐elective orthopedic surgery), were invited to participate and to undergo a research transthoracic echocardiography (TTE). Exclusion criteria were the absence of consent, LVEF < 50%, patients lost at 1‐year follow‐up, and missing echocardiographic parameters for H2FPEF score assessment. Baseline data were collected at admission by the main investigators (N.D., K.M., and D.A.) either by direct interview or by review of the charts, including medical history, actual symptoms, vital signs, ECG, and selected laboratory values. Functional geriatric evaluation scores were collected in all patients at admission by geriatricians, including BADL, IADL, FIM, and Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). However, MMSE was not repeated if it was already performed in the past 3 months, during the acute admission (Supporting Information 2). Comorbidity burden was assessed in all patients using the CCI, and a CCI > 3 was considered high comorbidity load [13].

Echocardiographic Assessment and H2FPEF Score Calculation

TTE was performed in all patients during the first week of admission by a certified technician on an EPIQ CVx machine (Philips, the Netherlands) and analyzed offline by a board‐certified cardiologist. A comprehensive examination including global longitudinal strain (GLS) and left atrial reservoir strain (LARS) was performed according to the current guidelines [14, 15, 16, 17]. Strain analysis was only performed on images of sufficient technical quality. Left ventricular filling pressures and diastolic function were assessed according to current guidelines [18, 19, 20]. Combining the clinical and echocardiographic data, the H2FPEF score was calculated for all patients, and the cohort was stratified into three classes of HFpEF probability: H2FPEF score ≤ 2 (low risk), H2FPEF 3‐5 (intermediate risk), and H2FPEF ≥ 6 (high risk) [8].

Follow‐up and End‐Points

Follow‐up at 1 year was complete for all patients. Vital status and hospitalization data were collected by telephone based on patient or proxy report and review of the available discharge documents. The primary endpoint was a composite of ACM or cardiovascular rehospitalization (CR), with censoring after the occurrence of the first event. Secondary endpoints were ACM, rehospitalization for cardiovascular causes, and rehospitalization for non‐cardiovascular causes, respectively.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared across the H2FPEF probability classes using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and were compared across the 3 H2FPEF classes using a Chi2 test. A p for trend was calculated for all variables according to Cuzick [21]. The correlations between the H2FPEF score and either dyspnea, previous HF hospitalization, or evidence of elevated filling pressures were first assessed by bi‐variate logistic regression and then by logistic regression with adjustment for age and sex. Similarly, the correlations between the H2FPEF score and either IADL score, FIM score, or CCI were first assessed by bi‐variate linear regression and then by linear regression with adjustment for age and sex. The combined outcome of ACM and rehospitalization for cardiovascular cause according to H2FPEF score groups were displayed using Kaplan−Meier curves and compared using the log‐rank test. The association of increasing H2FPEF probability classes, respectively high CCI, with the combined outcome were assessed using bivariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models (results reported as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals). Multivariable analyses were adjusted for age and sex, and interaction was assessed between H2FPEF high probability class and the presence of dyspnea, previous HF hospitalization and/or high CCI. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in STATA 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Study Population

A total of 1022 patients were screened for the study, and 302 patients were finally included for analysis after applying the exclusion criteria (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics are summarized in the Table 1. Median age was 83 [77–87] years and 40% were men; dyspnea was reported in 78 (26%), 48 (16%) had AF at the time of TTE and 33 (11%) had previous myocardial infarction. 84 (28%) had previous admission for HF, 110 (37%) moderate‐to‐severe renal failure and 74 (25%) chronic pulmonary disease. The median number of different medications was 7 [5, 6, 7, 8, 9] and a CCI > 3 was present in 128 (42%). Functional assessment revealed reduced independence (BADL score = 5.5 [4–6], IADL score = 4 [2–6]) and 83 patients (27%) had cognitive impairment at admission (MMSE ≤ 24). By TTE, median LVEF was 65 [61–68]%; 37 (12%) had segmental wall motion abnormalities and 48 (16%) left ventricular hypertrophy. Median GLS was 20.6 [18.1–23.0]% and median LARS was 25.2 [14.8–33.3]%. LV filling pressures were elevated by TTE in 112 (37%), normal in 156 (52%) and indeterminate in 34 (11%). Moderate or severe aortic stenosis was present in 31 (10%), ≥ 2+ mitral regurgitation in 14 (5%), and ≥ 2+ tricuspid regurgitation in 36 (12%).

One‐Year Prognostic Value of High H2FPEF Risk Class and Relation With Comorbidities

The median follow‐up duration was 387 [372−410] days. The outcome results according to H2FPEF risk classes are summarized in the Table 3 and in the Figure 2. The primary endpoint of ACM or rehospitalization significantly increased across the 3 H2FPEF groups from 17% in low‐risk to 44% in the high‐risk H2FPEF class (p = 0.0001). Compared with patients in low‐risk H2FPEF class, those in high‐risk class had higher risk of the primary endpoint after adjustment for age and sex (adjusted HR 2.89 [95%‐CI 1.63–5.10], p < 0.001). No evidence of effect modification was found for dyspnea or history of HF hospitalization (p for interaction 0.74 and 0.78, respectively). Likewise, compared to low CCI, a high CCI (>3 points) was associated with the primary endpoint with an adjusted HR of 2.66 [95%‐CI 1.67−4.21], p < 0.001. No interaction was found between a CCI > 3 and an H2FPEF score ≥ 6 for the association with the primary endpoint in multivariate analysis (p for interaction 0.63).

Compared to low‐risk H2FPEF class, the high‐risk H2FPEF class had a higher risk of the secondary endpoints of ACM (adjusted HR 2.63 [95%‐CI 1.28–5.39], p = 0.008) and rehospitalization for cardiovascular cause (adjusted HR 3.86 [95%‐CI 1.70–8.80], p = 0.001), but not of rehospitalizations for non‐cardiovascular causes (adjusted HR 1.24 [95%‐CI 0.78–1.94], p = 0.36). Conversely, a high CCI was also significantly associated with ACM (adjusted HR 4.30 [95%‐CI 2.99–8.04], p < 0.001) and non‐CR (adjusted HR 2.14 [95%‐CI 1.53–2.99], p < 0.001), but not with CR (adjusted HR 1.37 [95%‐CI 0.73–2.59], p = 0.33).

Association Between H2FPEF Risk Class and Surrogate Markers of HF and Functional Disability

The prevalence of dyspnea significantly increased from 20% in the low risk to 34% in the high‐risk class (p for trend 0.03). By logistic regression adjusted for age and sex, the H2FPEF score had a significant correlation with dyspnea (OR 1.23, 95%‐CI 1.08–1.40, p = 0.002). A similar stepwise increase in history of previous HF hospitalization was observed between low and high‐risk H2FPEF classes (from 11% to 61%, p for trend < 0.0001), and there was a significant adjusted correlation between H2FPEF score and history of HF hospitalization (OR 1.71, 95%‐CI 1.47–1.99, p < 0.001). Expectedly, elevated filling pressures estimated by TTE were present in 26% of low‐risk and 56% of high‐risk H2FPEF class patients, and the H2FPEF score had a significant adjusted correlation with elevated filling pressures (OR 1.27, 95%‐CI 1.21–1.43, p < 0.001). A significant linear correlation was observed between H2FPEF score and the CCI after adjustment for age and sex (β = 0.27, p < 0.01), as well as an inverse adjusted linear correlation of H2FPEF score with the IADL score (β = −0.17, p = 0.003). An inverse linear correlation was also found between H2FPEF score and FIM in bivariate analysis, which was no more significant after adjustment for age and sex (β = −0.08, p = 0.14).

Discussion

In a population with a median age of 83 years undergoing inpatient rehabilitation after acute hospitalization, our study provided several important clinical findings. First, one in four patients had a H2FPEF score ≥ 6, which had been associated with a > 90% probability of HFpEF diagnosis [8]. Second, the presence of a high‐risk H2FPEF score in older patients had a strong prognostic value as it was associated with a higher risk of ACM or CR after 1 year, even after adjustment for age and sex. Third, despite a significant correlation between the H2FPEF score and the CCI, both scores were independently (p for interaction 0.63) associated with the 1‐year primary endpoint. Fourth, although higher H2FPEF score was associated with a lower grade of independence in the activities of daily living, the cognitive performance remained equally preserved in patients with high and low risk H2FPEF scores.

HFpEF is a particularly challenging diagnosis due to the low specificity of dyspnea and/or peripheral oedema, and the multiplicity of coexisting comorbidities, such as AF, obesity, metabolic syndrome, sarcopenia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and chronic kidney disease [1, 12]. Moreover, it is recognized that typical HF signs may not be present on physical examination in the early stages of HFpEF [5], and the use of scores and biomarkers is recommended to improve detection. The H2FPEF score was derived and validated against invasive hemodynamic measurements in a relatively younger cohort of dyspneic patients with a mean age of 64 years, including two‐thirds with HFpEF diagnosis and one‐third with non‐cardiac dyspnea [8]. Therefore, its external validity in our cohort of older inpatients (median age 83 years) may be questioned. Yet, in the ARIC study, (mean age 75 years), among the 13% of the cohort complaining of unexplained dyspnea, a high‐risk H2FPEF score was present in 35% and their prognosis was comparable to the 10% of the cohort with a confirmed HFpEF diagnosis [22], supporting the validity of the score in older patients. Applied to a subset of the TOPCAT HFpEF trial, the H2FPEF score was shown to correlate with the risk of cardiovascular adverse events. However, the score was only partially and non‐significantly associated with natriuretic peptides and a high proportion (25%–41%) of those patients with confirmed HFpEF had H2FPEF scores indicating a low (< 55%) HFpEF probability. These findings emphasized the need to better understand the validity of this score in larger heterogenous populations [23]. In our study, we applied the H2FPEF score to a specific but heterogenous population of very old patients undergoing inpatient rehabilitation program after acute hospitalization for various indications, including orthopedic surgery, infections, or decompensated HF. In that population, a high proportion of 24% of patients had a H2FPEF score ≥ 6 indicating a high risk of HFpEF. Although neither invasive hemodynamic measurements nor natriuretic peptides were systematically available, several findings supported the presence of a significant cardiac involvement in the high‐risk H2FPEF group, including a high prevalence of previous HF episodes (72%) or hospitalizations (61%), a high prevalence of current (46%) or history of (100%) AF, lower LARS and lower TAPSE, as well as a high proportion of patients with elevated LV filling pressures (56%). Higher H2FPEF score was also associated with slightly lower LVEF and lower LV GLS, suggesting that subtle systolic dysfunction may contribute to HFpEF pathophysiology in older inpatients, in line with Dunlay et al. [24].

As reported in several studies, the H2FPEF score has a significant prognostic value [10, 11, 23]. This was also the case in our cohort, where patients with H2FPEF ≥ 6 had a two to threefold higher risk of death or CR at 1 year compared to patients with H2FPEF < 3. Furthermore, our population was characterized by polypharmacy and a high burden of comorbidity, as often seen in geriatric post‐acute care rehabilitation, and a high CCI (>3 points) was present in 42% of the patients. Like the H2FPEF score, a high CCI was associated with a two‐ to threefold higher risk to reach the primary endpoint at 1 year. Although both scores showed significant correlation, there was no interaction between the two scores for the association with the primary endpoint and, in patients with both an H2FPEF score ≥ 6 and a CCI > 3, the risk of adverse outcome increased to 4−5‐fold. Moreover, while H2FPEF ≥ 6 was associated with CRs, a CCI > 3 was associated with non‐CRs, suggesting that H2FPEF score and CCI should relate to different aspects—cardiac versus non‐cardiac, of adverse prognosis in our population. These findings illustrate the added value for clinical usefulness of both scores in guiding tailored management: the H2FPEF score to identify patients who may benefit from targeted cardiac interventions, and the CCI those in whom broader geriatric strategies addressing polypharmacy, frailty and non‐cardiac comorbidities may be warranted.

AF appears to play a particularly important role for outcome prediction in this population. It was highly prevalent in patients with a high‐risk H2FPEF score and had been shown to be associated with adverse cardiovascular outcome in several studies. In a propensity score matched analysis of the TOPCAT Americas trial including patients with HFpEF, AF was consistently associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular death, cardiovascular hospitalizations and HF hospitalizations over of median follow‐up of 2.9 years [25]. In a post‐hoc analysis of the AFFIRM trial, which randomized patients with recent AF to either rate or rhythm control strategy, HF occurrence was more frequent with a rate control strategy, and HF development was associated with a 3−4 times higher risk of cardiovascular mortality and cardiovascular hospitalizations at 5 years [26]. Interestingly, while it has a strong weight in the H2FPEF score, AF is not part of the CCI calculation; this could in part explain the specific association of H2FPEF score with cardiovascular adverse outcomes while CCI was merely associated with non‐cardiovascular adverse outcomes.

An overlap between HF and geriatric comorbidities is frequent among older patients, and the presence of physical, social, or cognitive limitations negatively impacts prognosis [27, 28, 29]. In our cohort, we only observed a significant correlation between the H2FPEF score and impaired autonomy (IADL score). However, although 27% of the cohort had cognitive impairment, no association was found between MMSE score and the H2FPEF score. In elderly patients with HF, a holistic approach is recommended, and admission to a rehabilitation unit offers an opportunity for interventions that may improve quality of life and outcomes. This may include exercise and physical rehabilitation, nutritional support, and cognitive support [27]. Whereas geriatricians are proficient to detect physical, social, or cognitive frailty in HF patients, HFpEF diagnosis remains a challenge in elderly patients. According to a recent survey, compared to HF specialists, both cardiologists and non‐cardiologists were significantly less likely to use specific HFpEF scores or natriuretic peptides, or to consider specific echocardiographic markers to identify HFpEF [30]. Therefore, despite well‐defined diagnostic criteria, there was a substantial risk of HFpEF underdiagnosis, especially for non HF‐specialists [30]. In line with those results, our study suggests that a systematic use of the H2FPEF score could be valuable to improve early detection of patients at highest cardiovascular risk, and timely access to optimal HFpEF management, including specific pharmacologic treatment, non‐pharmacological approaches with exercise prescription and weight management, as well as management of associated comorbid conditions like hypertension, diabetes, AF and chronic kidney disease [5, 12, 31].

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include its focus on a real‐world geriatric rehabilitation population, for which data are scarce in the HFpEF literature, as well as the systematic comprehensive echocardiographic evaluation combined with simultaneous geriatric assessment. Several limitations need to be mentioned. First, it was a single‐center study in a university clinic, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, only 30% of the eligible patients eventually participated to the study. The most common cause of exclusion was refusal or inability to consent, suggesting an inclusion bias toward patients with a lower grade of frailty. However, since most patients were excluded due to an absence of consent, it was not possible to compare the baseline characteristics of included vs excluded patients to confirm this hypothesis. Third, while the H2FPEF score was interpreted according to the published criteria, no invasive hemodynamic confirmation of HFpEF diagnosis was available; likewise, natriuretic peptides were not systematically performed. Fourth, outcome comparisons were performed according to three groups of H2FPEF score severity only; a comparison based on matched groups of patients could have provided more accurate outcome estimation, but the limited size of our cohort did not allow to perform such adjustment. Fifth, geriatric assessment was only based on few standard scores. While MMSE is an important screening tool for cognitive dysfunction, it is insufficient for a comprehensive evaluation of mental status. Furthermore, other metrics of frailty, including gait speed or grip strength, were not systematically performed and could not be included in our analysis. Sixth, although the cause of rehospitalization could be obtained from hospital files, the cause of death could not be ascertained for a majority of very old patients, especially if death occurred outside the hospital. For this reason, it was not possible to segregate the mortality outcome as cardiovascular versus non‐cardiovascular in our cohort. Seventh, some patients underwent rehabilitation following an episode of acute HF decompensation, which may have transiently altered echocardiographic indices of diastolic dysfunction and potentially influenced H2FPEF score classification and interpretation in these cases. However, Hullin et al. demonstrated E/e’ remained stable following decongestion in acute HFpEF patients (no significant change between admission and discharge TTE despite 1.8 kg weight loss) [32], supporting the validity of diastolic function assessment in our geriatric cohort. Finally, although follow‐up data were obtained for most patients, some outcomes relied partly on proxy reports, potentially introducing an information bias.

Conclusion

High‐risk H2FPEF score is present in one fourth and a high CCI in 42% of older patients admitted for rehabilitation after acute hospitalization. While both high‐risk H2FPEF score and high CCI were independently associated with an adverse cardiovascular outcome at 1‐year follow‐up, the association of both scores in the high‐risk range identified patients with 4‐to‐5‐fold higher risk of mortality or CR at 1 year. A systematic implementation of the H2FPEF score in this multimorbid population has the potential to not only to detect patients with a high likelihood of HFpEF diagnosis but also to improve the prognosis with a timely introduction of pharmacologic and non‐pharmacologic management of HFpEF and associated comorbid conditions.
