Ginger as a low-cost option for postoperative nausea and vomiting prevention after laparoscopic surgery in low- and middle-income countries: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Saad Arsalan Wasti, Areesha Wasti, Haris Bin Khalid, Beenish Sabir, Saif-Ur Rehman, Aiema Hamid, Eman Shafquat, Muhammad Usman Iqbal, Fatima Hajj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is a common complication following laparoscopic surgery and remains a significant source of patient discomfort and healthcare burden. Although pharmacologic prophylaxis is widely used, PONV incidence remains considerable, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resource constraints may limit access to newer antiemetics. Ginger (Zingiber officinale), a widely available and inexpensive herbal remedy, has demonstrated potential antiemetic effects in clinical trials. However, evidence remains inconsistent, and no focused synthesis has evaluated its role specifically in laparoscopic procedures in LMICs. This protocol outlines a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy and safety of ginger versus placebo for preventing PONV after laparoscopic surgery in LMICs.Methods:PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov will be searched through February 2026 to identify randomized controlled trials comparing ginger with placebo in adults undergoing laparoscopic procedures in LMICs. Gray literature, including Google Scholar and citation tracking, will also be searched. Study selection, data extraction, and risk-of-bias assessment will be conducted independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Primary outcomes will include PONV incidence. Secondary outcomes will include nausea and vomiting severity, rescue antiemetic use, and adverse events. Random-effects meta-analysis will be performed using risk ratios or mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. Pre-specified subgroup and sensitivity analyses will be conducted, and evidence certainty will be assessed using GRADE.Results:This review aims to provide a focused synthesis of randomized evidence evaluating ginger for PONV prevention after laparoscopic surgery in LMICs. The findings are expected to clarify its comparative efficacy and safety profile relative to placebo.Conclusion:The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis are expected to inform perioperative care strategies in resource-limited settings and clarify the potential role of ginger as a low-cost option for PONV prevention.

Introduction

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), defined as nausea and/or vomiting occurring within the first 24–48 hours after surgery, remains one of the most common and distressing complications following anesthesia[1,2]. Its incidence is nearly 30% in the general surgical population and up to 80% in high-risk populations, such as females[3–5]. Literature has reported that after certain surgical procedures, particularly laparoscopic procedures, the risk of PONV is substantially higher[5–7]. In the resource-constrained settings of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), laparoscopic surgeries continue to demonstrate a considerable burden of PONV, where reported rates frequently exceed 30–40%[8,9]. PONV contributes to dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, delayed recovery and discharge, wound complications, aspiration risk, increased healthcare costs, and reduced patient satisfaction[10].

Current recommended antiemetic strategies for PONV include drugs that target central and peripheral pathways involved in emesis and include dopamine (D2) antagonists (such as metoclopramide and droperidol), serotonin (5-HT3) receptor antagonists (including ondansetron and granisetron), antihistamines, anticholinergics, and neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists[11]. While these agents are effective, their use may be limited by adverse effects such as sedation, extrapyramidal symptoms, QT prolongation, and higher acquisition costs[11]. In LMIC settings, cost and drug availability remain important considerations, particularly for day-case laparoscopic procedures[12,13].

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) has been widely used in traditional medicine for the management of nausea and vomiting[14,15]. Experimental evidence suggests that its bioactive constituents, including gingerols and shogaols, may exert antiemetic effects through antagonism of serotonin (5-HT3) receptors[16]. Clinical trials have evaluated ginger in various contexts, including pregnancy-related nausea, chemotherapy-induced nausea, and postoperative settings, with generally favorable safety profiles[16–18].

Several randomized controlled trials have investigated the efficacy of ginger in preventing PONV following laparoscopic surgery[19–25]. However, findings remain inconsistent. Considerable heterogeneity exists regarding ginger formulation (powder, capsule, extract), dosage, timing of administration, type of laparoscopic procedure, comparator interventions, and outcome assessment methods. Furthermore, existing systematic reviews have either pooled heterogeneous surgical populations without examining ginger’s efficacy and safety for laparoscopic procedures or have focused on herbal medicine as a whole in laparoscopic procedures, instead of ginger in particular[26–28]. Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses have also not synthesized evidence from several randomized trials published in non-English languages, thus not fully capturing the existing evidence base[29].

Importantly, no previous systematic review has specifically examined the role of ginger for PONV prevention within LMICs. This represents a critical gap, as healthcare resource availability, access to pharmacologic antiemetics, perioperative protocols, and cost considerations differ substantially between high-income and LMIC settings. Given that ginger is inexpensive, widely accessible, and culturally accepted in many LMICs, understanding its effectiveness in these contexts has direct clinical and policy relevance[30,31]. Therefore, despite the growing body of randomized evidence, the overall efficacy and safety of ginger for preventing PONV after laparoscopic surgery in LMIC settings remain uncertain. A focused and methodologically rigorous synthesis is needed to clarify its comparative effectiveness and inform perioperative practice in resource-limited environments.

Methods

Study registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis has been registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) under the identifier OSF: 9PDWT (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/9PDWT)[32]. The present protocol has been reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols) reporting standards (Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/ISJP/A28)[33]. Methodological conduct will follow the principles outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions[34]. Where applicable, methodological transparency will also align with the Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews standards for systematic reviews (Supplemental Digital Content Table S2, available at: http://links.lww.com/ISJP/A28), as well as the Transparency in the Reporting of Artificial Intelligence guidelines for responsible use of AI[35,36].

Eligibility criteria

Study design

Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) will be included. Eligible studies must prospectively allocate participants to ginger or placebo groups using a randomization procedure. Non-randomized studies, quasi-experimental designs, observational studies, case reports, case series, reviews, and animal studies will be excluded. We define study eligibility based on actual study characteristics rather than the descriptions used by the authors[34]. We operationalize the definition of an RCT as a study that prospectively randomly allocates participants into intervention and control groups.

Participants

Studies enrolling adult patients undergoing laparoscopic surgical procedures will be eligible. Trials conducted exclusively in pediatric populations will be excluded.

Studies including mixed populations (e.g., laparoscopic and open procedures, adult and pediatric participants, or heterogeneous surgical cohorts) will be included only if data specific to the population of interest (adult patients undergoing laparoscopic procedures) can be extracted separately. Where such subgroup-specific data are not available, studies will be excluded to maintain the validity of pooled estimates for the relevant population of interest.

Interventions and comparators

The intervention of interest is ginger (Z. officinale), administered for the prevention of PONV. Any formulation, dosage regimen, or timing of administration will be considered eligible. The comparator must be a placebo. Trials lacking a placebo control arm will be excluded unless placebo-specific data can be extracted separately.

This restriction to placebo-controlled trials is intended to establish the intrinsic antiemetic efficacy of ginger, independent of comparisons with active pharmacologic agents. Existing evidence on ginger for PONV prevention remains inconsistent, with prior trials and meta-analyses reporting mixed findings[19–25]. In this context, comparison against placebo provides the most direct and internally valid assessment of whether ginger has a true therapeutic effect. Inclusion of trials with active comparators (e.g., ondansetron or metoclopramide) may introduce substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, particularly in LMIC settings where standard antiemetic regimens, drug availability, perioperative protocols, and dosing strategies vary considerably. Such variability may reduce the interpretability of pooled estimates and obscure the independent effect of ginger. Restricting to a single comparator would also retain the methodological consistency of the review. A placebo-controlled synthesis was, therefore, considered appropriate. If the results find ginger does not demonstrate meaningful efficacy versus placebo, further trials comparing it with active antiemetics may potentially not be justified. Conversely, demonstration of efficacy would support the rationale for future head-to-head trials against standard pharmacologic agents, which require greater logistical and financial resources, especially considering the additional costs of procuring, storing, and administering active comparators, particularly in LMIC settings. For multi-arm trials (e.g., ginger versus placebo versus active comparator), only the relevant ginger and placebo arms will be included, while other comparator arms will be excluded. This approach is consistent with guidance outlined in Chapter 5 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, under MECIR Box 5.3.b[34].

Outcomes

To be eligible, studies must report at least one efficacy or safety outcome. The primary efficacy outcome is the incidence of PONV as a composite outcome, and secondary endpoints include the incidence of postoperative nausea, the incidence of postoperative vomiting, the severity of nausea, the severity of vomiting, and the use of rescue antiemetic medication. Safety outcomes include adverse event rates. Data for the postoperative time points at 2, 3, 6, and 24 hours will be abstracted and analyzed; these are based on commonly reported intervals in existing literature on PONV prophylaxis and their clinical relevance in perioperative care[28].

Language and setting

Studies published in any language are eligible for inclusion. Only studies conducted in LMICs, as defined by the World Bank Income Group classification at the time of the conduct of the present review, will be included (see Supplemental Digital Content Table S3A, available at: http://links.lww.com/ISJP/A28).

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search will be conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov from database inception to February 2026. These databases were selected to maximize coverage of biomedical and clinical research literature. Gray literature will be explored using Google Scholar and citation chaining to reduce publication bias. Additional relevant studies may be identified through manual searches of relevant websites and academic platforms, such as ResearchGate.

Search strategy

Two reviewers will independently perform the search process. The search strategy will combine controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH and Emtree) and free-text keywords related to RCTs, laparoscopic surgery, and ginger (Z. officinale). Searches will be updated prior to the final analysis if more than 6 months have passed from the literature search to the point of analysis. Full search strategies have been provided in the supplementary file (Supplemental Digital Content Table S3B, available at: http://links.lww.com/ISJP/A28).

Study selection

All search results will be imported into Rayyan for management, duplicate removal, and screening[37]. Title and abstract screening will be conducted independently by two reviewers according to predefined eligibility criteria. Reviewers will be blinded to each other’s decisions during screening to reduce selection bias. Studies deemed potentially eligible will undergo full-text review. Discrepancies between reviewers will be resolved through discussion, and if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will adjudicate. The study selection process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram, detailing the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included.

Data extraction

Data extraction will be performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized and pilot-tested data extraction form. Extracted information will include study characteristics (author, year, country, and setting), baseline participant demographics, details of the laparoscopic procedure, ginger formulation and dosage regimen, comparator characteristics, outcome definitions and measurement tools, follow-up duration, and reported adverse events. Where data are incomplete or unclear, attempts will be made to contact study authors to obtain missing information. Extracted data will be organized into structured tables to facilitate comparison and synthesis. Discrepancies in extracted data will be resolved through discussion or third-reviewer adjudication.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included trials will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. The domains evaluated will include bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of outcomes, and bias in the selection of reported results[38]. Each domain will be rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” according to RoB 2 guidance, and an overall risk-of-bias judgment will be assigned. Assessments will be conducted independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. To enhance validity, retracted studies will be identified through searches of Retraction Watch prior to data synthesis.

Data synthesis

For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be calculated. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs will be used; if different scales are employed across studies, standardized mean differences will be calculated. A random-effects meta-analysis will be performed using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method with HKSJ adjustment as the primary analytical approach, in view of anticipated between-study variability. The DerSimonian–Laird (DL) estimator will be applied in sensitivity analyses to assess robustness. Prediction intervals will be calculated to estimate the expected range of treatment effects across future similar settings. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I2 statistic. If substantial heterogeneity is observed, potential sources will be explored through subgroup and sensitivity analyses. If most of the included trials are judged to be at high risk of bias or if data are insufficiently comparable, a narrative synthesis will be conducted instead of a meta-analysis[34]. Statistical analyses will be performed using R software (meta, metafor, metasens, readxl, and openxlsx packages).

Pre-specified subgroup analyses will explore differences according to the type of laparoscopic procedure, ginger dosage, formulation of ginger, timing of administration, and risk-of-bias category. Sensitivity analyses will include leave-one-out analyses to evaluate the influence of individual studies, the application of the DL estimator, and the exclusion of studies assessed as high risk of bias. These procedures will assess the stability and robustness of pooled estimates.

Publication bias

If ten or more studies are available for a given outcome, publication bias will be evaluated using funnel plots, with the presence of substantial asymmetry indicating possible publication bias.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty of evidence will be rated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework through the domains of risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias[39]. A summary of findings table will be generated using GRADEpro[39].

Ethics and dissemination

As this review involves the synthesis of data from previously published studies, ethical approval is not required. The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at relevant scientific conferences.

Discussion

Current evidence from randomized trials has provided mixed findings regarding ginger’s role as a potential antiemetic for PONV prevention after laparoscopic surgery. Several studies have reported a reduced incidence of nausea compared to placebo, while other trials have reported no statistically significant differences [19–25,29]. Prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses have also yielded inconsistent findings, often pooling data across all surgical procedures or all herbal medicines[26–28]. For instance, Chaiyakunapruk et al[26] concluded that ginger was significantly better than placebo for preventing PONV, whereas Lu et al[27] found no significant differences in the incidence rates of PONV when using ginger compared to placebo. Zhu et al[40] pooled data from 1417 participants across several trials and reported that ginger was effective in PONV prevention. A Bayesian network meta-analysis by Zhao et al[41], did not find any obvious advantages to using ginger for preventing postoperative nausea. Another meta-analysis by Bahrami et al[42] incorporated data from twenty studies and assessed the antiemetic effects of oral ginger on PONV prophylaxis. They concluded that ginger capsules significantly reduced nausea severity and vomiting incidence 2 hours after surgery but showed no significant effect on nausea severity reduction or PONV incidence at other time points (6 hours and 24 hours postoperatively). They also highlighted the need for further high-quality trials. This marked variability in findings, both at the trial level and the review level, has not been adequately characterized or explained through stratifications. Thus, a dedicated review on ginger’s efficacy and safety in PONV prevention for laparoscopic procedures is needed to guide perioperative care strategies in LMIC settings.

This review will benefit from several strengths. It will employ advanced random-effects modeling with the REML method, offering superior handling of heterogeneity compared to traditional DL approaches used in prior syntheses. Comprehensive searches without language restrictions across major databases and gray literature will minimize publication bias, capturing LMIC trials often overlooked in English-only, Western-biased reviews. The exclusive focus on LMICs will also address an important gap, as resource constraints amplify PONV’s impact in these settings. Pre-specified subgroup analyses by procedure type, dosage, and formulation will enhance applicability, and GRADE assessments will clarify evidence certainty for clinical translation. Preliminary scoping has identified several eligible RCTs from LMICs, comprising locations across Iran, Thailand, and India, enabling robust meta-analysis without extrapolation from high-income data.

However, limitations are anticipated. Included trials are expected to vary in ginger preparation (such as powder and extract), administration timing, and laparoscopic procedure types, potentially introducing heterogeneity. In addition, studies in LMIC settings are often single-center with small samples, highlighting the need for larger multicenter trials. Variability in herbal standardization and potential challenges in maintaining blinding due to ginger’s distinctive sensory properties may introduce performance bias. Also, restricting inclusion to placebo-controlled trials may limit broader comparability against currently used standard-of-care interventions. Finally, although funnel plots will be used when 10 or more studies are available for a given outcome, the number of eligible trials, particularly within LMIC settings, is expected to be small. In such cases, funnel plot asymmetry may be difficult to interpret and may potentially reflect small-study effects or between-study heterogeneity rather than true publication bias. Accordingly, any assessment of publication bias will be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, in the event that formal assessment is not feasible due to an insufficient number of studies, the absence of evidence of publication bias should not be interpreted as evidence of absence.

In summary, while preliminary evidence provides mixed results on ginger’s antiemetic potential, no prior review has synthesized LMIC-specific data for laparoscopic PONV. This protocol outlines a rigorous synthesis to resolve these uncertainties and guide low-cost prophylaxis in resource-limited environments.

Conclusion

This review protocol details a plan for the systematic evaluation of ginger versus placebo for PONV prevention after laparoscopic surgery in LMICs. The findings are hoped to inform cost-effective perioperative strategies, highlight evidence gaps, and advocate for ginger’s role in global surgery equity.
