Seborrheic Keratosis of the Nasal Vestibule: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Jee Won Moon, Tae Sung Jeon, Heung-Man Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeborrheic keratosis (SK) is a common benign epithelial neoplasm most often found on sun-exposed skin, particularly in elderly individuals. Despite its prevalence, involvement of the nasal vestibule is extraordinarily rare, with only a few documented cases. We present the case of a 59-year-old man with a verrucous lesion in the left nasal vestibule that was completely excised and histopathologically confirmed as SK. This case emphasizes the importance of distinguishing SK from benign and malignant tumors such as basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, which occur more frequently in this anatomical site. A comprehensive review of the literature regarding the presentation, diagnosis, histopathology, and treatment of SK at atypical locations is also provided.Keywords: Seborrheic keratosis, Nose, Nose neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Seborrheic keratosis (SK) is among the most frequently encountered benign skin tumors in dermatology, characterized by sharply demarcated, pigmented, and often exophytic lesions with a verrucous surface [1]. Typically observed on sun-exposed areas such as the face, chest, and back, SK is rarely found in the nasal vestibule [2]. Most cases of SK are readily diagnosed due to their characteristic clinical appearance. However, when arising in unusual sites such as the nasal vestibule, these lesions may mimic both benign and malignant conditions, making a thorough differential diagnosis essential.

The nasal vestibule represents a distinct anatomical region at the junction of skin and mucosa and is a recognized site for a range of cutaneous pathologies, including benign and malignant tumors such as basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and melanoma [3].

This case report describes a rare occurrence of SK in the nasal vestibule, outlines its clinical management, and reviews relevant literature with emphasis on diagnostic and therapeutic considerations.

CASE REPORT

A 59-year-old man presented to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery with a 1-month history of a slowly enlarging mass in the left nasal vestibule. He denied pain, epistaxis, or nasal obstruction. The patient had undergone endoscopic sinus surgery at our hospital a year earlier for chronic maxillary sinusitis, during which no abnormalities were observed in the nasal vestibule.

Nasal endoscopy revealed a well-circumscribed, verrucous, and slightly pigmented lesion measuring approximately 0.7 cm in diameter, located on the lateral wall of the left nasal vestibule about 2 mm anterior to the mucocutaneous junction (Fig. 1). The lesion’s gross appearance suggested a benign etiology, although the differential diagnosis included verruca vulgaris, skin tag, and SK. Given its site and the need to exclude malignancy such as BCC or SCC, complete excision was planned.

Nasal endoscopy of the left nasal vestibule showing a grayish papillomatous mass.

The lesion was excised under local anesthesia, and the specimen was submitted for histopathological evaluation. Gross examination revealed a grayish, hyperkeratotic lesion with a verrucous surface. Microscopic analysis demonstrated hallmark features of SK, including hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and pseudohorn cysts (Fig. 2). The lesion was composed of basaloid keratinocytes with scattered melanophages, explaining the pigmentation noted on gross inspection. There was no evidence of cytologic atypia, increased mitotic activity, or invasion into the deeper dermis, effectively ruling out malignancy. As part of the differential diagnosis, immunohistochemical staining was performed. The lesion was positive for BCL2, a marker commonly expressed in benign tumors such as SK, and negative for cytokeratin 19, which is associated with more aggressive cutaneous neoplasms. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of SK and definitively excluded malignancy. The postoperative course was uneventful. The surgical site healed without infection, scarring, or deformity. The patient reported no cosmetic concerns, and no recurrence was detected during the 1-year follow-up period.

Histopathology of seborrheic keratosis showing characteristic pseudohorn cysts (black arrow), representing intraepidermal invaginations filled with keratin, along with proliferation of small basaloid keratinocytes (asterisk), reflecting the benign acanthotic growth pattern on hematoxylin and eosin stain (magnification, ×100) (A). Immunohistochemistry reveals partial BCL2 positivity (B) and focal CD10 expression (C) at ×100 magnification, supporting the benign diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

SK is a benign epidermal lesion most often associated with body regions exposed to ultraviolet radiation. It typically presents as hyperkeratotic, pigmented papules or plaques that may display a verrucous surface [1]. The development of SK in the nasal vestibule, an area with minimal sun exposure, is extremely rare. To date, only one case has been reported, describing an 80-year-old woman with a 0.5 cm firm, red-brown polypoid lesion at the skin–mucosa junction of the right nasal vestibule [3]. Her lesion was excised under local anesthesia, and no recurrence occurred during a 1-year follow-up. Compared with that case, our patient was younger and presented with a slightly pigmented verrucous lesion on the lateral vestibular wall. Furthermore, we employed immunohistochemical staining to exclude malignancy, which was not performed in the previously reported case. Because of the unusual site and the potential for clinical mimicry of malignant lesions, histopathological confirmation, supplemented with immunohistochemistry when indicated, remains critical for accurate diagnosis.

SK, also known as senile wart, seborrheic wart, or basal cell acanthoma, is one of the most common benign epithelial tumors of the skin [4]. It can occur on any hair-bearing surface, with predilection for the face, chest, back, and shoulders, but it spares the palms, soles, and mucous membranes. The lesions are typically well-circumscribed, round or oval, with irregular surfaces and variable pigmentation. Their diameter generally ranges from 0.5 cm to 1 cm, with slow growth, and most patients develop multiple lesions. Although the term “seborrheic” suggests an association with sebaceous gland activity, SK is not related to sebaceous function [4].

The nasal vestibule represents a transitional zone containing keratinized squamous epithelium, sebaceous glands, and vibrissae follicles, differing substantially from the areas where SK is usually observed [5]. In this site, benign tumors such as fibroma and papilloma, inflammatory conditions such as lupus vulgaris, and malignant neoplasms including BCC and melanoma may clinically resemble SK [5]. Therefore, recognition of the anatomical context and careful histologic assessment are essential.

Although the epidemiology and pathophysiology of SK are not fully understood, the condition is thought to be more common in Caucasians, affecting both sexes equally. The prevalence and number of lesions increase with age, and ultraviolet light exposure is considered an important risk factor. Approximately one-third of SK lesions are hyperpigmented, although pigmentation shows no correlation with site. In recent years, advances in molecular studies have provided further insight into pathogenesis. Somatic mutations in FGFR3 have been detected in 40%–85% of SK lesions, with at least eight distinct mutations identified, while PIK3CA mutations have also been reported [1]. Although these mutations are well established in SK pathogenesis, they are not linked to recurrence or specific site predilection [6,7]. Nevertheless, their presence underscores the clonal and benign nature of SK, even in unusual anatomical locations [8].

The differential diagnosis of SK includes both benign and malignant tumors (Table 1). Pigmented SK may resemble malignant melanoma or pigmented BCC, lightly pigmented SK can mimic BCC or Bowen disease, and irritated SK may be confused with SCC [9]. Accordingly, histopathological confirmation is necessary to validate the clinical impression. Although hematoxylin and eosin staining usually suffices, immunohistochemistry can be helpful in differentiating SK from malignant tumors such as BCC or SCC [9,10]. BCL2 and CD10 are among the commonly employed markers. BCL2 is often expressed in SK and other benign adnexal tumors, whereas strong CD10 expression is more frequently associated with BCC [10]. In our case, weak and partial CD10 staining, combined with BCL2 positivity, supported a benign diagnosis and excluded BCC.

Treatment of SK is generally unnecessary given its benign nature, but patients may seek removal for cosmetic reasons or to alleviate symptoms. Excision may also be indicated when the diagnosis is uncertain to rule out malignancy. Available treatments include curettage, shave excision, cryosurgery, laser vaporization, or surgical removal, while topical steroid ointment can be used to relieve symptoms such as pruritus [11]. For rhinologists, excision using either cold knife or electrocautery is a familiar technique, and complete removal is recommended to prevent recurrence.

When a pigmented tumor arises within the nasal vestibule, it presents a diagnostic challenge. Although SK in this region is exceptionally rare, with only one prior case described in the literature [3], rhinologists should remain aware of this possibility and recognize its benign behavior.
