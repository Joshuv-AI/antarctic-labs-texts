Short-time existence of Lagrangian mean curvature flow
Spandan Ghosh

Abstract
In his paper ‘Conjectures on Bridgeland Stability’ [ Joy15 ] , Joyce asked if one can desingularise the transverse intersection point of an immersed Lagrangian using JLT expanders such that one gets a Lagrangian mean curvature flow via the desingularisations. Begley and Moore [ BM16 ] answered this in the affirmative by constructing a family of desingularisations and showing that a certain limit along their flows satisfies LMCF along with convergence to the immersed Lagrangian in the sense of varifolds. We prove that there exists a solution with convergence in a stronger sense, using the notion of manifolds with corners and a-corners as introduced by Joyce [ Joy16 ] . Our methods are a direct P.D.E. based approach, along the lines of the proof of short-time existence for network flow by Lira, Mazzeo, Pluda and Saez [ LMPS23 ] .

Introduction

Lagrangian mean curvature flow (LMCF) is a way to deform Lagrangian submanifolds inside a Calabi–Yau manifold
X
, according to the negative gradient of the area functional. A major point of interest for studying this flow is that its stationary points are given by special Lagrangian submanifolds. However, one often runs into singularities when one tries to evolve a Lagrangian by LMCF. Motivated by string theory, Thomas and Yau
[
TY02
]
proposed the Thomas–Yau conjecture, which roughly states that the question of whether a Lagrangian can be deformed to a special Lagrangian is related to an algebraic notion, namely that of stability. They conjectured that given a Lagrangian
L\subset X
inside a Calabi–Yau manifold
X
that is stable in the sense of Thomas–Yau, the mean curvature flow of
L
exists for all time and converges to a special Lagrangian. A vast extension of this conjecture was proposed by Joyce
[
Joy15
]
, giving a detailed conjectural picture of how singularities form in LMCF and how one might flow past them via surgery.

In particular, one important point in the conjectural picture of
[
Joy15
]
for evolving a Lagrangian via LMCF with surgery is that the Floer homology of the Lagrangian brane should remain unobstructed at all times. However, as described in §3.4 of
[
Joy15
]
, the Floer homology can go from unobstructed to obstructed at a time
T
due to a transverse intersection point of the immersed Lagrangian. As a way of resolving this, it is proposed to evolve the Lagrangian by doing a surgery at time
t=T
by gluing in an expander, so that the flow can continue and the Floer homology of the new evolving Lagrangian remains unobstructed for time
t>T
. We formulate this as a general analytical problem, following Problem 3.14 of
[
Joy15
]
.

Overview of the problem.

Let
(X,\omega,\Omega,J)
be a given ambient Calabi–Yau manifold. Let
\iota:L\rightarrow X
be a compact, oriented, conically singular Lagrangian embedding, with a finite number of conical singularities
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, such that the corresponding tangent cones
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
at each singular point are a union of special Lagrangian cones. Suppose that for each cone
C_{i}
, there is an asymptotically conical expander
E_{i}
that is asymptotic to the cone. Then, show that there is a family of Lagrangians
(L_{t})_{t\in(0,\varepsilon)}
evolving by LMCF, such that
L_{t}
converges to
L
as
T\downarrow 0
in a suitable sense, and we have
L_{t}\approx\sqrt{2t}E_{i}
near each conical singularity
x_{i}
, and
L_{t}\approx L+tH_{L}
away from the singularities, for
H_{L}
being the mean curvature vector of
L
. Note that our formulation is more general than the previous problem as stated in
[
Joy15
]
, which arises in the case the conical singularities are transverse intersection points of an immersed Lagrangian.

Outline of proof

We approach this problem by first constructing an approximate solution. Following a similar construction to Joyce
[
Joy04
]
, we construct a family of desingularizations of
L
by gluing in the expanders at a scale of approximately
\sqrt{2t}
to the singularities of
L
. Then, we perturb this approximate solution to an exact one. Since we are working with Lagrangians, we can invoke Lagrangian neighbourhood theorems as in
[
Joy04
]
, which allows us to write the quasilinear P.D.E. for mean curvature flow at the level of
1
-forms, and then integrate it to obtain a fully nonlinear scalar P.D.E. We solve this nonlinear P.D.E. by the standard method of reformulating it as a fixed point problem between small balls in certain Banach spaces and invoking the Banach fixed point theorem. The main difficulty in this approach is to construct appropriate Sobolev spaces between which the linearised parabolic operator is an isomorphism. In particular, since the approximate solution we constructed degenerates at the singular point at the starting time, one cannot apply standard theory about solving parabolic P.D.E.s on space-time
M\times[0,\varepsilon)
. Instead, we need to do our analysis on a modified space, on which the geometry of the approximate solution and the linearised P.D.E. becomes appropriately nonsingular. This is achieved by the notion of a
parabolic blow-up
at the singular point in space-time, which turns out to be the natural space on which to do our analysis. In order to define these spaces globally, we introduce the notion of
manifolds with corners
.

Manifolds with corners and a-corners

A major conceptual tool we use in this problem is the notion of manifolds with corners and a-corners, as introduced by Joyce in
[
Joy16
]
, following earlier work by Melrose
[
Mel90
,
Mel93
,
Mel96
]
. The main idea is that instead of thinking of the evolving Lagrangian as starting from a singularity, we can do a real oriented blow-up (as manifolds with corners) of both the domain and range. This allows us to write the evolving Lagrangian as well as the corresponding P.D.E. in a non-singular way on the blown-up spaces. The language of manifolds with corners and a-corners and
b
-geometry is a convenient way to package the correct notions of boundary behaviour that one needs to work with in these blown-up spaces (as well as other kinds of singular spaces). Our approach of using manifolds with corners in parabolic P.D.E. problems is also heavily inspired by the recent work of Lira, Mazzeo, Pluda and Saez
[
LMPS23
]
, which we now briefly describe:

Short time existence for the network flow

The problem of short-time existence for the network flow asks to show that given any
planar network
, i.e. a planar graph with finitely many nodes and edges in a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists a
network flow
for a short time interval, i.e. a family of
regular networks
evolving by curve shortening flow, such that as
t\downarrow 0
, the networks converge to the initial network in some sense (at least in
C^{0}_{\mathrm{loc}}
). One can further ask the set of possible networks that can flow out of a given one (in some sense of convergence). This question was first answered by Ilmanen-Neves-Schulze
[
INS19
]
, where they construct a family of regular networks by gluing in expanding solutions at the irregular vertices, then take a subsequential limit along the flows generated by this family to obtain a solution to the network flow. A new proof of their result, giving much more detailed information about how the flow ‘resolves’ the irregular network appeared in the paper
[
LMPS23
]
, where they stated and solved the problem using the language of manifolds with corners. In particular, they first construct a
parabolic blow-up

X
of the range (which is space-time
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times[0,\varepsilon)
), by doing a real oriented blow up at the vertices of the initial network (c.f. §4. of this paper, where we blow up at the conical singularities).
They prove that, considering a solution to network flow as a manifold with corners
M
along with a smooth map to
X
satisfying the curve shortening equation, there always exists such a solution for short time. Moreover, the possible flow-outs are classified by the possible topological types of self-expanders for the curve shortening flow that are asymptotic to the tangent rays at the vertices of the initial network. In contrast to their result, we do not obtain a complete classification of flow-outs (we shouldn’t expect so anyway, since the classification of possible self-expanders for higher dimensions is highly non-trivial). However, modulo the classification of self-expanders, our result is slightly stronger, since we show uniqueness as a manifold with corners and a-corners, which is a weaker smooth structure than a manifold with corners.

Isomorphism of linearised operator

Given our approximate solution as a family of Lagrangian embeddings
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(0,\varepsilon)}
, the linearised operator can be written as

\partial_{v}-\Delta_{L_{t}},

where
v
is a ‘time-like’ vector field on the family, i.e. it is the unique vector field that is orthogonal to the time-slices and whose flow generates the time-evolution of the family, and
\Delta_{L_{t}}
is the Laplacian on
L_{t}
in the induced metric from
X
. Once we compactify the approximate solution in the parabolic blow-up, a scaling of the above P.D.E. becomes approximately translation-invariant near the
front faces
of the compactification (note that front faces roughly correspond to asymptotically cylindrical ends in the natural
b
-metric
). Due to this, we can follow a general strategy to study Fredholmness of this operator as in Lockhart-McOwen
[
LM85
]
, by introducing
weighted Sobolev spaces
which are weighted by powers of the boundary-defining function for the front face. We first prove
a-priori estimates
for these Sobolev spaces, by adding up
interior estimates
over local models that approximate our operator with standard parabolic operators on Euclidean space. Then, we prove Fredholm results for a model space approximating the region near the front face away from some
critical rates
, and glue this estimate with the interior region which is compact. The model operator on the model space is parabolic in a non-standard way, so we cannot directly apply the theory of Lockhart-McOwen to this setting. However, the general method we use to prove the result is similar, see for example the work of Agmon-Nirenberg
[
AN63
]
. Note that the model space is the compactified family of expanders
\{\sqrt{2t}E\}_{t\in(0,\infty)}
, and the model operator is the same as above, i.e.
\partial_{v}-\Delta_{\sqrt{2t}E}
. So, our Fredholm result on the model space can be seen as part of a
parabolic Fredholm theory
for expanders, an extension of the elliptic Fredholm theory for expanders as developed by Lotay–Neves
[
LN13
]
. Indeed, we use their result as a key starting point in our proof.

Main results

The main result we prove (Theorems
7.15
,
7.19
) can be stated as follows: Let
L
be a compact, embedded conically singular Lagrangian with a finite number of singular points
x_{j}\in X
, such that each of its asymptotic cones is a union of special Lagrangian cones
C_{j}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}C_{ij}
(which may have different phases). For each
j
, suppose that
E_{j}
is a Lagrangian expander which is asymptotic to the cone
C_{j}
. Then, there exists a smooth family of embedded Lagrangians
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(0,\varepsilon)}
evolving by Lagrangian mean curvature flow, such that
L_{t}\downarrow L
in a strong sense to be described below, such that
L_{t}\approx\sqrt{2t}E_{j}
near each singular point
x_{j}
. Moreover, the family is locally unique in the
C^{2}
-norm, and unique up to
t=0
in a strong sense. Further, polyhomogeneous conormal asymptotics of the initial Lagrangian at its singularities leads to controlled polyhomogeneous conormal asymptotics of the solution at
t=0
.

Along the way to the proof and as the main analytical ingredient, we develop a parabolic Fredholm theory for asymptotically conical Lagrangian expanders in §
5
, which is an extension of the elliptic Fredholm theory for expanders as developed by Lotay–Neves
[
LN13
]
, and may be of independent interest.

Organisation of paper

We begin with recalling some background on Lagrangians, Lagrangian expanders, and neighbourhood theorems in section
2
. Section
3
recalls the basic definitions of manifolds with corners and a-corners we will be using in the paper, and standard estimates on parabolic Sobolev spaces. Section
4
constructs the approximate solution to Lagrangian mean curvature flow, and computes the linearisation of the corresponding scalar P.D.E.  Section
5
proves a key Fredholm result for the linearised parabolic operator on asymptotically conical Lagrangian expanders. Section
6
proves isomorphism of the linearised operator for our problem. Section
7
solves the full nonlinear P.D.E.  proving our main theorem. Finally, subsection
7.6
shows that the existence of a polyhomogeneous conormal asymptotic expansion of the initial Lagrangian implies such an expansion for the solution.

Calabi–Yau and Lagrangian Geometry

2.1
Calabi–Yau geometry

In this subsection, we recall some standard definitions from Calabi–Yau geometry. Some references for this subsection include the book by Gross, Huybrechts and Joyce
[
GHJ03
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Calabi–Yau manifold
is a five-tuple
(M,J,g,\omega,\Omega)
where
M
is a complex manifold of complex dimension
m
with complex structure
J
,
\omega
is a symplectic form on
M
and
g
is a Riemannian metric on
M
such that
(J,g,\omega)
are compatible with each other, and
\Omega
is a nowhere-vanishing holomorphic
(m,0)
-form on
M
satisfying

\frac{\omega^{m}}{m!}=(-1)^{m(m-1)/2}\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^{m}\Omega\wedge\overline{\Omega}.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a Calabi–Yau manifold
X
,
a
Lagrangian
submanifold
L\subset X
is a real
m
-dimensional submanifold such that
\omega|_{L}\equiv 0
.

Remark 2.3
.

We can define Lagrangians more generally for symplectic manifolds.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a Calabi–Yau manifold
X
, a
special Lagrangian
in
X
is a Lagrangian submanifold
L\subset X
such that
\Omega|_{L}=e^{i\theta}\textrm{vol}_{L}
for a real constant
\theta
called the
phase
of
L
.

2.2
Lagrangian mean curvature flow

In this subsection, we introduce the basic notions of Lagrangian mean curvature flow. Throughout, we assume we have a given Calabi–Yau manifold
X
. Some references for this subsection include the book by Mantegazza
[
Man11
]
for general mean curvature flow, and the paper by Thomas–Yau
[
TY02
]
and the survey by Smoczyk
[
Smo12
]
for Lagrangian mean curvature flow.

Definition 2.5
.

Given an immersed Lagrangian submanifold
L\subset X
, the
mean curvature flow
of
L
for time
t\in[0,\varepsilon)
is a smooth mapping
F:L\times[0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow X
such that it is an immersion on the time-slices, i.e.
F(\cdot,t)
is an immersion, and it satisfies

\left(\frac{d}{dt}F(x,t)\right)^{\perp}=H_{F(\cdot,t)}(x),

where
H
is the mean curvature vector of the immersed submanifold, and
(\cdot)^{\perp}
denotes the projection onto the tangent bundle of the immersed submanifold.

We have the following foundational result due to Smoczyk
[
Smo96
]
, which shows that the evolution is in fact through Lagrangians:

Theorem 2.6
.

Given a compact Lagrangian submanifold
L\subset X
, the mean curvature flow of
L
exists for a positive time
\varepsilon>0
and is unique. Further, the time-slices
F(\cdot,t)
are Lagrangian submanifolds.

Remark 2.7
.

The above result also holds if
X
is Kähler-Einstein.

Due to this result, we denote the flow of a Lagrangian via mean curvature flow as
Lagrangian mean curvature flow
, abbreviated to LMCF.

Remark 2.8
.

There is also a version of LMCF for
almost Calabi–Yau
manifolds, c.f. the thesis of Behrndt
[
Beh11
]
.

2.3
Cohomological invariants of LMCF

In this section, we define some cohomology classes associated to a given immersed Lagrangian
L\subset X
, and study how they change under LMCF. Some references for this section include the paper
[
TY02
]
; see also the book by McDuff and Salamon
[
MS98
]
for the relevant symplectic geometry. First, we recall the Lagrangian angle and the Maslov class.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
L\subset X
be an oriented Lagrangian. There is a
grading
,
\theta:L\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
, defined using the formula
e^{i\theta}\mathrm{vol}_{L}=\Omega|_{L}
. We can also write it as a locally-defined real-valued function, well-defined up to multiples of
2\pi
, called the
Lagrangian angle
. Corresponding to this, we have a globally-defined
1
-form
d\theta
on
L
. The
Maslov class
is its representative
[d\theta]
in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(L)
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
L\subset X
be an oriented Lagrangian as before. We say
L
is
zero-Maslov
if
[d\theta]
vanishes in
H^{1}_{\mathrm{dR}}(L)
, or equivalently, the Lagrangian angle is a globally defined function
\theta:L\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. We say
L
is
almost calibrated
if it is zero-Maslov and the Lagrangian angle satisfies
(\sup_{x\in L}\theta-\inf_{x\in L}\theta)<\pi
.

Let
U_{L}\subset T^{*}L
be an open subset of the zero section of the cotangent bundle of
L
.
Suppose we are given a Lagrangian embedding
\Psi:U_{L}\rightarrow X
. Then, we can identify Lagrangians in
X
that are ‘nearby’ to
L
via small
1
-forms on
L
. More precisely, we have:

Theorem 2.11
.

There is a
1
-
1
correspondence between small (in the
C^{1,\alpha}
-norm) closed
1
-forms
\alpha
on
L
and Lagrangians
L_{\alpha}
nearby (in the
C^{1,\alpha}
-norm) to
L
, given by

\alpha\mapsto L_{\alpha}:=\Psi(\Gamma_{\alpha}),

where
\Gamma_{\alpha}:=\{(x,\alpha(x):x\in L)\}
is the graph of the
1
-form
\alpha
.
Moreover, two nearby Lagrangians
L_{\alpha},L_{\beta}
are Hamiltonian isotopic if and only if
\alpha-\beta
is an exact
1
-form. Therefore, the Hamiltonian isotopy classes of nearby Lagrangians are in bijective correspondence with
C^{1}
-small forms in
H_{\mathrm{dR}}^{1}(L)
. In particular, the dimension of the ‘deformation space’ of Lagrangians up to Hamiltonian isotopy is given by
b^{1}(L)
.

In particular, we have that the Lagrangian given by the graph of an exact
1
-form over a Lagrangian is Hamiltonian isotopic to it. Now, we can consider the LMCF of the Lagrangian
L
, and write it using
1
-forms via the above correspondence for a short time:

Theorem 2.12
.

Suppose that the family of Lagrangian immersions
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
with
L_{0}=L
satisfies LMCF. As before, let us choose a Lagrangian neighbourhood and embedding for
L
, and let the Lagrangians
L_{t}
be the graphs of
1
-forms
\alpha_{t}
over
L
. Then, the corresponding
1
-forms
\alpha_{t}
satisfy

\frac{d}{dt}\alpha_{t}\Big|_{t=0}=d\theta,

(2.1)

where
\theta
is the Lagrangian angle of
L
. Conversely, if we have a family of Lagrangians
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
such that we can write, for each
t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
,
L_{t+\delta}
as the graph of a
1
-form
\alpha_{\delta}
over
L_{t}
satisfying equation (
2.1
) at
\delta=0
, then the family evolves by LMCF.

To prove this, we need a lemma about the geometry of the Lagrangian embedding:

Lemma 2.13
.

Suppose we have a Lagrangian embedding
\Psi
for
L
as before. Then, there is a bijection between the normal vector space to
L
at a point
\iota(x)
and the fibre of the cotangent bundle to
L
at
x
, given by

\alpha\mapsto v:=(\Psi_{*}\alpha)^{\perp}.

(2.2)

Note that
v
is the unique vector in the normal bundle of
L
such that
\omega(v,\iota_{*}w)=\alpha(w)
for
w\in TL
. Under this bijection, we have that
J\nabla\theta
corresponds to
d\theta
.

Proof.

The map is well-defined due to
\omega
being non-degenerate, and
\omega|_{\iota(L)}=0
. To see the correspondence, note that
\omega(J\nabla\theta,\iota_{*}w)=d\theta(w)
. To see the other characterisation of

, note that
\omega(\Psi_{*}\alpha^{\perp},\iota_{*}w)=\omega(\Psi_{*}\alpha,\iota_{*}w)=\widehat{\omega}(\alpha,w)=\alpha(w)
, with the last equality arising from writing out
\widehat{\omega}
in coordinates.
∎

Now, we can prove Theorem
2.12
:

Proof.

Note that in LMCF, the Lagrangian deforms by the mean curvature vector, which is orthogonal to the Lagrangian and is given by
H=J\nabla\theta
(for e.g. see Lemma 2.1 in
[
TY02
]
). Therefore, making use of the isomorphism (

) and the above lemma, the variational field generated by

at

gives rise to the same flow as

. Since the variational field generated by the family of graphs
\Gamma(\alpha_{t})

is given by
\frac{d}{dt}\alpha_{t}

∎

Taking the de Rham cohomology classes in (
2.1
), we get

Corollary 2.14
.

Suppose that the family of immersed Lagrangians satisfies LMCF. Then, we have that

\frac{d}{dt}[\alpha_{t}]=[d\theta]\;\textrm{ in }H^{1}_{\textrm{dR}}(L),

(2.3)

for all times

. Note that the Maslov class is preserved since
\{L_{t}\}
is a Lagrangian isotopy.

2.3.1
Symplectic Area of Discs

Given a smoothly varying family of Lagrangian immersions
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
and an element
\gamma\in H_{1}(L;\mathbb{Z})

D_{\gamma,[t_{1},t_{2}]}(s,t):=F_{t}(\gamma(s)):t\in[t_{1},t_{2}],s\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

Lemma 2.15
.

The symplectic area of
D_{\gamma,[t_{1},t_{2}]}
determines the difference in Hamiltonian isotopy classes between
L_{t_{1}}
and
L_{t_{2}}
over the loop

. In particular, we have that if

has Hamiltonian isotopy class represented by a

\alpha_{t}
on

, then the area of

\alpha_{t}\cdot[\gamma]

Now, combining this with Corollary

, we obtain:

Corollary 2.16
.

Let
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in[0,\varepsilon)}
be a family of Lagrangians evolving by LMCF. Then, the area of the annulus

as constructed above satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Area}(D_{\gamma,[0,t]})=[d\theta]\cdot[\gamma],

(2.4)

for
t\in[0,\varepsilon)

2.4
Asymptotically conical Lagrangian expanders

First, we recall the definition of an expander of LMCF:

Definition 2.17
.

An
expander

E\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
of LMCF is an embedded Lagrangian submanifold satisfying the elliptic P.D.E.

(\overrightarrow{x})^{\perp}=H(x),

for
x\in E
, where
H(x)
denotes the mean curvature vector at
x
. As a result,
E
evolves under LMCF as
E_{t}=\sqrt{2(t+\frac{1}{2})}E
for
t\in(-\frac{1}{2},\infty)
.

Also, we recall the definition of asymptotically conical Lagrangians:

Definition 2.18
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
be a cone, with link
\Sigma:=C\cap\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}
. A Lagrangian
E\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
is said to be
asymptotically conical
with cone
C
and rate
\gamma<2
, if there is a compact subset

of

and a diffeomorphism
\phi_{E}:\Sigma\times(R,\infty)\rightarrow E\backslash K
such that

|\nabla^{k}(\phi_{E}-\iota_{C})|=O(r^{\gamma-1-k}),

as
r\rightarrow\infty

. Here,
\iota_{C}
is the embedding of the cone

in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
given by
\Sigma\times(0,\infty)\ni(\sigma,r)\mapsto\sigma r
, and the norm is with respect to the induced metric on
\Sigma\times(0,R)
from
\iota_{C}
.

Now, given an
m
-tuple of angles
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m}\}
, consider the oriented plane in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
spanned by the vectors
\{e^{i\phi_{j}}\overrightarrow{e_{j}}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, with orientation arising from this ordering. Note that
\Pi_{0}
represents the plane
\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{m}):\mathrm{Im}(z_{i})=0\textrm{ for }i=1,\dots,m\}.

Given a pair of oriented planes
\Pi_{0},\Pi_{\phi}
, the oriented union
\Pi_{\phi}-\Pi_{0}
is non-area-minimising if and only if the characterising angles
\{\tilde{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
between them satisfy
\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\phi}_{i}<\pi
, as shown by Lawlor
[
Law89
]
(for a pair as above, the characterising angles
\{\tilde{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
are the angles
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
in ascending order, hence
\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\phi_{i}
). Given such a pair of non-area-minimising planes with a phase difference
\phi
between them, there is a JLT expander that is asymptotically conical with cone
\Pi_{0}\sqcup\Pi_{\phi}
, as constructed by Joyce, Lee and Tsui
[
JLT10
]
, which we now describe:

Theorem 2.19
.

Let
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{m})\in(0,\pi)
be the
m
-tuple of angles between the planes
\Pi_{\phi}
and
\Pi_{0}
, such that
\phi=\sum{\phi_{i}}<\pi
. Then, there is an
m
-tuple of real constants
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}>0
such that we have

\frac{1}{2}\phi_{j}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{(\frac{1}{a_{j}}+t^{2})\sqrt{P(t)}}\;\;\textrm{ for }i=1,\dots,m,

where

P(t)=\frac{1}{t^{2}}(\Pi_{k=1}^{n}(1+a_{k}t^{2})e^{t^{2}}-1)
. For
i=1,\dots,m
, define

, where
r_{j}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{a_{j}}+y^{2}}
and
q_{j}(y)=\int_{0}^{y}\frac{dt}{(\frac{1}{a_{j}}+t^{2})\sqrt{P(t)}}-\frac{1}{2}{\phi}_{j}

L:=\{(x_{1}w_{1}(y),\dots,x_{m}w_{m}(y)):(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1},y\in\mathbb{R}\}

is a closed, embedded Lagrangian expander diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\times\mathbb{R}
which is asymptotic to the two planes
\Pi_{\phi}
and
\Pi_{0}
.

Moreover, this is the unique closed, exact expander asymptotic to those planes, as shown by Lotay and Neves
[
LN13
]
for

[
IJdS16
]
for

In this section, we recall the definition of conically singular Lagrangians, following Definition 3.4 of

Definition 2.22

2.6
An overview of Begley–Moore

Begley and Moore
[
BM16
]
proved the following:

Theorem 2.23
.

Let
L\subset X
be a compact immersed Lagrangian submanifold in a Calabi–Yau manifold, with a finite number of singularities, such that each of them is asymptotic to a pair of transversely intersecting non-area-minimising planes. Then, there exists
T>0
and an embedded Lagrangian mean curvature flow
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(0,T)}
such that as

,

as varifolds and in

away from the singularities.

In order to prove this, they first construct an approximate family of initial Lagrangians
\{L^{s}\}
to the flow via gluing in evolving expanders that are asymptotic to the tangent planes. Then, they allow these Lagrangians to flow for a short time, to get a family of flows
\{L^{s}_{t}\}_{t\in(0,\delta_{s})}
. They get the desired flow
\{L_{t}\}_{t\in(0,\varepsilon)}
by showing that there is a uniform lower bound for the time of existence
\delta_{s}
for
s
sufficiently small, and then taking a limit along the flows
\{L^{s}_{t}\}
. In order to show these bounds, they prove uniform estimates on the Gaussian density ratios and uniform curvature estimates for the evolving family. We remark that although Begley–Moore prove convergence as varifolds, their methods can directly lead to further strong convergence of parabolic rescalings in backward time of the flow to the expander.

3
Analysis on manifolds with corners and a-corners

3.1
Manifolds with corners and a-corners

In this section, we define some notions of
b
-geometry for manifolds with corners and a-corners, mainly following
[
Joy16
]
, where more details can be found. These notions are based on previous work by Melrose, see for example
[
Mel93
,
Mel96
]
.

3.1.1
Manifolds with corners

A manifold with corners is modelled on the space
\mathbb{R}^{s}_{k}:=[0,\infty)^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{s-k}
, with coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
with
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
in
[0,\infty)
and
x_{k+1},\dots,x_{s}
in
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A continuous map
f:U\rightarrow V
between open subsets
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{s}_{k},V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{l}
is called
smooth
if all the derivatives of
f
exist (we consider one-sided derivatives at the boundary) and are continuous.

A diffeomorphism between
U,V
as above is defined in the usual way, via the existence of an inverse map. Now, we define a manifold with corners:

Definition 3.2
.

A
manifold with corners
is a Hausdorff, second countable topological space with charts to
\mathbb{R}^{m}_{k}
such that the transition charts are diffeomorphisms, i.e. they are smooth and have smooth inverses.

For a manifold with corners, we can define the notions of tangent bundle, cotangent bundle, metric, connections, etc. in a similar way as in ordinary manifolds. All these structures can be essentially obtained by restricting them from an ordinary manifold.

3.1.2
Manifolds with a-corners

A
manifold with a-corners
is modelled on the space
\mathbb{R}^{k,s}:=\llbracket 0,\infty)^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{s-k}
, with coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
with
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
in
\llbracket 0,\infty)
and
x_{k+1},\dots,x_{s}
in
\mathbb{R}
. Here, the symbol
\llbracket 0,\infty)
denotes a space homeomorphic to
[0,\infty)
, but endowed with an a-smooth structure instead of a smooth structure.

The simplest example of such a space is
\Omega\times\llbracket 0,\varepsilon)
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is a domain. Although this space is homeomorphic to
\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon)
, it has a different smooth structure. For example, given coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
on this space, the function
x_{n}^{\alpha}
is not smooth for
\alpha\not\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, however it is a-smooth for all
\alpha\geq 0
. A natural way to think about this space is to consider the boundary ‘at infinity’, so that its smooth structure is similar to that of
\Omega\times(-\infty,\log(\varepsilon))
via the change of coordinate
x_{n}\mapsto\log(x_{n})
, with suitable decay conditions near infinity.

For brevity, we shall not define manifolds with a-corners, but rather define
manifolds with corners and a-corners
, which includes the case of manifolds with a-corners.

3.1.3
Manifolds with corners and a-corners

A
manifold with corners and a-corners
is modelled on the space
\mathbb{R}^{k,s}_{l}=\llbracket 0,\infty)^{k}\times[0,\infty)^{l}\times\mathbb{R}^{s-k-l}
, with coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
with
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
in
\llbracket 0,\infty)
,
x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+l}
in
[0,\infty)
, and
x_{k+l+1},\dots,x_{s}
in
\mathbb{R}
. Here, the coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
are boundary-defining functions for the
a-boundaries
, while the coordinates
(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+l})
are boundary-defining functions for the
ordinary boundaries
. Near the a-boundaries, the smooth structure resembles that of a manifold with a-corners, while near the ordinary boundaries, the smooth structure resembles that of a manifold with corners.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{k,s}_{l}
be an open set and

be a continuous map. It is said to have a
mixed derivative
or
m

{}^{m}\partial f

{}^{m}\partial f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{s}
, written as
{}^{m}\partial f=(^{m}\partial_{1}f,\dots,^{m}\partial f_{s})
for
{}^{m}\partial_{i}f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

{}^{m}\partial_{i}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})=\begin{cases}0,&x_{i}=0,\>i=1,\dots,k\\
x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}f&x_{i}>0,\>i=1,\dots,k\\
\partial_{x_{i}}f&i=k+1,\dots,s\par\end{cases}

(where we take one-sided derivatives for

U

. We can iterate

-derivatives, if they exist, to get maps

by taking
m
-derivatives of the components. Then,

•

we say that

|^{m}\partial_{i}f(x)|\leq Cx_{i}^{\alpha}\;\;\>\;\textrm{for all}\>(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})\in S.

•

we say that

We note that a-smooth functions defined as above contain identities and are closed under composition. Now, we can define manifolds with corners and a-corners:

Definition 3.5

A

, the interior of
\partial X
consists of points of the form

Example 3.8
.

Consider the example of a manifold with corners and a-corners given by

We define the notion of a vector bundle on elements of

Now, we define the notion of the

.
of

Definition 3.11

Let

of

Remark 3.12

Suppose that

We can define the

as an ordinary manifold

3.1.6
Relative bundles over a
b
-fibration

Let us work with a manifold with corners and a-corners
X
, along with an m-metric
g
, and a
b
-fibration of the form
\pi:X\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon)
, representing a time variable. Some examples of
b
-fibrations are the mapping
f:\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times[0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon)
given by
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=x_{m}
, and the mapping
g:\mathbb{R}^{n-2}\times\llbracket 0,\varepsilon)\times[0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon)
given by
g(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1},x_{m})=x_{m-1}x_{m}
. In this subsection, we will define the notions of the relative tangent bundle, the relative cotangent bundle, and the relative Levi-Civita connection on
X
with respect to
\pi
.

Definition 3.22
.

The
relative tangent bundle
of
X
, denoted
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X
, is defined as the sub-bundle of
{}^{b}TX
given by
\mathrm{ker}(^{b}d\pi)
. This is an a-smooth sub-bundle of
{}^{b}TX
.

Due to this, there is a natural inclusion
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X\hookrightarrow{{}^{b}TX}\hookrightarrow{{}^{m}TX}
. Now, we define the
relative cotangent bundle
of
X
,
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}X
, as the dual to the relative tangent bundle. Taking duals of the inclusion of
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X\hookrightarrow{{}^{m}TX}
, we get a map
{}^{m}T^{*}X\rightarrow{{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}}X
which is surjective on the fibres. This allows us to represent a-smooth sections of
{{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}}X
by using a-smooth sections of
{}^{m}T^{*}X
.

Definition 3.23
.

We define the
relative
b
-differential
as a map

{}^{b}d_{\mathrm{rel}}:C^{\infty}(X)\rightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}X),

by composing the
b
-differential
{}^{b}d
with the projection map
{}^{b}T^{*}X\rightarrow{{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}}X
.

Now, we can similarly define the notion of a relative
b
-metric over a fibration. we use this to define a relative Levi-Civita connection:

Definition 3.24
.

A relative
b
-metric on
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X
is a section of
\textrm{Sym}^{2}(^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X)
that is positive definite at each point. We define the
relative Levi-Civita connection
associated to a relative
b
-metric
g
as an
\mathbb{R}
-linear map

{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}:\Gamma^{\infty}(^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X)\rightarrow\Gamma^{\infty}(^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X\otimes{{}^{b}}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}X),

defined as the ordinary Levi-Civita connection on each of the fibres.

Remark 3.25
.

We can restrict an m-metric on
{}^{m}TX
to
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X
, to get a relative
b
-metric.

Remark 3.26
.

Note that the embedding
X_{t}:=\pi^{-1}(\{t\})\hookrightarrow X
gives us an isomorphism

TX_{t}\cong{{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}X}.

(3.1)

Taking duals, we get an isomorphism of the cotangent bundles.

Now, given a
k
-differentiable function
u
, we can consider
{}^{b}\nabla^{k}_{\mathrm{rel}}u
as a section of
{}^{b}T^{*\otimes k}_{\mathrm{rel}}X
. We have an embedding
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}X\hookrightarrow{{}^{m}T}^{*}X
, arising from the m-metric. Therefore, we can pushforward
{}^{b}\nabla^{k}_{\mathrm{rel}}u
under this embedding, and take its further derivatives, to obtain
{}^{m}\nabla^{l}({{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}}u)
as a section of
{}^{m}T^{*\otimes(k+l)}X
. This allows us to take the norm of
{}^{m}\nabla^{l}({{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}}u)
. Essentially, this is analogous to taking
l
derivatives in time and
(l+k)
derivatives in space.

[

.

3.2.1
Parabolic Sobolev spaces

We consider our model space as
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}:=\{(x,t):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t\in[0,\infty)\}
, where
x
represents the space variable and
t
represents the time variable.

Definition 3.27
.

For a differentiable function
u
having continuous derivatives of the form
\partial^{k}_{{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}}u
for
(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we denote by
\nabla^{k}u
the tensor given by

\nabla^{k}u:=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq\dots i_{k}\leq n}(\partial^{k}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}u)dx^{i_{1}}\dots dx^{i_{k}}.

Similarly, for a function
u
having continuous derivatives of the form
\partial_{t}^{l}\partial^{k}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}u
, we denote by
\partial_{t}^{l}\nabla^{k}u
the tensor given by

\partial_{t}^{l}\nabla^{k}u:=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq\dots i_{k}\leq n}(\partial_{t}^{l}\partial^{k}_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}u)dx^{i_{1}}\dots dx^{i_{k}}.

Now, we define the
L^{p}_{k+2r,r}
-norms on our model space.

Definition 3.28
.

Let
k,r
be non-negative integers. For a locally integrable function
u
having weak derivatives of the form
\partial_{t}^{l}\partial^{m}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}u
for all
l\leq r
and
m+2l\leq k+2r
, we define

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}}:=\sum_{i\leq l}\sum_{j\leq k+2r-2i}\|\partial_{t}^{i}\nabla^{j}u\|_{L^{p}}.

We define the parabolic Sobolev spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}
as follows:

Definition 3.29
.

The Sobolev space
L^{p}_{k+2r,r}
is defined to be the space of locally integrable functions
u
having weak derivatives of the form
\partial_{t}^{l}\partial^{m}_{i_{1}\dots i_{m}}u
for all
l\leq r
and
m+2l\leq k+2r
and having a finite
L^{p}_{k+2r,r}
-norm.

3.2.2
Parabolic Hölder spaces

We recall interior Hölder estimates for parabolic operators, following
[
Kry96
]
. Let us denote the parabolic distance function
\rho(z_{1},z_{2})
between two points
z_{1}=(x_{1},t_{1})
and
z_{2}=(x_{2},t_{2})
as
\rho(z_{1},z_{2}):=\sqrt{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}+|t_{1}-t_{2}|}
. First, we define the parabolic Hölder spaces:

Definition 3.30
.

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
be a domain. Then, we denote the norm

[u]_{\delta,\delta/2;Q}:=\sup_{z_{1}\neq z_{2},\;z_{i}\in Q}\frac{|u(z_{1})-u(z_{2})|}{\rho^{\delta}(z_{1},z_{2})}.

We define the Hölder norm, for non-negative integers
k,r\geq 0
as

|u|_{C^{k+2r+\delta,r+\delta/2}(Q)}:=|u|_{C^{k+2r,r}(Q)}+\sum_{2\alpha+|\beta|=k+2r,\alpha\leq r}[\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\nabla^{\beta}u]_{\delta,\delta/2;Q}.

3.2.3
Interior Sobolev estimates

Let
Lu:=a^{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}u+b^{i}\partial_{i}u+cu
be a second order, elliptic partial differential operator defined on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\kappa|\xi|^{2}\geq\sum_{i,j}a^{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\kappa^{-1}|\xi|^{2}

for a fixed constant
\kappa
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the
C^{k+2r-2,r-1,\alpha}
-norms of
a^{ij},b^{i},c
are bounded in
\Omega
by a constant
\Lambda
. We will require two kinds of domains for our interior estimates, one near the boundary and one away from the boundary:

1.

Let
B_{1}\subset\joinrel\subset B_{2}
be two concentric Euclidean balls in
\Omega
with radii
r_{2}>r_{1}>0
, and
I_{i}:=(-\varepsilon_{i},0)
for
i=1,2
be two open intervals, such that
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
. We denote
Q_{i}:=B_{i}\times I_{i}
for
i=1,2
, so that
Q_{1}\subset Q_{2}
. These will be used for estimates away from the boundary
t=0
.

2.

Let
B_{1}\subset\joinrel\subset B_{2}
be two open balls as before. Let
I:=[0,\varepsilon)
be a half-open interval. We denote
P_{i}:=B_{i}\times I
for
i=1,2
, such that
P_{1}\subset P_{2}
. These will be used for estimates near the boundary
t=0
.

Theorem 3.31
.

Let
u\in L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(Q_{2})
and let us denote
h:=(\partial_{t}-L)u
. Then, we have that

\|u|_{Q_{1}}\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C\|h|_{Q_{2}}\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r-2,r-1}}+C^{\prime}\|u|_{Q_{2}}\|_{L^{p}},

(3.2)

for constants
C=C(\kappa,\Lambda)
and
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\kappa,\Lambda,B_{1},B_{2},\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
.

Proof.

The case
r=1
follows from the interior estimate in
[
Kry08
]
Theorem 5.2.5, along with an estimate over an intermediate ball
Q_{R^{\prime}}
. The case
r>1
follows by induction, and compactness of mappings from a Sobolev space with higher derivatives to one with fewer derivatives.
∎

Theorem 3.32
.

Let
u\in L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(P_{2})
and let us denote
h:=(\partial_{t}-L)u
. Let
v\in L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(P_{2})
such that
u-v\in\mathring{L}^{p}_{k+2r,r}(P_{2})
Then, we have that

\|u|_{P_{1}}\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C(\|h|_{P_{2}}\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r-2,r-1}}+\|v|_{P_{2}}\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}})+C^{\prime}\|u|_{P_{2}}\|_{L^{p}},

(3.3)

for a constant
C=C(\kappa,\Lambda)
and
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\kappa,\Lambda,B_{1},B_{2},\varepsilon)
. Moreover, the constant
C^{\prime}
is monotonically increasing in
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

The case
r=1
follows from an a-priori estimate from
[
Kry08
]
Theorem 5.2.10, along with using a cutoff function to get an interior estimate. The case
r>1
follows by induction and compactness as before.
∎

3.2.4
Parabolic Sobolev Embedding

We recall some Sobolev embedding theorems for parabolic Sobolev spaces. The first two embeddings in the following result are as in Theorem 10.4 of the book by Besov, Il’in and Nikol’skiĭ
[
BIN78
]
.

Theorem 3.33
.

Define the quantity

\xi:=\frac{s+(1/p)-(1/q)}{r}+(n-1)\frac{l+2s+(1/p)-(1/q)}{k+2r}.

(3.4)

Let
\xi\leq 1
, and suppose that for
\xi=1
, either

1<p\leq q<\infty\;\mathrm{or}\;1=p<q=\infty.

Then, we have the embedding

L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0})\hookrightarrow L^{q}_{l+2s,s}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}).

(3.5)

Now, suppose we define
q=\infty
and
\xi
as before. Then, we have the embedding

L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0})\hookrightarrow C^{l+2s,s}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}).

(3.6)

Moreover, if
\xi<1
, we have the embedding

L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0})\hookrightarrow C^{l+2s+\delta,s+\delta/2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}),

(3.7)

for
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

The main idea of the proof is define mollifications
u_{\varepsilon}:=u*\phi_{\varepsilon}
, and then to write
\partial_{i}u_{\varepsilon}
pointwise as an integral involving
\nabla^{2}u_{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
. Then, we estimate the pointwise norms using Minkowski’s and Hölder’s inequalities.
∎

Remark 3.34
.

In
[
BIN78
]
, the theorem is stated more generally for open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying a ‘weak
l
-horn condition’. The half-space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}
satisfies this condition.

3.2.5
Traces in parabolic Sobolev spaces

Consider the space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{\geq 0}
. We note that there exists a bounded linear mapping,

T:L^{p}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{\geq 0})\rightarrow L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

such that for
u\in L^{p}_{1}\cap C^{0}
, we have
T(u)=u|_{t=0}
. This mapping is called the trace, for example see §5.5 of Evans’ book
[
Eva10
]
. We can extend the trace map to parabolic Sobolev spaces to define a bounded linear map

T:L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{\geq 0})\rightarrow L^{p}_{k+2r-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

This map is also not surjective in general.
However, for
p=2
, we do obtain a surjectivity result, following Krylov:

Lemma 3.35
.

Let
u\in L^{2}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, there exists a function
v\in L^{2}_{2,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{\geq 0})
such that
\|v\|_{L^{2}_{2,1}}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}_{1}}
for a constant
C
independent of
u
, and
T(u)=v
.

Proof.

We define
v
to be the solution to the heat equation

(\partial_{t}-\Delta+1)v=0,\;v_{t=0}=u.

Then, taking Fourier transforms in space, we get for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(\partial_{t}+|\xi|^{2}+1)\hat{v}(\xi)(t)=0,\;\hat{v}(\xi)(0)=\hat{u}(\xi).

This has unique solution
\hat{v}(\xi)(t)=e^{-(1+|\xi|^{2})t}\hat{u}(\xi)
, from which we get

\begin{split}\|\nabla_{x}^{2}v\|_{L^{2}}&=\||\xi|^{2}e^{-(1+|\xi|^{2})t}\hat{u}(\xi)\|_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\xi|^{4}\hat{u}^{2}(\xi)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2(1+|\xi|^{2})t}dtd\xi\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\xi|^{4}}{2(1+|\xi|^{2})}\hat{u}^{2}(\xi)d\xi\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.\end{split}

Similarly, we can show that
\|v\|_{L^{2}_{2,1}}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}_{1}}
.
∎

Now, we can extend the above to domains of the form
\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon)
, to get:

Lemma 3.36
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Let
u\in L^{2}_{1}(\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon))
. Then, there exists a function
v\in L^{2}_{2,1}(\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon))
such that
\|v\|_{L^{2}_{2,1}}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}_{1}}
, for a constant
C
depending only on the domain.

Proof.

By Stein’s extension theorem, we can first extend
u
to a function
\tilde{u}
on all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we can use the previous lemma to get a function
v
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{\geq 0}
, then restrict it to
\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon)
.
∎

Therefore, we obtain:

Lemma 3.37
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Let
u\in L^{2}_{k+2r-1}(\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon))
. Then, there exists
v\in L^{2}_{k+2r,r}(\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon))
and a constant
C
depending only on
\Omega
such that

\|v\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-1}},\;v|_{t=0}=u.

Proof.

Let us construct
v
as a solution to the heat equation as before. Then, the estimates follow since the derivatives
\nabla^{k+2r-j}_{x}v
for
2\leq j\leq k+2r
satisfy the same heat equation, with initial data
\nabla^{k+2r-j}_{x}u
.
∎

4
Constructing the approximate solution

Recall that we have a given ambient Calabi–Yau manifold

, and a compact, oriented, conically singular Lagrangian embedding
\iota:L\rightarrow X
with a finite number of conical singularities
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. For simplicity of notation, we work with a single conical singularity
x
– the general case follows in the same way with only notational changes. Consider a Darboux neighbourhood and embedding
\Upsilon
as constructed in Theorem
2.25
. Suppose that the corresponding tangent cone
C
to
L
at
x
is a union of special Lagrangian cones and there is an asymptotically conical expander
E
that is asymptotic to

L_{0}:=L

4.1
The approximate solution as a family of embeddings

4.1.1
Gluing the expanders

Recall that the expander
E
has asymptotic cone
C=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}C_{j}
, where the
C_{j}
are special Lagrangian, and we denote the link of
C_{j}
by
\Sigma_{j}
.
Let us denote the scale of gluing
\zeta(t):=\sqrt{2t}
. Choose two radii
R_{1},R_{2}
such that
R_{1}\gg 0
and
1\gg R_{2}>0
. For
t
sufficiently small, we have that
{\zeta(t)}R_{1}<R_{2}
. Now, we choose two time-dependent cutoff functions
\chi_{1}(t,r)
and
\chi_{2}(t,r)
, to be determined later. They are assumed to satisfy the property that

\chi_{i}(t,r)=0\textrm{ for }r\in[0,\zeta(t)R_{1})\textrm{ and }\chi_{i}(t,r)=1\textrm{ for }r\in(R_{2},\infty).

(4.1)

We will construct our embedding in three pieces:

1.

The graph of a
1
-form
td\theta
over
L\backslash\iota^{-1}(\Upsilon(B_{R_{2}}))
under the embedding
\Phi_{L}
.

2.

Consider the annulus
\Sigma_{j}\times({\zeta(t)}R_{1},R_{2})\subset C_{j}
. Over these annuli, we take the graphs of 1-forms

d[(1-\chi_{1}(t,r)){\zeta(t)}^{2}\beta_{j}(\sigma,{\zeta(t)}^{-1}r)+\chi_{2}(t,r)(A_{j}(\sigma,r)+t(\theta-\theta_{j}))]

under the embedding
\Upsilon\circ\Phi_{C}
, where
\theta_{j}
is the phase of the cone
C_{j}
and
\theta
is a lift of the Lagrangian angle of
L
such that
\theta(p)=\theta_{j}
at the singularity.

3.

The scaled expander
{\zeta(t)}E\cap B_{\zeta(t)R_{1}}
under the embedding
\Upsilon
.

Lemma 4.1
.

This construction defines an embedded Lagrangian submanifold in
X
for a suitable choice of constants.

Proof.

Note that since we are working with graphs of closed
1
-forms under Lagrangian embeddings, the three pieces are Lagrangian submanifolds. Now, we need to check two things – that the graphs lie in the Lagrangian neighbourhoods for embedding, and that the graphs agree on overlap. It is routine to check that these claims follow due to the choice of interpolating functions, similarly as in §6.1 of
[
Joy04
]
.
∎

We will denote the desingularisation at time
t
by
\widetilde{L}_{t}
.

4.1.2
Construction of Lagrangian neighbourhoods for the desingularisations

We define the Lagrangian neighbourhoods and embedding of the family of desingularisations
\{\tilde{L}_{t}\}_{t\in(0,\varepsilon)}
, by describing them over the three pieces of the Lagrangian:

•

For the graph over
\iota(L_{0})\backslash\Upsilon(B_{R_{2}})
, the Lagrangian embedding is given by
\Psi_{t}:U_{L}\rightarrow X
such that
\Psi_{t}(x,v)=\Phi_{L}(x,F_{t}^{*}v+td\theta)
, where
F_{t}:L_{0}\backslash\iota^{-1}(\Upsilon(B_{R_{2}}))\rightarrow X
is the diffeomorphism from the Lagrangian onto the graph
\Gamma(du_{t}+td\theta)
.

•

For the embedding of the graph over
\Sigma_{j}\times(\zeta(t)R_{1},R_{2})
, we take the Lagrangian embedding
\Psi_{t}:U_{L}\rightarrow X
such that
\Psi_{t}(x,v)=\Upsilon\circ\Phi_{C}(x,G_{t}^{*}v+dp_{t})
where
G_{t}:\Sigma\times(\zeta(t)R_{1},R_{2})\rightarrow X
is the diffeomorphism from the annulus to the graph
\Gamma(dp(t))
, for

p(t)=(1-\chi_{1}(t,r)){\zeta(t)}^{2}\beta_{j}(\sigma,{\zeta(t)}^{-1}r)+\chi_{2}(t,r)(A_{j}(\sigma,r)+t(\theta-\theta_{j})).

•

For the graph over the expander, we take the Lagrangian embedding
\Psi_{t}:=\Upsilon\circ\Phi_{\zeta(t)E}:U_{\zeta(t)E}\rightarrow X
as defined earlier, such that
\Psi_{t}(x,v)=\Upsilon\circ\zeta(t)\Phi_{E}(x,\zeta(t)^{-2}v).

Note that this gives us a family of Lagrangian neighbourhoods
U_{t}\subset T^{*}(\tilde{L}_{t})
due to the compatibility theorem, and embeddings
\Psi_{t}:U_{t}\rightarrow X
. Moreover, we have that for a family of
1
-forms
\alpha_{t}
such that
|\alpha_{t}|_{C^{0}}\leq C\rho
for a sufficiently small constant
C>0
,
\Gamma(\alpha_{t})\subset U_{t}
for
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
(here, the norm is with respect to the pullback of the metric from
X
).

Remark 4.2
.

In our construction of approximate solutions and Lagrangian embeddings, the construction near the scaled expander does not extend smoothly at
t=0
, due to shrinking the expander. However, we will show that they can be extended a-smoothly over
t=0
on doing the parabolic blow-up.

4.1.3
Hamiltonian isotopy class of desingularisations

We will show that the constructed Lagrangians lie in the correct Hamiltonian isotopy class for LMCF, i.e. they satisfy (
2.3
) by writing
\widetilde{L}_{t}
as the graph of a
1
-form
\alpha_{t}
over
\widetilde{L}_{t_{0}}
.

First, we consider the scaled expander
\zeta(t)E\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
as the graph of a 1-form
\Gamma(\alpha_{E_{t}})
over
\zeta(t_{0})E
. Then, we have that

\alpha_{E_{t}}=(t-t_{0})d\theta_{E}.

Therefore, we can write
\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}=d\theta_{E}
on the scaled expander. Now, making use of the compatibility of Lagrangian neighbourhoods, we can write the form
\alpha_{t}
on the gluing annulus as

\alpha_{t}=d[(1-\chi_{1}(t,r)){\zeta(t)}^{2}\beta_{j}(\sigma,{\zeta(t)}^{-1}r)+\chi_{2}(t,r)(A_{j}(\sigma,r)+t(\theta-\theta_{j}))]-\alpha_{t_{0}}.

Therefore, over the annulus we can write

\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}=d[(1-\chi_{1}(t,r))(\theta_{E}-\theta_{C_{k}})+\chi_{2}(t,r)(\theta_{L}-\theta_{C_{k}})],

where
\theta_{C_{k}}
denotes the choice of Lagrangian angle for the cone
C_{k}
, so that
\theta_{E}-\theta_{C_{k}}
goes to zero as
r\rightarrow\infty
, and
\theta_{L}-\theta_{C_{k}}
goes to zero as
r\rightarrow 0
. Outside the ball
B_{R_{2}}
, we can write
\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}=d\theta_{L}
. Therefore, we can write
\partial_{t}\alpha_{t}=d\widetilde{\theta}_{t}
, where
\widetilde{\theta}_{t}
is a locally-defined function which is globally well-defined up to multiples of
2\pi
, defined as:

\widetilde{\theta}_{t}:=\begin{cases}\theta_{E}&\textrm{on the scaled expander}\\
\theta_{C_{k}}+(1-\chi_{1}(t,r))(\theta_{E}-\theta_{C_{k}})+\chi_{2}(t,r)(\theta_{L}-\theta_{C_{k}})&\textrm{on the gluing annulus}\\
\theta_{L}&\textrm{outside the ball $B_{2R}$}\end{cases}

Now, we need to show that

[d\widetilde{\theta}_{t}]=[d\theta_{t}].

(4.2)

Note that the cohomology class
[d\widetilde{\theta}_{t}]
is independent of
t
; this is essentially because of the decay properties of
\theta_{E}-\theta_{C_{k}}
and
\theta_{L}-\theta_{C_{k}}
. The cohomology class
[d\theta_{t}]
is also independent of
t
, since the family
\{\widetilde{L}_{t}\}
is a Lagrangian isotopy. Now, due to the construction of the family
\{\widetilde{L}_{t}\}
, one can show similarly as in Proposition 6.4 of
[
Joy04
]
that the difference
\widetilde{\theta}_{t}-\theta_{t}
has
C^{0}
-norm going to zero as
t\downarrow 0
. Therefore, the equality (
4.2
) holds as
t\downarrow 0
, therefore due to the classes being constant in time, the equality holds at all times, i.e. we have shown that:

Proposition 4.3
.

The constructed family of Lagrangians lies in the correct Hamiltonian isotopy class for LMCF.

4.2
The parabolic blow-up of the domain

In this section, we define the target space of our immersion as a manifold with corners and a-corners. In particular, we will construct a parabolic blow-up of the space
X\times[0,\varepsilon)
at the point
(x_{0},0)
(c.f. §3 of
[
LMPS23
]
).

4.2.1
Blow-up of
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)

Let us write each point
((z_{1},\dots,z_{m}),t)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)
with respect to parameters
\rho\geq 0,\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{\geq 0}
, such that we have a blow-down map
\pi:\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{\geq 0}\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)
, sending

((\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},..,\sigma_{m},\sigma_{m+1}),\rho)\mapsto((\rho\sigma_{1},\rho\sigma_{2},\dots,\rho\sigma_{m}),\rho^{2}\sigma_{m+1}^{2}).

We denote this blow-up by
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))
. (Note that here we parametrize
\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{\geq 0}
with the complex coordinates
(\sigma_{1},..,\sigma_{m})
and one real coordinate
\sigma_{m+1}\geq 0
.) Now, we endow the blow-up with the natural a-smooth structure on
\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{\geq 0}\times\llbracket 0,\infty)
, with a single a-boundary at
r=0
, a single ordinary boundary at
\sigma_{m+1}=0
, and a codimension
2
corner which is the intersection of these two boundaries. This defines the parabolic blow-up of
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\epsilon)
at the point
(\mathbf{0},0)
, as an object in
\mathbf{Man}^{\mathbf{c,ac}}
.

The exceptional divisor, or the
front face
, is the submanifold
\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{0}\times 0)\cong\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{\geq 0}\times\{0\}
. The
bottom face
is the submanifold
\overline{\pi^{-1}((\mathbb{C}^{m}\backslash\mathbf{0})\times\{0\})}
. We can write down projective coordinates on the interior of the front face and the intersection of front and bottom faces:

•

The front face: away from the corner, this has projective coordinates in a neighbourhood given by
(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{m},\tau)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\times\llbracket 0,\epsilon)
(identifying
\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{+}
with
\mathbb{C}^{m}
via the map
(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots,\sigma_{m+1})\mapsto(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sigma_{m+1}},\dots,\frac{\sigma_{m}}{\sigma_{m+1}})
), such that
(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{m},\tau)\mapsto(z_{1}\tau,\dots,z_{m}\tau,\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2})
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)
along the blow-down map.

•

The intersection of the bottom and front faces: this has a neighbourhood locally modeled as
\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}\times[0,\epsilon)\times\llbracket 0,\epsilon)
, with local coordinates given by
((\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}),\rho,s)\mapsto(\sigma_{1}\rho,\dots,\sigma_{m}\rho,\rho^{2}s)
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)
.

Note that these projective coordinates also locally define the smooth structure on our blow-up, i.e a function is smooth if and only if it is smooth in these projective coordinates locally.

Remark 4.4
.

Note that
\tau
is not an a-smooth function on the blow-up, since near the corner, it is given by
\tau=\rho(s/2)^{1/2}
, and
s
is a boundary-defining function for the ordinary boundary near the corner.

Definition 4.5
.

The front face has a natural boundary-defining function
\rho
, given by the lift of the function
\sqrt{|z|^{2}+t}
– this is an a-smooth function on the blow-up.

4.2.2
The
b
-metric on
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))

We define a
b
-metric on
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))
as:

g:=\frac{g_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}}{\rho^{2}}+\frac{dt^{2}}{\rho^{4}}.

In order to show this is a well-defined
b
-metric, we compute in local coordinates near the front face and near the corner (it is clearly an ordinary metric away from the a-boundary where
\rho>0
). Note that in
(w^{1},..,w^{2m},\tau)
coordinates, we have

g_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\sum{dz^{i}}^{2}=\tau^{2}\sum{dw^{i}}^{2}+\sum\tau^{2}w^{i}(\tau^{-1}d\tau\otimes dw^{i}+dw^{i}\otimes\tau^{-1}d\tau)+\tau^{2}\cdot\left(\sum{w^{i}}^{2}\right)(\tau^{-1}d\tau)^{2},

dt^{2}=\tau^{4}(\tau^{-1}d\tau)^{2}.

And, we also have that
\rho(x,t)=\sqrt{t+|z|^{2}}=\tau(\frac{1}{2}+|w|^{2})^{1/2}
. So, we have

g=s\left[\sum{dw^{i}}^{2}+\sum w^{i}(\tau^{-1}d\tau\otimes dw^{i}+dw^{i}\otimes\tau^{-1}d\tau)+\left(s+\sum{w^{i}}^{2}\right)(\tau^{-1}d\tau)^{2}\right],

where
s=\frac{t}{\rho^{2}}=(\frac{1}{2}+|w|^{2})^{-1}
.
Then, this is a positive-definite form on the front face, since the determinant of the associated matrix is
(\frac{1}{2}+|w|^{2})^{-2m-2}
. Now, we can compute at the codimension-2 corner in
(\sigma,r,s)\in\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}\times\llbracket 0,\epsilon)\times[0,\epsilon)
coordinates with
t=r^{2}s
,
\rho=r
, and write the metric there to be

g_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=r^{2}g_{\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}}+dr^{2},\;\;dt^{2}=4r^{2}s^{2}dr^{2}+r^{4}ds^{2}+2r^{2}s(dr\otimes ds+ds\otimes dr),

so that we have

g=g_{\mathbb{S}^{2m-1}}+(1+4s^{2})(r^{-1}dr)^{2}+ds^{2}+2s(r^{-1}dr\otimes ds+ds\otimes r^{-1}dr).

This is also a positive-definite
2
-form. Therefore,
g
is a
b
-metric.

Consider the point of transverse intersection of

Since the blow-down mapping

(\Upsilon\times\textrm{id})\circ\pi:\pi^{-1}((B_{r}(\mathbf{0})\times[0,\varepsilon))\backslash(\mathbf{0},0))\rightarrow(X\times[0,\varepsilon))\backslash(x_{0},0).

We denote
\mathbb{B}:=\pi^{-1}((B_{r}(\mathbf{0})\times[0,\varepsilon)))

4.2.4
Choice of

-metric on

We define the
b
-metric on
\mathbb{X}
as the pullback of a certain metric on
(X\times[0,\varepsilon))\backslash(x_{0},0)

g:=\frac{1}{\rho(x,t)^{2}}g_{X}+\frac{1}{\rho(x,t)^{4}}dt^{2},

(4.3)

where

is the Calabi–Yau metric on

, and
\rho(x,t)
is a boundary defining function for the a-boundary of

. We define

by extending the earlier boundary defining function on
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))

Definition 4.7
.

The boundary defining function
\rho
for the front face of
\mathbb{X}
is defined to be

\rho(x,t)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{|x|^{2}+t},&(x,t)\in\pi^{-1}(\Upsilon_{0}\times\mathrm{id}(B_{r/2}(\mathbf{0})\times[0,\varepsilon))),\\

To see that the defined metric (

-metric on

\Upsilon^{-1}(\rho^{-2}g_{X})-\rho^{-2}g_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\rho^{-2}r^{2}h,

4.2.5

\mathbb{X}

Due to the Darboux neighbourhood

\widetilde{\Upsilon}:\mathbb{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}

4.3.1
Construction in

Note that
\mathbb{B}
has one a-boundary, one ordinary boundary, and one codimension 2 corner. We will use coordinates on

similarly as defined earlier on
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))

•

Near the front face: Here,consider the scaled expander
\zeta(t)E
inside the ball

as

inside the ball

Since we have that

-coordinates by extending to

. Therefore, the scaled expanders compactify to an a-smooth submanifold near the front face, which is diffeomorphic to
(E\cap B_{R})\times\llbracket 0,\varepsilon)

•

Near the corner:
First, let us note that a neighbourhood of the codimension
2
corner in
\mathbb{B}
is given by a union of annuli
(B_{R_{2}}\backslash B_{\sqrt{2t}R_{1}})\times\{\tau\}
for

, which corresponds to
\{(\sigma,r,s):\sigma\in\mathbb{S}^{2m-1},s\in[0,\epsilon),r\in\llbracket 0,\epsilon)\}
in
(\sigma,r,s)
-coordinates introduced earlier. Now in the annulus, we defined
\widetilde{L}_{t}
as a graph over the cone

. So, we first note that the cones
(C\cap(B_{R_{2}}\backslash B_{\sqrt{2t}R_{1}}))\times\{t\}
compactify to an a-smooth submanifold in
\mathbb{B}
near the corner, that is a-diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{A}:=\{\sigma^{i}\in\Sigma,s\in[0,\varepsilon),r\in\llbracket 0,R_{2})\}
in projective coordinates.

First, we need to study how the relative cotangent bundle of
\mathbb{A}
embeds into
\mathbb{B}
under the Lagrangian embedding for the cone
\Phi_{C}
. Let us write a-smooth coordinates on
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{A}

{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{A}=\{(\sigma,r,s,\nu,\mu):\sigma\in\Sigma,r\in\llbracket 0,\varepsilon),s\in[0,\varepsilon),\nu\in T^{*}\Sigma,\mu\in\mathbb{R}\},

such that

denotes the relative

-form
\nu+\mu(r^{-1}dr)\in{{}^{b}}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}(\sigma,r,s)}\mathbb{A}

under the mapping
\Sigma\mapsto\Sigma\times\{(r,s)\}

Now, the action of

\varepsilon:(\sigma,r,s)\mapsto(\sigma,\varepsilon\cdot r,\varepsilon^{-2}s).

Therefore, we must have that

\varepsilon\cdot\Phi_{C}(\sigma,r,s,\nu,\mu)=\Phi_{C}(\sigma,\varepsilon\cdot r,\varepsilon^{-2}s,\varepsilon^{2}\nu,\varepsilon^{2}\cdot\mu)

(we get

Hence, we can put together the embeddings

for

to get a mapping,
\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}:U_{\mathbb{A}}\subset{{}^{b}T}^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
such that by the equivariance

\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}(\sigma,r,s,\tau,\mu)=\left(\frac{\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}(\sigma,1,r^{2}s,r^{-2}\nu,r^{-2}\mu)}{\|\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}(\sigma,1,r^{2}s,r^{-2}\nu,r^{-2}\mu)\|},r\cdot\|\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}(\sigma,1,r^{2}s,r^{-2}\nu,r^{-2}\mu)\|,s\right)\in\mathbb{B}.

(4.4)

Clearly, this mapping is smooth in

away from

with multiplication by

\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}\circ r^{2}:\tilde{U}_{\mathbb{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}
. Now, given a function

over the compactified annuli, the graphs
\Gamma(du_{t})
over the Lagrangian embeddings
\Phi_{C}
corresponds to the embedding of the graph
\Gamma(r^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u)
over
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{A}
under
\widetilde{\Phi}_{C}

f(\sigma,r,s)=(1-\chi_{1}(r,s))2r^{2}s\beta_{E_{t}}(\sigma,s^{-1/2})+\chi_{2}(r,s)(A_{j}(\sigma,r)+r^{2}s(\theta-\theta_{j})),

Lemma 4.8

∎

Now, by the following Lemma, it follows that
\Upsilon_{t}^{-1}(\tilde{L}_{t})
near the corner, given by the embedding of

Lemma 4.9

The relative
1

Proof.

This follows since

∎

Therefore, the embeddings

.

•

Finally, we provide charts for

4.3.2
The symplectic form and Lagrangian embedding

First, recall that
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
has been defined as a vector bundle over
\mathbb{L}
, and it is also a manifold with corners and a-corners, with a single a-boundary.

Definition 4.13
.

There is a canonical relative symplectic form
\hat{\omega}
, i.e a non-degenerate element of
\Lambda^{2}(^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}(^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}))
. Given local coordinates
(x^{i},y^{i},t)
on
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
, we can write it as
\hat{\omega}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}dx^{i}\wedge dy^{i}
.

Under the embedding
T^{*}L_{t}\hookrightarrow{{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}}\mathbb{L}
, this symplectic form pulls back to the standard symplectic form on
T^{*}L_{t}
. Also, note that it is non-degenerate, i.e
.
we have that
\hat{\omega}(v,\cdot)\not\equiv 0
for
v\in{{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}}({{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}})
and
v\not=0
.

The Lagrangian embeddings
\Psi_{t}
on the time-slices for
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
combine to give us an embedding of a neighbourhood of
T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
over the interior of
\mathbb{L}
, as
\Psi_{\mathbb{L}^{\circ}}:U_{\mathbb{L}^{\circ}}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}
. However, this does not extend to an a-smooth embedding over the a-boundary, because of the scaling by
\rho^{-2}
near the corner as explained in the second point of the previous subsection §
4.3.1
. In order to make it a-smooth, we need to pre-compose this map with multiplying each fibre by
\rho^{2}
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Consider the map

\rho^{2}:T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}\rightarrow T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L},

given by multiplying each fibre by
\rho(x)^{2}
. Then, we have that
\tilde{U}_{\mathbb{L}}:=\overline{(\rho^{2})^{-1}(U_{\mathbb{L}^{\circ}})}
is an open neighbourhood of the zero section of
T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
, and
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{L}}:=\Psi_{\mathbb{L}^{\circ}}\circ\rho^{2}
extends to an a-smooth embedding of
\tilde{U}_{\mathbb{L}}
into
\mathbb{X}
.

Proof.

This follows in the same way as we argued in §
4.3.1
. One needs to work in local coordinates near the front face of
\mathbb{L}
, and note the form of the embedding
\Psi_{\mathbb{L}}
near the front face, as given in (
4.4
), to conclude that
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{L}}
is a-smooth.
∎

Note that the graph of a function
\Gamma(d_{\mathrm{rel}}u)\subset U_{\mathbb{L}}
corresponds to the graph
\Gamma(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u)\subset\widetilde{U}_{\mathbb{L}}
.

Remark 4.15
.

We can write the modified map
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{L}}
in a cleaner way by considering it as a map from
T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}\otimes L_{2}
to
\mathbb{X}
, where
L_{2}
is the weight
2
line bundle on
\mathbb{L}
, as defined in §4.4 of
[
Joy16
]
.
L_{2}
has a flat connection
\nabla
and a flat section

, such that if we identify
L_{2}
with the trivial line bundle
\mathbb{R}
over
\mathbb{L}
, then
s
corresponds to a section with a zero of order
2
at the front face. Then, we can define a map,

T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}\otimes L_{2}\rightarrow T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L},

as
\mathrm{id}\otimes(s\mapsto\rho^{2})
. Composing this map with the Lagrangian embeddings on the time-slices
\Psi_{t}
, we get a map
(\mathrm{id}\otimes(s\mapsto\rho^{2}))\circ\Psi
, which gives us an a-smooth embedding of a neighbourhood of the zero-section of

.

Therefore, we have the following:

Proposition 4.16
.

Let
L\subset X
be a given embedded conically singular Lagrangian, with a single conical singularity at
x_{0}\in X
such that the asymptotic cone is a union of special Lagrangian cones, and let

be an asymptotically conical expander with the same asymptotic cone. We can construct a manifold with corners and a-corners

, along with an embedding
\mathbb{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
, such that
\mathbb{L}|_{\textrm{bf}}=\overline{\widetilde{L_{0}}}
, and
\mathbb{L}|_{\textrm{ff}}=\overline{E}
. Moreover, there is a Lagrangian neighbourhood
\widetilde{U}_{\mathbb{L}}
of the zero section of
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
, along with an a-smooth embedding
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{L}}:\widetilde{U}_{\mathbb{L}}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}
, which is Lagrangian up to a factor of

, i.e. such that
\tilde{\Psi}_{\mathbb{L}}^{*}(\omega_{\mathbb{X}})=\rho^{2}\hat{\omega}
.

4.4
The Geometry of
\mathbb{L}\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon)

We have that the time variable on
\mathbb{L}
defines an a-smooth mapping,
t:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon)
, which is a
b
-fibration. We can understand the geometry of this fibration via its ‘local models’.

4.4.1
The local models for
\mathbb{L}

In this subsection, we will study what our metric looks like locally on
\mathbb{L}
. More precisely, we construct ‘model neighbourhoods’ of
\mathbb{L}
, where the metric is of
\mathbb{L}
is close to a collection of model metrics for manifolds with corners and a-corners. First, we define the local model spaces:

1.

U\times(a,b)
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an open ball, we have a time variable
t\in(a,b)
, and we have a metric given by
g_{0}+dt^{2}
.

2.

U\times[0,\delta)
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an open ball and
U\times\{0\}
is an ordinary boundary, we have a time variable
t\in[0,\delta)
, and we have a metric given by
g_{0}+dt^{2}
.

3.

U\times\llbracket 0,\delta)
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an open ball and
U\times\{0\}
is an a-boundary, we have a time variable
t\in\llbracket 0,\delta)
, and we have a
b
-metric given by
g_{0}+t^{-2}dt^{2}
.

4.

V\times\llbracket 0,\delta)\times[0,\delta)
, parametrized by
(\sigma,r,s)
, where
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
is an open ball,
r=0
is an a-boundary,
s=0
is an ordinary boundary, we have a time variable
t=r^{2}s
, and we have a
b
-metric
g_{0}+ds^{2}+r^{-2}dr^{2}
.

Now, we claim the following:

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}>0
be a given positive constant. Then, there is a finite collection of local models
\{(M_{i},g_{i}):i\in I\}
, along with maps
\Psi_{i}:M_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
from local models such that their images are open, they are a-diffeomorphisms onto their images,
\bigcup_{i\in I}\Psi_{i}(M_{i})
gives a covering of
\mathbb{L}
, the time variable of
M_{i}
matches with the time variable of
\mathbb{L}
, and
\|\Psi_{i}^{*}g_{\mathbb{L}}-g_{i}\|_{C^{0}}<\varepsilon_{1}
. Moreover, we have that
\|\nabla^{k}(\Psi_{i}^{*}g_{\mathbb{L}}-g_{i})\|_{C^{0}}<C_{k}
for
k\geq 0
, where the connection and norm is with respect to the metric
g_{i}
.

Proof.

We use an inductive process to choose the local models. To choose a finite cover, we work with the compactification
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
of
\mathbb{L}
given by adding the time-slice at
t=\varepsilon
.

1.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a finite covering of the codimension-two corner
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\sqcup\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
of
\mathbb{L}
. Then, we can find charts
\Psi_{i}:U_{i}\times\llbracket 0,\delta)\times[0,\delta)\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
, mapping
(\sigma,r,s)\mapsto(\sigma r,r^{2}s)
such that
\Psi_{i}
is an isometry at a point
\{\sigma_{0},0,0\}
. This is because we can find such charts for the compactification of the expander near the corner, and
\mathbb{L}
is an a-smooth perturbation of it. Then, it follows that
|\Psi_{i}^{*}g_{\mathbb{L}}-{{}^{b}g}_{0}|<\varepsilon
,
|^{b}\nabla^{k}(\Psi_{i}^{*}g_{\mathbb{L}}-{{}^{b}g}_{0})|<C
, and the time variable matches.

2.

Let
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a finite cover of the front face minus the previous covering, such that we have charts
\Phi_{i}:V_{i}\times\llbracket 0,\delta)\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
, mapping
(w^{i},\tau)\mapsto(\tau w^{i},\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2})
, such that it is an isometry at a point
(x_{0},0)
. We can pre-compose it with the a-diffeomorphism
t\mapsto t^{\frac{1}{2}}=\tau
so that the time variable matches.

3.

Let
\{W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
be a finite cover of the bottom face minus the first covering, so we have charts
\Phi_{i}:W_{i}\times[0,\delta)\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
by flowing along the time-like vector field such that it is an isometry at a point
(x_{0},0)
, and the time variable matches.

4.

The rest of the manifold can now be covered by finitely many balls of the form
U\times(a,b)
(since
\overline{\mathbb{L}}
is compact) such that the metric is close in them, and the time variable matches.

This gives us a finite covering of
\mathbb{L}
by model neighbourhoods.
∎

Remark 4.18
.

The above proof can be carried out for more general ‘fibrations’, by considering local models with an arbitrary number of ordinary boundaries and a-boundaries.

Having these local models, statements on
\mathbb{L}
can be proven by proving corresponding statements for the local models.

4.4.2
The time-like vector field

Definition 4.19
.

We define the
time-like vector field
over the interior
\mathbb{\mathring{L}}
as a vector field
v\in\Gamma(T\mathring{\mathbb{L}})
such that
\pi_{*}(v)=\partial_{t}
, and
v
is orthogonal to the time-slices in the natural metric.

This is not an a-smooth vector field on
\mathbb{L}
, since it does not vanish at the front face. However, we can make it a-smooth by multiplying with a weight:

Proposition 4.20
.

The vector field
\rho^{2}v
is a non-vanishing a-smooth section of
{}^{m}T\mathbb{L}
.

Proof.

To prove this, it is enough to prove the statement for the corresponding local models near the front face, since the vector fields are clearly smooth away from the front face. Then, we have:

1.

For the model
U\times\llbracket 0,\delta)
,
v=\partial_{t},\rho=C\sqrt{t}
for a non-vanishing a-smooth function
C
, hence
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}=Ct\partial_{t}
, which is a-smooth and non-vanishing.

2.

For the model
U\times\llbracket 0,\delta)\times[0,\delta)
, the time-like vector field is
(r^{-2}\partial_{s}+2sr^{-1}\partial_{r})(1+4s^{2})^{-1}
, and
\rho=Cr
. Therefore,
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}=C(1+4s^{2})^{-1}(\partial_{s}-2sr\partial_{r})
, which is a-smooth and non-vanishing.

∎

Remark 4.21
.

The fact that
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}
is non-vanishing as an a-smooth vector field is relevant to the linearised operator being parabolic.

4.5
Sobolev and Hölder spaces on
\mathbb{L}

In this section, we define some natural Sobolev and Hölder spaces on
\mathbb{L}
using the a-smooth structure on
\mathbb{L}
in which
\rho=0
is the a-boundary, with the corresponding
b
-metric on
\mathbb{L}
. These can also be considered as defining Sobolev spaces over the fibration
(\mathbb{L}\backslash(t=0))\rightarrow(0,\varepsilon)
.

Remark 4.22
.

The main reason why we do not define the bottom face of
\mathbb{L}
as an a-boundary is that near the bottom face, the linearised operator is a multiple of the standard parabolic operator
\partial_{t}-\Delta
– if the bottom face were an a-boundary, we would expect the operator near it to be of the form
t\partial_{t}-\Delta
.

As before, given the
b
-fibration
\pi:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow[0,\varepsilon)
, we can consider the relative tangent bundle of
\mathbb{L}
, given by
\mathrm{ker}(d\pi)
and is a sub-bundle of
{}^{b}T\mathbb{L}
. There is a natural relative
b
-metric on
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
induced from the
b
-metric on
\mathbb{L}
, and corresponding natural Levi-Civita connections
{}^{b}\nabla
on
{}^{m}T\mathbb{L}
and
{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}
on
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
.

Proposition 4.23
.

We have the following about the relative constructions on
\mathbb{L}
:

1.

The relative tangent bundle
{}^{b}T_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
is an a-smooth vector sub-bundle of
{}^{b}T\mathbb{L}
, and is therefore itself an a-smooth vector bundle on
\mathbb{L}
. As a corollary, so are its duals and tensor products.

2.

The relative
b
-metric is an a-smooth section of
\textrm{Sym}^{2}(^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L})
.

Proof.

As before, it suffices to check a-smoothness on the local models for
\mathbb{L}
near the front face.

1.

In the local model
U\times\llbracket 0,\varepsilon)
, the relative tangent bundle is the sub-bundle spanned by
\partial_{x_{i}}
, which are a-smooth sections. The relative
b
-metric is given by
\sum\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}
.

2.

In the local model
V\times\llbracket 0,\varepsilon)\times[0,\varepsilon)
, the relative tangent bundle is spanned by
\partial_{x_{i}},(\rho\partial_{\rho}-2s\partial_{s})
. The relative
b
-metric is given by
\sum d{x_{i}}^{2}+(\rho^{-1}d\rho-sds)^{2}
. These sections are a-smooth.

∎

Corollary 4.24
.

As a corollary to the relative metric and tangent bundle being a-smooth, we get that the relative Levi-Civita connection is an a-smooth
b
-connection.

We define our Sobolev spaces on
\mathbb{L}
as
L^{p}_{k+2r,r}(\mathbb{L})
, using the norm:

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}}:=\left(\sum_{\beta\leq r}\left[\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k-2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{L}}|\nabla^{\beta}\nabla_{\textrm{rel}}^{\alpha}u|^{p}dV_{\mathbb{L}}\right]\right)^{1/p},

where
dV_{\mathbb{L}}
is the
b
-volume form induced on
\mathbb{L}
from the
b
-metric. It is a section of
\Lambda^{n}({{}^{m}T^{*}\mathbb{L}})
. Note that we have the following Hölder’s inequality for our spaces:

\|fg\|_{L^{r}_{k+2r,r}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}}\|g\|_{L^{q}_{k+2r,r}}

for
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
.

We will define the Hölder distance between points
x,y\in\mathbb{L}
for
d_{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)
sufficiently small as follows:

d(x,y):=\sqrt{d_{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)^{2}+\int_{0}^{l}|\langle\gamma^{\prime}(s),\rho^{2}\partial_{v}(\gamma(s))\rangle|ds},

where
d_{\mathbb{L}}
is the distance function for the
b
-metric, and
\gamma:[0,l]\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
is the geodesic in the
b
-metric joining the points
x,y
. The key point about this definition is that it localises correctly to the usual notion of Hölder distances on the model space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{\geq 0}
. Then, we can also define Hölder spaces on
\mathbb{L}
, given by
C^{k+2r+\delta,r+\delta/2}(\mathbb{L})
, with norm

\|u\|_{C^{k+2r+\delta,r+\delta/2}(\mathbb{L})}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{L},\alpha+2\beta\leq k+2r,\beta\leq r}|\nabla^{\beta}\nabla_{\textrm{rel}}^{\alpha}u|+[\nabla^{r}\nabla_{\textrm{rel}}^{k}u]_{\delta/2;\delta},

where
[\cdot]_{\delta/2;\delta}
is the norm given by

[v]_{\delta/2,\delta}:=\sup_{d_{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)<\textrm{inj}_{\mathbb{L}},\;x\neq y}\frac{|v(x)-v(y)|}{d(x,y)^{\delta}}.

4.5.1
Weighted spaces

Now, for a weight
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, define the weighted Banach spaces
L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})
using the norm:

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}:=\|\rho^{-\lambda}u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r}}.

Note that we have the following Hölder’s inequality for our spaces:

\|fg\|_{L^{r}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda^{\prime}}}\|g\|_{L^{q}_{k+2r,r,\lambda^{\prime\prime}}}

for
\frac{1}{r}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
and
\lambda=\lambda^{\prime}+\lambda^{\prime\prime}
.

Similarly, we define weighted Hölder spaces on
\mathbb{L}
, given by
C^{k+2r+\delta,r+\delta/2,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})
, with norm

\|u\|_{C^{k+2r+\delta,r+\delta/2,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{L},\alpha+2\beta\leq k+2r,\beta\leq r}|\rho^{-\lambda}\nabla^{\beta}\nabla_{\textrm{rel}}^{\alpha}u|+[\rho^{-\lambda}\nabla^{r}\nabla_{\textrm{rel}}^{k}u]_{\delta}.

Remark 4.25
.

1.

We have that
{}^{b}\nabla^{k}(\rho^{-\lambda}u)=\rho^{-\lambda}{{}^{b}\nabla^{k}}u+\sum C_{j}{{}^{b}\nabla^{j}}(\rho^{-\lambda}){{}^{b}\nabla^{k-j}}u
, in which
|{{}^{b}\nabla^{j}}\rho^{-\lambda}|=O(\rho^{-\lambda})
and
C_{j}
are bounded a-smooth functions. Therefore, we can equivalently define the weighted spaces using norms

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}:=\left(\sum_{\beta\leq r}\left[\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k-2\beta}\int_{\mathbb{L}}|\rho^{-\lambda}\nabla^{\beta}\nabla_{\textrm{rel}}^{\alpha}u|^{p}dV_{\mathbb{L}}\right]\right)^{1/p}.

2.

We have that
\rho^{\lambda}\in L^{p}_{0,0,0}
if and only if
\lambda>0
. This is because by computation of
\int_{\mathbb{L}}\rho^{\lambda}dV_{\mathbb{L}}
, near the front face we get a term of the form
\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rho^{\lambda}\rho^{-1}d\rho
, which is finite if and only if
\lambda>0
.

We also note another useful result about our Sobolev spaces:

Lemma 4.26
.

Let
C^{\infty}_{\textrm{ff}}
be the subset of compactly supported smooth functions on
\mathbb{L}
supported away from the front face. Then, we have that it is dense in
L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
for
p<\infty
.

Proof.

The key point is that since we use a natural
b
-metric and
b
-volume form on
\mathbb{L}
, we can construct cutoff functions which interpolate to
0
in a neighbourhood of the front face,
1
outside of a neighbourhood of the front face, and such that all its derivatives are uniformly bounded and supported in an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of the front face. Multiplying a given function with such cutoff function proves the claim.
∎

4.6
The LMCF equation on potentials and linearisation

Let
u:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
\|u\|_{C^{2,0,2}}
is bounded by a constant that ensures the graphs
\Gamma(d_{\mathrm{rel}}u_{t})\subset T^{*}L_{t}
is contained in the Lagrangian neighbourhood for

. In this section, we will write the LMCF equation as a P.D.E. in terms of

, such that the embeddings of the graphs of

under the Lagrangian embeddings, i.e.
\Psi_{t}(\Gamma(d_{\mathrm{rel}}u_{t}))
, is a family of Lagrangians evolving by LMCF. We think of this as a perturbation of the constructed approximate solution
\mathbb{L}
to an exact solution. We also compute the linearisation of this P.D.E. at the approximate solution
\mathbb{L}
.

4.6.1
The P.D.E. for LMCF

Let
u
be a function as before, with
\|u\|_{C^{2,0,2}}
sufficiently small. There is a natural diffeomorphism
\sigma_{d_{\mathrm{rel}}u}:{{}^{b}T}^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}\rightarrow{{}^{b}T}^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
, given by
(x,\alpha(x))\mapsto(x,\alpha(x)+d_{\mathrm{rel}}u(x))
, preserving the relative
2
-form
\hat{\omega}
. In particular, this gives a map
\sigma_{d_{\mathrm{rel}}u}:\mathbb{L}^{\circ}=\Gamma(0)\rightarrow\Gamma(d_{\mathrm{rel}}u)
. Now, we have the Lagrangian embedding over the interior,

(4.7)

1
-forms on
\mathbb{L}

4.6.3
The Lagrangian angle

Now, we prove that the Lagrangian angle decomposes as claimed. Instead of the embedding

Theorem 4.32

There is an underlying a-smooth function

Proof.

Using this isomorphism (
4.8

\widetilde{\nabla}
, we will associate to each point

Now, we can write

Then, we can estimate the nonlinear term using the following from

Lemma 4.33

Let

be a normed vector space, and let

Using this and the a-smoothness of

Let

denote the nonlinear part of

First, note that

Now, we compute the linear part of the a-smooth function

4.6.4
The symplectic form

Now, we will show an analogous theorem for the symplectic form:

Proposition 4.35
.

We have the following decomposition,

-\hat{\omega}(v-{(\Psi\circ\sigma_{d_{\mathrm{rel}}u})^{*}(\partial_{t})},\rule{7.11317pt}{0.14226pt})|_{\mathbb{L}}=-\hat{\omega}(\Psi^{*}(\partial_{t})^{\perp}|_{\mathbb{L}},\rule{7.11317pt}{0.14226pt})|_{\mathbb{L}}+d_{\mathrm{rel}}(\partial_{v}u+V_{L_{t}}(u)+Q[\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u]),

.

Proof.

Let us consider local coordinates on
{}^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{L}
given by
(x^{i},y^{i},t)
, such that
v=\partial_{t}
,

. Then, the symplectic form is
\sum dx^{i}\wedge dy^{i}
. Now, let us write the pull back vector field
\Psi^{*}(\partial_{t})
as

\Psi^{*}(\partial_{t})=\partial_{t}+a^{i}\partial_{x^{i}}+b^{i}\partial_{y^{i}},

for locally-defined functions

being a family of Lagrangian embeddings, the relative

4.7

In this section, we will construct a ‘good cover’ of

\mathbb{L}

•

2.

For each point

3.

The charts satisfy

Now, we prove the existence of a good cover on

Lemma 4.38

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a given constant. There exists parameters

Due to the presence of finitely many model neighbourhoods as in Lemma

First, let us consider a model neighbourhood at the front face

(\sigma,r,s)

V\subset\mathbb{R}^{m-1}

b

\varepsilon

Now, we have that:

\mathbb{L}
). Then, we have that

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2,1}}\leq C_{k}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{p}_{k,0}}+\|u\|_{L^{p}}+\|v\|_{L^{p}_{k+2,1}}\right).

(4.13)

As a result, for
u
satisfying
u|_{\textrm{bf}}\equiv 0
(where we consider the trace of
u
on the bottom face), we get

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{k+2,1}}\leq C_{k}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{p}_{k,0}}+\|u\|_{L^{p}}\right).

(4.14)

Proof.

Consider a good cover of
\mathbb{L}
as before, given by
\{U_{i},\Psi\}_{i\in I}
. First, note that the pullback of the operator
\mathcal{L}
under
\Psi_{i}
will be an elliptic operator with uniformly bounded norms of coefficients and constants of ellipticity over the good cover (due to the norms being
C^{0}
-close and
C^{k}
-bounded). By the estimates (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we get local interior estimates for the standard parabolic operator on each open set
U_{i}
. We transfer these estimates to the pullback of
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}
by using estimates 2. and 3. in Lemma
4.40
for the good cover, and taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small. Finally, we invoke estimate 1. of Lemma
4.40
in order to transfer these estimates from
U_{i}
to local estimates on
\mathbb{L}
.
Now, we add them up as follows: raising the above estimates to the
p^{\mathrm{th}}
power, we have an
L^{p}
-estimate of the form

\|u|_{U_{i}}\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{k+2,1}}\leq C\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-{\mathcal{L}})u|_{U^{i}}\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{k,0}}+\|u|_{U_{i}}\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{0,0}}+\|u|_{\partial U_{i}}\|_{L^{p}_{2}}\right).

Summing up all these estimates over the cover
\{U_{i}\}
, and using the fact that each point of
\mathbb{L}
is covered by at most
N
open sets of the good cover, we would get that the RHS of the above is bounded above by

CN(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-{\mathcal{L}})u\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{k,0}}+\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p}}),

while the LHS is bounded below by
\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{k+2,1}}
. Therefore, taking
p^{\mathrm{th}}
roots, we get a global estimate of the form (
4.13
).

∎

Remark 4.45
.

An equivalent, but conceptually simpler, way to get the above result is to define parabolic operators on the model neighbourhoods themselves, and prove the parabolic interior estimates by considering good covers of the model neighbourhoods. Then, the above estimate follows by adding up finitely many estimates on
\mathbb{L}
arising from the model neighbourhoods.

In the case
p=2
, we have the following lemma regarding the trace function on
\mathbb{L}
, which follows easily from Lemma
3.37
:

Lemma 4.46
.

There exists a constant
C
depending only on
L_{0}
, such that for
u\in L^{2}_{k+2,1}(\mathbb{L})
, there exists a function
v\in L^{2}_{k+2,1}(\mathbb{L})
such that

\|v\|_{L^{2}_{k+2,1}(\mathbb{L})}\leq C\|u|_{t=0}\|_{L^{2}_{k+1}(\mathbb{L})},

and
u|_{t=0}=v|_{t=0}
in
L^{2}_{k+1}(L_{0})
.

As a result, we can state a slightly improved a-priori estimate for

which does not require an implicit function:

Proposition 4.47
.

Let
u\in L^{2}_{k+2,1}(\mathbb{L})
, and
\mathcal{L}
be a uniformly elliptic second-order partial differential operator. We have that

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2,1}}\leq C_{k}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{k,0}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\|u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{k+1}}\right),

(4.15)

for a constant

.

Then, we can take higher derivatives to show that:

Proposition 4.48
.

For integers
k,r\geq 1
, let
u\in L^{2}_{k+2r,r}(\mathbb{L})
, and
\mathcal{L}
be a uniformly elliptic second-order partial differential operator. We have that

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C_{k,r}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\|u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-1}}\right),

(4.16)

for a constant

.

4.7.4
Sobolev embedding

As a corollary to the above construction of model neighbourhoods and a good cover, we can get a global version of Theorem
3.33
:

Theorem 4.49
.

Consider the weighted Sobolev spaces defined in §
4.5.1
. Given parameters
k,r,l,s,p
and taking
q=\infty
, let
\xi
as defined in (
3.4
) satisfy
\xi<1
. Then, we have a continuous embedding
L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})\hookrightarrow C^{l+2s+\delta,s+\delta/2,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})
.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to prove the embedding with weight
\lambda=0
, since we can obtain the weighted estimates by applying the unweighted estimates to
\rho^{-\lambda}u
. Now, due to the hypotheses on the parameters, we have an embedding

L^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0})\hookrightarrow C^{l+2s+\delta,s+\delta/2,\lambda}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{0}),

(4.17)

by Theorem
3.33
. We know that there exists a good cover of
\mathbb{L}
with model pairs
\{(V_{i}\subset U_{i})\}_{i\in I}
and maps
\Psi_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
satisfying the properties outlined in Definition
4.37
. Now, we can slightly modify the argument and obtain a good cover with model pairs
\{(V_{i}\subset U_{i})\}_{i\in I}
which are of the form

•

U=B_{R_{2}}\times(-\varepsilon_{2},0)
,
V=B_{R_{1}}\times(-\varepsilon_{1},0)
.

•

U=B_{R_{4}}\times[0,\varepsilon_{3})
,
V=B_{R_{3}}\times[0,\varepsilon_{4})
,

where
\varepsilon_{4}<\varepsilon_{3}
is a new parameter. Then, in each case we can write down a cutoff function
\psi
such that in each of the above model pairs,
\psi|_{V}\equiv 1
,
\psi|_{U\backslash V^{\prime}}\equiv 0
for a fixed open set

, and

for

. Using these, we can apply the embedding (
4.17
) for the functions

and use Lemma
4.40
and Corollary

∎

4.7.5
Estimates with shortened time interval

In this subsection, we will formulate estimates such that we can shorten the time interval over which the solution exists without changing the constants in the estimates. These will be useful later in proving uniqueness of solutions to the nonlinear problem. Henceforth, we will consider a positive
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, and take all functions and norms over the shortened time interval
[0,\eta)
, i.e. we prove estimates for operators on the space
\mathbb{L}|_{\pi^{-1}([0,\eta))}
.

Lemma 4.50
.

Let
f=(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u
. Given any
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon)
,
we have the following a-priori estimate,

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\|\partial^{i}_{s}f|_{s=0}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2(r-i)-1,2}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\right),

(4.18)

and the constant is independent of
\eta
.

Proof.

In order to obtain this estimate, we need to choose a good cover for the restriction
\mathbb{L}|_{[0,\eta)}
, and then obtain estimates for the model neighbourhoods that have constant independent of
\eta
. First, we note that when shortening the time interval, we can choose a good cover as in Definition
4.37
, such that the only parameter that needs to be changed with
\eta
is
\varepsilon_{3}
– we need to choose
\varepsilon_{3}=O(\eta)
. The rest of the parameters of the good cover do not need to be changed. Therefore, we only need to obtain estimates on model neighbourhoods of the form
U=B_{R_{4}}\times[0,\varepsilon_{3}),V=B_{R_{3}}\times[0,\varepsilon_{3})
, with constant independent of
\varepsilon_{3}\downarrow 0
. We can obtain such estimates by applying Theorem
3.32
and Lemma
3.37
, noting that in both of these estimates the constant is non-increasing on decreasing
\varepsilon_{3}
. We add up these estimates, along with similar estimates for higher derivatives, to get the estimate (
4.18
) on the local models with constant independent of
\eta
, then add over
\mathbb{L}
.
∎

Lemma 4.51
.

Let us assume we are given
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon)
. Let
u\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
be a function such that
\partial_{s}^{k}u|_{s=0}\equiv 0
for
k\geq 0
, i.e.
u
vanishes to all orders at the bottom face. Then, we have the Sobolev embedding

\|u\|_{C^{l+2s,s,\alpha}}\leq C(\|u\|_{L^{k+2r,r,2}}),

and the constant is independent of
\eta
.

Proof.

To prove the Sobolev embedding, one needs to multiply by some cutoff functions to make the function supported away from the boundary. We need to ensure that these cutoff functions have norms of their derivatives bounded independent of shortening the time interval. The key point is that due to
u
vanishing to all orders at the bottom face, we only need to use cutoff functions in the spatial direction – since the function
\Psi_{i}^{*}u
on
B_{R}\times[0,\eta)
can be extended by zero to a function on
B_{R}\times(-\infty,\eta)
. Therefore, we obtain a constant independent of
\eta
.
∎

5
The linearised problem near the front face

Recall the parabolic blow-up of
\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)
we constructed earlier as
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))
. We will work interchangeably in two sets of coordinates – coordinates away from the corner given by
(\tau,w^{i})
for
\tau\in\llbracket 0,\infty),w^{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and coordinates in a neighbourhood of the front face given by
(\rho,\sigma^{i})
for
\rho\in\llbracket 0,\infty),\sigma^{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{2m}_{+}
. Recall that these coordinates are related to the

-coordinates in the blown-down space

Also, note that

is the boundary-defining function for the front face, given by

Definition 5.1
.

We define the manifold with corners and a-corners

as the compactification of the family
\{(\sqrt{2t}E,t):t\in(0,\infty)\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty)

\mathbb{L}
. Thus,
\mathbb{E}
has one ordinary boundary, which we call the bottom face, and one a-boundary, which we call the front face.

Similarly as
\mathbb{L}
, there is a time-like vector field on
\mathbb{E}
, as the unique vector field
v
satisfying
\pi_{*}(v)=\partial_{t}
, and
v
being orthogonal to the time-slices. Then. we similarly define the
b
-metric,
b
-volume form,
b
-Levi-Civita connections, and weighted Sobolev spaces and Hölder spaces on
\mathbb{E}
as in §
4.5
. We consider the corresponding linearised LMCF operator on
\mathbb{E}
given by

\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L},

(5.1)

for

\mathcal{L}=\rho^{2}\Delta_{\sqrt{2t}E}={{}^{b}\Delta}_{\mathrm{rel}}-\langle\rho^{-1}{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}\rho},{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}\cdot}\rangle.

Then, we can consider for integers
k,r\geq 1
, and a weight
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the linear mapping on
\mathbb{E}
between
\rho
-weighted Sobolev spaces as defined in §

(5.2)

By the same method as earlier, we have an a-priori estimate on

for the above mapping,

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}\leq C_{k,r,\lambda}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,\lambda}}+\|u|_{\textrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-1,\lambda}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\lambda}}\right).

The main result we will prove is the following:

Theorem 5.2
.

1.

The mapping

is semi-Fredholm and injective for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{E}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{E}\subset\mathbb{R}
is a certain discrete set of rates (Corollary
5.43
).

2.

The set
\mathcal{D}_{E}=\Re(\mathcal{C}_{E})
satisfies
\mathcal{D}_{E}\subset(-\infty,2-m]
.

Here, the sets
\mathcal{D}_{E},\mathcal{C}_{E}
are defined in §
5.2
. As a corollary to the above, we obtain:

Corollary 5.3
.

The mapping
T^{p}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
is semi-Fredholm and injective for
\lambda>2-m
.

In their paper
[
LN13
]
, Lotay–Neves study the deformation equation for Lagrangian expanders, and prove isomorphism theorems about the operator on certain natural Sobolev spaces. While they studied an (degenerate) elliptic operator, our work in this section is in a sense an extension and generalization of their results to the parabolic case. We describe their relevant results in §
5.4
. In order to prove Theorem
5.2
, it will be useful to first work with
k=0,r=1
, and then improve it to
k,r\geq 1
by the following result:

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose that the mapping
T^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}
is semi-Fredholm and injective. Then, the mapping
T^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
is also semi-Fredholm and injective, for
k,r\geq 1
.

Proof.

First, we have the global a-priori estimate on
\mathbb{E}
, as

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C_{k,r}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}}+\|u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-1}}\right).

Then, knowing that
T^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}
is semi-Fredholm and injective gives us the estimate

\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\left(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0}}+\|u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\right),

combining the above two estimates, we are done.
∎

By this lemma, it is enough to work with
k=0,r=1
, which we assume henceforth.

5.1
Fourier analysis

To prove our result, we will invoke Fourier analysis in order to reduce the problem to analysing the isomorphism properties of a parametric operator on the front face. Note that we work with complex-valued
L^{2}
-spaces throughout. We recall the definition we will be using here.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
M
be a smooth, compact manifold, and let
f:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function in
L^{2}(M\times\mathbb{R})
. The
Fourier transform
of

is the function
\hat{f}\in L^{2}(M\times\mathbb{R})
, defined as:

\hat{f}(p,\xi):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}f(p,x)dx.

A basic property of the Fourier transform is:

\mathcal{F}(\partial_{x}f)(p,\xi)=i\xi\hat{f}(p,\xi).

One of the key results about the Fourier transform is that:

Theorem 5.6

(Plancherel’s theorem)
.

The Fourier transform is an isometry on
L^{2}(M\times\mathbb{R})
up to a constant.

5.2

\mathbb{E}

Note that in

restricts to the expander

at

-P_{0}=\Phi^{*}((r\partial_{r})^{\top}-\Delta_{E}),\;w=\Phi^{*}(\partial_{r}),\;h_{1}=\Phi^{*}(|(r\partial_{r})^{\top}|^{2}_{E}),

for

Proof.

Let us begin by writing down the time-like vector field in the embedding

5.2.3
Fourier transform of the weighted operator

Since the mapping (

T^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}=(\rho^{-\lambda}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\rho^{\lambda},|_{\textrm{bf}}):L^{2}_{2,1}(\mathbb{E})\rightarrow L^{2}_{0,0}(\mathbb{E})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{E}|_{\textrm{bf}}),

for the function

obtained from restriction. First, we note that

Lemma 5.19
.

The operator
-P_{z}
defines a well-defined continuous map,

(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}):\check{L}^{2}_{2,1}({\overline{E}})\rightarrow\check{L}^{2}_{0,0}({\overline{E}})\oplus L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}}).

(5.7)

Remark 5.20
.

Note that since the functions are complex-valued, we are considering the complex norms of the functions in our spaces.

We define the sets

as follows:

The

Now, we state the main result we will prove about the parametric operator:

(a)

(b)

(c)

(Proposition

5.3
Elliptic estimates for the operator on
{\overline{E}}

We first note a-priori estimates for the mapping (
5.7
), obtained by restricting the estimate (
5.3
) to
{\overline{E}}
:

Lemma 5.24
.

For each
z\in\mathbb{C}
and

, we have the estimate,

\|u\|_{\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C_{z}(\|-P_{z}(u)\|_{\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1}}+\|u\|_{\check{L}^{2}}+\|u|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-1}})

(5.8)

Proof.

Given a

on
\mathbb{E}
, for

being a smooth, compactly supported bump function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
supported on
[1/2,1]
. Then, we write the a-priori estimate on
\mathbb{E}
for

to deduce the a-priori estimate on
{\overline{E}}
.
∎

Now, we note the following analogue of Rellich-Kondrachov:

Lemma 5.25
.

The inclusion
\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r,r}\hookrightarrow\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r-1,r-1}

Proof.

For a function

for

∎

As a corollary to the above, using the fact that

is a

-vector field on

we obtain:

Corollary 5.26
.

The mapping
u\mapsto w(u)
from
\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r,r}\rightarrow\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1}
is compact.

We also obtain that:

Corollary 5.27
.

The mapping (
5.7
) has closed image.

Proof.

This follows from the a-priori estimate (
5.8
), along with the compactness of the inclusion
\check{L}^{2}_{2,1}({\overline{E}})\hookrightarrow\check{L}^{2}({\overline{E}})
.
∎

Now, note the following maximum principle, which follows from considering the point at which
|u|^{2}
attains its maximum.

Lemma 5.28
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded, smooth domain with smooth boundary. Consider a weakly elliptic partial differential operator

on

, given by

P(u):=a^{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}u+b^{i}\partial_{i}u+cu,

such that

1.

the coefficients are smooth and bounded, with

being real-valued functions, while

being a complex-valued function satisfying

2.

The matrix

is positive definite at each interior point of the domain.

Suppose that a complex-valued

. Then,

attains its maximum on the boundary of
\Omega
.

Now, we have a regularity result for the kernel of (

):

Lemma 5.29
.

Suppose that

. Then, we have that
u\in C^{\infty}({\overline{E}})
(note that here, we have the ordinary smooth structure on

near the boundary).

By the interior estimate and

\|u\|_{\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C_{k,r}(\|u\|_{L^{2}}),

for

Now, we use the Sobolev embedding,
\check{L}^{2}_{k+2r,r}({\overline{E}})\hookrightarrow C^{r-1,\alpha}({\overline{E}})

As a corollary to this, we obtain:

.

Suppose that

This follows from writing

∎

5.4
The work of Lotay–Neves

In the coordinates
(\tau,w^{i})
near the front face, the operator can be written as

\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}=\tilde{w}^{2}(\tau\partial_{\tau}-(w\partial_{w})^{\top}-\Delta_{E}),

where
\tilde{w}:=(\frac{1}{2}+|w|^{2})^{1/2}
, and
w\partial_{w}
denotes the vector field
\sum w^{i}\partial_{w^{i}}
.
Now, a deformation of the expander corresponds in these coordinates to a solution for the above equation of the form
\tau^{2}u(w^{i})
. Plugging this in to the equation, we get the operator

\tilde{w}^{2}(2-(w\partial_{w})^{\top}-\Delta_{E})u.

Therefore, to study the deformation theory of the expander, we can study the operator on
E
given by

\mathcal{K}:=2-(r\partial_{r})^{\top}-\Delta_{E}.

(5.9)

5.4.1
Fredholm theory for the operator (
5.9
)

Now, we need to construct Sobolev spaces on the expander
E
such that the map defined by (
5.9
) is an isomorphism. Such Sobolev spaces were constructed by Lotay–Neves, and they showed that (
5.9
) is an isomorphism.

They define the Sobolev spaces
H^{k}_{*}(E)
and
H^{k}(E)
, where
H^{k}
is the usual Hilbert space of functions having
k
weak derivatives in
L^{2}
and
H^{k}_{*}
is defined by the norm:

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{k}_{*}(E)}:=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\|\nabla^{i}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(E)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\|\langle\nabla^{i}u,x^{\top}\rangle\|^{2}_{L^{2}(E)}.

Then,
\mathcal{K}
is a well-defined continuous mapping between the Sobolev spaces,

\mathcal{K}:H^{k+2}_{*}(E)\rightarrow H^{k}(E).

(5.10)

Then, Lotay–Neves prove the following (they prove this for a Lagrangian expander asymptotic to the pair of planes
\Pi_{\phi}\sqcup\Pi_{0}
, but the same argument works for a general asymptotic cone which is a union of special Lagrangians):

Theorem 5.31
.

[c.f. Theorem 4.3 of
[
LN13
]
]
The mapping
\mathcal{K}
between the Sobolev spaces (
5.10
) is an isomorphism.

Now, we can write the norm
H^{k}(E)
on the compactification
{\overline{E}}
as:

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{k}(E)}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\int_{E}|\nabla^{j}_{E}u|^{2}dV_{E}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\int_{{\overline{E}}}|^{b}\nabla^{j}_{{\overline{E}}}u|^{2}s^{-m/2-2}dV_{\overline{E}},

and similarly,

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{k}_{*}(E)}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\int_{\overline{E}}|^{b}\nabla^{j}_{{\overline{E}}}u|^{2}s^{-m/2-2}dV_{\overline{E}}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}\int_{{\overline{E}}}|\partial_{s}(^{b}\nabla^{j}_{{\overline{E}}}u)|^{2}s^{-m/2-2}dV_{\overline{E}}.

Therefore, the Sobolev spaces used in their analysis are the same as our Sobolev spaces
\check{L}^{2}_{k,1}
, up to a weight of
s^{-m/2-2}
. Due to the weight
s^{-m/2-2}
blowing up at the boundary of
{\overline{E}}
, we note that:

Lemma 5.32
.

If a function
u\in H^{k}_{*}(E)
, then the pull back of the function to
{\overline{E}}
has zero trace on the boundary
\partial{\overline{E}}
.

Now, we write the operator
\mathcal{K}
on
{\overline{E}}
. For a function
u
defined on
E
, we have,

\mathcal{K}(u)=\rho^{-2}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})(\rho^{2}su)=(-P_{2})(su).

Therefore,
\mathcal{K}(u)=f
translates to
-P_{2}(su)=f
on
{\overline{E}}
. So, from Theorem
5.31
we get:

Lemma 5.33
.

Let
f
be a smooth, compactly supported function on
{\overline{E}}
, supported away from
\partial{\overline{E}}
. Then, there exists a unique function
u\in L^{2}_{k,1}({\overline{E}})
such that
u|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}=0
and
(-P_{2})u=f
.

Proof.

We can pull back
f
to
E
to obtain an element in
H^{k}(E)
, since it has compact support. Now, we can find an element
u\in H^{k}_{*}(E)
, such that
\mathcal{K}(u)=f
. Now, since
u
vanishes very quickly infinity on
E
, we can pull it back to obtain a smooth function on
{\overline{E}}
. Then, we have that
-P_{2}(su)=f
, which implies the claim.
∎

As a result, we can show that:

Lemma 5.34
.

The mapping
(-P_{2},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
defined in (
5.7
) is an isomorphism.

Proof.

First, we note that the mapping has no kernel – this follows since if it has an element
u
in the kernel, then by Lemma
5.29
and Corollary
5.30
,
u
is a smooth function on
{\overline{E}}
vanishing to all orders at the boundary. Therefore, we can consider the function
us^{-1}
and push it forward to
E
to obtain an element in the kernel of
\mathcal{K}
, which is a contradiction.

Now, we claim that the mapping is surjective. To show this, by Corollary
5.27
it is enough to show surjectivity onto pairs of smooth functions
v\in C^{\infty}({\overline{E}})
,
g\in C^{\infty}(\partial{\overline{E}})
, since such pairs
(v,g)
are dense in the space
\check{L}^{2}({\overline{E}})\oplus L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}})
(note that
\check{L}^{2}({\overline{E}})
is the usual
L^{2}
-space on
{\overline{E}}
with the ordinary metric). Now, given such a pair, we can find a smooth function
u\in C^{\infty}({\overline{E}})
such that
u|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}=g
and
-P_{2}(u)-v
vanishes to all orders at the boundary of
{\overline{E}}
, by solving in a Taylor series expansion at the boundary of
{\overline{E}}
. Therefore, we have reduced to showing surjectivity onto pairs of functions
(v,0)
such that
v
vanishes to all orders at the boundary of
{\overline{E}}
. Since such functions can be approximated by compactly supported functions away from
\partial{\overline{E}}
, we are reduced to the case of Lemma
5.33
. Thus, the claim follows.
∎

Therefore, we have that

Proposition 5.35
.

The mapping
(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
has zero index for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Note that since the terms
zw+zh_{2}-z^{2}h_{1}
in the parametric operator
-P_{z}
are compact, it is sufficient to show that
(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
has zero index for some
z\in\mathbb{C}
. However, we know that
(-P_{2},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
is an isomorphism from Lemma
5.34
. Thus, the claim follows.
∎

5.5
No critical rates for
\Re(z)>2-m

In this section, we will prove part
(b)
of Theorem
5.22
. The method we use is the following: by Corollary
5.30
, we have that if the operator
(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
has a nontrivial element in the kernel, it must decay to all orders at the boundary. Therefore, we can consider our operator weighted by a power of the boundary-defining function
s^{(\lambda+i\xi)/2+\mu}
, for
\mu>0
being a positive rate. If
-P_{z}
has a nontrivial kernel, so would this weighted operator. Then, we can apply the maximum principle to the weighted operator to rule out a nontrivial kernel, proving that
(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
has a trivial kernel at the specific rate.

5.5.1
The operator in front face coordinates

The operator
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}
in front face coordinates is given by

\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}=\tilde{w}^{2}(\tau\partial_{\tau}-(w\partial_{w})^{\top}-\Delta_{E}),

(5.11)

where
\tilde{w}:=(\frac{1}{2}+|w|^{2})^{1/2}
, and
w\partial_{w}
denotes the vector field
\sum w^{i}\partial_{w^{i}}
. Now, due to
E
being an expander, we have

\Delta_{E}r^{2}=2m+2|r^{\perp}|^{2},\>\>|\nabla_{E}r^{2}|^{2}=4r^{2}-4|r^{\perp}|^{2}.

5.5.2
Weighting by
s

First, note that we define the boundary-defining function
s
for
\partial{\overline{E}}
as

s:=2\sigma_{2m+1}^{2}.

Then, we have that
\tau=rs^{1/2}
. Now, consider the operator

P^{\prime}_{z}(u):=s^{-(\lambda+i\xi)/2}P_{z}(s^{(\lambda+i\xi)/2}u).

Due to
s
being independent of
r
, we can equivalently write this as a restriction,

P^{\prime}_{z}=s^{-(\lambda+i\xi)/2}(r^{-\lambda+i\xi}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})r^{\lambda+i\xi})(s^{(\lambda+i\xi)/2})|_{{\overline{E}}}.

Now, this becomes

P^{\prime}_{z}=\tau^{-(\lambda+i\xi)}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tau^{(\lambda+i\xi)}|_{{\overline{E}}}.

Since we know that the operator is given as (
5.11
), we can compute in front face coordinates to get that

\tau^{-(\lambda+i\xi)}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tau^{(\lambda+i\xi)}=\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}+(\lambda+i\xi)s^{-1}.

Therefore, since we know that the restriction of the operator
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}
is
-P_{0}
, we get

P^{\prime}_{z}=-P_{0}+(\lambda+i\xi)s^{-1}.

By the maximum principle, this has no nontrivial zeroes for
\lambda>0
. Therefore, we have:

Proposition 5.36
.

The parametric operator
(-P_{z},|_{\overline{E}})
has trivial kernel for
\Re(z)>0
.

Remark 5.37
.

We didn’t use any geometric facts about the expander here. The same argument would work for any other asymptotically conical Lagrangian.

Now, we further weight the operator by
s_{C}^{\mu}
, for some
\mu>0
, where
s_{C}=(C+|w|^{2})^{-1}
, and show that there are no nontrivial zeroes for
\lambda>2-m
. Similarly as before, we can define the operator

P^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}(u):=s_{C}^{-\mu}P^{\prime}_{z}(s_{C}^{\mu}u).

Then, we can again write this as a restriction,

P^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}=s_{C}^{-\mu}\tau^{-(\lambda+i\xi)}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tau^{(\lambda+i\xi)}s_{C}^{\mu}|_{{\overline{E}}},

which on writing in
(\tau,w^{i})
-coordinates, with
\tilde{w}_{C}=(C+|w|^{2})^{1/2}
, gives us

\begin{split}&s_{C}^{-\mu}\tau^{-(\lambda+i\xi)}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tau^{(\lambda+i\xi)}s_{C}^{\mu}\\
&=\tilde{w}^{2}(\tau\partial_{\tau}-(w\partial_{w})^{\top}-\Delta_{E})\\
&+\tilde{w}^{2}\left(\lambda+i\xi+2\mu(\tilde{w}_{C}^{-1}(w\partial_{w})^{\top}\tilde{w}_{C})-2\mu(2\mu+1)|\tilde{w}_{C}^{-1}\nabla_{E}\tilde{w}|^{2}+2\mu\tilde{w}_{C}^{-1}\Delta_{E}\tilde{w}_{C}\right).\end{split}

(5.12)

Let us denote the function

N:=\tilde{w}^{2}\left(\lambda+i\xi+2\mu(\tilde{w}_{C}^{-1}(w\partial_{w})^{\top}\tilde{w}_{C})-2\mu(2\mu+1)|\tilde{w}_{C}^{-1}\nabla_{E}\tilde{w}|^{2}+2\mu\tilde{w}_{C}^{-1}\Delta_{E}\tilde{w}_{C}\right).

Then, we have that

P^{\prime\prime}_{\mu}=s_{C}^{-\mu}\tau^{-(\lambda+i\xi)}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tau^{(\lambda+i\xi)}s_{C}^{\mu}|_{{\overline{E}}}=-P_{0}+N.

Therefore, by the maximum principle,
\Re(N)>0
on
{\overline{E}}
implies that the kernel is trivial. Now, we compute the function
N
. We have,

N=\tilde{w}^{2}(\lambda+i\xi)+2\mu\tilde{w}^{2}\tilde{w}_{C}^{-2}|w|^{2}+(2\mu^{2}+4\mu)\tilde{w}^{2}\tilde{w}_{C}^{-4}|w^{\perp}|^{2}+2\mu(m-2-\mu)\tilde{w}^{2}\tilde{w}_{C}^{-2}.

Now, let us pick
\mu=\frac{m-2}{2}
. Then, we get

\Re(N)=\tilde{w}^{2}\left(\frac{(\lambda+m-2)|w|^{2}}{|w|^{2}+C}+\frac{C\lambda}{|w|^{2}+C}+\frac{(m-2)^{2}}{2(|w|^{2}+C)}\right)+\frac{m^{2}-4}{2}\tilde{w}_{C}^{-4}\tilde{w}^{2}|w^{\perp}|^{2}.

Then, we get that
\Re(N)
is uniformly bounded away from
0
for
\lambda>2-m
, by choosing
C
sufficiently small. Thus, we have shown:

Proposition 5.38
.

The parametric operator
(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
has trivial kernel for
\Re(z)>2-m
.

5.6
Discreteness of critical rates

In this section, we will prove part
(c)
of Theorem
5.22
. The proof is very similar to the proof that an elliptic operator on a compact manifold has discrete spectrum. Recall that the parametric operator is given by

-P_{z}=-P_{0}+z\otimes w+zh_{2}-z^{2}h_{1},

for
h_{1},h_{2}
being smooth function on
{\overline{E}}
, and
w
a
b
-vector field on
{\overline{E}}
.

Suppose to the contrary that we have a collection of critical rates
z_{j}\rightarrow z
, so that we have elements
u_{j}
such that
(-P_{z_{j}},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})u_{j}=(0,0)
. We choose a

which is not a critical rate (thus
(-P_{z_{0}},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
is an isomorphism) and such that

; in particular, by the previous sections, we can take any
z_{0}
with

. Since the set of non-critical rates is open (by continuity of the inverse norm when it exists), there are no critical rates in a neighbourhood of

. Let us denote
Z:=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}z_{i}
. If
Z
is finite, then we already have discreteness since the kernel at each critical rate is finite-dimensional by Proposition
5.35
. Therefore, we can assume
Z
is infinite. Let us define the vector subspaces formed by the

as
V_{k}:=\textrm{span}(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
, and their union
V=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}V_{i}
. First, we show that:

Lemma 5.39
.

The vector space
V
is infinite-dimensional.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary, that
V
is spanned by
k
linearly independent solutions
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}
. The main claim here is that there exists
j\geq 1
such that the
k
functions

\{(w+h_{2})u_{i}+(z_{j}+z_{i})h_{1}u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}

are linearly independent. Indeed, this follows by writing out projections of these functions to
V
in terms of the basis
\{u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\}
, and noting that the determinant of these is given by evaluating a polynomial
P(z_{j})
, where
P(z)=\Pi_{i=1}^{k}(z+z_{i})h_{1}^{k}+\{\textrm{lower order terms}\}
with coefficients being functions. Due to the coefficient of
z^{k}
being a non-zero function, this has only finitely many zeroes – combined with
Z
being infinite, this implies the existence of
z_{j}
such that the determinant is nonzero, hence the functions are linearly independent.

Then, choosing such a
z_{j}
, we note that since
P_{z_{i}}(u_{i})=0
for
i=1,\dots,k
, we have for
j>k

P_{z_{j}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}u_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}((z_{j}-z_{i})(w+h_{2})(u_{i})+(z_{j}^{2}-z_{i}^{2})h_{1}u_{i}),

which cannot be zero by linear independence. This is a contradiction. ∎

Now, we extract an infinite subspace of
V
inductively in the following way: at the first step, we choose
V^{\prime}_{1}:=(u_{1})
. At the
k^{\textrm{th}}
-step, having defined
V^{\prime}_{k}
, we choose the smallest
l>k
such that
u_{l}\not\in\langle u_{1},\dots,u_{k},(w+h_{2})u_{1},\dots,(w+h_{2})u_{k}\rangle
. Then, we define
V^{\prime}_{k+1}:=(u_{l})+V^{\prime}_{k}
.

Then, we choose an orthogonal set of functions
w_{i}\in\check{L}^{2}_{0,0}({\overline{E}})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(\partial{\overline{E}})
, such that
\langle w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\rangle=V^{\prime}_{k}
,
w_{k}\perp V^{\prime}_{k-1}
, and normalised to
\|w_{k}\|_{\check{L}^{2}_{0,0}\oplus L^{2}_{2}}=1
. In particular, we can write
w_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}u_{i}
for some
\{a_{i}\}
. Now, denoting
R=(-P_{z_{0}},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
, we can write,

\begin{split}(-P_{z_{k}},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})&=R+(z_{k}-z_{0})(w+h_{2})+(z_{k}^{2}-z_{0}^{2})h_{1}\\
&=(z^{2}_{k}-z_{0}^{2})((z_{k}^{2}-z_{0}^{2})^{-1}+\frac{(z_{k}-z_{0})}{(z_{k}^{2}-z_{0}^{2})}(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}+h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1})\circ R.\end{split}

(5.13)

Then,
(-P_{z_{k}},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})(u_{k})=0
implies that

((z_{k}^{2}-z_{0}^{2})^{-1}+\frac{1}{(z_{k}+z_{0})}(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}+h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1})u_{k}=0.

(5.14)

Now, we note that both the mappings
h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1}
and
(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}
from
\check{L}^{2}_{0,0}({\overline{E}})\oplus L^{2}_{2}(\partial{\overline{E}})
to itself are compact by Lemma
5.25
and Corollary
5.26
, and
\frac{1}{(z_{k}+z_{0})}
is convergent to a finite value since
z_{0}\neq-z
. Hence we can find a subsequence of
w_{k}
such that the sequence

\left(\frac{1}{(z_{k}+z_{0})}(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}+h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1}\right)w_{k}

is convergent. From equation (
5.14
) and writing
w_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}u_{i}
, we get

\left(\frac{1}{(z_{k}+z_{0})}(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}+h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1}\right)w_{k}=-(z_{k}^{2}-z_{0}^{2})^{-1}w_{k}+w_{k}^{\prime},

for a function
w_{k}^{\prime}\in\langle u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1},(w+h_{2})u_{1},\dots,(w+h_{2})u_{k-1}\rangle
. Therefore for
l>k
, we have

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{1}{(z_{l}+z_{0})}(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}+h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1}\right)w_{l}-\left(\frac{1}{(z_{k}+z_{0})}(w+h_{2})\circ R^{-1}+h_{1}\textrm{Id}\circ R^{-1}\right)w_{k}\\
&=-(z_{l}^{2}-z_{0}^{2})^{-1}w_{l}+w^{\prime\prime}_{l},\end{split}

(5.15)

for a function
w^{\prime\prime}_{l}\in\langle u_{1},\dots,u_{l-1},(w+h_{2})u_{1},\dots,(w+h_{2})u_{l-1}\rangle
.
Since
w_{k}
is orthogonal to
w^{\prime\prime}_{k}
by construction, we have that the norm of the above difference is
\geq\frac{1}{2}|z^{2}-z_{0}^{2}|\neq 0
for
l
sufficiently large, contradicting convergence. Therefore, we obtain:

Proposition 5.40
.

The set of critical rates
\mathcal{C}_{E}
is a discrete subset of
\mathbb{C}
.

5.7
Proof of Theorem
5.2

In this section, we will prove part
(d)
of Theorem
5.22
and Theorem
5.2
. We first prove the following:

Proposition 5.41
.

The set of critical rates in any bounded real strip
\{z:a<\Re(z)<b\}
is finite.

Proof.

To show this, we will use the Fourier transform. Let us denote
\tilde{u}:=\rho^{-\lambda}u
. From the a-priori estimate on
\mathbb{E}
, we have the estimate

\|^{b}\nabla_{\rho\partial_{\rho}}\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{E})}\leq C_{\lambda}(\|\rho^{-\lambda}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\rho^{\lambda}\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{E})}+\|\tilde{u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{E})}+\|\tilde{u}|_{\textrm{bf}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(\partial\mathbb{E})}).

Taking the Fourier transform in the variable
\rho^{\prime}=\log(\rho)
, we get

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\xi\hat{u}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi\\
&\leq C_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|-P_{\lambda+i\xi}(\hat{u}(\xi))\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}})}d\xi\right),\end{split}

(5.16)

where
\hat{u}:=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{u})
. Note that we have used

\mathcal{F}(^{b}\nabla_{\rho\partial_{\rho}}\tilde{u})(\xi)=i\xi\hat{u}(\xi),\;\>\mathcal{F}(\rho^{-\lambda}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\rho^{\lambda}\tilde{u})(\xi)=-P_{\lambda+i\xi}(\hat{u}(\xi)).

Now, we claim that there are no critical rates for
|\xi|>2\sqrt{C_{\lambda}}
. Suppose to the contrary, we have a nontrivial element
u_{\xi_{0}}
in the kernel of
(-P_{\lambda+i\xi_{0}},|_{\textrm{bf}})
. Then, by multiplying with a cutoff function
\psi(\xi)
, we can extend
u_{\xi_{0}}
to a nonzero function
\tilde{u}_{\xi_{0}}:=\psi(\xi)\cdot u_{\xi_{0}}
on all of
\mathbb{E}
, such that we have an estimate

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|-P_{\lambda+i\xi}(\tilde{u}_{\xi_{0}}(\xi))\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\tilde{u}_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}})}d\xi<\varepsilon\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\tilde{u}_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi

with
\varepsilon
being arbitrarily small.
Then, since
\tilde{u}_{\xi_{0}}(\xi)
has rapidly deacaying derivatives of all orders, it is the Fourier transform of a
L^{2}_{2,1}(\mathbb{E})
-function. Therefore, by the estimate and
|\xi_{0}|>2\sqrt{C_{\lambda}}
, we arrive at a contradiction.

So, it follows that there are no critical rates for
|\xi|>2\sqrt{C_{\lambda}}
. Combining the fact that critical rates are discrete (Proposition
5.40
) and the continuity of the constant

, we obtain the proposition.
∎

Lemma 5.42
.

Suppose that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{E}
is not the real part of a critical rate. Then, there exists a constant
C^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
for which

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{E})}\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{\lambda}(\mathbb{E})}+\|u|_{\textrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{1,\lambda}(\partial\mathbb{E})}).

Proof.

We have from the estimate (
5.16
) that

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\xi\hat{u}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi\\
&\leq C_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|-P_{\lambda+i\xi}(\hat{u}(\xi))\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}})}d\xi\right).\end{split}

(5.17)

Since we assumed that
\lambda\not\in\mathcal{D}_{E}
, due to continuity of the norm, we have that there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{\lambda}
such that

\|\hat{u}(\xi)\|_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}\leq C^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\|-P_{\lambda+i\xi}(\hat{u}(\xi))\|_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}+\|\hat{u}(\xi)|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}\|_{L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}})})\textrm{ for }|\xi|^{2}\leq C_{\lambda}+1.

(5.18)

Therefore, we can use (
5.18
) to estimate the term
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi
in (
5.17
) and obtain a constant
C^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|-P_{\lambda+i\xi}(\hat{u}(\xi))\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\overline{E}})}d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|\hat{u}(\xi)|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{1}(\partial{\overline{E}})}d\xi\right).

Then, taking the inverse Fourier transform and invoking Plancherel’s theorem gives us the claimed estimate.
∎

As a corollary, we obtain:

The mapping

6
Isomorphism of linearised operator

Now, we will show that the mapping (

) between the following spaces:

(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L},|_{\textrm{bf}}):L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})\rightarrow L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,\lambda}(\mathbb{L})\oplus L^{2}_{k+2r-1}(\widetilde{L_{0}})

is an isomorphism for the rates

Remark 6.1

Note that for our problem, the relevant rate is

The argument is essentially to write the operator on

6.1
Semi-Fredholmness of the operator

First, we prove that the linearised operator is semi-Fredholm, i.e
.
it has finite dimensional kernel and closed range on the whole of
\mathbb{L}
, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathcal{D}_{E}
. First, cover
\mathbb{L}
using two open sets
U_{1}:=\mathbb{L}\cap\{0\leq\rho<\varepsilon\}
and
U_{2}:=\mathbb{L}\cap\{\rho>\varepsilon/2\}
. Let
\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\}
be a partition of unity with respect to this cover of
\mathbb{L}
. Then, we have the following::

Lemma 6.2
.

For a given rate
\lambda
, suppose we have that

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}\leq C(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}+\|u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{1,\lambda}}),

(6.2)

for
u
being supported in
U_{1}
. Then, the mapping (
6.1
) has finite-dimensional kernel and closed range.

In order to prove this, we need the following:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\psi
be a smooth function supported away from the front face of
\mathbb{L}
. Then, the mapping
\psi:L^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}\rightarrow L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}
, sending

is a compact mapping.

Proof.

This basically follows from classical Rellich-Kondrachov, since the Sobolev spaces we are dealing with are non-singular as
\rho
is bounded below in the support of
\psi
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
T:X\rightarrow Y
be a bounded, linear, map and
K:X\rightarrow Z
be a bounded, linear, compact map. Suppose we have the estimate

\|u\|_{X}\leq C(\|Tu\|_{Y}+\|Ku\|_{Z})

for a constant
C>0
. Then, we have that
T
has finite dimensional kernel and closed image.

Proof.

Finte dimensional kernel:
Suppose not, then there is an infinite sequence of linearly independent elements in the kernel, say
\{r_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
, such that by scaling we have
\|r_{i}\|=1
and
\|r_{i}-r_{j}\|_{X}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Then, we have that for
i\neq j
,

\frac{1}{2}\leq\|r_{i}-r_{j}\|_{X}\leq C(\|K(r_{i}-r_{j})\|_{Z})\implies\|K(r_{i}-r_{j})\|_{Z}\geq\frac{1}{2C}>0.

However, due to compactness of
K
, we have that there is a convergent subsequence, say
\{r_{i_{j}}\}_{j\geq 1}
, such that
Kr_{i_{j}}\rightarrow s\in Z
as
j\rightarrow\infty
. This implies that
K(r_{i_{j}})
is a Cauchy sequence in
Y
, which contradicts the above inequality.

Closed range:
Since we have a finite dimensional kernel, we can consider the quotient mapping

T:X/\textrm{ker}(T)\rightarrow Y,

which is injective. Now, we recall that for a bounded, linear, injective map, showing closed image is equivalent to showing an inequality of the form
\|u\|_{X}\leq C\|Tu\|_{Y}
for a constant
C>0
. Now, suppose this does not hold. Then, we can find a sequence of linearly independent elements
\{s_{i}\}\subset X/\textrm{ker}(T)
such that
\|s_{i}\|_{X/\textrm{ker}(T)}=1
,
\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|_{X/\textrm{ker}(T)}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\|Ts_{i}\|_{Y}\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, we have from our estimate that

\frac{1}{2}\leq\|s_{i}-s_{j}\|_{X/\textrm{ker}(T)}\leq C(\|T(s_{i}-s_{j})\|_{Y}+\|K(s_{i}-s_{j})\|_{Z})\implies\|K(s_{i}-s_{j})\|_{Z}\geq\frac{1}{2C}-\delta>0,

for
i\neq j
and
i,j\gg 0
so that
\|T(s_{i})-T(s_{j})\|<\delta
. Now, due to compactness of
K
, we can again find a convergent subsequence in
\{K(s_{i})\}
, which implies it must be Cauchy in
Z
, which is a contradiction to the above inequality.
∎

Now, we can prove Lemma
6.2
:

Proof.

Let
(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
be a partition of unity corresponding to the cover
(U_{1},U_{2})
of
\mathbb{L}
. The main idea is to consider the estimates for
\phi_{1}u
and
\phi_{2}u
, and add them up, such that we can get a global estimate of

by the norms of
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}

u|_{\textrm{bf}}
, and the norms of compact error terms. Then, we would get closed image and finite kernel by the Lemma above. Now, for

\begin{split}\|\phi_{1}u\|_{L^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}}&\leq C(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\phi_{1}u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{1}u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{1,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{1}u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}})\\
&\leq C^{\prime}(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\phi_{1}u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{1}u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{1,\lambda}})\\
&\leq C^{\prime}(\|\phi_{1}(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{1}u|_{\textrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{1,\lambda}}+A\|{{}^{b}}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}\phi_{1}\cdot{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}}u\|_{L^{2}_{{0,0,\lambda}}}\\
&\>\>\>\>\>\>+B\|{{}^{b}\Delta_{\mathrm{rel}}}\phi_{1}u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}),\\
\end{split}

(6.3)

for bounded constants
A,B
. Similarly, we have from the a-priori estimate,

\begin{split}\|\phi_{2}u\|_{L^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}}&\leq C(\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\phi_{2}u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{2}u|_{\mathrm{bf}}\|_{L^{2}_{1,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{2}u\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}})\\

\end{split}

(6.4)

Therefore, we have by adding the above two estimates,

\begin{split}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}}&\leq\|\phi_{1}u\|_{L^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}}+\|\phi_{2}u\|_{L^{2}_{2,1,\lambda}}\\

Now, since each of

. The case

Note that there is an a-diffeomorphism

Therefore, it remains to prove the estimate (

Let

The mapping (

6.2
Proof of surjectivity and isomorphism

To prove surjectivity of the mapping (
6.1
), we show surjectivity onto
C^{\infty}
functions supported away from the front face, which we denote as before by
C^{\infty}_{\textrm{ff}}
. Since such functions are dense in
L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,\lambda}\oplus L^{2}_{k+2r-1,\lambda}
, we would be done since the image is closed.

Let
f,g
be
C^{\infty}_{\textrm{ff}}
functions on
\mathbb{L}
and
\{t=0\}
respectively. We will construct a function in
L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,\lambda}
that maps to
(f,g)
under (
6.1
). First, we construct a
C^{\infty}_{\textrm{ff}}
function
\tilde{u}
, supported away from the front face, such that

(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tilde{u}=\tilde{f},\;\;\tilde{u}|_{t=0}=g,

such that
f-\tilde{f}\in\mathring{L}^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
, i.e. we can find a sequence of smooth functions
h_{i}
supported away from
t=0
such that
h_{i}\rightarrow f-\tilde{f}
in
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
. To construct
\tilde{u}
, we will solve the operator for the function
\tilde{u}
using a Taylor expansion at
t=0
.

Suppose we have that
f(x,t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}q_{k}(x)t^{k}
, for
q_{k}(x)
being functions essentially on
L\backslash B_{\varepsilon}(x)
for
\varepsilon
small. Then, if
\tilde{u}=\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k}(x)t^{k}
, we have that

(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})\tilde{u}=\sum_{k\geq 0}(\rho^{2}(k+1)s_{k+1}-\mathcal{L}s_{k})t^{k}.

Now, note that
\mathcal{L}
is not constant in time. Therefore, we need to further expand
\mathcal{L}(s_{k})
as a Taylor series. Doing so, we can recursively calculate the functions
s_{k}
so that the Taylor series of the output matches the Taylor series of
f
, with
s_{0}=g
. Further, the functions will also be uniformly supported away from the front face. Then by Borel’s Lemma, we can get a
C^{\infty}_{\textrm{ff}}
function
\tilde{f}
satisfying the condition above.

Now, having constructed
\tilde{f}
, our problem reduces to showing surjectivity onto
C^{\infty}_{\textrm{ff}}
functions vanishing to sufficiently high orders at
t=0
, and boundary condition being the zero function. However, such functions can be approximated by smooth functions that are supported in
t\geq\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon>0
. Clearly, we can solve for these functions as they are the standard Cauchy problem (i.e. without any degeneracy). Therefore, since the image is closed, we can surject onto functions vanishing to all orders, and hence onto all functions in the output space as claimed.

Therefore, we have shown the following:

Theorem 6.7
.

The linearised operator for LMCF (
6.1
), considered as a map between the weighted Sobolev spaces
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
and
L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,\lambda}\oplus L^{2}_{k+2r,\lambda}(\overline{L})
, is an isomorphism for
\lambda>0
, and
k\geq 0,r\geq 1
.

Proof.

We already know that the mapping is surjective and has finite-dimensional kernel. The mapping is injective for
\lambda>0
by an application of the maximum principle for
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L}=\rho^{2}(\partial_{v}-\Delta_{L_{t}})
. In particular, if an element
u
lies in the kernel at a rate
\lambda>0
, it implies that
u\in C^{k+2r,r,\lambda}
for
k,r\gg 1
by the a-priori estimate and Sobolev embedding. Therefore, it follows that
\rho^{-\lambda}u
is a bounded, smooth function on
\mathbb{L}
, so
u
is a bounded, smooth function on
\mathbb{L}
decaying to order
\rho^{-\lambda}
at the front face, and
u|_{s=0}\equiv 0
. Now, the maximum principle applied to
\partial_{v}-\Delta_{L_{t}}
over a time interval
(\nu,\varepsilon)
tells us that the supremum of
u
is bounded by
\sup(u|_{t=\nu})
. Letting
\nu\downarrow 0
, we know that
\sup(u|_{t=\nu})\downarrow 0
given the decay of
u
at the front face and
u|_{s=0}\equiv 0
. Therefore, we obtain that
u
is identically zero on
\mathbb{L}
.
∎

7
Solving the nonlinear problem

In this section, we solve the nonlinear LMCF equation (
4.7
) on potentials, and prove that the resulting solution corresponds to an a-smooth Lagrangian submanifold in
\mathbb{X}
, satisfying the conditions of Theorem
7.15
as claimed.

7.1
The zeroth order error term

Note that the zeroth order term in the LMCF equation is given by
\rho^{2}E
, where
E
satisfies
d_{\mathrm{rel}}(E)=d_{\mathrm{rel}}\theta_{0}-\hat{\omega}(v-\Psi^{*}(\partial_{t}),\_)
. Firstly, we note that:

Lemma 7.1
.

We have that
d_{\mathrm{rel}}(E)=0
at the boundaries of
\mathbb{L}
.

Proof.

This follows due to the construction of approximate solution, and in particular the choice of gluing functions. The choice of functions
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
ensures that the approximate solution is the graph
\Gamma(td\theta_{L})
over
L
outside of a shrinking ball, and it is a perturbation of the expander near the singularity. Therefore,
d_{\mathrm{rel}}(E)
converges to zero on both the bottom face and front face of
\mathbb{L}
.
∎

Now, we note the following important lemma about the relative derivative on
\mathbb{L}
:

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\alpha
be an a-smooth, exact, relative
1
-form on
\mathbb{L}
such that
\alpha
vanishes at the boundaries of
\mathbb{L}
. Then, there exists an a-smooth function
f
such that
\alpha=d_{\mathrm{rel}}f
,
f
vanishes at the boundaries, and
f
is unique up to adding a time dependent constant.

Proof.

The uniqueness statement about
f
is clear, since any other function
f^{\prime}
will differ from
f
by a time-dependent constant, and since it is smooth at the ordinary boundaries of
\mathbb{L}
, it is smooth in time. Therefore, it remains to prove existence.

To prove existence, we consider the local models for
\mathbb{L}
and follow an inductive procedure, constructing the function
f
on each piece and then patching them up by adding time-dependent constants. Note that such an inductive construction will give a globally well-defined function due to
\alpha
being exact. Therefore, it is enough to construct such
f
on local models, and in particular, for models near the front face.

Let us consider a local model
U_{i}
near the front face. First, since
\alpha
is a-smooth and vanishes near the front face, we have that
|\alpha|=O(\rho^{\delta})
for some
\delta>0
. We construct the function
f
in the following manner: consider a smooth choice of points
\{x_{t}\}_{t\in[0,\varepsilon)}
on the time slices of
U_{i}
, converging to a point on the boundary. Then, we define
f_{t}
on each slice to be the unique solution of
d_{\mathrm{rel}}f_{t}=\alpha,f_{t}(x_{t})=0
. Clearly, this gives us a function
f:U_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is smooth in the interior and at the boundary of
U_{i}
, and such that
f|_{\partial U_{i}}\equiv 0
. Then, it remains to show that
f
extends a-smoothly at the front faces. For each point
x_{t}\in U_{i}
on a time-slice, we can construct a curve
\gamma_{t}(\rho)
lying in the time slice, parametrised by the radial variable
\rho
, starting at
\rho=R
and ending at
\rho=r
, such that
{}^{b}|\rho{\partial_{\rho}}\gamma_{t}|<C
, for a uniform constant
C
independent of the point
x_{t}
. Then, we have
f(\gamma_{t}(r))=\int_{R}^{r}d_{\mathrm{rel}}f(\partial_{r}(\gamma_{t}))dr
. Now, note that by choice of the curve,
|d_{\mathrm{rel}}f(\partial_{r}(\gamma_{t}))|=|\alpha(\partial_{r}\gamma_{t})|\leq r^{\delta}\cdot{{}^{b}|}\partial_{r}(\gamma_{t})|\leq Cr^{\delta-1}
. Therefore,
f(x_{t})\leq C(r^{\delta}-R^{\delta})+\sup_{x\in U^{c}}f(x)
. Hence,
f
is a bounded function on
U
. Now, we note that
\partial_{v}(d_{\mathrm{rel}}f)=d_{\mathrm{rel}}(\partial_{v}f)
due to the time-like vector field
v
being orthogonal to the time slices. Therefore, we get by the same method as before that
\rho^{2}\partial_{v}f=O(r^{\alpha})
. Continuing similarly with higher order derivatives, we get that
f
is a-smooth on
U_{i}
. This proves the claim.
∎

Therefore, it follows that we can take the function
E
to be an a-smooth function that vanishes at the boundaries. Now, by
a
-smoothness, this implies that
E
decays at rate
r^{\alpha}
near the front face for some
\alpha>0
, hence
E\in L^{p}
. Similarly, due to
E
being a-smooth, we get
E\in L^{2}_{k+2r,r}
for all
k,r\geq 1
. Hence, the error term
\rho^{2}E
lies in
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
for all
k,r\geq 1
.

7.2
Solving formally near the bottom face

Suppose we have constructed an approximate solution to LMCF as before, so that we can write the P.D.E. (
4.10
) at the level of potentials,

(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u=\rho^{2}E+\textrm{ higher-order nonlinear terms in }u,

where
E
is the error which vanishes at the boundaries. In this subsection, we will solve the equation formally on the bottom face. To do so, we consider an ansatz,

u=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s^{k}\rho^{2}u_{k},

for
u_{k}:\widetilde{L_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
being a-smooth functions. Now, as we have that the (unweighted by
\rho^{2}
) nonlinear operator is

P=E+\partial_{v}-\rho^{-2}\mathcal{L}+Q[\rho^{-2}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}u],

we can write

\begin{split}P[u]\sim&E+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)u_{k+1}s^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s^{k}{\mathcal{L}|_{L_{0}}}(u_{k})\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}^{\infty}s^{k}P_{ij}^{\prime\prime}[\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}u_{i},\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}u_{j}],\end{split}

(7.1)

as
s\downarrow 0
, where
P_{ij}^{\prime\prime}
is a partial differential operator with terms of degree
\geq 1
, and a-smooth coefficients. From this, it is clear that once we fix
u_{0}\equiv 0
,
u_{k+1}
is uniquely determined from
\{u_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq k}
. Also, we note that
u_{k+1}
is also a-smooth on
\widetilde{L_{0}}
in the radial variable
\rho
, since the partial differential operators are a-smooth. Therefore, we have formally solved for the functions
\{u_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
on
\widetilde{L_{0}}
. In order to construct an a-smooth function
u
on
\mathbb{L}
with these asymptotics, we invoke Borel’s lemma, whose simplest form says that given any sequence of smooth functions at the boundary of a collar neighbourhood
U\times[0,\varepsilon)
, there exists a smooth function on the neighbourhood such that its Taylor series expansion at the boundary has coefficients given by these functions. It is straightforward to extend this result to our case to obtain an a-smooth function
\tilde{u}:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with the asymptotic expansion at
s=0
as above. Therefore by considering our new approximate solution as
\Gamma(d_{\mathrm{rel}}\tilde{u})
over
\mathbb{L}
, we have:

Proposition 7.3
.

There exists an a-smooth embedding
\mathbb{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
with the given boundary conditions, such that the error term in the nonlinear P.D.E. (
4.10
) decays at rate
O(s^{\infty})
as
s\downarrow 0
.

7.3
Estimates on the nonlinear operator

We will prove an estimate on the nonlinear part of our P.D.E., similar to Pacini
[
Pac13
]
. First, we note that the derivatives of the nonlinear term
\rho^{2}Q(\rho^{-2}{d_{\mathrm{rel}}u},\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u})
can be written as an a-smooth function on a sum of vector bundles, with it consisting of nonlinear terms of degree at least
2
(since differentiation preserves degree of nonlinearity). Then, we choose
k,r
large enough so that there exists a Sobolev embedding
L^{2}_{k+2r,r}\hookrightarrow C^{k/2+r,r/2}
, and we have a continuous mapping

L^{2}_{k+2r,r}\times L^{2}_{k+2r,r}\rightarrow L^{2}_{k+2r,r},

i.e.
\|fg\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r}}\cdot\|g\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r}}
. Given this, we have the following:

Theorem 7.4
.

Let us consider the nonlinear term of our P.D.E. (
4.10
) given by
\rho^{-2}Q(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u})
, which we abbreviate to
Q[u]
. For
k,r
large enough such that the Sobolev embedding holds, and for

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}},\|v\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}\leq K\ll 1,

there is an estimate

\|Q[u]-Q[v]\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,\lambda}}\leq C_{K}\|u-v\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}}\cdot(\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}+\|v\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}),

(7.2)

for a constant
C_{K}
depending on
K
.

Proof.

First, we note that

\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}Q[u]=(\nabla\rho^{2}Q)(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u})\cdot(\rho^{-2}\partial_{x}u,\rho^{-2}\partial_{x}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}\partial_{x}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}u),

and similarly

\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}\nabla^{r}Q[u]=\sum(\nabla^{i+j}\rho^{2}Q)(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u})\cdot P_{ij}[u],

for
P_{ij}[u]
being a polynomial of degree
i+j
in the derivatives of
u
weighted by
\rho^{-2}
, with
P_{ij}[0]=0
, and
(\nabla^{i+j}\rho^{2}Q)(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u})
being an a-smooth function in
\rho^{-2}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u
weighted by
\rho^{2}
. Now, we can write pointwise, by using the parallel transport along fibres,

\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}\nabla^{r}Q[u]-\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}\nabla^{r}Q[v]=\sum((\nabla^{i+j}\rho^{2}Q)[u]-(\nabla^{i+j}\rho^{2}Q)[v])\cdot P_{ij}[u]+\sum(\nabla^{i+j}\rho^{2}Q)[v](P_{ij}[u]-P_{ij}[v]).

Now, we note that we have the pointwise estimates with weighted norms,

|Q[u]-Q[v]|\leq C|u-v|_{C^{2,0,2}}\cdot|u+v|_{C^{2,0,2}},\;|\nabla^{k}Q[u]-\nabla^{k}Q[v]|\leq C|u-v|_{C^{2,0,2}},

|Q[u]|\leq C|u|_{C^{2,0,2}}^{2},\;|\nabla Q[u]|\leq C|u|_{C^{2,0,2}},\;|\nabla^{k}Q[u]|\leq C.

Also, we have estimates on the polynomials,

|P_{ij}[u]-P_{ij}[v]|\leq C|u-v|_{C^{k+2r-i-2j,r-j,2}},

and for
i+j>1
, we have

|P_{ij}[u]-P_{ij}[v]|\leq C|u-v|_{C^{k+2r-i-2j,r-j,2}}\cdot(|u|_{C^{k+2r-i-2j,r-j,2}}+|v|_{C^{k+2r-i-2j,r-j,2}}).

Using these pointwise estimates and the continuity of multiplication, and noting down the weights of
\rho
, we have the claimed estimate.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

It is likely possible to prove the theorem with a weaker hypothesis, that we merely have a Sobolev embedding
L^{p}_{k+2r,r}\hookrightarrow C^{0,0,\alpha}
.

7.4
Fixed point argument

Consider the iteration map

I:u\mapsto(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})^{-1}(\rho^{2}Q[\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla}_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u]+\rho^{2}E,0|_{\textrm{bf}}).

(7.3)

For
u\in L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
such that
\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}
is sufficiently small and
k,r
are sufficiently large, we note that this is a well-defined mapping from
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
to itself. This is because both
\rho^{2}E
and
\rho^{2}Q[u]
lie in
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
, and the operator
(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})
is invertible. Clearly, a fixed point for this mapping is a solution to the nonlinear P.D.E. Now, we will show that such a fixed point exists, while reducing the time interval of existence.

7.4.1
Reducing the error

In order to find a fixed point for (
7.3
), we first reduce the error
E
by multiplying it with a cutoff function:

Definition 7.6
.

Define the
reduced error
to be the function
E_{\eta}:=\chi(\eta^{-1}t)E
, where
\chi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\chi=1
on
[0,1)
and
\chi=0
on
[2,\infty)
, and
\eta>0
is a small positive constant.

Note that we have
E_{\eta}=E
for
t\in[0,\eta)
. Therefore, if we find a solution to the nonlinear problem with error
E_{\eta}
, we obtain a solution to the original problem on restricting to the time interval
[0,\eta)
. First, we show that we can make the norm of the error arbitrarily small by choosing
\eta
small enough:

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
E
vanish to all orders at the bottom face. Then, given any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that
\|E_{\eta}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We essentially need to show that the derivatives of
E_{\eta}
have uniformly bounded pointwise norms over
\eta
; then, the claim follows since reducing
\eta
reduces the support of
E
. First, we note that due to
E
being smooth near the bottom face and
E
vanishing to all orders at the bottom face, all the
b
-derivatives of
E
also vanish to all orders at the bottom face. Now, we note the
b
-derivatives of the cutoff function
\chi(\eta^{-1}t)
, which consist of derivatives
\partial_{s}
and
\rho\partial_{\rho}
:

\partial_{s}(\chi(\eta^{-1}t))=\chi^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}t)\cdot\eta^{-1}\rho^{2}\leq Cs^{-1},

\rho\partial_{\rho}(\chi(\eta^{-1}t))=\chi^{\prime}(\eta^{-1}t)\cdot 2\eta^{-1}\rho^{2}s\leq C.

Similarly, taking higher derivatives, we would obtain that
|\partial_{s}^{k}{{}^{b}\nabla^{\alpha}_{\mathrm{rel}}\chi(\eta^{-1}t)}|\leq C_{k,\alpha}s^{-k}
, for a uniform constant
C_{k,\alpha}
independent of
\eta
. Therefore, due to
E
and all its derivatives vanishing to all orders in
s
, the derivatives of
E_{\eta}
are pointwise bounded by sums of functions of the form
C_{k,\alpha}s^{-k}{{}^{b}\nabla}^{r}E
. Now, we note that these functions decay to the front face at the same rate as
E
, and these are smooth functions at the bottom face. Therefore, the functions
C_{k,\alpha}s^{-k}{{}^{b}\nabla^{k}}E
are integrable on
\mathbb{L}
. Thus, since reducing
\eta
makes the support of
E_{\eta}
smaller, and
E_{\eta}
and its derivatives are bounded by
L^{2}
-integrable functions, the
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
-norm of
E_{\eta}
decreases to
0
as
\eta\downarrow 0
.
∎

Now, we show that (
7.3
) is a contraction mapping in a small ball:

Lemma 7.8
.

There exist sufficiently small positive constants
\eta,\mu>0
such that (
7.3
) with error term
E_{\eta}
is a well-defined contraction mapping
I:B_{\mu}\rightarrow B_{\mu}
, where
B_{\mu}
is the open ball of radius
\mu
in
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
.

Proof.

Let
C_{1}
be the norm of the inverse linear operator, and
C_{2}
be the norm for the nonlinear estimate (
7.2
). For the image to lie in the ball
B_{\mu}
, we must have that

C_{1}(C_{2}\mu^{2}+E_{\eta})<\mu\iff E_{\eta}<C_{1}^{-1}\mu-C_{2}\mu^{2},

(7.4)

while to be a contraction mapping we need

1>2C_{1}C_{2}\mu.

(7.5)

So, we can first choose
\mu
sufficiently small, so that (
7.5
) holds, and then make it smaller if necessary so that
C_{1}^{-1}\mu-C_{2}\mu^{2}>0
, and finally choose
\eta
sufficiently small so that (
7.4
) holds. Thus for example, we can choose
\mu=\frac{1}{2(1+C_{1})(1+C_{2})}
, and
\eta
small so that
E_{\eta}<\frac{1}{4(1+C_{1})^{2}(1+C_{2})}
. ∎

Therefore, we obtain a weak solution to the nonlinear P.D.E.:

Corollary 7.9
.

There exists a weak solution to the nonlinear problem (
4.10
) for a short time interval
t\in[0,\varepsilon)
, in the space
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
.

7.5
Regularity and
uniqueness of the solution

To show that the weak solution we obtained has derivatives of all orders, we invoke some parabolic regularity results from the book by Ladyzhenskaya, Solonnikov and Ural’ceva
[
LSU68
]
. First, we have Theorem 12.1 from Chapter 3 of
[
LSU68
]
:

Theorem 7.10
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
[a^{ij}],b^{i},c
be continuous on
\Omega_{T}
,
[a^{ij}]
being elliptic, and define the linear operator

Lu=\partial_{t}u-\partial_{i}(a^{ij}\partial_{j}u)-b^{i}\partial_{i}u-cu.

Suppose that
u
is a weak solution of
Lu=f
. If the coefficients of
L
and
f
lie in
C^{k,l,\alpha}((0,T)\times\Omega)
, then
u
lies in
C^{k+2,l+1,\alpha}(I\times\Omega^{\prime})
for
I\subset\subset(0,T)
and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
.

We will need a version of this theorem going up to the boundary
t=0
, which follows from the previous result and Theorem 5.1 from Chapter 4 of
[
LSU68
]
(c.f. the discussion following Theorem 12.1 of
[
LSU68
]
):

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
[a^{ij}],b^{i},c
be continuous on
\Omega_{T}
,
[a^{ij}]
being elliptic, and define the linear operator

Lu=\partial_{t}u-\partial_{i}(a^{ij}\partial_{j}u)-b^{i}\partial_{i}u-cu.

Suppose that
u
is a weak solution of
Lu=f
, such that
u\in C^{0}(\Omega_{T})
and
u|_{t=0}\equiv 0
. If the coefficients of
L
and
f
lie in
C^{k,l,\alpha}([0,T)\times\Omega)
, then
u
lies in
C^{k+2,l+1,\alpha}([0,t)\times\Omega^{\prime})
for
t<T
and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
.

To apply this theorem, we take our nonlinear P.D.E. and differentiate it to obtain linear P.D.E.s having continuous coefficients, such that they have weak solutions given by
\partial_{i}u
, and gain higher regularity by a bootstrapping argument.

7.5.1
Bootstrapping

By Sobolev embedding, the solution we have obtained in
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
lies in
C^{2+\delta,1+\delta/2}
by choosing
k,r\gg 1
. Now, we will show that we can bootstrap to obtain that our solution has derivatives of all orders. Let us first note the form of our equation. We can write it as

E+\partial_{v}u-\Delta_{L_{t}}u+Q(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}u})=0,

which we can take a derivative of by
{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}
, and multiply it by
\rho^{2}
, to get the equation

\rho^{2}\partial_{v}(^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}u)-\sum a_{ij}(^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u,{{}^{b}\nabla}_{\mathrm{rel}}u){{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel,}}}^{2}_{ij}(^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k}u)+b(^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k-1}u,\dots,{{}^{b}\nabla}_{\mathrm{rel}}u,u)=0,

(7.6)

for
a_{ij},b
being a-smooth functions. Assuming that we have already shown
u
has continuous relative derivatives up to order
k
, it follows that we have a weak solution
\tilde{u}:={{}^{b}\nabla^{k}_{\mathrm{rel}}u}
of the P.D.E.

\rho^{2}\partial_{v}(\tilde{u})-\sum a_{ij}(^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{2}u,{{}^{b}\nabla}_{\mathrm{rel}}u){{}^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel,}}}^{2}_{ij}(\tilde{u})+b(^{b}\nabla_{\mathrm{rel}}^{k-1}u,\dots,{{}^{b}\nabla}_{\mathrm{rel}}u,u)=0,

where the coefficients are in
C^{0}
. Now, we note that the above P.D.E. is just the linearisation of the nonlinear P.D.E. at the solution
u
. Therefore, the matrix
[a_{ij}]
is positive-definite as well, and the equation is a linear parabolic P.D.E. Hence, by applying Proposition
7.11
, we get the existence of continuous relative derivatives of
u
up to order
k+1
. Continuing in this fashion, we obtain by induction that all relative derivatives of
u
are continuous. By taking further derivatives of the equation in
\partial_{s}
and proceeding similarly, we get
u\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
. Then, we can show that
u
is a-smooth, by noting that we can do the iteration argument for any
k,r\gg 1
over a short interval of time and invoke Sobolev embedding.

7.5.2
Uniqueness of the solution

By the uniqueness inherent in the contraction mapping argument, our solution is unique in a small
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
-neighbourhood of itself. However, it is also unique in the whole space
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
over the time interval
[0,\varepsilon)
, provided the boundary condition
u|_{s=0}\equiv 0
. To show this, suppose there exists another solution
u\in L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}
to the nonlinear problem. Firstly, we note that by the regularity theorems of the previous section, the new solution is also smooth near the bottom face. Therefore, it follows that it must have the same asymptotic expansion near the bottom face as our solution. In particular, linearising the nonlinear P.D.E. at the solution we obtained, the new solution decays to all orders at the bottom face. We first note the following:

Proposition 7.12
.

Given that
f=(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})u
decays to all orders at the bottom face, and given any
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, we have the estimate,

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}\leq C(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,2}}),

where the norm is taken over
\mathbb{L}|_{\pi^{-1}(\llbracket 0,\eta))}
, and the constant is independent of
\eta
.

Proof.

Let us work over
\mathbb{L}|_{[0,\eta)}
. We have the following a-priori estimate from Lemma
4.50
,

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\|\partial^{i}_{s}f|_{s=0}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2(r-i)-1,2}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\right),

where the constant
C
is independent of
\eta
. The vanishing of
f
to all orders implies that the restriction terms above vanish. Thus, we obtain an estimate

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}\leq C\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,2}}+\|u\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\right).

Thus, we are reduced to showing the following estimate over
[0,\eta)
:

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}(\|f\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,2}}+\|u|_{t=0}\|_{L^{2}_{1,2}}),

(7.7)

such that
C^{\prime}
is independent of
\eta
, since then we can combine this with the previous estimate to conclude. To prove this, note that we know the estimate (
7.7
) holds over the time interval
[0,\varepsilon)
from Theorem
6.7
. Now, for a given
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}|_{[0,\eta)})
, we can extend the function
f=Tu\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}|_{[0,\eta)})
to a function
f^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{L}|_{[0,\varepsilon)})
such that
|\|f\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}-\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{0,0,\lambda}}|
is arbitrarily small and
f|_{[0,\eta)}\equiv f^{\prime}|_{[0,\eta)}
. Further, we can extend
u
to
u^{\prime}
such that we have
Tu^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
, by solving the initial value problem
u^{\prime}|_{t=\eta}=u|_{t=\eta}
,
Tu^{\prime}=f^{\prime}
on
[\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon)
. The solution
u^{\prime}
will be smooth on
[0,\varepsilon)
as well, due to the equation being uniformly parabolic. Therefore, from estimate (
7.7
) on
u^{\prime}
, we obtain the corresponding estimate for
u
with the same constant. This proves the estimate (
7.7
) with constant independent of
\eta
, thus proves the claim.
∎

Now, we have that:

Lemma 7.13
.

Let us assume that
u
decays to all orders at the bottom face and
\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}\ll 1
. Then, we have that for any
\eta\in(0,\varepsilon)
, the following estimate from Theorem
7.4
,

\|Q[u]\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r-2,r-1,2}}\leq C\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}},

for a constant
C
independent of
\eta
.

Proof.

First, we apply Lemma
4.51
to obtain a uniform Sobolev estimate for
u
with constant independent of
\eta
. Then, we follow the proof of Theorem
7.4
– the constant in the theorem does not change on shortening the time interval, since the Sobolev embedding constant does not change.
∎

Now, given our new solution
u
to the nonlinear problem, it is stationary under the iteration mapping, i.e.

u\mapsto(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})^{-1}(Q[u])=u.

However, we have from the previous lemmas the estimate,

\|(\rho^{2}\partial_{v}-\mathcal{L})^{-1}(Q[u])\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}\leq C^{\prime}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}},

for a constant
C^{\prime}
independent of
\eta
. Therefore, we can reduce the time interval to
\llbracket 0,\eta)
to make
\|u\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}
small enough, in order to conclude that
\|u|_{\llbracket 0,\eta)}\|_{L^{2}_{k+2r,r,2}}=0
for a sufficiently small
\eta
. This implies that the new solution must be identically zero for short time, hence identically zero on the whole of
[0,\varepsilon)
.

7.5.3
Uniqueness as an embedding

Now, we want to show our solution is unique as an a-smooth embedding of the manifold with corners
\mathbb{L}
into
\mathbb{X}
, such that it matches with the initial condition on the bottom face and the choice of expander on the front face.

Proposition 7.14
.

The embedding
\iota:\mathbb{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
for the given restrictions to the boundaries,
\iota_{\textrm{bf}}:\mathbb{L}|_{\textrm{bf}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}|_{\textrm{bf}}
and
\iota_{\textrm{ff}}:\mathbb{L}|_{\textrm{ff}}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}|_{\textrm{ff}}
, such that the time-slices satisfy LMCF, is unique. More precisely, given any other embedding
\iota^{\prime}:\mathbb{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
satisfying the same boundary conditions, we can decrease the time interval if necessary to
[0,\eta)
, such that there is an a-smooth diffeomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{L}|_{[0,\eta)}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}
such that
\iota^{\prime}|_{[0,\eta)}=\iota\circ\phi
.

Proof.

First, due to a-smoothness and the given boundary conditions, we can use an implicit function theorem to write
\mathbb{L}^{\prime}
as the graph of a relative closed a-smooth
1
-form
\alpha
over the Lagrangian neighbourhood of
\mathbb{L}
for sufficiently small time, such that the form vanishes at the boundaries. Then,
\alpha
is exact because the Lagrangian mean curvature flow dictates the Hamiltonian isotopy classes in which the Lagrangians must lie. Then, by Lemma
7.2
, there exists an a-smooth function
f:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
d_{\mathrm{rel}}f=\alpha
, and
f|_{\partial\mathbb{L}}\equiv 0
. By making the time interval short enough, the uniqueness of solution to the nonlinear P.D.E. implies that
f\equiv 0
. Therefore, the mapping
\iota^{\prime}
is onto the image of the mapping
\iota
for short time. Due to both being a-smooth embeddings into
\mathbb{X}
, the claim follows.
∎

Therefore, we have proved the following main theorem:

Theorem 7.15
.

Let
\iota:L\rightarrow X
be an embedding of a conically singular Lagrangian with a finite number of singular points
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subset X
, such that each of its asymptotic cones
C_{i}
is a union of special Lagrangian cones
C_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}C_{ij}
(which can have different phases). For each
i
, let
E_{i}
be a Lagrangian expander which is asymptotic to the cone
C_{i}
, which decays at rate
-\infty
to
C_{i}
by Proposition
2.20
. There exists a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, such that the following holds: Suppose we construct
\mathbb{X}\in\mathbf{Man}^{\mathbf{c,ac}}
as explained before, by parabolically blowing up
X\times[0,\varepsilon)
at the singular points
\{(x_{i},0)\}_{i=1}^{k}
at time
0
. Then, there exists
\mathbb{L}\in\mathbf{Man}^{\mathbf{c,ac}}
and an a-smooth embedding
i:\mathbb{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
such that

•

\mathbb{L}
has an ordinary boundary, called the bottom face, such that
\mathbb{L}|_{\mathrm{bf}}=\overline{\widetilde{L_{0}}}
, where
\overline{\widetilde{L_{0}}}
denotes the closure of the radial blow-up of the initial Lagrangian at its singular points, and
i|_{\mathrm{bf}}:\overline{\widetilde{L_{0}}}\rightarrow\overline{\widetilde{X}}=\mathbb{X}|_{\mathrm{bf}}
is the lift of the embedding
L\subset X
to the blow-ups.

•

\mathbb{L}
has an a-boundary, which is the disjoint union of
k
distinct pieces, called the front faces, such that
\mathbb{L}|_{\mathrm{ff}_{i}}=\overline{E_{i}}
, where
E_{i}
is the expander asymptotic to the cone
C_{i}
as above, and
\iota|_{\mathrm{ff}_{i}}:\overline{E}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\cong\mathbb{X}|_{\mathrm{ff}_{i}}
is the compactification of the embedding of
E
into the
i^{th}
front face of
\mathbb{X}
.

•

The embeddings on the time slices,
\iota|_{t}:\mathbb{L}_{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}_{t}\cong X
evolve by LMCF, for
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
.

Moreover, given the prescribed embeddings on the bottom face and the front faces, the embedding is unique up to diffeomorphism.

7.6
Asymptotics of the solution near the front face

In this subsection, we make the assumption that the initial Lagrangian
L_{0}
has a polyhomogeneous conormal expansion with index set
\mathcal{I}
near it singularities, i.e. it can be written as a graph
\Gamma(df)
over the special Lagrangian cone
C
, with a polyhomogeneous conormal function
f
of the form

f\sim\sum_{(z,p)\in\mathcal{I}}r^{z}\log(r)^{p}f_{z,p},

for
f_{z,p}
being smooth functions on the link
\Sigma
, and
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
being the index set for
f
(for details about the asymptotics defined by
\sim
, see Grieser
[
Gri01
]
). Since we assume convergence to the cone with rate
\mu>2
, we have
\Re(\mathcal{I})\subset(2,\infty)
. By definition of index sets, the set
\mathcal{I}
is finite in any half-plane
\{z:\Re(z)<s\}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
(z,p)\in\mathcal{I}
implies that
(z,q)\in\mathcal{I}
for
q\leq p
. We also assume that
(j,0)\in\mathcal{I}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
j>2
. Then, we first note that:

Lemma 7.16
.

Consider the approximate solution
\iota:\mathbb{L}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
as constructed in Proposition
7.3
, such that the error term vanishes to all orders in
s
. Then, the embedding of this approximate solution into
\mathbb{X}
is also polyhomogeneous conormal function with the same index set. More precisely, we can write
\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{-1}(\iota(\mathbb{L}))
near the front face of
\mathrm{Bl}(\mathbb{C}^{m}\times[0,\infty);(\mathbf{0},0))
as the graph of a polyhomogeneous conormal function over
\mathbb{E}
, with the same index set
\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

This follows from the construction of the approximate solution in §
4
– the hypothesis that
L_{0}
admits a polyhomogeneous conormal expansion and choice of smooth interpolating cutoff functions implies that the compactification of the approximate solutions also admits an expansion with the same index set over the compactification of the expander.
∎

Now, we will similarly solve the nonlinear P.D.E. (
4.10
) to infinite order at the front face. As before in Section
5.2
, we work with coordinates near the front face given by
(r,\sigma^{i})
for
r\in[0,\infty),\sigma^{i}\in{\overline{E}}
.

Lemma 7.17
.

We can write the operator on
\mathbb{L}
near the front face in the coordinates
(\sigma,r)\in{\overline{E}}\times[0,\infty)
, as a perturbation of the corresponding operator on
\mathbb{E}
,

\rho^{2}P[u]=\rho^{2}\nu-(\rho\partial_{\rho})^{2}u+(\rho\partial_{\rho})(w+h)u-P_{0}u+Q((\rho\partial_{\rho}+{{}^{b}\nabla}_{{\overline{E}}})u,(\rho\partial_{\rho}+{{}^{b}\nabla}_{{\overline{E}}})^{2}u)+\rho^{2}Q^{\prime}[u],

(7.8)

where
\nu
denotes a smooth error function arising from the embedding
\widetilde{\Upsilon}:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}
. Further, we can write

\rho^{2}Q^{\prime}[u]=\sum_{i,j\geq 0}a_{ij}\cdot(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u)^{i}\otimes(\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}}u)^{j},

(7.9)

such that the coefficients
a_{ij}
admit polyhomogeneous conormal expansions near the front face, with index set given by

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\cup_{k\geq 1}k\mathcal{I}+\mathbb{N},

(7.10)

where
k\mathcal{I}
denotes the sum of the index set with itself
k
times. Moreover, the weighted error
\rho^{2}E
for the embedding
\iota:\mathbb{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{X}
admits an expansion with index set
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Here, we first work over a neighbourhood
\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{E}}
of the front face of the compactification
\mathbb{E}
. Then, we can write the nonlinear part of the P.D.E. over
\mathbb{B}_{\mathbb{E}}
as a
smooth
function on
U_{\mathbb{E}}\oplus\textrm{Sym}^{2}(^{b}T^{*}_{\mathrm{rel}}\mathbb{E})
, since the embedding
\mathbb{E}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}
is smooth instead of merely a-smooth. Then, we can Taylor expand it in the fibre directions, to get that

Q[\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u,\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}}u]=\sum_{i,j\geq 0}a_{ij}\cdot(\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}u)^{i}\otimes(\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}}u)^{j},

where the coefficients
a_{ij}
are smooth
b
-tensors. Then, we Taylor expand the coefficients in the radial directions, which proves the claim for
\mathbb{E}
, i.e. with index set
\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
. To prove the claim for
\mathbb{L}
, we can consider the nonlinear part
Q[\rho^{-2}d_{\mathrm{rel}}(u+f),\rho^{-2}{{}^{b}\nabla^{2}_{\mathrm{rel}}(u+f)}]
, where we write
\mathbb{L}=\Gamma(d_{\mathrm{rel}}f)
over
\mathbb{E}
. From Lemma
7.16
, we know the asymptotics of
f
. Then, we note that the coefficients
a^{\prime}_{ij}
will have index sets as a subset of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
as defined. This proves the expansion (
7.9
) for the coefficient of the operator on
\mathbb{L}
. Noting that the error for the embedding
\iota
corresponds to the term
\rho^{2}\nu+a_{00}
, it follows that
\rho^{2}E
has index set
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.
∎

Now, we make the following ansatz for the solution,

u\sim\sum_{(z,p)\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}r^{z}\log(r)^{p}u_{z,p}(s)\textrm{ near the front face},

(7.11)

for
u_{z,p}(s)
being smooth functions on
{\overline{E}}
. Here, the index set
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is as defined earlier,

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\cup_{k\geq 1}k\mathcal{I}+\mathbb{N}.

Now, we find functions
u_{z,p}
such that the corresponding function
u
as in (
7.11
) satisfies
P[u]\sim 0
as
r\rightarrow 0
, i.e. near the front face. First, we note that

(\rho\partial_{\rho})(r^{z}\log(r)^{p})=zr^{z}\log(r)^{p}+pr^{z}\log(r)^{p-1},

(\rho\partial_{\rho})^{2}(r^{z}\log(r)^{p})=z^{2}r^{z}\log(r)^{p}+2pzr^{z}\log(r)^{p-1}+p(p-1)r^{z}\log(r)^{p-2}.

Therefore, we get that

\begin{split}\left(-(\rho\partial_{\rho})^{2}+(\rho\partial_{\rho})(w+h)-P_{0}\right)(r^{z}\log(r)^{p}u_{z,p})&=-r^{z}\log(r)^{p}P_{z}(u_{z,p})\\
&+\textrm{ lower order terms in }\{u_{z,p-1},u_{z,p-2}\}.\end{split}

(7.12)

So, the P.D.E. (
7.8
) along with the boundary condition
u|_{s=0}\equiv 0
can be written as a system of equations on
{\overline{E}}
,

-P_{z}(u_{z,p})=(\rho^{2}\nu+a_{0,0})_{z,p}+F(u_{z,p+1},u_{z,p+2},\dots)+\textrm{ nonlinear terms in }\{u_{z-k,q}\},\;u_{z,p}|_{\partial{\overline{E}}}=0,

for
(z,p)\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.

Now, the main idea is to solve for these equations as
(z,p)\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
inductively: we introduce a partial order
\geq
on the set
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, given by
(z_{1},p_{1})\geq(z_{2},p_{2})
if we have
\Re(z_{1})\geq\Re(z_{2})
, or we have
\Re(z_{1})=\Re(z_{2})
and
p_{1}\leq p_{2}
. Then, at each step of induction, we solve for the
u_{z,p}
which has its index smallest in the partial order (note that such a
(z,p)
may not be unique; however, we can solve for them independently). Due to the way the ordering is defined, the P.D.E. for
u_{z,p}
has coefficients depending only on
u_{z^{\prime},p^{\prime}}
with
(z^{\prime},p^{\prime})<(z,p)
. Moreover, the solutions
u_{z,p}
are uniquely determined as the mapping
(-P_{z},|_{\partial{\overline{E}}})
is an isomorphism for
\Re(z)>0
by Proposition
5.38
. Therefore, we obtain a formal series solution solving the P.D.E. to high order at the front face, which extends to a polyhomogeneous conormal function via Borel’s lemma for polyhomogeneous conormal expansions. So, we obtain:

Proposition 7.18
.

There is a polyhomogeneous conormal solution
u^{\prime}
of the P.D.E. such that
P[u^{\prime}]\sim 0
as
r\rightarrow 0
, and
u^{\prime}|_{s=0}\equiv 0
.

Therefore, we can prove that:

Theorem 7.19
.

Consider the setup of Theorem
7.15
. If the initial Lagrangian is further prescribed a polyhomogeneous conormal expansion at each of the singularities, then the solution has a (uniquely determined) polyhomogeneous conormal expansion at the front faces.

Proof.

This follows from first solving the problem to infinite order in both
\rho,s
by Propositions
7.3
and
7.18
, and performing the iteration mapping argument for
L^{2}_{k+2r,r,\lambda}
with
\lambda\gg 0
– the error lies in this space due to vanishing at all orders at the boundaries.
∎

As a corollary to this, we obtain:

Corollary 7.20
.

In the case of the problem of desingularising an immersed Lagrangian by gluing in Joyce-Lee-Tsui expanders, the resulting solution is a smooth manifold with corners.

Proof.

In the course of the proof, we have shown that if the initial Lagrangian has a polyhomogeneous conormal expansion with index set given by
\mathcal{I}
, then the solution can be taken to have index set
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
given as in (
7.10
). For an immersed Lagrangian, the index set is
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, therefore the index set of the solution also lies in
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
, i.e. it is a smooth embedding.
∎
