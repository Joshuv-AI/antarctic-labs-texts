Automatic optimal-rate convergence of randomized nets using median-of-means
Zexin Pan

Abstract
We study the sample median of independently generated quasi-Monte Carlo estimators based on randomized digital nets and prove it approximates the target integral value at almost the optimal convergence rate for various function spaces. In contrast to previous methods, the algorithm does not require a priori knowledge of underlying function spaces or even an input of pre-designed ( t , m , s ) (t,m,s) -digital nets, and is therefore easier to implement. This study provides further evidence that quasi-Monte Carlo estimators are heavy-tailed when applied to smooth integrands and taking the median can significantly improve the error by filtering out the outliers.



1
Introduction

Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method
[
8
]
has been used extensively as an alternative to the usual Monte Carlo (MC) algorithm. Just like MC, QMC uses the average of
n
function evaluations as an estimate for the target integral value. The places of sampling are, however, carefully designed to explore the underlying sampling space efficiently. As a result, QMC is less sensitive to the curse of dimensionality compared to classical quadrature rules while enjoying a faster error convergence rate compared to MC.

Traditionally, the error bound is given by the Koksma-Hlawka inequality
[
15
]
, which proves a
O(n^{-1+\epsilon})
convergence rate for arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
when the integrand
f
has a finite total variation in the sense of Hardy and Krause. Later
[
22
]
introduces the method of scrambling, which randomizes the sampling points without breaking their design. Randomization boosts the convergence rate to
O(n^{-3/2+\epsilon})
as measured by the rooted mean squared error (RMSE) when
f
has continuous dominating mixed derivatives of order
1
.

An issue with the standard QMC design is the convergence rate cannot be further improved even if
f
satisfies a stronger smoothness assumption
[
25
]
. An exception is the lattice rules, for which point sets can be designed to obtain the optimal convergence rate for a range of smoothness conditions
[
7
]
, but this often requires
f
to satisfy a periodic boundary condition.

To improve the convergence rate,
[
6
]
constructs the higher order scrambled digital nets, which achieve a better than
O(n^{-3/2})
RMSE convergence rate for sufficiently smooth
f
. A drawback with this design is the smoothness parameter is needed as an input for the construction, and still the algorithm fails to converge at the optimal rate once the smoothness of
f
exceeds the parameter we set.

This paper offers a solution using the median trick. The median of QMC estimators is first analyzed in
[
25
]
and proven to converge at
O(n^{-c\log_{2}(n)})
rate for any
c<3\log(2)/\pi^{2}\approx 0.21
when
f
is a one-dimensional analytic function over
[0,1]
. Later
[
27
]
generalizes this result to higher-dimensional
f
. Meanwhile,
[
25
]
also proves a
O(n^{-\alpha+\epsilon})
convergence rate when
f
is
\alpha
-times continuously differentiable over
[0,1]
. While the rate is not optimal, it demonstrates a feature of automatic convergence rate speedup without the knowledge of the smoothness of
f
. In this paper, we will improve this result by both generalizing it to higher-dimensional
f
and proving a
O(n^{-\alpha-1/2+\epsilon})
almost optimal rate. Our analysis will be focused on base-2 scrambled digital nets, although the idea can be generalized to other numerical integration methods
[
3
,
12
,
13
]
. As an additional merit of the median trick, we do not need to input a pre-designed digital net to implement the algorithm.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces necessary background knowledge and notation for later sections. Subsection
2.4
in particular explains how the median trick boosts the convergence rate. Section
3
is devoted to the study of convergence rate of the median estimator constructed by randomized digital nets under various smoothness assumptions. Subsection
3.1
studies integrands with bounded variation of order
\alpha
used by
[
6
]
. We are going to show the median estimator attains almost the same convergence rate as the higher order scrambled digital nets without knowing the smoothness parameter
\alpha
. The proof is rather short thanks to a key lemma proven in
[
6
]
, and it illustrates the standard analysis scheme of the median estimator. Subsection
3.2
studies integrands whose dominating mixed derivatives of order
\alpha
are continuous with bounded fractional variation used by
[
5
]
. We will prove an almost
1/2
convergence rate improvement over the optimal deterministic error rate achieved by the digital
(t,\alpha,s)
-sequence demonstrated in
[
5
]
. Subsection
3.3
studies integrands whose dominating mixed derivatives belong to the fractional Sobolev space used by
[
4
]
and
[
10
]
. Again we will show an almost
1/2
convergence rate improvement over the optimal deterministic rate. The proof resembles that of Subsection
3.2
, so we will omit the repeated part and highlight where a different analysis is required. Section
4
showcases many numerical experiments that test how well median estimators perform compared to higher order digital nets. Section
5
concludes the paper with a discussion of the limitations of our analysis as well as interesting research directions.

Background and notation

We use
\mathbb{N}
for the set of natural numbers
\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. Because we often need to remove the zero frequency, we let
\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}=\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
. We further define
\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\ell}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{<\ell}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The dimension is denoted as
s
in this paper. For
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
1{:}s=\{1,2,\dots,s\}
. For a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{s}
and a subset
u\subseteq 1{:}s
, we use

\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
to denote the
|u|
-dimensional vector of elements
x_{j}
for
j\in u
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}
to denote the
(s-|u|)
-dimensional vector of elements
x_{j}
for
j\not\in u
. We use
\boldsymbol{1}_{1{:}s}
for the vector of
s
ones and
\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathcal{A}\}
for the indicator function that equals
1
if event
\mathcal{A}
occurs and equals
0
if not. For
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
, we use
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}
to denote
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\ell_{j}
. The cardinality of a set
K
is denoted by
|K|
. For positive sequence
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
, we write
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
if there exists a constant
C<\infty
such that
a_{n}\leqslant Cb_{n}
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The integrand
f
is assumed to have domain
[0,1]^{s}
. The
L^{p}
-norm of
f
over
[0,1]^{s}
is denoted as
\|f\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{s})}
and the spaces of
f
with finite
L^{p}
-norm is denoted as
L^{p}([0,1]^{s})
. We also use
C([0,1]^{s})
to denote the space of continuous functions over
[0,1]^{s}
and
\|f\|_{\infty}=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,1]^{s}}|f(\boldsymbol{x})|
for
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
. We always assume our integrand

belongs to
C([0,1]^{s})
. QMC methods aim to approximate

\mu=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\quad\text{by}\quad\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})

for points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in[0,1]^{s}
. We will choose
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
according to the base-2 digital net construction described below.

2.1
Digital nets and randomization

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}}
, we represent the binary expansion
i=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}i_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
compactly by
\vec{i}=\vec{i}[m]=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. Similarly for
a\in[0,1)
and a precision
E
, we truncate the binary expansion
a=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{-\ell}
at the
E
’th digit and represent it by
\vec{a}=\vec{a}[E]=(a_{1},\dots,a_{E})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
. When
a
admits two binary expansions, we always choose the one with finitely many
a_{\ell}=1
.

Our base-2 digital nets over
[0,1]^{s}
are constructed using
s
matrices
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The unrandomized digital nets are defined as

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2

(1)

for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}}
and
j\in 1{:}s
. Here
\vec{x}_{ij}
is the precision
E
binary representation of
x_{ij}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{is})
. The truncated digits beyond the
E
’th digit are assumed to be all
0
. For unrandomized digital nets we typically have
E\leqslant m
.

We will study two types of randomization. They both have the form

\displaystyle\vec{x}_{ij}=C_{j}\vec{i}+\vec{D}_{j}\ \ \mathsf{mod}\ 2

(2)

for a random matrix
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
and a random vector
\vec{D}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
with precision
E\geqslant m
. The vectors
\vec{D}_{j}
are called digital shifts and consist of independent
\mathbb{U}\{0,1\}
random entries in both cases. The difference is in the randomization of
C_{j}
.

In the first type of randomization, which we will call completely random designs, all entries of
C_{j}
are drawn independently from
\mathbb{U}\{0,1\}
. It is very easy to implement, but may generate bad designs especially when
m
is small. The second type of randomization comes from
[
20
]
and is called random linear scrambling. It requires
s
pre-designed generating matrices
\mathcal{C}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
as input and uses
C_{j}=M_{j}\mathcal{C}_{j}
for a random lower-triangle matrix
M_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{E\times m}
. All entries on the diagonal of
M_{j}
are
1
and those below the diagonal are drawn independently from
\mathbb{U}\{0,1\}
. Typical choices of
\mathcal{C}_{j}
are nets of Sobol’
[
28
]
and those of Niederreiter and Xing
[
21
]
because they have low
t
parameters that we will introduce below. Linear scrambling preserves the
t
parameters and guarantees the resulting designs are as good as the unrandomized ones.

Letting
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}[E]
denote the above points
constructed with precision
E
, our estimate is

\hat{\mu}_{E}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\quad\text{for $\boldsymbol{x}_{i}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}[E]$}.

(3)

We will conveniently assume
E=\infty
and focus our analysis on
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
. In practice,
E
is constrained by the floating point representation in use. Lemma 1 of
[
27
]
shows the difference between
\hat{\mu}_{E}
and
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
is negligible when the error
|\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu|
is significantly larger than
\omega_{f}(\sqrt{s}2^{-E})
where
\omega_{f}
is the modulus of continuity of
f
.

2.2
Elementary intervals and the
t
parameter

An elementary interval in base
2
is
a Cartesian product of the form

\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{a})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Bigl[\frac{a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}}},\frac{a_{j}+1}{2^{\ell_{j}}}\Bigr)

for
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
and
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{s})\mid a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{\ell_{j}}}\}
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we say matrices
C_{j},j\in 1{:}s
generate a
(t,m,s)
-net in base
2
if
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{2^{m}-1}
defined by equation (
1
) have the property that every elementary interval of volume
2^{t-m}
contains precisely
2^{t}
of those points. A lower value of
t
implies better equidistribution of the point set. Sobol’s construction has
t
bounded in
m
but growing at
O(s\log s)
rate in
s

[
28
]
.

2.3
Fourier-Walsh decomposition

Walsh functions are the natural Fourier basis to study base-2 digital nets. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x\in[0,1)
, the
k
’th univariate
Walsh function
\mathrm{wal}_{k}
is defined by

\mathrm{wal}_{k}(x)=(-1)^{\vec{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}}.

Here
\vec{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}
is computed using the infinite precision binary expansion of
k
and
x
, but since
\vec{k}
only contains finitely many nonzero entries, a finite precision representation suffices for the computation.

For
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}
,

\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}:[0,1)^{s}\to\{-1,1\}
is defined by the tensor product

\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathrm{wal}_{k_{j}}(x_{j})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}_{j}}.

The functions
\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}

form a complete orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,1]^{s})

[
8
]
, which implies

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad\text{for}

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

where equality (
4
) holds in the
L^{2}
sense. Using the above Walsh expansion, we can get the following error decomposition:

Theorem 1
.

Let
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
be defined
by equation (
2
) for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{m}}
.
Then

\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}}Z(\boldsymbol{k})S(\boldsymbol{k})\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}),

(5)

where

Z(\boldsymbol{k})=\boldsymbol{1}\{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k{}}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2\},

S(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{D}_{j}}.

Proof.

Theorem 1 of
[
27
]
proves the statement under the stronger assumption
f
is analytic, but the proof only needs equality (
4
) to hold in the pointwise sense, which is guaranteed by
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
and the arguments in Section 3.3 of
[
5
]
.
∎

The set of
\boldsymbol{k}
with
Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1
depends on the random matrices
C_{j}
and therefore
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)
depends on our randomization. This motivates the following definition:

Definition 1
.

A randomization for
C_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{\infty\times m},j\in 1{:}s
is said to be
asymptotically full-rank
if there exists a constant
R
independent of
m
and
\boldsymbol{k}
such that
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)\leqslant 2^{-m+R}
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}
.

Lemma 1
.

When
E=\infty
, both completely random designs and random linear scrambling are asymptotically full-rank.

Proof.

For completely random designs,
\sum_{j=1}^{s}\vec{k{}}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}C_{j}\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2
is uniformly distributed over
\{0,1\}^{m}
when
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
and
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)=2^{-m}
. For random linear scrambling,
Corollary 1 of
[
27
]
proves
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)\leqslant 2^{-m+t+s}
, so it satisfies the definition with
R=t+s
.
∎

2.4
Mean squared error of the median

Analysis of equation (
5
) is facilitated by the fact that
S(\boldsymbol{k}),\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}
are mean 0 and pairwise independent (see Lemma 4 of
[
25
]
for a proof when
s=1
). In particular, we can compute the variance of
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
and apply Chebyshev’s inequality to bound the probability of large
|\hat{\mu}_{\infty}-\mu|
occurrence. We can further sharpen the bound by identifying a small probability event
\mathcal{A}
such that

\mathbb{E}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})=\mu
and

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})\ll\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty})
. We then turn this bound into a reduced RMSE by taking the median and filtering out the outliers. This idea leads to the following lemma:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an event with

\Pr(\mathcal{A})\leqslant\delta
and

\mathbb{E}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})=\mu
. Then the sample median

\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
of
2r-1
independently generated values of

\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
defined by equation (
3
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}((\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu)^{2})\leqslant\delta^{-1}{\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})+(8\delta)^{r}\Delta^{2}_{m}

where
\Delta_{m}
is the worst-case error of
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
when
n=2^{m}
and
\Delta_{m}\leqslant 2\|f\|_{\infty}
if
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
. For random linear scrambling of base-2
(t,m,s)
-nets, we further have
\Delta_{m}=O(m^{s-1}2^{-m})
if
f
has a finite total variation in the sense of Hardy and Krause.

Proof.

Lemma 5 of
[
27
]
proves the statement under the random linear scrambling, but the proof applies to any randomization on
C_{j}
because the linear scrambling is assumed only to prove a tighter bound on
\Delta_{m}
. The bound
\Delta_{m}\leqslant 2\|f\|_{\infty}
holds because both
\mu
and
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
cannot exceed the maximum of
|f|
.
∎

As seen in Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
, the two types of randomization differ only in the bounds of
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)
and
\Delta_{m}
, so we will not emphasize which randomization is used when we state our convergence rate results in Section
3
with the understanding that the proof applies to any asymptotically full-rank randomization.

We will set
\mathcal{A}
to be the event
\{Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in K\}
for a chosen
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}
. Because
Z(\boldsymbol{k})
is determined by
C_{j}
,
j\in 1{:}s
and
S(\boldsymbol{k})
is independent of those
C_{j}
, we know
S(\boldsymbol{k})
is independent of
\mathcal{A}
and still has mean 0 conditioned on
\mathcal{A}^{c}
, and therefore
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})=\mu
. We also have
\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\mid\mathcal{A}^{c})=0
for
\boldsymbol{k}\in K
and

\displaystyle{\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})

\displaystyle={\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\mid\mathcal{A}^{c})|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}\Pr(\{Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1\}\cap\mathcal{A}^{c})|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}.

A key ingredient in our later proof is to choose a proper set
K
and make the above sum converge at a desired rate.

3
Convergence rate of the median

Below we will study the convergence rate of the sample median

\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
under different smoothness assumptions on
f
. In each subsection, we will first state the assumption and give related bounds of the Walsh coefficient
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
. Then we will construct an event
\mathcal{A}
and use Lemma
2
to prove the RMSE of
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
converges at almost the optimal rate.

3.1
When
f
has bounded variation of order
\alpha

In
[
6
]
, the author constructs higher order scrambled digital nets and shows their RMSE achieves the optimal
O(n^{-\alpha-1/2+\epsilon})
convergence rate for any
\epsilon>0
when
f
has bounded variation of order
\alpha
. We will show
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
automatically achieves the same convergence rate without knowing the smoothness parameter
\alpha
as long as
r
is large enough.

First, we will define the variation of order
\alpha
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. We will mostly follow the notations from
[
6
]
. For
f
over
[0,1]
and
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{\alpha})\in(-1,1)^{\alpha}
, we define the one-dimensional finite difference operator
\Delta_{\alpha},\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
recursively as

\Delta_{0}(x)f=f(x),

\Delta_{\alpha}(x;z_{1},\dots,z_{\alpha})f=\Delta_{\alpha-1}(x+z_{\alpha};z_{1},\dots,z_{\alpha-1})f-\Delta_{\alpha-1}(x;z_{1},\dots,z_{\alpha-1})f.

We assume
x+\sum_{r=1}^{\alpha^{\prime}}z_{r}\in[0,1]
for all
1\leqslant\alpha^{\prime}\leqslant\alpha
.

For
f
over
[0,1]^{s}
, we let
\Delta_{i,\alpha}
be the finite difference operator applied to the
i
th coordinate of
f
. For
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{j}=(z_{j,1},\dots,z_{j,\alpha_{j}})
, we define

\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})f=\Delta_{s,\alpha_{s}}(x_{s};\boldsymbol{z}_{s})\cdots\Delta_{1,\alpha_{1}}(x_{1};\boldsymbol{z}_{1})f.

The generalized Vitali variation of
f
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\{1,\dots,\alpha\}^{s}
is defined as

V^{(s)}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(f)=\sup_{\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}}\Bigl(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}}\mathrm{Vol}(J)\sup_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s}}\Bigl|\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{t};\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s})f}{\prod_{j=1}^{s}\prod_{r=1}^{\alpha_{j}}z_{j,r}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

(6)

where
\mathrm{Vol}(J)
is the Lebesgue measure of
J
,
\sup_{\mathcal{P_{\alpha}}}
is over all partitions of
[0,1)^{s}
into subcubes of the form
J=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[2^{-\alpha\ell_{j}}a_{j},2^{-\alpha\ell_{j}}(a_{j}+1))
for
a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{\alpha\ell_{j}}}
and
\ell_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\sup_{\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{s}}
is over
\boldsymbol{t}\in J
and
z_{j,r}=2^{-\alpha(\ell_{j}-1)-r}
or
-2^{-\alpha(\ell_{j}-1)-r}
such that
2^{-\alpha(\ell_{j}-1)}\lfloor 2^{-\alpha}a_{j}\rfloor\leqslant t_{j}+\sum_{r=1}^{\alpha^{\prime}_{j}}z_{j,r}<2^{-\alpha(\ell_{j}-1)}(\lfloor 2^{-\alpha}a_{j}\rfloor+1)
for all
1\leqslant\alpha^{\prime}_{j}\leqslant\alpha_{j}
. We note that the definition used by
[
6
]
allows partitions of more general shape, but it is always possible to find refinements from our set of partitions, so the two definitions agree.

The generalized Hardy and Krause variation of
f
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
is defined as

V_{\alpha}(f)=\Bigl(\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\{1,\dots,\alpha\}^{|u|}}(V^{(|u|)}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(f_{u}))^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

where

f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})=\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{1{:}s\setminus u}
if
u\neq\varnothing
and
V^{(\varnothing)}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(f_{\varnothing})=|\mu|
.

Next for
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the digit interlacing function

\mathcal{E}_{d}:\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
as

\mathcal{E}_{d}(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r=1}^{d}\vec{k}_{r}(\ell)2^{d(\ell-1)+r-1}

where
\vec{k}_{r}(\ell)
is the
\ell
’th entry of
\vec{k}_{r}
. The vectorized version
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{d}:\mathbb{N}^{ds}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
is given by

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{d}(k_{1},\dots,k_{ds})=(\mathcal{E}_{d}(k_{1},\dots,k_{d}),\dots,\mathcal{E}_{d}(k_{d(s-1)+1},\dots,k_{ds})).

It is easy to see
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{d}
is a bijection between
\mathbb{N}^{ds}_{0}
and
\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
.

With the above definition,
[
6
]
proves the following bounds on the Walsh coefficients
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
. We will prove a similar result in the next subsection under a different assumption.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
f
over
[0,1]^{s}
satisfies
V_{\alpha}(f)<\infty
.
For
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{\alpha s})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}
, define

B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\mid\lfloor 2^{\ell_{j}-1}\rfloor\leqslant k_{j}<2^{\ell_{j}}\text{ for }j\in 1{:}\alpha s\}.

(7)

Then we have

\Bigl(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}))|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leqslant C_{\alpha,s}2^{-\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}V_{\alpha}(f)

where
C_{\alpha,s}
is an explicit constant depending on
\alpha
and
s
and
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}
is the sum of the
\alpha s
components of
\boldsymbol{\ell}
.

Proof.

This is Lemma 9 of
[
6
]
with
d=\alpha
and
b=2
. The constant
C_{\alpha,s}
is given by

C_{\alpha,s}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}}2^{\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}\gamma(\boldsymbol{\ell})=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\prod_{j=1}^{\alpha}2^{-j+(i-1)\alpha+\alpha}=2^{s(s+1)\alpha^{2}/2-s\alpha(\alpha+1)/2},

where
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\ell})
is defined as in that lemma.
∎

Motivated by the above lemma, we define event
\mathcal{A}
to be
\{Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1
for some
\boldsymbol{k}\in K\}
with

K=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s},\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}\leqslant m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\}.

The next lemma will help us bound the size of
|K|
.

Lemma 4
.

For
\rho>1
and any nondecreasing function
g:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

\sum_{N=0}^{N_{m}}g(N)\rho^{N}\leqslant\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}g(N_{m})\rho^{N_{m}}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{N=0}^{N_{m}}g(N)\rho^{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=0}^{N_{m}}g(N)\frac{\rho^{N+1}-\rho^{N}}{\rho-1}

\displaystyle=g(N_{m})\frac{\rho^{N_{m}+1}}{\rho-1}-\sum_{N=1}^{N_{m}}(g(N)-g(N-1))\frac{\rho^{N}}{\rho-1}-g(0)\frac{1}{\rho-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\rho}{\rho-1}g(N_{m})\rho^{N_{m}}.\qed

Lemma 5
.

With
\mathcal{A}
defined as above, we have
\Pr(\mathcal{A})=O(1/m)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
1
and a union bound argument, it suffices to show
|K|=O(2^{m}/m)
. Because
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}
is a bijection and
B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}
are disjoint for different values of
\boldsymbol{\ell}
, we need to show

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}\leqslant m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}|B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}|=O(\frac{2^{m}}{m}).

From equation (
7
), we see that

|B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}|=\prod_{j=1}^{\alpha s}\max(2^{\ell_{j}-1},1)\leqslant 2^{\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}.

Because
\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\mid\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}=N\}
corresponds to the set of
\alpha s
ordered nonnegative integers summing to
N
,

|\{\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\mid\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}=N\}|={N+\alpha s-1\choose\alpha s-1}.

(8)

By applying Lemma
4

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}\leqslant m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}|B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{N=0}^{\lfloor m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rfloor}{N+\alpha s-1\choose\alpha s-1}2^{N}

\displaystyle\leqslant{m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)+\alpha s-1\choose\alpha s-1}2^{m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)+1}

\displaystyle=O(\frac{2^{m}}{m}),

which implies our conclusion.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
V_{\alpha}(f)<\infty
. When
r\geqslant m
, we have
\mathbb{E}((\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu)^{2})=O(n^{-2\alpha-1+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, we can apply Lemma
2
with
\delta=C/m
for some large enough
C>0
. Then for large enough
m
,
8\delta<1
and
(8\delta)^{r}\leqslant(8C/m)^{m}\to 0
at a rate faster than
n^{-2\alpha-1}=2^{-(2\alpha+1)m}
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. It remains to show

{\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})\leqslant\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}=O(n^{-2\alpha-1+\epsilon}).

By Lemma
1
, for some constant
R

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{-m+R}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{-m+R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{k}))|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{-m+R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}C^{2}_{\alpha,s}4^{-\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}V_{\alpha}(f)^{2},

where we applied Lemma
3
in the last step.

Next we use equation (
8
) and get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}4^{-\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=\lceil m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rceil}^{\infty}{N+\alpha s-1\choose\alpha s-1}4^{-\alpha N}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{N=\lceil m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{(N+\alpha s-1)^{\alpha s-1}}{(\alpha s-1)!}4^{-\alpha N}.

For arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
, we have
(N+\alpha s-1)^{\alpha s-1}<4^{\epsilon N}
for large enough
N
. Therefore for large enough
m

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\alpha s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}4^{-\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{(\alpha s-1)!}\sum_{N=\lceil m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rceil}^{\infty}4^{-(\alpha-\epsilon)N}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{-(\alpha-\epsilon)\lceil m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rceil}}{(\alpha s-1)!(1-4^{-\alpha+\epsilon})}.

(9)

Finally we have for large enough
m

{\Pr(\mathcal{A}^{c})}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})\leqslant 2^{-m+R}C^{2}_{\alpha,s}V_{\alpha}(f)^{2}\frac{4^{-(\alpha-\epsilon)\lceil m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rceil}}{(\alpha s-1)!(1-2^{-\alpha+\epsilon})}.

Because
n=2^{m}
and
\log_{2}(m)<\epsilon m
for large enough
m
, our proof is complete once we choose a small enough
\epsilon
.
∎

3.2
When dominating mixed derivatives of
f
up to order
\alpha
are continuous with bounded fractional variation

Next we consider the case when
f
has dominating mixed derivatives up to order
\alpha
, namely
f
can be partially differentiated in each variable up to
\alpha
times. We will use the notation

f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=f^{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})}=\frac{\partial^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{j}}f}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{s}^{\alpha_{s}}}.

We conventionally define
f^{(\boldsymbol{0})}=f
. We say
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
if
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in C([0,1]^{s})
for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}_{\leqslant\alpha}
.

Notice that if
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}
is continuous, equation (
6
) can be viewed as a Riemann sum for the integral

\Bigl(\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}(\boldsymbol{x})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Bigr)^{1/2}

and we immediately have
V^{(s)}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(f)<\infty
. Hence
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
implies
V_{\alpha}(f)<\infty
and the results from the previous subsection applies here as well.

Next we introduce the fractional Vitali variation with
\mathfrak{p}=2
from
[
5
]
. For a cube
J=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[d_{j},e_{j})
, we let
\Delta(f,J)
to be the alternating sum of
f
at vertices of
J
where adjacent vertices have opposite signs. In other words, if we use
(\boldsymbol{d}_{u},\boldsymbol{e}_{u^{c}})
to denote the
\boldsymbol{x}
with
x_{j}=d_{j}
if
j\in u
and
x_{j}=e_{j}
if
j\notin u
, then

\Delta(f,J)=\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}(-1)^{s-|u|}f\Big((\boldsymbol{d}_{u},\boldsymbol{e}_{u^{c}})\Big).

(10)

For
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
, the fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
is defined as

V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(f)=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Vol}(J)\Bigl|\frac{\Delta(f,J)}{\mathrm{Vol}(J)^{\lambda}}\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}

where
\sup_{\mathcal{P}}
is over all partitions of
[0,1)^{s}
into subcubes
J
of forms
\prod_{j=1}^{s}[a_{j},b_{j})
. For instance, a one-dimensional Brownian motion path over
[0,1]
almost surely has a finite variation of order
\lambda
for any
\lambda<1/2
because it is almost surely
\lambda-
Hölder continuous for
\lambda<1/2

[
9
]
.

We also need a notion of variation with respect to a margin. For this purpose, we define, for
u\subseteq 1{:}s
, the partial integral operator
I_{u}:L^{1}([0,1]^{s})\to L^{1}([0,1]^{|u|})
as

I_{u}(f)(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})=\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}};\boldsymbol{x}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}.

(11)

When
u=1{:}s
,
I_{1{:}s}
simply maps
f
to itself. We also define

D_{u^{c}}=\{\rho_{u^{c}}\mid\rho_{u^{c}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j\in u^{c}}\rho_{j}(x_{j}),\rho_{j}\in C([0,1]),\rho_{j}\geqslant 0,\|\rho_{j}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1\},

(12)

namely functions that are products of one-dimensional nonnegative continuous functions
\rho_{j}
in variable
x_{j}
with
\|\rho_{j}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
for
j\in u^{c}
. The fractional Vitali variation of order
\lambda
with respect to a nonempty subset
u\subseteq 1{:}s
is defined as

V^{u}_{\lambda}(f)=\sup_{\rho_{u^{c}}\in D_{u^{c}}}V^{(|u|)}_{\lambda}\Big(I_{u}(f\rho_{u^{c}})\Big).

(13)

When
u=1{:}s
, we conventionally define
V^{1{:}s}_{\lambda}(f)=V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(f)
. We note that because
\|\rho_{j}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
, for any cube
J\subseteq[0,1]^{|u|}
,

\displaystyle\Big|\Delta(I_{u}(f\rho_{u^{c}}),J)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\Delta(f(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}),J)\prod_{j\in u^{c}}\rho_{j}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\Big|\Delta(f(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}),J)\Big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}.

Hence

\displaystyle\Big(V^{(|u|)}_{\lambda}\Big(I_{u}(f\rho_{u^{c}})\Big)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Vol}(J)\Bigl|\frac{\Delta(I_{u}(f\rho_{u^{c}}),J)}{\mathrm{Vol}(J)^{\lambda}}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Vol}(J)\Bigl|\frac{\Delta(f(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}),J)}{\mathrm{Vol}(J)^{\lambda}}\Bigr|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\Bigl(V^{(|u|)}_{\lambda}\Bigl(f(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}})\Bigr)\Bigr)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}

where the last inequality follows by taking the supremum over
\mathcal{P}
inside the integral. Therefore, we know
V^{u}_{\lambda}(f)<\infty
if every section
f(\text{ }\cdot\text{ };\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}})
has a bounded fractional Vitali variation.

Finally for
f\in C^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}([0,1]^{s})
and
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
, we define

N_{\alpha,\lambda}(f)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq 1{:}s\\
u\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\Bigl(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{s}}\Bigl(V^{u}_{\lambda}(f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})})\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Our goal in this subsection is to show the RMSE of
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
has a
O(n^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/2+\epsilon})
convergence rate when
N_{\alpha,\lambda}(f)<\infty
. This rate is almost optimal because Theorem 5 of
[
14
]
says the optimal rate is of order
n^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/2}
for the Hölder class
F^{\alpha,\lambda}_{1}
, which is a subspace of functions with finite
N_{\alpha,\lambda}(f)
when
s=1
.

A crucial step in our proof is to bound
|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|
when
N_{\alpha,\lambda}(f)<\infty
. We are going to bound
|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|
differently according to the number of nonzeros in the binary representation of
\boldsymbol{k}
, so we introduce the following notations. For
k=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}a_{\ell}2^{\ell-1}
, we define the set of nonzero bits
\kappa=\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\mid a_{\ell}=1\}
. We will use
k
and
\kappa
interchangeably since one specifies the other. The number of nonzero bits of
k
is given by the cardinality of
\kappa
denoted as
|\kappa|
. We further use
\lceil\kappa\rceil_{q}
to denote the
q
’th largest element of
\kappa
and
\lceil\kappa\rceil_{1{:}q}
to denote the subset of
\kappa
containing the
q
largest elements if
q\leqslant|\kappa|
. We conventionally define
\lceil\kappa\rceil_{1{:}q}=\varnothing
if
q=0
and
\lceil\kappa\rceil_{q}=0
if
q>|\kappa|
. Finally we let
\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{q}=(\lceil\kappa_{1}\rceil_{q},\dots,\lceil\kappa_{s}\rceil_{q})
.

The following lemma relates
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
to the derivatives of
f
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{0}
and
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in C([0,1]^{s})
. If
|\kappa_{j}|\geqslant\alpha_{j}
for all
j\in 1{:}s
,

\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})=(-1)^{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\alpha_{j}}\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa_{j}\setminus\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha_{j}}}}(x_{j})W_{\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha_{j}}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

(14)

where

{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa}}(x)=(-1)^{\vec{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{x}}=\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}(-1)^{\vec{x}(\ell)}

with
\vec{x}(\ell)
denoting the
\ell
’th component of
\vec{x}
and
W_{\kappa}(x)
is defined recursively as

W_{\varnothing}(x)=1,

W_{\kappa}(x)=\int_{[0,1)}(-1)^{\vec{x}(\lfloor\kappa\rfloor)}W_{\kappa\setminus\lfloor\kappa\rfloor}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(15)

where
\lfloor\kappa\rfloor
denotes the smallest element of
\kappa
. In particular, for nonempty
\kappa
we have
W_{\kappa}(x)
is continuous, nonnegative, periodic with period
2^{-\lfloor\kappa\rfloor+1}
and

\int_{[0,1]}W_{\kappa}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{-\ell-1},

(16)

\max_{x\in[0,1]}W_{\kappa}(x)=2\prod_{\ell\in\kappa}2^{-\ell-1}.

(17)

Proof.

Equation (
14
) comes from Theorem 2.5 of
[
29
]
while properties of
W_{\kappa}(x)
is proven in Section 3 of
[
29
]
.
∎

Because we will often take the maximum and minimum over
j\in 1{:}s
, we will omit the argument
j\in 1{:}s
when it is clear from the context. For instance, we will denote the maximum and minimum of
|\kappa_{j}|
over
j\in 1{:}s
as
\max|\kappa_{j}|
and
\min|\kappa_{j}|
.

First we derive a crude bound on
|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|
that applies to all
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
f\in C^{(1,\dots,1)}([0,1]^{s})
. If
\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then

|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|\leqslant C_{f}2^{-\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1}}

where

C_{f}=\max\Bigl\|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}\Bigr\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{s})}<\infty.

Proof.

f\in C^{(1,\dots,1)}([0,1]^{s})
implies
\partial f/{\partial x_{j}}\in C([0,1]^{s})
for each
j\in 1{:}s
and hence
C_{f}<\infty
. Let
J
be any index
j
that maximizes
\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1}
. By applying Lemma
6
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})
where
\alpha_{j}=1
if
j=J
and
\alpha_{j}=0
if
j\neq J
, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{[0,1]^{s}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{J}}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa_{j}\setminus\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha_{j}}}}(x_{j})\Big)W_{\{\lceil\kappa_{J}\rceil_{1}\}}(x_{J})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(\max_{x\in[0,1]}W_{\{\lceil\kappa_{J}\rceil_{1}\}}(x)\Big)\int_{[0,1]^{s}}\Big|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{J}}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=2^{-\lceil\kappa_{J}\rceil_{1}}\Bigl\|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}\Bigr\|_{L^{1}([0,1]^{s})},

where we have used
|{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa}}(x)|=1
and equation (
17
). The conclusion follows once we maximize the bound over
j\in 1{:}s
.
∎

We need a finer bound when
\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. First we study the case when all components of
\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}
are positive.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
. Suppose
f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}\in C([0,1]^{s})
and
V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)})<\infty
. For
\boldsymbol{\ell}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
, we define

B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}=\boldsymbol{\ell}\}.

Then

\Bigl(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leqslant V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)})2^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}.

Proof.

Note that
\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}=\boldsymbol{\ell}
implies
\min|\kappa_{j}|>\alpha
because
\min\ell_{j}>0
. For simplicity, we denote
\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha}
as
\kappa^{+}_{j}
and
\kappa_{j}\setminus\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1{:}\alpha+1}
as
\kappa^{-}_{j}
. For each
\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}
,
\kappa_{j}
is the disjoint union of
\kappa^{+}_{j},\kappa^{-}_{j}
and
\ell_{j}
.
\kappa^{+}_{j}
comprises of
\alpha
distinct numbers larger than
\ell_{j}
.
\kappa^{-}_{j}
is an arbitrary subset of
1{:}\ell_{j}-1
, where
1{:}\ell_{j}-1=\varnothing
if
\ell_{j}=1
.

To simplify the proof, we let
g(\boldsymbol{x})=f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Equation (
14
) with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)
for
\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}
can be written as

\displaystyle|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\int_{[0,1]^{s}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(x_{j})(-1)^{\vec{x}_{j}(\ell_{j})}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl|\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1{:}s},\boldsymbol{a})}g(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(x_{j})(-1)^{\vec{x}_{j}(\ell_{j})}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Bigr|^{2}

where

\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1{:}s},\boldsymbol{a})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[\frac{a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}},\frac{a_{j}+1}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}}),

A=\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{s})\mid a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{<2^{\ell_{j}-1}}\}.

Because
{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(x_{j})
is constant over
x_{j}\in[\frac{a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}},\frac{a_{j}+1}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}})
and
W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})
has a period that divides
2^{-\ell_{j}}
, we can use
\Delta(f,J)
defined in equation (
10
) and write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1{:}s},\boldsymbol{a})}g(\boldsymbol{x})\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(x_{j})(-1)^{\vec{x}_{j}[\ell_{j}]}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(\frac{a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}})\Big)\underbrace{\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}_{f_{\boldsymbol{a}}}

(18)

where

\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[\frac{2a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}}},\frac{2a_{j}+1}{2^{\ell_{j}}}),

\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=\prod_{j=1}^{s}[x_{j},x_{j}+2^{-\ell_{j}}).

Next we notice that just as
\{\mathrm{wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mid\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\}
form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([0,1]^{s})
,

\Bigl\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(\frac{a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}})\Bigl|\kappa^{-}_{j}\subseteq 1{:}\ell_{j}-1,j\in 1{:}s\Bigr\}

form an orthonormal basis of the
|A|
-dimensional vector space indexed by
\boldsymbol{a}\in A
. Hence once we sum
|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}
over all possible
\kappa^{-}_{j}\subseteq 1{:}\ell_{j}-1,j\in 1{:}s
while fixing
\kappa^{+}_{j},j\in 1{:}s
, we have by equation (
18
) and Parseval’s identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}=\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}\Bigl|\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|A|}}\prod_{j=1}^{s}{\mathrm{wal}_{\kappa^{-}_{j}}}(\frac{a_{j}}{2^{\ell_{j}-1}})\Bigr)\sqrt{|A|}f_{\boldsymbol{a}}\Bigr|^{2}=|A|\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}|f_{\boldsymbol{a}}|^{2}.

(19)

Next, Hölder’s inequality implies

|f_{\boldsymbol{a}}|\leqslant\Big(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}|\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})|\Big)\Bigl(\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Bigr).

(20)

Using periodicity of
W_{\kappa}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\int_{[2a_{j}2^{-\ell_{j}},(2a_{j}+1)2^{-\ell_{j}})}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{s}2^{-\ell_{j}}\int_{[0,1)}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{s}2^{-\ell_{j}}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}2^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}

where we applied equation (
16
) in the last step. Putting
|A|=\prod_{j=1}^{s}2^{\ell_{j}-1},\mathrm{Vol}(J_{\ell})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}2^{-\ell_{j}}
, equation (
20
) and the above equation into equation (
19
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}2^{\ell_{j}-1}\Bigr)\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\Big(\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}|\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})|\Big)^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{s}4^{-\ell_{j}}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle=2^{-s}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}4^{-\lambda\ell_{j}}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\Bigr)\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}\mathrm{Vol}(J_{\ell})\Bigl(\frac{\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}{\mathrm{Vol}(J_{\ell})^{\lambda}}\Bigr)^{2}

For any
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})
, we know
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{a}}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\subseteq\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1{:}s},\boldsymbol{a})
and the set of cubes
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{a}}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mid\boldsymbol{a}\in A\}
are mutually disjoint, so there exists a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
[0,1)^{s}
into subcubes that includes
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{a}}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mid\boldsymbol{a}\in A\}
as a subset. Hence

\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\mathrm{Vol}(J_{\ell})\Bigl(\frac{\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{a}}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})}{\mathrm{Vol}(J_{\ell})^{\lambda}}\Bigr)^{2}\leqslant\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Vol}(J)\Bigl|\frac{\Delta(g,J)}{\mathrm{Vol}(J)^{\lambda}}\Bigr|^{2}\leqslant\Bigl(V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(g)\Bigr)^{2}.

Taking the supremum over
\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant 2^{-s}\Bigl(V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(g)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}4^{-\lambda\ell_{j}}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\Bigr).

When
\alpha=0
,
\kappa^{+}_{j}
is empty and we have proven our conclusion. When
\alpha>0
,
\kappa^{+}_{j}
can be any
\alpha
distinct numbers larger than
\ell_{j}
. After summing over all possible choice of
\kappa^{+}_{j}
, we get

\sum_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\leqslant\frac{1}{\alpha!}\Bigl(\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=\ell_{j}+1}^{\infty}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\Bigr)^{\alpha}=\frac{4^{-\alpha\ell_{j}}12^{-\alpha}}{\alpha!}

(21)

where the inequality holds because every permutation of
\kappa^{+}_{j}
is contained in the
\alpha
-fold product of all natural numbers larger than
\ell_{j}
. Summing over all possible choice of
\kappa^{+}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{+}_{s}
, we finally get

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{+}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{+}_{s}}\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{-s}\Bigl(V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(g)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}4^{-\lambda\ell_{j}}\frac{4^{-\alpha\ell_{j}}12^{-\alpha}}{\alpha!}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigl(V^{(s)}_{\lambda}(g)\Bigr)^{2}4^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}.

The proof is complete once we notice the sum over all possible
\kappa^{+}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{+}_{s}
and
\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}
is precisely the sum of
\boldsymbol{k}
over
B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}
.
∎

(23)

The proof is complete because

over all choice of

.

With

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma

Consider

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
N_{\alpha,\lambda}(f)<\infty
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
. When
r\geqslant m
, we have
\mathbb{E}((\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu)^{2})=O(n^{-2\alpha-2\lambda-1+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
2
, it suffices to show

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}=O(n^{-2\alpha-2\lambda-1+\epsilon}).

Lemma
1
and the definition of
K
imply

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K}\Pr(Z(\boldsymbol{k})=1)|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{-m+R}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\setminus K}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{-m+R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}>m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

(26)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2^{-m+R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}\leqslant m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\\
\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil>(\alpha+\lambda+1)m\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}.

(27)

We apply Corollary
1
to the sum in line (
26
) and get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}>m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}\Bigl(N_{\alpha,\lambda}(f)\Bigr)^{2}4^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}

By a similar argument used in equation (
9
), we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{s}\\
\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}>m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}4^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}=O(2^{-(2\alpha+2\lambda-\epsilon)m}).

Therefore when

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}>m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}=O(2^{-(2\alpha+2\lambda-\epsilon)m}).

Next we notice the sum in line (
27
) is empty if
\alpha=0
because
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{1}\|_{1}\geqslant\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil
. When
\alpha\geqslant 1
, we apply Lemma
7
and get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}\leqslant m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\\
\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil>(\alpha+\lambda+1)m\end{subarray}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant C_{f}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}\leqslant m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\\
\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil>(\alpha+\lambda+1)m\end{subarray}}4^{-\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1}}.

With
K_{T}
defined as in equation (
24
), we can apply the bound from equation (
25
) and get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}_{*}\\
\|\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}\|_{1}\leqslant m-(\alpha+1)s\log_{2}(m)\\
\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil>(\alpha+\lambda+1)m\end{subarray}}4^{-\max\lceil\kappa_{j}\rceil_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{T=\lfloor(\alpha+\lambda+1)m\rfloor+1}^{\infty}|K_{T}\setminus K_{T-1}|4^{-T}

Remark 1
.

Theorem
4
can be generalized to integrands with “weak derivatives”
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in L^{1}([0,1]^{s})
that fulfill equation (
14
). In particular,
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in C([0,1]^{s})
is assumed in Lemma
6
only to validate integration by part and derive equation (
14
), and Lemma
7
and Theorem
3
are both direct consequences of equation (
14
). A noticeable case where such generalization can always be done is when
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{0}
, for which equation (
14
) holds with
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}=f
because this is the definition of
\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})
. Subsection
4.1
gives a one-dimensional example where equation (
14
) holds despite a point of discontinuity in the derivative, and we see there the
O(n^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/2+\epsilon})
RMSE convergence rate holds despite
f^{(\alpha)}\notin C([0,1])
.

3.3
When
f
is in the Sobolev space of order
\alpha+\lambda

Sobolev spaces, especially reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, have been used extensively in the study of QMC
[
8
]
. In this subsection, we will show the RMSE of
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
has a
O(n^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/2+\epsilon})
convergence rate when the dominating mixed derivatives of
f
up to order
\alpha
belong to the fractional Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
with
p\geqslant 2
as defined below.

Following
[
10
]
, we say
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
with
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
and
p>\lambda^{-1}
if there exists a set of “fractional derivatives”
D^{\lambda}_{u}f,u\subseteq 1{:}s
such that
D^{\lambda}_{u}f\in L^{p}([0,1]^{|u|})
and

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\Gamma(\lambda)^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\prod_{j\in u}(x_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

(28)

where
\Gamma(\lambda)
is the Gamma function and
(x-t)_{+}=\max(x-t,0)
. When
u=\varnothing
,
D^{\lambda}_{\varnothing}f
is simply a real number with
L^{p}
-norm equal to its absolute value, and the integral over
[0,1]^{|\varnothing|}
evaluates to
D^{\lambda}_{\varnothing}f
. Lemma 7 of
[
10
]
shows
D^{\lambda}_{u}f
is unique in the
L_{p}
sense, so we will refer to
D^{\lambda}_{u}f
as the
\lambda
-fractional derivative of
f
in variables
u
. When
p\geqslant 2
, we further define the norm

\|f\|_{\lambda,s}=\Bigl(\sum_{u\subseteq 1{:}s}\|D^{\lambda}_{u}f\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{|u|})}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Notice when
\lambda=1
, this norm is equal to the usual Sobolev norm of order
1
anchored at
\boldsymbol{0}

[
4
]
.

Lemma 8 of
[
10
]
proves
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
is Hölder continuous with Hölder exponent
\lambda-p^{-1}
, so in particular
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
implies
f\in C([0,1]^{s})
. A slightly modified proof shows the fractional variation
V^{u}_{\lambda-p^{-1}}(f)
is also finite if
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
. Therefore, we can apply Theorem
4
and prove the
O(n^{-\alpha-\lambda-1/2+\epsilon})
RMSE convergence rate if
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
with
p=\infty
for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{s}
. We are going to prove the same rate holds under the weaker assumption
p\geqslant 2
.

We will use the norm

\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}=\Bigl(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{s}}\|f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\|^{2}_{\lambda,s}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

First, we present a lemma showing
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
can be embedded into
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda^{\prime},s,p^{\prime}}
with
\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda
but
p^{\prime}>p
. The proof technique is useful when we later prove Theorem
5
.

Lemma 9
.

For
1\leqslant p<p^{\prime}<\infty
and
\lambda\in(1/p-1/p^{\prime},1]
,
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
implies
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda^{\prime},s,p^{\prime}}
with any
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,\lambda-1/p+1/p^{\prime})
.

Proof.

By the beta integral identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(x-y)^{\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{+}(y-t)_{+}^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(x-t)^{\lambda-1}_{+}\int_{0}^{1}(1-y)^{\lambda^{\prime}-1}y^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=(x-t)^{\lambda-1}_{+}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda^{\prime})\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})}{\Gamma(\lambda)}.

Hence for any
u\subseteq 1{:}s

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda)^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\prod_{j\in u}(x_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda)^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\Big(\prod_{j\in u}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda)}{\Gamma(\lambda^{\prime})\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})}\int_{0}^{1}(x_{j}-y_{j})^{\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{+}(y_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\,\mathrm{d}y_{j}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda^{\prime})^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\Big(\prod_{j\in u}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})}(x_{j}-y_{j})^{\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{+}(y_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{u}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda^{\prime})^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u})\prod_{j\in u}(x_{j}-y_{j})^{\lambda^{\prime}-1}_{+}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}_{u}

where

D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u})=\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\prod_{j\in u}(y_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}.

(29)

To show
D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f
is the
\lambda^{\prime}
-fractional derivative of
f
in variables
u
, it remains to show
D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f\in L^{p^{\prime}}([0,1]^{|u|})
. First note that
\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{|u|}D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u})
is the convolution of
D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{t}_{u}\in[0,1]^{|u|}\}
with
(\prod_{j\in u}t_{j})^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{t}_{u}\in[0,1]^{|u|}\}
over
\mathbb{R}^{|u|}
when
\boldsymbol{y}_{u}
is restricted to
[0,1]^{|u|}
. Extending the domain of
D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f
to
\mathbb{R}^{|u|}
using the above convolution interpretation, we can apply Young’s convolution inequality
[
1
]
and get for
q=(1-1/p+1/p^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{|u|}\|D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}([0,1]^{|u|})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\Gamma(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})^{|u|}\|D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f\|_{L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{|u|})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|D^{\lambda}_{u}f(\boldsymbol{t}_{u})\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{t}_{u}\in[0,1]^{|u|}\}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{|u|})}\|(\prod_{j\in u}t_{j})^{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1}\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{t}_{u}\in[0,1]^{|u|}\}\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{|u|})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|D^{\lambda}_{u}f\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{|u|})}\Big(\prod_{j\in u}\int_{0}^{1}t^{q(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1)}_{j}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}\Big)^{1/q}.

Because
\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda-1/p+1/p^{\prime}
,
q(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1)>-q(1-1/p+1/p^{\prime})=-1
. Therefore,
t^{q(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}-1)}_{j}
is integrable over
[0,1]
and
\|D_{u}^{\lambda^{\prime}}f\|_{L^{q}([0,1]^{|u|})}<\infty
. Since this applies for all
u\subseteq 1{:}s
, we conclude
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda^{\prime},s,p^{\prime}}
.
∎

.

Suppose

for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{s}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
p\in[1,2)
and
\lambda\in(1/p-1/2,1]
. Then
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda^{\prime}}<\infty
with any
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,\lambda-1/p+1/2)
.

Applying Lemma
9
with
p^{\prime}=2
, we see

implies
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda^{\prime},s,2}
with any
\lambda^{\prime}\in(0,\lambda-1/p+1/2)
. Hence
\|f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\|_{\lambda^{\prime},s}<\infty
for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha}^{s}
and
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda^{\prime}}<\infty
.
∎

The above corollary shows we can focus our analysis on
p\geqslant 2
case. The next theorem is the counterpart of Theorem

.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
. Suppose

for
p\geqslant 2
.
Then for
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}

\Bigl(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leqslant C^{s}_{\lambda}\|D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{s})}2^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}

(30)

where
C_{\lambda}
is a constant defined in equation (
35
).

Proof.

We will use the notation from the proof of Theorem
3
. Again let
g(\boldsymbol{x})=f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}(\boldsymbol{x})
. First we note that Lemma
6
still applies here because
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
implies
g\in C([0,1]^{s})
. By equation (
18
) and (
19
),

\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}=|A|\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\Big|\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\prod_{j=1}^{s}W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big|^{2}.

(31)

Using equation (
17
) to bound
W_{\kappa^{+}_{j}}
and applying Hölder’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 4^{s}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\Bigr)|A|\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in A}\Big(\int_{\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})}|\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{s}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\Bigr)\int_{[0,1]^{s}}|\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\boldsymbol{1}\{x_{j}\in[0,1-2^{-\ell_{j}})\}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x},

(32)

where we have used
\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a})
are disjoint for
\boldsymbol{a}\in A
and
|A|\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell},2\boldsymbol{a}))=2^{-s}|A|\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{EI}(\boldsymbol{\ell}-\boldsymbol{1}_{1{:}s},\boldsymbol{a}))=2^{-s}
in the last inequality.

By equation (
10
) and (
28
), when

for all

,

with

Define

F_{\lambda,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{t})=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Big(\lambda(t_{j}+2^{-\ell_{j}})_{+}^{\lambda-1}-\lambda(t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-1}\Big).

Then
(\lambda\Gamma(\lambda))^{s}\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})
can be viewed as the convolution of
D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}g(\boldsymbol{t})\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{t}\in[0,1]^{s}\}
with
F_{\lambda,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{t})
over
\mathbb{R}^{s}
. Like in the proof of Lemma
9
, we extend the domain of
\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})
to
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
using this convolution interpretation and apply Young’s convolution inequality to get

\displaystyle(\lambda\Gamma(\lambda))^{2s}\int_{[0,1]^{s}}|\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{s}\boldsymbol{1}\{x_{j}\in[0,1-2^{-\ell_{j}})\}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|(\lambda\Gamma(\lambda))^{s}\Delta(g,\boldsymbol{x}+J_{\boldsymbol{\ell}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}g(\boldsymbol{t})\boldsymbol{1}\{\boldsymbol{t}\in[0,1]^{s}\}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}\|F_{\lambda,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{t})\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}g(\boldsymbol{t})\}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{s})}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}|F_{\lambda,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{t})|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}\Big)^{2}.

(33)

By the definition of
F_{\lambda,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{t})
, we can further write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}|F_{\lambda,\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\boldsymbol{t})|\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Big(\int_{-2^{-\ell_{j}}}^{0}\lambda(t_{j}+2^{-\ell_{j}})^{\lambda-1}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda t_{j}^{\lambda-1}-\lambda(t_{j}+2^{-\ell_{j}})^{\lambda-1}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Big(2^{-\lambda\ell_{j}}+\lim_{t_{j}\to\infty}t^{\lambda}_{j}-(t_{j}+2^{-\ell_{j}})^{\lambda}+2^{-\lambda\ell_{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}2^{s-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}&\text{ if }\lambda\in(0,1),\\
2^{-\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}&\text{ if }\lambda=1.\end{cases}

(34)

Putting equation (
33
) and (
34
) into equation (
32
), we get

\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant C^{2s}_{\lambda}\|D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{s})}4^{-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\kappa^{+}_{j}}4^{-\ell^{\prime}-1}\Bigr)

with

C_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}2\sqrt{2}\Gamma(\lambda+1)^{-1}&\text{ if }0<\lambda<1,\\
\sqrt{2}&\text{ if }\lambda=1.\end{cases}

(35)

Notice we have used
\Gamma(\lambda+1)=\lambda\Gamma(\lambda)
to simplify the expression.

Finally by equation (
21
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa^{+}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{+}_{s}}\sum_{\kappa^{-}_{1},\dots,\kappa^{-}_{s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant C^{2s}_{\lambda}\|D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{s})}4^{-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{s}\frac{4^{-\alpha\ell_{j}}12^{-\alpha}}{\alpha!}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant C^{2s}_{\lambda}\|D^{\lambda}_{1{:}s}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{s})}4^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}

which completes the proof since the left sum is equivalent to the sum of
\boldsymbol{k}
over
B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Suppose
\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}<\infty
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
0<\lambda\leqslant 1
. Then for
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}
and
B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{*}^{s}\mid\lceil\boldsymbol{k}\rceil_{\alpha+1}=\boldsymbol{\ell}\}

\Bigl(\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\leqslant C^{s}_{\lambda}\|f\|_{\alpha,\lambda}2^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}

where

is the constant defined in Theorem

.

Proof.

We again let
u=\{j\in 1{:}s\mid\ell_{j}>0\}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s})
with
\alpha_{j}=\alpha
if
j\in u
. By the proof of Corollary
1
with equation (
22
) replaced by

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}_{\kappa_{u^{c}}}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|^{2}\leqslant C^{2|u|}_{\lambda}\|D^{\lambda}_{u}g_{\kappa_{u^{c}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]^{|u|})}4^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}},

equation (
23
) becomes

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}\leqslant C^{2|u|}_{\lambda}4^{-(\alpha+\lambda)\|\boldsymbol{\ell}\|_{1}}\sum_{\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leqslant\alpha},j\in u^{c}}\|f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}\|^{2}_{\lambda,u}

(36)

where

\|g\|_{\lambda,u}=\sup_{\rho_{u^{c}}\in D_{u^{c}}}\|D^{\lambda}_{u}I_{u}(g\rho_{u^{c}})\|_{L^{2}([0,1]^{|u|})}

for
g\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
with
I_{u}
and
D_{u^{c}}
defined in equation (
11
) and (
12
). By equation (
28
),

\displaystyle I_{u}(g\rho_{u^{c}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\Big(\sum_{v\subseteq 1{:}s}\Gamma(\lambda)^{-|v|}\int_{[0,1]^{|v|}}D^{\lambda}_{v}g(\boldsymbol{t}_{v})\prod_{j\in v}(x_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{v}\Big)\prod_{j\in u^{c}}\rho_{j}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w^{\prime}\subseteq u^{c}}\Gamma(\lambda)^{-|w^{\prime}|}\int_{[0,1]^{|w^{\prime}|}}D^{\lambda}_{u\cup w^{\prime}}g(\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{x}_{u})

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\prod_{j\in w^{\prime}}(x_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-1}\prod_{j\in u^{c}}\rho_{j}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}}.

Using

, we get the bound

\displaystyle|D^{\lambda}_{u}I_{u}(g\rho_{u^{c}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})|

\displaystyle\sum_{w^{\prime}\subseteq u^{c}}\Gamma(\lambda)^{-|w^{\prime}|}\int_{[0,1]^{|w^{\prime}|}}|D^{\lambda}_{u\cup w^{\prime}}g(\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{x}_{u})|\int_{[0,1]^{s-|u|}}\prod_{j\in w^{\prime}}(x_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{\lambda-1}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{w^{\prime}\subseteq u^{c}}(\lambda\Gamma(\lambda))^{-|w^{\prime}|}\int_{[0,1]^{|w^{\prime}|}}|D^{\lambda}_{u\cup w^{\prime}}g(\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{x}_{u})|\prod_{j\in w^{\prime}}(1-t_{j})^{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{w^{\prime}\subseteq u^{c}}(\lambda\Gamma(\lambda))^{-|w^{\prime}|}\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{|w^{\prime}|}}|D^{\lambda}_{u\cup w^{\prime}}g(\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{x}_{u})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\int_{[0,1]^{|w^{\prime}|}}\prod_{j\in w^{\prime}}(1-t_{j})^{2\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle|D^{\lambda}_{u}I_{u}(g\rho_{u^{c}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})|^{2}

\displaystyle(1+\Gamma(\lambda+1)^{-2}(2\lambda+1)^{-1})^{|u^{c}|}\sum_{w^{\prime}\subseteq u^{c}}\int_{[0,1]^{|u|}}\int_{[0,1]^{|w^{\prime}|}}|D^{\lambda}_{u\cup w^{\prime}}g(\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{x}_{u})|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{w^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u}

\displaystyle(1+\Gamma(\lambda+1)^{-2}(2\lambda+1)^{-1})^{|u^{c}|}\sum_{w^{\prime}\subseteq u^{c}}\|D^{\lambda}_{u\cup w^{\prime}}g\|^{2}_{L^{2}[0,1]^{|u\cup w^{\prime}|}}

\displaystyle(1+\Gamma(\lambda+1)^{-2}(2\lambda+1)^{-1})^{|u^{c}|}\|g\|^{2}_{\lambda,s}.

Putting the above bound into equation (

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in B_{\alpha,\boldsymbol{\ell},s}}|\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{k})|^{2}

where we have used the fact that
\Gamma(\lambda+1)\leqslant 1
for

and hence

\displaystyle\geqslant 2\Gamma(\lambda+1)^{-2}>1+\Gamma(\lambda+1)^{-2}(2\lambda+1)^{-1}.\qed

Remark 2
.

Our results here can be used to analyze
f
satisfying Owen’s boundary growth condition
[
23
]
, which assumes for all
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\{0,1\}^{s}
,
f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}
is continuous over the open box
(0,1)^{s}
and

|f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha})}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leqslant B\prod_{j=1}^{s}\phi(x_{j})^{-A^{*}-\alpha_{j}}

(37)

with
\phi(x)=\min(x,1-x)
,

. To simplify our notation, we will write
\partial^{u}f

f^{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{u})}

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{u}_{j}=1

j\in u
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{u}_{j}=0
if
j\notin u
.

To apply our results, first we change the anchor point to the box center
(1/2,\dots,1/2)
and analyze each of the
2^{s}

[1/2,1)^{s}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{s}
we define

\psi(\boldsymbol{y})=\Big(\frac{1+y_{1}}{2},\dots,\frac{1+y_{s}}{2}\Big)

. Clearly
\partial^{u}f_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{y})
can be defined over
\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{s}
using the chain rule. In order to show
f_{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda^{*},s,2}
with
\lambda^{*}\in(0,1/2-A^{*})
, we use equation (
29
) with
\lambda=1
and
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda^{*}
to define for
u\subseteq 1{:}s

D_{u}^{\lambda^{*}}f_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{y}_{u})=\Gamma(1-\lambda^{*})^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1)^{|u|}}\partial^{u}f_{\psi}\Big((\boldsymbol{t}_{u},\boldsymbol{0}_{u^{c}})\Big)\prod_{j\in u}(y_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{-\lambda^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

where
(\boldsymbol{t}_{u},\boldsymbol{0}_{u^{c}})
is the
s
-dimensional vector whose
j
-th component equals
t_{j}
if
j\in u
and equals zero otherwise. Notice that although
\partial^{u}f_{\psi}
might not belong to
L^{1}([0,1]^{s})
due to its boundary growth, it is continuous over
[0,1)^{s}
and a calculation similar to what we did in Lemma
9
shows equation (
28
) is satisfied for
f_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{s}
with the above
D_{u}^{\lambda^{*}}f_{\psi},u\subseteq 1{:}s
. To see
D_{u}^{\lambda^{*}}f_{\psi}
has a finite
L^{2}-
norm, we let
\delta=1/2-A^{*}-\lambda^{*}
and use equation (
37
) and the chain rule to bound

\displaystyle|D_{u}^{\lambda^{*}}f_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{y}_{u})|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\Gamma(1-\lambda^{*})^{-|u|}\int_{[0,1)^{|u|}}\Big(\frac{B}{2^{|u|-(s-|u|)A^{*}}}\prod_{j\in u}\Big(\frac{1-t_{j}}{2}\Big)^{-A^{*}-1}\Big)\prod_{j\in u}(y_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{-\lambda^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{sA^{*}}B}{\Gamma(1-\lambda^{*})^{|u|}}\int_{[0,1)^{|u|}}\prod_{j\in u}(1-t_{j})^{-A^{*}-1}(y_{j}-t_{j})_{+}^{-1/2+A^{*}+\delta}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}_{u}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{2^{sA^{*}}B}{\Gamma(1-\lambda^{*})^{|u|}}\Big(\prod_{j\in u}(1-y_{j})^{-1/2+\delta/2}\int_{0}^{y_{j}}(y_{j}-t_{j})^{-1+\delta/2}\,\mathrm{d}t_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{2^{sA^{*}+|u|}B}{(\delta\Gamma(1-\lambda^{*}))^{|u|}}\Big(\prod_{j\in u}(1-y_{j})\Big)^{-1/2+\delta/2},

where we have used
\delta>0
because
\lambda^{*}<1/2-A^{*}
. Hence,
|D_{u}^{\lambda^{*}}f_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{y}_{u})|^{2}
is integrable over
[0,1]^{|u|}
and we prove
f_{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\lambda^{*},s,2}
. After analyzing all of the
2^{s}
orthants in a similar manner, we can establish a counterpart of Theorem
5
with
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
, which eventually leads to
\mathbb{E}((\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}-\mu)^{2})=O(n^{-2+2A^{*}+\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
. We do not elaborate out the details here given a similar analysis has been done in
[
19
]
. It is also straightforward to generalize our analysis to the
\alpha\geqslant 1
case with
f^{(\alpha,\dots,\alpha)}
satisfying the Owen’s boundary growth condition.

As we have seen above, it is not always trivial to determine the right function space to analyze the integrand. Practical integrands may be too complicated to analyze or even be defined only in black boxes. 5
Discussion

What we have studied in Section
3
is far from a complete list of interesting function spaces people have used for the analysis of QMC algorithms. For instance,
[
10
]
studies the Haar wavelet spaces, Bessel potential spaces and Besov spaces of mixed dominated smoothness, all of which include the fractional Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}_{\lambda,s,p}
as a subspace. One may ask whether there exists a universal proof that shows the median
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
attains almost the optimal convergence rate for well-conditioned notions of smoothness. It is not clear how this could be done, but it can save our efforts to analyze each smoothness condition individually as we have done in Section
3
if such proof exists.

Our analysis is restricted to base-2 digital nets, but the idea should be applicable to digital nets of other prime basis. In particular,
[
6
]
proves Lemma
3
in any prime basis and
[
29
]
has a version of Lemma
6
applicable to odd prime basis. A complete analysis is beyond the scope of this paper and we will leave it for future research.

A more serious limitation of our analysis is the curse of dimensionality. For example, the bound in equation (
32
) contains a constant that grows exponentially with the dimension
s
. Another example is when we bound
(N+\alpha s-1)^{\alpha s-1}
by
4^{\epsilon N}
for all
N\geqslant\lceil m-\alpha s\log_{2}(m)\rceil
in the proof of Theorem
2
. The minimal
m
for the inequality to hold grows super-linearly in
s
, so the required sample size
n=2^{m}
grows super-exponentially in
s
. One way to solve this issue is to focus on a finite-sample analysis and study functions with low effective dimensions as introduced in
[
2
]
. See Section 5 of
[
27
]
for an example of this approach when the integrand is analytic over
[0,1]^{s}
. Another solution is to introduce the weighted Sobolev space. A recent paper
[
13
]
uses this idea and gives many dimension-independent bounds on the worst-case error of the median of QMC estimators. Both are interesting directions to explore and we will save it for future research.

Another big topic that deserves further study is how to estimate the error of our median estimate in a reliable way, especially given the bias of
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
. To simplify the discussion, we again assume the precision
E=\infty
. There have been lots of efforts to construct reliable confidence intervals using randomized QMC
[
24
]
. In our notation, the standard approach is to generate
r
randomized
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
with the random linear scrambling and compute a
t
-interval based on a normal approximation heuristic. A recent work
[
11
]
applies robust estimation techniques to
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
and establishes reliable finite-sample bounds for
\mu
. However, when applying the above methods to function spaces with smoothness parameter
\alpha+\lambda>1
, the resulting confidence intervals tend to be too wide because their lengths are proportional to the standard deviation of
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
, which only converges at
n^{-3/2}
rate due to the influence of outliers
[
25
]
. This phenomenon is also observed in numerical experiments
[
17
]
. A remedy is to reduce the variance by applying robust estimation techniques in
[
11
]
to higher order scrambled digital net estimates instead, but the problem is again we need a priori knowledge on the function smoothness to choose the order properly.

Ideally, we want to construct confidence intervals that automatically adapt to the function smoothness without a priori knowledge. One possibility is to choose suitable sample quantiles as lower and upper bounds for
\mu
. Just like our median estimate
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
, any
q
-sample quantile of
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
converges to
\mu
at almost the optimal RMSE rate provided
q\in(0,1)
and
r\geqslant m
. Nevertheless, the target coverage level is only guaranteed in an asymptotic sense, and even that requires both
\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}<\mu)
and
\Pr(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}>\mu)
to be bounded away from zero as
m\to\infty
, which is not easy to prove. In a personal communication, Pierre L’Ecuyer proposed a two-stage adaptive procedure. In the first stage, we generate
r
randomized
\hat{\mu}_{\infty}
using either completely random designs or the random linear scrambling. We then find which realization gives the sample median
\hat{\mu}^{(r)}_{\infty}
and record the generating matrices
C_{j}
used by that realization. In the second stage, we generate a new set of randomized
\hat{\mu}^{\prime}_{\infty}
by fixing the generating matrices
C_{j}
to be those recorded in the first stage and only randomizing the digital shifts
\vec{D}_{j}
by filling them with independent
\mathbb{U}\{0,1\}
entries. One can then construct finite-sample bounds from
\hat{\mu}^{\prime}_{\infty}
by applying techniques in
[
11
]
. Intuitively, this method should work well because we expect the variance of
\hat{\mu}^{\prime}_{\infty}
to be comparable to the
\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\infty}\!\mid\!\mathcal{A}^{c})
studied in Lemma
2
, which we have shown to decrease at almost an optimal rate. How to coin this intuition into a rigorous theory is an open question leaving for future research.
