Percutaneous Coil Embolization of an Anomalous Coronary Artery–Pulmonary Artery Fistula with Aneurysmal Segment
Authors: Claudiu Mihai Ciuciureanu, Ricardo Orlando Escarcega, Stefan Milutinovic, Richard A Chazal, Kamaldeep Singh
Published: 2026

Coronary artery fistulae are rare anomalies, and those with a coronary-to-pulmonary artery communication and aneurysmal segment are a recognized subset. Affected patients carry risk for coronary steal, thrombosis, rupture, and external compression.1

A 66-year-old man underwent coronary CT angiography for anginal chest pain, which revealed an incidental collateral vessel from the proximal right coronary artery with an aneurysmal dilation measuring 11 mm (Figure 1).

Coronary angiography confirmed a fistula from an atrial branch to the pulmonary artery with a sizable aneurysmal segment. Following multidisciplinary discussion with cardiothoracic surgery and interventional radiology, coil embolization was selected given minimal distal myocardial perfusion, and the advantage of quicker recovery. Four coils were densely packed within the aneurysm, distal vessel, aneurysmal ostium, and proximal segment. Final angiography demonstrated complete occlusion without complications, and normal right coronary artery opacification without flow in the fistulous tract (Supplementary Video 1).

Coronary artery fistulae are reported in roughly 0.05% of angiographic populations, with pulmonary artery termination representing an even less common subset.1 Typical anatomic patterns most often originate from the left coronary system and terminate in the main pulmonary artery, and rupture of such aneurysmal fistulae has been well described.2 Presentations range from incidental discovery to chest pain from coronary steal. In a series of 29 patients undergoing percutaneous closure, chest pain was the most common presenting symptom.3 Known hazards include progressive dilation, thrombosis, aneurysmal rupture, and mediastinal compression.1–3 Transcatheter closure achieves high immediate occlusion rates in appropriately selected patients, though late recanalization warrants follow-up imaging.1,3
