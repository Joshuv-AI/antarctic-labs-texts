Historic and future spending estimates of maternal health and family planning in 139 low- and middle-income countries, 2000–2030: a modelling study
Authors: Angela E Apeagyei, Hans Elliott, Golsum Tsakalos, Joseph L Dieleman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhile estimates of donor-provided resources for maternal health and family planning are readily available, government and household spending on these activities is limited. This study aims to generate comprehensive estimates of spending on maternal health and family planning for 139 low- and middle-income countries.MethodsData sources used to generate the spending estimates include data from WHO National Health Accounts (NHA), Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation’s Financing Global Health database, TRACK20’s family planning spending assessment and Family Planning (FP) 2030’s annual reports. We used spatio-temporal Gaussian regression modelling techniques to generate a complete series of historic estimates. For estimates of future spending, we focused solely on expected donor spending and based estimates on total development assistance for health that accounted for announced cuts in aid.ResultsIn 2022, total spending on maternal health and family planning across 139 low- and middle-income countries was $35.6 billion (95% uncertainty interval of US$31.0–$41.0 billion) and US$14.1 billion (US$12.4–$17.2 billion), of which $10.7 billion (US$9.0–$12.8 billion) and US$8.8 billion (US$8.8–8.9 billion) was spending in China. By 2030, total donor funding provided for maternal health across the countries included in this study is projected to reach $505.0 million (compared with US$470.4 million in 2000), while total donor funding for family planning is projected to be $349.2 million (compared with US$929.4 million in 2000).ConclusionAid cuts announced in 2025 will likely exacerbate the gap between available funding and need unless national governments of impacted countries proactively respond.

Introduction

The year 2024 marked 5 years since the Nairobi Summit celebrated the 25th anniversary of the first International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD).1 To celebrate this milestone, the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), in partnership with the Kenyan and Danish governments, organised a high-level conference.2 The conference brought together stakeholders to deliberate on specific actions needed to invigorate the implementation of the Programme of Action of the ICPD. In line with UNFPA’s vision of attaining universal access to sexual and reproductive health rights globally, the conference highlighted the resource needs and funding gaps related to the achievement of UNFPA’s three transformative results.24 These three transformative results are: (1) ending preventable maternal deaths; (2) ending the unmet need for family planning and (3) ending gender-based violence and all harmful practices, including female genital mutilation and child, early and forced marriage.

Globally, 148 per 100 000 women die annually during pregnancy, and on average 35% of women have family planning needs that are unmet.5 6 The provision of comprehensive reproductive and maternal health services in health systems is an important component of ensuring that citizens have universal healthcare coverage.7 In many countries, however, such comprehensive provision of reproductive and maternal health services is lacking.8 While there are many reasons for this deficit, limited availability of financial resources is key, and understanding how much is being spent is a critical first input for countries to begin to address the shortages in service availability and provision. Furthermore, recent cuts to aid by major donors present important ramifications for the availability of resources for these health areas in the near future.

While estimates of donor contributions towards maternal health and family planning are readily available, estimates of domestic spending on these activities are limited.912 The UNFPA, through a partnership with the Netherlands Interdisciplinary Demographic Institute (NIDI), reported domestic and donor spending on population activities starting in 1997. However, the domestic component was less precise, and the specific reproductive health-related project ended in 2020.13 Recent resource tracking efforts, including those from Track 20, Performance Monitoring and Accountability, and Reproductive Health Supplies Coalition, have focused on tracking spending on family planning.1416 To date, while some estimates exist for select countries, no comparable or comprehensive estimates of spending on maternal health and/or family planning exist for a large set of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The objective of this study is to fill this gap by generating estimates of total spending on maternal health and family planning for 139 LMICs. Furthermore, the study disaggregates domestic (government spending and out-of-pocket from 2000 till 2022) and donor funding (till 2030) in order to estimate the availability of donor funds in the near future.

Methods

Overview

Using a modelled descriptive study design, we estimated annual spending for maternal health and family planning for 139 countries (LMICs tracked by the Global Burden of Disease study with the exception of Montenegro) disaggregated by government, donor and out-of-pocket spending from 2000 through 2022 and developed projections of donor contributions through 2030. We defined maternal health services to include antenatal and postnatal care, delivery care, management of abortion complications and family planning to include prevention and treatment of infertility and other fertility regulation services. These definitions are similar to and guided by what is outlined in the NHA User Guide for producing Reproductive Health Accounts.17 We aggregated financing source-specific estimates to obtain the total spending. We report our final estimates in constant 2023 US dollars and report the uncertainty intervals associated with all modelled estimates except for the development assistance estimates, since the retrospective data are not modelled, and the forecasting methods do not estimate uncertainty. All analyses were completed in R (version V.4.4.2) and Stata (V.15).

Estimating spending on maternal health and family planning, 2000–2022

Government spending

For family planning, we extracted data from the UNFPA/NIDI Resource Flows project from 2007 to 2019, NHA from 2010 through 2022 and Track20 Family Planning Spending Assessments for 2014 to 2022.13 18 19 For maternal health, we relied on data from NHA and the UNFPA/NIDI Financial Resource Flows project. In total, we had 427 and 880 country-years of data that informed our maternal health and family planning government spending estimates, respectively.

To generate the historic estimates, we completed two steps. First, we standardised the data across the various data sources by completing a currency conversion from national local units to 2023 US dollars. Additionally, after comparing data collection methodologies across the three sources, we decided to exclude capital formation expenditures from the extracted government spending values whenever possible. This helped to ensure that the sources were measuring the same type of government spending—namely, recurrent expenditures on maternal health or family planning. Subsequently, to generate a fully complete series of estimates for each country-year of interest, we used spatio-temporal Gaussian process regression (ST-GPR). ST-GPR is an analytical method that uses data where available and leverages location, time and relevant covariate information to generate estimates for country-years for which data is not available or observed.6 20 As an outcome variable, we used the proportion of total government health spending that went to maternal health or family planning. This proportion (necessarily between 0 and 1) was logit-transformed, and thus the modelling process occurred in logit space.

Out-of-pocket spending

We used a price-volume approach to generate the estimates of out-of-pocket spending on maternal health and family planning. From the Reproductive Health Supply Coalition Commodity Gap Analysis/Landscape and Projection of Reproductive Health Supply Needs reports, we extracted cost-per-user data for family planning commodities in the private sector. Furthermore, from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), we extracted data to calculate the utilisation rates across the various types of modern contraception methods in the private sector. We used an ST-GPR model to fill in the missing utilisation data and a linear model to generate country-specific cost data. These two series (cost per user and utilisation in the private sector) were then multiplied to get estimates of out-of-pocket spending on each contraceptive method, and we took the sum of spending across all methods as total spending on family planning from out-of-pocket.

Similarly, for out-of-pocket spending on maternal health, we used a similar price-volume approach based on service utilisation and cost of services in the private sector. We, however, adapted this approach to leverage the limited available data. We extracted utilisation data from the DHS and the Global Burden of Disease study for antenatal care, postnatal care and delivery care. We also completed a scoping literature review to estimate the costs of antenatal, postnatal and delivery care across the countries of interest. The utilisation and cost data for each subcategory were then multiplied to get spending per year for each subcategory. We then summed across all categories—antenatal care, public delivery, private delivery, home delivery, public c-section, private c-section, postnatal care—to get the total estimate for the country-year of out-of-pocket spending on maternal health.

Additional details on the approach are provided in the online supplemental methods appendix.

Development assistance

We leveraged development assistance estimates of family planning and maternal health from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) Financing Global Health Development Assistance for Health (DAH) database.21 The database is based on project-level data and financial reports of development assistance contributions provided through the major international development agencies. The database reported donor support from 1990 through 2025, with recipient country estimates available through 2024. The estimates used in this study excluded spending on administrative expenses and global projects to capture spending at the country level. Detailed explanation of how these estimates are generated are published elsewhere.12 22 23

The forecasts for maternal health and family planning development assistance followed a multistep approach. First, estimates of donor-level forecasts of DAH generated internally at IHME and the historic estimates of development assistance for health described in the paragraph above were extracted from relevant databases. The estimates of donor-level forecasts were generated based on official development assistance as a share of national income (ODA per GNI) ratio policies for countries for which this was applicable and linear models for all others. Second, the estimates of donor-level forecasts were disaggregated into forecasts of DAH by disbursing entity. This was done by calculating the historic average fraction of DAH from each donor that goes to each disbursing entity and applying this fraction to the 2026 through 2030 donor-level estimates. Third, the forecasted estimates of DAH by disbursing entity are then updated using evidence collected from official government budgets, other financial documents and news articles.2427 This information is used to estimate future trajectories of DAH from 2026 through 2030. Fourth, for each disbursing entity other than the US bilateral agencies, we calculated the average fraction of annual DAH that was disbursed to each health focus area in the database and assumed this fraction remained constant from 2026 to 2030. These fractions were applied to the disbursing entity totals for 2026 to 2030 to obtain forecasted estimates by health focus area. Lastly, we calculated average recipient country fractions for each disbursing entity and health focus area pair and assumed they held constant from 2025 to 2030. We applied these fractions to the disbursing entity-health focus forecasts to generate recipient country estimates.

For the US bilateral agencies, we used additional publicly available information to further adjust the forecasts. We used data on the outlays associated with the Global Health Programmes federal account from usaspending.gov to estimate the drop in total US bilateral DAH in 2025. We then use detailed disbursement data from the International Aid and Transparency Initiative reported by United States Agency for International Development and the Department of State to estimate the distribution of US bilateral global health funding across programme areas and recipient countries.28 Lastly, we used data from the Department of State’s fiscal year 2026 appropriations and information collected from the US government’s 2026–2030 Memorandums of Understanding to estimate the trajectory of US bilateral DAH and its distribution across health focus areas and recipients.29 30

Results

The spending on maternal health and family planning varied across countries. Across all LMICs included in this study, a total of US$35.6 billion (95% uncertainty interval of US$31.0–$41.0 billion) and US$14.1 billion (US$12.4–$17.2 billion) was spent on maternal health and family planning, respectively, in 2022, with US$$21.51 and $8.50 per woman of reproductive age in spending on average. In 2022, this spending level made up 1.8% (1.5%–2.0%) and 0.7% (0.6%–0.9%) of total health spending across these countries. The per woman of reproductive age spending on both areas rose as country income group increased. For maternal health, average per woman of reproductive age spending across low-income groups was US$6.10, while average per woman of reproductive age spending across middle-income groups was US$23.16. Similarly, low-income groups spent US$2.63 per person on family planning on average, whereas middle-income country groups spent US$9.15 on average. Per woman of reproductive age, spending on maternal health ranged from a high of US$67.41 (US$37.97–$115.87) in Panama to a low of US$0.63 (US$0.58–$0.68) in Eritrea. Likewise, family planning spending per woman of reproductive age ranged between US$52.95 (US$47.75–$63.28) in Palau (driven by donor spending) to a low of US$0.54 (US$0.47–$0.62) in Serbia. The range in spending per woman of reproductive age highlights the spread in available resources targeting these two key health areas across the countries included in this study. The top recipient countries in 2022 based on total DAH for maternal health were Afghanistan, Democratic Republic of the Congo and Tanzania. For family planning, the top recipient countries were Nigeria, Ethiopia and Uganda. There are distinct patterns in the magnitude and sources of spending across the two health areas. Figure 1a presents spending on maternal health and family planning by source from 2000 through 2022. Excluding China, households were the primary source of maternal health spending across countries until 2017. However, since 2017, governments have become the main source of funding, providing more than 48% of funding for maternal health products and activities. Similarly, the primary source of spending for family planning across these countries has also changed over time, with households being the primary source of funding until 2011, when governments became the main source of funding for family planning services and products. In terms of order of magnitude, total maternal health spending excluding China is over four times (US$24.9 billion (US$21.5–$28.6 billion) in 2022) the total spending on family planning excluding China (US$5.2 billion (US$3.6–$8.4 billion) in 2022). In China, households have consistently been the primary source of maternal health spending, and the government has consistently been the primary source of family planning spending (figure 1b). In 2022, maternal health spending in China was only 1.2 times (US$10.7 billion (US$9.0–$12.8 billion)) the total spending on family planning (US$8.8 billion (US$8.8–$8.9 billion)), and as recently as 2019 family planning spending was higher than maternal health spending.

Across sub-Saharan Africa, 11 countries, including Malawi, South Sudan and Somalia, receive <5 percent of their funding for maternal health services from their governments. For family planning, across 57 countries, the share of total family planning spending from the government is <20%, with 26 of these countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Discussion

This study focused on generating estimates of spending on maternal health and family planning. In addition to total spending, we generated disaggregated spending by source—government, donor and out-of-pocket spending. Domestic spending estimates of maternal health and family planning spanned 2000 through 2022. Considering recent aid cuts, estimates of donor funding extend till 2030. A key finding is that the source of funding for the two health areas is closely aligned with the income level of countries. Specifically, low-income countries, mostly in Africa, primarily financed their maternal health through out-of-pocket and development assistance. In Asia and Latin America, with more middle-income countries, there were more countries with governments as the primary financiers of maternal health services. While the overall patterns may be similar, for family planning, there were across all income groups more countries within which governments provided more of the spending. Out-of-pocket spending was also relatively low, while development assistance dominated, especially in sub-Saharan Africa. Near-term forecasts for both health areas show a decline in donor spending to levels comparable to 2002 after a dramatic drop in 2025.

The Adding It Up Study is an ongoing study that estimates the need for, impact of and cost of providing access to essential sexual and reproductive health services to women of reproductive age in 132 LMICs. The study estimates that it will cost $68.8 billion annually to provide these services.31 Our estimates show that for the women of reproductive age living in the 139 LMICs included annually, a total of $49.3 billion ($44.8 - $54.9 billion) was spent on maternal health and family planning. This level of spending is only 71.7% (65.0%–79.9%) of what was estimated to be needed in 2019. This suggests a large shortfall in terms of availability of funding to provide the reproductive health services needed.

These findings have ramifications for the achievement of global access to reproductive health services advocated for by the ICPD programme of work and Sustainable Development Goals.32 33 First, as it pertains to maternal health, the findings suggest that in low-income countries, funding for maternal health services is reliant on households and external sources and less so on governments, which is concerning. Existing research has shown that the cost of seeking care, especially related to items like childbirth, can impoverish families, and as evidenced by recent aid cuts, reliance on external sources creates vulnerabilities for health systems.3436 Second, while governments seemed to support family planning in more countries than maternal health, family planning was also more reliant on external sources of funding in more countries, especially in Africa. Therefore, systematic efforts will be needed to ensure that the most vulnerable groups are not left behind. Such systematic efforts could include actively seeking out innovative ways of bridging gaps and integrating reproductive health service provision with well-established HIV care programmes.37

Furthermore, the exercise of generating these estimates has highlighted the opportunities for domestic resource mobilisation. While this strategy may not be prudent in all countries, in some countries the opportunity exists and could be used to mobilise additional sources of funding in support of the national maternal and reproductive health goals. Similarly, in some settings where development assistance has been the dominant funding source, domestic funding may prove critical for ensuring access to maternal and reproductive health commodities and services for all. In a few countries, such as Argentina, Azerbaijan, Chile and Colombia, household spending on both maternal health and family planning is quite high. This has implications for the availability of financial protection for the populace and the ability to achieve universal health coverage.

The study has several limitations. Most critically, there was generally limited observed data to input into the study. Except for government spending on family planning, for which there were more observed data points, the other estimates relied on modelling and a bottom-up estimation approach. The methods we use are the best approaches, given the availability and constraints associated with the data. Furthermore, there were some assumptions made during analyses due to data limitations. These include presuming a proportional relationship between total spending and capital formation spending in the data extracted from the Reproductive Health subaccounts for instances where capital spending data were not available (in order to remove capital spending from government spending), assuming that recurrent expenditures recorded with zero were due to a lack of data as opposed to actual values of zero and for out-of-pocket spending on family planning, we held prices used as constant over time. While these assumptions were made to facilitate the filling of missingness in the data, we acknowledge that in some instances, they may result in underestimating values. Additionally, the comparison of the costs of needed services and the available funding is not perfect in terms of the populations covered. While the need analyses covered 132 LMICs, the spending analyses covered 139 countries. Nonetheless, it still provides insight into the magnitude of the shortfall in funding required. Notably, the estimates of future availability of donor funds are preliminary and were developed in a still evolving context that is subject to change as data becomes more available. We acknowledge that these forecasts are one of the possible outcome scenarios that is dependent on currently available information, and the final donor funds contributed might vary substantially.

Conclusion

Whereas much progress has been made since the initial convening of the ICPD, a lot remains to be done to ensure that no one is left behind in the delivery of services for sexual and reproductive health. Recent aid cuts suggest that near-term future availability of funds for maternal health and family planning services will plateau at historically low levels. This projected future scenario calls for an intentional reassessment of how available funds are to be used going forward and a concerted effort to ensure that more health can be achieved with the limited funds that are likely to be available.
