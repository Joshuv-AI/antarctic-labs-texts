. Let q > 1 q>1 , ( 1 − 1 q ) ​ a ≥ 1 (1-\frac{1}{q})a\geq 1 and let Ω ⊂ ℝ 2 \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} be Lipschitz domain. We show that planar mappings in the second order Sobolev space f ∈ W 2 , q ​ ( Ω , ℝ 2 ) f\in W^{2,q}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2}) with | J f | − a ∈ L 1 ​ ( Ω ) |J_{f}|^{-a}\in L^{1}(\Omega) are homeomorphism if they agree with a homeomorphism on the boundary. 1.
Introduction

We study models of Nonlinear Elasticity that involve the second gradient, and in particular, we would like to study injectivity of such mappings which corresponds to the physical assumption of “noninterpenetration of the matter”.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain and let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In this paper we study mappings with finite energy (with some proper convex functions
W
and
\Psi
)

E(f):=\int_{\Omega}\bigl(W(Df(x))+\Psi(D^{2}f(x))\bigr)\;dx

such that there are
q\geq 1
and
a>0
so that

(1.1)

W(Df)\geq\frac{1}{|J_{f}|^{a}}\text{ and }\Psi(D^{2}f)\geq|D^{2}f|^{q},

i.e.
f\in W^{2,q}
and
J_{f}^{-a}\in L^{1}
. Moreover, we assume that the mapping
f
is equal to a given homeomorphism
f_{0}
on
\partial\Omega
so we prescribe our deformation there.
Models with the second gradient were introduced by Toupin
[
10
]
,
[
11
]

and later considered by many other authors, see e.g. Ball, Curie, Olver
[
1
]
, Ball, Mora-Corral
[
2
]
, Müller
[
8
, Section 6]
, Ciarlet
[
4
, page 93]
and references given there
(for references to some newer results see e.g. introduction in
[
3
]
).
The contribution of the higher gradient is usually connected with interfacial energies and is used to model various phenomena like elastoplasticity or damage and the term
1/|J_{f}|^{a}
corresponds to the penalisation of compression.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n=2
,
q>1
and
(1-\frac{1}{q})a\geq 1
.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz domain and let
f_{0}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a given homeomorphism.
Assume that
f\in W^{2,q}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a mapping such that
|J_{f}|^{-a}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
f=f_{0}
on
\partial\Omega
in the sense of traces.
Then
f
is a homeomorphism in
\Omega
.

The above theorem was originaly shown in
[
6
]
only for
q>2
under the very restrictive assumption
(1-\frac{n}{q})a\geq n
. Then it was generalized also to
q>4/3
in
[
3
]
under the condition
(\frac{3}{2}-\frac{2}{q})a\geq 1
which is better than
[
6
]
. We are able to show the result in full generality
q>1
under the weakest condition.
Moreover, the simple “folding” example in
[
3
, Example 5.1]
shows that our condition is sharp as there are counterexamples for
(1-\frac{1}{q})a<1
.
Let us note that
[
6
]
and
[
3
, Theorem 1.2]
contain some result also for
n\geq 3
but our approach does not improve those.

The main tool for the proof is the following estimate of the measure of the set where the Jacobian can vanish.
In
[
6
]
it was shown that the set
\{J_{f}=0\}
is empty. Our estimate on the dimension of
\{J_{f}=0\}
was shown in
[
3
, Theorem 1.3 and Corollary 1.4]
only in the case
q>n
and in the case

\frac{n^{2}}{2n-1}<q<n
they needed much stricter assumption. Again we have the result in full generality
q>1
and our assumption
(1-\frac{1}{q})a\geq 1
is sharp even in this case as shown by
[
3
, Example 5.1]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a domain,
q>1
and let
(1-\frac{1}{q})a\geq 1
.
Assume that
f\in W^{2,q}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a mapping with
|J_{f}|^{-a}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and let
\pi
denote the projection of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to some hyperplane. Then the precise representative of
Df
satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\bigl(\pi(\{J_{f}=0\})\bigr)=0
.

It follows that either
J_{f}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
or
J_{f}<0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

In the theorem above we use the standard representative
Df(x)=\limsup_{r\to 0+}-\hskip-11.0pt\int_{B(x,r)}Df
and
J_{f}(x)
is the determinant of this precise representative.

The main difference with the proof in
[
3
]
is the following. Since the Jacobian is essentially a product of
n
first order derivatives we can estimate its derivative by product rule as

|DJ_{f}|\leq C|D^{2}f||Df|^{n-1},

Since
f\in W^{2,q}
we know that for
q>n
we have
|Df|\in L^{\infty}
and hence it is possible to show that
J_{f}\in W^{1,q}
by the above inequality but for
q<n
we get only a weaker result (see
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
for details). In our proof for
q<n
we know that
|Df|
is not bounded a.e. but it is in fact bounded on a set which is big enough, so we can use similar tools to
[
3
]
-like analogue of Poincaré inequality on a big enough set and clever coverings to make the proof work.

Proof of the Theorems

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha>2
and let
E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy
0<|E|<\infty
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\{Q_{i}\}_{i}
, a collection of closed cubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that

(2.1)

\text{diam}(Q_{i})<\varepsilon,E\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}Q_{i}\text{ and }\sum_{i}|Q_{i}|<2|E|.

Moreover, for the the set

\Gamma:=\bigl\{i\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ |Q_{i}|\leq\alpha|Q_{i}\cap E|\bigr\}\text{ we have }(1-\frac{2}{\alpha})|E|\leq\sum_{i\in\Gamma}|Q_{i}|.

Proof.

We can find an open set
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
E\subset G
and
|G|<2|E|
. We can cover this
G
by disjoint dyadic cubes (boundaries of cubes can intersect) so that we have (
2.1
). Simple estimate gives us

\sum_{i\notin\Gamma}|Q_{i}\cap E|\leq\sum_{i\notin\Gamma}\frac{1}{\alpha}|Q_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i}|Q_{i}|<\frac{2}{\alpha}|E|.

It follows that

|E|=\sum_{i\in\Gamma}|Q_{i}\cap E|+\sum_{i\notin\Gamma}|Q_{i}\cap E|\leq\sum_{i\in\Gamma}|Q_{i}|+\frac{2}{\alpha}|E|.

and our conlusion follows easily.
∎

We need the well-known ACL characterization of Sobolev spaces (see e.g.
[
5
, Chapter 4.9]
or
[
6
, Theorem A.15]
)

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set and let
p\in[1,\infty)
. Then
h\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
if and only if it satisfies the ACL condition, i.e. it has a representative that is absolutely continuous on almost all line segments parallel to coordinate axes and moreover, the partial derivatives of these absolutely continuous functions belong to
L^{p}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\pi:(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n-1})
be the standard projection and let
\tilde{E}\subset(-1,1)^{n-1}
satisfy
0<\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\tilde{E})<\infty
.
Assume that
g:(-1,1)^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the ACL condition and that
\pi\left(g^{-1}(0)\right)=\tilde{E}
.
Let
E\subset(-1,1)^{n}
be a measurable set so that
\pi(E)=\tilde{E}
and for each
x^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}
we know that
\pi^{-1}(x^{\prime})\cap E
is an interval
I_{x}
of sidelength
\ell(I)
and that

I_{x^{\prime}}\cap\{g^{-1}(0)\}\neq 0
.
Then we have

\int_{E}|g|\,dx\leq\ell(I)|E|^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int_{E}|Dg|^{q}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

Proof.

As
g
satisfies the ACL condition, it is absolutely continuous on almost all segments and thus
Dg
is well defined a.e. If
\int_{E}|Dg|=\infty
, then there is nothing to prove. Hence, for the rest of the proof, we assume that
\int_{E}|Dg|<\infty
. For each
x^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}
, consider the vertical line-segment
L(x^{\prime}):=I_{x^{\prime}}
. By the ACL condition on
g
, we get, for a.e.
x^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}
, and for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in I_{x^{\prime}}
,

|g(x^{\prime},t_{1})-g(x^{\prime},t_{2})|\leq\int_{L(x^{\prime})}|Dg(x^{\prime},t)|d\mathcal{H}^{1}(t).

From the hypothesis
I_{x^{\prime}}\cap\{g^{-1}(0)\}\neq 0
, we know that we may choose
t_{2}\in I_{x^{\prime}}
such that
g(x^{\prime},t_{2})=0
. We choose such a
t_{2}
and integrate over
x^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}
and over

t_{1}\in I_{x^{\prime}}
to get

\int_{x^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}}\int_{t_{1}\in I_{x^{\prime}}}|g(x^{\prime},t_{1})|d\mathcal{H}^{1}(t_{1})d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x^{\prime})\leq\int_{x^{\prime}\in\tilde{E}}\int_{t_{1}\in I_{x^{\prime}}}\int_{I_{x}^{\prime}}|Dg(x^{\prime},t)|d\mathcal{H}^{1}(t)d\mathcal{H}^{1}(t_{1})d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x^{\prime}).

Now, Fubini’s theorem, followed by Hölder’s inequality gives

\int_{x\in E}|g(x)|dx\leq\ell(I)|E|^{1-\frac{1}{q}}\left(\int_{x\in E}|Dg(x)|^{q}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

From Theorem
1.2
we know that either
J_{f}>0
a.e. or
J_{f}<0
a.e. Without loss of generality we assume that
J_{f}>0
a.e.. Note that, since
n=2
, we have
q^{*}>2
.
Since
f\in W^{2,q}
implies that
f\in W^{1,q^{*}}\subset W^{1,2}
and thus

, we obtain that

is a mapping of finite distortion (see e.g.
[
7
]
for the definition). Moreover, we obtain that

is also continuous.

To obtain our conclusion it is enough to apply
[
3

and hence it is enough to check that
K_{f}:=\frac{|Df|^{2}}{J_{f}}\in L^{1}
.
For
q>2
we obtain that
|Df|\in L^{\infty}
and the condition
a\geq 1
implies that
K_{f}\in L^{1}
.
In the case
1<q<2
we use Hölder’s inequality

\int_{\Omega}\frac{|Df|^{2}}{J_{f}}\leq\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{J_{f}^{a}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{a}}\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}|Df|^{2\frac{a}{a-1}}\Bigr)^{\frac{a-1}{a}}.

Clearly
(2-\frac{2}{q})a\geq(1-\frac{1}{q})a\geq 1
and thus

(2-\frac{2}{q})a\geq 1
implies that

2\frac{a}{a-1}\leq q^{*}=\frac{2q}{2-q}

and the last integral is finite by
Df\in L^{q*}
. Similarly for
q=2
we use Hölder’s inequality,
a\geq 2
and
Df\in L^{4}
.

∎
