Machine learning identifies ac4C-related prognostic signature and TUBA1C as therapeutic target in COAD
Authors: Baizhi Zhai, Yuxiang Fan, Heng Ma, Xiaowei Xia, Yi Zhao, Jia Lu, Zhihong Zhang, Yi Wang, Bo Wu, Xia Yuan, Zhihui Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the role of N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C)-related genes (acRGs) in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) and identify reliable prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets. Multi-source transcriptomic datasets (TCGA-COAD, GSE39582, GSE17536) and single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed. Ten machine learning algorithms were integrated to construct an acRG-based prognostic signature (acRGBS). Immune microenvironment (TME) and genomic profiling were performed, with in vitro functional experiments validating TUBA1C’s role. acRGBS, comprising four hub genes (SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, TUBA1C), effectively stratified COAD patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct survival outcomes and was an independent prognostic factor. High-risk patients exhibited increased genomic instability and immunosuppressive TME, while low-risk patients had favorable immunotherapy response. TUBA1C was overexpressed in COAD cells, and its knockdown inhibited proliferation/migration and induced apoptosis. The acRGBS is a robust prognostic tool for COAD, and TUBA1C serves as a candidate therapeutic target, providing new insights for personalized COAD management.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00335-026-10267-8.

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2020, it was estimated that over 1.9 million new cases of colorectal cancer (including anal cancer) and 935,000 related deaths occurred in 2020, accounting for approximately one in every 10 cancer cases and deaths worldwide (Sung et al. 2021). Colon cancer (CC) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality among adult patients in the United States. Despite significant advancements in novel therapeutic strategies that have improved clinical outcomes, the 5-year relative survival rate remains suboptimal at only 64.7%. Prognosis is even poorer for patients with metastatic disease, whose 5-year survival rate is less than 20% (Fabregas 2022).

However, targeted therapies (e.g., BRAF inhibitors) serve as a therapeutic option for patients with poor prognosis, in whom cytotoxic chemotherapy exhibits limited efficacy (Tabernero et al. 2021; Carlino et al. 2021).

Notably, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), a conserved nucleoside present on transfer RNA (tRNA) and ribosomal RNA (rRNA), plays a crucial role in cell proliferation, metastasis, and apoptosis across multiple tumor types (Xie et al. 2023; Bártová and Stixová 2025). N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10), the key enzyme mediating ac4C acetylation, regulates the expression of numerous tumor progression-related genes through this acetylation modification. Consistent with this, NAT10 is overexpressed in various cancer types (Wang et al. 2025b; Ma et al. 2024; Gao et al. 2024) and patients with high NAT10 expression exhibit significantly shorter survival times (Ma et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2022). Specifically, NAT10 enhances tRNA abundance in an ac4C-dependent manner, thereby promoting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) protein translation efficiency and facilitating esophageal carcinoma (ESCA) malignancy (Wei et al. 2023).

Here, we comprehensively analyzed and characterized the association between ac4C modification activity and colon adenocarcinoma (COAD). We utilized single-cell datasets to investigate the impact of ac4C modification on specific cell subpopulations at the single-cell resolution, as well as its regulatory effects on immune cell subsets. Additionally, we identified four overall survival (OS)-related key genes: SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, and TUBA1C, and confirmed that these genes are closely associated with patient survival outcomes.

Methods

Single cell RNA-seq analysis

To comprehensively evaluate the relative contribution of each data type at single-cell resolution within the GSE1324657 dataset, we employed an unsupervised learning framework. This approach enables unbiased characterization of the underlying data structures without relying on prior knowledge of cell identities or biological phenotypes, thereby facilitating a data-driven exploration of cellular heterogeneity and functional diversity (Hao et al. 2021). The single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package (version 4.4). Prior to unsupervised analysis, a stringent quality control (QC) step was applied to refine the dataset: cells with mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 15% were excluded, and low-quality cells were filtered out to minimize technical noise. Following QC, the top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected to capture the core biological variability underlying cellular heterogeneity and functional diversity, thereby establishing a foundation for subsequent downstream analyses. To mitigate potential batch effects introduced during sample processing or data acquisition, the preprocessed dataset was further integrated using the Harmony algorithm (version 1.2), which enhances the consistency of cross-sample cell comparisons. Based on the integrated data, we performed cell clustering and subsequently annotated cell types by leveraging cluster-specific marker genes, referencing well-established literature and canonical cell-type marker databases to ensure accurate biological assignment of each cell cluster.

Immune analysis

The relative fractions of various immune and stromal cell types were inferred from the gene expression data using the CIBERSORT deconvolution algorithm (Newman et al. 2015). This approach is widely recognized for its capacity to deconvolve heterogeneous tissue samples and quantify the abundance of specific cell subsets, offering critical insights into the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment. To further assess the functional status of the immune system in individual colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) samples, we applied single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) (Subramanian et al. 2005). This method enables the evaluation of activation levels for predefined immune-related gene sets at the single-sample level, facilitating a detailed dissection of immune response heterogeneity across different COAD samples and supporting the exploration of potential associations between immune pathway activation and acRGBS-related phenotypes.

The TIDE algorithm, a computational approach designed to capture two primary mechanisms of tumor immune evasion—namely, T cell dysfunction in tumors with high cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) infiltration and T cell exclusion in tumors with low CTL infiltration—was applied to assess T cell dysfunction signatures in COAD samples (Jiang et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2024).

Construction of acRG prognostic model based on machine learning

The panel of algorithms included Random Survival Forest (RSF), Elastic Net (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, plsRcox, SuperPC, GBM, and Survival-SVM. The acRGBS was developed using the “Mime1” R package through the following steps: (a) prognostic acRGs were screened via univariate Cox regression in the TCGA-COAD cohort; (b) these genes were then used to construct predictive models by applying 88 algorithm combinations under a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework within the same cohort.

Cell culture

The normal human colonic mucosal epithelial cell line (NCM-460) and two human colorectal cancer cell lines (HCT116 and HT29) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were recently verified to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin mixture, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37℃ with 5% CO2.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from colorectal cancer cell lines using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from purified RNA with the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper), which effectively eliminates genomic DNA contamination. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was subsequently performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix.

Colony formation assay

Cells were placed on ice and rinsed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 4 ℃). Subsequently, cells were fixed with 100% methanol (4 ℃) for 10 min, with the methanol volume ensured to fully submerge the cells to achieve homogeneous fixation efficiency. After carefully aspirating the methanol from the wells, the cell culture plate was removed from ice and equilibrated to room temperature. An appropriate volume of crystal violet staining solution was then added to cover the cells completely, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min. Following the removal of the crystal violet staining solution, cells were rinsed repeatedly with distilled water until no residual crystal violet was detected in the rinse effluent. This step was critical to eliminate excess dye and minimize background interference during subsequent observation.

Plasmids and transfection

Human TUBA1C complementary DNA (cDNA) was cloned into the pcDNA3.0-Myc vector. The shRNA sequences against TUBA1C (shTUBA1C-1: GGGATGAGTGCTTTGTGTGC and shTUBA1C-2: GTGTTAAAAGGCGCAGGGAG) were cloned into the pLKO.1-puro vector. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for transfection according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 assay was conducted using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 400 cells per well. Subsequently, CCK-8 solution was added to the culture medium and incubated for 1.5 h. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

EdU assay

An EdU incorporation assay was carried out using the Cell-Light EdU DNA Imaging Kit (C10310-3, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well. After 24 h of culture, 50 µM EdU was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed and stained following the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were captured using a microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE Ti, Shanghai, China).

Annexin V staining protocol

Apoptosis detection was performed using the Thermo Annexin V Staining Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1× binding buffer was prepared by diluting 1 volume of 10× binding buffer with 9 volumes of distilled water. Cells were then harvested and washed sequentially with 1× PBS once and 1× binding buffer once. After washing, cells were resuspended in 1× binding buffer to a final concentration of 1–5 × 10⁶ cells/mL.

For staining, 5 µL of fluorochrome-conjugated Annexin V was added to 100 µL of the cell suspension, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10–15 min in the dark to prevent photobleaching. Subsequently, 2 mL of 1× binding buffer was added, and the cells were centrifuged at 400–600 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was then discarded, and the cell pellet was gently resuspended in 200 µL of 1× binding buffer.

Finally, 5 µL of Propidium Iodide (PI) Staining Solution or 7-AAD Viability Staining Solution was added, followed by incubation on ice or at room temperature for 5–15 min. Stained cells were immediately analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.0). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were made using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The transcriptomic atlas of COAD

To construct a comprehensive transcriptomic atlas of colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), this study integrated large-scale transcriptomic profiling datasets, including the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE132465. The integrated cohort comprised 23 colorectal cancer (CRC) patients from Samsung Medical Center (SMC, Korea) and 6 CRC patients from Katholieke Universiteit Leuven (designated as KUL3, Belgium). Specifically, the SMC cohort included 23 tumor samples derived from five anatomical sites (hepatic flexure, ascending colon, sigmoid colon, rectosigmoid colon, and rectum), along with 10 adjacent normal tissue samples.

Given the inherent batch effects associated with multi-source transcriptomic data, the Harmony algorithm was applied for batch effect correction to ensure data consistency. Subsequent unsupervised clustering analysis identified 23 discrete cell clusters (Fig. 1A). Cell type annotation was further performed by leveraging cluster-enriched marker genes, which enabled the classification of these 23 clusters into eight major cell lineages: B cells, Endothelial cells, Epithelial cells, Fibroblasts, Mast cells, Myeloid cells, Plasma cells, and T cells (Fig. 1B, C). The expression patterns of representative marker genes for each cell lineage were validated through Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization, confirming the reliability of cell type annotations (Fig. 1D). Notably, all eight major cell types were conserved across both the patient cohort and control group; however, their relative proportions differed significantly between the two groups (Fig. 1E).

acRG activity scores exhibit significant differences

As reported in previous studies, N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), an evolutionarily conserved mRNA modification catalyzed by the acetyltransferase NAT10, has been globally associated with the downregulation of target mRNA expression (Shi et al. 2023; Qiu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). Based on this observation, we utilized a set of 2135 ac4C-related genes (acRGs) to evaluate single-cell activity in COAD patients. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization revealed that epithelial cells exhibited substantially higher acRG scores compared to other cell lineages (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, we mapped the spatial distribution of cells stratified into two subgroups based on the median acRG score (acRG-high and acRG-low groups), which further underscored the distinct enrichment of acRG activity in epithelial cells (Fig. 2B).

To quantify the differences in acRG activity across cell types, violin plots were generated, confirming that epithelial cells had significantly elevated acRG scores relative to other lineages (Fig. 2C). Notably, acRG-high epithelial cells accounted for over 90% of the total epithelial cell population (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these results indicate that ac4C modification is dynamically regulated in epithelial cells and may play a pivotal role in shaping the transcriptional landscape of this cell type in COAD.

Subsequently, cell-cell interaction analysis was performed to explore the functional implications of differential acRG activity. The results demonstrated that the acRG-high group exhibited more frequent and stronger interaction connections with other cell types compared to the acRG-low group (Fig. 2E, F). To further dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying these phenotypic differences, we performed differential expression analysis on each major cell population between the acRG-high and acRG-low groups. Differential expression analysis between the acRG-high and acRG-low cell groups was then performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Genes with adjusted p value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.25 were considered ac4C activity-associated differentially expressed genes. A total of 2,135 genes met these criteria and were used for subsequent integrative prognostic analysis. Functional enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed that the acRG-high group was significantly enriched in biological processes related to cell-cell adhesion (Fig. 2G), suggesting that elevated ac4C modification may modulate epithelial cell function by enhancing intercellular adhesive properties.

Construction of the acRG-based prognostic signature in COAD

To identify DEGs associated with overall survival (OS) in COAD, we further prioritized core DEGs from the cell-cell adhesion-related DEGs identified in Fig. 2I. A total of 10 machine learning algorithms were employed to construct predictive models, with the aim of selecting the optimal approach for core DEG identification (Fig. 3A). These analyses were performed using three independent cohorts: TCGA-COAD, GSE39582 (Marisa et al. 2013), and GSE17536 (Smith et al. 2010).

Based on model performance evaluation, the optimal predictive framework was established by integrating Lasso regression and Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithms (Fig. 3B, C). This combined model ultimately identified four OS-related key genes: SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, and TUBA1C, with corresponding coefficients of -0.32, -0.22, 0.53, and − 0.53, respectively. Using these four key genes, we constructed an acRG-based prognostic signature, which effectively stratified COAD patients into high-risk and low-risk groups across all three cohorts. Survival analysis confirmed that patients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer OS outcomes compared to those in the low-risk group (Fig. 3D). To improve model interpretability, we applied Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to quantify each feature’s contribution to the survival risk prediction. Ranking features by their mean absolute SHAP values revealed TUBA1C as the most influential predictor (mean SHAP: 0.208), followed by TSPYL2 (0.198), CDC42SE2 (0.197), and SARAF (0.135) (Fig. 3E). These results indicate that specific genes, particularly TUBA1C, are key determinants of high-risk classification. Visualization of per-patient SHAP values further confirmed the robust and consistent contributions of these features, clearly delineating between high- and low-expression states.

Validation of the acRG-based prognostic signature in COAD

To assess the discriminatory power of the acRG-based prognostic signature for binary classification, the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was calculated. The ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate across all possible threshold values. Results demonstrated that the acRG-based signature exhibited robust predictive performance in distinguishing between high-risk and low-risk COAD patients (Fig. 4A).

Consistent with the previous stratification, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the risk scores derived from the four core genes (SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, and TUBA1C). Survival analysis revealed a significant correlation between risk scores and OS outcomes: patients with lower risk scores exhibited substantially longer survival times compared to those with higher risk scores (Fig. 4B).

Furthermore, univariate, and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the independence of the acRG-based signature as a prognostic factor. Results indicated that the acRG-based signature was an independent predictor of OS in the TCGA-COAD cohort, after adjusting for other potential confounding factors (Fig. 4C, D).

To further validate the clinical relevance of the signature, Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was conducted in stratified subgroups based on tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging and microsatellite instability (MSI) status. TNM staging assesses the extent of the primary tumor (T), regional lymph node involvement (N), and distant metastasis (M), while MSI status reflects genomic instability. Collectively, these subgroup analyses revealed that patients with lower risk scores were associated with favorable clinical features, including reduced cancer progression, smaller primary tumor size (lower T stage), decreased lymph node metastasis (lower N stage), and diminished distant metastasis (lower M stage), as well as stable microsatellite status (Fig. 4E). These findings demonstrate that the acRGBS effectively stratifies patients with distinct clinical outcomes across different TNM stages and MSI statuses, further supporting its potential as a clinically applicable prognostic tool for COAD patients. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to determine whether the acRGBS risk score was an independent prognostic factor in COAD. The variables included in the Cox regression analyses were age, MSI status, gender, M stage, N stage, T stage, overall stage, and the acRGBS risk score. In univariate Cox regression analysis, the acRGBS risk score was significantly associated with overall survival (HR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.17–2.68, P = 0.00646; Fig. 4F). After adjustment for available clinicopathological variables in multivariate Cox regression analysis, the acRGBS risk score remained an independent prognostic factor (HR = 1.81, 95% CI: 1.16–2.83, P = 0.00859; Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that the acRGBS may provide additional prognostic information beyond conventional clinical parameters.

Genomic characterization associated with the acRG-based prognostic signature in COAD

To investigate the impact of the acRG-based signature on genomic landscapes, we analyzed somatic mutation data from the TCGA-COAD cohort. The most frequently mutated genes in the high-risk group were APC, TP53, and TTN. Functionally, APC is a key regulator that inhibits intestinal epithelial cell proliferation and maintains cellular differentiation; its dysregulation is closely associated with colorectal cancer initiation and progression. TP53 is a well-characterized tumor suppressor gene critical for DNA damage repair and cell cycle arrest across multiple cancer types. TTN, which encodes a component of striated muscle assembly, has been increasingly implicated in cancer, with high mutation rates correlating with poor clinical outcomes in various malignancies.

In contrast, the low-risk group exhibited a notably lower overall gene mutation rate, with the mutation frequencies of the top three genes (APC, TP53, TTN) reduced by approximately 10% compared to the high-risk group (Fig. 5A). To further explore copy number variations (CNVs), we employed the GISTIC (Genomic Identification of Significant Targets in Cancer) module to identify genomic regions with significant amplification or deletion across samples. Results revealed that the high-risk group harbored more extensive copy number alterations (Fig. 5B). Additionally, we quantified the amplification and deletion rates of the top mutated genes (APC, TP53, TTN) in both two groups, and consistent with previous findings, the high-risk group exhibited significantly higher rates of CNVs for these key genes (Fig. 5C).

The immune microenvironment associated with the acRG-based prognostic signature in COAD

To delineate how the acRG-based signature (acRGBS) modulates the tumor immune microenvironment (TIM), we analyzed the expression profiles of multiple immune cell subsets (Fig. 6A). Results showed that the high-acRGBS group exhibited significantly elevated scores for Type II interferon (IFN-γ) responses, immune checkpoint molecule expression, and pro-inflammatory activity. We further compared the expression levels of immune checkpoint genes between the two risk groups and identified seven genes with significant differential expression: CD244, CD274 (PD-L1), CD276 (B7-H3), CD48, CD80, ICOS, and TNFSF9 (Fig. 6B). These genes are key regulators involved in immune cell activation, immunosuppression, and intercellular signaling cascades. Collectively, these differential expression patterns reflect distinct TIM states between the high-risk and low-risk groups, which may profoundly contribute to the association between the high-acRGBS group and enhanced pro-inflammatory activity.

Subsequently, we employed the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) model to estimate the potential immune escape characteristics of the two acRGBS-defined risk groups. The high-acRGBS group exhibited significantly higher TIDE scores, as well as elevated immune exclusion and immune dysfunction scores, compared with the low-acRGBS group (Fig. 6C), suggesting a greater predicted tendency toward immune evasion and impaired antitumor immune activity. To further predict the potential response to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy, we performed immunophenoscore (IPS) analysis. The low-acRGBS group showed higher IPS values across multiple predicted immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatment settings, including anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1/PD-L1, and combined therapy settings (Fig. 6D). These findings suggest that patients in the low-acRGBS group may have higher predicted sensitivity to ICI therapy. However, because both TIDE and IPS are computational prediction frameworks rather than clinical immunotherapy response data, these results should be interpreted cautiously and require further validation in COAD cohorts treated with ICIs.

Validation of the four-hub-gene acRG-based prognostic signature in COAD

To comprehensively validate the reliability, robustness, and clinical relevance of the four hub acRG-based signature (acRGBS) identified in this study, we performed multi-dimensional verification encompassing transcriptomic, spatial transcriptomic, and clinical survival-related analyses.

At the transcriptomic level, we quantified the expression patterns of the four hub acRGs (SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, and TUBA1C) using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from the TCGA-COAD cohort. Results confirmed consistent and significant differential expression of these hub genes between COAD tumor tissues and adjacent normal colorectal tissues, further supporting their association with COAD pathogenesis (Fig. 7A, B).

To further elucidate the spatial distribution and functional localization of these hub acRGs, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic data as previously explored in Gao et al. (2022). This analysis revealed enriched expression of the four hub acRGs in specific tumor core regions, providing spatial insights into their potential roles in driving tumor progression and maintaining the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 7C). To strengthen the connection between single-cell analysis and prognostic modeling, we examined the expression distribution of the four acRGBS hub genes across major cell populations. The candidate ac4C activity-associated genes were first derived from differential expression analysis between acRG-high and acRG-low cells and were then integrated with bulk transcriptomic cohorts and machine-learning algorithms to construct the final acRGBS. Thus, the single-cell analysis contributed to the upstream identification of ac4C activity-associated candidate genes and provided cellular-context annotation for the final prognostic model.

Among the four hub genes, TUBA1C showed the most prominent enrichment in malignant cells, with higher average expression and a larger proportion of expressing cells compared with other cell populations (Fig. 7D). In contrast, SARAF, CDC42SE2, and TSPYL2 displayed more heterogeneous or non-malignant-cell-dominant expression patterns (Fig. 7E). Moreover, TUBA1C expression was significantly positively correlated with the acRG score at the single-cell level, further suggesting that TUBA1C is associated with ac4C-related transcriptional activity in malignant cells. These findings support the biological relevance of TUBA1C within the acRGBS and provide a cellular basis for its subsequent functional validation (Fig. 7F).

Collectively, these multi-omics and clinical validations systematically underscore the robustness and reproducibility of the four hub acRG-based signature. Moreover, they highlight its potential as a novel, clinically applicable prognostic biomarker, and a promising therapeutic target for COAD.

TUBA1C as a candidate therapeutic target in COAD

Although the acRGBS consisted of four hub genes, TUBA1C was selected for functional validation based on a comprehensive prioritization strategy rather than SHAP importance alone. TUBA1C showed the highest SHAP value, suggesting that it made the largest contribution to risk stratification in the model. In addition, TUBA1C was significantly upregulated in COAD, associated with poor prognosis, and biologically linked to microtubule organization, mitotic progression, proliferation, and cell migration. To further determine the cellular distribution of the four hub genes, we analyzed their expression across major cell populations using single-cell RNA-seq data. Among SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, and TUBA1C, TUBA1C was the only gene showing preferentially high average expression in malignant cells, with a relatively high proportion of TUBA1C-expressing malignant cells. In contrast, SARAF, CDC42SE2, and TSPYL2 did not display a comparable malignant-cell-enriched expression pattern. Together with its highest SHAP importance, significant association with poor prognosis, and known role in microtubule organization and cell proliferation, this single-cell evidence supported the prioritization of TUBA1C for experimental validation. These features made TUBA1C the most suitable candidate for initial functional validation. The remaining three signature genes may also have biological relevance in COAD. SARAF regulates store-operated calcium entry and may participate in calcium-dependent signaling in tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment. CDC42SE2 is associated with CDC42-related cytoskeletal organization and may affect cell morphology and motility. TSPYL2 has been implicated in transcriptional regulation, cell-cycle control, and tumor-suppressive processes. Although SARAF, CDC42SE2, and TSPYL2 were not experimentally investigated in the present study due to the focused scope of our validation experiments, they remain important components of the acRGBS and warrant further mechanistic investigation. To further confirm the expression pattern and biological function of TUBA1C in COAD, we performed in vitro experiments using normal human colon mucosal epithelial cells (NCM-460) and two human colon cancer cell lines (HCT116 and HT29). First, we quantified TUBA1C expression in these cell lines, and results confirmed that TUBA1C was significantly overexpressed in HCT116 and HT29 colon cancer cells compared to the normal NCM-460 cells (Fig. 8A).

Subsequently, TUBA1C loss-of-function assays were conducted via small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown to investigate its role in cancer cell malignant phenotypes. The knockdown efficiency of TUBA1C was verified by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), confirming successful downregulation of TUBA1C expression in both HCT116 and HT29 cells (Fig. 8B). Functional assays demonstrated that TUBA1C knockdown significantly impaired the proliferation and migration abilities of colon cancer cells (Fig. 8C-E). Notably, loss of TUBA1C function also induced a marked increase in apoptosis of HCT116 and HT29 cells, as evidenced by flow cytometry or immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 8F). To further validate the oncogenic role of TUBA1C, we performed gain-of-function experiments in COAD cells. CCK8 assays demonstrated that TUBA1C overexpression significantly improved the proliferation and migration abilities of colon cancer cells (Fig. 9A). It’s also significantly enhanced colony formation ability, indicating increased proliferative potential (Fig. 9B). Moreover, flow cytometry analysis showed that TUBA1C overexpression reduced the proportion of apoptotic cells compared with the control group (Fig. 9C).

Although both knockdown and overexpression experiments support an oncogenic role of TUBA1C in COAD cells, the current evidence remains insufficient to define TUBA1C as a fully validated therapeutic target. Rescue experiments are still needed to confirm the specificity of the observed knockdown effects, and in vivo xenograft or orthotopic models are required to evaluate the role of TUBA1C in tumor growth and metastasis under physiological conditions. Therefore, TUBA1C should be considered a candidate therapeutic target that warrants further preclinical investigation.

Mechanistic insights into ac4C-mediated immune remodeling

To explore how specific acRGs might regulate the immune microenvironment, we performed GSEA analysis. Results showed that high acRG score patients were associated with activation of pro-inflammatory and immunosuppressive pathways (TNF-α, IL-6) butsuppression of adaptive immune responses (IFN-γ) (Fig. 10A, B). This suggests that acRG creates a paradoxically inflamed yet immunosuppressed microenvironment. CellChat analysis revealed that the high-risk group exhibited significantly increased overall intercellular communication compared to the low-risk group. The communication networks were predominantly driven by epithelial cells as major signal senders and immune cells as major signal receivers. It revelaed that specific ligand-receptor pairs that contribute to the immunosuppressive TME in acRG high activity group. The MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4) axis, identified as a key driver of CD8 + T cell exhaustion, showed significantly enhanced interaction probability in acRG high activity group (Fig. 10C, D). The CellChat analysis further suggested that acRG-high states were associated with altered intercellular communication in the tumor microenvironment, particularly involving the MIF-CD74-CXCR4 signaling axis. However, this finding should be interpreted as a microenvironmental association rather than direct evidence that TUBA1C regulates MIF-CD74-CXCR4 signaling. Further studies combining perturbation experiments, spatial validation, and ligand-receptor functional assays will be required to determine whether TUBA1C or ac4C-related programs actively modulate this communication axis.

Discussion

Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality worldwide, highlighting the need for reliable prognostic biomarkers and biologically informative molecular signatures. N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C), a dynamically regulated RNA modification mainly catalyzed by NAT10, has been implicated in the regulation of RNA stability, translation, and tumor-related biological processes in several malignancies. However, the clinical relevance of ac4C-related transcriptional programs in COAD, as well as their associations with the tumor microenvironment (TME) and genomic characteristics, remains incompletely understood. In the present study, we developed an ac4C-related gene-based prognostic signature (acRGBS) using multi-source transcriptomic datasets and evaluated its prognostic value through multidimensional analyses. We further investigated the cellular distribution of signature genes using single-cell RNA-seq data and explored the functional role of the prioritized hub gene TUBA1C in COAD cells.

By integrating bulk transcriptomic datasets with single-cell RNA-seq analysis, we characterized the cellular heterogeneity of COAD and identified major cell populations across tumor and control samples. Epithelial cells showed relatively higher acRG activity, with a large proportion of epithelial cells classified into the acRG-high subgroup. This finding suggests that ac4C-related transcriptional activity may be more prominent in epithelial/tumor-associated compartments, which is biologically relevant given the epithelial origin of COAD. However, this result should not be interpreted as direct evidence of ac4C modification in these cells. Rather, it indicates that ac4C-related gene programs may be associated with epithelial malignant states. Consistently, cell-cell communication analysis suggested that the acRG-high group exhibited more active intercellular interactions with other cell types, while differentially expressed genes between acRG-high and acRG-low cells were enriched in cell-cell adhesion-related processes. Because cell-cell adhesion is involved in tumor invasion, migration, and TME remodeling, these findings suggest a possible association between ac4C-related activity and intercellular communication or adhesive properties in COAD. Nevertheless, further experimental validation will be required to determine whether ac4C modification directly regulates these biological processes.

A critical novelty of our study is the identification of four hub acRGs (SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, TUBA1C) via a combination of 10 machine learning algorithms, which were integrated into a prognostic signature. This signature effectively stratified COAD patients into high- and low-risk groups across multiple independent cohorts (TCGA-COAD, GSE39582, GSE17536), with high-risk patients exhibiting significantly poorer survival days. ROC curve analysis confirmed the robust discriminatory power of the signature, and Cox regression analyses validated it as an independent prognostic factor after adjusting for clinicopathological variables such as TNM stage. These results demonstrate that the acRGBS possesses superior clinical applicability compared to traditional prognostic indicators, providing a reliable tool for risk stratification and personalized treatment decision-making in COAD patients.

To further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the prognostic value of the acRGBS, we analyzed genomic alterations and TME features between the two risk groups. The high-acRGBS group exhibited a higher overall mutation rate, with the top mutated genes being APC, TP53, and TTN—all of which are well-characterized drivers of COAD. APC dysregulation is a hallmark of early COAD, leading to uncontrolled intestinal epithelial cell proliferation, while TP53 mutation disrupts DNA damage repair and cell cycle checkpoint, promoting genomic instability. Additionally, the high-risk group harbored more extensive copy number variations, including amplified/deleted rates of the top mutated genes, indicating that ac4C modification may be closely associated with genomic instability in COAD. Genomic instability is a key driver of tumor heterogeneity and progression, which may explain the poorer clinical outcomes of high-risk patients.

The TME plays a pivotal role in tumor progression and response to immunotherapy. Our analysis revealed that the high-acRGBS group had elevated scores for Type II interferon responses, immune checkpoint expression, and pro-inflammatory activity. Differential expression of key immune checkpoint genes between the two groups further confirmed distinct TME states. Notably, TIDE model analysis showed that the high-risk group had higher TIDE, immune exclusion, and immune dysfunction scores, while IPS analysis indicated that the low-risk group had higher IPS values and thus a more favorable response to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. These findings suggest that the acRGBS may serve as a predictive biomarker for ICI therapy response in COAD patients. High-risk patients with an immunosuppressive TME may benefit from combined ICI therapy and targeted therapies against ac4C-related pathways, while low-risk patients may be more sensitive to single-agent ICI therapy.

The finding that the high-risk group exhibits higher TIDE scores and immunosuppressive characteristics raises an important question regarding how ac4C modification, mediated primarily by the writer protein NAT10, influences tumor immunity (Li et al. 2025). Recent advances in epitranscriptomics have begun to elucidate the mechanistic links between NAT10-mediated ac4C modification and immune regulation across multiple cancer types (Wang et al. 2025c). NAT10 catalyzes ac4C modification at RRACH motifs, primarily affecting mRNA stability and translation efficiency, and several immune-related genes have been identified as direct targets of NAT10 regulation (Li et al. 2025). In pancreatic cancer, Wang et al. demonstrated that NAT10 stabilizes ETS2 mRNA through ac4C acetylation, forming a positive feedback loop that upregulates PD-L1 expression, thereby suppressing CD8 + T cell infiltration and promoting immune evasion (Wang et al. 2025a). Similarly, in gastric cancer, NAT10-mediated ac4C modification enhances the stability of CXCL2 and KLF5 mRNAs, promoting M2 macrophage infiltration and polarization (Li et al. 2025). These findings align with our observations that high-risk tumors exhibit elevated PD-L1 expression and increased M2 macrophage infiltration. Furthermore, the suppression of T cell-recruiting chemokines in our high-risk group (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11) is consistent with recent studies demonstrating that NAT10 inhibition upregulates CXCL9/10/11 chemokines and increases cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration in gastric cancer models (Li et al. 2025).

In nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Xie et al. demonstrated that NAT10-mediated ac4C modification stabilizes transcripts of CEBPG, DDX5, and HLTF, leading to HMGB1 upregulation, which recruits immunosuppressive cells and induces T cell exhaustion (Zhao 2025).

This provides a mechanistic explanation for the reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration observed in our high-risk patients. Our findings in colorectal cancer both corroborate and extend this growing body of evidence, demonstrating that high acRG scores are associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment characterized by reduced CD8 + T cells, increased Tregs, and elevated TIDE scores. TUBA1C, identified as the core prognostic gene and functionally validated oncogene in our study, correlated significantly with these immunosuppressive markers, suggesting that it was prioritized from an ac4C-related prognostic framework.

Among the four hub genes, TUBA1C was selected for functional validation due to its significant overexpression in COAD tissues and cell lines. TUBA1C encodes a tubulin alpha chain, which is a key component of microtubules involved in cell division, migration, and intracellular transport. Emerging studies have shown that TUBA1C is overexpressed in multiple cancers and promotes tumor progression. Our in vitro experiments confirmed that TUBA1C knockdown significantly impaired the proliferation and migration abilities of COAD cells (HCT116 and HT29) and induced apoptosis. These results validate the oncogenic role of TUBA1C in COAD, suggesting that it may be a candidate therapeutic target. Notably, TUBA1C is an ac4C-related gene, implying that its expression and function may be regulated by ac4C modification. Future studies are needed to explore whether NAT10-mediated ac4C modification directly regulates TUBA1C mRNA stability or translation efficiency, which would provide new insights into the regulatory mechanism of ac4C in COAD.

Despite the significant findings, our study has several limitations. First, the hub genes and acRGBS were identified and validated based on public datasets, and further prospective clinical studies are needed to confirm their clinical utility. Second, the in vitro functional experiments only explored the role of TUBA1C, and in vivo animal models are required to verify the oncogenic role of TUBA1C and the therapeutic potential of targeting ac4C-related pathways. Third, the regulatory mechanism underlying the interaction between ac4C modification and the identified hub genes remains unclear, and future studies should employ molecular biology techniques to elucidate these mechanisms. We acknowledge that the current study, while providing robust computational and in vitro evidence, lacks independent clinical validation and in vivo experimental confirmation. These are important limitations that should be addressed before the acRG-based prognostic signature (acRGBS) and TUBA1C as a therapeutic target can be translated into clinical practice.

In our subsequent research work, we intend to conduct independent clinical validation using retrospective multi-center cohorts and prospective patient enrollment to confirm the prognostic value of the acRGBS in real-world clinical settings. Additionally, we plan to perform in vivoexperiments, including subcutaneous and orthotopic xenograft mouse models, to validate the functional role of TUBA1C in tumor growth and metastasis, as well as to explore TUBA1C-targeted therapeutic strategies. These future studies will provide more comprehensive evidence for the clinical utility of the acRGBS and the therapeutic potential of TUBA1C, ultimately bridging the gap between computational discovery and clinical application. Notably, the high-acRGBS group showed both enhanced IFN-γ-related signaling and increased T-cell dysfunction scores. These findings are not necessarily contradictory. Increased IFN-γ signaling may reflect a pre-existing inflammatory or immune-activated tumor microenvironment, whereas elevated T-cell dysfunction indicates that antitumor immune responses may be functionally impaired. Thus, the high-acRGBS group may represent an inflamed but immunosuppressive immune state, in which immune activation coexists with T-cell exhaustion, immune escape, or compensatory immunosuppressive mechanisms. This may partly explain why the high-acRGBS group displayed higher TIDE scores despite the presence of immune activation signals. The immunotherapy-related conclusions of this study were based on computational tools, including TIDE and IPS, rather than clinical cohorts receiving immune checkpoint blockade. Therefore, the predictive value of acRGBS for actual ICI response should be further validated in independent COAD immunotherapy cohorts.

In conclusion, our study systematically constructed and validated an ac4C-related prognostic signature for COAD, which exhibits robust prognostic value and potential predictive utility for immunotherapy response. We systematically evaluated 10 machine learning algorithms within a rigorous leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework to develop an acRG-based prognostic signature (acRGBS), ensuring robust and unbiased model selection. Through conservative multi-algorithm intersection, we identified TUBA1C as the most significant contributor to the signature and experimentally validated its oncogenic role in promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion in COAD cells, establishing it as a functionally relevant therapeutic target downstream of ac4C modification. The acRGBS demonstrated strong prognostic performance and generalizability across multiple independent cohorts, providing a clinically applicable tool for risk stratification and a rationale for targeting TUBA1C in high-risk COAD patients. These findings not only provide new insights into the biological functions of ac4C modification in COAD but also offer a novel prognostic biomarker and promising therapeutic targets for clinical management of COAD patients. Although TUBA1C was identified as a key component of the ac4C-related prognostic signature and was experimentally validated as a pro-tumorigenic gene in COAD cells, the present study does not provide direct evidence that TUBA1C is modified by ac4C or directly regulated by NAT10. Therefore, TUBA1C should be interpreted as an ac4C-associated prognostic candidate rather than a definitively validated ac4C-modified transcript. Establishing a direct NAT10/ac4C–TUBA1C regulatory axis will require further experiments, including acRIP-qPCR/acRIP-seq to detect ac4C enrichment on TUBA1C mRNA, NAT10 RIP-qPCR to verify NAT10 binding to TUBA1C transcripts, NAT10 knockdown or overexpression assays to assess TUBA1C dependence on NAT10, and Actinomycin D chase assays to evaluate TUBA1C mRNA stability. We have therefore moderated the relevant conclusions and highlighted this issue as an important limitation of the present study. Another limitation is that only TUBA1C was selected for functional validation, whereas SARAF, CDC42SE2, and TSPYL2 were not experimentally investigated. Future studies should further explore the biological functions and regulatory mechanisms of these genes in COAD.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically delineates the landscape of ac4C modifications in COAD and establishes a robust acRGBS comprising four hub genes (SARAF, CDC42SE2, TSPYL2, and TUBA1C). Through multi-cohort validation and multi-omics analyses, we confirm that acRGBS effectively stratifies COAD patients into high- and low-risk subgroups with distinct survival outcomes, and serves as an independent prognostic factor independent of clinicopathological variables. Genomic profiling reveals that high acRGBS risk correlates with increased genomic instability, while tumor microenvironment analyses highlight its potential to predict immunotherapy response—offering a valuable tool for personalized treatment stratification. Functional in vitro experiments further validate TUBA1C as a key oncogenic driver in COAD, with its knockdown impairing cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis, supporting its utility as a novel therapeutic target. Collectively, our findings advance the understanding of ac4C modification in COAD pathogenesis and provide clinically applicable prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic candidates, paving the way for improved risk assessment and precision medicine approaches in COAD management. Future prospective clinical trials and in vivo studies are warranted to further translate these discoveries into clinical practice.
