Exploring Water Storage Practices and Determinants for Sustainable Water Supply in Tropical Environments: Advancing SDG 6 Implementation
Authors: Timothy O. Ogunbode, Victor O. Oyebamiji, Timothy A. Akinkuolie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seasonal variability in water supply in tropical humid environments makes household water storage essential for reliable domestic access. This study examined water storage practices and determinants of storage behaviour in Iwo Township, Osun State, Nigeria. Using stratified random sampling, 756 households across 4 sections—Gidigbo, Molete, Oke-Oba I, and Oke-Oba II—were surveyed between February and November 2023, prioritising female household heads due to their central role in water management. Household sizes ranged from fewer than 5 members (32.3%) to more than 15 (12.3%), with most comprising 6 to 10 persons (34.0%). Educational levels were predominantly post-primary (57.7%) and tertiary (25.0%), while religious affiliation was split between Islam (51.9%) and Christianity (45.4%). Storage relied mainly on steel and plastic drums (44.2%), followed by buckets (29.5%) and overhead tanks (20.4%), with deep wells and boreholes serving 89.3% of households. Factor analysis identified 8 determinants of storage preferences, explaining 82.7% of the variance: age composition (17.8%), number of toilets (12.2%), water supply reliability (11.6%), religious activities (10.9%), beliefs about storage (9.0%), household assets (8.9%), storage method (6.8%), and alternative sources (5.7%). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure (0.827) and Bartlett’s test (P < .005) confirmed data suitability. Findings indicate that socio-demographic characteristics and household assets influence storage behaviour more than water availability alone. To enhance water security and support SDG 6, interventions should provide affordable household storage, promote safe storage practices through community awareness, incentivise improved behaviours, and integrate storage considerations into urban planning. Effective implementation requires collaboration between local governments, water agencies, and communities to ensure context-sensitive, equitable, and sustainable water management.

Introduction

Ensuring a reliable and resilient domestic water supply in tropical environments remains a persistent challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where climate variability, seasonal rainfall fluctuations, rapid population growth, and infrastructural deficiencies converge to undermine supply reliability.1-3 In such contexts, households are frequently unable to depend on continuous piped water systems and must adopt adaptive strategies to meet daily water needs.2,4 Among these strategies, household water storage has emerged as a critical mechanism for coping with intermittent supply and seasonal scarcity, shaping both water availability and household resilience. 1

Household water storage plays a dual role in tropical water systems. On one hand, it enhances domestic water sufficiency by buffering households against supply disruptions; on the other, inappropriate storage practices can compromise water quality, increase exposure to waterborne diseases, and undermine public health outcomes.5,6 Storage practices vary widely across households and are influenced by environmental conditions, cultural norms, gender roles, socio-economic status, and access to water sources.7,8 These variations underscore that water storage is not merely a technical response to scarcity but a socially embedded practice with significant implications for water security and sustainable development.

Cultural and gender dimensions strongly shape household storage decisions in tropical environments. Traditional preferences for specific storage containers, such as earthenware pots valued for their cooling properties, reflect long-standing adaptive strategies to high temperatures but may not always align with modern water safety standards.9,10 Women, who are typically responsible for domestic water management, play a central role in determining storage methods, container hygiene, and water use priorities, often within socio-cultural constraints that limit access to improved storage technologies.11,12 These gendered responsibilities link water storage directly to broader issues of equity and social vulnerability.

Socio-economic conditions further differentiate household storage practices. Higher-income households are more likely to invest in large-capacity tanks, sealed containers, and water treatment options, while lower-income households often rely on smaller, uncovered, or poorly maintained storage facilities that heighten contamination risks.13,14 Educational attainment has also been shown to influence awareness of safe storage practices and health risks associated with improper water handling.15,16 In tropical climates, prolonged storage during dry seasons increases the risk of mosquito breeding and disease transmission, reinforcing the need for storage practices that balance water sufficiency with public health protection.6,17

Despite the growing literature on household water storage in Africa and other LMICs, existing studies exhibit notable limitations. Much of the research adopts descriptive or health-focussed approaches, emphasising microbial contamination, disease prevalence, or container hygiene, often in isolation from broader household water use dynamics.15,18 Other studies focus on technological aspects such as container types or rainwater harvesting systems, treating storage largely as a passive coping mechanism rather than as an active determinant of domestic water sufficiency. 7 Critically, there remains a lack of robust quantitative, factor-based analyses that systematically examine how socio-demographic variables shape household water storage practices, particularly in medium-sized urban settings.

This gap is especially pronounced in Nigeria, where water storage is commonly regarded as a stopgap response to supply failure, with limited policy attention to its implications for efficiency, equity, and sustainability.19,20 Existing studies rarely employ multivariate or factor-analytic techniques to disentangle the relative influence of socio-demographic characteristics—such as income, education, household size, and gender—on storage behaviour. Moreover, few studies explicitly link household storage determinants to the targets of Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG 6), which emphasises safe water access (Target 6.1), improved water quality (Target 6.3), efficient water use (Target 6.4), and resilience to climate variability (Target 6.6).21-23 As a result, the evidence base needed to translate household-level water storage data into actionable water policy remains weak.

These limitations are particularly evident in medium-sized towns, which are under-represented in African water research compared to large metropolitan centres and rural communities. Such towns experience rapid population growth and increasing domestic water demand but often lack the infrastructural investment and institutional capacity of major cities. In southwestern Nigeria, towns such as Iwo in Osun State exemplify these dynamics. Households rely on a mix of piped water, wells, boreholes, and surface water sources, with frequent supply interruptions necessitating widespread water storage. However, empirical evidence on the determinants, adequacy, and efficiency of household storage practices in Iwo remains sparse, fragmented, and largely qualitative.

The absence of quantitative, factor-based evidence from Iwo limits the ability of policymakers and water managers to design context-sensitive interventions that integrate household storage into water supply planning and SDG 6 implementation. Without a clear understanding of how socio-demographic factors drive storage behaviour, policies risk being generic, ineffective, or misaligned with local realities, particularly for vulnerable groups such as women and low-income households.

This study addresses these gaps by undertaking a quantitative assessment of household water storage practices in Iwo, Osun State, using factor-based analytical techniques to examine the socio-demographic determinants of storage behaviour and their implications for domestic water sufficiency. Specifically, the study aims to: (i) identify and characterise major domestic water sources, (ii) quantify household water use patterns, (iii) classify households based on storage approaches, and (iv) analyse the influence of socio-demographic factors on storage practices within the framework of SDG 6. By explicitly linking household-level storage behaviour to sustainable water access, efficiency, and resilience, the study provides novel empirical evidence from an under-researched urban scale and offers clear policy-relevant insights for improving water security in Nigeria and comparable LMIC contexts.

Method of Study

Study Area

This study was conducted in Iwo Township, located in Osun State, southwestern Nigeria, as shown in Figure 1. Iwo covers an area of 245 km2 and had a recorded population of 191 348 in the 2006 census. The township lies at coordinates 7°03′8″N and 4°01′1″E and experiences a tropical climate, characterised by a long rainy season lasting approximately 8 months (March–October) and a dry season from November to February. Annual rainfall in tropical wet climate regions such as Iwo typically ranges between 1000 to 2000 mm, with peak precipitation occurring in July and September. 1

Map of Osun State showing the location of Iwo (Inset: Map of Nigeria showing the location of Osun State).

Water supply in Iwo Township is dominated by groundwater, which serves as the primary source for domestic use and is accessed mainly through hand-dug wells and boreholes. 24 Supplementary sources include surface water, rainwater, and limited public piped supply, largely drawn from the Aiba water reservoir. Surface water bodies such as the Oba, Aiba, and Ponyan rivers support small-scale irrigation and dry-season farming, particularly within riverine and wetland zones. Groundwater availability and yield vary considerably due to seasonal fluctuations and the underlying Precambrian Basement Complex geology, which confines aquifer development to zones of deep weathering and fractured rock formations.25,26

Rainwater harvesting serves as an important supplementary source during the rainy season, with households collecting water from rooftop catchments for domestic purposes. Access to public piped water provided by the Osun State Water Corporation remains limited and unreliable, compelling many households—particularly during the dry season—to rely on alternatives such as private boreholes, water vendors, and packaged (sachet) water.23,26

The inhabitants of Iwo are predominantly engaged in farming and rely heavily on rainfall for agricultural activities. The area is characterised by tropical forest vegetation, including hardwood species such as obeche, walnut, acacia, and eucalyptus. Agricultural practices involve both permanent and annual crops, including cocoa, kolanut, oil palm, yam, and maize, with dry-season farming concentrated around wetlands and riverbanks to mitigate water scarcity.

Geologically, Iwo is underlain by the Precambrian Basement Complex, composed mainly of gneiss, granite, schist, and undifferentiated metasedimentary rocks. The overburden consists largely of clay, sand, and silt soils, which are generally non-porous and impermeable, except in areas of intense weathering or along structural weaknesses. 25 These geological conditions significantly influence groundwater storage, accessibility, and household dependence on water storage facilities.

Overall, Iwo’s geographical, climatic, geological, and hydrological characteristics provide essential context for analysing household water availability, storage practices, and utilisation patterns. Understanding the diversity and reliability of water sources in the township is critical for interpreting water storage behaviour and identifying pathways towards sustainable domestic water management in the context of Osun State’s broader water scarcity challenges.

The Aiba Water Reservoir, located within the Government Forest Reservation Area in Iwo, has served as a crucial source of potable water for domestic use since its commissioning in 1959. However, over the years, the dam has suffered from varying degrees of neglect, resulting in limited service provision, primarily benefitting residents near the dam. 25 Due to the unreliable and sporadic supply from the Aiba Water Works, coupled with concerns over surface water contamination, many inhabitants have resorted to subsurface sources. Yet, this alternative presents challenges, as well yields are only reliable during the rainy season. During periods of intense heat and reduced rainfall, water levels drop significantly due to the shallow nature of the wells. Furthermore, some wells are compromised due to unhygienic locations, posing health risks.1,28

Sampling Techniques and Data Collection

The sampling frame comprised all occupied residential households within Iwo township, Osun State, Nigeria. The town was stratified into 4 sections—Gidigbo, Molete, Oke-Oba I, and Oke-Oba II—based on land area rather than population size to ensure comprehensive geographic coverage. Land-area stratification was chosen to capture spatial variations in water access, infrastructure distribution, and environmental conditions that population-based sampling may overlook, particularly where neighbourhood population data are limited or unreliable. This approach also reduced spatial clustering and facilitated efficient fieldwork. A recognised limitation of land-area stratification is the potential over-representation of sparsely populated areas and under-representation of high-density neighbourhoods. To address this, equal numbers of households were sampled from each section, and population differences were considered during data analysis through cautious interpretation of aggregated results.

Households were the primary sampling unit. Inclusion criteria were permanently occupied residential buildings with at least one adult (⩾18 years) able to provide information on household water sources, use, and storage practices. Vacant buildings, commercial premises, and households that declined consent were excluded. Due to financial constraints, a repeated cross-sectional sampling design was adopted. Between February and November 2023, twenty (20) households were randomly selected from each section per month, yielding 80 households monthly. To avoid repeated sampling, discreet, non-intrusive stickers were placed on selected houses in inconspicuous locations.

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire comprising 5 sections: (i) socio-demographic characteristics, (ii) domestic water sources, (iii) household water use patterns, (iv) water storage practices (container type, capacity, and storage duration), and (v) perceptions of water sufficiency and coping strategies. The questionnaire was pre-tested in a neighbouring community. Female household heads or primary female water managers were prioritised as respondents due to their central role in domestic water management; where unavailable, male heads or informed adults were interviewed. Of the 800 households targeted, 756 valid questionnaires were retrieved, representing a 94.5% response rate. Potential limitations include gender bias, sample size constraints, and uneven population representation, which were considered during analysis and interpretation.

Questionnaire Design, Structure, and Validation

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire designed to obtain consistent information on household water supply, storage practices, and utilisation in Iwo Township. The instrument was developed through a review of relevant literature on domestic water access and management in urban and semi-urban settings, ensuring alignment with the study objectives.

The questionnaire comprised 5 sections. The first captured respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics, including gender, age, education, occupation, household size, and length of residence, providing a basis for analysing socio-economic variations. The second focussed on household water sources, documenting types used—groundwater, surface water, rainwater harvesting, public piped supply, water vendors, and sachet water—along with frequency of use, seasonal reliability, and source preference. The third section examined water storage practices, including storage facilities, capacity, duration, refilling frequency, and treatment prior to storage. The fourth addressed water utilisation patterns across domestic activities and seasonal variations, while the final section explored challenges and coping strategies related to water scarcity, quality, and cost.

Most items were close-ended, using multiple-choice and Likert-scale formats to facilitate quantitative analysis, with a limited number of open-ended questions included to capture contextual insights. The questionnaire was administered in English and, where necessary, orally translated into Yoruba.

Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was pre-tested among households outside the sampling areas to assess clarity and relevance, leading to minor revisions. Content validity was ensured through literature review and expert consultation, while reliability was strengthened by clearly defined response categories and a structured format, promoting consistency throughout the 10-month data collection period.

Data Analysis

The acquired data were analysed using both descriptive and inferential statistical methods. Descriptive analysis included the tabulation of respondent characteristics such as age categories, income levels, beliefs about water storage, educational attainment, water-use categories, religious affiliations, household sizes, and gender distribution. Inferential statistics, specifically Factor Analysis (FA), were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software. FA was employed to identify and rank the key variables influencing water storage behaviours in the study area.

To assess the suitability of the dataset for FA, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s tests were conducted. The results indicated a factorable dataset, with a KMO value of 0.827 at a significance level of P < .05. In the analysis, the ranking factor, Eigenvalue, was set at a minimum threshold of 1.000, meaning that any variable falling below this threshold was deemed insignificant in explaining the factors influencing water storage.

Dominance of Household-Level Factors Over Water Availability

The predominance of household-associated variables indicates that water storage in the study area is associated with household-level adaptive responses to perceived water insecurity rather than being solely a direct outcome of physical water scarcity. While several African and LMIC studies have interpreted water storage primarily as a reaction to unreliable supply and infrastructural deficits,8,29 the present findings suggest that households tend to internalise supply uncertainty through storage practices regardless of actual source availability. This observation is consistent with arguments by Refs.9,30 who conceptualise households as risk managers that buffer uncertainty through behavioural and material adjustments, although causal relationships cannot be inferred from the cross-sectional data.

From an SDG 6 implementation perspective, this pattern points to the importance of household-responsive interventions that complement supply-side improvements. Such interventions include demand management policies, household-level storage support, and behaviourally informed water-use programmes aligned with SDG targets 6.1 and 6.4. These measures are generally more cost-effective and institutionally feasible than large-scale infrastructure expansion, particularly in resource-constrained LMIC settings. For example, targeted subsidies for safe storage containers, promotion of household water efficiency practices, and incremental metring schemes can be implemented at relatively low to moderate cost while strengthening household resilience to supply fluctuations.31,32

Household Age Composition

Household age composition emerged as the most influential factor, explaining 17.763% of the total variance (eigenvalue = 4.618). Rather than implying a causal effect, this association suggests that age composition reflects internal labour organisation within households, where younger members often assume responsibility for water collection. In such contexts, water storage is associated with efforts to reduce physical burden and time costs, helping households manage daily water needs more efficiently. This interpretation extends earlier findings by Refs.33,34 by clarifying the functional role of age structure in shaping storage behaviour.

From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.b), this association highlights the potential value of labour- and time-saving interventions, such as improved access points, shorter collection distances, and household-level storage support in areas with a high proportion of younger residents. These interventions are generally low- to medium-cost and operationally feasible within existing local government and community-based frameworks, offering a pragmatic complement to large-scale infrastructure investments. Recognising the associative nature of this relationship ensures that policy responses remain adaptive and sensitive to household dynamics rather than prescriptive.

Sanitation Infrastructure as a Driver of Storage Demand

The number of toilets within a household contributed 12.154% to the variance (eigenvalue = 3.160), highlighting sanitation infrastructure as a key demand multiplier. Households with multiple toilets have higher and more continuous water needs, making storage important for maintaining sanitation standards during supply interruptions.24,34,35 From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.2), this association suggests that integrated water–sanitation planning, such as pairing sanitation upgrades with household-level storage support or minimum water reliability standards, could help maintain hygiene without relying solely on supply improvements. These interventions are generally low- to moderate-cost and feasible in LMIC contexts, and recognising the cross-sectional design, the observed relationship should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Water Availability at the Source

Availability of water at the source explained 11.548% of the variance (eigenvalue = 3.003), indicating that households tend to increase storage when supply is perceived as limited, particularly during dry periods. 29 While this factor has comparatively lower influence than household characteristics, it highlights the importance of supply reliability in shaping storage behaviour. From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.4), interventions such as community-scale storage systems, seasonal buffering, and aquifer protection could help households cope with fluctuations. These measures are generally moderate-cost and feasible in LMIC contexts, and given the cross-sectional design, the association should be interpreted as indicative rather than causal, serving to guide adaptive water management strategies rather than assuming direct influence.

Religious Practices and Normative Water Use

Religious beliefs accounted for 10.882% of the variance (eigenvalue = 2.829), reflecting the role of culturally embedded water use practices. Regular religious activities, such as ablution and ritual cleanliness, are associated with households maintaining reliable water reserves.37-39 From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.b), this suggests that faith-based engagement and culturally sensitive awareness campaigns could encourage safe storage, hygiene, and efficient water use. These interventions are generally low-cost, operationally feasible, and can leverage existing community structures, while recognising that the cross-sectional data indicate association rather than causation.

Beliefs About Water Storage and Behavioural Orientation

Respondents’ beliefs regarding water storage explained 8.980% of the variance (eigenvalue = 2.335), highlighting the role of perception and attitude in influencing storage behaviour. 40 Some households viewed storage as essential, while others did not, even under similar supply conditions, demonstrating that infrastructure alone may not change practices. 41 From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.b), this suggests that behavioural and risk communication interventions, such as household education on safe storage, water conservation messaging, and community engagement programmes, could complement technical solutions. These interventions are generally low-cost and highly feasible in LMIC contexts, and the cross-sectional design indicates association rather than causation, guiding policy in a context-sensitive manner.

Household Assets as Mechanisms of Behavioural Change

Household asset ownership contributed 8.934% to the variance (eigenvalue = 2.303), reflecting the influence of socio-economic status on water storage behaviour.9,42-44 Households with water-dependent appliances often have higher baseline demand and greater capacity to invest in storage infrastructure, such as large tanks or pumps. From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.4), interventions could include tiered water tariffs, incentivised metring, and targeted support for household-level storage systems, which are generally moderate-cost and feasible in LMIC urban and peri-urban settings. The cross-sectional nature of the data means these associations should be interpreted as indicative rather than causal, serving to guide context-sensitive, equitable water management policies.

Storage Methods and Alternative Water Sources

Storage methodology explained 6.775% of the variance (eigenvalue = 1.762), reflecting how container size and type are associated with household water conservation behaviours.45-47 Smaller containers often require more frequent refilling, which can reinforce habitual storage practices. Similarly, the availability of alternative water sources accounted for 5.677% of variance (eigenvalue = 1.476), with households storing more water where alternative sources are limited.48,49 From an SDG 6 perspective (Targets 6.1 and 6.4), interventions could promote safe, appropriately sized storage containers, rainwater harvesting, and regulated access to alternative sources, which are generally low- to moderate-cost and operationally feasible in LMIC contexts. The cross-sectional design indicates these relationships are associative rather than causal, providing guidance for context-sensitive and behaviourally informed water management strategies.

Integrative Synthesis

Collectively, 6 of the 8 extracted factors relate to household characteristics, accounting for 65.489% of the total variance (79.18% of the absolute explained variance), while water availability variables contributed only 17.225%. This imbalance underscores that household dynamics—including age composition, sanitation infrastructure, asset ownership, beliefs, religious practices, and storage methods—are strongly associated with water storage behaviour in African and LMIC contexts.9,50 Rather than reflecting a direct response to scarcity, these associations suggest that storage practices are shaped by household agency, socio-economic status, behavioural orientation, and adaptive capacity.

From an SDG 6 perspective, these findings highlight the importance of household-focussed interventions that complement supply-side improvements. Policy options include household-level storage support, targeted education and behavioural campaigns, culturally sensitive engagement with religious institutions, metring, tiered tariffs, and source diversification strategies, which are generally low- to moderate-cost and operationally feasible in LMIC contexts. When designed to align with household realities, such measures can strengthen resilience to supply fluctuations and promote more sustainable water management, while recognising that the cross-sectional nature of the data limits causal inference.

Water storage practices in Iwo Township closely resemble those in regions facing similar climatic, economic, and infrastructural challenges. Factor analysis (FA) highlights key variables influencing these practices, including family age composition (17.763%), number of toilets (12.154%), and water availability at the source (11.548%). Households with younger members are more likely to prioritise water storage, a trend also observed by Refs.51,52 in rural African countries, where younger individuals frequently handle water collection.

The number of toilets increases household water demand for sanitation, aligning with findings by Refs.53-55 in rapidly urbanising areas of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where inadequate infrastructure necessitates home-based storage. Religious beliefs (10.882%) also significantly influence storage practices, as seen in Bangladesh, where water is essential for religious rituals.56,57

Wealthier households, influenced by home assets (8.934%), are more likely to invest in larger storage systems, mirroring trends noted by Ref. 58 in Turkey. Additionally, the choice of storage method (6.775%), often involving smaller containers, leads to more frequent water collection, a pattern also observed by Ref. 16 in Tanzania.

Overall, the socio-demographic, cultural, and economic factors shaping water storage in Iwo Township reflect broader regional trends, underscoring the need for context-specific water management strategies.

Policy Implications of the Findings

Existing water policy frameworks across Africa and other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have largely prioritised supply-side interventions, particularly the expansion of infrastructure and centralised water systems, on the assumption that improved availability will automatically translate into improved household water security. While such approaches remain important, the findings of this study challenge this dominant paradigm by demonstrating that household-level socio-demographic and behavioural factors play a more decisive role in shaping water storage practices than water availability alone. This divergence highlights a critical policy gap in current water management strategies within tropical and resource-abundant settings.

To translate these insights into actionable policy, local and regional governments should adopt household-centric water management frameworks that explicitly account for variations in household composition, assets, and beliefs. Rather than uniform interventions, policies should support decentralised and affordable water storage solutions tailored to different household profiles, including targeted subsidies for improved storage containers, incentives for household-scale rainwater harvesting, and support for community-managed supplementary storage systems where appropriate.

Furthermore, unlike conventional public awareness campaigns in many African and LMIC contexts—which are often generic and infrastructure-focussed—this study underscores the need for targeted behavioural and socio-cultural engagement strategies. Water education and risk communication programmes should be designed to reflect household size, economic capacity, and religious or cultural practices that influence water use and storage. Such tailored messaging can improve uptake of safe storage practices and reduce water-related health risks without imposing high infrastructural costs.

Finally, effective policy translation requires empowering households as active water managers rather than passive recipients of supply. Policymakers should institutionalise household-level capacity-building initiatives, including training on safe water storage, basic treatment methods, rainwater harvesting techniques, and routine maintenance of storage facilities. Embedding these programmes within local governance structures and community organisations can enhance resilience to supply fluctuations and improve long-term sustainability.

To sum it up, the study provides evidence that achieving water security and advancing SDG 6 in African and LMIC contexts demands a shift from infrastructure-dominated policy models towards integrated approaches that combine supply improvements with household-focussed behavioural and socio-demographic interventions. This policy reorientation offers a pragmatic and context-sensitive pathway for strengthening water management outcomes in tropical urban environments.

Future Research Directions

This study underscores the need for further investigation into how household characteristics influence water storage behaviour. Future research should explore:

(i) The role of socio-economic status, gender dynamics, and cultural practices in water management decisions. (ii) The impact of education and community-based interventions on changing water-related behaviours. (iii) Longitudinal studies to assess how changes in household composition or income affect water storage practices over time.

By understanding these factors in greater detail, policymakers can design more nuanced and effective interventions, ensuring sustainable water access in line with SDG 6.

Limitations of the Study

Despite the insights generated, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is based on self-reported questionnaire data, which may be affected by recall bias and subjective responses, particularly regarding water usage volumes, storage duration, and seasonal variations. Such responses may not always reflect actual household practices.

Second, the sample size was constrained by financial and logistical limitations, which may limit the extent to which the findings can be generalised to the entire population of Iwo Township. Although efforts were made to ensure spatial coverage across the 4 sections of the town, dividing the study area based on land area rather than population size may have resulted in uneven representation of densely and sparsely populated neighbourhoods.

Third, the prioritisation of female household heads, while contextually justified given their primary role in domestic water management, may have under-represented male perspectives on water access and decision-making. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the survey limits the ability to capture long-term changes in water availability and household behaviour beyond the study period.

Finally, the study did not include direct measurement of water quantity or quality, relying instead on respondents’ perceptions and reported practices. Future studies could address these limitations by incorporating longitudinal designs, larger sample sizes, and objective measurements of water supply reliability and quality

Conclusion and Recommendations

This study demonstrates that water insecurity in tropical humid regions is not merely a function of physical water availability but is fundamentally shaped by household-level socio-demographic and behavioural dynamics. By foregrounding household characteristics as the primary drivers of water storage behaviour, the study reframes prevailing assumptions in African and LMIC water research that infrastructure expansion alone is sufficient to secure domestic water access. This shift in emphasis represents a critical contribution to understanding water management in resource-abundant yet supply-unstable environments.

The findings suggest that effective water governance must move beyond supply-centred models towards integrated, household-responsive strategies that recognise households as active managers of water rather than passive beneficiaries. For policymakers, this implies embedding socio-demographic considerations into water planning, designing context-specific storage and demand-management interventions, and aligning water policies with everyday household practices. Such an approach strengthens the social sustainability of water systems and enhances their resilience to supply fluctuations.

From an SDG 6 perspective, the study offers a forward-looking insight: achieving universal and safely managed water services will depend as much on transforming household water behaviours and capacities as on expanding infrastructure coverage. Progress towards SDG 6 in African and LMIC contexts therefore requires policies that simultaneously address service provision, household empowerment, and behavioural adaptation. Recognising this interdependence provides a more realistic and inclusive pathway for advancing water security in rapidly urbanising tropical regions.

Future research should build on this framework through comparative studies across ecological zones and settlement types to assess the generalisability of household-centred water management models. Such evidence will be essential for developing adaptive, scalable, and behaviourally informed policies capable of accelerating SDG 6 implementation across sub-Saharan Africa and other LMIC settings.
