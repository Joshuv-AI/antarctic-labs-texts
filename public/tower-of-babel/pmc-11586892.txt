Efficacy of Low‐Dose Ketamine and Propofol in the Treatment of Experimental Refractory Status Epilepticus on Male Rats
Authors: Gaye Boztepe Yılmaz, Kemal Tolga Saraçoğlu, Uğur Aykın, Metehan Akça, Cumaali Demirtaş, Ayten Saraçoğlu, Mehmet Yıldırım
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRefractory status epilepticus (RSE) is a condition with serious mortality and morbidity rate, resistant to benzodiazepine and second‐line antiepileptic drugs. This study aimed to electrophysiologically investigate the combination of NMDA receptor antagonist ketamine and GABAergic agent propofol in an RSE model induced by lithium‐pilocarpine in male Sprague–Dawley rats. Seventy‐two male Sprague–Dawley rats were divided into nine groups. The RSE model was induced by subcutaneous injection of lithium‐CI (5 mEq/kg) and intraperitoneal injection of pilocarpine‐HCl (320 mg/kg), after implanting tripolar EEG electrode. Ketamine (30, 60, and 90 mg/kg), propofol (20, 40, and 80 mg/kg), and combinations of both drugs (15 + 20 and 30 + 40 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally to animals with RSE. Video‐EEG recordings were taken after inducing model and 48 h later. The efficacy of drugs was statistically evaluated based on spike frequencies (spikes/min) and amplitudes (mV). Compared to RSE group, it was determined that 30 and 60 mg/kg doses of ketamine provided effective seizure control and prevented mortality (p < 0.001), while the 90 mg/kg showed toxic effects in all animals and caused mortality. The 80 mg/kg dose of propofol provided seizure control and reduced the mortality rate to 16.7% (p < 0.001), whereas the 20 mg/kg resulted in a 100% mortality rate. The low‐dose ketamine+propofol (15 + 20 mg/kg) combination provided early onset seizure control and were as effective as 80 mg/kg propofol (p < 0.05). The study concluded that in the experimental RSE model, seizure control could be achieved with low‐dose combination of ketamine and propofol without the need for high doses as in monotherapy, thus preventing dose‐related adverse effects.Keywords: ketamine, lithium‐pilocarpine, propofol, status epilepticus

Introduction

Refractory status epilepticus (RSE) is defined as seizure activity that does not respond to first‐line benzodiazepines and second‐line non‐sedative antiepileptic drug treatment (Vasquez, Farias‐Moeller, and Tatum 2019). Intracranial infections, head trauma, and stroke constitute the main etiology of RSE. RSE develops in 10% to 30% of status epilepticus patients (Lowenstein and Alldredge 1998; Claassen et al. 2002; Mayer et al. 2002), with mortality rates ranging from 16% to 48% (Claassen et al. 2002; Zeiler et al. 2014).

For the treatment of RSE, intravenous administration of third‐line sedative and anesthetic antiepileptic drugs such as midazolam, pentobarbital (or thiopental as a prodrug), propofol, and ketamine is recommended (Rai and Drislane 2018). Despite the use of these recommended drugs, 20% of RSE patients do not respond to treatment for more than 24 h, resulting in super RSE (Rai and Drislane 2018). Benzodiazepines are effective in the early phase of status epilepticus but fail to treat prolonged status epilepticus, as documented both experimentally and clinically (Mayer et al. 2002). In prolonged seizure activity in both RSE and super RSE, gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors, the main targets of many drugs, become internalized, reducing the number of active GABAA receptors and mobilizing excitatory glutamate receptors to the cell surface. This reduces the effectiveness of commonly used treatment protocols and contributes to treatment resistance (Feng et al. 2008). Ketamine is a non‐competitive N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist. When GABAergic agents are inadequate in the treatment of epileptic seizures, ketamine can be effective in seizure control as an alternative drug (Fang and Wang 2015). The 2017 Hong Kong Status Epilepticus Guideline recommends ketamine as a fourth‐line treatment (Fung et al. 2017). However, it is also reported that there is insufficient clinical evidence for its use at this stage in the management of status epilepticus. In a meta‐analysis, the success rate was found to be 56.5% in 110 adult patients and 63.5% in 52 pediatric patients who were treated with ketamine for RSE (Zeiler et al. 2014). Conducting sufficient research on the efficacy of ketamine in the treatment of RSE appears quite challenging in clinical settings. In one of the few experimental studies, it was found that pre‐treatment with ketamine prevented epileptic seizures in rats convulsed with bicuculline (Schneider, de Lores, and Arnaiz 2013). There is a need to investigate and elucidate the efficacy of ketamine in RSE, particularly through experimental studies.

Another third‐line sedative and anesthetic antiepileptic drug used in the treatment of RSE is propofol (Rai and Drislane 2018). Propofol suppresses the central nervous system by activating GABAA receptors and inhibiting NMDA receptors, thereby preventing seizures. It is also effective on sodium and calcium channels (Kanai et al. 1999). Propofol is highly lipophilic, acts quickly, and unlike midazolam, does not accumulate in the body over time. The combined use of ketamine and propofol is often preferred, particularly in anesthesia applications, due to their mutual balance on the cardiovascular system and lack of respiratory depression (Nevešćanin Biliškov et al. 2022). Considering their effects on NMDA receptors, ketamine, and propofol hold significant positions as alternatives among numerous antiepileptics targeting GABA receptors.

Ketamine and propofol are generally applied separately as final treatment options for RSE, and the percentage of patients who achieve complete response appears low (Gaspard et al. 2013; Alvarez and Rossetti 2016). In cases of non‐response, doses are increased, leading to more frequent observation of undesirable side effects. Therefore, it is believed that better outcomes can be achieved in RSE patients through early simultaneous polytherapy with these drugs. In a retrospective study involving 67 patients, it was reported that RSE was resolved in 91% of cases when ketamine and propofol were used together (Sabharwal et al. 2015). The average duration of ketofol (ketamine and propofol) use was determined to be 3.6 days. A meta‐analysis in the literature, encompassing 18 studies, demonstrated that ketofol is associated with fewer complications and more effective outcomes compared to propofol alone (Jalili et al. 2016). However, it has been found that there are no studies in the literature examining the efficacy of ketamine and propofol in an experimental status epilepticus model. The concurrent use of multiple drugs in RSE appears to be a highly significant and effective treatment approach. However, due to the limitations of studies, there is no consensus on the effective dose of ketofol. This study aims to investigate the efficacy of propofol and ketamine applied concurrently in varying doses as polytherapy in a lithium‐pilocarpine‐induced RSE model in Sprague–Dawley rats.

Materials and Methods

Animals

A total of 72 male Sprague Dawley rats, aged 10–12 weeks, were divided into nine groups (n = 8) and used in the experiments. The number of animals for experimental groups decided with G*Power statistical power analysis software. The rats were housed under a 12/12 h light–dark cycle, at a temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and a humidity of 65%, with unlimited access to food and water. Animals were housed with four rats per cage, and cages were cleaned every 3 days. Physical enrichment was provided with wooden blocks and hiding boxes. The experimental protocol for this study was approved by the Hamidiye Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (decision number 06‐11, dated November 20, 2020).

Drugs

In this study, 90 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride and 10 mg/kg xylazine hydrochloride were used for anesthesia during surgical procedures; 3 mEq/kg lithium chloride and 320 mg/kg pilocarpine hydrochloride, a cholinergic‐muscarinic agonist, were used to induce the experimental RSE model. Scopolamine methyl bromide, a muscarinic antagonist, was used to reduce peripheral cholinergic effects, and scopolamine hydrochloride, another muscarinic antagonist, was used to counteract the effects of the seizure inducer. To terminate the induced seizure activity, ketamine hydrochloride was administered at doses of 20, 30, and 90 mg/kg, and propofol at doses of 20, 40, and 80 mg/kg.

Lithium chloride, pilocarpine hydrochloride, and scopolamine methyl bromide were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO); scopolamine hydrochloride from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, USA); ketamine hydrochloride from Pfizer (Istanbul, Turkey); xylazine hydrochloride from Bayer (Istanbul, Turkey); and propofol from Polifarma (Istanbul, Turkey).

Experimental Design

This study investigated the effects of the combined early use of ketamine and propofol on an RSE model induced by lithium‐pilocarpine, aiming to determine safe usage doses to reduce potential side effects of these anesthetic drugs in humans. The doses and routes of administration of ketamine and propofol were determined based on literature (Borris, Bertram, and Kapur 2000; Alves et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2012; Schneider, de Lores, and Arnaiz 2013; Gonzales et al. 2015; Niquet et al. 2016, 2019).

Surgery and Electrode Placement

After the anesthesia induction, the depth of anesthesia was monitored every 10 min by pinching the foot to create a painful stimulus. To prevent hypothermia, a homeothermic blanket (Homeothermic Blanket System, Harvard Apparatus, South Natick, MA, USA) was used to maintain body temperature at 37°C. A scalp incision was made, exposing the Bregma. A tripolar EEG electrode (MS333/2A, P1 Technologies, Roanoke, VA) was fixed to the skull with two stainless steel screws and dental acrylic without pressing on the cortex. One active electrode of the tripolar EEG was placed in the left frontal cortex (AP +2 mm, L − 3.5 mm from Bregma), the other in the left parietal cortex (AP‐6 mm, L − 4 mm from Bregma), and the reference electrode was placed epidurally over the cerebellum at the midline. After electrode placement, the animals were allowed a 7‐day recovery period (Efendioglu et al. 2020; Gedikli, Akca, and Yildirim 2023) (Figure 1).

Refractory Status Epilepticus Model

To induce the RSE model, 3 mEq/kg lithium chloride (s.c.) was first injected to facilitate the effect of pilocarpine. Sixteen hours after lithium injection, 320 mg/kg pilocarpine hydrochloride (i.p.) was administered, and 30 min before pilocarpine, 1 mg/kg muscarinic antagonist scopolamine methyl bromide (i.p.) was given to reduce peripheral cholinergic effects (Niquet et al. 2016, 2017b). About 25 min after the onset of RSE, 10 mg/kg scopolamine hydrochloride (i.p.) was administered to all animals to counteract the effect of the seizure‐inducing pilocarpine, though it does not stop status epilepticus. Thirty minutes after the onset of RSE, the respective treatment regimens were administered intraperitoneally to each group (Figure 1).

Electrophysiological Recording

EEG recording was performed using a physiological data acquisition unit (PowerLab 16/35, AD Instruments, Sydney, Australia) with 16 channels from 16 subjects simultaneously. The EEG cable was connected to the EEG electrode, and each rat was allowed 30 min to acclimate to the environment and cables after. The EEG signals, amplified via a preamplifier (ML136 AnimalBioAmp, AD Instruments, Sydney, Australia) connected to the EEG cable, were digitized and monitored in real time by the PowerLab data acquisition unit and recorded. EEG recordings were performed and analyzed using LabChart Pro v8.1 software (AD Instruments, Castle Hill, Australia). The EEG sampling rate was set at 1024 Hz, and data were filtered within a 0.1–100 Hz band range (Velioglu et al. 2017; Gedikli, Akca, and Yildirim 2021) (Figure 2).

EEG trace examples of status epilepticus group with no treatment and ketamine, propofol and antiepileptic drug combination groups. Except the status epilepticus group, larger EEG traces represent activity between 90 and 100 min after administration of treatments (between 10 and 20 min for status epilepticus group). Smaller EEG traces represents activity of the chosen 1 min period in the larger EEG trace.

A baseline activity recording was taken for 30 min before the initiation of RSE. After the onset of status, approximately 3 h of EEG monitoring and behavioral seizure scoring (Racine 1972) were performed. The spikes occurring post‐pilocarpine injection, exceeding twice the baseline EEG amplitude, were considered status‐related discharges and seizure activity. The return of EEG amplitude to pre‐pilocarpine levels and the absence of spikes or sharp waves for at least 1 min were considered indicators of seizure suppression and termination. Additional 150‐min EEG recordings were taken 48 h after the induction of status epilepticus for further electrophysiological and behavioral seizure scoring (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

Data on spike frequency (Hz) and amplitude (mV) from EEG recordings were converted to numerical values using EEG recording software (LabChart v8.1, AD Instruments, Castle Hill, Australia). These data were compared using statistical analysis software (SPSS v.23, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of data distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which revealed non‐normal distribution. Consequently, data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis variance analysis, followed by the Mann–Whitney U test for pairwise comparisons.

The latency to the onset of status epilepticus, the percentage of mortality due to status, and mortality latency were compared between the other experimental groups and the status epilepticus group. Since all animals in the status epilepticus group died shortly after, comparisons of spike frequency and amplitude data between groups were made using the 20 mg/kg propofol group, which was considered to have a lesser effect. As all animals in the 20 mg/kg propofol group also died over time due to status, intergroup comparisons of data obtained at 48 h post‐status induction were made using the 40 mg/kg propofol group. All data were expressed as median (min‐max) and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Along with the p value, the chi‐square value (H) and degrees of freedom (df) for the Kruskal–Wallis (KW) test were also presented.

Results

A model of RSE was created in rats using lithium‐pilocarpine to investigate the effects of the simultaneous early administration of ketamine and propofol in patients who developed RSE. It was observed that Racine stage 4 and 5 seizures developed in every animal administered pilocarpine before medication. Since no treatment was applied to the status epilepticus group, all animals in this group died within a short period. In the other experimental groups, the spike frequencies after the RSE model was established but before drug administration were determined for the groups receiving 30 mg/kg ketamine, 60 mg/kg ketamine, 20 mg/kg propofol, 40 mg/kg propofol, 15 mg/kg ketamine +20 mg/kg propofol, and 30 mg/kg ketamine +40 mg/kg propofol as 527 (509–685), 511 (516–648), 495 (462–531), 491 (396–547), 521 (511–572), 502 (462–541), and 636 (470–692) spikes/min, respectively. When comparing spike frequencies, no significant difference was found between the groups in terms of seizure efficacy, indicating that the RSE model developed consistently. Three different doses (30, 60, and 90 mg/kg) of ketamine and (20, 40, and 80 mg/kg) propofol monotherapies and two different dose combinations (15–20 mg/kg and 30–40 mg/kg) of ketamine‐propofol polytherapies were administered to the relevant groups 30 min after RSE was established.

When the EEG of the 20 mg/kg propofol group was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, it was found that the pre‐drug spike frequency was 495 (462–531), and post‐drug spike frequencies at 40, 80, and 120 min were 251 (141–289), 129 (74–141), and 17 (15–110) spikes/min, respectively. A statistical comparison of the spike frequencies recorded 150 min after propofol injection with the pre‐injection spike frequency on the first day in the 20 mg/kg propofol group showed no significant difference. Post‐propofol spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 1.7 (0.9–2.6), 1.8 (0.9–2.1), and 0.8 (0.5–1.2) mV, respectively. The Racine score for all of the animals in the 20 mg/kg propofol group was 4–5 even after drug therapy. Since behavioral and electrophysiological seizure activity continued throughout the recording period in this group, and all subjects in the status epilepticus group were lost due to mortality, the 20 mg/kg propofol group was designated as the valid control group for statistical comparisons of the first‐day epileptiform activity recordings.

When the EEG of the 30 mg/kg ketamine group was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, the post‐ketamine spike frequencies at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 26 (1–336), 0 (0–224), and 0 (0–8) spikes/min, respectively. Compared to the control group (20 mg/kg propofol) on the first‐day EEG recordings, a significant decrease in spike frequency was detected at 90 (KW test H = 16.750, df = 6, p = 0.048) and 120 min (KW test H = 14.330, df = 6, p = 0.022) in the 30 mg/kg ketamine group (Figure 3). Post‐ketamine spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 0.7 (0–1.2), 0 (0–1.0), and 0 (0–0.6) mV, respectively. A significant decrease in spike amplitude was observed at 30 min (KW test H = 15.534, df = 6, p = 0.027) and between 70 and 110 min (for 70th min KW test H = 13.967, df = 6, p = 0.014; for 80th min KW test H = 11.050, df = 6, p = 0.025; for 90th min KW test H = 12.950, df = 6, p = 0.021; for 100th min KW test H = 14.498, df = 6, p = 0.021; for 110th min KW test H = 12.673, df = 6, p = 0.039) compared to the control group (20 mg/kg propofol) (Figure 4). The Racine score was 1 or 2 for most of the animals in the 30 mg/kg ketamine group after antiepileptic drugs, however, some animals showed racine stage 3 behavior rarely.

When the EEG of the 60 mg/kg ketamine group was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, the post‐ketamine spike frequencies at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 37 (1–161), 6 (0–37), and 1 (0–32) spikes/min, respectively. A significant decrease in spike frequency was detected at 40 (KW test H = 19.246, df = 6, p = 0.019), 50 (KW test H = 18.785, df = 6, p = 0.011), 60 (KW test H = 17.369, df = 6, p = 0.01), 70 (KW test H = 17.005, df = 6, p = 0.01), 80 (KW test H = 13.639, df = 6, p = 0.01), and 90 min (KW test H = 17.005, df = 6, p = 0.014) compared to the control group (20 mg/kg propofol) (Figure 3). Post‐ketamine spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 0.9 (0–1.1), 0.7 (0–1.3), and 0.4 (0–0.9) mV, respectively. Compared to the control group (20 mg/kg propofol), a significant decrease in spike amplitude was detected at 50 (KW test H = 15.137, df = 6, p = 0.020), 60 (KW test H = 14.968, df = 6, p = 0.032), 70 (KW test H = 13.967, df = 6, p = 0.032), and 80 min (KW test H = 11.050, df = 6, p = 0.032; Figure 4). Racine scores of animals in the 60 mg/kg ketamine group after the antiepileptic drug therapy were similar to ketamine 30 mg/kg.

In the 90 mg/kg ketamine group, all animals died within an average of 2 min after intraperitoneal injection of 90 mg/kg ketamine, even though status epilepticus developed following pilocarpine injection. It was evaluated that the high dose of 90 mg/kg ketamine used in this study caused a toxic effect.

When the EEG of the 40 mg/kg propofol group was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, the post‐propofol spike frequencies at 20, 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 136 (0–336), 121 (0–221), 41 (0–241), and 40 (0–62) spikes/min, respectively. A significant decrease in spike frequency was detected at 40 (KW test H = 19.246, df = 6, p = 0.042), 50 (KW test H = 18.785, df = 6, p = 0.033), 60 (KW test H = 17.369, df = 6, p = 0.014), and 70 min (KW test H = 17.005, df = 6, p = 0.027) compared to the control group (20 mg/kg propofol) (Figure 3). Post‐propofol spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 1.1 (0–1.9), 0.8 (0–1.9), and 0.7 (0–0.9) mV, respectively. Compared to the control group (20 mg/kg propofol), a significant decrease in spike amplitude was detected only at 50 min (KW test H = 15.137, df = 6, p = 0.039; Figure 4). Racine scores of animals in the 40 mg/kg propofol group after antiepileptic drugs were highly diversive. Some of the animals showed seizure behavior equal to Racine stage 1 or 2, while others continued seizing on Racine stages 3, 4, and 5.

When the EEG of the 80 mg/kg propofol group was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, the post‐propofol spike frequencies at 20, 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 0 (0–0), 0 (0–1), 0 (0–4), and 0 (0–0) spikes/min, respectively. A significant decrease in spike frequency was detected at all time points from 10 to 130 min (for 10th min KW test H = 22.298, df = 6, p = 0.011, and for 130th min KW test H = 12.796, df = 6, p = 0.016) and at 150 min KW test H = 10.139, df = 6, p = 0.033 compared to the control group (Figure 3). Post‐propofol spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 0 (0–0.7), 0 (0–1.0), and 0 (0–0) mV, respectively. A significant decrease in spike amplitude was detected between 10 and 130 min after propofol injection (for 10th min KW test H = 17.883, df = 6, p = 0.011 and for 130th min KW test H = 12.521, df = 6, p = 0.016; Figure 4). Racine scores of animals in the 80 mg/kg propofol after antiepileptic drugs were 0 or 1.

When the EEG of the low‐dose interaction group (15 mg/kg ketamine +20 mg/kg propofol) was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, the post‐drug spike frequencies at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 2 (1–5), 0 (0–2), and 0 (0–0) spikes/min, respectively. In this low‐dose combination group, a significant decrease in spike frequency was observed at all time points from 10 to 120 min compared to the control group. The p values at 20, 60, and 120 min were p = 0.034, p = 0.028, and p = 0.037, respectively (Figure 3). Post‐drug spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min in the 15 mg/kg ketamine +20 mg/kg propofol group were found to be 0.7 (0.5–0.8), 0 (0–0.9), and 0 (0–0) mV, respectively. A significant decrease in spike amplitude was observed at 20–30‐40 (p = 0.034), 60–70 (p = 0.028), 90–100 (p = 0.034), and 110 min (p = 0.037) compared to the control group (Figure 4). Racine scores of animals in the low‐dose interaction group after antiepileptic drugs were 0 or 1.

When the EEG of the high‐dose interaction group (30 mg/kg ketamine +40 mg/kg propofol) was examined on the first day after pilocarpine administration, the post‐drug spike frequencies at 40, 80, and 120 min were found to be 5 (0–7), 8 (0–8), and 3 (2–3) spikes/min, respectively. In this high‐dose combination group, a significant decrease in spike frequency was detected at all time points from 10 to 80 min compared to the control group (Figure 3). The p values at 20, 60, and 80 min were p = 0.032, p = 0.034, and p = 0.032, respectively. Post‐drug spike amplitude values at 40, 80, and 120 min in the 30 mg/kg ketamine +40 mg/kg propofol group were found to be 0.4 (0–0.4), 0.5 (0–0.6), and 0.5 (0.5–0.6) mV, respectively. A significant decrease in spike amplitude was observed at 10 (p = 0.028), 20 (p = 0.032), 30–40 (p = 0.034), and 60–80 min (p = 0.034) compared to the control group (Figure 4). Racine scores of animals in the high‐dose interaction group after antiepileptic drugs were 0 or 1.

After pilocarpine injection on the first day, due to the lack of treatment in the status epilepticus group, all animals in this group died due to seizures and no healthy recordings could be obtained due to drug toxicity in the 90 mg/kg ketamine group. EEG recordings were taken on the first day of status epilepticus in all remaining groups, allowing statistical comparisons. However, since all rats in the control group (20 mg/kg propofol) died due to seizures after the first day, the 40 mg/kg propofol group was used as the control group for the 48‐h EEG recordings. It was confirmed that the status epilepticus model consistently developed across all groups. The initial latency of drug efficacy was calculated by identifying the first minute with a significant reduction in spike frequency in the groups. Combination groups together with the high‐dose propofol group were determined to achieve drug efficacy at the earliest (Figure 5). As drug efficacy latency was based on data from statistical comparisons of spike frequency among groups, direct statistical comparison for drug efficacy latency itself is not available. Discussion

In the presented study, administration of 60 mg/kg ketamine suppressed status epilepticus‐related seizures starting from the 40th minute, while 80 mg/kg propofol suppressed seizures from the moment of injection. Low‐dose administration of 20 mg/kg propofol, however, did not show a significant effect in preventing seizures either behaviorally or in terms of EEG activity (Figure 2). Nevertheless, the application of 20 mg/kg propofol and 15 mg/kg ketamine as a low‐dose combination, resulted in a dramatic decrease and suppression in spike frequency and amplitude while also significantly halted seizures behaviorally. The effects of this low‐dose combination were comparable to those obtained in the high‐dose propofol group. Thus, in cases of RSE, it has been demonstrated that instead of increasing the dose in monotherapy and raising the risk of unwanted side effects, a low‐dose combination with an additional agent might be a better option for similar therapeutic potential. Interestingly, administration of ketamine at a dose of 90 mg/kg exhibited a toxic effect, leading to the sudden death of all administered animals within 2–3 min. This dose, which is frequently used for anesthesia in adult rats, is not expected to cause such severe and high mortality rates. This observation in the study suggests that high doses of ketamine might cause cardiovascular and/or respiratory toxic effects in status animals. When the high‐dose combination group was examined, it was observed that seizures were strongly suppressed, but mortality was higher compared to the low‐dose combination group. At the 48th hour of the study, significant suppression of spike frequency and amplitude persisted in the 60 mg/kg ketamine group, whereas the effect of suppressing seizures did not persist in the 80 mg/kg propofol group. A limitation of our study is only male rats were used to avoid any variations caused by sex differences and the estrous cycle. Further research was needed to examine whether the effects of drugs used in our study to prevent RSE would be the same on female Sprague–Dawley rats.

There are limited studies comparing the effects of monotherapy and polytherapy in the first‐line treatment of RSE (Alvarez and Rossetti 2016). In an animal model of status epilepticus, it was found that when a combination of benzodiazepine, ketamine, and valproate was applied, only the ketamine‐valproate combination provided more effective seizure control and exhibited fewer toxic effects compared to benzodiazepine monotherapy (Wasterlain et al. 2011). In the presented study, the aim was to exhibit an antagonistic effect against the increased NMDA receptor activity due to status by using ketamine. The use of GABAA receptor agonists and NMDA receptor antagonists together is not recommended as first‐line therapy in current therapeutic guidelines (Kanner et al. 2018). The efficacy of such combinations has been studied in animal models (Niquet et al. 2017a; Niquet et al. 2019; Lumley et al. 2021; Marrero‐Rosado et al. 2020). In a lithium‐pilocarpine‐induced status epilepticus model, the combination of midazolam‐ketamine was shown to reduce and even stop seizures more effectively compared to high‐dose monotherapy groups, and also more effectively improved low performance in the Morris water maze test related to spatial memory (Niquet et al. 2016). In another similar study, when the density of NeuN (+) cells in the thalamus, amygdala, and piriform cortex was examined 36 days after soman exposure, the number of cells in the midazolam‐ketamine combination group was found to be the same as in the control group not exposed to soman (Lumley et al. 2019). The strong effects of different drug combinations such as diazepam‐ketamine or diazepam‐phenobarbital‐scopolamine have also been examined in preclinical studies and it has been determined that such combinations can stop seizures even when applied in the late period after the onset of status epilepticus, unlike high‐dose monotherapies (Löscher 2015). Drug combinations planned according to the receptor change hypothesis not only terminate RSE but also appear to prevent long‐term neuronal damage, spatial memory loss, and epileptogenesis. A retrospective study also reported good results for ketamine and propofol treatment in 67 patients with super RSE (Sabharwal et al. 2015).

In conclusion, in an experimental RSE induced by lithium‐pilocarpine in rats, it was shown that seizure control can be achieved without the need for high‐dose monotherapies using a low‐dose combination of ketamine and propofol, thereby avoiding unwanted effects associated with dose escalation.
