Bilateralist base-extension semantics with incompatible proofs and refutations
Victor Barroso-Nascimento, Maria Osório Costa, Elaine Pimentel

Abstract
Logical bilateralism challenges traditional concepts of logic by treating assertion and denial as independent yet opposed acts. While initially devised to justify classical logic, its constructive variants show that both acts admit intuitionistic interpretations. This paper presents a bilateral system where a formula cannot be both provable and refutable without contradiction, offering a framework for modelling epistemic entities, such as mathematical proofs and refutations, that exclude inconsistency. The logic is formalised through a bilateral natural deduction system with desirable proof-theoretic properties, including normalisation. We also introduce a base-extension semantics requiring explicit constructions of proofs and refutations while preventing them from being established for the same formula. The semantics is proven sound and complete with respect to the calculus. Finally, we show that our notion of refutation corresponds to David Nelson’s constructive falsity, extending rather than revising intuitionistic logic and reinforcing the system’s suitability for representing constructive epistemic reasoning.

Introduction

A central issue in constructive logic concerns the nature of refutation and how falsity should be represented within a constructive framework. In this context, David Nelson
[
10
]
was the first to highlight a subtle but important distinction between two kinds of refutative results that can be established constructively for statements of the form
\forall x.\phi
.

The first, which we may call
weak falsity
, is demonstrated by exhibiting a procedure that transforms any construction of the mathematical object into a construction of a contradiction. This is the notion of falsity traditionally adopted in constructive logics, as witnessed by the fact that
\neg\phi
is often defined as
\phi\to\bot
. In contrast,
strong falsity
(represented by Nelson’s strong negation
\sim\!\!\phi
) is proven by explicitly constructing witnesses to the falsity of the statement itself. In short, to establish the weak falsity of a statement such as
x=y
, one assumes
x=y
and derives a contradiction; to establish its strong falsity, one must instead construct two objects
x
and
y
for which
x=y
does not hold.

The significance of this difference resides in the fact that from the existence of a counterexample to a claim we can trivially obtain a procedure reducing purported constructions of its object to a proof of absurdity, but we
cannot
in general obtain a particular counterexample to a claim from a procedure reducing it to a contradiction. After proving that
P(a)
does not hold for a particular
a
and assuming that
P(x)
holds for all
x
we may obtain a contradiction by simply substituting
x
by
a
. However,
from the existence of a proof that assuming
P(x)
for all
x
leads to a contradiction, it does not follow that we can exhibit a specific object
a
for which
P(a)
fails. This is exactly why
\neg\forall xP(x)
does not imply
\exists x\neg P(x)
in,
e.g.
, intuitionistic or minimal logic.

Nelson argues in
[
10
]
that this novel conception of negation naturally entails a revision of the principles of intuitionistic logic, thereby explaining why these systems are often regarded as
alternatives
to intuitionism rather than mere extensions of it.
In fact, although both weak and strong falsity may reasonably be regarded as constructive, the contents of a proof of strong negation are substantially stronger. This also leads to a surprising (constructive) recovery of some classical equalities. In Nelson’s original definitions, from a proof of
\sim\!\!\forall xP(x)
it follows the existence of a particular
a
for which
\sim\!\!P(a)
holds, hence also
\exists x\!\sim\!\!P(x)
. Since a proof of
\exists x\!\sim\!\!P(x)
requires production of some object
a
for which
\sim\!\!P(a)
holds, if such a proof is available
a
itself is an effective counterexample to the claim that
\forall xP(x)
, hence it follows that
\sim\!\!\forall xP(x)
. This means that, just as in classical logic,
\exists x\!\sim\!\!P(x)
is equivalent to
\sim\!\!\forall xP(x)
, although the reason for this equivalence is entirely different. In classical logic, it holds because the conditions for proving an existential statement are
weaker
, whereas in Nelson’s logic it holds because the conditions for proving a negation are
stronger
.

Since to constructively prove
\phi
we need to provide an effective construction of
\phi
, and to constructively prove
\sim\!\!\phi
we need to provide an effective counterexample to
\phi
, Nelson’s constructive falsity can be interpreted as putting the contents of positive and negative statements on equal footing. This is essentially what is also done in
logical bilateralism
, an approach to logic in which positive and negative statements are not taken as mutually interdefinable. Bilateralism was originally presented by Rumfitt to provide a proof-theoretic justification of classical logic
[
18
]
, but it was later repurposed for intuitionism and its falsificationalist dual
[
23
]
through the logic
\sf{2Int}
, presented by Wansing in
[
24
]
.

In short,
\sf{2Int}
combines the verificationist account of constructivism expressed through intuitionistic logic and its falsificationalist dual-intuitionistic counterpart in a single, unified framework.
\sf{2Int}
is shown not only to be a conservative extension of both intuitionistic and dual-intuitionistic logic, but also to be embeddable in both. As suggested in
[
25
]
, one can associate the
disprovability
of
\phi
with the constructive strong negation
\sim\!\!\phi
:

\vdash^{dis}\phi\mbox{ iff }\vdash\sim\!\!\phi\mbox{ and }\vdash^{dis}\sim\!\!\phi\mbox{ iff }\vdash\phi

Nelson proposed several formalisations of this idea
[
10
,
13
]
. It was first introduced through the system
\sf{N3}
, explicitly designed to provide a formal account of falsity in mathematics
[
10
]
.
System
\sf{N4}
, a paraconsistent variant of
\sf{N3}
, is obtained by dropping the explosive axiom
\phi\to(\sim\!\!\phi\to\psi)
.
Semantically, this distinction is reflected in the fact that
\sf{N4}
allows for simultaneous proofs and refutations of the same atomic formulas, whereas
\sf{N3}
does not
[
24
, p. 447]

\sf{N3}

have also been extensively studied (see,

One may interpret this difference as indicating that
\sf{N4}
and
\sf{N3}
formalise
distinct
notions of strong falsity. For instance, if the aim is to model the set of beliefs of an agent, it is reasonable to allow models that support both truth and falsity without leading to explosion, as an individual may hold inconsistent beliefs without thereby accepting every statement as true or false. This perspective is well captured by Wansing’s
\sf{2Int}

[
25
]
. By contrast, if the goal is to capture constructive falsity within mathematics, such behaviour is undesirable, since a mathematical proof of an object cannot coherently coexist with its refutation.

In this work, we focus on the latter notion of strong falsity. Specifically, we investigate the proof-theoretic structure of falsity by introducing a natural deduction system for a logic of proofs and refutations,
\sf{BPR}
, which extends
\sf{2Int}
(Section
2
). We establish that
\sf{BPR}
enjoys weak normalisability, the subformula property, and consistency, both for proofs and for refutations (Section
3

We then turn to semantics, introducing a base-extension semantics for
\sf{BPR}
(Section
4

[

[

, epistemic constraints are proposed here for the first time. The semantics makes explicit the idea of counterexample construction through atomic refutational rules, ensuring that no formula is simultaneously provable and refutable within the same atomic base. Furthermore, it retains all properties established in
[
2

, including the possibility of semantic embeddings via bilateral semantic harmony.

Finally, we prove that
\sf{BPR}
is sound and complete with respect to this semantics (Section
5

6
).

As noted in

In

\Delta

3
Normalisation and consistency

In this section we prove the normalisation theorem for
\sf{BPR}
. The aim is to show that if there exists a proof (respectively, a refutation) of a formula

\phi
from a set of assumptions
\Theta
and contra-assumptions
\Delta
(that is, a set of premises
\Gamma=\Delta\cup\Theta
), then there exists a derivation of the same kind in
normal form
.

The proof of the normalisation theorem provides a procedure that allows the elimination of detours, that is, redundant combinations of inference steps that do not contribute to the essential logical structure of the derivation. In standard natural deduction, these detours typically correspond to successive introduction and elimination steps for the same connective, but in our setting they also include certain
proof-refutation
interactions and other mixed rule combinations.

Once normalisation is established, the standard results follow. In particular, the subformula property holds: every formula occurring in a normal derivation is a subformula of either an undischarged assumption or the final conclusion. Consistency also follows; in the case of
\sf{2Int}
, this means showing both that
\bot
is not provable and that
\top
is not refutable.

The presence of paired proof and refutation rules in
\sf{BPR}
introduces additional normalisation cases, which are largely analogous to the standard ones, except for the distinctive cases
BPR(+)
and
BPR(-)
. These require certain adaptations to the usual normalisation strategy, but beyond these modifications, the overall approach remains unchanged.

Base-extension semantics

In this section we provide a base-extension semantics (

Proof-theoretic semantics
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
(
\sf{PtS}

\sf{PtS}

proof
, emphasizing the fundamental nature of proofs as a means through which we gain demonstrative knowledge, particularly in mathematical contexts. This makes
\sf{PtS}
a superior approach for comprehending reasoning since it ensures that the meaning of logical operators, such as connectives in logics, is defined based on their usage in inferences.

Base-extension semantics
[
19
]
(
\sf{BeS}
) is a strand of
\sf{PtS}
where proof-theoretic validity is defined relative to a given collection
S
of inference rules defined over basic formulas of the language.
Indeed, validity w.r.t. a set
S
of atomic rules has the general shape

\Vdash_{S}p\qquad\mbox{iff}\qquad\vdash_{S}p

where
\vdash_{S}p
indicates that
p
is
derivable
in the proof system determined by
S
. After defining validity for atoms one can also define validity for logical connectives via semantic clauses that express proof conditions (
e.g.
,
A\land B
is provable from
S
if and only if both
A
and
B
are provable from
S
), which results in a framework that evaluates propositions exclusively in terms of proofs of its constituents.

Our semantics require use of some modifications presented in
[
3
]
concerning the use of bases with consistency constraints, without which our essential Theorem
4.1
would not hold. Broadly speaking, we combine the bilateral semantics presented in
[
2
]
with the modifications presented in
[
3
]
plus a few novel ones. Incidentally, a conceptual introduction to
\sf{BeS}
which contextualizes the difference introduced by the consistency constraint is presented in
[
3
]
, whereas a introduction to
\sf{BeS}
which relates it to logical bilateralism is presented in
[
2
]
.

Constructive falsity and refutation

As mentioned in the introduction, David Nelson was the first to point out the existence of a constructive concept of falsity which is stronger than that of intuitionistic logic. To prove
\neg\phi

\phi

\phi
into a contradiction, whereas to prove
\sim\!\!\phi
, which establishes the strong falsity of
\phi
, we are required to build an explicit counterexample to
\phi

7
Conclusion

In this paper we advance a form of bilateralism in which proofs and refutations are treated as distinct but mutually incompatible assertoric acts. This is done by presenting both syntactic and semantic results for our new logic
\sf{BPR}
.

On the syntactic side, we show that our natural deduction system for
\sf{BPR}
, which is a simple extension of an (adapted) version of Wansing’s
\sf{2Int}
, satisfies the property of weak normalisability, from which the subformula property and consistency can be obtained as corollaries. This ensures that the proof system is well-behaved and that all proofs and refutations can be made analytic.

On the semantic side, we provide a base-extension semantics which is sound and complete with respect to our natural deduction system. Since our semantics require explicit construction of objects (for proofs) or counterexamples (for refutations) through atomic rules, its structure stays faithful to the idea of formalising mathematical proofs and refutations, but it can also be applied to analogous epistemic concepts. The completeness proof uses the proofs of normalisation and consistency in an essential way. The proof of Theorem
4.1
, which makes essential use of the epistemic adequacy of bases, is of particular interest since it shows that the semantics match our intuitive reading of
\sf{BPR}
by precluding the possibility of formulas being both provable and refutable.

The logic
\sf{BPR}
is also intimately connected with Nelson’s concept of constructive falsity.
\sf{BPR}
is essentially a logic which internalizes constructive falsity through the notion of refutation while allowing the definition of additional operators, such as standard intuitionistic negation. The relationship between
\sf{BPR}
and Nelson’s
N3
is analogous to the relationship between
\sf{2Int}
and Nelson’s
N4
.

From this we conclude that, aside from having a well-behaved proof system,
\sf{BPR}
allows a semantics that is conceptually adequate for its intended interpretation. In future works we intend to both study proof-theoretic formalisation of
\sf{BPR}
through different proof systems (in particular, through sequent calculus) and to further study its proof-theoretic and semantic properties.

Figure 4

Permutative reductions.

Let
\Pi
be any deduction which is not in normal form. Transform it in a deduction
\Pi^{\prime}
which is in in pre-normal form using Lemmas
1
and
2
(notice that this procedure may increase its inductive value). We prove the result by induction on the inductive value of the obtained pre-normal form deduction
\Pi^{\prime}
.

Let
\Pi^{\prime}
have induction value
\langle d,l\rangle
. Pick any maximal segment
\alpha
with degree
d
such that no other maximal segment with degree
d
has all of its formula occurrences appearing above the last formula occurrence in
\alpha
or above any formula occurrences side connected with it. Notice that, as usual, such a segment can always be found; if we pick a random segment
\alpha
with degree
d
and there is a maximal segment
\beta
with all formula occurrences above the last formula occurence of
\alpha
or above a formula occurrence side connected with it, we can just switch to the maximal segment
\beta
, check again if it satisfies the desired conditions and jump to another maximal segment above it if it does not. Since deductions are finite we will always end up with a maximal segment satisfying the desired conditions. Notice also that two segments of the same degree may occur side by side, in the sense that both of them may pass through distinct minor premises of the same rule application, but in this case we are allowed to pick either one.

If the chosen maximal segment has length greater than
1
, we apply a permutative reduction to the application of
\lor(+)
or
\land(-)
which concludes its last formula occurrence. Since there are no maximal segments with degree
d
above formula occurrence side connected with it the inductive value of the deduction is not increased by possible duplications of the deductions of those side connected occurrences. This procedure reduces the length of the segment by at least
1
(if there are two maximal segments passing through different minor premises of the rule application then both are shortened and the sum of the lengths decreases by
2
, but if there is only one then it decreases by
1
), so we obtain a deduction
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
with inductive value
\langle d,s^{\prime}\rangle
such that
d=d
and
s^{\prime}<s
, hence
\langle d,s\rangle<\langle d,s^{\prime}\rangle
.

If the maximal segment has length
1
, we apply a proper reduction to it and obtain a deduction
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
with inductive value
\langle d^{\prime},s^{\prime}\rangle
. Since there are no maximal segments of the same degree above the last formula occurrences of the segment or side connected occurrences (hence by multiplying occurrences of parts of the deduction we do not increase the inductive value) and any new maximal segments created by the procedure always have lesser degree than that of the original maximal segment, if that maximal segment was the last one with degree
d
in the derivation then
d^{\prime}<d
; if it wasn’t, the reduction removes a segment of degree
d
and thus
d=d^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime}=(s-1)
, so in any case we have
\langle d^{\prime},s^{\prime}\rangle<\langle d,s\rangle
. Notice also that neither permutative nor proper reductions add new formulas to the set of premises the deductions depend on. By successively applying this procedure we eventually reach the desired deduction with degree
\langle 0,0\rangle
.

Proof
(of Theorem
5.1
)

We prove the result by induction on the length of derivations, understood as the number of rule applications it contains.

Case 1
. The derivation has length
0
. Then it either consists in a single occurrence of proof assumption
\phi
and is a deduction showing
\phi;\emptyset\vdash^{+}_{\sf{N2Int^{*}}}\phi
or in a single occurrence of a refutation assumption
\phi
and is a deduction showing
\emptyset;\phi\vdash^{-}_{\sf{N2Int^{*}}}\phi
. It follows that
\phi;\emptyset\Vdash^{+}_{\mathcal{B}}\phi
holds for every
\mathcal{B}
, whence
\phi;\emptyset\Vdash^{+}\phi
. It also follows immediately that
\emptyset;\phi\Vdash^{-}_{\mathcal{B}}\phi
holds for every
\mathcal{B}
, whence
\emptyset;\phi\Vdash^{-}\phi
.

Case 2
. The derivation has length greater than
0
. Then the result is proved by considering the last rule applied in it, so we have to show that each rule application preserves semantic validity. Notice, however, that even though a deduction ending with an application of
(\top+)
or
(\bot-)
has no open assumptions it can still be considered a deduction showing
\Gamma;\Delta^{+}\vdash_{\sf{N2Int^{*}}}\top
or
\Gamma;\Delta\vdash^{-}_{\sf{N2Int^{*}}}\bot
, respectively. We only show the case of rules
PR(+)
, as
PR(-)
follows analogously.

1.

PR(+)
If
\Gamma_{1};\Delta_{1}\Vdash^{+}\phi
and
\Gamma_{2};\Delta_{2}\Vdash^{-}\phi
, then
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2};\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\Vdash^{+}\phi
.

Assume that
\Gamma_{1};\Delta_{1}\Vdash^{+}\phi
and
\Gamma_{2};\Delta_{2}\Vdash^{-}\phi
. Assume, for the sake of proving a contradiction, that there is
\mathcal{C}\supseteq\mathcal{B}
such that
\Vdash^{+}_{\mathcal{C}}\gamma
and
\Vdash^{-}_{\mathcal{C}}\delta
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
and
\delta\in\Delta_{1}\cup\Delta_{2}
.
Then, by
\Gamma_{1};\Delta_{1}\Vdash^{+}\phi
, we have that
\Vdash^{+}_{\mathcal{C}}\phi
, and by
\Gamma_{2};\Delta_{2}\Vdash^{-}\phi
, we have
\Vdash^{-}_{\mathcal{C}}\phi
, contradicting
theorem
4.1
. Hence there is no extension
\mathcal{C}
of
\mathcal{B}
such that
\Vdash^{+}_{\mathcal{C}}\gamma
and
\Vdash^{-}_{\mathcal{C}}\delta
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
and
\delta\in\Delta_{1}\cup\Delta_{2}
, so
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2};\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\Vdash^{+}\phi
holds vacuously.
