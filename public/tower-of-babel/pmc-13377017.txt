Enhancing NO-assisted oxygen desorption for efficient N2O decomposition by introducing iron into Co/β catalysts
Authors: Liangliang Zhang, Minxia Yang, Shize Hu, Ruifang Wu, Jiajun Ma, Hui Dang, Ke Zheng, Xiangqian Lin, Yongzhao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDirect catalytic decomposition is one of the green methods for N2O elimination. However, the activity of catalyst is limited by the slow oxygen desorption rate and the unsatisfactory electronic structure of the active site. In this work, Fe species were introduced into Co/β catalyst to regulate the microstructure of Co active sites and oxygen desorption pathways. A combination of characterization techniques such as XRD, TEM, XPS, DR UV–Vis, etc were employed to elucidate the nature of Co and Fe species in the catalysts. The results demonstrated that the introduction of Fe not only reduced the particle size of CoOx species, but also effectively improved the reducibility and deoxygenation capacity of the catalysts. Moreover, in-situ FT-IR and N2O-TPD experiments revealed that the oxidization of N2O to NO and the decomposition of nitrate species were significantly enhanced over Fe doped Co/β catalyst, thereby facilitated the NO-assisted oxygen desorption process during N2O decomposition. As a result, the N2O conversion at 420 °C was elevated from 85% of Co/β catalyst to 100% of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, which is much higher than the 22% conversion achieved over Fe/β catalyst. This work could provide the reference for the design of N2O decomposition catalysts and understanding of catalytic reaction mechanism. Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Pollution remediation

Introduction

Nitric acid is an important basic chemical raw material that is widely utilized in the production of fertilizers, explosives, fuels and medicines1. However, the incomplete oxidation of ammonia during the nitric acid production process generates a large amount of NOx pollutants2. In particular, nitrous oxide (N2O), one of the six greenhouse gases defined in the Kyoto Protocol, possesses a global warming potential (GWP) approximately 265 times that of carbon dioxide (CO2) and can persist stably in the atmosphere for more than 150 years, exerting a severe greenhouse effect3,4. The reduction of N2O emission has attracted a huge attention for government and experts due to the increasingly serious situation of global warming5,6. At present, various technologies has been put forward to remove N2O, such as the high temperature thermal decomposition, selective catalytic reduction, benzene oxidation one-step phenol, etc7,8. Nevertheless, these technologies suffer from drawbacks including complex operation, high cost, and the generation of secondary pollution. In contrast, the direct catalytic decomposition of N2O to N2 and O2 is recognized as the most ideal and sustainable technology for N2O elimination due to its advantages of simple process, environmental friendliness, and low cost.

The N-O bond in N2O molecule is difficult to cleave owing to its high bond energy (250–270 kJ mol−1), thus requiring catalysts to promote N-O bond scission. Noble metals (such as Ru and Pt), transition metal oxides (such as spinel Co3O4, NiO) and perovskite have been extensively investigated for this reaction due to their high activity and tunability9–11. However, the agglomeration and poison of active sites significantly restrict their practical industrial application6,12. In contrast, transition metal doped molecular sieve catalysts exhibit excellent anti-impurity performance and high catalytic activity13,14. And the unique confinement effect provided by molecular sieve carrier can effectively inhibit the migration and aggregation of active metal species in high temperature environment. Therefore, the transition metal-molecular sieve catalysts exhibited a broad application prospect in N2O catalytic decomposition reaction.

Cobalt-based molecular sieve catalysts exhibit superior N2O decomposition performance compared to other transition metal-molecular sieve catalysts due to the high electron-donating capacity and weak metal-oxygen bonds of CoOx species. Abu-Zied et al.15. carried out N2O decomposition reaction over different transition metal exchanged ZSM-5 catalysts, and found that the activity of Co-ZSM-5 catalysts was signally higher than that of Ni-,Y-, Mn-, and Cd-ZSM-5 catalysts. Among different molecular sieve carriers (ZSM-5, MOR, and β), the β molecular sieve is more suitable for N2O decomposition reactions due to its large pore structure, which can facilitate the contact between internal metal active sites and reactant molecules16. Kang et al.17 compared the N2O catalytic decomposition performance of a series of Co-based molecular sieves catalysts with different topological structures and found that their catalytic activity followed the order: Co/Beta > Co/Mordenite > Co/ZSM-5 > Co/MCM-49 > Co/ZSM-23 > Co/ZSM-35 > Co/ SSZ-13. Rutkowska et al.18 found that mesoporous Co/β catalysts with low Co loading showed similar catalytic performance to microporous Co/β catalysts with high Co loading.

The N2O decomposition reaction catalyzed by Co-based molecular sieves follows the cationic redox mechanism19. Firstly, the N-O bond can be broken by donating the electrons from Co2+ to the antibonding orbits of N2O molecule. Then, the Co2+ active sites will be oxidized to Co3+-O species by the remained active oxygen atom20. Finally, the Co2+ active site can be restored after the desorption of oxygen. The desorption of oxygen is considered as the key step for N2O decomposition because the accumulation of ion-adsorbed oxygen intermediates can hinder the interfacial electron transfer21. It is well documented that surface oxygen species can be desorbed through multiple pathways. On the one hand, the remained oxygen species can migrate and reorganize on the catalyst surface and desorb in form of O2 to release Co active sites (L-H mechanism). On the other hand, a second N2O molecule also can adsorb on the generated oxidative active site (Co3+-O species). After breaking the N-O bond, the newly generated oxygen atom combines with the oxygen of Co3+-O species and eventually desorb in the form of O2 (E-R mechanism)3,22,23. Among these two mechanisms, a relatively high reaction energy barrier needs to be overcome for O2 desorption, which is regarded as a rate-controlled reaction step for N2O decomposition.

In addition, the NO-assisted oxygen desorption mechanism has been revealed for N2O decomposition reactions catalyzed by other transition metal molecular sieves catalysts (Cu-based and Fe-based)24,25. During this process, NO can act as intermediate for the storage or transfer of oxygen species in form of NOx species, thereby promoting the recombination and desorption of surface oxygen atoms26. Compared with the L-H and E-R mechanisms, the NO-assisted oxygen desorption features a much lower reaction energy barrier. Xia et al.27 found that introducing a small amount of NO could reduce the complete conversion temperature of N2O by 60 °C for Fe/ZSM-5, and the corresponding apparent activation energy was significantly reduced from 184 to 140 kJ mol−1. Turek et al.28 revealed that the NO could be oxidized into nitrite/nitrate species by surface oxygen species and further decomposed into NOx and O2 during N2O decomposition process. Smeet et al.29,30 discovered the same phenomenon in the study of the Cu/ZSM-5 catalyzed N2O decomposition reaction. Though a series of comparative experiments and in-situ infrared characterization, they found that NO molecule could remove the deposited O atoms in form of NO2 during the Cu/ZSM-5 catalyzed N2O decomposition process. Gao et al31. reported that the energy barrier of N2O decomposition reaction catalyzed by Cu-ZMS-5 was reduced from the original 217.3 kJ mol-1 to 64.12 kJ mol-1 in the presence of NO.

In the current research, the NO-assisted N2O decomposition process requires the additional introduction of NO in the reaction system. However, NO is an air pollutant and is easily oxidized to NO2 by air or oxygen atmosphere, failing to meet actual industrial requirements. If a portion of N2O can be in-situ converted into NO, the NO-assisted deoxidation process can be achieved without the additional introduction of NO, thereby enhancing the decomposition efficiency of N2O.

It was reported that α-oxygen atoms generated by breaking the N-O bond of N2O molecules possessed strong oxidizing properties over Fe-based molecular sieves. Sobalik et al.32 found that these reactive oxygen species could oxidize the N2O to NOx species over Fe-FER catalyst, and transform the Fe-O to Fe-NOx species at a low temperature. Based on a series of programmed temperature desorption and in-situ infrared experiments, Jíša et al.33 found that reactive oxygen species could be rapidly generated on the surface of the Fe-FER catalyst during the decomposition process of N2O at 280 °C, and a large number of NOx species were generated in subsequent reaction time. Therefore, it can be imagined that if an appropriate amount of Fe species was introduced into Co-based molecular sieve catalysts, a small amount of N2O can be oxidized to mobilizable NOx species due to strong oxidizing property of Fe-O species. And the recombination and desorption of oxygen species can be enhanced with the assistance of these NOx species.

In this work, Fe promoters were introduced into Co/β catalyst through co-impregnation method. The effect of Fe promoters on the nature of Co species and the catalytic performance for N2O decomposition were systematically studied. The microstructure properties of Co and Fe species on the surface of catalyst were characterized by various technologies (XRD, TEM, XPS, DR UV–Vis and et al). The evolution of N2O molecule and adsorbed species during the reaction process was specially analyzed by in-situ infrared and temperature-programmed desorption experiments. Based on the above experimental characterization results, the effects of Fe promoters on the micro-electronic structure and redox performance of Co active species, as well as the role of Fe promoters in regulating the N2O decomposition pathway, were comprehensively elucidated. This work can provide a reference for the reaction mechanism and catalyst design for N2O decomposition reaction.

Results and discussion

Catalytic performance

Figure 1 shows the catalytic performance of synthesized catalysts for N2O decomposition. As we can see in Fig. 1a, Fe-doped Co-based catalysts exhibit significantly higher catalytic efficiency than Co/β and Fe/β catalyst. In particular, the N2O conversion over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst increases from 32% of Co/β to 62% at 380 °C, and further rises from 85% of Co/β to 100% at 420 °C. In contrast, the N2O conversion over the Fe/β catalyst (2.1 wt% of Fe measured by XRF) remains at approximately 5% with increasing reaction temperature from 280 to 380 °C, indicating almost no catalytic activity in this low-temperature range. In addition, the mass specific reaction rates respected to Co species at 380 °C and the activation energy were calculated for Co/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts. As the results shown in Fig. 1b, c, the mass specific reaction rate is markedly increased from 1.97 μmol g−1 s−1 of Co/β to 3.82 μmol g−1 s−1 of 1.0Fe-Co/β, while the activation energy is reduced from 152.3 kJ mol−1 of Co/β to 135.6 kJ mol−1 of 1.0Fe-Co/β. These results clearly demonstrate that the introduction of Fe species significantly improves the catalytic activity of Co active sites. However, further changing the Fe/Co ratio (0.25Fe-Co/β, 0.5Fe-Co/β and 1.25Fe-Co/β) has only a slight effect on the N2O conversion of Fe doped Co/β catalysts. This suggested that introducing small amount of Fe was enough for improving performance, and excess Fe species exert negligible influence on the activity of Co-based catalysts. In addition, the activity evaluation results shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 demonstrate that the co-impregnation method affords Fe-Co/β catalysts with superior catalytic activity compared with the stepwise impregnation method.

Catalytic performance of Fe/β, Co/β, and Fe‑doped Co/β catalysts for N2O decomposition.

a N2O conversion curves; b mass specific reaction rate of Co active sites at 380 °C; c Arrhenius plots for apparent activation energy calculation; d impurities resistance of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst.

The impurities resistance of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst was further investigated by introducing 0.1% NO, 3% O2, or 5% H2O into the reaction stream flow, respectively. Apparently, the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst showed an excellent resistance to different impurity gas (Fig. 1d). The N2O decomposition performance of 1.0Fe-Co/β was basically unchanged upon the introduction of 3% O2, with N2O conversion reaching 65% and 100% at the reaction temperature of 380 °C and 420 °C, respectively. In comparison, the complete N2O decomposition temperature (T100) slightly increases from 420 to 440 °C when 5% H2O was imported, which may cause by the competitive adsorption between H2O and N2O molecules34. Different from the effects of O2 and H2O, the N2O conversion is significantly improved from 62% to 87% at 380 °C when 0.1% NO was introduced, and the T100 was reduced to 400 °C, indicating a promotional effect of NO on the catalytic reaction. These results are consistent with previous reports in the literature24,26,35. Furthermore, the N2O decomposition activity of the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst was compared with different catalysts reported in recent works, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Although it is difficult to accurately compare the catalytic conversion rate due to the different actual evaluation conditions of reported catalysts, the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst still exhibits considerable catalytic performance compared with that of mono-metallic Co-, Cu-, and Fe-molecular sieve catalysts.

Morphology and crystal structure of synthesized catalysts

The morphology of prepared catalysts and commercial H-type β molecular sieve carrier was characterized by TEM. It could be found that the commercial H-type β crystal grains exhibited two types stacking morphology (Supplementary Fig. 2): one is composed of regular cubic grains with a particle size of about 90 nm, and the other consists of small irregular spherical microparticles. These morphological variations arise from non-uniform distribution of material concentrations and temperature gradients of the feed liquid during large-scale crystallization processes. Such inhomogeneity leads to the various reaction rates of silica-alumina species in different regions and thus the morphological inhomogeneity of commercial β molecular sieves36.

After impregnation with Co and Fe precursors, the Co/β, Fe/β, and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts exhibit similar morphology with the commercial H-type β molecular sieve carrier, with the same morphological separation phenomenon (Fig. 2a–c). In addition, a certain number of black spots are observed on all catalyst samples, indicating that Co or Fe species have been successfully introduced onto the surface of β molecular sieve carrier. Furthermore, the particle size distribution of metal species (Fig. 2d–f) show that Co particle size over Co/β catalyst predominantly concentrate within the range of 7-10 nm, while the Fe particle size increase to 10–13 nm for Fe/β catalyst. Interestingly, the particle sizes of both Co and Fe species over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst are significantly reduced to 4–7 nm.

Morphology and metal particle size distribution of typical catalysts.

TEM images of a Co/β; b Fe/β; c 1.0Fe-Co/β; catalysts, corresponding metal particle size distribution are shown in d–f respectively.

The distribution of Co and Fe species of prepared catalysts were further characterized by high-angle annular darkfield scanning (HAADF-STEM) images and EDX-mapping image (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 3). As displayed in Fig. 3a1, b1, bright spots corresponding to Co particles and Fe particles are observed over Co/β catalyst and Fe/β catalyst, respectively. The corresponding EDX-mapping images evidenced the presence of Co species, which were well dispersed throughout the whole β molecular sieve grains (Fig. 3a3). Different with the highly dispersed Co element in Co/β catalysts, part of iron elements aggregated to form a distinct red spot in the EDX-mapping images for Fe/β catalyst, which indicated the formation of larger Fe particles (Fig. 3b3). Surprisingly, no large agglomerates of Co or Fe species are observed in the EDX-mapping images of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, indicating that the dispersion of Co and Fe particles is significantly enhanced over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst (Fig. 3c1–4). These results suggested that a strong interaction exists between surface Co and Fe species for 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, which effectively inhibits their migration and aggregation.

HAADF-STEM and EDX elemental mapping image of a1–a3 Co/β, b1–b3 Fe/β and c1–c4 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts.

Figure 4a shows the XRD patterns for prepared catalysts and β molecular sieve carrier. The main diffraction peak at 2θ = 22.5° attributed to the (302) crystal plane of β molecular sieve could be observed for all samples, which suggested that the crystal structure of β molecular sieve was basically preserved during the preparation of catalysts14,37. No additional diffraction peaks ascribed to Co and Fe species are observed for Co/β, Fe/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts, indicating that the introduced Co and Fe species are highly dispersed on the surface of β molecular sieve carrier or their particle sizes are below the detection limit of XRD38. However, the intensity of the diffraction peak attributed to the β molecular sieve was reduced after the introduction of metal species. The relative crystallinity of the catalyst was calculated based on the ratio of the diffraction peak area at 2θ = 22.5° to that of pure commercial β molecular sieve (defined as 100%). As shown in Fig. 4b, the relative crystallinity of Co/β and Fe/β decrease to 91% and 82%, respectively, and is further reduced to 65% for 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst. This phenomenon is attributed to the hydrolysis of the molecular sieve framework induced by residual water in the pores during the high-temperature calcination process, which consequently leads to a decrease in the relative crystallinity of the catalyst39.

The pore structure of synthesized samples was analyzed through N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms, and the corresponding results and texture properties were showed in Fig. 4c and Table 1. It could be found that the commercial β molecular sieves exhibited the typical type IV isotherms with a narrow H4-type hysteresis loop at 0.6 < P/P0 < 1.0, suggesting the coexistence of micropores and intergranular mesopores in the sample18,40. These mesoporous are formed by the aggregation of β molecular sieves grains. The pore size distribution presented in Fig. 4d shows that the pore size of mesopore is mainly concentrated at 2–15 nm. The Co/β, Fe/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts exhibited the similar N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and pore size distribution to the commercial β molecular sieve carrier. The texture properties of carrier and catalyst are summarized in Table 1. Due to the blockage of Co and Fe particles, the total surface area of Co/β and Fe/β catalyst decrease from 553.5 m2 g−1 of β molecular sieve carrier to 529.5 m2 g−1 and 473.1 m2 g−1, respectively, and further reduces to 394.2 m2 g−1 for 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst. The total pore volume of Fe/β catalyst and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst correspondingly decrease from 0.52 m3 g−1 of β molecular sieve carrier to 0.46 m3 g−1 and 0.47 m3 g-1, respectively. The slightly increased pore volume of Co/β catalyst (0.56 m3 g−1) may cause by the mildly destruction of skeleton structure during calcination, which inducing the formation of additional mesoporous pores41.

Nature of surface metal species

Diffuse reflectance ultraviolet-visible (DR UV–Vis) spectroscopy were used to identify the types of surface metal species (Fig. 5). For comparation, the DR UV–Vis spectrum of commercial β molecular sieve is also collected, which exhibits almost no clear absorption band in the range of 300–800 nm. In contrast, the Co/β catalyst shows two broad absorption bands at approximately 400–550 nm and 700–750 nm, which can be attributed to the ligand-metal charge-transfer adsorption of 1T1g(F) → 1T1(P) and 1A1g → 1T1g for Co(Ⅱ) and Co(Ⅲ) in CoOx species, respectively42,43. The Fe/β catalyst exhibits many bands in the range of 200–600 nm: the absorption band at about 275 nm can be assigned to t1 → t2 transitions for the isolated Fe3+ species in the extra-framework of Fe/β catalysts; two bands concentrated at 395 and 471 nm are related to d-d transitions of oligomerized FexOy nano-clusters and particles44,45. As for 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, both the absorption bands attributed to CoOx species (420, 510, and 720 nm) and FexOy species (275, 395 and 471 nm) can be observed. These results reveal that Co and Fe species mainly exist in forms of metal oxides over all as-prepared catalysts.

DR UV–Vis spectra of β zeolite, Co/β, Fe/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts for identifying metal species.

Figure 6a, b display the Co 2p XPS spectra and the corresponding peak fitting results of the Co/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts. The peaks with binding energies in the ranges of 775–790 eV and 792–800 eV correspond to the typical spin‑orbit splitting core levels of Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2, respectively. In addition, two broad peaks at approximately 785.98 eV and 804.21 eV are attributed to the satellite peaks of Co2+42. The Co 2p3/2 spectrum of Co/β can be fitted into Co3+ and Co2+ species at approximately 780.12 eV and 782.17 eV, respectively46. In comparison, the binding energies of Co3+ and Co2+ for 1.0Fe-Co/β clearly decrease to 779.65 eV and 781.77 eV, respectively. The reduced binding energy suggested that the introduction of low electronegativity Fe species (Co, 1.88 vs. Fe, 1.83) can effectively increase the electron cloud density of Co sites. Figure 6c, d display the Fe 2p XPS spectra of the Fe/β and 1.0 Fe-Co/β catalysts, respectively. Both samples show characteristic Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2 signals with binding energies in the ranges of 720–727 eV and 705–717 eV, respectively. The Fe 2p3/2 spectra can be fitted into Fe2+ and Fe3+ species at approximately 709.55 eV and 711.45 eV, respectively47,48. Based on the integrated areas of the corresponding XPS peaks, the relative contents of various Co and Fe species were calculated in detail (Table 2). It can be clearly seen that Co3+ is the dominant Co species over the Co/β catalyst with a relative content of 53.9%, and the Co2+/Co3+ molar ratio is 0.8. After introducing Fe species, the content of Co3+ species in the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst is significantly reduced to 47.9%, while the content of Co2+ species is correspondingly increased from 46.1% of Co/β catalyst to 52.1%, with the Co2+/Co3+ ratio rising to 1.1. Similarly, the relative content of Fe2+ species increase from 51.6% of Fe/β catalyst to 53.9% of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, accompanied by the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio increasing from 1.1 to 1.2. These results indicate that the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst exhibits stronger metal-support interactions compared with the Co/β and Fe/β catalysts, which effectively inhibits the oxidation of Co2+ to Co3+ and Fe2+ to Fe3+.

Reduction and oxidation properties of catalysts

The reduction properties of the prepared catalysts were characterized by H2-TPR technique, and the results are exhibited in Fig. 7. It is reported that the impregnated metal species can exist on the surface of molecular sieve in various forms: (ⅰ) the cations located at ion exchange sites, (ⅱ) the oligomerized multinuclear cations or metal-oxo complexes, and (ⅲ) the bulk metal oxide species17. These metal species exhibit different hydrogen reduction properties due to their different microscopic environments and aggregation structures. As we can see in Fig. 7a, the H2-TPR curve of Co/β catalyst presents four peaks in 300–700 °C, which can be ascribed to the different reduction processes of cobalt species. The reduction peak at about 370.1 °C and 410.2 °C were attributed to the reduction process of bulk Co3O4 to CoO and bulk CoO particles to metallic Co, respectively49. These bulk cobalt-oxygen species are usually formed on the external surface of β grains and easily reduced under hydrogen atmosphere. The obvious reduction peak at about 542.9 °C is assigned to the reduction of the small CoOx clusters located in the inner channels of molecular sieve carrier, indicating a strong electron interaction between the β molecular sieve skeleton and internal CoO clusters50,51. The peak at about 590.4 °C is ascribed to the isolated Co2+ cations at ion-exchange sites, which are the most difficult to reduce50,52.

Redox property characterization of catalysts by H2-TPR and O2-TPD.

a–c H2-TPR curves and d O2-TPD curves for Co/β, Fe/β, and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts.

Different with that of Co/β catalyst, the H2-TPR curve of Fe/β exhibits a huge reduction peak in the range of 250–650 °C, which can be deconvoluted into three peaks by Gaussian fitting. As shown in Fig. 7b, the reduction peaks around 375.5 °C and 443.1 °C is assigned to the reduction of the isolated Fe3+ to Fe2+ at different ion-exchange sites, and the reduction of bulk Fe2O3 particles to Fe3O4 also contributed to the generation of these peaks53. In addition, the peak at high reduction temperature (509.3 °C) is attributed to the further reduction of Fe3O4 to FeO54. No apparent peaks could be found above 600 °C suggest that the FeO and the isolated Fe2+ are difficult to be further reduced to Fe0 under the test environment54.

As we can see in Fig. 7c, the H2-TPR curve of 1.0Fe-Co/β exhibits a broader reduction peak than those of the Co/β and Fe/β catalysts in the range of 300–700 °C. The reduction peaks in the range of 300–400 °C were attributed to the reduction of bulk Co3O4 and CoO particles. The peaks in the range of 400–520 °C are assigned to: (ⅰ) reduction of bulk Fe2O3 to Fe3O4 at 425.5 °C, (ⅱ) reduction of Fe3O4 to FeO at 462.1 °C, (ⅲ) reduction of isolated Fe3+ to Fe2+ at 501.6 °C, respectively54. And the peaks at 543.9 °C and 589.3 °C are attributed to the reduction of CoOx clusters to Co0 in the internal pore and the isolated Co2+ to Co0 at ion exchange site, respectively. Obviously, the reduction temperature of these bulk oxides over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst are significantly lower than those over Co/β and Fe/β catalysts. Although the amount of metal is doubled, the total hydrogen consumption of 1.0Fe-Co/β (0.045 μmol g−1) is lower than the sum of Co/β (0.013 μmol g−1) and Fe/β (0.039 μmol g−1), which suggests that the average valence of Co and Fe species over 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst are lower than those over the monometallic catalysts. These results strongly demonstrate that a strong electronic interaction exists between surface Fe and Co species over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, which significantly improves the reducibility of the catalyst.

Figure 7d exhibits the O2-TPD curves of above three catalysts. It can observe that all catalysts shows two desorption peaks at 100–200 °C and 300–600 °C, which are attributed to the desorption of surface adsorbed oxygen and lattice oxygen species of surface metal oxide particles, respectively55. Compared with the Co/β (151.4 °C) and Fe/β (153.2 °C) catalysts, the desorption temperature of surface adsorbed oxygen species over 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst is significantly reduced to 146.5 °C. And the corresponding amount of surface adsorbed oxygen decreases obviously from 1.5 × 10-4 μmol g−1 of Co/β to 4.5 × 10−5 μmol g−1 of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, indicating an improved deoxidation capacity for 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst.

Evolution of the adsorbed N2O molecules

The N2O adsorption energy over the isolated Co2+ sites and [Co-O-Fe]2+ sites outside the molecular sieve framework were calculated through first-principles methods. The calculation results show that the adsorption energy (Ead) of N2O on isolated Co2+ site is only −0.22 eV, while that on [Co-O-Fe]2+ site is as high as −0.40 eV (Supplementary Fig. 4). This result reveals that the introduction of Fe species significantly enhances the N2O adsorption capacity of the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst. Furthermore, the in situ infrared measurements (in-situ FT-IR) were carried out to explore the evolution of the adsorbed species over the Fe/β, Co/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts during the N2O catalytic decomposition reaction. As the FT-IR spectra of Co/β catalyst shown in Fig. 8a, an intense band ascribed to nitrate species appeared almost immediately at 1624 cm−1 after introducing 0.1% N2O/He into the reaction cell. At the same time, week bands attributed to linear-nitrosyl and dinitrosyl species are observed at 1812 cm−1 and 1940 cm−1, respectively56,57. And the doublet bands at 2205 cm−1 and 2238 cm−1 are characteristic for gas-phase N2O25,58. With the increase of experimental temperature from 50 to 100 °C, the peak intensity of nitrate species increases sharply. It is well documented that the oxygen species with strong oxidizing capacity can be generated by breaking N-O band of N2O over active sites, which can further oxidize N2O into NO or NO232,59,60. Notably, these adsorbed oxygen species undergo sluggish desorption at lower temperatures, which further facilitates the oxidation of N2O as well as the formation of nitrate species. However, these stable nitrate species will cover numerous of active sites, which drastically impairs the low-temperature catalytic activity of the Co/β catalyst.

In-situ FT-IR spectra of N2O decomposition over various catalysts.

Temperature-resolved spectra of a Co/β, b Fe/β, c 1.0Fe-Co/β; time-resolved spectra at 380 °C of a’ Co/β, b’ Fe/β, c’ 1.0Fe-Co/β.

With the temperature further elevated above 340 °C, the absorption bands of surface nitrate and nitrosyl species (1624, 1812, and 1940 cm−1) over the Co/β catalyst gradually decrease and eventually disappear at elevated temperatures. Meanwhile, the Co/β catalyst begins to exhibit enhanced catalytic activity over the same temperature interval (Fig. 1a). However, the in-situ FT-IR spectra of NO adsorption reveals that the nitrosyl species exist stably over Co/β catalyst even the temperature increased to 420 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results indicate that the reduction of surface nitrate/nitrosyl species on the Co/β is not only caused by thermal decomposition at elevated temperatures (>340 °C), but also by the rapid recombination and desorption of surface oxygen species, which inhibits the oxidation of N2O to NO/NO2. And the coverage of active sites by nitrate/nitrosyl (formed via the oxidation of N2O by adsorbed oxygen species) constitutes the primary cause for the inferior low-temperature catalytic activity of Co/β catalyst.

Figure 8b shows the in-situ FT-IR spectra for Fe/β catalyst for N2O decomposition. Abundant nitrate species (1624 cm⁻¹) and nitrosyl species (1876 cm−1, assigned to linear-nitrosyl; 1940 cm−1, assigned to dinitrosyl species) are rapidly formed over the Fe/β catalyst immediately upon N2O introduction. In contrast to the Co/β catalyst, surface nitrate species on the Fe/β disappeared instantaneously at temperatures above 340 °C, while the nitrosyl species increased gradually. Considering the poor catalytic activity of Fe/β within this temperature range (due to oxygen species enrichment), the disappearance of surface-adsorbed nitrate species is not caused by the inhibition of N2O oxidation from the rapid desorption of surface oxygen species, but rather by their thermal decomposition at elevated temperatures. In addition, the promoted N-O bond cleavage of N2O aggravates the accumulation of surface oxygen species at elevated temperatures (>340 °C), which in turn accelerates the conversion of N2O to NO, thereby generating a large amount of nitrosyl species. These results demonstrate that Fe active sites are more prone to catalyze the decomposition of nitrate species then Co active sites, and can also facilitate the oxidation of N2O to NO as well as the stable adsorption of NO molecules (nitrosyl species) at elevated temperatures (>340 °C).

As for the 1.0 Fe-Co/β catalyst, although the infrared absorption peaks at 1624, 1814, and 1940 cm–1 also gradually decreased with increasing temperature above 340 °C, an absorption band at 1893 cm–1 (assigned to linear nitrosyl species on Fe active sites) appeared and is progressively intensified at the same temperature range (Fig. 8c). Furthermore, the intensity of the peak at 1624 cm–1 for the 1.0 Fe-Co/β catalyst was significantly weaker than that for the Co/β catalyst. These results indicate that the introduction of Fe species into the Co/β catalyst can promote the oxidation of N2O to NO even at relatively high temperatures, while also facilitating the decomposition of nitrate species.

Furthermore, the time-resolved in-situ FT-IR spectra were also carried out to explore the role of Fe specie on N2O decomposition reaction over 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst. The experiments were conducted at relatively low temperature (380 °C) to capture more surface adsorbed species. The results of activity evaluation in Fig. 1 showed that most of N2O molecules can be decomposed over Co/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts at this temperature. As we can see, the intensity of the nitrate band at 1624 cm–1 gradually increases with reaction time for all three catalysts (Fig. 8d–f), accompanied by a broad shoulder at 1525 cm–1. Both bands are attributed to nitrate species. The increase of nitrate species suggests that the surface oxygen species gradually accumulate with the increased of reaction time. However, the intensity of bands attributed to nitrate species for Fe/β catalyst is significantly weaker than that of Co/β (Fig. 8d, e). Meanwhile, an obvious band attributed to nitrosyl species is observed at approximately 1876 cm–1 for the Fe/β and 1.0 Fe-Co/β catalysts, while it is invisible for Co/β catalyst. These results strongly demonstrate that the introduction of Fe species into the Co/β catalyst can significantly promote the decomposition of nitrate species and boost the oxidative conversion of N2O to NO, thereby initiating the NO-assisted N2O decomposition process.

N2O temperature-programmed desorption experiments over the Co/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts were further implemented. The desorbed species (N2O, m/z = 44; N2, m/z = 28; NO, m/z = 30) were monitored in real time through an on-line mass spectrometer25. As we can see in Fig. 9a, the rapid desorption of N2O molecules is started at 300 °C for above two catalysts. Accompanied by N2O desorption, a strong N2 signal is observed as the temperature improved to 300 °C and its intensity gradually increases with increasing temperature (Fig. 9c, d). These results indicated that the N2O decomposition occurs at 300 °C, which is consistent with the results of activity evaluation. However, the N2O desorption signal over 1.0Fe-Co/β is obviously weaker than that of Co/β catalyst as the temperature is further increased to 420 °C, while the intensity of N2 signal is higher than that of Co/β catalyst at the same temperature window. These results indicate that the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst can more effectively catalyze the decomposition of adsorbed N2O into N2 and O2 than the Co/β catalyst in this temperature range.

In addition, the monitored signal of NO during desorption process was presented in Fig. 9b. Both the Co/β and 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts exhibit a slight NO signal at 250–300 °C, and a gradually increased N2 signal is also detected at the same temperature. Combining the changes in these two signals, it can be considered that the detected NO is generated by the oxidization of partial N2O molecules with surface oxygen species (produced by breaking the N-O band of N2O), which seemed to happen easily than the direct decomposition of N2O to N2 and O2. Different with that of Co/β catalyst, the NO signal of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst further increases with increasing temperature from 300 to 420 °C. This result as consistent with that of in-situ FT-IR spectra, which revealed that the introduction of Fe into 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst is conducive to the oxidization of N2O to NO. It is widely reported that NO molecule could work as the oxygen carrier to promote the reorganization and desorption of surface oxygen species during N2O catalytic decomposition reaction27,61. Both the decomposition of nitrate species and the oxidation of N2O can generate NO, thereby facilitating the desorption of surface oxygen species. Therefore, it can be considered that the improved catalytic performance of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst can be ascribed to the promoted NO-assisted N2O decomposition process induced by Fe introduction.

Discussion

For the direct catalytic decomposition of N2O, the activation of N-O bond depends on the electron transfer form divalent Co2+ to N2O molecules, the regeneration of Co2+ active sites via O2 desorption from Co3+-O species is considered as the rate-determining step19. Therefore, increasing the amount of Co2+ and enhancing the deoxidation capacity are beneficial for improving N2O catalytic decomposition activity. In addition, numerous theoretical calculations and experimental results confirmed that NO molecules can act as oxygen carriers to promote the recombination of surface oxygen species and facilitate oxygen desorption through the decomposition of nitrate species26,61.

Through the above characterizations and analysis, the Fe additives in bimetallic 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst play a crucial role in modulating the nature of Co species and enhancing the catalytic performance for N2O decomposition (Fig. 10). On the one hand, the introduction of Fe additives significantly promotes the dispersion of Co species and modulates their electronic states. TEM and EDX characterization results show that the particle size of metal species on the bimetallic 1.0Fe-Co/β is signally decreased from 10 to 13 nm of monometallic Fe/β and Co/β catalysts to 5–7 nm. XPS and H2-TPR analyses demonstrate that the introduction of Fe enhanced the reduction properties of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst and promoted the generation of Co2+ species, the Co2+/Co3+ molar ratio over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst increase from 0.84 of Co/β catalyst to 1.1. O2-TPD result show that the amount of surface adsorbed oxygen is obviously decreased from 1.5 × 10−4 μmol g−1 of Co/β to 4.5 × 10−5 μmol g−1 of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst, indicating an enhanced deoxygenation capacity of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst. On the other hand, in-situ FT-IR and N2O-TPD experiments demonstrate that Fe species over 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst can catalyze the oxidization of N2O to NO at relatively high temperatures (>340 °C), while the decomposition of generated nitrite/nitrate species over Fe species were more favorable than that over Co active sites.

Schematic illustration of the effects of Fe promoters on the properties of Co active sites and the N2O decomposition pathway over 1.0Fe-Co/β catalysts.

As a result, the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst satisfactorily achieved 100% conversion of N2O at 420 °C, while the N2O conversion of Co/β catalyst was only 85% under the same reaction conditions. And the activation energy of 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst was also reduced from 152.3 kJ mol−1 of Co/β catalyst to 135.6 kJ mol−1. Despite these significant advances, the catalytic performance of the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst is slightly affected by H2O (the T100 increases by 20 °C in the presence of 5% H2O), which highlights the need to optimize the hydrophobicity of the β molecular sieve framework. Future work will focus on the modification of β molecular sieve with hydrophobic coatings to mitigate the competitive adsorption of H2O, which is expected to extend the catalyst lifespan in industrial humid reaction environments.

Conclusions

In this work, the bimetallic 1.0Fe-Co/β molecular sieve catalysts was prepared by co-impregnation method. The influence of Fe auxiliaries on the electronic property of Co active sites and the catalytic performance for N2O decomposition were systematically investigated. Advanced characterization techniques revealed Fe introduction effectively inhibits the agglomeration of surface metal species over the β molecular sieve support and significantly improves the reducibility of Co species, leading to a high content of divalent Co²⁺ species over the 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst. Compared with that of mono-metallic Fe/β and Co/β, the H2 reduction temperature and O2 desorption temperature for Fe-Co/β were remarkably reduced, indicating enhanced redox properties and deoxygenation capacity. In addition, the N2O-TPD and in situ FT-IR experiments confirm that the introduction of Fe species effectively promoted the oxidation of N2O to NO at high temperature, and strengthened the decomposition of nitrate species, which greatly facilitated the NO-assisted surface oxygen desorption process. As a result, the bimetallic 1.0Fe-Co/β catalyst achieved superior N2O conversion (62% vs. 32%) at 380 °C than that of Co/β catalyst, along with good resistance to NO and O2. These findings deepen the understanding of the role of auxiliary metals in N2O decomposition reaction and provide valuable insights for the rational design of high-efficiency N2O decomposition catalysts.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

Bimetallic Fe-Co/β catalysts was prepared by co-impregnation method. Briefly, 0.49 g of Co(NO3)2·6H2O and a certain amount of Fe(NO3)3·9 H2O were dissolved in 1 mL deionized water in a beaker. The resulting solution was dropwise added to 5 g commercial H-type β molecular sieves under constant magnetic stirring. After ageing process at 40 °C for 4 h, the mixture was dried at 100 °C for 12 h using a draught drying cabinet. The obtained dry solid sample was fully ground in a mortar and then calcined at 550 °C for 5 h in a muffle furnace with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. Thus, the Co-Fe doped β molecular sieves catalyst was prepared and was denoted as xFe-Co/β, where x represented the mole ratio of Fe/Co (x = 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2.0). For comparation, the Co doped β molecular sieves catalyst (Co/β) and Fe doped β molecular sieves catalyst (Fe/β) was prepared via a similar method without the addition of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O and Co(NO3)2·6H2O, respectively.

Catalyst evaluation

The N2O decomposition reaction was conducted in a micro fixed bed reactor with an inner diameter of 4 mm. In a typical experiment, 0.3 g of catalysts was firstly pelletized, crushed, and sieved to 40-60 mesh, then packed into the center region of reactor. A 0.1% N2O/He gas mixture was fed into the reactor at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1, corresponding to a gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) of 10,000 h−1. After 30 min of continuous ventilation, the reactor temperature was gradually raised from room temperature to 280 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, and then further increased to 500 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/min. The N2O concentration in the outlet gases at 280–500 °C was analyzed online through an online Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a Pora PLOT Q column (50 m × 0.35 mm × 20 μm).
