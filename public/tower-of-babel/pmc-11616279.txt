Influence of COVID-19 public health restrictions on community-acquired pneumonia pathogens in children in Henan, China: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Yalin Guo, Debin Yang, Yang Cao, Xianfei Ding, LiXia Chen, Beibei Huo, Yuanzhe Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the distribution of pathogens and epidemiological changes in children hospitalized with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) during and after the COVID-19 pandemic public health restrictive measures. aiming to provide a foundation for clinical diagnosis, treatment, and policy formulation.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study spanned January 2019 to December 2023. The study included 78,256 children hospitalized for CAP in four hospitals in Henan, China, among which 27,580 cases (35.2%) were tested for pathogens using multiplex real-time fluorescent polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The pathogens detected include Streptococcus pneumoniae (SP), Staphylococcus aureus (SA), Haemophilus influenzae (HI), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza A virus (Flu A), influenza B virus (Flu B), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP).ResultsPathogens were identified in 18,690 of the 27,580 children, resulting in a 67.8% positive detection rate. Of these cases, 15,105 (54.8%) were single pathogen infections and 3,585 (13%) were mixed pathogen infections. The pathogen positivity rate was lowest in the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), at 54.7%, and peaked at 79.1% in 2023, after public health restrictions were lifted. During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), seasonal variation in pathogen prevalence was disrupted. Post-restriction, there was a significant increase in RSV and MP cases. SP remained the leading bacterial cause of CAP, especially in young children. RSV was the predominant viral pathogen, particularly affecting infants. MP showed a rising trend with age, yet it also affected younger individuals.ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic altered the epidemiological characteristics of pathogens in children with CAP. This impact is likely to persist, necessitating enhanced surveillance of CAP pathogens to mitigate the healthcare burden in children.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is a prevalent respiratory disease and a leading cause of child mortality in developing countries [1]. The World Health Organization reports that CAP accounts for over 19% of deaths in children under 5 years [2].

During the global Coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, countries implemented restrictive public health measures to curb Severe acute respiratory syndrome-2 (SARS-CoV-2) spread [3, 4]. Non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) such as mask use, home isolation, and reduced social interaction were widely adopted, impacting the epidemiological patterns of common CAP pathogens [5–7]. These measures complicated pathogen identification; however, they helped slow COVID-19 transmission. However, they have created an “immune debt,” especially in infants and young children with underdeveloped immune systems [8]. As significant restrictions are lifted, specific respiratory pathogens could resurge, potentially leading to localized outbreaks [9–11] and adversely affecting children’s health. Therefore, the impact of COVID-19 pandemic public health restrictions on the epidemiological characteristics of common pathogens in children with CAP warrants considerable attention.

During the 3 years of the COVID-19 pandemic (January 2020 to December 2022), China implemented various preventive, control, and restrictive measures. These measures were almost wholly lifted after December 2022. Compared to other countries, the prolonged restrictions in China may have resulted in a more severe “immunization debt” among children [12]. Our study aimed to characterize the changes in the prevalence of common CAP pathogens during and after the lifting of public health restrictions imposed during the COVID-19 pandemic. This research provides a basis for accurately assessing the prevalence trends of childhood CAP pathogens in the post-pandemic period and informs the development of relevant policies. Furthermore, this study enhances our understanding of how emerging respiratory infectious diseases, such as COVID-19, affect the epidemiological profile of common pathogens in children with CAP.

Materials and methods

A multicenter retrospective study assessed the impact of COVID-19 public health restrictions on the epidemiological characteristics of common pathogens in childhood CAP. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University (No. 2024-k-130), who also waived the written informed consent due to the retrospective design of the study.

General information

The laboratory results and clinical data from children admitted with CAP to Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University, General Hospital of Jinshui District Zhengzhou City, First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, and Kaifeng Children’s Hospital were analyzed to investigate the impact of COVID-19 public health measures on the epidemiology of childhood CAP pathogens. The study period spanned five years (January 2019 to December 2023) in Henan Province. Data from before the COVID-19 pandemic (2019), during the COVID-19 pandemic public health restrictions (2020–2022), and after the restrictions were lifted (2023). Common pathogens of childhood CAP, including gram-positive bacteria (Streptococcus pneumoniae [SP] and Staphylococcus aureus [SA]), gram-negative bacteria (Haemophilus influenzae [HI]), viruses (respiratory syncytial virus [RSV], influenza A virus [Flu A], and influenza B virus [Flu B], and atypical pathogens (Mycoplasma pneumoniae [MP]).

Inclusion criteria: (1) children aged 28 days to 18 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of CAP, based on the diagnostic criteria in the “Guideline for diagnosis and treatment of community-acquired pneumonia in children (2019 version)”: (1) Symptoms (fever, cough, etc.); (2) Signs (lung rales, respiratory rate, etc.); (3) Lung imaging [13]. Exclusion criteria: (1) the presence of genetic or metabolic disorders, hematologic disorders, immune dysfunction, structural abnormalities of the respiratory tract, and hemodynamic changes from preexisting heart disease; (2) children with tuberculosis, pertussis, non-infectious pneumonia, and hospital-acquired pneumonia. (3) positive test for SARS-CoV-2.

Children with CAP were categorized into four age groups: (1) infant group (28 days to 1 year); (2) toddler group (1–3 years); (3) preschooler group (3–6 years); (4) school-age group (≥ 6 years) to examine the effect of COVID-19 public health restrictions on pathogens infecting children with CAP across different ages.

Considering the seasonality in epidemiology, seasonal data were included: spring (March-May), summer (June-August), autumn (September-November), and winter (December-February of the following year).

Specimen collection

Sputum and nasopharyngeal swab specimens were collected by professionals using strict aseptic procedures within 24 h of admission and sent immediately to the laboratory for testing. For children requiring alveolar lavage, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) obtained by electron bronchoscopy was collected in sterile tubes and sent immediately to the laboratory. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, mandatory SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid testing was conducted for patients with fever or respiratory symptoms, all enrolled children tested negative for SARS-CoV-2. Demographic and clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records.

Pathogen detection

Sputum, nasopharyngeal swabs, and BALF samples were placed in collection tubes. Multiplex real-time fluorescent polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to detect the following pathogens: SP, SA, HI, RSV, Flu A, Flu B, MP. The reagent was purchased from Guangzhou Daan Gene Co., Ltd. All operations are strictly carried out in accordance with the instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) statistical software (version 22.0, International Business Machines Corporation, New York, USA). Count data were expressed as percentages (%), and group comparisons were made using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability method. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Detection characteristics of pathogens

Throughout the study period, 78,256 children hospitalized for CAP in four hospitals in Henan, China, were included, of whom 27,580 (35.2%) underwent PCR pathogen testing and met the inclusion criteria: 6,620 in 2019, 3,310 in 2020, 4,136 in 2021, 4,689 in 2022, and 8,825 in 2023. Of these children with CAP, 18,690(Mono-infections and mixed infections)tested positive for pathogens, resulting in a detection rate of 67.8%. The positive rate of pathogens was statistically significant among different years (Chi-square test, χ2 = 1057.809, P < 0.001). Among these, 15,105 cases (54.8%) were classified as mono-infections, while 3,585 cases (13.0%) were mixed infections. Among the mixed infections, two pathogens were detected in 2939 cases (10.7%) and three or more pathogens were detected in 646 cases (2.3%). The most common mixed infection in infants and young children (≤ 3 years) was SP + RVS, the most common mixed infection in the preschool group (3–6 years) was SP + Flu B, and the most common mixed infection in the school-age group (≥ 6 years) was SP + MP. Distribution by age groups showed that the infant group (28 days to 1 year) had 7,177 positive cases (26.0%), the toddler group (1–3 years) 5,490 cases (19.9%), the preschool group (3–6 years) 3,562 cases (12.9%), and the school-age group (≥ 6 years)) 2,461 cases (8.9%). A total of 16,355 (59.3%) of the children were boys (Table 1).

Comparative analysis of pathogen detection in different years

The overall positive rate for pathogen detection significantly varied over time, showing a decrease before the pandemic (2019), followed by a decline during the pandemic (2020–2022), and then an increase after the restrictions were lifted in 2023 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1a, b). The number of positive specimens decreased notably during the initial year of the pandemic. It increased significantly after lifting restrictive measures, comparing the analysis of the number of positive cases of pathogens in different years, the differences in the number of positive cases, Flu A and MP in 2020–2023 compared to 2019 were statistically significant (p < 0.05); furthermore, the number of positives for HI, RSV and FLu B was significantly increased in 2023 compared to 2019 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1b). MP, Flu A, and RSV exhibited a sharp rise in positive detections among the pediatric population following the easing of restrictions (Fig. 1c).

We analysed the monthly changes of seven common pathogens before the pandemic (2019), during the pandemic (2020.01-2022.12) and after the relaxation of the restriction policy (2023). The following trends were observed: in 2019, before the COVID-19 pandemic, SA, RSV, Influenza A and Influenza B had a higher number of positives and positive rates in autumn and winter; SA and HI pathogens had an increase in positives and positive rates in winter and spring, whereas MP had a higher number of positives and positive rates throughout the year. Pathogen detection across different age groups

Our analysis of pathogen detection by age group in different years revealed that infants (28 days to 1 year) were the most frequently infected, with positivity rates of 26.4% in 2019, 22.9% in 2020, 21.6% in 2021, 23.0% in 2022, and 30.6% in 2023 (Fig. 3). Conversely, school-age children (≥ 6 years) had the lowest detection rates, with positivity rates of 7.6% in 2019, 4.7% in 2020, 3.8% in 2021, 7.7% in 2022, and 14.6% in 2023 (Fig. 3a). During the pandemic, there was a decrease in hospitalizations and pathogen detections among school-age children. However, following the lifting of restrictions, there was a significant increase in both hospitalizations and pathogen detection rates compared to the pre-restriction period. RSV had a substantially higher positivity rate in infants than in other age groups (Fig. 3e). In contrast, SP had the highest positivity rate in infants and the lowest in school-age children (Fig. 3b). MP showed the highest positivity rate among school-age children. However, there was a noticeable trend toward an earlier age of infection (Fig. 3h).

Discussion

CAP is a high incidence disease in childhood, frequently leading to severe complications. The detection rate of CAP pathogens in children varies across different regions of China [14]. During the global COVID-19 pandemic, China implemented stringent public health measures and a dynamic zero-COVID policy, which likely altered the epidemiological trends of respiratory pathogens [15]. These strict measures complicated the accurate prediction of the magnitude and onset of epidemic peaks for respiratory pathogen infections [16, 17]. Furthermore, prolonged restrictions can create an “immune debt,” especially in children with lower immunity, making them more susceptible to infectious diseases and transmission of respiratory pathogens [18–20]. In summary, severe restrictions make it more difficult to predict CAP pathogens in children and pose a potential threat to infants and young children with lower immunity.

This multicenter retrospective study investigated the changes in the epidemiological characteristics of common pathogens in children with CAP before the COVID-19 pandemic, during the public health restrictive measures, and after these measures were lifted in Henan, China. The study analyzed and compared the relevant data to understand these changes comprehensively.

The pathogen characteristics of infections in children hospitalized with CAP from 2019 to 2023 were analyzed. The overall positive rate of pathogen detection was 67.8%. The epidemiological dynamics of common CAP pathogens in children continued to evolve after the COVID-19 pandemic. During the pandemic, there was a significant decrease in positive detections and rates for pathogens such as SP, SA, HI, RSV, Flu A, Flu B, and MP. However, following the lifting of restrictions, there was a brief and rapid increase in the number of positive cases of these pathogens in the pediatric population, leading to outbreaks. Our study is similar to relevant studies in other countries: surveillance data from 26 countries and territories showed a significant reduction in the detection of SP and HI during the COVID-19 pandemic [21]. A significant reduction in the proportion of children positive for Flu A, Flu B, RSV in Melbourne due to the public health measures of COVID-19 [22]. In addition, New Zealand has seen a significant reduction in the number of positive tests for influenza and other respiratory viruses during the COVID-19 pandemic [23], and in Denmark, a national, population-based cohort study found that MP infections and hospitalisations increased three-fold in 2023–2024 compared with the pre-COVID-19 seasons [24].

The most common bacterial pathogens in pediatric CAP, gram-positive bacteria (SP and SA) and gram-negative bacteria (HI), were focused upon. The organisms SP, SA, and HI primarily infected infants and children younger than 3 years. SP had the highest number of positive detections per year, with a total of 3,538 cases (12.8%), indicating that SP remains the most significant bacterial pathogen of CAP in children. However, the prevalence of SP was not substantially affected by the COVID-19 restrictive measures. The detection positivity of SP during the COVID-19 pandemic was higher in early childhood (1–3 years) compared to infancy (28 days to 1 year), preschool age (3–6 years), and school-age (≥ 6 years). Considering that early childhood is the age at which children enroll in day care, and that the immune system is not well developed and poorly adapted to the environment, and that SP is more likely to be detected due to a higher rate of SP carriage in this age group [25, 26]. Furthermore, SP can cause severe pneumonia and necrotizing pneumonia. Mixed infections of SP and viruses are more common, exacerbating the condition. Community-associated methicillin-resistant SA infections, primarily affecting young children with CAP, have been reported in recent years and warrant serious attention [27].

Viral infections are a common cause of CAP in infants and children, distinguishing pediatric CAP from adult CAP. The positive detection rate of viral pathogens decreases with age. RSV detection rates were 15.6%, 15.4%, 13.8%, 14.5%, and 21.2% from 2019 to 2023, respectively. RSV is the leading viral pathogen causing CAP, followed by influenza viruses. The detection rate of RSV was highest in the infant group and decreased with age. After the restrictions were lifted, the number of RSV positives surged across all age groups, causing a significant disease burden. Public health measures during the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in a 98.0% and 99.4% reduction in RSV and influenza detection rates, respectively, among children in Western Australia [28]. However, the interseasonal resurgence of RSV in Australian children following the reduction of COVID-19-related public health measures [29]. This surge may be due to the susceptibility of newborns born after the COVID-19 pandemic and an increase in the number of children, including preschoolers, who were not infected with RSV during early infancy and who play an essential role in RSV transmission. Other contributing factors may include reduced antibody levels in breast milk, insufficient physical activity, and sunlight exposure in children during the pandemic [11, 30–32].

Among the detected pathogens, MP was the most prevalent cause of CAP infections in children in this region. The number of positive detections and positivity rates for MP from 2019 to 2023 were 1,775 (26.8%), 682 (20.6%), 799 (19.3%), 915 (19.5%), 2,818 cases (29.7%), respectively. There was a significant decrease in MP infections in 2020, while an epidemic of MP emerged after lifting restrictions. This resurgence may be linked to lifting restrictive policies, as children returning to kindergarten and primary school experienced increased close contact and transmission [33, 34]. Although the detection rate of MP increased with age, 37. 1% of MP-infected children were still < 3 years of age, suggesting a trend of MP infection in younger age groups.

Seasonality plays a crucial role in transmitting CAP pathogens [35, 36]. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, respiratory pathogens exhibited distinct seasonal patterns: SA infections peaked in autumn and winter; RSV, Flu A, and Flu B were more active in winter and spring, while MP showed a high detection rate throughout all seasons. However, after the outbreak, these seasonal patterns diminished. As public health restrictions have eased, seasonal patterns gradually return to their pre-pandemic state.

This study also has several limitations. First, our analysis was limited to the dynamic changes in aetiology within the Henan region. Although this region is representative, it may not capture the full spectrum of variation, so our findings may differ from those in other parts of China or other countries. In addition, our study focused exclusively on hospitalised children, excluding data from outpatients, which may limit the generalisability of our findings.

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic public health restrictions significantly altered the epidemiological characteristics of pathogens in pediatric CAP. The usual seasonality of these pathogens largely disappeared during the restriction period and has not yet recovered to pre-pandemic trends following the lifting of restrictions. The number of positive cases and the positivity rate for various pathogens in children of different ages changed both during and after the pandemic restrictions. Continuous monitoring of these pathogens is essential to inform health system planning, prevent and control future respiratory pathogen pandemics, and reduce the incidence of CAP, especially severe CAP, in children.
