Simultaneous identification of the parameters in the plasticity function for power hardening materials : A Bayesian approach
Salih Tatar, Mohamed BenSalah

Abstract
In this paper, we study simultaneous determination of the strain hardening exponent, the shear modulus and the yield stress in an inverse problem. First, we analyze the direct and the inverse problems. Then we formulate the inverse problem in the Bayesian framework. After solving the direct problem by an iterative approach, we propose a numerical method based on a Bayesian approach for the numerical solution of the inverse problem. Numerical examples with noisy data illustrate applicability and accuracy of the proposed method to some extent. Keywords: Bayesian inverse problems; iterative regularizing ensemble Kalman method; elastoplastic deflections; Ramberg-Osgood curve MSC 2010: 35R30; 35J60; 65N21; 35Q74; 62F15

3
Iterative regularizing ensemble Kalman method

In this section, we propose a numerical technique for recovering the plasticity function in the inverse problem (
1
) by using the measured output data
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\varphi)
defined by (
1.16
). In practice, the measured data can only be given with some measurement error leading to inaccuracies in the numerical solution of the inverse problem. To address this challenge, some regularization methods are considered. A modified optimal perturbation algorithm
[
16
]
was introduced to regularize the ill-posed problems. While this method provides point estimates for the parameters, our interest extends beyond more point estimations, we seek to understand their statistical properties. Bayesian inference offers an exact solution to this requirement by incorporating uncertainties from observational noise and prior information, yielding the posterior probability density of the unknown parameters. For a comprehensive understanding of the well-posedness and underlying theory of this approach, one can refer to
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Furthermore, Yan et al. in
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
and Stuart et al. in
[
26
]
have explored various numerical methods for solving Bayesian inverse problems. We begin by establishing some notation. Let
\displaystyle\theta=(\kappa,\xi_{0}^{2},G)
represent the discrete input, and define the observation operator
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
as follows:

\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{F}(\theta).

Let
\displaystyle\varepsilon
denote additive Gaussian noise for observations, i.e.,
\displaystyle\varepsilon\sim N(0,C)
, where
\displaystyle C
is a variance matrix represented by
\displaystyle C=\sigma^{2}I
, where
\displaystyle I
is the identity matrix and
\displaystyle\sigma
represents the standard deviation. Consequently, the observation data can be expressed as:

d=\mathbb{F}(\theta)+\varepsilon.

(3.1)

Here, we assume that
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is independent of
\displaystyle\theta
. In the Bayesian context, both
\displaystyle\theta
and
\displaystyle d
are treated as random variables. Bayes’ rule yields the posterior probability density, given by:

\mathbb{P}(\theta|d)\propto\mathbb{P}(d|\theta)\mathbb{P}(\theta)

(3.2)

Here,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\theta)=\mathbb{P}(\kappa)\otimes\mathbb{P}(\xi_{0}^{2})\otimes\mathbb{P}(G)
represents the prior distribution before observing the data, where
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\kappa)=U(a,b)
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi_{0}^{2})=U(c,d)
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G)=U(e,f)
. Additionally,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d|\theta)
denotes the likelihood defined by:

\mathbb{P}(d|\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|C^{-\frac{1}{2}}(d-\mathbb{F}(\theta))\|^{2}\right).

Obtaining the posterior distribution marks the initial stage of the Bayesian inverse problem. The subsequent challenge lies in extracting the desired information from the posterior probability density. Typically, statistical information from the posterior density is derived through sampling methods. A commonly used approach for sampling the posterior distribution is based on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). However, this approach often demands a large number of evaluations for solving the forward model, which can be impractical for large-scale problems. Therefore, it may be infeasible to fully exploit the information provided by the posterior probability density function. Nevertheless, sampling can serve as a valuable tool for benchmarking and assessing methods that are feasible for practical application. This holds true for ensemble methods, which have emerged as an excellent approach for capturing features of the posterior distribution
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\theta|d)
within a reasonable computing time.

In this paper, we extend the iterative regularizing ensemble Kalman method (
IREKM

[

to solve the inverse problem (

This characteristic makes it particularly advantageous to deal with the problems where computing gradients is challenging. Consequently, the method has gained significant attention. For a deeper exploration of numerical theory, references

[

4.1
Numerical solution of the direct problem

This section is devoted to introduce an iterative approach for solving the direct problem (
1
). Due to the natural complexity of solving nonlinear models numerically, we will employ a linearization technique as an efficient approach to deal with the challenges posed by nonlinearities. In doing so, we initialize
\displaystyle u^{(0)}\equiv 0
and for
\displaystyle n=1,2,\dots
,
\displaystyle u^{(n)}
represents the solution to the following linear model:

(\mathcal{LP}^{(n)})\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla.(g(|\nabla u^{(n-1)}|)\,\nabla u^{(n)})&=2\varphi&\text{in}\quad&\Omega,\\
u^{(n)}&=0&\text{on}\quad&\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

As it is mentioned in the section (
2
), the solution of the forward problem (
1
) can be obtained as the limit of the sequence
\displaystyle\{u^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. To address the partial differential equation
\displaystyle-\text{div}(g(|\nabla u^{(n-1)}|^{2})\nabla u^{(n)})=2\varphi
over the simplified computational domain
\displaystyle\Omega=[0,a]\times[0,b]
, we employ the finite difference method with central difference approximations for the Laplacian and divergence terms. We set up a grid with nodes
\displaystyle(x_{i},y_{j})
where
\displaystyle x_{i}=i\Delta_{x}
and
\displaystyle y_{j}=j\Delta_{y}
for
\displaystyle i=0,\dots,N_{x}
and
\displaystyle j=0,\dots,N_{y}
. The mesh sizes are determined by
\displaystyle\Delta_{x}=a/N_{x}
and
\displaystyle\Delta_{y}=b/N_{y}
. At each grid point
\displaystyle(x_{i},y_{j})
, the divergence of the gradient of
\displaystyle u^{(n)}
, influenced by a spatial coefficient
\displaystyle g(|\nabla u^{(n-1)}|^{2})
, is approximated by:

\begin{split}-\text{div}(g(|\nabla u^{(n-1)}|^{2})\nabla u^{(n)})&\approx-\frac{1}{\Delta_{x}^{2}}\left[\tilde{g}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}(u_{i+1,j}-u_{i,j})-\tilde{g}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}(u_{i,j}-u_{i-1,j})\right]\\
&-\frac{1}{\Delta_{y}^{2}}\left[\tilde{g}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}(u_{i,j+1}-u_{i,j})-\tilde{g}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}(u_{i,j}-u_{i,j-1})\right],\end{split}

for
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,N_{x}-1
and
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,N_{y}-1
. Here,
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{i+\frac{1}{2},j}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{i-\frac{1}{2},j}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}
, and
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}
represent the averaged values of
\displaystyle\tilde{g}(x,y)=g(|\nabla u^{(n-1)}(x,y)|^{2})
at respective cell interfaces to maintain accuracy at the boundaries of each finite element, i.e,

\tilde{g}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2},j}=\frac{\tilde{g}(x_{i\pm 1},y_{j})+\tilde{g}(x_{i},y_{j})}{2}\;\text{and}\;\tilde{g}_{i,j\pm 1}=\frac{\tilde{g}(x_{i},y_{j\pm 1})+\tilde{g}(x_{i},y_{j})}{2}.

Boundary conditions are strictly enforced with
\displaystyle u_{0,j}^{(n)}=u_{N_{x},j}^{(n)}=u_{i,0}^{(n)}=u_{i,N_{y}}^{(n)}=0
for all
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
, corresponding to
\displaystyle u^{(n)}=0
on
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. The main steps of the proposed iterative process to solve the direct problem (
1
) are summarized in the following algorithm:

Algorithm 2

Iterative Approach for Solving Linearized Problem

Step 1

Choose a tolerance parameter
\displaystyle\overline{\epsilon}
and set
\displaystyle n=0
. Initialize
\displaystyle u^{(0)}
as
\displaystyle 0
.

Step 2

Update the solution by solving problem
\displaystyle(\mathcal{LP}^{(n+1)})
using the finite difference
method to obtain
\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}
.

Step 3

Compute
\displaystyle\|u^{(n+1)}-u^{(n)}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
. If
\displaystyle\|u^{(n+1)}-u^{(n)}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\overline{\epsilon}
go to
Step 5
. Otherwise, proceed to the next step.

Step 4

Set
\displaystyle n\rightarrow n+1
. Go to
Step 2
.

Step 5

The approximated solution
\displaystyle u^{\star}
is
\displaystyle u^{(n+1)}
.

4.2
Reconstruction Results for the Inverse Problem

In this subsection, we focus on demonstrating the effectiveness of Algorithm
1
in solving the considered inverse problem. Specifically, we apply the algorithm to estimate three key parameters simultaneously: the strain hardening exponent
\displaystyle\kappa
, the yield stress
\displaystyle\xi_{0}^{2}
, and the shear modulus
\displaystyle G
. To verify the accuracy of our numerical results, we compute the approximate relative errors as follows:

e_{\kappa_{n}}=\frac{|\overline{\kappa}_{n}-\kappa^{\dagger}|}{|\kappa^{\dagger}|},\quad e_{\xi_{0,n}^{2}}=\frac{|\overline{\xi}_{0,n}^{2}-\xi_{0}^{2,\dagger}|}{|\xi_{0}^{2,\dagger}|},\quad e_{G_{n}}=\frac{|\overline{G}_{n}-G^{\dagger}|}{|G^{\dagger}|},\quad e_{n}=\frac{\|\overline{\theta}_{n}-\theta^{\dagger}\|}{\|\theta^{\dagger}\|},

where
\displaystyle\overline{\theta}_{n}=(\overline{\kappa}_{n},\overline{\xi}_{0,n}^{2},\overline{G}_{n})
represents the mean values at the
\displaystyle n
-th iteration in
IREKM
,
\displaystyle\theta^{\dagger}=(\kappa^{\dagger},\xi_{0}^{2,\dagger},G^{\dagger})
indicates the exact solution of the inverse problem and
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm. We define the residual at each iteration
\displaystyle n
as:

R_{n}=\|C^{-\frac{1}{2}}(d-\overline{w}_{n})\|.

A critical component of iterative algorithms is the implementation of an appropriate stopping rule. We employ the stopping criterion from references
[
28
,
27
,
20
]

R_{n}\leq\tau\delta,

(4.1)

where
\displaystyle\tau\approx\frac{1}{\rho}
(see
[
27
]

\displaystyle\overline{\kappa}_{0}

\displaystyle\overline{\xi}_{0,0}^{2}

\displaystyle\overline{G}_{0}

\displaystyle N_{e}=200
ensemble members participating. We conduct two test cases encountered in practical applications to test the algorithm’s capability. Concluding remarks:
The numerical study outlined above allows us to draw the following conclusions:

•

The accuracy of our algorithm progressively improves with an increasing number of measurements, achieving optimal results when using four measurements.

•

In terms of noise levels, the algorithm performs best with the lowest noise level tested, which is
\displaystyle 0.0001
. As the noise level increases to
\displaystyle 0.001
and
\displaystyle 0.01
, the accuracy becomes less but still acceptable.

Based on the numerical experiments detailed above, it is evident that
IREKM
is both effective and stable for addressing our inverse problem, which involves the simultaneous recovery of multiple parameters in the elastoplastic torsion of a strain-hardening bar.
