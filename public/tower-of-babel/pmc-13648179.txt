The Personhood Pentadigm: A transcultural framework for analyzing existential distress in palliative care
Authors: Han Yee Neo, Cuiwen Fa, Jiaxin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to address three systemic barriers in conventional palliative psychotherapy—the “somatic-reflective disconnect,” compressed terminal timelines, and Western individualistic biases—by developing an integrative, transcultural, and secular care-planning framework to map and systematically disrupt end-of-life existential suffering.MethodsWe employed a dual-stage qualitative design. First, we developed the Personhood Pentadigm (PP) by translating the Eastern psychology of the Five Aggregates into five secular dimensions: Physical Image, Somatosensory Tone, Role and Narrative, Volitional Behavior, and Consciousness. We then integrated these dimensions with Western clinical humanities. Second, we operationalized the PP using purposive qualitative case vignettes of contrasting terminal trajectories, analyzed through a three-phase interpretive procedure: 1) domain-specific coding, 2) causal pathway mapping, and 3) interdisciplinary alignment.ResultsThe analysis revealed existential distress as a dynamic, multi-nodal collapse of personhood rather than a static symptom. The vignettes demonstrated two distinct configurations of suffering: 1) a sequential linear cascade (Case A) in which physical and somatosensory fractures systematically dismantle global macro-narratives, and 2) a non-linear recursive loop (Case B) where somatic distress and loss of roles feed back to drive death anxiety and fear of sleep. Operationally, establishing a somatic runway via physical stabilization is a strict prerequisite for reflective therapies. Furthermore, the PP successfully aligns clinical actions across multidisciplinary specialties.ConclusionsBy providing a shared, translational lexicon and clear role demarcation, the PP empowers multidisciplinary teams to disrupt cascading distress loops. It serves as an operational roadmap that democratizes existential care, enabling psychospiritual support to be directly integrated into bedside palliative care.

Introduction

Existential distress at the end of life is fundamentally a crisis of fractured personhood [1]. As Harvey Chochinov explains, this suffering arises when advanced illness causes a profound rupture that transcends biomedical symptoms, disintegrating the narrative, embodied, and social substrates of the self. To conceptualize this complex disintegration, palliative care has developed specific models of personhood. This theoretical lineage begins with Dame Cicely Saunders’ seminal “Total Pain” model [2] and its subsequent expansion into Christina Puchalski’s holistic, multidisciplinary biopsychosocial-spiritual frameworks [3]. Chochinov operationalized these concepts into a clinical Dignity Model, which categorizes threats to personhood into illness-related concerns, the patient’s internal coping repertoire, and their social dignity inventory [4]. Newer heuristics offer even greater structural precision in locating these identity threats: Lalit Krishna’s Ring Theory maps concentric layers of the self (innate, individual, relational, and societal) [[5], [6], [7]]; Kitwood’s model targets core person-centered needs such as comfort, inclusion and identity [8]; and Senβfelder et al. [9] identify the empirical factors (communication, knowledge, relationships, confidence) that actively erode or restore personhood. These frameworks catalog discrete, compartmentalized facets of suffering but lack the mechanistic granularity to map how psychosomatic disruptions dynamically cascade to erode social agency or narrative integrity. Most remain primarily descriptive, static taxonomies and have not been translated into an actionable clinical roadmap.

Collectively, these models of personhood are vital for identifying the parameters of suffering that guide targeted psychotherapeutic interventions—whether focusing on dignity preservation [4], meaning-making [10], or existential confrontation [11]. When applied to the acute, fast-moving crises of advanced disease, existing psychotherapy models for existential distress exhibit several key limitations. First, they suffer from a “somatic-reflective disconnect” and a timeline barrier imposed by shortened life expectancies; psychotherapies assume a cognitive and chronological “runway” and a level of physical stability—that terminally ill patients overwhelmed by acute somatic distress and steeply progressive illnesses simply do not possess. Second, these models are hindered by operational diffuseness and a relatively unchallenged Western-centric emphasis on autonomy and individualism, focusing heavily on individual remediation while neglecting the deeply interdependent, relational, and culturally mediated dimensions of personhood. A critical gap remains: the need for an integrative, transcultural framework that traces how somatic, narrative, socio-relational, and consciousness disruptions coalesce into the lived experience of existential despair, allowing clinicians to move toward a truly hermeneutic understanding of the patient’s world.

This paper argues that bridging this gap requires a deliberate theoretical synthesis, drawing from diverse wisdom traditions to construct an analytic lens that is both structurally precise and phenomenologically rich. The observed limitations of prevailing research paradigms in palliative care amplify the call for such a framework. As noted in the literature, a biomedical positivist orientation has shaped the field by prioritizing quantifiable outcomes and often marginalizing the thick, contextualized meaning-making processes that define the illness experience [12]. To address this, the proposed framework must move beyond the conventional assumption that psychotherapists and chaplains alone manage existential distress. It must provide a clear, targeted roadmap for multidisciplinary action that clarifies roles across the entire care provider spectrum. By mapping abstract suffering into discrete, interconnected dimensions, the framework equips physicians, nurses, occupational/physical therapists, social workers, and chaplains to work in parallel within their distinct professional boundaries, uniting them under a shared lexicon and transforming holistic care into a systematic, actionable bedside practice.

To this end, we introduce the Personhood Pentadigm (PP), a framework adapted from the Buddhist concept of the five clinging aggregates (pañca-upādāna-khandha)—form (rūpa), feeling (vedanā), perception (saññā), volitional formations (saṅkhāra), and consciousness (viññāṇa)—which serve as the interdependent building blocks of personhood that the mind weaves into a continuous, self-perpetuating self-narrative [13]. Buddhist philosophy posits that suffering arises from the rigid clinging to a static, perceived ideal of the aggregates. When the aggregates deviate from the idealized norm due to advanced illness, the patient’s attachment to what “should be” violently clashes with the biological reality of what “is,” triggering existential distress. This Eastern perspective finds a resonant echo in the Western medical humanities, notably in Cassell’s seminal assertion that “suffering is experienced by persons, not merely by bodies, and has its source in challenges that threaten the intactness of the person as a complex social and psychological entity” [14].

The PP’s critical contribution lies in its focus on the dynamic interactions amongst collapsing constituents of personhood. Deterioration of physical image undermines agency; disruption of somatosensory tone (e.g., through pain) alters emotional capacity; erosion of role and narrative compromises purpose; impairment of volitional behavior erodes autonomy; and altered consciousness shatters continuity. Crucially, these fractures are not isolated. They cascade and amplify one another through feedback loops, invariably mediated by the individual’s relational and cultural context. This synergistic collapse, analyzed through the PP, culminates in profound existential distress.

Methods

This section delineates our qualitative and conceptual methodology. Because this is a conceptual and interpretive study rather than an empirical clinical trial, we employed a dual-stage qualitative design. This design comprises: 1) conceptual framework development through the systematic, transcultural synthesis of Eastern philosophies and Western palliative medicine constructs, and 2) interpretive case-based analysis utilizing contrasting, purposively designed clinical vignettes to operationalize and test the model’s analytical utility.

Conceptual framework development and transcultural synthesis

The PP was constructed as a transcultural analytical framework through a structured, three-step theoretical synthesis.

Step 1: Secular de-dogmatization and reconceptualization

The five clinging aggregates were systematically stripped of metaphysical assumptions and recast as secular, phenomenological categories representing the physical, sensory, socio-relational, behavioral, and cognitive dimensions of lived terminal illness.

Form (rūpa) was translated into Physical Image, capturing the physical markers of illness and embodied identity. Feeling (vedanā) became Somatosensory Tone, representing sensory symptoms and baseline affective states. Perception (saññā) was mapped into Role and Narrative, depicting a patient’s identity, social positioning, and purpose. Volitional Formation (saṅkhāra) was translated into Volitional Behavior, denoting functional independence and coping agency. The term Consciousness (viññāṇa) was retained but adapted to represent the self-reflective knowing awareness that integrates momentary experiences into a continuous sense of self.

Step 2: Theoretical integration with Western clinical models

To ensure clinical grounding, these five dimensions were integrated with modern Western-centric constructs from existentialism, palliative care, and grief and bereavement.

Physical Image and Somatosensory Tone synthesize Eric Cassell’s theories on embodied suffering [14] and Dame Cicely Saunders’ seminal model of “Total Pain” [2]. Role and Narrative are mapped to Harvey Chochinov’s Dignity Model (focusing on role preservation and social support) [15] and Robert Neimeyer’s grief models of narrative disruption and reconstruction [16]. Here, Neimeyer’s concepts of macro-narrative (the cultural, religious, and relational scripts constituting the global assumptive world) and micro-narrative (the immediate story of physical decline, medical dependency, and symptom crises) explain how clinical deterioration deconstructs a patient’s life story. Volitional Behavior integrates theories of functional capacity, coping repertoires, and behavioral agency. Consciousness is synthesized with psychiatric studies linking death anxiety and nightmares to nocturnal panic and insomnia [17].

Step 3: Relational and cultural positioning

A major limitation of existing Western frameworks is an unbalanced emphasis on individualistic autonomy, which equates dignity with self-reliance, personal choices, and individual coping. To resolve this, relationality and social embeddedness are positioned as primary structural dimensions of the PP. Specifically, the Role and Narrative and Volitional Behavior dimensions capture collectivist, Eastern familial and social scripts natively, mapping how a patient’s existential worth is co-constructed with family, and how losing filial or societal utility triggers a devastating “crisis of recognition”.

Case selection criteria and source of materials

To demonstrate and test the PP’s analytical utility, we used a purposive qualitative case vignette methodology. Rather than serving as empirical proof of clinical efficacy, these vignettes function as interpretive, highly detailed models that render visible the complex, cascading, and recursive disruptions theorized by the framework. The case materials were derived from anonymized, synthesized clinical histories and detailed clinical documentation of in-depth therapeutic conversations conducted during routine bedside care collected within a tertiary palliative care service. These histories were integrated to construct two representative cases that reflect the typical patterns of clinical presentation, cultural challenges, and existential collapse observed in end-of-life care. We selected cases based on three strict criteria. 1) Pathophysiological divergence: One oncology case presenting with highly visible, progressive structural deterioration of the body and poorly managed pain (Case A: Linda - Metastatic Breast Cancer) and one non-oncology case presenting with chronic organ failure and severe, debilitating dyspnea (Case B: Arthur - COPD/Post-COVID). 2) Explicit multi-dimensional distress: Both cases had to demonstrate profound, multi-layered suffering, including a desire for hastened death or a profound sense of meaninglessness and hopelessness. 3) Mechanistic contrast: The cases were chosen to represent two distinct pathways of suffering—a linear, downstream cascade (Linda) versus a complex, non-linear recursive feedback loop (Arthur)—to demonstrate the PP’s versatility in mapping different configurations of distress.

Interpretive analytic procedures

Each case was analyzed using a systematic, three-phase interpretive procedure. Phase 1, domain-specific coding (empirical extraction), categorized the clinical data, narratives, and behavioral expressions from each case into the five discrete dimensions to identify localized “sites of fracture.” Phase 2, interaction and pathway mapping (causal analysis), analyzed how these fractures interacted. Rather than examining dimensions in isolation, we traced the directional pathways and recursive feedback loops between domains and visually modeled these pathways to illustrate the systemic collapse of personhood. Phase 3, interdisciplinary alignment (care synthesis), mapped specific, sequenced care interventions to the corresponding domains, identifying lead clinical disciplines for each domain to construct the final interdisciplinary clinical strategies and synthesis matrices.

To ensure epistemological transparency in our case analysis, we adopted a two-tiered narrative approach. Descriptive case narratives (presented in plain text) were constructed from anonymized clinical histories and clinical documentation of in-depth therapeutic conversations with patients/families, preserving the patients’ own words and experiential accounts wherever possible. Interpretive analyses (presented under the “applying the PP to …” subheadings) represent the authors’ theoretical framings, explicitly applying the PP’s five-dimensional lens to deconstruct the mechanisms of distress. This distinction allows readers to clearly separate what patients articulated from how the PP explains their suffering.

To systematically trace how fractures in one dimension propagate to others and form self-reinforcing feedback loops, we adapted a causal pathway mapping technique commonly used in systems thinking and qualitative health research. Each dimension was first coded independently; then, directional arrows and reciprocal influences were mapped iteratively across cases, yielding visual schemas (Fig. 1 for Linda’s linear-cascading pattern; Fig. 2 for Arthur’s non-linear recursive pattern). This analytic procedure aligns with the study’s overarching interpretivist epistemological tradition, which holds that human experiences—including suffering and meaning-making—are socially and culturally constructed and best understood through in-depth, contextualized analysis. Translating analytic heuristic to bedside action: The PP as a care-planning roadmap

To translate the PP from a theoretical, analytical heuristic into an operational bedside reality, clinicians must design individualized care plans that target multiple PP domains concurrently. This simultaneous approach is essential to arrest the cascading collapse of personhood. Relying on traditional, siloed single-domain interventions—such as treating severe nociceptive pain while neglecting the shame behind an unsightly, malodorous wound, or offering reflective psychotherapy without first stabilizing overwhelming physical symptoms—will inevitably be undermined by unaddressed, recursive feedback loops that reinforce and amplify existential suffering.

In contrast, a tailored, multi-nodal intervention plan recognizes that bodily image, sensory thresholds, social roles, physical action, and conscious awareness are not isolated clinical variables. Instead, they reciprocally influence and structurally reinforce one another, converging to define a patient’s integrated self-identity. By using the PP as an actionable care-planning roadmap, interdisciplinary teams can clarify roles (e.g., nursing wound care for Physical Image, physiotherapy for Volitional Behavior, and social work for Role and Narrative) while speaking a shared, translational language. This coordinated approach systematically breaks the destructive feedback loops, providing the patient with a stable somatic foundation and a viable pathway to reclaim their dignity and agency at the end of life.

Illustrative case analysis

To demonstrate the PP's analytic and operational utility, we present two detailed case vignettes. These are not intended as proofs of clinical efficacy but as interpretive illustrations that render visible the complex, cascading disruptions theorized by the framework. The analysis traces how existential distress emerges from the dynamic interplay between somatic insults, narrative collapse, and relational fractures across the five dimensions. Furthermore, these vignettes show how these diagnostic insights translate into structured, multi-dimensional care plans that target each disrupted domain. By mapping specific interdisciplinary interventions to corresponding pentadic fractures, we illustrate a practical roadmap for clinical teams to systematically disrupt these recursive loops of suffering at the bedside.

Case A

Linda — Metastatic breast cancer and the cascading collapse of self

Linda was a 65-year-old retired middle school teacher, formerly cheerful, outgoing, and passionate about education, travel, and volunteering. Two years ago, she was diagnosed with metastatic breast cancer. Despite surgery and two lines of chemotherapy, her condition relapsed. She suffered from a fungating breast mass with foul-smelling discharge, leaving her daily routine consumed by pain, embarrassment, and repeated wound dressings. Linda was widowed, and her son worked abroad. Although her sister visited frequently, Linda refused her help for fear of “being a burden.” Once a confident teacher, she withdrew from social contact. She became emotionally volatile, frequently breaking into tears, and harboring a growing sense of hopelessness and helplessness.

Spiritually, she questioned her life’s meaning, explicitly expressing a wish for euthanasia and struggling with existential questions like, “Why me?” and “What meaning, if any, does my life still hold?” In the silence of the night, she dared not fall asleep, fearing sinking into oblivion. Previously religious, she turned away from her faith when her prayers went unanswered.

Linda was eventually hospitalized for severe cancer pain and started on an infusion of opioids for analgesia. Despite this, she received multiple injections for additional pain relief (breakthrough doses) as she remained unsatisfied with her symptom control. Unfortunately, cumulative doses led to drowsiness and signs of opioid overdose. On questioning, Linda admitted she had figured out the duration of action of a single-dose injection and would ask for an additional booster before its end to prevent a return of the pain. She also welcomed the stuporous state induced by the analgesia, remarking that it helped her escape constant negative ruminations in her mind.

Applying the Personhood Pentadigm to Linda

Physical Image: The fungating, malodorous tumor catastrophically assaulted Linda’s embodied identity, shifting her self-perception from a respected educator to an object of shame. This drove profound social withdrawal, stripping away the daily social affirmations that once sustained her sense of self.

Somatosensory Tone: Relentless pain and dread of wound care created a self-reinforcing loop that epitomized Saunders’ “Total Pain” [18]. This acute distress monopolized Linda’s cognitive resources, leaving no somatic or psychic stability for the reflective work required by psychotherapeutic or meaning-making interventions.

Role and Narrative: Terminal illness stripped Linda of her socially validated roles as an educator and volunteer, collapsing cultural scripts of reciprocity and utility. She experienced a stark discrepancy between her “assumptive world”—where worth equaled active contribution—and her physically dependent reality. Unable to emplot her suffering into a meaningful storyline, her narrative scaffolding collapsed, leaving her existentially lonely and alienated from her own life story.

Volitional Behavior: Her social withdrawal and refusal of help (being a burden) severed her from supportive witnesses who could help reconstruct meaning. Obsessive preemptive analgesia (clock-watching) and sedation to silence pain (chemical coping) [19] were not simple compliance failures but desperate, maladaptive coping behaviors through which she tried to assert control and escape reality; ultimately, these behaviors deepened her isolation and increased her risk of toxicity.

Consciousness: The cascade of somatic and narrative collapse implodes into Linda’s consciousness. Unanswered prayers caused a terrifying “theological rupture,” turning death from a transition into a meaningless descent into annihilation, somatically manifesting as a fear of sleep. Deprived of a coherent religious worldview and its associated coping resources, her plea for euthanasia was not merely an escape from pain but a desperate bid to terminate an unbearable, disintegrating self.

Ultimately, Linda’s existential crisis represents a recursive, closed-loop failure in which each dimension of the PP systematically destabilizes and accelerates the collapse of the next. The violation of her Physical Image drives shame and social withdrawal, which fuse with her persistently adverse Somatosensory Tone—creating a relentless pain-anxiety loop that strips her of the somatic stability necessary for any reflective cognitive coping. This somatic gridlock forces a defensive contraction in her Volitional Behaviour through self-isolation and chemical escape, which severs her from the relational network needed to sustain her Role and Narrative. Lacking “attuned witnesses” to validate her worth, her narrative void crashes into her Consciousness, leaving her knowing awareness completely unanchored and hijacked by a primal terror of annihilation—ultimately driving her plea for euthanasia. The intense negative affect arising from violation of Role and Narrative, as well as Consciousness, loops back to intensify her perception of pain – manifesting as “Total Pain” [2] (Fig. 1).

Linda’s interdisciplinary clinical strategy

To break Linda’s cascading existential collapse, physicians and nurses can first establish a stable somatic foundation by targeting her Somatosensory Tone and Physical Image. Aggressive pain, wound, and odor management is paired with dignity-conserving nursing care (e.g., meticulous skin care and grooming) and a soothing sensory environment. This will help calm her autonomic panic, restore her self-respect, and provide the necessary “somatic runway” (physical and mental stability) for reflective existential therapies.

Next, interventions can shift to rebuilding relational, behavioral, and existential architecture. Medical social workers can then support her Volitional Behavior, as well as Role and Narrative by introducing legacy projects, such as audio recordings for her former students, and organizing video calls with her overseas son. Such engagement shifts her focus away from seeking euthanasia. To address her fractured Consciousness, a pastoral care provider can help her co-construct a protective spiritual narrative by gently reframing her religious queries, while guiding her towards alternative spiritual meanings to her suffering. They then use dignity therapy and life-review to validate her legacy of mentorship, volunteerism, and maternal care, shifting her core self-narrative from “divine abandonment” to one of enduring, transcendent value and symbolic continuity. Case B

Arthur — COPD, total dyspnea, and the strangulation of agency

Arthur was a 55-year-old man with COPD and severe sequelae from COVID-19 pneumonia, which had resulted in irreversible functional deterioration. His physical baseline had shifted drastically, leaving him visibly dependent on medical equipment. Since discharge, Arthur had to constantly wear a nasal cannula; leaving home required a portable oxygen concentrator, a logistical burden restricting his mobility and reinforcing his sense of invalidism. While comfortable at rest, slight exertion triggered severe dyspnea. These episodes precipitated acute distress—coughing, chest tightness, air hunger, panic, and intense death anxiety, characterized by catastrophic thoughts such as “I’m suffocating, I’m going to die!”

His illness had forced a profound shift in his social and familial roles. Previously the primary breadwinner, Arthur had lost his provider status and depended on his wife for care, including assistance with intimate activities of daily living like bathing and toileting. Joyful social activities, like meeting friends or running after grandchildren, were no longer physically possible, and his visible oxygen tubing added a layer of shame to his social withdrawal. Arthur’s anxiety also dominated his sleep patterns; he feared sleeping at night, worried he would lose the ability to breathe purposefully and die suddenly.

This loss of autonomy had negatively impacted his temperament. He had become verbally aggressive toward his wife, often lashing out when household tasks were not performed to his exact specifications. This aggression, coupled with his high-needs dependency, had severely strained their relationship. Overwhelmed by these changes, Arthur expressed a profound lack of purpose and repeatedly verbalized the belief that he would be better off dead.

Applying the personhood Pentadigm to Arthur

Arthur’s case highlights a distinct pathway to existential distress, where the central threat emanates from the Somatosensory Tone dimension and rapidly constricts the entire field of personhood. His experience is defined by acute dyspnea—chest tightness, air hunger, and suffocation—triggering catastrophic cognitions (“I’m going to die!”) and panic. This establishes a vicious dyspnea-anxiety cycle where fear worsens breathlessness perception. Repeated traumatic episodes condition a state of hypervigilance akin to PTSD, where mere exertion or the sight of oxygen tubing becomes a trigger [20]. This multi-dimensional syndrome is comprehensively described as “Total Dyspnea”, adapting Saunders’ model of “Total Pain” to respiratory suffering [21].

This somatic threat directly contracts his Volitional Behavior. To avoid triggers, Arthur withdrew from cherished activities, causing physical deconditioning. Concurrently, medical equipment (nasal cannula, oxygen) deformed his Physical Image, making him feel frail. This eroded “somatic agency” is painfully epitomized by his reliance on his wife for intimate care, representing a profound, embodied humiliation.

The collapse of autonomous action deconstructed Arthur’s Role and Narrative. Culturally, his self-worth is tethered to the patriarchal script of being a productive provider; socially, his legacy rested on being a vital grandfather. Terminal illness dismantled both scaffoldings. This sudden descent from an authoritative patriarch to a dependent invalid plunged him into a profound crisis of recognition, in which he could neither fulfil nor feel valued through these culturally prized roles. This narrative void generated intense existential loneliness—an intolerable empty sadness exacerbated by his anxious awareness of impending death [22].

To reclaim agency within Volitional Behavior, Arthur obsessed over micro-controlled domestic details and directed verbal aggression toward his wife—a displaced protest against his invalidated, dependent self. Finally, these fractures converged into a disordered Consciousness. His awareness is dominated by intrusive catastrophic imagery, bodily hypervigilance, and a paralyzing fear of sleep, which leads him to interpret the loss of conscious control as a threat to breathing itself. Arthur remained trapped in an existential gridlock, exhausted by the conflict between the biological terror of dying and the inability to derive meaning from living.

The PP analysis thus reveals his wish for hastened death as not mere depression, but as a logical, though tragic, plea to end the strangulation of his agentic self – a devastating climax of a synergistic feedback loop where biological alarms completely dismantle his narrative, behavior, and identity.

While Linda’s experience of existential distress unfolds as a sequential, downstream cascade (Fig. 1), Arthur’s trajectory highlights the non-linear complexity and recursive feedback loops that bind the five PP dimensions (Fig. 2). At the epicenter, acute dyspnea (Somatosensory Tone) triggers panic and a vicious dyspnea-anxiety cycle. This forces a contraction in Volitional Behavior (activity avoidance) that deconditions his body and exacerbates breathlessness. Concurrently, visible oxygen reliance deforms his Physical Image, driving social withdrawal to escape stigma. To regain control, his Volitional Behavior shifts to domestic micromanagement and verbal aggression, straining his marriage. This relational rupture and physical decline demolish his Role and Narrative, triggering a “crisis of recognition” as culturally prized roles dissolve. Finally, this narrative void crashes into Consciousness, where respiratory hypervigilance and fear of sleep hijack his awareness, culminating in a wish for hastened death that loops back to amplify his “Total Dyspnea.”

Arthur’s interdisciplinary clinical strategy

To break Arthur’s cascading existential collapse, the interdisciplinary team first establishes a secure somatic foundation by targeting his Somatosensory Tone and Physical Image. Physicians initiate rapid, multi-modal symptom control—combining bronchodilators, inhaled steroids, opioids, and anxiolytics with non-pharmacological strategies like a handheld fan and pursed-lip breathing—to interrupt the dyspnea-anxiety loop [23]. This is paired with dignity-conserving care, including optimized grooming and clothing, to restore embodied agency. Next, physiotherapists and occupational therapists expand his Volitional Behavior space through energy-conservation training, electronic mobility devices, and mindfulness to mitigate functional dependency. This enables medical social workers to rebuild his fractured Role and Narrative through couples counseling that renegotiates relational dynamics, helping Arthur adapt his identity from a physical provider to a nurturing grandfather who verbally mentors his grandchildren. Finally, to calm a Consciousness hijacked by severe sleep disruption and death anxiety, the team can offer chaplaincy support for closure and, if dyspnea becomes intractable at the end of life, palliative sedation. Discussion

The PP, as illustrated through the cases of Linda and Arthur, provides more than a clinical checklist; it offers a critical analytic framework for deconstructing the lived experience of existential distress at the end of life. By integrating an Asian philosophical model of interdependent selfhood with Western existential and dignity-based perspectives, the PP moves beyond compartmentalized approaches. It allows us to trace how suffering is simultaneously embodied, narratively constituted, and socially mediated. This discussion elucidates the framework’s key theoretical contributions to health social science and palliative care, places it in dialogue with existing models, and considers its implications for research and practice.

Toward an integrative phenomenology of suffering: Bridging the embodied and the narrative

The PP’s primary contribution is an integrated phenomenology systematically connecting somatic experience with life narrative. While prevailing models (Logotherapy, Meaning-Centered Therapy, Dignity Therapy) focus deeply on cognitive reconstruction, they can inadvertently overlook immediate, overwhelming somatic crises. Furthermore, terminally ill patients often lack the cognitive and chronological “runway” required by high-fidelity psychotherapies; untreated and severe physical symptoms deny them the somatic stability necessary to find psychic space for reflective attitudinal shifts [24]. This barrier likely explains the heterogeneous empirical benefits of stand-alone existential interventions near death [25].

By foregrounding Physical Image and Somatosensory Tone as foundational dimensions of personhood, the PP asserts that the body is the primary ground where identity and meaning are destabilized. Linda’s shame and Arthur’s dyspnea are not peripheral symptoms but the generative cores fracturing narratives and behaviors. Ultimately, the PP extends Saunders’ “Total Pain” concept by mapping how localized physical suffering cascades across socio-relational and narrative domains to culminate in profound existential distress.

From descriptive models to causal explanation: Mapping the mechanisms of distress

The PP advances the field by moving beyond a taxonomy of suffering toward tracing its generative, directional pathways. While Chochinov’s Dignity Model powerfully catalogs illness-related concerns and dignity threats, it does not specify how a particular threat (such as functional loss) propagates across a patient’s life domains to erode their sense of self. By defining five interactive dimensions, the PP maps these explicit connections. For instance, it traces how Arthur’s dyspnea (Somatosensory Tone) constricts his physical capacity (Volitional Behavior), which in turn dismantles his social role (Role/Narrative), culminating in a consciousness dominated by fear and futility.

This structural granularity addresses a recognized gap in psychotherapeutic models for advanced illnesses. As Nissim et al. noted in their work on Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully (CALM) therapy, a key challenge is the discrepancy between the abstract nature of existential concerns and the need for concrete clinical interventions [26]. The PP provides a structured map that translates abstract constructs like meaninglessness into distinct clinical targets, creating actionable leverage points for clinical multidisciplinary teams.

A pragmatic actionable roadmap for interdisciplinary practice

The PP operationalizes care by resolving the clinical challenge of fragmented, parallel clinical interventions where disciplines work in isolation. By translating complex existential distress into five distinct dimensions, the framework enables precise role demarcation: medical and nursing staff stabilize Physical Image and Somatosensory Tone, while social workers and chaplains lead narrative reconstruction and spiritual care. This mapping ensures each specialty intervenes within its boundaries while remaining aligned with a singular, integrated, and person-centered therapeutic plan.

Crucially, the PP does not sequester psychospiritual support within scarce, specialized psychotherapy or chaplaincy resources. Instead, its straightforward, non-dogmatic structure equips clinicians across all specialties to adopt an existential approach to routine bedside care, lowering conceptual barriers and enabling broad, cross-disciplinary training. This aligns with recent literature advocating that existential support be woven directly into the daily, holistic fabric of routine palliative practice, rather than treated as a peripheral specialty consult [27].

Highlighting relational scaffolding to counterbalance an over-emphasis on autonomous personhood

Palliative care ethics are heavily dominated by Western, individualistic constructs of autonomy. Challenging these assumptions, the PP’s adaptation of Buddhist psychology aligns with calls for cultural humility and non-Western integration to create more inclusive, person-centred care [28,29]. Rather than viewing the self as an isolated unit, the PP conceptualizes personhood as an interdependent, relational, and socially embedded system. In Eastern contexts, identity and purpose are deeply tied to familial roles and social utility—dynamics captured natively by the PP’s Role and Narrative dimension. When a terminal illness limits a patient’s ability to contribute, it violates cultural scripts of reciprocity, plunging them into an ontological “crisis of recognition.” Furthermore, fear of “being a burden” often drives social withdrawal, severing patients from family and community members vital to validating their enduring worth.

Treating relationality as a primary dimension rather than a secondary variable, the PP provides a shared, pragmatic lexicon for interdisciplinary teams to address family-level fractures. Clinicians can look beyond isolated cognitive reframing to integrate family-focused interventions, including counselling to renegotiate caregiving roles and designing legacy projects that reinforce the patient’s symbolic continuity and ongoing value to the family unit. Fundamentally, these steps systematically restore a sense of social reciprocity.

Limitations and future directions

As a conceptual and interpretative study, the primary limitation of this work lies in its lack of empirical validation through quantitative or prospective interventional data. The PP framework, as presented, is a heuristic tool whose clinical efficacy in improving patient-reported existential distress and dignity remains to be tested. To transition into a robust, evidence-based paradigm, the PP must emulate the developmental pathway of established frameworks such as the Dignity-Conserving Care (DCC) model [30]. Currently, the PP lacks standardized psychometric instruments to systematically identify and stratify distress. Future research must prioritize operationalizing its five dimensions into a validated, multi-dimensional questionnaire enabling clinical teams to screen and score existential suffering at the bedside.

Furthermore, while the PP maps how suffering cascades, it lacks a manualized intervention protocol. To achieve clinical utility, future efforts must focus on publishing a comprehensive interdisciplinary manual that maps each dimension to a structured, modular menu of targeted allied health interventions. By explicitly detailing how medical, nursing, physical/occupational therapy, social work, and chaplaincy roles coordinate around specific pentadic fractures, this manual will transform the PP from an analytical heuristic into an actionable clinical playbook. This should be followed by feasibility studies and randomized controlled trials evaluating PP-informed care models against standard care. Finally, cross-cultural validation is essential to explore how these dimensions are weighted and interrelate in diverse sociocultural contexts beyond the clinical narratives examined here.

Conclusions

The PP offers a transcultural framework reframing terminal existential distress from an abstract concept into a dynamic, systemic collapse of personhood. By bridging Eastern psychology with Western clinical models, the PP captures the family-centric, socially embedded realities of a patient’s lived experience. It moves beyond static taxonomies to provide a highly translational roadmap mapping how somatic insults, self-image shifts, and narrative fractures recursively loop to hijack consciousness. By establishing a shared clinical language, the framework dissolves professional silos, empowering front-line clinicians to routinely integrate bedside existential care rather than sequestering it as the exclusive domain of scarce specialists. As future work operationalizes these dimensions into validated screening tools and structured intervention manuals, the PP provides a powerful blueprint for holistic palliative medicine. Ultimately, it challenges clinical teams to move beyond fragmented treatments to systematically disrupt cascading loops of suffering, helping patients reweave their fractured narratives and find dignity, continuity, and peace in their final chapters.
