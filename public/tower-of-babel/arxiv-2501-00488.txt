Abstract
According to Russell, strict uses of the definite article ‘the’ in a definite description ‘the F F ’ involve uniqueness; in case there is more than one F F , ‘the F F ’ is used somewhat loosely, and an indefinite description ‘an F F ’ should be preferred. We give an account of constructions of the form ‘the F F is G G ’ in which the definite article is used loosely (and in which ‘the F F ’ is, therefore, incomplete), essentially by replacing the usual notion of identity in Russell’s uniqueness clause with the notion of qualified identity, i.e., ‘ a a is the same as b b in all 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} -respects’, where 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} is a subset of the set of predicates 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . This modification gives us qualified notions of uniqueness and definiteness. A qualified definiteness statement ‘the 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} -unique F F is G G ’ is strict in case 𝒬 = 𝒫 \mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P} and loose in case 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} is a proper subset of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . The account is made formally precise in terms of proof theory and proof-theoretic semantics. Keywords: definiteness, incomplete descriptions, proof-theoretic semantics, uniqueness

Introduction

Sometimes we use the definite description ‘the

According to Russell (

According to Russell, such loose uses of ‘the

In this paper, we propose a formal account of both uses of

On a Russellian analysis, a construction of the form

If, on the other hand, we put

We may use this reading only in case there are at least two things

What is subject to restriction, on this account, is thus the set

\mathcal{Q}
