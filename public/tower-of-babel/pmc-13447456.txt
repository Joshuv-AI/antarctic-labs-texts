Predicting success after laparoscopic heller myotomy: lessons from 22 years in a resource-limited context
Authors: Mohammed Raiss, Hamza Sekkat, Othmane Bourouail, Mohamed Ben Lahsen, Mouna Mhamdi Alaoui, Farid Sabbah, Youness Bakali, Abdelmalek Hrora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objective Achalasia is a rare primary esophageal motility disorder. While laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) is a well-established treatment worldwide, no studies to date have evaluated its long-term outcomes and risk factors for failure in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In our context, peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) remains uncommon, and surgery is still the first-line treatment. This study aims to evaluate the functional and symptomatic outcomes of laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM), identify risk factors associated with surgical failure, and highlight the benefits of concomitant anti-reflux procedures in reducing the incidence of gastroesophageal reflux. Methods and materials We conducted a retrospective analysis of 55 consecutive patients who underwent LHM with or without an anti-reflux system (ARS) between July 2000 and December 2022. Clinical success was defined as the resolution of dysphagia with a concomitant improvement in the Eckardt score, corroborated by objective assessments including lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure measurements and 24-hour pH-metry. Iatrogenic reflux was diagnosed based on clinical symptoms and confirmed through esophageal pH-metric evaluation. Potential risk factors contributing to treatment failure were systematically analyzed. Results Dysphagia (98.2%) and weight loss (74.5%) were the predominant symptoms. In our series, two conversions were reported (3.6%) following esophageal perforation. Nineteen patients (34.5%) had undergone ARS, with 10 of DOR type (18.2%) and 9 of Toupet/Nissen type (16.4%). With a mean follow-up of 24.47 months, dysphagia had disappeared in 51 (92.8%) and the mean Eckardt score was improved by 0.92 (+/- 1.25) vs. 5.46 (+/- 1.77) with P=<0.05. All patients with persistent dysphagia had lost weight preoperatively (P=0.05). The absence of an anti-reflux system was associated with more gastro-esophageal reflux disease (16.7% vs. 5.2%, P=0.181) and more esophageal exposure to gastric acid (9.1% vs. 16.4%, P=0.224). ARS, whether Dor or Toupet, were effective in preventing postoperative reflux. Conclusion In our LMIC setting, LHM remains the gold standard for achalasia treatment given the limited availability of POEM. When combined with ARS, it provides durable symptom control, prevents reflux, and maintains good functional results. Preoperative weight loss may predict poorer postoperative outcomes.

Introduction

Achalasia cardia (AC) or idiopathic megaesophagus is a rare primary esophageal motility disorder characterized by impaired relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter and the absence of coordinated peristalsis in the esophageal body, leading to progressive dysphagia, regurgitation, and weight loss [1]. Its global incidence is estimated at 1–5 cases per 100,000 population annually, with no sex predominance but an increasing frequency with age [2]. The condition is chronic and progressive, often diagnosed late in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where access to high-resolution manometry and specialized surgical expertise remains limited [3].

The therapeutic goal in AC is to relieve functional obstruction at the gastroesophageal junction. Multiple treatment modalities exist, including pneumatic dilation, botulinum toxin injection, peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), and surgical myotomy [4, 5]. Laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) with partial fundoplication is considered the gold-standard surgical approach in many high-income countries, given its durable symptom relief and the ability to combine myotomy with an anti-reflux procedure [6, 7]. In LMICs, open surgery is still considered as the first-line definitive treatment [3].

Only a limited number of studies from Africa, mainly case reports and small case series, have addressed the surgical management of achalasia [3]. The majority of these describe open Heller myotomy performed through laparotomy, while reports of laparoscopic approaches remain relatively uncommon [8]. As a result, evidence regarding long-term functional outcomes, postoperative reflux control, and predictors of surgical failure in African populations is still very limited [8].

In this context, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the largest documented series of LHM for AC in Africa and the first series in which 100% of cases were performed laparoscopically. The primary objective is to assess both functional and symptomatic outcomes and to identify risk factors for surgical failure. A secondary objective is to examine the impact of concomitant anti-reflux procedures on postoperative gastroesophageal reflux in a setting where POEM remains uncommon and surgical intervention is the prevailing standard of care.

Methods and materials

This retrospective study spans a 22-year period from July 2000 to December 2022 and includes 65 patients diagnosed with esophageal achalasia. Inclusion criteria were: age over 18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of achalasia established by at least one paraclinical investigation (barium swallow, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, or manometry), a complete medical record, and having undergone Heller myotomy performed exclusively via laparoscopy. Of the 65 eligible patients, 55 met these criteria and underwent laparoscopic Heller myotomy, either alone or in combination with fundoplication. Ten patients who underwent alternative surgical procedures (open Heller myotomy or other esophageal interventions) were excluded (Fig. 1). The diagnosis of achalasia was established on the basis of clinical findings, barium swallow (BSE), upper gastrointestinal (UGI) endoscopy and manometry. In the earliest cases of our series, fundoplication was not routinely performed as it was not yet systematically recommended at that time; it was subsequently adopted as standard practice in our institution.

The success of laparoscopic Heller myotomy was assessed by postoperative resolution of dysphagia, clinically using the Eckardt score, and by measuring lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure. Surgery procedure

Surgery procedure

All procedures were performed by three surgeons. Although the surgical technique evolved over the 22-year study period, from 2015 onward a standardized Heller myotomy protocol was discussed and unanimously adopted by all surgeons in order to minimize potential study bias. The myotomy was consistently performed using a monopolar hook cautery, with bipolar energy applied selectively for hemostasis during hiatal dissection. The surgical procedure begins with exposure of the hiatus and His angle, followed by a section of the pars flaccida and pars condensa of the lesser omentum, and then the phreno-oesophageal ligament. Dissection of the anterior surface of esophagus continues into the mediastinum for at least 7 cm, associated with a preparation of the myotomy zone in the cardia. The anterior vagus nerve is located and separated from the ligament. The vertical myotomy, performed with a monopolar hook, is made just above the cardia and involves first the longitudinal muscle fibers, then the circular fibers before reaching the submucosa. It continues upwards along the esophagus for 5 to 6 cm, then extends downwards on the gastric side in an axis that slopes to the left and downwards for approximately 1.5 to 2 cm. The edges of the myotomy are spread to ensure that circular muscle fibers are absent, the mucosa is intact and more than a third of the anterior circumference of the mucosa is exposed. In cases of inadvertent mucosal injury, primary laparoscopic repair was performed using interrupted absorbable sutures (4 − 0 Vicryl), followed by intraoperative leak testing with air insufflation after placement of a nasogastric tube.

The operation ends with the fitting of an anti-reflux system. Previously, a posterior hemi valve was performed using the Toupet, while, the Dor fundoplication technique has become as our preferred choice for fundoplication, except in cases where a hiatal hernia is identified, in which instance the Toupet technique is employed.2Post-operative follow-up and monitoring

Post-operative follow-up and monitoring

Patients who underwent surgery without intraoperative complications were authorized to initiate oral intake on the day following the surgical procedure, and discharge clearance was granted upon the successful tolerance of liquid feeding. Follow-up assessments were scheduled at intervals of 15 days, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and subsequently on an annual basis. Clinical manifestations were documented during these consultations. Individuals exhibiting persistent dysphagia underwent an esophagram, while those presenting with gastroesophageal reflux disease underwent systematic esophageal pH-metry.3Statistical analysisAll the data harvested was analyzed. Qualitative variables were presented as frequency and percentage (n, %), and quantitative variables were expressed as means and medians according to interquartile intervals. A univariate analysis with the chi2 test was performed. P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. These statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi.

Our study comprised 55 patients, with 31 females (56.4%), exhibiting a mean age of 39.5 +/- 15 years. Predominant symptoms included dysphagia (98.2%) and weight loss (74.5%). Additional symptoms were documented in the study, albeit with lower frequencies, including regurgitation (40%), chest pain (20%), and occurrences of cough and false routes (4%).

The mean duration since the initiation of symptoms was 45.5 +/- 42 months. Achalasia diagnosis, confirmed through esophageal transit assessments in 90.9% of cases (n = 50), revealed that 70% were categorized as type II according to the Ressano Malenchini classification(8,9). Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (n = 51, 92.7%) identified achalasia-related signs (dilatation, stasis, popping sign) in 80.1%, with only 34.6% exhibiting inflammation of the esogastric mucosa. All patients (100%) underwent esophageal manometry. High-resolution manometry was implemented in our institution in 2017 and was performed in 17 patients (30.9%), whereas earlier cases were assessed using conventional manometry. This limited use may affect the reliability of subtype classification according to the Chicago Classification. Among patients evaluated with high-resolution manometry, classification according to Chicago version 3.0 revealed a predominance of type II achalasia (64.7%).

All Heller myotomies were executed laparoscopically, with a conversion rate of 3.6%. Intraoperatively, complications occurred in 10.9% of cases, comprising five wounds of the eso-cardiac mucosa (9.1%) and one perforation of the left pleura (1.8%). The ARS-group contained 19 patients (34.6%), with 10 (18.2%) undergoing anterior fundoplication (Dor) and 9 (16.4%) opting for posterior anti-reflux surgery (ARS) (toupet: n = 8/Nissen: n = 1). The decision to perform Nissen fundoplication was based on the intraoperative identification of hiatal hernia. Postoperatively, two patients (3.36%) encountered complications: one resolved with antibiotic therapy for pneumopathy, and the other experienced immediate postoperative respiratory distress, successfully extubated on the same day.

At a mean follow-up of 3 years, dysphagia ceased in 51 patients (92.8%), with 9 individuals reporting occasional, well-tolerated swallowing difficulties, which subsequently resolved within a few months postoperatively. However, 7.2% of patients expressed complaints of moderate and intermittent dysphagia. The mean initial Eckardt score reduced from 5.46 +/- 1.77 to 0.92 +/- 1.25 (P < 0.05) postoperatively. Mean lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure decreased to 1.66 Kpa (+/- 0,71) compared to the preoperative value of 5.1 Kpa (+/−1,92) (P < 0.05). All cases of postoperative dysphagia were associated with preoperative weight loss (P = 0.05). The mean postoperative Eckardt score was also lower in the presence of preoperative weight loss (1.1 vs. 0.42, P = 0.08), although not statistically significant. Statistically, pre-therapeutic esophageal diameter (P = 0.289), achalasia subtype according to Chicago (P = 0.555), intraoperative complications (P = 0.318), and myotomy length (P = 0.995) exhibited no impact on the efficacy of the Heller technique.

Postoperatively, 12.7% of patients reported reflux symptoms, with 85.7% of those without fundoplication. In the ARS group (34,6%), only one (1/19, 5.2%) complained of gastroesophageal reflux symptoms, compared to 6 (6/36, 16.7%) in the non-ARS group (65,4%) (P = 0.181). Pathological findings in postoperative 24-hour total pH-metry occurred in 25.5% of cases, with 16.4% without fundoplication versus 9.1% with it (P = 0.613). Positive standing pH-metry was observed in 9.1% (n = 5) without ARS versus 0% with it (P = 0.128). Positive supine pH-metry was reported in 21.8% of patients without fundoplication versus 9.1% with it (P = 0.456). Upon comparing various ARS types, it was noted that both the Dor and Toupet techniques are effective in preventing GERD, with a subtle inclination towards the Dor fundoplication (90% vs. 85%), albeit lacking statistical significance (P = 0.833). Impact of high-resolution manometry

Following the introduction of high-resolution manometry, we aimed to better assess its impact on both management and outcomes. To this end, we analyzed a subgroup of 18 patients who underwent surgery after the implementation of HRM (HRM group, HRMG), and compared them with patients evaluated using conventional manometry (MG group).

The mean age was comparable between the two groups (39.94 ± 16.61 years in the HRMG vs. 39.41 ± 14.85 years in the MG group). Dysphagia was reported in 100% of patients in the HRMG compared to 97.3% in the MG group. Overall, both groups were comparable across all baseline variables (Table 3).

Discussion

Achalasia of the esophagus is a rare primary motility disorder of unknown etiology, characterized by elevated lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure and disordered esophageal body contractions [1, 2]. Dysphagia remains the predominant and most persistent clinical manifestation [9]. Diagnosis relies primarily on esophageal manometry and barium swallow esophagram (BSE), while upper gastrointestinal endoscopy plays an essential role in excluding organic pathologies [10, 11]. With the advent of high-resolution manometry (HRM), diagnostic accuracy has improved, making it the current gold-standard investigation for early detection, given its superior sensitivity compared with BSE and endoscopy [11]. Diagnostic confirmation is based on two principal criteria: absence of coordinated peristalsis in the esophageal body and impaired relaxation of the LES, although these features may be incomplete in early disease stages [12]. More recently, timed barium swallow (TBS) has been increasingly recommended as a complementary tool due to its diagnostic value and its utility in assessing treatment outcomes [13].

The therapeutic objective in achalasia is to relieve functional obstruction at the gastroesophageal junction [14]. Sir Ernest Heller first described esophagocardiomyotomy in 1913, proposing both anterior and posterior myotomy. Over time, multiple refinements and technical modifications have been introduced in response to advanced disease stages, intra- and postoperative complications, and the development of minimally invasive surgery [1]. The laparoscopic approach offers the same well-recognized advantages as other minimally invasive abdominal procedures, including reduced wound complications, less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery [14, 15]. Furthermore, mastery of laparoscopy facilitates the management of intraoperative complications such as esophageal perforation and allows simultaneous treatment of associated conditions, including hiatal hernia [16].

With the emergence of novel endoscopic approaches, particularly peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), numerous studies have reassessed the role of laparoscopic Heller myotomy (LHM) in idiopathic achalasia [17]. In our series, clinical success was achieved in 92.8% of cases, with complete resolution of dysphagia in most patients, while a minority experienced transient postoperative dysphagia that resolved within months. These results are comparable to those reported in the literature: Fukushima et al. reported an 80.1% dysphagia resolution rate, Schlottmann’s meta-analysis found a pooled clinical success rate of 91%, and Werner’s randomized trial reported 82% success [6, 18, 19].

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), data on LHM remain limited but generally demonstrate favorable outcomes despite resource constraints. In Cameroon, Kamla et al. reported a 96% success rate after Heller myotomy, with only one postoperative complication among 26 patients [3]. In India, Gupta et al. observed recurrent dysphagia in 11.4% of patients after a median follow-up of 9.5 months [20]. In Morocco, a pediatric series reported persistent dysphagia in approximately 14% of patients [21]. Although these studies involve small cohorts, they support the effectiveness of LHM in LMICs and emphasize the importance of standardized technique and structured follow-up [3].

Numerous factors influencing LHM outcomes have been identified. Several studies demonstrate a clear correlation between achalasia subtype and surgical success, with type II and type I showing superior outcomes compared to type III according to the Chicago classification [10]. In a series of 246 patients, failure rates were 14.6% for type I, 4.7% for type II, and 30.4% for type III (P = 0.0007) [11, 22]. A meta-analysis of 727 patients similarly found that type II achalasia was significantly more likely to respond favorably to LHM compared with type III (OR = 6.86, P = 0.000) [23]. The presence of a sigmoid esophagus (Ressano Malenchini stage VI) has also been associated with poorer outcomes, although LHM may still provide symptomatic improvement in selected cases [11, 23]. In our series, high-resolution manometry was performed in only 30.9% of patients, thereby limiting the reliability of subtype classification according to the Chicago system. As conventional manometry was used in earlier cases, potential misclassification cannot be excluded. Consequently, the absence of a significant association between achalasia subtype and surgical outcomes in our study should be interpreted with caution.

Preoperative interventions such as pneumatic dilation or botulinum toxin injection have been associated with higher rates of surgical failure. Snyder et al. reported a 28% failure rate in patients with prior endoscopic therapy compared with 7% in treatment-naïve patients [24]. Similarly, in a cohort of 200 patients, failure occurred in 17% of those previously treated endoscopically versus 4% in untreated individuals [25]. Finley et al. also noted that prior endoscopic therapy was associated with delayed recurrence of severe dysphagia after surgery [26].

In our study, preoperative weight loss was the only factor significantly associated with persistent postoperative dysphagia: all patients with postoperative dysphagia had lost weight before surgery (100%, P = 0.057). This likely reflects that preoperative weight loss is a marker of advanced disease and severe esophageal dysfunction, potentially limiting full symptomatic recovery after LHM. This finding aligns with Khajanchee et al., who identified severe preoperative dysphagia as a predictor of poorer outcomes [27]. However, Ceribelli et al. reported that a 10% weight loss was paradoxically associated with better postoperative outcomes in their series [28], highlighting the complexity of prognostic factors.

The extent of myotomy has been widely debated. Several authors advocate extending the gastric component to at least 3 cm to reduce the risk of recurrent dysphagia [29, 30]. However, few studies have directly compared different proximal myotomy lengths, with most suggesting that a safe range is between 6 and 8 cm [11]. In our cohort, the mean myotomy length was 6 cm on the esophageal side and 2–3 cm on the gastric side, with no significant correlation between myotomy length and clinical outcome. Regardless of technique, myotomy inevitably disrupts the gastroesophageal junction and compromises the physiological anti-reflux barrier of the LES, frequently leading to iatrogenic gastroesophageal reflux [31, 32]. This has been described as the “Achilles’ heel” of endoscopic treatments, with reported postoperative reflux rates as high as 53.4% after POEM [33]. In contrast, LHM allows simultaneous construction of an anti-reflux procedure, which is also possible in open surgery [7]. Historically, in the absence of an anti-reflux system (ARS), symptomatic GERD occurred in up to 48% of patients following myotomy [7, 30].

Multiple studies have demonstrated the benefit of combining fundoplication with LHM, showing significantly lower rates of pathological reflux (31.5% vs. 8.8% without ARS, P = 0.003) while maintaining equivalent dysphagia control [15, 34, 35]. Additionally, fundoplication has been associated with improved quality of life [15, 36]. Our results are consistent with these findings: patients who underwent LHM with fundoplication had lower rates of symptomatic reflux (5.2% vs. 16.7%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.401). Pathological 24-hour pH exposure was also less frequent in the ARS group (9.1% vs. 16.4%, P = 0.128).

In LMICs, published data on postoperative reflux after LHM are scarce. A recent Moroccan study compared LHM with limited hiatal dissection versus LHM with ARS, concluding that limited dissection could offer comparable reflux control [37]. However, their postoperative reflux rate was 20.7%, slightly higher than in patients receiving fundoplication. Another Moroccan pediatric series reported reflux in 21.4% of cases [21], while an Indian study found symptomatic GERD in 8.5% of patients [20]. Despite variability, the broader literature supports the protective role of fundoplication in reducing postoperative reflux [3].

The optimal type of fundoplication remains debated. Some authors advocate Nissen fundoplication (360°) [38], but evidence suggests this increases postoperative dysphagia without improving reflux control compared to partial wraps [39]. Consequently, most authors now favor partial fundoplication after LHM [11, 39]. Randomized trials have shown better reflux control with Dor compared to Toupet, with lower esophageal acid exposure on pH monitoring [40–43]. Our findings align with this, as Dor fundoplication demonstrated slightly better outcomes than Toupet or Nissen in reducing reflux, possibly due to less dissection and better preservation of the meso-esophagus [44].

A major strength of our study is its 22-year duration, capturing the evolution of LHM in an LMIC context. With 55 patients, this is, to our knowledge, the largest fully laparoscopic series of achalasia reported in Africa and the first in which all cases were managed laparoscopically, distinguishing it from earlier reports relying predominantly on open surgery [45].

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design is subject to missing or incomplete data. The relatively small sample size limits statistical power for subgroup comparisons, particularly regarding fundoplication types. Additionally, follow-up was not uniform for all patients, which may affect assessment of long-term durability. Finally, evolving diagnostic and surgical practices over 22 years may have introduced heterogeneity that is difficult to fully control.

Future research should focus on multicentric prospective studies in LMICs with larger cohorts, standardized surgical protocols, and uniform long-term follow-up. Such studies would strengthen evidence-based recommendations and improve care for achalasia patients in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

Apart from preoperative weight loss, which was linked to persistent dysphagia, laparoscopic Heller myotomy proved to be a safe and effective treatment in our series, providing durable functional and symptomatic relief. In emerging countries, where peroral endoscopic myotomy remains uncommon, LHM combined with partial fundoplication, particularly Dor, offers a reliable strategy, ensuring effective control of achalasia while reducing the risk of postoperative gastroesophageal reflux.
