A preliminary study on the clinical value of simultaneous amplification and testing for tuberculosis (SAT-TB) for early therapeutic monitoring in multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis
Authors: Xiaoqing Ma, Han Tu, Guangzhi Ying, Fang Wang, Yaojie Shen, Yuxia Yu, Geng Chen, Yuanyuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) poses a major global health challenge due to its complex treatment regimen and the lack of rapid and effective monitoring tools. Conventional methods, such as the long-culture-period MGIT™960 and molecular assays like Xpert, which cannot distinguish between viable and nonviable bacteria, are unable to meet the clinical demand for dynamic monitoring.MethodsA total of 125 patients with MDR/RR-TB diagnosed at Hangzhou Red Cross Hospital between January 2022 and October 2024 were included in the study. Sputum samples were collected at baseline (T0), the end of the 1st month of treatment (T1), and the end of the 2nd month of treatment (T2). Samples were tested using sputum simultaneous amplification and testing for tuberculosis (SAT-TB), acid-fast staining (AFS), and MGIT™960 culture. With MGIT™960 culture serving as the gold standard, the diagnostic performance of SAT-TB and AFS was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and consistency using Kappa values and receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsAt T0, the sensitivity of sputum SAT-TB and AFS was 81.6% and 55.2%, respectively. At T1, SAT-TB demonstrated a sensitivity of 87.1% and specificity of 100.0% compared to 54.8% sensitivity and 98.9% specificity of AFS. Kappa values were 0.910 (SAT-TB) and 0.626 (AFS), with significant statistical differences (P < 0.05). Positive predictive values (PPV) were 100% and 94.4%, while negative predictive values (NPV) were 95.9% and 86.9% for SAT-TB and AFS, respectively. The area under the curve (AUC) values were 0.935 for SAT-TB and 0.769 for AFS, with a significant statistical difference (P = 0.0006). At T2, SAT-TB and AFS sensitivities declined to 25.0% and 16.7%, respectively, with respective specificities of 100.0% and 98.2%. The corresponding Kappa values were 0.376 and 0.212, with significant statistical differences (P < 0.05). PPVs were 100% and 50%, and NPVs were 92.6% and 91.7%. The AUC values dropped to 0.625 for SAT-TB and 0.574 for AFS, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.2305).ConclusionSputum SAT-TB demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity than AFS. At T1, it showed high consistency with MGIT™960 culture, suggesting that sputum SAT-TB is a promising tool for early therapeutic efficacy evaluation in MDR/RR-TB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11917-z.

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading infectious diseases causing death worldwide and poses a significant threat to public health. In 2023, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 400,000 new cases of multidrug-resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) were reported globally, only 44% of which were diagnosed and treated [1]. In China, the estimated number of MDR/RR-TB cases was approximately 29,000, accounting for 7.3% of the global burden [1]. The emergence of MDR/RR-TB has considerably complicated TB treatment [2]. With the advent of new anti-TB drugs—such as bedaquiline (BDQ), delamanid (DLM), and pretomanid (PMD)—and treatment regimens, significant progress has been made in addressing MDR/RR-TB [3–5]. These therapies can shorten treatment duration, improve cure rates, and reduce side effects. However, there remains a critical need for rapid, cost-effective, and reliable methods to monitor and evaluate therapeutic efficacy in drug-resistant TB.

According to the 2024 WHO diagnostic guidelines [6, 7], recommended diagnostic tools for drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) include Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, Xpert MTB/XDR, Truenat, and targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS), with MGIT™960 liquid culture system recognized as the diagnostic gold standard. Although these methods enable rapid diagnosis, they are infrequently used to monitor early treatment response [7–9]. For instance, Xpert MTB/RIF detects DNA, not viable bacteria, which can lead to false positives in treated patients and cannot distinguish between active and past infections. Moreover, its high cost limits it use for routine monitoring [10, 11]. While tNGS offers comprehensive drug resistance profiling, it requires advanced computational resources and remains cost-prohibitive for regular clinical use. It is primarily used for assessing drug sensitivity and not for clinical monitoring of therapeutic efficacy [12]. The 2024 WHO guidelines [6] reaffirm the MGIT™960 liquid culture system (BACTEC™ MGIT™960) as the gold standard for MDR/RR-TB diagnosis due to its high sensitivity and specificity. However, the long culture time hinders rapid diagnosis and timely treatment monitoring [13]. Therefore, identifying a rapid, accurate, and clinically suitable method for early therapeutic efficacy monitoring is essential for effective management of MDR/RR-TB.

In recent years, real-time fluorescence-based nucleic acid isothermal amplification technologies—specifically simultaneous amplification and testing for tuberculosis (SAT-TB)—have gained increasing attention. SAT-TB uses T7 RNA polymerase to amplify nucleic acids at a constant temperature (42 °C) and targets the 16 S rRNA of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) for rapid diagnosis. As 16 S rRNA is a key component of bacterial ribosomes, abundantly expressed in live bacteria and rapidly degraded in dead ones, SAT-TB can effectively distinguish between active infections and residual bacterial remnants, thereby significantly reducing false positives [14–17]. Additionally, SAT-TB provides test results within 2 h [14, 15]. The MGIT™960 system, which uses a modified Middlebrook 7H9 broth as the liquid culture medium and a ruthenium pentahydrate fluorescent oxygen sensor to automatically detect mycobacterial growth based on oxygen consumption, remains a cornerstone in MDR/RR-TB diagnostics. Despite its high sensitivity (70%–98%), the prolonged culture period (averaging 4 − 6 weeks) and reliance on phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) to confirm drug resistance limit its applicability for early treatment monitoring [18, 19]. Traditional sputum acid-fast staining (AFS) remains widely used screening method for TB in many regions. AFS exploits the mycolic acid-rich cell wall of mycobacteria and uses the Ziehl − Neelsen staining technique to distinguish acid-fast bacilli (red) against a blue background. Its main advantages include simplicity, low cost, and speed (results within 1–2 h), with specificity exceeding 95%. However, it has limitations such as low sensitivity (50%−70%), particularly in HIV-infected individuals or patients with scant or no sputum. It also cannot differentiate between live and dead bacteria or between MTB and non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) [20–22].

Given these technical characteristics, sputum SAT-TB presents unique diagnostic advantages. It enables rapid and highly specific detection of viable MTB during treatment, making it a potentially reliable method for evaluating therapeutic efficacy [23, 24]. However, clinical research on the application of sputum SAT-TB for monitoring early therapeutic response in MDR/RR-TB remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to explore the clinical utility of sputum SAT-TB in monitoring early therapeutic efficacy in patients with MDR/RR-TB, providing data to support its broader clinical application.

Methods

Patients

This study included 125 patients with MDR/RR-TB, selected from 1,700 individuals diagnosed with TB at Hangzhou Red Cross Hospital from January 2022 to October 2024. All included patients had positive sputum results confirmed by Xpert and MGIT™960 tests. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Detailed demographic and clinical data, including TB characteristics, prior anti-TB treatment history, and comorbidities, were collected from medical records (Fig. 1).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Willingness to undergo anti-TB treatment and follow-up, with signed informed consent; (2) rifampicin resistance confirmed by molecular or phenotypic drug susceptibility testing; and (3) positive sputum culture using the MGIT™960 system.

Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) Severe illness with a life expectancy of < 2 months, as judged by the attending physician; (2) known pregnancy or breastfeeding; (3) inability to participate in treatment or follow-up; (4) inability to take oral medications; (5) any safety concerns identified by the investigator; (6) known allergies or intolerance to anti-TB drugs that would prevent an effective regimen; and (7) history of epilepsy, severe depression, agitation, or other psychiatric disorders.

Sample collection and testing

Sputum samples were collected at three time points: baseline (T0), at the end of the 1st month of treatment (T1), and at the end of the 2nd month of treatment (T2). All samples were tested using the MGIT™960 culture system, sputum AFS, and sputum SAT-TB technique.

According to the WHO guidelines, the MGIT™960 culture system is considered the gold standard for diagnosing MDR/RR-TB. The BACTEC™ MGIT™960 automated mycobacterial culture system (Becton Dickinson, USA), automatically monitors oxygen consumption to detect mycobacterial growth. Sputum samples (1 − 5 mL) were collected and mixed with an equal volume of NALC − NaOH, vortexed for 30 s, and left at room temperature for 15 min. Phosphate-buffered saline was then added until the total volume reached approximately 45 mL, and the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 15 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (pH of ~ 7.0). The mixture was then centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 15 min, and after discarding the supernatant, the pellet was added to a MGIT™ culture tube containing 0.8 mL of oleic acid − albumin − dextrose − catalase supplement. The culture tube was sealed, shaken well, and placed into the designated slot of the MGIT™960 instrument for automated monitoring. Cultures were deemed positive if the culture system generated an alert within 4 − 6 weeks; results were reported as negative if no alert was generated after 42 days of continuous culture [25]. The sputum SAT-TB detection technology, developed by Shanghai Real Bio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., is particularly designed for the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB). A 1.5 mL sample was treated with NaLC-NaOH for liquefaction. After discarding the supernatant, TB diluent was added. A 50 µL test sample was processed using ultrasonic lysis for 15 min, and 2 µL of the processed material was added to a reaction plate containing 30 µL of amplification solution. The T7 RNA polymerase was used to amplify the 16 S rRNA of MTB (with a target sequence length of 170 bp). Hybridization was performed using a FAM-DABCYL probe (sequence: 5′-CCAGCCACGGGAUGCAUGCUGG-3′). Fluorescence signals were detected using a Roche Cobas z 480 PCR instrument. A cycle threshold (CT) of ≤ 40 was considered positive, while a CT of > 40 or no amplification curve was considered negative [16, 26].

Sputum AFS was performed using the Ziehl − Neelsen method. A sputum smear (10 mm × 20 mm) was prepared, heated thrice with a flame, stained with carbol fuchsin for approximately 15 min, decolorized with acid − alcohol for 1–2 min, and counterstained with methylene blue for 30–60 s. Acid-fast bacilli appeared red against a blue background. Results were graded as “+” to “++++” based on the number of acid-fast bacilli observed [27].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 30.0. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Diagnostic consistency was assessed using the Kappa value, and efficacy of each method in monitoring treatment was assessed via the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The MedCalc software was used for relevant calculations. The DeLong test was applied for comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 125 patients with MDR/RR-TB with positive sputum cultures confirmed by MGIT™960 system were included in the study. The mean age was 46.9 ± 18.2 years. The proportion of male patients was significantly higher than that of females, with most participants being middle-aged and young adults. A relatively high percentage of patients were smokers, whereas alcohol consumption and a history of dust exposure were less frequent. Most cases were newly diagnosed, and a high percentage exhibited pulmonary cavities on lung CT scans. MDR/RR-TB represented the predominant form of drug-resistant TB in this cohort. Among comorbid conditions, diabetes was relatively common, while other comorbidities occurred less frequently (Table 1).

Using MGIT™960 culture results as the gold standard, at baseline, the sensitivities of sputum SAT-TB and AFS were 81.6% and 55.2%, respectively. At T1, the sensitivities of sputum SAT-TB and AFS were 87.1% and 54.8%, respectively, with specificities of 100.0% and 98.9%, respectively. Discussion

MDR/RR-TB continues to pose a significant challenge to global TB prevention and control. A substantial number of patients with MDR/RR-TB remain undiagnosed and inadequately managed, fueling spread of the epidemic. Compared with drug-sensitive TB, MDR/RR-TB is considerably more difficult to treat. Although the introduction of new anti-TB drugs has significantly enhanced therapeutic outcomes, effective monitoring of treatment response remains challenging. Current technologies used to monitor MDR/RR-TB treatment have notable limitations. The MGIT™960 culture system, while recognized by the WHO as the gold standard due its high accuracy, has a prolonged detection cycle (4 − 6 weeks), making it unsuitable for real-time treatment monitoring. Rapid molecular assays such as Xpert offer speed but cannot distinguish between viable and nonviable bacteria, leading to false positives. Similarly, conventional AFS has low sensitivity and cannot indicate bacterial activity.

To address these limitations, it is essential to develop a rapid and reliable method for evaluating therapeutic efficacy in MDR/RR-TB. The SAT-TB assay offers several advantages, as it targets 16 S rRNA specific to viable bacteria, delivers results within 2 h, distinguishes live from dead bacteria, is relatively simple to perform, and is cost-effective. Regardless of these advantages, 16 S rRNA is unstable and can easily degrade owing to various factors during sample collection, storage, and transportation. To mitigate these degradation risks, we strictly adhered to standard operating procedures for sample collection, storage, and handling to minimize the risk of false negatives [28]. Previous studies by Cui et al. have shown that the consistency between SAT-TB results and MGIT™960 culture is 80.5%–95.6%, with sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of 45.18%−97.6%, 79.4%−100%, 93.0%−100%, and 58.91%−62.0%, respectively [16, 17, 23, 29]. SAT-TB also excludes NTM effectively, making a promising diagnostic and monitoring tool [16, 17]. Although SAT-TB testing has standardized operating procedures, unlike the fully automated detection mechanism of Xpert [30], it requires manual operations by laboratory personnel in processes such as sample liquefaction and nucleic acid extraction. Moreover, variability in sampling time points, differences in test kit batches, variations in how samples are handled by laboratory personnel, and the time interval from sampling to submission for testing may all lead to a lack of comparability in SAT-TB detection Ct values at different time points. Therefore, this study focuses on qualitative analysis of SAT-TB to provide a reference standard for clinical laboratory testing. In future research, we aim to unify the standards of the test kits, standardize the sample submission times, and standardize the laboratory operators to minimize these influencing factors. Only after achieving comparability of the Ct values will we conduct comparative analyses, which will serve as a supplementary explanation for future studies.

In our study, SAT-TB demonstrated a high consistency with MGIT™960 culture at T1 (Kappa = 0.910). However, at T2, this consistency declined (Kappa = 0.376), likely due to a significant reduction in bacterial burden following treatment, lowering the 16 S rRNA levels below the assay’s limit of detection (< 100 RNA copies/mL). This, in turn, resulted in fewer positive detections compared to sputum MGIT™960 culture. These findings are consistent with previous reports [23, 24]. For instance, Yan et al. [31] monitored 377 patients with drug-sensitive TB using sputum MGIT™960 culture as the gold standard and observed decreasing positivity rates for sputum SAT-TB at weeks 2 (58.1%), 8 (30.5%), and 20 (15.9%). Similarly, Lai et al. evaluated 62 patients with newly diagnosed smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis. They monitored the positivity rates using sputum SAT-TB and the Löwenstein − Jensen culture method and reported positivity rates for sputum SAT-TB of 58.1%, 41.9%, and 24.2% at weeks 2, 4, and 8, respectively, whereas those for the Löwenstein − Jensen culture method were 71.0%, 40.3%, and 21.0%, respectively. With the Löwenstein − Jensen culture method as the gold standard, the sensitivity and specificity of sputum SAT-TB at weeks 2, 4, and 8 were 81.8%, 100%; 100%, 97.3%; and 100%, 95.9%; respectively [24]. A comparison between the current study and that of Lai’s study revealed a consistent decline in the positivity rate of sputum SAT-TB at T2. The differences between the studies lie in the fact that Lai’s study focused on newly diagnosed smear-positive patients with rifampicin-sensitive TB, whereas our study was conducted on patients with drug-resistant TB. Therefore, the positivity rates for sputum SAT-TB and MGIT™960 culture at T2 were higher in our study than in Lai’s study. Lai’s study used the Löwenstein − Jensen culture method as the gold standard. Although a consistency test was not conducted, the results showed consistency in the negative conversion rate between the Löwenstein − Jensen culture method and sputum SAT-TB. In our study, using sputum MGIT™960 culture as the gold standard, the consistency test revealed a consistency between sputum SAT-TB and sputum MGIT™960 culture.

Furthermore, in our study, AFS showed lower positivity rates than sputum SAT-TB at T1 and T2, and was prone to false positives due to nonviable bacteria. Therefore, AFS is not recommended as an indicator for monitoring and evaluating treatment efficacy. Our ROC curve analysis confirmed sputum SAT-TB outperformed AFS at T1 (AUC = 0.935 vs. 0.769 P < 0.05), indicating that sputum SAT-TB had better monitoring performance than AFS at T1. However, at T2, neither method demonstrated adequate monitoring performance (AUC values of sputum SAT-TB and AFS were 0.625 and 0.574, respectively; P > 0.05), thereby suggesting diminished utility at later stages without supplemental testing.

Our study showed that compared with AFS, sputum SAT-TB had better dynamic monitoring capability in the early treatment of MDR/RR-TB, particularly at T1, SAT-TB could detect a significant decrease in MTB. Therefore, sputum SAT-TB appears particularly valuable in the early phase of treatment, offering real-time, viable-pathogen-specific monitoring that may better reflect microbiological response than conventional methods.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of patients with MDR/RR-TB was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability and statistical power of this study. Second, we excluded patients who were MGIT™960 negative but Xpert positive, potentially narrowing the scope of comparison between different testing platforms. Third, this study only focuses on the first 2 months of treatment without long-term follow-up; therefore, it cannot evaluate the effectiveness of SAT-TB for monitoring from mid to late stages. Future studies should include multicenter designs, larger sample sizes, and more diverse patient populations, with longitudinal monitoring throughout the treatment process and additional monitoring time points to fully determine the clinical utility of sputum SAT-TB as a tool for treatment monitoring. This study uses SAT-TB for qualitative analysis. Considering that quantitative research is currently limited by numerous uncontrollable factors, we have not yet conducted quantitative studies. In the future, we plan to use more scientific and precise methods to reduce interfering factors, and then consider incorporating the Ct values of SAT-TB into quantitative research as a further extension and supplement to this study.

Conclusion

Despite its limitations, our study highlights the clinical value of sputum SAT-TB in monitoring early therapeutic efficacy in MDR/RR-TB. At T1, sputum SAT-TB showed excellent agreement with MGIT™960 culture and demonstrated superior performance over AFS. It is thus recommended as a reliable bacteriological monitoring method during this period. However, due to reduced sensitivity and consistency with MGIT™960 culture at T2, sputum SAT-TB should not be solely relied upon for efficacy assessment at this later stage, and complementary tests are advised. Monitoring of sputum SAT-TB performance at even earlier time points, such as at the end of the 2nd week of MDR/RR-TB treatment, may offer additional clinical insights and warrants further investigation.
