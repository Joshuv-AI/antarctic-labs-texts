On the Hypomonotone Class of Variational Inequalities


Abstract
This paper studies the behavior of the extragradient algorithm ( Korpelevich, 1976 ) when applied to hypomonotone operators, a class of problems that extends beyond the classical monotone setting. To support the understanding of this variational inequality problem class, we focus on a subclass of hypomonotone linear operators, characterizing them based on their eigenvalues and providing concrete examples. While the extragradient method is widely recognized for its efficiency in solving variational inequalities involving monotone and Lipschitz continuous operators, we demonstrate that it does not guarantee convergence in the hypomonotone case. In particular, we construct a counterexample where the extragradient method diverges regardless of the step size. A numerical experiment is presented to support this result.

Introduction

We are interested in numerically solving the following
Variational Inequality

(
Stampacchia, 1964
;
Facchinei and Pang, 2003
, VI,)
problem. Given an
operator

F\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
, the goal is to:

\text{\emph{find }}\ {\bm{x}}^{\star}\qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad\langle{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}^{\star},{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{x}}^{\star})\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\,.

(VI)

Special instances of (
VI
) include standard minimization with
F\equiv\nabla f,f\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{R}}
, zero-sum min-max, and general sum multi-player games.
This problem has gained recent popularity in machine learning, due to several instances that cannot be modeled by minimization only, such as Generative Adversarial Networks
(
Goodfellow et al., 2014
)
, robust versions of classification
(
Szegedy et al., 2014
;
Madry et al., 2018
;
Mazuelas et al., 2020
;
Christiansen et al., 2020
, e.g.,)
, actor-critic methods
(
Konda and Tsitsiklis, 1999
)
, and multi-agent reinforcement learning
(
Bertsekas, 2021
;
Lowe et al., 2017
, e.g.,)
.

The extragradient algorithm, introduced by
Korpelevich (1976)
in 1976, is a foundational iterative method used to solve variational inequality problems involving monotone operators. A detailed description of the algorithm is provided in §
3
.
Unlike gradient descent, the latest output (last iterate) of the extragradient method converges when applied to monotone and Lipschitz continuous operators; definitions can be found in §
3
. Its widespread use stems from its simplicity and efficiency for this class of problems.

However, in real-world applications, operators may not always exhibit monotonicity or cocoercivity. Instead, they may exhibit hypomonotonicity, a weaker form of the monotonicity condition.
Hypomonotone operators enable the study of more general and complex scenarios, where finding solutions is more challenging but remains feasible for analysis.

Hypomonotonicity arises in a variety of contexts, including equilibrium problems, optimization with non-convex structures, and certain game-theoretic models. While monotone problems have been extensively studied, the class of hypomonotone problems remains relatively underexplored despite its practical significance; refer to §
2
for an overview. Understanding hypomonotone problems is crucial for assessing whether methods that guarantee convergence for monotone problems can solve more general problem classes.

Contributions.

Our contributions include:

•

We present two illustrative examples of problems that are hypomonotone but not monotone. Additionally, we characterize a simple class of problems exhibiting hypomonotonicity, based on their spectral properties. See §
4
for details.

•

We demonstrate that the seminal extragradient method fails to converge for general instances of hypomonotone
VI
s. Specifically, we construct a counterexample that is hypomonotone (but not monotone) and show that the extragradient method diverges for this instance regardless of the step size. Refer to §
5
.

We study the extragradient algorithm defined by the following update rule.

Extragradient
(
Korpelevich, 1976
)
.
At iteration

it applies the operator at an iterate

\begin{aligned} {\bm{y}}_{k+1}&={\bm{x}}_{k}-\gamma{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{x}}_{k})\\
{\bm{x}}_{k+1}&={\bm{x}}_{k}-\gamma{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{y}}_{k+1})\end{aligned}\,,

(EG)

Monotonicity is defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Monotonicity)
.

An operator
{\operatorname{F}}\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
is said to be
monotone
iff:

\langle{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle\geq 0\,,\qquad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{n}.

(Mnt)

The following definition introduces a relaxed form of monotonicity where an operator’s inner product with the difference of its arguments is bounded below by a negative value.

Definition 2

(Hypomonotonicity)
.

An operator
{\operatorname{F}}\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
is
hypomonotone
with modulus
\mu\geq 0
iff:

\langle{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{y}})\,,{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle\geq-\mu\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|^{2},\qquad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\,.

(HM)

The following property ensures bounded changes in
{\operatorname{F}}
with respect to changes in its input, which is essential for proving convergence results in iterative algorithms.

Definition 3

(Lipschitz operator)
.

An operator
{\operatorname{F}}\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
is said to be
L
-
Lipschitz continuous
with constant
L>0
if:

\left\|{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{y}})\right\|\leq L\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\right\|,\qquad\forall{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{n}\,.

(Lip)

Normal matrices play a crucial role in diagonalization and spectral analysis.

Definition 4

(Normal Matrix)
.

A matrix
\mathbf{A}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
is
normal
if it commutes with its conjugate transpose, that is,

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}\,.

For normal operators
{\operatorname{T}}
, the following spectral theorem guarantees a decomposition into orthogonal eigenvectors and eigenvalues, simplifying the analysis of
{\operatorname{T}}
.

Theorem 5

(Spectral Theorem)
.

Any normal matrix
\mathbf{A}
—as per Def.
4
—can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix
\mathbf{S}
, yielding:

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\,,

where
\mathbf{D}
is a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

{lemm}

[Normality of Matrix Representation]
Let
{\operatorname{T}}\colon{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}
be a normal operator on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Consider an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}=\{{\bm{e}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{e}}_{n}\}
of
{\mathcal{H}}
. Then the matrix representation of
{\operatorname{T}}
with respect to
\mathcal{B}
is a normal matrix, i.e., it satisfies

{\bm{M}}{\bm{M}}^{\ddagger}={\bm{M}}^{\ddagger}{\bm{M}}
, where
{\bm{M}}
is the matrix representation of
{\operatorname{T}}
with respect to
\mathcal{B}
and
{\bm{M}}^{\ddagger}
is its conjugate transpose.

Hypomonotone Operators: Examples & Subclass Characterization

In this section, we first give two simple examples of hypomonotone operators that are not monotone.
Following that, we characterize a parametric hypomonotone subclass that is not monotone.

Example 1: concave problem.

Consider the following operator
{\operatorname{T}}_{1}\colon{\mathcal{R}}^{2}\to{\mathcal{R}}^{2}
, defined as:

{\operatorname{T}}_{1}({\bm{x}})\triangleq-\mu{\bm{x}},\qquad\text{with}\quad\mu\geq 0\,.

To show non-monotonicity, consider the following two points

{\bm{x}}=[1,0]^{\intercal},{\bm{y}}=[0,1]^{\intercal}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{2}
.
We have,
\langle{\operatorname{T}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{T}}_{1}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle=-2\mu<0\,.

To show that
{\operatorname{T}}_{1}
is hypomonotone, replacing for the LHS of (
HM
) gives:

\langle-\mu{\bm{x}}+\mu{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle=-\mu\langle{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}},{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle\geq-\mu\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}\,.

Since
\mu>0
,
{\operatorname{T}}_{1}
is
\mu
-hypomonotone.

Also notice that,
\|{\operatorname{T}}_{1}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{T}}_{1}({\bm{y}})\|=|-\mu|\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|\leq\mu\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|,
hence
{\operatorname{T}}_{1}
is
\mu
-Lipschitz.

Example 2: affine problem.

Consider the operator

{\operatorname{T}}_{2}(\begin{bmatrix}x\\

-x-1.5y+1\end{bmatrix}\,.

To show that
{\operatorname{T}}_{2}
is not monotone,
consider the vectors

.
We have
\langle{\operatorname{T}}_{2}({\bm{z}}_{1})-{\operatorname{T}}_{2}({\bm{z}}_{2}),{\bm{z}}_{1}-{\bm{z}}_{2}\rangle=-\frac{3}{2}<0\,

4.1
Hypomonotone Subclass: Operators with Negative Eigenvalues

Theorem 6

(Normal Spectral Characterization Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space over
{\mathcal{R}}
and let
{\operatorname{T}}\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
be a
normal
linear operator with at least one eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}\in\text{Spec}({\operatorname{T}})
whose real part is negative
\mathfrak{R}(\lambda_{i})<0
. Then, the operator
{\operatorname{T}}
is hypomonotone and not monotone.

Proof.

of Theorem
6
: Normal Spectral Characterization Theorem.

We first show that
{\operatorname{T}}
is hypomonotone.

Hypomonotonicity of
{\operatorname{T}}
.

Let
n
be the dimension of
{\mathcal{H}}
and let
{\bm{e}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{e}}_{n}
be the standard basis of
{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
. Let
{\bm{A}}
be the matrix representation of
{\operatorname{T}}
with respect to the standard basis. By Lemma
3
Preliminaries
, it follows that
{\bm{A}}
is normal. By the
Theorem 5

(Spectral Theorem)
.
, there exists a unitary matrix
\mathbf{S}
and complex numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
such that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{\operatorname{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\,\mathbf{S}^{-1}
, that is
\mathbf{\operatorname{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\mathbf{S}^{-1}\,\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{S}
.

Consider a point
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, with respect to the new basis representation, write
{\bm{x}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}v_{k}
with
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
and
{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{n}
are eigenvectors that form the orthonormal basis. We show next that for every
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
the inequality (
HM
) is valid.

Since
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\in{\mathcal{R}}
, then

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle=\mathfrak{R}\Big(\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}}\rangle-\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}}\rangle-\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}\rangle+\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{y}}\rangle\Big)\,.

Let
{\bm{x}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}{\bm{v}}_{k}\,,
and

{\bm{y}}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}b_{l}{\bm{v}}_{l}
.
Since
\langle{\bm{v}}_{i},{\bm{v}}_{j}\rangle=0
for
i\neq j
it follows:

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}a_{k}\overline{b_{k}}\,,\qquad\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\overline{a_{k}}b_{k}\,,

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{x}}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}|a_{k}|^{2},\qquad\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{y}}\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}|b_{k}|^{2}.

Now it follows that

\mathfrak{R}\big(\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle\big)=\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(|a_{k}|^{2}+|b_{k}|^{2}-(a_{k}\overline{b_{k}}+\overline{a_{k}}b_{k}))\big)

=\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k}|a_{k}|^{2}+|b_{k}|^{2})\big)-\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2\lambda_{k}\operatorname{Re}(a_{k}b_{k}))\big)

\geq\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k}|a_{k}|^{2}+|b_{k}|^{2})\big)-\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(2\lambda_{k}|a_{k}b_{k}|)\big)

=\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\lambda_{k}(|a_{k}|^{2}+|b_{k}|^{2}-2|a_{k}b_{k}|))\big)

\geq\mathfrak{R}\big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}|a_{k}-b_{k}|^{2}\big)\geq\mathfrak{R}(\lambda_{min})\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\|^{2}.

By the assumption of the theorem, there exists an eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
with a negative real part, i.e.,
\mathfrak{R}(\lambda_{\min})<0
.
Let
\mu=-\mathfrak{R}(\lambda_{\min})
. This establishes the result in equation (
HM
).

Non-monotonicity.

It remains to show that
{\operatorname{T}}
is not monotone.
By assumption,
{\operatorname{T}}
has at least one eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
whose real part is negative, say

\lambda\;=\;a+i\,b\quad\text{with}\quad a=\mathfrak{R}(\lambda)<0.

Let
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be an eigenvector corresponding to
\lambda
. Write

{\bm{v}}\;=\;{\bm{p}}\;+\;i\,{\bm{q}},\quad\text{where }{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}\in{\mathcal{R}}^{n}.

Notice that the real span of
\{{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}\}\subset{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
is invariant under
{\operatorname{T}}
. Indeed, in the basis
\{{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}\}
of this 2D real subspace, the matrix representation of
{\operatorname{T}}
is

\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{bmatrix},

which acts on any
\,{\bm{x}}=x_{1}\,{\bm{p}}+x_{2}\,{\bm{q}}\in\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}\}\subset{\mathcal{R}}^{n}
as a rotation-and-scaling by
(a,b)
.

We now claim that there is a nonzero real vector
{\bm{x}}
for which it holds:

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),\,{\bm{x}}\rangle\;<\;0,

violating monotonicity.
Indeed, take
{\bm{x}}=x_{1}\,{\bm{p}}+x_{2}\,{\bm{q}}
. A straightforward calculation in this 2D subspace shows

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),\,{\bm{x}}\rangle\;=\;\big\langle\,\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix},\;\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix}\big\rangle_{\!{\mathcal{R}}^{2}}\;=\;a\,(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}).

Since
a<0
, we immediately get

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),\,{\bm{x}}\rangle\;<\;0\quad\text{for any nonzero }{\bm{x}}\in\mathrm{span}\{{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}\}.

Hence for
{\bm{y}}=\mathbf{0}
,

\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}})-{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{y}}),\,{\bm{x}}-{\bm{y}}\rangle\;=\;\langle{\operatorname{T}}({\bm{x}}),\,{\bm{x}}\rangle\;<\;0,

which contradicts the monotonicity definition. Therefore,
T
is
not
monotone.

∎

Divergence of Extragradient on Hypomonotone VIs

In this section, we show that the extragradient method does not converge for a general instance of the hypomonotone class of VI problems. In particular, we provide a simple counter-example.

Theorem 7

(
EG
divergence on
HM
.)
.

Let
\gamma>0
be the step size for the
EG
method.
Let
{\bm{x}}_{0}=[1,0]^{\intercal}
be the initial point for the
EG
method, and let
{\operatorname{F}}({\bm{x}})={\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}
to be an operator such that:

{\bm{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}-2&0\\
-1&-2\end{bmatrix}\,.

Then, the sequence
\|{{\bm{x}}_{n}}\|
generated by
EG
diverges as
n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

By the extragradient (
EG
) update rule, we have
{\bm{y}}_{k+1}={\bm{x}}_{k}-\gamma{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}_{k}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{x}}_{k}-\gamma{\bm{A}}{\bm{y}}_{k+1}
.
Hence,
{\bm{x}}_{k+1}={\bm{x}}_{k}(\mathbf{I}-\gamma{\bm{A}}+\gamma^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2})
.

Define
{\bm{M}}(\gamma)={\bm{I}}-\gamma{\bm{A}}+\gamma^{2}{\bm{A}}^{2}
.
By the definition of
{\bm{A}}
, we can see that:

{\bm{M}}(\gamma)=\begin{bmatrix}1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2}&0\\
\gamma+4\gamma^{2}&1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Now consider
{\bm{x}}_{0}
as defined above and
{\bm{x}}_{1}=\mathbf{M}(\gamma){\bm{x}}_{0}=(1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2},\gamma+4\gamma^{2})
.

Let
\|\cdot\|
to be the euclidean norm and for all
\gamma
such that
0<\gamma<1
:

\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|^{2}=(1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2})^{2}+(\gamma+4\gamma^{2})^{2}>1\,.

Calculating
{\bm{x}}_{2}
, we have:

{\bm{x}}_{2}=\mathbf{M}(\gamma){\bm{x}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2})^{2}\\
2(\gamma+4\gamma^{2})(1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2})\end{bmatrix},

and
\|{\bm{x}}_{2}\|>\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|
.
Furthermore iteratively

{\bm{x}}_{k}=\mathbf{M}(\gamma)^{k}{\bm{x}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}(1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2})^{k}\\
k(\gamma+4\gamma^{2})(1+2\gamma+4\gamma^{2})^{k-1}\end{bmatrix}

Thus,
\|{\bm{x}}_{k}\|>\|{\bm{x}}_{k-1}\|
; and hence
\|{\bm{x}}_{k}\|\rightarrow\infty.

∎

6
Discussion

In this paper, we focus on a specific subclass of hypomonotone problems by considering operators that are both normal and linear. For this restricted subclass, we provide a detailed characterization based on the eigenvalues of the associated operators.
We demonstrate the divergence of the extragradient method in the hypomonotone setting by constructing a counterexample. In this example, for a given initial point, the generated sequence diverges in norm regardless of the chosen step size. This highlights the pressing need for a deeper understanding of hypomonotone problems and the development of methods that can ensure convergence for problems that are hypomonotone but not monotone.
