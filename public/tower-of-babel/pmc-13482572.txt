Reference-guided texture transfer with deformable convolutions for indoor image dehazing
Authors: Esteban Reyes-Saldaña, Mariano Rivera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indoor image dehazing requires recovering clear scene content while preserving structural fidelity and avoiding artificial textures. We propose DTTN, a reference-guided indoor image dehazing framework that extends our previous texture-transfer model with deformable feature alignment. The method extracts multi-scale features from the hazy input and a clean reference image, retrieves transferable textures using Top-K patch matching, and integrates them through a multi-scale deformable feature integration module. A gradient density enhancement module is further introduced to reinforce edge and structural consistency. On the RESIDE-indoor benchmark, DTTN achieves the best SSIM (0.992) among the compared methods and competitive PSNR (36.59 dB), while maintaining a favorable quality–complexity trade-off. Additional internal analysis and ablation studies indicate that reference-guided transfer and deformable alignment improve structural fidelity and remain stable under imperfect reference pairing. These results show that reference-guided texture transfer with deformable alignment is an effective strategy for indoor image dehazing under synthetic benchmark conditions.

Introduction

Image restoration aims to recover visually and structurally faithful content from degraded observations. Among its subproblems, image dehazing focuses on removing the effects of haze, smoke, or suspended particles that reduce visibility, contrast, and color fidelity1. Traditional dehazing approaches are often based on physical priors and atmospheric scattering models2–4, whereas recent methods have achieved substantial improvements through deep learning, particularly with convolutional neural networks (CNNs)5–7.

Most prior dehazing research has focused on outdoor scenes, such as natural landscapes and urban environments, where the primary objective is often to improve visual quality and perceptual realism. In contrast, indoor haze presents a different restoration setting. Indoor degradation may arise, for example, from smoke in enclosed environments, where visibility can be severely compromised. In such scenarios, restoration should preserve the underlying scene as faithfully as possible, avoiding the introduction of artificial textures or structures. This makes indoor dehazing a particularly challenging problem, since the goal is not only to remove haze but also to maintain structural consistency and scene fidelity.

Recent deep learning methods have addressed image dehazing using increasingly powerful architectures. Multi-scale encoder–decoder models such as U-Net7 and its dehazing variants8 have shown strong performance by aggregating features across resolutions. More recently, transformer-based models9, including vision transformers (ViT)10, Uformer11, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf {U}}^2$$\end{document}-Former12, have demonstrated the ability to capture long-range dependencies and global image context. In parallel, recent dehazing research has explored complementary directions such as fuzzy prior modeling, physically guided normalization, and frequency-aware or wavelet-based representations to improve robustness under complex haze conditions13–15.

Despite these advances, most existing methods remain focused on single-image dehazing, where all restoration cues must be inferred from the degraded input alone. However, in many image restoration problems, external reference information can provide useful structural and textural guidance. This idea has been successfully explored in tasks such as super-resolution and exemplar-based restoration, where clean reference images can help recover details that are difficult to reconstruct directly from the degraded observation16–18. Motivated by this perspective, we study reference-guided indoor image dehazing, where an additional clean image is used to support the restoration process.

A key challenge in reference-guided restoration is that the reference image is typically not spatially aligned with the target input. Even when the reference contains useful textures or semantically related structures, its local geometry may differ significantly from that of the degraded scene. As a result, directly transferring reference features can lead to misaligned textures, structural inconsistencies, or visually implausible reconstructions. Existing reference-based restoration methods often assume relatively stable correspondences or rely on fixed local aggregation, which can be insufficient when haze degradation and geometric mismatch coexist.

To address this limitation, we propose DTTN (deformable texture transfer network), a reference-guided framework for indoor image dehazing that explicitly models spatial mismatch between the degraded input and the reference image. DTTN extends our previous texture transfer dehazing network (TTDN)19 by introducing deformable feature alignment, allowing transferred reference textures to adapt spatially before fusion. The model extracts multi-scale features from the hazy input and the reference image, retrieves transferable regions using a Top-K patch matching strategy, and integrates them through a multi-scale deformable feature integration (MSDI) module. To further preserve scene structure, we incorporate a gradient density enhancement (GDE) module. Unlike prior reference-based restoration approaches, DTTN is designed to remain effective even when the reference is only partially related to the target scene and does not require precise alignment.

Method

In this section, we introduce the proposed Deformable texture transfer network for dehazing (DTTN), a reference-guided architecture designed to improve texture recovery and structural consistency in hazy indoor images. DTTN extends our previous texture transfer dehazing network (TTDN)19 by incorporating deformable convolutions and a streamlined architecture designed to better model nonlocal correspondences and spatially adaptive texture transfer. Overview

Given a hazy input image and a clean reference image, DTTN first extracts multi-scale features from both inputs using a shared encoder. The search and transfer module then retrieves relevant textures from the reference based on feature similarity. These transferred textures are fused with haze features through deformable alignment across multiple scales, allowing spatially adaptive integration under local geometric mismatch. Finally, the gradient density enhancement module reinforces edge and structural consistency to produce the restored output.

Multi-scale deformable feature integration (MSDI) module

Inspired by recent texture and style transfer methods16,23,24, we adopt a multi-scale integration strategy to fuse transferred reference textures with haze-corrupted image features.

Given the retrieved Top-K texture regions and their associated similarity scores, the key challenge is to integrate this transferred information while accounting for geometric mismatch between the hazy input and the reference image. In particular, the local neighborhoods of the reference image should be spatially adaptive rather than fixed. Unlike the cross-scale feature integration strategy used in TTSR16, we adopt a simpler fusion design. Compared with recent matching-based methods17,22,25, the proposed formulation is more flexible under local variations in appearance, including contrast, brightness, and structural displacement. Following recent matching-based restoration work18, we therefore adopt deformable convolutions26.

Standard convolutions aggregate local context over a fixed regular sampling grid. Thus, for each location \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_0$$\end{document} in the output feature map y, the regular convolution can be defined by8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} y(p_0) = \sum _{p_j \in \mathcal {R}} w(p_j) \cdot x(p_0 + p_j); \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where w is a learnable kernel.

In contrast, deformable convolutions learn both the kernel weights w and the sampling displacements \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta P = \{ \Delta p_j: j \in \mathcal {J}\}$$\end{document}, and a weight mask \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta \mathcal {M} = \{ \Delta m_j: j \in \mathcal {J}\}$$\end{document} with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {J} = \{1, 2, \dots , N \}$$\end{document}. The learnable offset and mask are computed by9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta P= & Conv(x; w_p) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta \mathcal {M}= & Sigmoid(Conv(x; w_m)). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Conv is a conventional two-dimensional convolutional layer, Sigmoid is the activation function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(w_p, w_m)$$\end{document} are the learnable weights. Details of the implementation can be found in27.

Deformable feature fusion

Based on this formulation, we perform feature fusion using a mechanism inspired by deformable convolutions. In the following, we describe such a procedure.

Given the hazy feature representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^l$$\end{document} at level l, we fuse it with the transferred texture set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{T_n^l\}_{n=1}^{K}$$\end{document} and the corresponding score maps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S^l$$\end{document} via deformable convolutions:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} A^l (p) = \sum _{n = 1}^K \sum _{j \in \mathcal {J}} w^l(p_j) \, T_n^l (p + p_j + \Delta p^l_j) \, \Delta m^l_j \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Figure 2 depicts the fusion process.

At each level l, let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^l$$\end{document} denote the hazy feature tensor entering the deformable fusion stage. In particular, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^3 = F_0$$\end{document}, while the finer-resolution inputs are obtained recursively as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^2 = Up(F^3)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^1 = Up(F^2)$$\end{document}. Following the standard masked deformable convolution formulation26, the offset and modulation mask are estimated as12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{x}^l= & Cat( \left[ x^l, T_n^l\right] ), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta P^l= & Conv(\textbf{x}^l; w_p), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta \mathcal {M}^l= & Sigmoid (Conv(\textbf{x}^l; w_m)). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where Cat denotes concatenation. The offset and mask are obtained via separate convolutional layers; see Eq. (12). The goal is to model the feature-space mismatch between the transferred textures and the degraded input features. Unlike regular convolutions, a learnable offset can correct the position of textures so that the mask adaptively weights them before the final output. Unlike some prior matching-based implementations18, our deformable formulation naturally supports input and reference images with different spatial dimensions, avoiding the need for square padding. In our implementation, the offset is estimated from the transferred textures \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{T_n^l\}$$\end{document} and the hazy feature representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^l$$\end{document}, which share the same spatial resolution. This avoids directly using the full reference tensor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V^l$$\end{document} and the hard matching maps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H^l$$\end{document}, reducing memory overhead and avoiding unnecessary padding.

Hence, we fuse the hazy feature representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^l$$\end{document} from the input branch and the attended features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A^l$$\end{document} from the reference branch [computed with Eq. We further reinforce structural and edge information using gradient density cues.

At each level, the deformable fusion output is projected back to a 64-channel feature tensor, allowing a consistent coarse-to-fine propagation across scales. Thus, the fusion stream progressively propagates transferred information from coarse to fine spatial resolutions.

PixelShuffle reorganizes groups of channels into local spatial neighborhoods, doubling the spatial resolution while reducing the channel dimension accordingly. However, bicubic interpolation only doubles the spatial dimension by keeping the channel number fixed.

Gradient density enhancement (GDE) module

To better preserve structural information from the hazy input, prior work has explored gradient-based guidance29,30. We incorporate a gradient density enhancement (GDE) module to add structural and edge information to the partial output of MSDI(· ). First, we compute gradient density for each RGB channel by convolving the image with 3 × 3 Sobel kernels31 in the horizontal and vertical derivative directions, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_x$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_y$$\end{document}, respectively. We calculate the Gradient Density as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} GD(x_h) = \sqrt{(K_x * x_h)^2 + (K_y * x_h)^2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g = GD(x_h)$$\end{document} denote the gradient density map. Next, we pass the gradient density map g through a shallow feature extraction module \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SFE_g(\cdot )$$\end{document}, distinct from the one used in Eq. (7), and composed of several residual blocks:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} G = SFE_g(g). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Finally, the gradient-enhanced features are fused with the MSDI output to produce the final dehazing reconstruction:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} G^3&= RBs^3\big (Conv\big (Cat([G, F^3])\big )\big ), \\ G^2&= RBs^2\big (Conv\big (Cat([G^3 \uparrow _{2 \times }, F^2])\big )\big ), \\ G^1&= RBs^1\big (Conv\big (Cat([G^2 \uparrow _{2 \times }, F^1])\big )\big ), \\ x_d&= x_h + Conv\big (Cat([G^1, F_d])\big ). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Here RBs are residual blocks defined as in Eq. (15). The final residual addition allows the network to enhance the hazy input with transferred texture and structural refinements.

Loss function

The overall loss is21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{total} = (chw)^{-1}|| x - x_d ||_1 , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where c, h, and w denote the number of channels, height, and width of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_h$$\end{document}, respectively, and x denotes the corresponding haze-free target image.

We intentionally adopt a simple L1 loss to avoid introducing perceptual bias or hallucinated structures, which is critical in indoor dehazing where fidelity is preferred over perceptual enhancement.

Experiments and results

Metrics

Following recent work, we use two metrics to evaluate our results: Peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index (SSIM)32. The PSNR is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} PSNR(f, g) = 10 \log _{10} \left( \frac{255^2}{\textrm{MSE}(f, g)} \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{MSE}$$\end{document} denotes the Mean Squared Error. SSIM is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} SSIM(f, g) = l(f, g) c(f, g) s(f, g) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l(f, g) = (2 \mu _f \mu _g + k_1) / (\mu _f^2 + \mu _g^2 + k_1)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c(f, g) = (2 \sigma _f \sigma _g + k_2)/(\sigma _f^2 + \sigma _g^2 + k_2)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s(f, g) = (2 \sigma _{fg} + k_3)/(\sigma _f \sigma _g + k_3)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\,\{c_k\}_{k=1,2,3}\,\,$$\end{document} are parameters (constants) of the metrics, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\,\,\,\,\{\mu _z\}_{z=f, g}\,\,\,$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\sigma ^2_z\}_{z=f,g, fg}$$\end{document} are the mean values and (co)variances, respectively. The first term compares luminance, the second term compares the contrast, and the third term compares the structure.

PSNR and SSIM are the preferred metrics given the nature of the problem: our goal is to achieve a reconstruction that remains as close as possible to the ground truth. For this reason, we prefer metrics that evaluate pixel-wise fidelity between the restored and the true image: We do not want the method to introduce textures or elements that are not present in the original image. Perceptual metrics may favor generative methods that introduce elements absent from the actual scene. Nevertheless, for completeness and benchmark-level comparison, we also report the FID33 metric.

Datasets

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed reference-guided dehazing framework, we use the RESIDE-indoor dataset34, specifically the RESIDE-Standard benchmark available in35. The dataset contains 13,990 synthetic indoor hazy images with varying haze densities. Following common evaluation practice, we use the provided benchmark split and report results on 500 synthetic haze test images. All dehazing results are reported using PSNR and SSIM computed on the Y channel of the YCbCr color space.

Since RESIDE-Indoor does not provide paired or ranked reference images, we assign one image from the dataset as the reference image for each hazy input under a fixed random seed. This protocol allows us to evaluate the proposed reference-guided framework under imperfect but reproducible reference conditions, rather than under idealized retrieval assumptions. In this way, the experiments test whether the model can still extract useful transferable information when reference quality is not explicitly optimized.

This experimental design serves two purposes. First, it enables evaluation of the proposed reference-guided transfer framework even in the absence of explicit reference annotations. Second, it allows us to assess whether the model remains effective when the reference image is not perfectly matched to the target scene, which is relevant for practical reference-guided restoration settings where ideal references may not always be available.

Limitations

Because RESIDE-Indoor does not provide paired or similarity-ranked reference images, references are assigned randomly rather than selected according to semantic or struc tural similarity. As a result, the present experiments evaluate robustness under imperfect reference conditions rather than performance under an ideal reference selection strategy. Although the seed-based analysis reported later demonstrates that the model is stable with respect to random pairing, the present study does not fully disentangle the role of semantic or structural reference similarity. Therefore, the conclusions of this work should be interpreted as evidence that reference-guided transfer is effective even under imperfect synthetic indoor benchmark conditions, rather than as a complete characterization of optimal reference-guided dehazing. Conclusion and future work

In this work, we introduced DTTN, a reference-guided texture transfer framework for indoor image dehazing that combines deformable alignment with gradient-guided structural enhancement. The proposed design addresses two central challenges in reference-based restoration: spatial mismatch between transferred textures and degraded features, and the need to preserve structural fidelity under haze degradation.

Quantitative evaluation on the RESIDE-indoor benchmark shows that DTTN achieves the best SSIM among the compared methods and competitive PSNR, while maintaining a favorable balance between restoration quality and model complexity. Additional ablation and internal analysis further support the contribution of the proposed Top-K matching, deformable fusion, and GDE modules. Overall, the results indicate that reference-guided transfer with deformable alignment is an effective restoration strategy for synthetic indoor dehazing.

A limitation of this work is that all experiments are conducted on a synthetic indoor benchmark with imperfectly assigned references rather than curated semantic matches. Therefore, the conclusions should be interpreted as evidence of robustness under controlled benchmark conditions rather than as a complete characterization of real-world deployment scenarios.

Future work will focus on evaluating the method on real smoke-degraded images, improving reference selection strategies, and exploring multi-reference extensions for more robust reference-guided restoration.
