High Titers, Hidden Disease: Asymptomatic Neurosyphilis in Secondary Syphilis – A Case Series from Western Maharashtra
Authors: Viren A Rohra, Kiran J Dange, Shekhar Nana Pradhan, Vasudha A Belgaumkar, Neelam K Bhat, Suraj Wani, Kanchan B Rathore, Saanavi S Pradhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Syphilis can involve the central nervous system at any stage; however, routine cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination is rarely performed in neurologically asymptomatic patients with secondary syphilis. We describe a case series of eight male patients with secondary syphilis and high serum venereal disease research laboratory (VDRL) titers (≥1:16) evaluated at a tertiary teaching hospital in western Maharashtra, India (2024–2025). Five patients were human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-positive and on antiretroviral therapy. None had neurological symptoms or signs; however, all had CSF abnormalities, consistent with asymptomatic neurosyphilis, including reactive CSF–VDRL in all patients, with mild pleocytosis and/or elevated protein in some. All patients received intravenous aqueous crystalline penicillin G for 14 days, with a serological response observed in most follow-up cases. These findings suggest that high titers in secondary syphilis, particularly in the presence of HIV co-infection and behavioral risk factors, may warrant consideration of CSF evaluation even in the absence of neurological manifestations.

Introduction

Neurosyphilis can occur at any stage of syphilis, including secondary syphilis.[1] Asymptomatic neurosyphilis, defined by reactive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)–venereal disease research laboratory (VDRL) with or without pleocytosis (>5 cells/μL in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-negative; >10 cells/μL in HIV-positive patients on antiretroviral therapy) and elevated protein (>45 mg/dL), in the absence of neurological manifestations,[23] is particularly relevant in individuals with HIV co-infection and high non-treponemal titers.[4] Current guidelines recommend CSF examination only in symptomatic patients,[5] potentially leading to underdiagnosis of asymptomatic cases. We describe a case series of patients with secondary syphilis and high VDRL titers who demonstrated CSF evidence of asymptomatic neurosyphilis despite normal neurological examinations.

Case series

In this retrospective case series, eight male patients aged 25–48 years with serologically confirmed secondary syphilis and serum VDRL titers ≥1:16 were identified at a tertiary teaching hospital in western Maharashtra, India, during 2024–2025. All patients received intravenous aqueous crystalline penicillin G (18–24 million units/day) for 14 days,[5] followed by a single intramuscular dose of benzathine penicillin G for concomitant secondary syphilis because benzathine penicillin alone does not achieve therapeutic CSF concentrations.[6] None developed neurological symptoms during follow-up.

Discussion

Neurosyphilis is not exclusively a late-stage complication of syphilis because neuroinvasion occurs in up to 23% of secondary syphilis cases and may manifest as acute meningitis, cranial nerve palsies, ocular involvement, or remain entirely asymptomatic.[7] HIV-induced CD4+ T-cell depletion impairs clearance of Treponema pallidum from the CSF, accelerating neuroinvasion and increasing the risk of serological failure, thus necessitating a lower threshold for CSF evaluation in coinfected patients.[89] High VDRL titers, low CD4 counts, and high viral load are recognized risk factors for asymptomatic neurosyphilis in people living with HIV.[10] Substance use may contribute to sexually transmitted infection acquisition through behavioral disinhibition; thus, integrated interventions targeting sexually transmitted infection and substance use may be beneficial.[1112]

In our series, all patients had reactive CSF–VDRL results without neurological symptoms, indicating asymptomatic central nervous system (CNS) involvement. Notably, CSF abnormalities were present even in patients with CD4 counts >350 cells/μL. Inflammatory changes were mild, consistent with early neurosyphilis.[13] Asymptomatic neurosyphilis is also observed in the serofast state, in which the CNS may serve as an immune sanctuary.[14]

These findings have therapeutic implications, as neurosyphilis requires intravenous penicillin. Routine lumbar puncture in all patients with secondary syphilis is not feasible in resource-limited settings; however, targeted CSF analysis in high-risk groups may yield important diagnostic information. A threshold of ≥1:16 was used in our series, although ≥1:32 is more commonly cited.[4] A lower cutoff, in the presence of behavioral risk factors, may help identify additional at-risk cases in highly endemic areas. Our findings support a risk-stratified approach to lumbar puncture in secondary syphilis. Limitations include the small sample size, single-center retrospective design, and lack of CSF FTA–ABS and viral load data. Although CSF–VDRL is highly specific, its limited sensitivity (30%–70%) is acknowledged.[15] Larger studies are needed to define screening indications in India.

Conclusions

This case series demonstrates that patients with secondary syphilis who have high serum VDRL titers, particularly those with HIV co-infection and high-risk behavior, may harbor neurosyphilis despite a normal neurological examination. Targeted CSF evaluation in high-risk subgroups can facilitate timely neurosyphilis-directed therapy and prevent missed CNS involvement.
