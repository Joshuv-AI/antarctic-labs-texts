Effectiveness of scenario-based high-fidelity simulation training on management of medical emergencies in dental settings: a theory-based intervention with a long-term follow-up
Authors: Yasmine N. Alawaji, Nesrin A. Alharthy, Abdulrhman Alghamdi, Abdullah Alshibani, Meshary Binhotan, Hussam Alqahtani, Farraj Albalawi, Aram Alshehri, Ali Aboalela, Sahr H. Altuwaijri, Sara A. Kalagi, Abdulmohsen Alfadley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSkills pertaining to management of medical emergencies learned though basic life support courses were reported to decline within a few months. Despite the effectiveness of scenario-based simulation training in managing medical emergencies in dental settings, the long-term stability of the findings remains unclear. The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a theory- and scenario-based high-fidelity simulation training in managing common medical emergencies in dental settings.MethodsA single-day simulation training based on the social cognitive theory was conducted to train senior dental students at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences in the management of common medical emergencies in dental settings. A refresher review and demonstration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation were provided to enhance the students’ behavioral capability (Knowing what and how to perform a certain behavior). In addition, four known approaches for enhancing the self-efficacy (Perceived confidence) were incorporated in designing the medical emergency scenarios to allow students’ learning through: a self-modeling (Learning from own experience), a vicarious modeling (Learning from peers and instructors’ experience), debriefing and feedback, and provoking engagement through detailed and realistic scenarios utilizing high-fidelity simulation to mimic real-life experience. A 13-item self-efficacy and 19-item behavioral capability validated scales were measured at baseline, immediately post-training, and at long-term (9 to 11 months) follow-up; a paired t-test was used for mean comparisons. The effectiveness of training was evaluated by the students for its importance, usefulness, recommendation to others, and possibility of registering in a similar course.ResultsA total of 34 students participated in the training course and 20 of them participated in the long-term follow-up. Statistically significant mean (SD) increased behavioral capability by 4.7 (1.5) points and self-efficacy by 41.4 (8.3) were found immediately post-training. Negligible and non-statistically significant declines in behavioral capability and self-efficacy were found in 9 to 11 months post-training; indicating long-term stability. Students’ assessment of the training course regarding its importance, usefulness, and recommendation ranged from 97.0 to 99.0%.ConclusionsThe high-fidelity simulation training was effective in increasing the behavioral capability and self-efficacy of managing medical emergencies in dental settings and long-term stability was achieved. We recommend tailoring the training on managing medical emergencies to dental settings and utilizing scenarios that mimics real-life experiences to enhance the self-efficacy and behavioral capability in managing medical emergencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09182-y.

Introduction

Medical emergencies in the dental clinic may be experienced by dentists about once every two years [1]. Commonly reported medical emergencies occurring in dental clinics include vasovagal syncope, hypoglycemia, asthmatic attack, epileptic seizure, cardiac arrest, foreign body airway obstruction, hyperventilation, and anaphylactic shock [1–5]. Taking a thorough medical and dental history, enhancing knowledge, using posters that summarizes the emergency management steps, having medical emergency equipment in dental clinics, and receiving resuscitation training were among the suggested strategies for managing medical emergencies in dental clinics [4, 6, 7]. Previous recommendations for dental students include the need for a didactic medical emergency and basic life support (BLS) training within the dental school curriculums [8–10]. Despite internationally recognized recommendations, reported levels of knowledge and confidence in the ability to manage medical emergencies in dental clinics were generally low [3, 8–10]. In addition, skills acquired through BLS training by health care providers, including physicians, dentists, and dental students, declined in a few months following certification [11–14].

Due to the relatively infrequent incidence of medical emergencies in dental clinics, it was recommended to conduct simulation training to ensure patient safety [15]. In non-dental settings, simulation and practical training programs were found to be effective in enhancing behavioral capability and self-efficacy for managing different medical and emergency management skills [16–19]. The behavioral capability, a suggested concept by psychologist Albert Bandura in social cognitive theory, is the perceived ability to perform a behavior by acquiring necessary knowledge and skills and it is primarily developed through didactic training [20, 21]. Self-efficacy, another concept suggested by Bandura in social cognitive theory, is the perceived confidence to perform a certain behavior and can be developed through 4 main sources including a self-modeling, which is developed through an individual’s own experience; a vicarious modeling, which is developed through observing other people’s experiences; a verbal persuasion, provided through feedback, and a physiological feedback by provoking emotions that could increase the experience’s informative values [20, 22, 23]. After training for behavioral changes, individuals with higher self-efficacy often have consistent long-term results [17, 23, 24]. The long-term retention of self-efficacy and knowledge after simulation training has been reported for various medical skills and emergency management [16, 17, 19, 24].

Simulation training for management of medical emergencies for dental students was generally found to be effective [15, 25, 26]. Role-play on simulated scenarios was used as a mandatory annual training for Saudi dental students and found a dose-response increased knowledge per year regarding management of medical emergencies over a three-year period [25]. In another study on French dental students, practical skills were evaluated 3-months post-simulation training and found to be highly effective compared to a control group [26]. To the best of our knowledge, simulation training on management of medical emergency studies that focused on dental students were mostly limited to assessments of post-training knowledge and skills without evaluation of self-efficacy or long-term results [15, 25, 26]. However, enhancement of self-efficacy was suggested to be predictive of long-term stability of results, even without further training [16, 17, 19, 24]. In addition, behavioral capability was evaluated by the tutor either through use of a questionnaire or checklist for skill performance, and none of those studies reported using validated and reliable measurements of behavioral capability or self-efficacy [15, 25, 27]. Recently, new unidimensional scales measuring these two concepts were developed and validated for their construct validity and psychometric properties in our previous study [14].

This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of scenario-based high-fidelity simulation training on management of medical emergencies in dental clinics, via non-invasive procedures, within the scope of general dental practice. Specific objectives were to evaluate the effectiveness of simulation training in enhancing the behavioral capability and self-efficacy in managing medical emergencies using previously validated scales and to evaluate the long-term stability of outcomes. A secondary objective was to validate the recently developed behavioral capability and self-efficacy scales for their ability to demonstrate changes post-interventions.

Methods

Data collection

This study evaluated the immediate and long-term effectiveness of scenario-based simulation training course. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board at King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (Study approval no. NRC23R/244/03). Eligibility criteria: Senior dental students at the College of Dentistry at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS). The senior students were invited to participate in this optional training course through their university emails and baseline data was collected over a 3-months period from early November 2023 up to the end of January 2024. Due to limited availability within the students’ academic schedules, the training program was held during the inter-semester break. The students were divided into two groups, and the training was delivered over two days (February 21st and 22nd 2024). Immediate post-training responses were collected and once again in 9 to 11 months (Long-term follow-up was collected after graduation from the dental program by ≥ 6 months).

Data was collected digitally using a questionnaire that inquired about gender, grade point average (GPA), a 13-item self-efficacy scale, and a 19-item behavioral capability scale (Appendices A, B, and C); scoring methods for behavioral capability scale was outlined in Appendix C. The self-efficacy and behavioral capability scales were previously developed and validated for their construct validity and psychometric properties [14]. In addition, the simulation course was evaluated by students immediately post-training regarding its usefulness, importance, recommending the course to others, and the possibility of registering in a similar course in the future. Students also provided their written feedback regarding the course strengths, weaknesses, and suggestions for future improvements. Informed consents were obtained from all study participants prior to data collection.

Scenario-based simulation training study design

A single day high-fidelity simulation training on management of medical emergencies in dental settings (Dental clinic and waiting area), was conducted with a long-term (Up to 11 months) follow-up. The instructors directly involved in the training were four paramedics, one consultant emergency physician, and one consultant periodontist. The scenario-based simulation training was structured based on two concepts of social cognitive theory: behavioral capability and self-efficacy. For enhancing the behavioral capability, a review of the knowledge and skills required for BLS was conducted at the beginning of the course. Additional instructions and training were provided as needed until each student could conduct high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) for an adult victim.

The training course focused on enhancing self-efficacy through 4 sources, based on the social cognitive theory proposed by Bandura. First, the self-modeling source was used by allowing students to learn from their own experiences through participating in scenario-based simulations. Second, the vicarious modeling source was utilized by allowing students to observe, learn from their peers, and from stories shared by the instructors about managing similar real-life emergencies. Third, the verbal persuasion was utilized after debriefing following each scenario where students were given instructions, suggestions, and feedback. Lastly, the physiological feedback source was utilized by provoking excitement through an experience that mimicked real-life emergencies while maintaining a friendly, safe environment for learning, and avoiding provoking excessive anxiety.

The course instructors were involved in creating and approving a detailed script of 10 realistic emergency scenarios which were tailored for dental settings and covered the following conditions: unwitnessed cardiac arrest (Occurred in waiting area), witnessed cardiac arrest, asthmatic attack, anaphylactic shock, vasovagal syncope, hypoglycemia, cardiac emergency (Angina pectoris and myocardial infarction), stroke, excessive bleeding with systemic symptoms, and foreign body airway obstruction. The scenarios were created to mimic real-life conditions of patients who developed a medical emergency based on the previous experience of the emergency physician and paramedics. The intended learning outcomes for students by the end of the training course included: Learn how to identify a medical emergency and manage it via non-invasive procedures within the scope of general dental practice; master the skills of BLS for an adult victim; understand how to prevent and identify early signs and symptoms; understand the differential diagnoses of some medical conditions; develop closed loop communication; proper team dynamics; practice debriefing and communication with emergency medical services (EMS) team.

Each scenario was assigned to a group of students to act as if they were treating a patient in a dental clinic within the dental college clinics or a private practice. For most of the scenarios, one of the students played the role of dentist who was expected to be the primary respondent. Another student played the role of a dental assistant and was expected to be the secondary respondent, and two more students acted as paramedic personnel who arrived at the scene after activation of the EMS. The scenario roles were assigned to students on the day of the training course without prior notification. The scenario starts by giving them a few hints about the patient’s name and address where the dental clinic is located. The students were asked to act as if they were managing a new patient in their daily practice and were expected to ask about the chief complaint, history of chief complaints, medical history (Including prior hospitalization, allergies, and medications), and social history. The scenario progressively advances if students are unable or insufficiently able to manage the condition, ultimately leading to cardiac or respiratory arrest. Based on actual case encountered by paramedics, the anaphylactic shock scenario resulted in patient death due to inadequate management by the dental team. After each scenario, students were expected to debrief with faculty members, who provided detailed feedback and shared stories about real-life cases. Additionally, students were given the opportunity to ask questions and discuss what they had learned following each scenario.

Equipment

A high-fidelity SimMan™ 3G simulator (Laerdal Medical, Stavanger, Norway) was used which is an adult sized manikin that can speak through microphone and produce realistic sounds for normal and abnormal breathing, heart beats, moaning, crying and coughing. Pulse can be felt if present/absent. Chest movement, cyanosis of lips, and angioedema (Swelling of tongue) can be observed. Automated external defibrillator (AED) simulator can be attached to the manikin and automated feedback was provided for students on monitor screens regarding location, depth, and rate of chest compressions. The Laerdal simulation software was used to control the manikin based on the scenario. Low-fidelity manikins with lighter weight were also used to demonstrate the abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver) and unwitnessed cardiac arrest of a patient found unconscious in the waiting area. Dental examination kits, gloves (Latex and non-latex), latex-containing rubber dam, face masks, blood pressure cuff, AED (For training purpose only), and bag-valve-mask were provided. Medications that were made available included epinephrine injections (EpiPen), glucagon, and Salbutamol and steroid inhalers (To identify if the students knew which medication should be used during emergencies).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were done using STATA software version 17.0. (StataCorp LLC., TX, USA). Frequency distribution of background characteristics was described and compared between students who participated in the training, participated in the long-term follow-up and those who completed the baseline questionnaire but refrained from participation. The minimum sample size required was estimated as 15 participants using G*power [28]; based on alpha = 0.05, power = 0.9, mean (SD) score of pre-training self-efficacy = 5.6 (1.4), and post-training = 7.1 (1.8) [19]. After considering a reported retention rate of 85.0% [19], the minimum sample size was estimated as 18 participants. The internal consistency using Cronbach alpha was 0.88 for behavioral capability and 0.93 for self-efficacy scale. The internal structures were previously confirmed to be unidimensional scales using confirmatory factor item analyses. To evaluate the effectiveness of the program, the immediately post-intervention mean scores of behavioral capability and self-efficacy were compared with pre-training using paired t-test. The long-term effectiveness (9-11-months) was evaluated by comparing the long-term mean scores of self-efficacy and behavioral capability to immediately post-training scores.

Results

Out of 89 senior dental students, 70 students completed the questionnaire measuring the baseline data (78.7%), a total of 34 students participated in the intervention (38.2% of invited students) and 20 of them participated in the long-term follow-up (58.8% of participants in the training) as illustrated in the flowchart (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of students who accepted or declined participation in the training are depicted in Table 1. In comparison to non-participants, participants in the intervention had a higher proportion of females, slightly higher GPA, behavioral capability, and slightly lower self-efficacy. However, such differences were only trivial.

The mean (SD) course evaluation scores by students were generally high (S.Table 1, Appendix D) regarding the training course usefulness: 97.0 (2.0) %, importance: 98.9 (3.7) %, recommending the course to others: 99.0 (2.5) %, and possibility of registering in similar courses in the future: 97.6 (5.6) %. Students’ written feedback regarding the course strengths, weaknesses, and suggestions for course improvement were listed in S.Table 2, Appendix D. The students generally recommended making the course mandatory in dental program curriculums and dividing the training into several sessions. Another notable suggestion by many of the students was to provide a summary of the course content to be reviewed both before and after the end of the simulation training.

Discussion

The theory- and scenario-based simulation training on management of medical emergencies in dental settings was found to be effective in enhancing behavioral capability and self-efficacy, which were retained in the long term. The training course was found to be highly effective from the students’ perspective as well.

Our training course achieved long-term stability of outcomes despite being conducted on a single day. This finding aligns well with previous studies on enhancing self-efficacy for behavioral change that achieved long-term stability of self-efficacy without further training [17, 23, 24]. It is especially important to highlight this outcome since the inability to retain learned skills through BLS has long been a challenge. This study also described approaches to utilizing the four sources of self-efficacy (Self-modeling, vicarious modeling, verbal persuasion, and physiological feedback) based on the social cognitive theory, and we recommend focusing on these when designing future programs [20–22].

Compared to BLS courses provided by the American Heart Association (AHA) [29], our training course differed in terms of having detailed scenarios tailored to dental settings, emphasizing the early identification of signs and symptoms, and listing differential diagnoses, since dentists must be aware of relevant patient information, and medical history including allergies, medications, performed dental procedures, time since the patient collapsed, and possible provoking factors. Debriefing, team dynamics, and communication with EMS were also emphasized since a dentist will be required to hand over cases properly to an EMS responder with the aim of having improved lifesaving outcomes. Unlike the BLS courses by the AHA, our intervention was limited by not displaying educational videos, providing summary notes, and was restricted to adult victims. Thus, a combination of the two approaches would have been ideal.

In comparison to previous studies on simulation emergency training for dental students [15, 25, 26], this study covered higher number of emergency scenarios, and the scope of management of medical emergencies remained non-invasive and suitable to the general dental practitioners, who mainly practice in a private dental clinic that does not have direct access to hospital equipment, advanced emergency settings, and cannot get immediate assistance from in-house nurse or emergency teams. We avoided including any special equipment that is not typically part of the clinical setting of a general dental practice such as pulse oximeters, monitors, or intravenous medications [15, 25, 26]. Limiting the training to general dental practice was essential to make the learning outcomes realistic and can be applied immediately when needed.

The current study also focused on sources of self-efficacy and measured it at 3-time points (Pre-training, immediately post-training, and in 9–11 months post-training). Other studies did not report using validated measures and they limited their evaluations to the knowledge and skills measured immediately post-training [15, 25]. Only one study evaluated the skills in 3-months post-training [26]. However, based on previous studies that evaluated the long-term retention of BLS skills, they tend to decline in 3–6 months [11–13]. Thus, the findings of this study provided a unique insight regarding the long-term evaluation of outcomes which is lacking in the literature. In addition, this study confirmed the ability of the previously validated measures of behavioral capability and self-efficacy to demonstrate the changes in students’ scores over 3-time points [14].

This study was potentially limited by the absence of a control group since it was conducted in a single dental school with one classroom for each cohort, and therefore, spillover of contamination effects may occur i.e., students from the same classroom can potentially disseminate the knowledge and skills that they learn amongst each other [30, 31]. Consequently, the clustering of participants may have compromised the independence of observations. In addition, dividing the students into intervention and control groups may have ethical limitations for not providing a fair chance for students to benefit from the training. Therefore, dividing our study participants into an intervention and control group would not have been an achievable goal unless multiple centers were considered in a cluster-controlled trial [31]. Other studies considered having a cross-over design which could have been utilized [25, 27]. However, this may not prevent spillover effects where the experimental and control group could potentially disseminate their knowledge, skills, and experiences.

Strengths of the study include designing theory-based training with great attention to details regarding the medical scenarios which were written and reviewed by a panel of faculty members with relevant background expertise (Emergency physician, paramedics, and dentist). Real-life stories and tips were shared when providing the feedback, which may have provided a more realistic experience. In addition, we considered designing a realistic course that trains the students on managing common medical scenarios using non-invasive procedures which are within the scope of general dental practice and can be readily applied, when needed. We used validated scales to collect our data and considered the evaluation of self-efficacy alongside behavioral capability. To the best of our knowledge, this study provided the longest follow-up time compared to previous simulation training on medical emergencies in dental settings.

Among the potential limitations of our study design was giving a larger weight to self-efficacy compared to behavioral capability when designing the course and this could be improved by providing written notes and summary of steps to be reviewed before and after the training course. Another limitation was the non-response and loss to follow-up which could be partially explained by scheduling the course during inter-semester break, where many students may already have other plans during their time off school. In addition, the long-term follow-up responses were collected several months after students’ graduation which may explain the dropout rate of 41.2%. Alternatively, these shortcomings could have been avoided by planning those training sessions as part of the academic schedule.

Theoretical implications of this study for researchers include recommending the evaluation of self-efficacy as a measure of effectiveness of simulation training on medical emergencies targeting dental students. The study suggests a detailed approach for designing an intervention to enforce self-efficacy in managing medical emergencies in dental settings through the 4 known sources (Self-modeling, vicarious modeling, verbal persuasion, and physiological feedback). This study confirmed further validity of the newly developed scales measuring behavioral capability and self-efficacy regarding their sensitivity to change post-intervention. We also recommend for future research to evaluate the effectiveness of delivering the training course over several sessions and evaluating the long-term outcome.

Practical implications for tutors and educational institutes include recommending designing medical emergency sessions that are theory- and scenario-based, which are tailored to dental settings. We also recommend providing training content that is focused to non-invasive procedures which are permitted for general dental practice by federal or national laws. Otherwise, the take-home message out of the training may be neither realistic nor useful. Although many studies relied on providing a lecture as a refresher for students’ knowledge before starting the practical training, this seems to be inadequate based on the students’ feedback, and we recommend providing students with a written summary to review it before and after the session.

Conclusions

The theory- and scenario-based high-fidelity simulation training was effective in increasing behavioral capability and self-efficacy of managing medical emergencies in dental settings and long-term stability of findings was achieved. Our findings were mainly limited to delivering the course on a single day. Based on students’ feedback, delivering the training content over several sessions and allowing the students to review a summary of the content before and after the session may further improve our findings. We recommend for dental educators to tailor the training on management of medical emergencies to dental settings and utilize scenarios that mimic real-life experiences for enhancing the self-efficacy and behavioral capability in managing medical emergencies. We recommend for future researchers to utilize the validated scales for interventional studies since they demonstrated the ability to reflect the changes in behavioral capability and self-efficacy at three time points (Pre-training, immediately post-training, and long-term follow-up). We also propose replicating our study methods while considering a repeated delivery of the training content over extended periods.
