Aggregation-Confinement-Diffusion Evolutions with Saturation: Regularity and Long-Time Asymptotics
Yousef Alamri

Abstract
. We establish Hölder regularity for the weak solution to a degenerate diffusion equation in the presence of a local (drift) potential and nonlocal (interaction) term, posed in a bounded domain with no-flux boundary conditions. The degeneracy is due to saturation, i.e., it occurs when the solution reaches its maximal value. As a byproduct of the established regularity and the underlying dissipative structure of the evolution, we prove the uniform convergence of contractive solutions to a stationary state as t → ∞ t\to\infty .

1.
Introduction and main results

We study the regularity and long-time asymptotic of some density
\rho=\rho(x,t)
governed by the equation

(1.1)

\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\sigma(\rho)\nabla\dfrac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\rho}\right)\ \ \ \text{in}\ \Omega\times[0,\infty),

with a nonnegative initial datum
\rho(x,0)=\rho_{0}
bounded above by some maximal density
\rho_{\text{max}}
, and no-flux boundary conditions. The spatial domain
\Omega
is some open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geq 1
, with a piecewise smooth boundary, the
saturation

s\mapsto\sigma(s)
is a given continuous function vanishing at
\rho_{\text{max}}
, and
\delta\mathcal{F}/\delta\rho
is the variational derivative of the free energy functional

(1.2)

\mathcal{F}[\rho]=\int_{\Omega}U(\rho)+\rho V+\dfrac{1}{2}\rho(W*\rho)dx.

Here,
U:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the energy density that dictates the nature of the diffusion in the model (e.g., linear vs. nonlinear), while
V,W:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
are sufficiently smooth functions representing a confining potential and an interaction kernel, respectively. The convolution term
W*\rho
is understood in a bounded domain sense. See
Section

1.1
below for precise assumptions.

In the absence of saturation (i.e.,
\sigma(\rho)\equiv 1
), the pair (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) represents standard aggregation-diffusion models, with an additional external force/drift term due to the local potential
V
, that find applications in biology, physics, and population dynamics, see
[
4
,
1
]
for recent and comprehensive reviews of such models and their motivations. Without saturation, the evolution (
1.1
) defines a gradient flow of the functional (
1.2
) on the space of probability measures on
\Omega
with respect to the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein metric
[
27
]
. Particular instances of such models include the Fokker–Planck equation
[
25
,
8
]
and McKean–Vlasov equation
[
12
,
6
]
, and equations involving porous medium-type diffusion
[
11
,
7
]
.

Physically faithful models require the incorporation of nonlinearity in the quantity of interest; indeed, the inclusion of the saturation function, which renders the mobility term
\rho\sigma(\rho)
in (
1.1
) nonlinear as opposed to the case without saturation, is an example. It is called for to take into account the volume-filling effect in chemotaxis models
[
28
,
3
]
, prevention of overcrowding in population models
[
24
,
33
]
, or phase segregation in physics
[
22
]
, to name a few. While such nonlinearity is not manifested in the associated energy functional (
1.2
), it enters into its dissipation rate over time, thus playing a crucial role in the steady state profile of the solution. Furthermore, the saturation function imposes natural global bounds on the solution, determined by the bounds of the initial datum. While a generalization of the
L^{2}
-Wasserstein metric has been proposed to accommodate the nonlinear mobility in (
1.1
)
[
19
,
9
,
26
]
, the lack of geodesic convexity with respect to this proposed metric in the presence of nontrivial potential and interaction term limits its applicability.

A series of works has studied the well-posedness of the solution to the one-dimensional version of (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) via approximation by particle schemes. For instance, the nonlocal transport equation with saturation (
U\equiv V\equiv 0
) has been studied in
[
14
]
, the local-nonlocal transport equation with generic nonlinear mobility in
[
21
]
, and the aggregation-diffusion problem with one degeneracy and saturation in
[
20
]
. More recently, in higher dimensions, the existence of a
C_{0}
-semigroup of
L^{1}
-contractions of certain weak solutions constructed by approximation, well-posedness of the minimizers of (
1.2
), and long-time behaviors for solutions to (
1.1
) with a sufficiently smooth and convex
U
and no interaction term were established in
[
5
]
. See also the recent preprint
[
23
]
where an existence theory for the case
W\neq 0
was developed. Numerical methods and illustrations of transient and long-time profiles for this class of problems are presented in
[
2
,
10
,
5
]
.

The objective of this work is to establish the uniform continuity in space and time of weak solutions to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). As an application of such uniform regularity estimates, we demonstrate the uniform convergence of contractive weak solutions to a stationary state in the limit
t\to\infty
. Whereas our focus will be on diffusions of Boltzmann-type, that is,
U(\rho):=\rho(\log\rho-1)
, the techniques developed herein extend to other classes of diffusion (e.g., porous medium); see
Remark

1.2
below.

1.1.
Assumptions

Upon computing the variational derivative of (
1.2
), the problem of interest reads

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho-\nabla\cdot\big(\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho+\rho\sigma(\rho)\nabla(V+W*\rho)\big)=0&\text{in}\ \Omega_{T},\\
\big(\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho+\rho\sigma(\rho)\nabla(V+W*\rho)\big)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0&\text{on}\ S_{T},\\
\rho(x,0)=\rho_{0}(x)&\text{in}\ \ \Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T]
and
S_{T}=\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
, for some
0<T<+\infty
. Here
\bf{n}
denotes the outward unit normal on
\partial\Omega
.
We denote the parabolic boundary of
\Omega_{T}
by
\partial_{p}\Omega:=S_{T}\cup(\Omega\times\{0\})
.
The convolution term is understood in the bounded domain sense:
\nabla W*\rho(x)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla W(x-y)\rho(y)dy
. As stated earlier, we assume that the initial datum
\rho_{0}
is nonnegative and bounded above by some maximal positive density
\rho_{\text{max}}
. We further assume the following:

S1)

\sigma:[0,\rho_{\text{max}}]\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous function such that
\sigma(s)>0
when

s\in[0,\rho_{\text{max}})
and
\sigma(\rho_{\text{max}})=0
with the fixed finite quantity

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm{max}}:=\max_{s\in\left[0,\rho_{\text{max}}\right]}\sigma(s).

S2)
There exist constants
0<c_{0}\leq c_{1}
such that

c_{0}\Theta(\rho_{\text{max}}-s)\leq\sigma(s)\leq c_{1}\Theta(\rho_{\text{max}}-s),

where
\Theta(s)=s^{\beta}
for some constant
\beta>0
.

V)
The confinement potential
V\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

W)
The interaction kernel
W\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

The constant
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
depends on the choice of the saturation function. A typical choice for the saturation function is that of a monotone decreasing type, such as

(1.4)

\sigma(\rho)=(\rho_{\text{max}}-\rho)^{m}

for
m>0
; nonetheless, the range of the constants
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
in
\bf{S2}
allows for accommodating non-monotone saturations as long as they behave like a decreasing power near the saturation value,
\rho_{\text{max}}
. The prototypical example of the confining and interaction potentials is
V(x)=W(x)=|x|^{2}/2
. The well-posedness and regularity of solutions to (
1.3
) with local and nonlocal potentials belonging to a lower integrability class is an interesting problem and is left for future investigation.

1.2.
Statement of results

1.2.1.
Hölder Regularity

Our main result is the interior Hölder regularity for weak solutions to (
1.3
). The proof relies on the method of intrinsic scaling
[
17
,
31
]
. Such a technique has been used to establish Hölder regularity for the porous medium equation
[
15
]
, parabolic
p
-Laplace-type equations
[
17
,
18
]
, and doubly nonlinear parabolic equations
[
29
]
. We adapt the technique to accommodate a generic saturation function as well as local potential and nonlocal interaction terms.

For a compact subset
K\subset\Omega_{T}
, introduce the parabolic distance from
K
to
\partial_{p}\Omega
:

{\rm{dist}}(K;\partial_{p}\Omega)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t)\in K\\
(y,s)\in\partial_{p}\Omega\end{subarray}}\left\{|x-y|+|t-s|^{1/2}\right\}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\rho
be a bounded weak solution for (
1.3
) in
\Omega_{T}
, then
\rho
is locally Hölder continuous. More precisely, there exist constants
\Gamma>1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that for every compact set
K\subset\Omega_{T}
,

\left|\rho(x_{1},t_{1})-\rho(x_{2},t_{2})\right|\leq\Gamma\left(\dfrac{|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{1/2}}{\mathrm{dist}(K;\partial_{p}\Omega)}\right)^{\alpha}

for every pair
(x_{1},t_{1}),(x_{2},t_{2})\in K
. The constants
\alpha
and
\Gamma
are independent of
(x_{1},t_{1}),(x_{2},t_{2})
and can be determined a priori only in terms of the data of the problem.

The theorem is a consequence of the oscillation decay established in
Proposition

2.6
and a standard covering argument
[
31
, Theorem 4.10]
.

Remark 1.1
.

As (
1.3
) has no-flux boundary condition, if
\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{1,\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
, standard adaptations and localizations of the energy estimates developed in the present work near the boundary enable extending the regularity up to
\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T]
; see
[
17
, Sections 1-(iii) and 13 of Ch. III]
.

Remark 1.2
.

Hölder regularity of the solution still holds if the energy density
U(s)=s(\log s-1)
is replaced by a porous medium-type density
U(s)=s^{m}/(m-1)
for
m>1
as long as the saturation function is of a power type, i.e., (
1.4
). In the case of the latter, the diffusion coefficient is the continuous function
s\mapsto ms^{m-1}\sigma(s)

that is degenerate at two values:
s=0
and
s=\rho_{\mathrm{max}}
. Hölder continuity in such a regime without the confinement and interaction terms has been established in
[
30
]
with energy estimates that accommodate the two degeneracies. Nonetheless, the task of assimilating the potential and interaction terms can be performed as presented in this work.

1.2.2.
Convergence to steady-state

For evolutions with an energy-dissipative structure, the stabilization to equilibrum as
t\to\infty
is of interest. In the recent work
[
5
]
, provided
W=0
, the authors have demonstrated the global-in-time existence of a particular weak solution for the pair (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) that satisfies energy dissipation and
L^{1}
-contraction properties.
As an application of the demonstrated regularity herein, we prove the convergence of such solutions to a limit that satisfies the stationary problem in the uniform topology. In particular, it was shown in
[
5
, Section 2.4]
that if
V\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and
W=0
, then the solution converges in
L^{1}(\Omega)
to a unique limiting state as
t\to\infty
. Here, we obtain the following time-asymptotic result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\rho
be an
L^{1}
-contractive weak solution of (
1.3
) in the sense of
[
5
]
with
V\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
,
W=0
, and an initial datum satisfying
0\leq\rho_{0}(x)\leq\rho_{\mathrm{max}}
, a.e. in
\Omega
. Then,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\rho_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=0,

where
\rho_{\infty}
is a weak stationary solution of (
1.3
).

The proof of
Theorem

1.2
is given in
Section

3
.

2.
Hölder regularity

We prove interior Hölder regularity for the weak solution to
(
1.3
)
. The challenge in this setting is that the principal part of the equation vanishes when the density
\rho
reaches its maximal value
\rho_{\text{max}}
. For simplicity in this section, we will consider the normalized solution
\tilde{\rho}=\rho/\rho_{\text{max}}
of (
1.3
) corresponding to the normalized initial datum
\tilde{\rho}_{0}=\rho_{0}/\rho_{\text{max}}
such that, without loss of generality, we are restricted to the case
\rho_{\text{max}}=1
. We suppress the tilde throughout.

A key idea in the regularity proof is to work with an
N
-dimensional space-time cylinder vertexed at some
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Omega_{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
whose dimensions are dynamically rescaled to reflect the degeneracy in the equation. The remaining reasoning follows an idea originally due to DeGiorgi
[
13
]
in the context of degenerate elliptic equations and later generalized to deal with degenerate parabolic equations by DiBenedetto
[
16
]
, culminating in the so-called
intrinsic scaling
method
[
17
,
31
]
: if the set where the equation degenerates can be confined within a small portion of the parabolic cylinder (cf. (
2.16
)), then this set can be somehow controlled and the equation is thus essentially nondegenerate. On the other hand, if the set of degeneracy occupies a large portion of the parabolic cylinder (cf. (
2.17
)), then such a set can be made comparable to its radius.

2.1.
Notion of weak solution

We begin by defining the notion of a weak solution, which we shall consider for the regularity proof.

Definition 2.1
.

A weak solution to
(
1.3
)
is a measurable function
\rho=\rho(x,t)
such that

\displaystyle\rho\in C(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T}),

\displaystyle\Sigma(\rho):=\int^{\rho}_{0}\sigma(s)ds\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

and for any
\varphi=\varphi(x,t)\in H^{1}(\Omega_{T})
satisfying
\varphi(\cdot,T)=0
,

(2.1)

\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\varphi(x,0)dx+\iint_{\Omega_{T}}\rho\partial_{t}\varphi dxdt-\iint_{\Omega_{T}}\Big\{\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho+\rho\sigma(\rho)\nabla\left(V+W*\rho\right)\Big\}\cdot\nabla\varphi dxdt=0.

Energy estimates for the weak solutions are fundamental ingredients for the intrinsic scaling approach. To construct such estimates, test functions that depend on the solution itself are needed. A difficulty arises as weak solutions possess insufficient time regularity. This can be overcome by working with a time-averaged (alternatively, -mollified via convolution) version of the solution. Concretely, given a function
v\in L^{1}(\Omega_{T})
and
0<h<T
, we consider the Steklov average of
v

v_{h}(\cdot,t):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{h}\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+h}v(\cdot,s)ds,&0<t<T-h,\\
0,&t>T-h,\end{cases}

for
0<t<T
whose time derivative is

\partial_{t}v_{h}=\dfrac{v(x,t+h)-v(x,t)}{h}.

Consult
[
17
,
32
]
for standard properties of the Steklov average. Of special use herein, we will employ

[
32
, Lemma 2.1.2]
to show that
v_{h}\to v
as
h\to 0
in an appropriate topology. Putting this to use, we can introduce the following equivalent and more convenient definition of the weak solution given in
Definition

2.1
.

Definition 2.2
.

For every compact subset
\tilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
and
t\in(0,T-h]
, a weak solution of
(
1.3
)
is a function
\rho_{h}
satisfying,

(2.2)

\int_{\tilde{\Omega}\times\{t\}}\Big\{\partial_{t}\rho_{h}\phi+(\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho)_{h}\cdot\nabla\phi+(\rho\sigma(\rho))_{h}\nabla(V+W*\rho_{h})\cdot\nabla\phi\Big\}dx=0.

for any
\phi\in H^{1}_{0}(\tilde{\Omega})
.

2.2.
Intrinsic geometry

For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, define the
N
-dimensional ball centered at
x_{0}
with radius
r>0
as

(2.3)

B_{r}(x_{0}):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|x-x_{0}|<r\right\},

and the parabolic cylinder with a vertex at
(x_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
, radius
r
, and height

as

(2.4)

(x_{0},t_{0})+Q(t,r):=B_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-t,t_{0}).

with its Lebesgue measure

(2.5)

|(x_{0},t_{0})+Q(t,r)|=t|B_{r}(x_{0})|,

where
|B_{r}(x_{0})|=C(N)\cdot r^{N}
.
Up to a translation, we may assume that
(x_{0},t_{0})
coincides
with the origin and work with the notations
B_{r}
and

in place of (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), respectively.
For
0<R<1
and sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
such that

\mu_{+}:=\esssup_{Q(R^{2-\epsilon},R)}\rho,\ \ \ \mu_{-}:=\essinf_{Q(R^{2-\epsilon},R)}\rho,\ \ \ \omega:=\essosc_{Q(R^{2-\epsilon},R)}\rho=\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}.

We also define the
intrinsically scaled
parabolic cylinder

(2.6)

Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)\subset\Omega_{T}\ \ \ \ \text{with}\ \ \ \dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}}:=c_{0}\Theta\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right),

where
c_{0}
and
\Theta(\cdot)
are specified by assumption

. It is noteworthy to observe that the time length of the cylinder is scaled by the inverse of the lower bound on the saturation function; thus, the degeneracy is absorbed in the dimensions of the cylinder. Furthermore, the rescaled cylinder reduces to the standard parabolic cylinder associated with the heat equation if no degeneracy is present in the equation, i.e., if we fix
\beta=0
and
c_{0}=c_{1}=1
in assumption
\bf{S2}
, see
Figure

1
for a schematic.

Figure 1.
The standard parabolic cylinder

contained in the rescaled cylinder

.

As
0\leq\rho\leq 1
by assumption, we may fix
\mu^{-}:=0
and
\mu^{+}:=1
throughout for convenience. We enforce that

entailing

and consequently

(2.8)

Remark 2.1
.

If (
2.7
) does not hold, then it would hold that
\theta_{1}^{-1}<R^{\epsilon}
, which implies that the oscillation
\omega
tends to zero as the radius

does. The desired oscillation decay in such a situation is obvious.

Remark 2.2
.

We will frequently encounter quantities of the form
C_{i}R^{\hat{b}_{i}N\kappa}\omega^{-b_{i}}
for a fixed
\kappa>1
and some constants
C_{i},b_{j}>1
,
\hat{b}_{j}>0
that can be determined a priori from the data of the problem. We may enforce, without loss of generality, that

C_{i}R^{\hat{b}_{i}N\kappa}\omega^{-b_{i}}\leq 1.

If not, we have that

, where

and
\epsilon_{0}:=\min_{j}\hat{b}_{j}N\kappa/\max_{j}b_{j}

Remark 2.3
.

Future calculations reveal the explicit values of the constants in the previous remark:
\kappa=1+4/N
,
\min_{j}\hat{b}_{j}=2/(N+4)
,
\min_{j}b_{j}=\beta+1
,
\max_{j}b_{j}=2(\beta+1)
, and
\max_{i}C_{i}=\max\{c_{0}^{-2},1\}2^{4\beta+2s_{*}}

is some fixed finite number that depends on the data.

B_{r}(x_{0})\subset\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
k_{1}<k_{0}
be two real numbers. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(N)>0
such that

(k_{0}-k_{1})|v>k_{0}|\leq C(N)\dfrac{r^{N+1}}{|v<k_{1}|}\int_{[k_{1}<\rho<k_{0}]}|\nabla v|dx.

2.4.
Energy estimates

We shall consider two complementary cases. Given
\nu\in(0,1)
, to be determined depending only on the data, the weak solution
\rho
of (
1.3
) satisfies either

(2.16)

\left|\left\{(x,t)\in Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R):\rho(x,t)>1-\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right\}\right|\leq\nu|Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)|,

or

(2.17)

\left|\left\{(x,t)\in Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R):\rho(x,t)<\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right\}\right|<(1-\nu)|Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)|.

The aim is to show that in either case, decreasing the size of the parabolic cylinder of
Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)
is accompanied by a reduction in the oscillation of the solution. We stress that such a reduction is controlled by a factor that does
not
depend on the oscillation
\omega
nor the radius
R
of the parabolic cylinder.

As mentioned earlier, the intrinsic scaling approach relies on crafting particular energy estimates employed to reach desired conclusions when either (
2.16
) or (
2.17
) holds. In the following proposition, we construct a Caccioppoli-type estimate for the weak solution.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
0\leq\rho\leq 1
be a weak solution for (
1.3
) in
\Omega_{T}
. There exists constants
C(\cdot)>0
, depending only on the data, such that for every parabolic cylinder
Q(\tau,r)\subset\Omega_{T}
, every
k\in\mathbb{R}
, and every nonnegative, piecewise, smooth cutoff function
\zeta
in
Q(\tau,r)
, there holds

\displaystyle\esssup_{-\tau<t<0}\int_{B_{r}\times\{t\}}

\displaystyle(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}\zeta^{2}dx+\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{r}\times\{-\tau\}}(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}\zeta^{2}dx+C\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt

(2.18)

\displaystyle+C\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}\zeta\partial_{t}\zeta+C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)\left[\int_{-\tau}^{0}|A_{k,r}^{\pm}(t)|dt\right].

Proof.

In the weak formulation (
2.2
), we take the test function
\phi=\pm(\rho_{h}-k)_{\pm}\zeta^{2}
, and integrate in time over the range
(-\tau,t_{\circ})
for
t_{\circ}\in(-\tau,0)
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\partial_{t}\rho_{h}\left[\pm\left(\rho_{h}-k\right)_{\pm}\zeta^{2}\right]dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}+\underbrace{\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}(\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho)_{h}\cdot\nabla\left[\pm\left(\rho_{h}-k\right)_{\pm}\zeta^{2}\right]dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}(\rho\sigma(\rho))_{h}\nabla\big[V+W*\rho_{h}\big]\cdot\nabla\left[\pm\left(\rho_{h}-k\right)_{\pm}\zeta^{2}\right]dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{3}}=0.

We analyze each term separately. Integrating by parts and passing to the limit as
h\to 0
(see
[
32
, Lemma 2.1.2]
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\partial_{t}\left[(\rho_{h}-k)_{\pm}^{2}\right]\zeta^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\to\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}\times\{t_{\circ}\}}(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}\zeta^{2}dx-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{B_{r}\times\{-\tau\}}(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}\zeta^{2}dx

(2.19)

\displaystyle-\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}(\rho-k)_{\pm}^{2}\zeta\partial_{t}\zeta dxdt.

As to the second and third terms, we again pass to the limit
h\to 0
and estimate from below:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle\to\pm\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}\zeta^{2}dxdt\pm 2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho(\rho-k)_{\pm}\zeta\cdot\nabla\zeta dxdt

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon_{1})\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}|^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)(\rho-k)^{2}_{\pm}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt,

where we have applied Young’s inequality to the second term in
\mathcal{I}_{2}
:

\displaystyle\left|\pm 2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}(\rho-k)_{\pm}\zeta\cdot\nabla\zeta dxdt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{1}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}|^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)(\rho-k)^{2}_{\pm}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt.

Likewise, recalling the quantity
\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}
fixed in assumption
\bf{S1}
,
\Lambda
given in (
2.15
), and the assumption
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, we estimate

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\Lambda\sigma(\rho)|\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}|\zeta^{2}dxdt+2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\Lambda\sigma(\rho)(\rho-k)_{\pm}\zeta|\nabla\zeta|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{2\varepsilon_{2}}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}|^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{3}}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)(\rho-k)^{2}_{\pm}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_{2}}{2}+\varepsilon_{3}\right)\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\Lambda\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\chi_{\{\pm\rho>\pm k\}}dxdt.

Choosing
\varepsilon_{1}=1/4,\varepsilon_{2}=2
,
\varepsilon_{3}=1
and recalling the definition (
2.13
) yield

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}+\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla(\rho-k)_{\pm}|^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt-5\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)(\rho-k)^{2}_{+}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt

(2.20)

\displaystyle-2\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\cdot\Lambda\left[\int_{-\tau}^{0}|A_{k,r}^{\pm}(t)|dt\right].

Rearranging (
2.19
)-(
2.20
) and noting that
t_{\circ}\in(-\tau,0)
is arbitrary yield the desired estimate.
∎

The next proposition demonstrates a logarithmic energy estimate for the weak solution. Such an estimate will be employed to expand, in time, certain claims established in parabolic subcylinders to hold in the entirety of the containing cylinder. The logarithmic function
\psi^{\pm}
and its properties have been listed in
Section

2.3
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let

and

\displaystyle\esssup_{-\tau<t<0}

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}\times\{t\}}[\psi^{\pm}(\rho)]^{2}\zeta^{2}dx\leq\int_{B_{r}\times\{-\tau\}}[\psi^{\pm}(\rho)]^{2}\zeta^{2}dx

(2.21)

\displaystyle+C\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\psi^{\pm}(\rho)|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt+\dfrac{C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)}{c^{2}}\left(1+\log\dfrac{H^{\pm}_{\rho,k}}{c}\right)\left[\int_{-\tau}^{0}|A_{k,r}^{\pm}(t)|dt\right].

Proof.

Recall the logarithmic function (
2.9
) and set

\psi^{\pm}:=\psi^{\pm}_{\{H_{\rho,k}^{\pm},(\rho-k)_{\pm},c\}}(\rho)

for some
0<c<H_{\rho,k}^{\pm}
. In (
2.2
), take the test function

\phi=\partial_{\rho_{h}}[\psi^{\pm}(\rho_{h})]^{2}\zeta^{2}=2\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}\zeta^{2},

to obtain

\displaystyle\underbrace{\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\partial_{t}\rho_{h}\left[2\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}\zeta^{2}\right]dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}+\underbrace{\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}(\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho)_{h}\cdot\nabla\left[2\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}\zeta^{2}\right]dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}(\rho\sigma(\rho))_{h}\nabla\big[V+W*\rho_{h}\big]\cdot\nabla\left[2\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}\zeta^{2}\right]dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{3}}=0.

Sending

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}\to\int_{B_{r}\times\{t_{\circ}\}}[\psi^{\pm}(\rho)]^{2}\zeta^{2}dx-\int_{B_{r}\times\{-\tau\}}[\psi^{\pm}(\rho)]^{2}\zeta^{2}dx.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle\to 2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\rho(1+\psi^{\pm})[(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}]^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt+4\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\zeta\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}\zeta dxdt

\displaystyle\geq 2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}(1+\psi^{\pm})[(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}]^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle-2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\varepsilon_{1}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt-2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}}\sigma(\rho)|\nabla\rho|^{2}[\psi^{\pm}]^{2}[(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}]^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt.

The third term is estimated as

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\Lambda\sigma(\rho)|\nabla\rho|(1+\psi^{\pm})[(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}]^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt+4\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\Lambda\sigma(\rho)|\nabla\zeta|\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}\zeta dxdt

\displaystyle+2\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\left(\varepsilon_{3}|\nabla\zeta|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_{3}}\Lambda^{2}[\psi^{\pm}(\psi^{\pm})^{\prime}]^{2}\zeta^{2}\right)dxdt.

Choosing

,
\varepsilon_{2}=1

and noting

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}+\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle\geq-6\iint\limits_{Q(\tau,r)}\sigma(\rho)\psi^{\pm}(\rho)|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle-3\dfrac{\sigma_{\textnormal{max}}\cdot\Lambda^{2}}{c^{2}}\left(1+\log\dfrac{H^{\pm}_{\rho,k}}{c}\right)\left[\int_{-\tau}^{0}|A_{k,r}^{\pm}(t)|dt\right],

where we have used the estimates (
2.11

∎

2.5.
Behavior near the saturation value

We begin by assuming that (
2.16
) holds in the parabolic cylinder

. The aim of the following proposition is to show that the value of the solution
\rho
is below the saturation value in a smaller parabolic cylinder, along with determining

Proposition 2.3
.

There exists a number
\nu\in(0,1)
, depending only on the data, such that if (

) holds, then,

\rho(x,t)<1-\dfrac{\omega}{4}\ \ \ \text{ a.e. in }\ \ (x,t)\in Q\bigg(\theta_{1}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2},\frac{R}{2}\bigg).

\displaystyle=\dfrac{R}{2}+\dfrac{R}{2^{n+1}},

along with the corresponding cutoff functions

\begin{cases}\zeta_{n}\equiv 1\ \ \ \ \ \text{in}\ Q_{n+1}^{1},\\

2.2

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\esssup_{-\theta_{1}R_{n}^{2}<t<0}

\displaystyle\int_{B_{n}\times\{t\}}(\rho_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}^{2}\zeta_{n}^{2}dx+\dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}}\iint\limits_{Q_{n}^{1}}|\nabla(\rho_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}|^{2}\zeta_{n}^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1})\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}\bigg(\dfrac{2^{2(n+2)}}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\bigg)\iint\limits_{Q_{n}^{1}}\chi_{\{\rho_{\omega}>k_{n}\}}dxdt

(2.35)

\displaystyle+C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)\theta_{1}^{2}\left[\dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}}\int_{-\theta_{1}R^{2}_{n}}^{0}|A_{k_{n},R_{n}}^{+}(t)|dt\right].

Now, we wish to write the above estimate in terms of the norm (
2.14
). To this end, we perform a change of the time variable by introducing

(2.36)

\tilde{t}=\dfrac{t}{\theta_{1}},

such that we have

\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}(\cdot,\tilde{t}):=\rho_{\omega}(\cdot,t=\theta_{1}\tilde{t}),\ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \tilde{\zeta}_{n}(\cdot,\tilde{t}):=\zeta_{n}(\cdot,t=\theta_{1}\tilde{t}).

Furthermore, owing to the definition of
\theta_{1}
given in (
2.6
), we estimate

\theta_{1}^{2}=c_{0}^{-2}2^{4(\beta+1)}\omega^{-2\left(\beta+1\right)}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}\leq R^{-N\kappa}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2},

where the inequality is due to the assumption
c_{0}^{-2}2^{4(\beta+1)}\omega^{-2\left(\beta+1\right)}R^{N\kappa}\leq 1
(cf.
Remark

2.2
) for some
\kappa>1
to be fixed momentarily. Consequently, the estimate (
2.35
) reads

\displaystyle\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\tilde{\zeta_{n}}\|_{V^{2}(Q_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1})\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}\bigg(\dfrac{2^{2(n+2)}}{R^{2}}\bigg)|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|

(2.37)

\displaystyle+C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)R^{-N\kappa}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}\left[\int_{-R^{2}_{n}}^{0}|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}(\tilde{t})|d\tilde{t}\right],

with
Q_{n}:=Q(R^{2}_{n},R_{n})
and

\tilde{A}_{n}^{+}(\tilde{t}):=\{x\in B_{n}:\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}(x,\tilde{t})>k_{n}\},\ \ \ |\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|=\int_{-R^{2}_{n}}^{0}|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}(\tilde{t})|d\tilde{t}.

Observing that
R_{n+1}<R_{n}
, employing a Sobolev embedding theorem
[
17
, Corollary 3.1 of Ch. I]
, and using (
2.37
), we get the following inequalities

\displaystyle\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{n+1})}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\tilde{\zeta}_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C(N)\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\tilde{\zeta_{n}}\|^{2}_{V^{2}(Q_{n})}|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|^{\frac{2}{N+2}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda,N)\left[\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}\bigg(\dfrac{2^{2(n+2)}}{R^{2}}\bigg)|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|^{1+\frac{2}{N+2}}\right.

(2.38)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.+R^{-N\kappa}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|^{1+\frac{2}{N+2}}\right].

As
k_{n}<k_{n+1}
, the leftmost side of (
2.38
) is estimated below by

(2.39)

\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{n+1})}\geq|k_{n}-k_{n+1}|^{2}|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n+1}|\geq\dfrac{1}{2^{2(n+2)}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n+1}|.

Dividing (
2.38
)-(
2.39
) by
|Q_{n+1}|
and introducing the quantities

Y_{n}:=\dfrac{|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|}{|Q_{n}|},\ \ \ \ \ \ Z_{n}:=\dfrac{|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|^{\frac{1}{1+\kappa}}}{|B_{n}|},

we obtain

(2.40)

\displaystyle Y_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda,N)4^{2n}(Y_{n}^{1+\frac{2}{N+2}}+Y_{n}^{\frac{2}{N+2}}Z_{n}^{1+\kappa}),

where we have employed the following estimates

\displaystyle\dfrac{R_{n}}{R_{n+1}}\leq 2,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \dfrac{|Q(R^{2}_{n},R_{n})|^{1+\frac{2}{N+2}}}{|Q(R^{2}_{n+1},R_{n+1})|}\leq C(N)R^{2},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \dfrac{|Q(R^{2}_{n},R_{n})|^{\frac{2}{N+2}}}{|Q(R^{2}_{n+1},R_{n+1})|}\leq C(N)R^{-N}.

Likewise, noting that
|B_{n+1}|\geq C(N)2^{-N}R^{N}
, we estimate

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2^{2(n+2)}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}Z_{n+1}\leq|k_{n}-k_{n+1}|^{2}Z_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{|B_{n+1}|}\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\|_{L^{1+\kappa}(Q_{n+1})}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{|B_{n+1}|}\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\tilde{\zeta_{n}}\|_{L^{1+\kappa}(Q_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C(N)R^{-N}\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\tilde{\zeta_{n}}\|_{V^{2}(Q_{n})},

where the last inequality is due to an application of a Sobolev embedding (cf.
[
32
, Lemma 2.1.1]
) with
\kappa:=1+4/N
. Substituting the estimate (
2.37
), we derive

\displaystyle Z_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq C(N)2^{2n}R^{-N}\|(\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}-k_{n})_{+}\tilde{\zeta_{n}}\|_{V^{2}(Q_{n})}

(2.41)

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda,N)4^{2n}\left(Y_{n}+Z^{1+\kappa}_{n}\right).

By
Lemma

2.1
, if (
2.40
) and (
2.41
) hold, and if, in addition,

(2.42)

Y_{0}+Z_{0}^{1+\kappa}\leq(2C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda,N))^{-\tfrac{1+\kappa}{d}}4^{-\tfrac{2(1+\kappa)}{d^{2}}}=:\nu\in(0,1),

with
d:=\min\{\kappa,2/(N+2)\}=2/(N+2)
for any
N\geq 1
, then it follows that
Y_{n},Z_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
But (
2.42
) is precisely our initial assumption (
2.16
), provided we fix
\nu
as indicated in (
2.42
).
Hence, as
n\to\infty
,

R_{n}\searrow\dfrac{R}{2},\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ k_{n}\nearrow 1-\dfrac{\omega}{4},

and
|\tilde{A}^{+}_{n}|\to 0
. Finally, upon undoing the change of the time variable (
2.36
), we conclude

\displaystyle\left|\left\{(x,\tilde{t})\in Q\bigg(\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2},\frac{R}{2}\bigg):\tilde{\rho}_{\omega}(x,\tilde{t})\geq 1-\frac{\omega}{4}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left\{(x,t)\in Q\bigg(\theta_{1}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2},\frac{R}{2}\bigg):\rho(x,t)\geq 1-\frac{\omega}{4}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=0,

whence (
2.24
) follows.
∎

As a result of the foregoing analysis, whenever (
2.16
) holds, we deduce the following oscillation reduction.

Corollary 2.1
.

Assume (
2.7
) and (
2.16
) hold. Then, there exists a constant
\gamma_{1}\in(0,1)
, depending solely on the data, such that

\essosc_{Q\left(\theta_{1}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2},\frac{R}{2}\right)}\rho\leq\gamma_{1}\omega.

Proof.

Proposition

2.3
asserts that

\esssup_{Q\left(\theta_{1}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2},\frac{R}{2}\right)}\rho\leq 1-\dfrac{\omega}{4}.

It follows that

\essosc_{Q\left(\theta_{1}\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{2},\frac{R}{2}\right)}\rho\leq 1-\dfrac{\omega}{4}-\mu^{-}=\omega-\dfrac{\omega}{4}-0=\gamma_{1}\omega,

where

\gamma_{1}:=\dfrac{3}{4}.

∎

2.6.
Behavior away from the saturation value

If (
2.16
) does not hold in
Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)
, then (
2.17
) must hold with the exact
\nu
determined in (
2.42
). We begin the analysis of this case by demonstrating the following auxiliary lemma which states that, at some time level
\hat{t}
, the portion of the ball
B_{R}
in which
\rho(x)
is close to its infimum, that is 0, can be compared to the radius of the ball.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose (
2.17
) holds; then there exists a time level

(2.43)

\hat{t}\in\left[-\theta_{1}R^{2},-\dfrac{\nu}{2}\theta_{1}R^{2}\right],

such that

(2.44)

\left|\left\{x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,\hat{t}\,)<\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right\}\right|<\left(\dfrac{1-\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)|B_{R}|.

Proof.

Suppose that the conclusion does not hold, then we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\{x\in Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R):\rho(x,t)<\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{-\theta_{1}R^{2}}^{0}\left|\left\{x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,\hat{t}\,)<\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right\}\right|dt

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-\theta_{1}R^{2}}^{-\frac{\nu}{2}\theta_{1}R^{2}}\left|\left\{x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,\hat{t}\,)<\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right\}\right|dt

\displaystyle\geq\left(\dfrac{1-\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)|B_{R}|\left(\theta_{1}R^{2}-\dfrac{\nu}{2}\theta_{1}R^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\nu\right)|B_{R}|\theta_{1}R^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\nu\right)|Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)|

contradicting (
2.17
).
∎

Next, we expand the claim above so that it holds in the upper portion of the parabolic cylinder
Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)
, that is, up to
t=0
. We will make use of the logarithmic energy estimate obtained in
Proposition

2.2
.

Proposition 2.4
.

There exists
s_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending only on the data, such that for every
t\in(\hat{t},0)
we have

(2.45)

\left|\left\{x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,t)<\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s_{1}}}\right\}\right|\leq\Big(1-\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)^{2}\Big)|B_{R}|.

Proof.

Consider the logarithmic energy estimate (
2.21
) over the parabolic cylinder
Q(\hat{t},R)
with the choices

\hat{k}:=\dfrac{\omega}{2},\ \ \ \ c:=\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s_{0}+1}},

for
s_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
to be chosen. In the parabolic cylinder
Q(\hat{t},R)
, there holds

\hat{k}-\rho\leq H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}:=\esssup_{Q(\hat{t},R)}\ (\rho-\hat{k})_{-}\leq\dfrac{\omega}{2}.

If
H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}\leq\frac{\omega}{4}
, then the conclusion (
2.45
) follows trivially with the choice
s_{1}=2
. Hence, we hereafter assume
H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}>\frac{\omega}{4}
. Recall the logarithmic function (
2.9
):

\psi^{-}(\rho):=\begin{cases}\log\left(\dfrac{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}}{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}+\rho-\hat{k}+c}\right)&\text{if }\rho<\hat{k}-c,\\
0&\text{if }\rho\geq\hat{k}-c.\end{cases}

Observe also that

\dfrac{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}}{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}+\rho-\hat{k}+c}\leq\dfrac{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}}{c}\leq\dfrac{\tfrac{\omega}{2}}{c}=2^{s_{0}},

and by the estimates (
2.11
), we have

(2.46)

\psi^{-}(\rho)\leq\log\dfrac{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}}{c}=s_{0}\log 2.

Choose a piecewise, smooth cutoff function
x\mapsto\zeta(x)
satisfying

(2.47)

\begin{cases}0\leq\zeta(x)\leq 1&\text{in }B_{R},\\
\zeta\equiv 1&\text{in}\ B_{(1-\lambda)R},\ \ \ \lambda\in(0,1),\\
|\nabla\zeta|\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda R}.\end{cases}

Applying assumption
\bf{S2}
on the set where
\psi^{-}(\rho)
is nonzero, we have

(2.48)

\sigma(\rho)\leq c_{1}\Theta\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2}\right)\leq 2^{\beta}\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{0}}\dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}}.

In view of (
2.43
)-(
2.44
), (
2.46
)-(
2.48
),
the logarithmic estimate (
2.21
) reads

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\times\{t\}}[\psi^{-}(\rho)]^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{R}\times\{\hat{t}\}}[\psi^{-}(\rho)]^{2}dx+\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1})}{\theta_{1}}\cdot\iint\limits_{Q(\hat{t},R)}\psi^{-}(\rho)|\nabla\zeta|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle+\dfrac{C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)}{c^{2}}\left(1+\log\dfrac{H_{\rho,\hat{k}}^{-}}{c}\right)\left[\int_{\hat{t}}^{0}|A_{\hat{k},R}^{-}(t)|dt\right]

\displaystyle\leq s_{0}^{2}(\log 2)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)|B_{R}|+\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1})}{\lambda^{2}}(s_{0}\log 2)|B_{R}|

\displaystyle+C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)(s_{0}\log 2)c_{0}^{-1}2^{2(\beta+s_{0})}\omega^{-(\beta+2)}R^{\hat{b}N\kappa}|B_{R}|

(2.49)

\displaystyle\leq\left[s_{0}^{2}(\log 2)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)+C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)\dfrac{s_{0}\log 2}{\lambda^{2}}\right]|B_{R}|,

where
\kappa:=1+4/N
and
\hat{b}:=2/(N+4)
. Thanks to
Remark

2.2
, we have assumed without loss of generality that

c_{0}^{-1}2^{2(\beta+s_{0})}\omega^{-(\beta+2)}R^{\hat{b}N\kappa}\leq 1,

leading to the last inequality in (
2.49
). We now estimate the leftmost side of (
2.49
) below by integrating
[\psi^{-}(\rho)]^{2}
over a smaller set
S\subset B_{R}
defined as

S:=\left\{x\in B_{(1-\lambda)R}:\rho(x,t)<c\right\}.

To this end, observe that on the set
S
,
\zeta=1
and the map

H^{-}_{\rho,k}\mapsto\dfrac{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}}{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}+\rho-\hat{k}+c}

is decreasing since
\rho-\hat{k}+c<0
. It follows from the definition of the set
S
that

\dfrac{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}}{H^{-}_{\rho,\hat{k}}+\rho-\hat{k}+c}\geq\dfrac{\tfrac{\omega}{2}}{\tfrac{\omega}{2}+\rho-\hat{k}+c}>\dfrac{\tfrac{\omega}{2}}{\tfrac{\omega}{2^{s_{0}}}}=2^{s_{0}-1}.

We thus have the estimate

\psi^{-}(\rho)\geq(s_{0}-1)\log 2,

and consequently

(2.50)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}\times\{t\}}[\psi^{-}(\rho)]^{2}dx\geq\int_{B_{(1-\lambda)R}\times\{t\}}[\psi^{-}(\rho)]^{2}dx\geq(s_{0}-1)^{2}(\log 2)^{2}|S|.

Combining
(
2.49
)
with
(
2.50
)
and noting that
s_{0}(s_{0}-1)^{-2}\leq s_{0}^{-1}
, we deduce

|S|\leq\left[\left(\dfrac{s_{0}}{s_{0}-1}\right)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)+\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)}{\lambda^{2}s_{0}}\right]|B_{R}|,

and hence for all
t\in(\hat{t},0)
, we have

\displaystyle\left|x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,t)<\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s_{0}+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq|S|+|B_{R}\backslash B_{(1-\lambda)R}|

\displaystyle\leq|S|+|B_{R}|\cdot N\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\left[\left(\dfrac{s_{0}}{s_{0}-1}\right)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-\nu}{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}\right)+\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)}{\lambda^{2}s_{0}}+N\lambda\right]|B_{R}|.

The proof concludes by choosing
\lambda
sufficiently small such that

N\lambda\leq\dfrac{3}{8}\nu^{2}

and
s_{0}
sufficiently large such that

\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)}{\lambda^{2}s_{0}}\leq\dfrac{3}{8}\nu^{2}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \left(\dfrac{s_{0}}{s_{0}-1}\right)^{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\left(1+\nu\right).

With these choices, (
2.45
) follows upon taking
s_{1}:=s_{0}+1
.
∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition, we have the following estimate of the measure of
\rho
near 0.

Corollary 2.2
.

There exists a number
s_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending only on the data, such that for all
t\in\left(-\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}R^{2},0\right)
,

(2.51)

\left|x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,t)<\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s_{1}}}\right|<\Big(1-\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)^{2}\Big)|B_{R}|.

To simplify the notation in the sequel, we set

(2.52)

\theta_{*}:=\dfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}.

We use Corollary
2.2
and the basic energy estimate constructed in
Proposition

2.1
to deduce that, within a parabolic subcylinder
Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)\subset Q(\theta_{1}R^{2},R)
and for a given
\nu_{*}\in(0,1)
, the measure of the set where
\rho
is near its infimum is further smaller. This reduction is achieved by determining a smaller level (prescribed by a number
s_{*}>s_{1}
) below which such a measure can be made arbitrarily small.

Proposition 2.5
.

For every
\nu_{*}\in(0,1)
, there exists
s_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
,
s_{*}>s_{1}
, depending only on the data, such that

(2.53)

\left|\left\{(x,t)\in Q\left(\theta_{*}R^{2},R\right):\rho(x,t)<\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s_{*}}}\right\}\right|\leq\nu_{*}\left|Q\left(\theta_{*}R^{2},R\right)\right|.

Proof.

Consider the energy estimate (
2.18
) over the parabolic cylinder
Q(2\theta_{*}R^{2},2R)
for the quantity
(\rho-k_{0})_{-}
, where

k_{0}:=\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}},

for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
s\geq s_{1}>1
and
s_{*}\geq s
to be chosen. Let
0\leq\zeta(x,t)\leq 1
smooth piecewise cutoff function in
Q(2\theta_{*}R^{2},2R)
satisfying

(2.54)

\begin{cases}\zeta(x,t)\equiv 1,&\text{in}\ \ Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R),\\
\zeta(x,t)\equiv 0,&\text{on}\ \ \partial_{p}Q(2\theta_{*}R^{2},2R),\\
0\leq\partial_{t}\zeta\leq\dfrac{2}{\theta_{1}R^{2}},&|\nabla\zeta|\leq\dfrac{1}{R}.\end{cases}

To begin with, note that the first term on the left-hand side of (
2.18
) is nonnegative; thus, it may be disregarded. As to the second term therein, we apply assumption
\bf{S2}
where
\nabla(\rho-k_{0})_{-}
is nonzero to obtain

\displaystyle\sigma(\rho)

\displaystyle\geq c_{0}\Theta\left(1-k_{0}\right)\geq c_{0}\Theta\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)=\dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}},

\displaystyle\sigma(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\Theta\left(\omega\right)=2^{2\beta}c_{1}\Theta\left(\dfrac{\omega}{4}\right)\leq 2^{2\beta}\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{0}}\dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}}.

With these bounds and the choice of the cutoff function (
2.54
), estimate (
2.18
) reads

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\theta_{1}}

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)}|\nabla(\rho-k_{0})_{-}|^{2}\zeta^{2}dxdt\leq\underbrace{\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1})}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\iint\limits_{Q(2\theta_{*}R^{2},2R)}(\rho-k_{0})_{-}^{2}dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}

(2.55)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\dfrac{2}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\iint\limits_{Q(2\theta_{*}R^{2},2R)}(\rho-k_{0})_{-}^{2}dxdt}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}+\underbrace{C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda)\left[\int_{-2\theta_{*}R^{2}}^{0}|A^{-}_{k_{0},2R}(t)|dt\right]}_{\mathcal{I}_{3}}.

Observing that
(\rho-k_{0})_{-}\leq\frac{\omega}{2^{s}}
, we estimate each term

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1})}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}}\right)^{2}2^{N+1}\left|Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},N)}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}}\right)^{2}\left|Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)\right|,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C(N)}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}}\right)^{2}\left|Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)\right|,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C(\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda,N)}{\theta_{1}R^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}}\right)^{2}\left|Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)\right|c_{0}^{-1}2^{2(\beta+s_{*})}\omega^{-(\beta+2)}R^{\hat{b}N\kappa},

where
\kappa:=1+4/N
and
\hat{b}:=2/(N+4)
. By
Remark

2.2
, we may estimate

c_{0}^{-1}2^{2(\beta+s_{*})}\omega^{-(\beta+2)}R^{\hat{b}N\kappa}\leq 1.

Consequently, (
2.55
) reads

(2.56)

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)}|\nabla(\rho-k_{0})_{-}|^{2}dxdt\leq\dfrac{C(\beta,c_{0},c_{1},\sigma_{\textnormal{max}},\Lambda,N)}{R^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}}\right)^{2}\left|Q(\theta_{*}R^{2},R)\right|.

Now, consider another level

k_{1}:=\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s+1}}<k_{0},

and define, for
t\in(-\theta_{*}R^{2},0)
, the set

A_{s}(t):=\left\{x\in B_{R}:\rho(x,t)<k_{0}\right\},

along with its measure

|A_{s}|=\int_{-\theta_{*}R^{2}}^{0}|A_{s}(t)|dt.

Applying
Lemma

2.2
to
\rho(\cdot,t)
with the preceding choices for
k_{0}
and
k_{1}
, we obtain

(2.57)

\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s+1}}\right)|A_{s+1}(t)|\leq C(N)\dfrac{R^{N+1}}{|B_{R}\backslash A_{s}(t)|}\int_{A_{s}(t)\backslash A_{s+1}(t)}|\nabla\rho|dx.

Owing to the observation

(2.58)

\frac{\omega}{2^{s}}\leq\frac{\omega}{2^{s_{1}}}<\frac{\omega}{2},

and
Corollary

2.2
we have that, for
t\in(-\theta_{*}R^{2},0)
,

|A_{s}(t)|\leq|A_{s_{{}_{1}}}(t)|<\Big(1-\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)^{2}\Big)|B_{R}|,

which implies

|B_{R}\backslash A_{s}(t)|\geq\left(\dfrac{\nu}{2}\right)^{2},

and thus (
2.57
) becomes

(2.59)

\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s+1}}\right)|A_{s+1}(t)|\leq\dfrac{C(N)}{\nu^{2}}R\int_{A_{s}(t)\backslash A_{s+1}(t)}|\nabla\rho|dx.

Integrating (
2.59
) in time over the interval
(-\theta_{*}R^{2},0)
and applying Hölder’s inequality yield

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\omega}{2^{s}}\right)|A_{s+1}|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{C(N)}{\nu^{2}}R\int_{-\theta_{*}R^{2}}^{0}\int_{A_{s}\backslash A_{s+1}}|\nabla\rho|dx

3.
Energy dissipation and asymptotics

Problem (
1.3
) can be viewed as the time evolution of the energy functional

(3.1)

\mathcal{F}[\rho]=\int_{\Omega}\rho(\log\rho-1)+\rho V+\dfrac{1}{2}\rho(W*\rho)dx,

on the admissible set
\{\rho\in L^{1}(\Omega):0\leq\rho\leq\rho_{\text{max}}\}
. The minimization problem for (
3.1
) and long-time behaviors of solutions to (
1.3
) have recently been studied in
[
5
]
under the assumptions that
V\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
and
W=0
.
Here, we take advantage of the established regularity in the previous section to demonstrate the uniform convergence of such a solution to a stationary limit.

Upon formally differentiating the free energy (
3.1
), with respect to time, along smooth solutions of (
1.3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\log\rho+V+W*\rho\right)\partial_{t}\rho dx

(3.2)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\rho\sigma(\rho)\left|\nabla\left(\log\rho+V+W*\rho\right)\right|^{2}dx\leq 0,

where we have integrated by parts and applied the no-flux boundary conditions in (
1.3
). It follows that the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}[\rho(\cdot,t)]
is monotone non-increasing. Integrating (
3.2
) in time over the interval
0<t_{0}<t_{1}
allows us to define the
dissipation
functional:

(3.3)

\mathcal{D}[\rho]:=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\int_{\Omega}\rho\sigma(\rho)\left|\nabla\left(\log\rho+V+W*\rho\right)\right|^{2}dxdt.

Evidently, the functional (
3.3
) is nonnegative. It is also lower semicontinuous with respect to
L^{1}
convergence (
[
5
, Lemma 3.9]
). Moreover, weak solutions to (
1.3
) constructed in
[
5
]
satisfies the following dissipation inequality (
[
5
, Sec. 3]
)

\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\|\partial_{t}\rho\|^{2}_{W^{-1,1}(\Omega)}\leq C\Big(\mathcal{F}[\rho(\cdot,t_{0})]-\mathcal{F}[\rho(\cdot,t_{1})]\Big),

for some constant
C>0
that depends on the data of the problem and an
L^{1}
-contraction principle (
[
5
, Sec. 2.1]
), i.e., for two admissible initial data
\rho_{0}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{0}
and corresponding weak solutions
\rho
and
\tilde{\rho}
, it holds that

\|\rho(\cdot,t)-\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\rho_{0}-\tilde{\rho}_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\ \ \ \text{for}\ \ \ t>0.

With these preliminaries, we proceed to show the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
\rho
is a weak solution to (
1.3
) in the sense of
[
5
]
and the potentials
V,W\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
. Then, for any diverging sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a subsequence
\{t_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and a limit
\rho_{*}
such that
\rho(\cdot,t_{j})\to\rho_{*}
uniformly in
\Omega
as
j\to\infty
. Furthermore,
\rho_{*}
is a weak stationary solution.

Proof.

By
Remark

1.1
, we may work in
\Omega
. Define
\rho_{n}(x,t):=\rho(x,t_{n}+t)
in
\Omega\times[0,1]
. By
Theorem

1.1
, for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Omega
and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle|\rho_{n}(x_{1},\tau_{1})-\rho_{n}(x_{2},\tau_{2})|

\displaystyle=|\rho(x_{1},t_{n}+\tau_{1})-\rho(x_{2},t_{n}+\tau_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}},

for some
C>0
independent of
n
and the choices of
(x_{1},\tau_{1})
and
(x_{2},\tau_{2})
, showing that the sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly equicontinuous with respect to
n
. Applying Arzelà-Ascoli Theorem, we find a subsequence, denoted by
\{\rho_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, and a continuous function
\rho_{*}(x,t)
such that as
j\to\infty
,

(3.4)

\rho_{j}\to\rho_{*}\ \ \ \text{uniformly in }\Omega\times[0,1].

Since

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\|\partial_{t}\rho_{j}(x,t)\|^{2}_{W^{-1,1}(\Omega)}=0,

we may conclude that
\rho_{*}
is stationary, i.e.,
\rho_{*}(x,t)=\rho_{*}(x)
. Finally, using the nonnegativity and lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{D}[\cdot]
, as well as the monotonicity and decay of
\mathcal{F}[\cdot]
in time to a stationary value, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{D}[\rho_{*}(x)]
