Predictors of anxiety and depression in nurses: The role of social capital
Authors: Fatemeh J. Khosrooabadi, Gholamreza Kheirabadi, Zahra Heidari, Mohammad R. Maracy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Nursing is recognized as a highly demanding profession, often leading to psychological issues such as stress, anxiety, hopelessness, and depression. Social capital, defined as the network of relationships and social trust among individuals and groups, can influence interactions with organizations and social institutions, potentially affecting mood disorders. This study aims to explore the relationship between social capital, anxiety, and depression among nurses in private and public sectors in Isfahan city in 2022.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022, including nurses from both private and public hospitals in Isfahan. A total sample of 350 nurses, with 110 from private and 240 from public hospitals, were participated. Data collection instruments included demographic forms, Putnam’s Social Capital Questionnaire, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20, employing descriptive statistics, Chi-square test, independent t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, and ordinal logistic regression.RESULTS:The findings revealed no significant difference in anxiety and depression levels between nurses in private and public hospitals (P > 0.05). However, cognitive social capital was significantly associated with depression (P < 0.05), where higher cognitive component scores were linked to a reduced likelihood of anxiety at lower levels. Additionally, the extra group social capital component was significantly correlated with anxiety (P < 0.05), with higher extra group scores associated with a lower likelihood of depression at lower levels.CONCLUSION:Improving social capital components, such as cognitive and extra group social capital, could potentially help reduce anxiety and depression among nurses. Integrating social capital development into nursing management and training practices may offer a beneficial strategy for mental health support.

Introduction

Nursing is a professional, scientific discipline firmly rooted in the integration of comprehensive knowledge with skills, enriched through education and research. Its ultimate goal is to provide optimal care in alignment with professional vision and mission to beneficiaries. Nursing is undoubtedly one of the challenging professions.[1] Nurses operate around the clock in different shifts, fully committed to patient care. Their demanding schedules, including long hours and sleep deprivation, combined with workplace challenges and separation from their families, present them with a range of difficulties. Given the inherent demands of their profession, nurses regularly confront numerous stress-inducing factors within their work environment.[2] Stress, anxiety, hopelessness, and depression are emotional consequences considered painful and challenging in life.[3] Anxiety is a state of heightened mental agitation characterized primarily by fear, doubt, and excessive worry, often accompanied by physical sensations such as palpitations, shortness of breath, headaches, upset stomach, and restlessness.[4] In contemporary life, psychological pressure from anxiety is a well-known issue, becoming a persistent aspect of human existence as individuals encounter stress from an early age. The World Health Organization has reported a rising incidence of psychological disorders, with anxiety affecting 6.38% of people in developing countries and 2.83% in developed nations.[5]

Anxiety disorder was first recognized in 1980 by the American Psychological Association. Before that, similar conditions were referred to as stress or irritability.[6] Anxiety is an unsettling and vague emotional state often characterized as scattered, irrational fear. Unlike fear, the source of anxiety is unclear and typically manifests with symptoms such as dizziness, high blood pressure, tremors, restlessness, excessive sweating, nausea, frequent urination, and dilated pupils. The main feature of this disorder is excessive fear, worry, and doubt. Individuals experiencing anxiety constantly feel fear and worry without a clear reason, where the level of fear exceeds the actual threat, lacking logical proportionality between the intensity of fear and the threat. This fear and worry cause distress and prevent them from moving forward in life, maintaining their job, or establishing long-term relationships with friends and family members.[78] Depression, following anxiety, is recognized as a significant mood disorder. It is a prevalent and severe psychological condition marked by a loss of motivation and hope, reduced self-esteem, pessimism, and pervasive feelings of despair and sadness.[9]

Often, individuals suffering from depression are persistently sad and find no pleasure in activities they once enjoyed, or experience a combination of both states mentioned above. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition of the American Psychiatric Association, to diagnose depression in an individual, five or more symptoms must be present most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 consecutive weeks, along with clinically significant impairment in social, occupational, and other important areas of functioning.[10] Depression can be considered a mood and emotional disorder characterized by disturbances in sleep and appetite, along with reduced sexual desire, interest, and pleasure in usual activities, feelings of worthlessness and guilt, severe sadness, and withdrawal from others In addition, depression is accompanied by impaired functioning, migraines, alcohol and substance abuse, excessive stress, low social support, feelings of humiliation at school and work, feelings of emptiness and worthlessness, physical symptoms, and conflicts with parents and peers.[11]

Social capital refers to the web of relationships and connections that shape social trust and interactions among individuals, groups, organizations, and institutions. It encompasses the social cohesion, mutual trust, reciprocal relationships, and shared commitment that facilitate both individual and collective actions towards achieving goals. While definitions may vary, social capital is broadly recognized as the degree of integration, trust, and cooperative engagement within a community or group.[12]

The discussion on social capital began with Lyda Hanifan (1916), who stated that social capital is not only a valuable asset for improving the quality of life but also a means of enhancing social welfare across society as a whole.[13] Based on the perspectives of social capital theorists, it has five structural, cognitive, communication, intragroup, and intragroup components.[14] Research has shown that social capital increases with a decrease in depression symptoms among multicultural adolescents and reduces stress. In addition, increased social capital is associated with decreased anxiety. Cohen’s study (2018) demonstrated that cognitive social capital has a negative relationship with depression symptoms. Furthermore, significant correlation has been established between job burnout and stress, anxiety, and depression in nurses.[15]

Despite extensive research on anxiety, depression, and social capital, there remain significant gaps in specifically examining the impact of social capital on anxiety and depression among nurses. Many previous studies have explored the effects of social capital on mental health more broadly, but research specifically addressing nurses and their unique challenges is limited. This study aims to provide a more detailed examination of the relationship between social capital and psychological issues such as anxiety and depression among nurses in both private and public sectors. Furthermore, this research will investigate how social capital influences the improvement of nurses’ mental health and the reduction of job-related stress. The findings of this study could contribute to the development of targeted strategies for enhancing nurses’ mental health and improving their performance in work environments.

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

This research utilized a cross-sectional descriptive method to investigate the relationship between social capital and the reduction of stress, anxiety, and depression among nurses in both private and public hospitals in Isfahan, Iran, in 2022. The study aimed to compare the psychological wellbeing of nurses working in these sectors and assess the influence of social capital.

Study participants and sampling

A cluster random sampling method was employed to ensure a balanced representation of public and private hospitals. Out of the 17 public hospitals in Isfahan, 8 (47%) were randomly selected, and from the 8 private hospitals, 4 (50%) were chosen. The sample consisted of 350 nurses, with 110 nurses from private hospitals and 240 from public hospitals. The proportion of nurses from public hospitals (70%) and private hospitals (30%) reflected the distribution of hospitals in Isfahan. The required number of nurses from each hospital was randomly selected based on the total number of nurses in each hospital.

Data collection tools and technique

Data were collected using three tools

Demographic Information Questionnaire: Collected data on nurses’ age, gender, education, work experience, and exposure to COVID-19 patients during hospital service.

Putnam’s Social Capital Questionnaire: Developed by Putnam (1999), it contained 39 questions rated on a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 to 5), assessing two dimensions: intragroup and extra group social capital. Total scores ranged from 39 to 195.[1617]

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS): Developed by Zigmond and Snaith (1983), this scale assessed anxiety and depression using a 4-point Likert scale (scored 0 to 3). Scores for each subscale ranged from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater severity. The cutoff for clinical significance was 11.[18]

After obtaining informed consent, the nurses completed the questionnaires. Data were then analyzed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency), and various statistical tests, including Chi-square, independent t-test, ANOVA, Tukey test, and ordinal logistic regression, were employed to analyze the data.

Ethical consideration

The study adhered to ethical guidelines by obtaining written informed consent from all participants before data collection. Confidentiality and anonymity of the respondents were strictly maintained throughout the research. Participation was voluntary, and nurses were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without repercussions. The research was approved by the relevant ethics committee in accordance with established standards for human research.

Discussion

In relation to the first objective of the research, which is “determining the structural relationship of social capital and anxiety among nurses”, the results indicated a significant difference between the level of structural components and anxiety levels among nurses. In addition, the results showed that, with control over demographic variables, the structural component does not predict anxiety. The study by Shah Bakhsh et al.[19] (2021), investigating the relationship between social capital and anxiety and depression among nurses in hospitals affiliated with Zahedan University of Medical Sciences, demonstrated an inverse and significant relationship between social capital and anxiety. The findings of the current study are in line with this research. To elaborate on these findings, it can be said that the conditions and structure of interpersonal communication, warm relationships with colleagues, and personal bonds can influence nurses’ anxiety. Social capital, in the form of social connections and bonds, serves as an emotional resource among individuals, allowing community members to live together securely and thereby enhance their mental well-being.

Regarding the second aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the structural components of social capital and depression among nurses,” the results indicated a significant difference between the structural component and levels of depression among nurses. However, with controlling for demographic variables, the structural component did not predict depression. The findings of the study by Shah Bakhsh et al.[19] (2021) demonstrated an inverse and significant relationship between social capital and depression. The results of the study by Bassett and Moore[20] (2013) demonstrated that social capital and social psychological dimensions reduce the likelihood of depressive symptoms. The findings of the current research regarding the relationship between the structural component of social capital and depression in nurses are consistent with the mentioned studies. It can be explained that social support, workplace relationships, and warm personal relationships with peers are key social factors determining mental health. In addition, humans are social beings and throughout their lives, they need companionship and connections with others. If an individual believes they are respected and liked by others, are valued and have dignity, and belong to a network of mutual relationships and commitments, these beliefs ultimately lead to a sense of well-being and ultimately reduce depression.

Regarding the third aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the cognitive component of social capital and anxiety among nurses,” the results indicated no significant difference between the cognitive component and levels of anxiety among nurses. In addition, when controlling for demographic variables, the cognitive component did not predict anxiety. Similarly, the study by Jie Chang et al.[21] in 2017, which explored the relationship between social capital and symptoms of anxiety and depression among urban elderly individuals in China, also found no significant link between social capital factors and anxiety.

Regarding the fourth aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the cognitive component of social capital and depression among nurses,” the results showed that there is a significant difference between the cognitive component and levels of depression among nurses. In addition, with controlling for demographic variables, the cognitive component predicts depression and increased cognitive capital is associated with reduced depression.

Nur Yilmaz et al.[22] (2022) found that participants’ depression scores decreased by 0.947 with increased emotional job demand scores, while depression scores increased by 1.116 with increased stress scale scores. In addition, a 2018 study by Hannah Cohen-Cline et al.[15] showed that low social cognitive capital is associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms. In other words, individuals with lower social cognitive resources are more likely to experience symptoms of depression. Huang et al.[23] (2023) revealed that 54.6% of study participants exhibited symptoms of depression, and 48.1% experienced severe job burnout. A positive correlation was observed between role stress, job burnout, and depression, explaining approximately 37.1% of the variance in depression through job burnout components. Emergency nurses experience high levels of role stress, job burnout, and depression, necessitating preventive approaches to improve their mental health and quality of work life. In addition, Cohen-Cline et al.[15] (2018) investigated the relationship between social capital and depression, finding that all measures of cognitive and neighborhood social capital were associated with fewer depressive symptoms. The current study’s results regarding the relationship between the cognitive component of social capital and depression among nurses, both overall and in the public sector, are consistent with the mentioned research. The findings suggest that national and societal policies aimed at increasing social capital in various organizations can contribute to reducing symptoms of depression. If an individual lacks supportive resources, feels isolated and undervalued by others, and is hesitant to establish connections, it is expected that they may develop depression as a result.[22]

Regarding the fifth aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the communicational component of social capital and anxiety among nurses,” the results showed that there is a significant difference between the relational component and levels of anxiety among nurses. However, when controlling for demographic variables, the relational component does not predict anxiety. These findings are consistent with Bae’s[24] (2020) study, which demonstrated that increased social capital is associated with reduced symptoms of depression and decreased stress and anxiety. Chunkai Li et al.[25] (2018) reported that higher levels of social capital are associated with reduced anxiety. Their study found that individuals who have more robust social networks and stronger social connections tend to experience lower levels of anxiety. This research underscores the significance of social capital in mitigating anxiety, suggesting that supportive relationships and active social engagement can play a crucial role in alleviating anxiety symptoms. By enhancing social networks and fostering a supportive community environment, individuals are likely to experience a decrease in anxiety and an improvement in overall psychological well-being. Considering the association between social capital and job burnout, it appears that social capital can contribute to reducing job-related anxiety and stress, thereby enhancing nurses’ performance. Therefore, efforts to increase and promote social capital dimensions among nurses are essential.

Regarding the sixth aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the communicational component of social capital and depression among nurses,” the results indicated a significant difference between the communicational component and levels of depression among nurses. However, when controlling for demographic variables, the communicational component does not predict depression. This finding aligns with Chen and Meier’s[26] (2021) research, which identified a positive correlation between job burnout and depression, as well as emotional exhaustion among nurses. In addition, Namazadeh and Hashemi[27] (2022) showed a significant relationship between job burnout and stress, anxiety, and depression. Krystyna Golonka et al.[28] (2019) also highlighted that depression is a significant determinant of fatigue. They emphasized that depression not only contributes to the onset of fatigue but can also exacerbate its severity, leading to a more pronounced impact on individuals’ overall well-being and daily functioning.

Regarding the seventh aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the intragroup component of social capital and anxiety among nurses,” the results indicated a significant difference between the intragroup component and levels of anxiety among nurses. However, when controlling for demographic variables, the intragroup component does not predict anxiety. These findings are consistent with those of Bakhtiari et al.[29] (2022). Inward-oriented individuals often strive to fulfill their responsibilities diligently at work, which can lead to anxiety. Although they generally prefer peace and quiet, nurses with inward-oriented traits may engage in constant talking due to the demanding work conditions and congestion in hospital environments, potentially contributing to their anxiety. In another study (2018), it was observed that introverted individuals, who typically focus on personal responsibilities and self-discipline, often experience high levels of anxiety in high-pressure work environments. Despite their preference for calm and minimal external stimulation, these individuals may experience significant stress and anxiety due to the intense demands and continuous interactions required in their roles.[30] Therefore, based on the results of the present study, a significant relationship was reported between the intragroup component of social capital and anxiety.

Regarding the eighth aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the intragroup component of social capital and depression among nurses,” the results showed a significant difference between the intragroup component and levels of depression among nurses. However, when controlling for demographic variables, the intragroup component does not predict depression. Chen et al. (2018) conducted a study on the relationship between personal social capital and depressive symptoms. Their findings corroborate the protective effect of social capital on mental health observed in other countries. In addition, they identified a positive relationship between negative social capital and depression.[31]

Regarding the ninth aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the extragroup component of social capital and anxiety among nurses,” the results indicated a significant difference between the extragroup component and levels of anxiety among nurses. Moreover, when controlling for demographic variables, the extragroup component predicts anxiety. Zhang, et al.[32] (2024) found that role conflict ambiguity among intensive care unit nurses was significantly and positively correlated with anxiety. This suggests that role ambiguity has a notable impact on the mental health of ICU nurses, with emotional intelligence serving as a key factor in reducing both role ambiguity and anxiety. The results of the present study regarding the relationship between the extragroup component of social capital and anxiety among nurses are consistent with studies by Zhang, et al. (2024), Cohen-Cline et al. (2018), and Shah Bakhsh et al. (2021). The findings suggest that factors such as different interests and lifestyles can affect anxiety levels in various situations, such as the workplace, which can significantly impact their performance in the work environment.[151932]

Regarding the tenth aim of the research, which is to “determine the relationship between the extragroup component of social capital and depression among nurses,” the results showed that there is a significant difference between the extragroup component and levels of depression among nurses. Similarly, with controlling for demographic variables, the extragroup component does not predict depression. Ashuri[33] (2016) demonstrated that social capital, perceived social support, and organizational citizenship behavior had a significant negative relationship with job burnout among nurses. In other words, increasing social capital, perceived social support, and organizational citizenship behavior reduce job burnout among nurses.

Based on the findings of the research, it seems logical for hospital managers to consider necessary actions and facilities to enhance the social capital of nurses. They can implement educational programs aimed at increasing trust, intimacy, participation, commitment, respectful communication, and emphasizing individual and social values among this occupational group.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. Potential challenges related to the noncooperation of some hospital officials and staff may have affected data collection and overall results. In addition, cultural differences in the population and the timing of data collection, which occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, may have influenced the nurses’ levels of anxiety and depression, potentially impacting the findings. These factors necessitate caution when generalizing the results to other contexts or healthcare settings.

Recommendations

Based on the findings, several recommendations can be made for future research and practical applications. First, conducting comparative analyses between different types of nurses (e.g., ICU, emergency, and administrative) across various healthcare settings would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how social capital influences anxiety and depression in specific nursing environments. Future studies could also explore the effectiveness of psychological interventions specifically designed to reduce anxiety, depression, and stress among nurses. Another avenue of research could focus on examining the relationship between social capital and job responsibilities, investigating how various social capital components correlate with anxiety and overall well-being in different nursing roles.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this study, there is a clear indication that enhancing certain dimensions of social capital can significantly impact the mental health of nurses, specifically in terms of reducing anxiety and depression. The results suggest that while structural and relational components of social capital are critical for improving mental health, specific interventions focusing on cognitive and extragroup social capital could offer substantial benefits. Given the significant association between cognitive social capital and decreased depression, and extragroup social capital and reduced anxiety, health policy should prioritize the development of programs that foster these elements.

Health policies should aim to integrate targeted educational and support initiatives within healthcare settings. This includes creating structured programs that enhance cognitive and extragroup social capital through increased trust, communication, and supportive interactions among nurses. By focusing on these areas, hospital administrators and policymakers can address the psychological well-being of healthcare professionals more effectively. Such policies not only contribute to the reduction of stress and mental health issues among nurses but also enhance overall job satisfaction and performance, ultimately improving patient care outcomes.
