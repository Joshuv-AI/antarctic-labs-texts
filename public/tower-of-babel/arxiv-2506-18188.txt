Poverty Targeting with Imperfect Information
Juan C. Yamin

Abstract
A key challenge for targeted antipoverty programs in developing countries is that policymakers must rely on estimated rather than observed income, which leads to substantial targeting errors. I propose a statistical decision framework in which a benevolent planner, subject to a budget constraint and equipped only with noisy income estimates, allocates cash transfers to the poorest individuals. In this setting, the commonly used plug-in rule, which allocates transfers based on point estimates, is inadmissible and uniformly dominated by a shrinkage-based alternative. Building on this result, I propose an empirical Bayes (EB) targeting rule. I show that the regret of the empirical Bayes rule converges at the same rate as that of the posterior mean estimator, despite applying a nonsmooth transformation to it. Simulations show that the EB rule delivers large improvements over the plug-in approach in an idealized setting and modest but consistent gains in a more realistic application.



Keywords:
Poverty targeting; Empirical Bayes; Statistical decision theory; James–Stein estimator

Introduction

Poverty alleviation remains one of the most pressing policy challenges, especially in developing countries, where tight government budgets severely limit policy responses. To maximize the impact of scarce resources, governments have increasingly implemented targeted unconditional cash transfer (UCT) programs, which provide financial support to the poorest households. By 2020, UCT programs covered at least 1% of the population in low-income countries
Banerjee et al. (2023)
, a reflection of their growing importance in the global policy landscape. Prominent examples of such programs include South Africa’s Child Support Grant and China’s Minimum Living Standard Scheme, each of which reaches millions of beneficiaries
Barrientos (2013)
.

The success of targeted transfer programs hinges on the government’s ability to accurately identify poor households, a task made difficult by widespread informality and administrative limitations, which often prevent reliable measurement of income or consumption. To address this, policymakers typically predict household income based on available information and allocate transfers accordingly. One common approach, offering simplicity and low implementation costs, is geographic targeting, whereby aid is delivered to all households within regions estimated to be poor
Baker & Grosh (1994)
;
Elbers et al. (2007)
.
1
1

1

Geographic targeting allows policymakers to predict income and allocate transfers using only one dataset and without household-level data
Baker & Grosh (1994)
;
Elbers et al. (2007)
. Examples of programs implementing this approach include the Productive Safety Net in Ethiopia, Challenging the Frontiers of Poverty Reduction in Bangladesh, and Chile Solidario
Barrientos (2013)
.
Another widely used method is proxy means testing (PMT), whereby household income is predicted based on easily observable characteristics such as housing quality, education, and asset ownership.
2
2

2

PMT implementation involves using a representative survey to
train
a regression model to predict income at the household level and then applying the model to a broader dataset, typically a census or administrative registry, to target poor households. Examples of programs using PMT include SISBEN in Colombia, BISP in Pakistan, Progresa/Oportunidades in Mexico, and Takaful and Karama in Egypt
Barrientos (2013)
.

Targeting approaches used to identify impoverished households have been found to have high error rates in both inclusion and exclusion. Inclusion errors occur when transfers are provided to nonpoor households, while exclusion errors happen when impoverished households are not targeted. For example,
C. Brown et al. (2018)
find, for several African countries, that the inclusion error rate under a PMT approach is approximately 40% and the exclusion error rate approximately 70% when the poorest 20% of the population is targeted.
3
3

3

In probabilistic terms, the inclusion error rate is the probability that a household is not poor conditional on its being identified as poor, while the exclusion error rate is the probability that a household is not identified as poor given that it is indeed poor.
Despite advancements in machine learning, more complex models have generally failed to significantly improve the precision of income targeting
McBride & Nichols (2018)
;
Baez et al. (2019)
;
Areias & Wai-Poi (2022)
;
Corral et al. (2025)
.

This paper offers a distinct yet related perspective by shifting the focus from improving prediction accuracy to optimizing the use of available data. Specifically, I take the estimates as given and focus on how policymakers can optimally allocate the antipoverty budget using these estimates. In this sense, my approach complements the literature that seeks to improve income prediction.

Following
Bourguignon & Fields (1990)
;
Bourguignon & Fields (1997)
, I first characterize the optimal allocation of an antipoverty budget under complete information, where a benevolent planner implements an unconditional cash transfer program to assist the poorest individuals while adhering to a budget constraint. In practice, however, policymakers must rely on noisy income estimates from geographic targeting or PMT rather than true income data. This uncertainty transforms the allocation problem into a statistical decision problem: The planner must determine how to distribute transfers to maximize aid to the neediest while ensuring subsidies assistance (no taxation) and adherence to a budget constraint.

Given this setup and under the assumption of normality of the estimates, I show that the transfer assignment problem is equivalent to estimating the mean of a normally distributed variable with constraints on both the estimates (actions) and the mean (parameters). In particular, the parameter space is constrained by households’ incomes being nonnegative, while the planner’s decision is restricted by the no-taxation condition (transfers must be nonnegative) and the budget constraint (total transfers cannot exceed the available budget).

The equivalence between transfer assignment and constrained estimation allows the use of Stein’s lemma to establish that the plug-in rule is inadmissible. This rule, which is commonly used by policymakers, assigns transfers based directly on observed estimates while respecting the budget constraint. Specifically, I show that a James–Stein-type estimator achieves uniformly lower risk than the plug-in rule across the entire parameter space. While shrinkage estimators have traditionally been studied in the context of statistical estimation risk, I show that their benefits extend directly to policy decisions on transfer allocations. To my knowledge, this work is the first to make this connection. Overall, the dominance results suggest that
costless
improvements to the plug-in rule, such as introducing shrinkage, can enhance targeting accuracy without requiring additional data or complicating implementation.

To propose a more structured and flexible approach to shrinkage than the linear shrinkage of the James–Stein-type estimator, I present a nonparametric empirical Bayes (EB) method for poverty targeting. I begin by characterizing the oracle decision rule that minimizes Bayes risk, assuming the policymaker knows the true distribution of the parameters, namely the conditional expectation of income for each household. This yields a nonsmooth transfer rule: The optimal allocation depends on a thresholded version of the posterior mean, adjusted by a Lagrange multiplier that reflects the shadow price of budgetary resources. This transfer schedule minimizes the Bayes risk over all Borel measurable decision rules subject to the budget constraint, which means it weakly outperforms alternative methods restricted to narrower function classes
Jiang & Zhang (2009)
.

Since the true distribution of the conditional expectation of income is typically unknown, the oracle rule is generally infeasible and cannot be implemented directly. Hence, I follow the EB literature in approximating the optimal decision rule by estimating the prior using the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
Kiefer & Wolfowitz (1956)
;
Koenker & Mizera (2014)
. I then examine the theoretical statistical properties of the EB method in this setup. To measure performance, I define the Bayes regret as the difference in expected loss between the EB and oracle Bayes decision rules. Building on the results of
Jiang (2020)
, I show that, although the decision rule is a nonsmooth transformation of the posterior mean, the convergence rate of Bayes regret in the poverty-targeting problem is no slower than that of estimating the posterior mean under mean squared error loss. This finding highlights that optimal targeting decisions can be learned as efficiently as posterior means, even in the presence of budget and nonnegativity constraints.

Using consumption data from nine African countries collected by
C. Brown et al. (2018)
, I simulate a realistic targeting environment calibrated to the observed distribution of incomes and estimated noise levels. In this ideal setting, where the income estimates are assumed to be normally distributed, the empirical Bayes (EB) approach substantially outperforms conventional methods. Specifically, at a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 0.25, the EB rule achieves 15–30% of the loss reduction that would be possible under full-information transfers and performs nearly as well as an infeasible oracle benchmark that knows the true underlying distribution. In addition to improving aggregate performance, the EB method reduces exclusion errors by approximately 25 percentage points relative to the plug-in rule, allowing a substantially larger share of the poor population to be reached while maintaining a similar inclusion error rate.

In a more realistic exercise based on data from Mexico collected by
Corral et al. (2025)
, I do not impose normality and instead use the actual distribution of the income estimates. In this case, the EB approach continues to outperform the plug-in rule, achieving a modest 2% reduction in the gap relative to full-information targeting, on average. EB also delivers higher minimum targeting performance and substantially lower variability across samples. Compared to machine learning methods such as XGBoost, EB achieves better average targeting performance.

This paper contributes to several strands of the literature. In poverty targeting, most work emphasizes income prediction, often using plug-in rules under which true income is replaced with its estimate (see
Banerjee et al. (2023)
). A notable exception is
Follett & Henderson (2023)
, who introduce a Bayesian framework that blends PMT with community-based targeting, rather than directly improving targeting accuracy. In contrast, this paper adopts a statistical decision framework that moves beyond prediction to make better use of the available data. While related to work applying decision theory to policy design
Manski (2004)
;
Dehejia (2005)
;
Stoye (2012)
;
Tetenov (2012)
;
Kitagawa & Tetenov (2018)
;
Athey & Wager (2021)
, this paper shifts the focus from treatment assignment to targeting deprivation.

Second, this paper contributes to the predominantly statistical literature on the inadmissibility of constrained normal location estimators. While there is extensive research on the dominance of MLEs under restricted parameter spaces
Charras & Eeden (1992)
;
Marchand & Perron (2001)
;
Hartigan (2004)
;
Fourdrinier et al. (2018)
, these studies neither consider scenarios where the parameter space is a simplex nor address constraints on the action space. This paper extends these results by incorporating a constraint on the parameter space alongside an additional simplex constraint on the action space.

Third, this paper applies EB methods to a novel class of problems: constrained assignment problems with noisy estimates. While EB techniques have been extensively used for denoising and for ranking and selection tasks
Gu & Koenker (2023)
;
Kline et al. (2022)
;
Montiel Olea et al. (2021)
;
Chetty et al. (2014)
;
Chetty & Hendren (2018)
, their application to constrained decision-making environments has been limited. Two recent exceptions are
Moon (2025)
, who develops an empirical Bayes approach for optimizing social welfare under policy uncertainty, and
Kline et al. (2024)
, who use EB methods to assign discrete grades to firms based on noisy estimates of discriminatory conduct, balancing informativeness against reliability. In this paper, I show that nonparametric empirical Bayes (NPMLE) approaches can significantly enhance performance in structured allocation problems, expanding the practical scope of EB methods to real-world scenarios where resources must be optimally deployed under uncertainty.

This paper contributes to the literature on Bayes regret for EB estimators. Early results by
Zhang (2009)
and
Jiang & Zhang (2009)
, building on
Kiefer & Wolfowitz (1956)
, established regret bounds tied to the tail behavior of the prior. These were extended to multivariate and heteroskedastic settings by
Saha & Guntuboyina (2020)
,
Jiang (2020)
, and
Soloff et al. (2025)
and refined by
Chen (2023)
with the CLOSE-NPMLE. I add to this line of research by showing that a nonsmooth transformation of the posterior mean attains the same regret rates as the posterior mean itself.

First, I characterize the optimal transfer assignments when the planner has perfect household income information.

4

Accurately measuring the income of poor households presents significant challenges in practice, and income alone often fails to provide a complete picture of household welfare. In developing countries, factors such as nonmarketed subsistence production and informal in-kind transfers play a crucial role in shaping people’s livelihoods, making it difficult to capture their true economic well-being through income metrics alone. Additionally, seasonal income fluctuations further complicate efforts to measure household economic status consistently
Merfeld & Morduch (2023)
. For simplicity, I abstract from these complexities and assume that the planner can make perfectly informed targeting decisions based solely on household income.
I follow
Bourguignon & Fields (1990)
;
Bourguignon & Fields (1997)
to model the optimal decision of a benevolent social planner trying to minimize poverty. I assume there is a population of households with observable incomes, denoted by a column vector
\bm{y}=(y_{1},...,y_{n})^{\top}
.
5
5

5

For simplicity, I assume each household has a single member, abstracting from variation in household size.
The planner has to determine how to allocate a fixed but insufficient budget
B
through income transfers
\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{n})^{\top}
. I do not consider how the antipoverty budget
B
is financed: the policymaker cares only about how to spend it (i.e.,
\tau_{i}\geq 0,i=1,...,n
). Let
z
be the poverty line defined in terms of income.

To measure poverty, I consider the
Foster et al. (1984)
measure:

\displaystyle\rho_{\alpha}(y_{i}+\tau_{i})=\Bigg(\frac{z-y_{i}-\tau_{i}}{z}\Bigg)^{\alpha}\text{.}

The power
\alpha
can be interpreted as reflecting society’s ethical preferences concerning poverty, referred to as “aversion to poverty”
Barrientos (2013)
. Throughout, I assume that the planner aims to target the most deprived households and, for simplicity, set
\alpha=2
.
6
6

6

Haushofer et al. (2022)
argue that focusing solely on deprivation is typically suboptimal for maximizing social welfare, even when redistribution is a priority.
Thus, the function
\rho_{2}(\cdot)
is continuous, differentiable, and strictly convex.

Unlike the planner in
Bourguignon & Fields (1990)
;
Bourguignon & Fields (1997)
, who evaluates poverty only among those below the threshold, the planner in this framework considers the poverty index of all households. With
\alpha=2
, even households with income above the poverty line (
y_{i}\geq z
) contribute a positive loss,
(z-y_{i})^{2}
, despite the fact that the planner cannot reduce this loss through taxation. This modeling choice plays a central conceptual role. It ensures that the planner directly accounts for the opportunity cost of allocating scarce resources to less poor households. While a transfer to a household just above the threshold may generate only a small inefficiency, transfers to substantially better-off households crowd out resources to those in greater need. The loss function therefore internalizes the full cost of inclusion errors and by penalizing both underpayment and overpayment, it reflects the planner’s dual concern with reaching the poor and avoiding waste.
7
7

7

The squared-gap loss is also analytically convenient. Its symmetry and differentiability allow the application of tools like Stein’s lemma, which underlie the admissibility results in Section
3
. A monotone alternative such as
L_{+}=(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i})_{+}^{2}
, which penalizes only under-transfers, breaks the quadratic orthogonality identity required for these comparisons. While the theoretical results do not directly extend to that setting, Appendix Figures
C.1
and
C.2
show that the empirical Bayes rule of Section
4
continues to outperform the plug-in rule under
L_{+}
, even though the James–Stein-type estimator performs slightly worse due to underspending.

2.1.2
Optimal Allocation

Building on this setup, I now formalize the planner’s objective.
8
8

8

The model does not consider the potential impact of transfers on labor supply. This is consistent with empirical evidence suggesting that cash transfers do not significantly affect labor market behavior
Banerjee et al. (2017)
;
Handa et al. (2018)
.
Since
z
is a constant and does not affect the optimal allocation, I work with the unscaled loss function. The planner seeks to allocate a limited budget
B
, which is typically smaller than the total poverty gap,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{y_{i}<z\}(z-y_{i})
, through nonnegative transfers
\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})^{\top}
to minimize the average social loss from poverty:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\tau}=(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{n})}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i}\Big)^{2}\text{ subject to }

\displaystyle\text{(i) }\sum_{i}^{n}\tau_{i}\leq B,

(1)

\displaystyle\text{(ii) }\tau_{i}\geq 0,\forall i.

That is, the planner aims to minimize the sum of squared poverty gaps subject to a fixed antipoverty budget
B
. Proposition
1

The KKT conditions provide an analytical solution, the projection method offers a convenient characterization to derive the results of the next sections, and the “leveling-up” algorithm provides an intuitive interpretation useful for policy implementation. The proof of Proposition
1

A

\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\max\Bigg\{0,z-y_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigg\}\text{, }i=1,...,n\text{.}

(2)

\bm{1}z-\bm{y}

The first characterization follows directly from applying the KKT conditions to the strictly convex minimization problem. Because the objective is a sum of squared terms and the constraints are linear, the KKT system is both necessary and sufficient for optimality. Equation (
2
) implies that when a household’s “budget-adjusted” poverty gap
z-y_{i}-\frac{\lambda}{2}
is positive, the household receives a transfer; otherwise, it receives none. The Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
is the shadow price of public funds: It ensures that the total resources allocated to households do not exceed the budget.
9
9

9

Let
\mathrm{T}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\{0,z-y_{i}-\lambda/2\}
.
\mathrm{T}(\lambda)
is continuous and strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
with
\mathrm{T}(0)=\sum_{i}(z-y_{i})>0
and
\lim{\lambda\to\infty}\mathrm{T}(\lambda)=0
. Hence, a unique
\lambda^{*}\geq 0
solving
\mathrm{T}(\lambda^{*})=\min\{\mathrm{T}(0),B\}
exists by the intermediate-value theorem; if
\Phi(0)\leq B
, then
\lambda^{*}=0
(budget slack); otherwise,
\lambda^{*}>0
, and the budget binds. This monotonicity argument applies verbatim whether the planner works with

, the plug-in rule, the posterior means, or their EB estimates, guaranteeing a well-defined and unique transfer vector in every case.
Intuitively, the planner chooses
\lambda
to “trim” each individual’s poverty gap by a uniform amount, reflecting the fact that a single marginal dollar of transfers must be allocated where it reduces the overall sum of squared poverty gaps the most.

An alternative viewpoint is geometric: The feasible set
\{\bm{\tau}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\leq B,\tau_{i}\geq 0\}
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Because the objective function can be written as a squared euclidean distance between
\bm{\tau}
and the poverty-gap vector
\bm{1}z-\bm{y}

\bm{1}z-\bm{y}
onto that simplex.

A more constructive interpretation emphasizes the intuitive way in which the planner allocates transfers from the bottom of the income distribution upward. Starting with the poorest household, the planner incrementally equalizes its income with that of the next poorest, pooling resources to “level them up” together. This process continues, one household at a time, until the available budget is exhausted. Formally, there exists an income level
y\in[y_{p},y_{p+1})
and an integer
p
such that all households with
i\leq p
receive transfers bringing their income up to
\underline{y}
while households with
i>p
receive no transfers. The level
\underline{y}
and cutoff
p
are determined jointly so that the budget is exactly spent, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{p}(y-y_{i})=B
.

2.2
Noisy Measurements

The solution to the antipoverty budget assignment problem in Section
2.1
yields an optimal transfer for each household
i=1,...,n
. Nevertheless, the assignment rule is not feasible without direct knowledge of the income vector
\bm{y}
. As discussed in the introduction, it is uncommon for policymakers to have a precise measure of income or consumption for poor households in developing countries. In this subsection, I consider the problem of finding the optimal transfer when the planner observes only noisy measurements of household income.

The targeting decisions are based on predictions of income generated by parametric or nonparametric specifications of observable variables
W_{i}
, such as household characteristics or geographic location. These predictions, commonly implemented via PMT or geographic targeting, are trained with separate data sources and are treated here as primitives. In other words, the planner is not involved in building the model but still must act upon its outputs.

Formally, the planner observes a noisy estimate
\bm{\hat{Y}}=(\hat{Y}_{1},...,\hat{Y}_{n})^{\top}
and its standard error
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})^{\top}
. The predictions
\hat{Y}_{i}
are interpreted as noisy estimates of the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid W_{i}]=\mu_{i}
. Specifically,
\bm{\hat{Y}}
is a random vector taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and whose realizations
\bm{\hat{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},...,\hat{y}_{n})^{\top}
are referred as
data
. Motivated by the central limit theorem, I assume normality of the estimates and consider them a noisy but unbiased signal of
\mu_{i}
. In addition, to rule out degenerate or pathological cases, I bound
\sigma_{i}^{2}
within a minimum and maximum range.

(Normality with Bounded Variance)
Let
W_{i}
be the vector of covariates used to predict income for each household
i=1,...,n
. The income estimates
\bm{\hat{Y}}
are conditionally normally distributed, centered at their conditional mean, and independent across
i

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{i}\mid\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})\text{ , }i=1,...,n\text{,}

(3)

where
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid W_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and

The following two remarks discuss why the normality assumption is a reasonable approximation under the two most common targeting approaches: geographic targeting and PMT.

Remark 2.1

Geographic Targeting.
Geographic targeting is a frequently used approach in poverty alleviation programs whereby specific regions or communities with high poverty rates are identified and prioritized for assistance. Although policymakers aim to target regions rather than individual households, there is a direct mapping between the two problems. In this setting, the objective function must be redefined to depend on region-level income rather than household-level income. However, because each region can still be treated as a single unit with an associated signal and target, the structure of the problem remains unchanged in terms of the budget constraint, the action space, and the decision rule.

The approach consists of using representative survey data to estimate the average income and then target geographic regions using that estimate. Specifically, the estimate is given by

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{j}=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}y_{ij},\quad j=1,...,J\text{, }

where
j
denotes the administrative region,
y_{ij}
the income or consumption of households in region
j
, and
n_{j}
the number of households in region
j
. That is, in the regression framework, the covariates
W_{i}
used for prediction are simply a set of region-specific dummy variables indicating the household’s location.

The normal distribution assumption is reasonable as long as the population from which the samples are drawn has a finite variance and the effective sample size for each administrative region is relatively large. That is, as the sample size grows to infinity,

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{y}_{j}-\mu_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad j=1,...,J\text{,}

where
\mu_{j}
is the true average income and
\sigma_{j}
is the standard error of the estimate for region
j
. The same argument extends to more sophisticated approaches that use census data, administrative datasets, or satellite information to target smaller geographic units (e.g.,
Elbers et al. (2003)
;
Blumenstock et al. (2015)
).

Remark 2.2

Proxy Means Testing.
Under PMT, observable household characteristics, such as household assets or housing quality, are taken as proxies for household income or consumption. These characteristics are used to construct a poverty score that, in turn, determines eligibility for the program. Specifically, on representative survey data, the following regression is estimated by ordinary least squares (OLS):

\displaystyle y_{i}=W_{i}^{\prime}\beta+\epsilon_{i}\text{, }\quad i=1,...,n\text{,}

(4)

where
i
denotes the household,
y_{i}
is income or consumption, and

\hat{y}_{i}=W_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\beta}

, which capture the planner’s uncertainty about the consequences of its actions.
.

For each household
i
, after the planner observes the data
\hat{Y}_{i}=\hat{y}_{i}
, it has to determine what the size of the transfer
\tau_{i}
should be. Since the planner is not allowed to tax households and has to respect the budget constraint, the action space is
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\;\middle|\;\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{x}\leq B\right\}
.

Motivated by the model in Section
2.1
, the loss function
L:\mathcal{A}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
faced by the planner, which captures the poverty index under the choice of action
\bm{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})^{\top}
subject to the budget constraint, is given by

\displaystyle L(\bm{t},\bm{\mu})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(z-\mu_{i}-t_{i}\Big)^{2}\text{.}

This loss function reflects the planner’s objective, which is to minimize the poverty index as determined by household conditional mean income including transfers, subject to a feasibility constraint that total transfers cannot exceed the available antipoverty budget
B
. That is, the planner must balance the gain from more transfers and the additional cost in terms of approaching the budget constraint. Crucially, since the planner observes only the noisy estimates of income
\bm{\hat{Y}}
, there is uncertainty about the poverty index of each action.

A statistical decision rule in this problem is a mapping
\bm{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{A}
that, for each observed value of the data
\bm{\hat{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, indicates what action in space
\mathcal{A}
to take. In other words, for each possible realization of the income
data
, a statistical decision rule has to specify the transfer amount each household should receive. Note that the action space does not coincide with the parameter space as in traditional estimation problems, as the planner is forced to satisfy the budget constraint. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{A}\}
denote the set of all measurable decision rules.

The next remark provides an important insight: Minimizing the loss with respect to
\bm{\mu}
is equivalent (up to a constant) to minimizing it with respect to
\mathbf{y}
.

Remark 2.3

Loss Equivalence Between
\bm{y}
and
\bm{\mu}
.

Let
y_{i}=\mu_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}
with
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid W_{i}]
and

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\mid W_{i}]=0
. For any (measurable) decision rule

\bm{\tau}(W)=(\tau_{1}(W_{1}),\ldots,\tau_{n}(W_{n}))
, the quadratic
loss satisfies, household by household,

\displaystyle L_{i}(\tau_{i},y_{i})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i}(W_{i}))^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[(z-\mu_{i}-\tau_{i}(W_{i}))^{2}\bigr]-2\,\mathbb{E}\!\bigl[(z-\mu_{i}-\tau_{i}(W_{i}))\,\varepsilon_{i}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=L_{i}(\tau_{i},\mu_{i})\;+\;\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}],

where the cross-term vanishes by
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}\mid W_{i}]=0
and the

i=1,\dots,n
gives

L(\bm{\tau},\bm{y})\;=\;L(\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu})\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}],

and the additive constant

\bm{\mu}
. Even though the planner ultimately cares about true income, all subsequent risk comparisons can therefore be
conducted with
\bm{\mu}

The mean loss or (frequentist) risk function
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{D}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
of a statistical decision rule
\bm{\tau}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\hat{Y}}}[L(\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu})]\text{,}

where the expectation is taken with respect to the probability distribution of
\bm{\hat{Y}}
so
\mathcal{R}(\bm{\tau},\bm{\mu})
represents the average loss to the planner when
\bm{\tau}
is used across different realizations of the data.

3
Inadmissibility of Plug-In Rule

3.1
Equivalence

The previous section describes a statistical decision problem in which the planner allocates transfers to minimize expected poverty given noisy income observations. To facilitate the analysis and leverage existing statistical results, it is useful to recast this problem in an equivalent form. Specifically, rather than framing it as a transfer assignment problem, we can view it as the problem of estimating a Gaussian location vector under convex constraints.

Specifically, let the observed poverty gap be given by
X_{i}=z-\hat{Y}_{i}\sim N(\theta_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
for
i=1,...,N
, where
\theta_{i}=z-\mu_{i}
. Crucially, although this is set as a transfer assignment problem, it is equivalent to the problem of selecting an estimator
\bm{\tau}(\bm{X})\in\mathcal{A}
to minimize loss
||\bm{\theta}-\bm{\tau}(\bm{X})||^{2}
while observing
\bm{X}=(\bm{1}z-\bm{\hat{Y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
drawn from a normal distribution with location
\bm{\theta}\in(-\infty,z]^{n}
. In other words, it is equivalent to the problem of estimating the Gaussian location vector when the estimator and the parameter are constrained to be in convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Proposition
2
formalizes the equivalence between the two problems.

Proposition 2

(
Problem Equivalence
):

The problem of optimally allocating transfers to minimize expected poverty based on noisy income observations is statistically equivalent to the constrained Gaussian location estimation problem in the following sense:
There exist bijective mappings between the parameter spaces, sample spaces, and action spaces of the two problems that:

1.

preserve the sampling distributions,

2.

preserve the loss incurred by each action–parameter pair, and

3.

induce a one-to-one correspondence between decision rules that preserve their risk functions at all parameter values.

Hence, every decision rule in one problem corresponds uniquely to a rule in the other with the same risk at each parameter value.

Using this equivalence to the problem of estimating a Gaussian location vector, I show in this section that the projection of
\bm{X}
onto
\mathcal{A}
, known as the plug-in rule, is dominated by a James–Stein-type estimator that achieves uniformly lower risk across the entire parameter space.

3.2
Plug-In Rule

To simplify the exposition and facilitate comparison with traditional shrinkage estimators, I assume throughout this subsection that the income estimates are conditionally independent and homoskedastic, i.e.,
\hat{Y}_{i}\mid\mu_{i}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma^{2}),\quad i=1,\dots,n
, where the variance
\sigma^{2}
is known and constant across households.

A plug-in rule is a decision-making approach in which the unknown parameter of interest is replaced by its estimate when decisions are made. That is, the action that optimizes the decision criterion is chosen based on an estimated value, typically obtained through maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or the method of moments, rather than the true, unobserved parameter
Hirano & Porter (2019)
.

In the context of poverty targeting, the plug-in rule involves using estimates of household incomes to determine the optimal allocation of transfers. Specifically, the planner applies the full-information allocation rule from Proposition
1
using predicted incomes in place of the true values. That is, transfers are computed as if the noisy estimates were fully accurate.

By Proposition
1
, the plug-in estimator is the projection of the observed poverty gap
\bm{X}=\bm{1}z-\bm{\hat{Y}}
onto the set
\mathcal{A}
. Formally, it can be defined as the solution to the convex optimization problem

\bm{\tau}^{\text{plug}}(\bm{X})=\arg\min_{\bm{\tau}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\bm{X}-\bm{\tau}\|^{2}.

This corresponds to finding the vector of feasible transfers that is closest (in euclidean norm) to the observed poverty gaps. Intuitively, the solution sets to zero any components of
\bm{X}
that are negative. In other words, households estimated to be above the poverty line receive no transfer, and the remaining components are scaled to ensure that the budget constraint is satisfied.

The projection admits a closed-form solution based on a thresholding rule. Let
\gamma^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the unique value such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\{0,X_{i}-\gamma^{\star}\}=B
. Then, the plug-in estimator is given by

\tau^{\text{plug}}_{i}(X)=\max\{0,X_{i}-\gamma^{\star}\},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

This estimator is piecewise linear and Lipschitz continuous; it is differentiable almost everywhere, and the associated projection map is nonexpansive. That is, for any
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\|\bm{\tau}^{\text{plug}}(X)-\bm{\tau}^{\text{plug}}(X^{\prime})\|\leq\|X-X^{\prime}\|
. These properties play a central role in the analysis of the estimator’s risk and in the application of Stein-type dominance results. Appendix
B
provides a detailed analysis of the mathematical properties of this estimator that are necessary to study its risk and apply Stein-type results.

3.3
Dominance Results

The plug-in estimator
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
, while natural and interpretable, is known to be inadmissible in constrained settings. This result follows from the work of
Charras & Eeden (1992)
, which shows that estimators lying on the boundary of compact parameter spaces are generally dominated under squared error loss. The intuition is rooted in complete class theorems: Admissible estimators must be Bayes estimators with respect to some prior, and Bayes estimators with nondegenerate priors rarely take boundary values.
12
12

12

I focus on nonrandomized estimators, as the combination of a quadratic loss and a convex constraint set implies that the class of nonrandomized rules is essentially complete; see
Ferguson (2014)
.
Importantly, in the current proof of the inadmissibility of the plug-in rule, I do not determine which estimators dominate.

In this subsection, I extend previous results in the statistics literature that have shown that the natural estimator with
L_{2}
loss is dominated by a James–Stein-type shrinkage estimator when the action space is restricted to a polyhedral cone
Fourdrinier et al. (2018)
. Specifically, in this section, I propose and analyze a James–Stein-type estimator that improves upon
\tau^{\text{plug}}
in terms of risk. Classical Stein-type results rely on the availability of weak derivatives for both the estimator and its normalized direction, which are established in Appendix
B
.

Let
\mathcal{K}(X)=\{i:\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}(X)>0\}
denote the set of indices where the plug-in estimator is positive, and let
s=|\mathcal{K}(X)|
denote its cardinality. Define also

g(X):=\frac{\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}},

the normalized direction of the plug-in estimator. The constrained James–Stein estimator is given by

\displaystyle\tau^{\text{JS}}(X)=\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s-3)}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}}\right)_{\text{shrink}}\cdot\tau^{\text{plug}}(X),

(5)

where the truncation operator
(x)_{\text{shrink}}
is defined as
(x)_{\text{shrink}}:=x
if
0<x<1
and
(x)_{\text{shrink}}:=1
otherwise. Note that for any
s\leq 3
,
\tau^{\text{JS}}(X)=\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
.

The purpose of the truncation is twofold. First, it ensures that shrinkage is applied only when it is actually expected to improve risk: If the optimal shrinkage factor
1-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s-3)}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}}
lies strictly between zero and one, then partial shrinkage is beneficial and is applied. If the factor is either negative (implying excessive shrinkage) or greater than one (implying that no shrinkage is needed), the estimator defaults to the plug-in rule with no modification.

Second, the truncation guarantees that the estimator remains in the action space. Since
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
belongs to the simplex by construction, any positive scaling by a factor between 0 and 1 preserves both the nonnegativity of the components and the total sum constraint. By setting the shrinkage factor to 1 when necessary, the construction avoids any possibility of the estimator moving outside the simplex.

The classical James–Stein estimator improves risk performance by shrinking the observed data toward a central point, exploiting the fact that in high dimensions, such shrinkage can yield uniformly lower risk under squared loss. However, in the poverty-targeting problem, this idea must be adapted to respect the planner’s constraints: namely, that transfers must be nonnegative and the total allocation must remain within the available budget. The plug-in estimator
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
satisfies these constraints by construction but can still exhibit high variance, particularly when many components are active.

To address this, the James–Stein-type estimator
\tau^{\text{JS}}(X)
applies shrinkage selectively, only when doing so is expected to reduce risk without violating feasibility. The estimator reduces the variance of
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
by shrinking its magnitude toward zero, especially in high-dimensional settings where
s>3
. This mirrors the classical James–Stein insight that, for estimation of a high-dimensional mean vector, a shrunken estimate can achieve lower risk. In the constrained setting, the shrinkage intensity is governed by the ratio
\sigma^{2}(s-3)/\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}
, which captures the trade-off between variance reduction and bias.

The next proposition formalizes the risk improvement offered by the James–Stein-type estimator over the plug-in rule in high-dimensional settings.

Proposition 3

(
James–Stein-Type Estimator Dominance
):

If
\mathbb{E}[\|g(X)\|^{2}]<\infty
, then the James–Stein-type estimator
\tau^{\text{JS}}(X)
in (
5
) strictly dominates the plug-in rule
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
whenever
\Pr(s>3,0<\sigma^{2}[s-3]<||\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)||^{2})>0
.

As in the unrestricted case, Stein’s lemma
Stein (1981)
is key to finding a dominating estimator since it provides a mechanism to calculate the expected risk reduction when an estimator is shrunk toward a central point. By leveraging the Stein lemma, I can express one of the expectations in the risk calculation as a function of
\bm{X}
and not of the parameters, which allows to show that the James–Stein-type estimator has lower risk than the plug-in rule. The fundamental condition for the use of Stein’s lemma is that the estimator
\bm{\tau}^{\text{plug}}(X)
and its normalized direction
\frac{\bm{\tau}^{\text{plug}}(X)}{||\bm{\tau}^{\text{plug}}(X)||^{2}}
are weakly differentiable, which is verified in Appendix
B
.

To clarify the distinction between the plug-in rule and the James–Stein-type shrinkage rule, I include a simple numerical illustration with five households. The households have the following income estimates
\bm{\hat{Y}}=(20,35,45,60,100)^{\top}
, the poverty line is
z=100
and the planner’s budget is
B=100
. The plug-in rule projects the estimated poverty gaps onto the budget simplex, allocating
(45,30,20,5,0)^{\top}
units across the five households. It disburses the entire budget but heavily favors the household with the largest (and possibly noisiest) gap. In contrast, the James–Stein rule multiplies this allocation by a shrinkage factor to reduce overall risk. In this example, the shrinkage factor is approximately
0.731
, producing the shrunk allocation
(32.9,21.9,14.6,3.7,0)^{\top}
. This rule retains the ranking of needs but distributes transfers more conservatively, spending only 73% of the budget.

Figure 1
:
Transfer allocations under the plug in and James–Stein–type rules.

Figure
1
plots these allocations. The plug in estimator spends every dollar but may over-allocate to extreme, noise-driven signals. The James–Stein rule trims these values proportionally, reducing exposure to estimation error and lowering mean squared error uniformly across all states of the world. While the resulting under-spending may appear suboptimal, it is in fact
optimal
under a risk minimization objective: Proposition
3
guarantees that, so long as at least three households receive positive transfers, the James–Stein rule achieves strictly lower risk than the plug in benchmark. Intuitively, the shrinkage mechanism dampens large, potentially spurious over-allocations, which are especially costly under the squared loss.

Even though the proposed James–Stein-type estimator dominates the plug-in rule, it is likely not admissible. In the unrestricted case, shrinkage estimators such as the James–Stein estimator or the positive-part Stein estimator are themselves inadmissible, as shown by
Shao & Strawderman (1994)
. This suggests that when the location parameter is constrained to lie in the simplex, there may be alternative estimators that dominate the proposed
\bm{\tau}^{\text{JS}}(\bm{X})
.

4
(Empirical) Bayes Rules

4.1
EB Framework

In the previous section, I established that the conventional plug-in rule is inadmissible: it can be uniformly improved upon with no additional information. However, inadmissibility, while important, provides only negative guidance. It rules out bad procedures but offers no clear path to selecting a good one. Without a principled way to choose among admissible alternatives, policymakers risk making arbitrary or inefficient decisions. To move from negative to positive guidance, I adopt the
Bayes risk principle
, which prescribes selecting the decision rule that minimizes the expected loss averaged over both the data and a prior distribution on the parameters.

The Bayes risk principle formalizes the idea that a good decision rule should perform well not just on the observed data but across all possible parameter values, weighted by how plausible they are under a prior distribution. Formally, for a general nonnegative loss function that evaluates the consequences of a decision rule
\bm{\tau}
at a parameter vector
\bm{\mu}\sim G
, the Bayes risk minimizer
\bm{\tau^{*}}
solves:

\bm{\tau^{*}}\in\arg\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\hat{Y}}}\left[L(\bm{\tau}(\bm{\hat{Y}}),\bm{\mu})\right]\right].

Here, the loss is averaged first over the randomness in the observed estimates
\bm{\hat{Y}}
and then over the uncertainty in the true parameters themselves, with the prior distribution
G
weighting different regions of the parameter space. Note that this framework conceptualizes
\bm{\mu}
as random draws from
G
, in contrast to the framework of the previous section, where
\bm{\mu}
was treated as fixed.

A key practical challenge arises from the need to specify a prior distribution
G
, which is rarely straightforward in applied settings. In poverty targeting, for example, policymakers typically have limited reliable information about the distribution of the conditional expectation of household incomes beyond the noisy estimates available. Fully subjective priors may therefore appear arbitrary, difficult to defend, or even politically contentious. This lack of credible prior knowledge makes direct application of the Bayes risk principle problematic. To overcome this obstacle, a widely used and practical alternative is to adopt an EB approach, whereby the prior is estimated directly from the data rather than on the basis of external assumptions.

The EB approach adopts the Bayes risk principle in spirit by seeking to approximate the oracle Bayes rule without assuming knowledge of the true prior distribution. Specifically, the objective is to construct a decision rule that, although based on an estimated prior, achieves an expected loss close to that of the true Bayes-optimal rule corresponding to the unknown prior. In the formulation of nonparametric EB, as developed by
L. Brown & Greenshtein (2009)
, the unknown parameters are treated as realizations from an unknown distribution, and the goal is to design a data-driven estimator whose Bayes risk asymptotically matches that under the oracle Bayes rule. This framework motivates the EB procedure proposed in this paper, where the prior distribution is estimated nonparametrically from the data and then the corresponding Bayes rule applied to the observed data.
13
13

13

Strictly speaking, the EB rule is not a Bayes rule in Berger’s sense because the uncertainty about G is not integrated out. Proposition
5
shows, however, that its risk converges to that of the oracle Bayes rule.

To formalize the empirical Bayes framework, I impose a standard assumption consistent with this perspective. First, I treat the vector of variances
\bm{\sigma}^{2}=(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}^{2})^{\top}
as fixed and known. Conditional on the noise variances, the true conditional expectations of income are modeled as independent and identically distributed draws from a common probabilistic distribution
G
. This structure enables estimation of the prior from the observed data and justifies application of an EB rule as an approximation of the oracle Bayes rule.

Assumption 2
(Prior)
Given
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{n})^{\top}
, the conditional expectation of incomes is generated by i.i.d. draws from a common probabilistic distribution
G
:

\mu_{i}\mid\bm{\sigma}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}G,\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Thus, in this particular setup,
G
represents an objective description of the variation in parameters rather than subjective beliefs about their likely values. Assumption 2 implies prior independence: The true conditional expectation of income and the precision of the income estimates must be uncorrelated, i.e.,
\mu_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp\sigma_{i}
for all
i
. However, this assumption may be implausible in the current context. For instance, households with observable characteristics associated with higher income, such as tile roofing on their dwellings, tend to be relatively rare in developing countries. For such households, both their incomes (
\mu_{i}
) and the uncertainty of the respective income estimates (
\sigma_{i}
) are higher, which creates a positive correlation between the two. For simplicity of analysis, I focus on the case where
\sigma_{i}
and
\mu_{i}
are independent, and I leave an extension of the CLOSE approach proposed by
Chen (2023)
to my setup as a case for future research.

4.2
Oracle Rule

In this subsection, I characterize the decision rule that minimizes the Bayes risk when the true prior distribution
G
is known. This oracle rule provides a benchmark for optimal behavior under uncertainty, serving as a guide for the EB procedure developed later.

Because the loss function is nonnegative and measurable, Fubini’s theorem allows interchanging of the order of expectations. Thus, the Bayes risk conditional on
\bm{\sigma}^{2}
can be expressed as

r(G,\bm{\tau}\mid\bm{\sigma}^{2})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\hat{Y}}\mid\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{G\mid\bm{\hat{Y}},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\left[L(\bm{\tau}(\bm{\hat{Y}}),\bm{\mu})\right]\right],

where the outer expectation is taken with respect to the marginal distribution of
\bm{\hat{Y}}
given
\bm{\sigma}^{2}
and the inner expectation is taken with respect to the posterior distribution of
\bm{\mu}
.

Minimizing the Bayes risk amounts to minimizing the posterior expected loss
pointwise
for each realization of the data. To formalize this, fix a realization
\bm{\hat{y}}
of
\bm{\hat{Y}}
. The posterior risk of an action
\bm{t}\in\mathcal{A}
is defined as

r(G,\bm{t}\mid\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\sigma}^{2}):=\mathbb{E}_{G\mid\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\sigma}^{2}}\left[L(\bm{t},\bm{\mu})\right].

Thus, for each realization
\bm{\hat{y}}
, the optimal action satisfies

\bm{t}^{*}(\bm{\hat{y}})\in\arg\min_{\bm{t}\in\mathcal{A}}\,r(G,\bm{t}\mid\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\sigma}^{2}).

Assembling these optimal actions across all realizations yields the oracle Bayes rule
\bm{\tau}^{*}
.

To characterize the optimal action for a given realization
\bm{\hat{y}}
, observe that the posterior risk admits a simple decomposition. Because the prior
G
implies that the
\mu_{i}
s are conditionally independent given
\bm{\hat{y}}
and the loss is additive across components, the posterior risk is decomposed as

\displaystyle r(G,\bm{t}\mid\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\sigma}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{G\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left[(z-\mu_{i}-t_{i})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(z-\mathbb{E}_{G}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]-t_{i})^{2}+\mathrm{Var}_{G}(\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})\right].

Since the variance terms
\mathrm{Var}_{G}(\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
do not depend on the actions
\bm{t}
, they can be ignored for optimization purposes. Minimizing the posterior risk thus reduces to a constrained least squares problem with the following Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{t},\lambda,\bm{\xi})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(z-\mathbb{E}_{G}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]-t_{i}\right)^{2}+\lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}-B\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}t_{i},

where
\lambda\geq 0
and
\xi_{i}\geq 0
are the Lagrange multipliers corresponding to the budget and nonnegativity constraints, respectively. The solution to this optimization problem is characterized in the following proposition.

Proposition 4

(
Oracle Bayes Rule
):

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, for each data realization
\bm{\hat{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\dots,\hat{y}_{n})^{\top}
, the oracle Bayes action is given componentwise by

t_{i}^{*}(\bm{\hat{y}};\lambda)=\max\left\{0,\,z-\mathbb{E}_{G}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right\},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

where the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
is chosen to satisfy the budget constraint:

\lambda^{*}=\inf\left\{\lambda\geq 0\;:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{*}(\bm{\hat{y}};\lambda)\leq B\right\}.

The oracle decision rule is the mapping

\bm{\tau}^{*}:\bm{\hat{y}}\longmapsto\left(t_{1}^{*}(\bm{\hat{y}};\lambda^{*}),\dots,t_{n}^{*}(\bm{\hat{y}};\lambda^{*})\right)^{\top},

which uniquely minimizes the Bayes risk
r(G,\bm{\tau}\mid\bm{\sigma}^{2})
over the class of decision rules
\mathcal{D}
.

The Bayesian planner’s behavior fundamentally differs from planner behavior in the perfect information case (Proposition
1
). Under perfect information, the planner simply ranks households by their true incomes and transfers resources to the poorest until the budget is exhausted. When only noisy income estimates are available, however, it is no longer obvious which households should be prioritized or how much they should receive. A household with a low estimated income either could be truly poor or could merely appear poor due to random variation in the estimation process, and the planner must account for this uncertainty when making allocation decisions.

The oracle Bayes rule addresses this challenge by allocating transfers based on each household’s posterior expected poverty gap, adjusted downward by the shadow price of budgetary resources. Households with low posterior expected incomes are prioritized, while those with sufficiently high posterior means receive no transfers. This approach ensures that resources are directed toward households where the expected marginal benefit, measured in terms of posterior risk reduction, is greatest.

A key feature of the Bayesian allocation rule is its use of shrinkage, a correction mechanism formalized by Tweedie’s formula. In this setting, Tweedie’s formula states that the posterior mean can be expressed as

\mathbb{E}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]=\hat{y}_{i}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{y}_{i}}\log f(\hat{y}_{i}),

where
f(\hat{y}_{i})
denotes the marginal density of
\hat{y}_{i}
under the prior. The posterior mean corrects the raw estimate
\hat{y}_{i}
by a term that depends on the local shape of the data distribution and the noise level
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, effectively shrinking extreme or highly uncertain observations toward more plausible values.

This shrinkage mechanism plays a crucial role in minimizing costly misallocations. Rather than taking noisy estimates at face value, the Bayesian planner adjusts the observed data for uncertainty. As a result, transfers to households with highly uncertain or extreme estimates are moderated, reducing the risk of large, costly errors even if this means many smaller mistakes end up being accepted.

More broadly, the Bayesian rule explicitly balances the trade-off between bias and variance inherent in the risk function. The plug-in rule minimizes bias conditional on the observed data but ignores variance, leading to overly aggressive decision-making prone to costly errors. In contrast, the Bayesian rule tempers bias reduction by controlling variance, yielding decisions that are more stable and less sensitive to estimation noise
S.J. Brown (1978)
.

4.3
Empirical Bayes Procedure

In practice, the oracle Bayes rule
\bm{\tau}^{*}
cannot be implemented because the true prior distribution
G
is unknown. The EB approach addresses this challenge by approximating the oracle rule:
G
is estimated directly from the observed data, and this estimate is used to construct a feasible decision rule. In this way, nonparametric EB procedures aim at the smaller benchmark risk, which means they are greedier than traditional methods such as linear shrinkage or thresholding rules
Jiang & Zhang (2009)
.

Throughout this paper, admissibility has been a central concern: Good decision rules should not be uniformly dominated by others. Although EB estimators are not necessarily admissible, they approximate the performance of the oracle Bayes procedure, which is itself likely to be admissible. Thus, EB rules inherit much of the oracle’s desirable structure, particularly in large samples. Moreover, as demonstrated by the simulation results in Section
5
, EB rules consistently outperform both the plug-in rule and the James–Stein-type estimator across a wide range of realistic settings, achieving substantial loss reductions, even though they are not necessarily admissible.

4.3.1
Prior Estimation

Following the recent literature, and rather than assuming a specific parametric form for the latent distribution
G
, I estimate the unknown prior nonparametrically using the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator (NPMLE) of
Kiefer & Wolfowitz (1956)
. An NPMLE is any
\hat{G}\in\mathcal{G}
, the set of all probability distributions, that maximizes the marginal log-likelihood of the observations
(\hat{y}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
. Specifically,
\hat{G}
solves

\displaystyle\hat{G}\in\arg\max_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\int\varphi\left(\frac{\hat{y}_{i}-\mu}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\,dG(\mu),

where
\varphi(\cdot)
denotes the probability density function of a standard normal random variable. In other words, the NPMLE searches over all possible distributions
G
to find the one that best explains the observed, noisy income estimates, without imposing restrictive parametric assumptions
Soloff et al. (2025)
.

The resulting optimization problem is infinite-dimensional but convex, with a strictly concave objective and linear constraints. Thanks to this structure, modern interior-point algorithms can efficiently compute an approximate solution
Koenker & Mizera (2014)
. In practice, the likelihood is maximized over a discretized grid of candidate support points. As shown by
Dicker & Zhao (2016)
, a good approximation can be achieved provided that the grid includes at least
\sqrt{n}
support points.

The NPMLE exhibits several properties that make it particularly attractive for shrinkage estimation. First, the estimated prior
\hat{G}
is discrete, supported on at most
n
points
Koenker & Gu (2017)
, which facilitates efficient computation of posterior quantities such as the posterior mean. Second,
\hat{G}
is a consistent estimator of the true prior
G
under mild regularity conditions
Kiefer & Wolfowitz (1956)
. Third,
Polyanskiy & Wu (2020)
show that with high probability, the number of support points selected by the optimization grows only logarithmically with the sample size,
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, despite allowing for arbitrary complexity. This “self-regularization” property eliminates the need for external tuning parameters, allowing the NPMLE to adapt flexibly to the underlying distribution while controlling model complexity automatically.

4.3.2
Feasible Rule

Having estimated the prior distribution nonparametrically using the NPMLE
\hat{G}
, I next construct a feasible decision rule that approximates the oracle Bayes rule. The empirical Bayes (EB) procedure replaces the unknown prior
G
with the estimate
\hat{G}
and sets transfers accordingly.

For each realization of the data
\bm{\hat{y}}=(\hat{y}_{1},\dots,\hat{y}_{n})^{\top}
, define the empirical Bayes decision rule as

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{i}^{\text{EB}}(\bm{\hat{y}};\lambda)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{0,\,z-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right\},\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

with the Lagrange multiplier
\hat{\lambda}^{*}
solving the budget constraint:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}^{*}=\inf\left\{\lambda\geq 0\;:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{t}_{i}^{\text{EB}}(\bm{\hat{y}};\lambda)\leq B\right\}.

The final EB allocation is then given by

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{\tau}}^{\text{EB}}(\bm{\hat{y}})=\left(\hat{t}_{1}^{\text{EB}}(\bm{\hat{y}};\hat{\lambda}^{*}),\dots,\hat{t}_{n}^{\text{EB}}(\bm{\hat{y}};\hat{\lambda}^{*})\right)^{\top}.

This rule mirrors the structure of the oracle Bayes rule but substitutes the unknown posterior mean
\mathbb{E}_{G}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]
with its empirical counterpart under
\hat{G}
. The term
\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}]
shrinks each noisy estimate toward more plausible values inferred from the observed data, correcting for estimation uncertainty in a fully data-driven way. As the sample size increases,
\hat{G}
consistently approximates
G
, so the EB decision rule
\bm{\hat{\tau}}^{\text{EB}}
converges to the oracle rule
\bm{\tau}^{*}
.

To further illustrate the construction and advantages of the feasible rule, the next two remarks provide concrete examples and conceptual comparisons. First, the following example shows how the EB rule operates in a concrete parametric setting:

Remark 4.1

Truncated Normal.
Assume homoskedastic noise
\sigma_{i}=1
for all
i=1,\dots,n
, and let the prior
G
for the conditional expectation
\mu_{i}
be a left-truncated normal distribution with mean
\alpha
and variance
\gamma^{2}
, supported on
[0,\infty)
. Its density is

\displaystyle g(\mu)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\varphi\left(\frac{\mu-\alpha}{\gamma}\right)}{1-\Phi\left(-\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)},

where
\varphi
and
\Phi
denote the standard normal probability density and cumulative distribution functions, respectively.

Ignoring the truncation, standard normal–normal conjugacy yields the posterior mean and variance:

\displaystyle\delta(\hat{y}_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma^{2}+1}\right)\hat{y}_{i}+\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}+1}\right)\alpha=\alpha+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma^{2}+1}(\hat{y}_{i}-\alpha),

\displaystyle\nu^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\gamma^{2}+1}.

Since the prior is truncated, the posterior distribution
\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i}
is also a left-truncated normal. Its posterior mean is

\displaystyle m(\hat{y}_{i})=\delta(\hat{y}_{i})+\nu\cdot\kappa\left(\frac{-\delta(\hat{y}_{i})}{\nu}\right),\quad\text{where }\kappa(x)=\frac{\varphi(x)}{1-\Phi(x)}.

By Proposition
4
, the Bayes-optimal transfer is given by

\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{*}=\max\left\{0,z-m(\hat{y}_{i})-\frac{\lambda^{*}}{2}\right\},

(6)

where
\lambda^{*}
is the Lagrange multiplier ensuring feasibility.

The EB approach approximates (
6
) by estimating the prior parameters using maximum likelihood or the method of moments:

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{y}_{i},\quad\hat{\gamma}^{2}=\max\left\{0,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha})^{2}-1\right\},\quad\hat{\nu}^{2}=\frac{\hat{\gamma}^{2}}{\hat{\gamma}^{2}+1}.

Define the empirical shrinkage term and posterior mean:

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}(\hat{y}_{i})

\displaystyle=\hat{\alpha}+\frac{\hat{\gamma}^{2}}{\hat{\gamma}^{2}+1}(\hat{y}_{i}-\hat{\alpha}),

\displaystyle\hat{m}(\hat{y}_{i})

\displaystyle=\hat{\delta}(\hat{y}_{i})+\hat{\nu}\cdot\kappa\left(\frac{-\hat{\delta}(\hat{y}_{i})}{\hat{\nu}}\right),

and compute the empirical Bayes rule:

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}_{i}^{*}=\max\left\{0,z-\hat{m}(\hat{y}_{i})-\frac{\hat{\lambda}^{*}}{2}\right\}.

Thus, the optimal transfer is a decreasing function of the posterior mean, which combines a linear shrinkage toward the prior mean
\alpha
and an adjustment term due to the truncation of
G
. Assuming a Gaussian (nontruncated) prior or using a James–Stein-type rule leads to the elegant closed-form expression
\hat{\delta}(\hat{y}_{i})
, which is analytically convenient but restrictive. Notably, such approaches would omit the truncation adjustment, which can seriously affect the performance of the rule.

Second, the next remark clarifies the conceptual differences between the EB and standard machine learning approaches.

Remark 4.2

Nonparametric EB vs. Machine Learning.
This paper adopts a nonparametric EB (NPEB) approach to shrink noisy income estimates prior to the allocation of transfers. While shrinkage also underlies standard regularization techniques in machine learning, such as ridge and lasso regression, these approaches rely on parametric priors, with normal in the case of ridge and Laplace in the case of lasso, which impose a rigid structure on the shrinkage pattern
Walters (2024)
. In contrast, NPEB estimates the prior distribution flexibly from the data, allowing the degree and shape of shrinkage to be determined by the data. This data-adaptive property is especially useful in environments, such as poverty targeting, where the true distribution of latent parameters is unknown and potentially far from the assumptions embedded in parametric shrinkage.

As shown in
Abadie & Kasy (2019)
, NPEB dominates parametric alternatives when the number of parameters is large, there is no sparsity, and the true parameter distribution is highly dispersed. These are precisely the conditions that characterize the poverty targeting problem. In poverty targeting, income is a continuous variable that exhibits wide dispersion across households, with few, if any, observations concentrated near zero. Moreover, the planner typically works with large administrative datasets, ensuring a high-dimensional setting where the advantages of NPEB become most pronounced. Thus, both the theoretical properties and the simulation evidence from Abadie and Kasy support the use of NPEB methods in this context.

Other machine learning methods such as regression trees, random forests, and XGBoost can improve predictive accuracy but offer little guidance on how to account for estimation uncertainty in subsequent allocation decisions. Similarly to the plug-in rule, they treat point estimates as if they were known with certainty, without adjusting for the noise inherent in the income predictions. As a result, they suffer from the same fundamental limitation: an inability to optimally balance the trade-off between bias and variance in allocations of scarce resources under uncertainty. In contrast, the EB approach explicitly incorporates this uncertainty into the decision rule, producing better targeting outcomes.

4.4
Statistical Properties

In this subsection, I discuss the regret rates associated with the nonsmooth transformation of the posterior mean used to compute the feasible rule.

4.4.1
Assumptions

Following
Jiang (2020)
, I allow
\hat{G}
to be an approximate solution to the NPMLE problem.

Assumption 3
(Approximate Solution)
Let
f_{G}(\hat{y}_{i})=\int\varphi\left(\frac{\hat{y}_{i}-\mu}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\,dG(\mu)
denote the marginal density of
\hat{y}_{i}
induced by a distribution
G
over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
\varphi(\cdot)
is the standard normal probability density function.
The NPMLE
\hat{G}
satisfies

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\hat{G}}(\hat{y}_{i})\geq q_{n}\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{G}(\hat{y}_{i}),

where
\mathcal{G}
is the family of all distribution functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
q_{n}=(e\sqrt{2\pi}/n^{2})\land 1
.

Assumption 3 allows the EB estimator
\hat{G}
to approximately maximize the marginal likelihood instead of requiring exact maximization. Specifically, it demands that the likelihood achieved by
\hat{G}
be within a factor
q_{n}
of the global maximum likelihood across all possible priors, where
q_{n}
shrinks slowly at the rate
1/n^{2}
as the sample size grows. This condition serves two purposes. First, it responds to the computational challenges of solving an infinite-dimensional optimization problem exactly, especially as
n
becomes large. Second, it guarantees that the approximation error is sufficiently small to preserve the key statistical properties of
\hat{G}
. In particular, by allowing a small slack in optimization, the assumption enables practical computation while ensuring that
\hat{G}
remains “good enough” for the EB procedure to attain near-optimal regret rates.

I also impose a moment condition on
G
.

Assumption 4
(Moment Condition)
Let
\mathcal{M}_{p}(G)=\left\{\int|y|^{p}\,dG(y)\right\}^{1/p}
denote the
p
-th absolute moment of the prior distribution
G
.
The prior distribution
G
satisfies
\mathcal{M}_{p}(G)=\mathcal{O}(1)
for some
p\geq 2
.

Assumption 4 imposes a mild regularity condition on the true prior
G
, requiring that its
p
-th absolute moment remain uniformly bounded as the sample size increases. This assumption rules out extremely heavy-tailed priors, such as the Cauchy distribution or Pareto distributions with shape parameters less than
p
, since these would lead to infinite moments. In contrast to
Jiang (2020)
, who assumes only the existence of the first absolute moment (i.e.,
p\geq 1
), I impose a slightly stronger condition by requiring
p\geq 2
, which ensures that the prior
G
has finite variance.

4.4.2
Bayes Regret

In evaluating the performance of EB procedures, it is natural to compare them to the oracle Bayes rule, which has access to the true prior distribution
G
. To do so, I adopt the notion of
Bayes regret
, which quantifies the additional expected loss incurred by using the EB rule
\hat{\tau}^{*}
instead of the oracle Bayes rule
\tau^{*}
. This regret reflects the cost of estimating the prior rather than knowing it.

To formalize this comparison, I introduce the following notation. Let
P_{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot)
denote the euclidean projection onto the action space
\mathcal{A}
. Define the oracle posterior mean as
m_{i}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{G}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}]
and the EB posterior mean as
\hat{m}_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{G}}[\mu_{i}\mid\hat{y}_{i},\sigma_{i}]
. The corresponding transfer rules are given by:

\tau_{i}^{*}=P_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{1}z-\mathbf{m}^{*})_{i},\quad\hat{\tau}_{i}=P_{\mathcal{A}}(\bm{1}z-\bm{\hat{m}})_{i}.

Following
Soloff et al. (2025)
and
Chen (2023)
, I define the Bayes regret as:

\text{Bayes Regret}(\bm{\hat{\tau}},\bm{\tau}^{*})=\mathbb{E}\left[L(\bm{\hat{\tau}},\bm{\mu})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[L(\bm{\tau}^{*},\bm{\mu})\right],

where
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is the loss function and the expectation is taken over both the prior draw
\bm{\mu}\sim G
and the observed data
\hat{\bm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mu},\bm{\sigma}
, conditional on the known noise levels
\bm{\sigma}
.

Intuitively, the Bayes regret isolates the performance gap due solely to the use of an estimated prior. Clearly, a Bayes regret value close to zero indicates that the EB rule successfully mimics the oracle rule, yielding near-optimal performance even without full knowledge of
G
.

Using the framework outlined above, the following proposition establishes a finite-sample upper bound on the regret incurred by the feasible EB rule. The result builds on the convergence rates derived by
Jiang (2020)
for NPMLEs under finite moment conditions.

Proposition 5

(
Regret Rates
):

Let Assumptions 1–4 hold and fix
p>0
. Then there exist universal constants
M_{0},C_{0}>0
(independent of
n
) such that for all
n
satisfying
\log n>1/p
,

\displaystyle\text{Bayes Regret }(\bm{\hat{\tau}},\bm{\tau^{*}})\leq C_{0}C_{n}+M_{0}C_{n}n^{-p/(2+2p)}(\log n)^{(7p+8)/(4+4p)}\text{.}

Because
C_{0}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
C_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
, the inequality implies

\displaystyle\text{Bayes Regret }(\bm{\hat{\tau}},\bm{\tau^{*}})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-p/(2+2p)}(\log n)^{(7p+8)/(4+4p)})+\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})\text{.}

Hence the Bayes regret of the empirical‑Bayes projection estimator converges to zero, and its dominant asymptotic rate is
n^{-p/(2+2p)}(\log n)^{(7p+8)/(4+4p)}
.

The main idea behind the proof is that projecting a vector onto the simplex cannot increase the estimation error. In other words, this projection step is stable: It preserves or reduces differences between the estimated and true quantities. This property ensures that any error from estimating the underlying means is not made worse by enforcing feasibility. As a result, the analysis can focus entirely on bounding the error in the initial plug-in estimates, without worrying about additional complications introduced by the projection. This allows the regret of the empirical-Bayes targeting rule to inherit the same convergence rates as the unconstrained posterior mean estimator.

Proposition
5
formalizes this result. For any fixed value of the tail parameter
p>0
, the Bayes regret of the empirical-Bayes projection rule converges to zero and admits a non-asymptotic bound with two distinct components: one decaying at the familiar
n^{-1/2}
parametric rate, and a second term that reflects the difficulty of learning the prior distribution. The latter dominates when the prior is heavy-tailed and improves with smoother priors. In particular, when the prior has finite second moments (i.e.,
p=2
), the regret rate is approximately
n^{-1/3}
up to logarithmic corrections. As the prior becomes smoother, meaning it has lighter tails, the rate accelerates and approaches the ideal
n^{-1/2}
as
p
tends to infinity.

This result highlights two key insights. First, even without knowing the true prior, the empirical-Bayes regret decays at a known rate, determined by how difficult the prior is to learn. Second, the projection step, which ensures that the resulting targeting vector is feasible (e.g., respects the budget and nonnegativity constraints), does not degrade performance. Its non-expansiveness guarantees that estimation error is not amplified in the process.

In short, Proposition
5
shows that the empirical-Bayes projection rule delivers theoretically sound performance guarantees. It adapts to the unknown prior distribution, enforces feasibility, and matches the convergence rates for nonparametric posterior mean estimation.

5
Simulation Evidence

I conduct two simulation studies to develop a richer quantitative understanding of the performance of different decision rules. First, I design a data-generating process calibrated to consumption data from nine African countries: Burkina Faso, Ethiopia, Ghana, Malawi, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Tanzania, and Uganda, as collected by

5.2
Mexico

5.2.1
Setup

This section builds directly on the simulation framework proposed by
Corral et al. (2025)
, who use the 2015 Mexican Inter-Censal Survey to construct a high-quality pseudo-census and evaluate poverty mapping methods under repeated sampling. Following their approach, I use the same pseudo-census dataset, comprising approximately 3.9 million households, and adopt their sampling procedure to generate 500 survey samples. Each sample is drawn using a two-stage clustered design, with primary sampling units (PSUs) selected with probability proportional to size and 10 households drawn per selected PSU.

While I replicate the underlying data and sampling structure of
Corral et al. (2025)
, my focus diverges from theirs. Rather than predicting poverty status as a binary indicator, I focus on predicting
true household income
, treating income as a continuous outcome. For each of the 500 samples, I estimate a predictive model where the dependent variable is the observed income in the census. The predictors include a rich set of household-, PSU-, and municipality-level covariates. I implement two predictive models: an OLS regression and an XGBoost model trained via Bayesian hyperparameter optimization. The OLS model is estimated with weighted least squares with sampling weights from the survey, and I compute heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC1) standard errors for all coefficients. These variances are used to construct standard errors for out-of-sample predictions. For the XGBoost model, I use the same set of covariates and allow for hyperparameter tuning.

After training each model on the 500 samples, I generate income predictions for all households in the full census. For the OLS model, I assume the prediction errors are approximately unbiased and normally distributed, which permits the use of an EB procedure to adjust the estimates via posterior means. In contrast, XGBoost predictions are treated as fixed point estimates without any distributional assumptions and thus are not subjected to posterior correction.

I then implement three targeting strategies based on these estimates. The first is a plug-in rule using the raw OLS predictions. The second applies the EB procedure to the OLS estimates and allocates transfers based on the resulting posterior means. The third uses the XGBoost predictions directly as the basis for the transfer decisions. All strategies are evaluated within the poverty alleviation framework of
Corral et al. (2025)
, in which the poverty line is defined as the 25th percentile of the true income distribution in the census and the total transfer budget is given by the total poverty gap.

6
Conclusion

The application here of shrinkage and EB methods for allocation of antipoverty budgets has demonstrated the considerable promise of these approaches for addressing the challenges posed by noisy income measurements. My analysis establishes several key theoretical and practical advances in this domain. First, I prove the inadmissibility of traditional plug-in approaches relative to James–Stein-type estimators, providing a theoretical foundation for improved allocation methods. Furthermore, I develop a nonsmooth empirical Bayes rule that achieves the same Bayes regret bound as the posterior mean. Simulation results confirm these theoretical insights, showing that the EB approach significantly outperforms conventional plug-in estimators across various scenarios.

These methodological advances yield meaningful policy implications. The framework provides policymakers with a straightforward yet effective method for targeting subsidies to the poorest households, addressing a practical and potentially high-impact policy issue. This enhancement in targeting efficiency not only allows more judicious use of limited antipoverty resources but also potentially increases the overall impact of poverty alleviation programs.

The broader implications of this work extend beyond poverty alleviation. The setup and methods presented here can be readily adapted to other policy domains where decision-makers must contend with noisy measurements, such as in optimizing healthcare interventions with imperfect patient data, targeting educational resources based on school or teacher value added, or allocating resources for crime prevention based on noisy crime statistics. As policymakers increasingly face the challenge of making data-driven decisions with imperfect information, the EB approach offers a theoretically grounded way to improve policy targeting and resource allocation.

1.

Karush-Kuhn-Tucker Conditions. The Lagrangian is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\tau},\lambda,\mu_{1},...,\mu_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i}\Big)^{2}+\lambda\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}-B\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\tau_{i}\text{. }

Since the problem is strictly convex and has affine constraints, the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for optimality. I check the KKT conditions below:

•

Stacionarity Conditions
For each
i
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\tau_{i}}

\displaystyle=-2(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i})+\lambda-\xi_{i}=0

(SC)

•

Primal feasibility

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\leq B\text{, }\tau_{i}\geq 0,\forall i

(PF)

•

Dual feasibility

\displaystyle\lambda\geq 0\text{, }\xi_{i}\geq 0,\forall i

(DF)

•

Complementary slackness

\displaystyle\lambda\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}-B\Big)=0\text{, }\xi_{i}\tau_{i}=0,\forall i

(CS)

From (SC)-(CS), for each
i
, if
\tau_{i}>0
, then
\xi_{i}=0
and
\lambda=2(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i})
. Instead, if
\tau_{i}=0
, then
\xi_{i}\geq 0
and
\lambda\geq 2(z-y_{i})
. Hence, for all
i
,
\tau_{i}^{\star}=\max\{0,z-y_{i}-\lambda/2\}
. Again, since the objective is strictly convex in
\bm{\tau}
and the constraints are linear, the optimum is unique.

2.

Projection Onto Simplex. By
Boyd & Vandenberghe (2004)
, since the feasible set is closed and convex, then the unique minimizer is precisely the orthogonal projection of the vector
\bm{1}z-\bm{y}
onto that set. Furthermore, the authors show that the orthogonality (normal-cone) condition satisfied by the projection is equivalent to the KKT stationarity and complementary slackness constraints derived above. Therefore, the solution given by the KKT system must be the same as the orthogonal projection.

3.

Progressive “Leveling Up” Algorithm.
Gangopadhyay & Subramanian (1992)
shows, using a proof by contradiction based on permissible transfers, that the algorithm leads to the optimal transfer schedule. I verify below that the algorithm is exactly the solution given by the KKT/projection approach. I want to show that the result of the algorithm,
\tau_{i}^{A}
, is equivalent to the solution found by KKT,
\tau_{i}^{\star}
.

Step 1
:

Clearly,
\tau_{i}^{A}\geq 0
by construction (the poorest get “leveled up” to at least their current income) and the total sum of
\tau_{i}^{A}
equals
B
, so it is a feasible point.

Step 2
:

In terms of stationarity, for
i\leq p
,
\tau_{i}^{A}=\underline{y}-y_{i}
, so
\tau_{i}^{A}>0
(assuming strict inequality
\underline{y}>y_{i}
). By KKT complementary slackness,
\mu_{i}=0
for all
i\leq p
. Then stationarity requires:

\displaystyle-2\Big(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i}^{A}\Big)+\lambda=0\Rightarrow\lambda=2\Big(z-y_{i}-\tau_{i}^{A}\Big)=2\Big(z-y_{i}-\underline{y}+y_{i}\Big)=2(z-\underline{y}).

For
i>p
,
\tau_{i}^{A}=0
. By stationarity and dual feasibility, it must be that

\displaystyle\mu_{i}=\lambda-2(z-y_{i})\geq 0\Rightarrow\lambda\geq 2(z-y_{i}).

Since for the
i\leq p
,
\lambda=2(z-\underline{y})
,

\displaystyle 2(z-\underline{y})\geq 2(z-y_{i})\iff y_{i}\geq\underline{y}.

Thus, all households not receiving a transfer are those that began with higher income than
\underline{y}
.

Hence, I have constructed a single
\lambda=2(z-\underline{y})
and
\mu_{i}\geq 0
that satisfy the KKT conditions with

QED

. Since
\hat{m}_{i}-m_{i}^{*}
is measurable with respect to the data, the law of iterated expectations gives the orthogonality
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{m}_{i}-m_{i}^{*})(m_{i}^{*}-\mu_{i})]=0
. Therefore, summing over
i
and diving by
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{\hat{m}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{\hat{m}}-\bm{m^{*}}||^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{m^{*}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}.

Since the second term on the right-hand side is non-negative,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{\hat{m}}-\bm{m^{*}}||^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{\hat{m}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}.

Since
Jiang (2020)
, Theorem 1 (eq. 2.4), states that for a universal constant
M_{0}
and all
\log n>1/p
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{\hat{m}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{m^{*}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}}\leq M_{0}n^{-p/(2+2p)}(\log n)^{(7p+8)/(4+4p)}.

Since the posterior mean minimizes conditional MSE,
\mathbb{E}[(m_{i}^{*}-\mu_{i})^{2}]\leq\sigma_{i}^{2}
, then, by Assumption 1,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{m^{*}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}^{2}.

So,
\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{m^{*}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}}\leq\sigma_{\max}=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}||\bm{\hat{m}}-\bm{\mu}||^{2}}\leq\sigma_{\max}+M_{0}n^{-p/(2+2p)}(\log n)^{(7p+8)/(4+4p)}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[L(\bm{\hat{\tau}},\bm{\theta})-L(\bm{\tau^{*}},\bm{\theta})\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\Big[\sigma_{\max}+M_{0}n^{-p/(2+2p)}(\log n)^{(7p+8)/(4+4p)}\Big]C_{n}.

QED
.

B.1
Geometric Decomposition of the Projection Map

The plug-in estimator
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
is defined as the projection of
X
onto the convex polyhedron
\mathcal{A}=\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\leq B\}
. This set is a truncated
n
-dimensional simplex, and the projection map inherits a rich geometric structure from this constraint set.

We begin by decomposing
\mathcal{A}
into its relative interiors. Let
\mathcal{A}_{0}
denote the interior of the simplex, and let
\mathcal{A}_{j}
, for
j=1,\ldots,m
, denote the relative interiors of the proper faces of
\mathcal{A}
.
18
18

18

A full
n
-dimensional simplex has
2^{n+1}-1
nonempty faces, including the full-dimensional interior and all lower-dimensional faces. Since we label
\mathcal{A}_{0}
as the interior, we index the remaining faces by
j=1,\ldots,m=2^{n+1}-2
.
For each face
\mathcal{A}_{j}
, define the region
D_{j}=(\tau^{\text{plug}})^{-1}(\mathcal{A}_{j})
, which is the pre-image of
\mathcal{A}_{j}
under the projection map. That is,
D_{j}
consists of all points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose projection lands in the relative interior of face
\mathcal{A}_{j}
. These sets
D_{j}
form a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-2}D_{j}.

Within each region
D_{j}
, the plug-in estimator behaves as a linear projection onto the affine span of face
\mathcal{A}_{j}
. That is,

\displaystyle\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n+1}-2}\mathbbm{1}\{X\in D_{j}\}\cdot\tau^{j,\text{plug}}(X),

where
\tau^{j,\text{plug}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the
s_{j}
-dimensional affine subspace spanned by
\mathcal{A}_{j}
, and
s_{j}=\dim(\mathcal{A}_{j})+1
is the number of active (positive) coordinates in that face.

B

B.2
Weak Differentiability of
\tau^{\text{plug}}

Weak differentiability generalizes the classical notion of differentiability by allowing derivatives to be interpreted in a distributional sense. Hence, it allows one to meaningfully talk about derivatives for functions that may not be differentiable in the classical sense due to kinks or discontinuities on sets of measure zero. Intuitively, a function is weakly differentiable if its derivative exists ”on average,” as formalized by an integration-by-parts identity.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set. A function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
weakly differentiable
with respect to variable
x_{i}
if there exists a function
v\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
such that the following integration-by-parts identity holds:

\int_{\Omega}u(x)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}v(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),

where
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is the space of smooth functions with compact support, and
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
denotes the space of functions that are integrable on every compact subset of
\Omega
.

We now prove that each component
\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}
of the plug-in estimator is weakly differentiable.

Step 1: Lipschitz continuity implies local integrability.

The estimator
\tau^{\text{plug}}
is defined as a projection onto a convex set, and projections onto closed convex sets in Euclidean space are non-expansive and Lipschitz continuous. In particular,
\tau^{\text{plug}}
is constructed from max and affine functions and is known to be Lipschitz. Therefore, each component
\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}
is locally integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Step 2: Differentiability within each region.

Let
\mathcal{K}(X)=\{i:X_{i}>\lambda^{*}(X)\}
denote the active set where
\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}(X)>0
, and let
s=|\mathcal{K}(X)|
. Fix a region where
\mathcal{K}(X)
remains constant; define

\Omega_{\mathcal{K}}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathcal{K}(X)=\mathcal{K}\}.

Within
\Omega_{\mathcal{K}}
, the projection is affine. More precisely, for
i\in\mathcal{K}
,

\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}(X)=X_{i}-\lambda^{*}(X),\quad\text{where }\lambda^{*}(X)=\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}X_{i}-B}{s}.

Differentiating this expression with respect to each component
X_{l}
, we obtain:

\frac{\partial\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}}{\partial X_{l}}=\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{s}&\text{if }i=l\in\mathcal{K},\\
-\frac{1}{s}&\text{if }i\neq l\in\mathcal{K},\\
0&\text{if }i\notin\mathcal{K}.\end{cases}

These partial derivatives are constant within
\Omega_{\mathcal{K}}
, hence they are measurable and bounded. Thus, they are integrable over any compact subset.

Step 3: Boundaries and measure zero sets.

The boundaries where
\mathcal{K}(X)
changes are hyperplanes defined by
X_{i}=\lambda^{*}(X)
. These boundaries are unions of lower-dimensional affine subspaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and therefore have Lebesgue measure zero. Since classical differentiability holds on a full-measure subset, and
\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}
is locally integrable and its weak partials are integrable, weak differentiability follows
Ziemer & Ziemer (1989)
;
Bressan (2012)
.

Lemma 1
(Weak Differentiability Plug-in Rule)

Each component
\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}(X)
of the plug-in estimator is weakly differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

B.3 Weak Differentiability and Divergence of
g(X)

Let
g(X):=\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)/\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}
. This normalized direction vector plays a central role in the Stein-type shrinkage estimator. In this section, we establish that
g(X)
is weakly differentiable and derive an explicit expression for its divergence.

Step 1: Domain of
g
.

Observe that
g(X)
is only undefined at points
X
where
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)=0
, which occurs only if
X_{i}\leq\lambda^{*}(X)
for all
i
. Since
\tau^{\text{plug}}
satisfies
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}=B>0
, this situation occurs on a measure-zero subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
g(X)
is well-defined almost everywhere.

Step 2: Quotient rule for weak derivatives.

Within any region
\Omega_{\mathcal{K}}
where the active set
\mathcal{K}
is fixed, both
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
and its partial derivatives
\partial\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}/\partial X_{j}
are constant. The function
\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}
is a smooth, positive function on
\Omega_{\mathcal{K}}
, hence differentiable. Using the quotient rule for derivatives of vector-valued functions, we obtain:

\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial X_{j}}=\frac{1}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}\|^{2}}\cdot\frac{\partial\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}}{\partial X_{j}}-\frac{2\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}\|^{4}}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tau_{k}^{\text{plug}}\cdot\frac{\partial\tau_{k}^{\text{plug}}}{\partial X_{j}}.

Step 3: Computing divergence.

Let
s
denote the number of positive entries in
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)
, i.e.,
s=|\mathcal{K}|
. Recall from B.2 that, for
i\in\mathcal{K}
, we have
\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}=X_{i}-\lambda^{*}
, and
\partial\tau_{i}^{\text{plug}}/\partial X_{j}
is constant. The divergence of
g(X)
, defined as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial g_{i}/\partial X_{i}
, becomes:

\text{div }g(X)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}\left[\frac{1-1/s}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}\|^{2}}-\frac{2\tau_{i}^{2}}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}\|^{4}}+\frac{2\tau_{i}B}{s\cdot\|\tau^{\text{plug}}\|^{4}}\right].

After simplification, this yields:

\text{div }g(X)=\frac{s-3}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}}+\frac{2B^{2}}{s\cdot\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{4}},

where
s=|\mathcal{K}(X)|
.

Lemma 2
(Weak Differentiability and Divergence of
g(X)
)

The function
g(X)=\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)/\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}
is weakly differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and its divergence is given by:

\text{div }g(X)=\frac{s-3}{\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{2}}+\frac{2B^{2}}{s\cdot\|\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\|^{4}},

for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\tau^{\text{plug}}(X)\neq 0
. The divergence is strictly positive when
s\geq 3
.
