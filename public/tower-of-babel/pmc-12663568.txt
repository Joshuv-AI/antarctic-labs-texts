Gap-Filling for Daily Latent Heat Flux Observations with the Full-factorial method at Global Flux Sites
Authors: Xiaowei Wang, Fujiao Tang, Yazhen Jiang, Yunsheng Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Latent heat flux (LE) plays a crucial role in the water-energy cycle. Eddy covariance (EC) measurements of LE frequently suffer from data gaps due to weather or equipment malfunctions. This study presents a novel framework combining median-adjusted full-factorial and iterative methods to fill LE data gaps. The findings indicate that the framework exhibits stable gap-filling across land surfaces, showing RMSEs of 14.69–19.51 W/m2 for isolated gaps and averaging 24.41 W/m2 for long-term missing periods (5–50 days). Gap-filled LE data closely matched temporal dynamics and fluctuations of adjacent measurements. Based on gap-filled LE data, all sites demonstrated strong correlations between available energy and turbulent fluxes, with RMSE values ranging 28.41–38.01 W/m2 and achieving an average energy closure of 0.79 across sites. Compared to ERA5-Land and GLEAM products, the gap-filled data significantly outperformed both LE products. Overall, the filled LE data are of high quality and significantly improve the usability of observed data. The complete time-series data from 265 sites (1509 site-years) are valuable for LE model validation and water demand assessments.

Background & Summary

Latent heat flux (LE) describes the phase transition of water from liquid to gas at the surface, and plays a central role in linking the water, energy and carbon cycles1,2. On an annual average basis over global land surfaces, LE, representing energy consumed by evapotranspiration, accounts for over 60% of incident precipitation returned to the atmosphere as water vapor and consumes approximately 50% of net surface radiation3,4. Driven primarily by plant transpiration regulated through stomatal activity, LE is tightly integrated with the carbon cycle because these same stomatal pores also control CO2 uptake for photosynthesis. This integration underpins LE’s critical influence on weather, agricultural water management, and ecosystem health5–8. Therefore, long-term and effective observations of LE are crucial for advancing our understanding of dynamics in water budget, energy balance, and carbon cycle9,10.

A plethora of LE products has been developed, each of which is grounded in distinct algorithms that include empirical formulas11,12, physical models13,14, or machine learning-based methods15. These products address various parameterization issues such as vegetation cover, soil moisture, and atmospheric conditions16–18. Regional and global assessments have unveiled significant disparities among these LE products19–22, highlighting the absence of a universally accepted standard for precise in LE estimation23–28. As a result, integrating and using reliable, measured LE data for comparative validation is still essential for enhancing the accuracy and credibility of LE products.

The eddy covariance (EC) method, which quantifies LE by measuring the covariance between vertical wind speed and water vapor mixing ratio over a defined time period, is regarded as one of the most direct and reliable techniques for LE measurement29. Major global EC flux networks including FLUXNET, AmeriFlux, OzFlux, EuroFlux, AsiaFlux, and ChinaFlux systematically gather LE across more than 1,000 sites characterized by diverse land cover types30. Nevertheless, the continuity of these observations is frequently interrupted by several factors including instrument malfunctions, severe meteorological conditions, and maintenance disruptions. As a result, the complete coverage of LE data obtained from most flux network sites typically ranges between 65% to 75%, and this issue has been extensively documented31,32. For instance, at an EC site, named as Joseph W. Jones Ecological Research Center (JJERC) and located on the southeastern United States coastal plain, the average nighttime data gap rate is 60%33. At the Walker Branch Watershed AmeriFlux site, approximately 22% of the half-hour daytime LE measurements exhibit data gaps34, while this figure rises to 35% at the Duke site35, and remains 20% at the Niwot Ridge site36. These data gaps hinder the detection of shifts in LE, precipitation, and land water availability37, significantly affecting the accuracy of water resource management and hydrological models38,39, and posing challenges for scientists in precisely assessing the impacts of climate change on the water and carbon cycles40,41. Several methods have been developed to fill gaps in EC measured LE, enabling the acquisition of extended and continuous observational datasets. These methods mainly include the look-up table32, the statistical filter42, the marginal distribution sampling43, the constant evaporative fraction (EF)44, the mean diurnal variation (MDV)45, the marginal distribution sampling (MDS)46,47, and the machine learning-based models48. Although exiting methods offer viable solutions for filling LE gaps, most lack a robust physical foundation and largely rely on the selection of specific inputs or possess complex model structures. For instance, the look-up table method is highly dependent on extensive historical data, which limits its applicability for addressing long-term data gaps. The MDS method, while effective, necessitates stable data distributions and is sensitive to meteorological similarities49. The EF method is notably influenced by climatic conditions and vegetation types, thereby requiring localized calibration for accurate implementation50. Similarly, the MDV method is inadequate for long-term gap filling and tends to introduce significant bias under extreme weather conditions51. Machine learning-based approaches, though promising, require large datasets for initial training and exhibit region-specific variations in accuracy, primarily due to differences in input features such as meteorological conditions and vegetation characteristics1 (For details on these methods, including equations, limitations, and inputs, see Table S1). Furthermore, the required input data for these methods are often difficult to obtain, which compromises their applicability and reduces their spatiotemporal scalability. For instance, the FLUXNET dataset’s LE observations undergo gap-filling as part of its standard processing pipeline using the MDS method, based on the assumption that LE remains consistent under similar meteorological conditions over short intervals52. Nonetheless, the validity of this method may be compromised by sudden shifts in soil moisture, even under stable meteorological conditions53.

Given the constraints and drawbacks of these methods, there exists a notable deficiency in the availability of comprehensive gap-filled LE datasets; to our knowledge, only one such dataset, published by Winck et al.54, has been identified. Recently, Jiang et al.55 proposed a full-factorial method based on the physical mechanism of the Penman-Monteith equation, incorporating a full range of influential factors of the overall LE from atmosphere, vegetation and soil. The full-factorial method surpasses various other gap-filling methods because it fully incorporates influential factors on LE. Additionally, this method demonstrates superior accuracy in gap-filling at the daily scale. Within a random search window, for missing data lengths ranging from 1 to 6 days, it achieves a mean bias error (MBE) range of 2.0 W/m2~4.9 W/m2 and a root mean squared error (RMSE) range of 19.9 W/m2~38.6 W/m2.

Although theoretically validated for high accuracy, this full-factorial method remains unimplemented for generating gap-free LE time series at global flux tower sites. Aiming to compile a comprehensive gap-filled LE dataset, we apply this method after median-adjustment to address the LE data gaps across global flux networks, thereby facilitating the acquisition of high-frequency EC time series data for LE. This study encompasses the following primary objectives: (1) processing of site data from multiple source flux networks at global scale; (2) gap-filling of LE at each site with the median-adjusted full-factorial method; and (3) verification analysis of the filled LE data. The gap-filled LE measurements are expected to become valuable for enhancing the accuracy and credibility of LE data for applications related to water cycle and energy balance.

This full-factorial method has been appropriately adjusted in its application for the following reasons: (1) at certain points during LE measurements, the values of Rn and G can be very close to each other, leading to anomalous LEgap estimates when Eq. 1 is directly applied; (2) based on accuracy evaluation results from Jiang et al.55, when artificial gaps of 1 to 6 days were introduced into a continuous seven-day period, the data fill accuracy showed a significant decline when the gap duration exceeded two days from the nearest actual observation. The adjustment process is as follows (Eq. 9): within a continuous seven-day window, where at least five days LE measurements are available, the LEgap is calculated individually based on these measured values, and then the median of all computed values is used for gap-filling. This median-based approach mitigates the impact of anomalies in individual estimates (for more details, see Fig. S1). Gap-filling schedule

To gap-fill LE measurements using the median-adjusted full-factorial method, this study adhered to the following procedure: Data acquisition and pre-processing; Inspection of the median-adjusted full-factorial gap-filling method via artificial gap; Gap-filling actual LE gaps; and Comparison and evaluation of the gap-filled LE data.

Data acquisition and pre-processing

Acquisition and pre-processing of LE observations

LE observations were collected from global flux networks and data centers, including AmeriFlux (https://ameriflux.lbl.gov; initiated in 1991; 444 sites with records spanning 1–32 years)56, FLUXNET (https://fluxnet.org; initiated in 1991; over 1000 active and historical sites with time series of 1–22 years)57, EuroFlux (http://www.europe-fluxdata.eu; since 1996; 487 sites), OzFlux (https://ozflux.org.au; since 2001; 34 sites covering 3–22 years)58, ChinaFlux (http://www.chinaflux.org), the National Tibet Plateau Data Center (TPDC, https://data.tpdc.ac.cn), and the National Cryosphere Desert Data Center (NCDDC, http://www.ncdc.ac.cn; the latter three totaling 79 sites with records from 1 to 23 years, since 2002)59–61. From this resource, we collected half-hourly LE measurements and meteorological data from 212 sites within FLUXNET, 195 sites within AmeriFlux, 172 sites within EuroFlux, 22 sites within OzFlux, and 44 sites across ChinaFlux, TPDC, and NCDDC. These data were subsequently resampled to a daily scale by averaging, subject to the criterion that all 48 half-hourly measurements for a given day were available. Site-level data fusion was applied to reconcile overlapping records of varying lengths. Furthermore, site-years containing continuous gaps longer than 100 days were excluded, resulting in a final curated dataset of daily LE and meteorological variables from 265 sites (1509 site-years).

The dataset integrates a wide range of land-cover types, classified by MODIS IGBP62, and spans major climate regimes according to the Köppen–Geiger system63 (Table S2). The sites encompass 107 forests (DBF/DNF/EBF/ENF/MF), 27 shrublands (CSH/OSH), 73 grasslands (GRA/SAV/WSA), 34 croplands (CRO/CVM), 23 wetlands (WET), and 1 barren site (BAR). Climate zone representation includes: 105 sites in Temperate and Subarctic climates (Dfa, Dfb, Dfc, Dsb, Dwa, Dwb), 99 in Subtropical climates (Cfa, Cfb, Cfc, Csa, Csb, Cwa), 40 in Dry climates (BSh, BSk, BWh, BWk), 16 in Tropical climates (Af, Am, Aw), and 5 in Tundra climates (ET). Acquisition and pre-processing of reanalysis meteorological data

Meteorological reanalysis data were obtained from the land component of the Fifth Generation of European Reanalysis (ERA5-Land, https://www.ecmwf.int/en/era5-land)64, the Global Land Data Assimilation System (GLDAS, https://ldas.gsfc.nasa.gov/gldas/)65, and the Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications Version 2 (MERRA-2, https://gmao.gsfc.nasa.gov/reanalysis/MERRA-2/)66 (Table 2). Daily data from ERA5-Land and GLDAS were spatially resampled to a 0.01° grid via cubic convolution interpolation. For MERRA-2, provided at hourly resolution, we first spatially interpolated to the same 0.01° grid and then temporally aggregated to daily resolution by averaging. Daily ERA5-Land data were used to fill gaps in station measurements of Ta, PA, WS, RH, VPD, and Rn—with WS, RH, VPD, and Rn derived algorithmically as detailed in Table S3. To evaluate the accuracy of the resampled reanalysis products, 10% of the site’s observational data were randomly selected for validation (Fig. 2). The results demonstrated strong agreement between the reanalysis-based estimates and observations for Ta, RH, VPD, PA, and Rn, with an average coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.92. Slightly lower R2 values were observed for WS (0.58) and G (0.56). Latent heat flux products for intercomparison

Daily LE products from the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model (GLEAM) and the ERA5-Land are utilized for intercomparison with gap-filled LE. The comparison of LE between GLEAM version 3 and LE flux data yielded average correlation coefficients between 0.78 and 0.8167. With a spatial resolution of 0.25°, it spans from 1980 to 2022 and provides LE on daily, monthly, and annual time scales. ERA5-Land delivers LE products at a spatial resolution of 9 kilometres and a daily temporal resolution. To ensure comparability between the two LE products and the observed LE data at the site scale, cubic convolution interpolation was used to resample their spatial resolution to 0.01°.

Inspection of the median-adjusted full-factorial gap-filling method via artificial gaps

Sensitivity analysis of short and sporadic gaps (Isolated missing days). To assess the method’s accuracy in filling brief and non-consecutive data gaps typical of instrument malfunctions or short-term noise, we randomly removed 1 to 2 individual LE values within continuous seven-day segments of measured LE data. The median-adjusted full-factorial method was applied to fill these artificially created single-day gaps. The filled LE values at the specific gap locations were then compared to the original measured values to calculate error metrics (e.g., RMSE, MAE, R2). Sensitivity analysis of long-term continuous gaps. To evaluate the method’s performance in reconstructing extended periods of missing LE, such as those caused by prolonged sensor failure or maintenance, we retained only the first five days and the last five days of data within each segment of consecutive measured LE days (lengths: 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 60 days). All intermediate days were designated as artificially missing. This created controlled gaps of specific lengths: 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50 days, respectively. The gap-filling method was applied to reconstruct the entire missing interval within each segment. The filled LE values across all artificially missing days were also compared against the original measured LEs for those days to compute comprehensive error metrics. This scheme corresponds to the test windows labelled as LG1-LG4 in Fig. 3.

Gap-filling actual LE gaps with the median-adjusted full-factorial method

For each individual LE gap, we first assessed the availability of valid filling windows (Step 1 in Fig. 3). A valid window was defined as any continuous 7-day period overlapping the gap, containing at least 5 days of observed LE data. If no valid window was identified for a specific gap, the gap was temporarily excluded from processing in the current iteration (Step 2 in Fig. 3). Conversely, for gaps with at least one valid window, the missing LE values were filled using the median-adjusted full-factorial method (Step 3 in Fig. 3). The successfully filled values were then immediately integrated into the dataset, replacing the original missing entries (Step 4 in Fig. 3). Following this update, the dataset was reviewed for remaining gaps (including those previously excluded). If gaps persisted, the entire process (Steps 1–4) was iteratively applied to the updated dataset. Crucially, in subsequent iterations, LE values filled in prior iterations were treated as valid observed data when identifying valid windows. This iterative approach, where newly filled data progressively enables the filling of previously intractable gaps, continued until all missing LE data points were successfully filled (Step 5 in Fig. 3).

Comparison and evaluation of the gap-filled LE data

The filled LE data were then compared with observed LE in terms of long-term trends and dynamic variation ranges. Furthermore, for gap-filled LE data, the Energy Balance Ratio (EBR = (LE + H)/(Rn − G)) was calculated when corresponding H measurements were available. Additionally, multiple evaluation metrics, including MAE, RMSE, Relative Mean Error (RME), Relative Root Mean Squared Error (RRMSE), Correlation Coefficient (R), and Taylor Score (TS)68, were employed to benchmark the filled LE data by intercomparing with two widely used LE products at the site scale. Subsequently, the total scores for each site (Z_Score)69 were normalized after ranking the outcomes of these evaluation metrics to analyze the consistency and deviation between the filled LE and LE products (Fig. 3).

Shrubland demonstrated the highest performance in terms of RMSE (14.69 W/m2) and MAE (8.93 W/m2), while wetlands yielded the optimal R2 value of 0.89 among all land cover types. Long-term continuous gaps scenario

As illustrated in Fig. 6 and Table S4, a decline in data fill accuracy was observed with increasing durations of continuous missing data. This was reflected in increasing RMSE and MAE values, alongside a gradual decrease in R-values. For instance, under forest land cover, as the gap length increased from 5 to 50 days, the RMSE increased from 21.69 W/m2 to 31.23 W/m2, and the MAE rose from 14.18 W/m2 to 19.05 W/m2, while the R-value decreased from 0.82 to 0.71. Despite demonstrating high sensitivity to gap duration, the fill accuracy remained superior to that of the two LE products (ERA5-Land_LE and GLEAM_LE). Across all land cover types, the mean RMSE and MAE of the fill results were reduced by 13.96 W/m2 and 9.64 W/m2, respectively, compared to the corresponding mean values of the two LE products for a 5-day gap duration. For a 50-day gap duration, the corresponding reductions were 5.50 W/m2 and 5.44 W/m2. As shown in Fig. 7, the gap-filling accuracy exhibits certain fluctuations across different seasons and climate zones under all prolonged continuous missing-data scenarios (5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50 days). Overall, the mean RMSE and MAE across all scenarios are 21.53 W/m2 and 14.27 W/m2, respectively, indicating that the proposed gap-filling method maintains high accuracy under diverse seasonal and climatic zones. Notably, the method demonstrates enhanced performance during December to February (DJF) and September to November (SON), with the average RMSE and MAE decreasing to 17.17 W/m2 and 11.34 W/m2, respectively. Beyond error metrics, the R values reveal additional fluctuations in the seasonal performance of the gap-filling method. Under prolonged missing-data scenarios, R values during DJF tended to be relatively low in snow and polar climates, often remaining below 0.40, indicating weaker correspondence between filled and observed temporal variations in winter. Usage Notes

The global flux site dataset, based on gap-filled data using a median-adjusted full-factorial method, provides continuous, multi-site, and long-term daily LE data. Although the median-adjusted full-factorial method used for gap-filling is physically sound, the uncertainty in the reanalysis data products used to fill gaps in site-level meteorological data critically impacts the gap-filling quality. To facilitate potential improvements by researchers in future studies, we designed a sensitivity test to quantify the impact of variations in meteorological input data on LE gap-filling accuracy. Specifically, we selected time periods where all measured variables (Ta, VPD, PA, WS, Rn, G) are available for at least 7 consecutive days. Then, we artificially introduced random gaps in LE, deleting 30% of the measurements. Subsequently, for each gap, we systematically perturbed one meteorological variable at a time (Ta, VPD, PA, WS, Rn, or G) by −30%, −20%, −10%, −5%, +5%, +10%, +20%, or +30%, while holding the other variables constant at their measured values. We then calculated error metrics between the gap-filled LE and the actual measurements for each perturbation scenario. Figure 12 illustrates the changes in gap-filling accuracy of LE under perturbations of each variable relative to the non-perturbed scenario. The perturbations in Rn and Ta exhibited the strongest influence, with RMSE changes ranging from −2.2 to 8.5 W/m2 and MAE changes from −0.4 to 3.6 W/m2. Notably, positive perturbations (+5% to +30%) generally had a more pronounced detrimental effect on accuracy than negative perturbations (−5% to −30%) of the same magnitude. Changes in variables other than Ta had relatively minor effects on the correlation coefficient R. In contrast, decreasing Ta reduced R, and this reduction became more substantial as the magnitude of the negative Ta perturbation increased. Therefore, in subsequent gap-filling efforts for missing site-level meteorological data, particular attention should be paid to the quality of the Ta and Rn data used, as they are critical for ensuring the accuracy of the filled LE data.Fig. 12Sensitivity of gap-filled LE accuracy to meteorological input perturbations.
