Enhancing charge transfer in low-light conditions through the incorporation of carbon nanotubes in carbon-based perovskite solar cells for indoor applications
Authors: Piyapond Makming, Watcharapong Pudkon, Araya Ruengsuk, Kumaree Thongimboon, Thanawat Kanlayapattamapong, Theerapat Arpornrat, Chaowaphat Seriwattanachai, Duangmanee Wongratanaphisan, Pasit Pakawatpanurut, Pongsakorn Kanjanaboos, Pipat Ruankham, Akarin Intaniwet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerovskite solar cells (PSCs) have emerged as a promising photovoltaic technology owing to their high power conversion efficiency (PCE) and low-cost fabrication, making them suitable for both outdoor and indoor applications. Nevertheless, achieving high performance under indoor lighting and ensuring long-term stability remain critical challenges. In this work, we introduce an antisolvent engineering strategy incorporating carbon nanotubes (CNTs) to enhance charge transport and suppress charge recombination. The incorporation of CNTs improves the structural and electrical properties of the perovskite film by increasing surface conductivity, thereby facilitating more efficient charge extraction and faster interfacial charge transfer, while reducing recombination losses. Consequently, the CNT-based devices exhibit an outstanding indoor PCE of 32.63% and a fill factor of 73.60% under 1000 lx LED illumination at ambient conditions (40–50% relative humidity, 25 ± 5 °C). This simple antisolvent engineering approach provides an effective pathway for developing efficient and stable carbon-based PSCs, highlighting their potential for next-generation indoor photovoltaic and self-powered electronic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25516-0.Keywords: Carbon nanotubes, Perovskite solar cells, Indoor light, Efficiency, StabilitySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Organic-inorganic perovskite solar cells (PSCs) are an efficient and cost-effective innovation in solar power generation technology that utilises a photo-absorber based on a perovskite crystal structure. These devices exhibit excellent light absorption and efficient charge transport1, enabling high efficiency in converting light into electricity. PSCs have garnered significant attention due to their potential to replace or supplement silicon-based solar cells in the future2. They can be fabricated using simple processes such as solution coating, which makes them lightweight, flexible3, and suitable for use on a wide range of surfaces. Recently, optimising perovskite thin-film fabrication has enabled power conversion efficiencies (PCE) of up to 26.95%4 under AM 1.5G standard conditions. Beyond outdoor solar applications, PSCs are particularly promising for indoor energy harvesting. Their high efficiency under low-light conditions allows effective conversion of indoor illumination into electricity to power low-energy electronic devices such as Internet of Things (IoT) sensors, wearable electronics, and self-powered systems5. However, indoor applications still face notable limitations due to the spectral mismatch between indoor and outdoor light sources. Specifically, the lower intensity and narrower spectral range of indoor illumination diminish charge-carrier generation, thereby impeding charge extraction and transport. To achieve comparable device performance, it is essential to optimise charge transport pathways and improve the conductivity of interfacial layers. Thus, enhancing charge conductivity remains one of the critical challenges in improving the efficiency of PSCs under indoor lighting6.

Recent research on perovskite indoor photovoltaics has centered on optimizing material composition and device structure for improved performance. For example, Lim et al. found that bromine doping in MAPbI3 enhances crystallinity and suppresses trap states, achieving PCEs over 34% under 1000 lx LED illumination7. He et al. further improved efficiency by applying a comprehensive passivation strategy that combines guanidinium and CH3O-PEABr surface treatments in micrometer-thick films, achieving a record 40.1% indoor efficiency8. Srathongsian et al. later adjusted the Cs and Br ratios in triple-cation perovskites, reducing hysteresis and enhancing the bandgap for indoor lighting, which resulted in stable, high-performance devices9. These advancements highlight the crucial role of compositional engineering and defect passivation in improving indoor perovskite photovoltaics. Nevertheless, despite these achievements, the use of advanced carbon nanomaterials, such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), in indoor perovskite applications remains infrequent. While CNTs have demonstrated benefits as interfacial modifiers outdoors10,11, their full potential to improve charge extraction and device stability indoors remains largely unexplored.

CNTs exist in two forms: single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), composed of a single graphene sheet, and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), composed of multiple concentric graphene sheets. Both structures have attracted considerable attention due to their excellent electrical conductivity, optical properties, tubular nanostructure, high chemical stability, and mechanical strength, making them suitable for applications in optoelectronic devices12,13. Their distinctive properties enable the formation of efficient conductive pathways within the perovskite matrix, thereby facilitating directional carrier transport. Moreover, their intrinsic hydrophobicity helps mitigate moisture-induced degradation, a major obstacle to PSC durability. In addition, their ability to bridge grain boundaries reduces energy barriers at critical interfaces, promoting more efficient charge extraction14. To improve PSC performance, CNTs have been incorporated into various device layers, including the perovskite absorber15–17, electron transport layer (ETL)18, hole transport layer (HTL)19, and electrode20,21. During fabrication, an antisolvent - typically does not dissolve the perovskite precursor- is applied to facilitate the crystallisation of the perovskite film during the spin-coating process. Common antisolvents include toluene22, chlorobenzene (CB)23, ethyl acetate24, and isopropanol (IPA)25,26. Owing to their advantageous properties, CNTs have emerged as promising additives for improving charge-transfer efficiency in PSCs under indoor lighting conditions. Incorporating CNTs into the antisolvent represents a particularly effective strategy, as the antisolvent process plays a key role in enhancing perovskite film quality. Specifically, CNTs can increase grain size density during film formation, resulting in uniform, pinhole-free films with improved efficiency, reduced hysteresis, and enhanced stability of the device. Several studies have demonstrated the benefit of this approach. Ryu et al. introduced MWCNTs in CB into the perovskite layer via a dripping method, improving the perovskite/carbon interface and achieving a PCE of 13.57% under one sun. This approach reduced series resistance and facilitated more efficient hole extraction, resulting in nearly hysteresis-free operation27. Recently, Sajid et al. presented a lead-free and HTM-free perovskite solar cell using an innovative two-step fabrication process to create a perovskite homojunction. Their work highlights the potential of alternative two-step methods to improve charge transport and device performance by creating built-in electric fields28. Wang et al. advanced this concept with a CNT bridging strategy by embedding SWCNTs in CB into both the perovskite and carbon electrode layers, forming a continuous conductive pathway across the interface. The devices achieved a PCE of 15.73% under one sun with an improved fill factor (FF) of 0.72 and exhibited excellent thermal and humidity stability over 90 days without encapsulation29. Tiing et al. functionalised SWCNTs in CB solution with octadecylamine (ODA) to enhance their dispersibility in the perovskite precursor. This modification not only improved the interfacial contact and perovskite crystallinity but also effectively suppressed trap-assisted recombination, yielding a PCE of 12.8% under one sun with reduced hysteresis and improved reproducibility30. Subsequently, Wei et al. conducted a systematic comparison of CNTs, carbon black, and graphite, demonstrating that CNT-based devices offer superior performance21. Their results further substantiate our conclusion that CNTs are an effective material for enhancing charge transport, while also underscoring their distinct advantages over other carbon-based additives. Collectively, these results highlight the dual function of CNTs’ conductive pathways and moisture barriers, making them compelling additives for the development of efficient and stable perovskite solar cells. Therefore, the incorporation of CNTs requires the selection of an appropriate antisolvent to maximize device performance. While the general benefits of CNTs are well established, their specific application in an IPA antisolvent for optimising PSCs under indoor light conditions remains a largely unexplored area, motivating the present study.

In this work, MWCNTs were incorporated into perovskite layers using an antisolvent approach to enhance charge transfer and boost the indoor performance of PSCs. MWCNTs were dispersed in IPA and used as the antisolvent during the deposition of the Cs0.17FA0.83 Pb(I0.83 Br0.17)3 or CsFA perovskite layer. The photo-absorber CsFA perovskite was selected because of its suitable band gap (1.67 eV)31 and its absorption range in the indoor spectrum (6500 K LED light bulb)9,31. The incorporation of CNTs yielded a perovskite film superior to the pristine film, with a uniform distribution of CNTs across the film surface and at grain boundaries. This configuration facilitated efficient charge extraction and improved charge transport to the HTL. The CNT-based device achieved a maximum PCE of 32.63% under 1000 lx indoor illumination. Furthermore, the device exhibited excellent long-term stability, retaining over 50% of its initial efficiency after 150 days of ambient storage without encapsulation. These results highlight the role of CNTs in the antisolvent process for PSCs and demonstrate their ability to enhance charge extraction and suppress recombination. Overall, this approach led to significant improvements in both the photovoltaic performance and long-term stability of PSCs.

Experimental

Device Fabrication: Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)-coated glass substrates (TEC 7) were sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in Alconox detergent solution, deionized water (DI), acetone, and isopropanol (IPA) for 15 min each, followed by ultraviolet-ozone (UVO) treatment for 15 min. A 0.8 M zinc tin oxide (ZTO) sol-gel solution was spin-coated onto the cleaned FTO substrates31. A Cs0.17FA0.83 Pb(I0.83 Br0.17)3 or CsFA perovskite precursor solution was prepared using 56 mg of cesium iodide (CsI), 188 mg of formamidinium iodide (FAI), 119 mg of lead(II) bromide (PbBr2), and 467 mg of lead(II) iodide (PbI2) in 1 ml of a 9:1 (v/v) mixture of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The final concentration of the precursor was 1.3 M with a 4% excess of PbI2. The CsFA perovskite layer was deposited using a modified one-step antisolvent dripping method, as described previously32. The perovskite precursor was spin-coated onto the FTO/ZTO substrates at 1000 rpm for 10 s and then at 6000 rpm for 30 s. During the second step, 180 µL of the IPA antisolvent, with or without CNTs, was dropped onto the spinning FTO/ZTO substrates 10 s before completion. After that, the films were heated at 100 °C for 15 min (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information). For the hole-transporting layer, a 35 mg/mL precursor solution of copper(I) thiocyanate (CuSCN) in diethyl sulfide was prepared and stirred overnight at room temperature. The solution was spin-coated onto the perovskite film at 2,500 rpm for 30 s. Finally, the carbon electrode was fabricated using an ethanol-mediated solvent interaction process33. It was pressed directly onto the HTL at room temperature for 3 min, followed by 50 °C for 180 s under a pressure of 0.6 MPa.

CNT antisolvent modification: The antisolvent with MWCNTs was prepared by mixing 4 wt% of MWCNT (purity > 96%, OD: 45–72 nm, length: 8–28 µm, Nanografi) with anhydrous IPA to achieve a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. The dispersion was then ultrasonicated for 1 h while maintaining the temperature below 35 °C to ensure better dispersion of the nanotubes. To further improve dispersion, a high-pressure homogenizer was used for 10 cycles (Figure S2 in the Supporting Information). The resulting CNTs dispersion was subsequently employed as the antisolvent in perovskite deposition.

Characterisations

Cross-sectional morphology was examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM-7001 F, JEOL). Atomic force microscopy (AFM; Park NX-10) was performed using a conductive contact probe (ANSCM-PC; spring constant k = 0.3 N/m, resonance frequency 15 kHz). Current density-voltage (J-V) characteristics of PSCs with an active area of 0.09 cm2 were measured using a Keithley 2401 source meter under AM 1.5G solar simulator (100 mW/cm2) at 25 °C. Indoor performance was evaluated under 1000 lx illumination (0.3651 mW/cm2, Philips 8 W LED lamp, 6500 K cool daylight) with a scan range of −0.2 V to 2.0 V, and a scan step of 0.05 V. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted with an Autolab PGSTAT 302 N, and data were fitted using an equivalent circuit model to extract the EIS parameters. Steady-state photoluminescence (PL) was recorded using a HAMAMATSU Quantaurus-Tau C16361-01, with emission collected through a monochromator in the spectral range of 500–900 nm.

Results and discussion

Quality of the perovskite layer

To verify the incorporation of CNTs into the perovskite film, FE-SEM analysis was conducted. Top-view FE-SEM images of the perovskite films with and without CNTs are shown in Fig. 1a and b. CNTs were predominantly distributed on the perovskite surface and along grain boundaries. Some CNTs may also have become embedded within the perovskite grains or at grain boundary sites that are not directly visible in the top-view FE-SEM images (Fig. 1b). The CNTs retained their cylindrical morphology, indicating that the CsFA formation process did not damage their core structure. They were uniformly distributed across the CsFA film surface. Furthermore, no significant difference in perovskite grain size (≈ 300 nm) was observed between CsFA films with and without CNTs, suggesting that CNT incorporation does not interfere with perovskite crystallization and preserves the structural integrity of the perovskite layer.

Cross-sectional FE-SEM images of perovskite films with CNTs are presented in Fig. 1c–d. In Fig. 1c, the FTO/ZTO/perovskite structure shows distinct CNT traces on the perovskite layer, confirming their successful incorporation. These CNTs appear in various orientations, including lying horizontally across the surface, standing vertically, and partially covering the top of the perovskite film. Such orientations may indicate that CNTs bridge the perovskite and HTL layers. The cross-sectional FE-SEM image of the pristine device (without CNTs) is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S3) for comparison. Additionally, Fig. 1 d shows the FTO/ZTO/perovskite/CuSCN structure, where the perovskite layer maintains a uniform thickness of approximately 400–500 nm (comparable to the pristine CsFA thickness of 500 nm), even after CNT incorporation. This suggests that CNTs do not significantly affect film thickness. Once the HTL is deposited, it fully covers the CNTs, preventing direct contact between the CNTs and the top electrode. The presence of CNTs on the perovskite surface is expected to facilitate more efficient charge extraction and improve charge transfer to the HTL, ultimately contributing to enhanced device performance.

Morphology of perovskite films: top-view FE-SEM images of CsFA films (a) without CNT and (b) with CNT; Cross-sectional FE-SEM images of (c) FTO/ETL/CsFA with CNT and (d) FTO/ZTO/CsFA with CNT/CuSCN.

Photovoltaic performance

To evaluate device performance, FTO/ZTO/CsFA with and without CNTs/CuSCN/carbon solar cells were fabricated by varying CNTs concentrations (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 1.0 mg/mL in IPA as the antisolvent). The photovoltaic performance under standard AM 1.5G illumination is summarised in Table S1 (Supporting Information). The pristine device achieved a maximum PCE of 14.13% and an average PCE of 12.13 ± 1.98%. In comparison, the device with an optimal CNT concentration of 0.3 mg/mL exhibited improved performance, achieving a champion PCE of 14.38% and a higher average PCE of 12.73 ± 0.93%. This improvement is attributed to more efficient charge extraction and transport enabled by the conductive CNTs distributed on the perovskite surface. However, the concentration of CNTs is crucial. A concentration that is too low creates an insufficient conductive network, which weakens charge transport. On the other hand, increasing the CNT concentration above 0.3 mg/mL led to reduced efficiency, likely due to CNT aggregation. These aggregates can disrupt the interface between the perovskite layer and the transport layer, creating additional defect states and hindering charge collection. Based on these results, 0.3 mg/mL was identified as the optimal concentration for evaluation under indoor lighting. While our study focuses on optimization within a specific concentration range, the methodology outlined by Sun et al. provides a broader framework that may guide future efforts toward fully optimizing CNT concentration34. This result not only establishes a practical benchmark for device fabrication but also underscores the importance of systematic concentration tuning in achieving balanced performance and material quality.

The indoor device performance was tested under 1000 lx illumination using a Philips 8 W LED lamp (6500 K cool daylight). The J–V curves of the pristine and CNT-modified devices are shown in Fig. 2a, and the photovoltaic parameters summarised in Fig. 2b–c. Under indoor conditions, the CNT-based device achieved a Jsc of 0.168 mA/cm2, a Voc of 0.825 V, a FF of 73.60%, and a maximum PCE of 32.63%. In contrast, the pristine device exhibited lower performance, with a Jsc of 0.155 mA/cm², a Voc of 0.816 V, an FF of 63.02%, and a PCE of 29.61%. These results confirm that the incorporation of CNTs enhances efficiency under both standard and indoor illumination, with particularly notable improvements in Voc and FF.

(a) Current density − voltage (J − V) characteristics of the best-performing PSCs without/with CNT. Photovoltaic parameters of the PSCs under indoor light; (b) open-circuit voltage (Voc), power conversion efficiency (PCE); (c) fill factor (FF), short-current density (Jsc).

To investigate the charge-collection ability of CNTs on CsFA films, conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) was performed on the FTO/ZTO/CsFA structure. Short-circuit current (Isc) mapping was obtained to evaluate charge conductivity pixel by pixel at zero bias under 0.285 mW/cm2 irradiation, following the methods reported previously9,35. The corresponding AFM images are shown in Fig. 3a–b. The average current (Iavg) of the CsFA film with CNTs reached 18.69 pA, significantly higher than that of the pristine CsFA film (12.75 pA). Regions of increased photocurrent, highlighted by brighter contrast in the Isc mapping, spatially coincided with CNT-rich areas. This enhancement is attributed to the conductive properties of CNTs distributed along perovskite grain boundaries, which serve as efficient charge pathways that facilitate charge extraction and improve local current generation. In particular, the observed high-current regions confirm the presence of CNTs at the perovskite surface, consistent with FE-SEM analysis.

To investigate the charge recombination at the nanoscale, Voc mapping was gathered at a forward bias of 0.5 V applied to the AFM cantilever. This bias simulates the Voc condition, where the net photocurrent remains nearly zero9,35, as shown in Fig. 3c–d. To account for imperfections due to the lack of HTL in this structure, the average Voc is assumed to be biased at 0.5 V instead of approximately 1.2 V, which is typical for an actual device (Table S2, JV-1sun/lowlight). The regions with positive current in the Voc mapping represent areas where the local Voc exceeds 0.5 V, indicating a perovskite surface with lower current. In contrast, darker regions correspond to higher current densities, as Voc is inversely correlated with defect concentration, where charge recombination occurs. Since Voc is linked to current density, dark-coloured regions correspond to perovskite surfaces with lower defect concentration. As illustrated in Fig. 3c–d, the CNTs-modified sample exhibited a higher average current (4.901 pA) compared to the pristine sample (2.858 pA), indicating improved surface potential uniformity. This result is consistent with the elevated Iavg values observed in the corresponding Isc mapping. Notably, the brighter regions in the CNT-based perovskite film align with CNT locations, indicating that photocurrent generated within the perovskite layer is directly collected by the CNTs.

The physical topography of the film, which was collected simultaneously with the C-AFM mappings, was also analyzed to extract the root-mean-square (RMS) roughness and confirm the CNTs’ physical integration. The pristine film shows an RMS roughness of 22.88 nm. Conversely, the CsFA film with CNTs demonstrates a significantly increased RMS roughness of 46.88 nm, showing clear surface alterations indicative of CNT incorporation and network formation. Crucially, while this increase in numerical roughness is observed, it is attributed to the successful integration and CNT network formation along the surface and grain boundaries. This enhanced surface characteristic is vital for improving interfacial contact and charge extraction efficiency, which directly contributes to the superior current generation measured by C-AFM. Therefore, both the electrical (Isc, Voc mappings) and morphological (RMS roughness) observations consistently confirm the spatial role of CNTs as efficient charge collectors and emphasize their excellent electrical conductivity, acting as nanoscale pathways that promote charge transport.

To confirm the impact of CNT incorporation on charge recombination behaviour, EIS was conducted under dark conditions at a forward bias of 0.9 V. The corresponding Nyquist plots are shown in Fig. 4a. A larger semicircle along the impedance (Z’) indicates higher recombination resistance (Rrec) and, therefore, reduced recombination. The CNT-based device displayed a broader semicircle than the pristine device. Specifically, the Rrec of the CNT-based device was 836 Ω, significantly higher than the 686 Ω measured for the pristine device. These results indicate that CNTs effectively suppress interfacial charge recombination, which contributes to the enhanced Voc observed in the CNT-based device36.

Conductive atomic force microscopy (C-AFM) mappings of perovskite films without and with CNT; (a, b) short-circuit current (Isc) mapping; (c, d) open-circuit voltage (Voc) mapping.

To further investigate the effect of CNTs on Voc, Mott–Schottky analysis was performed on both pristine and CNT-based devices (Fig. 4b). The built-in potential (Vbi) was calculated using the Mott–Schottky Eqs37,38. The pristine device exhibited a Vbi of 1.03 V, whereas the CNT-based device showed a higher Vbi of 1.09 V. A stronger built-in field promotes more efficient charge separation and transport, thereby reducing interfacial recombination. Thus, the enhanced Vbi in the CNT-based device directly contributes to its improved Voc.

PL spectroscopy was also employed to investigate the influence of CNT incorporation on charge transfer dynamics in perovskite films. The PL spectra are shown in Fig. 4c, recorded under 500 nm excitation. In the FTO/CsFA structure, CsFA films were deposited directly on FTO substrates to observe charge transport efficiency from the CsFA film to the CNTs. The CsFA film with CNTs exhibited lower PL emission intensity (649.6 a.u.) compared to the pristine (688.7 a.u.), indicating that the photogenerated charges were transferred to the CNTs through their conductive pathways.

Additionally, CuSCN was deposited on the perovskite film to assess charge extraction and transportation at the CsFA/HTL interface. The PL emission intensity of CNT-modified CsFA was lower than that of pristine CsFA, consistent with enhanced charge extraction and transportation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that CNT incorporation improves interfacial charge dynamics and contributes to the superior performance of CNT-based perovskite solar cells.

Stability

To investigate the effect of CNT incorporation on device stability under continuous operation, steady-state photocurrent measurements at the maximum power point (MPPT) were conducted over one minute (Fig. 5a). The CNT-based device exhibited a higher normalised power output than the pristine device (0.8 and 0.7, respectively), indicating that it sustained its performance more effectively under illumination. This enhanced power retention and operational stability was attributed to the role of CNTs in suppressing defect formation at the perovskite interface. By mitigating charge-trap formation, CNTs facilitated more efficient charge extraction and reduced non-radiative recombination, thereby improving device durability.

Characteristics of the PSCs without/with CNT; (a) Nyquist plots with the equivalent circuit in the inset; (b) Mott − Schottky plots of PSCs; (c) Steady-state PL spectra of perovskite layers.

Long-term stability was further evaluated under ambient conditions (room temperature, 40–50% RH) without encapsulation, using continuous LED illumination at 1000 lx. The results, monitored over more than 3,600 h (150 days), are shown in Fig. 5b. The CNT-based device maintained over 80% of its initial PCE (T80) for approximately 50 days and retained more than 50% (T50) beyond 150 days. Throughout the entire testing period, it consistently outperformed the pristine device, showing higher normalized PCE values. This improved stability was attributed to enhanced charge transfer, as CNTs promoted rapid charge extraction and transport, suppressing interfacial recombination and related degradation mechanisms. In addition, the intrinsic hydrophobicity of CNTs acted as a physical barrier against moisture ingress. Although unencapsulated devices gradually degraded under ambient exposure, CNTs effectively delayed moisture-induced degradation, thereby extending device lifetime.

A reproducibility analysis was also conducted to evaluate the consistency of device performance under both AM 1.5G illumination and indoor light at 1000 lx (Fig. 5c and d). Across all testing conditions, the CNT-based devices consistently achieved higher PCEs than pristine devices. Moreover, the CNT-based group exhibited a narrower distribution of PCE values, confirming superior reproducibility. This high level of consistency demonstrated the robustness of the CNT incorporation method against minor variations in processing and environmental conditions. Reduced performance fluctuations and lower risk of device failure highlight the role of CNTs in enhancing both the reliability and long-term stability of perovskite solar cells.

In addition to enhancing performance, our work addresses key challenges associated with the commercialization of indoor PSCs. The incorporation of CNTs as a low-cost and easily processable additive improves charge transport and provides a viable pathway to reducing overall fabrication costs. Furthermore, the demonstrated stability and consistent performance, supported by reproducibility data, are critical for ensuring long-term reliability. Although further investigations on large-area production and stability under diverse environmental conditions are still required, our findings offer a promising direction for the development of cost-effective and robust indoor photovoltaic devices.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated a highly effective strategy concerning the incorporation of CNTs via a simple antisolvent engineering method as a successful approach for advancing indoor PSCs. This success was attributed to the excellent charge transfer properties of CNTs. These properties enhanced charge extraction and suppressed recombination. This mechanism led to a champion indoor PCE of 32.63% and significantly improved unencapsulated long-term stability. The work established a practical and reproducible pathway to overcome key stability challenges for indoor PSCs. Future research should focus on scaling this technique to larger areas and testing its robustness under varied indoor lighting spectra to accelerate real-world deployment.
