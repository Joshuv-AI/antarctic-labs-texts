Hypergeometric motives from Euler integral representations
Tyler L. Kelly, John Voight

Abstract
. We revisit certain one-parameter families of affine covers arising naturally from Euler’s integral representation of hypergeometric functions. We introduce a partial compactification of this family. We show that the zeta function of the fibers in the family can be written as an explicit product of L L -series attached to nondegenerate hypergeometric motives and zeta functions of tori, twisted by Hecke Grossencharacters. This permits a combinatorial algorithm for computing the Hodge numbers of the family.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

The world of hypergeometric functions invites a rich interplay between complex analysis, algebraic geometry, number theory, and physics guided by the hypergeometric differential equation. The simplest version of this is found in the Legendre family of elliptic curves, defined by

y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-t).

The period associated to this family can be computed to be hypergeometric, following Euler
[
Eul48
]
. Two centuries later, Igusa found an arithmetic analogue to Euler’s integral formula: the point counts on the Legendre family of elliptic curves can be related to a (truncated) hypergeometric equation
[
Igu58
]
. Starting in the 1980s, Katz gave a vast generalization
[
Kat96
]
in his theory of motives attached to rigid local systems. Since then, the literature has proposed many families of varieties that exemplify hypergeometric motives in varying generality: see Roberts–Rodriguez-Villegas
[
RRV22
, §3]
for an overview and the recent monograph by Fuselier–Long–Ramakrishna–Swisher–Tu
[
FLRST22
]
for a history and further references.

We focus on a symmetrized version of a generalization of the Legendre family above. Let
n,m\geq 1
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Inspired by Euler’s formula, we start with the family of affine cyclic covers over
\mathbb{Z}[1/m]

(1.1.1)

\displaystyle Y=Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}\colon y^{m}

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(-x_{i})^{a_{i}}(1-x_{i})^{b_{i}-a_{i}}

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=tx_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}

\displaystyle 0,1

\displaystyle\neq x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}

in the parameter
t
for
\mathbb{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}
. We note that our generalization has introduced a new sign. This choice of sign yields substantial streamlining; its absence already shows up as an obtrusive quadratic character when counting points on the Legendre family.

The fibers
Y_{t}
of this family are smooth of dimension
n-1
. When
a_{i}-b_{j}\not\in m\mathbb{Z}
for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
, Euler’s integral formula directly implies at least one period of

is a hypergeometric function in the parameter
t
, namely
F(a_{1}/m,\dots,a_{n}/m;b_{1}/m,\dots,b_{n}/m;t)
(
Lemma

3.1.5
), a feature not as apparent in other hypergeometric families. Other periods, however, may be less transparent.

We turn to the arithmetic story for
Y
and computing
\#Y_{t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. To do so, we first look at defining an analogue of hypergeometric functions over finite fields. The literature contains multiple definitions and normalizations
[
Gre87
,
McC12
,
Kat90
,
BCM15
,
FLRST22
]
, each having their own strengths and contexts.
In particular, Fuselier–Long–Ramakrishna–Swisher–Tu
define
a new finite field analogue of the hypergeometric function in a recursive way to count the number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points in the affine variety defined by the first two equations in (
1.1.1
) directly
[
FLRST22
, Proposition 4.2]
. The other definitions of hypergeometric functions differ, but under a nondegeneracy condition (namely,
\gcd(m,b_{i}-a_{i})=1
for all

[

]
coincides with that of, say
[
McC12
]
.

However, the interpretation of this formula can run into problems when there are degeneracies. This happens more than one expects, as degenerate hypergeometric parameters arise when
a_{i}-b_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}
for some
d\mid m
. In these cases, the formula in
[
FLRST22
]
uses their recursive formulae with degenerate parameters. In this paper, we introduce a new model

that is a partial compactification of
Y
(see §
3.2
) whose motive avoids degenerate hypergeometric motives. Indeed, its
L
-series is an explicit product of
L
-series attached to nondegenerate hypergeometric motives and zeta functions of tori twisted by a Hecke Grossencharacter. In turn, the mixed Hodge numbers for
X
can be computed directly from this motivic perspective
[
CG11
,
Fed18
,
RV19
]
.

1.2.
Results

Let
t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}
and write
S
for the set of primes dividing
m
together with the primes dividing the numerator or denominator of
t
or

) over

(inverting the primes in

). For

, write
Y_{t,\mathbb{F}_{p}}
for the base change to

(1.2.1)

Z(Y_{t,\mathbb{F}_{p}},T)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\exp\left(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\#Y_{t}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})\frac{T^{r}}{r}\right)\in(1+T\mathbb{Z}[[T]])\cap\mathbb{Q}(T).

Let

(1.2.2)

\zeta_{S}(Y_{t},s)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{p\not\in S}Z(Y_{t,\mathbb{F}_{p}},p^{-s})^{-1}

Roughly speaking, our main result will show that there is a partial compactification

whose zeta function, similarly defined, is an explicit product of

-series attached to nondegenerate hypergeometric parameters and twists of zeta functions of tori by Hecke Grossencharacters.

We proceed by making this precise by introducing our notation. Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}

\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{j})_{j}\in(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{n}

. We say that
\alpha_{i}
or

is a

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
are nondegenerate. Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
be minimal such that
m\boldsymbol{\alpha},m\boldsymbol{\beta}\subset\mathbb{Z}
. In

-series

L_{S}(H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m}),s),

defined by an Euler product and convergent in a right-half plane. We note that this

-series depends on the ordering of the parameters, but compensates by being invariant under shifts (
Remark

4.4.5
). Based on deep results of Katz, we explain that the period-normalized hypergeometric

-series for nondegenerate parameters has degree
n\phi(m)

\mathbb{Q}
(

4.6.6
).

The variety
Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
is a branched cover of the affine variety
U
defined by
1=tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}
and
0,1\neq x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. We partially compactify to
V\supseteq U
defined by
1=tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}
and
0\neq x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
to provide a smooth branched cover of a toric hypersurface, as follows.

Theorem 1.2.4
.

There exists a partial
\mu_{m}
-equivariant compactification
X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}\supseteq Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
over
V\supseteq U
such that
for all
t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}
,

\zeta_{S}(X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t},s)=\prod_{d\mid m}\begin{cases}L_{S}(H(\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d,t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d}),s),&\text{if $\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d$ is nondegenerate;}\\
\zeta_{S}((\Gm)^{e-1},\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d}),s,\psi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,t})^{(-1)^{n-e}},&\begin{aligned} &\parbox[t]{120.7521pt}{if $\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d$ is isotypically degenerate, where $e=e(\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d)$;}\end{aligned}\\
1,&\text{else};\end{cases}

where
\psi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,t}
is an explicit Hecke Grossencharacter.

Theorem

1.2.4
describes the full (mixed) motive of the family of varieties whose periods arise from Euler’s integral formula. The Hecke Grossencharacters arising in
Theorem

1.2.4
are described in (
5.4.2
); we briefly recall in
section

4.3
how Hecke Grossencharacters may be obtained from Jacobi sums (after Weil and Anderson).

If one tries to extend the hypersurface defined by
y^{m}=f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}(x)
across
V
without modification, then the variety is badly singular. We require a more elaborate partial compactification to obtain

1.2.4
, see (
3.2.4
). In a nutshell, we organize the factors of
f(x)
according to the greatest common divisor of their multiplicity with
m
, and show that these glue together appropriately (
Proposition

3.2.5
).

As the Hodge numbers of nondegenerate hypergeometric motives and Hecke Grossencharacters are known, as a corollary we conclude that the Hodge numbers of the family
X_{t}
are effectively computable, in particular the degrees of the
L
-series appearing in
\zeta(X_{t},s)
. Indeed, for the nondegenerate case, one can find the Hodge numbers associated to the hypergeometric motive associated to a BCM hypergeometric function, following the zigzag procedure
[
RRV22
]
. In the isotypically degenerate case, we obtain a torus factor twisted by a Hecke Grossencharacter, which can then be computed
[
Wat11
]
.

Our method of proof uses character sums. In the nondegenerate case, we give a new non-inductive proof (
Proposition

4.5.1
). But the heart of our work is to show a certain
Möbius-like cancellation
: terms that show up above fibers where
x_{i}=1
in the degenerate case either line up when isotypically degenerate and cancel otherwise (
Theorem

5.3.1
).

1.3.
Precise comparison to previous results

There is a rich literature of where hypergeometric functions over finite fields have been shown to relate to rational point counts (see, e.g., the first paragraph of
[
OSS23
]
for a long list).
We now focus on the history of the family given in (
1.1.1
), where many authors have studied it in varying degrees of generality. As stated above, the intuition that the point counts on the Legendre family of elliptic curves can be related to a (truncated) hypergeometric equation corresponding to the family’s period can be traced back to work of Igusa
[
Igu58
]
. Its relation to finite field hypergeometric functions has been articulated in numerous ways (see
[
Koi95
, §4]
,
[
Ono98
, Theorem 1]
,
[
Rou06
, Theorem 2]
), then proven for a new example
[
Goo18
]
by considering a quadruple cover.

More recently, Deines, Fusilier, Long, Swisher, and Tu analyzed the family

(1.3.1)

y^{n}=(x_{1}\cdots x_{n-1})^{n-1}(1-x_{1})\cdots(1-x_{n-1})(x_{1}-\lambda x_{2}\cdots x_{n-1})

as a higher-dimensional version of the Legendre curves, and show in
[
DFLST16
, Theorem 2]
that its rational point count is the sum of a polynomial and finite-field hypergeometric functions as defined by Greene
[
Gre87
]
. Fusilier, Long, Ramakrishna, Swisher, and Tu then generalized this result
[
FLRST22
]
to the case

(1.3.2)

y^{m}=x_{1}^{a_{1}}(1-x_{1})^{b_{1}}\cdots x_{n-1}^{a_{n}}(1-x_{n-1})^{b_{n-1}}(1-tx_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n-1})^{b_{n}}.

(Compare also with Katz
[
Kat96
, Theorem 8.4.1]
.)

Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, when
q
is a prime power so that
q\equiv 1\pmod{m}
, they prove
[
FLRST22
, Proposition 4.2]
that the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational point counts correspond to a polynomial in
q
and a sum of finite field hypergeometric functions. However, to do so, they created a new recursive definition for finite field hypergeometric functions (see (4.4) of loc. cit.) to allow them to use character theory to directly imply the result.

Their finite field hypergeometric function definition uses a
period normalization
, and therefore differs from those given in the literature by previous authors Greene
[
Gre87
]
, McCarthy
[
McC12
]
, Katz
[
Kat90
]
, and Beukers–Cohen–Mellit
[
BCM15
]
. The authors
[
FLRST22
, §4.4]
provide a relation between their hypergeometric function and that given in Greene’s paper. Also, they related theirs to that given by McCarthy, but only in the so-called
primitive
case
[
FLRST22
, Definition 4.3]
when
a_{i}-b_{j}\not\in m\mathbb{Z}
for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
. (Note that primitive implies nondegenerate, but not conversely.)

Given these differences, we found it less than straightforward to combine results from these papers. We navigate around this obstacle by restricting ourselves to only allowing formulas involving the nondegenerate hypergeometric functions, where there is more agreement and the theorem of Katz allows us to recognize the associated
L
-series as motivic. Indeed, one may interpret our main result as saying that the
L
-series of a degenerate hypergeometric motive arises from a Hecke Grossencharacter. We found that the period normalization (following Fusilier–Long–Ramakrishna–Swisher–Tu) gives the simplest formulation in our main result (
Theorem

1.2.4
); but the choice of signs is essential.

Stepping back a bit further, there are other important families which realise hypergeometric motives. When the parameters are
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
—meaning that for all
k\in(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
we have
k\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\alpha
and
k\boldsymbol{\beta}=\beta
as multisets—there is a particularly nice setup: see the toric description of
source varieties
described by Roberts–Rodriguez-Villegas
[
RRV22
, §3]
and related explicitly to the models defined by Beukers–Cohen–Mellit
[
BCM15
]
. However, these models are not available when the parameters are not defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, and so this hypothesis is substantial (the Klein–Mukai pencil of K3 surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined by
x^{4}+y^{3}z+z^{3}w+w^{3}y-4txyzw=0

[
DKSSVW20
]
already sees hypergeometric motives not defined over
\mathbb{Q}
).

Lastly, we remark the family (
1.3.2
) is affine, and therefore a singular compactification of the starting family
Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
(our equation has been symmetrized). It is unclear how to proceed to understand the geometry of these families (for example, to understand the Hodge numbers) without a partial desingularization and compatification. In sum, then, our contribution is to define a nicer geometric realization of hypergeometric motives that is available for all parameters and permits the computation of the zeta function over
\mathbb{Q}
as a product of
L
-series of nondegenerate hypergeometric motives and Hecke Grossencharacters.

2.
Hypergeometric functions and integral representations

For motivation, we begin with the classical, complex theory of (generalized) hypergeometric functions. We present this standard material in a symmetric way.

2.1.
Differential equation

Let
(x)_{k}

and

2.2.
Integral representation

Remark 2.2.4

Although the hypergeometric function is visibly independent of permutation of the parameters, the integral representation is not: at least in its symmetrized form, it depends on a matching between numerator and denominator pairings.

In the below, we work with a version with a different choice of signs; this changes the constant

, the fiber

.

Proof.

Immediate, as we have removed the branch locus.
∎

It is an important step in the theory of motives to recognize the corresponding period integrals, as follows.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}

We refer the reader to

Corollary 3.1.10

With notation as in

Proof.

It is an expression of the interplay mentioned in the introduction that the periods as expressed by the hypergeometric series above match the point as expressed by the finite field hypergeometric functions in our main result.

Proof of
Lemma

3.1.5
.

We compute that (after an appropriate branch cut),

(3.1.13)

\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i}(-x_{i})^{c_{i}}(1-x_{i})^{d_{i}}}{y^{k}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(-x_{i})^{c_{i}-ka_{i}/m}(1-x_{i})^{d_{i}+k(b_{i}-a_{i})(-1)/m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-ka_{i}/m)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{c_{i}-ka_{i}/m}(1-x_{i})^{d_{i}-k(b_{i}-a_{i})/m}.

We now apply

2.2.2
and match exponents. We are required to take

so

. But then

\displaystyle x_{n}^{c_{n}-ka_{n}/m}

\displaystyle=x_{n}^{c_{n}+k(b_{n}-a_{n})/m-kb_{n}/m}=x_{n}^{c_{n}+d_{n}+\alpha_{n}}x_{n}^{-kb_{n}/m}

\displaystyle=x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}(tx_{1}\cdots x_{n-1})^{kb_{n}/m}.

Expansion and substitution in (

Now for

∎

3.2.
Partial compactification

We now define a partial compactification (birational model) for the family
Y
defined in (
3.1.3
). This model will admit an open immersion from
Y
, giving a partial compactification at points
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y)
where some
x_{i}=1
.

First off: without loss of generality (considering modulo
m
), we may and do suppose that
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\geq 0
and
b_{i}\geq a_{i}
for all
i
. (We avoided making this hypothesis before now for uniformity in the description, but now we are trying to find a compactification so we need to clear denominators. In principle, we only need to ask this for
b_{i}-a_{i}\geq 0
.)

We first subdivide the product defining
f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}
according to a greatest common divisor with
m
. For
d\mid m
a (positive) divisor, define

(3.2.1)

f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\\
\gcd(m,a_{i})=d\end{subarray}}(-x_{i})^{a_{i}/d}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\\
\gcd(m,b_{i}-a_{i})=d\end{subarray}}(1-x_{i})^{(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}.

Then

(3.2.2)

f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\prod_{d\mid m}f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{d}

since all factors occur exactly once in the product, with the right exponent.

We work over
V\supseteq U
defined by

(3.2.3)

\displaystyle tx_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{n}

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}

\displaystyle\neq 0;

i.e., we close up the axes where a variable
x_{i}=1
. However, our branched cover now comes with variables also indexed by divisors, i.e., we work in
(\Gm)^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{\tau(m)}
with toric variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and affine variables
y_{d}
for
d\mid m
, where
\tau(m)
is the number of divisors of
m
. The branched cover
X=X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
is defined to be the subvariety in
V
cut out by the system of equations

(3.2.4)

y_{d}^{d/h}=y_{h}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid m\\
h\mid e\\
d\nmid e\end{subarray}}f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},e}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{e/h},

with an equation for all
h,d
such that
h\mid d\mid m
, together with the equation
y_{1}=1
.

Proposition 3.2.5
.

There is an open immersion

(3.2.6)

Y\hookrightarrow X

defined by the identity on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and

(3.2.7)

y_{d}=\frac{y^{m/d}}{\prod_{e:d\mid e\mid m}f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},e}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{e/d}}

for each
d\mid m
. The birational inverse is defined by the identity on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and

(3.2.8)

y=y_{m}f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{m}.

Moreover, the action of
\mu_{m}
by
y\mapsto\zeta_{m}y
extends to
X
equivariantly using (
3.2.7
).

Proof.

One verifies that the map
X\dashrightarrow Y
is an isomorphism (that is to say, defined) away from the points
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y)
where
x_{i}=1
for some
i=1,\dots,d
. This map can be seen through considering (
3.2.7
) in the case where
d=m
and clearing denominators. ∎

For
I\subseteq[n]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

(3.2.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}_{I}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(a_{i})_{i\in I}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{I}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(b_{i})_{i\in I}

\displaystyle m_{I}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\gcd(\{b_{i}-a_{i}:i\not\in I\}\cup\{m\}).

We define a ‘twist’ of
Y_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m_{I}}
by
(-1)^{\sum_{i\not\in I}a_{i}}
as follows. Define
the (quasi-)affine variety in
\mathbb{A}^{\#I}
with variables
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in I}
defined by the equations

(3.2.10)

Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I}^{\prime}\colon\quad\begin{aligned} y^{m_{I}}&=(-1)^{\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}}\prod_{i\in I}(-x_{i})^{a_{i}}(1-x_{i})^{b_{i}-a_{i}}\\
1&=t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}\\
x_{i}&\neq 0,1\text{ for all $i\in I$}.\end{aligned}

Lemma 3.2.11
.

Let
I\subseteq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
be a subset. Then the intersection of
X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
with the affine subvariety of
V
defined to be the locus where
x_{i}\neq 1
for
i\in I
and
x_{i}=1
for
i\not\in I
is isomorphic to
Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We look back at the defining equations (
3.2.4
). First, we see that if
h\mid d
and
y_{h}=0
, then
y_{d}=0
. Let
d\mid m
. Take
h=1
to get

(3.2.12)

\displaystyle y_{d}^{d}

\displaystyle=y_{1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid m\\
d\nmid e\end{subarray}}f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},e}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{e}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid m\\
d\nmid e\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\\
\gcd(m,a_{i})=e\end{subarray}}(-x_{i})^{a_{i}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\\
\gcd(m,b_{i}-a_{i})=e\end{subarray}}(1-x_{i})^{b_{i}-a_{i}}.

So
y_{d}=0
if and only if
(1-x_{i})\mid f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},e}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for some

and

with

. By definition, this happens if and only if there exists

such that

. By the contrapositive, this says

if and only if

for all

, i.e.,

.

Let

according as

or not. Plugging in, this leaves

y_{m_{I}}^{m_{I}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\mid m\\
m_{I}\nmid e\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\\

(As confirmation, we see that if

then

, so the only terms

that appear have

(If
m_{i}\mid e
then the twist by
(-1)^{e}
can be absorbed into the isomorphism.) Replacing

by
m_{I}
, the birational maps in

then define an isomorphism to

.
∎

4.
Finite field hypergeometric functions

In this section, we recall and compare definitions of finite field analogues of complex hypergeometric functions
[
Gre87
,
Kat90
,
McC12
,
FLRST22
,
BCM15
]
.

4.1.
Setup

Let
q=p^{r}
be a prime power and let

(4.1.1)

q^{\times}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}q-1.

We write
\omega^{1/q^{\times}}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
for a generator of the character group on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, so we can write
\omega^{\mu}
for
\mu\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
q^{\times}\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
. We extend
\omega\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
by setting
\omega(0)=0
including for the trivial character
\varepsilon
. Further, let
\Theta\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be a nontrivial (additive) character.

Definition 4.1.2
.

For
\alpha\in(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}
, we define the
Gauss sum

(4.1.3)

g(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\Theta(x).

The Gauss sum takes values in
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{q-1},\zeta_{q})\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and is well-defined on
k\in\bigl(\frac{1}{q^{\times}}\mathbb{Z}\bigr)/\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 4.1.4
.

While the definition of the Gauss sum also depends on the choice of additive character
\Theta
, the definitions for the Jacobi sum in
Definition

4.2.1
, the definition of the Hecke Grossencharacter in (
4.3.3
), and the period normalized hypergeometric function in
Definition

4.4.1
do not.

Remark 4.1.5
.

Note the Gauss sum in this definition sums over the elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
rather than
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The literature in finite field hypergeometric sums is not uniform; there can be a discrepancy when one defines
\varepsilon(0)
to be nonzero or defines the set of summation in the Gauss sum to include
0
(over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, instead of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
) in the Gauss sum. First, some authors impose that
\varepsilon(0)=0

[
FLRST22
,
McC12
]
while others use the convention that
\varepsilon(0)=1

[
IR90
,
BCM15
,
Coh2
]
. Second, some authors will alter the summation in the definition of a Gauss sum to be over the elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
rather than
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
(e.g.,
[
IR90
,
McC12
]
sum over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
but
[
FLRST22
,
BCM15
]
sum over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
).

With this definition for Gauss sum, we have the following standard properties:

Proposition 4.1.6
.

We have that
g(0)=-1
and, for
\alpha\in(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}\smallsetminus\mathbb{Z}
,

(4.1.7)

g(\alpha)g(-\alpha)=\omega^{\alpha}(-1)q.

Proof.

See Cohen
[
Coh2
, Lemma 2.5.8 and Proposition 2.5.9]
.
∎

4.2.
Jacobi sums

We recall a definition for Jacobi sums.

Definition 4.2.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}
. We define the
Jacobi sum

J(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{\beta}(1-x).

Remark 4.2.2
.

Again, there are different conventions for the set of summation of a Jacobi sum, which can lead to discrepancies just as in the case of Gauss sums. We have adopted the convention that
\varepsilon(0)=0
, so the set of summation in
Definition

4.2.1
can be extended to
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, adding zero.

The rest of this subsection presents identities for Jacobi sums and Gauss sums that we find useful to compute
\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q})
in the next section.

Proposition 4.2.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}
. Then

(4.2.4)

J(\alpha,-\beta)=\frac{g(\alpha)g(-\beta)}{g(\alpha-\beta)}+\begin{cases}\omega^{-\beta}(-1)q^{\times},&\text{ if $\alpha-\beta\in\mathbb{Z}$};\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We give a proof that is a straightforward modification of Ireland–Rosen
[
IR90
, §8.3, Theorem 1]
once we carefully track the consequences of taking
\varepsilon(0)=0
instead.

We have the following expansion of the product
g(\alpha)g(-\beta)
:

(4.2.5)

\displaystyle g(\alpha)g(-\beta)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\Theta(x)\right)\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\omega^{-\beta}(y)\Theta(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{-\beta}(y)\Theta(x+y)=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x+y=u\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{-\beta}(y)\right)\Theta(u)

Note that when
u=0
we can use the fact that
y=-x
to obtain the following

(4.2.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x+y=0\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{-\beta}(y)=\omega^{-\beta}(-1)\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\omega^{\alpha-\beta}(x)=\begin{cases}\omega^{-\beta}(-1)q^{\times}&\text{ if $\alpha-\beta\in\mathbb{Z}$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Next, if
u\neq 0
then we can rescale by a factor of
u
to get the following

(4.2.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x+y=u\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{-\beta}(y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(ux^{\prime})\omega^{-\beta}(uy^{\prime})=\omega^{\alpha-\beta}(u)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(x^{\prime})\omega^{-\beta}(y^{\prime}).

So then, if
\alpha-\beta\notin\mathbb{Z}
, we can simplify to get

(4.2.8)

\displaystyle g(\alpha)g(-\beta)=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\omega^{\alpha-\beta}(u)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x+y=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{-\beta}(y)\right)\Theta(u)=g(\alpha-\beta)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
x+y=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{-\beta}(y).

Changing the summation to be over one variable, dividing by
g(\alpha-\beta)
, and using the fact that
g(0)=-1
, we obtain

(4.2.9)

\frac{g(\alpha)g(-\beta)}{g(\alpha-\beta)}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega(x)^{\alpha}\omega(1-x)^{\beta}-\begin{cases}\omega^{-\beta}(-1)q^{\times},&\text{ if $\alpha-\beta\in\mathbb{Z}$};\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

∎

Corollary 4.2.10
.

We have

(4.2.11)

\frac{g(\alpha)g(-\beta)}{g(\alpha-\beta)}=\begin{cases}J(\alpha,-\beta)&\text{ if $\alpha-\beta\notin\mathbb{Z}$};\\
-1&\text{ if $\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}$ or $\beta\in\mathbb{Z}$};\\
-\omega^{\alpha}(-1)q&\text{ if $\alpha-\beta\in\mathbb{Z}$ but $\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The first case is
Proposition

4.2.3
and the latter two cases follow immediately from
Proposition

4.1.6
.
∎

Corollary 4.2.12
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
and suppose that
\alpha-\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
, but
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}
. Then

J(\alpha,-\beta)=-\omega^{\alpha}(-1)=q^{-1}\frac{g(\alpha)g(-\beta)}{g(\alpha-\beta)}.

Proof.

Since
\alpha-\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\omega^{\alpha}(-1)=\omega^{-\beta}(-1)
. By combining
Proposition

4.2.3
and
Corollary

4.2.10
, we compute

J(\alpha,-\beta)=-\omega^{\alpha}(-1)q+\omega^{-\beta}(-1)q^{\times}=\omega^{\alpha}(-1)(-q+q^{\times})=-\omega^{\alpha}(-1).

Applying
Corollary

4.2.10
again proves the second identity.
∎

The following lemma yields a relation for Jacobi sums induced that we use in the next section. This identity is known (e.g., see
[
FLRST22
, Lemma 2.8]
), but we present a short geometric proof using a Möbius transformation.

Lemma 4.2.13
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}
. We have

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{\beta}(1-x)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha}(-x)\omega^{-\beta-\alpha}(1-x).

Proof.

We apply the Möbius transformation
M(x)=x/(x-1)
to the left hand side to rearrange the sum. Note the summing indexation does not change as
M(\{0,1,\infty\})=\{0,1,\infty\}
. After computing that
1-M(x)=(1-x)^{-1}
under this transformation, we compute

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha}(x)\omega^{\beta}(1-x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha}(x/(x-1))\omega^{-\beta}(1-x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha}(-x)\omega^{-\beta-\alpha}(1-x).\qed

4.3.
Hecke Grossencharacters

In this section, we briefly review how Gauss sums and Jacobi sums yield Hecke Grossencharacters (or Grössencharaktere) over cyclotomic fields. This idea goes back to Weil
[
Wei52
]
and has been investigated by many authors; see Watkins
[
Wat18
]
for an explicit version.

Although the theory is quite general, we focus on the case of interest. Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. We work with
K\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
with ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{m}]
. We consider
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})\subset\mathbb{C}
by taking
\zeta_{m}=\exp(2\pi i/m)
. Let
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{K}
be a prime ideal that is coprime to
m
, and write

q=\Nm(\mathfrak{p})
for its norm and let
\mathbb{F}_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{Z}_{K}/\mathfrak{p}
for its residue field. Denote by

(4.3.1)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}^{1/m}\colon\mathbb{F}_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\times}\to\langle\zeta_{m}\rangle

the character uniquely characterized by the condition that

\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}^{1/m}(x)\equiv x^{(q-1)/m}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}.

We extend by zero to
\mathbb{F}_{\mathfrak{p}}
. The character
\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}^{1/m}
has order
m
, so under a choice of isomorphism
\mathbb{Z}_{K}/\mathfrak{p}\simeq\mathbb{F}_{q}
it corresponds to
\omega^{1/m}
(see
Remark

4.1.4
).

We may therefore extend our notation to this setting as follows. Given
\alpha\in(1/m)\mathbb{Z}
, we define the
Gauss sum
for
\mathfrak{p}
as

(4.3.2)

g_{\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}/\mathfrak{p}}\chi_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\alpha}(x)\Theta(x).

And for
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\subset(1/m)\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
\#\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\#\boldsymbol{\beta}=n
, we define

(4.3.3)

\psi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\mathfrak{p})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g_{\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha_{i})g_{\mathfrak{p}}(-\beta_{i})}{g_{\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})}.

Theorem 4.3.4
.

The assignment
\mathfrak{p}\mapsto\psi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\mathfrak{p})
defines a Hecke Grossencharacter
\psi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
for
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
with the property that if
\sigma_{k}(\zeta_{m})=\zeta_{m}^{k}
for
k\in(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, then
\sigma_{k}(\psi_{\alpha,\beta})=\psi_{k\alpha,k\beta}
.

Proof.

See Watkins
[
Wat18
, §2.2]
. Galois equivariance can also be proven directly from the definitions using the fact that
\sigma_{k}\circ\omega=\omega^{k}
and
\sigma_{k}\circ\Theta=\Theta
(analogously to
[
DKSSVW20
, Lemma 4.9.1(a)]
).
∎

4.4.
A formula for (period-normalized) hypergeometric functions

With the analogy between Gauss sums and the
\Gamma
-function in mind, but thinking in terms of periods, we make the following definition.

Definition 4.4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\subset(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}
be hypergeometric parameters with
\#\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\#\boldsymbol{\beta}=n
. For
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we define the
(period-normalized) hypergeometric sum
by

(4.4.2)

H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{-1}{q^{\times}}\sum_{\mu=0}^{q^{\times}-1}G(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mu/q^{\times},\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mu/q^{\times})\omega((-1)^{n}t)^{\mu/q^{\times}},

where

(4.4.3)

G(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g(\alpha_{i})g(-\beta_{i})}{g(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})}.

In
Definition

4.4.1
, note that we do not require the standard assumption in complex hypergeometric functions that
\beta_{n}=1
. However, one may reduce to this case by introducing a twist, shifting all parameters by
1-\beta_{n}
as in the complex case, according to the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4.4
.

If
q^{\times}\delta\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

H_{q}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\delta,\boldsymbol{\beta}+\delta,t)=\omega((-1)^{n}t)^{\delta}H_{q}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t).

Proof.

Immediate from reordering the sum, replacing
\mu\leftarrow\mu+q^{\times}\delta
.
∎

Remark 4.4.5
.

Definition

4.4.1
is called
period-normalized
because it takes into account the factor
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in Euler’s integral formula (
Lemma

2.2.2
), following the convention of Fuselier–Long–Ramakrishna–Swisher–Tu
[
FLRST22
]
. It is a twist of the definition in Beukers–Cohen–Mellit
[
BCM15
]
by (the value of) a Hecke Grossencharacter over
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
(
Theorem

4.3.4
), more precisely we have

H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)=G(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})H^{\text{BCM}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t).

Definition

4.4.1
depends on pairing up the numerator and denominator parameters (retaining simultaneous permutation by
S_{n}
), whereas the definition given by Beukers–Cohen–Mellit
[
BCM15
]
allows permuting numerator and denominator parameters separately, by
S_{n}\times S_{n}
. On the other hand, our definition has a simpler shift given in
Lemma

4.4.4
and gives us the simplest theorem statement. Both normalizations have their advantages and reflect the fact that one can always twist hypergeometric motives. For a complete comparison with the many other versions of finite field hypergeometric functions given in the literature, we refer the reader to
[
FLRST22
, §4.4]
.

Recall the canonical isomorphism
\Gal(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})\,|\,\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Let
H
be the subgroup of
k\in(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
such that
k\boldsymbol{\alpha},k\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a permutation of
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
(simultaneous reordering), and let
K_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})^{H}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{\times}
be the subfield fixed under
H
.

Lemma 4.4.6
.

We have
H_{q}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)\in K_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
H_{q}(p\boldsymbol{\alpha},p\boldsymbol{\beta},t)=H_{q}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t^{p})
.

Proof.

Repeat the proof of
[
DKSSVW20
, Lemma 3.2.10]
, observing that
G(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
has the same invariance so the conclusion holds as well for the period-normalized sum.
∎

Definition 4.4.7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
be hypergeometric parameters with
\#\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\#\boldsymbol{\beta}
. We say that
\alpha_{i}
or
\beta_{i}
is a
degenerate parameter
if
\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. We say the parameters are
degenerate
if there exists a degenerate parameter, else they are
nondegenerate
. Finally, we say the parameters are
isotypically degenerate
if there is exactly one degenerate parameter.

Example 4.4.8
.

The parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1/2,3/4)
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0,1/4)
are nondegenerate;
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1/2,1/2,1/4)
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(1/2,1/2,3/4)
are isotypically degenerate with the unique degenerate parameter
1/2
having multiplicity
2
, and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1/2,0,1/4)
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(1/2,0,3/4)
are degenerate but not isotypically degenerate.

Remark 4.4.9
.

The condition in
Definition

4.4.7
has previously been known as
primitive
in the literature
[
FLRST22
]
; however, the condition is not that a failure of coprimality of some kind, but rather that the period-normalized hypergeometric sum decomposes nontrivially as sums in the degenerate case when one considers the Gauss sums in light of
Proposition

4.2.3
.

4.5.
Hypersurface point counts

We now prove an identity that allows us to use our period-normalized hypergeometric sum to count points when the hypergeometric parameters are nondegenerate. This is in analogy with the recursive definition given in
[
FLRST22
]
, but our proof does not require any recursive argument or definition.

For the remainder of this section, we suppress the dependence on
q
and write just
H=H_{q}
.

Proposition 4.5.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\subset(1/q^{\times})\mathbb{Z}
be nondegenerate parameters such that
\#\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\#\boldsymbol{\beta}=n
. Let
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\}
. Then

(4.5.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{n}\\
tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega^{\alpha_{i}}(-x_{i})\omega^{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}(1-x_{i})\right)=-H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t).

Remark 4.5.3
.

The summation in (
4.5.2
) does not change if we add in points where
x_{i}=0
or
1
for any
i
since our convention in §
4
imposes that
\chi(0)=0
for all characters
\chi
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.2.3
using the nondegeneracy hypothesis along with
Definition

4.2.1
,

(4.5.4)

\displaystyle G(\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\mu/q^{\times},\boldsymbol{\beta}+\mu/q^{\times})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g(\alpha_{i}+\mu/q^{\times})g(-\beta_{i}-\mu/q^{\times})}{g(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i})}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}J(\alpha_{i}+\mu/q^{\times},-\mu/q^{\times}-\beta_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\mu/q^{\times}+\alpha_{i}}(x_{i})\omega^{-\mu/q^{\times}-\beta_{i}}(1-x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\mu/q^{\times}+\alpha_{i}}(-x_{i})\omega^{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}(1-x_{i})\right),

where the last line follows from applying
Lemma

4.2.13
.

We then plug (
4.5.4
) into
Definition

4.4.1
to obtain

(4.5.5)

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{q^{\times}}\sum_{\mu=0}^{q^{\times}-1}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{\alpha_{i}}(-x_{i})\omega^{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}(1-x_{i})\omega^{\mu/q^{\times}}(-x_{i})\right)\omega((-1)^{n}t)^{\mu/q^{\times}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega^{\alpha_{i}}(-x_{i})\omega^{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}(1-x_{i})\left(\frac{1}{q^{\times}}\sum_{\mu=0}^{q^{\times}-1}\omega(tx_{1}\cdots x_{d})^{\mu/q^{\times}}\right).

Finally, by character theory the latter term is
1
or
0
as
tx_{1}\cdots x_{d}=1
or not; so the sum becomes

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{n}\\
tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega^{\alpha_{i}}(-x_{i})\omega^{(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})}(1-x_{i}).\qed

4.6.
Hypergeometric
L
-series

To conclude, we globalize. Suppose that
\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
i,j=1,\dots,n
(a fundamental premise of our paper is to analyze degeneracies separately).
Let
t\in\mathbb{Q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\}
, and let
S(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)
be the set of primes dividing
m
together with the numerator or denominator of either
t
or
t-1
; we call these primes
bad
.

Let
p\not\in S(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
be a good prime, let
q=p^{f}=\Nm(\mathfrak{p})
for any prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{m}]
above
p
. Then in particular
q^{\times}\boldsymbol{\alpha},q^{\times}\boldsymbol{\beta}\subset\mathbb{Z}
. We recall (
Definition

4.4.1
,
Lemma

4.4.6
) the period-normalized hypergeometric sum
H_{q}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)\in K_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
. We define its exponential generating series

(4.6.1)

L_{q}(H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t),T)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\exp\left(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}-H_{q^{r}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)\frac{T^{r}}{r}\right)\in 1+TK_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}[[T]].

and the product

(4.6.2)

\displaystyle L_{p}(H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m}),T)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\langle p\rangle}L_{q}(H(k\boldsymbol{\alpha},k\boldsymbol{\beta},t),T^{f})\in 1+TK_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}[[T]].

Lemma 4.6.3
.

We have

(4.6.4)

L_{p}(H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m}),T)\in 1+T\mathbb{Q}[[T]]

and

(4.6.5)

L_{p}(H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m}),T)=L_{p}(H(k\boldsymbol{\alpha};k\boldsymbol{\beta},t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m}),T)

for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
coprime to
p
and
m
.

Proof.

Repeat the argument in
[
DKSSVW20
, Lemma 4.1.9]
(our sum is period-normalized, but the same argument applies).
∎

The following result due to Katz is foundational.

Theorem 4.6.6

(Katz)
.

L_{q}(H(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},T)\in 1+T\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})[T]
is a polynomial of degree
n
.

Proof.

See
[
Kat90
, Theorem 8.4.2]
. The finite field hypergeometric sum
[
Kat90
, (8.2.7)]
is the trace on a sheaf of rank
n
obtained from convolution. (More generally, see also Katz’s work on rigid local systems
[
Kat96
, Chapter 8]
.)
∎

5.
Point counts in cyclic covers and for the family
Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}

In this section, we count points on fibers of the family
X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
defined in (
3.2.4
), obtained as a partial compactification of the family defined in (
3.1.3
). We continue the notation from the previous sections.

5.1.
Character sums and primitive point counts

Throughout this section, let
q
be a prime power with
\gcd(m,q)=1
, and let
m_{q}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\gcd(m,q^{\times})
. To count points over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we require the following basic proposition which relates them to character sums.

Proposition 5.1.1
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
,

\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}:y^{m}=x\}=\#\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}:y^{m_{q}}=x\}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{q}}\omega^{k/m_{q}}(x).

Proof.

Straightforward character theory, e.g. generalizing Ireland–Rosen
[
IR90
, Proposition 8.1.5]
.
∎

In particular,
Proposition

5.1.1
implies that our point counts below over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
will only need to consider characters of order dividing
m_{q}
.

We organize the sum in
Proposition

5.1.1
into pieces as follows. Suppose that
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\}
. We define the
primitive
point count for
Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m_{q},t}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
by

(5.1.2)

P_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m_{q},t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/m_{q}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\})^{n}\\
tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{k/m_{q}}(f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}(x)).

Example 5.1.3
.

When
m_{q}=1
, we interpret the sum as over one index
k=0
, and

P_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},1,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#U_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#\{x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\})^{n}:tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\}.

We have

\#U_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=(q-2)^{n-1}-\#U_{n-1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

and
\#U_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=1
(since
t\neq 1
), so

P_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},1,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{i}(q-2)^{n-1-i}=\frac{(q-2)^{n}-(-1)^{n}}{q-1}.

Lemma 5.1.4
.

We have

\#Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m_{q},t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{d\mid m_{q}}P_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

Proof.

Applying
Proposition

5.1.1
and organizing according to
d=\gcd(k,m_{q})
, still with
m_{q}=\gcd(m,q^{\times})
we have

(5.1.5)

\displaystyle\#Y_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\})^{n}\\
tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{q}}\omega^{k/m_{q}}(f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m_{q}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/(m_{q}/d)\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\})^{n}\\
tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{dk/m_{q}}(f_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}(x))=\sum_{d\mid m_{q}}P_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m_{q}/d,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

which gives the result swapping
d
and
m_{q}/d
.
∎

Recall
Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}
from (
3.2.10
). Since
Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}
is a twist of
Y_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m,t}
, defining

(5.1.6)

P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/m_{q}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}(-1)^{(kq^{\times}/m)(\sum_{i\not\in I}a_{i})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\})^{\#I}\\
t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{k/m_{q}}(f_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I}}(x)),

in much the same way we conclude the following.

Corollary 5.1.7
.

We have

\#Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\#Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m_{q},I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{d\mid m_{q}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

Proof.

The proof is almost verbatim that of
Lemma

5.1.4
, after taking into consideration the twist.∎

Remark 5.1.8
.

We use the word
primitive
because we are motivated by thinking about these point counts as arising from the action of Frobenius on compactly supported étale cohomology; then the group scheme
\mu_{m}
acts, and we think of the above pieces as corresponding to those where this group acts by a primitive
d
th root of unity for
d\mid m_{q}
. Unfortunately, we are not able to make this analogy a method of proof, because we do not have the necessary control over this cohomological action—so we must be indirect instead.

5.2.
Setup

By definition, the scheme
X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
is naturally stratified based on whether or not
x_{i}=1
. We recall (
3.2.10
), that for
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
, the restriction of
X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m}
to the subset where
x_{i}\neq 1
for
i\in I
and
x_{i}=1
for

is given by
Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I}
by

3.2.11
. Hence

(5.2.1)

\#X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}\#Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}\#Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m_{q},I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

We remark and emphasize that this is exactly possible due to the construction of the partial compactification. In light of (
5.2.1
), without loss of generality we may suppose that
m=m_{q}=\gcd(m,q^{\times})
.

Next, we decompose the point counts on the open sets into their primitive parts using
Corollary

5.1.7
. By substituting into (
5.2.1
), recalling
\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m_{I},
in (

), and interchanging the order of summation, we obtain:

(5.2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{I}\#Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\sum_{d\mid m_{I}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}\\
t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{k\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}/d}(-1)\omega^{k/d}(f_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m_{I}}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
d\mid m_{I}\end{subarray}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}\\
t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{k\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}/d}(-1)\omega^{k/d}(f_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m_{I}}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
d\mid m_{I}\end{subarray}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

To proceed, we need to study the fibers of the map

.

Lemma 5.2.3
.

The map

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\{1,\dots,n\})

\displaystyle\to\{d:d\mid m\}

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\mapsto m_{I}=\gcd(\{b_{i}-a_{i}:i\not\in I\}\cup\{m\})

from the power set to the set of divisors of
m
is a map of posets: if
I\subseteq I^{\prime}
then
m_{I}\mid m_{I^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

This is straightforward upon unravelling the partial orderings.
∎

To organize the subsets
I
as in (
5.2.2
), it is natural to look at the subset of indices
i
such that

; in terms of posets, by definition this set is the maximal

such that

. However, it is more convenient to work with the complement (so a minimal set). We define

(5.2.4)

I_{d}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{i:d\nmid(b_{i}-a_{i})\}\subseteq[n]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{1,\dots,n\}.

We thus can write, using
Lemma

5.2.3
,

(5.2.5)

\sum_{I}\#Y^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{d\mid m}\sum_{I\supseteq I_{d}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

5.3.
Explicit hypergeometric point count

We now observe a remarkable Möbius-like cancellation; this theorem forms the technical heart of the paper.

Theorem 5.3.1
.

Let

Q^{\prime}_{d,t}=Q^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,t}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{I\supseteq I_{d}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

(a)

If
[n]=I_{d}
then

Q^{\prime}_{d,t}=-\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}H_{q}(k\boldsymbol{a}/d,k\boldsymbol{b}/d,t).

(b)

Suppose
[n]\neq I_{d}
and
a_{i}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}
for all
i,j\in[n]\setminus I_{d}
. Let
j
such that
j\notin[n]\setminus I_{d}
, and let
c=a_{j}
. Then

(5.3.2)

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{d,t}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\#I_{d}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\omega^{-kc/d}(t)(q^{\times})^{n-\#I_{d}-1}

\displaystyle\cdot\Biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
c-b_{i}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\psi_{\tfrac{k(a_{i}-c)}{d},\tfrac{k(b_{i}-a_{i})}{d}}\Biggr)\Biggl(\omega^{k\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}/d}(-1)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
c-b_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\omega^{k(a_{i}-c)/d}(-1)\Biggr).

(c)

If
[n]\neq I_{d}
and there exists
i,j\in[n]\setminus I_{d}
so that
a_{i}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}
, then
Q^{\prime}_{d,t}=0
.

Proof.

For (a), we start with the definition (
5.1.6
) and use the hypothesis to simplify to

\sum_{I\supseteq I_{d}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}=\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\smallsetminus\{0,1\})^{n}\\
tx_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i}).

Applying
Proposition

4.5.1
obtains the result.

For (b) and (c), choose some
j\in[n]\setminus I_{d}
, i.e.,
b_{j}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}
and write
I_{j}:=I\cup\{j\}
. Then

(5.3.3)

\displaystyle Q_{d,t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I_{d}\subseteq I\subseteq[n]\setminus\{j\}\end{subarray}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}.+P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I_{j},t}.

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\prod_{i\notin I}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-1)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}\\
t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i})\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I_{j}}\\
t\prod_{i\in I_{j}}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{ka_{j}/d}(x_{j})\prod_{i\in I}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}\sum_{k}\omega^{k\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}/d}(-1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}\\
x_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}\\
t\prod_{i\in I_{j}}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{ka_{j}/d}(x_{j})\prod_{i\in I}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i}).

The first equality is from partitioning the summation over when
I
contains
j
or not, the second is pulling out a common character, and the third is realizing one can combine the sums into a summation over all
x_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
since the first sum is when
x_{j}=1
. We now substitute
x_{j}=(t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i})^{-1}
and continue.

For each
I\supseteq I_{d}
and
k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, we have

(5.3.4)

\displaystyle\prod_{i\notin I}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-1)\sum_{(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}}\omega^{-ka_{j}/d}(t\textstyle{\prod}_{i\in I}x_{i})\prod_{i\in I}\omega^{ka_{i}/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega^{k(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i})/d}(-1)\omega^{-ka_{j}/d}(t)\sum_{(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}}\prod_{i\in I}\omega^{k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega^{k(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i})/d}(-1)\omega^{-ka_{j}/d}(t)\prod_{i\in I}\sum_{x_{i}\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})}\omega^{k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d}(-x_{i})\omega^{k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d}(1-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\omega^{k(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i})/d}(-1)\omega^{-ka_{j}/d}(t)\prod_{i\in I}J(k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d,k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d).

We focus on the quantity
J(k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d,k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d)
. This quantity can be made explicit. If
i\in I_{d}
, then we apply
Corollary

4.2.10
when
a_{j}-b_{i}\notin d\mathbb{Z}
and
Corollary

4.2.12
when
a_{j}-b_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}
to obtain

(5.3.5)

J(k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d,k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d)=\begin{cases}\psi_{\tfrac{k(a_{i}-a_{j})}{d},\tfrac{k(b_{i}-a_{i})}{d}},&\text{ if $b_{i}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}$};\\
-\omega^{k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d}(-1),&\text{ if $b_{i}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}$}.\end{cases}

If
i\notin I_{d}
then
b_{i}-a_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}
, so

(5.3.6)

J(k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d,k(b_{i}-a_{i})/d)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}}\omega^{k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d}(x)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if $a_{i}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}$};\\
q^{\times}-1&\text{ if $a_{i}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}$}.\end{cases}

Substituting these into (
5.3.4
), summing over
I
and
k
, and substituting back into (
5.3.3
) we obtain

(5.3.7)

\displaystyle Q_{d,t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\supseteq I_{d}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}\\
t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{k\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}/d}(-1)\omega^{k/d}(f_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m_{I}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\omega^{k\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}/d}(-1)\omega^{-ka_{j}/d}(t)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
a_{j}-b_{i}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\psi_{\tfrac{k(a_{i}-a_{j})}{d},\tfrac{k(b_{i}-a_{i})}{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
a_{j}-b_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}-\omega^{k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d}(-1)\right)\cdot\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I_{d}\subseteq I\subseteq[n]\setminus\{j\}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\setminus I_{d}\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}-1\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\setminus I_{d}\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}q^{\times}-1\right)

We focus on this second line. We can compute by rearranging the sum into a product of binomials

(5.3.8)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\\
I_{d}\subseteq I\subseteq[n]\setminus\{j\}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\setminus I_{d}\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}(-1)\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\setminus I_{d}\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}(q^{\times}-1)\right)=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\setminus I_{d}\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}((-1)+1)\right)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\setminus I_{d}\\
a_{i}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}((q^{\times}-1)+1)\right).

So the entire quantity vanishes if there exists
j,k\in[n]\setminus I_{d}
so that
a_{k}-a_{j}\notin d\mathbb{Z}
, proving (c). Otherwise, if
a_{k}-a_{j}\in d\mathbb{Z}
for all
j,k\in[n]\setminus I_{d}
, we obtain

(5.3.9)

\displaystyle Q_{d,t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\supseteq I_{d}}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\})^{I}\\
t\prod_{i\in I}x_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\omega^{k\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}/d}(-1)\omega^{k/d}(f_{\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\boldsymbol{b}_{I},m_{I}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\omega^{k\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}/d}(-1)\omega^{-ka_{j}/d}(t)(q^{\times})^{n-\#I_{d}-1}

\displaystyle\cdot\Biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
a_{j}-b_{i}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\psi_{\tfrac{k(a_{i}-a_{j})}{d},\tfrac{k(b_{i}-a_{i})}{d}}\Biggr)\Biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
a_{j}-b_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}-\omega^{k(a_{i}-a_{j})/d}(-1)\Biggr)

as claimed.
∎

Remark 5.3.10
.

We are led to think of the outcome of
Theorem

5.3.1
as some indication that in a degenerate setting, the hypergeometric motive becomes a Jacobi motive. The expression in
Theorem

5.3.1
(b) is complicated because we work over the torus, leaving pieces in every dimension. We leave the further pursuit of this notion of degeneration for future work.

5.4.
Proof of main result

We now are now staged to complete the proof of our main result, taking the point counts over finite fields and assembling them into the zeta function.

Recalling §
4.6
, let
S=S(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},t)
be the set of bad primes. Then by definition

(5.4.1)

\zeta_{S}(X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t},s)=\prod_{p\not\in S}Z_{p}(X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t},p^{-s})

where
Z_{p}(X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t},T)\in\mathbb{Q}(T)
is the zeta function (exponential generating series) over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

We recall (

4.3.4
) that Gauss sums give rise to Hecke Grossencharacters for
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
. Anticipating (
5.3.2
) (in
Theorem

5.3.1
(b)), we will need a twist, defined as follows. Let
d\mid m
, and suppose that
\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d
is isotypically degenerate for
c/d
. As before, we write
e(\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d)
for the multiplicity of
c/d
in
\boldsymbol{a}/d
. Then for
\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{d}]
above a good prime
p
,
we define

(5.4.2)

\psi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,t}(\mathfrak{p})=\omega^{-c/d}(t)\Biggl(\omega^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}/d}(-1)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
c-b_{i}\in d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\omega^{k(a_{i}-c)/d}(-1)\Biggr)\Biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{d}\\
c-b_{i}\notin d\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\psi_{\tfrac{a_{i}-c}{d},\tfrac{b_{i}-a_{i}}{d}}(\mathfrak{p})\Biggr)

where we recall (
5.2.4
)

I_{d}=\{i:d\nmid(b_{i}-a_{i})\}\subseteq[n]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{1,\dots,n\}.

Theorem 5.4.3
.

For
p
good, we have

Z_{p}(X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t},T)=\prod_{d\mid m}\begin{cases}L_{p}(H(\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d,t),\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d}),T),&\text{if $\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d$ is nondegenerate;}\\
Z_{p}((\Gm)^{e(\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d)-1},\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d}),T,\psi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,t})^{(-1)^{n-e(\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d)}},&\begin{aligned} &\parbox[t]{120.7521pt}{if $\boldsymbol{a}/d,\boldsymbol{b}/d$ is isotypically \\
\phantom{yup} degenerate;}\end{aligned}\\
1,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
q=p^{r}
with
r\geq 1
. We get organized by (
5.2.1
) and note that after that we need to substitute
m_{q}=\gcd(m,q^{\times})
for
m
. Then plug in (
5.2.5
) to get

(5.4.4)

\#X_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},m,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{d\mid m_{q}}\sum_{I\supseteq I_{d}}P^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b},d,I,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\sum_{d\mid m_{q}}Q^{\prime}_{d,t}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),

where the latter suppresses notation, staging for
Theorem

5.3.1
. Thus

(5.4.5)

\displaystyle\log Z_{p}(X,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\#X(\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})\frac{T^{r}}{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\biggl(\sum_{d\mid\gcd(m,p^{r}-1)}Q_{d,t}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})\biggr)\frac{T^{r}}{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\\
d\mid(p^{r}-1)\end{subarray}}Q_{d,t}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}})\frac{T^{r}}{r}.

Now let
f_{d}
be the order of
p
modulo
d
, and let
q_{d}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}p^{f_{d}}
; then
d\mid(p^{r}-1)
if and only if
f_{d}\mid r
. Exponentiating (
5.4.5
) and substituting then gives

(5.4.6)

\displaystyle Z_{p}(X,T)

\displaystyle=\prod_{d\mid m}\exp\biggl(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}Q_{d,t}^{\prime}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{d}^{r}})\frac{(T^{f_{d}})^{r}}{r}\biggr).

To finish, we plug in
Theorem

5.3.1
in three cases. In the first case, we recognize the sum over Galois conjugates in part (a) from (
4.6.2
). In the second case, we apply part (b) having defined the Hecke Grossencharacter in (
5.4.2
), again with its Galois conjugates. The third case gives no contribution.
∎
