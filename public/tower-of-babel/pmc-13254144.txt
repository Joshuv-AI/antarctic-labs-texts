Machine learning driven discovery of low modulus biomedical titanium alloys for additive manufacturing
Authors: Jinlong Su, Fulin Jiang, Jin Wu, Yuehua Li, Ri Han, Lu Wang, Raymond Chung Wen Wong, Jian Wang, Swee Leong Sing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing alloys for additive manufacturing opens opportunities for next-generation biomedical orthopaedic implants. However, most biomedical alloys currently in use are legacy compositions that cannot fully harness the potential of additive manufacturing. Here, we present a machine-learning-driven computational framework for discovering additive-manufacturing-specific β-titanium alloys with low modulus, optimised printability, mechanical and corrosion properties, and biocompatibility. Using this framework, an additive-manufacturing-specific Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-Sn alloy was designed and validated via laser powder bed fusion. The alloy shows good printability and reduced sensitivity to keyhole pore formation compared with commercial Ti-6Al-4V. Under optimised process parameters, the as-built alloy combines a low Young’s modulus (~42.7 GPa) with high ductility (~30.9%), attributed to its metastable β-phase microstructure, cubic <001> texture, and reduced dislocation density. In this work, we show that this machine-learning-driven approach can facilitate the efficient design of biomedical β-titanium alloys, offering promising opportunities for advancing additive manufacturing in medical and related applications.

Introduction

Metals and alloys play an indispensable role in modern clinical practice, offering a unique combination of mechanical strength, biocompatibility and corrosion resistance—traits essential for long-term load-bearing orthopaedic implants1. However, the Young’s moduli of conventional biomedical implant alloys, such as 316 L stainless steel (~190 GPa), Co-Cr-Mo alloy (~220 GPa) and Ti-6Al-4V (Ti64) alloy (~110 GPa) are significantly higher than that of human cortical bone (~10–30 GPa)2. This stiffness mismatch disrupts physiological load transfer, leading to localised bone resorption and eventual implant loosening. In response to this challenge, β-Ti alloys have garnered increasing clinical attention due to their lower Young’s modulus (~30–80 GPa), superior biocompatibility and greater flexibility3. Furthermore, additive manufacturing (AM) expands the potential of β-Ti alloys in orthopaedic implant applications by allowing precise control over implant geometry and local properties, which are critical for osseointegration and long-term clinical success.

Despite these advantages provided by AM, the majority of state-of-the-art biomedical β-Ti alloys used in AM, such as Ti-13Nb-13Zr4, Ti-35Nb-7Zr-5Ta5 and Ti-24Nb-4Zr-8Sn6, are legacy compositions originally developed for conventional manufacturing routes. These alloys typically rely on time-consuming and energy-intensive thermo-mechanical treatments to achieve their required microstructures and properties. In contrast, the intrinsic near-net-shape capability of AM limits the use of extensive post-processing, making alloy composition and AM process parameters the primary factors influencing the microstructure, mechanical performance and osseointegration. Therefore, there is an urgent need to design advanced β-Ti alloys specifically tailored for AM, so as to realise its full potential in biomedical applications.

Earlier studies have primarily focused on the development of β-Ti alloys using in-situ alloying AM techniques7,8. For example, Sing et al. reported9 that laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) of blended Ti-50Ta elemental powders produces a β-Ti matrix containing dispersed Ta particles and equiaxed β grains, achieving a relatively low Young’s modulus (~75.77 GPa). Similar microstructures with partially-melted particles have also been reported in LPBF-built Ti-Nb10 and Ti-Mo11 alloys, indicating that in-situ alloying AM is not suitable for biomedical β-Ti alloy development due to the refractory nature of the biocompatible isomorphous β-stabilisers (e.g., Nb, Ta, Mo). In addition, Alabort et al. reported12 an “alloy-by-design” strategy that integrates high-throughput thermodynamic calculations and property screening to develop β-Ti alloys for AM. Nevertheless, this approach depends on computationally intensive thermodynamic calculations and remains limited in capturing the complex trade-offs among different properties.

These challenges highlight the need for a systematic, efficient and predictive framework to reveal the composition–property relationships of AM β-Ti alloys. In this regard, machine learning (ML) offers significant potential in rapidly learning complex, multidimensional relationships from limited datasets while reducing reliance on resource-intensive trial-and-error experimentation13,14. Coupled with physical modelling and multi-objective optimisation, ML has the capability to facilitate the accelerated discovery of alloys with optimised properties15. To date, however, the integrated application of ML, physics-based modelling and multi-objective optimisation to design biomedical alloys, especially for AM, remains largely underexplored.

In this work, we develop an integrated alloy design framework that couples CALculation of PHAse Diagram (CALPHAD), ML, physics-based modelling and multi-objective optimisation to identify β-Ti compositions tailored for AM. The framework incorporates CALPHAD-informed solidification descriptors and physics-based property models to simultaneously optimise printability and key performance criteria. Based on this framework, a previously unexplored Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-Sn composition is discovered, which has then been validated via LPBF. The LPBF process parameters were fine-tuned to achieve optimal performance of the designed β-Ti alloy. The printability, microstructure, mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and cell viability were systematically evaluated, with commercial Ti64 as the benchmark. Overall, this study not only establishes a general and efficient route for designing AM-specific biomedical β-Ti alloys, but demonstrates a promising candidate for load-bearing orthopaedic implant applications as well.

Results

ML-based surrogate models for the composition-metallurgy relations

Herein, Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-Sn was selected as the model system for load-bearing orthopaedic implants16. The Nb and Ta were adopted to stabilise the β-phase considering their excellent biocompatibility and favourable immunomodulatory effects17. Zr, as a neutral element, was introduced to provide solid-solution strengthening, enhance corrosion resistance, and stimulate osteogenic differentiation, thereby accelerating osseointegration16. Meanwhile, Sn was incorporated to provide solid-solution strengthening while suppressing the formation of the undesirable brittle ω phase, thereby ensuring sufficient ductility and reducing Young’s modulus17.

Figure 1 presents the schematic of the proposed alloy design framework, which integrates ML, physical modelling, and multi-objective optimisation. Firstly, a design-of-experiments (DoE) table was listed to define the initial set of alloy compositions. Specifically, Nb and Ta were varied from 0 to 30 wt.% in 6 wt.% increments, while Zr and Sn were varied from 0 to 10 wt.% in 5 wt.% increments. The upper limits for Nb and Ta (30 wt.%) were selected to ensure adequate β-phase stability without imposing excessive density or cost. Similarly, the upper limits for Zr and Sn (10 wt.%) were chosen as their effective compositional ranges for reducing Young’s modulus, while avoiding the formation of brittle phases (e.g., ω, Ti₂Sn, Ti₃Sn)18. Considering the ultrahigh solidification rate of AM (105–107 K s−1 for LPBF19), the Scheil model was employed to simulate the non-equilibrium solidification behaviour of AM. Key physical properties related to non-equilibrium solidification, including growth restriction factor (GRF), solidification freezing range (SFR), and hot-cracking susceptibility (HCS), were evaluated, as presented in Supplementary Note 1. GRF is inversely proportional to grain size, thereby influencing grain boundary strengthening and mechanical strength20,21. A larger SFR promotes higher undercooling and nucleation rates, which suppresses columnar growth, ultimately improving mechanical isotropy22. Besides, as solidification cracking is controlled predominantly by the terminal stage of solidification, the HCS is evaluated following the classical Clyne-Davies model, defined as the ratio between the time required to solidify the final ~10% of liquid and the time required for the solid fraction to increase from 40 to 90%23. Reducing HCS is thus essential to ensure defect-free printing. Collectively, these parameters serve as effective proxies to bridge alloy composition with printability and properties. An example of the CALPHAD simulation is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In total, CALPHAD simulations were conducted on the 320 Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-Sn compositions, and the extracted properties were stored in a dataset to facilitate efficient data retrieval.

Once the database was built, ML-based surrogate modelling was then adopted to accelerate the alloy design process, thereby enabling the subsequent high-throughput predictions and the multi-objective optimisation process. Four typical ML models—K-nearest neighbours (KNN), AdaBoost, decision tree (DT) and random forest (RF)—were employed as surrogate models considering their good interpretability and robustness with small to moderate datasets24. Figure 2a presents a Spearman correlation matrix, illustrating the relationships between alloy composition and resulting physical properties. Notably, all the elements exhibit a positive correlation with GRF and SFR, with Nb and Ta exerting the strongest influence, followed by Sn and Zr. The dataset was then randomly split into training and testing subsets using a 7:3 ratio. Physical models for composition-properties relations

To link the composition-property relations, physical models were then used to estimate three key properties for load-bearing orthopaedic implants—Young’s modulus, yield strength, and corrosion resistance (Fig. 2). Among them, a low Young’s modulus is highly desirable for biomedical implant applications as it reduces stress shielding, preserving natural bone loading to prevent bone resorption or implant loosening, while also enhancing osteoblast proliferation and differentiation for faster and more effective bone regeneration25. Multi-objective optimisation and decision-making

In the previous sections, five objective functions—YS, SFR, Ip, HCS, and BF—were formulated using physics-based models. To optimise these potentially conflicting objectives, a multi-objective genetic algorithm (MOGA) approach based on the non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) was employed38. The NSGA-II method was selected for its proven capability to efficiently approximate the Pareto front and maintain diversity among solutions39. The MOGA aimed to obtain a set of Pareto-optimal compositions in which no single objective can be improved without compromising others. The optimisation problem was formulated with four decision variables representing the alloying element contents, each constrained within predefined limits (Nb and Ta ∈ [0, 30]; Zr and Sn ∈ [0, 10], wt%). The objectives are defined as improving YS, SFR and Ip, while reducing HCS and BF, which corresponds with the objectives of enhancing strength, reducing mechanical anisotropy, increasing corrosion resistance, improving printability and lowering Young’s modulus.

Meanwhile, two domain knowledge constraints were imposed to the MOGA process to ensure both economic feasibility and microstructural stability of the designed alloy: (1) composition constraint: the total content of alloying elements was restricted to less than 50 wt%, ensuring that the material cost remains reasonable and maintaining the alloy as Ti-based; (2) physical property constraint: the martensite start temperature (MS) was constrained to between 0 °C and 200 °C to achieve the desirable metastable β phase under the LPBF-built state. The NSGA-II method was implemented with a population size of 200 and evolved over 500 generations. Simulated binary crossover (SBX) and polynomial mutation operators were applied to enhance solution diversity and exploration within the search space. Tournament selection with a tournament size of 6 was employed to ensure robust competition among individuals, improving convergence toward the Pareto front. The final set of solutions represents optimal trade-offs among the five objective functions, offering various composition candidates. To select the representative composition for experimental validation, the TOPSIS method was then employed as the decision-making tool to identify the optimal composition that simultaneously balances printability, mechanical properties and corrosion resistance. TOPSIS is a multi-criteria decision-making method that ranks alternatives based on their relative closeness to an ideal solution, considering both the best and worst possible scenarios to ensure a balanced trade-off among conflicting objectives. Initially, the same weights were assigned to the five objectives, yielding an optimal solution of Ti-15.1Nb-15.1Ta-9.6Zr-9.5Sn, as shown in the grey dotted line in Fig. 3a. Overall, this composition has relatively favourable YS (1079.2 MPa), SFR (281.5 K) and HCS (0.19). However, its BF value is 17.71, which falls in the mid-range among all the alloy candidates, suggesting it is not a favourable choice for achieving low modulus.

To further optimise the alloy composition, a scatter plot matrix of the Pareto front (obtained using the NSGA-II, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4) was generated to elucidate the underlying relationships between different properties pairs. Notably, a strong positive correlation was observed between YS and Ip, as well as between HCS and -SFR. As a result, the objectives can be divided into three to avoid overweighting: (1) YS & Ip, (2) SFR & HCS, (3) BF. Following this analysis, a further TOPSIS selection is conducted, assigning a weight of 1/3 to BF, 1/3 to YS & Ip, 1/3 to SFR & HCS. This yields a more balanced alloy composition, Ti-15.8Nb-9.2Ta-10.0Zr-7.7Sn (as shown in the green dotted line in Fig. 3a), hereafter referred to as the ANTZS alloy. The 3D plots in Fig. 3b, c illustrate that the ANTZS alloy exhibits superior comprehensive properties across the vast compositional space. A more straightforward two-dimensional comparison of various objectives is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, which indicates that the selected ANTZS alloy outperforms other candidates within the Ti-xNb-yTa-zZr-wSn compositional space (x = 0–30 wt%, y = 0–30 wt%, z = 0–10 wt%, w = 0–10 wt%) and was therefore selected for experimental validation.

Printability and microstructural characterisation

To validate the alloy design framework, the ANTZS alloy was synthesised by casting first, followed by the preparation of pre-alloyed powder using gas atomisation. The gas-atomised powder was then processed via LPBF to fabricate the single-track and bulk samples. Firstly, single-track printing was used to screen the process parameters preliminarily (Supplementary Note 2). Initial experiments focused on analysing variations in melt pool width (Fig. 4a) and depth (Fig. 4b) under different processing parameters. Based on the single-track printing results (Supplementary Fig. 6), bulk ANTZS samples were fabricated within a more reasonable energy density range at laser powers of 100–400 W and scan speeds of 500–1500 mm s−1. The polished cross-sections of the LPBF-built ANTZS alloys under these process parameters are shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, where the lack-of-fusion, process window, and keyhole regimes can be distinguished. For comparison, Ti64 alloy was fabricated using the same process parameter sets, and the corresponding cross-sectional optical microscopy images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8. Overall, the ANTZS alloy exhibits a slightly wider process window (relative density >99%) than Ti64, as shown in Fig. 4c, d. Notably, ANTZS shows reduced sensitivity to keyhole pore formation in the high energy density region (lower right region of Fig. 4c, d). Notably, the microstructure evolution of the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy samples follows a three-step process: from casting ingot to gas-atomised powder and ultimately to LPBF built. Initially, the as-cast ANTZS alloy (Fig. 5a) exhibits a coarse-grained microstructure with quasi-random grain orientations. The subsequent gas atomisation process results in the formation of spherical powder particles (Fig. 5b). A closer characterisation to the powder’s internal microstructure (Fig. 5c) reveals the presence of fine β-phase (yellow arrows) and α“-phase (white arrows), which has also been validated by the XRD analysis in Fig. 5d. Upon processing via LPBF, the β-phase becomes the dominant phase in the LPBF built (Fig. 5d). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings of the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy (Fig. 5e) highlight the formation of columnar β-grains along the build direction (BD), with uniformly distributed alloying elements. The three-dimensional electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) figure and {100} pole figures in Fig. 5f–h reveal a unique cubic texture aligned along the BD and transverse direction (TD) axes, which can be attributed to the preferential grain alignment induced by the strong thermal gradient and the applied 90° interlayer rotation scan strategy (as discussed in Supplementary Note 3)42. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis demonstrates that the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy exhibits a single-phase body-centred cubic β-Ti microstructure (Fig. 5i–k). Dislocations formed as a result of the thermal loading of LPBF are uniformly distributed in the grain interior regions (Fig. 5i). Mechanical properties under varying process parameters

The ANTZS alloy was fabricated via LPBF under various process parameters to optimise its mechanical properties (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Fig. 10). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, the Young’s modulus decreases while ductility increases with increasing volumetric energy density. Considering the combined requirements of low Young’s modulus, high ductility, and high relative density, the optimal LPBF process parameter for the ANTZS alloy is identified as a laser power of 400 W and a scan speed of 750 mm s−1. Figure 6a presents the engineering stress-strain curves of the as-cast ANTZS alloy and the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy fabricated under the optimal process parameters. Notably, the strength and ductility of the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy are both superior to its as-cast counterpart. This improvement can be attributed to microstructural refinement and the formation of a metastable β-phase microstructure during the rapid solidification of LPBF. In contrast, the as-cast ANTZS alloy displays a double-yielding behaviour, arising from strain-induced β → α″ martensitic transformation (Supplementary Fig. 11). Besides, the as-cast ANTZS alloy exhibits a higher Young’s modulus than the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy (Supplementary Table 4), primarily due to the combined effects of weaker crystallographic texture and the presence of α″ martensite. To reveal the underlying mechanisms governing the low Young’s modulus, the crystallographic texture of LPBF-built ANTZS alloys was characterised using EBSD (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Fig. 13). Then, the Young’s modulus maps were generated based on the grain orientations and elastic constants of the ANTZS alloy, as presented in Fig. 6c. Notably, the low modulus region expands as laser power increases and scan speed decreases, which corresponds well with the tensile test result in Supplementary Fig. 9b. In addition, Fig. 6d indicates that the p<001> increases monotonously with volumetric energy density (VED)59,60, which can be attributed to preferential reorientation and growth of 〈001〉-oriented grains along the direction of maximum heat flux (i.e. towards the BD) during layer-by-layer remelting and rapid solidification in LPBF61. Additionally, Fig. 6e indicates that Young’s modulus of LPBF-built ANTZS alloys decreases monotonically with increasing p <001>. This monotonic dependence can be attributed to elastic anisotropy in cubic metals, where bonding stiffness (and thus the corresponding elastic modulus) along the <001> direction is comparatively lower than along other principal crystallographic directions42,62. Meanwhile, it is worth noting that the 90° interlayer rotation scan strategy aligns the <001> orientation family parallel to the X, Y, and Z axes, thereby enabling a low Young’s modulus along all three principal directions42,59 (a schematic diagram of texture formation is provided in Supplementary Fig. 14). Additionally, the ANTZS alloy exhibits a high tolerance for keyhole pore formation (Supplementary Fig. 7), allowing the low-modulus texture to be tuned without a pronounced increase in keyhole porosity. To benchmark the processing effect, ANTZS alloy fabricated by spark plasma sintering (SPS) was characterised in Supplementary Fig. 15, which exhibits an equiaxed grain microstructure with weak crystallographic texture. It is found that the Young’s modulus of SPS-fabricated ANTZS (55.2 ± 8.4 GPa) is much higher than that of the optimal LPBF-built ANTZS alloy (42.7 ± 2.1 GPa), further highlighting the intrinsic advantages of AM (i.e., steep thermal gradient and high cooling rate) in achieving low-modulus microstructures for the ANTZS alloy.

Additionally, the ductility of the LPBF ANTZS alloy also increases with increasing VED, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 9c. This trend is further supported by the fracture morphology analysis in Supplementary Fig. 10, where the sample printed at the highest VED exhibits the deepest dimpled morphology. This can be attributed to the reduced dislocation density at higher VED, as indicated by the XRD peak broadening analysis in Supplementary Fig. 16a, b. Consistently, TEM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 16c–h) reveals both a lower dislocation density and homogeneous dislocation distribution at higher VED, which is conducive to sustained work hardening and delayed strain localisation. Collectively, the combination of reduced dislocation density and improved dislocation uniformity accounts for the increased ductility under higher VED. Overall, the unique combination of excellent ductility and low Young’s modulus makes the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy a promising candidate for biomedical implant applications, particularly in scenarios where flexibility is essential.

Direct seeding of cells onto the metal plates revealed notable differences in cellular behaviour of the alloy samples (Fig. 7c). Cells cultured on LPBF-built ANTZS alloy surface exhibited the highest cell density, surpassing as-cast ANTZS alloy and LPBF-built Ti64. SEM observations further confirmed that MC3T3-E1 cells displayed a healthy, well-spread morphology with distinct filopodia extensions on all sample surfaces, indicative of strong cellular attachment and favourable biocompatibility. Notably, intercellular interactions were more prominent on LPBF-built ANTZS and as-cast ANTZS alloy surfaces, whereas LPBF-built Ti64 showed relatively moderate interactions. The enhanced intercellular interactions observed on the ANTZS alloy surfaces could be attributed to the presence of biocompatible elements Nb, Ta, Zr, and Sn, contributing to favourable cellular responses63. Overall, all the samples demonstrated favourable cytocompatibility in the alloy extract medium. When directly cultured on alloy surfaces, the LPBF-built ANTZS alloy exhibited the optimal performance, both in terms of higher cell density and enhanced cell-to-cell interactions, followed by the as-cast ANTZS alloy, with LPBF-built Ti64 alloy showing comparatively lower efficacy.

Supplementary Fig. 17 presents the comparison of the electrochemical corrosion behaviour of LPBF ANTZS and LPBF Ti64 through polarisation and EIS tests in the 0.9 wt % NaCl solution, with the results summarised in Supplementary Table 5. Overall, the LPBF Ti64 exhibits slightly better corrosion resistance than the LPBF ANTZS, as reflected by its lower corrosion current density (icorr = 0.0113 μA cm−2) and higher charge-transfer resistance (Rct = 5.15 × 106 Ω), compared with the LPBF ANTZS alloy (icorr = 0.0215 μA cm−2, Rct = 4.35 × 106 Ω). The slightly lower corrosion resistance of LPBF ANTZS could be attributed to factors not captured by composition-only ML predictions, including differences in passive-film nucleation and growth kinetics between β-dominated ANTZS and α′-dominated Ti64, as well as LPBF-induced residual stresses and minor porosity variations. These factors deserve further investigation, as they may provide guidance to the design of corrosion-resistant Ti alloys for AM. Nevertheless, the consistent trends observed in both the polarisation curves and the impedance spectra confirm that LPBF ANTZS exhibits stable passive behaviour and adequate corrosion resistance in physiological environments, supporting its potential as a promising alloy for biomedical implant applications.

In summary, this work establishes an integrated and efficient alloy design framework that couples CALPHAD-informed metallurgical descriptors, ML surrogate models, physics-based property modelling and multi-objective optimisation to accelerate the discovery of biomedical titanium alloys for AM. By systematically exploring the Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-Sn compositional space, the framework discovers an ANTZS alloy, which simultaneously optimises printability, mechanical properties and corrosion resistance. Experimental validation via LPBF confirms that the designed ANTZS alloy exhibits good printability, low Young’s modulus (~42.7 ± 2.1 GPa), and high ductility (~30.9 ± 3.2%). Cytocompatibility tests demonstrate excellent biocompatibility of the LPBF-built ANTZS, with enhanced cell attachment and proliferation compared with Ti64. More importantly, since the ANTZS alloy is designed for LPBF, its modulus can be further reduced by fabricating lattice metamaterials. Coupled with its excellent cytocompatibility and acceptable corrosion resistance, the synergy of low modulus and high ductility positions ANTZS as a compelling AM material for bone-tissue-engineering scaffolds and load-bearing orthopaedic implants. Overall, this study not only delivers a β-Ti alloy for orthopaedic implants but also provides a generalisable, data-driven route for designing next-generation AM-specific biomedical alloys.

Methods

Dataset preparation and alloy design process

In this study, CALPHAD simulations were employed to generate the raw data for the alloy design. In the CALPHAD simulations, Ti was selected as the base element, with Nb and Ta varied from 0 to 30 wt.% in 6 wt.% increments, and Zr and Sn varied from 0 to 10 wt.% in 5 wt.% increments. 320 CALPHAD simulations were carried out using the Scheil module in the Thermo-Calc 2023a software with TCTI1 database64. The classical Scheil solidification model was adopted and all elements were treated as normal elements without fast diffusers. The HCS, GRF and SFR data were extracted from the Scheil curves based on the Supplementary Eqs. 1–3.

ML surrogates were trained to map alloy composition to HCS, GRF and SFR. The dataset was split into 70% training and 30% test sets. Features were standardised using a standard scaler fit on the training set and applied to all splits. For each target, the model was selected among AdaBoost, DT, KNN and RF regressors using GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation and R2 as the scoring metric; performance was summarised with test-set R² and mean absolute error (MAE). Predicted versus actual values were visualised in parity plots to confirm the fidelity of the surrogates, and the best-performing estimators—RF for HCS and SFR and KNN for GRF—were selected as the final ML surrogate models. All ML computations in this study were performed on a standard laptop with an Intel® Core™ i5-11260H CPU and 16 GB RAM, which required approximately 5 mins of computation time.

A multi-objective genetic algorithm based on NSGA-II was employed to optimise the alloy composition with respect to five competing objectives: maximising YS, SFR and Ip (as a proxy for corrosion resistance), while minimising HCS and BF (as a proxy for Young’s modulus)65. The four decision variables are the Nb, Ta, Zr and Sn contents (in wt.%), each bounded within [0–30], [0–30], [0–10] and [0–10] wt.%. For a given composition, HCS and SFR were predicted from the RF surrogates, whereas GRF was provided by the KNN surrogate and subsequently incorporated into the YS model to estimate grain-boundary strengthening. The Ip and BF values were calculated from phenomenological electronic and thermodynamic relationships. Two additional constraints were imposed during optimisation: (i) the martensite start temperature is restricted to 0 °C – 200 °C to ensure a metastable β phase in the LPBF as-built state, and (ii) the total alloying content (Nb + Ta + Zr + Sn) is limited to <50 wt.% to maintain a Ti-based alloy with reasonable cost. NSGA-II is implemented with a population size of 200, 100 offspring per generation, simulated binary crossover (probability 0.9, η = 15), polynomial mutation (probability 1.0, η = 20), tournament selection (tournament size 6), and 500 generations, yielding a diverse Pareto front of non-dominated compositions.

Finally, to select a single optimal composition from the Pareto-front, the TOPSIS decision-making method was applied. The five objective values of the Pareto-optimal solutions were first normalised using min–max scaling and then weighted using a predefined vector, assigning a higher weight to BF (1/3) relative to the remaining four objectives (each 1/6). This weighting scheme reflects the priority of achieving a low Young’s modulus while maintaining adequate strength, printability, and corrosion resistance. The composition with the highest TOPSIS score was subsequently selected as the optimal alloy design and subjected to experimental validation.

Raw material preparation and additive manufacturing

The as-cast ANTZS alloy rod, with a nominal composition of Ti-15.8Nb-9.2Ta-10.0Zr-7.7Sn, was initially synthesised via ingot casting using high-purity metal raw materials. The as-cast ANTZS alloy rod was then subjected to gas atomisation to produce pre-alloyed powder, which was subsequently sieved to obtain the optimal particle size range of 15–53 μm for LPBF printing. LPBF experiments were conducted using an AconityMINI system with a laser spot size of 80 μm. Both single-track and bulk samples were printed. Single-track printing was performed with laser power ranging from 200 to 400 W and scan speed ranging from 250 to 1500 mm s−1. Bulk samples were fabricated using a laser power of 200–400 W and a scan speed of 500–1500 mm s−1. The hatch spacing is fixed as 100 μm, and the layer thickness is fixed as 30 μm. Additionally, LPBF-fabricated Ti64 alloy was adopted as the control group, as Ti64 remains the gold-standard alloy for orthopaedic implants and thus provides an essential baseline for evaluating new alloy developments.

Microstructure characterisation and mechanical property testing

Light optical microscopy (OM) was employed to examine internal defects in the alloy samples following standard sample preparation. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), in conjunction with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), were used to characterise the internal microstructure and phase constituents. Kroll’s reagent was used to reveal the microstructure of the ANTZS alloys. EBSD analysis was conducted at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV with a step size of 0.5 μm using an Oxford Symmetry 2 detector. The EBSD acquisition was performed using a binning of 1 × 1 and a frame averaging of 2, and the crystallographic indexing was based on the conventional Hough transform method. A one-step auto-cleaning routine was applied in AZtecCrystal 2.1 to remove zero-solution pixels from the EBSD maps. The elastic constants of the β-Ti phase in the ANTZS alloy were estimated using Thermo-Calc software as C11 = 131.1 GPa, C12 = 101.3 GPa, and C44 = 35.6 GPa. Based on these elastic constants and the crystallographic orientation determined from EBSD, the Young’s modulus map was generated using the Materials Properties module in AZtecCrystal 2.1, in which the elastic modulus of each grain is calculated according to its orientation-dependent elastic response.

Tensile specimens with a gauge dimension of 10 × 2 × 1.5 mm and an overall length of 30 mm were adopted. Tensile tests were performed on an Instron 5982 machine at a loading rate of 0.5 mm min⁻¹. To ensure repeatability, three parallel specimens were tested for each condition.

Electrochemical corrosion measurement

Electrochemical tests were conducted using a CHI electrochemical workstation in a conventional three-electrode configuration. The alloy specimens were mounted as the working electrode with an exposed area of approximately 1 cm2, while a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and a platinum mesh were employed as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. All measurements were performed in 0.9 wt% NaCl solution at room temperature. The open-circuit potential (OCP) was tested for 30 min before measurements. EIS tests were performed at OCP using a 5 mV sinusoidal voltage perturbation in the frequency range of 10 kHz to 0.01 Hz, with logarithmic spacing of 10 points per decade. Potentiodynamic polarisation curves were obtained by sweeping the potential from OCP − 0.5 V to OCP + 1.8 V at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1.

Cell viability evaluation

The MC3T3-E1 preosteoblast cell line was obtained from ATCC (CRL-2593) and cultured using Alpha-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco, USA). The cells were subsequently incubated under controlled conditions at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 95% humidity and 5% CO2. The cell seeding density is 50,000 cells ml−1 and the passage number was 8 (counting from P0 upon purchase from ATCC). The negative control (NC) group was exposed to a complete medium containing α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Metal extracts were prepared according to the ISO 10993 standard by incubating the materials for 72 h in α-MEM medium without FBS or P/S under standard cell culture conditions. Then a complete experimental treatment medium was prepared by adding FBS and P/S. A total of 5000 cells were seeded in each well of a 96-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight in a culture medium. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with the respective control or metal extraction medium. Each group underwent four repetitions. After incubation for 48 h, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead staining kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The viability assay was conducted using a microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany).

The adhesion and morphology of the MC3T3- E1 cells seeded on 10 mm circular metal discs in 48-well plates were examined after 48 h of incubation using SEM (JSM-6701F FEG SEM). All alloy samples were ground sequentially, starting with coarse sandpaper and progressing up to 2000-grit paper to achieve a consistent surface roughness across all alloys. The samples were treated with 75% ethanol for 30 mins for disinfection, followed by rinsing with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) to eliminate any residue ethanol before cell seeding. After 48 hrs, the cell medium was removed and samples were washed using PBS. Then cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and 4 °C for 2 h. The samples were progressively washed with ethanol at ascending concentrations (25, 50, 75, 95, and 100%), followed by a critical point drying session. Finally, the samples underwent gold sputtering to examine under SEM.
