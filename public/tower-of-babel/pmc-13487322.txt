Association between stress hyperglycemia ratio and 72-hour delirium in critically ill patients: A retrospective cohort study from the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Haoming Huang, Wenshan Xu, Jinxia Wu, Peitao Li, Yaxin Wu, Weixuan Zhao, Jianyi Zhang, Ningyi Zhang, Jiawei Fan, Ping Zeng, Xiliang Huang, Yuan Liao, Wenying Zhu, Xiaozhi Gu, Shaoce Bai, Jinjian Wu, Shujuan Qi, Zhiling He, Chunhua Fan, Quanfu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStress hyperglycemia, quantified by the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), reflects the acute glycemic response relative to chronic glycemic status. Its association with intensive care unit (ICU)-acquired delirium and the mechanistic pathway linking SHR to mortality remain poorly characterized.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study of 7397 critically ill adults from the MIMIC-IV database, we examined the association between SHR and 72-h delirium using multivariable Cox proportional-hazards regression. Restricted cubic spline analysis was used to characterize the dose-response relationship. Mediation analyses were employed to evaluate whether delirium mediates the effect of SHR on 28-day and 365-day all-cause mortality, with E-value analysis to assess robustness to unmeasured confounding.ResultsOverall, 1259 patients developed delirium within 72 h of ICU admission, with a median onset of 11.25 h. Patients in the highest SHR quartile had a significantly higher hazard of delirium than those in the lowest quartile (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.04-1.45; P = 0.014), with an approximately linear dose-response relationship. The association was consistent across GCS thresholds, glucose-measurement windows, diabetes/HbA1c strata, and subgroups. Delirium significantly mediated the indirect effect of SHR on both 28-day and 365-day mortality, whereas no significant direct effect of SHR on mortality was observed.ConclusionsElevated SHR is independently associated with an increased risk of ICU-acquired delirium in a linear, dose-dependent manner, and delirium fully mediates the indirect effect of SHR on short- and long-term mortality. These findings support SHR as a readily obtainable biomarker for early delirium risk stratification among critically ill patients.

Introduction

Delirium in the intensive care unit (ICU) is a prevalent neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by acute changes in attention, awareness, and cognition [1,2]. The reported prevalence of delirium in ICU settings varies widely, ranging from 20% to 80%, and a one-day point-prevalence study across 104 ICUs in 11 countries (DECCA study) reported a delirium prevalence of 32.3% [3]. In non-mechanically ventilated ICU patients, the incidence of delirium can approach 50%, while mechanically ventilated patients exhibit considerably higher rates, ranging from 60% to 80% [4]. It is associated with a cascade of adverse outcomes, including long-term cognitive impairment [5], prolonged mechanical ventilation, extended ICU and hospital length of stay, greater short- and long-term mortality [1,6]. Economic modeling estimates that this syndrome incurs annual healthcare expenditures of US$38 billion to US$152 billion [[7], [8], [9]], representing a substantial burden on global medical resources. Thus, early recognition of delirium and targeted intervention are critical in ICU management.

Stress-induced hyperglycemia is an acute elevation in blood glucose levels in response to various stressors [10]. Although the phenomenon is most recognized in individuals with pre-existing diabetes, non-diabetic patients undergoing significant physiological stress may also display elevated glucose levels [11]. It was suggested that in the general hospitalized population, hyperglycemia contributes to the onset of delirium through multiple mechanisms, including the induction of neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier, ultimately contributing to increased all-cause mortality [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. However, glycemic measures are often confounded by pre-existing diabetes status or glycemic control therapies, making it difficult to isolate the pure acute stress response. To address these limitations, Roberts et al. introduced the Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR). In contrast to absolute glucose measurements, the SHR uses HbA1c calibration to mitigate glycemic variability due to preexisting diabetes or other baseline factors, thereby providing a more accurate risk assessment [16].

Admission SHR is independently associated with delirium risk in the setting of critically ill patients. In a retrospective cohort study, 2946 patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database were analyzed, and a higher SHR based on the first-day glucose level and baseline glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was linked to a higher incidence of delirium after the first 24 h [17]. However, another study in 487 geriatric medical inpatients (≥70 years), SHR derived from fasting glucose in the first 48 h and HbA1c, with delirium assessed by Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) within 24 h, then three times daily, showed a U-shaped risk in Cox models: both low and high SHR tertiles increased delirium, especially when HbA1c < 6.5% [18]. Among 1111 elderly surgical/trauma ICU patients in MIMIC-IV, admission SHR based on fasting glucose and HbA1c, and repeatedly assessed by CAM for the ICU (CAM-ICU) for delirium, were analyzed using LASSO-informed logistic regression and propensity score matching, showing higher delirium odds in the highest SHR quartile and a linear increase in in-hospital mortality [19]. SHR is an independent predictor for short- and long-term mortality in acute brain injury patients [20], and it showed an S-shaped association between SHR and 28/365-day mortality among 1088 geriatric ICU patients with delirium [21], while a meta-analysis of 39 studies (102,414 patients) confirmed that higher SHR is an independent predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events and 30-day, 1-year, and all-cause mortality using dose-response models [22].

Although, admission SHR has been independently associated with both delirium risk and with 28-day and 365-day all-cause mortality in elderly ICU patients with delirium, and confirmed as an independent predictor of short- and long-term mortality across diverse critically ill populations [20,21], the interplay among SHR, delirium incidence, and clinical outcomes remains incompletely understood and warrants further in-depth investigation. Accordingly, the present study aims to characterize the dose-response relationship between admission SHR and 72-h delirium risk in a large cohort of critically ill adults, to assess its consistency across clinically relevant subgroups, and to evaluate whether delirium mediates the effect of admission SHR on subsequent 28-day and 365-day mortality.

Methods

Source of data

This retrospective study used publicly available data from the MIMIC-IV database (Version 3.1), developed and hosted by the MIT Laboratory for Computational Physiology. MIMIC-IV is an update to the critically acclaimed MIMIC-III database, offering contemporary data from 299,712 patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022, capturing 94,458 records of both emergency department visits and intensive care unit admissions. It incorporates anonymized structural data, including patient demographics, vital-sign monitoring, laboratory tests, medical management, procedures, disease diagnoses, and survival outcomes, without patient identifiers. The National Institutes of Health training course on protecting human study participants and the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative exams have been completed to gain full access to the database. As MIMIC-IV is a publicly available, de-identified database, ethical approval and informed consent were waived.

Study design and population

This study included adult patients (age > 18 years) admitted to the ICU with a documented HbA1c (%) within three months before ICU admission and a peak glucose value within 24 h after ICU admission. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first admission was analyzed. Delirium was assessed using the CAM-ICU, a validated screening tool for critically ill patients. We excluded patients who (1) lacked HbA1c, glucose, or valid CAM-ICU delirium assessment; (2) had an ICU stay shorter than 24 h; and (3) had a minimum Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score < 9. The GCS ≥ 9 threshold was chosen because the CAM-ICU is a non-verbal instrument specifically validated for patients with impaired consciousness, including those with GCS 9-14, and cannot be reliably completed only in unarousable patients (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale [RASS] −4/-5, generally GCS ≤ 8) [23,24]. Patients were classified as "unable to assess (UTA)" for delirium only if they had no valid CAM-ICU assessment throughout the entire ICU stay; those with at least one valid assessment were retained. To evaluate the robustness and generalizability of our findings, we performed prespecified sensitivity analyses further restricting the cohort to GCS ≥ 13 and GCS = 15. Patients whose first delirium episode occurred more than 72 h after ICU admission were reclassified as non-delirium for the 72-h primary endpoint.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted using Structured Query Language (SQL) queries on the MIMIC-IV database (PostgresSQL, version 17.2). Baseline characteristics included age, sex, race, body weight, comorbidities (myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, renal disease, peripheral vascular disease, paraplegia, malignancy, diabetes mellitus, and AIDS), and diabetes status. Laboratory values included HbA1c, peak glucose within 24 h of ICU admission, hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease [MDRD] formula). Vital signs (temperature, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate) were averaged over the first 24 h. Severity scores included the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS). The Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score and acute kidney injury (AKI) stage were also recorded. To address potential confounding and mediation by early treatments, we additionally extracted the following interventions administered within the first 24 h after ICU admission: insulin (number of patients used and the total dose), systemic glucocorticoids (intravenous and oral), dextrose-containing fluids (any use and total volume), and sedatives (propofol, benzodiazepines, dexmedetomidine, and opioid). Antipsychotic use during the entire ICU stay was also captured. Variables were defined using MIMIC-IV item identifiers and routes.

Basic code for data querying and extraction was provided by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology Laboratory for Computational Physiology (https://github.com/MIT-LCP/mimic-code), with slight modifications to adapt it to the current study. Variables with missing data of less than 25% were preserved; otherwise, they were excluded from the analysis. Missing data were then imputed with a random forest method implemented in the “missForest” package (version 1.5) in R.

Clinical outcome

The primary endpoint of the present study is the occurrence of 72-h delirium after ICU admission. The secondary outcomes were 28-day and 365-day all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Description and normal statistics

The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the skewness coefficient. Variables with absolute skewness ≤ 1 were regarded as approximately normally distributed, presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and compared using the t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Variables with absolute skewness > 1 were treated as non-normally distributed, presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using Pearson's chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves

The KM method was used to estimate the cumulative proportion of patients with delirium across SHR quartiles during the 72-h observation window. Between-group differences were compared using the log-rank test, and pairwise comparisons were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR).

Cox proportional hazards model

The independent association between SHR and 72-h delirium was assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Four nested models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted); Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race); Model 3 (further adjusted for eGFR, MELD score, cerebrovascular disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypertension, cancer, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes status, and dementia); and Model 4 (Model 3 + early treatments, including insulin, systemic glucocorticoids, dextrose-containing fluids, parenteral nutrition, benzodiazepines, dexmedetomidine, propofol, opioid and antipsychotic, to account for their potential confounding effect on both stress hyperglycemia and delirium). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. To further assess robustness to potential time-varying effects, we performed a competing-risk analysis using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model (cmprsk package), treating in-ICU death before delirium as a competing event, adjusted for age, sex, race and diabetes status.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis

The dose-response relationship between SHR trend (modeled as a continuous variable) and 72-h delirium was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles. To determine whether the association was linear or non-linear, we compared a linear Cox model with the RCS model using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and a likelihood ratio test for non-linearity. Potential threshold effects were further explored by fitting Cox models piecewise with zero to three breakpoints, selecting the optimal number using the minimum BIC with the ‘segmented’ package.

Subgroup analysis

Stratified analyses were performed by age (≤60 vs. > 60 years), sex, race, hypertension, heart failure, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, eGFR (≤60 vs. > 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), MELD score (≤20 vs. > 20), malignancy, and diabetes status. Interaction was assessed using a likelihood ratio test for the SHR × subgroup, and interaction P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the FDR method.

Mediation analysis

We examined whether delirium mediates the effect of SHR on 28-day and 365-day all-cause mortality using structural equation modeling (lavaan package, version 0.6-21), decomposing the total effect into direct (SHR to mortality) and indirect (SHR to delirium to mortality) components. Two model specifications were compared: a crude model and an adjusted model incorporating age, sex, race, diabetes status, and insulin, glucocorticoids, dextrose-containing fluids, sedative or analgesia, and antipsychotic use. Indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals were estimated using bias-corrected bootstrapping (1000 resamples). As interventions, such as insulin, sedatives, and antipsychotics, may act as mediators rather than confounders, we did not force them into the indirect path to avoid over-adjustment and collider bias; instead, we quantified robustness to unmeasured confounding using E-values for both the “SHR to delirium” and “delirium to mortality” associations with the ‘EValue’ package.

Sensitivity analyses

Robustness was assessed by (1) further restricting the cohort to GCS ≥ 13 and GCS = 15 to confirm that the association was not driven by the inclusion of patients with reduced arousal (GCS 9-12); (2) recalculating SHR using 6-h and 12-h peak glucose windows in addition to the 24-h window; and (3) stratifying by diabetes status combined with HbA1c (<6.5% vs. ≥ 6.5%), with a KM test for interaction between SHR and diabetic status.

Significant level

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with statistical significance set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

Baseline characteristics of study individuals

This study retrospectively analyzed 94,458 records from the MIMIC-IV database, including 16,967 records that documented HbA1c levels within 3 months before ICU admission and glucose levels from ICU admission to 24 h after admission. Of these, 2269 records were admitted to the ICU multiple times, and only the first record was included; 9 patients had invalid delirium assessments. We further excluded 1427 records with an ICU stay < 24 h, 4594 with no valid CAM-ICU assessment throughout the ICU stay (tagged as “unable to assess”), and 1271 with a minimum GCS < 9, yielding a final cohort of 7397 patients (Fig. 1). 143 patients whose first delirium episode occurred > 72 h after admission were reclassified as non-delirium for the 72-h endpoint. Patients were divided into SHR quartiles: Q1 (SHR < 1.02, n = 1851), Q2 (1.02 ≤ SHR < 1.28, n = 1851), Q3 (1.28 ≤ SHR < 1.53, n = 1848), and Q4 (SHR ≥ 1.53, n = 1847). Study outcomes

Overall, 1259 patients (17.0%) developed delirium within 72 h of ICU admission, with a median onset of 11.25 h (IQR 3.20-26.91). Incidence density increased across SHR quartiles, being highest in Q4 (97.21, 104.36, 95.03, and 117.14 per 1000 person-days for each quartile, respectively; Table 2). Kaplan-Meier curves for the cumulative incidence of delirium over the 72-h window differed across SHR quartiles (overall log-rank P = 0.04), with Q4 showing the highest cumulative incidence (Fig. 2). In univariate analysis, SHR was significantly higher in patients who developed delirium (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.06-1.24, P = 0.002; Table S2). Patients with delirium were older, more often female, and had longer ICU and hospital stays, higher in-ICU/in-hospital and 28-/365-day mortality, and a higher burden of cerebrovascular disease, dementia, heart failure, liver and renal disease, paraplegia, diabetes, and malignancy, together with higher AKI stage, sepsis-3, SOFA, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS, and MELD scores (all P < 0.05; Table S2).

In multivariable Cox models, patients in the highest SHR quartile had a significantly higher hazard of delirium than those in the lowest quartile across all models (Table 3): Model 1, HR 1.18 (95% CI 1.01-1.37, P = 0.037); Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race), HR 1.22 (95% CI 1.05-1.42, P = 0.012); Model 3 (Model 2 plus comorbidities), HR 1.26 (95% CI 1.08-1.48, P = 0.004); and Model 4 (Model3 plus treatments), HR 1.23 (95% CI 1.04-1.45, P = 0.014). The per-unit trend was significant in Models 1 to 3 (Model 3, HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.04-1.22, P = 0.003) and attenuated to borderline significance after adjustment for early treatments (Model 4, HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.00-1.17, P = 0.055). Delirium mediates the effect of SHR on mortality

Higher SHR was not directly associated with 28- or 365-day mortality (both non-significant), whereas delirium was strongly associated with both outcomes (P < 0.05). In structural-equation mediation models adjusted for age, sex, race, and diabetes status, delirium significantly mediated the association between SHR and mortality: the indirect effect was significant for both 28-day (1.004, 95% CI 1.002-1.006, P < 0.001) and 365-day mortality (1.006, 95% CI 1.003-1.009, P < 0.001), while the corresponding total and direct effects were non-significant, indicating an indirect-effect pattern which was consistent in crude and adjusted models (Fig. 5). E-value analysis indicated that unmeasured confounders would need to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome by a risk ratio of 1.41 for the SHR-delirium association, and 2.95 and 2.30 for the associations of delirium with 28-day and 365-day mortality, respectively, to fully explain these effects (Table S7), supporting robustness to residual confounding. The proportional-hazards assumption was met for SHR (Schoenfeld P = 0.66), although the global test indicated mild violation driven by a few covariates, such as race, myocardial infarction, and diabetes status (Table S8), and corresponding Schoenfeld residual plots are shown in Fig. S2. Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of 7397 critically ill adults from the MIMIC-IV database demonstrates that an elevated SHR is independently associated with an increased risk of 72-h delirium after ICU admission. In fully adjusted models that additionally accounted for early treatments, patients in the highest SHR quartile had a 23% higher hazard of delirium than those in the lowest quartile (Model 4 HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.04-1.45), and the association followed a monotonic, approximately linear pattern. The association strengthened progressively in cohorts restricted to higher levels of arousal (per-unit HR of 1.13, 1.18, and 1.26 for GCS ≥ 9, ≥ 13, and 15, respectively), indicating that inclusion of patients with mildly reduced consciousness yielded a conservative rather than an inflated estimate. Mediation analysis identified delirium as the pathway through which SHR exerts an indirect effect on both 28-day and 365-day all-cause mortality, with an indirect-only pattern that was robust to unmeasured confounding on E-value analysis. These associations were consistent across 12 demographic and clinical subgroups, with no significant interaction after FDR correction.

These findings extend and refine the evidence linking stress hyperglycemia to adverse neurological outcomes in critical illness. Wang et al. [25] analyzed 2946 MIMIC-IV patients and reported that a higher SHR, derived from first-day glucose and baseline HbA1c, was associated with delirium beyond the first 24 h, while Yao et al. [19] used LASSO-informed logistic regression and propensity-score matching in 1111 elderly surgical and trauma ICU patients to show higher delirium odds and a linear increase in in-hospital mortality with elevated SHR. Our study corroborates the direction of these associations in a substantially larger cohort while yielding a more conservative effect estimate, most likely because we modeled time-to-delirium with Cox regression rather than fixed-horizon logistic models, extended the observation window to 72 h to capture the natural trajectory of ICU-acquired delirium, and retained patients with mildly impaired arousal who are fully assessable with the CAM-ICU. In addition, our analysis explicitly accounted for early glucose-, glucocorticoid-, and sedation-related treatments as potential confounders, and the SHR-delirium association persisted after this adjustment, arguing against confounding by early management as a sole explanation.

The monotonic, linear relationship we observed contrasts with the U-shaped association reported by Song et al. [18] in 487 geriatric medical inpatients, in whom both low and high SHR tertiles were associated with increased delirium risk, particularly when HbA1c was < 6.5%. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in the study population (a mixed critically ill ICU cohort versus older medical inpatients), the glucose metric (24-h peak versus fasting glucose), and the outcome-ascertainment window. Notably, in our diabetes/HbA1c-stratified analysis, the association remained positive and of similar magnitude among non-diabetic patients with HbA1c < 6.5% (HR 1.12) and among patients with diabetes (HR 1.17), with no significant interaction (P = 0.722), providing no support for a paradoxical excess risk at low SHR in the critically ill. By anchoring the acute glucose value to the individual chronic glycemic baseline, SHR appears to capture the magnitude of the acute stress response more consistently across the glycemic spectrum than absolute glucose alone.

A noteworthy finding of this study is the mediating role of delirium in the relationship between SHR and mortality. Although SHR exhibited no significant total or direct effect on 28-day or 365-day mortality, it exerted a significant indirect effect operating entirely through delirium (indirect effects 1.004, 95% CI 1.002-1.006, and 1.006, 95% CI 1.003-1.009, for 28- and 365-day mortality, respectively; both P < 0.001). This indirect-only pattern is consistent with a model in which acute stress hyperglycemia influences survival not directly, but by precipitating delirium, which in turn increases the risk of death. We deliberately did not force post-admission interventions such as insulin, sedatives, or antipsychotics into the indirect pathway, because these treatments are administered in response to, and may lie on the causal path of hyperglycemia and delirium; conditioning on such post-exposure variables can introduce over-adjustment and collider-stratification bias and attenuate a genuine mediated effect [26]. Instead, we assessed robustness to unmeasured confounding using E-values. These values indicated that a confounder would need to be associated with both the exposure and the outcome, with risk ratios of 1.41 (SHR to delirium), 2.95 (delirium to 28-day mortality), and 2.30 (delirium to 365-day mortality). These associations are much stronger than those of most measured covariates, suggesting that the observed effects are unlikely to be fully explained by unmeasured confounding [27]. These findings align with a growing view of delirium not merely as a passive marker of illness severity, but as an active contributor to downstream adverse outcomes [1,5,6]. This interpretation is contrary to a previous study using a competing-risks framework that found that delirium extended ICU stays but did not independently raise the risk of death [28], underscoring that the causal status of delirium remains debated and that our mediation estimates, derived from observational data, require confirmation in prospective and interventional studies.

The pathophysiology of delirium is multifactorial, involving neuroinflammation, neurotransmitter imbalance, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroendocrine dysregulation [29,30]. The traditional cerebral metabolism hypothesis posits that an insufficient cerebral energy supply is the core trigger of delirium; however, this framework alone cannot account for delirium in patients with otherwise normal metabolic indices, suggesting the involvement of more complex, multisystem mechanisms [5,29]. Acute hyperglycemia amplifies neuroinflammation, which compromises the BBB, allowing further infiltration of inflammatory cells; oxidative stress exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction, impairing neuronal energy metabolism; and the neuroendocrine stress response perpetuates both hyperglycemia and neuroinflammation. A higher SHR, by capturing the magnitude of the acute metabolic derangement relative to an individual's chronic glycemic baseline, integrates across these pathways and thereby serves as a clinically accessible surrogate marker for the global intensity of the pathophysiological insult to the brain.

Acute hyperglycemia is a potent activator of neuroinflammation, as elevated glucose levels promote activation of the nuclear factor kappa B signaling cascade in neurons, microglia, and cerebrovascular endothelial cells, leading to upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and high-mobility group box 1 protein [13,31], that have been directly implicated in delirium pathogenesis [29]. A recent translational study by Yao et al. [15] provided direct experimental evidence that acute hyperglycemia exacerbates neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment by enhancing microglial glycolysis via the carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein/hypoxia-inducible factor-1α signaling pathway, promoting a pro-inflammatory M1 microglial phenotype through metabolic reprogramming [32]. Concurrently, hyperglycemia compromises BBB integrity by disrupting tight junction proteins (occludin, zonula occludens-1, and Claudin-5), facilitating the entry of peripheral inflammatory mediators into the central nervous system, further amplifying neuroinflammation, and contributing to the clinical manifestation of delirium [33,34]. At the level of oxidative stress, excessive production of reactive oxygen species, driven by polyol, hexosamine, and advanced glycation end products and their receptors [35,36], induces mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis [37], effects that are further amplified by glycemic variability. At the neurotransmitter level, metabolic stress reduces acetylcholine synthesis, while inflammation-driven activation of the kynurenine pathway generates excitotoxic metabolites such as quinolinic acid, which converge on attentional networks disrupted in delirium [38]. Furthermore, SHR reflects not only glycemic perturbation per se, but also the intensity of the neuroendocrine stress response. Elevated cortisol, catecholamines, and glucagon stimulate hepatic gluconeogenesis, glycogen breakdown, and peripheral insulin resistance through activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system [39]. This impairs hippocampal function and, on top of existing neurodegeneration, initiates an exaggerated neuroinflammatory response that can lead to delirium [40]. In vivo, glucose fluctuations caused significantly greater neuronal apoptosis than constant hyperglycemia alone, with elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-1β and observable structural damage to myelin sheaths and axons [41]. Thus, a higher SHR, which reflects the extent of acute glycemic fluctuations relative to a person's long-term glycemic level, serves as an easily accessible clinical indicator of overall brain damage.

SHR, a simple ratio derived from readily available glucose and HbA1c measurements, could be incorporated into ICU workflows as an early-warning biomarker for delirium risk stratification, supplementing existing scoring tools such as SOFA, APS III, and SAPS II. The mediation findings provide a mechanistic rationale for interventional trials evaluating whether SHR-guided glycemic optimization can reduce the incidence of delirium and improve downstream survival. Prior work has demonstrated that intensive glycemic management reduces both the incidence of delirium and mortality in ICU patients [42]. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that pre-treatment with liraglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, protects against delirium behaviors via microglial mitophagy and attenuation of neuroinflammation [43]. Moreover, metformin use has been associated with a lower risk of delirium onset (HR 0.77-0.81) [44]. These findings suggest that SHR may be a more precise therapeutic target than absolute glucose levels, as it accounts for chronic glycemic status. Since the association was linear without a clear threshold, the risk seems to increase steadily across the SHR range rather than only above a certain cutoff. This suggests that future risk stratification tools might benefit more from a continuous approach rather than a simple dichotomous one.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, observational design precludes causal inference; although we adjusted for a broad range of demographic, comorbidity, severity, and early-treatment covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as sleep disruption, pain, and the ICU environment, cannot be excluded, a concern we addressed quantitatively through E-value analysis. Second, delirium was ascertained from CAM-ICU documentation in the electronic health record, which, although validated, may underestimate hypoactive delirium; to minimize misclassification, we required at least one valid assessment and retained patients with mildly reduced arousal (GCS 9-14) who remained evaluable with the CAM-ICU. Third, the proportional-hazards assumption was globally violated to a mild degree, although it held for SHR itself; we therefore corroborated the primary analysis with a competing-risk Fine-Gray model, which yielded a concordant estimate. As the mediation outcome was all-cause mortality, competing risks did not apply to the final endpoint; the competing-risk analysis therefore focused on the SHR-delirium association, for which pre-delirium death constituted a competing event. Fourth, parenteral nutrition was recorded in only 16 patients, precluding meaningful evaluation of its contribution, and the database did not permit quantification of the glucose load delivered through parenteral nutrition. Fifth, although the mediation analysis suggested a pathway effect, it was conducted within a cross-sectional framework; establishing definitive temporal causality requires prospective longitudinal designs. Finally, the data were derived from a single academic center, and the mediation pathway, though robust in sensitivity analyses, should be confirmed in prospective, multicenter cohorts.

Conclusion

In this retrospective analysis of 7397 critically ill patients from the MIMIC-IV database, an elevated stress hyperglycemia ratio was independently associated with an increased risk of 72-h ICU delirium in a monotonic, approximately linear dose-response manner, with patients in the highest SHR quartile experiencing an approximately one-quarter higher hazard of delirium than those in the lowest quartile. This association was consistent across demographic and clinical subgroups and strengthened in cohorts with higher levels of arousal, indicating a conservative estimate. Mediation analysis further indicated that delirium fully mediates the indirect effect of SHR on 28- and 365-day all-cause mortality, with no direct effect of SHR on mortality, and this pattern was robust to unmeasured confounding. Collectively, these findings highlight SHR as an accessible biomarker for early delirium risk assessment in intensive care settings. They also offer a mechanistic basis for future prospective and interventional studies targeting SHR-guided glycemic optimization to mitigate delirium and its downstream consequences.
