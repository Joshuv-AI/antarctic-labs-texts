Evaluation of childbirth readiness and its associated factors and consequences in pregnant women referring to health centers in Tabriz, Iran and providing strategies to promote childbirth readiness: a sequential explanatory mixed method study protocol
Authors: Zohreh Alizadeh-Dibazari, Fatemeh Abbasalizadeh, Sakineh Mohammad-Alizadeh-Charandabi, Mojgan Mirghafourvand
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChildbirth readiness encompasses interventions conducted during pregnancy that encourage pregnant women, their families and communities to plan for a healthy pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum period. It enhances women’s childbirth experience and diminishes maternal mortality rates. This study seeks to assess the level of childbirth readiness, relevant factors and outcomes among pregnant women who have completed 37 or more weeks of gestational age and are attending health centres in Tabriz, Iran. The objective is to offer strategies for enhancing childbirth readiness.Methods and analysisThis sequential explanatory mixed-method study comprises three phases. The initial quantitative phase involves a descriptive-analytical longitudinal study focusing on pregnant women at 37 weeks of gestation or beyond. The quantitative data will be gathered by the sociodemographic and obstetric information questionnaire, the childbirth readiness scale, the pregnancy experience scale, the Wijma delivery expectancy/experience questionnaire (version A) (W-DEQ-Version A), the childbirth experience questionnaire-2, the childbirth outcome checklist, the Edinburgh’s postnatal depression scale (EPDS), the postpartum specific anxiety scale research short-form, the next pregnancy desire questionnaire and the exclusive breastfeeding checklist. In the subsequent qualitative phase, purposive sampling will be employed, and data will be collected through individual, in-depth and semi-structured interviews featuring open-ended questions. Data analysis will be conducted using conventional content analysis techniques. In the final mixed-method phase, strategies to enhance childbirth readiness will be formulated by integrating findings from the quantitative and qualitative studies, a comprehensive literature review, and employing the Delphi method.Ethics and disseminationThis study has received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences in Tabriz, Iran (code number: IR.TBZMED.REC.1401.557). All participants will provide written informed consent before taking part in the study. The outcomes will be shared through articles published in journals, presentations at medical conferences, the validation of a reliable scale for assessing the level of childbirth readiness in pregnant women and the development of a policy brief outlining supportive strategies to improve childbirth readiness. These resources will be valuable for healthcare providers.

Background

Given that childbirth encompasses physical, emotional, psychological, cultural and social dimensions, it is imperative for pregnant women to be thoroughly prepared across various aspects of delivery to effectively manage the challenges.1 Childbirth readiness, as an important component of prenatal care, plays a pivotal role in enhancing women’s childbirth experiences and reducing maternal mortality rates.2 This readiness involves interventions conducted during pregnancy that encourage pregnant women, their families and communities to plan for a healthy pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum period while also preparing to handle emergencies.3 Studies indicate that a lack of childbirth readiness can lead to prolonged labour, premature birth, heightened risks of obstetric complications, postnatal haemorrhage, increased maternal mortality rates,4 low birth weight, birth asphyxia,5 negative birth experiences, heightened incidence of birth trauma, postnatal depression, stress disorders6 and women’s potential reluctance to conceive again post childbirth.6

Iran is one of the nine countries that have successfully reached Millennium Development Goal 5A (improvement of maternal health), marked by an 80% reduction in maternal mortality rates and an annual decrease of 6.4%.7 Thanks to the implementation of maternal health strategies in Iran, over 90% of deliveries now occur in medical facilities.8 However, in high- to middle-income countries, particularly during the evolution of midwifery practices, concerns have arisen over the increased use of interventions like caesarean sections in healthcare settings.9 Iran, for instance, has witnessed a notable surge in caesarean delivery rates, escalating from below 7% in the 1970s to over 48% in 2018, with figures soaring even higher in private hospitals (ranging from 72% to 89%). This rise in caesarean sections in Iran is largely attributed to the growing prevalence of elective caesarean deliveries performed without medical necessity but rather at the request of patients.10

In a qualitative meta-synthesis, Shirzad et al highlighted that the fear of experiencing natural childbirth pain stands out as a significant personal factor influencing the preference for caesarean delivery.11 Studies indicate that the fear of childbirth encompasses various dimensions, including concerns related to pain, birth trauma, feelings of isolation, loss of autonomy, inadequate support and potential risks to the infant or mother.12 13 This fear can lead to intensified labour pain14; moreover, the anxiety and stress induced by childbirth fears can elevate catecholamine levels, potentially impeding uterine contractions and prolonging labour.13 15 Consequently, heightened fear of childbirth often correlates with increased rates of elective and urgent caesarean sections.16 Apart from impacting the delivery process, fear of childbirth can also give rise to physical complications, postnatal anxiety or depression and disruptions in mother-fetal bonding.11 In a systematic review, Aguilera-Martín et al demonstrated that interventions such as counselling, psychoeducation and prenatal education are effective in addressing childbirth fears. Nurses and midwives, as primary providers of prenatal care, can deliver these interventions given their close rapport with expectant mothers throughout pregnancy.17 While nurses and midwives may not offer formal psychological therapies, the WHO suggests that, as trained non-professional psychotherapists, they can play a vital role in promoting maternal mental well-being.18

Given that women who have had negative childbirth experiences are more inclined to opt for caesarean sections, prior negative childbirth encounters serve as a crucial non-medical factor influencing Iranian women’s decisions to request caesarean deliveries.19 This factor significantly impacts choices concerning subsequent pregnancies.20 Negative childbirth experiences can also have repercussions on women’s mental well-being,21 potentially leading to post-traumatic stress disorder,22 challenges in mother-infant attachment,23 sexual dysfunction, mood and behavioural disorders24 and postnatal depression.25 A positive childbirth experience can empower women and is often the result of adequate preparation, coping mechanisms and support provided by midwives and nurses during prenatal and antenatal care. Achieving such positive experiences requires concerted efforts and collaboration across various levels, including the individual, the family, the healthcare system and the educational sector.26

Nations worldwide have implemented various interventions to enhance women’s preparedness for childbirth. One notable example is the Birth Preparedness and Complications Readiness (BP/CR) package, a comprehensive initiative that emphasises prompt access to skilled maternal and neonatal care, widely employed by organisations supporting the Safe Motherhood Initiative globally.3 In a cross-sectional study in Kenya, it was observed that mothers with a high level of BP/CR experienced nearly three times more favourable delivery outcomes compared with mothers with a low level of BP/CR.27 A systematic review of studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia and Central America assessed the impact of BP/CR interventions on births attended by skilled professionals, revealing an increase in the number of births with skilled attendants or facility-based deliveries.2 Additionally, developed countries have introduced comprehensive prenatal education courses focusing on enhancing women’s understanding of childbirth, boosting self-efficacy and improving self-care abilities to enhance the overall childbirth experience and satisfaction.28 In alignment with these efforts, the Iranian Health Ministry has been conducting physiological childbirth preparation classes facilitated by trained midwives in specific hospitals since 2017, expanding to selected health centres across Iran from 2018 onwards. These eight-session courses commence in the second half of a woman’s pregnancy (around week 20) and conclude by week 37 of pregnancy.29 Research by Hassanzadeh et al has demonstrated that childbirth preparation education conducted by trained nurses and midwives correlates with increased women’s health knowledge, reduced maternal anxiety regarding childbirth and a heightened preference for natural labour and delivery.30

Many existing interventions aimed at enhancing childbirth readiness are comprehensive, consistent, yet lack timeliness. Assessing women’s preparedness for childbirth and identifying areas of potential inadequacy could pave the way for more tailored interventions. Therefore, employing a practical tool to gauge women’s readiness for childbirth is crucial.31 Various instruments are available for this purpose, with the most prevalent one being rooted in the framework of Childbirth Preparation and Complications Readiness, featuring 207 items spanning 12 dimensions.3 This tool primarily addresses physiological aspects, the presence of skilled birth attendants, financial readiness and emergency transportation, although overlooking psychosocial dimensions.31 While the Safe Motherhood Needs Assessment tool is widely used in resource-constrained settings, its applicability is limited in resource-rich environments where many of its criteria are already met.32 33 Recognising this gap, Mengmei et al devised a novel assessment tool known as the childbirth readiness scale (CRS). This scale is specifically tailored for a comprehensive evaluation of women’s preparedness for childbirth, exhibiting strong internal consistency and construct validity.31

The author’s investigation revealed a lack of qualitative studies exploring childbirth readiness in Iran. Qualitative research, aimed at delving deeper into human phenomena within specific social contexts, offers insights that quantitative analysis alone may not capture.34 To address this gap effectively and gain a comprehensive understanding of the subject, integrating both qualitative and quantitative analyses is advisable. By combining these methodologies, researchers can mitigate limitations and achieve a more nuanced comprehension of the topic compared with using each approach in isolation.35 It is important to underscore that the aim of mixed-method research is not to supplant individual approaches but to leverage their respective strengths and mitigate weaknesses within a single study. Given the lack of assessment of childbirth readiness among pregnant women in Iran using standard tools, along with the unusually high rates of elective caesarean deliveries in the country and their contradiction with population policies, it is suggested that conducting an evaluation that combines both qualitative and quantitative perspectives on childbirth readiness could provide valuable insights and appropriate strategies. This study, pioneering the utilisation of a mixed-method approach to explore childbirth readiness and potential enhancement strategies, aims to develop an assessment tool specifically tailored to measure childbirth readiness in the context of Iran. This instrument will undergo rigorous psychometric evaluation to ensure its applicability within the Iranian setting. Therefore, the findings of this study will be used by midwives, nurses and other healthcare providers in public and private centres to determine the level of childbirth readiness in women during pre-pregnancy counselling visits or the first visit during pregnancy. This assessment will help them plan subsequent actions to prepare pregnant women for childbirth.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study employs a mixed-method research design with a sequential explanatory approach, estimated to last from March 2023 to May 2024. The initial phase involves quantitative and longitudinal research, focusing on assessing the childbirth readiness of pregnant women attending health centres in Tabriz, Iran, along with associated factors and outcomes. The findings of this phase will be reported in accordance with the guidelines for reporting observational studies, specifically the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement (online supplemental appendix 1). Subsequently, the second phase entails qualitative research to explore pregnant women’s perspectives on childbirth readiness, with findings reported according to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (online supplemental appendix 2). Finally, the third phase will devise strategies to enhance childbirth readiness by amalgamating insights from both quantitative and qualitative research, supplemented by literature review findings and the application of the Delphi method (figure 1).

Study Algorithm Based on a Sequential Explanatory Mixed Methods Approach.

First phase: a quantitative research

This longitudinal descriptive-analytical study will focus on pregnant women accessing health centres in Tabriz, Iran. Given that the formation of prenatal medical records and care in Iran occurs at health centres, sampling of this phase of study will be conducted at these centres. The sample, comprising 360 pregnant women, will be selected through clustering, with one-fourth of the health centres chosen randomly via www.random.org. Subsequently, the researcher will visit these selected centres to compile a list of women at 37 weeks of pregnancy or more, sourced from the Integrated Health System, alongside their contact details. Following this, pregnant women will be randomly recruited, with the researcher contacting them to elucidate the study’s objectives and procedures, seeking their participation. On obtaining consent over the phone, women will be screened for eligibility, and those meeting the criteria will be invited to the health centre at a designated time. During face-to-face interactions, the study’s objectives and methodologies will be thoroughly explained. Participants willing to engage in the survey will provide written informed consent and complete questionnaires covering the sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, the CRS, the pregnancy experience scale (PES), and the Wijma delivery expectancy/experience questionnaire (version A) (W-DEQ-Version A). Subsequently, participants will be followed up for up to 6 weeks post-delivery, during which they will complete the childbirth experience questionnaire-2 (CEQ-2), the childbirth outcomes checklist, the Edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS), the postpartum specific anxiety scale research short-form (PSAS-RSF), the next pregnancy desire questionnaire and the exclusive breastfeeding checklist.

Scales and data collection

Quantitative data will be gathered through the administration of various tools including the sociodemographics and obstetric information questionnaire, the CRS, the PES, the W-DEQ-Version A, the CEQ-2, the childbirth outcomes checklist, the EPDS, the PSAS-RSF, the next pregnancy desire questionnaire and the exclusive breastfeeding checklist.

The sociodemographic and obstetric information questionnaire

This questionnaire covers a range of topics including the mother’s age; age at marriage; marital status; type of residence; adequacy of monthly income for expenses; educational background of both the mother and her husband; their respective occupations; the mother’s pre-pregnancy height and weight; level of support from the husband and family; satisfaction with marital relationship; gestational age; number of pregnancies, births and living children; history of miscarriages, stillbirths and fertility issues; participation in childbirth preparation courses; instances of unplanned pregnancies; and experiences of complicated childbirths.

The CRS

This tool comprises 18 items categorised into four domains: self-management (four items), information literacy (six items), birth confidence (four items) and birth plan (four items), each rated on a five-point Likert scale. The validity and reliability of this instrument were established within the Chinese pregnant women population. Responses include ‘Strongly disagree’ (scored as 1), ‘Disagree’ (scored as 2), ‘Neither agree nor disagree’ (scored as 3), ‘Agree’ (scored as 4) and ‘Strongly agree’ (scored as 5), resulting in a total score range of 18 to 90. Higher scores indicate a greater level of childbirth readiness.31

The validation of CRS

Reviews conducted by the psychometric research team indicate that this instrument has not been used in Iran. Initially, formal authorisation will be sought in writing from the developer of the instrument. The childbirth readiness scale will undergo validation processes including forward and backward translations, as well as assessments of content, face and construct validity.

For translation validity, the process will involve the following steps: professional translators proficient in both languages (at least two) will translate the instrument from English to Persian. Subsequently, the initial translations will be compared, discrepancies rectified and a final version created by consolidating the initial versions. Next, the translated questionnaire will be back-translated from Persian to English. The final version, the back-translated questionnaire, will then be reviewed by three or four translators fluent in both languages, including specialists in English, questionnaire translation, concept familiarity and a coordinator, to produce the final Persian version.36

Content validity will be assessed through quantitative and qualitative methods. The questionnaires will be distributed to 10 experts. Qualitative content validity evaluation will focus on appropriate word usage, grammar adherence, item importance and item placement. Based on the experts’ feedback, necessary modifications will be made to the instrument. Quantitative content validity assessment will involve expert judgments and the calculation of content validity ratio and content validity index.37

Face validity will be evaluated through qualitative and quantitative methodologies. The qualitative approach will involve input from the target population. Interviews with a sample of 10 pregnant women beyond 37 weeks of gestation will be conducted to identify challenges in understanding phrases and words, item relevance and coherence, potential ambiguities and comprehension issues. The quantitative face validity assessment will employ the item impact method using a five-point Likert scale. The completed questionnaire will be presented to the target group, and the impact score equation will be used to determine face validity, with impact scores exceeding 1.5 considered acceptable.

Construct validity will be assessed through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. Additionally, reliability will be measured using pre-test and post-test data for repeatability (intraclass correlation) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha).

Necessary sample size for instrument psychometrics

Coakes and Steed recommended a minimum of five samples for each item of an instrument, or a total of 100 samples, for factorial analysis.38 Nunnally and Bernstein proposed that ten samples per item are necessary for such analysis. For this study, a sample size of 10 per item was deemed essential for the factorial analysis.39 With 18 items in the childbirth readiness questionnaire, a total of 180 samples are required. Considering the clustered sampling approach with an effect of 2, the final sample size has been determined as 360 participants.

The PES

The short-form PES was developed by Jent DiPietro in 2004 . This scale comprises 20 items focusing on anxiety and happiness levels during pregnancy. Ten items assess anxiety (the degree of anxiety each item induces in the expectant mother), while the remaining ten items evaluate happiness during pregnancy (the level of joy each item brings to the pregnant woman). Participants rate each item on a Likert scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘very high’, with corresponding scores from 0 to 3. Consequently, a higher score indicates greater levels of concern or happiness.40 The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this questionnaire were confirmed in Iran, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.714 and an intra-class correlation coefficient of 0.721.41

The W-DEQ-version A

This 33-item questionnaire assesses fears and expectations prior to childbirth. Mothers express their emotions and personal insights using a six-point Likert scale (ranging from 0 for ‘never’ to 5 for ‘most times’). The total score is derived by summing the scores of all items, falling within the range of 0 to 165. A score of 100 serves as the threshold; higher scores indicate heightened fear of childbirth. Notably, items 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 19, 20, 24, 25, 27 and 31 are scored inversely. Wijma assessed the questionnaire’s reliability through split-half analysis, reporting respective Cronbach’s alphas of 0.89 and 0.92. Scores equal to or less than 37 suggest low fear, 38 to 65 indicate moderate fear, 66 or more signify high fear and scores exceeding 85 reflect severe fear.42 In Iran, Mortazavi validated this questionnaire, achieving a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.914.43

The CEQ-2

This questionnaire assesses women’s childbirth experiences through 25 items that explore various dimensions: personal capacity (including feelings of control, personal sentiments towards birth and labour pain), participation (ability to change positions, move and manage pain during labour), professional support (receiving midwifery information and care) and perceived safety (feeling of security and recollection of childbirth). Of the 25 items, 22 are answered using four options, while three employ a Visual Analogue Scale. The instrument’s validity and reliability have been established within the American female population. Responses range from ‘completely agree’ (score 1) to ‘completely disagree’ (score 4). For items using the visual scale, values are converted to a range of 1–4: 0–40 (1), 41–60 (2), 61–80 (3) and 81–100 (4). Items reflecting negative sentiments (such as fear, negative memories, severe pain and exhaustion) are inversely scored. A higher average score on this instrument signifies a more positive childbirth experience.44 The Persian version of this instrument underwent validation in Iran, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 for the items and a correlation coefficient of 0.97, confirming its validity and reliability.45

The childbirth outcome checklist

This checklist will include questions about the type of delivery, gestational age at birth and birth weight (online supplemental appendix 3).

The EPDS

Cox developed the EPDS to assess depression both during pregnancy and post-delivery. This tool comprises ten multiple-choice questions. Some items range from low to high severity (questions 1, 2 and 4), while others go from high to common (questions 10 to 3). Each question’s items are scored from zero to three based on the severity of symptoms, and participants’ scores are the sum of these scores, ranging from 0 to 30. The validity of this scale was determined to be 0.75 through the multiple correlation coefficient calculation between the Edinburgh scale and the Beck Depression Inventory. Its reliability, measured using Cronbach’s alpha and the split-half method, was found to be 0.75.46 In the postnatal period, Montazeri reported Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.77 to 0.86 and a correlation coefficient of 0.80 for Persian version of this questionnaire.47

The PSAS-RSF

Davies et al adapted and validated a 16-item self-report scale from the Specific Postnatal Anxiety Scale, originally comprising 51 items, to focus on the frequency rather than the intensity of postnatal anxiety among mothers with infants aged 0–12 months. The scale covers four domains: psychosocial adaptation to motherhood, practical anxieties about infant care, maternal competence and attachment anxiety and concerns regarding the infant’s safety and well-being, with four items dedicated to each domain. Response options include ‘never’, ‘sometimes’, ‘often’ and ‘almost always’, corresponding to scores of 1 to 4. The total score ranges from 16 to 64. The reliability of this four-factor instrument was deemed moderate, with McDonald’s ω values falling between 0.65 and 0.80, and an overall good reliability indicated by a McDonald’s ω of 0.88.48 Mashayekh-Amiri et al assessed the Persian version of this tool, reporting a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.72 and an intra-class correlation coefficient of 0.97, affirming its validity and reliability.49

The next pregnancy desire questionnaire

This questionnaire will inquire about the mother’s inclination towards a future pregnancy and potential timing considerations (online supplemental appendix 3).

The exclusive breastfeeding checklist

This checklist will cover aspects such as exclusive breastfeeding practices and the timing of breastfeeding initiation after delivery (online supplemental appendix 3).

Data analysis

On data collection, SPSS 26 was employed for analysis. Sociodemographic details, obstetric information and research variables will be elucidated using descriptive statistics, featuring median (25th–75th percentile) for non-normally distributed data, and frequency (percentage) and mean (SD) for normally distributed data. To explore the association between childbirth readiness and participation in educational classes, a one-way analysis of variance will be conducted in bivariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, the general linear model will be used, adjusting for sociodemographic and obstetric factors. The correlation between childbirth readiness and fear of childbirth, pregnancy and delivery experiences, as well as postnatal depression and anxiety, will be assessed through Pearson’s correlation test in bivariate analysis and the general linear model in multivariate analysis, with adjustments made for sociodemographic and obstetric information. The relationship between childbirth readiness and exclusive breastfeeding, alongside the desire for a subsequent pregnancy, will be evaluated using independent t-tests in bivariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, multivariate logistic regression will be applied, with adjustments for sociodemographic and obstetric variables.

Second phase: a qualitative study

The methodology employed in this phase of the study involves qualitative content analysis using a conventional approach. An essential benefit of this method lies in the direct extraction of information from the study data without imposing predefined categories or theories.35

Sample size and sampling method

The sampling approach will be purposive. Participants in the qualitative phase of the study will be selected from those in the quantitative phase. Specifically, participants whose childbirth readiness scores fall within the 10% upper and lower limits of the readiness score spectrum will be identified as extreme cases. Among these, women who have the desire and capacity to articulate their childbirth readiness experiences will be included in the study. Additionally, individuals exhibiting unique characteristics and those with unexpected insights will also be invited to participate in the interviews. To accommodate the time needed for quantitative data analysis and endpoint determination, participants for the qualitative phase will be chosen both during pregnancy and post-delivery. To address the second and third questions of the qualitative section, mothers who were interviewed during pregnancy will also be interviewed again in the postpartum period. Sampling will continue until data saturation is achieved, which signifies the point at which no new information or codes emerge.

Data collection

Qualitative data for this phase will be collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews that include open-ended questions based on the pre-designed interview guide (online supplemental appendix 4). The interviews will be conducted by the first author (Z A-D), a PhD student in midwifery with 20 years of teaching experience in providing care to mothers during pregnancy, childbirth and postpartum. Throughout these interviews, participants will be encouraged to freely articulate their perspectives and experiences on the topics outlined in the guide.35 The interview location will be chosen at the mother’s discretion. The questions in the qualitative phase will be tailored based on the insights from the quantitative phase, as well as relevant factors and themes. Commencing with the central query, ‘How do you prepare for childbirth?’ the interview will progress with follow-up inquiries like ‘According to you, what enhances childbirth readiness?’ and ‘What factors, in your view, impede childbirth readiness?’ The interview will encompass probing and exploratory questions triggered by each response to delve deeper into women’s experiences, such as ‘Could you elaborate on that? Why is that so? Can you provide an example to clarify your point?’ Throughout the interview, the researcher will document non-verbal cues like facial expressions, vocal intonations and participant positions in a dedicated form, noting the interview’s timestamp and location.

Data analysis

Data analysis will adhere to the conventional approach of qualitative content analysis, involving a thorough reading of the entire text iteratively to grasp its essence. Subsequently, a word-by-word examination will be conducted to extract codes. Initially, keywords expressing fundamental concepts will be pinpointed. The researcher will then progress by documenting insights from the initial analysis until a foundation for code emergence is established. Code labels encapsulating multiple primary ideas will be identified and derived during this phase. Following this, codes will be categorised based on their disparities or resemblances. Typically, 10–15 categories suffice for organising numerous codes effectively. These derived categories will serve to structure and group codes into coherent classifications. The study will employ the conventional content analysis method, which not only captures explicit content from interview texts but also unveils latent content and abstract concepts. To assess the qualitative analysis’s precision, five criteria—acceptability or credibility, reliability, verifiability, transferability and authenticity—will be used.50 It is imperative to mention that MAXQDA will be used to manage interview texts and codes effectively.

Third phase: developing strategies to enhance childbirth readiness in pregnant women

In this phase, the focus will be on devising strategies to enhance the childbirth readiness of pregnant women. Initially, an extensive literature review will be conducted to explore supportive strategies in this domain. Subsequently, strategies aimed at bolstering childbirth readiness will be formulated by presenting the findings from both quantitative and qualitative analyses to experts. Controlled feedback will be solicited by leveraging the Delphi method to gather and synthesise responses and comments effectively. The stages of the Delphi method will encompass:

Stage one

The panel of specialists will be selected from professionals in the fields of obstetrics and reproductive health among faculty members from various cities across the country to participate in the study. The selection of specialists will be purpose-driven, focusing on individuals with experience in providing clinical services to women and expertise in issues related to childbirth readiness. During a phone call with the panel members, the reasons and rationale for the study will be explained, and they will be invited to participate in the research.

Stage two

Each of the specialists will be asked via email to respond to issues or topics specified. At this stage, an open question will be posed with the theme, ‘What strategies do you suggest for enhancing childbirth readiness in pregnant women?’ The experts will be requested to provide their written responses to this question via email.

Stage three

Responses will be collated and synthesised into a questionnaire for the initial study round. Subsequently, the same panel members will be called on to prioritise the compiled statements.

Stage four

On receiving the first-round questionnaires, statistical analysis of the data will be conducted. The statements will be arranged based on their rankings to formulate another questionnaire for the second round.

Stage five

The second-round questionnaires, along with a statistical summary of the initial stage, will be distributed to the panel members for their responses.

Stage six

The process from Stage five will be reiterated for the third round of questionnaires. Participants whose opinions persist outside the scope of the third round’s results will be asked to provide justifications for maintaining their stances. At the conclusion of the third round, an evaluator will compile a report on the ranked statements, identify shifts in viewpoints and summarise the participants’ consensus opinions and associated recommendations. These findings will then be presented to stakeholders for informed decision-making.51

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

This study has received approval from the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences, Tabriz, Iran (code number: IR.TBZMED.REC.1401.557). Written informed consent will be sought from all participants involved in the quantitative, qualitative and Delphi phases of the study. Participants will be reassured that their personal information and identities will be treated confidentially in the report. Additionally, they will be informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any point.

Dissemination plan

The findings of this study will be disseminated in various formats. Initially, the results will be shared in journals and medical conferences to reach the international scientific community. Subsequently, a valid and reliable scale will be introduced for assessing the level of childbirth readiness in pregnant women. This scale will undergo psychometric evaluation tailored to the conditions present in Iran. Additionally, based on the study results, a policy brief will be developed concerning supportive strategies aimed at enhancing childbirth readiness. This brief will be directed towards health policymakers. Subsequently, midwives, nurses and other healthcare providers will have access to these strategies during pre-pregnancy counselling or prenatal care visits, enabling them to enhance the childbirth readiness of women effectively.
