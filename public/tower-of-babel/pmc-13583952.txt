Parenting concerns among Japanese parents with cancer raising minor children: A secondary analysis of a cross-sectional web-based survey of online patient support network members
Authors: Misa Yanai, Maho Aoyama, Kazuhiro Kosugi, Akemi Tsumura, Erika Nakanishi, Mitsunori Miyashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine sociodemographic, clinical, and support-related factors associated with parenting concerns among Japanese parents with cancer raising children aged < 18 years.MethodsThis secondary analysis used data from a cross-sectional web survey previously conducted to validate the Japanese Parenting Concerns Questionnaire (PCQ). Invitations were sent to 2852 members of a Japanese online patient support network, and 174 parents responded. PCQ analyses were restricted to 157 participants who selected Married in the survey marital-status variable. The PCQ total score was the primary outcome, and three subscales were exploratory outcomes. Multiple linear regression analyses used a post hoc parsimonious model and an expanded forced-entry sensitivity model.ResultsParticipants had a median age of 45 years, and 145 (83%) reported female sex. In the parsimonious total-score model, higher PCQ scores were associated with female sex (B = 5.33, 95% CI 1.03–9.64), uterine/ovarian cancer (B = 6.19, 95% CI 1.00–11.38), chemotherapy (B = 3.54, 95% CI 0.11–6.96), a self-reported past/current psychiatric history (B = 5.46, 95% CI 2.43–8.49), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (B = 5.74, 95% CI 1.98–9.49). In the expanded model, the associations with uterine/ovarian cancer, chemotherapy, self-reported past/current psychiatric history, and reported wanting practical support with parenting remained, whereas the female–sex association was attenuated (P = 0.076). Subscale findings were exploratory and several were model-dependent.ConclusionsDespite the limited single-network sample and low response rate, this study found that parenting concerns were associated with several self-reported characteristics and preferences for parenting support. However, these exploratory cross-sectional findings do not establish causal relationships or provide sufficient evidence to justify clinical targeting. Further confirmation in larger and more representative samples is needed.

Introduction

Cancer during the child-rearing years can affect both parents and their families. In developed countries, an estimated 14%–24.7% of adults with cancer are raising minor children.1 In Japan, approximately 56,000 new cancer cases are diagnosed annually among parents with children aged < 18 years, corresponding to approximately 87,000 affected children.2

A cancer diagnosis can substantially disrupt the lives of both parents and their families.3 These disruptions include altered daily routines for children, shifting household responsibilities among family members, and increased financial burdens.3, 4, 5, 6 Parents with cancer may experience psychological distress while balancing cancer treatment with ongoing childcare.3,7,8 Communication with children about a parent’s cancer and parental anxiety or depression can create additional challenges for families.9 Children may also experience emotional, behavioral, and family-functioning difficulties when a parent has cancer, although the available evidence is heterogeneous.10,11 Characterizing parenting concerns is therefore relevant to family-centered oncology nursing assessment.

To specifically measure these challenges, Muriel et al.12 developed the Parenting Concerns Questionnaire (PCQ). Previous research using the PCQ has found that greater parenting concerns are associated with poorer health-related quality of life among parents with cancer.13 In Japan, a validation study of the Japanese version of the PCQ revealed that 50% of parents with cancer reported concerns about the impact of the disease on their children’s daily lives, 29% about the emotional impact on their children, and 34% regarding their parenting partner.14 While the prevalence of these concerns is recognized, the sociodemographic, clinical, and support-related factors associated with them remain uncertain. Most existing Japanese studies on parenting concerns are limited to qualitative designs or retrospective medical record reviews, leaving a gap in quantitative evidence. Quantitatively characterizing factors associated with parenting concerns may inform family-centered oncology nursing assessment. Therefore, building on the previous validation study, this secondary analysis aimed to examine sociodemographic, clinical, and support-related factors associated with PCQ scores among parents with cancer raising minor children.

Methods

This study was a secondary analysis of the cross-sectional web-survey dataset previously used to validate the Japanese PCQ.14

Participants

Participants were parents with cancer raising at least one child aged < 18 years. To recruit participants, we requested cooperation from “Cancer Parents,” an online community service for patients with cancer raising minor children. As of July 2023, the service had approximately 4000 members, with a reported mean age of 45 years; approximately 30% were men and 70% were women. Members included individuals with various types of cancer at different disease stages.

Data collection

The source data were obtained from a cross-sectional web survey conducted between July and August 2023. Research staff from Cancer Parents sent an email inviting participation in the survey to 2852 email members of Cancer Parents. These 2852 individuals were selected because they had registered valid email addresses and had opted in to receive research-related communications. The remaining members were excluded because they lacked valid email addresses or were inactive accounts. No reminder emails or additional invitations were sent during the survey period. A survey company (Asmark Co., Ltd.) was commissioned to create the survey interface, convert the responses into data, and manage personal information. The same 174-participant dataset was previously reported in the validation study of the Japanese PCQ.14

Data source and prior publication

This study is a secondary analysis of the survey dataset previously used to validate the Japanese PCQ.14 The same 174 participants constitute the source cohort. The present analyses focus on multivariable associations and sensitivity analyses; previously published validation results and selected bivariate comparisons are not presented as novel findings.

Survey items

PCQ

The Japanese version of the PCQ was used to assess parenting concerns.14 The PCQ is a scale designed to measure concerns about parenthood among parents with cancer and consists of 15 items. The original version has a three-factor structure: (1) “The impact of my illness on the child’s daily life” (five items), (2) “The impact of my illness on the child’s feelings” (five items), and (3) “Concerns about my parenting partner” (five items).12 Items are scored from one to five using the validated response anchors “not at all agree,” “not very much agree,” “neither agree nor disagree,” “agree,” and “very much agree.” Total scores range from 15 to 75 and subscale scores from five to 25, with higher scores indicating greater parenting concern.12 Total and subscale scores were calculated by summing the corresponding item scores.

Participant characteristics

Participants reported age, sex, cancer type, stage at diagnosis, time since diagnosis, metastasis, recurrence, treatment in the past month, psychiatric history, marital status/partner status, number of children, and age of the youngest child. Cancer-site responses were non-mutually exclusive. The raw survey variable was labeled “Psychiatric history” with response categories No, Previous, and currently; for regression analyses, Previous and currently were combined and reported as self-reported past/current psychiatric history. Youngest-child age was categorized as 0–5, 6–12, and ≥ 13 years to represent broadly distinct developmental periods, informed by Erikson’s psychosocial developmental framework.15 The 6–12-year category broadly corresponds to the stage of industry versus inferiority, whereas the ≥ 13-year category broadly corresponds to identity versus role confusion; the 0–5-year category combines earlier childhood stages for analytical practicality.15

Further, to assess reported support availability and support preferences, the authors developed three study-specific items. The first item assessed participants’ perception of the availability of sources of parenting support (“Do you currently have sources of parenting support?”). The second item assessed whether participants wanted someone to consult about parenting (“Would you like to have someone to consult about parenting?”). The third item assessed whether participants wanted practical support with parenting (“Do you want practical support with parenting?”). The response options for all items were “Yes” and “No.” These single-item measures had not undergone formal validity or reliability testing; accordingly, they are described as reported support availability or support preferences and not as clinical or unmet needs.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the full source cohort (n = 174). No missing values were present in the supplied analysis variables, so no imputation was performed.

Because Factor 3 concerns the parenting partner, all PCQ-related analyses were restricted to the parenting-partner analytic cohort. The supplied raw dataset did not contain a separate binary partner-status item; the relevant raw variable was labeled “Marital status/partner” and had the response categories Married, Never married, Divorced, and Bereaved. The analytic cohort was operationally defined as the 157 participants who selected Married, the only category in the supplied coding that explicitly denoted a current spouse. Of the 174 respondents, 17 (2 Never married, 14 Divorced, and 1 Bereaved) were excluded from PCQ analyses. Thus, PCQ descriptive statistics, univariate analyses, regression models, and diagnostics all used n = 157. Univariate group comparisons used pooled independent-samples t tests for two-group variables and one-way analysis of variance for variables with three or more categories. For transparency, all categorical variables examined in these univariate analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Comparisons involving the support-preference items that were previously reported in the validation paper are not interpreted as novel findings here.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to estimate adjusted associations with PCQ scores. The PCQ total score was the primary outcome; the three subscale models were exploratory. All eligible partnered records were used, and no separate a priori sample-size calculation was performed for this secondary analysis. Given the sample size (n = 157) and number of candidate variables, a parsimonious model was used to limit overfitting. The parsimonious specification was developed during the analytical process and is therefore explicitly considered post hoc and exploratory rather than prespecified. It included age, sex, breast cancer, uterine/ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, self-reported past/current psychiatric history, number of children, age of the youngest child, reported wanting someone to consult about parenting, and reported wanting practical support with parenting. The cancer-site indicators were non-mutually exclusive binary variables; all participants remained in the cohort, and the “No” category for each indicator could therefore include participants with other cancer types. Treatment indicators were included as exploratory candidate variables; previous research has described treatment-related difficulties in maintaining parenting roles and caring for children, particularly during chemotherapy.16 An expanded forced-entry sensitivity model included sex, age, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, uterine/ovarian cancer, blood cancer, any other cancer site, stage at diagnosis, time since diagnosis, metastasis, recurrence, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, surgery, radiation therapy, observation without active treatment, self-reported past/current psychiatric history, number of children, age of the youngest child, reported availability of parenting support, reported wanting someone to consult about parenting, and reported wanting practical support with parenting.

Regression diagnostics were evaluated separately for the PCQ total score and each subscale in both model families. Linearity and residual patterns were examined, and residual normality, heteroscedasticity, and functional-form misspecification were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk, Breusch–Pagan, and Ramsey RESET tests, respectively. No important residual-normality or RESET concerns were detected. The Breusch–Pagan test indicated heteroscedasticity for Factor 3 in both the parsimonious (P = 0.002) and expanded (P = 0.005) models; HC3 robust standard errors were therefore calculated as a sensitivity analysis. Potentially influential observations were assessed with Cook’s distance. The maximum Cook’s distance was 0.062 among the parsimonious subscale models and 0.070 among the expanded subscale models; several observations exceeded the 4/n screening reference (4/157 = 0.025). Because this threshold is a screening heuristic rather than an automatic exclusion rule, no observation was removed solely on that basis, and influence diagnostics were retained for editorial review. Multicollinearity was assessed by the variance inflation factor (VIF): the maximum VIF was 2.91 in the parsimonious models and 7.05 in the expanded models, indicating greater collinearity and instability in the expanded sensitivity models. Because the analyses were exploratory and involved multiple comparisons across the PCQ total score and three subscales, no formal multiplicity adjustment was applied. Subscale and univariate findings were therefore interpreted cautiously, with consideration of effect estimates, 95% CIs, and sensitivity to model specification. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Student Edition 19.1.2 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA). All tests were two-sided.

Associated factors of parenting concerns (PCQ)

Univariate analyses

In univariate analyses among partnered participants (n = 157), higher PCQ total scores were observed for female sex (P < 0.001), breast cancer (P = 0.013), uterine/ovarian cancer (P = 0.017), hormone therapy (P = 0.023), a self-reported past/current psychiatric history (P < 0.001), reported no source of parenting support (P = 0.002), reported wanting someone to consult about parenting (P < 0.001), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S1). The support-availability comparison was based on only three partnered participants who reported no source of parenting support and is therefore interpreted cautiously.

For Factor 1, group differences were observed for sex (P = 0.017), breast cancer (P = 0.026), self-reported past/current psychiatric history (P < 0.001), youngest-child age as an omnibus comparison (P = 0.032), reported wanting someone to consult about parenting (P = 0.024), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (P < 0.001). For Factor 2, group differences were observed for uterine/ovarian cancer (P = 0.036), self-reported past/current psychiatric history (P < 0.001), support availability (P = 0.008), reported wanting someone to consult about parenting (P = 0.008), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (P < 0.001). For Factor 3, group differences were observed for sex (P < 0.001), breast cancer (P = 0.028), hormone therapy (P = 0.029), support availability (P = 0.003), reported wanting someone to consult about parenting (P = 0.004), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (P = 0.047). These univariate comparisons are descriptive and exploratory; support-preference comparisons reported previously in the validation paper are not considered novel findings.

In the parsimonious subscale models (Supplementary Table S2), Factor 1 was associated with chemotherapy (B = 1.81, 95% CI 0.34–3.28, P = 0.016), self-reported past/current psychiatric history (B = 2.15, 95% CI 0.85–3.45, P = 0.001), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (B = 1.91, 95% CI 0.30–3.52, P = 0.020). Factor 3 was associated with female sex (B = 3.07, 95% CI 0.77–5.37, P = 0.009) and reported wanting someone to consult about parenting (B = 2.16, 95% CI 0.17–4.16, P = 0.034).

Sensitivity analyses using the expanded forced-entry models are shown in Supplementary Table S3 and S4. For the PCQ total score, associations with uterine/ovarian cancer (B = 10.88, P = 0.005), chemotherapy (B = 8.03, P = 0.013), self-reported past/current psychiatric history (B = 5.32, P = 0.002), and reported wanting practical support with parenting (B = 5.35, P = 0.008) remained, whereas the female–sex association was attenuated (P = 0.076). Reporting no source of parenting support was associated with a higher total score (B = 16.35, 95% CI 4.70–28.00, P = 0.006), but this estimate was based on only three participants. For the subscales, self-reported past/current psychiatric history remained associated with Factors one and 2, while uterine/ovarian cancer and reported wanting practical support with parenting remained associated with Factor 2. The parsimonious associations of chemotherapy with Factor 1, youngest-child age with Factor 2, and reported wanting someone to consult about parenting with Factor 3 were attenuated in the expanded models. Several additional associations emerged only in the expanded models and were treated as model-dependent sensitivity signals rather than stable findings. The expanded models had higher VIFs (maximum 7.05). Factor 3 showed heteroscedasticity, and several individual expanded-model coefficients changed inference with HC3 robust standard errors; these Factor 3 sensitivity coefficients are therefore not interpreted as definitive.

Discussion

The main findings of this secondary analysis indicate that, among partnered parents with cancer participating in this online patient support network, parenting concerns were multidimensional and associated with several clinical and psychosocial characteristics. In the post hoc parsimonious model, higher total PCQ scores were associated with female sex, uterine/ovarian cancer, chemotherapy, a self-reported past/current psychiatric history, and reported wanting practical support with parenting. In the expanded sensitivity model, the associations with uterine/ovarian cancer, chemotherapy, psychiatric history, and reported wanting practical support with parenting remained, whereas the association with female sex was attenuated. Subscale analyses also demonstrated that different characteristics were associated with specific domains of parenting concern, although several associations were sensitive to model specification. These findings support a multidimensional interpretation of parenting concerns rather than the identification of a single high-risk group.

One comparatively consistent finding was the association between a self-reported past/current psychiatric history and higher PCQ scores. Psychiatric history was associated with the PCQ total score and with Factor 1 (The impact of my illness on the child’s daily life) and Factor 2 (The impact of my illness on the child’s feelings) in both the parsimonious and expanded models. This observation is compatible with previous literature describing substantial psychological burden among parents with cancer and associations between parental psychological well-being and family functioning.7,8,10 However, because psychiatric history was broadly assessed by self-report and the present study was cross-sectional, the temporal direction of this association cannot be determined. Parents experiencing greater parenting concerns may also experience greater psychological distress, and psychological difficulties may be related to how parenting concerns are perceived. Therefore, rather than using psychiatric history to define a group requiring automatic clinical prioritization, health care professionals may consider asking about parenting concerns and desired psychosocial support when discussing psychological well-being with parents with cancer.

The results also suggest differences in how specific characteristics relate to distinct domains of parenting concerns. Chemotherapy was associated with the PCQ total score in both the parsimonious and expanded models and with Factor 1 in the parsimonious model; however, the Factor 1 association was attenuated in the expanded model (P = 0.054). This pattern may reflect the practical challenges that can accompany cancer treatment while caring for minor children, although the present cross-sectional analysis cannot establish that chemotherapy itself causes greater parenting concerns. Reported wanting practical support with parenting was also associated with higher PCQ total, Factor 1, and Factor 2 scores in the parsimonious models and remained associated with the total score and Factor 2 in the expanded models. Whisenant et al.17 similarly described substantial practical childcare and tangible-support concerns among parents living with advanced cancer. Importantly, the item used in the present study measured whether participants reported wanting practical support with parenting; it did not measure a clinically defined or unmet need, the adequacy of available services, or actual support receipt. Thus, the observed association may partly reflect that parents with greater parenting concerns are more likely to express a preference for practical help. These findings suggest that health care professionals may find it useful to ask parents directly what forms of practical support they would prefer rather than assuming that a particular form of support is needed or would reduce parenting concerns.

Regarding Factor 3 (Concerns about my parenting partner), female sex and reported wanting someone to consult about parenting were associated with higher scores in the parsimonious model. However, both associations were attenuated in the expanded OLS model, and Factor 3 showed evidence of heteroscedasticity; moreover, inference for several expanded-model coefficients changed when HC3 robust standard errors were applied. Therefore, these Factor 3 findings should be regarded as model-dependent and exploratory rather than as evidence that participants reporting female sex necessarily experience greater partner-related concerns. Nevertheless, the findings may be considered within the broader social context of parenting in Japan. Although women’s participation in paid employment has increased and men’s involvement in childcare has been promoted, Japanese government data indicate that men’s time spent on childcare and housework remains comparatively low among developed countries.18 Previous Japanese research has also characterized maternal roles during the child-rearing period as broad and multifaceted.19 Such social and family-role contexts may contribute to variation in partner-related concerns, but they were not directly measured in the present study. Accordingly, rather than determining support according to sex, health care professionals may consider asking all parents about partner-related concerns, the distribution of parenting responsibilities, and whether they would like opportunities for consultation or practical assistance.

Having a youngest child aged ≥ 13 years was associated with higher Factor 2 scores in the parsimonious model, suggesting greater concern regarding the impact of parental illness on the child’s feelings; however, this association was attenuated in the expanded model (P = 0.093) and should therefore be considered model-dependent. Parents with cancer may experience uncertainty regarding how to communicate about illness and its implications with adolescent children, and previous studies have described challenges in parent-child communication in the context of parental cancer.5,20,21 Adolescence is also characterized by growing autonomy and identity development, which may add complexity to parent–child communication in the context of parental cancer.5,15 Resources available in Japan include programs such as Hope Tree,22 which provides support for children whose parents have cancer, as well as structured family programs and written materials intended to facilitate communication between parents and children.23, 24, 25 These resources may be useful options for health care professionals to discuss with families when appropriate. However, the present study did not evaluate whether these programs, books, or other forms of psychological support reduce PCQ scores, and no intervention effect can be inferred from the current findings.

An additional association in the expanded sensitivity model was a higher PCQ total score among participants reporting no source of parenting support. However, only three of the 157 partnered participants were in this category, so the estimate was based on a very sparse subgroup and should not be interpreted as a stable clinical effect. Larger studies are needed to distinguish the roles of support availability, support preferences, actual support receipt, and support adequacy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the response rate was low (6%), creating substantial potential for selection and nonresponse bias; no demographic or clinical data were available for nonrespondents. Second, recruitment was limited to one online patient network, restricting generalizability. Third, women and participants with breast or uterine/ovarian cancers were overrepresented, whereas men, unpartnered parents, and several cancer groups were represented by small numbers. Only 16 partnered participants had uterine/ovarian cancer, and sex and sex-specific cancer diagnoses were difficult to disentangle in this sample. The final PCQ analytic cohort was restricted to participants who selected Married because the source survey did not independently establish a non-marital parenting partner; the findings therefore cannot be generalized to unpartnered parents.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal or predictive inference. Fifth, all variables were self-reported, and no information was obtained directly from children or co-parents; the psychiatric-history variable was broad and self-reported. Sixth, the expanded models included 28 parameters in an analytic sample of 157 participants and showed VIFs up to 7.05, indicating greater collinearity and model instability; several associations changed across the parsimonious and expanded specifications. Factor 3 showed heteroscedasticity in both model families, and HC3 sensitivity analyses indicated that inference for some expanded-model coefficients was unstable. Seventh, only three partnered participants reported no source of parenting support, so the corresponding expanded-model estimate was based on a very sparse subgroup. Eighth, the subscale analyses and univariate comparisons involved multiple testing without formal multiplicity adjustment and should be interpreted as exploratory. Ninth, the study-specific support items were unvalidated single-item measures of support availability/preferences and did not establish objective clinical need, unmet need, actual support receipt, or service adequacy; conceptual overlap and common-method effects with PCQ scores are possible. Tenth, no established clinical cutoff or minimal clinically important difference is available for the PCQ. Finally, youngest-child age categories were analytic developmental groupings rather than validated thresholds.

Future studies should include larger and more diverse samples, including unpartnered parents, men with cancer who are parents, and participants with a wider range of cancer types. Future work should also distinguish support availability, support preferences, actual support receipt, and support adequacy, and should evaluate the effectiveness of child-rearing support interventions.

Conclusions

Despite the limited single-network sample and low response rate, this study found that parenting concerns were associated with several self-reported characteristics and preferences for parenting support. However, these exploratory cross-sectional findings do not establish causal relationships or provide sufficient evidence to justify clinical targeting. Further confirmation in larger and more representative samples is needed.
