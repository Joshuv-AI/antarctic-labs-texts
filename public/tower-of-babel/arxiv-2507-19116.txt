Graph Structure Learning with Privacy Guarantees for Open Graph Data
Muhao Guo, Jiaqi Wu, Yang Weng, Yizheng Liao, Shengzhe Chen

Abstract
Ensuring privacy in large-scale open datasets is increasingly challenging under regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). While differential privacy (DP) provides strong theoretical guarantees, it primarily focuses on noise injection during model training, neglecting privacy preservation at the data publishing stage. Existing privacy-preserving data publishing (PPDP) approaches struggle to balance privacy and utility, particularly when data publishers and users are distinct entities. To address this gap, we focus on the graph recovery problem and propose a novel privacy-preserving estimation framework for open graph data, leveraging Gaussian DP (GDP) with a structured noise-injection mechanism. Unlike traditional methods that perturb gradients or model updates, our approach ensures unbiased graph structure recovery while enforcing DP at the data publishing stage. Moreover, we provide theoretical guarantees on estimation accuracy and extend our method to discrete-variable graphs, a setting often overlooked in DP research. Experimental results in graph learning demonstrate robust performance, offering a viable solution for privacy-conscious graph analysis.

Preliminaries

3.1
Statistical Learning: Sparse Graph Estimation

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=\{V,E\}
denote an undirected graph, where
V=\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
represents the set of vertices and
E\subset V\times V
represents the set of edges. Each vertex
i\in V
corresponds to a random variable
\mathbf{X}_{i}
. Such a structure,
{\mathcal{G}}
, is recognized as a graphical model
[
31
]
. When each random variable follows a Gaussian distribution, we have a GGM.
In a GGM, any two disconnected nodes,
\mathbf{X}_{i}
and
\mathbf{X}_{j}
, are conditionally independent, .
meaning
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{ij}=0
for a multivariate Gaussian distribution
\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
. Thus, sparse graph estimation is equivalent to finding the inverse covariance matrix, also known as the precision matrix,
\bm{\Theta}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
. The most famous approach to estimating the precision matrix
\bm{\Theta}
is the graphical lasso
[
6
]
, which optimizes the penalized log-likelihood function:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}\in\argmax\limits_{\Theta\succeq 0}J(\Theta;\bm{S}),

(1)

where
J(\Theta;\bm{S})=\log\det(\Theta)-\Tr(\bm{S}\Theta)-\lambda\|\Theta\|_{1}
. Here,
\Theta\succeq 0
denotes that
\Theta
is positive semi-definite,
\log\det(\cdot)
is the logarithm of the determinant,
\Tr(\cdot)
is the trace operator,
\bm{S}
is the empirical covariance matrix,
\lambda
is the regularization parameter, and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the
L_{1}
norm for sparsity. Common methods to solve (
1
) include coordinate descent
[
23
]
and ADMM
[
9
]
.

3.2
Privacy-Preserving by Gaussian Differential Privacy

In this work, we employ DP
[
21
]
defined as follows.

Definition 1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}
is considered
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private with
0\leq\varepsilon,0\leq\delta\leq 1
if for any two datasets
X,X^{\prime}
differing in precisely one element, and for all events
\mathcal{X}
:

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathcal{M}(X)\in\mathcal{X}\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}]+\delta
.

Recent research indicates that Gaussian differential privacy (GDP) offers improved privacy-utility trade-offs and more natural privacy guarantees. Therefore, we introduce the following definition:

Definition 2

(
Trade-off function
T
)
.

For any two probability distributions
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
in the same space, the difficulty of distinguishing
\mathbf{P}
from
\mathbf{Q}
by hypothesis testing is quantified by the trade-off function

T(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q}):[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
. It takes as input the level of type I error,
0<\alpha<1
, and outputs the corresponding type II error
\beta=T(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})(\alpha)
.

The trade-off function is a complete characterization of the fundamental difficulty in distinguishing two distributions through hypotheses testing
[
20
]
. The greater this function, the harder it is to distinguish between the distributions.

Definition 3

(
Gaussian differential privacy
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}
is
\mu
- Gaussian differential private (
\mu
-GDP) if
T(\mathcal{M}(X),\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime}))\geq G_{\mu}
for all neighboring datasets
X
and
X^{\prime}
differing in precisely one element, where
G_{\mu}:=T(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}(\mu,1))
for
\mu\geq 0
.

GDP is pivotal in this study for evaluating privacy preservation, leveraging its association with
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. In the GDP framework,
T(\mathcal{M}(X),\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime}))\geq G_{\mu}
means to distinguish
\mathcal{M}(X)
from
\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})
is tougher than differentiating between normal distributions
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)
through hypothesis testing. If
\mathcal{M}
is
\mu
-GDP, it is also
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
[
20
]
. Therefore, this GDP tool can bridge more intuitively to the classical DP framework.

4
Methodology

We first propose our data encryption method that preserves user privacy. We prove that such a method satisfied the differential privacy condition. Then, we propose our main result, the sparse graph estimation algorithm for privacy-preserving datasets. At last, we extend the proposed approach to handle the discrete datasets, which are common in machine learning tasks. All the proofs are given in the Appendix A.

4.1
Privacy-Preserving Data Encryption

For the multivariate Gaussian graphical model, we would like to recover its underlying structure via the graphical lasso algorithm introduced in section
3.1
. Assume the raw data
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is already centered by subtracting the mean. To recover the topology, one needs to compute statistics, in specific, the covariance matrix of
\bm{X}
, which is
\bm{S}=f(\bm{X})=\frac{1}{n}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
.
However, when the underlying data contains sensitive user data, it is dangerous to share the data with third parties directly. Therefore, we propose to have additive noises on the raw data for open data purposes:

\tilde{\bm{X}}=\bm{X}+\bm{E},

(2)

where
\tilde{\bm{X}}
denotes the encrypted data. The noise term
\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
consists of i.i.d. Gaussian noise of
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. By this encryption, the covariance matrix of
\tilde{\bm{X}}
is

\tilde{\bm{S}}=f(\tilde{\bm{X}})=f(\bm{X})+\frac{1}{n}(\bm{E}^{\top}\bm{X}+\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{E}+\bm{E}^{\top}\bm{E}):=\mathcal{M}(\bm{X}).

(3)

Because of this covariance matrix, we can prove the following theorem that a direct encryption of the raw data satisfy the Gaussian differential private (GDP), which is equivalent to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP defined in Section
3.2
. This gives us a good bound and unbiased estimator.

Theorem 4.1

Encryption mechanism
\mathcal{M}
in (
3
) is at least
\Delta_{f}(\sigma\sqrt{2C})^{-1}
-GDP, which indicates
\mathcal{M}
is also
(\varepsilon,\delta(\varepsilon))-DP
for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\delta(\varepsilon)=\Phi\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{2C}}{\Delta_{f}}+\frac{\Delta_{f}}{2\sigma\sqrt{2C}}\right)-e^{\varepsilon}\Phi\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\sigma\sqrt{2C}}{\Delta_{f}}-\frac{\Delta_{f}}{2\sigma\sqrt{2C}}\right),

where
\Phi
is the CDF of normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
\Delta_{f}=\sup_{\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime}}|f(\bm{X})-f(\bm{X}^{\prime})|
is the sensitivity of two neighboring data
\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime}
, which differ in exactly one element, and
C=\min_{k\in\{1,2,...,p\}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\bm{X}^{2}_{lk}
, i.e. the minimum square sum of each column in
\bm{X}
.

From the theorem,
\mathcal{M}
is
\Delta_{f}(\sigma\sqrt{2C})^{-1}
-GDP. Such property ensures the utility of privacy preservation won’t be under-estimated by any specific
(\varepsilon,\delta)-DP
scheme. This is because GDP provides the tightest privacy bound
[
20
]
. With the GDP tool, we are also able to quantify the privacy preservation utility by trade-off functions
G_{\Delta_{f}(\sigma\sqrt{2C})^{-1}}
, e.g., the difficulty of telling apart
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{f}(\sigma\sqrt{2C})^{-1},1)
. The value of sensitivity
\Delta_{f}
can be determined by expertise when implementing in specific domains. As the strength of privacy utility is controlled by
\sigma
, a larger
\sigma
makes distinguishing
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{f}(\sigma\sqrt{2C})^{-1},1)
more difficult, thereby preserving more privacy.

4.2
Sparse Graph Estimation with Privacy Preserving

While the mechanism in (
3

\tilde{\bm{X}}
no longer reflect the correct sparse graph connection. Thus, there is no way to recover the topology merely according to
\tilde{\bm{X}}

Theorem 4.2

Let graph nodes
V=\left\{1,2,\cdots,i,\cdots,p\right\}
sparsely connected,
p>2
, and there exists a path connecting
\forall i,j\in V
. Then, for
\sigma^{2}<\|\bm{\Theta}\|^{-1}

\|\cdot\|
is arbitrary matrix norm,
\bm{\Theta}_{ij}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{ij}=0
leads to
\tilde{\bm{\Theta}}_{ij}=\left(\bm{\Sigma}+\sigma^{2}\bm{I}\right)^{-1}_{ij}\neq 0

Theorem
4.2
shows that the original graph structure will be totally damaged by adding sufficiently small Gaussian noise to encrypt. All zero entries of
\bm{\Theta}
will be non-zero, which means the sparsity no longer holds, and the encrypted graph structure represented by
\tilde{\bm{\Theta}}
exhibits density connections; thus, one cannot trivially reconstruct the original graph structure. A toy example is given in Appendix A.
A similar conclusion was also suggested and validated in
[
26
]
. This theorem validates the effectiveness of adding Gaussian noise as encryption.
To address the issue above, in this section, we propose a new algorithm to estimate the original graph structure
\bm{\Theta}
with the privacy-preserving data
\tilde{\bm{X}}=\bm{X}+\bm{E}
. As shown in Theorem
4.2

\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}+\sigma^{2}\bm{I})

1

However, with slightly little extra information, we are able to recover the original graph structure
\bm{\Theta}

Theorem 4.3

(
Sparse graph estimation with continuous privacy-preserving data

Given

\displaystyle J(\Theta;\tilde{\bm{S}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{I})

J(\Theta;\bm{S})=\mathbb{E}\left[J(\Theta;\tilde{\bm{S}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{I})\right].

(4)

\displaystyle J(\Theta;\tilde{\bm{S}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{I})

\widehat{\bm{\Theta}}\in\argmax\limits_{\Theta\succeq 0}J(\Theta;\tilde{\bm{S}}-\sigma^{2}\bm{I}).

(5)

Corollary
1

4.3
DP and Sparse Graph Estimation with Discrete Data

In the previous sections, we addressed the challenges of sparse graph estimation with continuous and privacy-preserving data. However, in many practical applications, such as social networks, the collected data is in the discrete domain. Applying Gaussian noise to discrete variables can significantly hinder interpretability
[
12
]
. In this section, we discuss how to preserve data privacy for the discrete Gaussian distribution
\mathbf{X}_{k}\in\mathbf{X}
and analyze the utility of the encryption algorithm by adding discrete Gaussian noise.

Definition 4

(
Discrete Gaussian
[
12
]
)
.

Discrete random variable
\mathbf{X}_{k}
following discrete Gaussian distribution is denoted as
\mathbf{X}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, with probability at each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\mathbb{P}\left[\mathbf{X}_{k}=x\right]=\frac{\exp\left\{-(x-\mu)/2\sigma^{2}\right\}}{\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left\{-(y-\mu)/2\sigma^{2}\right\}}.

(7)

(
7
) is a natural discretization of
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. Now, suppose our encryption in (
2
) adds a discrete Gaussian noise to the centered raw data
\bm{X}
, i.e.
\tilde{\bm{X}}=\bm{X}+\bm{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, where components of
\bm{E}_{\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{Z}}(0,\sigma^{2})
. We have the following theorem to evaluate
\mathcal{M}
’s privacy utility in (
3
).

Theorem 4.4

In the case of adding discrete Gaussian noise as encryption,
\mathcal{M}
is
\frac{\Delta_{f}+2K}{\sqrt{2n}K\sigma}
-GDP. It is also
(\varepsilon,\delta(\varepsilon))
-DP for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

\delta(\varepsilon)=\Phi\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{2n}K\sigma}{(\Delta_{f}+2K)}+\frac{\Delta_{f}+2K}{2\sqrt{2n}K\sigma}\right)-e^{\varepsilon}\Phi\left(-\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{2n}K\sigma}{(\Delta_{f}+2K)}-\frac{\Delta_{f}+2K}{2\sqrt{2n}K\sigma}\right)
,
where
\Phi
is the CDF of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
K=\frac{1}{n}\min_{\bm{x}_{li}\in\bm{X}}|\bm{x}_{li}|
, and
\Delta_{f}
is the sensitivity of two neighboring data differing in exactly one element.

When it comes to sparse graph estimation with discrete Gaussian noise encryption, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5

(

In the case of encrypting each

5.1
Validation Setup

Parameter Tuning.

Solving (
5
) and (
8
) involves optimally selecting the parameter
\lambda
for the
L_{1}
regularization term. As recommended by
[
23
]
, cross-validation is an effective strategy. This method involves dividing the data into
K
folds and iteratively training the graphical lasso model on
K-1
folds while the remaining fold serves for validation across a spectrum of
\lambda
values. This process is illustrated in Appendix B,
showing the optimal
\lambda
is the first to cause a dramatic MSE drop.

Evaluation Metrics.

The sparse graph estimation can be framed as a binary classification problem for each potential edge. So, we use Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) score as evaluation metrics. In cases where a dataset lacks a ground truth structure, we adopt the outcome of the standard graphical lasso as the de facto ground truth to check if our model can achieve performance similar to a vanilla graphical lasso.

Complexity Analysis.

Let
n
be the number of variables and
p
be the number of samples.
The complexity includes two parts.
The encryption complexity is
\mathcal{O}(np)
, as we add Gaussian noise to the data matrix
R^{n\times p}
.
The second part involves applying the reconstruction algorithm to recover the graph from the empirical covariance matrix
R^{n\times n}
. The complexity of this part depends on the method used, such as Coordinate Descent or ADMM. So, let
k
be the number of iterations required for convergence. The total complexity of the iterative part is
O(kn^{3})
, not considering the benefits of sparsity. It is important to note that methods like Coordinate Descent or ADMM are necessary for similar reconstruction methods, so our approach does not incur additional computational costs.

Privacy Protection.

We use signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in decibel (dB) to measure the noise applied to the actual data for obfuscation
[
45
]
.
Considering
\Delta_{f}=\sup_{\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime}}|f(\bm{X})-f(\bm{X}^{\prime})|
is the sensitivity of two neighboring data
\bm{X},\bm{X}^{\prime}
, which differ in exactly one element,
we use the
max(\bm{X})-min(\bm{X})
to denote the largest difference between two neighboring data. Since
f(\bm{X})
is the covariance matrix of
\bm{X}
, we get
\Delta_{f}=2\times\frac{(max(\bm{X})-min(\bm{X}))^{2}}{n}
. Then, the privacy parameter
\epsilon=\frac{\Delta_{f}}{\sigma_{n}}
can be obtained, where
\sigma_{n}
is the standard deviation of noise. We show each
\epsilon
corresponding to each SNR for each dataset in Table
2
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a performance-guaranteed, privacy-preserving approach to graph structure learning that ensures robust data protection while maintaining analytical utility. By integrating Gaussian differential privacy, we develop a maximum likelihood estimation framework for sparse graph learning that preserves the privacy of raw data and extends applicability from continuous to discrete datasets. Empirical evaluations on diverse real-world and synthetic datasets demonstrate that the proposed method effectively balances privacy protection and estimation accuracy, making it a viable solution for privacy-conscious graph analysis in modern machine learning. Future work will extend this approach to broader classes of graphical models, including dynamic and temporal networks, and refine privacy-utility tradeoffs through adaptive noise mechanisms.
