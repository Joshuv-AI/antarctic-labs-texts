A complete formalism for giant Goos-Hänchen shift of metasurface sensors with phase singularity Preprint: APS/123-QED
Lotfi Berguiga 1, Sébastien Cueff 2, Taha Benyattou 1, Xavier Letartre 2, Cécile Jamois 1

Abstract
Giant Goos-Hänchen shift is a promising effect for very highly sensitive sensors application. For sensors working at the critical coupling regime, we provide here a complete formalism that describes analytically the Goos-Hänchen shift, the Goos-Hänchen sensitivity and the limit of detection. It reveals the key parameters (quality factor, angular dispersion but also the phase singularity) which are involved in the GH shift. Approaching the critical coupling leads to ultimate Goos-Hänchen shift and sensitivity. Thanks to this theoretical description confirmed numerically, we propose lateral beam displacement that goes beyond giant GH shift and sensitivity values as high as 10 13 ​ μ ​ m / R ​ I ​ U 10^{13}\mu m/RIU which is an improvement of Goos-Hänchen sensitivity by more than one thousand compared to theoretical value and more than ten million if compared to experimental works. We illustrate it for surface plasmon resonance of thin metal film where the critical coupling is approached with the crystallisation of a phase change material GST and for metasurfaces supporting Bloch Surface Waves. We also discuss the effect of beam size on GH effect and showed that it is the main limitation for increasing GH sensitivty and is a parameter that must be considered. The limit of detection of GH sensors is finally described analytically. Theses results open the way for new sensing application needing high sensitivity.



I
Introduction

The Goos-Hänchen (GH) effect is the lateral displacement of a reflected beam on an interface. This lateral displacement can be large in resonant photonic structures such as Fabry-Perot cavity
Wang et al. 2008
, surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
Yin et al. 2004
;
Wan and Zubairy 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Yan et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2024
, 2D transition metal dichalocogenides, topological singularity
You et al. 2018
;
Guo et al. 2020
;
Yan et al. 2024
, Bloch surface wave metasurfaces
Wan et al. 2012
and Bound State in the Contiunum (BIC) of metasurfaces
Wu et al. 2019
;
Ruan et al. 2022
;
Du et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
;
Qi et al. 2024
. The lateral displacement could be reach several tens to thousands of wavelength and is sensitive to the local refractive index change at the interface.
The GH effect is a promising method to get very high sensitive photonic sensor pushing the limit of detection very low. With giant GH shift, sensitivity as high as
10^{6}\mu m/RIU
and limit of detection as low as
10^{-6}-10^{-7}
RIU have been reported experimentally
Wang et al. 2008
;
Wan et al. 2012
;
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
as well as detection of biomarkers with concentration as low as 0.1 fM
Zhu et al. 2024
. A key advantage of this method compared to phase sensor based on interferometry or ellipsometry
Kabashin et al. 1998
;
a.V. Kabashin et al. 1999
;
Kabashin et al. 2009
;
Ng et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2008
lies on its much simpler instrumental development.

Theoretical works predict even higher GH shift (few mm) and GH sensitivity (
10^{8}\mu m/RIU
)
Zhang et al. 2021
;
Youssef et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2024
, but the practical limits of the GH shift is still an open question.
While this effect is boosted in photonic resonances, theoretical framework for resonant photonic structure working are not complete. More specifically previous work is for particular photonic structures e.g. long range surface plasmon
Chen et al. 2011
and does not provide an analytical expression versus the physical parameters of the optical resonator. In the scientific literature, quality factor
Chen et al. 2023
;
Qi et al. 2024
and the critical coupling
Youssef et al. 2022
;
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Yan et al. 2024
seem to play a role but is at best of our knowledge not explicitly described and quantified. Another lack concerns the conditions of experimental measurements to obtain optimized GH shift and sensing.

In this work, with the help of temporal coupled mode theory (CMT), we propose a complete formalism that reveals the key parameters leading to giant GH shift and GH sensitivity for photonic structures. By approaching the critical coupling, we show how to reach ultimate GH shift and GH sensitivity with values 3 orders (resp. 5 orders) of magnitude larger than published theoretical GH shift (resp. GH sensitivity)..
Under the critical coupling conditions, we establish a direct relation between the GH shift, the lateral propagation of photons of the resonant mode and the amount of reflected light.

We also demonstrate the necessity to take into account the real experimental size of the light beam to design GH-based sensors. Finally, our theoretical framework provides an analytical description of the Limit of detection (LOD) which is the main parameter that characterize the efficiency of a sensor.

This paper is decomposed in five parts. The first part installs the theoretical framework of the analytical expressions of GH shift and sensitivity. The second part validates the first part by simulation for two kind of transducers. The first transducer is based on SPR assisted with PCM which is
Ge_{2}Sb_{2}Te_{5}
(GST). PCM allows to reach the critical coupling trough the control of the PCM phase. The second case is a metasurface based on Bloch Surface Wave (BSW). In the third part we will discuss the effect of the beam size on the GH shift. The four part deal with the limit of detection. The last part will discuss about trade-off between Ultimate sensitivity and experimental constraint to achieve it. Design of metasurface will be proposed for this purpose.

II
Formalism of sensor with Goos-Hänchen effect

When light is reflected by an interface at an angle of incidence
\theta_{0}
, the lateral shift can be estimated by the expression:

D_{GH}=\frac{\int xI_{r}(x)dx}{\int I_{r}(x)dx}cos\theta_{0}

(1)

With
I_{r}(x)=E_{r}(x)E_{r}^{*}(x)
the intensity spatial profile of the reflected field at plane
z=0
corresponding to the surface of the device.

This expression is valuable for any beam profile, and can be applied for the particular case of an infinite beam (i.e to a wave plane) and as shown by the Moreau’s team
Pollès et al. 2016
leads to the Artmann’s formula:

D_{GH}=-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{d\theta}

(2)

As it can be seen, an abrupt phase change, as observed at resonance in critical coupling regime, induces a large GH shift.
We propose to establish the theoretical framework of the lateral shift for an infinite beam for resonant photonic sensor working at critical coupling by modeling the reflectivity with temporal coupled mode theory. In a second step (section
IV
), unlighted by the theoretical framework, we will study the case of finite real beam.

In CMT, resonant photonic sensor working at critical coupling as for example surface plasmon resonance or Bloch surface wave can be modeled by a resonator of pulsation resonance
\omega_{res}
and internal constant loss
\tau_{i}
and one port of radiative loss characterized by the constant time
\tau_{rad}
. The complex reflectivity versus the pulsation
\omega
is expressed by the following analytical relation:

r(\omega)=r_{b}\cdot\frac{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}-j\left(\omega-\omega_{res}\right)}{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}+j\left(\omega-\omega_{res}\right)}

(3)

where
r_{b}
is the complex reflection coefficient of the reflecting part (Gold layer for SPR or Bragg mirror for BSW).
Since equation (
3
) is complex, we can derive the reflection phase as follow:

\phi=-atan\left(\frac{\omega-\omega_{res}}{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}\right)-atan\left(\frac{\omega-\omega_{res}}{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}\right)+C

(4)

where C depends on the sign of
\tau_{rad}^{-1}-\tau_{i}^{-1}
: either the resonance corresponds to the under-coupling regime (
C
=0 when negative), either it corresponds to the over-coupling regime (
C
=
\pi
when positive). The profile of the resonance in intensity has a dip inverted peak pattern and in phase becomes sharper and sharper when approaching the critical coupling.

The quality factor
Q
and
r_{min}
which is the minimum of reflectivity that occurs at resonance can be written versus the two time constants as:

Q=\frac{\omega_{res}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}\text{\hskip 4.30554ptand\hskip 4.30554pt}r_{min}=\frac{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}+\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}

(5)

From equation (
2
) and (
4
), we can express the GH shift:

\displaystyle D_{GH}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda_{res}}{2\pi}\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\omega_{res}}\frac{d\omega_{res}}{d\theta}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle D_{GH}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{aQ\lambda_{res}^{2}}{\pi cr_{min}}\frac{1}{1+\left(\frac{2aQ\lambda_{res}}{\pi cr_{min}}\right)^{2}\left(\theta-\theta_{res}\right)^{2}}

(7)

with a the angular dispersion near the resonance defined as
a=\frac{d\omega}{d\theta}
. The GH shift
D_{GH}
versus the angle of excitation is a Lorentz profile with a maximal value at resonance angle
\theta_{res}
( figure(
1
a))
By the same way, by two derivations of the phase equation we get the bulk GH sensitivity versus the angle of excitation when the refractive index of the medium of analyte
n
varies as:

\displaystyle S_{GH}

\displaystyle=\frac{dD_{GH}}{dn}

(8)

\displaystyle S_{GH}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2pi}\frac{d}{d\omega_{res}}\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\omega_{res}}\frac{d\omega_{res}}{d\theta}\right)\frac{d\omega_{res}}{d\lambda}\frac{d\lambda}{dn}

(9)

\displaystyle S_{GH}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-16\frac{a^{2}Q^{3}\lambda_{res}^{2}}{\pi^{3}c^{2}r_{min}^{3}}\frac{\theta-\theta_{res}}{\left(1+\left(\frac{2aQ\lambda_{res}}{\pi cr_{min}}\right)^{2}(\theta-\theta_{res})^{2}\right)^{2}}\cdot S_{\lambda}

(10)

With
c
the speed of light and
S_{\lambda}=\frac{d\lambda}{dn}
, the sensitivity in wavelength of the the transducer.

The GH sensitivity as a function of the angle of excitation from the equation is shown in figure (
1
b)).

Figure 1:
GH shift and sensitivity versus the angle of excitation for several value of
Q/r_{min}

the angle of resonance

For a given wavelength, sensor is not the most sensitive at angle resonance
\theta_{res}
but rather at an angle below and upper where the slope of the GH shift vs angle excitation is the most abrupt. By derivative one times the relation (
), the angle
\theta_{\pm}
follows the relation:

\theta_{\pm}=\theta_{res}\pm\frac{\omega_{res}}{2\sqrt{3}a}\frac{r_{min}}{Q}=\theta_{res}\pm\frac{\pi c}{\sqrt{3}a\lambda}\frac{r_{min}}{Q}

(11)

At these angles
\theta_{\pm}
, The GH shift can be written:

D_{GH}=-\frac{3a\lambda_{res}^{2}}{8\pi^{2}c}\frac{Q}{r_{min}}

(12)

The maximum bulk sensitivity
S_{GH}
that occurs is at
\theta_{\pm}
is described by the formula:

\displaystyle S_{GH}^{max}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3\sqrt{3}a\lambda_{res}}{8\pi^{2}c}\frac{Q^{2}}{r_{min}^{2}}S_{\lambda}

(13)

These analytical expressions in equation (
12
) and (
13
) shows the GH shift is proportional to the ratio
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
and the GH sensitivity proportional to the square of the same ratio
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
. Giant GH shift and sensitivity can be obtained when increasing the quality factor of the resonance or when approaching the critical coupling. Approaching the critical coupling means lowering the minimum of reflectivity
r_{min}
. This ratio
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
can be written as function of the time constants:

\frac{Q}{r_{min}}=\frac{\omega_{res}}{2}\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\tau_{rad}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{i}}}

(14)

The ratio
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
diverges when the constant times are equal (
\tau_{rad}=\tau_{i}
) which is the condition of critical coupling (citer papier). This ratio traduces the sharpness of the phase jump at resonance. Moreover the sensitivity
S_{GH}
increases more quickly than GH shift
D_{GH}
: the variation is quadratic with the coefficient
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
. We have to keep in mind GH sensitivity is proportional to wavelength sensitivity
S_{\lambda}
which traduces the spatial volume of the electromagnetic field of the mode outside the photonic structure hence accessible to sensing
El Beheiry et al. 2010
. Consequently a good GH sensor is firstly a good classical sensor (sensor interrogated spectrally or in intensity).

Moreover we can provide a more physical meaning of the GH shift. It can be related to the mean free propagation length of the photons of the resonant mode
L
during their time of life
\tau
(with
1/\tau=1/(1/\tau_{i}+1/\tau_{rad})
). The mean speed of the photons of the surface wave is the group velocity
v_{g}
.
v_{g}
and
L
can be formulated as follows:

v_{g}=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\cos\theta_{res}}a~~and~~L=v_{g}\tau=v_{g}.\frac{4Q}{w_{res}}

(15)

Rewriting this last expressions in equation (
12
), it leads for the GH shift to:

D_{GH}=\frac{3}{16.\cos\theta_{res}}\frac{L}{r_{min}}

(16)

This relation shows that the GH shift for an infinite beam (a wave plane i.e stationary phase) is governed both by the
propagation length of the surface mode
L
and by the critical coupling through the parameter
1/r_{min}
. Tamir and Bertoni
Tamir and Bertoni 1971
pointed out in 1971 the role of
L
for the GH shift of real finite beam.
L
can be defined also as the inverse of
k_{x}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
the imaginary part of the wavevector of the mode. Highlighted by this approach we will discuss later the case of finite real beam.

IV
Beam size effect

We need to provide more insight when a real beam is used. High GH shift induces that the angle of excitation should be very precise and should not suffer blurring due the divergence angle of the beam. For a Gaussian beam, the waist
\sigma
is related to the angle divergence
\theta_{0}
by the relation
\sigma=\frac{\lambda}{\pi\theta_{0}}
. Then reducing the angle divergence, leads to increase the beam size. In other words, the GH shift definition provided by equation (
2
) is true only for beam with infinite diameter (the limit of a plane-wave). R.G Wan and M. S. Zubary
Wan and Zubairy 2020
studied numerically the effect of the size of a Gaussian beam on the GH shift for SPR sensor and shows a diminution of the GH shift when the beam size decreases. Tamir and Bertoni
Tamir and Bertoni 1971
in 1971, showed theoretically and numerically that for a Gaussian beam, the lateral shift reaches a limit that is related to the inverse of the imaginary part of the wavevector
k_{x}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
of the resonant mode in other word the lateral propagation distance
L=1/|k_{x}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}|
of the surface wave. They showed for a real beam to ensure a good coupling from the incident light towards the surface wave, the beam size must be greater than
L
otherwise the GH shift is truncated. They also showed when the beam size is far greater than
L
, the GH shift reaches a limit.
In figure (
7
) we show similar results. To compute the GH shift for real beam with equation(
1
), the 1D spatial electric field
E_{r}(x)
at plane
z=0
of Gaussian beam with a waist
\sigma
and an angle of incidence
\theta_{0}
can be decomposed in angular spectrum (i.e in wave planes ):

E_{r}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{E}(k_{x})e^{ik_{x}x}dk_{x}

(17)

where

\tilde{E}(k_{x})=\frac{\sigma_{x}}{\sqrt{2}}exp\left(-\frac{\sigma_{x}^{2}(k_{x}-k_{0})^{2}}{4}\right)

(18)

with
\sigma_{x}=\frac{\sigma}{cos\theta_{0}}
the projected waist on the interface and
k_{x}=\frac{2\pi n_{0}}{\lambda}sin\theta

The reflected beam en-goes the reflection
r(\theta)=r(k_{x})
and can so be written as:

E_{r}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}r(k_{x})\tilde{E}(k_{x})e^{ik_{x}x}dk_{x}

(19)

Figure 7:
SPR Giant Goos-Hänchen shift for a finite Gaussian beam for different crystallisation fraction of the PCM for SPR transducer. a) Gaussian beam displacement for SPR. b) Goos-Hänchen shift versus the Gaussian beam waist. c) Normalized Goos-Hänchen shift versus the normalised beam size.

From figure(
7
b), for each phase state of the PCM, the GH shift increases when the size of the beam increases and reaches always a plateau. This maximal GH shift value correspond to value found with relation (
2
). It confirms that the GH effect is attenuated if the real beam size is low. At best of our knowledge, no work tried to find a general law for this behavior when the GH shift change. In figure(
7
c), a generalized trend can be found when both the GH shift of the real beam and the waist of the laser are normalized by the GH shift of the infinite beam (i.e obtained with Artmann’s equation (
2
)). The normalized GH shift value is studied versus the normalised waist of the laser. With this normalization, all the curves for the different coupling conditions hence for the different GH shift are mostly superposed. More striking the normalized value is limited by one. It is worth to mention it since it means that even for Giant GH shift, this displacement remains a fraction of the beam size. The displacement never goes over the size of the beam. In other words there is still a common part between the incoming beam and the reflected beam. This remark seems obvious but is usually forgotten when experiments with GH effect is reported and the size of the beam is never informed despite its basic role on the GH effect. Actually Tamir and Bertoni have provided this kind of conclusion
Tamir and Bertoni 1971
and we generalized it for photonic structures working at critical coupling. From these normalized curves, it can be concluded that if we do not want to attenuate the GH shift by more than 40%, the real beam should have a waist at least equal to the GH shift of the infinite beam i.e. the diameter should be two times greater than the maximal GH shift. And if we want to limit the attenuation to only
20\%
, the beam diameter should be at least 4 times greater than the maximal GH shift. For example, for the crystallisation fraction of
43\%
where the critical coupling is the nearest, the GH shift is 1 mm for an infinite beam. Consequently, the real beam should be at least 4 mm in diameter to get a real GH shift of 0.8 mm. To observe giant GH shift and beyond, wide beam is needed. And for crystallisation fraction of
46\%
the width of beam should be 36 cm tp obatain a GH shift of 7.2 cm

It is essential to take into account the GH shift attenuation by the size of the real beam when designing and/or making experimental measurements. It limits the sensor efficiency and its field of application since for a lot of applications it is not realistic to use a beam with a size of 36 cm. This is the main limitation to reach the highest possible GH shift and sensitivity. Nevertheless we will see later in order to reach very high sensitivity that this constrain can be tackled by optimization .

V
Limit of detection for sensing

In sensing application, the limit of detection (LOD) characterizes the efficiency of the sensor. VI
Discussion

We have shown in this work that there is no limit in the lateral displacement of a reflected beam by the Goos-Hänchen effect for resonant photonic systems working in the critical coupling regime. In this work, for the first time to the best of our knowledge, the physical parameters that rule the GH shift as well as the GH sensitivity are provided. We have showed with equation ((
12
)) and (
13
) for GH shift and GhH sensitivity the special role of the ratio
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
. The GH sensitivity evolves far more faster than the GH shift when approaching the critical coupling condition and its phase singularity (i;e when increasing the the ratio
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
). It leads to sensor far more sensitive than any other method. However GH sensor with GH shift beyond current limit are not useful since ultimate sensitivity needs huge beam in size (several tens of centimeters to meters). A solution to tackle this issue is to get a trad-off between having the highest sensitivity and not having a too much wide beam. To succeed, we will use the two properties that GH shift varies more slowly than GH sensitivity when
\frac{Q}{r_{min}}
is increased and that GH shift diminish more fastly than GH sensitivity when the wavelength of resonance
\lambda
decreases.
In order to achieve this, the best is to reduce the angle dispersion

\lambda

VII
Conclusion

With the temporal coupling mode theory, a formalism for the Goos-Hänchen effect of resonators working at the critical coupling regime is provided and has been verified numerically on canonical examples, namely SPR and BSW based transducers. This formalism allows to reveal that there no limit in the Goos-Hänchen shift and it will be possible to get very giant Goos-Hänchen shift by approaching the critical coupling and its phase singularity. By the same formalism, the sensitivity of Goos-Hänchen sensors is described analytically and leads to ultimate sensitivity at the critical coupling (equations (
12
) and (
13
)). It lift the lid about the possibility of Goos-Hânchen effect for ultimate sensitivity when metasurface are considered instead of the usual surface plasmon resonance. In this work, it is also pointed out the basic role of the real beam size on the Goos-Hänchen shift. This is the main constrain and limitation of Goos-Hänchen sensing application. For this we propose a trade-off to achieve realistic high sensitivity. By determining all the physical parameters involved in the Goos-Hänchen effect, this works is a road map to the design of metasurfaces or photonic transducers the most suited or the most sensitive reaching ultimate limit of sensing measure in the photonic sensor domain. This works proves theoretically that when the limit of detection is governed by the quantum limit, the critical coupling plays no role (equation (
26
)) but in case of more usual realistic experimental cases it plays as an amplifier (equation (
28
)). Limit of detection as low as
10^{-13}
RIU can be reached. Such low limit opens the way in new sensing application, in gas sensing, quantum sensing and metrology.
