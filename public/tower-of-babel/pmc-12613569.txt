Identification and characterization of Jingmen tick virus in Jiangsu, China
Authors: Heng Rong, Zhiming Wu, Kangchen Zhao, Yuhan Ding, Shuo Ning, Ye Tian, Xiang Wang, Qiao Qiao, Xiaojuan Zhu, Tao Wu, Yiyue Ge, Hongliang Chu, Lunbiao Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractJingmen virus (JMV) is a group of viruses that belong to the Flaviviridae family. These viruses have been shown to cause widespread infections in various hosts and can lead to febrile illnesses in humans. Jingmen tick virus (JMTV) as an important member of the JMV group, has been detected in multiple countries worldwide and poses a significant threat to public health. This study utilized metagenomic sequencing technology to detect JMV in tick samples collected in Jiangsu Province. The results demonstrated the presence of JMTV in Jiangsu and identified two complete genomes (ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7-9) from Haemaphysalis campanulate ticks. These gene sequences exhibited the highest sequence similarity to the known Japanese isolate of JMTV. Phylogenetic analysis showed that the JMTV identified in this study clustered within the same clade as the Japanese JMTV. In summary, this study reported for the first time that JMTV is prevalent in Jiangsu Province, China. These findings expand the known geographic distribution and genetic diversity of JMTV, providing new insights into its epidemiology and viral evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02994-2.Keywords: Jiangsu Province, Tick-borne viruses, Jingmen tick virus (JMTV), Phylogenetic analysis

Introduction

Jingmen viruses (JMV) are a group of viruses including Alongshan virus (ALSV), Takachi virus, Yanggou tick virus, Pteropus lylei jingmen virus (PLJV) and Jingmen tick virus (JMTV). These viruses are currently classified by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses as unclassified Flaviviridae [1]. JMV have been detected across a wide range of the world, particularly in tick species that are widespread, in close contact with vertebrates, and known to be vectors for other diseases. These viruses have caused over a hundred febrile illnesses and flu-like symptoms among humans [2]. The JMTV is an important member of the JMV group. The genome of JMTV is segmented positive-sense single-stranded RNA (+ ssRNA viruses). The viral genome is 11 kb in length and can be divided into four fragments: S1, S2, S3 (which are monocistronic segments), and S4 (which is a bicistronic segment). The complete genome encodes five proteins: NSP1, NSP2, VP1, VP2, and VP3. The NSP1 and NSP2 proteins genes are associated with the non-structural protein genes NS5 and NS2B/NS3 of the Flaviviridae family, while the remaining proteins are unique to the JMTV [3].

The Jingmen tick virus was first discovered in Jingmen City, Hubei Province, China in 2010. Since its discovery, JMTV has been detected in tick and mosquito samples from multiple hosts, including cows, monkeys, bats, and rodents. Currently, JMTV has been confirmed that it could persist in tick intestines and salivary gland tissues [4]. Despite regional variations in the species of ticks reported to have detected JMTV, the most common species in which JMTV RNA has been detected is Rhipicephalus microplus. Data from recent studies showed that a high prevalence of JMTV has been found in Rhipicephalus microplus from China (53%–63%), Brazil (25%–67%), Tobago (6%–46%), and the French Antilles (24%–77%). These results suggested that JMTV may be able to enter new regions and cause infection through the transmission of Rhipiocephalus microplus [2]. Humans were primarily infected with the JMTV through tick bites, which caused febrile diseases. In China, four JMTV-infected patients were identified by high-throughput sequencing of skin biopsies and blood samples. The virus replication in skin tissue was visualised by in situ hybridisation. The four patients all had an itchy or painful eschar at the site of tick bite, with lymphadenopathy. Immunohistochemical examination revealed remarkable local inflammation manifested as infiltration by neutrophils. Eight patients were identified by serological testing and showed more severe clinical manifestations [5]. Additionally, the genome of JMTV was detected in patients with Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) patients in Kosovo and in febrile patients in China. These results suggested JMTV is pathogenic to humans and may be co-infected with other viruses [6].

To date, the Jingmen tick virus has been reported in multiple regions worldwide, including Asia [7], Europe [8], the Americas [9] and Africa [10]. In China, JMTV has shown a broad distribution across several provinces, including Heilongjiang [11], Guangxi [5], Xinjiang [12], Henan, Guizhou [13], Fujian [14], Sichuan [15], Yunnan [16], Hubei [17], Jiangxi [18], Hainan [19], and Hunan [20]. Notably, JMTV has also been detected in the provinces adjacent to Jiangsu Province, including Shandong, Anhui [21], and Zhejiang. With the ongoing expansion of tick activity, tick-borne viruses such as JMTV may pose an increasing threat to public health and safety in Jiangsu Province.

This study utilized metagenomic sequencing technology to detect the presence of JMV in tick samples collected in Jiangsu Province. The results demonstrated the presence of JMTV in Jiangsu, this is the first report in Jiangsu. This discovery further expanded the geographical distribution and genetic diversity of JMTV and offers new insights into its epidemiology and evolution dynamics. These findings suggest potential public health implications, underscoring the need for further evaluation of its impact.

Materials and methods

Samples collection

In 2022, ticks were collected from Zhenjiang, Nantong, Suzhou, Suqian and Taizhou, which are geographically located in the northern, central, and southern regions of Jiangsu Province to form sample pools. Free-living ticks were gathered from vegetation, while parasitic ticks were sampled directly from various host animals, including dogs, goats, and sheep. A total of 227 ticks were collected and divided into 10 sample pools (Among them, Taizhou and Suqian each contributed one sample pool, Zhenjiang and Nantong each contributed two sample pools, and Suzhou contributed four sample pools). Specimens with intact morphology were selected for the morphological identification of tick samples collected from the same batch. The Ultra-Depth Three-Dimensional Microscope (KEYENCE VHX-2000) was used to observe the key features for morphological identification. Live ticks were collected in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and stored at −80 ℃ to ensure sample integrity. Before using the tick samples, they were washed with PBS, and then the 4 mm grinding beads (Cat: JX-YG0116), 500 µL lysis buffer, 20 µL protease K, and 500 µL Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (DMEM) buffer were added to the centrifuge tubes. The samples were ground and homogenized using a cryogenic grinder (DeHe Technology Company, China Cat: N9596R) under the following conditions: a temperature of 4 ℃, a frequency of 65 Hz, and a duration of 5 min. The homogenized samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatant was collected for nucleic acid extraction (ENLIGHTEN BIOTECH, Cat: GB1001239).

Extraction of DNA and RNA, RT-PCR and sequencing

The RNA was extracted from different pools. After cleaning and homogenization, the sample was centrifuged to extract the whole genome. The Virus DNA/RNA extraction kit (TIANLONG Company, China Cat: T035) was used to extract virus RNA from the treated supernatant. The extraction was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The supernatant was treated with a proprietary lysis buffer containing proteinase K to disrupt viral capsids and release nucleic acids. The lysate was then processed using a spin column with a silica-based membrane. After washing with the supplied buffers to remove impurities, pure viral nucleic acids (vDNA and vRNA) were eluted in elution buffer and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Library construction was performed using an RNA library preparation kit (MGI, China, 1000006384). The library preparation process typically involved the following steps: (a) DNA fragmentation; (b) end repair and A-tailing to generate blunt-ended, 5′-phosphorylated fragments with a 3′ A-overhang; and (c) adapter ligation, during which MGI-specific duplex adapters were ligated to the fragments. Following ligation, the library was treated with the Library DNA Digestion Preparation Kit (MGI, China, Cat# 1000006988) to reduce background noise and improve the quality and efficiency of subsequent sequencing reactions. The adapter-ligated DNA was then denatured by heating to produce single-stranded DNA (ssDNA). Using a ligase, the linear ssDNA was circularized to form single-stranded circular DNA molecules. These circular templates were amplified via rolling circle amplification (RCA), yielding long tandem-repeat DNA products. These were then condensed into DNA nanoballs (DNBs) through mechanical or chemical processing. Library quality and concentration were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer. Libraries meeting quality thresholds were pooled in equimolar ratios and sequenced on the MGI G99 platform with a 150 bp paired-end (PE150) strategy, yielding raw viral metagenomic sequencing data.

Sequence analysis

Based on the obtained gene sequences, genetic analysis was performed, and sequence similarity was assessed using the NT (Last modified: 2024-02-08) and NR databases (Last modified: 2024-02-07) through BLASTn and BLASTp searches in GenBank to identify virus species and infer their closest evolutionary relationships. The metagenomic datasets of all 227 tick samples were analyzed, and 57 ticks from Zhenjiang were among the 227 ticks. To analyze the sample sequencing data, we imported it into CLC Genomics Workbench 22.0 and performed comparative analysis using Taxonomic Profiling software, referencing the DRM feature database (http://data.mypathogen.org/pgdb/pubdb/9/view). The two JMTV gene sequences underwent metagenomic sequencing, preliminary virus identification via the BLAST method, and whole genome resequencing. The resulting sequences were then compared to existing JMTV gene sequences (Jingmen tick virus 18EH12, GenBank ID: NC-024111.1/NC-024112.1/NC-024113.1/NC-024114.1) for assembly and obtained the full-length JMTV genome sequence. For the ZJ7-4-2 group, there were 49.48 million reads with a total data size of 2.6 GB, the JMTV-specific reads counts were 2,118,442 bp, the average depth was 18770.71X. The coverage for 4 fragments was 100%, 100%, 100% and 99.93% respectively. For the ZJ7-9 group, there were 35 million reads with a total data size of 1.84 GB, the JMTV-specific reads counts were 87,622 bp, and the average depth was 760.63X. The coverage for 4 fragments was 96.98%, 97.93%, 100.18% and 99.93% respectively (Supplementary Table S1). The relevant sequence data in this study were deposited at the National Microbiology Data Center (NMDC) with accession numbers NMDC60201018 and NMDC6020119.

Phylogenetic analysis

Two whole genome sequences of Jingmen tick virus (S1-S4 fragments) were obtained through metagenomic sequencing to construct a phylogenetic tree for analysis. Fifty-two JMTV strains with complete gene sequences were selected from GenBank based on host, collection date, and geographic region for phylogenetic analysis. Detailed genotypic information for these strains is provided in Supplementary Table S2. The sequence was aligned with mafft-7.526 [22, 23]. The optimal evolutionary model was calculated using IQ-tree 2 software, and the GTR + F + I + G4 model was selected as the optimal model [24]. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 5.1 version, and a molecular evolutionary tree based on nucleic acid sequences for the four segments was constructed using the maximum likelihood method. Branches with bootstrap (1,000 replicates) values higher than 70 were considered reliable.

JMTV selection pressure and evolution rate analysis

The non synonymous substitution (dN)/silent synymous substitution (dS) values of 5 proteins were calculated using MEGA5.1 software to analyze selection pressure. The nucleotide evolution rates of four segments were estimated using the Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method of BEAST v1.8.2 software [25]. The selected model is the relaxed (uncorrelated lognormal) molecular clock model and Bayesian skyline coalescent. MCMC was run with a chain length of 5,00,000,000 and convergence of all parameters in the results was confirmed using Tracer v1.7.2 (effective sampling size (ESS) >200).

Result

Metagenomic analysis identifies JMTV

In 2022, we conducted tick sample collection and sampling from multiple cities in Jiangsu Province, resulting in a total of 227 ticks were collected (Fig. 1). The ticks were identified based on microscopic morphology, and all tick species in this study belong to the genus Haemaphysalis, mainly including Haemaphysalis longicornis, Haemaphysalis flava, and Haemaphysalis campanulata. A total of 57 tick samples were collected in the Zhenjiang and the main tick species was identified as Haemaphysalis campanulate. The collected ticks were sealed in sample tubes based on their collection location and blood-engorged state.

The distributions of JMTV reported in China (Black) and the location of collecting ticks in current study (Pink). The map code: GS (2022) 4303

The entire gene sequence was aligned with the tick host sequence to eliminate background sequence information. The results showed that JMTV gene sequences were only detected in Zhenjiang tick samples. No other JMV were detected. In this study, JMTV was detected in 2/10 pools (n = 227 ticks). Two gene sequences corresponding to the complete genome reference sequence (Jingmen tick virus 18EH12, GenBank ID: NC-024111.1/NC-024112.1/NC-024113.1/NC-024114.1) for assembly and the full-length JMTV genome sequence were obtained through a comparative analysis of effective sequences and gene databases. These two samples were designated as ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9. Finally, the full-length sequences of ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 were obtained. The relevant sequence data have been submitted to the National Microbiology Data Center (NMDC) under accession numbers NMDC60201018 and NMDC6020119.

Genetic analysis between Jiangsu strain and other known JMTV strains

A genetic analysis was conducted between the Jiangsu JMTV gene sequence and existing JMTV sequences in GenBank (Table 1). The comparison results of nucleic acid (NA) showed that the ZJ-7-4-2 JMTV S1-S4 fragment had the highest similarity with ISK55 S1 fragment (2019, Japan: Ishikawa, Kaga, GenBank: LC628176.1), IM-OI96 S2 fragment (2020, Japan: Ehime, Imabari, GenBank: LC628165.1), IM-OI108 S3 fragment (2020, Japan: Imabari City, Ehime Prefecture, GenBank: LC628170.1) and 22IK30 S4 fragment (2022, Japan: Kanagawa, Aikawa, GenBank: LC775766.1), with similarity rates of 98.68%,98.54%, 98.87% and 98.78% respectively. The comparison of the S1-S4 fragments of ZJ-7–9 strain JMTV showed similar results, and also had the highest similarity with these four strains of JMTV, with similarity of 98.70%, 98.76%, 98.94%, and 98.97%, respectively. The amino acid (AA) comparison results showed that the amino acids of ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 JMTV S1-S4 fragments had the highest similarity with ISK55-S1 (BCU46445.1), IM-OI96-S2 (BCU46431.1), IM-OI108-S3 (BCU46437.1), and 22IK30-S4 (BEP27578.1), with similarity of 99.67%, 98.94%, 99.88%, 99.81% and 98.94%, 98.94%, 99.75%, 99.63%, respectively.

Phylogenetic relationship between Jiangsu strain and other known JMTV strains

A total of 54 JMTV strains, including ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 identified in this study, clustered together to form a large clade. The phylogenetic trees of the four segments revealed three groups: Asian group, South American group, and European group [26] (Fig. 2). Within South American group, the strains from Guinea in West Africa clustered together with those from South America. An in-depth analysis of the evolutionary relationships among different JMTV segments within the broader JMTV group indicated that the ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 JMTV strains have the closest evolutionary relationship with ISK55, IM-OI96, 22IK30 strains from Japan and WY01 strains from Jiangxi, China, but have a distant evolutionary relationship with other strains. The ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 JMTV S1-S4 fragments were clustered in the Asian group. The topological structures of the S1 (Fig. 2A), S2 (Fig. 2B), S3 (Fig. 2C), and S4 (Fig. 2D) fragments were similar, and all located within the same clade.

JMTV selection pressure and evolution rate analysis

Based on the collected genome sequences of 54 representative JMTV strains, the selection pressure (Table 2) and nucleotide evolution rate (Table 3) of JMTV during the evolution process were analyzed. The results showed that the dN/dS (Ka/Ks) ratios of the five proteins NSP1, VP1, NSP2, VP2, and VP3 in JMTV were 0.18, 0.32, 0.07, 0.20, and 0.30, respectively. The dN/dS ratios were all < 1, indicating that there are fewer non-synonymous mutations in the JMTV genome sequence, which suggests that it is under negative selection pressure during the evolutionary process.

Discussion

Since JMTV was first discovered in Jingmen, Hubei in 2010, the Jingmen tick virus had been reported to be widely distributed worldwide [27, 28]. In recent years, with the continuous expansion of tick activity range and advancements in detection methods, several provinces and cities in China have also reported the discovery of JMTV. Jiangsu Province, located in eastern coastal China, borders multiple provinces and cities, boasts convenient transportation and logistics, and experiences high personnel and biological mobility. Therefore, based on the observed, Jiangsu was likely vulnerable to significant public health risks related to the storage and transmission of viruses. However, the epidemiological situation of JMTV in Jiangsu was currently unclear. In this study, we conducted tick collection and sampling analysis in Jiangsu, and obtained 227 tick samples from different cities. Ultimately, two gene samples of Jingmen tick virus were identified through metagenomic sequencing in Haemaphysalis campanulate tick collected from Zhenjiang, confirming the presence of JMTV in Jiangsu.

To characterize the genetic background of the JMTV strain in Jiangsu Province, we constructed a phylogenetic tree. Notably, the JMTV strains ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 identified in this study showed the closest genetic relationship with the ISK55, IM-OI96, and 22IK30 strains from Japan, as well as the WY01 strain from Jiangxi, China. In contrast, their genetic relationship with JMTV strains found in surrounding provinces of Jiangsu is relatively distant, such as WZMf170 strain from Zhejiang and NXRpu28 strain from Henan. The gene sequences of the JMTV strains ZJ-7-4-2 and ZJ-7–9 S1-S4 exhibited high similarity with JMTV strains from Japan. This phenomenon might be attributed to Jiangsu’s geographical location in the eastern coastal region, adjacent to Japan across the sea. It is highly plausible that ticks carrying JMTV could parasitize migratory birds, facilitating cross-sea transmission [7, 29]. Additionally, international logistics trade and personnel exchange may pose potential risks for the cross-border import of pathogens between countries. This situation necessitates further investigation to clarify the routes of JMTV import across borders.

Gene reassortment significantly enhances the genetic diversity of viruses and plays a crucial role in the evolution of RNA viruses. Through continuous genetic rearrangement, viruses may achieve cross-species transmission [30]. In this study, we analyzed the S1-S4 fragment topology of JMTV strains by constructing a phylogenetic tree. It is worth noting that the four gene fragments exhibit similar topological structures. The analysis revealed that in all four gene segment trees, the same strain was found within the same clade, with no indication of reassortment. Previous literature has reported that JMV genome reassortment often occurs in different strains or within the same strain originating from different hosts, which makes their evolutionary relationships more complex and increases the risk of infecting humans. Meanwhile, JMTV strains from a single tick source tend to cluster in the same large branch [26]. Reassortment analysis showed that, despite subtle topological differences among the four gene segment trees, each strain consistently clustered within the same clade across all segments, providing no evidence of reassortment. The Jiangsu JMTV did not undergo significant gene reassortment during its evolutionary process. This may be attributed to their high genetic similarity and co-localization, as both strains were collected from the same geographic region. Their close spatial and genetic proximity likely reduced opportunities for genetic reassortment. Previous studies have similarly reported that JMTV strains from the same province in China often exhibited high sequence homology, indicating localized geographic clustering and limited viral diversification within specific regions [18]. In this study, two JMTV strains from the same region exhibited a high degree of homology in their evolution.

Selective pressure played a critical role in influencing the genetic composition and evolutionary trajectory of biological populations. In this study, we evaluated the selection pressure of JMTV for the first time. The frequency of silent synonymous substitution (dS) in JMTV genome was significantly higher than that of non-synonymous substitutions (dN). The dN/dS value among different segments of JMTV were less than 1, indicating that Jiangsu JMTV isolates seldom recombine and are under negative selection pressure. This shows that reassortment is not the main driving force of JMTV molecular evolution in this study; instead, negative selection prevails as the dominant evolutionary pressure on the JMTV genome [31]. Negative selection pressure typically arises from changes in survival and environmental factors, such as food availability and habitat [32]. Considering the diversity of tick hosts and the migratory changes in their activity areas, the JMTV carried by ticks adjusts its evolutionary trajectory by reducing maladaptive genotypes or phenotypes under negative selection pressure. This negative selection pressure is beneficial for JMTV, allowing it to adapt to constantly changing living environments and helping to maintain population adaptability and stability.

We evaluated the evolutionary rate of individual parts of four JMTV segments based on time. In previous studies, the focus was on calculating evolutionary rates for the Jingmen viruses (JMV) and the evolutionary rate range was between 2.518 × 10−4−1.289 × 10−3 [26]. In contrast, this study primarily calculates the evolutionary rates for Jingmen tick virus (JMTV) with greater precision and the evolutionary rate distribution of the JMTV four fragments was between 4.081 × 10−4−2.779 × 10−3. Our findings confirmed that the evolutionary rate of JMTV remained at a stable level, with no significant changes observed in the overall gene sequence. The four gene sequences of JMTV strains exhibited a relatively slow and consistent evolutionary rate. This finding suggestd that there have been no significant changes in the ecological environment or selection pressure affecting JMTV during its recorded existence [33]. It is worth noting that for S1 and S4 segments, the 95% HPD range of evolutionary rates spanned a 4 log10 range, indicating significant differences in these rates across regions in JMTV. Additionly, the results indicated that the S3 segment had the highest evolutionary rate, while the S4 segment exhibited the lowest. Investigating the evolutionary rate values of gene fragments is of significant importance for studying the function of proteins. It is generally accepted that important functional genes evolve slower than the other functional genes [26]. Other factors have also been found to influence the rate of protein evolution. There is a strong correlation between the amount of expression of a gene and its rate of evolution. The higher the expression of a gene is, the slower it evolves [34–36]. The NSP2 protein encoded by the S3 fragment is considered to be conserved in JMTV, resulting in a faster evolutionary rate. In contrast, the VP2 and VP3 proteins encoded by the S4 fragment, which have higher expression levels, exhibit a slower evolutionary rate. By comparing the evolutionary rate values of different gene fragments, we can identify which genes are subjected to stronger selective pressure during the process of evolution. Such differences may reflect the importance of protein functions, which will help to enhance the accuracy of predicting the functions of unknown proteins.

Conclusions

This study confirms the existence of JMTV in Jiangsu Province, which further expands the geographical distribution range of JMTV, and reveals its widespread regional distribution in China. These results have deepened our understanding of the evolution of JMTV, indicating that JMTV poses a potential threat to public health in Jiangsu Province. Future studies will require more comprehensive epidemiological investigations to enhance our understanding of its geographic distribution, host range, and evolutionary dynamics.
