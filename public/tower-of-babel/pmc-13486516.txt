Permeability calculation for glutenite reservoirs based on variable T2 cutoff value in nuclear magnetic resonance logging
Authors: Changqiang Ma, Xinghua Qi, Quancai Ji, Jun Wang, Jianwei Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Due to the strong heterogeneity of glutenite reservoirs, conventional well logging or nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) logging alone often fails to achieve satisfactory evaluation results. In this study, we propose a method to evaluate the permeability of glutenite reservoirs with a variable T2 cutoff value. First, the shale content of the glutenite reservoir is determined from conventional well logs. Then, based on the data of core porosity–permeability analysis, the relationship between shale content and the comprehensive pore structure index I (hereinafter referred to as I) is established. This I is calculated from core porosity and permeability data. Using the relationship between I and the T2 cutoff value, the T2 cutoff is determined and subsequently incorporated into the COATES model to evaluate reservoir permeability. Importantly, this method does not rely on costly core laboratory measurements such as mercury injection, centrifugation, or NMR experiments. Evaluation results show that the average error between the permeability obtained by this method and the core-measured permeability is reduced from 65.3% (SDR model) and 64.7% (COATES model) to 12.5%, while the correlation coefficient increases from 0.37 (SDR model) and 0.22 (COATES model) to 0.65. These results indicate a significant improvement in evaluation performance.

Introduction

Due to the strong heterogeneity, low porosity, and low permeability characteristics of glutenite(conglomerate) reservoirs, permeability is a key parameter affecting productivity. Traditionally, well logging data have been used to evaluate reservoir permeability by establishing relationships between permeability and porosity. To further improve accuracy, some studies have proposed classifying reservoirs based on lithological or physical property differences before constructing separate porosity-permeability relationship models1. However, due to the complex pore structure of glutenite reservoirs, these porosity-permeability based evaluation methods often have limited applicability and fail to achieve satisfactory results. Therefore, it is necessary to introduce parameters that can better characterize pore structure for permeability evaluation. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) logging technology can provide pore structure information that is not affected by lithology. Among these, the T2 cutoff value, as a key parameter for distinguishing movable fluid from irreducible fluid, is of great significance for characterizing the seepage capacity of reservoirs.

The T2 cutoff value is widely used to accurately define pore fluid mobility, providing a crucial basis for reservoir quality evaluation, productivity prediction, and fluid mobility analysis in unconventional reservoirs. Straley C et al. described in detail how to calibrate the T2 cutoff value through centrifugation methods and defined the “Free Fluid Index” (FFI) as early as 19952. In 2002, Sigal, R. F. systematically summarized two mainstream permeability models the COATES model and the SDR model established based on the classic T2 cutoff value and the geometric mean of T23. To improve the accuracy of permeability interpretation, regardless of which model is used, it is necessary to integrate NMR logging data with experimental data from core mercury injection, centrifugation, and NMR measurements4. Early domestic research on T2 cutoff values was conducted by Wang Z H et al. in 2004, who established T2 cutoff values for low-permeability reservoirs in a specific region through experiments, representing a localized application of classical methods in Chinese reservoirs5.

As the aforementioned classical methods and models have been widely applied and promoted in various types of reservoirs, their applicability boundaries and theoretical presuppositions have gradually become clearer. Since the late 1990s, the academic community has begun to systematically reflect on the limitations of treating the T2 cutoff value as a single fixed value, with research focus subsequently shifting towards in-depth analysis of influencing factors and model optimization for complex reservoirs. Westphal et al. (2005)6, in their review of NMR measurements in carbonate rocks, systematically elucidated the theoretical challenges faced by fixed T2 cutoff values in complex pore structures, laying an important foundation for related reflections. To enhance the adaptability of classical methods in heterogeneous reservoirs, Balossino et al. (2008) and Rios et al. (2016) focused on carbonate rocks7,8, attempting to obtain more reliable permeability profiles and adapted cutoff values through dynamic calibration by integrating conventional well logging data or combining high-pressure mercury injection experiments. In complex sandstone reservoirs such as tight sandstones, studies by Lis-śledziona (2019), Wei D F et al. (2015), and Lyu et al. (2020) jointly revealed that the T2 cutoff value exhibits a significant range of variation under low porosity and permeability conditions, emphasizing its control by key geological parameters such as petrophysical facies and core sources9–11. During the same period, domestic research also actively responded to this trend. In 2010, Su J L et al. explicitly proposed the concept and method of a “variable T2 cutoff value” in shaly sandstones, further promoting the application of dynamic T2 cutoff value models based on lithological characteristics in China’s practical production12.Building on this foundation, research on T2 cutoff values has further exhibited a trend towards diversification and intelligentization since the early 21st century. Scholars have gradually broken through the limitations of traditional centrifugal calibration methods, introducing new mathematical theories including fractal and multifractal analysis, driving fundamental methodological transformations. For instance, Ge X M et al. (2015) first applied multifractal theory to complex sandstone reservoirs, predicting T2 cutoff values based on the multifractal characteristics of pore structure13; Zheng S et al. (2019) and Yang Z et al. (2021) further extended this theory to more complex media such as coal rocks, demonstrating the good universality of the new methods14,15. Zheng S J et al. (2018) integrated NMR with fractal analysis to systematically characterize coal pore structure, reflecting a research approach of multi-technique integration16. Meanwhile, digital rock technology and multi-source data fusion have emerged as another important direction. Mao Z Q et al. (2013) optimized the interpretation of pore structure and T2 spectra through close integration of NMR and high-pressure mercury injection data17; Wang M et al. (2020) comprehensively utilized NMR and CT scanning techniques, providing a three-dimensional pore space structural basis for determining T2 cutoff values18; Mason H.E. et al. (2019), although focusing on permeability estimation, implied a refined requirement for T2 interpretation models through their approach based on calibrated NMR porosity19.

As research advances to deeper levels, the extreme conditions of unconventional and complex reservoirs have further expanded the application frontiers of T2 cutoff values. Minh C C. et al. (2011) revealed the special challenges faced by classical methods in determining effective T2 cutoff values in laminated heterogeneous reservoirs, addressing the averaging effects of NMR responses in thin sand-shale interbeds20. Testamanti M.N. et al. (2017) focused on determining the T2 cutoff value for clay-bound water in shales, pushing research to the scale limits of nanopores21. Furthermore, Mukhametdinova A et al. (2020), Pape H et al. (2009), and Krakowska P (2019) systematically explored the applicability boundaries of traditional T2 cutoff values under extreme petrophysical conditions and necessary adaptive adjustment strategies for difficult-to-measure reservoirs such as low-porosity-low-permeability, tight carbonate, and clastic reservoirs, collectively advancing the extension and application of this technology to more complex geological environments22–24. In the research process of variable T2 cutoff values, numerous scholars have successively proposed innovative methods to enhance determination efficiency and accuracy. Zhang Zhang W et al. (2020) pioneered the establishment of a multivariate statistical prediction model, reliably obtaining continuous T2 cutoff values using conventional well logging data, providing a basis for explaining the genesis of low-resistivity oil layers25. Subsequently, Zhu M et al. (2021) proposed a normal distribution fitting algorithm requiring only a single T2 spectrum, effectively identifying high-yield oil and gas layers in tight reservoirs26. Yu Y et al. (2022) focused on core analysis, proposing a pseudo-T2 cutoff value method based on T2 cutoff values to determine the surface relaxivity of tight sandstones in a rapid, non-destructive manner27. Research by Li P J et al. (2024) and Shen Z S et al. (2024) both determined variable T2 cutoff values through dynamic analysis of T2 spectrum morphology based on core and logging data, significantly improving the calculation accuracy of low-porosity-permeability reservoir parameters in the Daqing Oilfield28,29. Recently, Ji W et al. (2025) further developed a multi-parameter fitting method integrating Gaussian functions and reservoir classification, successfully solving the T2 cutoff value calculation problem for deep tight sandstones under high irreducible water saturation conditions in offshore areas30.

Unlike most existing variable T2 cutoff methods, which typically rely on expensive core experiments or adopt region-specific fixed cutoff values and thus struggle to capture the strong heterogeneity of glutenite reservoirs, the method proposed in this paper requires only conventional well logs. By establishing a statistical relationship between shale content and I, it achieves low-cost, high-accuracy permeability evaluation. Based on the unique geological characteristics of glutenite reservoirs, this paper presents a new permeability evaluation method using a variable T2 cutoff value. This method aims to construct a T2 cutoff value calculation model that adapts to variations in reservoir quality by deeply understanding the intrinsic relationship between NMR T2 spectra and macroscopic petrophysical properties as well as microscopic pore structures, thereby enabling more accurate and reliable permeability evaluation for glutenite reservoirs.

Theoretical basis

The application of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) technology in geophysics began in the 1960s, and within a few years, this method was introduced into the field of well logging. The core functions of NMR logging are primarily twofold: one is the identification of reservoir physical properties, and the other is reservoir fluid identification. Compared with other logging methods, NMR logging is one of the most effective techniques for permeability evaluation, complementing conventional methods such as core analysis, wireline formation testers, and resistivity-based approaches. It classifies rocks into five components: the solid matrix, clay-bound water, irreducible fluid, movable water, and movable hydrocarbon. Among these, movable water and movable hydrocarbon are collectively referred to as movable fluids. The sum of movable water, movable hydrocarbon, and irreducible fluid constitutes the effective porosity, while total porosity additionally includes the volume of clay-bound water. Thus, effective porosity reflects the pore space that can contribute to fluid flow, whereas total porosity represents the entire pore volume including water bound to clay minerals.In NMR logging, permeability is commonly calculated using the SDR model and the COATES model, as shown in Fig. 1. The SDR model posits that rock permeability is proportional to the fourth power of porosity and inversely proportional to the square of the specific surface area (surface area per unit pore volume). In NMR theory, surface relaxation determines the T2 distribution, and the logarithmic mean T2g is inversely related to the pore specific surface area. The primary advantage of the SDR model lies in its computational simplicity, requiring only two macroscopic parameters (porosity and T2g) without the need to determine the T2 cutoff value. Since this model is based on experiments conducted on rock samples fully saturated with water, when the formation contains hydrocarbons such as oil or natural gas, the T2 relaxation mechanisms of hydrocarbons differ from those of water, significantly altering the measured T2 spectrum distribution, thereby causing severe distortion of the T2g value. Under such conditions, if hydrocarbon correction is not performed, directly applying the SDR model to calculate permeability will result in substantial errors compared to the true values31,32.

The COATES model introduces the key concept of the T2 cutoff value (T2 cutoff). T2 cutoff is an empirical value used to distinguish movable fluid in macropores from irreducible fluid in micropores. The integral of signal amplitudes corresponding to T2 distributions greater than the cutoff value represents the movable fluid porosity; those less than the cutoff value represent the irreducible fluid porosity. However, these values may vary with lithology and reservoir characteristics; regional calibration using core data is recommended for improved accuracy.

The core of the COATES model lies in the accurate selection of the T2 cutoff value, which directly determines the distribution ratio between movable fluid and irreducible fluid, thereby exerting a decisive impact on the permeability calculation results. A higher cutoff value yields a lower calculated permeability; conversely, a lower value yields a higher calculated permeability. This value is not fixed but strongly depends on lithology: the typical value for sandstone is approximately 33 milliseconds, while for carbonate rocks with complex pore structures, it may range from 92 milliseconds to several hundred milliseconds. Therefore, reliable results can only be obtained through regionally calibrated core experiments. Given that residual oil may affect the SDR model without correction, and considering the strong heterogeneity of glutenite reservoirs, this study preferentially adopts the COATES model framework because it allows the use of a variable T2 cutoff to better capture fluid mobility.

In practical well log interpretation, the selection of an appropriate model requires comprehensive consideration of regional geological characteristics, core analysis data, and fluid properties. The common practice is to calibrate the regional empirical coefficients and cutoff values for both models simultaneously using core permeability data from key wells. Subsequently, the model with better fitting performance and greater consistency with geological understanding is selected for application and promotion. Occasionally, conducting a comparative analysis of the results derived from the two models serves as an effective quality control measure in itself.

Method for evaluating permeability using the T2 cutoff value

In the determination of NMR T2 cutoff values, existing research has generally focused on its intrinsic relationship with multiple reservoir parameters. Experimental analyses indicate that the T2 cutoff value exhibits a significant correlation with cation exchange capacity (Qv), showing a decreasing trend as shale content increases33. In tight shaly siltstone reservoirs, irreducible water saturation is primarily controlled by shale content, while pore structure also exerts an important influence on the distribution of irreducible water. Furthermore, core experimental data further confirm a good correlation between cation exchange capacity (Qv) and I34. Overall, parameters such as shale content, cation exchange capacity, irreducible water saturation, pore structure, and T2 cutoff value constitute an interrelated system, with complex coupling relationships among these elements. This holds significant theoretical importance for reservoir fluid identification and the evaluation of seepage characteristics.

The parameter I is defined as the comprehensive pore structure index, which quantitatively characterizes the combined effect of pore structure on fluid flow capacity by integrating the influence of both permeability and porosity into a single parameter. This index has been shown to correlate strongly with the T2 cutoff value5. Physically, I has the dimension of length (µm). However, in the empirical equations used in this study, the coefficients have been calibrated against core data and implicitly incorporate unit conversions. Therefore, for practical application, Ican be treated as a dimensionless empirical parameter and directly substituted into the equations without additional unit conversion.

Since the shale content significantly affects the pore structure, a good correlation may exist between the shale content and the I.

Therefore, a coherent technical workflow can be established: First, conventional logging data is used to invert the shale content in the glutenite reservoir. Then, by integrating porosity and permeability data obtained from core experimental analysis, a key parameter I is calculated. By constructing a quantitative relationship model between the two, the continuously calculated shale content can be used as a proxy variable to achieve a continuous evaluation of I. Ultimately, based on the inherent relationship between I and the T2 cutoff value, the NMR T2 cutoff value at any depth can be calculated. Determination of evaluation methods for shale content using well logs

Previous studies have confirmed a significant correlation between shale content and the NMR T2 cutoff value.Yan Q B et al. (2010) observed that the T2 cutoff value is not constant but inversely proportional to shale content; rocks with higher shale content exhibit smaller T2 cutoff values.Li J Y et al. (2010) reported that higher shale content leads to a smaller T2 cutoff value and that the T2 cutoff value is closely related to shale content. These findings provide a theoretical basis for continuously determining the T2 cutoff value using shale content in low-porosity, low-permeability glutenite reservoirs35,36.The key to implementing this method lies in selecting an optimal conventional logging approach suitable for the target block to accurately calculate shale content. Common methods for evaluating shale content include the natural gamma ray (GR) log, the spontaneous potential (SP) log, and the neutron-density porosity log combination. The optimal method should be selected based on specific geological conditions, prioritizing the one with the most pronounced response characteristics and the most reliable interpretation model.

The principle of the natural gamma ray (GR) log is based on the ability of shale to adsorb radioactive elements, and it performs well in simple sand-mudstone sequences. However, if the rock-forming minerals themselves are rich in radioactive elements (such as potassium feldspar, mica, etc.), significant interference can occur, leading to severely overestimated shale content calculations. Therefore, its application in glutenite reservoirs with complex mineral compositions requires particular caution.

The spontaneous potential (SP) log relies on the natural electromotive force generated by the electrochemical difference between formation water and mud filtrate in permeable zones. The influence of shale content is manifested through its selective adsorption of ions in the formation water, which alters the amplitude of the diffusion-adsorption potential, producing a characteristic response on the SP curve. Consequently, this method is applicable to permeable reservoirs where the formation water salinity is stable and significantly differs from that of the mud filtrate.

Previous research has proposed a method for calculating reservoir shale content by combining CMR nuclear magnetic resonance logging data with neutron porosity logging data37. The theoretical basis of this method lies in the significant linear relationship between the clay mineral content and the irreducible water porosity in glutenite reservoirs. As neutron logging is sensitive to the response of hydrogen nuclei in the formation, it can effectively capture information related to irreducible water. Therefore, integrating neutron logging response characteristics with other porosity logging data to evaluate shale content serves as an effective supplementary approach beyond the natural gamma ray and spontaneous potential methods. To validate the applicability of the aforementioned method in complex reservoirs, this study applies it to the glutenite reservoirs of the Mahu Oilfield in the Junggar Basin, Xinjiang, which are known for their strong heterogeneity. Evaluation of shale content in glutenite reservoirs

Applicability analysis of conventional GR/SP logging methods in the study area

Natural gamma ray (GR) and spontaneous potential (SP) logging are conventional methods for evaluating shale content in clastic reservoirs. However, the glutenite reservoirs in this study area exhibit significant unconventional characteristics. As shown in Fig. 6, in the interval xx76–xx80 m, both core data and FMI data clearly identify pure mudstone; nevertheless, the GR and SP curves in this interval do not display the expected pronounced high values and negative anomalies. This critical evidence indicates that, in such complex reservoirs, the response of GR and SP curves to shale is extremely weak and is no longer effective for lithology discrimination or accurate shale content calculation.

The core factor preventing the accurate determination of shale content in glutenite reservoirs using natural gamma ray logging data is the presence of abundant volcaniclastic materials within the reservoirs in this area. These volcaniclastic materials themselves possess high natural radioactivity, thereby interfering with the accurate assessment of shale content based on natural gamma ray logging data. This makes it difficult to effectively distinguish between glutenite reservoirs and non-reservoir mudstone intervals relying solely on natural gamma ray logging.

Regarding the limitations of the spontaneous potential logging method in evaluating shale content, the main cause can be attributed to the ultra-low permeability characteristics prevalent in the reservoirs of this area. This characteristic is evidenced by the high consistency between the deep resistivity (RT) log and the medium resistivity (RI) log, i.e., there is almost no significant difference between the two, consequently limiting the effectiveness of the spontaneous potential logging method in analyzing shale content.

Specifically, in the process of conducting density logging to obtain effective porosity, the core depth shifting step plays a fundamentally crucial role. Through meticulous and precise core depth shifting operations, the logging data can be highly matched with the actual core conditions, thereby laying a solid foundation for subsequent establishment of accurate and reliable models.

After completing the core depth shifting, the key phase of establishing a density logging evaluation model for formation effective porosity begins. In this process, the determination of the rock matrix density is one of the core elements of model construction. Discussion

As can be seen from Fig. 9, within the glutenite reservoir interval, the calculated cutoff values are generally lower than the commonly used 33 ms. This will cause the calculated free fluid volume to increase and the bound fluid volume to decrease, leading to an overall permeability value higher than that calculated by the COATES model using 33 ms as the cutoff value. When permeability values calculated by the COATES model are generally underestimates, the permeability values derived from the variable cutoff value model may be closer to the core permeability measurements. As shown in Trace 7 of Fig. 9, the evaluation effect of the conventional variable T2 cutoff value model on the glutenite reservoir in the study area is superior to both the SDR model and the COATES model. The comparison of these correlation coefficients fully demonstrates that using the variable cutoff value model to calculate the permeability of glutenite reservoirs yields results significantly superior to those obtained using models with fixed cutoff values and empirical parameters, allowing for the acquisition of more accurate formation permeability parameters.

Although the above comparison shows the clear advantages of the proposed method, we must honestly point out the limitations of the current method and directions for future improvement. It should be noted that, in order to establish a T2 cutoff value calculation model more suitable for the study area, the ideal approach would be to conduct NMR centrifugation experiments on local core samples to obtain measured T2 cutoff values, and then re-fit the regional empirical relationship using porosity and permeability data. However, due to limitations in experimental conditions and the high cost of core sampling, such experiments have not been completed in this study. We have honestly reflected this limitation in the manuscript and plan to supplement the NMR experimental data on local cores in future research, with the aim of establishing a more accurate regional T2 cutoff value formula and further improving the prediction accuracy of the variable cutoff value method.

In addition, this study has significantly improved the permeability calculation accuracy for glutenite reservoirs by introducing the variable T2 cutoff value method. However, in the final application of the COATES model for permeability calculation, constrained by the availability of core data from the study area, the key parameters (a, b, c) in the model were not systematically calibrated regionally; instead, general empirical values (a = 4, b = 2, c = 10) were adopted. These fixed empirical parameters essentially represent an “average solution” based on an ideal pore model and struggle to accurately quantify the nonlinear influence of the complex pore structure of glutenite in this area on fluid flow capacity. This has become a key factor limiting further improvement of the current model’s accuracy. If future research can optimize and calibrate the exponents (a, b) and the coefficient (c) in the COATES model locally using core permeability data, it is expected to achieve a comprehensive refinement from “fluid partitioning” to “flow modeling”, thereby more fully releasing the potential of the variable T2 cutoff value method and pushing the accuracy of permeability calculation to a new level.

Another limitation concerns the applicable wellbore conditions. Since the proposed T2 cutoff value calculation method is specifically designed for glutenite intervals, it is only applicable to reservoir sections without borehole enlargement. It has poor applicability for non-reservoir sections such as mudstones, especially those with borehole enlargement. If this method is used to determine cutoff values for shale gas reservoirs or other formations prone to washouts, its applicability is limited. Alternative methods for permeability evaluation in enlarged borehole sections should be investigated separately.

In addition, the neutron log response is also affected by formation water salinity. Historical formation water test data from the study area indicate that the salinity of the target interval is relatively stable; therefore, the salinity effect on the neutron porosity reading is within acceptable limits and does not affect the reliability of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F\left({V}_{sh}\right)$$\end{document} calculation. If the method is applied to areas with large vertical variations in salinity, salinity correction of the neutron log is recommended, or the method should be used with caution.

Finally, there is still room for further improvement in the calibration of COATES model parameters. Currently, this method calculates reservoir permeability using the variable T2 cutoff value approach but retains the default parameters in the COATES model; it does not yet involve calibrating these default parameters through additional experimental methods. Previous research has shown that constants such as a, b, and c may vary for different regions and ultimately affect permeability evaluation. For example, in China’s Liaohe Oilfield, values of a = 3.002, b = 1.676 and c = 6.969 are typically adopted. Therefore, further research to optimize these constants within the model could potentially enhance the accuracy of reservoir permeability calculations even further.

The above limitations highlight the challenges of permeability evaluation in glutenite reservoirs. As noted by Chen K G et al. (2015) and Zhou J et al. (2024), the complex pore structure and strong heterogeneity of glutenite reservoirs inherently make permeability prediction more difficult than in sandstone reservoirs. Therefore, while the proposed method offers a practical improvement, further research is needed to address the remaining issues38,39.
