Therapeutic applications of stem cell-derived exosomes in radiation-induced lung injury
Authors: Ying Li, Yan He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation-induced lung injury is a common complication of chest tumor radiotherapy; however, effective clinical treatments are still lacking. Stem cell-derived exosomes, which contain various signaling molecules such as proteins, lipids, and miRNAs, not only retain the tissue repair and reconstruction properties of stem cells but also offer improved stability and safety. This presents significant potential for treating radiation-induced lung injury. Nonetheless, the clinical adoption of stem cell-derived exosomes for this purpose remains limited due to scientific, practical, and regulatory challenges. In this review, we highlight the current pathology and therapies for radiation-induced lung injury, focusing on the potential applications and therapeutic mechanisms of stem cell-derived exosomes. We also discuss the limitations of existing stem cell-derived exosomes and outline future directions for exosome-based treatments for radiation-induced lung injury.Keywords: Radiation-induced lung injury, Radiation, Stem cell, Exosomes

Introduction

Radiotherapy is one of the most effective methods for treating localized solid malignant tumors [1]. The principle aim of radiotherapy is to enhance tumor control while minimizing toxicity to neighboring normal tissues as much as possible [2]. However, in clinical practice, severe radiation-relevant adverse events have constrained the dosage of radiation therapy, such as radiation-induced encephalitis, myelitis, pneumonia, and enteritis [3–5]. Radiation-induced lung injury (RILI) is a key dose-limiting factor in radiotherapy for thoracic tumors [6, 7]. According to the GLOBOCAN 2020 estimates of cancer incidence, breast cancer, lung cancer, and esophageal cancer rank first, second, and seventh among all malignant tumors with estimated new cases numbering 2.26 million, 2.21 million, and 0.6 million, respectively [8]. Over 80% of these patients required thoracic radiotherapy [9, 10]. Approximately 30% (1.4 million) of these patients report RILI [11, 12]. The treatment and prevention of RILI are still very limited [13, 14]. To minimize the severity of RILI, radiation oncologists usually adjust the radiation therapy plan by narrowing the irradiation field or reducing the radiotherapy dosage [15]. Unfortunately, the limitation of the tumor target area dosage could inevitably decrease tumor local control rates [16]. Therefore, exploring novel strategies for the preventing and treating RILI may play a crucial role in enhancing tumor control rates, overall survival, and the quality of life for patients with thoracic tumors.

The stem cell-derived exosomes (SC-Exos), which are extracellular vesicles released by stem cells with 30–200 nm in diameter, not only have the advantages of exosomes but also exhibit the characteristics of stem cells [17]. Due to their potential to influence tissue repair, modulate immune responses, and promote cell survival and regeneration, it has gained significant attention in the field of tissue engineering, treatment of degenerative diseases, and immune modulation [18]. Moreover, several studies reported the potential molecular mechanisms related to the regulation of SC-Exos in RILI [19]. Therefore, interest in the therapeutic potential of SC-Exos in RILI has increased significantly.

Here, this review summarizes the progress in the applications and mechanisms of SC-Exos to provide a reference for treating RILI. It highlights the potential of SC-Exos as a significant therapeutic option for RILI.

The pathology and therapy for RILI

The mechanisms of radiation injury and the cellular events in RILI

The pathology of RILI was originally described in 1925 by Evans who manifested acute RILI as radiation pneumonitis (RP) and chronic as radiation pulmonary fibrosis (RPF) [20]. RILI exhibits a dose-dependent relationship, where lower doses result in milder and reversible diseases that are recoverable or absorbable, while higher doses result in more severe RILI [21, 22]. As shown in Fig. 1, RILI is that radiation poses a threat to the lungs through reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxygen free radicals [23]. These elements cause damage to the genomic DNA of cells in the body, triggering a series of cellular responses, including (a) the production of ROS leads to oxidative stress, resulting in neutrophilic inflammatory infiltration, increased secretion of proteases, and the generation of numerous oxidative intermediates; (b) Endothelial cell damage lead to injury in blood vessels endothelial cells; (c) Release of inflammatory factors [15]. Ultimately, these processes induce various types of damage to the cells of lung, including apoptosis [24], epithelial-mesenchymal transition [25], senescence [26], ferroptosis [27], fibrosis [28], and so on. Alveolar epithelial cells. The alveolar epithelium is composed of alveolar epithelial cell type I (AE I) and alveolar epithelial type II (AE II), as crucial target cells when injury occurs [29]. AE II obtains stem cell characters enabling proliferate and transdifferentiate into AE I cells, which play a pivotal role in maintaining the stability of alveolar tissue structure and function [30, 31]. Radiation injury to AE I and AE II leads to surfactant loss and transudation of serum proteins into the alveoli [32]. Simultaneously, apoptosis occurs, causing the depletion of alveolar epithelial cells and triggering the release of corresponding inflammatory cytokines [33, 34].

Endothelial cells. The endothelial cells regulate vascular permeability and blood flow while releasing substances that control inflammation and coagulation [35]. Radiation-induced endothelial damage results in compromised function of the blood-alveolus barrier and vascular integrity, ultimately leading to a reduction in microvascular density and impaired oxygen perfusion [35]. In addition, ROS can overstimulate various cytokines and growth factors, promoting fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, and the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process [36, 37].

Macrophages. Normally, macrophages can phagocytize pathogens and debris while secreting cytokines to recruit immune cells, thereby aiding in inflammation resolution and tissue repair [38]. They are categorized into two types (classically activated macrophages (M1), regulated by the Th1-derived cytokine IFN-γ, and alternatively activated macrophages (M2), regulated by the Th2-derived cytokines IL-4 and IL-13) [38]. In the early stage of RILI, M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, inducing inflammation and generating a substantial amount of ROS, which further impairs lung tissue [39]. In the late stage, M2 macrophages secrete profibrotic cytokines, promoting the development of RPF [40]. Macrophages are among the initial cells recruited to sites of radiation-induced injury, where they contribute to tissue repair and propagate radiation-induced fibrogenesis [41, 42]. Meanwhile, macrophages can directly respond to ionizing radiation, triggering phenomena such as cellular aging and proptosis, and inducing the polarization of macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory direction [39].

Fibroblasts. Normally, an increase in the number of myofibroblasts in lung tissue triggers the cell apoptosis regulation system, and fibroblasts help maintain structural integrity by producing extracellular matrix components and secreting growth factors that support tissue remodeling and repair [43]. However, in patients with RILI, myofibroblasts exhibit resistance to apoptosis, accompanied by multifactor-induced extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [44]. Damaged epithelial cells can undergo EMT to generate fibroblasts, which directly respond to ionizing radiation stimulation, secreting TGF-β and inducing proliferation and differentiation of fibroblasts through autocrine mechanisms [45]. Ultimately, under the combined action of various cytokines, fibroblasts are activated to generate myofibroblasts, resulting in abnormal deposition of the ECM and triggering RPF [44, 46].

Therapies for RILI

It was a lack of efficiency methods to prevent and treat RILI. Corticosteroids remain the primary treatment, with other symptomatic-based interventions, such as oxygen inhalation, atomization, and nutritional support [47]. Amifostine, functioning as a radioprotective agent, diminishes irradiation-induced DNA damage by eliminating superoxide dismutase-2 and reducing the oxygen concentration around normal tissue [48]. Nonetheless, its application was limited by rapid metabolism and susceptibility to adverse reactions, such as hypotension and gastrointestinal toxicity [49]. Recently, several therapeutic strategies are researched in RILI, such as liposome-anchored mesenchymal stem cells [50], Spirulina [51], Monophosphoryl lipid A [39], and TGF-β1-based CRISPR/Cas9 Gene Therapy [52]. Additionally, the stem cell-derived exosomes (SC-Exos) also hold great promise in the treatment of RILI.

Stem cell-derived exosomes: biogenesis, composition and characteristics

SC-Exos, which are extracellular vesicles released by stem cells with 30–200 nm in diameter, not only have the advantages of exosomes but also exhibit the characteristics of stem cells [17]. Compared to mesenchymal stem cells, SC-Exos offer several advantages. Firstly, SC-Exos are smaller and can easily navigate through various tissues, demonstrating better biocompatibility and reducing the risk of immune rejection. Secondly, the extraction and storage of exosomes are relatively straightforward, providing better stability and a longer shelf life. Lastly, exosomes can effectively deliver a variety of bioactive molecules, regulating intercellular communication and promoting tissue repair [17].

SC-Exos could be derived from various cell sources, including bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSC-Exos) [53], dental pulp mesenchymal stem cells (DPMSC-Exos) [54], adipose mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSC-Exos) [55], umbilical cord blood stem cells (UCBSC-Exos) [56], and placental amniotic fluid stem cells (AFSC-Exos) [57]. SC-Exos contain a diverse array of cytoplasmic, proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, playing a crucial role in intercellular communication between cells by facilitating the transfer of bioactive molecules [58]. For example, it was reported that: (a) The overexpression of miR-21-5p in BMSC-Exos could promote angiogenesis by increasing the expression levels of VEGF, VEGFR2, Ang-1, and Tie-2, making them an attractive treatment strategy for stroke recovery [59]; (b) The overexpression of miR-223, miR-146b, miR-126, or miR-199a in BMSC-Exos could regulate alveolar macrophages, contributing to the improvement of anti-inflammatory effects [59, 60]; (c) BMSC-Exos can suppress TNF-alpha-induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis through the miR-155-5p/Trim32 Axis [61]; (d) BMSC-Exos could promote corneal wound healing by regulating the p44/42 MAPK pathway [62], and protect human dermal fibroblasts against UVB-induced activation of oxidative stress and inflammation through the MAPK/AP-1 signaling pathway [63]; (e) Elevating the content of pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) in ADMSC-Exos could activate autophagy and inhibit the apoptotic pathway to ameliorate cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [64]. Due to their potential to influence tissue repair, modulate immune responses, and promote cell survival and regeneration, it has gained significant attention in the field of tissue engineering, treatment of degenerative diseases, and immune modulation [18, 65, 66].

Notably, in the context of respiratory diseases, exosomes derived from AEII cells (AEII-Exos) show promise for the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for respiratory conditions [67]. AEII cells obtain stem cell characters enabling proliferate and transdifferentiate into AEI cells, which play a crucial role in maintaining lung function and integrity [68]. AEII-Exos contains a variety of bioactive molecules and specific markers related to the function of AEII cells [69, 70]. It plays a role in intercellular communication within the lung microenvironment, contributing to intercellular communication, and may have significant implications for respiratory health [71, 72]. They may carry information about the physiological state of AEII cells, potentially influencing neighboring cells and contributing to tissue homeostasis and repair [73]. Previous research found that STIM-activating enhancer-positive exosome, which is released by AEII cells with more than 10 times of CD63 + exosomes, could balance airway immunity, alleviates damage and fibrosis, involving calcineurin-PGC-1α pathway mediated mitochondrial biogenesis and mtDNA coding [74]. The potential applications of AEII-Exos may extend to areas of RILI.

As mentioned above, SC-Exos have a significant impact on cellular functions and various physiological processes [75]. Currently, methods for isolating SC-Exos involve techniques such as ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, and commercial exosome isolation kits [58]. Methods for characterizing exosomes typically involve assessing morphology, particle size distribution, and protein markers using techniques such as nanoparticle tracking analysis, western blot, flow cytometry analysis, transmission electron microscopy, atomic force microscopy, and scanning electron microscopy [76]. Several factors influence the function of SC-Exos, including the types of stem cells, donor characteristics, culture conditions, and cargo contents [77–79]. In different conditions, extracellular vesicles from the same source exhibit varying functions, and the mechanisms of action for extracellular vesicles with different components also differ. For example, it was reported that DPMSCs-Exos exhibited stronger immune-modulating activities compared to BMSCs-Exos [80]. In addition, hypoxia-activated ADMSC-Exos, with the overexpression of FGF10, were reported to protect salivary glands from apoptosis induced by ionizing radiation through the activation of the FGFR-PI3K signaling pathway [81]. Beyond the mentioned factors, ongoing research continues to reveal novel factors that influence the function of SC-Exos [82, 83].

Applications of SC-Exos in radiation-induced lung injury

SC-Exos contain a diverse array of cytoplasmic proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids that can regulate and modulate immune responses, angiogenesis, cell survival, and regeneration (Fig. 2). The details of how SC-Exos could regulate these processes in RILI are outlined below.

SC-Exos regulating polarization of alveolar macrophages

Alveolar macrophages can manifest distinct activation states, commonly classified as M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory), which have undergone extensive investigation in the context of lung injury, repair, and fibrosis, playing a role in innate inflammatory responses and T-cell adaptive immune reactions [84]. Mechanisms contributing to the regulation of alveolar macrophage polarization, including cytokine signaling, transcription factors, microRNA (miRNAs) regulation, metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic modifications, pattern recognition receptors, cell-cell interactions, and protein kinases [85]. Therefore, the regulation of SC-Exos involves complex molecular mechanisms, providing valuable insights into the dynamic control of alveolar macrophage polarization. Cytokines and growth factors released by neutrophils and CD4 + T cells continually induce the proliferation of fibroblasts and macrophages, leading to excessive damage to the body, and cytokines play a pivotal role in macrophage polarization [86]. For example, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are associated with M1 polarization, while interleukin (IL)−4 and IL-13 are linked to M2 polarization [87]. The balance and timing of cytokine signals influence macrophage activation [38]. It was reported that miRNA-21-overexpressing BMSC-Exos could inhibit M1 polarization of alveolar macrophages and block the proinflammatory pathway of macrophage, which highly correlated with IL-12, TNF-α, and IL-6 expressions and the severity of RILI [85]. Researchers have found that SC-Exos can increase IL-10 and TGF-β1 in human peripheral blood monocytes, promoting T lymphocyte proliferation and immune suppressive capabilities [88]. It was also reported that ADMSCs-Exos enriched with miR-30d-5p significantly reduced the oxygen- and glucose-deprived inflammatory response by suppressing autophagy and promoting M2 macrophage polarization [89]. In addition, Toll-like receptors (TLRs) can influence macrophage polarization, and it was found that miR-181c-expressing in UCBSC-Exos could downregulate the TLR4 signaling pathway to reduce inflammation [90].

SC-Exos inhibiting apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells

Various physiological and pathological conditions can influence apoptosis-related pathways, impacting cell survival or death in the alveolar microenvironment. Thus, several strategies can be applied for RILI via inhibiting apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells, including delivery of anti-apoptotic factors, transfer of miRNAs, activation of survival pathways, modulation of oxidative stress, and enhancement of cell viability [91]. It was reported that BMSC-Exos could not only improve viability and reduce apoptosis in AEII cells but also enhance the expression of ENaC protein and miR-199a-3p providing a new pharmacological tool for the treatment of acute lung injury [92]. SC-Exos could activate intracellular signaling pathways associated with cell survival. For instance, the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway is a well-known pro-survival pathway that might be modulated by exosomes. BMSC-Exos can regulate the expression of apoptosis-related proteins (Bcl-2, Bax, p-PI3K, p-Akt, and p-mTOR) via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway preventing hyperoxia-induced AEII cells apoptosis [93]. In addition, researchers found that BMSC-exos could reverse mitochondrial dysfunction to alleviate AECII apoptosis to provide novel strategies for preventing and treating sepsis-induced ARDS, including the upregulated expression of PGC1α, Tfam, Nrf1 proteins, and ATP contents, and prohibited p-Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission by promoting Nrf2 expression [94]. Furthermore, SC-Exos might carry molecules that reduce oxidative stress in alveolar epithelial cells. It was reported that exosomal miR-129-5p from SC-Exos alleviated ventricular dysfunction and suppressed oxidative stress, apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis by targeting TRAF3 and the NF-κB signaling to protect the heart from failure, which may extend to areas of RILI [95].

SC-Exos regulating the function of fibroblasts

SC-Exos contains abundant proteins associated with PDGF, fibroblast growth factor, and epidermal growth factor signaling pathways, influencing vascular remodeling, fibroblast proliferation, development, migration, adhesion, and cell survival [96]. For instance, miR-27a-3p could induce pulmonary fibrosis [97], and miR-34a induces lung fibroblast aging and inhibits proliferation [98]. ADMSC-Exos could impact multiple TGF-β signals, such as miR-23a inhibiting Smad2 and miR-203a inhibiting Smad3, aiding in attenuating acute RILI through anti-inflammation, anti-fibrosis and anti-apoptosis mechanisms [99]. BMSC-Exos significantly reduce cell apoptosis rates and inhibit ROS generation following oxidative stress, with miR-214 identified as the main effector molecule [100], which might regulate fibroblasts in RILI. As previous study reported, genetic knockout of miR-214-3p reverses epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and alleviates renal fibrosis in mice with unilateral ureteral obstruction. Conversely, overexpression of miR-214-3p promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and expression of fibrotic factors in renal tubular epithelial cells under hypoxic conditions [101]. Therefore, exosomes can reduce oxidative stress and cell apoptosis, with the release of miR-214 alleviating oxidative stress-induced damage. However, overexpression of miR-214 paradoxically promotes fibrosis [101]. Epithelial cadherin is a fibrosis-related gene, and hypoxia induces activation of the Twist/miR-214/epithelial cadherin axis, accelerating renal fibrosis [101]. Exosomes expressing se-cad activate the nuclear factor κB signaling cascade, associated with immune and inflammatory responses. Nuclear factor κB can inhibit the expression of miR-29b, and studies on radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis confirm decreased miR-29 expression in lung epithelium, promoting collagen and laminin production [102]. Thus, second-positive exosomes can promote pulmonary fibrosis by activating nuclear factor κB and inhibiting miR-29b expression [103].

SC-Exos promoting the integrity of vascular endothelium

Various physiological and pathological conditions can regulate the integrity of vascular endothelium, including angiogenic factors and growth factors, cellular communication and signaling regulation, microRNA-mediated regulation, anti-inflammatory effects, suppressing inflammation, antioxidant properties, reducing oxidative stress, extracellular matrix support and immunomodulation [104]. SC-Exos can carry angiogenic factors and growth factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), which promote endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis, contributing to the maintenance of vascular integrity [105, 106]. Meanwhile, SC-Exos could transfer specific miRNAs to endothelial cells, regulating genes involved in endothelial cell function, barrier integrity, and responses to stress [107]. SC-Exos could possess anti-inflammatory properties that help reduce inflammation in the vascular endothelium to promote endothelial cell health and integrity, and modulate the immune response in the vascular microenvironment, potentially influencing endothelial cell function for maintaining vascular homeostasis by balanced immune response [108]. In addition, it was reported that ADMSC-Exos may influence the synthesis and remodeling of the extracellular matrix surrounding endothelial cells, impacting the structural integrity of blood vessels and contributing to a healthy vascular environment [109].

The potential molecular mechanisms related to the regulation of SC-Exos in RILI

PI3K-AKT signaling pathway

Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) is a vital member of the growth factor receptor superfamily signaling transduction process, activated by various cytokines in vivo [110]. In RILI, alveolar epithelial cells undergo EMT, and insufficient apoptosis of lung myofibroblasts contributes to the further development of pulmonary fibrosis [111]. The P13K/AKT pathway is a significant mediator of EMT, and studies have shown that PI3K inhibitors can block the proliferation and differentiation of human lung fibroblasts into myofibroblasts [112]. TGF-β1 produced in lung tissue inflammatory reactions activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, promoting the transformation of alveolar epithelial cells into interstitial cells, indicating the involvement of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis [113, 114]. Meanwhile, activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway enhances the transcriptional activity of downstream Smad3 protein of TGF-β1 [115]. Therefore, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a critical pathway in the RILI, and intervening in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway can be an effective strategy to inhibit the proliferation of lung fibroblasts/myofibroblasts for the treatment of radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Li et al. utilized SC-Exos for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis in mice, and found that SC-Exos could effectively regulate EMT, and respond to the microenvironment, alleviating symptoms such as pulmonary collagen deposition and inflammation [116]. The protein cargo carried by SC-Exos is rich in components related to the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, which could potentially regulate cell survival, cell cycle regulation, and macrophage polarization [117].

TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway

The Transforming Growth Factor-β1/Smad signaling pathway (TGF-β1/Smad) regulates cell differentiation, proliferation, and stimulates extracellular matrix (ECM) protein production, promoting fibroblast proliferation to promote fibrosis of connective tissue [118]. TGF-β comprises three isoforms, including TGF-β1, TGF-β2, and TGF-β3, with TGF-β1 being a key cytokine in fibrosis and participating in RILI, and Smad proteins are key transducers in the TGF-β signaling pathway [119]. Smad2 and Smad3 are the two major downstream regulator that promote TGF-β1-mediated tissue fibrosis, while Smad7 effectively prevents Smad2 and Smad3 interaction to regulate TGF-β1/Smad pathway thereby protecting against TGF-β1-mediated fibrosis [120]. Currently, TGF-β is considered a pivotal molecular “switch” in RILI [121]. Studies by Roberts et al. on the impact of Smad3 gene knockout on radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis revealed that Smad3 regulates TGF-β-dependent induction of c-fos and c-jun expression, collagen production, and TGF-β self-induction [122]. It is speculated that Smad3 deficiency can reduce the infiltration of inflammatory cells, inhibiting the formation of radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis [122]. Meanwhile, it was confirmed that blocking the TGF-β pathway may reduce post-irradiation interstitial inflammatory reactions and decrease the degree of fibrosis [123]. Hence, the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the induction and development of RILI, especially in the stage of radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis [124]. It was reported that exosomal miRNAs could target various aspects of TGFβ signaling, including: (1) miR-23a could inhibits Smad 2; (2) miR‐203a could inhibit Smad 3 and promote the repression of TGFβ. In addition, miR‐34a could induces lung fibroblast senescence and suppresses fibroblast proliferation [98, 125]. Therefore, targeting TGF-β/Smad signaling proteins by SC-Exos presents a promising strategy for preventing and treating RILI.

HGF/c-Met signaling pathway

The HGF/c-Met signaling pathway, a crucial cellular communication system involving in various physiological processes including cell growth, survival, and tissue regeneration, which is initiated by the binding of Hepatocyte Growth Factor (HGF) to its receptor c-Met (mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor) [126]. The HGF/c-Met signaling pathway plays a crucial role in tissue damage repair. After tissue damage, mesenchymal stem cells could generate a large amount of HGF and c-Met, inhibiting the growth of myofibroblasts and alleviating lung tissue damage [127]. The binding of HGF to c-Met upregulates the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-9) at the radiation-damaged site through the PI3K/Akt/p70 pathway, promoting apoptosis of myofibroblasts [128]. Based on these findings, MSCs can, through paracrine pathways, secrete a significant amount of reparative and regenerative cytokines (HGF, PGE2, etc.), inhibiting immune responses, mitigating inflammation, and reducing organ damage [129].

STAT3 signaling pathway

Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription 3 (STAT3) is a group of versatile proteins primarily located in the cytoplasm [130]. It can be activated by JAK kinase as well as stimuli such as IL-6 and TGF-β [131]. Upon stimulation by JAK kinase and cytokines, STAT3 undergoes phosphorylation and aggregation within minutes, forming dimers that rapidly translocate to the cell nucleus, initiating the transcription of downstream target genes. Positive regulation of the STAT3 pathway can prevent or control the occurrence of lung injury [132]. Subsequent intraperitoneal injection of WP1066 (a STAT3 pathway inhibitor) two weeks after radiation extended the survival time of mice. This treatment inhibited the activation of the STAT3 pathway and reduced the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TGF-β. This discrepancy may be related to WP1066 acting as an immune activator, potentially inducing inflammatory reactions and increasing TNF-α levels. The findings suggest that inhibiting the STAT3 signaling pathway can alleviate the occurrence of RILI, particularly playing a significant role in the stage of radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis [132]. It was found that exosomal miR-210-5p could regulate endothelial barrier function by regulating JAK1/STAT3 pathway [19].

Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is an essential molecular information transmission system for lung development, and its abnormal expression is closely associated with pulmonary fibrosis [133]. GSK-3β, as a crucial signaling factor in the Wnt pathway, is a multifunctional serine/threonine kinase that regulates cell proliferation and apoptosis by inhibiting the Wnt pathway [134]. The activity of GSK-3β is regulated by phosphorylation at different sites. Upon phosphorylation (P-GSK3β), its activity is reduced, inhibiting the binding of GSK-3β to β-catenin and cyclin-D1, leading to overexpression of β-catenin and cyclin-D1, promoting excessive cell proliferation [134]. It can be inferred that radiation activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, playing a crucial role in the formation of radiation-induced pulmonary fibrosis [135]. Regulation of key signaling factors in this pathway may be attempted to prevent and treat RILI. Findings of these studies can lead us to identify more specific and effective strategies for SC-Exos on RILI.

NF-κB’s signaling pathway activation

NF-κB, one of the key transcription factors regulating the expression of inflammatory genes, is widely present in various tissue cells [136]. Various intracellular and extracellular stimuli can activate NF-κB by phosphorylating and ubiquitinating IKBα, leading to its degradation and release, highlighting radiation’s ability to activate NF-κB [137]. This exposure reveals NF-κB’s nuclear recognition site, allowing it to enter the nucleus and promote the transcription of target genes. The phosphorylation of IKBα relies on its IKBα kinase complex IKKα/β, with IKKβ being a crucial factor in the NF-κB pathway, often exerting protective effects closely related to RILI [137]. The research found that blocking IL-17 A could reduce IL-6 levels. IL-6 is an inflammatory factor downstream of the NF-κB pathway, suggesting that IL-17 A may regulate RILI through the NF-kB signaling pathway [138]. Therefore, inhibiting NF-κB pathway-related molecules can downregulate downstream cytokines’ activation, thus controlling the progression of RILI.

NrF2-ARE signaling pathway

The Nrf2-ARE signaling pathway is currently recognized as the most crucial oxidative stress-related signaling pathway [139]. It regulates antioxidant enzymes, including heme oxygenase-1, gamma-glutamylcysteine synthetase, and NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO-1). These enzymes metabolize various free radicals and ROS, preventing oxidative damage to cells and promoting cell survival under oxidative stress conditions [140]. In the physiological state, Nrf2, when bound to its cytoplasmic partner protein Keap-1, cannot enter the cell nucleus to function as a transcription factor [139]. Nrf2 and its downstream antioxidant enzymes protect lung tissue and alleviate lung injury. It was found that radiation-induced ROS attack Nrf2 molecules, altering the conformation of Keap-1 and causing its dissociation [141]. This allows Nrf2 to enter the nucleus, activate the antioxidant response element (ARE), and initiate the transcription of phase II detoxifying enzymes and antioxidant genes [142]. The transcriptional regulation controls downstream antioxidant enzymes, regulates cell cycle proteins to prevent DNA damage, and inhibits the production of inflammatory cytokines, thereby protecting against RILI [143]. Therefore, applying Nrf2-ARE pathway-specific antagonists to RILI could be valuable for assessing the severity of RILI and investigating the importance of this pathway.

The problems and challenges of the application of SC-Exos in RILI

SC-Exos provide a new strategy for treating RILI, and the current experimental models related to the potential mechanisms of action of SC-Exos in the treatment are summarized in Table 1. However, several limitations require further research. Firstly, SC-Exos show cell-protective, anti-apoptotic, and anti-fibrotic effects in various animal models, but the development is slowing down in clinical applications. Due to the lack of standardized methods for obtaining SC-Exos, the optimal dosage, timing, administration route, and transplantation time for treatment need to be explored. It remains a question to identify suitable types of stem cells to stimulate tissue regeneration and reshape the immune microenvironment. Furthermore, the modification of SC-Exos to enhance their effectiveness and safety is worth further investigation, providing more strategies and opportunities for treating RILI.

Potential mechanisms of SC-Exos for the treatment of RILI

Conclusion

RILI is a complex lung disease, and therapeutic methods for it remain very limited. Recently, several studies have reported potential molecular mechanisms related to the regulation of SC-Exos in RILI. However, further research is needed to establish standardized methods for obtaining SC-Exos, as well as to determine the optimal dosage, timing, administration route, and transplantation timing for treatment. In conclusion, SC-Exos have significant potential in the treatment of RILI.
