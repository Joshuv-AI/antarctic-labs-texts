Differentially Private Two-Stage Empirical Risk Minimization and Applications to Individualized Treatment Rule
Joowon Lee, Guanhua Chen

Abstract
Differential Privacy (DP) provides a rigorous framework for deriving privacy-preserving estimators by injecting calibrated noise to mask individual contributions while preserving population-level insights. Its central challenge lies in the privacy-utility trade-off: calibrating noise levels to ensure robust protection without compromising statistical performance. Standard DP methods struggle with a particular class of two-stage problems prevalent in individualized treatment rules (ITRs) and causal inference. In these settings, data-dependent weights are first computed to satisfy distributional constraints, such as covariate balance, before the final parameter of interest is estimated. Current DP approaches often privatize stages independently, which either degrades weight efficacy—leading to biased and inconsistent estimates—or introduces excessive noise to account for worst-case scenarios. To address these challenges, we propose the Differentially Private Two-Stage Empirical Risk Minimization (DP-2ERM), a framework that injects a carefully calibrated noise only into the second stage while maintaining privacy for the entire pipeline and preserving the integrity of the first stage weights. Our theoretical contributions include deterministic bounds on weight perturbations across various widely used weighting methods, and probabilistic bounds on sensitivity for the final estimator. Simulations and real-world applications in ITR demonstrate that DP-2ERM significantly enhances utility over existing methods while providing rigorous privacy guarantees.

Introduction

1.1
Differential Privacy and Weighted Empirical Risk Minimization

Differential privacy
(DP) has established itself as the gold standard for data protection, ensuring that the inclusion or exclusion of any single individual’s data does not significantly affect the outcome of an analysis (see Definition
2.1
). DP mechanisms achieve this by injecting calibrated random noise into the computation, thereby masking individual contributions while preserving population-level insights. The amount of this added noise is governed by a privacy budget: a smaller budget aims to providing more rigorous protection, necessitating a higher level of noise, while a larger budget allows for weaker protection with less noise and, consequently, greater utility such as statistical accuracy. This framework is particularly indispensable in sensitive domains such as healthcare and finance, where data confidentiality is paramount. Foundational approaches to private learning include objective perturbation for convex
empirical risk minimization
(ERM)
[
5
]
and DP-
stochastic gradient descent
(SGD)
[
1
]
.

Standard DP methods including DP-ERM effectively privatize single stage estimation procedures, meaning that the input data is used once in the whole pipeline by solving associated optimization problem. However, modern statistical pipelines often involve more complex, multi stage architectures. For instance, in the ERM framework, sample-specific weights are introduced to form an weighted average of the empirical objectives to account for data heterogeneity, confounding, or selection bias.

We formalize this class of problems as
Two-Stage Empirical Risk Minimization
(2ERM), which utilizes the input data in two distinct phases:

Stage 1:

(
Find optimal sample weights
) Use the data to compute sample-specific weights that satisfy a specific balancing criterion.

Stage 2:

(
Find optimal parameter
) Obtain the estimated parameter of interest by solving a weighted ERM problem with the weights obtained in the first stage.

See Section
3.2
for a precise problem formulation of 2ERM. Note that the data-dependent weights found in the first stage serve as “nuisance parameters”: they are instrumental for achieving various desired properties of the parameter of interest, yet they are not the primary objects of inference.

A paramount instance of 2ERM—and the primary motivation for this work—is the estimation of
individualized treatment rules
(ITRs) in precision medicine. The goal of ITR is to recommend personalized treatments by leveraging individual covariates to maximize clinical benefit. In observational studies, where treatment assignment is not random, this process is inherently two-stage. The first stage must estimate individual-specific weights—typically
inverse probability weights
(IPWs)
[
34
]
,
maximum mean discrepancy
(MMD) weights
[
8
]
, or
entropy balancing weights
(EBWs)
[
20
]
—to balance covariate distributions between treatment groups and address confounding bias. Then, in the second stage, the optimal treatment rule can be effectively estimated by solving the associated weighted ERM.

Beyond ITR, the 2ERM structure encapsulates a wide array of problems in statistics and machine learning. In domain adaptation, importance weighting is the standard remedy for covariate shift, where the first stage estimates density ratios—often via Kernel Mean Matching
[
19
]
or KL-divergence minimization
[
37
]
—to align source and target distributions. Similarly, in robust learning with noisy labels, the first stage estimates “cleanliness” weights
[
27
]
to down-weight corrupted samples. The framework is equally relevant to algorithmic fairness, where pre-processing techniques reweight datasets to enforce statistical independence between sensitive attributes and labels
[
24
,
4
]
prior to classification.

1.2
Differentially Private Two-Stage ERM

The overarching goal of this work is to develop a rigorous and effective framework for
Differentially Private Two-Stage ERM
(DP-2ERM), which can be directly applied to the diverse range of problems falling under the aforementioned framework. As a primary application, we provide the first end-to-end ITR estimation procedure with formal DP guarantees. Given the sensitivity of patient medical histories, the ability to estimate effective ITRs in a privacy-preserving manner is of utmost practical importance. However, developing such a DP-2ERM framework faces several significant challenges.

First, while extensive literature exists on guaranteeing DP for multi stage algorithms via the composition of private mechanisms—collectively known as composition theorems
[
14
,
16
,
22
,
30
,
3
,
11
,
1
,
39
]
—these results do not directly apply to 2ERM. Specifically, current literature lacks a robust weighted ERM solver capable of satisfying the technical hypotheses required by standard composition: it must be DP with a fixed privacy budget uniformly for any prescribed weights in the support of the first stage outputs (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Theorem 3.2]
and
[
15
, Theorem 3.20]
).

Second, even if such a robust weighted ERM solver existed, the stringent requirements for uniform privacy across all possible weight distributions would necessitate an overwhelming amount of noise that could result in a total loss of statistical utility of the final estimator. In 2ERM, the first stage output is the individualized weights, which may emphasize a small subpopulation (or even a single individual) in the worst-case scenario. Solving a weighted ERM with such extreme weighting creates a significant vulnerability: if a single individual’s data dominates the objective function, the final model parameters become highly sensitive to that individual’s values.
To prevent leaks in such conservative scenarios, a hypothetical solver would require so much noise that the final estimator’s statistical utility would be entirely lost (see Figure
2
).

Third, even if we assume a hypothetical, robust weighted ERM solver exists and the excessive noise required for worst-case protection could be tolerated. To use the composition approach, one must also privatize the first stage of computing sample-specific weights
[
31
]
. However, adding noise directly to the weights can disrupt the constraints they were designed to enforce. For example, in causal inference, weights balance covariate distributions between treatment groups to mitigate confounding. Even minor perturbations for DP can lead to a failure in covariate balance, resulting in biased and unreliable treatment effect estimates.

Lastly, allocating a significant portion of the privacy budget to the first stage is fundamentally inefficient. In the 2ERM paradigm, the sample-specific weights are internal to the estimation pipeline and are never released to the public. Using composition would thus exhaust a limited privacy budget on nuisance parameters rather than the primary objects of inference.

Fig 1:
Differentially Private Two-Stage ERM Framework.
Our approach maintains non-private weight computation in the first stage to effectively address covariate shift, while applying privacy mechanisms exclusively to the second stage where weighted ERM is solved. This design is critical because standard composition theorems require calibrating noise against worst-case sequential dependencies to guarantee privacy, resulting in excessive noise injection that destroys utility. Our method ensures end-to-end differential privacy while preserving the effectiveness of the weights for balancing covariates.

In our DP-2ERM framework, we therefore avoid the use of composition approach entirely. Instead, we maintain the first stage (weight computation) in its non-private form to ensure satisfying its distributional constraints, then apply a tailored version of objective perturbation
[
5
]
suitably extended to the 2ERM setting. Our framework is based on directly proving the end-to-end DP guarantee for the entire two-stage pipeline.

Crucially, 2ERM exhibits a unique amplification of data dependency: a change in a single data point shifts the weights for the entire sample, which in turn perturbs the objective function of the second stage. Because the objective function changes more aggressively than in a typical single stage setting or a parallel stage problem, the final output is significantly more reactive to individual data points. The primary theoretical contribution of our direct approach is the precise quantification of this amplification effect. By doing so, it allows us to determine the exact amount of noise required to guarantee privacy without sacrificing statistical utility.

1.3
Contributions

At a high level, our general framework bridges the gap between differential privacy and two-stage empirical risk minimization problems. Our main results can be divided into the following three categories:

(i)

Data Sensitivity for Objective Perturbation:
We derive probabilistic bounds that quantify exactly how much changing a single data point affects the distribution of the private estimator when using objective perturbation. The key challenge in 2ERM is a change in one data point perturbs the training data both directly and indirectly through the perturbation in the weights. Specifically, we bound the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the private estimator’s distributions before and after a data perturbation. This bound is expressed in terms of the parameters of the objective perturbation (i.e., noise level and
L_{2}
-regularization) and the change of the weights. See Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.4
.

(ii)

Stability Bounds for Covariate Balancing Weights:

The aforementioned data sensitivity bound is effective only if we can provide reasonable bounds on the change in the weights caused by a single data point perturbation. To address this, we establish deterministic bounds on the stability of covariate balancing weights, which quantify exactly how much the weights change resulting from a single data point modification. Our results cover a wide range of weighting schemes; IPWs for randomized trials (Proposition
4.4
) and observational study (Proposition
4.5
and
4.6
), MMD weights (Proposition
), and EBWs (Theorem
4.9
).

(iii)

Differential Privacy of DP-2ERM and Privacy-Utility Tradeoffs:

We establish that differential privacy for DP-2ERM can be guaranteed by carefully calibrating noise levels and regularization strength according to our results in (i) and (ii). Inherently, enhancing privacy guarantee necessitates a compromise in the utility of the estimator-measured by the overall objective value-due to the introduction of additional noise and regularization. To quantify such privacy-utility tradeoff, we provide probabilistic bounds on the suboptimality gap between our private estimator and the non-private optimal solution (Theorem
3.5
).

We apply the proposed DP-2ERM framework to the problem of identifying ITRs. Our results demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms approaches based on composition theorems, providing more effective treatment decisions while preserving patient privacy. Unlike typical differential privacy techniques that degrade utility significantly due to noise addition, our method maintains high effectiveness even under differential privacy constraints, thus achieving a better balance between privacy and utility. Our effective differential private approach maintains competitive utility against even the non-private methods.

2
Background and Related Work

2.1
Differential Privacy

The mathematical framework of differential privacy was first introduced by Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith in
[
14
]
to provide a rigorous foundation for privacy-preserving data analysis. Here, we provide a brief exposition of its core definitions.

Consider a randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}
that produces an output
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
from a given dataset
\mathcal{D}
. The core principle of differential privacy is to ensure that the output distribution does not depend sensitively on the presence of any single individual, thereby protecting sensitive information against adversarial inference. This requirement can be viewed as distributional robustness under single data perturbations: the probability distributions of
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
must remain statistically similar whenever
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
differ by at most one observation. The following definition of
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy formalizes this intuition
[
13
]
.

Definition 2.1
(Differential Privacy).

A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy if, for any two datasets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
differing in at most one element, and for any measurable subset
S
of the output space of
\mathcal{M}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})\in S)\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\in S)+\delta.

(2.1)

The parameter
\epsilon
represents the privacy budget, quantifying the maximum allowable divergence between the output distributions of neighboring datasets; smaller values of
\epsilon
indicate stronger privacy. The parameter
\delta
acts as a failure probability, relaxing the strict requirement of
\epsilon
-DP by introducing an additive error term.

2.2
Differentially Private Empirical Risk Minimization

In 2011, Chaudhuri, Monteleoni, and Sarwate
[
5
]
proposed an influential work on differentially private ERM. Their aim was to obtain the optimal solution of the standard (unweighted) ERM. The authors proposed the technique of objective perturbation, which is a combination of
L_{2}
-regularization and adding a random linear function to the objective to guarantee differential privacy of the minimizer of the perturbed objective. The work by Chaudhuri et al. has been highly influential in the development of differentially private learning algorithms. Their approach has inspired numerous extensions, including private SGD techniques that enabled private deep learning
[
1
]
, private federated learning methods
[
29
]
, and applications to emerging machine learning paradigms
[
12
]
.

However, the original objective perturbation analysis imposes restrictive regularity conditions, including convexity, differentiability, and strong convexity (typically enforced via
L_{2}
-regularization). As a result, it does not directly accommodate common nonsmooth formulations such as
L_{1}
-regularization for sparsity or other constrained/nonsmooth objectives without additional technical modifications. To address such settings, Kifer et al.
[
25
]
developed extensions that handle constrained optimization while maintaining privacy guarantees, and they also demonstrated how to leverage
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP with Gaussian perturbations.

A key limitation for our purposes is that these seminal works focus on equal-weight (unweighted) ERM. In contrast, 2ERM involves
data-dependent
weights in the second stage objective, creating a tighter coupling between the data, the weight map, and the final estimator. This coupling is common in causal inference, where data-dependent reweighting is routine—for example, through covariate balancing weights used to learn individualized treatment rules.

2.3
Covariate Balancing Weights and ITR

A motivating instance of 2ERM is learning ITRs from observational data. In observational studies, treatment assignment is confounded, so the treated and control groups typically differ in their covariate distributions; without adjustment, learning an ITR by naïve (unweighted) risk minimization can be biased toward regions of the covariate space that are over-represented in one arm.

A standard approach is inverse probability weighting (IPW), which weights each sample by the inverse of its propensity score. This requires specifying (and estimating) a propensity score model, and the resulting weights can be sensitive to model misspecification and limited overlap. For this reason, methods that directly estimate weights based on balance-seeking objectives have been proposed. These approaches include EBWs
[
20
]
, stable balancing weights
[
41
]
as well as distributional covariate balancing weights
[
21
,
23
]
. Distributional covariate balancing weights are modern alternatives to IPWs that directly minimize a discrepancy between the empirical covariate distributions induced by the weights, thereby targeting covariate balance without committing to a parametric propensity model.

Most covariate balancing weights, including IPWs and distributional covariate balancing weights, are computed by solving convex optimization problems of the form described in the first stage in (
3.1
). These weights, once computed, are then used to formulate a weighted ERM problem in the second stage (
3.2
) to estimate an ITR within a chosen decision-function class.

This two-stage structure is precisely what motivates our 2ERM framework and our privacy analysis: a perturbation of one record can change the entire weight vector computed in the first stage, which in turn affects the sensitivity of the second stage solution. We make the reduction to 2ERM explicit in Section
3.5
and return to the private ITR instantiation in Section
6
.

2.4
Differentially Private Causal Inference

Motivated by the two-stage ITR pipeline described above—where data-dependent balancing weights are estimated first and then plugged into a weighted ERM—we briefly review related work on differentially private causal inference. Existing approaches typically achieve privacy either by privatizing intermediate nuisance estimates (e.g., propensity/weight models and outcome regressions) and composing privacy budgets across stages, or by imposing stability assumptions that effectively eliminate the contribution of data-dependent weights to the final sensitivity. These strategies do not directly address optimization-based balancing weights in observational settings, which is the regime targeted by DP-2ERM.

Niu et al.
[
31
]
proposed a differentially private procedure for heterogeneous effect estimation that splits the data into three parts, privately estimates balancing weights and an outcome regression using budget
\epsilon/3
each, and then estimates the
conditional average treatment effect
(CATE) using the remaining
\epsilon/3
. However, their approach is tailored to parametric weighting schemes (e.g., IPW via a propensity model) and does not directly accommodate optimization-based balancing weights (e.g., EBW and distributional balancing weights), which are typically computed by solving convex programs. In addition, adding noise in the first stage can degrade covariate balance. More broadly, allocating privacy budget across intermediate releases can lead to conservative noise levels and reduced utility.

A more recent approach by Giddens et al.
[
17
]
addresses weighted ERM via output perturbation. While conceptually related to our framework, their analysis relies on the strong assumption that the product of outcome and IPW satisfies an asymptotic stability bound of
o_{\mathbb{P}}(1/n)
per individual. Under such a restrictive assumption, the sensitivity of the minimizer of the weighted ERM problem reduces to that of a standard unweighted ERM, sidestepping the primary technical challenge of DP estimation under weighted ERM framework. Furthermore, their methodology is evaluated primarily within the context of randomized clinical trials, where propensity scores are typically fixed and known.

In contrast, DP-2ERM targets the observational-study regime where the weights are
data-dependent
and may exhibit nonvanishing perturbations. We derive non-asymptotic stability bounds for a broad class of balancing weights (including EBW and MMD weights) and translate these bounds into calibrated noise levels for objective perturbation in the second stage weighted ERM. This yields end-to-end privacy guarantees while preserving utility in settings with complex confounding.

2.5
More on Limitations on Using Composition Theorems for 2ERM

As discussed in the introduction, a natural baseline is to privatize each stage separately and then apply composition
[
5
,
16
]
. Concretely, one could compute a private weight vector in Stage 1 with parameters
(\epsilon_{1},\delta_{1})
(e.g., via objective perturbation or noisy SGD), and then solve the Stage 2 weighted ERM with an independent mechanism satisfying
(\epsilon_{2},\delta_{2})
-DP. Standard composition yields an overall guarantee of at least
(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2},\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})

[
14
,
13
]
. Sharper accounting methods (e.g.,
16
,
22
,
11
) improve constants and scaling under repeated compositions, but they still effectively require Stage 2 to be private
uniformly
over all possible Stage 1 outputs.

However, existing composition theorems, including those building on the generalized f-DP, often rely on a strong assumption that subsequent mechanisms are “uniformly DP”
irrespective of the previous mechanisms’ outputs.
While this general assumption is valid in many contexts, in specific applications like ITR learning or transfer learning, where weights play a crucial role, it can be overly stringent and lead to suboptimal privacy-utility trade-offs. In the context of our 2ERM, this would require a privatized solution of the weighted ERM (
3.2
) to be
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP for some
(\epsilon,\delta)
, regardless of the given weight. This uniform guarantee is problematic since if an arbitrary weight is concentrated on one individual, then altering this individual’s covariate will significantly change the output, privatized solution, unless significantly large noise is used to privatize the weighted ERM. Thus, such a uniform DP-guarantee against arbitrary weight, if it exists, will be extremely poor. We also confirm this speculation through our experiments (see Figure
2
), which demonstrate how the choice of weights within a differentially private framework can lead to considerable variations in the ITR learning performance. Furthermore, as we have already discussed, excessive privatization of the covariate balancing weights is not our aim. Our objective is to develop an approach that provides differential privacy for the entire pipeline simultaneously, under weaker, context-appropriate assumptions for weighted ERM.

\displaystyle W_{1}:=\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{1}+\max_{1\leq i\leq n}(w_{i}\land w_{i}^{\prime}),\quad W_{2}:=\frac{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|^{2}+2\max_{1\leq i\leq n}w_{i}w_{i}^{\prime}}}{1/\sqrt{1+\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{0}}}.

(3.4)

Suppose
there exist
\lambda,\zeta
such that
\textup{tr}(\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{d}))\leq\lambda
and
\|\nabla\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{d})\|_{2}\leq\zeta
for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and
\mathbf{d}\in\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
.
Then for Algorithm

and for each Borel measurable set
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

(i)

With
Gamma noise

f_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(-\beta\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2})
, for each Borel measurable set
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\beta\zeta W_{1}+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma n}W_{2}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B}).

(3.5)

(ii)

With
Gaussian noise

\mathbf{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
, for each
\delta>0
and Borel measurable set
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\rho W_{1}+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma n}W_{2}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{B})+\delta,

\displaystyle\qquad\text{where }\rho

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(2\zeta\sigma\sqrt{\left(\sqrt{p}+\sqrt{\log\delta^{-1}}\right)^{2}+\log\delta^{-1}}+\zeta^{2}\right).

(3.6)

From the above result, one can immediately deduce a sufficient noise level and

-regularization in the objective perturbation to ensure differential privacy of the entire DP-2ERM procedure. Indeed, for the Gamma noise, we can easily guarantee
\epsilon
-DP of Algorithm

by choosing the noise level

small enough and the

-regularization parameter

and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. This gives us the following result on differential privacy guarantee of DP-2ERM.

Theorem 3.2
(Differential Privacy of DP-2ERM).

Suppose there exist
\lambda,\zeta
such that
\textup{tr}(\nabla^{2}\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{d}))\leq\lambda
and
\|\nabla\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{d})\|_{2}\leq\zeta
for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and for all feasible feature vectors
\mathbf{d}
. With
W_{i}
’s defined in (
3.4
), denote

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{i}:=\sup_{\mathcal{D}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}W_{i}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\qquad\textup{for $i=1,2$},

(3.7)

where the supremums are over all size-
n
datasets
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that differ by at most one point. Then the following holds for Algorithm
1
:

(i)

For the Gamma noise,
\epsilon
-DP is guaranteed provided

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}\geq\frac{2\zeta}{\epsilon}\overline{W}_{1}\quad\textup{and}\quad\gamma\geq\frac{2\lambda}{\epsilon n}\overline{W}_{2},

(3.8)

(ii)

For the Gaussian noise,
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP is guaranteed provided

\displaystyle\sigma\geq\frac{\zeta}{\epsilon}\left(\tilde{L}+\sqrt{\tilde{L}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{3n}}\right)\overline{W}_{1}\quad\textup{and}\quad\gamma\geq\frac{2\lambda}{\epsilon n}\overline{W}_{2},

(3.9)

where

.

A key feature in our privacy guarantee in Theorem

and
W_{2}
. Intuitively, these quantities are larger for more data-sensitive weighting schemes, which in turn requires larger noise level and regularization to guarantee privacy of DP-2ERM under the same privacy budget.

To make the privacy guarantee in Theorem

practical, we need to bound the worst-case weight sensitivity measures
\overline{W}_{1}
and
\overline{W}_{2}
. These quantities depend on the specific choice of the first stage weighting mechanism (see Section

). We first consider the simplest case: a single-stage weighted ERM where the weights
\mathbf{w}
are constant, data-independent (e.g.,
\mathbf{w}=(1,\dots,1)
). In this scenario, Theorem
3.2
simplifies as follows.

Corollary 3.3
(Differential Privacy of Data-Independent Weighted ERM).

Suppose data-independent weight
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
. Then Theorem
3.2
holds with
W_{1}=\max w_{i}
and
W_{2}=\sqrt{2}\max w_{i}
.

Note that when
\mathbf{w}=(1,\dots,1)
, Corollary
3.3
essentially recovers the DP-ERM privacy guarantee of Chaudhari et al. in
[
5
]
.

Next, we also discuss another corollary of Theorem
3.2
based on a crude but universal bound on
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
. Note that the sum-to-

constraint for the weights implies the universal weight stability bound
\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 2n
and trivial uniform bound
\max\mathbf{w}\leq n
. Using these universal bounds gives the following universal DP guarantee for a general non-private weighting mechanism.

Corollary 3.4
(Universal Differential Privacy of 2ERM via Objective Perturbation).

Theorem
3.2
holds with
W_{1}=3n
and
W_{2}=\sqrt{6}(n+1)^{3/2}
, irrespective of the weighting mechanism used in the first stage.

While the algorithm setting in Corollary

provides a universal differential privacy guarantee for DP-2ERM that is robust against the first stage solution, the amount of noise and regularization provided in Corollary
3.4
cannot guarantee strong utility. This is because these amounts were set high enough to prevent privacy leakage even in the most extreme weight shift scenario, such as achieving
\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{1}=2n
(e.g.,
\mathbf{w}
is supported on the first half of the coordinates and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
is supported on the second half of the coordinates). Therefore, to apply Theorem

more efficiently, we need a tighter bound on the weight perturbation
\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|
, which must inevitably depend on the nature of the first stage weighting mechanisms. We obtain more specific weight perturbation bounds for various standard weighting mechanisms in the causal inference literature, such as the IPW, MMD weights, and EBWs. These results are stated in Section
4
in detail.

We now turn our attention to the utility guarantees.
As long as
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP is guaranteed for the entire procedure, it would be beneficial to choose both the noise level (

for the Gamma noise and

-regularization. This can be justified through utility analysis. Namely, compared to the smallest possible objective value of

at the optimal non-private parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}}
, how small is the objective value at the private estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}
? In the following result, we show that the sub-optimality of the private estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}
is bounded by a decreasing function of
\beta
and
\gamma^{-1}
and an increasing function of
\sigma
.

Theorem 3.5
(Utility Guarantees for DP-2ERM).

Suppose
\mathcal{D}
be a size-
n
dataset and
let
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
be the non-private optimal weights solving (

) for dataset

. Then for each
t\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}}\|_{2}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{b}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}};\mathcal{D})\geq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}};\mathcal{D})+t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{b}}\left(\|\mathbf{b}\|_{2}\geq\frac{n}{2}\sqrt{2\gamma t-\gamma^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)

(3.10)

where
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the noise vector. Furthermore, denote
D_{t}:=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2\gamma t-\gamma^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}}\|_{2}^{2}}
.

(i)

If the pdf of
\mathbf{b}
is
f_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp(-\beta\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2})
, then for
t\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}}\|_{2}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{b}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}};\mathcal{D})\geq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}};\mathcal{D})+t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{D_{t}\beta n}{2}\right).

(3.11)

(ii)

If
\mathbf{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
, then for

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{b}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}};\mathcal{D})\geq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}};\mathcal{D})+t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{c\gamma^{2}n^{3}}{16\sigma^{4}}\left(\frac{t-\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{\gamma n}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{n}D_{t}+\sigma}\right)^{2}\right).

(3.12)

(iii)

If
\beta
and
\sigma
does not depend on

(see Corollary

(i)
-
(iii)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{b}}\left(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}};\mathcal{D})\geq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{opt}};\mathcal{D})+\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\begin{cases}\exp\left(-c\beta\sqrt{n}\right)&\textup{for the Gamma noise},\\
\exp\left(-c\sigma^{-6}n\right)&\textup{for the Gaussian noise}.\end{cases}

(3.13)

Part
(iii)
of Theorem
3.5
is particularly interesting. In both Gamma and Gaussian noise cases, with high probability, the objective value at the private estimator
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}
does not exceed the optimal value by more than

with high probability. However, tail bound on such

-utility gap event decays much faster in the Gaussian case than in the Gamma case (exponential in
-n
vs.
-\sqrt{n}
). We also remark that

depends on the dimension

. Namely, according to Corollary
3.4
, we may choose

where
\tilde{L}\sim\sqrt{p}
in (

. Regarding

. Thus, the sample size

Before developing weight stability bounds, we briefly show how learning ITRs from observational data naturally induces a two-stage weighted ERM structure, making
\|\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})-\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\|
the central quantity governing the privacy–utility tradeoff.

3.5
Running Example: ITR Learning as a 2ERM Problem

We sketch how ITR estimation fits into the 2ERM framework and why covariate balancing weights are needed. (See Section
6
for more details.) Let
A\in\{-1,1\}
denote treatment,
Y
the outcome, and
X
baseline covariates. Let
Y(a)
denote the potential outcome under treatment
a\in\mathcal{A}
. We assume the standard identification conditions in the potential outcome framework
[
35
]
: (i) SUTVA (
Y=Y(A)
), (ii) positivity (
0<\pi(a,\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})<1
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
), and (iii) no unmeasured confounding (
Y(a)\perp A\mid\mathbf{X}
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
).

Under the identification conditions, for a decision rule
d:\mathcal{X}\to\{-1,1\}
, its value can be expressed as

V(d)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Y\,\mathbf{1}\{A=d(\mathbf{X})\}}{\pi(A,\mathbf{X})}\right]

where
\pi(a,X)=\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid X)
is the propensity score. In observational studies, the treated and control groups typically differ in their covariate distributions, and the inverse-propensity reweighting in
V(d)
highlights that naïve (unweighted) learning can target the wrong covariate distribution.

In practice,
\pi
is unknown and directly modeling it can be unstable. A modern alternative is to compute covariate balancing weights that directly rebalance treated and control covariate distributions. These weights are obtained in a first stage by solving a convex problem of the form (
3.1
), yielding a data-dependent weight vector
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
. The ITR is then estimated in a second stage by solving a weighted ERM problem of the form (
3.2
), treating
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
as a nuisance input. This is precisely the two-stage dependence captured by DP-2ERM: a single-record perturbation affects
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\textup{priv}}
both directly through the second stage empirical risk and indirectly through the first stage weights. Consequently, bounding
\|\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})-\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\|
is the key step toward calibrating privacy noise with minimal loss of utility.

4
Stability of Covariate Balancing Weights

Motivated by Section
3.5
, we now develop stability bounds for covariate balancing weights that quantify how much
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
can change when one record in
\mathcal{D}
is modified. These bounds are the key ingredient needed to turn the sensitivity inequalities in Theorem
3.1
into concrete privacy calibrations with improved utility. We first give a general stability principle for weights defined as solutions to strongly convex optimization problems, and then instantiate it for several widely used weighting mechanisms.

Our key tool for this weight stability analysis is the following result, which provides a general stability bound on how much the optimal weights
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
are perturbed when a single point in the dataset
\mathcal{D}
is altered. Specifically, it states that the perturbation is small if the change in the gradient of the objective
\mathcal{E}(\cdot;\mathcal{D})
is small relative to its strong convexity parameter.

Theorem 4.1
(General Stability of Parameterized Weights).

Let
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be two
n
-sample datasets. Let
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
and
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
denote the corresponding optimal weights as in (
3.1
).
Suppose
F
is
L_{F}
-Lipschitz continuous and
\mathcal{E}(\cdot;\mathcal{D})
is
\rho
-strongly convex for some
\rho>0
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})-\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq\frac{2L_{F}}{\rho}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\Xi}\|\nabla\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\lambda};\mathcal{D})-\nabla\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{\lambda};\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\|_{2}.

(4.1)

Based on the general principle in Theorem
4.1
, we obtain stability bounds for various covariate balancing weights; IPWs with randomized trials (Proposition
4.4
), IPWs with logistic regression (Propositions
4.5
and
4.6
), MMD weights (Proposition
), and EBWs (Theorem
4.9
). Below in Theorem

, we summarize these results with many constants suppressed in the
O(\cdot)
notation.

Theorem 4.2
(Stability of Covariate Balancing Weights).

Let
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be two
n
-sample datasets that differ at one point. Then we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})-\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\|_{2}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}O(1/n)&\textup{IPWs for randomized trials; Proposition~\ref{prop:stability_IPW_randomized_trial}},\\
O(1)&\textup{IPWs with known regression parameter; Proposition~\ref{prop:IPW_known_beta}},\\
O(1/(1+\lambda_{\textup{IPW}}))&\textup{IPWs with logistic regression; Proposition~\ref{prop:IPW_estimated_stability}},\\
O(n/\lambda_{\textup{MMD}})&\textup{MMD weights; Proposition~\ref{prop:MMD_stability}},\\

To avoid confusion with the Hessian trace bound
\lambda
in Theorem
3.1
, we note that these

regularization coefficients for the weight optimization problems defined in Equations (

), (

. With positive

By combining Theorems
3.2
and

, we can obtain significantly improved privacy guarantee of DP-2ERM with covariate balancing weights in the sense that, for the same privacy budget, the required noise level (proportional to
\overline{W}_{1}
) and the
L_{2}
regularization (proportional to
\overline{W}_{2}
) in the objective perturbation for the second stage are much smaller. The improvement is at least a factor of

for the noise level and a factor of

for the regularization. These results are stated in the corollary below, where the constants in

terms can be traced from the corresponding results stated in (

).

Corollary 4.3
(Improved Privacy Guarantees of DP-2ERM with Covariate Balancing Weights).

Theorem
3.2
holds with the following improved bounds on
\overline{W}_{1}
and
\overline{W}_{2}

(i)

If
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
is the IPW with either randomized trial or known regression parameter, then
\overline{W}_{1}=O(1)
and
\overline{W}_{2}=O(\sqrt{n})
.

(ii)

If
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
is the IPW with estimated regression parameter, then

\overline{W}_{1}=O(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{1+\lambda_{\textup{IPW}}}+\sqrt{n})
and
\overline{W}_{2}=O(\sqrt{n})
.

(iii)

If
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})
is the MMD weight, then

\overline{W}_{1}=O(\frac{n^{3/2}}{\lambda_{\textup{MMD}}}+\sqrt{n})

\overline{W}_{2}=O(\frac{n^{3/2}}{\lambda_{\textup{MMD}}}+\sqrt{n})

4.1
Stability of Weights in Randomized Trials

First, we look at two instances of covariate balancing weights that can be found without any additional optimization procedure in the randomized trial setting. These are IPWs under randomized trials and IPWs with a known regression parameter.

4.1.1
Stability of IPWs under Randomized Trial

Consider an observed dataset
\mathcal{D}:=(\mathbf{x}_{i},a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
from a randomized trial, where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
represents the covariate vector of patient
i
and
a_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denotes the treatment assignment for patient
i
. The
propensity score
is defined as

\displaystyle\pi(1|\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(A=1\,|\,X=\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(\textup{A patient with covarite $\mathbf{x}$ receives treatment}).

(4.3)

In a randomized trial, the propensity score does not depend on the covariate
\mathbf{x}
and it depends only on the treatment, so
\pi(a|\mathbf{x})=p_{a}
for
a\in\{0,1\}
and for all
\mathbf{x}
.
The IPW vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=C\cdot\left(\frac{1}{p_{a_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{1}{p_{a_{n}}}\right)

(4.4)

where
p_{a_{i}}
is the probability of receiving treatment
a_{i}
, and
C
is a normalization constant that ensures the sum of weights equals
n
, given by
C=n/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{p_{a_{i}}}
.

Now consider a perturbed dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
of size
n
that differs from
\mathcal{D}
only in the last observation, with
(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{n},a^{\prime}_{n})
replacing
(\mathbf{x}_{n},a_{n})
. The new IPW vector, say
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
, will differ from the previous one
\mathbf{w}
if
a_{n}\neq a_{n}^{\prime}
and
p_{0}\neq p_{1}
. In this case, both the normalization constant
C
and the last coordinate of the un-normalized weight vectors,
\frac{1}{p_{a_{n}}}
and
\frac{1}{p_{a_{n}^{\prime}}}
, are different. However, we can bound their difference as follows.

Proposition 4.4
(Stability of IPWs under Randomized Trial).

Let
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
denote the IPW vectors under randomized trial corresponding to datasets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that differ at one point, as above. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}\left(\frac{p_{0}\lor p_{1}}{p_{0}\land p_{1}}\right)^{2}.

(4.5)

This demonstrates that the weight vector is asymptotically stable with respect to perturbations in a single observation, with the
L_{2}
-norm difference decreasing at a rate of
O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
as the sample size increases. However, despite this quadratic convergence rate, the analysis reveals that even in randomized trials, there remains a non-zero difference between the weight vectors when treatment assignments change. Though this difference is small, it highlights our subsequent development of a carefully designed privacy-preserving algorithm for ITR learning that addresses these sensitivity issues.

4.1.2
Stability of IPWs with Known Regression Parameters

Next, consider the correctly specified case via logistic regression. Namely, we assume the true propensity score model is known and is given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{i}=1\mid X=\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{\exp(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle)}{1+\exp(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle)},

(4.6)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is known true regression parameter vector. Under the propensity score model, IPW for patient
i
is defined as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(A_{i}=1\mid X=\mathbf{x}_{i})}=\frac{1+\exp(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle)}{\exp(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle)}=1+\exp(-\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle).

(4.7)

The weight vector
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})
is then obtained by normalizing the above IPWs so that their sum is
n

\displaystyle w_{i}=n\frac{1+\exp(-\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle).}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}1+\exp(-\langle\mathbf{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\rangle)},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n.

(4.8)

Proposition 4.5
(Stability of IPWs with Known Regression Parameters).

Let
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
denote the IPW vectors for datasets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
that differ at one point, as above, under the observational study with known
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
. Suppose that the
L_{2}
-norm of each data point is uniformly bounded by some constant
M
(i.e.,
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq M
) and
\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|_{2}\leq R
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{5p}\exp(MR)\cdot M^{2}R.

(4.9)

4.2
Stability of Weights in Observational Studies

The more interesting and more adaptive framework is when covariate balancing weight vector
\mathbf{w}
for a dataset
\mathcal{D}
is parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and such a parameter is found by a separate convex optimization using the dataset
\mathcal{D}
. This covers the most interesting cases, including (1) IPWs under observational study with estimated regression parameters, (2) MMD weights, and (3) EBWs.

4.2.1
Stability of IPWs under Observational Study with Estimated Regression Parameters

Suppose that we use logistic regression to estimate the propensity scores for all individuals.
The estimated regression parameter
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\argmin_{\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|_{2}\leq R}\left[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\lambda};\mathcal{D}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\underbrace{\log\left(1+\exp(\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle\right)-a_{i}\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\rangle}_{=:\ell_{\textup{nll}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda};\,\mathbf{x}_{i},a_{i})}+\frac{\lambda_{\textup{IPW}}}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(4.10)

where
\ell_{\textup{nll}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda};\,\mathbf{x}_{i},a_{i})
above is the negative log-likelihood of observed data
(\mathbf{x}_{i},a_{i})
under
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. We have added
L_{2}
-regularization term with coefficient
\lambda_{\textup{IPW}}\geq 0
for improving stability, which is a common practice for IPW. Once we compute
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, we then assign the IPWs for all

4.2.3
Stability of EBWs

We first review the construction of EBWs for

[
6

Suppose we have a population
\mathcal{S}
of
n
patients consisting of
n_{0}
patients in the control group
\mathcal{S}_{0}
and
n_{1}
patients in the treatment group
\mathcal{S}_{1}
.
The goal is to reweight every patient so that the first
K
moments in the reweighted control (resp., treatment) group match up with the corresponding moments for the entire population
\mathcal{S}
. Let
\mathbf{w}_{a}=(w_{a1},\dots,w_{an_{a}})\in n_{a}\Delta^{n_{a}}
for
a=0,1
denote the weight vectors for the control and the treatment groups with normalization condition
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{a}}w_{ai}=n_{a}
for
a=0,1
. Fix a base probability distribution for the entire group
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
, which is often taken as the uniform distribution (i.e.,
q_{i}\equiv 1/n
). We seek for weights
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{w}_{1})\in n_{0}\Delta^{n_{0}}\times n_{1}\Delta^{n_{1}}
that is as close as possible to the base distribution
\mathbf{q}
subject to the moment constraints with functionals
g_{0},\dots,g_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. This problem can be expressed as the following relative entropy minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}\in n\Delta^{n}}\,D_{KL}(\mathbf{w}\|\mathbf{q})\quad\textup{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{0}}w_{0i}g_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}g_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),

(4.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}w_{1i}g_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}g_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\quad\textup{for $r=0,\dots,K$}.

(4.19)

Here

denotes the KL divergence of
\mathbf{w}/n
from
\mathbf{q}
, which is used as a measure of ‘distance’ between
\mathbf{w}/n
and
\mathbf{q}
. (Recall that

and equals

is not absolutely continuous w.r.t
\mathbf{q}
). In order to force group-wise normalization condition

for

, the zeroth moment function.

3.2

Our work assumes positivity assumption is satisfied. This well-overlapping property of covariate distributions ensures that the moment-matching constraints in entropy balancing remain feasible, guaranteeing the existence of a solution to the optimization problem. Consequently, we can always find appropriate EBWs.

The following lemma shows how to control the perturbation in the pdf of parameters given by a function of noise vector.

\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\|\leq\frac{2}{\rho}\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\|\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\theta})\|.

6
Learning ITRs via Weighted Regression and DP-2ERM

As discussed in Section
3.5
, learning ITRs from observational data is a canonical instance of 2ERM: covariate balancing weights are computed in a first stage to mitigate confounding, and then treated as nuisance inputs in a second stage weighted ERM. In this section, we formalize the weighted-regression reduction for ITR learning and instantiate Algorithm
1
for private estimation of a linear decision function.

6.1
Setup and Optimal Decision Function

We observe i.i.d. data
(\mathbf{X},A,Y)
where
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{p})\in\mathcal{X}
denotes pre-treatment covariates,
A\in\mathcal{A}
is the received treatment, and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
is the outcome (larger values are more favorable). An ITR, also called a
treatment regime
, is a decision rule that maps a patient’s covariates to a treatment recommendation. In the binary-treatment setting considered here, an ITR is a function
d:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{A}=\{-1,1\}
. The goal is to learn
d
from data so as to improve expected outcomes by tailoring treatment to patient characteristics
[
33
]
. We learn a real-valued decision function
f
and use the induced rule
d(\mathbf{x})={\rm sign}\{f(\mathbf{x})\}
.

The
value
of an ITR
d
is
V(d):=\mathbb{E}[Y(d(\mathbf{X}))]
, which can be identified as

V(d)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Y\,\mathbf{1}\{A=d(\mathbf{X})\}}{\pi(A,\mathbf{X})}\right]

under the standard causal assumptions we stated in Section
3.5
. For binary treatments, the optimal ITR is determined by the conditional mean contrast. Define

\displaystyle f_{\textup{opt}}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=1)-\mathbb{E}(Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=-1),\qquad d^{\textup{opt}}(\mathbf{x})={\rm sign}\{f_{\textup{opt}}(\mathbf{x})\}.

Under the identification assumptions,
f_{\textup{opt}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left.\frac{YA}{\pi(A,\mathbf{X})}\right|\,\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\right]
.

6.2
Weighted Regression Reduction, 2ERM Structure, and Private Estimation

A key observation underlying the
weighted regression
framework for ITR learning
[
38
,
9
,
32
]
is that
f_{\textup{opt}}
solves the population risk minimization

\displaystyle f_{\textup{opt}}\in\argmin_{f}\,\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(2YA-f(\mathbf{X}))^{2}}{\pi(A,\mathbf{X})}\right].

Thus, with appropriate weights, one can estimate the contrast by regressing the transformed outcome
2YA
on covariates (or interaction features) and then set the rule by taking the sign.

We model the decision function linearly as
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}
, yielding the ITR
d_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})={\rm sign}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})
.
Given
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},a_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by the weighted ERM

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,l(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{x}_{i},a_{i},y_{i})+R(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\},\qquad l(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathbf{x},a,y):=\big(2ya-\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\big)^{2},

(6.1)

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}:=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{1}\leq\lambda_{1}\}
promotes sparsity and
R(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a convex regularizer.

When the propensity score
\pi(a,\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})
is known, setting
w_{i}=1/\pi(a_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
in (
6.1
) yields the usual inverse-propensity weighted regression criterion

9
Discussion

This paper introduces DP-2ERM, a framework for end-to-end differentially private estimation within two-stage architectures where a data-dependent first stage — designed to satisfy distributional constraints like covariate balance — feeds into a second stage weighted empirical risk minimization. A primary challenge in these sequential pipelines is that the sensitivity of the second stage solution depends on how a single data point perturbation propagates through the first stage weight perturbation. While generic composition-based analyses are often overly conservative and require excessive noise to account for worst-case sequential dependencies, DP-2ERM utilizes a direct approach. By explicitly bounding the deterministic stability of the weight perturbation and calibrating the objective perturbation to the resulting data-dependent sensitivity, we provide rigorous privacy guarantees that preserve the statistical integrity of the final released solution.

A practical implication is that the privacy–utility tradeoff is driven not only by the privacy mechanism but also by the
stability
of the weighting scheme. Our theory and experiments suggest that optimization-based balancing weights (e.g., EBW and MMD weights) can be substantially more stable than IPW, which translates into markedly improved private performance at the same privacy budget. We emphasize that DP-2ERM protects the
final output
(the second stage solution): intermediate objects such as weights, propensity estimates, or tuning choices are treated as internal nuisance parameters and are not released; releasing them would generally require additional privacy accounting.

Our theory relies on conditions typical for objective perturbation—convexity of the loss, and strong convexity induced by regularization—together with boundedness/regularity assumptions used to control sensitivity. Extending DP-2ERM to nonconvex objectives (e.g., deep policy networks) will require different private optimization tools and sensitivity analyses, such as DP-SGD or output perturbation under alternative stability conditions. Moreover, limited overlap can amplify instability of weights and therefore increase the required noise; understanding the precise interaction between overlap diagnostics, weight trimming, and privacy calibration is an important direction for further study.

Several extensions appear promising. First, DP-2ERM can be adapted to richer decision-function classes beyond linear rules and to multi-stage decision problems such as dynamic treatment regimes. Second, the same analysis blueprint applies to other 2ERM-style pipelines, including covariate-shift correction and transfer learning with balancing weights. Finally, it would be useful to develop privacy-preserving procedures for auxiliary quantities of scientific interest, such as private value/regret estimation and uncertainty quantification for the learned rule.
