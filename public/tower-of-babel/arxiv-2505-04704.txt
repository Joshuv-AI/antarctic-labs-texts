Disorder-Free Localization and Fragmentation in a Non-Abelian Lattice Gauge Theory
Giovanni Cataldi, Giuseppe Calajó, Pietro Silvi, Simone Montangero, Jad C. Halimeh

Abstract
We investigate how isolated quantum many-body systems dynamically equilibrate under non-Abelian gauge-symmetry constraints. By encoding gauge superselection sectors into static SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) background charges, we map out the dynamical phase diagram of a (1+1)D SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) lattice gauge theory with dynamical matter. We uncover three distinct regimes: (i) an ergodic phase, (ii) a fragmented phase that is nonthermal but delocalized, and (iii) a disorder-free many-body localized regime. In the latter, a superposition of gauge superselection sectors preserves spatial matter inhomogeneities in time, as evidenced by distinct temporal scalings of entropy. We highlight the non-Abelian nature of these phases and argue for potential realizations on qudit processors.

Disorder-free localization.—

To observe DFL, we initialize the dynamics from an equal superposition of all the
\mathcal{N}_{\rm{SS}}
gauge superselection sectors (SS) of background-charges:

\begin{split}\ket{\Psi_{\rm{SS}}}&{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\rm{SS}}}}\sum_{\kappa=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{\rm{SS}}}\ket{\Psi_{\{b^{\kappa}_{n}\}}},\;\;b_{n}^{\kappa}\in\quantity{0,1/2},\;\forall n,\kappa.\end{split}

(3)

Specifically, each background-charge sector contributes with two simple translation-invariant product states where (i) matter fields are in a 2-site charge density wave configuration (staggered chain with two empty sites alternated with two doubly occupied ones) and (ii) gauge fields can be in any of the
j\in\quantity{0,1/2}

\mathrm{SU}(2)
irreps.
In this way, the initial state encodes a finite imbalance in the matter configuration under the Hamiltonian in
Eq.

1
and evolves in a superposition of all the possible gauge field configurations.
A pictorial representation of some of these contributions is in
Fig.

1
(b,c).

For direct comparison, we also consider the same matter configuration of the state in
Eq.

3
with no active gauge fields, which belongs to the gauge-invariant (GI) sector without background charges (see
Fig.

1
(b)):

\begin{split}\ket{\Psi_{\rm{GI}}}&\in\ket{\Psi_{\quantity[0,0,\dots, 0]}}.\end{split}

(4)

To detect the occurrence of DFL in the system, we introduce the following matter imbalance:

\mathcal{I}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\rho_{n}(t)\,,

(5)

where
0\leq\rho_{n}(t)\leq 2
is the single-site fermion density:

\rho_{n}(t)\equiv\sum_{\alpha}\matrixelement{\Psi(t)}{\hat{\psi}_{\site,\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{\site,\alpha}}{\Psi(t)},

(6)

and the coefficients
w_{n}\!=\!\rho_{n}(0)\!-\!1
define the weights associated with the considered initial matter configuration.
The matter imbalance in
Eq.

5
quantifies how much the system departs from or retains the matter configuration of the initial state.
To compute the long-time average of this and any other local observable
\hat{O}(t)
, we use the diagonal ensemble (DE)
[
113
,
126
]
, which, for the initial state expressed in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis,
\ket{\Psi(0)}\!=\!\sum_{s}C_{s}\ket{\Phi_{s}}
, the long-time average is
O_{\mathrm{DE}}\!=\!\sum_{s}\absolutevalue{C_{s}}^{2}\braket{\Phi_{s}|\hat{O}|\Phi_{s}}
.
In
Fig.

2
(a), we plot the DE imbalance
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{DE}}
for the SS state of
Eq.

3
as a function of mass and gauge coupling strength.
The plot shows that for large coupling and moderately large masses,
(g\!\gg\!1,m\!\gtrsim\!1)
, the long-time average of the imbalance
\mathcal{I}_{DE}
acquires finite values, signaling long-time localization of the initial configuration.

The dynamics of the imbalance approaching the long-time average are shown in
Fig.

2
(b).
There, to mitigate the effect of finite-size oscillations, we consider the time-averaged imbalance
\overline{\mathcal{I}}(t)=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int^{t+\Delta t/2}_{t-\Delta t/2}ds\,\mathcal{I}(s)
, for an intermediate time window
\Delta t=300
.
We focus on three parameter points (1-3 circles in
Fig.

2
(a)) representing the ergodic (1), DFL (2), and HSF (3) regions sketched in
Fig.

1
(a), and compare dynamics from the GI and SS initial states.
For the GI initial state, the imbalance
\overline{\mathcal{I}}(t)
does not remain finite at long times in any of the three regimes; in the HSF region, it relaxes more slowly than in the ergodic case.
In contrast, for the SS initial state, the ergodic region shows decay of the imbalance, whereas in the HSF and DFL regions
\overline{\mathcal{I}}(t)
approaches a nonzero DE value, indicating persistent memory of the initial matter pattern.
These results suggest that the model may exhibit DFL in specific parameter regimes, as we show in more detail in the following analysis.

Particle creation.—

To confirm that the observed localization arises from the superposition of different superselection sectors, we must first ensure that the GI system (without background charges) exhibits ergodic behavior.
We define the single-site occupancy operator

\hat{Q}_{n}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\alpha}\quantity[\hpsi^{\dagger}_{\site,\alpha}\hpsi_{\site,\alpha}-\frac{1-(-1)^{\site}}{2}]\,,

(7)

which, in the staggered fermion description
[
136
,
20
,
19
]
counts the number of quarks (on even sites) or antiquarks (on odd sites).
Then, we compute the average quark population
p_{q}(t)
of each
\hat{Q}_{n}
eigenvalue (single-site quark occupancy,
q\in\{0,1,2\}
), projecting on the corresponding eigensubspace of
\hat{Q}_{n}
and averaging over all sites

p_{q}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\expectationvalue{\delta_{\hat{Q}_{\site}, q}}{\Psi(t)}\,,

(8)

where the Kronecker delta
\delta_{\hat{Q}_{n},q}
selects a specific occupancy.
In
Fig.

3

Eq.

4

The ME value, which reflects the thermal prediction, is obtained by uniformly averaging
\matrixelement{\Phi_s}{\delta_{\hat{Q}_{\site},q}}{\Phi_s}
over eigenstates
\ket{\Phi_{s}}
in a narrow energy window around the initial state energy (see SM).
In particular, we focus on the average deviation of the single
p_{1}
and double

\Delta_{p}\equiv\frac{\absolutevalue{p_{1}^\text{DE}-p_{1}^\text{ME}}+\absolutevalue{p_{2}^\text{DE}-p_{2}^\text{ME}}}{2},

In the regime of

To gain deeper insight into the nature of excitations driving the DFL phase observed in

In agreement with our previous analysis, in the GI sector the DE and ME predictions closely follow each other, confirming the thermal behavior of the initial state in
Eq.

Conversely, when the system is initialized in a superposition of SS in this regime, the ME and DE predictions diverge strongly due to the emergence of the previously discussed DFL phase.
As the gauge coupling increases, the dominant contribution of the dynamics comes from the double-occupancy population

This starkly contrasts the ME prediction, which instead anticipates a greater production of meson-like excitations (adjacent quark-antiquark pairs with an excited shared gauge link) as indicated by the single-occupancy population

The crucial role of baryonic excitations is a distinctive feature of the non-Abelian

Entanglement Entropy.—

A hallmark feature of DFL is the slow spreading of quantum correlations across the system, reminiscent of that in a many-body localized (MBL) phase
[
37
,
17
,
157
,
108
,
9
,
71
]
.
Indeed, while in ergodic systems governed by the ETH, quantum correlations spread linearly with time, in MBL phases, entanglement entropy grows logarithmically with time.
This phenomenon can be quantified by measuring the bipartite von Neumann entanglement entropy, defined as
S\!=\!-{\Tr}[\hat{\varrho}_{A}\log\hat{\varrho}_{A}]
, where
A
and
B
denote the two halves of the chain described by the density operator
\hat{\varrho}_{AB}
, and
\hat{\varrho}_{A}\!=\!\Tr_{B}[\hat{\varrho}_{AB}]
is the reduced density operator of subsystem
A
.
In
Fig.

4
, we show the entanglement entropy scaling for the SS initial state across a range of gauge couplings
g^{2}\!\in\![0,15]
at a fixed mass
m\!=\!1
.
Aside from late-time saturation effects due to the finite system size, the SS case exhibits a clear linear entanglement growth in
\log(t)
across almost all the coupling values, compatible with an MBL phase and similar to what was observed in the Abelian scenario
[
17
]
.
In detail, stronger gauge couplings limit the spread of entanglement, leading to lower saturation values.
Indeed, as
g^{2}
increases, gauge-link excitations are strongly penalized, and hopping processes are thereby suppressed.
This constrained dynamics affects the spread of quantum correlations in each superselection sector, including the GI case, where the entanglement entropy scales linearly in time (see SM
[
126
]
), which is compatible with ergodicity.

under periodic boundary conditions is not a product state, as in the Abelian case

, rather, an exact matrix product state with bond dimension
\chi\!=\!4

, thus the entanglement entropy at the initial time starts from
\mathcal{S}\!=\!\log\chi
.

Summary and outlook.—

We have investigated the dynamical phases of a (1+1)D truncated
\mathrm{SU}(2)
LGT with dynamical matter.
To explore the dynamics across different gauge superselection sectors, we developed a formalism based on static
\mathrm{SU}(2)
background charges, enabling the preparation of superpositions across distinct sectors.
Within this framework, we identified three different regimes: in addition to a thermal ergodic phase, we found a nonthermal yet delocalized fragmented regime and a DFL phase that emerges when superpositions of gauge superselection sectors are considered.
In the latter, an initial imbalance in the matter distribution persists over time, and the entanglement entropy grows logarithmically, reminiscent of MBL.
The dynamics in these regimes are strongly shaped by non-Abelian excitations such as baryons.
In the DFL regime, characterized by large gauge coupling and mass, the gauge-field and background-charge truncations are well controlled.
We expect the qualitative features of the dynamics to remain robust under larger truncation cutoffs, as supported by recent results on QMB scars in the same model
[
19
]
.

Several immediate directions follow from our work, such as the stability of the DFL phase in (2+1)D and under different non-Abelian gauge groups.
Another interesting avenue is to analyze the robustness of DFL to coherent error terms that explicitly break the non-Abelian gauge symmetry, which are commonly encountered in quantum simulators.
The effect of decoherence on this phase is also a critical direction worth investigating.

Finally, we point out that a qudit formulation with a 13-dimensional local (single-site) Hilbert space faithfully captures the DFL phenomenology described in this work.
This makes the model particularly suitable for digital quantum simulation on qudit-based quantum processors, as recently proposed for the same model in the absence of background charges
[
20
]
.
Platforms supporting such large local dimensions are already experimentally accessible, as demonstrated by recent implementations using 13-level trapped-ion qudits based on
{}^{137}\textrm{Ba}^{+}
[
91
]
, as well as other qudit architectures employing metastable states of ion isotopes
[
10
]
and circular Rydberg atoms
[
84
,
33
]
.

SII
Hardcore-gluon qudit model with Background charges

To map the Kogut–Susskind
\mathrm{SU}(2)
lattice Yang–Mills Hamiltonian of Eq.(1) to the qudit model used in numerical simulations, we:

1.

perform a hardcore-gluon truncation of the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge fields
[
22
]
;

2.

build the gauge-singlet local dressed basis accounting for the presence of eventual background charges; and

3.

gauge-defermionize.

The procedure is conveniently formulated by decomposing matter and gauging each Hilbert space in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
irreducible representations (irreps).
This preliminary step already appears in the bases introduced in the main text: for gauge links, we adopt the irrep basis
\ket{j,m_{\mathrm{L}},m_{\mathrm{R}}}
; for matter sites, the Fock basis

\{\ket{q}\}=\{{\ket{0}},\,{\ket{{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r}},\ket{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r},{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}\ket{0}},\,{\ket{2}=\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r}}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}\ket{0}}\}
.
Matter basis states are associated with the following spin labels
(j,m)
:

\ket{0}\leftrightarrow(0,0)\,\quad\ket{{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r}},\ket{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}\leftrightarrow(1/2,\pm 1/2)\,\quad\ket{2}\leftrightarrow(0,0)\,.

(S1)

Irrep decomposition specifies how local gauge rotations act on a site
n
and its neighboring links.
The generators of the infinitesimal rotations read
\forall\nu\in\{x,y,z\}
:

\hat{G}^{\nu}_{n}=\hat{R}_{n-1,n}^{\nu}+\hat{Q}_{n}^{\nu}+\hat{L}_{n,n+1}^{\nu}\,,

(S2)

where

\hat{Q}^{\nu}_{n}{=}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{n,\alpha}S^{(1/2)\nu}_{\alpha\beta}\hat{\psi}_{n,\beta}

rotates the quark field at
n
, while
\hat{R}_{n-1,n}^{\nu}
and
\hat{L}^{\nu}_{n,n+1}
account for the transformation of the gauge links at its left and right
[
159
]
:

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\spin^{\prime} \mL^{\prime} \mR^{\prime}}{\hat{L}^{\nu}}{ j \mL\mR}

\displaystyle=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}S^{(j)\nu}_{m_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime},m_{\mathrm{L}}}\delta_{m_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime},m_{\mathrm{R}}}\,,

(S3)

\displaystyle\matrixelement{\spin^{\prime} \mL^{\prime} \mR^{\prime} }{\hat{R}^{\nu}}{j \mL\mR}

\displaystyle=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime},m_{\mathrm{L}}}S^{(j)\nu}_{m_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime},m_{\mathrm{R}}}\,;

(S4)

where
S^{(j)\nu}
are the spin-
j

\mathfrak{su}(2)
matrices.
From these operators, we can build the chromoelectric energy operator

\hat{E}^{2}=\sum_{\nu}(\hat{R}^{\nu})^{2}=\sum_{\nu}(\hat{L}^{\nu})^{2}
,
i.e., quadratic Casimir
[
159
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{E}^{2}\ket{jm_{\mathrm{L}}m_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=j(j+1)\ket{jm_{\mathrm{L}}m_{\mathrm{R}}}\,.

(S5)

Correspondingly, the action of the parallel transporter is given in terms of Clebsch-Gordan coefficients
[
158
]
:

\matrixelement{\spin^{\prime}\mL^{\prime}\mR^{\prime}}{\hat{U}^{\alpha\beta}}{\spin\mL\mR}=\sqrt{\frac{2j+1}{2j^{\mathrlap{\prime}}+1}}\>\overline{C^{j,m_{\mathrm{L}}}_{\hskip-0.7pt{\frac{1}{2}}\hskip-0.7pt,\alpha;\,j^{\prime},m_{\mathrm{L}}^{\prime}}}C^{j^{\prime},m_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}}_{\hskip-0.7pt{\frac{1}{2}}\hskip-0.7pt,\beta;\,j,m_{\mathrm{R}}}.

(S6)

Then, at each site
n
, the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
Gauss law reads:

\hat{G}_{n}^{\nu}\ket{\Psi_{\quantity{b^{\kappa}_{n}}}}=b_{n}^{\nu,\kappa}\ket{\Psi_{\quantity{\vb{b}^{\kappa}_{\site}}}},\;\forall n,\nu,\kappa,

(S7)

where
b_{n}^{\nu,\kappa}
are the static background-charges present on the lattice site
n
of a QMB state with a specific
\kappa

super-selection sector
, i.e., a specific lattice configuration of background charges
\quantity[b^{\kappa}_{1}\dots b^{\kappa}_{N}]
(where for simplicity we omit the spin-component index
\nu
).

SII.1
Hardcore-gluon and background charge truncations

In principle, the gauge field and the static background charges could occupy arbitrarily high spin shells.
To deal with a finite gauge-link Hilbert space, we perform the
hardcore-gluon
approximation, which bounds
j\in\{0,1/2\}
and yields an energy cutoff
\norm*{\hat{E}^2}\leq 3/4
on the Casimir spectrum (
\norm{\:\cdot\:}
denotes the matrix norm)
[
22
,
20
,
19
]
.
A similar truncation is performed on the irreps of the static background charges
(j_{b},m_{b})
where
j_{b}\in\{0,1/2\}
.

Recovering the untruncated gauge group is
not
essential for the present investigation.
The hardcore-gluon approximation faithfully captures the relevant low-energy physics in the strong-coupling regime
g_{0}\gg 1
, where the chromoelectric energy dominates the Hamiltonian.
Remarkably, this is precisely the regime where the distinctive DFL behavior emerges.
Similarly, restricting the background charges to the lowest two spin irreps is already sufficient to resolve the onset and structure of DFL.

\hat{L}^{\nu}
and
\hat{R}^{\nu}

m_{\mathrm{R}}

We can then factorize each gauge link into a pair of new rishon degrees of freedom that live at its edges
[

The basis states of each rishon mode are labeled as

\ket{0}\!=\!\ket{j\,{=}\,0,m\,{=}\,0}

SII.3
Gauge defermionization

The dressed-site formalism introduced previously is equivalent to the original LGT description, provided that it recovers the original physical space.
Namely, it requires that the left and right rishons on each link must be in the same spin shell
j
by restricting to the even parity sector of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, whose action reads:

\hat{P}=+\outerproduct{0}{0}-(\outerproduct{\rla}{\rla}+\outerproduct{\gla}{\gla})\,.

(S10)

Correspondingly, the parallel transporter in
Eq.

S6
can be written as:

\hat{U}^{\alpha\beta}_{n,n+1}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{\zeta}^{\mathrm{(L)}\alpha}_{n,n+1}(\hat{\zeta}^{\mathrm{(R)}\beta}_{n,n+1})^{\dagger}\,,

(S11)

where the rishon modes read:

\hat{\zeta}^{{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r}}=\outerproduct{0}{\rla}+\outerproduct{\gla}{0}\,,\quad\hat{\zeta}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}=\outerproduct{0}{\gla}-\outerproduct{\rla}{0}\,.

(S12)

Observe that the right-hand side of
Eq.

S11
preserves the link parity in
Eq.

S10
, as desired.
Conversely, a single rishon operator always inverts it:

\anticommutator*{\hat{P}}{\rishon^{\alpha}}=0
.
The existence of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-symmetry in the rishon spaces allows to gauge-defermionize the model
[
22
,
7
]
:
we take
\hat{\zeta}^{\alpha}
to be fermionic and rewrite the Hamiltonian in Eq.(1) in terms of the following bosonic operators:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{n}^{\mathrm{(L,R)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}(\hat{\zeta}^{\mathrm{(L,R)}\alpha}_{n,n+1})^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{n,\alpha}\,,

(S13)

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\psi}_{n,\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{n,\alpha},

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\nu}\quantity[\hat{R}^{\nu,2}_{\site-1, \site}+\hat{L}^{\nu,2}_{\site, \site+1}]\,.

For potential quantum simulation implementations, it is convenient to rewrite these solely in terms of Hermitian operators, introducing
[
20
]
:

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\mathbin{=}\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(L)}}\mathop{+}\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(L)}\dagger}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\mathbin{=}\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(R)}}\mathop{+}\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(R)}\dagger}\,,

(S14)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\mathbin{=}i\big[\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(L)}}\mathop{-}\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(L)}\dagger}\big]\,,

\displaystyle\hat{B}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\mathbin{=}i\big[\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(R)}}\mathop{-}\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{(R)}\dagger}\big]\,.

Finally, we map Eq.(1) to the following 13-dimensional (6 with no background charge + 7 with finite background charge) qudit Hamiltonian
[
20
]
:

\hat{H}=\sum_{n,p}\hat{A}^{(p)}_{n}\hat{B}^{(p)}_{n+1}+m\sum_{n}(-1)^{n}\hat{M}_{n}+g^{2}\sum_{n}\hat{C}_{n}\,,

(S15)

where we rescaled energy to absorb the hopping pre-factor,
\hat{H}=4\sqrt{2}a_{0}\hat{H}_{0}
, and defined
the dimensionless couplings
m=4\sqrt{2}a_{0}m_{0}
, and
g^{2}=\frac{3\sqrt{2}}{4}a_{0}g_{0}^{2}
.

SII.4
Particle populations

Within this dressed-site basis, we can still easily measure various particle-density operators, which yield information about the physical processes occurring during the dynamics.
In particular, we can define:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[2]}

\displaystyle=\hat{\psi}_{n,{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r}}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{n,{\color[rgb]{0.8359,0.2422,0.3086}r}}\hat{\psi}_{n,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{n,{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}g}}

(S16a)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[1]}

\displaystyle=\hat{M}_{n}-2\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[2]}

(S16b)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[0]}

\displaystyle=1-\hat{\rho}^{[1]}_{n}-\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[2]},

(S16c)

where
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[0]}
,
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[1]}
, and
\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[2]}
measure the single-site zero, one, and double occupancy respectively.
Correspondingly, within the adopted staggered fermion solution
[
136
]
, these single-site observables allow us to compute true-particle quantities like the average (anti-)baryon, meson, and vacuum densities defined in Eq.(8) of the main text, which explicitly read:

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\quantity[
\frac{1+(-1)^{\site}}{2}\hat{\rho}_{\site}^{[2]}+
\frac{1-(-1)^{\site}}{2}\hat{\rho}_{\site}^{[0]}],

(S17a)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\hat{\rho}_{n}^{[1]},

(S17b)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\quantity[
\frac{1-(-1)^{\site}}{2}\hat{\rho}_{\site}^{[2]}+
\frac{1+(-1)^{\site}}{2}\hat{\rho}_{\site}^{[0]}].

(S17c)

SIII
Initial state candidates for dynamics

To observe DFL, we start the dynamics with an equal superposition of all the possible configurations of single-site background charges, i.e., a superposition of all the
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{SS}}

superselection sectors

\quantity{b^{\kappa}_{n}}=\quantity[b^{\kappa}_{1}\dots b^{\kappa}_{N}]_{\kappa=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{SS}}}
, as in Eq.(3).
The explicit construction of candidates for initial states within the proposed dressed-site formalism requires that the left- and right-gauge irreps of each link share the same Casimir, as imposed in
Eq.

S10
.
Dealing with staggered fermions
[
136
]
, finite mass coupling values
m
will favor configurations where even and odd sites host a complementary state of matter fields (vacuum and pair) respectively.
Therefore, good candidates for the initial state consist of staggered matter configurations where blocks of
x
empty (e) sites (
e_{1},\!\dots,e_{x}
) alternate with blocks of
x
doubly occupied (o) sites (
o_{1},\dots,o_{x}
).
These states have an initial matter imbalance, defined in Eq.(5), equal to 1, and their time evolution allows localization to be detected.

Practically, empty lattice sites are in a superposition of the states
\ket{1}
,
\ket{2}
,
\ket{7}
, and
\ket{8}
of
Eqs.

S8
and
S9
without matter fields.
Correspondingly, as for doubly occupied sites, we consider a superposition of the states
\ket{5}
,
\ket{6}
,
\ket{12}
, and
\ket{13}
of
Eqs.

S8
and
S9
.
According to the link symmetries in
Eq.

S10
, we discard all superpositions in which neighboring site states do not share the same gauge irrep on the attached semi-links.
Any staggered initial (BG) state is then constructed with the following rules:

\begin{split}\ket{1}_{e_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{1}_{e_{2}}+\ket{7}_{e_{2}}]\dots\ket{1}_{e_{x}}\otimes[\ket{5}_{o_{1}}+\ket{12}_{o_{1}}]\\
\ket{2}_{e_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{2}_{e_{2}}+\ket{8}_{e_{2}}]\dots\ket{2}_{e_{x}}\otimes[\ket{6}_{o_{1}}+\ket{13}_{o_{1}}]\\
\ket{7}_{e_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{2}_{e_{2}}+\ket{8}_{e_{2}}]\dots\ket{7}_{e_{x}}\otimes[\ket{6}_{o_{1}}+\ket{13}_{o_{1}}]\\
\ket{8}_{e_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{1}_{e_{2}}+\ket{7}_{e_{2}}]\dots\ket{8}_{e_{x}}\otimes[\ket{5}_{o_{1}}+\ket{12}_{o_{1}}]\\
\\
\ket{5}_{o_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{5}_{o_{2}}+\ket{12}_{o_{2}}]\dots\ket{5}_{o_{x}}\otimes[\ket{1}_{e_{1}}+\ket{7}_{e_{1}}]\\
\ket{6}_{o_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{6}_{o_{2}}+\ket{13}_{o_{2}}]\dots\ket{6}_{o_{x}}\otimes[\ket{2}_{e_{1}}+\ket{8}_{e_{1}}]\\
\ket{12}_{o_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{6}_{o_{2}}+\ket{13}_{o_{2}}]\dots\ket{12}_{o_{x}}\otimes[\ket{2}_{e_{1}}+\ket{8}_{e_{1}}]\\
\ket{13}_{o_{1}}&\otimes[\ket{5}_{o_{2}}+\ket{12}_{o_{2}}]\dots\ket{13}_{o_{x}}\otimes[\ket{1}_{e_{1}}+\ket{7}_{e_{1}}]\end{split}

(S18)

The simplest nontrivial staggered superposition of
superselection sectors
corresponds to
x=2
, where matter sites are in a sequence of two fully
empty
(
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
) and two fully
occupied
sites (
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
).
Such a configuration corresponds to the state defined in Eq.(3) and is used in all numerical simulations discussed in the main text.

Similar results can be obtained by choosing
x
as any exact divisor of
N/2

e_{1}\dots e_{N/2}
) and the other one with fully occupied sites (
o_{N/2+1}\dots o_{N}

The main challenge of the latter state is its long relaxation timescale, which may strain numerical resources.

SIII.1
MPS representation of the initial state

In exact diagonalization, for an
N

4\cdot 2^{N-1}
lattice configurations compatible with
Eqs.

S8
and
S9

Correspondingly, the same superposition can be written as an exact matrix product state (MPS) of the form

80

81

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i_{1},..i_{N}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[A^{(i_{1})}_{1}A^{(i_{2})}_{2}...A^{(i_{N})}_{N}\right]\ket{i_{1},i_{2},..i_{N}},

(S19)

where each matrix

\chi_{k-1}{\times}\chi_{k}
apart from the two edges,
\chi_{1}=1
and
\chi_{N}=1

\ket{8}&\ket{2}&0&0\\

(S21)

The two edges (

\ket{12}\\
\ket{13}\\
\ket{6}\end{pmatrix}\quad\quad A^{(i_{1(N)})}_{\rm e}=\begin{pmatrix}\ket{1}\\
\ket{8}\\
\ket{7}\\
\ket{2}\end{pmatrix}.

SV
Thermal and long-time predictions

To clearly distinguish between the thermal and nonthermal behavior of the model under consideration, we compare the very long-time prediction with the one from the microcanonical ensemble.
For a unitary dynamics of a generic initial state expressed in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis,
\ket{\Psi(0)}=\sum_{s}C_{s}\ket{\Phi_{s}}
, the long-time behavior of a generic observable
\hat{O}
corresponds to the expectation value predicted by the
diagonal
ensemble (DE)
[
113
]
:

\begin{split}\bar{O}&\equiv\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\braket{\Psi(t)|\hat{O}|\Psi(t)}\\
&=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\sum_{s,p}C^{*}_{s}C_{p}\braket{\Phi_{s}|e^{it\hat{H}}\hat{O}e^{-it\hat{H}}|\Phi_{p}}\\
&=\sum_{s,p}C^{*}_{s}C_{p}\braket{\Phi_{s}|\hat{O}|\Phi_{p}}\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}e^{i(E_{s}-E_{p})t}dt.\end{split}

(S24)

If the Hamiltonian has no degeneracies, the oscillatory phases
e^{i(E_{s}-E_{p})t}
dephase for
s\neq p
, yielding:

\bar{O}=\sum_{s}\absolutevalue{C_{s}}^{2}\braket{\Phi_{s}|\hat{O}|\Phi_{s}}\equiv\Tr\{\hat{O}\,\hat{\varrho}_{\rm DE}\},

(S25)

where
\hat{\varrho}_{DE}=\sum_{s}\absolutevalue{C_s}^{2}\outerproduct{\Phi_s}{\Phi_s}
.
If instead the spectrum contains degenerate energy subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
with projectors
\hat{P}_{\alpha}
, the long-time average retains all matrix elements within each degenerate block:

\bar{O}=\sum_{\alpha}\Tr\left(\hat{P}_{\alpha}\,\outerproduct{\Psi(0)}{\Psi(0)}\hat{P}_{\alpha}\right)\hat{O}.

(S26)

Correspondingly, the DE becomes block-diagonal

\hat{\varrho}_{\rm DE}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{P}_{\alpha},\outerproduct{\Psi(0)}{\Psi(0)}\hat{P}_{\alpha},

(S27)

and reduces to the usual diagonal form only when all energies are non-degenerate.

In any case, if the expectation value of the observable
\braket{\hat{O}(t)}
approaches the long-time average
\braket{\hat{O}}_{DE}
over long times, the system
equilibrates
.

Correspondingly, the expected thermal behavior can be computed with the microcanonical ensemble average (ME)

\braket{\hat{O}}_{\rm{ME}}=\Tr\{\hat{O}\hat{\varrho}_{\rm{ME}}\}\,,

(S28)

where the microcanonical state is defined as a superposition within the energy shell
[E_{\text{q}}\!-\!\delta E,E_{\text{q}}\!+\!\delta E]
, with the initial-state energy
E_{\text{q}}\!=\!\bra{\Psi(0)}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi(0)}
, containing
N_{E,\delta E}
eigenstates:

\hat{\varrho}_{\rm{ME}}=\frac{1}{N_{E,\delta E}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s;\\
|E_{s}-E_{\text{q}}|<\delta E\end{subarray}}\outerproduct{\Phi_s}{\Phi_s}\,.

(S29)

Whenever the ME and DE predictions of generic observables coincide, the system, quenched on the chosen initial state,
thermalizes
.
Conversely, it exhibits nonthermal behavior due to Hilbert-space fragmentation, scarring dynamics, or other exotic phenomena.

In the superposition of superselection sectors in Eq.(2), DE and ME predictions of the imbalance in Eq.(5) (and any other observable) are obtained as classical averages of the DE and ME values of each background-charge configuration.
Namely, we have:

\braket{\hat{O}}_{\rm{ME/DE}}^{\rm{BG}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{ss}}\sum_{\kappa=1}^{\mathcal{N}_{ss}}\braket{\hat{O}}_{\rm{ME/DE}}^{\quantity[b^{\kappa}_{1}\dots b^{\kappa}_{N}]}

(S30)

SVIII
Exact diagonalization methods

All numerical simulations presented in this work are performed using an Exact Diagonalization code
[
23
,
21
]
that exploits the model’s main symmetries.
Specifically, the Hamiltonian in Eq.(1) under PBC has the following symmetries:
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-link symmetries (related to
Eq.

S10
and resulting from the previously discussed dressed-site formalism
[
22
,
20
,
19
]
), which we resolve everywhere to satisfy SU(2) Gauss law; particle-number conservation, which we resolve by selecting the half-filling sector, corresponding to zero-baryon number density
\hat{N}_{b}\!=\!\hat{p}_{1}\!+\!2\hat{p}_{2}\!=\!0
; the momentum-symmetry, which we do not resolve as the initial SS and GI states in Eqs.(3) and (4) do not belong to a single sector; the spatial inversion symmetry, which maps each site
n
to its inverted partner
n^{\prime}\!=\!(2n_{0}\!-\!n)\!\!\mod\!N
(site-centered) or
n^{\prime}\!=\!(2n_{0}\!+\!1\!-\!n)\!\!\mod\!N
.
If left unresolved, this invariance introduces degeneracy between positive-
k
and negative-
k
momentum states.
However, since our initial states belong to the
+1
sector of the inversion parity, projecting the Hamiltonian onto this sector guarantees no degeneracy in the spectrum and allows us to compute the DE average using
Eq.

S25
.
Ultimately, thanks to the Gauss Law, we can independently access each superselection sector
\quantity{b^{\kappa}_{n}}=\quantity[b^{\kappa}_{1}\dots b^{\kappa}_{N}]
and recover the expectation values of the observables in Eq.(8) by averaging over the sectors as done in
Eq.

S30
.
This is impossible when measuring the entanglement entropy discussed in FIG.4, since this quantity is not extensive across sectors.
A correct evaluation of the entropy requires simulating the initial state as a superposition over different superselection sectors, whose Hilbert space grows exponentially,
\sim 13^{N}
.
This prevents the system size from increasing and the finite-size scaling analysis from being performed via ED
[
23
]
.

As discussed earlier, one could alternatively encode the initial state as an MPS using tensor network methods.
Unfortunately, since our study aims to investigate ergodicity breaking phenomena, which require access to the thermal and very long time dynamics of the model where entanglement increases, MPS algorithms such as TEBD (Time-Evolving Block Decimation)
[
140
]
and TDVP (Time-Dependent Variational Principle)
[
61
]
are already inefficient at intermediate system sizes, such as
N=8
, where exact diagonalization methods can instead efficiently access the full energy spectrum to investigate the onset of fragmentation.
