Kummer–Artin–Schreier–Witt Theory
Huy Dang, Khai-Hoan Nguyen-Dang

Abstract
We study the problem of lifting the Artin–Schreier–Witt isogeny from characteristic p > 0 p>0 to characteristic 0 0 , which is central to the lifting problem for Galois covers of algebraic schemes in positive characteristic. We introduce a new technique that associates a Kummer class, representing a tamely ramified cyclic extension, to a Witt vector via Matsuda’s Kummer–Artin–Schreier–Witt theory. This viewpoint leads to an explicit construction of a lift of the isogeny over a concrete base ring. Our results lay the groundwork for further applications, including the study of inseparable extensions and Kato’s refined Swan conductor.

14H30, 14H10, 11S15

Background

p
; Kummer theory, which describes tamely ramified cyclic extensions; and Matsuda’s Kummer–Artin–Schreier–Witt theory, which provides a unifying framework in mixed characteristic
(0,p)

2.1
Artin–Schreier–Witt theory

Suppose
k
is a field of characteristic
p
. Then we have the short exact sequence

\begin{split}0\xlongrightarrow{}\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}\cong W_{s}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\xlongrightarrow{}W_{s}&\xlongrightarrow{\wp}W_{s}\longrightarrow 0,\\
\underline{y}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{s-1})&\longmapsto\underline{y}-\sigma(\underline{y}),\end{split}

(8)

of group schemes over
\overline{k}

\cohom^{1}(k,\mathbb{Z}/p^{s})\cong W_{s}(k)/\wp(W_{s}(k)).

We denote by
\mathfrak{K}_{s}(\underline{x})

\cohom^{1}(k,\mathbb{Z}/p^{s})

\underline{x}\in W_{s}(k)

One may view

in (

-extension of the function field

associated with the Witt vector
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})

corresponds to a character

-subextension corresponding to the Witt vector

2.2
Kummer Theory

Fix

. Suppose

contains a primitive
m

\cohom^{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}/m)\cong K^{\times}/(K^{\times})^{m}.

This follows from the Kummer exact sequence over

0\longrightarrow\mu_{m}\longrightarrow\overline{K}^{\times}\xlongrightarrow{(\cdot)^{m}}\overline{K}^{\times}\longrightarrow 0.

We now specialize to the case

\chi\in\cohom^{1}(K,\mathbb{Z}/p^{s})
corresponds to an element
G\in K^{\times}/(K^{\times})^{p^{s}}

Proposition 2.1
.

Every class
[G]\in K^{\times}/(K^{\times})^{p^{s}}
has a representative of the form

G\equiv\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}G_{i}^{p^{i}}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod}\ (K^{\times})^{p^{s}}\big),

with each
G_{i}\in K^{\times}\setminus(K^{\times})^{p}
or
G_{i}=1
.

Proof.

Choose a set
S\subset K^{\times}
of representatives for

K^{\times}/(K^{\times})^{p}
such that
1\in S
and, for every
x\in S\setminus\{1\}
,
we have
x\notin(K^{\times})^{p}
.

If
s=1
, then any
G
is congruent modulo
(K^{\times})^{p}

to some
G_{0}\in S
, so
G\equiv G_{0}^{p^{0}}
.

Now suppose the statement holds for
s-1\geq 1
, i.e.,
s\geq 2
.
Take
g\in K^{\times}
representing
[G]\in K^{\times}/(K^{\times})^{p^{s}}
.
Choose
G_{0}\in S
such that
g\in G_{0}(K^{\times})^{p}
, i.e.

g=G_{0}a^{p},\qquad a\in K^{\times}.

By the induction hypothesis, there exist
H_{0},\dots,H_{s-2}\in S
with

a\equiv\prod_{i=0}^{s-2}H_{i}^{p^{i}}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod}\ (K^{\times})^{p^{s-1}}\big).

Raising this congruence to the
p
-th power and multiplying by

, we obtain

g\equiv G_{0}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{s-2}H_{i}^{p^{i}}\right)^{p}=G_{0}\prod_{i=0}^{s-2}H_{i}^{p^{i+1}}=\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}G_{i}^{p^{i}}\quad\big(\mathrm{mod}\ (K^{\times})^{p^{s}}\big),

where
G_{0}
is as chosen and
G_{i}:=H_{i-1}
for
i\geq 1
.
Each
G_{i}\in S
, hence either
1
or not a
p
-th power.
This completes the induction.
∎

\displaystyle(K^{\times})^{\oplus s}\big/\phi_{s}\big((K^{\times})^{\oplus s}\big)

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\sim}

\displaystyle\;K^{\times}\big/\big(K^{\times}\big)^{p^{s}}

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\sim}\cohom^{1}\big(K,\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}\big),

(10)

\displaystyle(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\;[a_{1}a_{2}^{p}\cdots a_{s}^{p^{s-1}}]

2.3.1
Deformed Artin–Hasse exponential function

We introduce a deformation of the Artin–Hasse exponential function and record basic properties.

Definition 2.3
.

The
Artin–Hasse exponential series at
p
is the formal power series

E_{p}(t):=\exp\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{p^{i}}}{p^{i}}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}\llbracket t\rrbracket.

Define

E_{s,p}(t):=\frac{E_{p}(\zeta_{p^{s}}t)}{E_{p}(t)}=\exp\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}(\zeta_{p^{s-i}}-1)\frac{t^{p^{i}}}{p^{i}}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket t\rrbracket,

(12)

and for
\underline{a}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{s-1})
,

E_{s,p}(\underline{a}):=\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}E_{s-i,p}(a_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket a_{0},\ldots,a_{s-1}\rrbracket,

(13)

which can be rewritten as

E_{s,p}(\underline{a})=\exp\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}(\zeta_{p^{s-i}}-1)\frac{w_{i}}{p^{i}}\right),

(14)

where
w_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{i}p^{j}a_{j}^{p^{i-j}}
is the
i
th ghost component of
\underline{a}
.

It follows from (
14
) that

E_{s,p}(\underline{a})E_{s,p}(\underline{b})=E_{s,p}(\underline{a}+\underline{b}).

(15)

Definition 2.4
.

Define

G_{s,p}(\underline{a}):=\frac{E_{s,p}(p\underline{a})}{E_{s-1,p}(\underline{a})}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket a_{0},\dots,a_{s-1}\rrbracket.

When
s=1
, we interpret
E_{0,p}=1
, so
G_{1,p}((a_{0}))=E_{1,p}(pa_{0})
.

By (
15
), we have

G_{s,p}(\underline{a})G_{s,p}(\underline{b})=G_{s,p}(\underline{a}+\underline{b}).

(16)

Lemma 2.5
.

We have

G_{s,p}(\underline{a})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)a_{0},\dots,(\zeta_{p}-1)a_{s-1}\rrbracket.

Proof.

Using the ghost-component form of
E_{m,p}
and the definition

G_{s,p}(\underline{a})=E_{s,p}(p\underline{a})E_{s-1,p}(\underline{a})^{-1}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle G_{s,p}(\underline{a})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\exp\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s-j-1}\Big(p(\zeta_{p^{s-j-i}}-1)-(\zeta_{p^{s-j-1-i}}-1)\Big)\frac{a_{j}^{p^{i}}}{p^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\exp\left(\sum_{i=0}^{s-j-1}(\zeta_{p^{s-j-i}}-1)\Big(p-(1+\zeta_{p^{s-j-i}}+\cdots+\zeta_{p^{s-j-i}}^{p-1})\Big)\frac{a_{j}^{p^{i}}}{p^{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}E_{s-j,p}\left(\Big(p-1-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]^{2}-\cdots-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]^{p-1}\Big)[a_{j}]\right),

where
[\,\cdot\,]
denotes the Teichmüller lift (in the Witt vectors of the appropriate length). Reducing modulo the ideal
(\zeta_{p^{s-j}}-1)
gives

[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]\equiv 1
, hence

p-1-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]-\cdots-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]^{p-1}\equiv p-p=0.

By the standard identity

\ker\big(W_{s-j}(A)\to W_{s-j}(A/I)\big)=W_{s-j}(I),

valid for any ring
A
and ideal
I\subset A
,

\ker\big(W_{s-j}(A)\to W_{s-j}(A/I)\big)=W_{s-j}(I)
, applied with

A=\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]
and
I=(\zeta_{p^{s-j}}-1)
, it follows that

p-1-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]-\cdots-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]^{p-1}\in W_{s-j}\big((\zeta_{p^{s-j}}-1)\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]\big).

Therefore the Witt vector

\Big(p-1-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]-\cdots-[\zeta_{p^{s-j}}]^{p-1}\Big)[a_{j}]

lies in the same Witt ideal, and the corresponding Artin–Hasse factor

E_{s-j,p}(\cdot)
is a power series in the single variable

(\zeta_{p^{s-j}}-1)a_{j}
with coefficients in

\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]
. Taking the product over
j=0,\dots,s-1

gives the stated inclusion.
∎

is defined as the ring of Laurent series that converge in some annulus
\{z\mid\rho<\lvert z\rvert<1\}
for some
0<\rho<1
. Let
\mathcal{R}^{\rm bd}
be the subring of
\mathcal{R}
consisting of formal sums with bounded coefficients. The ring
\mathcal{R}^{\rm bd}
carries a Gauss norm:

\left\lvert\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i}z^{i}\right\rvert=\max_{i}\{\lvert a_{i}\rvert\}.

Let
\mathcal{R}^{\integral}
be the subring of
\mathcal{R}^{\rm bd}
consisting of elements with Gauss norm at most
1
.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
14
, Proposition 3.2]
)
.

The ring
\mathcal{R}^{\integral}
is a Henselian discrete valuation ring. Consequently,
\mathcal{R}^{\rm bd}
is a Henselian local field with residue field
\kappa:=k\mathopen{((}z\mathclose{))}
.

Therefore, there is a canonical restriction map

\cohom^{1}(\kappa,\mathbb{Z}/p^{s})\longrightarrow\cohom^{1}(\mathcal{R}^{\rm bd},\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}).

(17)

Theorem 2.8

(
[
14
, Theorem 3.8]
)
.

The homomorphism in (
17
) is induced by a homomorphism

\displaystyle W_{s}(\mathcal{R}^{\integral})

\displaystyle\xlongrightarrow{\Psi_{s}}(\mathcal{R}^{\integral})^{\times},

(18)

\displaystyle\underline{a}

\displaystyle\mapsto E_{s,p}(p^{s}\underline{a}).

The homomorphism (
18
) establishes a link between Kummer and Artin–Schreier–Witt theories by associating to a Witt vector
\underline{a}
the Kummer class
E_{s,p}(p^{s}\underline{a})
. Our strategy in this paper is to define the cover using Kummer theory while using an associated Witt vector to prove that the cover satisfies the desired conditions.

3
Constructing the Kummer extension

3.1
The Witt Vector Associated to a Kummer Extension

Recall that any
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-cover of

\mathbb{P}^{s}_{R}=\operatorname{Proj}R[X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1},Y]
can be described by a Kummer equation of the form

Z_{s-1}^{p^{s}}=\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}(u_{i})^{p^{i}}=:u\in K(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}),

(19)

where
K
is the fraction field of
R
and
k
is the residue field of
K
.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a Henselian local field
\mathbb{K}
with residue field
k((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1}))
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
s\geq 1
. Here
k((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1}))
denotes the iterated Laurent series field

k((x_{0}))\cdots((x_{s-1}))
.

For
s=1
, Lemma
2.7
applied with base field
K
and Robba variable
X_{0}
shows that

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{K}(X_{0})
is a Henselian DVR with residue field
k((x_{0}))
. Its fraction field

\mathbb{K}_{1}:=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{bd}}_{K}(X_{0})

is therefore a Henselian local field with residue
k((x_{0}))
.

Assume the statement holds for
s-1
, so there exists a Henselian local field
\mathbb{K}_{s-1}
with residue field
k((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-2}))
. Applying Lemma
2.7
again, now with base field
\mathbb{K}_{s-1}
and a new Robba variable
X_{s-1}
, we find that

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}(X_{s-1})
is a Henselian DVR whose residue field is

\mathrm{res}\!\left(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}(X_{s-1})\right)=\mathrm{res}(\mathbb{K}_{s-1})((x_{s-1}))=k((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-2}))((x_{s-1}))=k((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})).

Its fraction field

\mathbb{K}_{s}:=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{bd}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}(X_{s-1})

is then a Henselian local field with this residue.

By induction, the claim follows for all
s\geq 1
, with
\mathbb{K}:=\mathbb{K}_{s}
.
∎

For the remainder of this section, we set
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{bd}}=\mathbb{K}
as in Proposition
3.1
. Its associated Henselian DVR is
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}(X_{s-1})
, as in the proof of the same proposition, and its residue field is
k((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1}))
.

Lemma 3.2
.

With the notation of Proposition
3.1
, there is a canonical injective ring homomorphism

R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket\hookrightarrow\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}(X_{s-1})=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}.

In particular, any series in
R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket
may be viewed as an element of the Henselian DVR

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
s
.

If
s=1
, then
R\llbracket X_{0}\rrbracket\subset\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{K}(X_{0})
because any series

f(X_{0})=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}X_{0}^{n}
with
a_{n}\in R
has Gauss norm

\max_{n}|a_{n}|\leq 1
, hence lies in the integral Robba ring.
Injectivity follows because the Robba variable
X_{0}
is transcendental over
K
.

Assume
s>1
and that we already have a canonical embedding

R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2}\rrbracket\hookrightarrow\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-2}}(X_{s-2})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}
.
Now take

f\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket
and write it uniquely as a power series in
X_{s-1}
,

f=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}\,X_{s-1}^{n},\qquad a_{n}\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2}\rrbracket.

Via the inductive embedding, each coefficient
a_{n}
defines an element of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}
.
Hence the same formula defines an element of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}\llbracket X_{s-1}\rrbracket
,
which is contained in
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}_{\mathbb{K}_{s-1}}(X_{s-1})
by the same Gauss-norm argument as for
s=1
.
This gives the desired map; injectivity again follows from the transcendence of
X_{s-1}
over
\mathbb{K}_{s-1}
.
∎

To construct the isogeny
\mathcal{W}_{s}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}
in (
2
), which is generically an endomorphism of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, we consider a
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-cover of
\mathbb{P}^{s}_{R}
defined by a Kummer equation of the form (
19
). We require that this cover has good reduction and that its special fiber is a
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-cover of
\mathbb{P}^{s}_{k}
associated with the Witt vector
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})
. Assume that the cover has separable reduction, and let
\underline{X}^{\prime}=(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\in W_{s}(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}})
be a Witt vector such that
\Psi_{s}(\underline{X}^{\prime})=u
, where
\Psi_{s}
is Matsuda’s morphism (
18
). Our task is to prove the existence of such a
u
for which, for each
i=0,1,\ldots,s-1
, the component
X_{i}^{\prime}
is congruent to
x_{i}
modulo the maximal ideal of
R
.

The proof of the following result is straightforward from the definition of
E_{i,p}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that
m<s
. Then

E_{s,p}((0,\ldots,0,X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{m-1}^{\prime}))=E_{m,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{m-1}^{\prime})).

Proposition 3.4
.

Recall that
\lambda:=\zeta_{p}-1
. With the above setting, we can compute the entries
X_{i}^{\prime}
of an associated Witt vector inductively as follows:

X_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(u_{0}),\quad X_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{u_{1}}{G_{2,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},0))}\right),\ldots,\quad X_{s-1}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{u_{s-1}}{G_{s,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}\right),

provided the
\ln
function is well-defined.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
s
. Suppose first that
s=1
. Then it is necessary that
\Psi_{1}(X_{0}^{\prime})=\exp(p\lambda X_{0}^{\prime})=u_{0}
. Therefore,
X_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(u_{0})
, as expected.

Assume the induction hypothesis for
s-1
. That is, one can find
X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime}
such that

E_{s-1,p}(p^{s-1}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime}))=\prod_{i=0}^{s-2}(u_{i})^{p^{i}}.

We want to find
X_{s-1}^{\prime}
such that

\begin{split}\Psi_{s}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}))=E_{s,p}(p^{s}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}))&=\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}(u_{i})^{p^{i}}\\
&=E_{s-1,p}(p^{s-1}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime}))(u_{s-1})^{p^{s-1}}.\end{split}

By (
14
), for any Witt vector
\underline{a}
of length
m
and any
N\geq 0
, we have

E_{m,p}(p^{N}\underline{a})=E_{m,p}(\underline{a})^{p^{N}}.

Hence

E_{s,p}(p^{s}\underline{X}^{\prime})=E_{s,p}(p\underline{X}^{\prime})^{p^{s-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad E_{s-1,p}(p^{s-1}\underline{X}^{\prime}_{<s-1})=E_{s-1,p}(\underline{X}^{\prime}_{<s-1})^{p^{s-1}}.

Therefore, to produce a solution at level
p^{s}
, it suffices to solve

\begin{split}E_{s,p}(p(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}))&=E_{s-1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime})u_{s-1},\\
E_{s,p}(p(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))E_{s,p}(p(0,\ldots,0,X_{s-1}^{\prime}))&=E_{s-1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime})u_{s-1}.\end{split}

The second equality follows from the decomposition

(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})=(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0)+(0,\ldots,0,X_{s-1}^{\prime})

and the commutativity of
E_{s,p}
in (
15
). By Proposition
3.3
, we have

E_{s,p}(p(0,\ldots,0,X_{s-1}^{\prime}))=E_{1,p}(p(X_{s-1}^{\prime})).

Therefore,

\exp(p\lambda X_{s-1}^{\prime})=u_{s-1}\frac{E_{s-1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime}))}{E_{s,p}(p(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}=\frac{u_{s-1}}{G_{s,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}.

Thus,

X_{s-1}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{u_{s-1}}{G_{s,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}\right),

which completes the induction.
∎

3.2
Constructing the Kummer Extension

Assume further that

u_{i}:=G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})+p\lambda X_{i},

(20)

for some
G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i+1}}][X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}]
. Set

G^{\prime}_{i+1}:=G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))-G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}),

where
X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime}
are derived from
u_{0},\ldots,u_{i-1}
and Proposition
3.4
.

Proposition 3.5
.

In the above setting, suppose
\nu(G_{i+1}^{\prime})>\frac{p}{p-1}
for
i=0,\ldots,s-1
. Then
X_{i}^{\prime}\equiv X_{i}\mod(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)
for all
i
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
i=0,\ldots,s-1
. We write
O(p^{a})
(resp.
o(p^{a})
) for an element of valuation

\geq a
(resp.
>a
).

For
i=0
, we have
u_{0}=G_{1}+p\lambda X_{0}=1+p\lambda X_{0}
and hence

X_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(u_{0})=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+p\lambda X_{0})=X_{0}+\frac{1}{p\lambda}\,o\bigl(p^{p/(p-1)}\bigr),

so
\nu(X_{0}^{\prime}-X_{0})>0
. Since
R=\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]
is a DVR and

\nu(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)=\frac{1}{p^{s-1}(p-1)}
is its smallest positive valuation, this implies

X_{0}^{\prime}\equiv X_{0}\pmod{(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)}
.

Assume now
1\leq i\leq s-1
and that
X_{j}^{\prime}\equiv X_{j}\pmod{(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)}
for all
j<i
.
By Proposition
3.4
and the definition of
u_{i}
in (
20
), we have

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{u_{i}}{G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))}\right)=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})+p\lambda X_{i}}{G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(1+\frac{-G_{i+1}^{\prime}+p\lambda X_{i}}{G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))}\right).

Set
D_{i}:=G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))
. By Lemma
2.5
, we have
\nu(D_{i}-1)\geq\nu(\zeta_{p^{i+1}}-1)
, so
D_{i}^{-1}=1+O\bigl(p^{\nu(\zeta_{p^{i+1}}-1)}\bigr)
.
Using the hypothesis
\nu(G_{i+1}^{\prime})>\frac{p}{p-1}
, we get

\frac{-G_{i+1}^{\prime}+p\lambda X_{i}}{D_{i}}=-G_{i+1}^{\prime}+p\lambda X_{i}+o\bigl(p^{p/(p-1)}\bigr).

Consequently,

X_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(1+p\lambda X_{i}-G_{i+1}^{\prime}+o\bigl(p^{p/(p-1)}\bigr)\right)=X_{i}+\frac{1}{p\lambda}\,o\bigl(p^{p/(p-1)}\bigr),

where the last equality uses the power-series expansion of
\ln(1+T)
and the fact that for
m>1
one has

\nu(T^{m}/m)>\frac{p}{p-1}
as soon as
\nu(T)\geq\frac{p}{p-1}
.
Thus
\nu(X_{i}^{\prime}-X_{i})>0
, so by discreteness of the valuation on
R
we conclude

X_{i}^{\prime}\equiv X_{i}\pmod{(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)}
.
∎

Proposition
3.5
suggests that one may construct the polynomial
G_{i+1}
in (
20
) as an approximation of
G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))
.

Definition 3.6
.

We say that

f=\sum_{n_{0},\ldots,n_{s-1}\geq 0}a_{n_{0},\ldots,n_{s-1}}X_{0}^{n_{0}}\cdots X_{s-1}^{n_{s-1}}\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket

satisfies the
Tate condition
if, for every multi-index
n=(n_{0},\ldots,n_{s-1})
, the coefficients
a_{n}
satisfy:

\nu(a_{n})\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad|n|=n_{0}+\cdots+n_{s-1}\to\infty.

The following lemma proves the stability of the Tate condition under substitution.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
R
be a discretely valued ring with valuation
\nu
.
Let
f\in R\llbracket T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\rrbracket
satisfy the Tate condition.
Suppose
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rrbracket
satisfy the Tate condition and have zero constant terms.
Then
f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in R\llbracket X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\rrbracket
satisfies the Tate condition.

Proof.

Write

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}}a_{\alpha}T^{\alpha},\qquad T^{\alpha}=T_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots T_{m}^{\alpha_{m}},\qquad|\alpha|:=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}.

Since each
g_{j}
has zero constant term, we have
g_{j}\in(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, hence

(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})^{\alpha}=g_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots g_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}\in(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{|\alpha|}.

Fix a multi-index
\beta
and write
c_{\beta}\in R
for the coefficient of
X^{\beta}
in

f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
. Since
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})^{\alpha}
has total degree
\geq|\alpha|
,
only indices
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\leq|\beta|
can contribute to
c_{\beta}
.

Now fix
M\in\mathbb{R}
. By the Tate condition on
f
, there exists
D
such that

\nu(a_{\alpha})>M
for all
\alpha
with
|\alpha|>D
.
Decompose

c_{\beta}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq D}a_{\alpha}\cdot\bigl[X^{\beta}\bigr](g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})^{\alpha}\;+\sum_{D<|\alpha|\leq|\beta|}a_{\alpha}\cdot\bigl[X^{\beta}\bigr](g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})^{\alpha},

where
[X^{\beta}](\cdots)
denotes “coefficient of
X^{\beta}
”.

(1) The contribution from
|\alpha|>D
.

Every term in the second sum has valuation
>M
because it is multiplied by
a_{\alpha}
.

(2) The contribution from
|\alpha|\leq D
.

There are only finitely many such
\alpha
. Fix one.
When we expand
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})^{\alpha}=g_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots g_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
,
the coefficient of
X^{\beta}
is a finite sum of products of
|\alpha|
coefficients taken from the
power series
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}
, where the total degrees of the chosen monomials add up to
|\beta|
.
Therefore at least one of these chosen monomials has total degree
\geq|\beta|/|\alpha|
.

Since each
g_{j}
satisfies the Tate condition, the valuations of coefficients of
g_{j}
tend to
\infty

as the total degree tends to
\infty
. Hence, for fixed
\alpha
, we have

\nu\!\left(\bigl[X^{\beta}\bigr](g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})^{\alpha}\right)\longrightarrow\infty\qquad\text{as }|\beta|\to\infty.

Multiplying by the fixed scalar
a_{\alpha}
does not affect the conclusion.

Combining (1) and (2), we see that for every
M
there exists
B
such that
\nu(c_{\beta})>M
for all

|\beta|>B
. This is precisely the Tate condition for
f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

For each
i
, one can compute

G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i+1}}][X_{0},\ldots,\allowbreak X_{i-1}]
inductively
such that:

1.

G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i+1}}]\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}\rrbracket

satisfies the Tate condition,

2.

\nu(G_{i+1}^{\prime})>\frac{p}{p-1}
and

G_{i+1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i+1}}]\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}\rrbracket

satisfies the Tate condition, and

3.

X_{i}^{\prime}
satisfies the Tate condition as a series in

\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i+1}}]\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i}\rrbracket
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
i\geq 0
.

Let’s consider the base case
i=0
. Take
G_{1}=1
, so
u_{0}=1+p\lambda X_{0}
.
By Proposition
3.4
, the associated Witt coordinate is

X_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+p\lambda X_{0})=\sum_{j\geq 1}(-1)^{j+1}\frac{(p\lambda)^{j-1}}{j}X_{0}^{j}.

Writing
j=p^{r}\ell
with
(p,\ell)=1
and using
\nu(\lambda)=\frac{1}{p-1}
and

\nu(j)=r
(since
\nu(p)=1
), we have

\nu\!\left(\frac{(p\lambda)^{j-1}}{j}\right)=(j-1)\,\nu(p\lambda)-\nu(j)=(j-1)\left(1+\frac{1}{p-1}\right)-r=\frac{p(j-1)}{p-1}-r.

In particular, as
j\to\infty
we have

\nu\!\left(\frac{(p\lambda)^{j-1}}{j}\right)=\frac{p(j-1)}{p-1}-r\longrightarrow\infty,

since
r=\nu(j)=\nu_{p}(j)
grows at most logarithmically in
j
.
Hence
X_{0}^{\prime}
satisfies the Tate condition, establishing the base case.

Assume that
G_{1},\ldots,G_{i}
have been constructed and that

X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime}
satisfy the Tate condition.

By Lemma
2.5
, the series

G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))
satisfies the Tate condition when viewed as a
power series in the variables
X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime}
.
Since each
X_{j}^{\prime}
itself satisfies the Tate condition as a power series in

X_{0},\ldots,X_{j}
and has zero constant term, we may substitute these series into

G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))
.
By Lemma
3.7
, the result again satisfies the Tate condition as a
series in
X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}
.
This proves
1
.

Define
G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})
to be the truncation of

G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))
obtained by keeping only those terms whose
coefficients have valuation at most
\frac{p}{p-1}
.
Then
G_{i+1}^{\prime}

has coefficientwise valuation strictly larger than
\frac{p}{p-1}
, and it still satisfies the Tate condition.
This proves
2
.

Finally, by Proposition
3.4
we have

X_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})+p\lambda X_{i}}{G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))}\right)=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+U),

where

U=\frac{-G_{i+1}^{\prime}+p\lambda X_{i}}{G_{i+1,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime},0))}.

The numerator and denominator of
U
satisfy the Tate condition, and the denominator is a unit
(with constant term
1
), so its inverse also satisfies the Tate condition; hence
U
satisfies the Tate condition in

\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i+1}}]\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i}\rrbracket
.

Now write

\ln(1+U)=\sum_{m\geq 1}(-1)^{m-1}\frac{U^{m}}{m}.

Set
\delta:=\nu(U)>0
. Fix
M\in\mathbb{R}
. Choose
m_{0}
such that

m\delta-\nu(m)>M\qquad(m\geq m_{0}).

Then for
m\geq m_{0}
, every coefficient of
U^{m}/m
has valuation
>M
.
For each of the finitely many
1\leq m<m_{0}
, since
U^{m}
satisfies the Tate condition, there exists
B_{m}
such that
all coefficients of
U^{m}/m
of total degree
>B_{m}
have valuation
>M
.
Let
B=\max_{1\leq m<m_{0}}B_{m}
. Then every coefficient of
\ln(1+U)
of total degree
>B
has valuation
>M
.
Therefore
\ln(1+U)
satisfies the Tate condition.
It follows that
X_{i}^{\prime}
satisfies the Tate condition as well, proving
3
.
∎

3.3
Defining the Étale Cover

Definition 3.9
.

Define
R:=\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]
and set
\pi:=\zeta_{p^{s}}-1
.
Let
\mathcal{V}_{s}
be the open subset of the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{s}_{R}=\operatorname{Proj}R[X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1},Y]
given by

\operatorname{Spec}R\!\left[X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1},\frac{1}{1+p\lambda X_{0}},\frac{1}{G_{2}(X_{0})+p\lambda X_{1}},\ldots,\frac{1}{G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})+p\lambda X_{s-1}}\right],

where each
G_{i}
is a polynomial constructed in Proposition
3.8
.
Let
\mathcal{W}_{s}
be the integral closure of
\mathcal{V}_{s}
in the extension of

K(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})
defined by

Z_{s-1}^{p^{s}}=(1+p\lambda X_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{s-1}\big(G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})+p\lambda X_{i}\big)^{p^{i}}.

(21)

Proposition 3.10
.

The special fiber of
\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
is birational to the
Artin–Schreier–Witt cover of

\operatorname{Proj}k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1},y]

associated with the Witt vector
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})
,
where
x_{i}
(resp.
y
) is the reduction of
X_{i}
(resp.
Y
) modulo
\pi
.

Proof.

Applying Proposition
3.1
with

K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p^{s}})
, we obtain a Henselian local field

\mathbb{K}=\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{bd}}
with associated ring

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}
and residue field

\kappa=\mathbb{F}_{p}((x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1}))
.

By construction, the Witt vector
(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})
is obtained from
(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})
by iterated
application of the formal series
\ln(1+\cdot)
(and multiplication by
(p\lambda)^{-1}
). Hence each
X_{i}^{\prime}
is a
formal power series in
X_{0},\ldots,X_{i}
with coefficients in
R=\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]
.
By Lemma
3.2
, we may view these series as elements of
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}}
.
In particular,
(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\in W_{s}(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{int}})
.

Therefore, by Theorem
2.8
, the Kummer class defined by
(
21
) corresponds to the Witt vector
(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})
.
Reducing modulo
\pi
gives
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})\in W_{s}(\kappa)
, so the special fiber is
birational to the Artin–Schreier–Witt cover associated with
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})
.
∎

Proposition 3.11
.

The cover
\mathcal{W}_{s}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}
is generically unramified.

Proof.

By Kummer theory, the extension of function fields defined by (
21
) is cyclic
of degree
p^{s}
(hence generically Galois with group
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
). Its branch locus on

\mathbb{P}^{s}_{R}
is contained in the complement of the maximal open subset on which the Kummer
class on the right-hand side of (
21
) is a unit. Concretely, it is contained in the
union of the divisor at infinity

Y=0

(taking
\mathbb{P}^{s}_{R}=\proj R[X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1},Y]
) together with the divisors

G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})+p\lambda X_{i}=0\quad(0\leq i\leq s-1).

By definition of
\mathcal{V}_{s}\subset D_{+}(Y)
, each

G_{i+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})+p\lambda X_{i}
is inverted on
\mathcal{V}_{s}
, hence is a unit there;
also
\mathcal{V}_{s}\subset D_{+}(Y)
is disjoint from
Y=0
.
Therefore the branch locus is disjoint from
\mathcal{V}_{s}
, so the induced morphism

\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
is generically unramified.
∎

4
Computing the
\mathcal{W}_{s}

In this section, we analyze the structure of the
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-cover
\mathcal{W}_{s}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}
defined in the previous section. Specifically, we compute the polynomials
E_{i}
in (
4
).

4.1
The case
s=1

As before,
\mathcal{W}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{1}
is given by

Z_{0}^{p}=1+p\lambda X_{0}.

(22)

Consider the change of variables
Z_{0}=1+\lambda Y_{0}
. This transforms (
22
) into

\begin{split}1+p\lambda X_{0}&=(1+\lambda Y_{0})^{p}\\
&=1+\lambda^{p}Y_{0}^{p}+p\lambda Y_{0}+\sum_{j=2}^{p-1}\binom{p}{j}\lambda^{j}Y_{0}^{j}.\end{split}

Hence it simplifies to

Y_{0}+\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{0}^{p}+o\left(p^{0}\right)=X_{0}.

(23)

Here,
o\left(p^{0}\right)
denotes terms with valuation strictly larger than zero. Note that, since

\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}\equiv-1\pmod{\lambda}

[
25
, Section 2.4]
, the equation above reduces modulo
\lambda
to the Artin–Schreier equation

y_{0}-y_{0}^{p}=x_{0}.

To generalize this change of variables to the case
s>1
, we follow the strategy of associating a Witt vector as in the previous section. Recall that the right-hand side of (
22
), due to Proposition
3.5
, is associated with the Witt vector
(X_{0}^{\prime})
, where
X_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p}]\llbracket X_{0}\rrbracket
is congruent to
X_{0}
modulo
\lambda
. We can rewrite the defining equation as

Z_{0}^{p}=E_{1,p}(p(X_{0}^{\prime})).

(24)

Note that if we can write
Z_{0}=E_{1,p}(Y_{0}^{\prime})
, then, due to (
24
),
Y_{0}^{\prime}=X_{0}^{\prime}
. Therefore, it makes sense to define the change of variables as an approximation of

E_{1,p}(Y_{0}^{\prime})=\exp(\lambda Y_{0}^{\prime})=1+\lambda Y_{0}^{\prime}+\frac{(\lambda Y_{0}^{\prime})^{2}}{2!}+\frac{(\lambda Y_{0}^{\prime})^{3}}{3!}+\cdots

Thus, we choose
Z_{0}=1+\lambda Y_{0}
. Then, as
Z_{0}=E_{1,p}(Y_{0}^{\prime})=\exp(\lambda Y_{0}^{\prime})
, one can write
Y_{0}^{\prime}
in terms of
Y_{0}
as follows:

\begin{split}Y_{0}^{\prime}&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1+\lambda Y_{0})\\
&=Y_{0}-\frac{\lambda Y_{0}^{2}}{2}+\cdots+(-1)^{p-1}\frac{\lambda^{p-1}Y_{0}^{p}}{p}+\cdots\end{split}

(25)

It follows that

Y_{0}^{\prime}\equiv Y_{0}-Y_{0}^{p}\pmod{\lambda}.

Thus, we can also conclude that
Y_{0}-Y_{0}^{p}-X_{0}\in\lambda\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p}]\llbracket X_{0},Y_{0}\rrbracket
.

4.2
Inductive step

In general, for
i=1,\ldots,s
, we aim to construct a system of change of variables

Z_{i-1}=E_{i}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{i-2})+\lambda Y_{i-1},

(26)

to better understand the structure of
\mathcal{W}_{s}
and to derive an Artin–Schreier-type equation similar to (
23
). As in Section
4.1
, beginning with the system of
\mathbb{Z}/p
-Kummer extensions

Z_{i-1}^{p}=Z_{i-2}\big(G_{i}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-2})+p\lambda X_{i-1}\big),

we seek a Witt vector
(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-1}^{\prime})
; equivalently, we write

(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})=(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-1}^{\prime})

for the same Witt vector viewed in the
X
-coordinates on
\mathcal{V}_{s}
. Then, for
1\leq i\leq s
,

Z_{i-1}=E_{i,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i-1}^{\prime})).

Proposition 4.1
.

With the above notations, the components
Y_{i}^{\prime}
of the Witt vector
(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-1}^{\prime})
can be computed inductively as follows:

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(1+\lambda Y_{0}\right),

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{E_{2}(Y_{0})+\lambda Y_{1}}{E_{2,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},0))}\right),

\displaystyle Y_{s-1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+\lambda Y_{s-1}}{E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}\right).

Proof.

By definition, we have

\begin{split}E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+\lambda Y_{s-1}&=E_{s,p}((\underline{Y}_{s}^{\prime}))\\
&=E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\cdot E_{s,p}((0,\ldots,0,Y_{s-1}^{\prime}))\\
&=E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\cdot E_{1,p}((Y_{s-1}^{\prime}))\\
&=E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\cdot\exp\left(\lambda Y_{s-1}^{\prime}\right).\end{split}

The desired formula for
Y_{s-1}^{\prime}
follows immediately. Replacing
s
by
i+1
in the same computation gives the displayed formula for each
Y_{i}^{\prime}
.
∎

The formula suggests that each
E_{i}
in (
26
) should be defined in terms of
E_{i,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{i-2}^{\prime},0))
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For each
i
, there exists a polynomial
E_{i}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{i-2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i}}][Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{i-2}]
such that, applying the change of variables (
26
), the associated Witt vector
(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-1}^{\prime})
satisfies the relation:

Y_{i-1}^{\prime}=(-1)^{p-1}\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{i-1}^{p}+Y_{i-1}+F_{i}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{i-1}),

where
F_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i}}]\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{i-1}\rrbracket
, and
F_{i}
reduces to a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[y_{0},\ldots,y_{i-2}]
modulo
\zeta_{p^{i}}-1
.

The proposition holds for
i=1
. We assume it holds for
i\leq s-1
.

4.2.1
Approximation of the Power Series
E_{s,p}

Proposition 4.3
.

All terms of
E_{i,p}((X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}))
, aside from the constant term
1
, have positive valuations that are sharply bounded below by
\frac{1}{p^{i-1}(p-1)}
.

Proof.

We write
\nu(f)
for the minimum valuation among the coefficients of a (nonzero) power series

f\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i}}]\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}\rrbracket
.
Equivalently,
\nu(f-1)
is the smallest valuation among the nonconstant terms of
f
.

Step 1: the case
i=1
.

We have

E_{1,p}((X_{0}))=\exp(\lambda X_{0})=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}X_{0}^{n}.

Using Legendre’s formula

\nu(n!)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\lfloor n/p^{j}\rfloor=\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1}
and
\nu(\lambda)=\frac{1}{p-1}
, we obtain

\nu\!\left(\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}\right)=\frac{n}{p-1}-\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1}=\frac{s_{p}(n)}{p-1}\geq\frac{1}{p-1}.

Hence every nonconstant coefficient of
E_{1,p}
has valuation
\geq\frac{1}{p-1}
, and the bound is sharp (e.g. for
n=1
).

Step 2: reduction to the valuation of
E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1
.

Fix
i>1
and write
\underline{X}=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1})
.
Set

E_{i,p}(\underline{X})=1+A,\qquad A\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i}}]\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}\rrbracket.

Since
E_{i,p}
is a homomorphism from the additive group of Witt vectors to units
(cf. (
15
)), we have

E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})=E_{i,p}(\underline{X})^{p}=(1+A)^{p}.

Therefore

E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1=A^{p}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\binom{p}{j}A^{j}.

Step 3: compute
\nu\big(E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1\big)
.

In the Witt ring, one has the standard decomposition

p\underline{X}=(pX_{0},0,\ldots,0)+(0,\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{i-1}),

where each
\widetilde{X}_{j}\equiv X_{j-1}^{p}\pmod{p}
(more precisely,
\widetilde{X}_{j}=X_{j-1}^{p}+p\cdot(\text{a series}))
.
Using again multiplicativity of
E_{i,p}
, we get

\displaystyle E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})

\displaystyle=E_{i,p}((pX_{0},0,\ldots,0))\cdot E_{i,p}((0,\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{i-1}))

\displaystyle=E_{i,p}((pX_{0},0,\ldots,0))\cdot E_{i-1,p}((\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{i-1})).

The first factor satisfies
\nu(\cdot-1)\geq 1
: indeed,

E_{i,p}((pX_{0},0,\ldots,0))=E_{i,p}(pX_{0})=\exp(T)

with

T=\sum_{r=0}^{i-1}(\zeta_{p^{i-r}}-1)\frac{(pX_{0})^{p^{r}}}{p^{r}}=\sum_{r=0}^{i-1}(\zeta_{p^{i-r}}-1)\,p^{p^{r}-r}\,X_{0}^{p^{r}}.

Each coefficient of
T
has valuation at least
1
, hence
T\in p\,\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{i}}]\llbracket X_{0}\rrbracket
and so
\exp(T)\equiv 1\pmod{p}
.
By the induction hypothesis applied to
E_{i-1,p}
and the congruences
\widetilde{X}_{j}\equiv X_{j-1}^{p}\pmod{p}
,
we obtain

\nu\Big(E_{i-1,p}((\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{i-1}))-1\Big)\geq\frac{1}{p^{i-2}(p-1)},

and this bound is sharp except in the exceptional case
(p,i)=(2,2)
.
Consequently,

\nu\big(E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1\big)=\frac{1}{p^{i-2}(p-1)}\qquad\text{for }(p,i)\neq(2,2).

Step 4: deduce the bound for
A
(hence for
E_{i,p}(\underline{X})-1
).

For
(p,i)\neq(2,2)
, we have
\nu(E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1)<1
, hence in particular
\nu(A)<1
.
Also
\nu\!\left(\binom{p}{j}\right)=1
for
1\leq j\leq p-1
.

We first note that in fact
\nu(A)<\frac{1}{p-1}
: if
\nu(A)\geq\frac{1}{p-1}
, then

\nu(A^{p})\geq\frac{p}{p-1}>1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu\!\left(\binom{p}{j}A^{j}\right)=1+j\nu(A)\geq 1+\frac{1}{p-1}=\frac{p}{p-1}>1,

so every term in

A^{p}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\binom{p}{j}A^{j}

has valuation
>1
, contradicting
\nu(E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1)<1
.

Thus
\nu(A)<\frac{1}{p-1}
, and consequently

\nu\!\left(\binom{p}{j}A^{j}\right)=1+j\nu(A)\geq 1+\nu(A).

Since
(p-1)\nu(A)<1
, we have
p\nu(A)<1+\nu(A)
, so the minimal valuation in

A^{p}+\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}\binom{p}{j}A^{j}
is
p\nu(A)
.
Therefore

\nu\big(E_{i,p}(p\underline{X})-1\big)=p\nu(A),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\nu(A)=\frac{1}{p^{i-1}(p-1)}.

This proves that every nonconstant term of
E_{i,p}((X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}))
has valuation

\geq\frac{1}{p^{i-1}(p-1)}
, and the bound is sharp.

Finally, a direct computation in the exceptional case
(p,i)=(2,2)
shows

\nu\big(E_{2,p}((X_{0},X_{1}))-1\big)=\frac{1}{2}
(with
p=2
).
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose
f(X)=\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{j}X^{j}\in R\llbracket X\rrbracket
, with
a_{0}\in R^{\times}
,
\nu(a_{j})>0
for
j>0
and the valuations bounded below by a number
B>0
. Suppose moreover that
f(X)^{p}
has a finite number of terms whose coefficients have valuations strictly smaller than
p/(p-1)
. Then
f(X)
itself has only finitely many terms whose coefficients have valuations strictly smaller than
1/(p-1)
.

Proof.

Let
u:=a_{0}^{-1}f
. Since
a_{0}\in R^{\times}
, multiplying by the unit
a_{0}^{-p}
does not change coefficient valuations, so

f^{p}
has a finite number of terms whose coefficients have valuation
<\tfrac{p}{p-1}
if and only if
u^{p}
does. Thus we may assume
a_{0}=1
.

We can write

\left(\sum_{j\geq 0}a_{j}X^{j}\right)^{p}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+m_{2}+\dots+m_{r}=p\\
m_{1}i_{1}+m_{2}i_{2}+\dots+m_{r}i_{r}=n\end{subarray}}\frac{p!}{m_{1}!m_{2}!\dots m_{r}!}\cdot a_{i_{1}}^{m_{1}}a_{i_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{r}}^{m_{r}}\right)X^{n}=:\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}X^{n}.

To compute the valuation of each summand in the inner sum, we find

\nu\left(\frac{p!}{\prod_{k}m_{k}!}\cdot a_{i_{1}}^{m_{1}}a_{i_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots a_{i_{r}}^{m_{r}}\right)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}\nu(a_{i_{j}})-\sum_{k=1}^{r}\nu(m_{k}!).

Consider the cases based on
r
:

1.

If
r=1
: Then
m_{1}=p
and
n=pi_{1}
. Thus, the valuation is
p\nu(a_{i_{1}})
.

2.

If
r>1
: Then
\nu(m_{k}!)=0
, so the valuation becomes
1+\sum_{j=1}^{r}m_{j}\nu(a_{i_{j}})
.

Set

l:=\max\{n\mid\nu(c_{n})<\tfrac{p}{p-1}\},

with the convention
l:=-1
if the set is empty. Assume there exists an index
m>\frac{l}{p}
such that
\nu(a_{m})<\frac{1}{p-1}
. Then
a_{m}^{p}
contributes to
c_{pm}
, and

\nu(a_{m}^{p})=p\nu(a_{m})<\frac{p}{p-1}.

Since
pm>l
, we have
\nu(c_{pm})\geq\frac{p}{p-1}>p\nu(a_{m})
.
By the non-archimedean property, if
\nu\!\left(\sum_{\alpha}t_{\alpha}\right)>\min_{\alpha}\nu(t_{\alpha})

then the minimum
\min_{\alpha}\nu(t_{\alpha})
is attained by at least two summands.
Applying this to the sum of multinomial summands defining
c_{pm}
, we deduce that there exists
a summand with
r^{\prime}>1
(hence with multinomial coefficient of valuation
1
) and distinct indices

i_{1},\ldots,i_{r^{\prime}}\leq pm
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{r^{\prime}}m_{j}i_{j}=pm
,
\sum_{j=1}^{r^{\prime}}m_{j}=p
, and

1+\sum_{j=1}^{r^{\prime}}m_{j}\nu(a_{i_{j}})\;\leq\;p\nu(a_{m}).

(27)

In particular, (
27
) is impossible if
\nu(a_{m})\leq\frac{1}{p}
, because the left-hand side
is
>1
(at least one index satisfies
i_{j}>0
since
\sum m_{j}i_{j}=pm>0
, so one term has
\nu(a_{i_{j}})>0
)
whereas the right-hand side is
\leq 1
. Hence
\nu(a_{m})>\frac{1}{p}
.
Also, since
\nu(a_{m})<\frac{1}{p-1}
, we have

p\nu(a_{m})-1<\nu(a_{m}).

From
\sum_{j=1}^{r^{\prime}}m_{j}i_{j}=pm>l
, at least one index satisfies
i_{s}>\frac{l}{p}
.
We claim
\nu(a_{i_{s}})<\frac{1}{p-1}
: otherwise

1+\sum_{j=1}^{r^{\prime}}m_{j}\nu(a_{i_{j}})\geq 1+\nu(a_{i_{s}})\geq\frac{p}{p-1},

contradicting (
27
) together with
p\nu(a_{m})<\frac{p}{p-1}
.
By the same argument as above applied to
i_{s}
, we also have
\nu(a_{i_{s}})>\frac{1}{p}
.
Finally, (
27
) gives

\nu(a_{i_{s}})\leq p\nu(a_{m})-1-\sum_{j\neq s}m_{j}\nu(a_{i_{j}})-(m_{s}-1)\nu(a_{i_{s}})\leq p\nu(a_{m})-1<\nu(a_{m}).

Thus, starting from
m_{0}:=m
, we recursively obtain indices
m_{t+1}>\frac{l}{p}
such that

\frac{1}{p}<\nu(a_{m_{t+1}})<\nu(a_{m_{t}})<\frac{1}{p-1}.

This gives an infinite strictly decreasing sequence in the discrete value group
\nu(K^{\times})
, impossible. Hence no such
m
exists, so
\nu(a_{m})\geq\frac{1}{p-1}
for all
m>\frac{l}{p}
, as required.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Proposition
4.4
extends to the multivariable case as follows.
Let
i>1
and write

f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{i}}a_{n}X^{n}\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{i-1}\rrbracket,\qquad X^{n}:=X_{0}^{n_{0}}\cdots X_{i-1}^{n_{i-1}}.

Assume
a_{\mathbf{0}}=1
,
\nu(a_{n})>0
for
n\neq\mathbf{0}
, and that the set
\{\nu(a_{n})\mid n\neq\mathbf{0}\}
is bounded below by some
B>0
.
If
f^{p}
has only finitely many monomials whose coefficients have valuation
<\frac{p}{p-1}
, then

f
has only finitely many monomials whose coefficients have valuation
<\frac{1}{p-1}
.

Proof.

For each
r\in\{0,\ldots,i-1\}
, write

f=\sum_{j\geq 0}A_{r,j}\,X_{r}^{j},\qquad A_{r,j}\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,\widehat{X_{r}},\ldots,X_{i-1}\rrbracket.

Equip the coefficient ring with the Gauss valuation (still denoted
\nu
)

\nu\!\left(\sum b_{m}X^{m}\right):=\inf_{m}\nu(b_{m}).

Then
A_{r,0}
is a unit,
\nu(A_{r,j})>0
for
j>0
, and these valuations are bounded below by
B>0
.

Write also

f^{p}=\sum_{j\geq 0}C_{r,j}X_{r}^{j}.

If infinitely many
j
satisfied
\nu(C_{r,j})<\tfrac{p}{p-1}
, then for each such
j
we can choose a monomial term of
C_{r,j}
whose coefficient attains this valuation (the value group is discrete). This yields infinitely many monomials of
f^{p}
with coefficient valuation
<\tfrac{p}{p-1}
, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence, for each fixed
r
, only finitely many
j
satisfy
\nu(C_{r,j})<\tfrac{p}{p-1}
.
Applying Proposition
4.4
to the one-variable series
\sum_{j\geq 0}A_{r,j}X_{r}^{j}
(over the Gauss-valued coefficient ring) gives that

J_{r}:=\{j\geq 0\mid\nu(A_{r,j})<\tfrac{1}{p-1}\}

is finite for each
r
.

Now let
a_{n}X^{n}
be a monomial of
f
with
\nu(a_{n})<\tfrac{1}{p-1}
. For each
r
, the coefficient
a_{n}
appears in
A_{r,n_{r}}
, so

\nu(A_{r,n_{r}})\leq\nu(a_{n})<\tfrac{1}{p-1},

hence
n_{r}\in J_{r}
. Therefore

n\in J_{0}\times\cdots\times J_{i-1},

and the right-hand side is finite. So
f
has only finitely many monomials whose coefficients have valuation
<\tfrac{1}{p-1}
.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

E_{s,p}(p(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))
contains only finitely many terms with coefficients of valuation strictly smaller than
p/(p-1)
.

Proof.

By definition, we have

\begin{split}E_{s,p}(p(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))&=E_{s-1,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime}))\cdot G_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\\
&=(E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2})\cdot G_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0)).\end{split}

The second equality is the defining relation
Z_{s-2}=E_{s-1,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime}))
together with the change of variables (
26
) for
i=s-1
.
The first factor meets the finiteness condition because it is a polynomial. For the second factor, Lemma
2.5
shows that

G_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,(\zeta_{p^{2}}-1)Y_{s-2}^{\prime}\rrbracket.

Since each
\zeta_{p^{m}}-1
has positive valuation and
Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime}
have coefficients in
R
(by the induction hypothesis in Proposition
4.2
), only finitely many monomials in this expansion can have coefficient valuation
<\frac{p}{p-1}
. The proposition follows.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

The series
E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\in R\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}\rrbracket
contains only finitely many terms with coefficients of valuation strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{p-1}
.

Proof.

Set
f:=E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\in R\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}\rrbracket
. By Proposition
4.3
,
f
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
4.5
.
Moreover,
f^{p}=E_{s,p}(p(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))
, and Proposition
4.6
shows that
f^{p}
has only finitely many terms with coefficient valuation
<\frac{p}{p-1}
. Applying Proposition
4.5
to
f
yields the claim.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

This result does not generally hold for
E_{s,p}((Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2},0))
.

Therefore, the following definition is well-defined.

Definition 4.9
.

Define
E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}][Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}]
as the polynomial obtained by truncating
E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}\rrbracket
at terms whose coefficients have valuations strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{p-1}
.

4.2.2
Computing the Minimal Polynomial

Proposition 4.10
.

With the choice of
E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})
as in Definition
4.9
, the entry
Y_{s-1}^{\prime}
of the associated Witt vector has the form

Y_{s-1}^{\prime}=(-1)^{p-1}\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{s-1}^{p}+Y_{s-1}+F_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1}),

where
F_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1})\in\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1}\rrbracket
.

Proof.

The case
s=1
is proved in Section
4.1
. For
s>1
, we have the following expansion from Proposition
4.1
for
Y_{s-1}^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}Y_{s-1}^{\prime}&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+\lambda Y_{s-1}}{E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(1+\frac{S_{s}+\lambda Y_{s-1}}{E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(1+\lambda Y_{s-1}+S_{s}+o\left(p^{1/(p-1)}\right)\right)\\
&=Y_{s-1}+(-1)^{p-1}\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{s-1}^{p}+\frac{S_{s}}{\lambda}+(-1)^{p-1}\frac{S_{s}^{p}}{p\lambda}+o\left(p^{0}\right)\end{split}

Here,
S_{s}:=E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})-E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))
, which has valuation at least
\frac{1}{p-1}
by definition. The rest follows similarly to the proof of Proposition
3.5
and the case for
s=1
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.11
.

The minimal polynomial of
Y_{s-1}
over
\mathbb{Q}[\zeta_{p^{s}}](Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2},X_{s-1})
is

\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{s-1}^{p}+Y_{s-1}+H_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1})-(E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2})X_{s-1}=0.

(28)

In particular,
X_{j}
lies in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{j+1}}]\left[Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{j},\frac{1}{1+\lambda Y_{0}},\ldots,\frac{1}{E_{j-1}+\lambda Y_{j-1}}\right]
for
j=0,\ldots,s-1
.

Proof.

Substituting
Z_{s-1}=E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+\lambda Y_{s-1}
into

Z_{s-1}^{p}=Z_{s-2}(G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})+p\lambda X_{s-1})

transforms the equation to

\begin{split}\frac{1}{p\lambda}\big((E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+\lambda Y_{s-1})^{p}&-(E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2})G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})\big)\\
&=X_{s-1}(E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2}).\end{split}

(29)

Let us analyze the left-hand side. By construction, we have

\begin{split}E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})&=E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))+O\left(p^{1/(p-1)}\right),\\
E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2}&=E_{s-1,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime})),\\
G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})&=G_{s,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))+o\left(p^{p/(p-1)}\right).\end{split}

The first two expressions are series in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}\rrbracket
, and the last is a series in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket X_{0},\allowbreak\ldots,X_{s-2}\rrbracket
. Additionally, for
j=0,\ldots,s-1
, we have
X_{j}^{\prime}=Y_{j}^{\prime}
, and
X_{j}
can be expressed as a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{j+1}}]\left[Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{j},\frac{1}{1+\lambda Y_{0}},\ldots,\frac{1}{E_{j-1}+\lambda Y_{j-1}}\right]
by induction. Thus, we obtain

G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})=G_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))+o\left(p^{p/(p-1)}\right)

as an element of
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}\rrbracket
. Recall also that

E_{s,p}(p(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2},Y_{s-1}))=E_{s-1,p}((Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2}))\cdot G_{s,p}((Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2},Y_{s-1})).

Therefore, the left-hand side of (
29
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\text{LHS}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\bigg(\left(E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))+O\left(p^{1/(p-1)}\right)+\lambda Y_{s-1}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle-E_{s-1,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime}))\left(G_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))+o\left(p^{p/(p-1)}\right)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{s-1}^{p}+Y_{s-1}+O\left(p^{1/(p-1)}\right)

as a series in
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1}\rrbracket
. This forces the left-hand side to be a polynomial in

\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]\big[Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1},\frac{1}{1+\lambda Y_{0}},\ldots,\frac{1}{E_{s-2}+\lambda Y_{s-2}}\big]
.
Thus the left-hand side can be written as

\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}Y_{s-1}^{p}+Y_{s-1}+H_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1})

for some
H_{s}
in that ring. Furthermore, the left-hand side is a degree-
p
expression in
Y_{s-1}
, and the Kummer equation
defines a degree-
p
extension over
\mathbb{Q}[\zeta_{p^{s}}](Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2},X_{s-1})
, so this polynomial is the minimal polynomial of
Y_{s-1}
.
Finally, for
j=s-1
the defining equation (
28
) explicitly solves for
X_{s-1}
by dividing by

(E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2})
, which is one of the inverted factors in the stated ring; the cases

j\leq s-2
follow by induction as stated.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

The series
F_{s}
from Proposition
4.10
reduces to a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[y_{0},\ldots,y_{s-2}]
modulo
(\zeta_{p^{s}}-1)
.

Proof.

Set
\pi:=\zeta_{p^{s}}-1
and
u:=\frac{\lambda^{p-1}}{p}(-1)^{p}
.
By Proposition
4.10
and the identity
Y_{s-1}^{\prime}=X_{s-1}^{\prime}
, we have

-uY_{s-1}^{p}+Y_{s-1}+F_{s}=X_{s-1}^{\prime}.

By Proposition
3.5
,
X_{s-1}^{\prime}\equiv X_{s-1}\pmod{\pi}
, hence

-uy_{s-1}^{p}+y_{s-1}+\overline{F}_{s}=x_{s-1}.

(30)

On the other hand, reducing (
28
) modulo
\pi
and using

E_{s-1}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-3})+\lambda Y_{s-2}\equiv 1\pmod{\pi},

we obtain

-uy_{s-1}^{p}+y_{s-1}+\overline{H}_{s}=x_{s-1}.

(31)

Comparing (
30
) and (
31
) gives
\overline{F}_{s}=\overline{H}_{s}
.

It remains to show
\overline{H}_{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[y_{0},\ldots,y_{s-2}]
. From (
29
),

(E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+\lambda Y_{s-1})^{p}=\lambda^{p}Y_{s-1}^{p}+p\lambda Y_{s-1}E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})+E_{s}^{p}+o\!\left(p^{p/(p-1)}\right),

where the omitted mixed terms have valuation
\geq p/(p-1)
. After dividing by
p\lambda
and reducing modulo
\pi
, these omitted terms vanish, and
E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})\equiv 1\pmod{\pi}
by Definition
4.9
. Therefore
\overline{H}_{s}
is a polynomial in
y_{0},\ldots,y_{s-2}
, so the same holds for
\overline{F}_{s}
.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
4.2
.

In fact, we can provide further details on the reduction of
F_{s}
and
H_{s}
in (

).

Corollary 4.13
.

We have, for
j=1,\ldots,s
,

H_{j}\equiv F_{j}\equiv f_{j}(y_{0},y_{1}+b_{1},\ldots,y_{j-2}+b_{j-2})+\wp(b_{j-1})\pmod{(\zeta_{p^{j}}-1)},

where

is the polynomial in (

\mathcal{W}_{s}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}
is birational to the cover of
\proj k[x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1},y]
defined by

\wp(y_{0},y_{1}+b_{1},\ldots,y_{s-1}+b_{s-1})=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{s-1}).

Proof.

Due to §
4.1
, the corollary holds for
s=1
.

For
s>1
, recall that the special fiber of
\mathcal{W}_{s}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}
is birational to the extension of

associated with the Witt vector

, which can be defined as a system of Artin–Schreier extensions as in (

). By induction, the last of these extensions is given by

.
∎

is normal (being a localization of a polynomial ring over the DVR
R
), contains
B_{s-1}
, and has fraction field
L_{s}
; therefore
\widetilde{B_{s-1}}\subseteq B_{s}
. Thus
\widetilde{B_{s-1}}=B_{s}
.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

The special fiber of
\mathcal{W}_{s}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}
is isomorphic to the morphism
\wp
in (
8
). In particular, it is unramified.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
,
B_{s}
is also the integral closure of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}
in
L_{s}
. Furthermore, the special fiber of
\mathcal{W}_{s}
(resp.
\mathcal{V}_{s}
) is
\mathbb{F}_{p}[y_{0},\ldots,y_{s-1}]
(resp.
\mathbb{F}_{p}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1}]
). Together with Corollary
4.13
, this shows that the special fiber is isomorphic to
\wp
in (
8
). Finally,
\wp
is unramified (equivalently étale) on
W_{s}
since
\ker(\wp)\cong\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
is finite étale by (
8
).
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

The cover
\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
is an étale cover with good reduction.

Proof.

The assertion of good reduction holds because the special fiber is an étale
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-torsor between smooth
k
-schemes, as established in Corollary
5.3
.

For flatness, fix
x\in\mathcal{W}_{s}
mapping to
y\in\mathcal{V}_{s}
, and set
A:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},y}
and
B:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}_{s},x}
. Since
\mathcal{V}_{s}
and
\mathcal{W}_{s}
are smooth over the DVR
R
, both
A
and
B
are regular local rings, hence
B
is Cohen–Macaulay. The morphism
\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
is finite, so
A\to B
is integral, and in particular
\dim A=\dim B
. By Miracle Flatness (Stacks Project, Lemma 10.128.1), it follows that
B
is flat over
A
. Hence
\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
is finite and flat.

For étaleness, let
M:=\Omega_{\mathcal{W}_{s}/\mathcal{V}_{s}}
. Since
\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
is of finite type,
M
is a coherent
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}
-module; since the morphism is finite,
M
is in fact finite over
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{W}_{s}}
. By Proposition
3.11
, the generic fiber is unramified, so
M[1/\pi]=0
. Thus, for each
x
, the stalk
M_{x}
is a finitely generated
B
-module with
(M_{x})[1/\pi]=0
, hence there exists
n\geq 1
such that
\pi^{n}M_{x}=0
. By Corollary
5.3
, the special fiber is unramified, so
M_{x}/\pi M_{x}=0
, i.e.
M_{x}=\pi M_{x}
. Therefore
M_{x}=\pi^{n}M_{x}=0
for all
x
, so
M=0
and the morphism is unramified. Since it is also flat, it is étale.
∎

6
The Group Structure

In this section, we explain how the covers constructed in the previous sections carry natural group scheme structures, and how the morphism
\psi_{s}\colon\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{V}_{s}
becomes a group homomorphism. The key point is that, after completing along the identity sections, certain explicit coordinate maps (defined below) identify the formal neighborhoods of
\mathcal{V}_{s}
and
\mathcal{W}_{s}
with the formal Witt vector scheme; we then transport Witt vector addition to obtain formal group laws, and finally descend these formal structures to algebraic Hopf algebras over
R
.

Let
R:=\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]
be a discrete valuation ring, and let
\pi:=\zeta_{p^{s}}-1
be its uniformizer.

Coordinate rings.

Let

A:=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{V}_{s})=R[X_{0},\dots,X_{s-1}]\bigl[U_{0}^{-1},\dots,U_{s-1}^{-1}\bigr],

with defining relations

U_{0}=1+p\lambda X_{0},\qquad U_{j}=G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})+p\lambda X_{j}\quad(1\leq j\leq s-1),

and

B:=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{W}_{s})=R[Y_{0},\dots,Y_{s-1}]\bigl[Z_{0}^{-1},\dots,Z_{s-1}^{-1}\bigr],

with defining relations

Z_{0}=1+\lambda Y_{0},\qquad Z_{j}=E_{j+1}(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{j-1})+\lambda Y_{j}\quad(1\leq j\leq s-1).

Completions along the identity sections.

Let
I_{\mathcal{V}}:=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})\subset A
and
I_{\mathcal{W}}:=(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1})\subset B
be the augmentation ideals, and let
\widehat{A}
(resp.
\widehat{B}
) be the
I_{\mathcal{V}}
-adic (resp.
I_{\mathcal{W}}
-adic) completion.
Since each
U_{j}
(resp.
Z_{j}

1
, we have
U_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{I_{\mathcal{V}}}
(resp.
Z_{j}\equiv 1\pmod{I_{\mathcal{W}}}

U_{j}
(resp.

) is automatically a unit in the completion. Consequently,

\widehat{A}\cong R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket,\qquad\widehat{B}\cong R\llbracket Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1}\rrbracket.

Let
e_{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathcal{V}_{s}(R)
and
e_{\mathcal{W}}\in\mathcal{W}_{s}(R)
denote the identity sections. The formal completions of
\mathcal{V}_{s}
and
\mathcal{W}_{s}

\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}:=\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s,e_{\mathcal{V}}}=\Spf(\widehat{A}),\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s}:=\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s,e_{\mathcal{W}}}=\Spf(\widehat{B}).

Similarly, letting
0\in W_{s}(R)
be the identity section, we write

\widehat{W}_{s}:=\widehat{W}_{s,0}=\Spf\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}\bigr)

for the formal completion of
W_{s}
at

.

From Proposition
4.1
and Proposition
3.4
, we have the (not necessarily algebraic) coordinate maps
\gamma
and
\tau
. On formal completions along the identity sections,
\tau
maps
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}
to
\widehat{W}_{s}
, and
\gamma
maps
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s}
to
\widehat{W}_{s}
. Concretely,

\gamma\colon\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s}\longrightarrow\widehat{W}_{s},\qquad(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-1})\longmapsto(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-1}^{\prime})

(37)

\tau\colon\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}\longrightarrow\widehat{W}_{s},\qquad(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})\longmapsto(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}).

(38)

More precisely, each
X_{i}^{\prime}
is computed inductively as follows:

\displaystyle X_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+p\lambda X_{0}),

\displaystyle X_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{G_{2}(X_{0})+p\lambda X_{1}}{G_{2,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},0))}\right),

\displaystyle X_{s-1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})+p\lambda X_{s-1}}{G_{s,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))}\right).

Conversely, given
(Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-1}^{\prime})\in\widehat{W}_{s}

(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\in\widehat{W}_{s}

(resp.
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}
) along the identity section as follows.

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\exp\left(\lambda Y_{0}^{\prime}\right)-1\right),

(39)

\displaystyle Y_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\exp\left(\lambda Y_{1}^{\prime}\right)E_{2,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},0))-E_{2}(Y_{0})\right),

\displaystyle Y_{s-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\exp\left(\lambda Y_{s-1}^{\prime}\right)E_{s,p}((Y_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{s-2}^{\prime},0))-E_{s}(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{s-2})\right).

(resp.

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\left(\exp\left(p\lambda X_{0}^{\prime}\right)-1\right),

(40)

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\left(\exp\left(p\lambda X_{1}^{\prime}\right)G_{2,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},0))-G_{2}(X_{0})\right),

\displaystyle X_{s-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\left(\exp\left(p\lambda X_{s-1}^{\prime}\right)G_{s,p}((X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-2}^{\prime},0))-G_{s}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-2})\right)

Lemma 6.1
.

With notation as above, we have the following.

(a)

The coordinate map
\tau
defined in (
38
) restricts to an isomorphism of formal schemes over
R
, which we denote

\widehat{\tau}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\widehat{W}_{s},

whose inverse is induced by the
X
-formulas in (
40
).
Equivalently, on completed local rings one gets mutually inverse continuous
R
-algebra homomorphisms

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},e_{\mathcal{V}}},\qquad(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},e_{\mathcal{V}}}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}.

(b)

The coordinate map
\gamma
defined in (
37
) restricts to an isomorphism of formal schemes over
R
, which we denote

\widehat{\gamma}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s}\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\widehat{W}_{s},

whose inverse is induced by the
Y
-formulas in (
39
).
Equivalently, on completed local rings one gets mutually inverse continuous
R
-algebra homomorphisms

\widehat{\gamma}^{\sharp}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{W}_{s},e_{\mathcal{W}}},\qquad(\widehat{\gamma}^{-1})^{\sharp}\colon\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{W}_{s},e_{\mathcal{W}}}\longrightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}.

Proof.

We prove
(a)
; the proof of
(b)
is identical, replacing
p\lambda
with
\lambda
and
G_{j+1},G_{j+1,p}
with
E_{j+1},E_{j+1,p}
.

Let
I_{\mathcal{V}}:=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})\subset A
and
I_{W}:=(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{O}(W_{s})
be the ideals of the identity sections.
By the discussion above, the
I_{\mathcal{V}}
-adic completion of
A
identifies with the completed local ring of
\mathcal{V}_{s}
at
e_{\mathcal{V}}
, i.e.

\widehat{A}\cong\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},e_{\mathcal{V}}}\cong R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket,\qquad\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}\cong R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket.

Step 1:
\tau
defines a morphism of formal schemes.

To define a morphism
\widehat{\tau}:\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}\to\widehat{W}_{s}
, it suffices to give a continuous
R
-algebra map

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}:R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket\to R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket

sending
I_{W}
into
I_{\mathcal{V}}
.

For
j=0
set

X_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+p\lambda X_{0})=\sum_{n\geq 1}(-1)^{n-1}\frac{(p\lambda)^{n-1}}{n}X_{0}^{n}.

Since
p\lambda\in pR
, one has
\nu((p\lambda)^{n-1})\geq n-1\geq\nu(n)
, so the coefficients lie in
R
and
X_{0}^{\prime}\in I_{\mathcal{V}}
.

Assume inductively that
X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime}\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}\rrbracket
and have zero constant term.
Define

X_{j}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})+p\lambda X_{j}}{G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)}\Bigr).

By Proposition
3.8
,

G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})\equiv G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)\pmod{p\lambda}.

Indeed,
G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})=G^{\prime}_{j+1}
has

\nu(G^{\prime}_{j+1})>\frac{p}{p-1}=\nu(p\lambda)
by Proposition
3.8
, hence
G^{\prime}_{j+1}\in(p\lambda)
.
Moreover
G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)
has constant term
1
, hence is a unit in the complete local ring
R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}\rrbracket
. Therefore the ratio is of the form
1+p\lambda\Delta
with
\Delta\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j}\rrbracket
, and since numerator and denominator both have constant term
1
, one has
\Delta\in I_{\mathcal{V}}\,R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j}\rrbracket
.

Thus
\ln(1+p\lambda\Delta)
is a well-defined formal power series in the
I_{\mathcal{V}}
-adic topology, and dividing by
p\lambda
yields
X_{j}^{\prime}\in I_{\mathcal{V}}
. This defines a continuous
R
-algebra map
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}
.

Step 2:
\tau^{-1}
defines a morphism of formal schemes.

Similarly, to define
\widehat{\tau}^{-1}:\widehat{W}_{s}\to\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}
it suffices to define a continuous map on completed local rings.
For
j=0
set

X_{0}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\bigl(\exp(p\lambda X_{0}^{\prime})-1\bigr)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(p\lambda)^{n-1}}{n!}(X_{0}^{\prime})^{n}.

As
p\lambda\in pR
, one has
\nu((p\lambda)^{n-1})\geq n-1\geq\nu(n!)
, hence
X_{0}\in R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime}\rrbracket
and
X_{0}\in I_{W}
.

Assume inductively that
X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}\in R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket
lie in
I_{W}
, and that
after substituting these series into the forward formulas, one recovers
X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime}
.
Define
X_{j}
by the recursion (
40
):

X_{j}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\Bigl(\exp(p\lambda X_{j}^{\prime})\,G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})\Bigr).

Write the numerator as

(\exp(p\lambda X_{j}^{\prime})-1)\,G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)\;+\;\Bigl(G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})\Bigr).

The first term is in
(p\lambda)
. For the second term, recall from Proposition
3.8
that

G^{\prime}_{j+1}(X):=G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime}(X),\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime}(X),0)-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})\in(p\lambda)\,R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}\rrbracket.

Substituting
X_{i}=X_{i}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{i}^{\prime})
and using the inductive identities

X_{i}^{\prime}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{i})=X_{i}^{\prime}
for
i<j
, we get

G_{j+1,p}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j-1}^{\prime},0)-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})\in(p\lambda).

Hence the numerator lies in
(p\lambda)
, so
X_{j}\in R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{j}^{\prime}\rrbracket
. Since both terms have constant term
1
, their difference has constant term
0
, so
X_{j}\in I_{W}
.

Step 3: the maps are inverse.

We check that
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}
and
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}
are mutually inverse by verifying on the topological generators.
Let
I_{\mathcal{V}}:=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})\subset R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket
and
I_{W}:=(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\subset R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket
.

The identities

\exp(\ln(1+T))=1+T,\qquad\ln(\exp(T))=T,

hold as formal identities in
R\llbracket T\rrbracket
; hence they hold after substitution for
T\in I_{\mathcal{V}}
(resp.
T\in I_{W}
) in the
I_{\mathcal{V}}
-adic (resp.
I_{W}
-adic) topology.

For
j=0
, we have

X_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+p\lambda X_{0}),\qquad X_{0}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}(\exp(p\lambda X_{0}^{\prime})-1),

so composing in either order reduces immediately to the identities above with
T=p\lambda X_{0}
(resp.
T=p\lambda X_{0}^{\prime}
).

Assume inductively that the two compositions are the identity on

X^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{j-1}
(equivalently on
X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}
).
Set

\Theta_{j}\,:=\,G_{j+1,p}(X^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{j-1},0)\in R\llbracket X^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{j-1}\rrbracket,\qquad\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}\,:=\,\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(\Theta_{j})\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}\rrbracket.

Both
\Theta_{j}
and
\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}
have constant term
1
, hence are units.

(1) Check
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}\circ\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})=X^{\prime}_{j}
.

Set

V_{j}\,:=\,\frac{G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})+p\lambda X_{j}}{\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}}\in R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{j}\rrbracket,

so by definition

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(V_{j}).

By Proposition
3.8
,
V_{j}=1+p\lambda\Delta_{j}
, so the logarithm is defined. Applying
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}
and using the induction hypothesis on lower coordinates gives

(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}\!\bigl(G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})\bigr)=G_{j+1}\bigl((\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{0}),\ldots,(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j-1})\bigr),

and similarly
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(\widetilde{\Theta}_{j})=\Theta_{j}
.
By the recursion (
40
),

p\lambda\,(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})=\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j})\,\Theta_{j}-G_{j+1}\bigl((\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{0}),\ldots,(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j-1})\bigr).

Substituting this into
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(V_{j})
yields

(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(V_{j})=\frac{G_{j+1}(\cdots)+p\lambda\,(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})}{\Theta_{j}}=\frac{G_{j+1}(\cdots)+\bigl(\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j})\,\Theta_{j}-G_{j+1}(\cdots)\bigr)}{\Theta_{j}}=\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j}).

Therefore

(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}\bigl(\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})\bigr)=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\!\bigl((\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(V_{j})\bigr)=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln\!\bigl(\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j})\bigr)=X^{\prime}_{j},

using
\ln(\exp(T))=T
for
T=p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j}
(with the stated valuation condition).

(2) Check
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\circ(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})=X_{j}
.

Start from the recursion (
40
), viewed as the definition of
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})
:

p\lambda\,(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})=\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j})\,\Theta_{j}-G_{j+1}\bigl((\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{0}),\ldots,(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j-1})\bigr).

Apply
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}
to both sides. By the induction hypothesis,

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\circ(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}
fixes
X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1}
, hence it fixes the polynomial

G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})
. Also
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(\Theta_{j})=\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}
, and

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j}))=\exp\bigl(p\lambda\,\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})\bigr)
.
But
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})
was defined so that

\exp\bigl(p\lambda\,\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})\bigr)=V_{j}.

Since
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(\Theta_{j})=\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}
, we get

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\bigl(\exp(p\lambda X^{\prime}_{j})\,\Theta_{j}\bigr)=\exp\bigl(p\lambda\,\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X^{\prime}_{j})\bigr)\,\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}=V_{j}\,\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}.

Therefore

p\lambda\cdot\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\bigl((\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})\bigr)=V_{j}\,\widetilde{\Theta}_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{j-1})=p\lambda X_{j},

so dividing by
p\lambda
gives

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\circ(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{j})=X_{j}
.
This completes the induction for
j
.

Thus
\widehat{\tau}
is an isomorphism of formal schemes with inverse induced by (
40
).
∎

\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}

Definition 6.2
.

Define a group structure on
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s}
(and similarly on
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}
) over

Proposition 6.3

The map
\tau
(resp.

(resp.
\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{s}

Proof.

We treat
\mathcal{V}_{s}

\mathcal{W}_{s}

\gamma

By Lemma

, the map

\widehat{\tau}:\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}\longrightarrow\widehat{W}_{s}

\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}:\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}\to\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},e_{\mathcal{V}}},\qquad(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}:\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},e_{\mathcal{V}}}\to\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}.

Step 1: Witt operations restrict to the formal completion.

Let
\mu_{W}:W_{s}\times_{R}W_{s}\to W_{s}
and
\iota_{W}:W_{s}\to W_{s}

Since these are morphisms of

\widehat{\mu}_{W}:\widehat{W}_{s}\times_{R}\widehat{W}_{s}\to\widehat{W}_{s},\qquad\widehat{\iota}_{W}:\widehat{W}_{s}\to\widehat{W}_{s}.

On coordinate rings, this means
\widehat{\mu}_{W}^{\sharp}

\widehat{\iota}_{W}^{\sharp}
are continuous homomorphisms

\displaystyle R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\longrightarrow R\llbracket X_{0,1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1,1}^{\prime},X_{0,2}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1,2}^{\prime}\rrbracket,

\displaystyle R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\longrightarrow R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket,

where the fiber product

Here

Step 2: Transport of structure.

Define the formal multiplication and inverse on
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{s}

Each map is a morphism of formal schemes because it is a composition of morphisms of formal schemes.

Step 3: Coordinate description (power series over
R
).

Using

\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{V}_{s},e_{\mathcal{V}}}\cong R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket

and

\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{W_{s},0}\cong R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}(X_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f_{i}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})\in(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})\,R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket,

\displaystyle(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}(X_{i})

\displaystyle=g_{i}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\in(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})\,R\llbracket X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime}\rrbracket.

(a) The inverse map.

The formal inverse
\widehat{\iota}_{\tau}
has comorphism

\widehat{\iota}_{\tau}^{\sharp}=\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}\circ\widehat{\iota}_{W}^{\sharp}\circ(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}\colon R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket\to R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket.

Witt inversion on

is given by universal polynomials
I_{0},\ldots,I_{s-1}\in\mathbb{Z}[\underline{U}]
, i.e.

\widehat{\iota}_{W}^{\sharp}(X_{i}^{\prime})=I_{i}(X_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{\prime})
.
Applying
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}
sends
X_{j}
to
g_{j}\in R\llbracket X^{\prime}\rrbracket
, so

I_{i}(g_{0},\ldots,g_{s-1})\in R\llbracket X^{\prime}\rrbracket
since
I_{i}
has integral coefficients.
Composing with
\widehat{\tau}^{\sharp}
then yields an element of
R\llbracket X\rrbracket
.
Hence each coordinate of
\widehat{\iota}_{\tau}
is a power series with coefficients in
R
.

(b) The multiplication map.

Likewise, the formal multiplication
\widehat{\mu}_{\tau}
has comorphism

\widehat{\mu}_{\tau}^{\sharp}=(\widehat{\tau}\times\widehat{\tau})^{\sharp}\circ\widehat{\mu}_{W}^{\sharp}\circ(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}\colon R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket\to R\llbracket X_{0,1},\ldots,X_{s-1,1},X_{0,2},\ldots,X_{s-1,2}\rrbracket.

Here
X_{i,1}=X_{i}\otimes 1
and
X_{i,2}=1\otimes X_{i}

Witt addition is given by universal Witt polynomials

\widehat{\mu}_{W}^{\sharp}(X_{i}^{\prime})=\Sigma_{i}(X_{0,1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1,1}^{\prime},X_{0,2}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1,2}^{\prime})

Applying
(\widehat{\tau}^{-1})^{\sharp}
and then
\widehat{\mu}_{W}^{\sharp}
gives an element of

R\llbracket X_{0,1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1,1}^{\prime},\allowbreak X_{0,2}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{s-1,2}^{\prime}\rrbracket

since each

is an integral polynomial.
Finally,
(\widehat{\tau}\times\widehat{\tau})^{\sharp}
substitutes

Since each

R\llbracket X_{0,1},\ldots,X_{s-1,1},X_{0,2},\ldots,X_{s-1,2}\rrbracket
.
Thus each coordinate of
\widehat{\mu}_{\tau}
is a power series with coefficients in
R
.

Step 4: Group axioms.

Associativity, commutativity, identity, and inverses hold because they hold for
(\widehat{W}_{s},\widehat{\mu}_{W},\widehat{\iota}_{W})
and we transported the structure along the isomorphism
\widehat{\tau}
.
∎

To describe these group schemes algebraically, we specify the Hopf algebra structures on their coordinate rings corresponding to Witt vector addition.

Let

Proposition 6.4
.

(a)

There exists a unique Hopf
K
-algebra structure

(\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{V}},\varepsilon_{K}^{\mathcal{V}},S_{K}^{\mathcal{V}})\colon A_{K}\longrightarrow A_{K}\otimes_{K}A_{K},\;K,\;A_{K}

such that, for all

\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(U_{j})=U_{j}\otimes U_{j},\qquad\varepsilon_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(U_{j})=1,\qquad S_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(U_{j})=U_{j}^{-1}.

With respect to the generators
X_{j}
, the structure maps are given recursively by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=0\qquad(0\leq j\leq s-1),

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{U_{0}\otimes U_{0}-1}{p\lambda}=\frac{(1+p\lambda X_{0})\otimes(1+p\lambda X_{0})-1}{p\lambda},

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{U_{j}\otimes U_{j}-G_{j+1}\bigl(\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{0}),\dots,\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{j-1})\bigr)}{p\lambda}\qquad(1\leq j\leq s-1),

\displaystyle S_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{U_{0}^{-1}-1}{p\lambda}=\frac{(1+p\lambda X_{0})^{-1}-1}{p\lambda},

\displaystyle S_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{U_{j}^{-1}-G_{j+1}\bigl(S_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{0}),\dots,S_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}(X_{j-1})\bigr)}{p\lambda}\qquad(1\leq j\leq s-1).

(b)

There exists a unique Hopf
K
-algebra structure

(\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{W}},\varepsilon_{K}^{\mathcal{W}},S_{K}^{\mathcal{W}})\colon B_{K}\longrightarrow B_{K}\otimes_{K}B_{K},\;K,\;B_{K}

such that, for all
0\leq j\leq s-1
,

\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Z_{j})=Z_{j}\otimes Z_{j},\qquad\varepsilon_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Z_{j})=1,\qquad S_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Z_{j})=Z_{j}^{-1}.

With respect to the generators
Y_{j}
, the structure maps are given recursively by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{j})

\displaystyle=0\qquad(0\leq j\leq s-1),

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{0}\otimes Z_{0}-1}{\lambda}=\frac{(1+\lambda Y_{0})\otimes(1+\lambda Y_{0})-1}{\lambda},

\displaystyle\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{j}\otimes Z_{j}-E_{j+1}\bigl(\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{0}),\dots,\Delta_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{j-1})\bigr)}{\lambda}\qquad(1\leq j\leq s-1),

\displaystyle S_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{0}^{-1}-1}{\lambda}=\frac{(1+\lambda Y_{0})^{-1}-1}{\lambda},

\displaystyle S_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{j}^{-1}-E_{j+1}\bigl(S_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{0}),\dots,S_{K}^{\mathcal{W}}(Y_{j-1})\bigr)}{\lambda}\qquad(1\leq j\leq s-1).

Proof.

We prove
(a)
and
(b)
in parallel, since the constructions are formally identical.

Convention.
We repeatedly use that each of the polynomials
G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})
and
E_{j+1}(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{j-1})
has constant term
1
(equivalently
G_{j+1}(0,\dots,0)=E_{j+1}(0,\dots,0)=1
).

Proof of
(a)
.

Step 1: Torus subalgebra.

Declare on

the standard split torus Hopf structure

\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=U_{j}\otimes U_{j},\qquad\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=1,\qquad S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=U_{j}^{-1}.

Then
T_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}\simeq\mathcal{O}((\mathbb{G}_{m})^{s}_{K})
is a Hopf
K
-algebra.

Step 2: Counit on
A_{K}
.

Define
\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\colon A_{K}\to K
by
\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})=0
for all
0\leq j\leq s-1
.
We check compatibility with the defining relations of
A
.
For
j=0
, the relation
U_{0}=1+p\lambda X_{0}
gives
\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{0})=1
.
For
j\geq 1
, using

U_{j}=G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})+p\lambda X_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{j+1}(0,\dots,0)=1,

we obtain
\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=1
.
Hence
\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
extends the counit on
T_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}
.

Step 3: Comultiplication on
A_{K}
.

Since
p\lambda\in K^{\times}
, the defining relations can be rewritten as

p\lambda X_{0}=U_{0}-1,\qquad p\lambda X_{j}=U_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})\quad(1\leq j\leq s-1).

We already fixed
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=U_{j}\otimes U_{j}
.
Define
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
recursively by

\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}):=\frac{U_{0}\otimes U_{0}-1}{p\lambda},\qquad\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j}):=\frac{U_{j}\otimes U_{j}-G_{j+1}\bigl(\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}),\dots,\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j-1})\bigr)}{p\lambda}.

Applying
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
to the relation

p\lambda X_{j}=U_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})

shows that the image of the relation is
0
in
A_{K}\otimes_{K}A_{K}
by construction.
Moreover, since each
U_{j}
is group-like,

\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j}^{-1})=(U_{j}\otimes U_{j})^{-1}=U_{j}^{-1}\otimes U_{j}^{-1}
,
so
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
respects the localization and extends uniquely to a
K
-algebra map

A_{K}\to A_{K}\otimes_{K}A_{K}
.

Step 4: Antipode on
A_{K}
.

Set
S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=U_{j}^{-1}
for all
j
.
Define
S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
recursively by

S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}):=\frac{U_{0}^{-1}-1}{p\lambda},\qquad S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j}):=\frac{U_{j}^{-1}-G_{j+1}\bigl(S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}),\dots,S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j-1})\bigr)}{p\lambda}.

Applying
S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
to the relation

p\lambda X_{j}=U_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})

shows it is preserved.
Also
S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j}^{-1})=U_{j}
, so
S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
respects the localization, hence extends to a

K
-algebra homomorphism
A_{K}\to A_{K}
.

Step 5: Hopf axioms and uniqueness.

The Hopf identities hold on the Hopf subalgebra
T_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}
.
Assume inductively that they hold on the
K
-subalgebra generated by

T_{K}^{\mathcal{V}}
and
X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1}
.
Consider the defining identity

p\lambda X_{j}=U_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1}).

Coassociativity.

Apply
(\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})\circ\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
and
(\mathrm{id}\otimes\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})\circ\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}

to the formula defining
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
.
The
U_{j}
-term gives
U_{j}\otimes U_{j}\otimes U_{j}
on both sides since
U_{j}
is group-like.
By the induction hypothesis,
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
is coassociative on
X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1}
, hence the
two ways of applying
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
to the polynomial

G_{j+1}\bigl(\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}),\dots,\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j-1})\bigr)

agree, and therefore coassociativity holds on
X_{j}
.

Counit.

Apply
(\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})\circ\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}
to the defining formula for
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
.
Since
\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=1
, we have

(\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})(U_{j}\otimes U_{j})=U_{j}.

By the induction hypothesis,
(\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{i})=X_{i}
for
i<j
, hence

(\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})\,G_{j+1}\bigl(\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}),\dots,\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j-1})\bigr)=G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1}).

Therefore

(\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})=\frac{U_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})}{p\lambda}=X_{j},

using the defining relation
p\lambda X_{j}=U_{j}-G_{j+1}(X_{0},\dots,X_{j-1})
.
Similarly
(\mathrm{id}\otimes\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})=X_{j}
.

Antipode axiom.

Apply
m\circ(\mathrm{id}\otimes S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
to the defining formula for
\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
.
The group-like property gives

m(\mathrm{id}\otimes S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(U_{j})=U_{j}\cdot U_{j}^{-1}=1
.
By the induction hypothesis,

m(\mathrm{id}\otimes S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{i})=\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{i})=0
for
i<j
,
so

m(\mathrm{id}\otimes S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})\,G_{j+1}\bigl(\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{0}),\dots,\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j-1})\bigr)=G_{j+1}(0,\dots,0)=1.

Hence
m(\mathrm{id}\otimes S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})=0=\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
.
The identity
m(S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}\otimes\mathrm{id})\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})=\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K}(X_{j})
is analogous.

This proves that
(A_{K},\Delta^{\mathcal{V}}_{K},\varepsilon^{\mathcal{V}}_{K},S^{\mathcal{V}}_{K})
is a Hopf
K
-algebra.
Uniqueness follows immediately: if

is any Hopf structure on
A_{K}

with all
U_{j}
group-like, then
\varepsilon(U_{j})=1
and
S(U_{j})=U_{j}^{-1}
are forced, and
applying
\Delta
(resp.

) to

and using

forces exactly the displayed recursive formulas for

\Delta(X_{j})
and
S(X_{j})
.

Proof of
(b)
.

The construction is the same. Declare the split torus Hopf structure on

Define

Using the relations

and the fact that

and

by

Lemma 6.6

(Intersection lemma)
.

(a)

Let
\widehat{A}
be the
(X_{0},\dots,X_{s-1})
-adic completion of
A
, and set

.

(i)

inside
\widehat{A}_{K}=\widehat{A}\otimes_{R}K

(ii)

inside

(b)

Let
\widehat{B}

(Y_{0},\dots,Y_{s-1})
-adic completion of

, and set

S_{B}:=\widehat{B}_{K}\widehat{\otimes}_{K}\widehat{B}_{K}
.
Then:

(i)

inside
\widehat{B}_{K}=\widehat{B}\otimes_{R}K
, one has

;

(ii)

inside
S_{B}
, one has
(B_{K}\otimes_{K}B_{K})\ \cap\ (\widehat{B}\widehat{\otimes}_{R}\widehat{B})=B\otimes_{R}B

Proof.

We prove
(a)
; the proof of
(b)
is identical (see the end).

Step 0: identify the completions.

By the discussion of coordinate rings and completions above, the
I_{\mathcal{V}}
-adic completion
\widehat{A}
identifies with

\widehat{A}\cong R\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket,\qquad\widehat{A}_{K}=\widehat{A}\otimes_{R}K\cong K\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket.

Proof of
(a)
(i).

Work inside
\widehat{A}_{K}\cong K\llbracket X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}\rrbracket
.
Let

.
Since

is the localization of
K[X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1}]
at the multiplicative set generated by
U_{0},\ldots,U_{s-1}
, we may (after clearing denominators) write

in

as

Then

and

Hence

is a unit in
\widehat{A}

Since

, we get

But

The reverse inclusion

Proof of
(a)
(ii).

Let

and
X_{i}^{(2)}:=1\otimes X_{i}
in

, and set

As above,

-adic completion. Consequently,

Inside

Now let

As above, since

is a localization of the polynomial ring

at the multiplicative set generated by the units

U_{j}^{(1)}
and
U_{j}^{(2)}
, we may (after clearing denominators) write

f=\frac{g}{u},\qquad g\in K[X_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{(1)},X_{0}^{(2)},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{(2)}],

with

Then

and

so

is a unit in

.
Since

g=f\cdot u\in\widehat{A}\widehat{\otimes}_{R}\widehat{A}\cong R\llbracket X_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{(1)},X_{0}^{(2)},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{(2)}\rrbracket.

But
g
is a polynomial over

, hence the above containment forces all its

R
, i.e.

g\in R[X_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{(1)},X_{0}^{(2)},\ldots,X_{s-1}^{(2)}].

Therefore

The reverse inclusion

Proof of
(b)
.

Replace

by

∎

Proof.

7
The Universality

In this section, we establish the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Our methodology follows standard procedures as outlined in
[
21
, Section 3]
and
[
17
, Section 6.4]
. To begin, we fix some notation. Let
R
be a finite extension of
\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}[\zeta_{p^{s}}]
, and let
\pi
be its uniformizer. Define
R_{\pi}:=R/\pi
, and denote by
\mathbb{G}_{m,R_{\pi}}
the special fiber of
\mathbb{G}_{m}:=\operatorname{Spec}R\left[X,\frac{1}{X}\right]
. We have a natural closed immersion
\mathbb{G}_{m,R_{\pi}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}
denoted by
\iota
.

For the computation of
\mathcal{W}_{1}=\operatorname{Spec}R\left[Y_{0},\frac{1}{1+\lambda Y_{0}}\right]
, the following exact sequence of sheaves on the étale site of
\operatorname{Spec}R
is particularly useful (see
[
20
, Lemma 1.1]
):

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{1}\xlongrightarrow{\alpha}\mathbb{G}_{m,R}\xlongrightarrow{\beta}\iota_{*}\mathbb{G}_{m,R_{\pi}}\longrightarrow 0

(42)

where
\alpha:x\mapsto 1+\lambda x
and
\beta:t\mapsto t\mod\pi
. The following result is derived from
[
21
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose
R
contains the
p^{s}
-th root of unity. If
B
is a flat local
R
-algebra, then

\displaystyle\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{1})=0.

Proof.

By considering the long exact sequence associated with (
42
) relative to
\spec B
, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{1}(B)\longrightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}(B)

\displaystyle=B^{*}\longrightarrow\iota_{*}\mathbb{G}_{m,R_{\pi}}(B)=(B/(\pi B))^{*}\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{1})\longrightarrow\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathbb{G}_{m})\longrightarrow\cdots

By Hilbert’s Theorem 90, we know that
\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathbb{G}_{m})=0
. Given that the map
f:B^{*}\to(B/(\pi B))^{*}
is surjective, the exactness of the long exact sequence implies that
\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{1})\cong\operatorname{coker}(f)=0
.
∎

We can employ induction by leveraging the following result.

Lemma 7.2
.

For any
s>1
, the following sequence of group schemes

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{s}\xlongrightarrow{}\mathcal{W}_{s-1}\longrightarrow 0

(43)

is exact and it fits into the following commutative diagram:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}/p}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}/p^{s-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{W}_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{W}_{s}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{W}_{s-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

Proof.

The morphism
\mathcal{W}_{s}\to\mathcal{W}_{s-1}
arises from the inclusion of the
R
-algebras that define the schemes, with the kernel given by

\spec R\left[Y_{s-1},\frac{1}{1+\lambda Y_{s-1}}\right]\cong\mathcal{W}_{1}.

Moreover, the Artin–Schreier–Witt theory establishes the desired commutative diagram.
∎

By induction, we obtain the following result.

Proposition 7.3
.

Suppose
R
contains the
p^{s}
-th root of unity. If
B
is a flat local
R
-algebra, then for every

, we obtain

\displaystyle\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s})=0.

Proof.

Assume the induction hypothesis that
\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s-1})=0
. We claim that
\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s})
vanishes. Taking the long exact sequence associated with (
43
) with respect to
\spec B
yields:

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{1}(B)\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{s}(B)\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{s-1}(B)\longrightarrow\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{1})\longrightarrow\\
\longrightarrow\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s})\longrightarrow\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s-1})\longrightarrow\cdots

Since
\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{1})=\cohom^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s-1})=0
, we get the desired result.
∎

We will now proceed to prove our main theorem, which states that the group scheme
\mathcal{W}_{s}
over
R
provides a unified framework for the Kummer–Artin–Schreier–Witt theory.

Theorem 7.4
.

Suppose
R
contains the
p^{s}
-th root of unity. If
B
is a flat local
R
-algebra, then any unramified
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-cover
\spec C\rightarrow\spec B
is given by a Cartesian diagram of the form:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\spec C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{W}_{s}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\spec B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{V}_{s}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{s}}

Proof.

Taking the long exact sequence from the Kummer sequence:

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}\longrightarrow\mathcal{W}_{s}\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}\longrightarrow 0

yields

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\mathcal{V}_{s}(B)/\mathcal{W}_{s}(B)\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(\spec B,\mathbb{Z}/p^{s})\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(\spec B,\mathcal{W}_{s})=0.

It implies that
\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}
-torsors over

are in one-to-one correspondence with elements of

\mathcal{V}_{s}(B)/\psi_{s}(\mathcal{W}_{s}(B))
.
∎

8
Matsuda’s Theory and the Refined Swan Conductor

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a complete discretely valued field with residue field
\kappa
of characteristic
p>0
and uniformizer
\pi
. Let
e
be the absolute ramification index of
\mathbb{K}
. When
\kappa
is perfect, classical ramification theory studies the behavior of finite Galois extensions in great detail. One important invariant arising from this theory is the Swan conductor
[
22
, Chapter IV]
, which measures the degree of wild ramification and has many applications in algebraic geometry.

For example, let
X
be a curve over an algebraically closed field
\kappa
, let
U\subset X
be a nonempty open subset, and let
\mathscr{F}
be a
\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}
-sheaf on
U
. Then, for each closed point
x\in X\setminus U
, the Swan conductor
\mathrm{sw}_{x}(\mathscr{F})
is a nonnegative integer measuring the wild ramification of the associated extension of
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{X,x}\cong\kappa\llbracket t\rrbracket
. The Grothendieck–Ogg–Shafarevich formula can be expressed in terms of
\mathrm{sw}_{x}(\mathscr{F})
as follows:

\chi_{c}(U_{\overline{\kappa}},\mathscr{F})=\operatorname{rank}(\mathscr{F})\cdot\chi_{c}(U_{\overline{\kappa}},\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})-\sum_{x\in X\setminus U}\mathrm{sw}_{x}(\mathscr{F}).

(44)

When the residue field is not perfect, there is an analogous notion of the

refined Swan conductor
, which may be viewed as the Swan conductor together with additional differential data. Note that the Swan conductor vanishes precisely when there is no wild ramification; in particular, in our setting this implies that the associated residue field extension is separable. When the Swan conductor is positive, one can write

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})=\pi^{-e\,\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})}\otimes\omega\in\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{-\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\kappa}.

Recall that

\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{t}:=\{x\in\mathbb{K}\mid\nu(x)\geq t\}.

The differential
\omega
is usually called the
differential Swan conductor
of
\mathscr{F}
. Introduced by Kato in 1989 for characters of degree one, this invariant has since been thoroughly studied by various authors, including Deligne, Laumon, Saito, and Abbes. They have approached the subject from different perspectives and obtained numerous applications in a wide range of contexts (see
[
15
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
). For instance, formula (
44
) extends to higher dimensions
[
9
]
and gives rise to a vanishing cycle formula
[
7
]
, a valuable tool for detecting good reduction.

The primary case of interest for the lifting problem is when
U
is an open dense subset of the closed unit disc
\mathcal{D}=\spec R\{X\}
, where
R
is a complete discrete valuation ring with residue field
\kappa
. Let
\mathbb{K}
be the function field of
\mathcal{D}
; its residue field
\kappa(x)
is imperfect.

8.1
The equal-characteristic case

When
\mathbb{K}
has equal characteristic
p>0
, the study of

\mathscr{F}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})

often suffices to understand many properties of the Swan conductor in this setting. Recall that

\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z}/p^{s})\cong W_{s}(\mathbb{K})\big/\wp\!\left(W_{s}(\mathbb{K})\right)

(see §
2.1
). In this context, we have the
refined Swan homomorphism

[
15
]
:

\begin{split}\mathrm{Rsw}:\mathrm{Fil}_{n}W_{s}(\mathbb{K})&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fil}_{n}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{K}}\big/\mathrm{Fil}_{\lfloor n/p\rfloor}\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{K}},\\
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{s-1})&\longmapsto\omega:=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}a_{i}^{p^{\,s-i-1}-1}\,da_{i},\end{split}

(45)

where
n\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
, and
\mathrm{Fil}
on the left-hand
side denotes Brylinski’s filtration of Witt vectors

[
5
]
. In particular, when
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{s-1})
is

best
(see
[
12
, Definition 2.2]
), the refined Swan
conductor is determined by
\omega
.

Theorem 8.1

(
[
8
, Lemma 3.7]
)
.

In the above notation, suppose
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{s-1})
is best. Then

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})=\omega\otimes 1=\pi^{e\nu(\omega)}\otimes\overline{\pi^{-e\nu(\omega)}\sum_{i}a_{i}^{p^{\,s-i-1}-1}\,da_{i}}\in\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{-\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\kappa},

where
\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})=-\nu(\omega)
is the Swan conductor.

This description makes it possible to compute and deduce numerous
properties of
\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})
; see

[
12
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
3
]
.

A length-one Witt vector
(a_{0})\in W_{1}(\mathbb{K})
is best if
\nu(a_{0})<0

and the reduction of
x_{0}:=\pi^{-e\nu(a_{0})}a_{0}
is not a
p
-power.

Corollary 8.2
.

In the above notation, suppose

\mathscr{F}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z}/p)

is represented by a best length-one Witt vector

(a_{0})\in\mathbb{K}
. Then

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})=da_{0}\otimes 1=\pi^{e\nu(a_{0})}\otimes d\overline{x}_{0}\in\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{-\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\kappa},

(46)

where
\mathrm{sw}(\mathscr{F})=-\nu(a_{0})
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
8.1
with
s=1
and note that for a best length-one Witt vector we have
\omega=da_{0}
,
\nu(\omega)=\nu(a_{0})
, and
d\overline{x}_{0}=\overline{\pi^{-e\nu(a_{0})}da_{0}}
.
∎

8.2
The mixed-characteristic case

There is no known algorithm to compute the refined Swan conductor of a cyclic character in mixed characteristic
(0,p)
using its Kummer class like Theorem
8.1
. The only known case is for

\mathscr{F}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z}/p)\cong\mathbb{K}^{\times}/(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{p}.

Under certain mild assumptions, we may assume that the extension
associated with
\mathscr{F}
is
fierce
(see
[
25
, §2.3]
).
We may then further assume that the corresponding Kummer element

u\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
is of one of the following forms

[
25
, Proposition 5.2]
:

1.

u\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
and

\overline{u}\notin\kappa^{p}
.

2.

u=1+\alpha^{p}w
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}
satisfying

0<\nu(\alpha)<\tfrac{1}{p-1}
, and

w\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}
such that

\overline{w}\notin\kappa^{p}
.

If
u
is of one of the above forms, we say that it is
reduced
.

Proposition 8.3

(
[
25
, Proposition 5.3]
)
.

Suppose

\mathscr{F}=\mathfrak{K}_{p}(u)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z}/p)

where
u
is reduced. Then the refined Swan conductor

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})
is given as follows.

1)

If
\overline{u}\notin\kappa^{p}

(Case
1
)
, then

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})=\lambda^{-p}\otimes\frac{d\overline{u}}{\overline{u}}.

2)

If
u=1+\alpha^{p}w

(Case
2
)
, then

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})=\alpha^{p}\lambda^{-p}\otimes d\overline{w}.

The following result recovers Corollary
8.2

in Case
2
.

Proposition 8.4
.

Suppose
\mathscr{F}=\mathfrak{K}_{p}(u)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Z}/p)
, where
u
is reduced as in Case
2
. Suppose moreover that
X_{0}\in\mathbb{K}
is the Witt vector coordinate associated with
u
. Set
x_{0}:=X_{0}\,\pi^{-e\nu(X_{0})}
. Then

\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})=-dX_{0}\otimes 1=\pi^{e\nu(X_{0})}\otimes\bigl(-d\overline{x}_{0}\bigr).

Proof.

We are in the situation of Case
2
of reduced form, so

u=1+\alpha^{p}w
. The first Witt vector coordinate is given by

X_{0}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(1+\alpha^{p}w).

We expand the terms on the right-hand side to isolate the leading term.
First, the expansion of the logarithm gives

\ln(1+\alpha^{p}w)=\alpha^{p}w+h_{1},\qquad\nu(h_{1})\geq 2p\nu(\alpha).

Second, consider the expansion
(1+\lambda)^{p}=1
, which gives
p\lambda+\lambda^{p}=-\sum_{k=2}^{p-1}\binom{p}{k}\lambda^{k}
.
If
p=2
, then this identity simply reads
2\lambda+\lambda^{2}=0
, i.e.
p\lambda=-\lambda^{p}
.
Assume now that
p>2
. The term with the lowest valuation on the right is
\binom{p}{2}\lambda^{2}
, which has valuation
1+\frac{2}{p-1}=\frac{p+1}{p-1}
.
Thus, in all cases we have the relation

p\lambda\equiv-\lambda^{p}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{\frac{p+1}{p-1}}}.

This implies

\frac{1}{p\lambda}=-\lambda^{-p}+\delta,

(47)

where the error
\delta
has valuation strictly larger than
\nu(\lambda^{-p})
.

Multiplying these expansions, we find

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=(-\lambda^{-p}+\delta)(\alpha^{p}w+h_{1})

\displaystyle=-\lambda^{-p}\alpha^{p}w+\left(-\lambda^{-p}h_{1}\,+\,\delta\alpha^{p}w\,+\,\delta h_{1}\right).

Let
C_{0}:=-\alpha^{p}\lambda^{-p}
. The main term
C_{0}w
has valuation

p\nu(\alpha)-\frac{p}{p-1}
. Since
p\nu(\alpha)>0
, one checks easily that every
term in the parenthesis has valuation strictly larger than
\nu(C_{0}w)
.
Thus, we have the key valuation inequality

\nu(X_{0}-C_{0}w)>\nu(C_{0}w)=\nu(X_{0}).

Now define the normalized unit

x_{0}:=X_{0}\pi^{-e\nu(X_{0})}.

The valuation inequality above implies that the difference
X_{0}-C_{0}w
has strictly larger valuation than
X_{0}
itself. After multiplying by
\pi^{-e\nu(X_{0})}
, we obtain an element of positive valuation, hence it vanishes in the residue field. Therefore, in
\kappa
we obtain the clean reduction

\overline{x}_{0}=\overline{C}\,\overline{w},\qquad C:=C_{0}\,\pi^{-e\nu(X_{0})}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}^{\times},

where
C
is a unit since
\overline{w}\neq 0
.
Since
\overline{C}
is a constant scalar in
\kappa^{\times}
, taking differentials gives the relation

d\overline{x}_{0}=\overline{C}\,d\overline{w}.

Substituting this into the expression for
\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})
in the statement:

\displaystyle\mathrm{rsw}(\mathscr{F})

\displaystyle=\pi^{e\nu(X_{0})}\otimes-d\overline{x}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\pi^{e\nu(X_{0})}\otimes\bigl(\overline{C}\,d\overline{w}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-C_{0}\otimes d\overline{w}\,=\,\alpha^{p}\lambda^{-p}\otimes d\overline{w}.

This matches the formula in Proposition
8.3

2)
.
∎

Remark 8.5
.

In Case
1
, we cannot compute the Witt vector
(X_{0})
associated with the Kummer class
u
, since

X_{0}=\frac{1}{p\lambda}\ln(u),

and
\ln(u)
is not defined when
u\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{K}}^{\times}
with
\overline{u}\notin\kappa^{p}
. Nevertheless, we may differentiate formally, writing
d\!\ln(u)=\tfrac{du}{u}
. In the filtered module
\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\kappa}
, we obtain

-dX_{0}\otimes 1=-\frac{1}{p\lambda}\frac{du}{u}\otimes 1.

The relation (
47
) implies

\frac{1}{p\lambda}\equiv-\lambda^{-p}\pmod{\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{K}}^{\frac{p+1}{p-1}}}.

Substituting yields the reduction

-dX_{0}\otimes 1=\lambda^{-p}\otimes\frac{d\overline{u}}{\overline{u}},

which agrees with Proposition
8.3

1)
.

Taken together, these computations indicate that the description provided by Theorem
8.1
should admit a mixed-characteristic analogue, with the Witt vector
-(X_{0},\ldots,X_{s-1})
playing the role of
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{s-1})
. Developing such an extension would offer a unified perspective on refined Swan conductors across characteristics. This direction is the subject of our ongoing work.
