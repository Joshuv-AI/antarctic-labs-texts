Preserved Limbic White Matter Predicts Favorable 2‐Year Outcomes in Neonatal Hypoxic‐Ischemic Encephalopathy
Authors: Xingyu Chen, Shuhei Tomoshige, Ria Wright, Vera J Burton, Gwendolyn Gerner, Nathanael Kuo, Jill S Chotiyanonta, Raul Chavez‐Valdez, Ernest M Graham, Frances J Northington, Kenichi Oishi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAccurate prediction of both favorable and unfavorable long‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes of neonatal hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) is essential for clinical care, yet the brain structures underlying a favorable prognosis remain unclear. This study aimed to identify diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures that can predict the 2‐year outcomes in neonates with HIE.MethodsWe analyzed whole‐brain diffusion MRI scans from a retrospective cohort of 40 neonates with HIE, acquired within 14 days of birth. Diffusion measures were quantified across 16 cerebrum regions covering the whole cerebrum. We used the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC) to evaluate the performance of regional fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) values in predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed at 2 years of age.ResultsReduced FA in the thalamus (AUC = 0.990), basal ganglia (AUC = 0.961), basal forebrain (AUC = 0.951), and limbic white matter (AUC = 0.961) strongly predicted severe disability or death, whereas preserved limbic white matter FA uniquely predicted favorable outcomes (AUC = 0.811).ConclusionWhile FA reduction in the thalamus, basal ganglia, and basal forebrain is a robust marker for severe outcomes, the limbic white matter demonstrated sensitivity to mild hypoxic‐ischemic injury. Therefore, the preservation can be a potential marker for predicting favorable neurodevelopmental trajectories.Keywords: diffusion tensor imaging, hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy, limbic system, neurodevelopmental outcome

Introduction

Neonatal hypoxic‐ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) results from perinatal reduction in cerebral oxygen and blood flow. Despite the use of therapeutic hypothermia, the only established treatment, HIE remains a leading cause of long‐term neurodevelopmental impairment, including cerebral palsy, epilepsy, and cognitive deficits [1]. Accurate prediction of neurodevelopmental outcomes is essential to stratifying for future adjunctive therapies, guiding rehabilitation, informing parental counseling, and optimizing healthcare resource allocation.

Previous studies have shown that the severity and location of brain injury on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) correlate with long‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes. Semi‐quantitative scoring systems, including those developed by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Neonatal Research Network (NICHD‐NRN) and others, highlight the prognostic importance of lesions in the thalamus, posterior limb of the internal capsule, and basal ganglia. Diffusion MRI (dMRI), which quantifies the microstructural integrity of white matter using metrics such as fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) [2, 3], has further refined prognostic models. FA decreases in the basal ganglia and thalamus during the first week in moderate to severe HIE [4, 5], and diffusion metrics from these regions are predictive for adverse outcomes, with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.73 [6, 7].

Most studies have adopted hypothesis‐driven region‐of‐interest (ROI) approaches, overlooking the prognostic value of other anatomical regions not historically associated with HIE. Whole‐brain, data‐driven methods such as tract‐based spatial statistics address some of these issues but fail to capture smaller white matter pathways and gray matter structures, limiting their scope [8]. Additionally, these studies often focus exclusively on predicting severe neurodevelopmental impairment, rather than identifying infants who will go on to develop typically. The ability to predict typical neurodevelopment trajectories is equally important for reassuring families, avoiding unnecessary interventions, and ensuring appropriate follow‐up for infants at risk.

To overcome these limitations, we proposed a whole‐cerebrum parcellation approach [9] and compared structural features between infants with imperceivable neurodevelopmental deficits and impaired neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18–24 months using a clinically interpretable cutoff‐based classification strategy. By evaluating both favorable and unfavorable trajectories, we provide a reproducible and clinically applicable framework for long‐term outcome prediction.

Methods

Participants

The study included participants from the Biomarkers in Neonates study (R01HD110091 and R01HD086058) data set, a cohort of neonates diagnosed with HIE based on the NICHD‐HIE Neonatal Research Network criteria [10] born between 2009 and 2019. The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Review Board, and written consent was obtained from the participants' legal guardians. Inclusion criteria were neonates born at ≥ 35 weeks of gestation, ≥ 1800 g of weight at birth, who received 72 h of therapeutic hypothermia, and who underwent brain dMRI within 14 days of birth. Among the initial cohort of 143 cases, dMRIs with recorded scanning error (n = 3) or those that did not meet the data harmonization criteria (different b value, n = 7; large voxel size, n = 5) were excluded. Among the 130 patients who remained, 40 had subsequent follow‐up at 2 years of age in our affiliated neurodevelopmental follow‐up clinic (Table 1).

MRI Quantification

The 40 participants were scanned at a median chronological age of 7 days and a median postmenstrual age of 40 weeks (Table 1), with further details in Supporting Information: Table 1. The dMRI images were parcellated into 44 anatomical regions according to the JHU‐MNI atlas Level 3 criteria [11] using OpenMAP‐Di (https://github.com/OishiLab/OpenMAP-Di), a deep‐learning model for neonatal dMRI parcellation. After excluding cerebrospinal fluid, ventricles, and brainstem regions, we included 16 cerebral regions (Supporting Information: Figure 1 and Table 2) from both the left and right hemispheres in our analysis. Since the injury pattern in neonatal HIE, including the putamina, thalami, and posterior limbs of the internal capsule, as well as parasagittal watershed regions, is typically bilateral and symmetric [12, 13, 14], we calculated the average of FA and MD measurements for each region from both hemispheres to minimize the minor asymmetries variabilities and labeling noise. In a secondary analysis, the left and right region was analyzed separately, as unilateral or asymmetric injury may have prognostic implications distinct from those of symmetric bilateral injury.

The data were harmonized using ComBat [15] to account for different scanning parameters, including scanner models (Aera, Avanto, Espree, Skyra, and TrioTim), voxel size (2.5 mm3 and less vs. greater than 2.5 mm3), and magnetic field strength (1.5 T vs. 3 T).

Neurodevelopmental Assessment

Participants were categorized into three outcome groups based on neurodevelopmental assessments at 2 years using the Capute Scales or Mullen Scales or both [16, 17], which evaluate cognitive development, and Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) for those diagnosed with cerebral palsy (CP). An imperceivable neurodevelopmental deficit was defined as a developmental quotient (DQ) of 85 or higher with no diagnosis of CP. Mild/moderate disability included a DQ of 51–84 and/or a diagnosis of CP with a GMFCS Level 1–3. Severe disability was a DQ below 50 and/or a diagnosis of CP with a GMFCS Level 4 or 5, indicating an inability to walk even with assistive devices, or death. If both Capute and Mullen scores were available with no discrepancy, the Mullen DQ was used for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (3.12.7). FA and MD values for each anatomical region were compared across groups using the Kruskal–Wallis analysis of variance (KW‐ANOVA) test, as the data did not follow a normal distribution. For regions with significant differences, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate predictive performance, and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Threshold values were tested to differentiate between the following pairs: (1) imperceivable versus all other outcomes and (2) severe disability or death versus all other outcomes. Statistical significance of AUC values was assessed using Mann–Whitney U. The p values of the KW‐ANOVA and AUC curves were strictly adjusted by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The single optimal cutoff point was determined by Youden's J index.

Results

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the study cohort with an average follow‐up of 26 months.

Among the 16 regions, mean FA values obtained from four brain regions showed significant differences between severe/death and other groups, including the basal forebrain, basal ganglia, thalamus, and limbic white matter regions, including the cingulum bundle, fornix, and stria terminalis. None of the mean MD values of the 16 regions were different between the severe/death and other, or the imperceivable and other group. When predicting severe outcomes or death (Figure 1 and Table 2), the thalamus achieves the highest AUC of 0.990, while other regions also show high prediction capabilities (basal ganglia: AUC = 0.961, limbic white matter: AUC = 0.961, basal forebrain: AUC = 0.951). For predicting imperceivable neurodevelopmental complications, these same regions showed modest AUC values ranging from 0.653 to 0.736. Among them, limbic white matter was the only structure with the ability to predict imperceivable disability at 2 years (AUC = 0.811, p = 0.038).

The NICHD scores achieved an AUC of 0.984 (sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.875 at a cutoff of ≥ 1B) for predicting severe outcomes, which is comparable to the AUC of FA quantification. However, the score shows less predictive power compared with the limbic white matter when predicting imperceivable disabilities. The predictive power of APGAR (imperceivable vs. rest AUC = 0.496, severe/death vs. rest AUC = 0.550) and Sarnat score (imperceivable vs. rest AUC = 0.668, severe/death vs. rest AUC = 0.713) showed limited association with neurodevelopmental outcome, which is not unexpected given that neither was designed for use in this manner.

When analyzing the left and right hemispheres independently (Supporting Information: Table 3), the FA value of the bilateral thalamus (left side: p = 0.028, right side: p = 0.033) and the limbic white matter (left side: p = 0.019, right side: p = 0.017) showed significantly different values across outcome groups. Only the right side of the limbic white matter was significantly associated with imperceivable outcome (AUC = 0.832, p = 0.034).

Discussion

Using dMRI, we parcellated the cerebrum into 16 regions and quantified FA and MD, identifying four regions predictive of 2‐year neurodevelopmental outcome. Limbic system injury occurred even in milder cases, whereas thalamic, basal ganglia, and basal forebrain injury were more indicative of severe outcomes. FA is sensitive to early disruptions in fiber coherence after hypoxic‐ischemic injury, whereas MD tends to pseudonormalize between 7 and 12 days of life [4]. This differential behavior likely explains the stronger correlations of FA with neurodevelopmental outcomes in our cohort.

While basal ganglia and thalamic injury are well‐established markers of poor prognosis, this study highlights a potential role for the basal forebrain [18] and limbic system [19, 20], which are vulnerable in animal HIE models but rarely emphasized in human imaging studies. The data‐driven approach adopted in this study, which simultaneously targeted both gray and white matter structures, suggests that injuries to these regions, traditionally overlooked in clinical research, are significantly associated with long‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes in human neonates with HIE.

The basal forebrain contains the nucleus basalis of Meynert, a major source of widespread cholinergic projections. Because the cholinergic system modulates global neural excitability and attention, we can speculate that injury to this region may contribute to the attentional deficits and generalized cognitive impairments seen in HIE [18].

This study uniquely identified the limbic system as critical for neurodevelopment, with reduced FA even in mildly impaired infants, but preservation associated with favorable outcomes. Consistent with animal models, its vulnerability to hypoxic‐ischemic injury may reflect the sensitivity of oligodendrocyte progenitors to excitotoxicity and oxidative stress [21]. Early injury to these tracts, which support emotional, memory, and learning functions [22], might be associated with adverse outcomes in both neonatal HIE and pediatric cardiac arrest [23].

When analyzing the right and left sides separately, right FA showed a stronger association with favorable neurodevelopmental outcomes than left FA. This finding is noteworthy because there is no clear evidence of one side being intrinsically more fragile. Left‐sided lesions may exert a stronger influence on scores in the language‐related subscales of the Capute and Mullen assessments than right‐sided lesions, which contradicts our findings. These lateralization findings are intriguing but should be interpreted cautiously due to the small sample size and the exploratory nature of the analysis.

This study is limited by a retrospective study design, a small number of participants, and class imbalance, as many participants (25/40) had imperceivable neurodevelopmental complications, which may not represent the general neonatal HIE population. Furthermore, although we performed data harmonization to account for different scanner models and parameters, residual variability from the imaging data acquisition remains a potential limitation. The timing of dMRI at a median of 7 days post‐birth also presents a limitation, as this may coincide with the period of MD value pseudonormalization, potentially affecting the sensitivity of this metric. Finally, although our protocol standardized their application, the reliance on two different neurodevelopmental scoring systems is another potential source of measurement variability in outcome classification. Therefore, validation in larger, prospective cohorts is warranted to confirm these findings.

In conclusion, a unique contribution of this study is its suggestion of the critical role of the limbic system in neurodevelopment, with reduced FA even in mildly impaired infants, yet preservation associated with favorable outcomes. Identifying a specific biomarker for favorable outcomes provides a powerful tool for reassuring families and tailoring long‐term neurodevelopmental follow‐up in clinical practice.
