A mathematical analysis of the discretized IPT-DMFT equations
E. Cancès, A. Kirsch, S. Perrin–Roussel

Abstract
In a previous contribution (E. Cancès, A. Kirsch and S. Perrin–Roussel, arXiv:2406.03384), we have proven the existence of a solution to the Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT) equations under the Iterated Perturbation Theory (IPT-DMFT) approximation. In view of numerical simulations, these equations need to be discretized. In this article, we are interested in a discretization of the IPT - DMFT functional equations, based on the restriction of the hybridization function and local self-energy to a finite number of points in the upper half-plane ( i ​ ω n ) n ∈ [ [ 0 , N ω ] ] \left(i\omega_{n}\right)_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]} , where ω n = ( 2 ​ n + 1 ) ​ π / β \omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi/\beta is the n n -th Matsubara frequency and N ω ∈ ℕ N_{\omega}\in\mathbb{N} . We first prove the existence of solutions to the discretized equations in some parameter range depending on N ω N_{\omega} . We then prove uniqueness for a smaller range of parameters. We also study more in depth the case of bipartite systems exhibiting particle-hole symmetry. In this case, the discretized IPT-DMFT equations have purely imaginary solutions, which can be obtained by solving a real algebraic system of ( N ω + 1 ) (N_{\omega}+1) equations with ( N ω + 1 ) (N_{\omega}+1) variables. We provide a complete characterization of the solutions for N ω = 0 N_{\omega}=0 and some results for N ω = 1 N_{\omega}=1 in the simple case of the Hubbard dimer. We finally present some numerical simulations on the Hubbard dimer. First, using the TRIQS Python library [ 29 ] , we describe how a conductor-to-insulator transition occurs in the Matsubara frequency discretized IPT-DMFT model in the small temperature regime. Finally, using a homemade Julia code allowing arbitrary finite-arithmetic precision, we show that for some parameters ( U , T ) (U,T) of the considered Hubbard model, this discretization method may provide values of the Green’s function at the lowest Matsubara frequencies which cannot be interpolated by a Pick function.

2
Matsubara frequency discretization of IPT-DMFT equations

2.1
Derivation of the IPT-DMFT equations

To make this article as self-contained as possible, let us start with a concise mathematical introduction to DMFT and the IPT approximation, and recall the derivation of Equations (
1
)-(
2
). If
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are Hilbert spaces, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})
the space of bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
, by
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y})
the space of bounded antilinear operators from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
, and we use the usual shorthand notation
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})
. Lastly, we denote by
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{X})
the space of self-adjoint operators on
\mathcal{X}
.

Consider an isolated fermionic quantum system with one-particle state space
\mathcal{H}
, which we assume finite-dimensional for simplicity. We denote respectively by

{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}):=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{N=0}^{\dim(\mathcal{H})}\bigwedge^{N}\mathcal{H},\qquad\widehat{a}_{\bullet}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H};\mathcal{L}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}))),\qquad\widehat{a}_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{H};\mathcal{L}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}))),\qquad\widehat{H}\in\mathcal{S}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})),

the Fock space, the creation and annihilation operators in the Schrödinger picture, and the Hamiltonian of the system in second quantization formalism. Recall that the creation and annihilation operators satisfy the
Canonical Anti-commutation Relations (CAR)
:

\forall\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H},\quad\{\widehat{a}_{\phi}^{\dagger},\widehat{a}_{\phi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=0,\quad\{\widehat{a}_{\phi},\widehat{a}_{\phi^{\prime}}\}=0,\quad\{\widehat{a}_{\phi},\widehat{a}_{\phi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\}=\langle\phi,\phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

We assume here that
\widehat{H}
is particle-number conserving.

The time-ordered one-body Green’s function associated to a quantum system with one-particle state space
\mathcal{H}
(assume
\dim(\mathcal{H})<\infty
for simplicity) and an equilibrium state
\widehat{\Gamma}
is the bounded function
G^{\rm TO}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{t};\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
of the time variable
t
with values in
{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{H})
defined by

\forall\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H},\quad\langle\phi,iG^{\rm TO}(t)\phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:={\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{\Gamma}\;\mathcal{T}\left(\widehat{a}_{\phi},\widehat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi^{\prime}}\right)(t,0)\right),

where
\mathcal{T}
is the time-ordering operator and
\widehat{a}_{\bullet}(\cdot)^{\dagger}
,
\widehat{a}_{\bullet}(\cdot)
the creation/annihilation operators in Heisenberg picture, that is

\langle\phi,iG^{\rm TO}(t)\phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{\Gamma}\;\widehat{a}_{\phi}(t)\widehat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi^{\prime}}(0)\right),&\mbox{ if }t\geq 0,\\
-{\rm Tr}\left(\widehat{\Gamma}\;\widehat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi^{\prime}}(0)\widehat{a}_{\phi}(t)\right),&\mbox{ if }t<0,\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widehat{a}_{\phi}(t)=e^{it\widehat{H}}\widehat{a}_{\phi}e^{-it\widehat{H}},\quad\widehat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi^{\prime}}(0)=\widehat{a}^{\dagger}_{\phi^{\prime}}.

By definition, an equilibrium state is a steady state of the quantum Liouville equation
i\partial_{t}\widehat{\Gamma}(t)=[\widehat{H},\widehat{\Gamma}(t)]
, that is an operator
\widehat{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{L}({\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}))
satisfying the mixed-state representability conditions
\widehat{\Gamma}^{\dagger}=\widehat{\Gamma}
,
\widehat{\Gamma}\geq 0
, and
{\rm Tr}(\widehat{\Gamma})=1
, and commuting with the Hamiltonian, i.e. such that
[\widehat{H},\widehat{\Gamma}]=0
. In practice,
\widehat{\Gamma}
is often an
N
-particle ground state (microcanonical equilibrium state), a Gibbs state in the
N
-particle sector of the Fock space (canonical equilibrium state), or a Gibbs state for the modified Hamiltonian
\mathcal{K}=\widehat{H}-\varepsilon_{F}\widehat{N}
(grand canonical equilibrium state), where
\varepsilon_{F}
is a chemical potential and
\widehat{N}
the number operator. Note that in all these cases,
\widehat{\Gamma}
commutes with
\widehat{N}
, an assumption we make from now on. Since
\widehat{H}
,
\widehat{\Gamma}
and
\widehat{N}
pairwise commute (recall that
\widehat{H}
is assumed to be particle-number conserving), they can be diagonalized in the same orthonormal basis
\{\Psi^{N}_{n}\ ;\ 0\leq N\leq\dim(\mathcal{H}),\,1\leq n\leq\tiny{\begin{pmatrix}\dim(\mathcal{H})\\
N\end{pmatrix}}\}
of the Fock space:

\widehat{N}\Psi^{N}_{n}=N\Psi^{N}_{n},\quad\widehat{H}\Psi^{N}_{n}=E^{N}_{n}\Psi^{N}_{n},\quad\widehat{\Gamma}\Psi^{N}_{n}=f^{N}_{n}\Psi^{N}_{n},

where
E^{N}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
is the energy of the state
\Psi^{N}_{n}\in\displaystyle\bigwedge^{N}\mathcal{H}\subset{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})
and
0\leq f^{N}_{n}\leq 1
its occupation number in the equilibrium state
\widehat{\Gamma}
. With this notation
\Psi^{0}_{0}
is the vacuum of the Fock space
{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})
.

In many applications, it is more relevant from a physical point of view to work in the frequency domain and focus instead on the time Fourier transform
G^{\rm TO}_{\rm freq}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}_{\omega};\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
of
G^{\rm TO}
. However, this object has singularities (it is in general an order-
1
tempered distribution) and it is more convenient to consider the Generalized Fourier Transform
G:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
of
G^{\rm TO}
in the upper half-plane. It indeed turns out that the function
G
has very interesting mathematical properties. In particular, it is the negative of an operator-valued Pick function
[
26
,
31
]
which means that it is analytic on
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and satisfies

\Im(-G(z)):=-\frac{G(z)-G(z)^{\dagger}}{2i}\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. It readily follows from basic properties of analytic functions that for any non-constant Pick function
f
,
\Im(f(z))>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
. This implies that for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
,
\Im(-G(z))
is in fact positive definite, so that
G(z)
is invertible.

In addition,
G(z)
vanishes when
\Im(z)\to+\infty
, so that it follows from the Nevanlinna-Riesz representation theorem
[
11
]
that

\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},\quad G(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon)}{z-\varepsilon},

where
\mathcal{A}
is an operator-valued Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\forall B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\mathcal{A}(B)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}),\quad 0\leq\mathcal{A}(B)\leq 1,\quad\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{R})=I_{\mathcal{H}},

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.
The operator-valued measure
\mathcal{A}
is called the spectral function of the system in the equilibrium state
\widehat{\Gamma}
under consideration. Another useful expression of
G
is its Källen–Lehmann representation, obtained by expanding the operators
\widehat{\Gamma}
and
\widehat{H}
in the orthonormal basis
(\Psi^{N}_{n})
diagonalizing both of them. We indeed have

\langle\phi,G(z)\phi^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{N=0}^{\tiny{\dim(\mathcal{H})-1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\tiny{\begin{pmatrix}\dim(\mathcal{H})\\
N\end{pmatrix}}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{\tiny{\begin{pmatrix}\dim(\mathcal{H})\\
N+1\end{pmatrix}}}\frac{f^{N}_{n}+f^{N+1}_{n^{\prime}}}{z-(E^{N+1}_{n^{\prime}}-E^{N}_{n})}\langle\Psi^{N}_{n},\widehat{a}_{\phi}\Psi^{N+1}_{n^{\prime}}\rangle_{{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})}\langle\Psi^{N+1}_{n^{\prime}},\widehat{a}_{\phi^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\Psi^{N}_{n}\rangle_{{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})}.

The Källen–Lehmann representation reveals that
G
is a rational function of
z
, the poles of which are some of the excitation energies of the system. More precisely, if
\Gamma
is the
N
-particle ground state, the poles of
G
are the excitation energies
E^{N+1}_{n}-E^{N}_{0}
(one-particle affinities) and
E^{N}_{0}-E^{N_{1}}_{n}
(one-particle ionization energies). The same holds for the canonical Gibbs state in the
N
-particle sector.

In the special case of a non-interacting system, i.e. if the Hamiltonian is given by
\widehat{H}^{0}=d\Gamma(H^{0})
for some hermitian operator
H^{0}
on
\mathcal{H}
, the Green’s function and the spectral function are given by

G^{0}(z)=(z-H^{0})^{-1},\quad\mathcal{A}_{0}(\bullet)=\Pi^{H^{0}}_{\bullet},

where
\Pi^{H^{0}}_{\bullet}
is the spectral projector of
H^{0}
. In particular,
G^{0}
is independent of
\widehat{\Gamma}
(in contrast with the interacting case) and is simply the restriction to
\mathbb{C}_{+}
of the resolvent of
H_{0}
.

Let us now consider a Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}
on
{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H})
of the form

\widehat{H}=d\Gamma(H^{0})+\widehat{H}^{\rm I}\qquad\mbox{(non-interacting part + interacting part)},

and
G:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
the Green’s function of
\widehat{H}
for some equilibrium state
\Gamma
. The self-energy is the function
\Sigma_{\rm g}:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
(the subscript
g
stands for global, in contrast with local, see below) defined by

\Sigma_{\rm g}(z):=G^{0}(z)^{-1}-G(z)^{-1}\qquad\mbox{or}\qquad G(z)=\left(z-\left(H^{0}+\Sigma_{\rm g}(z)\right)\right)^{-1}.

Note that
-\Sigma
also is an operator-valued Pick function. For later purposes, we introduce the functional
g

[g(\Sigma)](z)=(z-(H^{0}+\Sigma(z))^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})

which is well-defined on the set of negatives of operator-valued Pick functions from
\mathbb{C}_{+}
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and such that
G=g(\Sigma_{\rm g})
.

The Anderson impurity model (AIM) is a fermionic quantum many-body model with a special structure. The one-particle state space is of the form

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{AIM}}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\rm bath},

with
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp})
“small” and
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{\rm bath})
potentially large or even infinite, and the interactions are localized in the impurity component. As a consequence, the AIM Hamiltonian is of the form

\widehat{H}_{\rm AIM}=d\Gamma(H^{0}_{\rm AIM})+\widehat{H}_{\rm imp}^{\rm I}\otimes 1_{{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}{\rm bath})},\qquad H^{0}_{\rm AIM}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H^{0}_{\rm i}&H^{0}_{\rm ib}\\
{H^{0}_{\rm ib}}^{\dagger}&H^{0}_{\rm b}\end{array}\right)

(recall that with
{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm AIM})={\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp})\otimes{\rm Fock}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm bath})
).
It can be shown for finite-dimensional bath
[
23
,
5
]
that the AIM self-energy for the Gibbs state
Z^{-1}e^{-\beta(\widehat{H}_{\rm AIM}-\mu\widehat{N}_{\rm frag})}
is of the form

\Sigma_{\beta,\mu}(z)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{{\rm i},\beta,\mu}(z)&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Sigma_{{\rm i},\beta,\mu}=\Sigma^{\rm AIM}_{{\rm imp},\beta,\mu}\left(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp},H^{0}_{\rm i},\Delta,\widehat{H}_{\rm imp}^{\rm I}\right),

where
\Delta:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp})
is the
hybridization function

\Delta(z):=H^{0}_{\rm ib}(z-H^{0}_{\rm b})^{-1}{H^{0}_{\rm ib}}^{\dagger}.

Note that
\Delta
is the negative of an operator-valued Pick function as well. It is assumed in the physics literature that this also holds true for infinite-dimensional baths. For later purposes, we introduce the functional
g_{\rm imp}^{\rm AIM}
defined by

[g_{\rm imp}^{\rm AIM}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp},H^{0}_{\rm i},\Delta,\Sigma_{\rm i})](z)\!\!:=\!\!\!\!\ \left(\!z\!-\!\left(H^{0}_{\rm i}+\Delta(z)+\Sigma_{\rm i}(z)\right)\right)^{-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!=\!\!\left(\!z\!-\!\left(\!\!H_{\rm AIM}^{0}\!+\!\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{\rm i}(z)&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right)\right)^{-1}\!\bigg|_{\mathcal{H}{\rm imp}},

which is well-defined for
H^{0}_{\rm i}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp})
and
\Sigma_{\rm i}
,
\Delta
negatives of operator-valued Pick functions from
\mathbb{C}_{+}
to
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp})
. The impurity block of the exact Green’s function of the AIM for the considered Gibbs state is given by

G_{\rm AIM}|_{\mathcal{H}{\rm imp}}=g_{\rm imp}^{\rm AIM}(\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp},H^{0}_{\rm i},\Delta,\Sigma_{\rm i}).

DMFT is a quantum embedding method in which the system is decomposed in non-overlapping fragments, each of them being seen as an impurity embedded in a bath accounting in the presence of the other fragments. In mathematical terms, the system state space
\mathcal{H}
is decomposed as a direct sum of pairwise orthogonal impurity subspaces:

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp}^{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp}^{M}

and we denote by

H^{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}H^{0}_{j}&H^{0}_{j\bar{j}}\\
{H^{0}_{j\bar{j}}}^{\dagger}&H^{0}_{\bar{j}}\end{array}\right),\qquad\widehat{H}^{\rm I}=\widehat{H}^{\rm I}_{j}+\widehat{H}^{\rm I}_{j\bar{j}}+\widehat{H}^{\rm I}_{\bar{j}}

the block representation of
H^{0}
according to the decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{j}_{\rm frag}\oplus(\mathcal{H}^{j}_{\rm frag})^{\perp}
and the corresponding decomposition of the interaction Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}^{\rm I}
.

As other quantum embedding methods, such as DMET, DMFT can be seen as a fixed-point problem on a set of low-level descriptors of the state of the whole system. In DMFT, this set can be chosen as a set of block-diagonal approximations of the global self-energy

\mathfrak{S}_{\rm DMFT}:=\left\{\Sigma=\left(\begin{matrix}\Sigma_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\Sigma_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\Sigma_{M}\end{matrix}\right),\;\Sigma_{j}:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{j}_{\rm imp}),-\Sigma_{j}\mbox{ operator-valued Pick function}\right\}

The fixed-point iteration goes in two steps:

1.

AIM construction: given
\Sigma^{\rm in}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\rm DMFT}
, for each
j
, construct an AIM such that

g\left(\Sigma^{\rm in}\right)\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{\rm imp}^{j}}=g_{\rm imp}^{\rm AIM}\left(\mathcal{H}{\rm imp}^{j},H^{0}_{j},\Delta_{j}^{\rm out},\Sigma_{j}^{\rm in}\right)

This does not uniquely define the AIM, but this does uniquely define
\Delta_{j}^{\rm out}
:

\Delta_{j}^{\rm out}(z)=H^{0}_{j\bar{j}}\left(z-\left(H^{0}_{\bar{j}}+\bigoplus_{k\neq j}\Sigma^{\rm in}_{k}(z)\right)\right)^{-1}{H^{0}_{j\bar{j}}}^{\dagger};

In physical terms, the hybridization function
\Delta_{j}
encapsulates the effects of the environment of the
j
-th cluster and allows one to consider the latter as an impurity embedded in a bath.

2.

Impurity solver: for each of these AIMs, compute an updated
\Sigma^{\rm out}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\rm DMFT}
by

\Sigma_{j}^{\rm out}:=\Sigma^{\rm AIM}_{{\rm imp},\beta,\mu}\left(\mathcal{H}^{j}_{\rm imp},H^{0}_{j},\Delta_{j}^{\rm out},\widehat{H}^{\rm I}_{j}\right).

In physical terms, for each cluster, compute the self-energy of the associated AIM, and concatenate the so-obtained solutions in the updated low-level descriptor
\Sigma^{\rm out}
.

We now restrict ourselves to a translation-invariant Hubbard model with
L<\infty
sites, single-site impurities, and paramagnetic setting at half-filling. In this case

\quad M=L,\quad\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\rm imp}\cong\cdots\cong\mathcal{H}^{L}_{\rm imp}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad\Delta_{1}=\cdots=\Delta_{L}=\Delta{\mathds{1}}_{2},\quad\Sigma_{1}=\cdots=\Sigma_{L}=\Sigma{\mathds{1}}_{2}.

In addition, we assume that the AIMs are solved by Iterative Perturbation Theory:

[\Sigma^{\rm AIM-IPT}_{{\rm imp},\beta}(\Delta,U)](z)=U^{2}[F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}(\Delta)](z)\mathds{1}_{2},

where
F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}
is derived by perturbation theory at second-order in the on-site interaction parameter
U
. As we will see on Eq. (
8
) and the comments below,
F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}(\Delta)
can be conveniently characterized by its values on the imaginary Matsubara frequencies.

2.2
Matsubara frequency (MaF) discretization

As explained above, the Matsubara frequency (MaF) discretization of IPT-DMFT equations aims at seeking approximations
(\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{\Sigma})\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}\times-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
of the values of the hybridization function
\Delta
and local self-energy
\Sigma
at the lowest
(N_{\omega}+1)
imaginary Matsubara frequencies:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}=\left(\Delta_{n}\right)_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]},\quad\Delta_{n}\simeq\Delta(i\omega_{n}),\qquad\mathbf{\Sigma}=\left(\Sigma_{n}\right)_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]},\quad\Sigma_{n}\simeq\Sigma(i\omega_{n}),

(5)

for some Matsubara frequency cutoff
N_{\omega}\in\mathbb{N}
. To formulate the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations, it is convenient to set

\Delta_{-(n+1)}=\overline{\Delta_{n}}\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}},\quad\Sigma_{-(n+1)}=\overline{\Sigma_{n}}\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}

(6)

in accordance with the conventional extension of a Pick function to the lower half-plane
[
11
]

\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{-}:=-\mathbb{C}_{+},\quad f(z):=\overline{f(\overline{z})},

(7)

and the fact that
\omega_{-(n+1)}=-\omega_{n}
.

The choice of the collocation points
\left(i\omega_{n}\right)_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]}
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, represented in Figure
2
, is motivated by two reasons.
The first one is very generic. The imaginary Matsubara frequencies are the poles of the Fermi–Dirac function at inverse temperature
\beta
and therefore are natural collocation points when evaluating physical observables at Gibbs equilibrium using complex-plane contour integral techniques.
The second reason is related to the expression of the IPT solver. The latter is formally defined at the imaginary Matsubara frequencies by the formula

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}(\Delta)(i\omega_{n})=\int_{0}^{\beta}e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-i\omega_{n^{\prime}}\tau}\left(i\omega_{n^{\prime}}-\Delta(i\omega_{n^{\prime}})\right)^{-1}\right)^{3}d\tau,

(8)

where
\Delta
is extended to negative imaginary Matsubara frequencies according to (
7
).
It is well-know that an analytic function defined on an open subset
\Omega
of the complex plane is fully characterized by its values on a countable number of points of
\Omega
with an accumulation point in
\Omega
. But as the sequence of imaginary Matsubara frequencies
\left(i\omega_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has no accumulation point in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, it is not clear that (
8
) fully defines
F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}
. It is however the case if the function
\Delta
is the negative of a meromorphic Pick function with a finite number of poles. It can then be shown that the so-defined map can be uniquely extended by (weak-)continuity to a function
F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}
from the set of physically-admissible hybridization function to the set of physically-admissible local self-energies. We refer to
[
5
]
for more details on this point.

In view of (
8
), a natural MaF discretization of (
2

\mathbf{\Sigma}=U^{2}F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta,N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}),

with

\forall n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!],\quad[F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta,N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta})]_{n}:=\int_{0}^{\beta}e^{i\omega_{n}\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{n^{\prime}=-(N_{\omega}+1)}^{N_{\omega}}e^{-i\omega_{n^{\prime}}\tau}\left(i\omega_{n^{\prime}}-\Delta_{n^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\right)^{3}d\tau.

(9)

Note that the discretization of (
2

\Sigma_{n}
depends on
\left(\Delta_{n^{\prime}}\right)_{n^{\prime}\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]}
and not only on
\Delta_{n}
.

(comment on the set of admissible solutions)

3
Existence and uniqueness results

In this section, we present our theoretical results regarding the solution(s) to the dimensionless MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations (

)–(
20

3.1
Existence of solutions

Using Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem
[
35
]
, we are able to show that (
10
)–(
11
) admit a solution, but only within a specific parameter range, except for
N_{\omega}=0
. This limitation arises from the fact that, in general and for
N_{\omega}>0
, the map
F_{N_{\omega}}:\mathbb{C}^{N_{\omega}+1}\to\mathbb{C}^{N_{\omega}+1}
does not map
-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
onto itself. For instance, for
N_{\omega}=1
and
\mathbf{\Delta}=(-i\pi x_{0},-3i\pi x_{1})\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{2}
with
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

F_{1}(\mathbf{\Delta})_{1}=-\frac{i}{\left((1+x_{0})\pi\right)^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{9}\left(\frac{1+x_{0}}{1+x_{1}}\right)^{3}+2\frac{1+x_{0}}{1+x_{1}}-1\right)\xrightarrow{x_{1}\to+\infty}\frac{i}{\left((1+x_{0})\pi\right)^{3}}\notin-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}.

(23)

Such a problem does not hold for
N_{\omega}=0
, for which we have

\forall\Delta\in-\mathbb{C}_{+},\quad F_{0}(\Delta)=\frac{3}{|i\pi-\Delta|^{2}(i\pi-\Delta)}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}.

(24)

Theorem 3.1

(Existence of solution to the discretized
IPT
-
DMFT
equations.)
.

Let
N_{\omega}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and

C_{N_{\omega}}:=\left\{\bm{z}=(z_{n})_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}\;\big|\;|z_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{(2n+1)\pi}\right\}.

(25)

There exists
t_{N_{\omega}}>0
depending only on
N_{\omega}
such that for all

0\leq t\leq\frac{t_{N_{\omega}}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}}
, the MaF-discretized
IPT
-
DMFT
equations (
19
)–(
20
) have a solution

(\mathbf{\Delta},\mathbf{\Sigma})\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}\times u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}\right)\subset-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}\times-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1},

(26)

where
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
is given by

\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}=t^{2}\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})C_{N_{\omega}}=\left\{\bm{z}=(z_{n})_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}\;\big|\;|z_{n}|\leq\frac{t^{2}\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}{(2n+1)\pi}\right\}.

(27)

Remark 3.2

(on the non-interacting setting

)
.

As discussed in the next section, the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations admit a unique solution for

\forall n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!],\quad\Sigma_{n}=0,\quad\Delta_{n}=t^{2}w^{T}\left(i(2n+1)\pi-th^{0}_{\perp}\right)w,

which satisfies the condition

. This existence and uniqueness result does not extend automatically to the small

is on the boundary of the admissible domain

for

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The proof is based on Brouwer’s fixed point theorem. Let
\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}
be defined, for all
n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]
, by

\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})_{n}=t^{2}w^{T}\left(i(2n+1)\pi-th^{0}_{\perp}-\Sigma_{n}\right)^{-1}w,

so that (
19
)
\iff\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
.
We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
,
\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
is well defined and belongs to
-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
. Moreover, for
n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]
,

|{\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})_{n}}|\leq\frac{t^{2}\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}{(2n+1)\pi}.

In other words,
\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
, where
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
is defined in equation (
27
).

Proof.

The sign of the imaginary part is a direct consequence of the definition of the imaginary part of matrices and of the fact that if
f
is a nonzero Pick matrix, that is
(f-f^{\dagger})/(2i)
is definite positive, then so is
-f^{-1}
. Recall that
h^{0}_{\perp}\in\mathcal{S}_{L-1}(\mathbb{R})
, so that there exists
P\in\mathcal{M}_{L-1}(\mathbb{C})
unitary with
h^{0}_{\perp}=P\mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{L-1})P^{\dagger}
. From that, we have for all
n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]

\displaystyle t^{-2}|{\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})_{n}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L-1}\frac{|{(wP)_{k}}|^{2}}{|{i(2n+1)\pi-\Sigma_{n}-t\varepsilon_{k}}|}\leq\frac{\|{w}\|_{2}^{2}}{\left|(2n+1)\pi-\Im(\Sigma_{n})\right|}\leq\frac{\mathrm{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}{(2n+1)\pi},

(29)

where we have used that
\|{w}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})
, because
\mathcal{G}_{{H}}
is vertex transitive.
∎

The analogue of this lemma for
F_{N_{\omega}}
is more restrictive (see the counterexample given in equation (
23
)), as we state now.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all Matsubara frequency cutoff
N_{\omega}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, there exists

such that for all

Proof.

Let us first prove that
\Im(F_{N_{\omega}}(0)_{n})<0

\varphi(n,N_{\omega})=\pi^{3}\Im(F_{N_{\omega}}(0)_{n})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}=-(N_{\omega}+1)\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=n-1\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{2n_{i}+1}

Let us show that the positive rational number
\varphi(n,N_{\omega})
increases with
N_{\omega}
: by enumerating, we have for all
n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]
,

\displaystyle\varphi(n,N_{\omega}+1)-\varphi(n,N_{\omega})

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2N_{\omega}+3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=-(N_{\omega}+1)\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+N_{\omega}+1=n-1\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)(2n_{2}+1)}

\displaystyle-\frac{3}{2N_{\omega}+3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=-(N_{\omega}+1)\\
n_{1}+n_{2}-(N_{\omega}+2)=n-1\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)(2n_{2}+1)}

\displaystyle-\frac{6}{(2N_{\omega}+3)^{2}(2n+1)}.

(30)

Now for the first term in the
r.h.s.
of (
30
), we have for all
n_{1},n_{2}\in[\![{-(N_{\omega}+1)},{N_{\omega}}]\!]
satisfying

,

\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)(2n_{2}+1)}=\frac{1}{2(n-(N_{\omega}+1))}\left(\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}+\frac{1}{2n_{2}+1}\right)

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=-(N_{\omega}+1)\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+N_{\omega}+1=n-1\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)(2n_{2}+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-(N_{\omega}+1)}\sum_{n_{1}=-(N_{\omega}+1)}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\omega}+1-n}\sum_{n_{1}=n}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1},

where the last equality comes from the fact that
\sum_{n_{1}=-(N_{\omega}+1)}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}=0
.
For the second term, we also have for all

satisfying

\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)(2n_{2}+1)}=\frac{1}{2(n+N_{\omega}+2)}\left(\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}+\frac{1}{2n_{2}+1}\right),

so that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=-(N_{\omega}+1)\\
n_{1}+n_{2}-(N_{\omega}+2)=n-1\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{(2n_{1}+1)(2n_{2}+1)}=\frac{1}{n+N_{\omega}+2}\sum_{n_{1}=n+1}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}.

We end up with

\displaystyle\varphi(n,N_{\omega}+1)-\varphi(n,N_{\omega})=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2N_{\omega}+3}\left(\frac{1}{N_{\omega}+1-n}-\frac{1}{n+N_{\omega}+2}\right)\sum_{n_{1}=n+1}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}

\displaystyle+\frac{3}{(2N_{\omega}+3)(2n+1)}\left(\frac{1}{(N_{\omega}+1-n)}-\frac{2}{2N_{\omega}+3}\right),

which is positive, hence
\varphi(n,N_{\omega})
increases with
N_{\omega}
and so is
\Im(F_{N_{\omega}}(0)_{n})
. From equations (
8
) and (
9
), we obtain, using (
8
), (
9
),
[
5
, Proposition 2.18]
, and the dominated convergence theorem that

\lim_{N_{\omega}\to\infty}\Im\left(F_{N_{\omega}}(0)_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta}\Im\left(F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}(0)(i\omega_{n})\right)<0.

Therefore,

\Im(F_{N_{\omega}}(0)_{n})\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\Im(F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta}(0)(i\omega_{n}))<0.

This shows that
F_{N_{\omega}}(0)\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
. This set is open and
F_{N_{\omega}}
is continuous on
-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
. Since
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
is a compact convex set containing
z=0
, we conclude by defining
\alpha_{N_{\omega}}=\sup\left\{\alpha>0:F_{N_{\omega}}(\alpha C_{N_{\omega}})\subset-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}\right\}
, and
t_{N_{\omega}}=\sqrt{\alpha_{N_{\omega}}}
.
∎

We know that
0<t_{N_{\omega}}
, and for
N_{\omega}\geq 1
,
t_{N_{\omega}}<\infty
, as shown in the counter-example (
23
).
We conclude from the two previous lemmata, that for
t<t_{N_{\omega}}/\sqrt{\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}
, the map
F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}:\mathbf{\Delta}\mapsto\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}))
is well-defined and maps
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
onto itself: indeed, for all
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
,
u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta})\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
by lemma
3.4
, hence
\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}))\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}\subset-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
by Lemma
3.3
.

Since
F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}
is continuous and
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
is compact,
F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}
has a fixed-point in
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
by Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem, which concludes the proof.
∎

3.2
Uniqueness

In this section, we state a result which guarantees the uniqueness of the solution. Note first that, similarly as for the continuous

-
DMFT

•

(Uniqueness of the solution to the discretized

Let

(31)

then the dimensionless MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations (

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

The first ingredient of the proof is an estimate on the bath-update map.

Lemma 3.6
.

Given
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
, the following estimate holds.

|\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1})_{n}-\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2})_{n}|\leq\frac{t^{2}\mathrm{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}{((2n+1)\pi)^{2}}|\Sigma_{n}^{1}-\Sigma_{n}^{2}|.

Proof.

The resolvent identity yields, for all
n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1})_{n}-\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2})_{n}=t^{2}\left(\Sigma_{n}^{2}-\Sigma_{n}^{1}\right)w^{T}\left(i(2n+1)\pi-\Sigma_{n}^{1}-th^{0}_{\perp}\right)^{-1}\left(i(2n+1)\pi-\Sigma_{n}^{2}-th^{0}_{\perp}\right)^{-1}w,

and we have, diagonalizing

similarly as in the proof of Lemma

,

|{w^{T}\left(i(2n+1)\pi-\Sigma_{n}^{1}-th^{0}_{\perp}\right)^{-1}\left(i(2n+1)\pi-\Sigma_{n}^{2}-th^{0}_{\perp}\right)^{-1}w}|\leq\frac{\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}{\left((2n+1)\pi\right)^{2}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

In particular, Lemma
3.6
shows that
\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant

for the distance induced by the maximum norm. Now let

given by Theorem

. We know that under Assumption (

. Moreover, the function
F_{N_{\omega}}
is smooth on

, see Equation (

, which is then finite.
For all

\displaystyle\|{F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\Delta_{1})-F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\Delta_{2})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\|{\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}(\Delta_{1}))-\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}(\Delta_{2}))}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{tu}{\pi}\right)^{2}\mathrm{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})\|{F_{N_{\omega}}(\Delta_{1})-F_{N_{\omega}}(\Delta_{2})}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{tu}{\pi}\right)^{2}\mathrm{deg}(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})L_{N_{\omega}}\|{\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{1}}\|_{\infty},

and we conclude using Picard fixed-point theorem, with

∎

4
The case of bipartite systems

The purpose of this section is to investigate the special case of bipartite graphs
\mathcal{G}_{{H}}
. Examples of bipartite graphs considered in condensed matter physics include regular trees (also known as Bethe lattices
[
10
]
), the Lieb lattice, and hypercubic lattices of various dimensionalities
[
10
]
. Since only
infinite
Bethe lattices are vertex-transitive, Hubbard models on Bethe lattices do not fall into the scope of our analysis, which is restricted to finite translation-invariant systems. On the other hand, our results apply to periodic supercell models built from Lieb or hypercubic lattices with an even number of sites in each dimension.

4.1
Particle-hole symmetry

As detailed in
[
22
]
, the Hubbard model defined with a bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}_{{H}}
inherits a specific
particle-hole symmetry
.
More precisely, let
\Lambda=\Lambda_{A}\sqcup\Lambda_{B}
be a partition of the vertices such that

\{i,j\}\in E\implies i\in\Lambda_{A}\mbox{ and }j\in\Lambda_{B}\mbox{ (or the converse)},

and introduce the corresponding particle-hole transform
\mathcal{B}_{A,B}
which is the Bogoliubov transform from
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{Fock}(\mathcal{H}))
to itself, defined by

\mathcal{B}_{A,B}(\widehat{a}_{{i,\sigma}})=\left\{\begin{matrix}\widehat{a}_{{i,\sigma}}^{\dagger}\quad&\text{if }i\in A,\\
-\widehat{a}_{{i,\sigma}}^{\dagger}\quad&\text{if }i\in B.\end{matrix}\right.

(35)

One can check that the Hubbard Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{H}
(see
[
5
, Definition 2.5]
) is modified under this transformation as

\mathcal{B}_{A,B}(\hat{H}_{H})=\hat{H}_{H}+U(|{\Lambda}|-\hat{N}),

(36)

so that the grand canonical Hubbard Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}=\hat{H}_{H}-\varepsilon_{F}\hat{N}
is invariant under this transform for the half-filled case
\varepsilon_{F}=U/2
, with
\varepsilon_{F}
denoting the chemical potential. We stick to this case when using the IPT approximation, as discussed more in details in
[
5
, Section 2.5.2]
.

As a consequence, the Gibbs state is also invariant under this transformation:
\mathcal{B}_{A,B}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}})=e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}
.
From this, one proves that the time-ordered one-body Green’s function
G^{\mathrm{TO}}
associated with the Gibbs state
Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}
is anti-hermitian
G^{\mathrm{TO}}=-G^{\mathrm{TO},\dagger}
: indeed, given
t\geq 0
and for all one-particle basis vector
\phi=\phi_{i,\sigma},\text{with }i\in\Lambda,\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow,
, we have

\displaystyle Z\langle\phi,iG^{\mathrm{TO}}(t)\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}e^{it\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}\widehat{a}_{{\phi}}e^{-it\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}\widehat{a}_{{\phi}}^{\dagger}\right)=\overline{\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathcal{B}_{A,B}(\widehat{a}_{{\phi}}^{\dagger}e^{-it\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}\widehat{a}_{{\phi}}e^{it\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}})\right)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}e^{it\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}\widehat{a}_{{\phi}}e^{-it\hat{H}_{H}^{\prime}}\widehat{a}_{{\phi}}^{\dagger}\right)}=Z\overline{\langle\phi,iG^{\mathrm{TO}}\phi\rangle}=-Z\langle\phi,iG^{\mathrm{TO},\dagger}\phi\rangle,

where we make use of the cyclicity of the trace and (
36
). The result for
t<0
can be proved in a similar way.

Therefore, its Generalized Fourier transform
G
satisfies the following property: for all
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
,

G(z)=-G(-\bar{z})^{\dagger}.

(37)

From this, one infers that the Nevanlinna-Riesz measure of
G
(so to say the spectral function, see
[
5
, Section 3]
) is
symmetric
, or equivalently, that the Green’s function
G
is anti-hermitian on the imaginary axis.

The following corollary ensures that the MaF discretization of the IPT-DMFT equations preserves this property in the following sense: as discussed in Section
5
, one can extract an approximation
\mathbf{G}=(G_{n})_{n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
of the local Green’s function
G_{1,1}
at the lowest
(N_{\omega}+1)
Matsubara frequencies using the constitutive relation

G_{n}\simeq G(i\omega_{n})=(i\omega_{n}-(\Delta(i\omega_{n})+\Sigma(i\omega_{n})))^{-1}\simeq(i\omega_{n}-(\Delta_{n}+\Sigma_{n}))^{-1},

(38)

which is also purely imaginary.

Corollary 4.1

(MaF-discretized preserves particle-hole symmetry)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{{H}}
be a
bipartite
vertex transitive graph. Assume that
t
is small enough, in the sense that it verifies the assumption (
31
). Then the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT map
F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}
defined in (
34
) preserves purely imaginary vectors:

\mathbf{\Delta}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}\implies F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta})\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}.

(39)

In particular, if the assumptions (
31
)-(
32
) of Theorem
3.5
are satisfied, the solution
(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\star},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\star})
is purely imaginary, i.e.
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\star},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\star}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
.

This result is not inherent to this discretization, as a similar result can be derived for the original IPT-DMFT equations. Indeed, the IPT-DMFT map introduced in
[
5
, Section 3]
preserves symmetric measures when the Hubbard graph is bipartite.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{\Delta}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
. One can easily check that
\mathbf{\Sigma}=u^{2}F_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta})
is purely imaginary. If, furthermore,
\mathbf{\Delta}\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
, with
t
small enough to satisfy the assumption (
31
), then it has been shown in the proof of Theorem
3.1
that
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
, so that
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
. Suppose now that
\mathbf{\Delta}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
.

Let
X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
be given by
iX_{n}=i(2n+1)\pi-\Sigma_{n}
for
n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!]
. Using the Schur complement methods, we obtain

F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta})_{n}=\mathrm{BU}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})_{n}=iX_{n}-\left(\left(\left(iX_{n}-t\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}\right)^{-1}\right)_{1,1}\right)^{-1}.

(40)

Now, since
\mathcal{G}_{{H}}
is bipartite
[
3
]
, the associated adjacency matrix
\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}
reads

\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&a^{T}\\
a&0\end{matrix}\right)

(41)

and letting
J=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
, we have
J\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}J=-\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}
.
This implies that
x
is an eigenvector of
\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if
Jx
is also an eigenvector with eigenvalue
-\lambda
. The matrix
\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}
being symmetric real, it is therefore diagonalizable as follow:

\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}=PDP^{t}\text{ with }\left\{\begin{matrix}&D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots\lambda_{p},-\lambda_{1},\ldots,-\lambda_{p},0,\ldots,0),\\
&P=(x_{1}|\ldots|x_{p}|Jx_{1}|\ldots|Jx_{p}|\ldots).\end{matrix}\right.

(42)

Using this diagonalization, we have

\displaystyle\left(\left(iX_{n}-\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}\right)^{-1}\right)_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{P_{1,j}^{2}}{iX_{n}-\lambda_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{P_{1,j+p}^{2}}{iX_{n}+\lambda_{j}}+\sum_{j=2p+1}^{L}\frac{P_{1,j}^{2}}{iX_{n}}

(43)

\displaystyle=i\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{2P_{1,j}^{2}X_{n}}{X_{n}^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}-\sum_{j=2p+1}^{L}\frac{P_{1,j}^{2}}{X_{n}}\right)\in i\mathbb{R},

(44)

making use of the fact for all
j\in[\![{1},{p}]\!],P_{1,j}=x_{j,1}=(JxS_{j})_{1}=P_{1,j+p}
, proving thereby the first claim. Now, if the assumptions (
31
)-(
32
) of Theorem
3.5
are satisfied, the sequence
(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
defined by

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}\cap\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\ \mathbf{\Delta}^{(k+1)}=F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k)})

(45)

converges toward the unique solution
\mathbf{\Delta}_{*}
, therefore
\mathbf{\Delta}_{*}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
, and the same holds for the self-energy:
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}\in-i\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
.
∎

4.2
Algebraic structure of MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT for generic bipartite systems

In this section, we characterize the purely imaginary and physically admissible solutions to the dimensionless MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations (
19
)–(
20
) for bipartite systems. After showing that these equations boil down to a set of sparse algebraic equations for generic bipartite systems, we investigate more in depth the case of the Hubbard dimer.

Seeking the purely imaginary, physically admissible solutions to (
19
)–(
20
) amounts to seeking solutions of the form
\mathbf{\Delta}=(-i\pi y_{0},-i3\pi y_{1},\cdots,-i(2N_{\omega}+1)\pi y_{N_{\omega}})
, with
\bm{y}=(y_{0},\cdots,y_{N_{\omega}})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N_{\omega}+1}
. It is convenient to rewrite this problem in terms of the auxiliary variables

\bm{x}=(x_{0},\cdots,x_{N_{\omega}})\quad\mbox{where}\quad x_{n}:=\frac{1}{1+y_{n}}\quad\mbox{for all }0\leq n\leq N_{\omega}.

For bipartite systems, the spectrum of
h^{0}_{\perp}
has a special structure. Moreover, removing a vertex from a bipartite graph does not change the bipartite character. The diagonalization is therefore the same as for the total adjacency matrix (
42
): if
L
is even, the dimension of
h^{0}_{\perp}
is odd,
0
is an eigenvalue with odd multiplicity, and the nonzero eigenvalues come in pairs of real eigenvalues with opposite signs. If
L
is odd, the dimension of
h^{0}_{\perp}
is even and the eigenvalues come in pairs of real eigenvalues with opposite signs (
0
can be an eigenvalue, but if it is, it has an even multiplicity). In addition if
\nu
is a positive eigenvalue of
h^{0}_{\perp}
, then the orthogonal projectors verify
|\Pi_{\nu}v|=|\Pi_{-\nu}v|
, with
v=w/|w|
, that is
w=v\sqrt{\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})}
. Denoting
\Pi_{0}
the orthogonal projector on
\ker h^{0}_{\perp}
, which is set to zero if
0\notin\sigma(h^{0}_{\perp})
, it follows that (
19
)–(
20
) can be rewritten as

\frac{1-x_{n}}{x_{n}}=a\deg(\mathcal{G}_{{H}})\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\in\sigma(h^{0}_{\perp})\\
\nu>0\end{subarray}}\frac{2|\Pi_{\nu}v|^{2}\left((2n+1)^{2}-bP_{n,N_{\omega}}(\bm{x})\right)}{\left((2n+1)^{2}-bP_{n,N_{\omega}}(\bm{x})\right)^{2}+(2n+1)^{2}a\nu^{2}}+\frac{|\Pi_{0}v|^{2}}{(2n+1)^{2}-bP_{n,N_{\omega}}(\bm{x})}\right),

where the parameters
a
and
b
are related to
t
and
u
by

a=\frac{t^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad b=\frac{u^{2}}{\pi^{4}},

and where the homogeneous cubic polynomials
P_{n,N_{\omega}}
,
N_{\omega}\in\mathbb{N}
,
0\leq n\leq N_{\omega}
, are given by

\displaystyle P_{n,N_{\omega}}(\bm{x})=\!(2n+1)\bigg(

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{n\geq 1}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}=0\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=n-1\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{2n_{j}+1}x_{n_{j}}

\displaystyle-3\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=0\\
n\leq n_{1}+n_{2}\leq N_{\omega}+n\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}\frac{1}{2n_{2}+1}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+2n_{2}-2n+1}x_{n_{1}}x_{n_{2}}x_{n_{1}+n_{2}-n}

\displaystyle+3{\mathds{1}}_{n\leq N_{\omega}-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}=0\\
n_{1}+n_{2}\leq N_{\omega}-(n+1)\end{subarray}}^{N_{\omega}}\!\!\!\!\!\frac{1}{2n_{1}+1}\frac{1}{2n_{2}+1}\frac{1}{2n_{1}+2n_{2}+2n+3}x_{n_{1}}x_{n_{2}}x_{n_{1}+n_{2}+n+1}\bigg).

The above equations read as a system of
(N_{\omega}+1)
scalar algebraic equations in the
(N_{\omega}+1)
scalar variables
(x_{0},\dots,x_{N_{\omega}})
. Each of these equations can be written as

\sum_{j=0}^{2M+1}(c_{j,n,N_{\omega}}x_{n}+d_{j,n,N_{\omega}})P_{n,N_{\omega}}(\bm{x})^{j}=0,

(46)

where
M
is the number of distinct positive eigenvalues of
h^{0}_{\perp}
, and
c_{j,n,N_{\omega}}
and
d_{j,n,N_{\omega}}
are real coefficients, which are themselves polynomials in
a
and
b
(or equivalently
t
and
u
). Note that
M
is bounded by
\lfloor(L-1)/2\rfloor
. As the polynomials
P_{n,N_{\omega}}
are cubic, homogeneous, the total degree in
\bm{x}
of each of these scalar equations is exactly
6M+4
for any positive values of
a
and
b
. As the number of monomials in each
P_{n,N_{\omega}}
is of the order of
N_{\omega}^{2}
, the number of monomials in (
46
) is of order at most
N_{\omega}^{4M+2}
. For large values of
N_{\omega}
, this number is small compared with the total number
\begin{pmatrix}N_{\omega}+6M+5\\
6M+4\end{pmatrix}\sim N_{\omega}^{6M+4}
of monomials in a generic polynomial equation of degree
(6M+4)
with
(N_{\omega}+1)
variables.

A bound on the number of solutions for fixed parameters
t,u
can therefore be obtained using results from symbolic algebraic geometry, while the set of solutions can be explored numerically by homotopy methods from numerical algebraic geometry, although these methods are limited to relatively small values of
N_{\omega}
.

4.3
Hubbard dimer

The Hubbard dimer is the simplest non trivial case. Here
L=2
and the graph consists of two vertices and one edge. It is clearly bipartite, and we have
\mathrm{Adj}_{\mathcal{G}_{{H}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, so that
w=1
and

4.3.1
Low-temperature regime for

In this section, we are interested in the simplest case

We would like to investigate the solutions to this equation in the physical setting when the hopping parameter

(48)

Theorem 4.2
.

Proof.

Setting

\epsilon:=t^{-1},\quad z_{\epsilon}:=-i\pi^{-1}\Delta,\quad\lambda:=\frac{3\alpha^{2}}{\pi^{2}}>0,

Let us first study the limiting equation obtained by setting

This equation can be rewritten as

This shows that any solution

•

Numerical simulations of the Hubbard dimer

In this section, we report numerical simulations of the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations for the Hubbard dimer (
\Lambda=\{1,2\},E=\{\{1,2\}\}

In spite of its simplicity, this setting is enough to exhibit numerically an interaction-driven conductor-to-insulator transition, as illustrated in the next section. For this specific purpose, we use the Python/C++ library

3.1.0
[
29

, and take advantage of its analytic continuation functionalities.

Unfortunately, this library is not suited to perform simulations with a small number of Matsubara’s frequencies (

In order to exemplify the results of Section
4.3
, we then switch to a Julia implementation with arbitrary finite-arithmetic precision for the rest of the analysis.

With this implementation, we first perform a series of simulations revealing that the simple fixed point algorithm may converge to a solution that do not fulfill the Pick criterion, meaning that there is no Pick function exactly interpolating the computed values at the lowest
(N_{\omega}+1)
lowest Matsubara frequencies. Following on Remark
2.2
, this observation indicates that the MaF-discretization do not preserve the fact that
-\Delta,-\Sigma
are Pick functions
[
5

.

The section is organized as follows: in Section
5.1
, we perform a convergence analysis of the algorithm described in Theorem
3.5
using the
TRIQS

is devoted to the verification of the Pick criterion for the local Green’s function associated to the converged solution.

5.1
Conductor-to-insulator transition using the
TRIQS
library

In this section, we present numerical evidence of a phase transition for the Hubbard dimer from conductor to insulator in the IPT-DMFT approximation, the simulations being performed using the
TRIQS
library.

Before going any further, let us specify the meaning of the terms conductor and insulators for the Hubbard dimer. The system is called
a conductor if the Fermi level (zero in this setting) is in the support of the spectral function
[
24
]
, i.e. of the Nevanlinna-Riesz measure of
-G
, and an insulator otherwise
[
10
]
.

To study the conduction properties of a system with the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT, a strategy is therefore as follows:

1.

Solve the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equations
. As a self-consistent method, DMFT equations are usually solved using
an iterative scheme
: for the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT setting, we implement the simple iterative scheme described in Theorem
3.5
, namely, for a given
N_{\mathrm{iter}}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we compute sequences
\left(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k)}\right)_{k\in[\![{0},{N_{\mathrm{iter}}}]\!]}
defined by

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k+1)}=F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k)}),

(60)

for various initial guesses
\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}
.
The number of iterations
N_{\mathrm{iter}}
is chosen to ensure that for the considered set of parameters
T,U,\beta
,
\mathbf{\Delta}^{(N_{\mathrm{iter}})}\approx\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\infty)}
up to machine precision. We observe that the algorithm converges linearly for the two extreme initial guesses considered:
\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}=0
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{(0)}=1/(i\omega_{n})
(Figure

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
in both cases and are extreme in the sense that they belong to the boundary of
\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}}
. We check that the solution is converged up to machine precision, the latter being fixed by the implementation to
10^{-16}
.

2.

Compute the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT local Green’s function
\mathbf{G}
. With the converged solution in the variable
\mathbf{\Delta}
, we extract the associated self-energy
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(\infty)}=U^{2}F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta,N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\infty)})

\mathbf{G}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}

\forall n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!],\quad G_{n}=\left(i\omega_{n}-\Delta_{n}^{(\infty)}-\Sigma_{n}^{(\infty)}\right)^{-1}.

(61)

3.

Find an interpolation
G
of
\mathbf{G}
and extract the spectral function
A
.
Once a solution is found and that
\mathbf{G}
is defined, we seek an analytic interpolation
G
of
\mathbf{G}
to the whole upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}
, namely we solve the following Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation problem

\text{Find}-G:\mathbb{C}_{+}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}\text{ analytic, such that, }\forall n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!],\quad G(i\omega_{n})=G_{n}.

Once

is found (provided a solution to this problem exists, see the following section), we can get the values of the spectral function
A
by taking the limit of

on the real line, using the Stieljes inversion formula
[
11

: recall that for

a Pick matrix given by, for all

\frac{1}{2}\mu(\{x\})+\frac{1}{2}\mu(\{y\})+\mu((x,y))=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\int_{x}^{y}\Im(f(\varepsilon+i\eta))d\varepsilon.

In particular, assuming the spectral function
A
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure (which seems to hold in the examples below), and letting

\rho(\varepsilon)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\Im(G(\varepsilon+i\eta)).

(63)

In practice, several algorithms exist to perform this analytic continuation

For the present purpose, we used the

\mathbf{G}
, it returns the values
\rho_{\mathbb{R}}
of an interpolation of the above points on a given set of points on the real line.
The latter set is a regular mesh of

5.2
Pick criterion on the converged solution

In this section, we are interested in the existence of a solution to the Nevanlinna-Pick problem set in (
62
). Two criteria are available for determining whether such a problem has a solution, and whether this solution is unique (see
[
5
, Appendix A]
and references therein). In this article, we focus on the Pick criterion, which appears to be more robust that the Nevanlinna criterion with respect to small numerical errors.

As explained in details in
[
28
]
, the Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation problem (
62
) admits a solution if and only if the Pick matrix
P\in\mathcal{S}_{N_{\omega}+1}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
given by

P_{i,j}=\frac{1-\overline{W(-G_{i})}W(-G_{j})}{1-\overline{W(i\omega_{i})}W(i\omega_{j})},\text{ with the Cayley transform }W:\left\{\begin{matrix}&\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}\to\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C},|{z}|\leq 1\}\\
&z\mapsto(z-i)/(z+i)\end{matrix}\right.,

(65)

is positive semi-definite. The solution is unique if
0
is an eigenvalue of this matrix, in which case the solution is rational with real poles
[
6
,
5
]
. Testing numerically the existence (and uniqueness) of a solution using the Pick criterion therefore amounts to diagonalize the Pick matrix and to assess the sign of its eigenvalues.

As briefly mentioned in the previous section, the
TRIQS
library has been developed to run simulations for a large number of Matsubara frequencies. We find in practice that, for
N_{\omega}\leq 50
, the library does not manage to perform the
Fourier
method, which is used to compute (
9
).
Moreover, the latter method uses tail-fitting, which makes the
TRIQS
implementation of the method slighty different from the one analyzed in Theorem
3.5
.
In addition, the
TRIQS
library is limited to double-precision computations. In this Section, we are interested in assessing numerically the sign of the lowest eigenvalue of a matrix: to make sure that numerical errors due to the diagonalization do not prevail, we need to use arbitrary-precision finite-arithmetic computations.

For all these reasons, we have used for the rest of Section
5
our own implementation in
Julia
of the simple fixed-point algorithm described in (
9
).
We use the
BigFloat
package to handle arbitrary precision. In comparison with the
TRIQS
implementation, we do not compute numerically the integral defined in (
9
): instead, we make use of the alternative expression given in (
17
), and compute
F_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta})
directly. Doing so, our implementation is as close as possible to the simple fixed-point algorithm studied in this article, at the price of greatly reduced performance in terms of computing times.

We proceed as follows:

1.

Solve the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT equation
. The first step is the same as previously: we implement the simple iterative scheme described in Theorem
3.5
, namely, for a given
N_{\mathrm{iter}}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we compute the sequence
\left(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k)}\right)_{k\in[\![{0},{N_{\mathrm{iter}}}]\!]}
by

\mathbf{\Delta}^{(0)}\in\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{N_{\omega}},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\quad\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k+1)}=F^{\mathrm{DMFT}}_{N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(k)}).

(66)

This time, the number of iterations
N_{\mathrm{iter}}
has to be chosen to ensure that for the considered set of parameters
T,U,\beta
,
\mathbf{\Delta}^{(N_{\mathrm{iter}})}\approx\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\infty)}
up to a precision that allows us to compute the sign of the lowest eigenvalue of the associated Pick matrix. Our Julia implementation is set to tackle this issue, that is, the precision is chosen so that the numerical error due to the diagonalization of the Pick matrix does not prevent the sign of its smallest eigenvalue from being known exactly.

2.

Compute the MaF-discretized IPT-DMFT local Green’s function
\mathbf{G}
. As in the previous section, we extract the associated self-energy
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(\infty)}=U^{2}F^{\mathrm{IPT}}_{\beta,N_{\omega}}(\mathbf{\Delta}^{(\infty)})
and an approximation
\mathbf{G}\in-\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}^{N_{\omega}+1}
of the discretization of the local Green’s function
G_{1,1}
, defined by

\forall n\in[\![{0},{N_{\omega}}]\!],\quad G_{n}=\left(i\omega_{n}-\Delta_{n}^{(\infty)}-\Sigma_{n}^{(\infty)}\right)^{-1}.

(67)

3.

Check the Pick criterion.
Define the Pick matrices
P(\Delta),P(\Sigma),P(G)
respectively associated to
-\mathbf{\Delta},-\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
-\mathbf{G}
, using formula (
