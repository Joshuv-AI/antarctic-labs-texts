Square-bracket operations clubs
Osvaldo Guzmán, Stevo Todorcevic

Abstract
This paper continues the investigation of the three square-bracket operations [ ⋅ ⋅ ] [\cdot\cdot] from chapter 5 of [ 40 ] . We say that a square-bracket operation [ ⋅ ⋅ ] [\cdot\cdot] has the Ramsey club property if for every club C ⊆ ω 1 C\subseteq\omega_{1} , there is an uncountable subset W W ⊆ ω 1 \subseteq\omega_{1} such that [ α ​ β ] ∈ C \left[\alpha\beta\right]\in C for every α , β ∈ W . \alpha,\beta\in W. The second author proved that the Proper Forcing Axiom implies that all the square-bracket operations induced by Aronszajn trees have this property. We extend this result to the other two classes. We conclude that each of the statements “all square-bracket operations have the Ramsey club property” and “No square-bracket operation has the Ramsey club property” are consistent with ZFC. In other words, ZFC is unable to decide the status of the Ramsey club property for any square-bracket operation. Furthermore, we analyze the status of the Ramsey club property for square-bracket operations under Martin’s Axiom and the Continuum Hypothesis.

then forcing with

adds a

-generic filter. We will say that

\mathbb{P}
and

Definition 20

Let
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
be partial orders. We say that

is a dense embedding
if for every

the following conditions hold:

2.

If

3.

The image of

[
24
]
for more
information on regular suborders and dense embeddings.

The

is the smallest cardinal
\kappa
for

c.c.c.

\mathbb{P}
and a family

of

(

Theorem 21

Let

\beta

\gamma

\gamma
to
\alpha.

The following lemma is

Lemma 23

The set

\left(\omega_{1}\right)\}

\alpha

The conclusion of the lemma easily follows.

The following lemma is well-known (for example, it follows from Fact 2 in

[

Lemma 24

\delta<\omega_{1}.

Preliminaries on Baumgartner’s club forcing

Baumgartner’s club forcing
is the standard method for adding a club
subset to
\omega_{1}
using finite conditions. It was introduced and studied
by Baumgartner in
[
12
]
. This is a very interesting forcing
notion with several applications and is a natural example of a proper forcing
that is not
\omega
-proper. The finiteness of its conditions facilitates
various amalgamation arguments, which in turn yield rich combinatorial
properties for the generic club. This section reviews the fundamentals of this
forcing notion. For further reading, the reader is referred to

[
21
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
2
]
,

[
48
]
,

[
49
]
,
[
4
]
,

[
7
]
. By
Int
\left(\omega_{1}\right)

we denote the set
\left\{(\alpha,\beta]\mid\alpha<\beta<\omega_{1}\right\}.

Definition 25

Baumgartner’s club forcing
(denoted by
\mathbb{BC}
) consists of all
pairs
p=\left(a,I\right)
with the following properties:

1.

a\in\left[\omega_{1}\right]^{<\omega}.

2.

Int
\left(\omega_{1}\right)

3.

Given

This is the presentation of Baumgartner’s club
forcing from
[
48
]
(there are other several

will say that
\alpha

is above

Since

is a partial order of size

then it satisfies

\omega_{2}
-chain condition.

Furthermore, it is a proper

(

-generic condition.

2.

\delta_{M}\in a_{p}
(where

17

Corollary 27

Let
\mathbb{\dot{Q}}

-generic.

2.

\delta_{M}\in a_{p}.

The properness and the

that no cardinals are collapsed after performing a forcing extension. Let

Note that if

be a countable elementary submodel of some

\left(\kappa\right)

(M,\mathbb{BC})

2.

is

(see
[
41
]
). How can we add many Baumgartner clubs?
Since
\mathbb{BC}
is not

What about the countable support product? In general, if

[
4
]
Asperó found a method for adding many

The square-bracket operations

This section reviews the definitions of the three square-bracket operations
from chapter 5 of the book
[
40
]
, which are the protagonists of this
work. A key property of these operations is that every uncountable subset of

\omega_{1}
realizes club many values. We will study the Ramsey clubs
associated with each operation.

The first square-bracket operation is defined using walks on ordinals. Let

\mathcal{C}
be a
C
-sequence. Define
[\cdot\cdot]_{\mathcal{C}}:\left[\omega_{1}\right]^{2}\longrightarrow\omega_{1}
such that for
every
\alpha<\beta
:

[\alpha\beta]_{\mathcal{C}}

=

min
\{
Tr
(\triangle_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right),\beta)\setminus\alpha\}

=

min
\{
Tr
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\cap

Tr
(\triangle_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right),\beta)\}

In this way, when walking from
\beta
to
\triangle_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right),
the smallest point encountered that is greater than or
equal to
\alpha
is precisely
[\alpha\beta]_{\mathcal{C}}.
Equivalently, it
is the minimal point common to both the walk from
\beta
to
\alpha
and the
walk from
\beta
to
\triangle_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)

(see
[
40
]
). It is worth pointing out that the definition in

[
40
]
uses
\triangle_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)-1

instead of
\triangle_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right).
This
distinction is irrelevant to our arguments.

The second square-bracket operation is defined with a sequence of (distinct)
reals
\mathcal{R}=\left\{r_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\omega_{1}\right\}\subseteq 2^{\omega}
and a sequence
e=\left\langle e_{\beta}\mid\beta\in\omega_{1}\right\rangle
where
e_{\beta}:\beta+1\longrightarrow\omega
is
an injective function such that
e_{\beta}\left(\beta\right)=0.
For
convenience we will call a sequence with this properties a
nice
sequence
. For
\alpha
and
\beta
countable ordinals, denote
\triangle_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\triangle\left(r_{\alpha},r_{\beta}\right).
Define
[\cdot\cdot]_{\mathcal{R},e}:\left[\omega_{1}\right]^{2}\longrightarrow\omega_{1}
such that for every
\alpha<\beta
:

[\alpha\beta]_{\mathcal{R},e}=

min
\{\mathsf{\xi}\in\left[\alpha,\beta\right]\mid e_{\beta}\left(\xi\right)\leq\triangle_{\mathcal{R}}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)\}

The computation of
[\alpha\beta]_{\mathcal{R},e}
proceeds by finding the
smallest
m
for which
r_{\alpha}\left(m\right)\neq r_{\beta}\left(m\right)
and then taking the least
\xi\in

\left[\alpha,\beta\right]

such that
e_{\beta}\left(\xi\right)\leq m.

The third square-bracket operation is defined with a Hausdorff, special
Aronszajn tree
T
and an specialization mapping
a:T\longrightarrow\omega.

This time, the operation is defined on
T
instead of
\omega_{1}.
Given

s,t\in T,
define
s\wedge t
as the largest lower bound for both
s
and

t.
Note that
s\wedge t
is well-defined since
T
is a Hausdorff tree. For

s,t\in T,
let
[s

,t]=\left\{z\in T\mid s\leq_{T}z\leq_{T}t\right\}.

Define
[\cdot\cdot]_{T,a}:\left[T\right]^{2}\longrightarrow T
such that
for every
s,t\in T
with
\left|s\right|\leq\left|t\right|
:

[st]_{T,a}=

min
\{z\in[t\upharpoonright\left|s\right|

,t]\mid z=t\vee a\left(z\right)\leq a\left(s\wedge t\right)

\}

To compute
[st]_{T,a},
first set
m=a\left(s\wedge t\right).
Then, find
the first node
z\leq_{T}t
with
\left|z\right|\geq\left|s\right|
and
a\left(z\right)\leq m.
If no node such
z
exists,
let
[st]_{T,a}=t.
Since the operation is not defined on
\omega_{1},
in
this context the vertex set of the graph
G([\cdot\cdot],X)
(for

X\subseteq\omega_{1}
) is the tree
T.
Two nodes
s
and
t
are connected
by an edge if the height of
[st]_{T,a}
is in
X.

The reader is encouraged to review the proofs that the square-bracket
operations defined above all have the property that every uncountable subset

A,B\in\left[\omega_{1}\right]^{\omega_{1}}
there is
D\in\left[\omega_{1}\right]^{\omega_{1}}
such that
C\left(D\right)\subseteq C\left(A\right)\cap C\left(B\right)
”.

Of course, such statement follows from
PFA
, but we do not know much
more than that. Since we use the Proper Forcing Axiom to prove that all
square-bracket operations have the Ramsey club property, it is natural to ask
the following questions:

Problem 88

Is the statement “all square-bracket operations have the
Ramsey club property” consistent with an arbitrarily large continuum?

Problem 89

Is it possible to force that all square-bracket operations have the Ramsey
club property without any Large Cardinal hypothesis?
