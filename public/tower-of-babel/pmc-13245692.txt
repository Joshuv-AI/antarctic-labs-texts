INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) confers a 1.5- to 2-fold increased cardiovascular risk compared with the general population. Tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors generally reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.40–0.89); however, rare paradoxical immune-mediated cardiotoxic effects have been documented[1]. A systematic analysis of the World Health Organization (WHO) pharmacovigilance database identified 62 drugs associated with myocarditis, but TNF-α inhibitors were not among them[2]. Similarly, neither the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database nor a recent global analysis of the top 10 drugs most frequently associated with myocarditis identified TNF-α inhibitors[3].

We report clinically suspected myocarditis temporally associated with intravenous golimumab in a patient with seropositive RA, notable for a recent subcutaneous-to-intravenous route switch producing a 14-fold increase in peak drug exposure, progressive eosinophilia, and complete functional recovery paralleling drug elimination.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 51-year-old male with seropositive RA (anti- cyclic citrullinated peptide, CCP, 82 units), hypertension, and obesity (body mass index 38.4 kg/m2) presented with sudden-onset severe substernal chest tightness (8/10) radiating to the left arm, with dyspnoea and numbness after swimming. He denied fever, prodromal illness, or recent infection.

He had been switched from subcutaneous golimumab (Simponi) 50 mg monthly to intravenous golimumab (Simponi Aria) 2 mg/kg every 8 weeks approximately 6 months prior due to insurance reasons, receiving approximately 4 infusions. The last infusion was 4 weeks before presentation. Intravenous golimumab achieves a maximum serum concentration (Cmax) of 44.4 ± 11.3 μg/ml; approximately 14-fold higher than subcutaneous (Cmax 3.2 ± 1.4 μg/ml)[4]. Given a terminal half-life of ~14 days, approximately 25% of peak concentration remained at presentation. Concomitant medications included methotrexate 15 mg weekly (stable long-term), amlodipine, valsartan, and escitalopram 5 mg. RA was in clinical remission (C-reactive protein 5 mg/l, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 2 mm/hr).

On examination, he was hemodynamically stable and afebrile. Electrocardiogram showed sinus bradycardia without ST changes (Fig. 1). Serial high-sensitivity troponin I: 30 → 32,419 → 11,326 → 7,866 ng/l (reference < 54). creatine kinase 428 U/l; creatine kinase-myocardial band 44.8 ng/ml. B-type natriuretic peptide was normal. Antinuclear antibody was negative. Absolute eosinophil count rose progressively: 0.12 → 0.27 → 0.45 → 0.54 ×103/μl (4.5-fold increase) (Table 1).

Comprehensive viral serology was negative for acute infection: parvovirus B19 IgM negative (IgG positive); Epstein-Barr virus viral capsid antigen (VCA) IgM negative, VCA IgG positive, Epstein-Barr nuclear antigen (EBNA) IgG negative (reference ≥22 U/ml); consistent with past infection with absent EBNA seroconversion, a variant in 5–10% of previously infected individuals; cytomegalovirus IgM negative; human herpesvirus-6 IgM negative (IgG positive); enterovirus ribonucleic acid polymerase chain reaction (PCR) negative; severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2) antigen negative (Table 1).

Echocardiography showed left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 45–49% with global hypokinesis, no regional wall motion abnormalities (Fig. 2). Coronary angiography: non-obstructive arteries (Fig. 3). Computed tomography pulmonary angiography: no embolism. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was unavailable at our institution.

Chest pain resolved within 24 hours with supportive care. Golimumab was permanently discontinued. The patient was discharged on day 3 on metoprolol and sacubitril/valsartan. At 4-week follow-up, despite not initiating sacubitril/valsartan, repeat echocardiography demonstrated complete normalization of LVEF, coinciding with near-complete golimumab elimination (~8 weeks post-last infusion, ~6% residual Cmax). DISCUSSION

This case meets criteria for clinically suspected myocarditis per the 2024 ACC Expert Consensus Decision Pathway, based on compatible symptoms, elevated biomarkers, and new LV dysfunction without an alternative diagnosis[5]. It also meets Brighton Collaboration Level 2 (probable) criteria, based on compatible symptoms with at least one abnormal diagnostic test (elevated cardiac biomarkers and abnormal echocardiography) in the absence of an alternative diagnosis.

Systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses strengthens the case. Viral myocarditis was excluded by absent prodromal symptoms, negative IgM serology for all cardiotropic viruses, and negative enterovirus PCR. Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries is excluded when myocarditis is identified as the underlying cause. Takotsubo syndrome requires regional wall motion abnormalities (typically apical ballooning), not the global hypokinesis observed here. RA-associated myocarditis is unlikely given completely normal inflammatory markers in quiescent disease.

Several features support drug-induced hypersensitivity myocarditis. The progressive 4.5-fold rise in eosinophils is characteristic of drug hypersensitivity; a 2026 multicentre registry of 156 biopsy-proven eosinophilic myocarditis cases reported peripheral eosinophilia in only 57.4% (median 630/μl), with hypersensitivity forms accounting for 14.1%[6]. The 14-fold Cmax increase from route switch could theoretically have contributed to cumulative immune sensitization[4]. Although the 4-week latency exceeds the median time-to-onset of 15 days (IQR 10–23) for drug-induced myocarditis,[2] significant drug levels (~25% Cmax) remained at symptom onset[2]. Complete LVEF normalization paralleled drug elimination despite incomplete heart failure therapy, consistent with a self-limited drug-mediated process.

Regarding causality, the WHO - Uppsala Monitoring Centre system classifies this as “probable/likely” based on temporal relationship, exclusion of alternatives, and positive dechallenge. The Naranjo algorithm yields a score of 6 to 8 (probable) (Table 2)[7]. Question 1 was scored “yes” (+1) because rare pharmacovigilance signals for TNF-α inhibitors and myocarditis exist in WHO and FAERS databases[2,3]. Question 8 was scored “yes” (+1) because the subcutaneous-to-intravenous route switch produced a 14-fold Cmax increase, constituting a de facto dose escalation[4]. Both systems concordantly classify this case as “probable”.

Concomitant methotrexate (stable long-term, continued without recurrence; negative dechallenge) and escitalopram (not identified among drugs associated with myocarditis in pharmacovigilance analyses, continued without recurrence) are unlikely causative agents[1,3].

It is important to note that anti-TNF agents like infliximab have been used to treat certain types of myocarditis. They have been used as a third-line treatment for refractory sarcoidosis and as an option for myocarditis caused by immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, recent guidelines have moved away from using infliximab for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-myocarditis. The 2025 ACC Concise Clinical Guidance suggests avoiding infliximab because it may worsen heart failure[8]. The 2026 International Cardio-Oncology Society Position Statement also notes that infliximab is linked to higher rates of cardiovascular death[9]. This apparent paradox, that a TNF-α inhibitor could both treat and cause myocarditis, is explained by fundamentally different immunopathological mechanisms. In cardiac sarcoidosis and ICI-myocarditis, TNF-α is a key mediator of the pathological granulomatous or T-cell–mediated inflammatory response, and its inhibition targets the disease mechanism directly. In contrast, the myocarditis in our case is attributed to a drug hypersensitivity reaction to golimumab itself, as evidenced by progressive eosinophilia, absence of viral prodrome, and complete resolution paralleling drug elimination. Preclinical data support this complexity: Rolski et al. demonstrated that TNF-α has a dual, context-dependent role in myocarditis; promoting disease initiation through endothelial activation while simultaneously protecting against disease exacerbation through activation-induced cell death of heart-reactive T cells[10]. Therefore, the therapeutic immunosuppressive properties of anti-TNF agents in granulomatous myocarditis do not preclude their capacity to trigger hypersensitivity-mediated myocardial injury through an entirely distinct immunological pathway.

The primary limitation is the absence of cardiac MRI, which was unavailable at our institution. Endomyocardial biopsy was appropriately deferred by guidelines given hemodynamic stability[5]. Additional limitations include inability to rechallenge (contraindicated) and potential false-negative viral testing. Additionally, the FDA label cautions that the safety of switching between golimumab formulations has not been established, underscoring the clinical significance of this case.
