Rehabilitation Exercise Behaviors in Patients Undergoing Peritoneal Dialysis: A Mixed-Methods Study on Multidimensional Determinants
Authors: Haixue Lin, Yiru Wang, Beixia Zhu, Congping Xue, Qun Luo, Fangfang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Exercise intention correlates positively with muscle function in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis.•Three key barriers hinder home exercise: cognition, safety fears, and systemic barriers.•Most patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis are willing to exercise but lack guidance.•Multidimensional strategies needed to improve exercise adherence in this group.

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) serves as a primary renal replacement therapy for end-stage renal disease, offering distinct advantages such as home-based applicability and preservation of residual renal function.1 Patients undergoing long-term PD often develop various complications, such as cardiovascular risks and sarcopenia.2, 3, 4 Although combined aerobic and resistance training is proven to improve muscle mass and quality of life,5, 6, 7, 8 a significant evidence-practice gap remains. Understanding the multidimensional determinants of exercise behavior is urgently needed for this population.9 The barriers to exercise engagement in patients undergoing PD are complex and interrelated. Dialysis-related fatigue perpetuates a debilitating cycle of physical inactivity in most patients,10 whereas PD-specific concerns—particularly fears of catheter displacement or leakage of dialysate—significantly constrain participation in physical activity.11 Compounding these issues, fall-related anxiety further restricts mobility and community engagement.12 Beyond these documented factors, critical dimensions such as patients’ cognitive biases regarding exercise safety and systemic deficiencies in social support networks remain inadequately characterized. Furthermore, existing assessment instruments fail to capture PD-specific barriers, as they predominantly adapt generic chronic disease scales without incorporating disease-contextualized domains.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a mixed-methods study that integrates quantitative metrics with qualitative insights to holistically elucidate the determinants of exercise behavior. With this approach, we aim to generate actionable evidence for developing targeted clinical interventions to enhance exercise adherence in this vulnerable population.

Participants and Methods

Participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted on 144 patients receiving maintenance PD admitted to Ningbo No. 2 Hospital—a tertiary-level comprehensive hospital in Ningbo, China that houses a specialized nephrology center, dedicated inpatient and outpatient PD services, an on-site rehabilitation medicine department, and serves as the official host institution of the Ningbo Municipal Dialysis Quality Control Center—between June 1, 2024, and May 31, 2025. Inclusion criteria were as follows: aged ≥18 years; receiving regular PD treatment for ≥3 months; clear consciousness, with good communication skills and the ability to express true thoughts; and voluntary participation in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: severe complications such as acute infection or cardiovascular events; presence of cognitive impairment or mental illness.

For qualitative interviews, we employed purposive maximum variation sampling to select 16 patients willing to share their exercise experiences from the cross-sectional survey sample. This purposive sampling strategy was designed to capture maximum heterogeneity across 3 prespecified, study-relevant dimensions: age, disease severity, and education level. We first stratified the full cross-sectional cohort by these dimensions, then purposefully selected participants from each stratum to ensure diverse representation of patient experiences. Semistructured interviews were conducted, and the sample size was determined based on data saturation. General information on the qualitative interview subjects is shown in table 1. Methods

Cross-sectional survey

Data collection

(1)Baseline Information Questionnaire included demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level, and monthly income) and clinical characteristics (primary disease, dialysis duration, and complications).(2)Motor function tests: all tests included in this section are validated, widely accepted instruments for assessing physical function in patients receiving PD populations, with established reliability and clinical utility in this patient group.(a)6-m walking speed: subjects walked 6 m at their usual pace, with the elapsed time recorded twice and the average calculated.(b)5-time sit-to-stand test: subjects stood up from a seated position and sat down repeatedly 5 times. The elapsed time was recorded twice, and the average calculated.(c)timed Up and Go test: the time taken for subjects to stand up, walk, turn around, and sit down again was recorded 3 times, and the average of 2 valid measurements was calculated.(d)Grip strength measurement: using JAMAR 120604 dynamometer (Sammons Preston),a subjects maintained an upright posture with arms naturally hanging, held the dynamometer. Measurements were repeated 3 times, and the average was calculated.(e)Skeletal muscle mass: the multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 770, Biospace)b was used to directly measure body weight, total fat mass, muscle mass, total body water, and muscle mass of the trunk and limbs. All measurements were performed by the same examiner. Subjects were required to fast (≥8h) and drain peritoneal fluid before testing, while women also avoided menstruation. Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was calculated by summing limb muscle masses, with the ASM Index=ASM/height² (m²).(3)Exercise Benefits/Barriers Scale for patients undergoing PD(a)Scale structure: The scale included 24 items and was developed by researchers based on focus group interviews (N=16 patients undergoing PD), expert consultations (5 nephrologists and rehabilitation specialists), and a literature review. It comprised 12 items on exercise benefits and 12 items on exercise barriers. A 4-point Likert scale was used (strongly agree=4, strongly disagree=1), with barrier items reverse-scored. Total scores ranged from 24 to 96, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived benefits and fewer barriers.(b)Reliability and validity: The scale showed a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.81 and a content validity index of 0.96. The construct validity was verified by exploratory factor analysis (cumulative variance explanation rate: 81.6%).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS, version 25.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics; IBM Corp.).c Baseline characteristics were expressed as the mean ± SD, or percentages (%). Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to analyze correlations between exercise willingness scale scores and physical indicators. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative research

(1)Interview outline: This was developed based on research objectives, previous literature, and the opinions of clinical nursing experts. Its focus was on: regular exercise habits; perceived benefits of exercise for patients undergoing PD and concerns about risks; difficulties in home-based exercise rehabilitation and support from family or medical staff; preferred channels for obtaining home-based exercise guidance.(2)Procedures: before interview, subjects were contacted to explain the study purpose, confirm willingness, and obtain written informed consent. Subjects were anonymized as P1, P2, etc, in transcripts and reports. Each interview was conducted with the approval of the patient’s attending PD physician and primary nurse. The order of questions was adjusted during the interview according to the responses. Interviews were audio-recorded, and the subjects’ facial expressions, tone, and emotional changes were documented.(3)Data analysis: interviews were transcribed into text within 24 hours, verified by a senior nurse (>10y of experience), and confirmed with subjects. Conventional content analysis, described by Hsieh and Shannon,13 was used for a qualitative analytic approach: repeated reading of data to gain an overall understanding; open coding of transcripts, followed by grouping of similar/related codes; categorization of codes into themes and subthemes. Two researchers repeatedly compared, summarized, and coded recurring viewpoints to develop initial themes. These were subsequently refined through group discussions to finalize core themes.

Results

Results of cross-sectional survey

This study included 144 patients, among whom 54.9% were men, with a mean age of 57.2 years and a mean dialysis duration of 49.8 months. The primary disease was predominantly chronic glomerulonephritis (71.5%) (table 2). Analysis showed that exercise willingness was significantly positively correlated with left/right handgrip strength (r=0.219, P=.011; r=0.190, P=.027) and skeletal muscle mass (r=0.243, P=.005), and negatively correlated with timed Up and Go test time (r=–0.193, P=.025) (table 3). Results of qualitative research

Three core dimensions of barriers were extracted through interviews.(1)Embodied and socially reinforced perceptions of bodily fragility: misaligned understandings of exercise and disease-specific safety concerns:(a)Patients’ poor scientific grasp of exercise rehabilitation was reflected in limited awareness of home-based rehabilitation and its benefits, with some believing they were too frail for exercise and fearing it would worsen their condition. This misconception stemmed from patients/families’ lack of relevant knowledge and inadequate medical staff health education. For example:P2: “Can exercise cure my disease? …Wouldn't exercise make it worse? Wouldn't it be a heavier burden?”P9: “ Exercising with peritoneal dialysis fluid is inconvenient. I dare not squat up and down, and I don't dare to move at all. Moreover, I'm getting older, and my legs often cramp….”(b)Equating daily activities with rehabilitation exercise. Some patients confused the concepts of “housework” and “systematic exercise,” believing that “doing housework or daily work is already tiring enough, and there’s no need for additional exercise.” For example:P8: “…I think that going to the market to buy vegetables and cooking every day is also exercise.”(2)Safety dimension: complication fear drives exercise behavioral inhibition. Frailty and renal dysfunction in patients undergoing PD make them fear exercise-related complications (catheter displacement/infection, bleeding, and cardiovascular events), which severely restricts their rehabilitation and reflects their high safety focus. For example:P7: “I have anemia. I’m very worried about dizziness during walking exercise, which may make me faint. I will drink more water after exercise, and I’m also worried that my feet will swell” .P13: “… I’m worried that my knees will be swollen and painful after exercise. … I’m afraid that exercising and sweating will make me catch a cold and get peritonitis, which will require hospitalization again.”P16: “ I'm worried about high blood pressure and hypoglycemia during exercise.”(3)Systemic and care-delivery limitations: Inadequate professional guidance, structural resource gaps, and variable social support(a)Limited access to information: patients undergoing PD had scarce exercise rehabilitation information channels, relying mostly on medical staff’s verbal advice and simple materials, with almost no professional guidance from the internet, community activities, or other sources. This left them no scientific basis for exercise planning and method selection, hindering effective rehabilitation. For example:P3: “My nurse told me to walk after meals every day, but she didn't tell me how to walk specifically, how long to walk, or whether I could do other exercises.”P5: “The doctor and my nurse only said to exercise appropriately, but they didn't specify how to exercise or how long to exercise. If I check online, I'm afraid that the information is misleading and that doing it wrong will be more dangerous.”(b)Urgent need for guidance: patients sought continuous home rehabilitation guidance for exercise safety; 1-time inpatient guidance failed to meet their needs, and they wanted dynamic plan adjustments. For example:P4: “I have learned lesson of lower-limb exercises, but after a long time, if no one supervised me, I gradually forgot.”(c)Weak support system: poor family understanding and contradictory support. Families act as both potential support systems and exercise barriers: some patients quit exercise for lack of family companionship/support, while others had their activities overly restricted because of safety fears. For example:P11: “I’m unemployed because of this disease. My wife looks down on me. I hope society can help me. If I have a job, I'll have income, which will make me feel more active in exercise, and willing to consider exercising.”P15: “No one encourages or supervises me. I lack confidence in exercising alone, and I feel pressured. I try not to increase my family’s burden.”(d)Structural lack of community rehabilitation resources.Primary medical care lacked professional equipment and personnel for patients undergoing PD. This was particularly prominent among patients in suburban areas:P8: “Now the design of rehabilitation exercise places in our community is unreasonable, and the exercise facilities are useless. The planning is unreasonable. I like playing table tennis, but the table tennis court is outdoors, so I can't play when it rains.”P12: “I live in the suburbs. There’s no professional rehabilitation institution nearby, and I don't know if the online plans are suitable.”

Discussion

Strengthening cognitive and psychological interventions to correct cognitive gaps between exercise willingness and behavioral practice

Cognitive and psychological interventions for home-based rehabilitation exercise are crucial measures to improve patients’ quality of life.14 Studies have shown that home-based exercise has significant positive effects on physical function and health-related quality of life in patients undergoing PD.15,16 This study found significant correlations between exercise willingness in patients undergoing PD and certain physical function indicators, as well as with skeletal muscle mass. These positive correlations may be attributed to the fact that patients with higher exercise willingness are more likely to engage in strength training or physical activities. Appropriate exercise can stimulate muscle contraction, promote muscle protein synthesis, and increase muscle strength and mass.17 The lack of significant correlation between exercise willingness and 6-m walking speed or 5-time sit-to-stand test results may be related to factors such as limited sample size, large individual differences among patients, and the complexity of the disease itself.

Most patients undergoing PD engage in rehabilitation exercises spontaneously, which is mainly influenced by their living habits and subjective awareness. Our quantitative findings identified a striking intention-behavior gap (92% willingness vs 42% regular exercise) and significant positive correlations between higher exercise willingness and better muscle strength, skeletal muscle mass, and mobility. Our qualitative findings directly explain this gap: fear of exercise-related complications, socially reinforced perceptions of bodily fragility, and systemic barriers to tailored guidance all inhibit the translation of willingness into consistent behavior. Furthermore, the quantitative association between better physical function and higher exercise willingness aligns with patients’ accounts of feeling able to exercise only when their bodies feel reliable and strong. Framing these findings through the lens of the “fragile body,”18 we find patients’ exercise avoidance stems not solely from cognitive misconceptions, but from an acquired fragility identity shaped by lived PD experiences, clinical caution, family overprotection, and the practical realities of home PD. This framework clarifies why education alone is insufficient to change behavior, as exercise reluctance is rooted in embodied, socially mediated, and systemic constraints, not just individual knowledge deficits.

To overcome these barriers, personalized exercise plans and psychological support are necessary. Studies have shown that illustrated patient education manuals (including exercise risk warning flowcharts) and phased video courses (eg, demonstrations of seated training for beginners) can effectively correct cognitive biases.19 Luo et al20 reported that video-guided exercise interventions, through visual action demonstrations, significantly improved depression levels and sleep quality in patients undergoing PD after 12 weeks. These findings confirm that standalone education cannot close the exercise intention-behavior gap in patients undergoing PD, as exercise avoidance stems from not only knowledge gaps, but core psychosocial factors: internalized bodily fragility, low exercise self-efficacy, and inadequate social support/accountability. Sustained behavior change requires integrated interventions combining psychosocial support and multichannel education to improve patients’ quality of life and long-term health outcomes. Core effective components include peer support from experienced patients undergoing PD for confidence and shared learning, aligned clinician-caregiver-patient messaging balancing safety and activity guidance, and group exercise to reduce isolation and sustain accountability. These must be paired with personalized exercise prescription, safety monitoring, and system-level resource upgrades to meaningfully close this gap.

Constructing a safe exercise system to eliminate patient concerns about exercise risks

Significant knowledge gaps exist. International surveys have shown that most patients undergoing PD recognize the benefits of physical activity, but have knowledge deficits regarding specific exercise types and intensities.11 In this study, >70% of patients reported “lack of exercise guidance information” as a barrier, highlighting the need to strengthen standardized exercise prescription education for medical staff. This safety anxiety stems from dual knowledge gaps: patients cannot adequately monitor exercise-related physiological changes, while medical staff lack standardized assessment protocols for exercise prescription. Recommended measures include stratified exercise prescriptions, where high-barrier patients (eg, osteopathy, fall fear) are prioritized for safety assessments with low-intensity exercises recommended, and low-barrier patients are encouraged to do regular aerobic exercise (walking, tai chi), and technology-enabled safety monitoring via wearable devices for real-time heart rate and gait stability monitoring to reduce exercise risks.

Improving social support networks to enhance the synergistic effect of family care

Our qualitative findings of insufficient family support and limited access to professional/community rehabilitation resources directly align with the quantitative barriers of lack of exercise guidance and lack of exercise partners. A core set of barriers stems directly from system-level and care-delivery limitations, rather than individual patient factors. These include limited access to PD-specific tailored exercise guidance, lack of ongoing professional supervision and dynamic plan adjustment, insufficient community-based rehabilitation infrastructure for patients undergoing PD, and gaps in frontline clinical staff training for standardized exercise prescription. In resource-constrained environments, family members often assume the responsibility of caring for patients, but face numerous challenges because of a lack of professional knowledge and support.21 For example, family caregivers often have to act as “family doctors” without adequate training and support, and their burden is further aggravated by insufficient understanding of disease progression and limited access to professional support.22 The information and educational needs of family caregivers can be effectively met by providing knowledge and skill training, improving their preparedness, and supporting role identification and confidence building.23 Recommendations include multidisciplinary collaboration models: collaborate with rehabilitation departments to develop personalized plans through closed-loop management of “in-hospital assessment, home-based guidance, and community follow-up.”; family empowerment programs: Provide training for family members on caregiving to alleviate concerns about “companionship burden”; and integration of community resources: promote primary medical centers to establish exclusive exercise groups and courses for patients undergoing PD, offering low-intensity group activities such as Baduanjin and Tai Ji.

Study limitations

There are several limitations to this study on the factors influencing rehabilitation exercise behavior in patients undergoing PD. First, we included only a single-center sample, thus restricting its geographic representation and limiting its coverage of regions that have different medical resources and economic levels. Although our purposive maximum variation sampling strategy ensured representation of patients across key demographic and clinical dimensions, the sample was limited to patients willing to participate in qualitative interviews; thus, the perspectives of patients with introverted personalities or low communication willingness may be underrepresented. Although the 16 qualitative interview samples were determined based on data saturation, exercise barriers in special subgroups, such as patients with serious complications, may not have been fully captured. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study failed to track dynamic changes in the exercise behavior of patients undergoing PD over time, making it difficult to capture the long-term impact of disease progression or policy adjustments on exercise rehabilitation. Future studies should endeavor to expand the number of multicenter samples, introduce longitudinal follow-up, and combine internationally recognized scales to improve data comparability. This should enhance the generalizability and clinical transformation value of the research results.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study identified psychosocially reinforced bodily fragility, illness-related safety concerns, and systemic care gaps as key drivers of the exercise intention-behavior gap among patients undergoing PD and confirmed a positive correlation between exercise willingness and physical function. Effective interventions must prioritize psychosocial barriers alongside educational, safety, and system-level upgrades. We propose a multilevel model integrating targeted psychosocial support, precision education, standardized safety protocols, PD-specific exercise plans, and cross-sector coordination to improve patients’ exercise adherence and quality of life. Larger multicenter studies are needed to validate these interventions.
