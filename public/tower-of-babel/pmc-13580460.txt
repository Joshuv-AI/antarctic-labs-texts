Patient-Specific Virtual Reality Rehearsal for Technically Challenging Lumbar Transforaminal Epidural Steroid Injection: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Sunmin Kim, Jeewon Kim, Yookyung Jang, Hakmoo Cho, Taesan Kim, Jae Chul Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLumbar transforaminal epidural steroid injection (TFESI) can be technically challenging in patients with complex spinal anatomy. This pilot study aimed to evaluate whether patient-specific three-dimensional (3D) virtual reality (VR) rehearsal generated from pre-procedural CT images improves procedural performance during anatomically challenging lumbar TFESI.Patients and MethodsThis prospective, single-center, randomized controlled trial included 30 patients scheduled for technically challenging lumbar TFESI. Participants were randomly assigned to either the control group (n = 15) or the VR group (n = 15). In the VR group, patient-specific 3D models were generated from pre-procedural lumbar CT images and the operator performed VR rehearsal one day before the procedure. In the control group, pre-procedural CT images and, when available, magnetic resonance imaging findings were reviewed before the procedure. All procedures were performed by a single experienced pain specialist. The primary outcomes were procedure time and the number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions. Secondary outcomes included needle-bone contacts, intravascular contrast uptake, patient satisfaction, and procedure completion.ResultsAll patients received the allocated intervention and were included in the final analysis. Procedure time was shorter in the VR group than in the control group (215.7 ± 46.8 sec vs 254.5 ± 50.3 sec; mean difference, −38.9 sec; 95% CI, −75.2 to −2.5; p = 0.037). The number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions was also lower in the VR group (10.27 ± 2.74 vs 13.27 ± 3.65 images; mean difference, −3.00 images; 95% CI, −5.41 to −0.59; p = 0.017). The number of needle-bone contacts was numerically lower in the VR group, although this difference was not statistically significant (1.3 ± 1.1 vs 2.1 ± 1.3, p = 0.081). Patient satisfaction and intravascular contrast uptake did not differ significantly between groups. All procedures were completed as planned.ConclusionIn this pilot study, patient-specific VR rehearsal before technically challenging lumbar TFESI was associated with shorter procedure time and fewer fluoroscopic image acquisitions. These preliminary findings support further evaluation of patient-specific VR rehearsal as a useful pre-procedural planning tool for anatomically challenging TFESI cases.Keywords: virtual reality, simulation, transforaminal epidural steroid injection, 3d Modeling

Introduction

Lumbar transforaminal epidural steroid injection (TFESI) is a widely used non-surgical treatment for lumbosacral radicular pain.1 By delivering corticosteroids around the affected nerve root, TFESI may reduce inflammation and alleviate pain; however, the procedure can be technically challenging in some patients because of anatomical complexity and individual variations.2 In particular, cases involving chronic degenerative changes in the lumbar spine such as reduced disc height, facet joint hypertrophy, severe foraminal stenosis, lateral recess narrowing, and osteophyte formation require a high level of skill and experience because of the complexity of the needle trajectory and the likelihood of bony contact.3

TFESI can also be associated with several procedure-related complications, ranging from transient adverse events to rare but potentially catastrophic neurologic injuries. Reported complications include transient pain at the injection site, headache, nausea, intravascular injection, spinal epidural abscess, spinal cord infarction, epidural hematoma, and irreversible neurologic deficits, including paraplegia.4–8 Because TFESI requires accurate needle placement within a narrow and anatomically variable transforaminal area, careful pre-procedural assessment of patient-specific spinal anatomy and planning of the needle trajectory are essential. This is particularly important in anatomically challenging cases, in which degenerative changes, foraminal stenosis, lateral recess stenosis, or altered spinal alignment may make accurate needle placement more difficult and thereby increase the potential for unintended vascular uptake, neural irritation or injury, and other procedure-related adverse events.

Given these technical and anatomical challenges, virtual reality (VR) has been investigated as a potential tool for three-dimensional (3D) anatomical visualization and procedural simulation. VR-based simulation has been studied across a range of healthcare applications, from complex surgical procedures to hospital disaster-response training, and may allow clinicians to interact with 3D anatomy and rehearse procedural steps in advance.9–12

The application of immersive technologies to TFESI remains limited. Our group previously evaluated an augmented reality (AR) navigation system that displayed patient-specific spinal anatomy and tracked needle position in real time during transforaminal epidural injection.13 However, that system was designed to provide intraprocedural navigation, whereas most previous VR simulation studies have focused on generic procedural training using standardized anatomical models. The potential role of patient-specific VR rehearsal performed before TFESI has therefore not been sufficiently evaluated.

In this context, we developed a patient-specific 3D VR simulation system based on pre-procedural lumbar CT images and designed the present pilot randomized controlled trial. The study aimed to preliminarily evaluate the potential of this system as a pre-procedural planning tool for technically challenging lumbar TFESI by comparing procedural measures with those following conventional image-based preparation.

Material and Methods

Conversion of Patient Imaging Data

To enable patient-specific VR rehearsal, we developed a custom software platform that converts preprocedural lumbar CT images into individualized three-dimensional digital twin models while preserving patient-specific anatomical characteristics relevant to TFESI planning. CT images in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format were imported into the software and processed to identify clinically relevant anatomical structures, including the vertebrae, intervertebral foramina, spinal canal, and skin surface.

Using a method similar to that described in our previous study,13 anatomical information and spatial coordinate data were extracted from the CT images and transformed into an integrated data structure suitable for real-time simulation. This process enabled anatomical structures to retain spatial relationships and procedural attributes required for interaction within the virtual environment.

The segmented anatomical structures were reconstructed into three-dimensional mesh models using an isosurface extraction algorithm based on the marching cubes method. To support real-time visualization and simulation, mesh optimization procedures were applied to reduce polygon counts while preserving anatomical fidelity. In addition to geometric reconstruction, the processed data were converted into simulation-compatible digital twin models that could support virtual fluoroscopic imaging, anatomical interaction, and procedural rehearsal based on patient-specific anatomy.

To streamline the workflow, image processing, mesh generation, and model optimization were largely automated, enabling efficient generation of patient-specific digital twin models before the scheduled procedure (Figure 1).

Simulator Development

The patient-specific digital twin models were integrated into a VR simulation platform designed for preprocedural TFESI rehearsal. A dedicated server was developed to facilitate transmission and loading of patient-specific simulation data, enabling individualized rehearsal based on each patient’s imaging data. The VR environment was implemented for the Meta Quest platform (Meta Platforms, Menlo Park, CA, USA) and recreated a procedural setting consisting of a virtual procedure table, fluoroscopy monitors, and a virtual C-arm (Figure 2A).

The virtual reality simulation environment and key features. (A) Virtual procedure room mimicking a real-world clinical setting, including a virtual C-arm, surgical table, and monitors. (B) Simulation of needle insertion; the operator can position the needle on the patient’s back and fine-tune the angle using hand controllers. (C) Manipulation of the virtual C-arm and visualization of the corresponding fluoroscopic view on the monitor. (D) Visualization of the underlying skeletal anatomy by adjusting tissue transparency, providing feedback on the needle’s trajectory and its spatial relationship with the spine.

To enhance procedural realism, the simulator incorporated hand-tracking based interaction that allowed users to manipulate virtual instruments using their natural hand movements. The virtual needle could be grasped and positioned freely in space and advanced toward the patient model in a manner similar to actual clinical practice. After the needle entered the skin, virtual control buttons became available around the insertion site to facilitate precise adjustments beyond the accuracy achievable with hand tracking alone. These controls enabled incremental advancement, withdrawal, and angular modification of the needle. Angular adjustments were performed around the original skin entry point, thereby reproducing the pivoting motion commonly used during TFESI procedures (Figure 2B).

The virtual C-arm was programmed to reproduce the positioning capabilities of a conventional fluoroscopy system, including rotational, angular, and translational adjustments. These movements allowed users to obtain fluoroscopic views comparable to those used during actual TFESI procedures (Figure 2C). Virtual fluoroscopic images reflecting the current C-arm position, patient-specific anatomy, and needle location could be acquired throughout the rehearsal process, enabling evaluation of needle trajectories and anatomical relationships during simulated fluoroscopic guidance.

The simulator also incorporated anatomy-based interaction events to provide procedural feedback during rehearsal. Collision detection algorithms identified contact between the needle and osseous structures and prevented unrealistic advancement through bone. Additional anatomy-specific events were triggered when the needle entered predefined anatomical regions. For example, dural puncture automatically triggered a virtual cerebrospinal fluid leakage event, allowing users to recognize a potential procedural complication.

Following fluoroscopy-guided rehearsal, tissue transparency could be adjusted to reveal the underlying anatomy and the final needle position within the patient-specific model. This feature enabled users to directly visualize the spatial relationship between the simulated fluoroscopic image, needle trajectory, and surrounding anatomical structures, thereby providing intuitive feedback regarding procedural accuracy and anatomical targeting (Figure 2D).

The overall simulation framework was developed using an approach similar to that described in our previous study13 and was designed to support patient-specific procedural planning and rehearsal rather than generic skills training. By integrating patient-specific anatomy, virtual fluoroscopy, anatomy-based procedural events, and natural hand interaction, the system enabled operators to explore procedural strategies, understand patient-specific anatomical constraints, and anticipate technical challenges before the actual intervention.

During the study, the one physician performed the entire workflow for each patient. The physician transferred the DICOM data to a personal computer, imported the data into the custom software, and initiated the largely automated model-generation process using approximately two to three user commands. Model generation was generally completed within approximately 5–10 minutes, after which the model was uploaded to the dedicated server, downloaded to the VR device, and loaded into the simulation environment for rehearsal. No software failure prevented completion of this workflow during the study.

Study Design

This study was designed as a prospective, randomized, single-center pilot clinical trial. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea University Anam Hospital (approval No. 2024AN0442) and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06643572). Patient enrollment began on December 4, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. No lower or upper age limits were prespecified in the study protocol. Given the exploratory pilot design, no formal hypothesis-based sample size calculation was performed. The target sample size was set at 30 participants, with 15 participants in each group.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) were scheduled for lumbar TFESI for lumbar radiculopathy; (2) had available pre-procedural lumbar CT imaging; and (3) had imaging findings expected to make transforaminal needle access technically challenging, such as severe foraminal stenosis, lateral recess stenosis, marked degenerative changes, scoliosis, or spondylolisthesis. The imaging findings were reviewed by four board-certified pain specialists. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and enrollment required final consensus among all four specialists. The exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of prior TFESI at our institution; (2) spinal deformity or prior lumbar surgery that made a transforaminal approach technically infeasible; or (3) declined to provide written informed consent to participate in the study.

All included patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the VR or control group using a computer-generated randomization list. Group allocation was performed sequentially according to the pre-generated randomization sequence after each participant had provided written informed consent.

For participants in the VR group, a patient-specific 3D anatomical model was generated from each patient’s pre-procedural CT images one day before the scheduled procedure. The operator then rehearsed the procedure in the VR environment by interactively manipulating the anatomical model and using a virtual needle to explore potential trajectories from multiple perspectives and identify anatomical constraints relevant to TFESI. The planned trajectory was also evaluated using simulated fluoroscopic views provided by a virtual C-arm. In the control group, each patient’s CT images and, when available, MRI images were reviewed using a conventional two-dimensional picture archiving and communication system (PACS) viewer before the procedure according to standard clinical practice. All pre-procedural imaging reviews, VR rehearsals, and TFESI procedures were performed by a single pain physician with more than five years of experience in interventional pain management.

Because of the nature of the intervention, the operator could not be blinded to group assignment. Participants and the staff responsible for collecting patient satisfaction data were blinded to group assignment.

Transforaminal Epidural Steroid Injection Procedure

All participants underwent a single-level, unilateral lumbar TFESI with the patient in the prone position under fluoroscopic guidance and standard aseptic conditions. Multilevel and bilateral procedures were not permitted. After local anesthetic infiltration with 2% lidocaine, a 22-gauge, 3.5-inch Quincke spinal needle was advanced toward the target neural foramen using a conventional subpedicular approach. The final needle tip position was confirmed using anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views. A total of 0.5 mL of iodinated contrast medium (Omnipaque 300; GE Healthcare, Carrigtwohill, Co. Cork, Ireland) was then injected to confirm appropriate epidural spread and exclude intravascular uptake. After appropriate epidural spread and the absence of intravascular uptake were confirmed, a 5-mL injectate containing ropivacaine at a final concentration of 0.185%, dexamethasone 5 mg, and hyaluronidase 1500 IU was administered. The procedural protocol was identical in both study groups, and each participant contributed one procedure to the analysis.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were procedure time and the number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions. Procedure time was defined as the interval from the initiation of local anesthetic infiltration to final needle removal. The number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions was defined as the number of discrete C-arm image acquisitions performed during the procedure. Secondary outcomes included the number of needle-bone contacts, intravascular contrast uptake, patient satisfaction, and procedure completion. Needle-bone contacts were defined as instances in which the spinal needle contacted a bony structure during needle advancement. Each contact was counted in real time by the operator based on tactile feedback and recorded on the case report form by an assisting staff member. Intravascular contrast uptake was recorded as a binary outcome according to whether a vascular contrast pattern was observed during the contrast injection. Patient satisfaction was assessed immediately after the procedure using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied). Procedure completion was defined as successful completion of the planned lumbar TFESI without abandonment of the procedure or conversion to an alternative approach.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables, such as procedure time, the number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions, number of needle-bone contacts, and patient satisfaction scores, were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. To compare continuous variables between the VR group and the control group, we used the independent t-test after verifying normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For the primary outcomes, between-group mean differences (VR group minus the control group) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Categorical variables, including the incidence of intravascular contrast uptake, were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Procedure completion status was reported descriptively without inferential statistical comparison. A p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 35 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 5 patients were excluded before randomization: 3 declined participation, and 2 did not attend the scheduled study visit. The remaining 30 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the control group (n = 15) or the VR group (n = 15). All randomized participants underwent the assigned study protocol and were included in the final analysis. No data were missing for any baseline variable or study outcome. The participant flow is shown in Figure 3. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in age, sex, body mass index (BMI), magnetic resonance imaging findings (eg, herniated disc, spinal stenosis, or spondylolisthesis), or the target level of the procedure (all p > 0.05). The clinical and procedural outcomes are detailed in Table 2. For the primary outcomes, procedure time was shorter in the VR group than in the control group (215.7 ± 46.8 sec vs 254.5 ± 50.3 sec; mean difference, −38.9 seconds; 95% CI, −75.2 to −2.5; p = 0.037). The number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions was also lower in the VR group (10.27 ± 2.74 vs 13.27 ± 3.65 acquisitions; mean difference, −3.00 acquisitions; 95% CI, −5.41 to −0.59; p = 0.017). Regarding the secondary outcomes, the number of needle-bone contacts was numerically lower in the VR group (control vs VR: 2.1 ± 1.3 vs 1.3 ± 1.1), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.081). There were no significant differences between the control and VR groups in patient satisfaction scores measured using a 5-point Likert scale (control vs VR: 4.1 ± 0.9 vs 4.0 ± 0.7, p = 0.816) or in the incidence of intravascular contrast uptake (control vs VR: 1/15 [6.7%] vs 2/15 [13.3%], p = 1.000). All procedures were completed as planned in both groups, with no cases of procedure abandonment or conversion to an alternative approach. Procedure completion was therefore 100% in both groups and was reported descriptively without inferential statistical comparison.

Discussion

In this pilot randomized controlled trial, we evaluated a patient-specific 3D VR simulation system for pre-procedural rehearsal before technically challenging lumbar TFESI. The system converted pre-procedural lumbar CT images into interactive 3D models, allowing rehearsal of the planned procedure within a patient-specific virtual environment reconstructed from each patient’s CT data. Whereas previous studies, including our own, have primarily focused on VR simulation as an educational tool for trainees or novice operators,14 the present study evaluated its application to patient-specific pre-procedural rehearsal for an experienced operator managing anatomically challenging cases.

The principal finding was that pre-procedural VR rehearsal was associated with shorter procedure time and fewer fluoroscopic image acquisitions, even when the procedure was performed by an experienced pain specialist. However, these findings should be considered preliminary because of the pilot design, small sample size, and use of a single operator. The number of needle-bone contacts was also numerically lower in the VR group, although the difference was not statistically significant.

Importantly, the lower number of fluoroscopic image acquisitions should not be interpreted as evidence of reduced radiation exposure. Radiation burden is influenced by multiple factors, including fluoroscopy time, cumulative air kerma, dose-area product, magnification, collimation, and fluoroscopy settings. Because these parameters were not measured, the present findings indicate only a difference in the frequency of fluoroscopic image acquisitions and do not establish a reduction in radiation dose.

Nevertheless, the concurrent findings of shorter procedure time and fewer fluoroscopic image acquisitions provide preliminary support for further evaluating patient-specific VR rehearsal as a pre-procedural planning tool for technically challenging TFESI.

Previous research on VR simulation in medicine has primarily focused on training novices or residents. VR-based curricula have been applied to laparoscopic cholecystectomy,15 percutaneous iliosacral screw placement,16 cataract surgery,17 and disaster response,9 with reported improvements in technical performance, procedural understanding, or operator confidence. However, our study differs from previous work by focusing on patient-specific pre-procedural rehearsal for an experienced operator, rather than generic procedural training for trainees. Our findings suggest that even skilled operators may benefit from patient-specific rehearsal when managing anatomically challenging cases. In conventional practice, experienced physicians often rely on mental imagery based on 2D CT/MRI images. Our VR system upgrades this “mental rehearsal” to a “virtual rehearsal”, allowing the physician to visualize the 3D anatomy and plan the anticipated needle trajectory non-invasively before the procedure.

Our center has previously investigated VR simulation for lumbar TFESI education targeting residents.14 In that earlier study, technical limitations required the use of standardized 3D spine models rather than patient-specific models, and the simulator was primarily designed for procedural step training. In the present study, we developed a workflow that automates the generation of patient-specific 3D models from pre-procedural CT images, thereby making patient-specific simulation more feasible as a pre-procedural planning tool in clinical practice.

Patient-specific VR simulation may have both educational and clinical value in interventional pain practice. Interventional pain procedures require a detailed understanding of three-dimensional spinal anatomy. Traditional training often relies on apprenticeship, cadaver-based education, or phantom models. Although cadavers provide realistic anatomy, they are costly, limited in availability, and unsuitable for repeated practice. Phantoms, on the other hand, lack anatomical variability and tissue realism. Furthermore, increasing emphasis on patient safety has supported the use of simulation-based education as an adjunct to traditional hands-on training.18 In this context, patient-specific VR simulation may provide a safe and repeatable environment for understanding complex anatomy and rehearsing procedural steps. For experienced clinicians, it may also serve as a pre-procedural planning tool to identify patient-specific anatomical constraints, evaluate potential needle trajectories, and plan fluoroscopy-guided needle access before the actual procedure. The observed associations with shorter procedure time and fewer fluoroscopic image acquisitions provide preliminary support for further evaluating this potential application in anatomically challenging TFESI.

There are several limitations to this study. First, this was a single-center pilot study with a relatively small sample size. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require validation in larger multicenter studies. The small sample size and low number of intravascular contrast-uptake events also precluded meaningful evaluation of uncommon complications or procedural safety. Second, all procedures were performed by a single experienced pain specialist who was familiar with VR technology. Because operator experience and familiarity with the VR interface could influence how effectively the simulation is used, we used a single operator to maintain consistency across procedures and reduce operator-related variability in this pilot study. However, this design may limit the generalizability of the findings to other operators, particularly those less familiar with digital or VR-based interfaces. Third, the operator could not be blinded to group allocation because the intervention required pre-procedural VR simulation. Knowledge of group allocation may have influenced the operator’s procedural behavior, including the pace of the procedure and the decision to acquire fluoroscopic images; therefore, performance bias cannot be excluded for the primary outcomes. In addition, the number of needle-bone contacts was determined by the operator based on tactile feedback. Although an assisting staff member recorded the reported count, this outcome was not independently assessed and may have been subject to measurement and reporting bias. Fourth, case eligibility was based on pre-procedural imaging findings reviewed by four board-certified pain specialists, but we did not use a validated objective scoring system to quantify procedural difficulty. Therefore, some degree of subjectivity in the selection of technically challenging cases may have remained. Finally, this pilot study was designed to evaluate procedural performance and did not assess patient-centered clinical outcomes, such as post-procedural pain intensity, functional improvement, analgesic consumption, or treatment response. Accordingly, the observed differences in procedural measures should not be interpreted as evidence that VR rehearsal improves patient clinical outcomes following TFESI.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this pilot randomized controlled trial, patient-specific VR rehearsal before anatomically challenging lumbar TFESI was associated with shorter procedure time and fewer fluoroscopic image acquisitions. These preliminary findings support further evaluation of patient-specific VR rehearsal as a pre-procedural planning tool for anatomically challenging TFESI. Larger multicenter studies involving multiple operators and objective measures of procedural difficulty are needed to confirm these findings.
