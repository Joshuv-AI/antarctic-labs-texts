Shortest Circuits in Homology Classes of Graphs
Ye Luo

Abstract
. Recently, the study of circuits and cycles within the homology classes of graphs has attracted considerable research interest. However, the detection and counting of shorter circuits in homology classes, especially the shortest ones, remain underexplored. This paper aims to fill this gap by solving the problem of detecting and counting the shortest cycles in homology classes, leveraging the concept of direction-consistent circuits and extending classical results on Eulerian circuits such as Hierholzer’s algorithm and the BEST theorem. As an application, we propose the one-carrier transportation routing problem and relate it to a circuit detection problem in graph homology.

Introduction

In graph theory, the homology groups of graphs
[
SS13
]

[
LR24
]

In this paper, we will take a different approach by investigating shorter circuits, specifically focusing on the detecting and counting problems of the shortest circuits within the homology classes of graphs.

3.
Detecting and counting direction-consistent circuits for connected circulations

The notion of direction-consistent circuits on a graph can be considered as a generalization of the notion of Eulerian circuits on an Eulerian digraph. In this section, we will discuss the detecting and counting problems of direction-consistent circuits for connected circulations
\alpha\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
, with results generalizing the Hierholzer’s algorithm for detecting Eulerian circuits and the BEST theorem for counting Eulerian circuits.

3.1.
Detecting direction-consistent circuits: generalized Hierholzer’s algorithm

Hierholzer’s Algorithm
[
BLW98
]
is a classic method for finding Eulerian circuits on an Eulerian graph
G
, proposed by Carl Hierholzer in 1873. Hierholzer’s Algorithm is highly efficient, taking linear time
O(|E|)
where
|E|
is the number of edges on
G
. It constructs paths incrementally until an Eulerian circuit is formed, and can be described as follows:

(1)

Choose a starting point: Start from any vertex in
G
.

(2)

Construct an initial path: Traverse unvisited edges from the current vertex, marking them as visited, until you return to the starting vertex, forming a closed path.

(3)

Handle remaining edges: Check vertices in the current path. If a vertex has unvisited edges, start a new closed path from that vertex and embed it into the existing path.

(4)

Complete the path: Repeat the process until all edges are visited, resulting in the complete Eulerian circuit on
G
.

The following lemma can be straightforwardly verified.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each nonzero
\alpha\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{G}(\alpha,l)
is empty for all
l<\|\alpha\|
, and if
{\mathcal{C}}_{G}(\alpha,\|\alpha\|)
is nonempty, then
\alpha
is connected and all circuits in
{\mathcal{C}}_{G}(\alpha,\|\alpha\|)
are direction-consistent.

We will show in Theorem
3.3
that the converse statement is also true, i.e., as long as a nonzero
\alpha\in H_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
is connected, we may always find a direction-consistent circuit whose abelianization is
\alpha
. To prove this theorem, we may apply an approach analogous to Hierholzer’s algorithm to construct a desirable direction-consistent circuit. The procedures are shown in Algorithm
1
.

We will need the following lemma in our proof of Theorem
3.3
, which can be easily verified.

Lemma 3.2

For
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{G}

3.2.
Counting direction-consistent circuits: generalized BEST theorem

The well-known BEST theorem, named after de Bruijn and van Aardenne-Ehrenfest
[
vAEdB51
]
and Smith and Tutte
[
TS41
]
, provides an efficient algorithm of counting Eulerian cycles on an Eulerian digraph
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
(meaning that the in-degree and out-degree at each vertex of
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
are identical).

Recall that an arborescence
T
of
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
with a root vertex
w
is a spanning tree of
G
such that all edges on
T
are oriented towards
w
with respect to
{\mathfrak{o}}
.
Recall also that the Laplacian matrix of
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
, denoted
L(G_{\mathfrak{o}})
, is defined as: (i) for
i\neq j
,
(L(G_{\mathfrak{o}}))_{ij}
is the negation of the number of positively oriented edges with respect to
{\mathfrak{o}}
with initial vertex
v_{i}
and terminal vertex
v_{j}
, and (ii)
(L(G_{\mathfrak{o}}))_{ii}=-\sum_{j\neq i}(L(G_{\mathfrak{o}}))_{ij}
. Removing the row and column of
L(G_{\mathfrak{o}})
with respect to
w
, we get the reduced Laplacian
L^{\red(w)}(G_{\mathfrak{o}})
at
w
.

Denote by
\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
the number of Eulerian cycles on
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
, and
\In_{w}(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
the number of arborescences
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
rooted at
w
. Then the BEST theorem states that
\ec(G,{\mathfrak{o}})=\In_{w}(G,{\mathfrak{o}})\cdot\prod_{v\in V(G)}(\deg^{+}_{\mathfrak{o}}(v)-1)!
where
\deg^{+}_{\mathfrak{o}}(v)
is the out-degree of
G_{\mathfrak{o}}
at
v\in V(G)
. Note that by a directed version of Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theorem,
\In_{w}(G,{\mathfrak{o}})
is actually independent of
w
, and can be efficiently computed as the determinant of the reduced Laplacian matrix
L^{\red(w)}(G_{\mathfrak{o}})
.

Before making a generalization of the BEST theorem, we first introduce a notion of weighted Laplacian here.

Definition 3.5
.

Consider a graph
G
. Let
\alpha\in C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
be universal. Let
c_{\mathbf{e}}=\deg_{\alpha}({\mathbf{e}})
for all
{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}(G)
. Then

5.
An application to the one-carrier Transportation Routing Problem of type (1,1)

The Transportation Routing Problem
[
TGF74
]
is a specific type of optimization problem that focuses on determining the most efficient routes for vehicles to transport goods from a single or multiple sources to multiple destinations. The transportation routing problem is a key component of logistics and distribution planning, as it helps companies optimize their delivery operations, reduce transportation costs, and improve overall efficiency. Based on variants of assumptions of constraints, Transportation Routing Problem can be related to some classical optimization problems, such as the Traveling Salesman Problem
[
HPR+13
,
JM97
]
and the Vehicle Routing Problem
[
PGGM13
,
BRN16
]
.

Here we propose a special case of the Transportation Routing Problem, namely 1c-TRP of type (1,1).

Problem 5.1

(
One-carrier Transportation Routing Problem
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with each edge assigned a non-negative length. A set of items needs to be transported from their
source vertices
to their
destination vertices
. More precisely, each item is initially located at its source vertex and must be transported along the edges of
G
to its destination vertex. There is exactly one carrier with a specified
capacity
for the whole transportation task. The carrier starts at a specified
starting vertex
, follows a planned route to pick up items at their source vertices, delivers them to their designated destination vertices, and finally returns to the starting vertex. During the whole transportation process, the number of items on the carrier
can not exceed its capacity
. The objective is to
minimize the total traveling distance
in order to optimize delivery costs. This is called the
one-carrier Transportation Routing Problem
, or
1c-TRP
. If further assuming that the source and destination vertices for each item are always adjacent, and the capacity for the carrier is
1
, then we call such a special case
1c-TRP of type (1,1)
.

Our solution to this one-carrier Transportation Routing Problem of type (1,1) is based on relating it to the HSCDP.

Here let us first define some partial orderings.

Definition 5.2
.

We endow the space of partial orientations of
G
and
C_{1}(G,{\mathbb{Z}})
with partial orderings as follows:

(i)

For two partial orientations
{\mathfrak{o}}
and
{\mathfrak{o}}^{\prime}
of

, we say

if
\supp({\mathfrak{o}})
is a subgraph of

, and
{\mathfrak{o}}^{\prime}
coincides with
{\mathfrak{o}}
when restricted to
\supp({\mathfrak{o}})
.

(ii)

For

{\mathfrak{o}}
, we say

if
\supp(\beta-\alpha)
is a subgraph of
\supp({\mathfrak{o}})
and

\beta-\alpha=\sum_{{\mathbf{e}}\in{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathfrak{o}}(\supp({\mathfrak{o}}))}c_{\mathbf{e}}\cdot{\mathbf{e}}

for all

(iii)

For

Theorem 5.5
.

If
\beta
derived at Step 6 or Step 13 of Algorithm 3 is connected, then Algorithm 3 solves Problem
5.3
:

Proof.

First we note that each closed walk
C={\mathbf{a}}_{1}\cdots{\mathbf{a}}_{L}
on
G
can be lifted to a circuit
\hat{C}=\hat{{\mathbf{a}}}_{1}\cdots\hat{{\mathbf{a}}}_{L}
on
\hat{G}
in the following way: if
{\mathbf{a}}_{k}
orients from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, then
\hat{{\mathbf{a}}}_{k}
is the unique oriented edge from
\hat{v}_{i}
to
\hat{v}_{j}
with respect to
\hat{{\mathfrak{o}}}
. And in this sense,
\tau_{\mu}(C)=\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C})
. Therefore, finding a closed walk
C
on
G
with initial vertex
v
such that
\theta_{ij}(C)\geq q_{ij}
for all adjacent
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
is equivalent to finding a shortest circuit
\hat{C}
on
\hat{G}
with initial vertex
\hat{v}
such that
\hat{C}^{\ab}\geq\alpha
. Since
\hat{C}^{\ab}\in H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
, this means
\hat{C}^{\ab}\in\Lambda_{\hat{G}}(\alpha)\bigcap H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
. Note that
\Lambda_{\hat{G}}(\alpha)\bigcap H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
must be nonempty, since
\hat{G}
is bridgeless.

Suppose
\hat{v}\in\supp(\alpha)
at Step 5. If
\beta
derived at Step 6 is connected, then at Step 15, Algorithm 2 reduces to Algorithm 1, and the output
\hat{C}
is a direction consistent circuit satisfying
\|\beta\|_{\hat{\mu}}=\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C})
. Therefore, for all circuits
\hat{C^{\prime}}
such that
\hat{C^{\prime}}^{\ab}\in\Lambda_{\hat{G}}(\alpha)\bigcap H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
, by Lemma
2.4
, we know that
\hat{C^{\prime}}
goes through
\hat{v}
and
\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C^{\prime}})\geq\|\hat{C^{\prime}}^{\ab}\|_{\hat{\mu}}\geq\|\beta\|_{\hat{\mu}}=\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C})
, solving Problem
5.3
.

Now suppose
\hat{v}\notin\supp(\alpha)
. Then one can also observe that the desired shortest circuit
\hat{C}
must satisfy
\hat{C}^{\ab}\in\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}\Lambda_{\hat{G}}(\alpha_{i})\right)\bigcap H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
. If
\beta
derived at Step 6 is connected, then at Step 15, Algorithm 2 again reduces to Algorithm 1, and the output
\hat{C}
is a direction consistent circuit satisfying
\|\beta\|_{\hat{\mu}}=\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C})
. Now consider
\hat{C^{\prime}}^{\ab}\in\Lambda_{\hat{G}}(\alpha_{i})\bigcap H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
for some
1\leq i\leq s
, we know that
\hat{C^{\prime}}
goes through
\hat{v}
and
\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C^{\prime}})\geq\|\hat{C^{\prime}}^{\ab}\|_{\hat{\mu}}\geq\|\beta_{i}\|_{\hat{\mu}}\geq\|\beta\|_{\hat{\mu}}=\tau_{\hat{\mu}}(\hat{C})
, solving Problem
5.3
.

∎

Remark 5.6
.

In general, if
\beta
at Step 15 of Algorithm 3 is not connected, then the output of Algorithm 3 is not necessarily an optimal solution. The reason is that the extra paths in the Steiner tree derived when running Algorithm 2 introduces extra length to the output circuit. Actually for
\beta^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{\hat{G}}(\alpha)\bigcap H_{1}(\hat{G},{\mathbb{Z}})
, we may derive a Steiner tree
T_{\beta^{\prime}}
specific to
\beta^{\prime}
(
T_{\beta^{\prime}}
is trivial if and only if
\beta^{\prime}
is connected). Running Algorithm 2 for
\beta^{\prime}
, we get
\hat{C}_{\beta^{\prime}}
such that
\tau_{\mu}(\hat{C}_{\beta^{\prime}})=2\mu(T_{\beta^{\prime}})+\|\beta^{\prime}\|_{\mu}
by Theorem
4.4
. Because of the extra length term
2\mu(T_{\beta^{\prime}})
, optimal
\beta
does not necessarily afford optimal
\hat{C}
if
\beta
is not connected.
