Comparative genomic analysis of bHLH genes across multiple species uncovers a novel gene regulating ginsenoside biosynthesis in Panax ginseng
Authors: Xiaochen Yu, Mingming Liu, Jinghui Yu, Yang Jiang, Sizhang Liu, Jingang Mo, Yi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBasic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) transcription factors are the second largest transcription factor family in plants and regulate diverse physiological processes. This study aimed to investigate the evolution, expansion, and functional divergence of bHLH genes in Panax species and to identify new gene involved in ginsenoside biosynthesis. bHLH genes from six species (Daucus carota, Eleutherococcus senticosus, Panax notoginseng, Panax vietnamensis, Panax quinquefolius, and Panax ginseng) were identified using genome-wide screening. Phylogenetic analysis, inter-species synteny, and structure comparison were performed. Subcellular localization, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and in vivo functional validation were conducted for target bHLH gene from Panax ginseng.ResultsA total of 1,254 bHLH genes were identified and classified into 17 subfamilies. Differences in gene numbers suggested that whole-genome duplication events contributed to family expansion. Synteny analysis revealed 49 orthologous bHLH gene units, most of which showed variation in intron number, potentially leading to functional divergence. Nuclear-localized protein encoded by the sole Myelocytomatosis (MYC) gene Pg.bHLH203 among the orthologous bHLH genes, regulates ginsenoside biosynthesis by modulating the transcription of the key enzyme genes Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS in the ginsenoside biosynthetic pathway.ConclusionsThese results suggest that Pg.bHLH203 is a novel regulator of ginsenoside biosynthesis, identified through evolutionary insights and confirmed by functional validation. This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of bHLH gene evolution in Panax species, filling a key knowledge gap, advancing the understanding of the evolutionary trajectory of this important transcription factor family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07891-y.Keywords: Panax species, BHLH transcription factor, Orthologous gene, Ginsenoside, Metabolic regulation

Introduction

The regulatory functions of transcription factors (TFs) evolve progressively with species divergence [1]. The genomic evolution of specific TFs is associated with increasing biological complexity, including morphological innovations and metabolic diversity [2]. Although basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) TFs represent the second largest TF family in plants after MYB [3], bHLH proteins in modern plants have evolved from one or a few ancestral bHLH genes through extensive gene duplication events [4, 5].

Current studies on bHLH TFs have been carried out not only in major crops [6, 7] and model plants [8], but also in medicinal plants [9, 10], where numerous bHLH genes have been successively identified. Members of the bHLH gene family contain a highly conserved basic helix–loop–helix domain and regulate the expression of downstream genes by binding to specific cis-acting elements [11]. As a result, bHLH family members play widespread roles in regulating various biological processes, including plant growth and development [12, 13], stress responses [13, 14], and signal transduction pathway [15, 16].

Notably, members of the bHLH gene family also play important regulatory roles in the biosynthesis of plant secondary metabolites. According to related research reports, bHLH TFs that specifically regulate the biosynthesis of triterpenoids have been identified in several plant species. For example, a gene cluster (Bt cluster) containing multiple bHLH TFs, in the genome of Cucumis sativus regulates the synthesis and accumulation of the triterpenoid compound cucurbitacin in leaves and fruits by modulating the expression of related genes [17, 18]. In Medicago truncatula, two jasmonic acid (JA)-responsive bHLH TFs cooperate to regulate the expression of key genes in the triterpenoid biosynthesis pathway, thereby modulating triterpenoid production [19]. Highly homologous genes were found to be highly expressed in the seeds of Chenopodium quinoa, where triterpene saponins are abundant [20].

Most species in the Panax species of the Araliaceae family possess significant medicinal value [21], and studies on bHLH TFs in these plants have also been initiated. In Panax notoginseng, one bHLH TF has been shown to enhance triterpenoid biosynthesis by interacting with the E-box core sequence in the promoter region of its target gene [4]. Moreover, six bHLH genes identified in the Panax ginseng genome have been proposed to be involved in the regulation of ginsenoside biosynthesis [22].

Whole-genome duplication (WGD) events is one of the primary driving forces of adaptive evolution in angiosperms and is closely associated with species divergence and geographic distribution [23]. Studies have shown that WGD events is not merely a simple replication event, but rather a complex genomic reorganization process involving extensive changes in gene copy number, including amplification, reduction, and even complete gene loss. Genes with essential biological functions tend to be preferentially retained or even expanded in copy number, while others are gradually lost following an exponential gene loss model with an estimated half-life of 21.6 million years during species evolution [24].

Among currently known Panax species, in addition to several diploid species (2n = 24), Panax ginseng and Panax quinquefolius are allopolyploid species (2n = 48) that form a distinct evolutionary clade due to WGD events [25]. Phylogenetic evidence suggests that this clade originated during the late Oligocene, approximately 25 million years ago, and species divergence within the clade began in the late Miocene, around 14.5 million years ago [26]. To date, the evolutionary relationships of bHLH TFs following WGD events, as well as the functional roles of bHLH family members in regulating triterpenoid biosynthesis, have not yet been investigated in Panax species.

In this study, we identified bHLH genes from the genomes of two diploid Panax species (Panax notoginseng and Panax vietnamensis) and two allopolyploid Panax species (Panax ginseng and Panax quinquefolius). Based on the taxonomic classification of Panax species, we additionally identified bHLH genes from Eleutherococcus senticosus (family Araliaceae, order Apiales) and Daucus carota (family Apiaceae, order Apiales), and conducted a comparative evolutionary analysis of bHLH genes across all six plant species. Here we report 49 orthologous bHLH gene units. Within each unit, bHLH genes from different species share a common ancestral gene that likely existed before the divergence of these six plant species. 25 of these orthologous bHLH genes were duplicated in the genomes of Panax ginseng following WGD events. Furthermore, we validated the role of the sole Myelocytomatosis (MYC) gene among these orthologous bHLH genes in regulating ginsenoside biosynthesis.

Results

Identification of bHLH genes in six plant species

The identification of bHLH genes across six plant genomes revealed that 170 bHLH genes were present in D. carota, 193 in E. senticosus, 159 in P. notoginseng, 150 in P. vietnamensis, 266 in P. quinquefolius, and 316 in P. ginseng. The numbers of bHLH genes in P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng were significantly higher than those in the other four species, which may be attributed to the fact that both P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng are allotetraploid species that have undergone WGD events. The identified bHLH genes in each species were named as follows: Dc.bHLH1–170 for D. carota, Es.bHLH1–193 for E. senticosus, Pn.bHLH1–159 for P. notoginseng, Pv.bHLH1–150 for P. vietnamensis, Pq.bHLH1–256 for P. quinquefolius, and Pg.bHLH1–316 for P. ginseng (Table S1).

Phylogenetic analysis of bHLH genes

To investigate the evolutionary relationships among the bHLH genes from different species, we performed a phylogenetic analysis of the 1,254 identified bHLH genes. The resulting phylogenetic tree revealed that the bHLH genes clustered into 17 subfamilies based on their evolutionary relationships. Each subfamily contained bHLH genes from all six plant species, suggesting that the diversification of the bHLH gene family likely began before the divergence of these species (Fig. 1). Notably, in the species phylogenetic tree constructed based on the divergence times of the bHLH genes among species (Fig. 3), the terminal branches clustered P. quinquefolius and P. vietnamensis together. In other words, the bHLH genes of P. quinquefolius is more closely related to that of P. vietnamensis than to P. ginseng, although both P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng are allotetraploid species. This finding suggests that the diversification of the bHLH genes may have largely diverged prior to the WGD events.

Ka/Ks analysis of orthologous bHLH genes

To investigate the selective pressures acting on bHLH genes during evolution across six plant species, we calculated the ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitution rates (Ka/Ks) for orthologous bHLH gene pairs based on species divergence (Fig. 2). The results showed that the average Ka/Ks values of orthologous bHLH genes were 0.26 between D. carota and E. senticosus, 0.36 between E. senticosus and P. notoginseng, 0.45 between P. notoginseng and P. vietnamensis, 0.47 between P. vietnamensis and P. quinquefolius, and 0.58 between P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng. These results suggest that bHLH genes across species are subject to purifying selection. This further implies that most bHLH genes in these six species have been under strong evolutionary constraints to maintain their functional conservation.

Notably, one orthologous bHLH gene between E. senticosus and P. notoginseng, six between P. notoginseng and P. vietnamensis, nine between P. vietnamensis and P. quinquefolius, and twenty-one between P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng exhibited Ka/Ks ratios greater than 1.0. Moreover, the increasing number of orthologous bHLH genes with Ka/Ks ratios greater than 1.0 along the species divergence trajectory was consistent with the gradual rise in the average Ka/Ks values between species. This indicates that certain bHLH genes have undergone stronger positive selection during species divergence, potentially evolving novel functions to support species-specific adaptations to changing environments.

Collinearity analysis of 0rthologous bHLH genes

Through interspecies collinearity analysis, we identified a group of bHLH genes that have orthologs in all six plant species (Fig. 3). This gene group comprises 66 bHLH genes from D. carota, 51 from E. senticosus, 49 from P. notoginseng, 49 from P. vietnamensis, 81 from P. quinquefolius, and 74 from P. ginseng. These bHLH genes were grouped into 49 orthologous gene units, with detailed correspondences listed in Table S2. These findings indicate that significant expansions and contractions of the bHLH gene family occurred during the evolutionary processes of these six plant species. In addition, we found that not all of the 74 orthologous bHLH genes from P. ginseng exhibited intraspecies collinearity. Among them, 25 gene pairs showed intraspecies collinearity (Figure S1), while the remaining 24 bHLH genes existed as single-copy genes in the ginseng genome. This suggests that even among highly conserved orthologous bHLH genes, relatively independent evolutionary trajectories may have been maintained.

Structural analysis and functional annotation of the filtered orthologous bHLH genes

Comparative analysis of intron numbers in orthologous bHLH genes across the 49 orthologous gene units revealed that, in 18 of these units, intron numbers remained relatively conserved during evolution. In contrast, the remaining 31 orthologous gene units showed variation in intron numbers among different species. Interestingly, even among paralogous bHLH genes within the same species, intron numbers were not entirely identical, further supporting the notion that these bHLH genes have undergone independent evolutionary trajectories (Figure S2).

Functional annotation of the orthologous bHLH genes in P. ginseng revealed that only two collinear genes, Pg.bHLH14 and Pg.bHLH203, were annotated as members of the MYC subfamily (Table S3). In other words, they represent the only MYC subfamily members among the bHLH genes that have orthologs in all six species, existing as two copies in the ginseng genome as a result of WGD events. Moreover, their corresponding orthologous unit has never acquired introns during species divergence, highlighting their high degree of specificity. Given the high sequence similarity (98.86%) between the open reading frame (ORF) sequences of Pg.bHLH14 and Pg.bHLH203, and the fact that neither contains introns, we selected Pg.bHLH203 as the representative gene (target gene) for subsequent analyses.

Functional characterization of the target gene Pg.bHLH203

To determine the subcellular site where the Pg.bHLH203-encoded protein functions, we constructed a Pg.bHLH203-GFP fusion protein expressing recombinant plasmid and transiently transformed N. benthamiana leaves. The samples were then observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. 4). In the control group, GFP fluorescence was distributed throughout both the nucleus and cytoplasm, whereas the fluorescence signal of the Pg.bHLH203-GFP fusion protein was predominantly localized in the nucleus. This indicates that the Pg.bHLH203-encoded protein functions in the nucleus, consistent with the typical characteristics of TFs.

Given the high conservation of Pg.bHLH203 among all six plant species, we hypothesized that Pg.bHLH203 regulates ginsenoside biosynthesis at the initial steps of the biosynthetic pathway, rather than exerting a specific regulatory effect on the biosynthesis of a single ginsenoside monomer. To test this hypothesis, we examined whether Pg.bHLH203 could regulate the expression of Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS. Promoter structure analysis revealed that, in addition to typical promoter elements such as the TATA-box and CAAT-box, both Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS promoters contain MYC recognition site cis-acting elements, which may serve as potential binding sites for Pg.bHLH203 (Table S4). Dual-luciferase reporter assay results showed that, compared with the control, the LUC/REN ratios in the experimental group increased by 199.43% (Fig. 5A) and 120.05% (Fig. 5B), respectively, indicating that Pg.bHLH203 can effectively activate the transcriptional activity of the Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS promoters. Moreover, the activation effect on the Pg.DS promoter was more pronounced.

Functional validation of the target gene Pg.bHLH203

To further verify the function of Pg.bHLH203, we performed genetic transformation of P. ginseng adventitious roots following previously described methods. After cultivation, induction, and verification (Figure S3, S4), we obtained five positive individual P. ginseng hairy root lines that were capable of overexpressing Pg.bHLH203 while maintaining stable growth. Subsequently, individual P. ginseng hairy root line induced by Ag. rhizogenes strain C58C1 harboring an empty vector were used as the control. The expression levels of Pg.bHLH203, Pg.DS, and Pg.PPDS, as well as the contents of individual ginsenosides, were examined in cultures of each individual P. ginseng hairy root line.

The expression analysis revealed that, compared with the control group, all tested samples exhibited varying degrees of upregulation of Pg.bHLH203, Pg.DS, and Pg.PPDS. Specifically, Pg.bHLH203 expression increased by 35–427% (Fig. 6A), Pg.DS by 226–855% (Fig. 6B), and Pg.PPDS by 124–609% (Fig. 6C). Consistently, quantification of ginsenoside contents showed that all tested samples displayed increased levels of individual ginsenosides, with increases ranging from 23% to 753% (Fig. 6D). Notably, in sample S2, which exhibited the highest transcript levels of Pg.bHLH203, Pg.DS, and Pg.PPDS, the enhancement in ginsenoside accumulation was the most pronounced. In addition, the correlation analysis between the expression levels of Pg.bHLH203, Pg.DS, and Pg.PPDS and the total ginsenoside content in the tested samples is shown in Figure S6. At a significance level of p < 0.05, varying degrees of correlation were observed among these genes, as well as between genes and the total ginsenoside content. These results indicate that overexpression of Pg.bHLH203 can effectively enhance the expression of Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS, thereby promoting ginsenoside biosynthesis at the overall level.

Discussion

Comparative genomics has emerged as a powerful approach for exploring the evolutionary patterns of specific gene families across multiple species, offering advantages over traditional analytical methods in both depth and scope. By enabling cross-species comparisons, it facilitates the identification of conserved and divergent members, thereby providing insights into their functional diversification. In this study, we performed a comparative genomic analysis of the bHLH gene family in six plant species, elucidating their evolutionary relationships and identifying orthologous genes. Among them, we focused on a newly identified P.ginseng MYC gene, Pg.bHLH203, and preliminarily verified its role in regulating the expression of Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS, which in turn promotes ginsenoside biosynthesis. These findings not only expand our understanding of bHLH gene family evolution but also provide a valuable research paradigm for the functional exploration and sustainable utilization of genetic resources in medicinal plants such as P.ginseng.

The relationship between the evolution and functional diversification of bHLH genes

In this study, we found that the number of bHLH genes identified in the genomes of P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng was markedly higher than that in D. carota, E. senticosus, P. notoginseng, and P. vietnamensis. Such expansion in P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng is likely attributable to chromosomal polyploidy or WGD events, which are known to cause variation in genome size and gene content [27]. These genomic changes may have increased the biological complexity of P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng, thereby enhancing their environmental adaptability and contributing to the long-term preservation of their evolutionary lineages [28].

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that bHLH genes from the six plant species were clustered into 17 subfamilies. Genes within the same subfamily are likely to share conserved domains and may perform similar biological functions. Inter-species collinearity analysis identified 49 orthologous gene units, each containing bHLH genes with orthologs present in all six plant species. These orthologous bHLH genes likely originated from their respective common ancestors and have been retained through speciation. Notably, some orthologous gene units exhibited gene expansion during the course of species divergence. The two paralogous genes identified in this study, Pg.bHLH14 and Pg.bHLH203, share up to 98.86% sequence similarity, which may represent the outcome of such gene expansion events. Their high degree of sequence conservation also suggests functional redundancy. The additional chromosomal complements in P. quinquefolius and P. ginseng may alleviate functional constraints on certain genes, offering increased evolutionary flexibility that could promote the emergence of novel gene functions [29, 30].

It is worth noting, however, that these newly emerged genes may not necessarily share similar biological functions with their progenitors, as traditionally assumed. Instead, these orthologous genes may have undergone functional divergence and acquired species-specific roles [31]. Trans-regulatory changes, such as domain rearrangements, can facilitate the emergence of novel functional genes, whereas cis-regulatory changes, which affect gene expression, can fine-tune existing functions. Both mechanisms play crucial roles in shaping gene functional diversity. Therefore, further research is required to elucidate the evolutionary trajectories of the bHLH genes within these orthologous gene units.

The significance of introns in orthologous bHLH genes

Analyzing intron gain and loss during gene evolution can provide valuable insights into the origin of genes and their potential functions. Most previous studies have focused on the presence or absence of introns within genes, whereas few have examined the variation in intron number and distribution among orthologous genes. In this study, intron number analysis of bHLH genes within orthologous gene units revealed that most orthologous genes differed in their intron content. Based on intron number distribution, one orthologous gene unit contained intronless bHLH genes (no introns), 20 units contained intron-poor bHLH genes (three or fewer introns per gene), and 28 units contained intron-rich bHLH genes. Compared with intron-rich genes, intron-poor genes are thought to have emerged later and are subjected to stronger functional constraints, whereas intronless genes may have evolved under different selective pressures and are often associated with essential cellular functions [32–34]. We observed that, among the orthologous genen unit of bHLH genes, the number of newly gained introns generally exceeded that of ancestral intron losses, which may be attributed to the facilitation of intron insertions during gene duplication events [35]. Consequently, variations in intron number—whether between orthologs, paralogs, or even different copies of the same gene—can substantially influence the transcription process [36]. Previous studies have shown that ginseng, as an allotetraploid species, possesses two subgenomes and a large number of homologous genes, but exhibits preferential expression patterns within its genome [37]. Based on our findings, we speculate that such preferential expression of homologous genes may result from differences in intron number, which in turn affect the efficiency of gene expression [38].

Orthologous bHLH genes may possess comprehensive global regulatory functions

MYC TFs constitute a subfamily of bHLH TFs and play a pivotal role in regulating the biosynthesis of plant secondary metabolites, particularly terpenoids and phenylpropanoids [39]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the MYC TFs participates in the regulation of terpenoid biosynthesis in plants such as Arabidopsis [40], Solanum lycopersicum [41], and Chrysanthemum morifolium [42]. Plants of the Panax species generally exhibit broad pharmacological activities, with their secondary metabolites serving as the material basis for these effects [43]. These secondary metabolites are typically synthesized as protective compounds that enable plants to resist stresses such as insect herbivory [44] and microbial infection [45]. In this study, functional analysis of Pg.bHLH203—the only MYC gene among all orthologous gene units according to GO annotation—revealed that the protein encoded by Pg.bHLH203 can localize to the nucleus and positively regulate ginsenoside biosynthesis by activating the transcriptional activity of Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS promoters. This indicates that the function of Pg.bHLH203 is consistent with sutdies on MYC genes, acting as a key signaling-responsive factor that activates transcription of genes involved in related pathways [46]. Moreover, the observed general increase in the levels of individual ginsenosides across tested samples supports our hypothesis that, as an ancient and conserved MYC gene, Pg.bHLH203 can regulate ginsenoside biosynthesis at a global level.

Notably, promoter structure analysis revealed that both the Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS promoters contain two identical MYC recognition site cis-acting elements, while the Pg.PPDS promoter harbors an additional variant of the MYC recognition site compared with the Pg.DS promoter. Although this is consistent with the weaker correlationship between Pg.DS and Pg.bHLH203 expression levels showing in the correlation analysis between genes expression levels and total ginsenoside content, Pg.DS exhibited a more pronounced increase in expression across the tested samples. By sequence comparison, we found that the positions and orientations of the MYC recognition site cis-acting elements differ markedly between the Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS promoters. These differences may influence the binding affinity of Pg.bHLH203 to the two promoters and could help explain the potential mechanism underlying the promoter-specific transcriptional activation observed in this study. Therefore, integrating multi-sample transcriptomic data and other approaches to further investigate the regulatory characteristics of Pg.bHLH203 — particularly its potential role as a central gene in multiple signaling pathways and the functions of other orthologous bHLH genes — holds substantial scientific significance.

Conclusion

This study identified bHLH genes in D. carota (Dc.bHLH1–170), E. senticosus (Es.bHLH1–193), P. notoginseng (Pn.bHLH1–159), P. vietnamensis (Pv.bHLH1–150), P. quinquefolius (Pq.bHLH1–256), and P. ginseng (Pg.bHLH1–316), respectively. Comparative evolutionary analysis revealed that WGD events played a significant role in the expansion of the bHLH gene family. Furthermore, through orthologous gene screening, we identified a novel ginseng MYC gene, Pg.bHLH203, and confirmed its role in regulating ginsenoside biosynthesis by modulating the expression of Pg.DS and Pg.PPDS. In summary, this study provides a new research framework for gene family analysis and offers a novel evolutionary perspective for investigating the functions of previously uncharacterized genes.

Materials and methods

Strains, vectors, and plant materials

The chemically competent cells of Agrobacterium. rhizogenes C58C1, Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 and Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup) were purchased from Huayueyang Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. Plasmid pCAMBIA1300-35 S, pCAMBIA1302, pGreen II 62-SK and pGreen II-0800-Luc were purchased from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform, Wuhan, China. DNA fragment synthesis and recombinant plasmid construction were performed by Comate Bioscience Co., Ltd., Jilin, China. The adventitious roots of Panax ginseng and the plant of Nicotiana benthamiana used in this work were obtained from previous studies and were expanded and cultured in B5 medium and MS medium, respectively.

Acquisition of genomic data from multiple species and identification of bHLH genes

The assembly and annotation files of the genomics of P. ginseng, P. quinquefolius, P. notoginseng and D. carota were obtained from the National Genomics Data Center (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gwh/) under accession numbers GWHBEIL00000000.1, GWHBEIR00000000.1, GWHBHOE01000000 and GCA_001625215.1, respectively. The assembly and annotation files of the genomics of E. senticosus and P. vietnamensis were obtained from the China National GeneBank DataBase (https://db.cngb.org/) under project numbers CNP0001682 and CNP0002878, respectively.

Using the hidden Markov model (HMM) of bHLH TFs proteins obtained from Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org, PF00010) as queries, bHLH genes from the six plant species (Dc.bHLHs, Es.bHLHs, Pn.bHLHs, Pv.bHLHs, Pq.bHLHs, and Pg.bHLHs) were searched using HMMER (version 3.0) software, based on the protein amino acid sequences translated from the CDS sequences of their respective plant genomes. The retrieved results were then submitted to the iTAK online tool (http://itak.feilab.net/cgi-bin/itak/index.cgi) to eliminate false positives. Finally, the bHLH genes from each of the six plant species were individually numbered and named.

Phylogenetic analysis of bHLH genes

Multiple sequence alignment and orthology analysis of bHLH TFs protein amino acid sequences identified in six plant species were performed using OrthoFinder (version 2.5.4) with default parameters. The resulting alignments were trimmed and filtered using trimAl (version 2.0) to remove poorly aligned regions. Subsequently, RAxML (version 8.2.12) was used to construct the maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree with 1000 bootstrap replicates for the bHLH genes. Similarly, a species phylogenetic tree for the six plant species was also generated under the same parameters. The phylogenetic tree of bHLH genes was visualized using EvoView (https://www.evolgenius.info/). The Bayesian species divergence time tree was estimated using the MCMCTree tool from PAML (version 4.10.7), based on the species phylogeny and sequence alignments. A relaxed molecular clock model was applied to estimate divergence times of the bHLH genes among the six species.

Ka/Ks analysis of orthologous bHLH genes

Based on the homology identification of bHLH genes among six plant species, amino acid sequences encoded by orthologous bHLH genes were aligned using MAFFT (version 7.526) following the phylogenetic order of the species. The resulting alignments were converted into codon-based alignments using PAL2NAL (version 14). Subsequently, Ka and Ks values of the orthologous bHLH gene pairs were calculated using the KaKs Calculator. The results were visualized using the ggplot2 package in R (version 4.3.2).

Collinearity analysis of orthologous bHLH genes

Based on the genomic sequence data and annotation information of six plant species, interspecies gene collinearity analysis was conducted using the One Step MCScanX plugin in TBtools (version 2.119) with default parameters. The resulting outputs from different species comparisons were merged into a single file using the Text Merge for MCScanX plugin in TBtools. Combined with bHLH genes information from the six plant species, bHLH genes with orthologous genes present in all six genomes were filtered and visualized using the Multiple Synteny Plot plugin. Subsequently, a similar approach was applied to analyze the collinearity of these filtered orthologous bHLH genes within the genome of P. ginseng, and the results were visualized using the Advanced Circos plugin.

Structural analysis and functional annotation of the filtered orthologous bHLH genes

Based on the genome annotations of six plant species, the number of introns within the coding regions of the filtered orthologous bHLH genes was analyzed. Meanwhile, their orthologs in the P. ginseng genome were functionally annotated using Blast2GO software (version 6.0.3).

Transient transformation and subcellular localization analysis of the target gene

The stop-codon-free ORF of the target gene was synthesized and inserted into the pCAMBIA1302 vector between the Nco I and Spe I restriction sites, to generate a C-terminal fusion protein. The recombinant plasmid was introduced into chemically competent Ag. tumefaciens GV3101, and positive colonies were selected on YEP solid medium containing rifampicin, kanamycin, and gentamicin. The positive strains were cultured in YEP liquid medium containing the same antibiotics until the OD600 reached 0.6 and then infiltrated into the abaxial side of of 35-day-old N. benthamiana leaves. The samples infiltrated by the strain harboring the empty pCAMBIA1302 vector was used as the control. At 48 h post-infiltration, transiently transformed leaves were observed under a Nikon A1R HD25 high-resolution confocal laser scanning microscope.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Based on the ginseng genome sequence, the 2,000 bp DNA sequences upstream of the start codons (putative promoter regions) of two key genes involved in the initial steps of ginsenoside biosynthesis — dammarenediol synthase gene (Pg.DS) [47] and protopanaxadiol synthase gene (Pg.PPDS) [48] — were retrieved. The promoter sequences were analyzed using the Plant Cis-Acting Regulatory Element online tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), and after confirming the presence of typical promoter elements and cis-acting elements that could serve as potential binding sites, the promoter DNA fragments were individually synthesized. These promoter fragments were cloned into the pGreen II 0800-Luc vector between the EcoR V and BamH I restriction sites. The full length ORF of the target gene was synthesized and cloned into the plasmid pGreen II 62-SK between the Xba I and EcoR I restriction sites. The recombinant plasmids were separately transformed into Ag. tumefaciens GV3101 (pSoup) chemically competent cells, and positive colonies were screened as previously described. Strains carrying the target gene or the key genes` promoter were each cultured to an OD600 of 0.6, mixed at a 1:1 ratio, and the mixture was used to infiltrate N. benthamiana leaves as previously described. According to the instructions of the TransDetect® Dual-Luc Pro Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit from TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China, samples infiltrated by the mixture of strains carrying the empty pGreen II 62-SK plasmid and the key genes` promoter were used as controls. Luminescence was measured using the BioTek Synergy H1 multimode microplate reader.

Induction and cultivation of P. ginseng hairy roots overexpressing the target gene

Following previously published studies on functional validation of key genes involved in ginsenoside biosynthesis via gene overexpression [49, 50], the ORF of the target gene was synthesized and inserted into the pCAMBIA1300-35 S vector between the Sac I and Sal I restriction sites to construct a recombinant plasmid. The recombinant plasmid was then introduced into chemically competent Ag. rhizogenes C58C1. Positive transformants were selected on YEP solid medium containing rifampicin, kanamycin, and streptomycin. The positive strains were cultured in YEP liquid medium containing the same antibiotics until the OD600 reached 0.4. After centrifugation, the bacterial cells were resuspended in 1/2 MS liquid medium containing acetosyringone (AS), adjusting the OD600 to 0.5. Pre-cultured P. ginseng adventitious roots were then co-cultivated with the bacterial suspension. The infected roots were first placed on 1/2 MS solid medium containing 20 µM AS, and then transferred to 1/2 MS medium containing cefotaxime until hairy roots developed. Individual P. ginseng hairy root lines from the subsequent cultures were selected, and propagated in flasks using 1/2 MS liquid medium (22℃, 110 rpm). Part of the harvested material was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80℃ for genomic DNA extraction for PCR and total RNA extraction for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The remaining material was dried to constant weight and stored at 4℃ for ginsenoside extraction.

Validation of transgenic P. ginseng hairy roots overexpressing the target gene

Genomic DNA was extracted from each frozen individual P. ginseng hairy root line according to the instructions of the Plant Genomic DNA Kit form Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China. Using the extracted DNA as a template, PCR was performed with primers listed in Table S5, targeting the hygromycin resistance gene (HygR, 1026 bp) within the T-DNA region of the overexpression plasmid, a junction fragment including part of the inserted sequence and the NOS terminator (1,000 bp), and the rol C gene fragment (543 bp) located in the TL-DNA region of the Ri plasmid (pRiA4b) harbored by Ag. rhizogenes C58C1 to confirm the presence of transgenes and validate positive individual P. ginseng hairy root lines.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from each frozen positive individual P. ginseng hairy root line using the TRNzol Universal Reagent from Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China. Genomic DNA contamination was eliminated using the PrimeScript™ FAST RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser from Takara, Beijing, China, after which the RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA. Subsequently, qPCR was performed according to the instructions of the TB Green® Fast qPCR Mix from Takara, Beijing, China, using the synthesized cDNA as the template and primers listed in Table S5, on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System with Actin 1 [51] as the internal reference gene, to quantify the expression levels of target genes in the positive individual P. ginseng hairy root lines. Samples induced by Ag. rhizogenes C58C1 carrying the empty pCAMBIA1300-35 S vector were used as controls, and each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. The expression data of target genes were normalized against that of the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Extraction and detection of ginsenosides

Dried samples of each positive individual P. ginseng hairy root line were thoroughly ground into fine powder. A total of 1 g of powder was subjected to crude extraction of total ginsenosides by boiling. The filtered extract was then loaded onto a separation column packed with octadecylsilane-bonded silica (ODS). After the extract passed through the column, impurities were eluted with 5 column volumes of 40% methanol. The extracts were subsequently eluted using 5 column volumes of 100% methanol, the eluates were collected, evaporated to dryness, and the residues were dissolved in chromatographic-grade methanol. Each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates. The extracts were analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), with detailed parameters listed in Table S6.

Correlation analysis

The correlation coefficients between the expression levels of Pg.bHLH203, Pg.DS, Pg.PPDS and the total ginsenoside content in the tested samples were calculated using R (version 4.3.2). The resulting correlation network was subsequently visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).
