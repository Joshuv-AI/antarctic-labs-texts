Introduction

In the directed
st
-connectivity problem (
dstcon
), the input is a directed graph on
n
vertices with two distinguished vertices
s
and
t
, and the goal is to decide if there is a directed path from
s
to
t
. This fundamental problem underlies a wide range of applications in (e.g.) logistics, databases
[
AHV95
,
Ull89
]
, compilers
[
ALSU06
,
NNH99
]
, and model checking
[
CGP99
,
BK08
]
.

In addition to its practical applications,
dstcon
plays a central role in space-bounded complexity theory. It is
{\sf NL}
-complete (under
{\sf NC}^{1}
reductions), where
{\sf NL}
is the class of problems decidable by nondeterministic logspace machines. Consequently, understanding its complexity has broad implications for space-bounded computation. For example, a classical (or quantum) algorithm for
dstcon
using
O(\log(n))
space would show that
{\sf L}
, the class of problems solvable in
O(\log(n))
space (or its quantum analogue) contains
{\sf NL}
– a major breakthrough in either case.

Currently, the smallest space complexity of any classical or quantum algorithm for
dstcon
is
S=O(\log^{2}(n))
, achieved by Savitch’s (classical) algorithm. However, Savitch’s algorithm achieves this low space complexity at the expense of a large
quasipolynomial
time complexity, using

T\leq 2^{\log^{2}(n)+O(\log(n))}

steps of computation. In contrast, a simple breadth-first search (BFS) algorithm solves this problem in just
T=O(n^{3})
steps (in the adjacency-matrix model), but at the expense of a much larger
S={\widetilde{O}}(n)
space requirement. The fundamental nature of this problem, as well as its many applications, motivates understanding the best possible
tradeoff
between the time and space needed to solve it.
Progress was made by Barnes, Buss, Ruzzo and Schieber
[
BBRS98
]
, who gave a classical algorithm that runs in time

T\leq 2^{\log^{2}(\frac{n}{S})+O(\log n\log\log n)}

(1)

given any space
S\geq\log^{2}(n)
. Their approach combines a breadth-first search with a clever recursive algorithm.

For quantum algorithms, the study of space-bounded complexity is even more well motivated, as quantum memories are expected to be limited in size for the foreseeable future. It is an important question in which memory regimes we can still achieve speedups over classical algorithms, and time-space tradeoffs are a key part of this picture. We can make such a tradeoff even more useful by distinguishing between the
quantum
space and
classical
(or total) space needed by the algorithm, as quantum space is the scarce resource.

For
dstcon
, quantum speedups are known only at the two extreme regimes of space. In the high-space setting, Dürr, Heiligman, Høyer, and Mhalla
[
DHHM06
]
gave an
{\widetilde{O}}(n^{1.5})
-time,
{\widetilde{O}}(n)
-space algorithm using quantum search to build a spanning tree. In the low-space setting, Ref.
[
JP25
]
recently gave a quadratic quantum speedup over Savitch’s algorithm, running in time
T\leq 2^{\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}n+O(\log n)}
with
S=O(\log^{2}n)
space. Between these two extremes, however, no quantum improvements over classical tradeoffs were known.

For the
undirected
variant (
ustcon
), the picture is much clearer, at least as far as quantum algorithms go: in both the adjacency-matrix and edge-list models of graph access

However, existing classical and quantum approaches face significant obstacles in the directed case. A random walk on a directed graph, starting from

, may fail to find

is reachable from

Quantum walks, which are powerful tools for studying undirected graphs, do not generalize to directed graphs.
Moreover, known classical algorithms for

]

These difficulties highlight why directed connectivity remains a challenging and subtle problem. They also show that progress requires algorithmic ideas that go beyond random or quantum walks, and straightforward adaptations of classical tradeoffs.

Our Contribution:

We present the first nontrivial quantum time-space tradeoff for
dstcon
, by designing a new quantum algorithm for this problem
that, for any space bound
S\geq\log^{2}(n)
, runs in time

T\leq 2^{\frac{1}{2}\log(n)\log(\frac{n}{S})+O(\log n\log\log n)}.

In particular, our result yields a quantum speedup over the best known classical algorithm (see (
1

S=o(n^{1/2})
. We also show that, of the
S
space, the required
quantum
space is always
O(\log^{2}(n))
, which makes this result much more applicable to quantum computers with a limited number of qubits. This effectively means that we can tradeoff classical space for quantum time. We formally state this result in
Theorem
3.6

Our Techniques:

The classical algorithm of Barnes et al.
[
BBRS98
]
achieves a time-space tradeoff for
dstcon
by combining breadth-first search with a recursive subroutine that decides, for any pair of vertices
u
and
v
, if there is a directed path from
u
to
v
of length at most
L
. We call this problem
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
. The breadth-first search saves on space by not traversing the whole graph, but only those vertices at a distance from
s
that is a multiple of
L
(the space then decreases as
L
increases). The subroutine is used to find these vertices in a manner that is more space efficient, but less time efficient, than BFS.

The BFS portion of this classical algorithm could be sped up using quantum search techniques, but this saves at most a polynomial factor in the time (and nothing in the space), which is not very interesting in the small-space regime (where we get our quantum improvement) where such polynomial factors are hidden by the
O(\log(n))
in the exponent.
On the other hand, a direct quantization of their subroutine for
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
fails, in large part because this subroutine is not reversible, and making it so would increase the space complexity. Moreover, the subroutine calls itself recursively, with significant depth of recursion. A quantum speedup of this subroutine would most likely have bounded error. Naively composing bounded-error quantum subroutines to depth
d
results in
\log^{d}
factors, which can be significant. Recent techniques for composing bounded error quantum algorithms without log-factor overhead
[
BJY24
,
BJ25
]
could reduce this overhead to
c^{d}
for some constant, but this could still be significant, if
c>1
. Overcoming these limitation requires a fundamentally different approach.

To address this, we design a new quantum algorithm for
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
that is based on a recursively constructed

. A switching network (

Switching networks are a natural and well-motivated model in classical computing, and have been used to study classical space-bounded complexity (see, e.g.,
[

]
).
They also give a simple way of designing quantum algorithms, as any switching network can be compiled into a quantum algorithm for the associated function, whose time and space complexity depend on certain properties of the switching network.
Quantum algorithms for evaluating switching networks were developed in
[

]
using span program techniques inspired by
[

Switching networks lend themselves well to quantum algorithms in part because they are a naturally reversible structure, being defined by undirected graphs. Moreover, they give a natural way of defining recursive quantum algorithms: by defining a switching network recursively, and turning the final product into a quantum algorithm, bounded error is introduced only once, when turning the switching network into a quantum algorithm, and there are no factors of
c^{d}
or
\log^{d}
on the complexity of the algorithm, even when the depth of recursion in the switching network is
d
.

Because we keep the more space-intensive outer BFS algorithm classical, most of the space needed by the algorithm is classical. For any
S

O(\log^{2}(n))
qubits of
quantum
space to implement our quantum subroutine for
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
, the remainder of the
S
space is classical.

Our algorithm for
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
provides a compelling example of how switching networks can be used within quantum algorithms. It exploits the full power of the classical switching network model, while at the same time extending it into a setting where time-space tradeoffs become essential. It is the first time switching networks have been used to prove a quantum time-space tradeoff.
Beyond its role in our algorithm for
dstcon
, our algorithm for
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
may be of independent interest as a quantum primitive for other space-efficient graph algorithms.

Open Problems:

Our speedup over the best known classical algorithm is quadratic when
S=\log^{2}(n)
, and gets worse as
S
increases towards
\sqrt{n}
, at which point we no longer achieve any speedup. However, we do know there is a quadratic speedup at the other extreme,
S=n
. It is thus very natural to hope that we might get a quadratic speedup over the algorithm of
[
BBRS98
]
for
all

S
. One difficulty in this is that our algorithm is not just a quantization of the algorithm of
[
BBRS98
]
, but actually does something different.

As mentioned earlier, for the undirected variant (
ustcon
), quantum algorithms achieve optimal time and space simultaneously
[
BR12
,
AJPW23
]
. This naturally raises the question of whether a similar result is possible in the directed case.

We have already mentioned the compelling open problem of showing a
O(\log(n))
-space quantum algorithm for
dstcon
. This would necessarily be a polynomial-time algorithm, and so it would, in some rough sense, achieve the above goal of have optimal space and time simultaneously. It would also show that the quantum analogue of
{\sf L}
(logspace) is contained in
{\sf NL}
(nondeterministic logspace). Ref.
[
AE25
]
made some progress on this question by showing that a promise version of
dstcon
in which the input has few paths can be solved in
O(\log(n))
quantum space. We remark that any
o(\log^{2}(n))
upper bound on the quantum space complexity of
dstcon
would be interesting.

2.1
Generating superpositions

As part of our algorithms, we will often require a subroutine that makes a superposition over strings, with non-uniform amplitudes. The following lemma gives conditions under which we can do that efficiently.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
GR02
]
)
.

Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{\{0,1\}^{m}}
, and suppose there is a subroutine that can compute, given any prefix
p\in\{0,1\}^{\leq m}
, the partial sum of entries of
\alpha
with prefix
p
,
S(p)=\sum_{s\in\{0,1\}^{m}:s\text{ has prefix }p}\alpha_{s}^{2}
, in time
T
. Then the state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}^{2}}}\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}{\lvert}s\rangle
can be prepared in time
O(Tm)
.

Remark 2.2

(Larger alphabets)
.

The result of
Lemma
2.1
can be easily generalized to the case when indices are length-
m
strings over an alphabet

d=\left|A\right|
(e.g.,
A=\{0,1,2\}

In this case,
p

A

[
GR02
]

Since

is constant, the total complexity remains

2.4
Flows, Circulations, and the Cut Space

In this section, we will study the structure of

, of undirected graphs, with respect to the boundary
\{{\sf s},{\sf t}\}

{\cal B}^{-}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}\psi_{\star}^{-}({\sf u})\rangle:{\sf u}\in V({\cal N})\setminus\{{\sf s},{\sf t}\}\}\cup\{{\lvert}\leftarrow,{\sf s}\rangle+{\lvert}\psi_{\star}^{-}({\sf s})\rangle,{\lvert}\rightarrow,{\sf t}\rangle+{\lvert}\psi_{\star}^{-}({\sf t})\rangle\}.

This is called a cut space, because it is intuitively the span of

We first show the following simple fact.

Lemma 2.11
.

\dim{\cal B}^{-}=|V({\cal N})|
.

Proof.

We show this by proving that the states in the definition of

are independent. Let

{\lvert}\psi_{\sf u}\rangle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\lvert}\psi_{\star}^{-}({\sf u})\rangle&\mbox{if }{\sf u}\in V\setminus\{{\sf s},{\sf t}\}\\
{\lvert}\leftarrow,{\sf s}\rangle+{\lvert}\psi_{\star}^{-}({\sf s})\rangle&\mbox{if }{\sf u}={\sf s}\\
{\lvert}\rightarrow,{\sf t}\rangle+{\lvert}\psi_{\star}^{-}({\sf t})\rangle&\mbox{if }{\sf u}={\sf t}\\
\end{array}\right.

and for simplicity, write
{\lvert}e\rangle={\lvert}\rightarrow,e\rangle-{\lvert}\leftarrow,e\rangle
.
Suppose towards a contradiction that for some
{\sf u}\in V

{\lvert}\psi_{{\sf u}}\rangle=\sum_{{\sf v}\in V\setminus\{{\sf u}\}}\alpha_{{\sf v}}{\lvert}\psi_{{\sf v}}\rangle.

We will show by induction on distance

from
{\sf u}
, that for all
{\sf u}^{\prime}
holds
\left|\alpha_{{\sf u}^{\prime}}\right|=1
. For the base case, if
{\sf u}^{\prime}
and
{\sf u}
share an edge
e
, then

1=\left|{{\langle}e|}\psi_{\sf u}\rangle\right|=\left|\sum_{{\sf v}\in V\setminus\{{\sf u}\}}\alpha_{{\sf v}}{{\langle}e|}\psi_{\sf v}\rangle\right|=\left|\alpha_{{\sf u}^{\prime}}\right|,

since
\left|{{\langle}e|}\psi_{\sf v}\rangle\right|
is 1 if

is incident to
{\sf v}
and 0 otherwise.

For the induction step, if
{\sf u}^{\prime}
is at distance
d
from
{\sf u}
, it has a neighbour
{\sf u}^{\prime\prime}
at distance

from
{\sf u}
, to which the induction hypothesis applies, so letting

be the edge incident to
{\sf u}^{\prime}
and

, we have:

0=\left|\sum_{{\sf v}\in V\setminus\{{\sf u}\}}\alpha_{\sf v}{{\langle}e|}\psi_{\sf v}\rangle\right|=\left|\alpha_{{\sf u}^{\prime}}+\alpha_{{\sf u}^{\prime\prime}}\right|

so
\left|\alpha_{{\sf u}^{\prime}}\right|=1
, since
\left|\alpha_{{\sf u}^{\prime\prime}}\right|=1
by the induction hypothesis.

The contradiction arises because there is at least one boundary vertex that is not
{\sf u}
– without loss of generality, suppose

. Then since
{\lvert}\leftarrow,{\sf s}\rangle
only appears in
{\lvert}\psi_{\sf s}\rangle

0=\left|{{\langle}\leftarrow,{\sf s}|}\psi_{\sf u}\rangle\right|=\left|\sum_{{\sf v}\in V\setminus\{{\sf u}\}}\alpha_{{\sf v}}{{\langle}\leftarrow,{\sf s}|}\psi_{{\sf v}}\rangle\right|=\left|\alpha_{{\sf s}}\right|=1.

This is clearly a contradiction.
∎

Definition 2.12

We can naturally view any function

\displaystyle=-c^{\prime}n\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{(x^{\prime}+x)\cdot j}=0,

and otherwise, we must have

, so

\displaystyle{{\langle}\psi_{z^{\prime},x^{\prime}}|}\psi_{z,x}\rangle

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}n(n-1)\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x^{\prime}\cdot j}=0,

completing the proof.∎

We now describe the form of optimal flows in

-flow simply assigns a unit of flow to the single edge between

Lemma 4.15
.

For

{\lvert}\theta_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=-{\lvert}\leftarrow,[u]\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}\rangle+{\lvert}\rightarrow,[u,v_{j}]\rangle.

Proof.

Note that each

-flow in

By

has the following orthogonal basis:

\{{\lvert}\sigma\rangle{\lvert}b_{d}\rangle:\sigma\in\Sigma,d\in[D]\}\cup\left\{{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}\rangle:z,x\in\{0,1\}^{\log n},z\neq\bar{0}\right\},

where

,

4.3.2
Basis Generation

We show in this section how to prepare the basis from

in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time. All states considered in this section lie in the subspace in which each edge

is represented by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left({\lvert}\rightarrow,e\rangle-{\lvert}\leftarrow,e\rangle\right)
. Without loss of generality, we may therefore describe states in terms of the
canonical edge states
\{{\lvert}e\rangle={\lvert}\sigma,i\rangle:e\in E({\cal N}_{2^{\ell}})=\Sigma^{\ell}\times\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}
, and obtain the isomorphism with the above subspace by appending the auxiliary state
{\lvert}-\rangle\;=\;\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left({\lvert}\rightarrow\rangle-{\lvert}\leftarrow\rangle\right)
in an additional register.

Recall from
(
8
)
that edges
E_{2^{\ell}}
of
{\cal N}_{2^{\ell}}
are labeled
(\sigma,e_{i})=(\sigma,i)
for
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\ell}
and
i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
. For any
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\ell}
, letting
\bar{\sigma}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}
be such that for all
t\in[\ell]
,
\sigma_{t}=(\bar{\sigma}_{t},i)
for some
i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
(that is,
\bar{\sigma}
encodes the first part of each entry of
\sigma
), we can break
E_{2^{\ell}}
into
3^{\ell}

layers
, defined, for each
\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}
:

E_{\tau}:=\{(\sigma,i)\in E_{2^{\ell}}:\bar{\sigma}=\tau\}=\{(\sigma,i)\in\Sigma^{\ell}\times\{0,1\}^{\log n}:\bar{\sigma}=\tau\}.

(12)

A crucial step in our basis generation subroutine will be taking uniform superpositions over these layers. We first prove two lemmas about the size of these layers that will enable this.

Lemma 4.16
.

Fix
\ell\in[\log(L)]
.
Let
\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}
encode a layer
E_{\tau}
of edges in
{\cal N}_{2^{\ell}}
. Then
\left|E_{\tau}\right|=n^{1+\left|\tau\right|-\left|\tau\right|_{0}}
, where
\left|\tau\right|_{0}
denotes the number of zeros in
\tau
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction on
\ell
. For the base case, let
\ell=1
, so we have for any
\tau\in\{0,1,2\}
:

|E_{\tau}|=|\{(\sigma,i)\in\Sigma\times\{0,1\}^{\log n}:\bar{\sigma}=\tau\}|=n|\{\sigma\in\Sigma:\bar{\sigma}=\tau\}|.

If
\tau=0
, the only
\sigma\in\Sigma=\{(0,\bar{0}),(1,i),(2,j):i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}
such that
\bar{\sigma}=\tau
is
\sigma=(0,\bar{0})
, and so

|E_{\tau}|=n\cdot 1=n^{1+|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}

since
|\tau|=|\tau|_{0}=1
. Otherwise,
\tau\in\{1,2\}
, and we have:

|E_{\tau}|=n|\{(\tau,i):i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}|=n^{2}=n^{1+|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}

since
|\tau|=1
and
|\tau|_{0}=0
.

For the induction step, assume
\ell>1
.
Referring to
(
12
)
, we have, for any
\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}
:

\begin{split}E_{0\tau}&=\{((0,\bar{0})\sigma,i)\in E_{2^{\ell}}:\bar{\sigma}=\tau\}=\{((0,\bar{0}),e):e\in E_{\tau}\}\\
\mbox{and for $a\in\{1,2\}$, }E_{a\tau}&=\{((a,j)\sigma,i)\in E_{2^{\ell}}:\bar{\sigma}=\tau\}=\{((a,j),e):e\in E_{\tau},j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}.\end{split}

(13)

From this, and the induction hypothesis, we have:

\begin{split}|E_{0\tau}|&=|E_{\tau}|=n^{1+|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}=n^{1+|\tau|+1-(|\tau|_{0}+1)}=n^{1+|0\tau|-|0\tau|_{0}}\\
\mbox{and for $a\in\{1,2\}$, }|E_{a\tau}|&=n|E_{\tau}|=n\cdot n^{1+|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}=n^{1+(1+|\tau|)-|\tau|_{0}}=n^{1+|a\tau|-|a\tau|_{0}}.\end{split}

Thus, for any
\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}
, we can conclude
|E_{\tau^{\prime}}|=n^{1+|\tau^{\prime}|-|\tau^{\prime}|_{0}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

For all
\ell\in[\log(L)]
,
\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{|E_{\tau}|}=\frac{(n+2)^{\ell}}{n^{\ell+1}}=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\ell}
.

Proof.

We first use
Lemma
4.16
to compute:

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{|E_{\tau}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{n^{1+|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}}=\sum_{t=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{t}2^{\ell-t}n^{t-1-\ell}.

Above, we used the fact that for any
t\in\{0,\dots,\ell\}
, there are
\binom{\ell}{t}2^{\ell-t}
strings in
\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}
with exactly
t
0s. Continuing, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{|E_{\tau}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\ell+1}}\sum_{t=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{t}2^{\ell-t}n^{t}=\frac{1}{n^{\ell+1}}(n+2)^{\ell},

by the binomial theorem. The result follows.
∎

Next, we describe a subroutine for generating superpositions over all optimal flows, which will be a key subroutine in our basis generation.

Lemma 4.18
.

For all
\ell\in[\log(L)]
, a map that acts as

{\lvert}0\rangle\mapsto\propto\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell})\rangle

can be implemented in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
steps.

Proof.

We first show by induction that

\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=n\cdot\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle.

(14)

Using the definition in the statement of
Theorem
4.12
, we can write

\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}^{n}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2^{\ell})\rangle.

In the base case of
{\cal N}_{2}
(
\ell=1
), referring to the definition of
{\lvert}p_{ij}\rangle
in
Theorem
4.12
, we have

\displaystyle{\lvert}p_{ij}(2)\rangle

\displaystyle={\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}^{0}(1)\rangle+{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}^{1i}(1)\rangle-{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}^{2j}(1)\rangle={\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}\rangle+{\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}\rangle+{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle={\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}e_{i}\rangle+{\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}e_{j}\rangle+{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}e_{i}\rangle

by
(
10
)
, and the fact that an optimal
[u],[u,v_{i}]
-flow in
{\cal N}_{1}
simply assigns a unit of flow to
e_{i}
.

{\lvert}p_{ij}(2)\rangle={\lvert}e_{i}^{0}\rangle+{\lvert}e_{j}^{1i}\rangle+{\lvert}e_{i}^{2j}\rangle.

We thus get

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2)\rangle

\displaystyle=n\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}0,\bar{0},e_{i}\rangle+n\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}1,i,e_{j}\rangle+n\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}2,j,e_{i}\rangle.

Since the layer encoded by
0
consists of
n
edges
E_{0}=\{((0,\bar{0}),e_{i}):i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}
, and the layers encoded by
1
and
2

n^{2}
edges
E_{1}=\{{((1,i),e_{j})}:i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}
and
E_{2}=\{{((2,j),e_{i})}:i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}\}
respectively, we conclude that the equality holds in the base case.

Assume, for the induction step, that the equality from
(
14
)
holds for
{\cal N}_{2^{\ell-1}}
. We have

\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\!\!\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2^{\ell})\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\!\!\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\left({\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{0}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{1i}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle-{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{2j}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right)\\
=\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle,

where we again used
(
10
)
.
We can use the induction hypothesis on each of the three terms to get:

\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=n{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle+\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}1,i\rangle\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle\\
+\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}2,j\rangle\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle.

From
(
13
)
, and
Lemma
4.16
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{n^{|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}(0,\bar{0}),e\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\sum_{a\in\{1,2\}}\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{n^{1+|\tau|-|\tau|_{0}}}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n},e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}(a,i),e\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{n^{|0\tau|-|0\tau|_{0}}}\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E_{0\tau}}{\lvert}e^{\prime}\rangle+\sum_{a\in\{1,2\}}\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{n^{|a\tau|-|a\tau|_{0}}}\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E_{a\tau}}{\lvert}e^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=n\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{|E_{\tau^{\prime}}|}\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E_{\tau^{\prime}}}{\lvert}e^{\prime}\rangle,

as desired.
Next, we show how to prepare this state in two steps.

Step 1.

Prepare a superposition of layer names with the correct amplitudes:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau^{\prime}}\right|}}}\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}}{\lvert}\tau\rangle.

By
Remark

, this superposition can be prepared in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time if the amplitudes

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau^{\prime}}\right|}}}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}}

and partial sums

S(p)=\sum_{\tau:\tau\text{ has prefix }p}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}

can be computed in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time.
We find closed-form expressions for these quantities, which shows that they can indeed be evaluated efficiently. We start by applying
Lemma
4.16
and
Lemma
4.17
to compute the amplitudes:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau^{\prime}}\right|}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}}=\left(\frac{(n+2)^{\ell}}{n^{\ell+1}}\right)^{-1/2}\left(n^{1+\ell-\left|\tau\right|_{0}}\right)^{-1/2}=\sqrt{\frac{n^{|\tau|_{0}}}{(n+2)^{\ell}}}.

Finally, we compute the partial sums, again using
Lemma
4.16
and
Lemma
4.17
. Let
p\in\{0,1,2\}^{k}
for
k\in[\ell]
be a fixed prefix. Then

\displaystyle S(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau:\tau\text{ has prefix }p}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-k}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{p\tau^{\prime}}\right|}=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-k}}\frac{1}{n^{1+\ell-(\left|p\right|_{0}+\left|\tau^{\prime}\right|_{0})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k-\left|p\right|_{0}}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-k}}\frac{1}{n^{1+(\ell-k)-\left|\tau^{\prime}\right|_{0}}}=\frac{1}{n^{k-\left|p\right|_{0}}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell-k}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau^{\prime}}\right|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k-\left|p\right|_{0}+1}}\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\ell-k}=\frac{(n+2)^{\ell-k}}{n^{\ell+1-|p|_{0}}}.

Step 2.

Map each
{\lvert}\tau\rangle
to the uniform superposition over the edges in the layer
E_{\tau}
.
Since these edges are encoded as
{\lvert}\sigma,i\rangle
with
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\ell}
,
i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
, and
\bar{\sigma}=\tau
, this mapping can be performed in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time, as follows. For each
k\in[\ell]
, if
\tau_{k}\in\{1,2\}
, generate a uniform superposition over
j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
, to get a superposition over
\sigma_{k}
such that
\bar{\sigma}_{k}=\tau_{k}
. Finally, generate a uniform superposition over
i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
. Letting
S(\tau_{k})=\{\bar{0}\}
if
\tau_{k}=0
and
\{0,1\}^{\log n}
otherwise, this mapping acts as:

\displaystyle{\lvert}\tau\rangle={\lvert}\tau_{1}\rangle\otimes\dots\otimes{\lvert}\tau_{\ell}\rangle

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\bigotimes_{k=1}^{\ell}{\lvert}\tau_{k}\rangle\sum_{j\in S(\tau_{k})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|S(\tau_{k})|}}{\lvert}j\rangle\right)\otimes\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\lvert}i\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^{1+\ell-|\tau|_{0}}}}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|E_{\tau}|}}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle.

We thus end up with a state proportional to

\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}}{\lvert}e\rangle=\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{\left|E_{\tau}\right|}\sum_{e\in E_{\tau}}{\lvert}e\rangle,

(15)

which is proportional to
\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle

by
(
14
)
.
∎

Building on the previous lemma, we describe how to generate states that will shortly be shown to make up part of the states
{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}(2^{\ell})\rangle
from the basis in

4.12
.

Lemma 4.19
.

For all
\ell>0
, there is a circuit
C_{2^{\ell}}
that acts, for all

, as

{\lvert}x\rangle\mapsto\propto\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle

and uses
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
gates.

Proof.

We start by noticing that if
x=\bar{0}
then the desired state is
\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle
. By
Lemma
4.18
, a state proportional to this one can be prepared in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
. Hence, we assume that
x\neq\bar{0}
for the rest of the proof. We prove the statement by induction. Consider
{\cal N}_{2}
for the base case. For a fixed
x\neq\bar{0}
, we can write the following.

\begin{split}\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2)\rangle&=\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\left({\lvert}e^{0}_{i}\rangle+{\lvert}e^{1i}_{j}\rangle-{\lvert}e^{2j}_{i}\rangle\right)\\
&=\underbrace{\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}}_{=0}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!{\lvert}e^{0}_{i}\rangle+\!\!\!\!\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}e^{1i}_{j}\rangle-\!\!\!\!\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}e^{2j}_{i}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}j\rangle+\sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\!\!\!\!(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}i\rangle\\
&\propto{\lvert}1\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}x\rangle+{\lvert}2\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}x\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle.\end{split}

(16)

We used the fact that edges are encoded as
{\lvert}e^{1i}_{j}\rangle={\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}j\rangle
and
{\lvert}e^{2j}_{i}\rangle=-{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}i\rangle
(see
(
9
)
).
Thus, to prepare this state from
{\lvert}x\rangle
, first make a uniform superposition over
{\lvert}1\rangle
and
{\lvert}2\rangle
, and then swap the second and third register controlled on the first register:

{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\lvert}1,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\lvert}2,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\lvert}1\rangle{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\lvert}2\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle.

We can complete the computation by applying Hadamards to all but the first register. This can be done in
O(\log n)
gates.

Assume, for the induction step, that there is a circuit
C_{2^{\ell-1}}
with
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
gates that implements

{\lvert}x\rangle\mapsto\frac{\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle}{\left\|\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|}

for any
x
.
We rewrite the desired state analogously to the base case (all sums below are over
\{0,1\}^{\log n}
).

\begin{split}\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle&=\sum_{j}(-1)^{x\cdot j}\sum_{i}\left({\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{0}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{1i}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle-{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{2j}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right)\\
&=\underbrace{\sum_{j}(-1)^{x\cdot j}}_{=0}\sum_{i}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{0}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\sum_{j}\sum_{i}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{1i}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle-\sum_{j}\sum_{i}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{2j}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\\
&=\sum_{j}\sum_{i}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\sum_{j}\sum_{i}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle.\end{split}

We used
{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}^{1i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle={\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle
and
{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}^{2j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle={\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle
, by
(
9
)
. Continuing, we use the induction hypothesis to compute (again, all sums are over
\{0,1\}^{\log n}
):

\begin{split}\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}^{n}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle&=\sum_{i}{\lvert}1,i\rangle\otimes\sum_{j}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\sum_{j}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}2,j\rangle\otimes\sum_{i}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\\
&={\lvert}1\rangle\otimes\sqrt{n}H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle\otimes\left\|\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}x\rangle\\
&\qquad\qquad+{\lvert}2\rangle\otimes\sqrt{n}H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}x\rangle\otimes\left\|\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle.\end{split}

(17)

Thus, as in the base case, we first put appropriate weight on
{\lvert}1\rangle
and
{\lvert}2\rangle
, using a simple rotation:

{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle\mapsto\left(\sqrt{\alpha}{\lvert}1\rangle+\sqrt{1-\alpha}{\lvert}2\rangle\right){\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle,

where

\alpha=\frac{\left\|\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|^{2}}{\left\|\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|^{2}}.

We will describe how to do this first rotation shortly.
As in the base step, we next swap the second and third registered controlled on the first, and then complete the computation by applying
H^{\otimes\log n}
to the second register and
C_{2^{\ell-1}}
to the third register.

We complete the proof by describing how to do the first rotation in terms of
\alpha
. By
Lemma
2.1
, such a superposition can be prepared in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time if
\alpha
can be computed in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time. Hence, it suffices to compute closed-form expressions for the two norms in the definition of
\alpha
. From
(
14
)
and
Lemma
4.17
, we can conclude that for any
\ell>0
:

N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell})\coloneq\left\|\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell})\rangle\right\|^{2}=n^{2}\sum_{\tau\in\{0,1,2\}^{\ell}}\frac{1}{|E_{\tau}|}=n^{2}\frac{(n+2)^{\ell}}{n^{\ell+1}}.

(18)

Letting
N_{x}(2^{\ell})\coloneq\left\|\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle\right\|^{2}
, we show by induction that for
\ell\geq 1
:

N_{x}(2^{\ell})=n^{\ell+1}+\frac{n^{3}}{(n-2)(n+1)}\left(n^{\ell}-\frac{(n+2)^{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\right).

(19)

For the base step, we can observe from
(
16
)
that
N_{x}(2)=2n^{2}
, which is equal to the right-hand-side of
(
19
)
when
\ell=1
. For the induction step, we can use
(
17
)
to show that for
\ell>1
:

\begin{split}N_{x}(2^{\ell})&=\left\|\sqrt{n}H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{x}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}x\rangle\right\|^{2}+\left\|\sqrt{n}H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}x\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle\right\|^{2}\\
&=nN_{x}(2^{\ell-1})+nN_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})\\
&=n\left(n^{\ell}+\frac{n^{3}}{(n-2)(n+1)}\left(n^{\ell-1}-\frac{(n+2)^{\ell-1}}{n^{\ell-1}}\right)\right)+n^{2}\frac{(n+2)^{\ell-1}}{n^{\ell-1}},\end{split}

by induction. This is easily shown to be equal to the right-hand-side of
(
19
)
.
∎

We now describe how to generate the states
{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}(2^{\ell})\rangle
, which form most of the basis described in
Theorem
4.12
.

Lemma 4.20
.

For all
\ell\in[\log(L)]
, a map that acts, for all
x,z\in[n]
such that
z\neq\bar{0}
, as

{\lvert}x,z\rangle\mapsto\propto{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}(2^{\ell})\rangle

can be implemented in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
steps.

Proof.

For a fixed
z,x\in[n],z\neq\bar{0}
, we write down the state
{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}\rangle
by definition its definition (see
Theorem
4.12
) and split it into three orthogonal terms. (All sums below are over
\{0,1\}^{\log n}
).

\displaystyle{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}(2^{\ell})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{z\cdot i}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2^{\ell})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{z\cdot i}\left({\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{0}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{1i}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle-{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{2j}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(-1)^{x\cdot j}\sum_{i}(-1)^{z\cdot i}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{0}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\sum_{i,j}(-1)^{z\cdot i+x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{1i}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle-\sum_{i,j}(-1)^{z\cdot i+x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}^{2j}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle.

By
(
10
)
, we can write
{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}^{0}\rangle={\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}\rangle
,
{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}^{1i}\rangle={\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}\rangle
and
{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}^{2j}\rangle=-{\lvert}2,j\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}\rangle
.
If
x\neq\bar{0}
, the first sum vanishes, otherwise it adds up to
n
, giving:

\displaystyle{\lvert}\psi_{z,x}(2^{\ell})\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{x,\bar{0}}n\cdot{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{z\cdot i}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{z\cdot i}{\lvert}1,i\rangle\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{x\cdot j}{\lvert}2,j\rangle\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}(-1)^{z\cdot i}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{x,\bar{0}}n{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{z}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}z\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+{\lvert}1\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}z\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{x}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}x\rangle+{\lvert}2\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}x\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{z}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}z\rangle

where
C_{2^{\ell-1}}
is the circuit from
Lemma
4.19
, and
N_{z}(2^{\ell-1})
is as in
(
19
)
. Note by
(
19
)
that, for non-zero
z
, this is independent of
z
, so in that case, we simply write
N(2^{\ell-1})
.

To prepare the superposition of two or three terms, we begin by creating a superposition of
{\lvert}0\rangle
,
{\lvert}1\rangle
and
{\lvert}2\rangle
with appropriate amplitudes:

{\lvert}0\rangle{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle{\lvert}z\rangle\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{n\sqrt{N(2^{\ell-1})}{\lvert}0\rangle+\sqrt{N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}{\lvert}1\rangle+\sqrt{N(2^{\ell-1})}{\lvert}2\rangle}{\sqrt{(n^{2}+1)N(2^{\ell-1})+N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle{\lvert}z\rangle&\mbox{if }x=\bar{0}\\
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\lvert}1\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{\lvert}2\rangle\right){\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle{\lvert}x\rangle{\lvert}z\rangle&\mbox{else.}\end{array}\right.

We have used the fact that when
x\neq\bar{0}
,
N_{x}(2^{\ell-1})=N_{z}(2^{\ell-1})
, so the amplitudes on
{\lvert}1\rangle
and
{\lvert}2\rangle
are equal. Using the closed-form expressions in
(
18
)
and
(
19
)
, we can compute these amplitudes in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
steps, so we can implement this rotation in this number of steps as well.

Next, conditioned on
{\lvert}1\rangle
in the first register, we swap the second and third registers. Then we apply a Hadamard to the second register conditioned on
{\lvert}1\rangle
or
{\lvert}2\rangle
in the first register, and apply
C_{2^{\ell-1}}
to the last register. ∎

Finally, if we take
\ell=\log L
, and
v_{j}=t
, in the following lemma, this gives a procedure for preparing a state proportional to the optimal unit flow
{\lvert}\theta_{j}(L)\rangle
, which is the final state needed for the basis in
Theorem
4.12
.

Lemma 4.21
.

For all
\ell\in[\log(L)]
, a map that acts, for all
j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
, as

{\lvert}j\rangle\mapsto\propto{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2^{\ell})\rangle

can be implemented in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
steps.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction. Let
\ell=1
for the base case.

\begin{split}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2)\rangle&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}\left({\lvert}e^{0}_{i}\rangle+{\lvert}e^{1i}_{j}\rangle-{\lvert}e^{2j}_{i}\rangle\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}(0,\bar{0}),i\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}(1,i),j\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}(2,j),i\rangle.\end{split}

(20)

Clearly, a state proportional to this can be prepared in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time.

For the induction step, assume there is an efficient circuit
C_{2^{\ell-1}}^{\prime}
that implements the map

{\lvert}j\rangle\mapsto\frac{{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle}{\left\|{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|}

for any
j\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}
.
From
Lemma
4.15
(see also
(
3
)
) and the definition of
{\lvert}p_{ij}\rangle
in
Theorem
4.12
, we have:

\begin{split}&{n}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2^{\ell})\rangle\\
={}&{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}1,i\rangle{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle+{\lvert}2,j\rangle\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{i}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\\
={}&{\lvert}0,\bar{0}\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle+\sqrt{n}{\lvert}1\rangle H^{\otimes\log n}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle\otimes\left\|{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|C_{2^{\ell-1}}^{\prime}{\lvert}j\rangle+{\lvert}2,j\rangle\otimes\sqrt{N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}C_{2^{\ell-1}}{\lvert}\bar{0}\rangle\end{split}

(21)

where
C_{2^{\ell-1}}
is the circuit from
Lemma
4.19
, and
N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})
is as in
(
18
)
.

To prepare a state proportional to this, we first generate the superposition

\displaystyle{\lvert}j\rangle\mapsto

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}{\lvert}0\rangle+\sqrt{n}\cdot\left\|{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|{\lvert}1\rangle+\sqrt{N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}{\lvert}2\rangle}{\sqrt{n\cdot\left\|{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|^{2}+2\cdot N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})}}{\lvert}j\rangle,

before mapping
{\lvert}j\rangle
to the correct state, controlled on
{\lvert}0\rangle
,
{\lvert}1\rangle
, or
{\lvert}2\rangle
, using swap,
C_{2^{\ell-1}}
, and
H^{\otimes\log n}
.
By
Lemma
2.1
, such a superposition can be prepared in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time, provided that the amplitudes are computable in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
time. As we already have an efficiently computable closed form expression for
N_{\bar{0}}
(see
(
18
)
), it suffices to obtain a closed-form expression for
\left\|{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\rangle\right\|
. For
\ell>0
, define
F_{j}(2^{\ell})\coloneq\left\|{\lvert}\bar{\theta}_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle\right\|^{2}
. We will prove by induction that

F_{j}(2^{\ell})=\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{2(n+2)^{\ell}+n-1}{n+1}.

(22)

For the base step, let
\ell=1
. By
(
20
)
, we have
F_{j}(2)=3/n
, which is equal to the right-hand side of
(
22
)
for the setting
\ell=1
. Next, we show the induction step.

\displaystyle F_{j}(2^{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(2N_{\bar{0}}(2^{\ell-1})+nF_{j}(2^{\ell-1})\right)

by
(
21
)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)^{\ell-1}+\frac{1}{n}F_{j}(2^{\ell-1})

by
(
18
)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n^{\ell}}(n+2)^{\ell-1}+\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{2(n+2)^{\ell-1}+n-1}{n+1}

by i.h.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{2(n+1)(n+2)^{\ell-1}+2(n+2)^{\ell-1}+n-1}{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{2(n+2)(n+2)^{\ell-1}+n-1}{n+1}=\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\cdot\frac{2(n+2)^{\ell}+n-1}{n+1}.

∎

Corollary 4.22
.

For all
\ell\in[\log(L)]
, the map that prepares a state proportional to

{\lvert}\theta_{j}(2^{\ell})\rangle=-{\lvert}\leftarrow,[u]\rangle+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\{0,1\}^{\log n}}{\lvert}p_{ij}(2^{\ell})\rangle+{\lvert}\rightarrow,[u,v_{j}]\rangle

can be implemented in
{\widetilde{O}}(1)
steps.

Lemma 4.23
.

There exists an orthonormal basis of

.

4.4
Complexity of the subroutine

We conclude by combining the switching network and corresponding basis generation we have given in the previous sections with
Theorem
2.9
to prove
Theorem

, which states the existence of an algorithm for
\textsc{Dist}_{L}
.

Proof of
Theorem
3.1
.

From
Lemma
4.3
, it follows that the switching network
{\cal N}_{L}(s,t)
accepts
G
if and only if there is a path of length at most
L
from
s
to
t
in
G
. We apply
Theorem
2.9
to
{\cal N}_{L}(s,t)
. By
Lemma
4.10
, whenever there is a

-path of length
\leq L
in
G
, there is a path from the source
[s]
to the sink
[s,t]
in

of length at most

. By

|E({\cal N}_{L}(s,t))|\leq(2n+1)^{\log L}n
