Aptamer selection in a porous hydrogel
Authors: John J. Rossi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The process of nucleic acid aptamer selection can be quite laborious and fraught with artifacts. In a work published in Nature Biotechnology, Singh et al. describe an approach that should allow more facile aptamer selection.

Main text

Nucleic acid aptamers are single-stranded RNA or DNA sequences that fold into three-dimensional shapes that enable binding to specific ligands in a highly selective manner. The process of creating aptamers from random pools of polynucleotide sequences was first described by Ellington and Szostak over 30 years ago.1 Subsequent to this, Tuerk and Gold described a process for aptamer evolution and selection, which they termed SELEX.2 SELEX involves the systematic selection and amplification of nucleic acids that bind to selected ligands. It is a reiterative process that uses randomly generated RNA or DNA libraries mixed with the ligand of interest (Figure 1). Molecules that bind to the ligand area are amplified while unbound molecules are washed out of the reaction mix. This reiterative process can take as many as 20 cycles to achieve a pool of strong ligand binders. Many modifications to this process have been described that include the use of solid-phase platforms to bind the ligand.3Figure 1Schematic diagram of SELEX for mRNA aptamer selection and enrichmentA DNA template with fixed sequences flanking a fully randomized core is transcribed into a pool of random RNA sequences. The RNA library is put through a counter-selection step that involves incubating the library with cells that do not express the target protein. The bound sequences are discarded, and the unbound species are recovered for incubation with the target protein. The unbound sequences at this step are washed out whereas bound sequences are eluted and reverse transcribed to create DNA templates, which are once again transcribed and incubated with the ligand. This process is done reiteratively until the pool of sequences is sufficiently reduced in numbers, at which time the RNA aptamers are sequenced and tested for ligand binding affinity.

One of the challenges of aptamer selection is effectively eliminating poor or non-binding sequences from the huge pool of random sequences that comprise the starting libraries. When solid-phase platforms are used to secure the ligands, non-binders can be readily washed away from sequences bound to the ligands. This is appealing except for the fact that sequences that bind to the solid-state platform are also retained, and these are undesired. To mitigate these challenges, Singh et al.4 devised a porous polymer from polyethylene glycol (PEG) in which protein ligands are covalently attached to the resin via carbonyl linkers. The pores in the gel are made intentionally large via the polymerization conditions. This allows for the nucleic acid polymers to freely diffuse in and out of the gel while the protein ligand bound sequences are retained within the gel, as they are bound to the ligand that is covalently attached to the gel matrix. The removal of unbound sequences from the gel allows for only those sequences bound to the ligand of interest to be sequenced, increasing the efficiency of the process.

The authors first tested this concept utilizing a well-studied RNA aptamer ligand binding pair of anti-thrombin aptamer with thrombin bound within the matrix and demonstrated that the aptamer entered through the pores of the porous gel and bound to the immobilized thrombin protein within the gel matrix. They then went on to demonstrate that unique aptamers could be selected from a library with GM-CSF as the gel-bound ligand where sequences bound to the ligand were well retained, whereas non-specific unbound sequences leaked out of the gel.

This porous gel approach should allow the facile selection of aptamers without the usual loss of sequences via up to ten or more sequential steps of increasingly stringent washes. It represents an improvement in nucleic acid aptamer selection and hopefully will aid aptamer selection for many different groups, thereby increasing the availability of nucleic acid aptamers for biomedical applications.
