On spectral stability of one- and bi-frequency solitary waves in Soler model in (3+1)D
Nabile Boussaïd Université Marie et Louis Pasteur, CNRS, Institut UTINAM, équipe de physique théorique, F-25000 Besançon, France Andrew Comech Mathematics Department, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843-3368, USA Niranjana Kulkarni Mathematics Department, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843-3368, USA

Abstract
For the nonlinear Dirac equation with scalar self-interaction (the Soler model) in three spatial dimensions, we consider the linearization at solitary wave solutions and find the invariant spaces which correspond to different spherical harmonics, thus achieving the radial reduction of the spectral stability analysis. We apply the same technique to the bi-frequency solitary waves (which are generically present in the Soler model) and show that they can also possess linear stability properties similar to those of one-frequency solitary waves.

Introduction

Properties of fermionic fields
are still somewhat of a mystery.
Pre-history of spinors starts only in the 19th century
(if we do not take into account
the figures from the candle dance of Southeast Asia):
it could be traced back to
William Hamilton’s research on quaternions (1843).
Irreducible even-dimensional representations
of
\mathbf{SO}(3)
were studied by
Élie Cartan
[
Car13
]
.
Two-component vectors
– points of the space corresponding to the
irreducible representation
of
\mathbf{SO}(3)
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
–
appeared in Wolfgang Pauli’s theory

[
Pau27
]

and were subsequently called

spinors
by Paul Ehrenfest
and studied in van der Waerden’s

Spinoranalyse

[
vdW29
,
vdW32
]
.
Four-component spinors
are at the foundation of the relativistic theory
of Paul Dirac
[
Dir28
]
;
this theory led to correct values for the spin
and the magnetic moment of the electron
and subsequently
to the most accurate agreement of the spectrum of the Hydrogen
and the eigenvalues of the Dirac operator
in the Coulomb potential
(with the notable exception of the Lamb shift),
suggesting that the electrons are
to be described by the field
that takes values in the four-dimensional ‘‘spinor’’
space.

So nice an agreement is a delicate point:
the exact formula for the energy levels in the Dirac theory
was first obtained by Sommerfeld
in
[
Som16
]
,
twelve years before Dirac,
via applying
a completely empirical Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization
taking into account the relativistic precession of
elliptic orbits
known from Astronomy.

While it is said colloquially that
spinors “have no classical analogues”,
some geometrical properties of our three-dimensional world
could be related to spinorial representations
(or, more precisely, to the fact that the spinor group

\mathbf{Spin}(3)

is a simply connected double cover of
\mathbf{SO}(3)
,
which in turn is connected but not simply connected).
These phenomena are closely related
to the idea of the Möbious strip,
such as
the change of polarization of light
when moving along a closed contour
in a medium with a small diffraction index,
when the polarization plane rotates by
\pi

(see e.g.
[
Mon99
]
),
the “Berry phase”
[
Ber84
]
(also known as
“Pancharatnam–Berry phase” or “geometric phase”)
of an eigenvector corresponding to a simple eigenvalue,
which
can be
\pi n

(with
n
not necessarily even)
after the motion over a closed non-contractible contour,
or Dirac’s string trick
(related to the candle-dance trick).

The Dirac theory of spinor fields
– and the Atomic Physics in general –
still seems incomplete,
as Dirac himself was pointing out
in early eighties
[
Dir81
]
.
In particular,
using the Dirac equation for more than one electron
is still problematic.
The problems appearing in the description of
two electrons in Helium
based on the Dirac equation are discussed in

[
Kal81
]
,

[
Der12
]

(the discussion goes as far as to conjecture
that the electrons in Helium are not in a bound state
but rather that they form a
resonance
),
and then in
[
OKY14
]
.
Let us also mention an attempt
[
KTZ16
]

to interpret the Dirac field
as describing a more fundamental particle,
with electron and positron being
two of its different “topological spin” states;
see also the discussion in e.g.
[
BBBB20
]

on the presence of positronic component in the solution to the Dirac equation.
As the matter of fact, even our understanding
of a single electron seems far from complete.
Can we hope to have as its counterpart a localized solution
of the Dirac–Maxwell system
[
Gro66
,
Wak66
]

in the framework of classical, non-quantized, interacting fields
– are there stable localized solutions?
The quantum theory essentially operates with the plane waves
whose self-interaction is dropped: physically, one says that the
renormalization has been performed,
so that the “quanta” of the fermionic field
have the mass and the charge known from the experiment.
While mathematics had nothing to offer to the physics demand
when the quantum electrodynamics was being developed,
now the mathematical background
for the treatment of the Dirac–Maxwell system of classical fields
is being formed:
the local well-posedness of Dirac–Maxwell
was proved in
[
Bou96
]
;
the existence of standing waves
was demonstrated numerically in
[
Lis95
]

and proved in
[
EGS96
,
Abe98
]
.
The question
of stability of localized classical solutions
of Dirac–Maxwell
(and thus of its possible relation to Physics)
can finally be consistently stated and discussed.

According to the physics folklore,
since the Dirac energy density
is not positive-definite,
even the zero solution
(“vacuum”)
of the Dirac–Maxwell cannot be stable
due to the continuous creation and annihilation
of electron-positron pairs;
the way to a stable electron is via the second quantization,
with all negative energy states of the “Dirac sea” having been filled
and with the Pauli exclusion principle
preventing the birth of more negative-energy electrons.
On the other hand,
owing to the Dirac equation being the first order in time,
the physical intuition based on Newton’s second law (which is second order in time)
is not necessarily applicable;
see, e.g.,
[
Rañ83
, Section V]
.
Indeed, using the cubic nonlinear Dirac equation
[
Iva38
,
Sol70
]

to model the self-interaction of the the Dirac–Maxwell system,
one finds that solitary waves

\phi_{\omega}(x)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}

in the limit nonrelativistic limit
\omega\lesssim m
,
with
m
the electron’s mass,
in spatial dimension one and two are spectrally stable

[
BC12
,
Lak18
,
BC19b
]
.
According to
[
CMKS+16
]
,
solitary waves of the cubic Dirac equation in (3+1)D,
while spectrally unstable for
\omega\lesssim m
close to
m
,
seem to become spectrally stable for
\omega\lesssim\omega_{\ast}
,
with
\omega_{\ast}\approx 0.936\,m
.
It is at this value of
\omega

that the pair of real eigenvalues
– one positive (the one responsible for
linear instability) and one negative,
present in the spectrum of linearization operator
for

\omega_{\ast}<\omega<m
– collide
at the origin, producing two purely imaginary eigenvalues.
Computations of the spectrum of the linearization at solitary waves
have been performed in
[
CMKS+16
]
only in the radial case;
no approach to consider higher degree spherical harmonics has been developed.
We are going to overcome this situation in the present work,
paving the way for the future numeric computation
of the spectrum of the linearization at solitary waves
in the Soler model in (3+1)D for perturbations of different angular structure
and hoping that this approach would also be
applicable to the physically viable Dirac–Maxwell.
Let us mention that consideration of perturbations
which are
of the same angular structure as the soliton itself
suggests – but not proves –
the stability of solitons of Dirac–Maxwell
in the nonrelativistic limit

\omega\gtrsim-m
;
see
[
CZ13
,
CS18
]
.

Bi-frequency solitary waves.

One peculiarity of the Soler model is the existence
of bi-frequency solitary waves,
which is a consequence of the
\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
symmetry
of the Soler model:

\displaystyle\psi\mapsto(a+b\gamma^{2}\bm{K})\psi,\qquad\gamma^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_{2}\\
-\sigma_{2}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad a,\,b\in\mathbb{C},\quad|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1,

(1.1)

where
\bm{K}:\,\mathbb{C}^{4}\to\mathbb{C}^{4}
is the operator of complex conjugation.
We note that (
1.1
) contains
\mathbf{U}(1)
as its subgroup
(with
b=0
);
the generator
\mathrm{i}\gamma^{2}\bm{K}

is known as the charge conjugation
in Quantum Electrodynamics.
The symmetry transformation (
1.1
)
coincides with the Bogoliubov transformation

[
Bog58
]

from the solid state physics.
It seems that
the presence of this continuous symmetry
in the context of the Dirac equation
was first noticed in
[
Gal77
]
.
One can see that
the application of the Bogoliubov symmetry
(
1.1
)
to a solitary wave
\phi_{\omega}(x)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}

produces bi-frequency solitary waves

[
BC18
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

We point out that
while the Soler model seems to be merely a playground
for developing tools for the Dirac–Maxwell system,
with the symmetry (
1.1
) nothing but a curious artifact
(which is absent in Dirac–Maxwell),
a physically viable Dirac–Klein–Gordon system
describing interaction or self-interaction of fermions
via the exchange of scalar particles
(“Yukawa coupling”),
with the Higgs boson an example of such a scalar particle,
features the same
\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
symmetry,
and so does any spinor theory
with the interaction term
in the Hamiltonian density based on
the scalar quantity
\psi^{*}\beta\psi
.
The consequences of
\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
symmetry,
such as the existence of bi-frequency solitary waves
and the conservation of a complex-valued charge

\Lambda=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\psi^{*}(t,x)(-\mathrm{i}\gamma^{2})\bm{K}\psi(t,x)\,dx

(see
[
BC18
]
),
remain in effect for all such theories.

It seems tempting yet still too early
to try to see a relation of bi-frequency localized solutions
in fermionic models with scalar self-interaction
to actual physical phenomena,
since the effects that may be connected to the
interaction via the Higgs mechanism
– such as neuton–antineutron oscillations

[
PSB+16
]

or neutron–mirror neutron (the latter is considered
a hidden sector particle, related to the Dark Matter)

[
KTVB22
,
DES24
]

–
are presently under active study in High Energy Physics
which tries to evolve beyond the Standard Model.
Other localized solutions – or quasiparticles –
in the field theories
include polarons
(see
[
Ale08
,
FRSD21
]
)
and skyrmions (see the reviews
[
ZB86
,
ESMRK18
]
);
both have been found to be directly related to
different physical phenomena.

If a particular bi-frequency solitary
wave can be obtained by the application of

\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
transformation
to a one-frequency solitary wave,
then its spectral stability –
understood in a proper way –
would be the same as that
of one-frequency solitary waves
(take a bi-frequency solitary wave with a small perturbation;
apply the inverse transformation to obtain a one-frequency solitary wave with a small perturbation).
This approach does not work in spatial dimensions three and higher,
since the solitary manifold of bi-frequency solitary waves
is
larger
than the symmetry group
\mathbf{SU}(1,1)

[
BC18
]
.
For example, in three spatial dimensions,
while
the solitary manifold of one-frequency solitary waves

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{1}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}v(r,\omega)\bm{\xi}\\
\mathrm{i}u(r,\omega)\sigma_{r}\bm{\xi}\end{bmatrix}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t},\quad\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{C}^{2},\ |\bm{\xi}|=1\right\},

(1.2)

with
v(r,\omega),\,u(r,\omega)
real-valued
and
\sigma_{r}=|x|^{-1}x\cdot\sigma
,
is of (real) dimension
4
(we consider
\omega
as a parameter);
the group
\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
is of dimension
3
,
sharing the one-dimensional
\mathbf{U}(1)
-symmetry with
(
1.2
);
as a result, the set

\big\{(a+b\gamma^{2}\bm{K})\phi_{\omega}(x)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\big\}

is six-dimensional.
At the same time,
the dimension of the solitary manifold
of bi-frequency solitary waves,

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{2}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}v(r,\omega)\bm{\xi}\\
\mathrm{i}u(r,\omega)\sigma_{r}\bm{\xi}\end{bmatrix}e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}+\begin{bmatrix}-\mathrm{i}u(r,\omega)\sigma_{r}\bm{\eta}\\
v(r,\omega)\bm{\eta}\end{bmatrix}e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t},\quad\bm{\xi},\,\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{C}^{2},\ |\bm{\xi}|^{2}-|\bm{\eta}|^{2}=1\right\},

(1.3)

equals eight.
Bi-frequency solitary waves that
can be obtained from one-frequency solitary waves with a symmetry transformation
(
1.1
) are the ones with
\bm{\eta}
parallel to
\sigma_{2}\bm{K}\bm{\xi}
.
We note that
\bm{\xi}
and
\sigma_{2}\bm{K}\bm{\xi}
are mutually orthogonal;
thus, the solitary waves
that cannot be obtained as an element of

\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
acting on a one-frequency solitary wave
are the ones with
\bm{\xi}
,
\bm{\eta}
being non-orthogonal.
(We call solutions
of the form (
1.3
)

bi-frequency

rather than
two-frequency
solitary waves
to emphasize the fact that
the frequencies
\pm\omega
are mutually opposite.)

In four spatial dimensions,
the Soler model for four-component spinors
has
\mathbf{U}(1)
symmetry
but not the
\mathbf{SU}(1,1)
symmetry
as a result,
none of the bi-frequency solitary waves
(
1.3
) with
\bm{\eta}\neq 0

admits the reduction of stability analysis
to that of a one-frequency solitary wave.
At the same time,
a bi-frequency solitary wave from (
1.3
),
with
|\bm{\xi}|\gtrsim 1
and
\bm{\eta}
small,
can be considered as a small perturbation
of a one-frequency solitary wave from (
1.2
);
this means that the asymptotic stability for one-frequency solitary waves
is simply not possible: a small perturbation,
in the form of the bi-frequency solitary wave,
being an exact solution, would never relax to a one-frequency solitary wave.
This brings us to the need to perform the stability analysis
of bi-frequency solitary waves directly, including the cases
when the reduction to a one-frequency solitary wave is not possible.
An attempt at this stability analysis has been made in

[
BC18
]
,
but that approach seems erroneous for spatial dimensions

3
and
4

and is certainly useless
for potential applications to asymptotic stability.

In the present work, we develop a simple approach to the spectral stability
of bi-frequency solitary waves
in spatial dimensions three,
based on the radial reduction,
showing that the spectral stability of one-
and bi-frequency solitary waves is different.
As the matter of fact,
bi-frequency solitary waves
seem to have stability properties
which are the same or better
than those of one-frequency solitary waves;
see Remark
4.2
.

The article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
,
we describe the model and give the main notations.
In Section
3
,
we perform the radial reduction
for the linear stability analysis
of one-frequency solitary waves
via decomposition into invariant spectral subspaces
corresponding to different spherical harmonics.
In Section
4
,
we generalize this approach
to linearization at a bi-frequency solitary waves
and in Section
5

show that we considered all perturbations.
In Appendix
A
,
we provide our notations for the spherical harmonics.
In Appendix
B
,
we derive the needed properties
of the spin-orbit operator in the Dirac theory.

The Soler model

The Soler model
has the form

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\psi=D_{m}\psi-f(\psi^{*}\beta\psi)\beta\psi=-\mathrm{i}\bm{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\psi+(m-f(\psi^{*}\beta\psi))\beta\psi,\quad\psi(x,t)\in\mathbb{C}^{4},\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(2.1)

where

\alpha^{i}
(
1\leq i\leq 3
)
and
\beta

are self-adjoint Dirac matrices
of size
N=4
;

D_{m}=\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{p}+\beta m=-\mathrm{i}\bm{\alpha}\cdot\nabla+\beta m
,
m>0
,
is the Dirac operator.
The self-interaction in (
2.1
)
is represented by a real-valued function

f
, with
f(0)=0
;
the original Soler model

[
Iva38
,
Sol70
]

is cubic, so that
f(\tau)=\tau
,
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.
Below, we will use the notation

\displaystyle g(\tau)=m-f(\tau),\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.2)

The Dirac matrices are self-adjoint,
anticommuting, with their square being equal to one,
so that

D_{m}^{2}=(-\Delta^{2}+m^{2})I_{4},

with
I_{4}
is the unit matrix of size
N
.
It then follows that
\alpha^{j}
and
\beta

satisfy the standard relations

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j}\alpha_{i}=2\delta_{ij}I_{4},\qquad\alpha_{i}\beta+\beta\alpha_{i}=0,\qquad 1\leq i,\,j\leq 3;\qquad\beta^{2}=I_{4}.

(2.3)

The Dirac conjugate of
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{4}

is denoted by

\bar{\psi}=\psi^{*}\beta
,
with
\psi^{*}
the hermitian conjugate of
\psi
.

The Dirac matrices could be taken in the form

\alpha^{j}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_{j}\\
\sigma_{j}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\beta=\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&0\\
0&-I_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(2.4)

with

\sigma_{j}
,

1\leq j\leq 3
,
the Pauli matrices.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
,
r=|x|>0
, we denote

\sigma_{r}=r^{-1}x\cdot\bm{\sigma},\qquad\alpha_{r}=r^{-1}x\cdot\bm{\alpha},

and we introduce

\varSigma=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\nabla:\;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{C}^{2}),\qquad\mathfrak{D}(\varSigma)=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{C}^{2}),

and let

\varSigma_{\Omega}:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
,

\ \mathfrak{D}(\varSigma_{\Omega})=H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})

be the angular part of
\varSigma

defined by the relation

\varSigma=\sigma_{r}\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\varSigma_{\Omega}.

Here is the Dirac operator in spherical coordinates:

D_{0}=-\mathrm{i}\bm{\alpha}\cdot\nabla=-\mathrm{i}\left(\alpha_{r}\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\alpha_{\phi}\partial_{\phi}+r^{-1}\alpha_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}\right),

(2.5)

x^{1}=r\cos\phi\sin\theta
,

x^{2}=r\sin\phi\sin\theta
,

x^{3}=r\cos\theta
,

\phi\in[0,2\pi)
,

\theta\in[0,\pi]
,

\alpha_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_{r}\\
\sigma_{r}&0\end{bmatrix}
,

\alpha_{\phi}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_{\phi}\\
\sigma_{\phi}&0\end{bmatrix}
,

\alpha_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_{\theta}\\
\sigma_{\theta}&0\end{bmatrix}
,
with

\sigma_{r}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\sin\theta\\
e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma_{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\\
\mathrm{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta&e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\cos\theta\\
e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}.

We define the operator

\varSigma_{\Omega}

by the relation

\varSigma=\sigma_{r}\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\varSigma_{\Omega}
,
with

\varSigma=\bm{\sigma}\cdot\nabla=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sigma_{j}\partial_{j}=\sigma_{r}\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\sigma_{\phi}\partial_{\phi}+r^{-1}\sigma_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}
;
that is,

\varSigma_{\Omega}=\sigma_{\phi}\partial_{\phi}+\sigma_{\theta}\partial_{\theta}
.
Then

\displaystyle\varSigma_{\Omega}\circ\sigma_{r}=2I_{2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sin\theta}\sigma_{\theta}\partial_{\phi}-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta\,\sigma_{\phi}\partial_{\theta},\qquad\sigma_{r}\varSigma_{\Omega}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sin\theta}\sigma_{\theta}\partial_{\phi}+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta\,\sigma_{\phi}\partial_{\theta},

(2.6)

so that

\{\varSigma_{\Omega},\sigma_{r}\}=2I_{2}

(cf. Lemma
B.2
).
In the explicit form,

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}\varSigma=\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\sigma_{r}\varSigma_{\Omega}=\partial_{r}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{r\sin\theta}\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta&e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\cos\theta\\
e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\end{bmatrix}\partial_{\phi}+\frac{1}{r}\begin{bmatrix}0&e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\\
-e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}&0\end{bmatrix}\partial_{\theta}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

and order

,

For any vectors

Lemma 2.2
.

We have:

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{1}^{*}\sigma_{r}\varSigma_{\Omega}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}\bm{e}_{1}=-m\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m},\qquad\bm{e}_{2}^{*}\sigma_{r}\varSigma_{\Omega}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}\bm{e}_{2}=m\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m};

(2.8)

\displaystyle\bm{e}_{2}^{*}\sigma_{r}\varSigma_{\Omega}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}\bm{e}_{1}=m\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}+\frac{e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}}{2}\left[\sqrt{\ell+m}\sqrt{\ell-m+1}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m-1}-\sqrt{\ell-m}\sqrt{\ell+m+1}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m+1}\right];

\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

and
-\ell\leq m\leq\ell
.

Above,
\bm{e}_{1}

Proof.

The statement

Assume that there is a solitary wave

We note that for each set

,
Q_{\ell,m}
, and

(cf. Lemma

).
The inspection shows that

.
Indeed, multiplying (

-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{r}

gives:

\displaystyle(\bar{P}_{\ell,m}+\bar{Q}_{\ell,m}\sigma_{r}\varSigma)\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,-m}\bm{e}_{2}+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\sigma_{r}(A_{\ell,m}+B_{\ell,m}\sigma_{r}\varSigma)\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}\bm{e}_{1}-\bar{S}_{\ell}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,\ell}\bm{e}_{1}=0;

(5.7)

in the first term

Appendix A
Appendix: The spherical harmonics

Let us remind the construction
of spherical harmonics
from
[
vdW32
]
.
Let
Q(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a homogeneous harmonic polynomial
of degree
\ell
:

0=\Delta Q=\Delta(r^{\ell}\mbox{\sl h}(\Omega)),

where
h
is a function on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
One has

\Delta u=r^{-(n-1)}\partial_{r}(r^{n-1}\partial_{r}u)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{\Omega}u
,
where
\Delta_{\Omega}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
so

0=\Delta Q=\Delta(r^{\ell}\mbox{\sl h}(\Omega))=r^{-(n-1)}\partial_{r}(r^{n-1}\partial_{r}(r^{\ell}))\mbox{\sl h}(\Omega)+r^{-2}\Delta_{\Omega}(r^{\ell}\mbox{\sl h}(\Omega)).

It follows that

\sigma(\Delta_{\Omega})=\{-\varkappa_{\ell}{\rm;}\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\},

with
\varkappa_{\ell}=\ell(\ell+n-2)
.
For the completeness, we recall that the Legendre polynomials
can be defined by
(cf.
[
Tha92
, §4.6.4]
)

P_{\ell}(w)=\frac{1}{2^{\ell}\ell!}\Big(\frac{d}{dw}\Big)^{\ell}(w^{2}-1)^{\ell},\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

the associated Legendre polynomials
are defined for
0\leq m\leq\ell

as in
[
Sch49
, Chapter 14]
by

P_{\ell,m}(w)=(1-w^{2})^{m/2}\Big(\frac{d}{dw}\Big)^{m}P_{\ell}(w),\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad 0\leq m\leq\ell,

and then the spherical harmonics are defined by

\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}}e^{\mathrm{i}m\phi}P_{\ell,m}(\cos\theta)\quad\mbox{for}\quad m\geq 0,\qquad\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,-m}(\theta,\phi)=\overline{\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}(\theta,\phi)},

where

\phi\in[0,2\pi)
and
\theta\in[0,\pi]
.
We note that this differs from the definition adopted
in
[
Tha92
, §4.6.4]
,
where

P_{\ell}^{m}=(-1)^{m}P_{\ell,m}\quad\mbox{and}\quad Y_{\ell}^{m}=(-1)^{m}\mbox{\sl h}_{\ell,m}\quad\mbox{for}\quad m\geq 0,\qquad Y_{\ell}^{-m}=(-1)^{m}\overline{Y_{\ell}^{m}}.

Appendix:
Properties of the spin-orbit operator

For generality, we consider an arbitrary spatial dimension

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The Dirac matrices are self-adjoint
and chosen so that the Dirac operator

D_{m}=-\mathrm{i}\bm{\upalpha}\cdot\nabla+\upbeta m
,
with
m>0
,
satisfies

D_{m}^{2}=(-\Delta^{2}+m^{2})I_{N},

with
I_{N}
is the unit matrix of size
N
.
It then follows that
\upalpha^{j}
and
\upbeta

are of size
N=2^{[(n+1)/2]}
(or its integral factor)
and satisfy the standard relations

\displaystyle\upalpha_{i}\upalpha_{j}+\upalpha_{j}\upalpha_{i}=2\delta_{ij}I_{N},\qquad\upalpha_{i}\upbeta+\upbeta\upalpha_{i}=0,\qquad 1\leq i,\,j\leq n;\qquad\upbeta^{2}=I_{N}.

(B.1)

The Dirac conjugate of
\psi\in\mathbb{C}^{N}

is denoted by

\bar{\psi}=\psi^{*}\upbeta
,
with
\psi^{*}
the hermitian conjugate of
\psi
.
The Dirac matrices could be taken in the form

\upalpha^{j}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\upsigma_{j}^{*}\\
\upsigma_{j}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\upbeta=\begin{bmatrix}I_{N/2}&0\\
0&-I_{N/2}\end{bmatrix},

(B.2)

with

\upsigma_{j}
the “generalized”
Pauli matrices,
not necessarily selfadjoint,
which satisfy the relations

\displaystyle\upsigma_{i}\upsigma_{j}^{*}+\upsigma_{j}\upsigma_{i}^{*}=2\delta_{ij}I_{N/2},\qquad\upsigma_{i}^{*}\upsigma_{j}+\upsigma_{j}^{*}\upsigma_{i}=2\delta_{ij}I_{N/2},\qquad 1\leq i,\,j\leq n;

(B.3)

let us mention that
each of the above relations implies the other one.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
r=|x|>0
, let

\upsigma_{r}=r^{-1}x\cdot\bm{\upsigma},\qquad\upalpha_{r}=r^{-1}x\cdot\bm{\upalpha}.

We introduce

\varSigma=\bm{\upsigma}\cdot\nabla:\;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C}^{N/2}),\qquad\mathfrak{D}(\varSigma)=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{C}^{N/2}),

and define the angular part of
\varSigma
by

\varSigma_{\Omega}:\,L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\mathbb{C}^{N/2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\mathbb{C}^{N/2}),\qquad\mathfrak{D}(\varSigma_{\Omega})=H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\mathbb{C}^{N/2})

by the relation

\varSigma=\upsigma_{r}\partial_{r}+r^{-1}\varSigma_{\Omega}.

Remark B.1
.

In three spatial dimensions,

,

,

\upalpha^{4}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}I_{2}\\

Lemma B.3
.

There is the following identity:

\displaystyle\varSigma\upsigma_{r}^{*}\varSigma=2\varSigma\circ\partial_{r}+\frac{2}{r}\upsigma_{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{n-3}{r}\varSigma-\upsigma_{r}\Delta

\ \ r>0

For

\partial_{r}\circ\varSigma=\partial_{r}\circ\Big(\upsigma_{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\varSigma_{\Omega}}{r}\Big)=\Big(\upsigma_{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\varSigma_{\Omega}}{r}\Big)\circ\partial_{r}-\frac{\varSigma_{\Omega}}{r^{2}}=\varSigma\circ\partial_{r}-\frac{\varSigma}{r}+\frac{\upsigma_{r}}{r}\partial_{r}.

Therefore,
using Lemma
B.2
,

\varSigma\upsigma_{r}^{*}\varSigma=\Big(2\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{r}I-\upsigma_{r}(\bm{\upsigma}^{*}\cdot\nabla)\Big)\varSigma=2\varSigma\circ\partial_{r}-\frac{2}{r}\varSigma+\frac{2}{r}\upsigma_{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{r}\varSigma-\upsigma_{r}\Delta.\qed

Let us define the symmetric operator

p_{r}=-\mathrm{i}r^{(1-n)/2}\circ\partial_{r}\circ r^{(n-1)/2},\qquad D(p_{r})=C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}),

the matrix

\upalpha_{r}=r^{-1}\bm{\upalpha}\cdot x
,

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
and

\displaystyle G=-\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}\mathrm{i}\upalpha^{j}\upalpha^{k}(x_{j}p_{k}-x_{k}p_{j}),\qquad S=\frac{n-1}{2}+G.

(B.8)

We note that if

; from now on we assume that

One has:

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\bm{\upalpha}\cdot\nabla=\bm{\upalpha}\cdot\bm{p}=\upalpha_{r}\Big(p_{r}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{r}S\Big)=-\mathrm{i}\upalpha_{r}\Big(\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{2r}-\frac{1}{r}S\Big)=-\mathrm{i}\upalpha_{r}\Big(\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{r}G\Big).

The operator

]
).

Lemma B.5
.

The operator
S
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})

with domain
\mathfrak{D}(S)=H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\mathbb{C}^{N})

is selfadjoint,
anticommutes with
\upalpha_{r}
,
and commutes with
\Delta_{\Omega}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
B.2
,

\big\{\upalpha_{r},\bm{\upalpha}\cdot\nabla\}=\big(2\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{r}\big)I_{N}
,
hence

\big\{\upalpha_{r},\bm{\upalpha}\cdot\nabla-\upalpha_{r}\partial_{r}\}=\frac{n-1}{r}I_{N}

and so

(n-1)I_{N}=\big\{\upalpha_{r},r(\bm{\upalpha}\cdot\nabla-\upalpha_{r}\partial_{r})\big\}=\big\{\upalpha_{r},-\upalpha_{r}G\big\}=\Big\{\upalpha_{r},\upalpha_{r}\frac{n-1}{2}-\upalpha_{r}S\Big\};

it follows that

\upalpha_{r}
anticommutes with
\upalpha_{r}S

S
.

Since
S
and
\upalpha_{r}
anticommute and
\upalpha_{r}^{2}=I_{N}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{n-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\Delta_{\Omega}}{r^{2}}=\Big(\upalpha_{r}\Big(\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{2r}-\frac{S}{r}\Big)\Big)^{2}=\Big(\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{2r}+\frac{S}{r}\Big)\Big(\partial_{r}+\frac{n-1}{2r}-\frac{S}{r}\Big).

Collecting the terms with

,
one arrives at the identity

\Delta_{\Omega}=\Big(\frac{n-1}{2}\Big)^{2}-\frac{n-1}{2}+S-S^{2}=\Big(\frac{n-2}{2}\Big)^{2}-\Big(S-\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{2},

commutes with

.

Since

and hence so is
