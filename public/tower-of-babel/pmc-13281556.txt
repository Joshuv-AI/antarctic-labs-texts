Temporal remodeling of atherosclerotic lipoprotein profiles and inflammatory markers after percutaneous coronary intervention: a real-world longitudinal study
Authors: An Wang, Shuai Chen, Jianyu Wang, Xiangyang Yin, Chaoyang Du, Fenghua Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients who undergo percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remain exposed to residual lipid and inflammatory risk despite contemporary secondary prevention. Real-world longitudinal data describing how conventional lipids, apolipoproteins, small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL), lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], and C-reactive protein (CRP) remodel together after PCI are limited. We evaluated serial post-PCI changes in a broad atherosclerotic biomarker panel and explored the dissociation between cholesterol control and inflammatory control.MethodsWe performed a retrospective longitudinal study using a clinical laboratory dataset. Adults with at least one PCI-coded encounter and serial laboratory follow-up were included. The index date was defined as the first PCI-coded visit. Angiography-only patients without PCI were excluded. Biomarkers included triglycerides, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), Lp(a), apolipoprotein E (ApoE), sdLDL, free fatty acids, glycation, and CRP. Paired baseline-to-latest comparisons were performed in 397 patients. The prespecified primary endpoint was the paired change in LDL-C from index PCI to latest follow-up. Secondary endpoints included paired changes in other lipid and inflammatory biomarkers. Longitudinal associations across follow-up windows were explored using generalized estimating equations adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, baseline statin intensity, and time-updated lipid-lowering regimen category.ResultsAmong 1082 encounters from 526 individuals, 397 patients with PCI contributed 813 observations from index onward. Mean age was 62.0 ± 10.3 years, 77.8% were men, 69.5% had hypertension, and 35.0% had diabetes. Median follow-up was 393 days. Compared with index PCI, latest follow-up showed lower LDL-C (2.42 to 1.63 mmol/L; mean change − 0.79, 95% confidence interval [CI] -0.90 to -0.68; p < 0.001), non-HDL-C (3.07 to 2.11 mmol/L; mean change − 0.96, 95% CI -1.09 to -0.83; p < 0.001), ApoB (0.82 to 0.63 g/L; mean change − 0.19, 95% CI -0.22 to -0.16; p < 0.001), ApoB/ApoA1 ratio (0.74 to 0.59; p < 0.001), and CRP (4.94 to 2.71 mg/L; mean change − 2.23, 95% CI -3.36 to -1.09; p < 0.001). HDL-C rose modestly (1.07 to 1.11 mmol/L; p < 0.001). Lp(a), sdLDL, triglycerides, free fatty acids, and glycation changed little in paired analyses. In adjusted longitudinal models, lower LDL-C, ApoB, non-HDL-C, and log (CRP) persisted through 1 year, 1–2 years, and > 2 years, whereas Lp(a) showed little overall change, with only a modest decrease in the 1-2-year window. In expanded multivariable analyses, combination or PCSK9-containing regimen at latest follow-up was independently associated with dual attainment of LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L and CRP < 2 mg/L at latest follow-up. The proportion with both high LDL-C and high CRP fell from 42.3% at index to 15.4% at follow-up, but isolated residual inflammatory risk rose from 6.0% to 11.8%.ConclusionAfter PCI, real-world biomarker remodeling was characterized by durable reductions in LDL-C, ApoB, non-HDL-C, and CRP, but incomplete convergence of cholesterol-related and inflammatory biomarker profiles. Lp(a) and several nontraditional lipid measures appeared relatively resistant to routine care pathways. Because conventional CRP rather than hsCRP was available and no adjudicated clinical outcome data were available, the translational relevance of these findings is limited and they should be interpreted cautiously as biomarker-level and hypothesis-generating observations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05892-9.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease is driven by the convergence of cholesterol trafficking, lipoprotein retention, immune activation, inflammation, and maladaptive vascular repair [1–3]. Immune and inflammatory dysfunction are now recognized as central components of the pathogenesis of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), because imbalance between proatherogenic and antiatherogenic immune networks promotes plaque destabilization, thrombotic transition, and acute coronary events [4]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) reduces ischemia and seals culprit lesions, but it does not extinguish the systemic biology that promotes plaque progression in nonculprit segments [3, 5]. For that reason, post-PCI risk is increasingly understood as a composite of residual cholesterol risk, residual inflammatory risk, and lipoprotein-specific risk not fully captured by low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) alone [6–9].

LDL-C remains the primary therapeutic target after acute coronary syndromes and PCI, and early intensive lipid lowering is guideline-endorsed [5, 6]. Yet several observations complicate a purely LDL-centered view. ApoB reflects the total burden of atherogenic particles and may better quantify arterial exposure than cholesterol content alone [2, 10]. Small dense LDL is more susceptible to oxidation and arterial retention, while lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] has thrombogenic and proinflammatory properties that often persist despite statin-based therapy [11–15]. At the same time, C-reactive protein (CRP) and related inflammatory signals remain prognostically relevant after PCI even when LDL-C is apparently controlled [7, 8, 16, 17].

Most longitudinal post-PCI studies have focused on clinical events, a single lipid fraction, or a narrow biomarker set. Real-world serial data simultaneously integrating conventional lipids, apolipoproteins, small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL), Lp(a), and inflammatory markers are sparse, particularly outside trial populations [9, 18, 19]. This matters because therapeutic inertia, regimen intensification, and biomarker discordance are common in routine care.

We conducted a retrospective, hypothesis-generating longitudinal study to examine temporal remodeling of a comprehensive atherosclerotic biomarker panel after PCI in a real-world dataset. The prespecified primary endpoint was the within-patient change in LDL-C from index PCI to latest follow-up. Secondary endpoints included paired changes in non-HDL-C, ApoB, ApoB/ApoA1 ratio, HDL-C, ApoA1, CRP, Lp(a), sdLDL, triglycerides, free fatty acids, and glycation. Longitudinal analyses across follow-up windows, residual cholesterol-inflammatory risk profiling, and dual-target attainment analyses were considered exploratory and intended to characterize biomarker patterns and generate hypotheses for future prospective studies.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective longitudinal observational study based on routinely collected hospital clinical and laboratory records. The source dataset included 1,082 medical records from 526 individual patients who underwent at least two coronary angiography-related evaluations at our hospital between February 2021 and October 2025. Available variables included age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, treatment type, broad procedural descriptors from the index PCI report, lipid-lowering regimen, renal function, fasting glucose, and serial lipid and inflammatory biomarkers. When available from the index procedural report, culprit lesion phenotype and predefined procedural-complexity features were abstracted for sensitivity analyses. Because this was a real-world retrospective dataset assembled from routinely collected hospital clinical and laboratory records, medication exposure could only be captured at the level of recorded regimen category in the hospital record. Standardized pharmacy-dispensing data, longitudinal prescription reconciliation across institutions, and direct adherence-monitoring variables were not available in the source database.

Patient selection and definitions

The index date was defined as each patient’s first PCI-coded encounter. PCI was identified by treatment coding for stent implantation, drug-eluting balloon, or combined stent plus drug-eluting balloon. Patients were eligible for the longitudinal analysis if they had a PCI-coded index encounter and at least one post-index laboratory reassessment containing study biomarkers. We excluded patients who underwent angiography alone without PCI coding (n = 3), those with incomplete clinical data (n = 79), those with malignant tumors (n = 14), those with end-stage renal disease requiring dialysis (n = 11), those with autoimmune diseases (n = 15), and those with severe hepatic insufficiency (n = 7). One laboratory measurement occurring before the first PCI date was excluded from post-index longitudinal modeling but did not alter patient retention. The final analytic cohort comprised 397 patients contributing 813 observations from index onward, including 397 index visits and 416 post-index follow-up visits. Each follow-up observation was defined as a post-PCI clinical visit with available biomarker testing, and latest follow-up was defined as the last available post-index observation for each patient.

Procedural characterization

Index PCI reports were reviewed for broad procedural descriptors relevant to inflammatory burden. Complex PCI was defined as the presence of at least one of the following features: multivessel PCI, left main PCI, bifurcation PCI with a two-stent strategy, chronic total occlusion treatment, implantation of three or more stents, or total stent length greater than 40 mm. Culprit lesion phenotype was categorized from angiographic and procedural report descriptors as thrombotic/rupture-like lesion, calcific/fibrotic de novo stenosis, bifurcation or ostial lesion, diffuse long-segment nonthrombotic lesion, or in-stent restenosis. Intravascular imaging use at index PCI was also abstracted from the procedural record. Intravascular ultrasound or optical coherence tomography was used in a minority of cases and was not protocol mandated. Because intravascular imaging was not routinely used across the cohort, culprit lesion phenotype was classified primarily from procedural report language rather than imaging-confirmed plaque morphology. Detailed procedural characteristics, including intravascular imaging frequency, are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Biomarkers and derived measures

The primary biomarkers were triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), ApoB, Lp(a), apolipoprotein E (ApoE), sdLDL, free fatty acids, glycation, and CRP. Venous blood samples were analyzed in the hospital central laboratory according to standard operating procedures used in routine clinical care. Conventional lipid fractions were measured by automated enzymatic methods, and apolipoproteins were measured by immunoturbidimetric methods. Lp(a) was measured by an immunoturbidimetric assay and reported in g/L, whereas sdLDL was measured by a direct homogeneous assay and reported in mmol/L. In the source laboratory system, conventional CRP rather than high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) was available and was therefore analyzed as the inflammatory biomarker in this study. We recognize that hsCRP is a more sensitive and more extensively validated inflammatory marker for cardiovascular risk stratification than conventional CRP, particularly in ACS-oriented prognostic literature, and this should be considered when interpreting the present findings [20]. Derived measures included non-HDL-C and the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio. Selected laboratory methods, including the assays for Lp(a) and sdLDL, are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. For clinically interpretable residual risk profiling, we classified LDL-C ≥ 1.4 mmol/L as above the contemporary very-high-risk target and CRP ≥ 2 mg/L as elevated inflammatory risk, consistent with prior cardiovascular inflammation studies [6–8, 16, 17].

Exposure characterization

Lipid-lowering therapy was categorized at index PCI and at each follow-up observation as statin monotherapy, ezetimibe monotherapy, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitor monotherapy, statin plus ezetimibe, statin plus PCSK9 inhibitor, or ezetimibe plus PCSK9 inhibitor. Statin intensity was classified as high, moderate, or low according to guideline-equivalent daily doses. Treatment intensification was defined as escalation from low- or moderate-intensity to high-intensity statin therapy, addition of ezetimibe, addition of a PCSK9 inhibitor, or transition from monotherapy to combination therapy during follow-up. Because this retrospective cohort was assembled from hospital-based clinical and laboratory records rather than an integrated pharmacy database, the available medication information reflected clinician-documented treatment category rather than verified dispensed dose history. In addition, patients could receive prescription refills or medication adjustments outside our hospital system, and structured refill adherence metrics, pill counts, and medication possession data were not available. Therefore, prescription-fill information, dose persistence, and pill-level adherence could not be measured directly in this population.

Statistical analysis

This study combined one prespecified primary endpoint with broader secondary and exploratory analyses. The primary endpoint was the paired within-patient change in LDL-C from index PCI to latest follow-up. Secondary endpoints were paired changes in the other measured lipid and inflammatory biomarkers. The generalized estimating equation analyses across follow-up windows, the multivariable model for dual target attainment, and the residual cholesterol-inflammatory risk classification were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Paired comparisons between index and latest follow-up used paired t tests for approximately symmetric differences. Because Lp(a) and CRP showed right-skewed distributions, these markers were additionally examined on the logarithmic scale in longitudinal modeling. sdLDL was analyzed on its original scale but interpreted cautiously because both biological variability and assay-specific variability may be greater than for conventional cholesterol measures. Accordingly, Table 1 and the follow-up window summaries present skewed variables as medians with interquartile ranges, whereas Table 2 reports arithmetic means and mean paired changes for consistency with the paired comparison framework. To leverage all repeated observations, we fitted generalized estimating equation models with exchangeable correlation structure. The prespecified model included follow-up window, age, sex, body mass index, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, baseline statin intensity, and time-updated lipid-lowering regimen category. We also performed sensitivity analyses replacing regimen category with a binary treatment-intensification variable, restricting the analysis to patients receiving statin monotherapy at index PCI, and additionally adjusting the longitudinal inflammation models for complex PCI and culprit lesion phenotype. In this baseline statin-monotherapy subgroup, intensification was defined as escalation to statin plus ezetimibe, statin plus PCSK9 inhibitor, or PCSK9 inhibitor monotherapy by latest follow-up. Detailed treatment distributions are shown in Supplementary Table S1, full outputs of the expanded therapy-adjusted models are shown in Supplementary Table S2, and index procedural characteristics together with procedural-sensitivity analyses are shown in Supplementary Table S4. Because follow-up testing was not protocol scheduled, each observation was assigned to a follow-up window according to the elapsed time from the index PCI date. Standardized beta coefficients were reported to allow comparison across biomarkers with different units. We also fitted a multivariable logistic regression model for dual target attainment at latest follow-up, defined as LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L and CRP < 2 mg/L. For the paired baseline-to-latest analysis with 397 patients, the detectable standardized within-subject effect size was 0.141 at 80% power and 0.163 at 90% power with a two-sided alpha of 0.05. The observed paired LDL-C effect size was substantially larger (Cohen’s dz ≈ 0.71), indicating ample power for the prespecified primary endpoint of paired LDL-C change. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The study selection process and derivation of the final analytic cohort are shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Among 1,082 records from 526 unique individuals screened from February 2021 to October 2025, 129 patients were excluded on the basis of predefined exclusion criteria, including 3 patients with angiography alone and no PCI-coded encounter. The final longitudinal cohort consisted of 397 patients contributing 813 observations from index onward, including 397 index PCI visits and 416 post-index follow-up visits. Median follow-up to the latest available observation was 393 days (interquartile range, 366–493 days).

At index PCI, mean age was 62.04 ± 10.32 years, 77.8% were male, 69.5% had hypertension, 35.0% had diabetes, and 32.7% were current smokers. Stent implantation accounted for 92.2% of index procedures and combined stent plus drug-eluting balloon for 7.3%. In addition, 109 patients (27.5%) met the prespecified definition of complex PCI. Intravascular imaging at index PCI was used in 96 patients (24.2%), including intravascular ultrasound in 78 patients (19.6%) and optical coherence tomography in 18 patients (4.5%). Culprit lesion phenotype at index PCI was classified as thrombotic/rupture-like in 124 patients (31.2%), calcific/fibrotic de novo stenosis in 153 (38.5%), bifurcation or ostial lesion in 62 (15.6%), diffuse long-segment nonthrombotic lesion in 29 (7.3%), and in-stent restenosis in 29 (7.3%). Most patients were receiving statin monotherapy at baseline (89.7%), whereas combination therapy was uncommon at index. Detailed distributions of lipid-lowering regimen class and statin intensity at index PCI and latest follow-up are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and detailed procedural characteristics, including intravascular imaging use, are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Baseline demographic, clinical, procedural, and biomarker characteristics at index PCI are summarized in Table 1.

Paired biomarker remodeling from index PCI to latest follow-up

In the paired analysis of 397 patients, LDL-C fell from 2.42 to 1.63 mmol/L, non-HDL-C from 3.07 to 2.11 mmol/L, and ApoB from 0.82 to 0.63 g/L. ApoB/ApoA1 ratio also declined substantially. HDL-C rose modestly, while ApoA1 increased only slightly. CRP decreased significantly, but several nontraditional markers were relatively static. Lp(a) showed no significant paired change, and neither sdLDL nor triglycerides demonstrated a statistically robust reduction in the baseline-to-latest comparison. Longitudinal remodeling across follow-up windows

When all 813 observations from index onward were considered, median LDL-C dropped from 2.24 mmol/L at index to 1.60 mmol/L within 1 year, 1.57 mmol/L at 1–2 years, and 1.61 mmol/L beyond 2 years. Median CRP fell from 1.86 mg/L at index to 1.00 mg/L by ≤ 1 year and remained approximately 1.00 mg/L thereafter. Lp(a) was relatively stable across windows. In sensitivity analyses additionally accounting for complex PCI and culprit lesion phenotype, the direction and significance of the major LDL-C and CRP trajectories were materially unchanged (Supplementary Table S4). Lipid-lowering intensification and dual target attainment

At index PCI, statin monotherapy predominated, whereas use of combination lipid-lowering therapy increased during follow-up. Among 356 patients treated with statin monotherapy at baseline, 109 escalated to statin plus ezetimibe, statin plus PCSK9 inhibitor, or PCSK9 inhibitor monotherapy by latest follow-up. In unadjusted comparisons, treatment intensification was associated with larger reductions in LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and ApoB. In expanded multivariable analyses additionally accounting for baseline statin intensity and follow-up regimen category, more intensive lipid-lowering treatment remained independently associated with more favorable lipid remodeling and with a higher likelihood of dual attainment of LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L and CRP < 2 mg/L at latest follow-up (Supplementary Table S2). These findings were directionally consistent in sensitivity analyses restricted to patients receiving statin monotherapy at index PCI, and detailed regimen transitions are shown in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S2. Because medication dose history, refill data, and direct adherence measures were unavailable in the hospital record system, these treatment analyses should be interpreted as reflecting recorded treatment exposure rather than verified dose intensity or confirmed adherence.

Residual cholesterol-inflammatory risk profile

Despite clear improvement, dual risk suppression remained incomplete. LDL-C < 1.4 mmol/L increased from 15.1% at index to 39.5% at latest follow-up, while LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L rose from 31.0% to 64.7%. The proportion of patients with both elevated LDL-C and elevated CRP fell from 42.3% to 15.4%. However, isolated residual inflammatory risk increased from 6.0% to 11.8%, suggesting that cholesterol lowering outpaced inflammatory normalization in a substantial subset. Discussion

This study describes serial biomarker changes after PCI in routine clinical practice. From index to follow-up, LDL-C, non-HDL-C, ApoB, the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio, and CRP decreased, whereas Lp(a), sdLDL, triglycerides, and free fatty acids changed less consistently. Treatment intensification was associated with greater lipid lowering and a higher likelihood of simultaneous LDL-C and CRP target attainment. Despite these improvements, residual inflammatory risk persisted in a subset of patients.

LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and ApoB decreased in parallel after PCI. This pattern is consistent with current guideline-based lipid management and with trial evidence showing that more intensive lowering of ApoB-containing lipoproteins reduces cardiovascular risk [2, 5, 6, 10, 21–24]. Although our study did not assess clinical outcomes, the observed biomarker changes are directionally consistent with that literature.

ApoB deserves special comment. LDL-C remains the most familiar clinical target, but ApoB may better summarize the number of circulating atherogenic particles, especially when triglycerides are variable or discordance exists between cholesterol mass and particle count [2, 10]. The robust reduction in ApoB we observed, together with the fall in ApoB/ApoA1 ratio, suggests that post-PCI treatment in this cohort altered the atherogenic particle milieu more broadly than LDL-C alone might imply. That is important because arterial injury and repair after PCI occur within a vascular bed still exposed to systemic particle trafficking. A patient can have an improved LDL-C while continuing to carry a high burden of ApoB particles or residual inflammatory activation. Our data support the view that serial ApoB measurement may refine post-PCI surveillance, especially when clinicians are deciding whether apparent LDL-C success is biologically complete.

The CRP findings are potentially informative, but they require cautious interpretation. Conventional CRP fell substantially in both paired and adjusted longitudinal analyses, but inflammatory control appeared to lag behind cholesterol control. By latest follow-up, the proportion with both high LDL-C and high CRP had dropped sharply, yet a subgroup remained with persistently elevated CRP despite improved lipid parameters. This pattern is biologically plausible and consistent with the broader concept of residual inflammatory activity after PCI, but it should not be overinterpreted because conventional CRP is a nonspecific inflammatory marker and clinical outcomes were not available in this dataset. Importantly, hsCRP is a more sensitive and more extensively validated inflammatory biomarker for cardiovascular risk assessment than conventional CRP. In addition, the recent systematic review emphasizes that inflammation in ACS is multidimensional, that biomarker-based inflammatory assessment may aid residual risk stratification, and that inflammatory pathways remain clinically relevant even after revascularization rather than being fully captured by lipid metrics alone. That review also highlights the emerging therapeutic relevance of inflammation, including the possibility that anti-inflammatory strategies may improve outcomes in selected higher-risk ACS patients [20, 25]. In that context, our results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating evidence that lipid remodeling and inflammatory biomarker remodeling may not fully converge during routine post-PCI care [3, 7, 9, 25–27].

This apparent uncoupling between cholesterol and inflammatory biomarker trajectories was one of the more notable observations in the study. The post-PCI patient with LDL-C near target but CRP still elevated may represent a clinically relevant phenotype, but our data cannot determine whether this biomarker pattern translated into recurrent ischemic events or whether it would identify patients who benefit from anti-inflammatory treatment. Trials such as CANTOS, COLCOT, and LoDoCo2 support the broader importance of inflammatory pathways in atherosclerotic disease, and the recent review by Intravaia et al. further underscores that inflammatory assessment in ACS should be understood within a broader network of pathways and biomarkers rather than through a single marker alone. It also supports the idea that patients with persistent inflammatory activation despite contemporary lipid-lowering therapy may represent a subgroup in whom inflammation-modulating strategies deserve further prospective evaluation. Our dataset should therefore be interpreted more modestly: it suggests that the biology of residual risk after PCI may not be captured by LDL-C alone and that this issue warrants prospective study with hsCRP, broader inflammatory profiling, and adjudicated outcomes [8, 16, 17, 25]. Procedural and lesion-level features may also influence the inflammatory response after PCI. In the present analysis, however, the overall dissociation between lipid and inflammatory biomarker trajectories remained materially similar after additional adjustment for complex PCI and angiography-based culprit lesion phenotype. This suggests that the residual inflammatory signal we observed was not explained solely by greater index procedural burden or by more inflammatory-appearing culprit lesions. At the same time, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because phenotype classification was based on routine procedural reports rather than intravascular imaging or core-laboratory adjudication.

Recent studies also support a broader multimarker view of residual risk after PCI. Gao et al. reported that the C-reactive protein-triglyceride glucose index (CTI) was a practical indicator for cardiometabolic evaluation and, among the inflammatory and insulin-resistance indicators examined, the most promising predictor of recurrent cardiovascular risk in PCI-treated patients. They also found that TyG-associated cardiovascular risk was partially mediated by systemic inflammation, particularly in patients with metabolic abnormalities. In a separate ACS/PCI cohort, Zhang et al. showed that higher baseline neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was associated with greater cardiovascular risk, that relative risk reductions with evolocumab were consistent across NLR strata, and that the lowest residual risk was observed in patients with the lowest on-treatment NLR and LDL-C levels. Although CTI, TyG, and NLR were not available in our dataset, these studies reinforce the interpretation that residual risk after PCI may reflect interacting inflammatory, metabolic, and cholesterol-related pathways rather than LDL-C alone [28, 29].

The relative stability of Lp(a) is another striking and credible finding. Unlike LDL-C, Lp(a) is largely genetically determined and minimally modified by statins [11, 12]. PCSK9 inhibition can reduce Lp(a), but usually only modestly, and most routine-care regimens do not normalize high Lp(a) [12, 22, 30]. The absence of a substantial paired reduction in our dataset therefore reinforces rather than weakens the biological credibility of the study. At the same time, Lp(a) should be interpreted in assay-specific context: in our study it was measured by a routine clinical immunoturbidimetric assay and reported in g/L, and absolute Lp(a) values may vary across assay platforms because of apo(a) isoform-related heterogeneity. Even with that caveat, the longitudinal pattern observed here was biologically plausible and consistent with contemporary understanding that routine lipid-lowering care improves LDL-C and ApoB more readily than Lp(a). That matters because post-PCI patients with persistently high Lp(a) may carry thrombogenic and fibrocalcific risk that remains largely untouched by standard treatment [12]. The implication is practical: Lp(a) should be measured deliberately and interpreted as a relatively stable, genetically mediated residual risk marker rather than assumed to normalize with statin success.

The sdLDL result is more complicated. In this study, sdLDL was assessed using a direct homogeneous clinical assay and reported in mmol/L, which is practical for routine care but reflects an assay-defined sdLDL fraction rather than the full particle characterization obtainable with research-based separation methods. In the present analyses, sdLDL did not show a clear or durable longitudinal pattern. This likely reflects both biology and measurement context. Biologically, sdLDL is influenced by triglyceride-rich lipoprotein metabolism, insulin resistance, and hepatic lipase activity, so its behavior is often less linear than LDL-C [13–15]. Methodologically, sdLDL distributions are typically skewed and can be more variable across time and assay conditions than conventional cholesterol fractions. Still, the direction of the signal is consistent with growing evidence that sdLDL can carry residual risk beyond LDL-C in stable coronary disease and ACS cohorts [13–15]. The message is not that sdLDL should replace LDL-C, but that a multimarker framework may reveal residual atherogenicity missed by conventional cholesterol panels and that sdLDL findings in routine-care datasets should be interpreted cautiously.

Our treatment-intensification findings reinforce the clinical reality that what happens after PCI is not just natural history; it is management history. Patients who intensified beyond statin monotherapy had far greater reductions in LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and ApoB, and were much more likely to achieve dual LDL-C/CRP targets. That is directionally consistent with trial data showing that adding ezetimibe or a PCSK9 inhibitor after ACS produces larger lipid reductions and, in the case of PCSK9 inhibition, plaque modification and event reduction [21–24, 31, 32]. It is also consistent with real-world registry data showing that routine lipid goal attainment remains suboptimal unless treatment is actively escalated [18, 19]. The strength of the association in our study should not be overinterpreted causally, because treatment intensification was not randomized. Still, the signal is clinically intuitive and biologically coherent: more intensive therapy yielded more intensive remodeling.

The study also fits well with contemporary preclinical concepts of atherogenesis, immune imbalance, and plaque healing. Experimental work established decades ago that arterial retention of ApoB-containing lipoproteins is an initiating event in atherosclerosis [33]. Cholesterol crystal formation can then activate the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and amplify interleukin-1 signaling, linking lipoprotein accumulation to innate immune activation [34]. More recent translational work has further emphasized that ACS does not simply reflect passive plaque progression, but rather a shift in immune-inflammatory balance in which proatherogenic pathways outweigh antiatherogenic regulatory networks, promoting plaque instability and acute coronary events [3, 4, 27, 35, 36]. In that framework, our real-world observations make sense. Reducing ApoB exposure after PCI likely diminishes the substrate for continued lipid retention and intraplaque inflammatory activation, but inflammatory tone does not normalize instantly because vascular injury, metabolic disease, and immunologic memory continue to shape the arterial microenvironment. PCI fixes a focal obstruction. It does not reset macrophage biology, adipose inflammation, or the genetically mediated production of Lp(a). That is why a patient can exhibit excellent LDL-C lowering and still carry elevated CRP.

Another important point is that our findings align more closely with the biology of “plaque ecosystem remodeling” than with a simple before-and-after procedural effect. The biomarker changes were not confined to the immediate post-PCI period; they were sustained across one year and beyond. That matters because nonculprit plaques drive a substantial share of future events after ACS and PCI [31, 32]. Imaging trials such as GLAGOV and PACMAN-AMI showed that intensive lipid-lowering strategies can influence plaque burden and composition over time [31, 32]. Our study does not include intravascular imaging, but the parallel reduction in LDL-C, ApoB, and CRP suggests a systemic environment more compatible with plaque stabilization than the index state. At the same time, the persistence of isolated inflammatory risk and unchanged Lp(a) suggests that stabilization may remain incomplete in a meaningful minority.

This study contributes a real-world, serial, multimarker description of how conventional lipids, particle-related markers, and inflammation may remodel together rather than in isolation after PCI. Many prior studies have addressed one axis at a time, usually outcomes versus LDL-C, hsCRP, or triglyceride-related markers. Our data suggest that the post-PCI biomarker phenotype may be multidimensional, with improvement along the LDL-C axis, partial improvement along the inflammatory axis, and relative persistence along the Lp(a) axis. This interpretation is consistent with recent syntheses of ACS inflammation biology, which highlight that residual cardiovascular risk after ACS may reflect persistent inflammatory activity that is incompletely represented by lipid measures alone and may have therapeutic relevance in selected higher-risk patients. However, because the inflammatory signal was based on conventional CRP and no clinical outcome data were available, this layered biomarker pattern should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than prognostically validated, and its translational relevance remains limited until outcome-linked validation is available [9, 18, 19, 25].

The study has limitations, the most important of which is the absence of adjudicated clinical outcome data linking biomarker trajectories to recurrent events or prognosis after PCI. In addition, it was retrospective and observational, based on routinely collected laboratory data rather than a protocolized registry. Although we incorporated broad angiography-based procedural descriptors and culprit lesion phenotype categories into sensitivity analyses and documented the frequency of intravascular imaging use, intravascular imaging was performed in only a minority of patients and was not protocolized across the cohort. We therefore still lacked granular core-laboratory adjudicated lesion morphology, verified medication dose history, refill-based adherence data, and event outcomes. This was because the cohort was derived from routinely collected hospital clinical and laboratory records rather than an integrated pharmacy or claims platform, so medication documentation was available primarily as recorded regimen category and not as longitudinal dose-verified exposure. Importantly, conventional CRP rather than hsCRP was available. This is a meaningful limitation because hsCRP is the more sensitive and better validated inflammatory measure for cardiovascular prognostic assessment, whereas conventional CRP provides a broader and less specific inflammatory signal and may introduce greater measurement heterogeneity. In addition, newer integrated inflammatory-metabolic indices such as CTI, TyG, and NLR were not available in our dataset, which limited comparison with emerging post-PCI residual-risk models described in recent literature. Most importantly, the absence of adjudicated clinical outcome data is a major limitation of the present study and substantially limits its translational relevance, because we could not determine whether the observed biomarker patterns were associated with recurrent cardiovascular events, treatment response, or prognosis after PCI. Follow-up was not uniformly scheduled, most visits clustered around one year, and later windows were smaller. We also cannot exclude confounding by indication in the intensification analyses. Accordingly, the inflammation-related and multimarker findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. In addition, because Lp(a) and sdLDL were measured within a routine clinical laboratory workflow rather than a dedicated research core laboratory, some assay-specific heterogeneity cannot be excluded.

Despite these limitations, the dataset has strengths. Missingness was minimal, repeated measures were available for nearly all retained patients, the biomarker panel was unusually broad for routine practice, and the observed patterns were biologically plausible. The simultaneous fall in LDL-C, non-HDL-C, ApoB, and CRP, alongside the relative persistence of Lp(a), is exactly the kind of internally coherent signature one would expect in contemporary secondary prevention.

Clinically, these findings suggest that post-PCI follow-up may eventually benefit from assessment beyond LDL-C alone. ApoB or non-HDL-C may better reflect residual atherogenic particle burden, and conventional CRP may provide only a broad and imperfect signal of persistent inflammatory activity compared with hsCRP-based inflammatory assessment, and Lp(a) may identify a genetically mediated residual risk pathway that routine statin-based care does not address. Emerging evidence also suggests that integrated inflammatory-metabolic markers such as CTI and inflammatory cell-based markers such as NLR may further refine post-PCI residual-risk assessment. However, these implications remain highly provisional because hsCRP was not available and, more importantly, outcome validation was not possible in the present study.

Conclusion

In this real-world longitudinal PCI cohort, biomarker remodeling was characterized by durable reductions in LDL-C, non-HDL-C, ApoB, ApoB/ApoA1 ratio, and CRP, but incomplete harmonization between lipid and inflammatory biomarker profiles. Lp(a) remained largely resistant to routine care, and a subgroup showed persistent inflammatory biomarker elevation despite substantial cholesterol lowering. Because conventional CRP rather than the more sensitive hsCRP assay was available, and no clinical outcomes were assessed, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Prospective studies incorporating hsCRP, standardized follow-up, and adjudicated cardiovascular outcomes are needed before the clinical and translational significance of these biomarker patterns can be established.
