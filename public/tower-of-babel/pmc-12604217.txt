Atypical presentation of Lyme disease in a returning traveler: a case report
Authors: Andrea S. Salcedo, Xosse Carreras, Joe Saavedra, Sandy Saldaña, Nelson Diaz, Jose A. Gonzales-Zamora, Jorge Alave
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lyme disease, caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and transmitted by Ixodes ticks, is rare in South America and is often misdiagnosed in returning travelers. We report the case of a 19-year-old Peruvian male who presented with pruritic erythematous maculopapular lesions and abdominal pain after travel to Maryland, United State of America. His atypical cutaneous findings, including a crusted lesion with ill-defined borders and a maculopapular rash, were initially misinterpreted as bacterial cellulitis, resulting in multiple unsuccessful courses of antibiotics. Further evaluation, guided by his travel history, led to the diagnosis of Lyme disease through serological testing and histopathology. Treatment with doxycycline achieved complete resolution. This case illustrates the diagnostic challenges posed by atypical presentations of Lyme disease in nonendemic regions. Greater awareness among clinicians in South America is essential to recognize imported cases, facilitate early diagnosis, and prevent complications associated with delayed treatment.

Introduction

Lyme disease is a zoonotic infection transmitted by tick bites [1]. The main vectors include Ixodes scapularis (deer tick) in the northeastern and midwestern United States (US), where there is a high incidence of this disease [2]. Borrelia burgdorferi, a microaerophilic spirochete, is the main infectious agent in reported cases in North America [3, 4]. The geographical distribution of the disease has expanded significantly in almost all Latin American countries [5]. In a recent review, the presence of B. burgdorferi in Peru was identified in several studies since 1991, with 36 cases reported with positive serology, of which only two cases had clinical manifestations [3]. However, suspicion and diagnostic confirmation in South America is challenging even with the large influx of tourists and immigrants from endemic areas [6].

It is a multisystem infectious disease with a wide range of clinical presentations which makes the diagnosis complicated. It typically begins with a pathognomonic skin lesion, known as erythema migrans, and flu-like symptoms [7]. If left untreated, it can be followed for weeks to months later by cardiac (i.e., carditis, atrioventricular block, etc.), neurological (i.e., seizures, ataxia, peripheral neuropathies, etc.), and joint disease (especially intermittent inflammatory arthritis of one or more large joints) [8]. Some prominent skin findings, in addition to erythema migrans, include borrelial lymphocytoma and acrodermatitis chronica atrophicans (ACA) [9]. The former is described as an erythematous annular rash with a centrifugal extension or bull’s-eye appearance [10]. The other two occur exclusively in Europe [11]. Borrelial lymphocytoma is described as a solitary bluish-red nodule or plaque located on the ear lobe in children or the breast in adults [11]. The ACA is a late manifestation that, if left untreated, causes atrophic changes in the distal extremities [11]. All of this complicate the early detection of the disease.

Herein, we present a case of Lyme disease with atypical skin manifestations in a Peruvian patient from Maryland, United State of America (USA).

Case description

A 19-year-old Peruvian male resident of Maryland, USA, with no prior medical history, was admitted to the emergency room with a 28-day history of right flank abdominal pain, accompanied by pruritic, erythematous maculopapular lesions on the inner region of the left leg. The lesions stayed the same size, but there was increasing pain on palpation.

He denied fever, dyspnea, fatigue, headache, weight loss or joint pain. He lived in Maryland between February and July in a wooded area but did not recall any insect or tick bites. Sexual history was unremarkable. He arrived at Lima, Peru, from Maryland, USA, 3 weeks before admission. He was evaluated by a dermatologist and treated with oral clindamycin and topical mupirocin for 14 days for presumptive cellulitis. Owing to a lack of improvement, he saw a second dermatologist who started him on oral cephalexin and continued with clindamycin for 3 days.

On physical examination, vital signs were within the normal range. He was oriented to time, place, and person. Cardiac and neurological examinations were within normal limits. In the right lateral region of the abdomen, there was a crusty lesion of approximately 2 cm × 1 cm with an erythematous indurated area of undefined borders and slight tenderness on palpation (Fig. 1A). On the anterior aspect of the left leg, there was a tender erythematous lesion with undefined borders (Fig. 1B).

None of the lesions had edema or eschars. Direct examination for mites in the lesion of the left leg was negative. ECG was performed which revealed normal sinus rhythm. Laboratory studies revealed high C-reactive protein (113.75 mg/L) and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (45 mm/h). The complete blood count, peripheral blood smear, renal and liver profile, electrolytes, creatine kinase, urine exam, and lactate dehydrogenase were within normal limits. Serial parasitological examination of feces and urine toxicology were negative. At this point, blood and swab cultures were performed and empiric treatment was initiated with vancomycin due to suspicion of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) cellulitis. A biopsy of the lesions was performed. Blood and swap cultures came back negative. Owing to the patient’s place of origin, Lyme disease, other types of borreliosis and rickettsiosis were also suspected; thus, serological studies were ordered. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) testing for Borrelia burgdorferi demonstrated elevated IgM (45.1 U/mL; normal < 1.2 U/mL) with a normal IgG level (4.2 U/mL; normal < 25 U/mL). Additional tests were negative, including tube agglutination tests for Brucella antibodies (2-mercaptoethanol and Rose Bengal), HIV ELISA, hepatitis B markers (HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc IgM and IgG), serologic assays for Rickettsia conorii, Rickettsia typhi, Rickettsia prowazekii, and Rickettsia rickettsii (IgM and IgG by indirect immunofluorescence assay), cytomegalovirus (CMV) IgM, Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) IgM, and Leptospira serology. Other tests including antinuclear antibodies, rheumatoid factor and Anti-Cyclic Citrullinated Peptide antibody were negative. A confirmatory Western blot test on serum was performed for suspected atypical Lyme disease. The sample was sent to L’Hospitalet de Llobregat in Barcelona, Spain to be processed. The result was negative for IgG bands but positive for IgM antibodies against Borrelia spp. proteins, with bands corresponding to the 23 kDa (OspC, B. garinii) and 41 kDa (p41; weak intensity) proteins detected by immunoblotting. The biopsy revealed moderate chronic inflammatory infiltration consisting of lymphocytes and a few plasma cells in the deep dermis (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The diagnosis of Lyme disease is based on classic clinical findings, serological tests and epidemiological context. In the USA, the Northeastern, Mid-Atlantic and Northcentral regions –including Maryland- are endemic for Lyme disease, particularly during late spring and summer (May-August) [2, 12]. The latest estimation study concluded that around 476,000 persons were diagnosed with Lyme disease annually during 2010–2018 period [1].

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends a two-tiered diagnostic approach: initial screening with an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for IgM and IgG antibodies, followed by confirmatory Western blot testing [13]. According to CDC criteria, a positive IgM immunoblot requires at least two of the following three bands: 23, 39, and 41 kDa. For IgG, five or more of ten specific bands must be detected (18, 23, 28, 30, 39, 41, 45, 58, 66, and 93 kDa) [13]. As IgG seroconversion typically occurs after one to two months of untreated infection [14], the absence of IgG in our patient, who presented after 28 days of symptoms, was expected.

Nonetheless, this prompted the need to rule out other conditions that may yield false-positive Lyme ELISA results. We therefore conducted an extensive serological and rheumatological workup for differential diagnoses, including Rocky Mountain spotted fever, other spirochetal infections such as leptospirosis and syphilis, cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus—all of which were negative [14].

In our patient, the IgM Western blot was positive for two bands: 23 kDa (OspC) and 41 kDa. These are among the most detected IgM bands in patients with Lyme disease [15]. The 23 kDa band was identified as OspC for B. garinii, which likely reflects cross-reactivity between Borrelia species, as OspC proteins share over 70% sequence similarity across strains [15]. Notably, the test was performed in Spain, where B. garinii is prevalent. IgM testing is recommended only during the first 30 days of illness, with a sensitivity of approximately 57.7% and a specificity of 98.0% [13].

Despite decades of clinical familiarity with two-tiered testing, misdiagnosis remains common, often due to misinterpretation of serologic results [16]. In our case, the patient’s recent travel to Maryland during peak transmission season, the presence of two diagnostic IgM bands, and the exclusion of other potential causes supported a diagnosis of Lyme disease. When clinical suspicion is high, empirical treatment with doxycycline is recommended [9].

Importantly, serologic tests cannot reliably differentiate between active and past infection. This distinction typically requires documentation of seroreversion or a ≥ 4-fold decline in IgG titers through serial testing over time [14]. In our case, the expected absence of an IgG response precluded such longitudinal assessment.

Atypical cutaneous manifestations of Lyme disease include vesicular eruptions [17, 18], hyperpygmented skin lesiones [19, 20], erythematous papules [9], lymphangitic streaks [21], dematomyositis-like lesions [22] and cellulitis-like lesions [9]. In such presentations, serological testing and skin biopsy are often necessary to establish the diagnosis [9, 21]. Histopathological examination commonly reveals a perivascular dermal lymphohistiocytic infiltrate with occasional plasma cells [23]. Spirochetes can be detected in approximately 50% of erythema migrans lesions via Warthin-Starry staining [23]. As in our case, we found both the typical histopathological description and the spirochetes in Warthin-Starry staining, aiding in the final diagnosis. Despite increasing recognition of Lyme disease, atypical presentations may contribute to diagnostic delays, particularly in non-endemic settings. Early identification of cutaneous features remains critical for prompt diagnosis and treatment. In this case, the use of ancillary serological studies facilitated the diagnosis, highlighting the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients with a compatible clinical and epidemiological context. Syphilis was initially considered; however, serological testing was negative, and no risk factors for Treponema pallidum infection were identified.

Notably, our patient was evaluated by two different dermatologists who diagnosed and treated him with oral antibiotics for cellulitis. He also received vancomycin during hospitalization due to suspicion of MRSA infection. However, the patient improved only after treatment with doxycycline for Lyme disease. Interestingly, even in endemic countries, Lyme misdiagnosis is very common [16]. In more recent studies in the USA and United Kingdom, an accurate Lyme diagnosis was made in only 27.8% and 23.0% of the cases, respectively [16]. After the introduction of the two-tier approach, improved diagnostic accuracy was expected; nonetheless, current findings have suggested the opposite, with results falling within a range of 9.6% − 15.0% in endemic countries [16]. This diagnostic challenge is further compounded by the distinctive epidemiological and clinical complexities encountered when evaluating travelers returning from endemic regions [24].

The diagnostic challenge of Lyme disease, specifically in travelers, has been described previously [24]. Especially in South America, the low prevalence of disease makes the threshold for suspicion even higher. One case of classical erythema migrans misdiagnosed as cellulitis was reported in a Colombian hospital [25]. The increase in international travel has led to the spread of infectious diseases not commonly seen in nonendemic areas [26]. This may confuse healthcare professionals who are not familiar with the new clinical and epidemiological pictures. In one study, observing travelers who attended a GeoSentinel clinic from 1997−2016, found that the most common diagnoses were cosmopolitan infections, followed by arthropod bites with Lyme disease being the most diagnosed [6]. Although it is the most frequently reported arthropod-borne disease among non-USA resident travelers [6], it can be especially challenging to diagnose in nonendemic areas such as Peru.

Conclusion

Lyme disease should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with dermatologic lesions and a history of travel to endemic regions. Eliciting a detailed travel history is essential, as it may provide critical diagnostic clues, particularly in patients with atypical presentations. Diagnostic challenges persist even in endemic areas, and the increasing incidence of Lyme disease among travelers, combined with the potential for atypical clinical manifestations, as illustrated by this case, highlights the need for greater awareness in nonendemic countries such as Peru. Early recognition is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis, initiate timely treatment, and reduce the risk of complications.
