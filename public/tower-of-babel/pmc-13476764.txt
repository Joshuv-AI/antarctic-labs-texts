The digestive characteristics of avocado oil and the bioaccessibility of its nutrients
Authors: Xinyue Lin, Zongjun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe digestion process of edible oil involves the disintegration of the physical structure of lipids and the release of chemical components.This study, based on the standard INFOGEST in vitro digestion model, systematically analyzed the particle size, ζ potential, free fatty acid (FFA) release, micromorphology and the bioaccessibility of nutrients of avocado oil (AO). The digestion of AO is stable and FFA release rate of AO is 69.92 ± 1.78%. And the bioaccessibility of unsaturated fatty acids is 43.54 ± 1.24 g/100 g, that of saturated fatty acids is 8.62 ± 0.30 g/100 g, and that of total sterols is 267.27 ± 22.82 μg/g, and that of squalene is 50.35 ± 1.96 μg/g. This study explored the digestive characteristics of AO and the bioaccessibility of its nutritional components from a digestive perspective, providing a scientific basis for the actual nutritional and health effects of AO.Keywords: Avocado oil, Digestion, Bioaccessibility

Introduction

As global edible oil consumption continues to rise, the cultivation of traditional oil crops is constrained by the limited availability of arable land. According to the OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook 2026–2035 report, it is expected that global demand for food-grade vegetable oil will continue to grow strongly, and the prices of vegetable oil are projected to remain relatively strong due to the sustained growth in demand. This will require the entry of new and sustainable oil crops into the market. However, oilseed crops already occupy roughly 35% of the planet's arable land (FAO, 2026), rendering any further expansion unsustainable. The woody oils can alleviate the global shortage of edible oil, enhance energy security and promote sustainable agricultural development, because they do not require the occupation of arable land, can save water, and are perennial plants.Furthermore, compared to the high omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in seed oils, the high monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) in woody oils offer greater advantages. This study focuses on avocado oil (AO). AO is an oil extracted from avocado (Persea americana Miller) (Yang et al., 2024), and it is recommended as a healthy fat source in the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030. Its unique nutritional properties and functions are worthy of in-depth exploration.

AO is rich in various bioactive substances such as fatty acids, phytosterols, and squalene. However, previous studies on AO mainly focused on its chemical composition and nutrient content. The health effects of AO should not be directly determined by its own nutrients, but rather depend on its digestive characteristics and the bioaccessibility of nutrients after digestion and degradation in the digestive system (Hernández-Bautista, Gutiérrez, Tovar, & Bello-Pérez, 2025). The digestion and absorption of edible oil is a complex physiological process that occurs in stages.The digestion process of oil begins with limited hydrolysis in the mouth. It is then emulsified into larger droplets by the highly acidic environment and peristalsis of the stomach. Subsequently, it is further emulsified in the small intestine through bile acids, and is completely hydrolyzed into glycerol and fatty acids under the catalysis of pancreatic lipase. Finally, it is absorbed by the intestinal villi and enters the bloodstream to provide energy or be stored. The remaining undigested components further affect GMs, thereby influencing metabolic products and human health (Lin, Wu, & Li, 2025). There are significant differences in the pH environment, digestive enzyme composition and their action intensity of the oral cavity, stomach and small intestine of the human body. The in vitro simulated digestion model, due to its simple operation, controllable conditions and good repeatability, has become a core tool for studying digestive characteristics. Among them, the INFOGEST protocol can precisely simulate the physiological environment of each stage of the human digestive tract, providing a reliable platform for exploring the release patterns of nutritional components (Awlqadr et al., 2026; Brodkorb et al., 2019).

At present, there are few studies on the in vitro digestion of lipids, and insufficient attention has been paid to the overall digestion characteristics of oils. To precisely and clearly understand the in vitro digestion characteristics of AO and the bioaccessibility of its nutrients, this study introduced olive oil (OO), a classic healthy lipid whose in vitro digestion features have been widely characterized, as a reference. This study aims to establish a homogeneous baseline, thereby specifically highlighting the differentiated digestion characteristics and bioaccessibility of AO due to its unique nutrients.This provides a scientific basis for the nutritional assessment of AO and the dietary nutrition guidance.

Material and method

Materials

AO and OO are sourced from the same brand and the same pressing process through market procurement. Simulated saliva fluid, simulated gastric fluid and simulated intestinal fluid were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Salivary amylase, pepsin, trypsin, pancreatic lipase, anhydrous sodium bisulfate, Glycerol Tridodecanoate standard sample, squalene standard sample, β-sitosterol standard sample and Nile Red were purchased from Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Potassium hydroxide, methanol and n-hexane were purchased from Sinopharm Co., Ltd. 37 kinds of fatty acid methyl esters mixed standard was purchased from Tanmo Quality Inspection Technology Co., Ltd.

Method

In vitro simulated digestion

The in vitro digestion simulated model adopts the INFOGEST protocol (Brodkorb et al., 2019) and is conducted in the SDS-III Bipedal Animal Biological Digestion System (Hunan Zhongben Intelligent Technology Development Co., Ltd., China). Automatic enzyme addition and automatic cleaning reduce the system errors caused by manual operations.

Oral phase

Mix 5 ml of the sample with 1:1 ratio of simulated saliva fluid (containing 75 U/mL salivary amylase) in the MD44MM dialysis bag and incubate at 37 °C and pH 7.0 for 2 min. And it is recorded as the SSF stage.

Gastric phase

The products of the SSF stage were diluted 1:1 with simulated gastric fluid (with 2000 U/mL pepsin). Then, the dialysis bag was continuously rinsed with HCl solution for 2 h at 37 °C, maintaining the pH at 3. And it is recorded as the SGF stage.

Small intestinal phase

The products of the SGF stage were diluted 1:1 with simulated intestinal fluid, and bile salts and pancreatic enzymes (with trypsin at 100 U/mL and pancreatic lipase at 2000 U/mL) were added in the dialysis bag. Then, the dialysis bag was continuously rinsed with NaOH solution for 2 h, maintaining the pH at 7. And it is recorded as the SIF stage.

Particle size

Following the method of Li et al. (2024). After mixing the digestive juices from each stage, the particle size of the oil in the digestive juices was measured using a Malvern Master Size 3000 laser particle size meter (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK).

ζ potential

Following the method of Li et al. (2024). After mixing the digestive fluids from each digestion period, they were diluted with ultrapure water. The ζ potential values of the prepared digestive fluids were measured using the ZETASIZER NANO ZS nanoparticle sizer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK).

FFA released

The detection of FFA release volume is carried out using the pH-stat method. Simulated intestinal phase digestive fluid is collected and titrated with 1 M or 0.1 M NaOH every five minutes to maintain the pH value of the digestive fluid at 7.0, thereby measuring the release volume of FFA. The amount of free fatty acids generated is calculated based on the consumption of the required alkaline solution. Micromorphology

Following the method of Li et al. (2024), the oil phase in the intestinal digestive fluid was stained with Nile Red staining solution. The observation was conducted using the Carl Zeiss LSM780 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Oberkochen, Germany), and the obtained images were processed using the ZEN 2012 software.

Oil extraction

Following the method of Xiang et al. and making some improvements (Xiang et al., 2023). In short, centrifuge the product of the gastric phase at a speed of 8000 rpm for the three-stage digestive fluid for 10 min, and then discard the precipitate. Then, add the entire supernatant and the oil layer to the chloroform-methanol solution, vortex for 30 s, and centrifuge at 6000 r/min for 10 min. Collect the bottom layer. Repeat this process three times. Dry the mixed bottom layer under nitrogen and store the obtained lipids at −80 °C for further analysis.

Fatty acid composition

The determination of fatty acids is carried out by gas chromatography, equipped with a flame ionization detector (GC-FID) (National Standard of the People's Republic). In brief, 2.0 mL of Glycerol Tridodecanoate solution was added to the oil as the internal standard solution. Then, 4 mL of n-octane was added to dissolve the sample. After that, 200 μL of KOH-MeOH solution was added, and the mixture was shaken for 30s before being left to stand until clear. 1 g of sodium bisulfate was added to neutralize the potassium hydroxide. Then, the upper layer solution was filtered through a 0.2 μm filter membrane and sent to the instrument for analysis. The GC conditions are as follows: Chromatographic column: CD-2560 capillary gas chromatographic column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm). Injection port temperature: 100 °C. Injection volume: 1 μL. Programmed temperature rise: Initial temperature 100 °C, held for 5 min, then raised at a rate of 40 °C/min to 140 °C, held for 5 min, then raised at a rate of 4 °C/min to 240 °C, held for 25 min. FID conditions: Heater temperature 260 °C, air flow rate 300 ml/min, hydrogen gas flow rate 30 ml/min.

Total sterols

Total sterol determination follows the method of using 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1, 2, 4 and 8 mg of β-sitosterol as standard curves. In brief, the oil was added to the KOH solution and refluxed through a reflux condenser for 1 h. Then it was washed with butyl alcohol and the saponification solution was obtained using a separation funnel. The samples were washed with ultrapure water and KOH solution, and then dried using a nitrogen blow dryer to obtain the samples. In the samples, ethyl alcohol and Phosphorus and iron reagent (PS-FE reagent) were added, and the reaction was carried out at room temperature for 15 min, and its absorbance was detected at a wavelength of 442 nm.

Squalene

The determination of squalene is carried out by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) (Agricultural Industry Standard of the People's Republic of China). In brief, the oil is heated and ultrasonicated with a potassium hydroxide-ethanol solution, then cooled to room temperature. After that, it is extracted with ultrapure water and n-hexane and vortexed to mix. Centrifuge at 4500 r/min for 3 min. Take the supernatant, repeat this extraction operation twice with n-hexane, and combine the three supernatants. Vortex the supernatant and dry the solvent using a nitrogen blow dryer and re-dissolve it with n-hexane at 4500 r/min for 3 min. Filter through a 0.22 μm organic phase membrane and send it to GC–MS. The GC conditions are: chromatographic column: HP-5MS gas chromatographic column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Injection port temperature 110 °C, injection volume 1 μL. Programmed temperature rise: initial temperature 110 °C, rising at a rate of 30 °C/min to 310 °C, maintaining for 6 min. MS conditions: voltage 70 eV, ion source temperature 230 °C, electron ionization (EI) mode.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were conducted on three samples. One-way ANOVA analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0, and the results were recorded as mean ± standard deviation. The statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 10 was used to draw the figures.

Results and discussion

Particle size and ζ potential

The size of fat droplets in different oil samples at different digestion stages can clarify the rate of lipid digestion. During the digestion process of lipid, the smaller the particle size, the larger the exposed surface area per unit mass of the droplets, the more adsorption sites for digestive enzymes available, and the easier they are to hydrolyze. The changes in particle size are shown in Fig. 1A. In the SGF stage, the particle sizes of AO and OO significantly increased. This is because the acidic environment with a pH of 3 (mainly due to gastric acid) leads to a reduction in the steric hindrance effect of negative charges, causing the originally small and stable lipid droplets to coalesce, thereby increasing the particle size. Moreover, with the action of gastric lipase, the lipid itself may be partially hydrolyzed or combine with hydrolysis products, and the adsorption of gastric lipase on the surface of lipid droplets may also promote bridging flocculation between lipid droplets, further accelerating the polymerization process (Verkempinck et al., 2022). As lipids enter the small intestine, the particle sizes of AO significantly decrease. This is because the alkaline intestinal environment rapidly neutralizes gastric acid, preventing the phenomenon of lipid droplets aggregating and growing larger. At the same time, Bile Salts (BSs), as powerful surfactants, quickly adsorb onto the surface of lipid droplets, reducing the interfacial tension between oil and water, disrupting the original aggregation structure of lipid droplets, and causing the formation of negatively charged micelle layers on the surface of lipid droplets, breaking down the originally large lipid droplets into smaller microemulsified particles, thereby reducing the particle size (Infantes-Garcia, Verkempinck, Hendrickx, & Grauwet, 2021). In the early stage of the SIF process, the lipid droplets of AO particle size rapidly decreased. This is because pancreatic lipase, with the assistance of BSs, is more likely to adsorb onto the surface of the lipid droplets. The FFA and monoacylglycerol (MAG) produced by its hydrolysis have surface activity themselves, further promoting the refinement process of the lipid droplets (Reis, Holmberg, Watzke, Leser, & Miller, 2009). After 30 min of entering the SIF stage, the rate of lipid droplet reduction for AO particles gradually slows down. This is because as the hydrolysis proceeds, a large amount of FFA, MAG, and cholesterol esters combine with BSs to form Mixed Micelles. These Mixed Micelles occupy some of the BSs, resulting in a relative decrease in the concentration of free BSs and a tendency for the emulsifying ability to reach saturation (Singh, Ye, & Horne, 2009). And in the later stage of digestion, the sites where Pancreatic Lipase adsorbs on the surface of lipid droplets tend to become saturated, and its action rate tends to reach a plateau. The rate of FFA production gradually stabilizes, and the rate of particle size reduction also decreases accordingly (Reis et al., 2009).

The ζ potential is a core indicator reflecting the surface charge state of lipid droplets. Its dynamic changes affect the stability of lipid droplets and further determine the digestion efficiency. The changes in particle size are shown in Fig. 1B. Compared with the SSF stage, the absolute value of ζ potential significantly decreases during the SGF stage. This is because the pH value of the stomach is 3. In this acidic environment, the ester group (-COO−) on the surface of the oil will be protonated in large quantities and transformed into a carboxyl group (-COOH), resulting in the neutralization of the negative charge on the originally negatively charged surface of the lipid dropletb (Liu, Wang, Wang, & Dai Xiaodong, 2020). The weakened electrostatic repulsion between the lipid droplets of AO was weakened, allowing the droplets to aggregate more easily, manifested as an increase in ζ potential, accompanied by an increase in particle size. This is consistent with the measured particle size. Moreover, the BSs themselves carry negative charges. When they adsorb on the surface of the lipid droplets, they will directly inject a large amount of negative charges onto the lipid droplet surface. The rapid accumulation of these negative charges will cause the measured ζ potential to shift towards the negative direction (Maldonado-Valderrama et al., 2008).

The changes in particle size and potential of AO and OO and their similarities. Consistent with this result is that the lipid droplets of AO and OO are more likely to aggregate in the gastric digestion and more likely to hydrolyze in the intestinal digestion. During the SGF stage, AO and OO increased the particle size and reduced the absolute value of ζ potential, thereby preventing premature release of fatty acids. Moreover, this coarse emulsion had a lower lipolytic rate in the stomach compared to the fine emulsion. This indicates that the rate of fat breakdown in the stomach slows down, thereby delaying the subsequent digestion and absorption processes (Armand et al., 1999). During the SIF stage, by reducing particle size and increasing the absolute value of ζ potential, efficient emulsification and absorption can be achieved.Therefore, the results of particle size and ζ potential analysis in this study are consistent.

FFA release and micromorphology

Since the accumulation of free fatty acids is the result of lipase hydrolysis of triacylglycerol, the detection of FFA release by the pH-stat method allows for real-time observation of the hydrolysis degree of lipids in the small intestine. The changes in NaOH consumption and FFA release are shown in Fig. 1C and Fig. 1D. Consistent with previous studies, the NaOH consumption in the digestion fluid titrated within the first 30 min of the small intestine stage increased rapidly, indicating that during this stage, the lipase reaction rapidly converted triacylglycerol into monoacylglycerol, thereby generating FFA (Reis et al., 2009). During the subsequent digestion period, the NaOH consumption increased slowly, suggesting that the digestion of fat has nearly been completed. In this study, the FFA release rate of AO and OO were 69.92 ± 1.78% and 70.01 ± 2.03%. In the first 30 min before the SIF stage, the release of FFA showed a nearly linear rapid increase trend, as observed in Cheng's research. During this stage, it was limited by the maximum catalytic capacity of the enzymes, and there were almost no significant inhibitory factors (Cheng, 2022). At this stage, although a large amount of FFA was rapidly produced, its concentration remained relatively low, and its impact on enzyme activity and emulsification system was limited. Previous studies have also shown that in different types of oils, the duration of the rapid release phase in the intestine is approximately 20–28 min (Cheng, 2022). After 30 min in the SIF stage, as FFA continues to be released, its concentration increases and begins to exert an inhibitory effect on Pancreatic Lipase. Previous studies have shown that approximately 30 min after digestion in the small intestine, the accumulation of FFA leads to a decrease in enzyme activity, restricting the enzyme's action on the lipid droplet surface, resulting in a significant slowdown in the release rate (Wang, Gao, Yang, & Nishinari, 2021). As time goes by, the generated FFA combines with bile salts to form Mixed Micelles. The physical and chemical properties of the lipid droplet surface change, resulting in a reduction in the contact area between enzymes and substrates. Additionally, the parts of the lipid droplet that are easily hydrolyzed are depleted, leaving behind a structure that is more difficult for enzymes to recognize. This further slows down the release rate, which can prevent excessive accumulation of fat droplets in the body and thereby improve the overall lipid metabolism status (Xu et al., 2021).

Oils are mainly digested and absorbed in the small intestine. To more intuitively study the presentation form and dynamics of lipid droplets, CLSM was used to observe the microscopic structural changes of AO and OO in the intestinal digestion process (Fig. 2.). At 10 min after entering the intestinal digestion, the lipid droplets were in a flocculent and aggregated state. At this stage, the original emulsified state is maintained. The surface of the lipid droplets has not yet been covered by a thick layer of product. Nile Red can be well absorbed by the oil phase and emit fluorescence, resulting in a relatively smooth surface structure of the lipid droplets being displayed in the CLSM images. At the 30 min mark of the SIF process, the integrity of the lipid droplets is compromised, and the internal oil phase begins to seep out. Small lipid droplets start to spread around the large ones. At the 60 min mark of the SIF process, the surface of the lipid droplets is further gradually damaged. Digestive enzymes can penetrate into the interior of the lipid droplets. Mixed micelles will remove the fatty acids and glycerol ester residues on the surface of the lipid droplets, causing the volume of the lipid droplets to shrink and their morphology to become fragmented. At the 90 min and 120 min marks of the SIF process, the diameters of the lipid droplets gradually decrease, and some lipid droplets even completely disappear, leaving only sparse fine particles and scattered remnants.

Microstructure of avocado oil and olive oil during intestinal digestion (Scale bar:100 μm).

In summary, 30 min before the digestion in the small intestine, the rate of FFA release was extremely fast, and the amount released exceeded half of the total release. At the same time, the CLSM images showed that the integrity of the lipid droplets was disrupted, indicating that AO and OO are easily hydrolyzed and has a high bioaccessibility. Previous studies have also shown that in the emulsions prepared from OO, even though their digestion is slower and the FFA release rate is lower, they can significantly improve the bioaccessibility of active substances (Li et al., 2024). A fast digestion rate does not necessarily mean good absorption. Furthermore, releasing a large amount of FFA in a short period of time may actually increase the burden on the digestive system.This indicates that during the in vitro digestion process, AO exhibits similar stability to OO, does not increase the burden on the digestive system, and may possess the potential to serve as a delivery system like OO.

Fatty acid

The fatty acids in oils constitute the core components of the oils, and their structure and proportion determine the physical properties and nutritional and health associations of the oils. AO and OO are mainly composed of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs), with an average of 73.46 ± 0.84 g/100 g and 0.69.04 ± 0.36 g/100 g. The content of UFAs in AO was significantly higher than that in OO (P < 0.05). The main UFAs in the AO and OO are mainly MUFAs (Marín-Obispo et al., 2025). Among them, the content of oleic acid is the highest. The values for AO and OO are 56.53 ± 0.64 g/100 g and 57.36 ± 0.41 g/100 g (Table 1.). In this study, after in vitro digestion, fatty acids were mainly digested in the small intestine, and the bioaccessibility of UFAs in AO was 59.26 ± 1.21% (43.54 ± 1.24 g/100 g) and that of UFAs in OO was 53.38 ± 1.07% (36.85 ± 0.85 g/100 g) (Fig. 3A. and Fig. 4A.). The bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of UFAs in AO were significantly higher than those in OO (P < 0.05). The saturated fatty acids (SFAs) content of AO and OO are 13.96 ± 0.32 g/100 g and 14.19 ± 0.16 g/100 g, with palmitic acid accounting for the majority (12.52 ± 0.28 g/100 g and 12.07 ± 0.05 g/100 g) ((Table 1.). After in vitro digestion, the bioaccessibility of SFAs in AO was 61.72 ± 0.95% (8.62 ± 0.30 g/100 g) and that of SFAs in OO was 71.18 ± 1.60% (10.10 ± 0.34 g/100 g) (Fig. 4A. and Fig. 4B.). The bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of SFAs in AO were significantly lower than those in OO (P < 0.05). The bioaccessibility of SFAs is higher than that of UFAs. This is because the molecules of SFAs are in a straight chain form, which has stronger hydrophilicity and rigidity, and is thus more easily recognized and hydrolyzed by enzymes. In contrast, UFAs with branched structures have their ester bonds difficultly accessible to enzymes due to their curved structure, resulting in low hydrolysis efficiency. However, since AO contains a higher amount of UFAs and a lower amount of SFAs, its actual usable quantity of UFAs is higher than that of SFAs. The reason why the bioaccessibility of SFAs was not significantly higher than that of UFAs in this study might be due to the high oleic acid content in AO.

Previous studies have shown that in a 70:30 wt% blend of high-oleic sunflower and canola stearin (NIE), the bioaccessibility of oleic acid is higher than that of stearic acid (Thilakarathna et al., 2016). Oleic acid can reduce the chance of SFAs entering the aqueous phase by altering the physical structure of lipid droplets, thereby lowering their release rate and bioaccessibility (Thilakarathna et al., 2016). In this study, the content of UFAs in AO, as well as the bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of UFAs, were significantly higher than those in OO. And, the bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of SFAs in AO were significantly lower than those in OO.

Total sterols

Sterol is a type of natural organic compound widely present in both animals and plants, and it is a derivative of steroid compounds. The sterols present in vegetable oils are mainly phytosterols, and they exist either in free form or esterified with fatty acids (Cheng, 2022). From a functional perspective, Total sterols in vegetable oils are the key factor determining their ability to compete with cholesterol for absorption. The health effects of sterols depend on their total concentration rather than the contribution of a single variety (Faubel, Barbera, & Garcia-Llatas, 2024). In the INFOGEST model, determining the bioaccessibility of total sterols can more accurately reflect the total amount of sterols present in the mixed micelles that enter the small intestine, thereby providing a more realistic picture of the absorption potential.

AO is rich in sterols, with its content being twice that of olive oil (Lin & Li, 2024). In this study, the total sterol content of AO and OO were 3474.31 ± 17.25 μg/g and 1394.50 ± 22.59 μg/g (Fig. 3C.).The total sterol content of AO was significantly higher than that of OO (P < 0.05). After in vitro digestion, the bioaccessibility of total sterols in AO was 7.69 ± 0.63% (267.27 ± 22.82 μg/g) and that of total sterols in OO was 7.82 ± 1.34% (109.17 ± 19.83 μg/g) (Fig. 4C.). The bioaccessible amount of total sterol in AO were significantly higher than those in OO (P < 0.05).The solubility of sterols in aqueous phase is extremely low. This is because the molecular structure of sterols determines their Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance (HLB) value, which is relatively low, making them more hydrophobic. In the in vitro digestion model, without sufficient lipid carriers, they cannot be hydrolyzed by pancreatic lipase into more soluble esterified forms, nor can they enter the aqueous phase through the emulsifying action of bile salts. When sterols are added to lipid or emulsifier matrices, the proportion of sterols entering mixed micelles increases, and their bioaccessibility improves. SFAs are more capable of promoting the bioaccessibility of sterols compared to UFAs (Moran-Valero, Martin, Torrelo, Reglero, & Torres, 2012). Oleic acid can effectively enhance the efficiency of microemulsion formation, increase the solubility and absorption efficiency of SFAs, thereby promoting the improvement of sterol's bioaccessibility (Wang, Chen, Yang, Xiao, & Lai, 2024). Previous studies have shown that in the digestive conditions of the elderly, the rate of lipolysis decreases. At the same time, it effectively reduces the generation and release of sterol oxidation products while maintaining the bioaccessibility of sterols (Virginia et al., 2024). Although the FFA release rate of AO is not significantly lower than that of OO, the reduction in the FFA release rate of AO is also beneficial to the bioaccessibility of sterols.

In this study, the total sterol content and bioaccessible amount of AO was significantly higher than that of OO. Consuming AO will result in a higher total sterol content. Increasing the bioavailable amount of sterols (especially phytosterols) can competitively displace cholesterol through micelles, exerting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, and promoting healthy lipid metabolism.

Squalene

Squalene, as a naturally occurring polyunsaturated triterpenoid compound, plays a significant role in antioxidant properties, immune regulation, skin health maintenance, cardiovascular protection, and potential anti-tumor effects (Ahmed & Mhammad, 2024). Squalene is widely present in deep-sea organisms (such as shark liver oil) and vegetable oils (such as AO and OO). Due to the scarcity of shark resources and environmental pressure, OO and certain seed oils have become the main plant sources. The health benefits of squalene largely depend on whether it can be released in the digestive tract and form absorbable micelles. This study quantified its bioaccessibility to assess the actual nutritional content.In this study, The squalene content of AO and OO are 104.46 ± 4.39 μg/g and 507.28 ± 9.60 μg/g (Fig. 3D.). The amount of squalene in AO were significantly lower than those in OO (P < 0.05). During digestion, squalene is mainly released at the SIF stage. After digestion, the bioaccessibility of squalene in AO was 48.21 ± 0.46% (50.35 ± 1.96 μg/g) and that of squalene in OO was 51.04 ± 0.86% (258.88 ± 2.58 μg/g) (Fig. 4D.). The bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of squalene in AO were significantly lower than those in OO.Previous studies have shown that oleic acid can increase the apparent absorption rate of squalene (Suzki, 1968).The high oleic acid content of AO and OO may play a role in promoting the bioaccessibility of its natural squalene.

In this study, the content of squalene in AO, as well as the bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of squalene, were significantly lower than those in OO. Increasing the bioavailable amount of squalene can exert antioxidant effect. However, squalene is also prone to oxidation, and its effects in the body still need to be further verified.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this article studies and summarizes the digestive characteristics of AO and the biological utilization rate of its nutritional components during the in vitro digestion process (Fig. 5.). The particle size and ζ potential of AO increased in the stomach, preventing premature release of fatty acids. In the small intestine, the particle size and ζ potential decreased, enabling efficient emulsification and absorption. The FFA release rate of AO was 69.92 ± 1.78%. Before 30 min of intestinal digestion, the FFA release rate was extremely fast, and the oil phase began to seep out. Its digestive characteristics are similar to those of OO and it has digestive stability. After in vitro digestion, the bioaccessibility of UFAs was 43.54 ± 1.24 g/100 g, that of SFAs was 8.62 ± 0.30 g/100 g, and the bioaccessible amount of total sterols was 267.27 ± 22.82 μg/g, and that of squalene was 50.35 ± 1.96 μg/g. In this study, the content, bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of UFAs in AO were significantly higher than those in OO. The total sterol content and bioaccessible amount of AO were also significantly higher than those of OO. However, the bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of SFA in AO were significantly lower than those in OO. The content, bioaccessibility and bioaccessible amount of squalene in AO were significantly lower than those in OO. Humans can choose the type of oil according to their own needs. This study revealed the digestion characteristics of AO from the perspective of digestion features and bioaccessibility of nutrients. The bioaccessibility of nutrients in AO was quantified, providing a scientific basis for the true nutrition and health effects of AO.

Through the particle size, ζ-potential and microstructure changes during the in vitro digestion process of AO, its stable digestion characteristics were discovered. The actual bioaccessible amount of nutrients in AO was quantified by the bioaccessible amount of its nutrients.

This study also has certain limitations. The INFOGEST protocol only evaluates bioaccessibility, and the results cannot be directly extrapolated to in vivo bioavailability. Moreover, the exact molecular interactions at the oil-water interface of avocado oil still require experimental validation. In the future, by integrating in vitro simulations, in vivo experiments, and interfacial analysis, we aim to gain a more comprehensive understanding of lipid transformation during digestion, thereby bridging the gap between bioaccessibility and bioavailability.
