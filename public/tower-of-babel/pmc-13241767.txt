Intramuscular hemangioma of the thoracic wall: Diagnostic challenge mimicking breast pathology—A case report
Authors: Ma del Rosario García Calderón, Karina Pesce, Fernando Bañuelos Noriega, Lucia Alejandra Carrillo Calvo, Carolina Arroyo Zayas, Javier Cuevas Alvarado, Carolina Higuera Velázquez, Herman Isaac Tenorio Naranjo, Alberto Rubio Muñoz, Amador Ramírez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntramuscular hemangiomas are uncommon benign vascular tumors, accounting for less than 1% of all hemangiomas, and are particularly rare in the thoracic wall. When originating from the pectoralis major muscle, they may clinically and radiologically mimic primary breast pathology, creating a diagnostic challenge. We describe the case of a 54-year-old woman undergoing routine breast imaging who presented with breast asymmetry, with the left breast being larger than the right, and a suspected left breast lesion on initial evaluation. Multimodal imaging assessment, including mammography, ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging, and contrast-enhanced computed tomography, demonstrated a heterogeneous mass arising from the left pectoralis major muscle, clearly separated from the breast parenchyma, with imaging features consistent with an intramuscular hemangioma. Histopathological confirmation was not obtained due to patient refusal; however, the diagnosis was supported by characteristic findings on the multimodal imaging scan.Keywords: Intramuscular hemangioma, Pectoralis major muscle, Chest wall mass, Breast mimic, Breast imaging

Introduction

Hemangiomas are benign vascular tumors arising from endothelial cells and may involve the skin, subcutaneous tissues, or deeper soft tissues. Intramuscular hemangiomas account for less than 1% of all hemangiomas and most commonly occur within the skeletal muscles of the lower extremities, while involvement of the thoracic wall is distinctly uncommon. Owing to their deep location and slow growth, these lesions are frequently underrecognized and may remain undiagnosed for prolonged periods.

When intramuscular hemangiomas arise in the anterior chest wall, particularly from the pectoralis major muscle, they may clinically and radiologically mimic primary breast pathology. This presentation can lead to diagnostic confusion, as patients may exhibit apparent breast asymmetry or a palpable mass, prompting a breast-centered diagnostic approach. In such cases, conventional breast imaging may be insufficient to accurately localize the lesion or determine its true origin.

Cross-sectional imaging plays a critical role in the evaluation of these tumors. Magnetic resonance imaging is considered the modality of choice, as it allows precise delineation of lesion extent, characterization of vascular components, and differentiation from adjacent breast parenchyma and chest wall structures. Recognition of imaging features suggestive of an intramuscular vascular lesion is essential to avoid misdiagnosis, unnecessary biopsies, or inappropriate surgical management.

We present a case of an intramuscular hemangioma arising from the left pectoralis major muscle that initially simulated breast pathology, highlighting the diagnostic challenges associated with this rare entity and emphasizing the pivotal role of multimodality imaging in achieving an accurate diagnosis.

Case presentation

A 54-year-old woman with a history of hypercholesterolemia and fibromyalgia presented for evaluation of left breast asymmetry. She had a prior diagnosis of a cavernous hemangioma of the left breast, previously localized to the retroareolar region, without documented involvement of the chest wall musculature. The patient reported longstanding progressive breast asymmetry since childhood, which developed concurrently with the right breast but became more pronounced over time, resulting in persistent asymmetry. Associated symptoms included burning pain and venous engorgement, exacerbated during menstruation, pregnancy, and lactation.

Physical examination demonstrated increased volume of the left breast with prominent varicose periareolar venous pathways. No discrete palpable breast masses were identified on clinical examination (Fig. 1).

Mammography

Mammographic evaluation of the left breast demonstrated an ill-defined, irregular isodense area within the fibroglandular tissue of the retroareolar region, without associated microcalcifications or architectural distortion. The finding was nonspecific and did not allow confident differentiation between breast parenchymal and extramammary origin (Fig. 2).

Ultrasound

Targeted ultrasound revealed an irregular, heterogeneous mass measuring 17 × 16 × 17 mm, exhibiting internal vascularity on Doppler interrogation. The lesion was located deep to the breast parenchyma and clearly arising from the underlying left pectoralis major muscle, rather than arising from the breast tissue itself (Fig. 3).

The lesion is heterogeneous and predominantly hypoechoic, located deep to the breast parenchyma and arising from the left pectoralis major muscle. Power Doppler evaluation shows internal vascularity within the lesion, supporting its vascular nature.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an irregular intramuscular mass within the left pectoralis major muscle, showing heterogeneous signal intensity with predominantly isointense signal on T1-weighted images and hyperintense signal on T2-weighted images, along with heterogeneous enhancement on postcontrast sequences. The lesion measured 38 × 36 × 24 mm. Additionally, a separate rounded lesion was identified in the left retroareolar region, measuring 17 × 21 mm, consistent with the previously diagnosed breast hemangioma (Fig. 4).

Intramuscular hemangiomas are rare benign vascular tumors that may pose a diagnostic challenge when they arise in atypical locations such as the anterior chest wall. Lesions originating from the pectoralis major muscle are particularly prone to clinical and radiologic misinterpretation because they may present as apparent breast asymmetry or a suspected breast mass, leading to an initial breast-centered diagnostic approach. This distinction is clinically relevant, as the diagnostic pathways, management strategies, and prognostic implications differ substantially between breast parenchymal lesions and extramammary chest wall tumors.

MRI characteristics and diagnostic value

Magnetic resonance imaging is considered the imaging modality of choice for intramuscular hemangiomas because it allows accurate localization of the lesion within the muscular compartment, delineates its full extent, and improves tissue characterization. Typical MRI features include intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and heterogeneous high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, reflecting variable proportions of vascular channels, fibrous tissue, fat, and areas of thrombosis or hemorrhage. After contrast administration, enhancement is usually heterogeneous and progressive. These characteristics are helpful in distinguishing intramuscular hemangiomas from breast parenchymal lesions and from other soft-tissue tumors.

In the study by Wu et al, MRI demonstrated a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 90% for predicting intramuscular hemangiomas, highlighting its central role in preoperative assessment and in reducing misdiagnosis. Accurate identification of the compartment of origin is particularly important in cases mimicking breast pathology, as it can prevent unnecessary breast biopsies or inappropriate surgical planning.

Differential diagnosis

The imaging appearance of vascular chest wall masses may overlap with that of more aggressive entities, including soft-tissue sarcomas and vascular malignancies such as angiosarcoma. Consequently, careful evaluation of lesion margins, internal architecture, enhancement pattern, and relationship to adjacent structures is essential. Although imaging cannot replace histopathology in all cases, recognition of characteristic MRI features and extramammary localization can substantially narrow the differential diagnosis and guide appropriate management.

Surgical approach and recurrence rates

Clear definition of surgical terminology is essential when discussing outcomes. A wide excision refers to removal of the lesion with a cuff of surrounding uninvolved tissue, aiming to minimize residual microscopic disease. In contrast, a marginal excision involves removal of the lesion along its reactive boundary or pseudocapsule, without an additional margin of normal tissue, which may increase the risk of local recurrence in infiltrative tumors.

Reported recurrence rates for intramuscular hemangiomas vary widely in the literature, reflecting differences in tumor subtype, infiltrative behavior, and adequacy of surgical margins. Historical series have reported recurrence rates ranging from approximately 18% to over 60%, particularly following marginal excision. More recent data suggest that adequate preoperative imaging and appropriately planned resection can reduce recurrence risk. In the series reported by Wu et al, only one local recurrence (5%) was observed, occurring after marginal excision, underscoring the importance of accurate preoperative delineation of tumor extent to guide surgical planning.

Role of histopathology and imaging correlation

Histopathological examination remains the reference standard for definitive diagnosis of vascular tumors. However, in the present case, histopathological confirmation was not obtained because the patient declined tissue sampling. Under such circumstances, diagnosis relies on careful correlation of clinical presentation with characteristic multimodality imaging findings and expected lesion behavior. MRI localization of the lesion to the pectoralis major muscle and its typical signal and enhancement characteristics strongly supported the diagnosis of an intramuscular hemangioma and helped exclude primary breast pathology.

Clinical implications

This case highlights an important teaching point for radiologists involved in breast imaging and chest wall evaluation. Not all lesions detected during breast imaging originate from the breast parenchyma. When apparent breast asymmetry or a suspected retroareolar abnormality is identified, careful assessment of the chest wall musculature and timely use of cross-sectional imaging are essential. Awareness of intramuscular hemangiomas as potential mimickers of breast disease can improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, and facilitate appropriate surgical planning when intervention is indicated.

Conclusion

Intramuscular hemangiomas of the pectoralis major muscle are rare benign vascular tumors that may closely mimic primary breast pathology and lead to diagnostic uncertainty. This case underscores the critical importance of recognizing an extramammary origin when evaluating apparent breast asymmetry or suspected breast masses. Multimodality imaging, with magnetic resonance imaging playing a central role, enables accurate localization, characterization, and differentiation of these lesions from true breast parenchymal disease. Early and correct radiologic identification can prevent unnecessary invasive procedures, guide appropriate surgical planning when intervention is indicated, and ultimately optimize patient outcomes while reducing diagnostic delays and overtreatment.
