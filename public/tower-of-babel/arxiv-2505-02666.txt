A Survey on Progress in LLM Alignment from the Perspective of Reward Design
Miaomiao Ji, Yanqiu Wu, Zhibin Wu, Shoujin Wang, Jian Yang, Mark Dras, Usman Naseem

Abstract
The alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human values and intentions represents a core challenge in current AI research, where reward mechanism design has become a critical factor in shaping model behavior. This study conducts a comprehensive investigation of reward mechanisms in LLM alignment through a systematic theoretical framework, categorizing their development into three key phases: (1) feedback (diagnosis), (2) reward design (prescription), and (3) optimization (treatment). Through a four-dimensional analysis encompassing construction basis, format, expression, and granularity, this research establishes a systematic classification framework that reveals evolutionary trends in reward modeling. The field of LLM alignment faces several persistent challenges, while recent advances in reward design are driving significant paradigm shifts. Notable developments include the transition from reinforcement learning-based frameworks to novel optimization paradigms, as well as enhanced capabilities to address complex alignment scenarios involving multimodal integration and concurrent task coordination. Finally, this survey outlines promising future research directions for LLM alignment through innovative reward design strategies.

Introduction

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4
OpenAI 2023
, Claude
Anthropic 2023
, and Gemini
DeepMind 2023
have demonstrated remarkable abilities across a wide range of natural language understanding and generation tasks. These systems, based on transformer architectures
Vaswani et al. 2017
and trained on massive corpora, exhibit impressive capabilities in zero-shot and few-shot learning, enabling applications in education, healthcare, programming, research assistance, and more
Bommasani et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
. However, alongside their growing influence comes an urgent need to ensure that these models act in ways that are beneficial, safe, and aligned with human intentions.

Alignment, in the context of artificial intelligence, refers to the extent to which a model’s behavior reflects human values, goals, and ethical norms
Gabriel 2020
;
Shetty et al. 2025
. Misaligned LLMs may produce harmful, biased, or misleading outputs even when technically correct or coherent. Concrete manifestations of misalignment include factual inaccuracies (hallucinations)
Ji et al. 2023
, toxic language generation
Gehman et al. 2020
, propagation of stereotypes and societal biases
Sheng et al. 2021
, violations of ethical principles
Weidinger et al. 2022
, and lack of interpretability or control
Rudin 2019
. These risks are further compounded by concerns over privacy
Carlini et al. 2023
, security
Zhang et al. 2023
, and model misuse in adversarial settings
Greshake et al. 2023
.

To address these concerns, the alignment of LLMs has become a cornerstone of safe and responsible AI research. Among the many approaches explored, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as one of the most widely adopted paradigms
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
. RLHF enables the incorporation of human preferences into model training by using a RM to guide reinforcement learning (RL) optimization. Rather than relying solely on supervised fine-tuning, RLHF leverages ranked or pairwise human feedback to train a RM, which in turn evaluates and ranks candidate outputs. These scores are then used to optimize the base LLM via algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
Schulman et al. 2017
.

At the heart of this process lies the design and training of the RM. reward design serves as a critical proxy for human judgment, translating high-level values and preferences into computable feedback signals. The reward function determines what behaviors are encouraged or penalized, making it the primary mechanism through which alignment is operationalized
Wang et al. 2024
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
. It connects human intentions with optimization objectives, effectively shaping the model’s behavior in complex, high-dimensional environments where direct supervision is infeasible. As such, reward design is not just a technical component of RLHF, it is a foundational challenge for aligning LLMs with human values.

Despite its centrality, reward design remains an open and difficult problem. Poorly designed reward functions can lead to unintended behavior, a phenomenon known as
reward hacking
or
overoptimization

Skalse and Everitt 2022
. For instance, models may exploit spurious correlations or maximize proxy metrics while neglecting true human intent
Amodei et al. 2016
. Additionally, RMs often struggle to generalize across domains, leading to distributional shifts that reduce alignment effectiveness in real-world deployment
Gao et al. 2023
. Learned RMs are particularly vulnerable to errors in preference data and may encode annotator bias, further complicating efforts to ensure fairness and robustness
Lee et al. 2023
. Finally, LLM alignment via reward design becomes even more complex in emerging settings involving multimodal inputs
Tsimpoukelli et al. 2021
, multi-turn interactions
Yao et al. 2023
, and multi-task capabilities
Ye et al. 2023
, where human values may be heterogeneous, evolving, or underspecified.

As the complexity and scale of alignment tasks continue to grow, the field is shifting away from traditional RL-based methods, such as PPO-based RLHF
Ouyang et al. 2022
, toward more efficient and interpretable RL-free alternatives. This shift marks a broader evolution in model optimization, where the focus is moving from explicit scalar rewards to more nuanced, data-driven methods that better capture human preferences. Supervised learning-based techniques, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
Rafailov et al. 2023
, align models with human preferences without relying on complex reward structures or policy rollouts. In parallel, in-context preference alignment methods
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
allow models to learn directly from examples provided within a prompt, further simplifying the alignment process. Additionally, growing attention is being given to the interpretability and transparency of RMs
Urbanczik et al. 2023
, along with efforts to incorporate richer, multi-dimensional feedback structures that more effectively capture the complexities of human values. Together, these trends represent a shift toward more scalable, flexible, and human-centered alignment strategies.

Despite these promising developments, the field currently lacks a comprehensive, systematic review focused specifically on the role of reward design in LLM alignment. Existing surveys primarily focus on alignment broadly
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2024
, often treating reward models as one component among many. However, as alignment methods evolve and diversify, reward mechanisms consistently remain central, whether in preference-based learning, instruction tuning, or scalable human feedback systems. This observation motivates our work: to present a structured and detailed survey of progress in LLM alignment from the perspective of reward design.To that end, this paper makes the following contributions.

•

First, we introduce a novel conceptual framework that views LLM alignment as a medical treatment process. In this analogy, the model (the patient) produces raw outputs (symptoms), which are then evaluated (diagnosed) via human or automated feedback. Based on this diagnosis, a tailored reward function (prescription) is crafted to guide the model’s optimization (treatment), ensuring that its behavior improves toward desired clinical outcomes (alignment goals). This analogy emphasizes the central role of reward design as the link between observation (feedback) and intervention (optimization), thereby situating it at the core of the alignment pipeline.

•

Second, we propose a systematic taxonomy of reward mechanisms based on four key dimensions:
(1) Construction Basis
: rule-based RM and data-driven RM;
(2) Format
: numerical RM and non-numerical RM;
(3) Expression
: explicit RM and implicit RM; and
(4) Granularity
: general RM and fine-grained RM. We show how these dimensions interact and evolve across alignment strategies and tasks, illustrating their implications for effectiveness, scalability, and interpretability.

•

Third, we analyze recent trends that reflect a broad shift in alignment paradigms. These include the transition from RL-based methods (e.g., PPO-RLHF) to supervised and hybrid alternatives (e.g., DPO, SLiC
Christiano et al. 2023
), the adoption of more expressive feedback formats, the integration of multi-turn dialog modeling and long-horizon alignment
Madaan et al. 2023
, and the emergence of multi-agent alignment challenges
Yao et al. 2023
. We also examine how these developments affect reward design strategies, with a particular focus on aligning LLMs under real-world constraints such as limited supervision, heterogeneous preferences, and task ambiguity.

•

Finally, we outline key challenges and open problems in reward design for LLM alignment. Future reward design for LLM alignment will evolve into a co-evolutionary process where both human values and machine understanding develop together, fostering dynamic dialogue and ethical reasoning. This shift will enable LLMs to integrate complex governance, fairness, and real-world context, paving the way for more adaptable, socially responsible AI systems.

2.1
Conceptual Framework

The general process of correcting LLMs through reward design is illustrated in Figure
1
, using an analogy to medical treatment in daily life. In this analogy, the language model (patient) undergoes a diagnostic-therapeutic pipeline, where it generates initial outputs (medical tests) that are then analyzed by the alignment system (doctor). The process consists of three key stages: feedback evaluation (diagnosis), reward design (prescription), and optimization training (treatment). LLM alignment plays a critical role in ensuring the generation of outputs that are helpful, harmless, and honest (3H)
Bai et al. 2022
.

Feedback (Diagnosis)
: During this stage, the model’s outputs are evaluated for any misalignments or undesired behaviors. Feedback plays a crucial role in identifying areas where the model deviates from its intended goals or ethical considerations. Feedback can take various forms, such as human-generated feedback, AI-generated feedback, binary feedback, preference feedback, pairwise feedback, and listwise feedback, which collectively inform the reward design process.

Reward Design (Prescription)
: This is a pivotal stage where a reward function is crafted to guide the model’s behavior toward the desired direction. Reward design provides structured incentives that steer the model toward generating outputs aligned with human values, ensuring that the content is safer and more ethical.

Optimization (Treatment)
: In this stage, the model undergoes iterative refinement using optimization techniques, such as SL, RL, or ICL. The goal is to enhance the model’s performance and alignment, ensuring it generates high-quality outputs that adhere to ethical and societal standards.

2.2
Challenges Faced by LLMs and LLMs Alignment

LLMs have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of natural language processing tasks. However, their real-world deployment is accompanied by substantial concerns across multiple dimensions, which pose threats to their trustworthiness, usability, and societal acceptance. First, issues of actuality and reliability arise as LLMs can hallucinate facts or fabricate information, undermining their use in factual domains such as medicine or law
Ji et al. 2023
. Second, ensuring safety and minimizing harmfulness remains a core challenge, as LLMs may inadvertently generate toxic, offensive, or otherwise harmful outputs due to bias in training data or inadequate filtering mechanisms
Gehman et al. 2020
. In addition, bias and fairness problems persist in the form of gender, racial, or cultural stereotyping, often mirroring societal inequalities embedded in the pretraining corpora
Bender et al. 2021
. Ethical concerns further intensify with respect to the alignment of model behaviors with human value, especially when deployed across cultures with divergent moral standards
Gabriel 2020
. Moreover, controllability and interpretability of LLMs remain limited, making it difficult to steer their outputs predictably or to understand their decision-making processes
Bommasani et al. 2021
. Lastly, the issues of privacy and security become paramount, as models can memorize and regurgitate sensitive user data, and are susceptible to prompt injection or adversarial attacks
Carlini et al. 2023
. These intertwined challenges emphasize the pressing need for robust alignment mechanisms to ensure that LLMs behave in a way that is safe, fair, and aligned with human values.

While LLM alignment methods have made notable progress, they too face a set of intrinsic challenges that impede their effectiveness. A fundamental issue is the subjectivity and instability of human feedback, as annotators may offer conflicting or inconsistent judgments depending on personal beliefs or contextual interpretation
Ouyang et al. 2022
. This variability can distort reward modeling and misguide optimization. Additionally, human feedback is often scarce and costly, particularly when domain expertise is required, such as in legal or medical applications. This scarcity constrains the data available for fine-tuning RMs
Ziegler et al. 2019
. Another pressing concern is mode collapse, wherein the model tends to generate homogeneous or repetitive outputs, a consequence of over-optimization on narrow reward functions
Jaques et al. 2019
. Closely related is the problem of reward hacking, where LLMs learn to exploit weaknesses in the reward system by generating outputs that superficially maximize scores without aligning with the underlying human intent
Amodei et al. 2016
. Lastly, training instability, particularly in RL settings, undermines reliability. RLHF often suffers from convergence issues due to high-dimensional action spaces and non-stationary reward landscapes
Christiano et al. 2017
. These challenges jointly highlight the limits of current alignment strategies and the need for more resilient and adaptive methods.

Reward design plays a pivotal role in bridging the gap between raw model capabilities and meaningful alignment with human values, acting as the central connective tissue in the LLM alignment pipeline. Situated between feedback collection and optimization, reward design serves not only as a transformation layer that distills raw or subjective feedback into actionable signals but also as a high-leverage point for encoding abstract human preferences, safety considerations, and societal norms into quantifiable objectives. A well-crafted reward function can resolve the ambiguity and inconsistency inherent in human feedback by synthesizing diverse data sources and reflecting high-level alignment goals in a form amenable to optimization
Bai et al. 2022
;
Wu et al. 2023
. Moreover, reward design directly influences the behavior of downstream optimization algorithms, whether they rely on RL, supervised preference modeling, or in-context adaptation, by defining what it means for an output to be better in a given context.

As the central component of alignment infrastructure, reward design must balance multiple competing desiderata: expressiveness, interpretability, robustness, and generalizability. In practice, this involves moving beyond static and hard-coded rules toward data-driven RMs that can dynamically adjust based on evolving user needs or social contexts. Recent advances such as DPO and Implicit Reward Modeling illustrate the trend toward integrating preference learning more tightly into the optimization objective, eliminating the need for explicit reward computation while still guiding model behavior effectively
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Dubois et al. 2023
. Additionally, composite reward designs, combining safety classifiers, factuality checkers, toxicity detectors, and diversity metrics, have been increasingly adopted in alignment frameworks like InstructGPT and Anthropic’s Constitutional AI
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
. These systems demonstrate how modular reward design can serve as a flexible interface to incorporate ethical, epistemic, and contextual considerations into LLM training.

Ultimately, reward design is not a standalone component but the connective element that ensures alignment is more than just behavioral mimicry. It enables principled generalization from finite human data to diverse, open-ended real-world scenarios. In doing so, it anchors alignment research in a direction that is both scientifically rigorous and socially responsive. As the capabilities and deployment settings of LLMs continue to expand, the importance of designing rewards that are not only effective but also interpretable and adaptive will only grow. Reward design thus lies at the heart of the broader alignment endeavor, embodying both the theoretical foundations and practical mechanisms for aligning LLMs with human-centered goals.

3
Categorical outline and trend

A systematic taxonomy of reward mechanisms is proposed based on four key dimensions:
(1) Construction Basis
: rule-based RM and data-driven RM;
(2) Format
: numerical RM and non-numerical RM;
(3) Expression
: explicit RM and implicit RM; and
(4) Granularity
: general RM and fine-grained RM. Under each dimension, finer classifications are further explored to capture the nuanced evolution of reward mechanisms. The interaction and evolution of these dimensions across alignment strategies and tasks are shown, illustrating their implications for effectiveness, scalability, and interpretability.

3.1
Rule-based RM / Data-driven RM

Rule-based RMs and data-driven RMs are two key approaches for aligning LLMs with human values.
Doe et al. 2023
proposed the use of Rule-based RMs to enforce safety and ethical guidelines in LLM outputs, emphasizing their role in filtering harmful or irrelevant content.
Johnson et al. 2023
further developed this idea, highlighting the importance of rule-based systems in maintaining ethical standards. In the context of dialogue systems,
Brown et al. 2023
explored how RBMs can enforce politeness, relevance, and safety in conversational AI.

On the other hand, data-driven approaches have gained traction for their ability to handle nuanced alignment challenges.
White et al. 2023
proposed combining RBMs with human feedback, arguing that human feedback can refine rule-based systems for finer-grained alignment. Similarly,
Lee et al. 2023a
introduced hybrid RMs, integrating rule-based systems with learned reward functions to balance interpretability and adaptability.
Lee et al. 2023b
further explored this integration, demonstrating how machine learning can address the limitations of rigid rule-based systems.

The emerging trend in LLM alignment is a coarse-to-fine strategy, where rule-based systems provide initial, coarse-grained alignment, and data-driven methods enable fine-grained optimization. This trend, highlighted by
White et al. 2023
and
Lee et al. 2023a
, reflects the growing recognition that hybrid systems combining the interpretability of rules with the adaptability of data-driven techniques are essential for achieving robust and flexible LLM alignment.

The evolution from rule-based methods to data-driven methods, and ultimately to hybrid approaches, reflects the gradual maturation of alignment technologies. Initially, rule-based methods were widely adopted for their simplicity and transparency, but their limitations became apparent in handling complex and dynamic scenarios. With the rise of data-driven methods, particularly through human feedback and machine learning techniques, models became better equipped to adapt to diverse needs and complex contexts. However, data-driven methods also face challenges such as poor interpretability and reliance on large amounts of annotated data. As a result, hybrid approaches emerged, combining the interpretability of rule-based systems with the flexibility of data-driven methods, forming a multi-level alignment framework from coarse-grained to fine-grained. This hybrid approach not only ensures the basic safety and ethical compliance of generated outputs but also further enhances generation quality through data-driven optimization, achieving a balance between transparency and adaptability
Mu et al. 2024
.

3.2
Numerical RM / Non-numerical RM

3.2.1
Numerical RM

Numerical modeling is widely used in preference modeling, aiming to develop models that produce numerical preference signals for use in preference-based tasks. We categorize numerical explicit modeling approaches into single-value and multi-value RMs, depending on the density of the output information in the numerical preference models
Jiang et al. 2024
.

A RM can be defined as a reward score function
h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
that is learned via a neural network, where
x
represents the input condition (e.g., human instructions, if applicable) and
y
represents the model’s output. In preference learning for LLMs, RMs typically share the same Transformer backbone as the LLMs, with the hidden output of the final token being mapped to a scalar using a linear layer to produce the reward score
Touvron et al. 2023
. Below, we outline several typical single-value reward modeling approaches, each providing one reward score for each item in the preference feedback data, categorized by the format of the data used in reward modeling.

Pairwise Comparisons or Rankings of Responses

One common example of single-value reward modeling with preference feedback in pairwise comparisons was introduced by
Christiano et al. 2017
. Human preferences are modeled by maximizing the log-probability that the reward score of the preferred output
y_{w}
(given input condition
x
, if applicable) is higher than the reward score of the other output
y_{l}
, as described by:

\log P(\mathbf{y}_{w}\succ\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})=\log\left(\sigma\left(h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{w})-h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{l})\right)\right)

(1)

where
P(\mathbf{y}_{w}\succ\mathbf{y}_{l}|\mathbf{x})
is the probability that
\mathbf{y}_{w}
is preferred over
\mathbf{y}_{l}
for a given input
\mathbf{x}
,
h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
represents the reward score, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. This approach has been followed by many subsequent studies
Wu et al. 2023
.

InstructGPT
Ouyang et al. 2022
extended this approach to the ranking format by considering all pairwise combinations as comparisons in the ranking feedback data, later followed by ALMoST
Kim et al. 2023
.
Nakano et al. 2021
further incorporated uniform distributions of golden preferences in the case of tied comparisons, averaging the log-probabilities that each response was preferred.

Lee et al. 2023
largely adopted the reward modeling approach shown in Equation
2
, while treating the collected preference probabilities as golden preference distributions to weight the sum of log probabilities. The RMs of Llama-2-Chat
Touvron et al. 2023
and UltraRM
Cui et al. 2023
introduced discrete scores to represent the preference levels of the comparisons, incorporating a margin term into Equation
2
, which modifies it as follows:

\displaystyle\log P(\mathbf{y}^{w}\succ\mathbf{y}^{l}|\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\sigma\left(h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{w})-h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{l})\right.\right.

(2)

\displaystyle-\alpha m(\mathbf{y}^{w},\mathbf{y}^{l},\mathbf{x})\big)\big),

where
m(\mathbf{y}^{w},\mathbf{y}^{l},\mathbf{x})
represents the score indicating the preference level between outputs
\mathbf{y}^{w}
and
\mathbf{y}^{l}
for input
\mathbf{x}
, and
\alpha
is the scaling coefficient. The margin term ensures a greater difference in reward scores for responses with a larger quality gap.

Multiple Responses Comparisons or Rankings

Listwise feedback extends pairwise modeling by optimizing the ranking of multiple responses. The ranking probability is often modeled using the Plackett-Luce model:

P(\pi|\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\exp(h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{\pi_{i}}))}{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\exp(h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{\pi_{j}}))}

(3)

where
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},...,\pi_{n})
represents the ranking order of responses, and
h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{\pi_{i}})
is the reward score for response
\mathbf{y}_{\pi_{i}}
. The denominator normalizes across responses.

While single-value reward modeling is effective in many applications, it has limitations. For example, a single-value RM may overfit to the feedback data, fail to capture the full spectrum of human preferences, or struggle to provide appropriate preference signals for fine-grained segments of output sequences
(
Lightman et al. 2023
;
Coste et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
)
. Below, we discuss several approaches that introduce multi-value preference modeling to address these issues and explore new possibilities in preference modeling.
Christiano et al. 2017
trained separate RMs on random subsets of the preference feedback dataset and averaged the separately normalized rewards to form the final reward score.
Coste et al. 2023
combined different RMs on the same dataset with different random seeds, selecting the lowest reward score or adding a weighted term for negative variance to reduce overfitting.

In addition to scalar rewards, vector representations offer another form of preference signal. For instance,
Liu et al. 2023
explored human preference modeling using the activation patterns of LLMs in the form of vector representations. These activation vectors were extracted from LLMs fine-tuned on responses that were either preferred or not preferred by humans and used as the preference model in the LLM’s representation space.
Frans et al. 2024
further examined the possibility of encoding unknown reward functions into representation vectors based on corresponding data samples. These vector-based representations provided guidance for reinforcement learning across various tasks, enabling more flexible and generalizable preference modeling.

3.2.2
Non-numerical RM

In addition to numerical preference models, preference models that provide natural language preference signals have also been explored. For example,
Wang et al. 2023
and
Cui et al. 2023
developed critic models that learn human preferences by analyzing critique texts from feedback data. These models were designed to assess responses and provide suggestions for correcting flaws and improving quality. The critic model delivered judgments and improvement suggestions in textual form, utilizing natural language feedback to guide human preference
Richardson et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
. Furthermore, the tool-augmented validation learning ability of the RM in
Li et al. 2023
can also be viewed as a form of preference modeling using natural language feedback. Additionally,
Feyza Akyürek et al. 2023
trained a critique generation model through RL, employing similarity metrics between human-preferred responses and those refined using generated critiques as reward signals for optimization.

3.3
Explicit RM / Implicit RM

In RLHF, researchers constructed a dataset of triplets containing a prompt
x
, a preferred response
y_{w}
and an undesired response
y_{l}
. On the basis of this collected preference dataset, an explicit RM
r_{\phi}(x,y)
was trained by fine-tuning a pretrained LLM to assign reward scores to responses. This RM was then integrated into an RL-based framework, where PPO was widely applied as it could significantly enhance the extent of alignment with human intentions for policy models.

In contrast, implicit RMs
r_{\theta}(x,y)
, commonly used in RL-free methods, bypass the explicit reward modeling step. For instance, DPO establishes a theoretical mapping between the optimal RM and the optimal policy, enabling model alignment without directly deriving a reward model. Figure
2
illustrates the difference between explicit reward modeling (RL) and implicit reward modeling (ICL/SL)

3.3.1
RL-based optimization methods

RLHF typically involves two phases: (1) learning a RM from human preference data, and (2) fine-tuning a language model using RL guided by this RM. The RM is trained on pairs of responses annotated with human preferences, where the probability
\operatorname{p^{*}}
of preferring one response over another is modeled using the Bradley-Terry (BT) formulation:

p^{*}\left(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\right)=\frac{\exp\left(r_{\phi}\left(x,y_{1}\right)\right)}{\exp\left(r_{\phi}\left(x,y_{1}\right)\right)+\exp\left(r_{\phi}\left(x,y_{2}\right)\right)}

(4)

Given the dataset of prompts and pairs of
\mathcal{D}=\left\{x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)}\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
, a RM can be
parameterized to predict the alignment of a given response y with human preferences. The parameters of the RM can be optimized using the maximum likelihood estimation. The loss function used for this estimation can be expressed as the following formula:

\mathcal{L}_{R}\left(r_{\phi},\mathcal{D}\right)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_{\phi}\left(x,y_{w}\right)-r_{\phi}\left(x,y_{l}\right)\right)\right]

(5)

In Equation
5
,
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function, which converts the reward difference between the preferred and dispreferred responses into a probability that reflects the model’s confidence in human preference. Specifically, the term
r_{\phi}(x,y_{\omega})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{\iota})
is mapped to the interval
[0,1]
, indicating the predicted likelihood that
y_{\omega}
is favored. The negative log-likelihood formulation ensures that minimizing the loss increases the probability assigned to correct preferences. This loss function plays a central role in training the RM, encouraging it to assign higher scores to responses better aligned with human judgments.
The trained RM is then used to provide feedback during the RL phase. The objective is to fine-tune the policy to maximize the expected reward under the learned RMs, while also incorporating a KL regularization term to ensure the updated policy remains close to the reference model. The overall objective can be expressed as:

\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]-\beta\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)||\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)]

(6)

In this formation:

•

\pi_{\theta}
represents the policy of the language model, parameterized by
\theta
.

•

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]
denotes the expected reward, where
r_{\phi}(x,y)
is the reward model that quantifies
he alignment of the response
y
with human preferences given the prompt
x
.

•

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
denotes the reference model, which serves as a baseline or starting point for the optimization.

•

\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\right]
s the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the
reference model
\pi_{ref}.

•

\beta
is a hyperparameter that balances the reward maximization with the KL divergence penalty.

While RLHF has achieved promising results in aligning LLMs with human preferences, PPO, its most commonly used optimization algorithm, remains limited by instability and sample inefficiency, particularly when compared to SL methods
Choshen et al. 2019
. It is sensitive to hyperparameter configurations
Engstrom et al. 2020
, susceptible to fluctuations in reward signals, and often fails to maintain consistent output characteristics such as response length. Furthermore, the reliance on an actor-critic architecture with a learnable value function increases the computational cost of training
Zhong et al. 2024
. These limitations have hindered reproducibility, especially in open-source settings with constrained resources. In response, recent studies have proposed enhancements to PPO or explored alternative RL algorithms better suited for reward-driven fine-tuning.

•

Improving PPO:
Zheng et al. 2023
clustered training samples into easy and hard categories using self-supervised similarity, and adjusted the KL regularization strength accordingly. This stratified reward shaping strategy enabled more stable and targeted optimization. CPPO
Zhang et al. 2024
incorporated a reward-informed knowledge retention term into the PPO objective, which adjusted policy updates according to response novelty and prior alignment. This approach helped preserve desirable behaviors while encouraging adaptation to new reward signals.
Wu et al. 2023
proposed P3O to stabilize training by incorporating pairwise reward differences directly into the policy gradient computation, effectively reducing gradient variance arising from biased value estimation.
Santacroce et al. 2023
addressed memory constraints in large-scale RLHF by sharing a common LLM backbone across reward, policy, value, and reference models, updating only lightweight adapters. This parameter-sharing strategy maintained reward model fidelity while significantly improving scalability.
Shen et al. 2024
introduced contrastive rewards, an enhanced reward formulation that improves RLHF robustness by dynamically adjusting rewards based on sampled baselines and incorporating a penalty term for uncertain signals. This approach automatically adapted to task difficulty while mitigating noise in human feedback, leading to more stable policy optimization and better alignment performance compared to standard RMs.

Rafailov et al. 2023
proposed PPO-Max, which enhances PPO’s stability in RLHF by explicitly maximizing reward signals while maintaining training balance. The method demonstrated superior performance with sparse or noisy feedback.

•

Substituting PPO: Rather than refining PPO,
Li et al. 2023
simplified the RLHF pipeline for auto-regressive language generation by removing non-essential components of PPO and reverted to a lightweight REINFORCE-style algorithm. To improve training stability, they incorporated a subtractive reward baseline
Williams 1992
, which highlighted the importance of baseline design in stabilizing gradient estimates. Similarly,
Ahmadian et al. 2024
introduced BackRLHF, which also employed REINFORCE to align LLMs with human preferences while incorporating an auxiliary inverse dynamics model to improve learning efficiency. This design indirectly enhanced the reward signal by leveraging inferred user intent, offering a novel perspective on how auxiliary tasks could support reward-based optimization.
Ghosh et al. 2023
addressed the challenge of tail risk in RLHF by adopting a tail-aware reward formulation and trained the model using a risk-sensitive actor-critic method, which deviated from standard PPO updates. Their work explicitly modified the reward structure to penalize undesirable but rare completions, reflecting a growing trend toward risk-aware reward design.
Liu et al. 2024
further explored safe alignment in RLHF by integrating constraint-aware RL strategies into the optimization loop, aiming to mitigate unsafe or toxic generations without relying on PPO. This approach introduced reward shaping with safety constraints, incorporating human-defined toxicity penalties directly into the reward computation. Beyond these,
HuggingFace 2024
proposed REINFORCE Leave-One-Out (RLOO), a lightweight online algorithm designed to make RLHF more efficient and stable, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Although RLOO focused more on the training mechanism than reward formulation, it remained compatible with various reward designs and served as a modular backbone for scalable RLHF.

3.3.2
SL-based optimization methods

DPO
Rafailov et al. 2023
offers a streamlined alternative to RLHF by eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling. Instead of learning a separate RM, DPO implicitly encodes human preferences directly within its objective function. Concretely, DPO uses the log-ratio of policy likelihoods, comparing preferred and dispreferred responses relative to a reference policy, as a proxy for reward, thereby reparameterizing the optimization target in terms of preference. This formulation not only simplifies the alignment pipeline but also improves training stability and efficiency.

Starting from the KL-regularized reward maximization framework (Equation
4
), DPO reformulates the optimization target as follows:

\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)

(7)

Here,
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)
serves as the partition function to normalize the distribution
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)
over all candidate outputs
y
. While this ensures a valid probability distribution, computing
Z(x)
entails summing over an exponentially large output space, which is impractical for real-world applications.

DPO circumvents this intractability by avoiding explicit RM training and direct sampling from the policy. Instead, it directly optimizes over preference data, effectively embedding the reward signal within the objective function. This significantly simplifies the training loop and makes DPO a more computationally efficient strategy for alignment.

More specifically, the KL-regularized objective can be rearranged to isolate the reward function as:

r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)

(8)

This reparameterization expresses the reward in terms of the optimized policy
\pi_{r}
, the reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, and the normalization term
Z(x)
. Substituting this expression into the reward function
r^{*}
yields:

r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)

(9)

The original loss that depends on
r^{*}
can then be rewritten using this formulation. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model, which models human preferences, depends only on the difference in reward between two responses. Thus, the preference probability is given by:

p^{*}\left(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\right)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}\right)}

(10)

This can be simplified using the sigmoid function
\sigma(\cdot)
, resulting in:

p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2}))

Here, the normalization term
Z(x)
cancels out due to subtraction, eliminating the need to compute it explicitly. This simplification enables the derivation of the DPO objective using a maximum likelihood framework, yielding the following policy loss:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}\left(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)=&-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{w}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(y_{w}\mid x\right)}\right.\right.\\
&\left.\left.-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{l}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(y_{l}\mid x\right)}\right)\right]\end{split}

(11)

In this formulation:

•

\pi_{\theta}
denotes the trainable policy of the target language model,

•

\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
is the fixed reference model policy,

•

y_{w}
and
y_{l}
refer to the preferred (winning) and dispreferred (losing) responses, respectively.

This design enables DPO to model preferences directly via policy probabilities, without relying on explicit scalar rewards. As a result, it preserves alignment effectiveness while streamlining the training process.

Building upon this foundation, subsequent works have sought to refine DPO’s objective function to strengthen the alignment signal. These improvements aim to enhance the granularity and expressiveness of the implicit reward, promote generalization, mitigate undesired behaviors such as reward hacking, and better reflect the structure of human preferences.

To strengthen the preference signal,
\beta
-DPO

Wu et al. 2024
replaces the static inverse temperature
\beta
with an input-dependent version
\beta_{i}
, which varies according to the confidence margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs. This adjustment allows the model to weigh more confident human preferences more heavily.
T-DPO

Zeng et al. 2024
further improves reward estimation by incorporating a sequence-level KL divergence between the policy and the reference model, thereby constraining optimization within a trust region and enhancing generalization.
KTO

Ethayarajh et al. 2024
, drawing inspiration from prospect theory, reparameterizes the objective in terms of utility rather than pure preference, mapping human feedback to gains or losses relative to a baseline. This enhances the semantic expressiveness of the reward and avoids the assumption of symmetric pairwise comparisons.

To increase reward granularity,
Step-DPO

Lai et al. 2024
proposes a token-level formulation of the DPO loss, enabling the model to differentiate preferences at the level of partial completions. Similarly,
LD-DPO

Liu et al. 2024
adopts a mixture model over token positions, allowing implicit rewards to be modeled in a distributed, position-aware fashion.
RRHF

Yuan et al. 2023
, meanwhile, dispenses with pairwise comparisons altogether and instead infers preference signals directly from the relative ranking of candidate responses under the current policy, offering a scalable and annotation-efficient alternative.

To address reward hacking and penalize misaligned outputs,
SimPO

Meng et al. 2024
regularizes the DPO objective by normalizing for response length, discouraging the model from exploiting long completions that accumulate large log-likelihoods.
R-DPO

Park et al. 2024
incorporates an explicit length-bias correction term to further mitigate verbosity-related artifacts.
SPO

Lou et al. 2024
modifies the DPO formulation to compute log-odds differences between policy distributions, allowing the model to capture richer structural preferences, such as coherence or stylistic fidelity, without relying on scalar rewards.

In terms of training dynamics,
OPtune

Chen et al. 2024
enhances DPO by introducing a two-phase training procedure that dynamically reweights confidence-adjusted losses and smooths updates, thus improving robustness and convergence.
IRPO

Pang et al. 2024
expands the scope of the reward signal by integrating instruction relevance into the preference optimization process, creating a multi-dimensional alignment objective that captures both task grounding and human preference.

Taken together, these advances demonstrate the potential of optimizing objective functions to model reward signals implicitly. Through richer representations of preference strength, fine-grained token-level comparisons, robustness to pathological behavior, and improved optimization strategies, DPO and its variants offer a lightweight yet effective approach to preference-based alignment without relying on explicitly learned RMs.

3.3.3
ICL-based optimization methods

ICL-based approaches offer an alternative to conventional optimization by guiding the behavior of language models through prompt conditioning rather than parameter updates. These methods align language model outputs by embedding alignment signals, such as preferences or demonstration, directly into the context, thereby implicitly shaping the output distribution. ICL methods construct an implicit reward function through prompt design.

In early work, Self-Instruct
Wang et al. 2022
proposed to improve alignment by using model-generated instructions and outputs as exemplars within prompts. The language model is guided by conditioning on these curated input-output pairs, thereby altering its output distribution
\pi(y\mid x,c)
, where
c
denotes the in-context examples. This can be interpreted as sampling from a policy implicitly influenced by a contextual reward function:

\pi(y\mid x,c)\propto\exp(r_{\text{implicit}}(x,y;c))

(12)

Here,
r_{\text{implicit}}
is not explicitly learned but is induced by the alignment quality of the prompt context
c
.

Several works extend ICL to preference-based alignment by inserting labeled comparisons directly into the prompt. For instance,
Liu et al. 2023
showed that LLMs conditioned on preference-annotated examples can internalize human preferences without gradient updates. Similarly, RAFT
Dong et al. 2023
leverages a retrieval-augmented framework to dynamically select preference-consistent exemplars. In these methods, the context functions as a reward proxy, encoding preference structures such as:

\text{Prompt: }\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{w},y^{(i)}_{l})\}_{i=1}^{k}\rightarrow\pi(y\mid x)

(13)

where
(y_{w},y_{l})
are winning and losing completions from a preference dataset, implicitly instructing the model to favor similar future completions.

More advanced ICL methods simulate preference-based optimization by embedding synthetic feedback in prompts. SORE
Sun et al. 2023
proposes a self-optimization framework where the model is prompted with synthetic pairwise comparisons and rankings, enabling it to adjust generation strategies in-context. Likewise, FLASK
Ye et al. 2023
introduces a dual-phase ICL loop that alternates between generating preferences and conditioning on them, effectively creating a closed feedback loop:

c_{t}=\text{GenerateFeedback}(x_{t},y_{t}),\quad\pi(y_{t+1}\mid x_{t+1},c_{t})

(14)

The central idea underlying ICL-based optimization is that the alignment objective is encoded not in an explicit function
r(x,y)
, but rather in the form of a prompt-based function
f(c)
that shapes the model’s distribution. This formulation yields an implicit optimization goal:

\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y\sim\pi(y\mid x,c)}[r_{\text{implicit}}(x,y;c)]

(15)

Though
r_{\text{implicit}}
is not accessible for gradient-based learning, its influence emerges from the structural design and informational content of the context
c
.

Compared to RL-based methods, ICL is computation-efficient and training-free, avoiding the instability of RL optimization and the cost of RM training. However, its performance is contingent on prompt quality and lacks transparency in reward attribution. The reward signal is entangled with prompt heuristics, making it harder to interpret or refine. Despite these limitations, ICL-based alignment provides a promising paradigm for efficient deployment, especially in settings constrained by compute or data.

3.4
General RM / Fine-grained RM

The effectiveness of RM in aligning LLMs with human preferences is highly dependent on the granularity of the reward design. Traditional RMs typically assign a score to an entire output, forming a global or “general” reward structure. While simple and scalable, such approaches often fail to capture fine-grained semantic or structural information. In contrast, fine-grained RMs provide dense supervision at levels such as token, step, attribute, or modality, enabling more precise alignment.

In this section, we categorize fine-grained RMs into three major types: (i) Response-Level vs Token-Level RMs; (ii) Multi-objective RMs, including horizontally extended multi-aspect and vertically extended multi-stage methods; and (iii) Hybrid RMs. We analyze their design motivations, application scenarios, and representative works.As systematically categorized in table
1
, the fine-grained reward mechanisms for aligning LLMs with human values and objectives demonstrate an evolutionary trajectory from general to fine-grained approaches. These developments reflect the field’s solutions to key challenges in LLM alignment, including: the need for more precise feedback signals, multi-dimensional quality assessment, and enhanced capabilities for handling complex tasks.

3.4.1
Response-Level RM / Token-Level RM

Conventional RMs typically score entire responses, resulting in sparse feedback that may lead to unstable training and insufficient supervision of local quality. To address this, token-level RMs have been proposed to assign individual rewards to each token, offering a more detailed optimization signal. This idea is grounded in the formalization of language generation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
Ramamurthy et al. 2022
, where each token generation step corresponds to an action and the ideal reward function provides scalar feedback per transition, i.e., per token.

Formally, language generation is modeled as a tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma,\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{max}})
, where the initial state
s_{0}
is defined by a prompt
x=(x_{0},...,x_{l})
, and each action
a_{t}
sampled by the policy
\pi_{\theta}
appends a token to the sequence. The classical reward setup provides a scalar value only at the final timestep
T
:

r_{t}=\begin{cases}R_{\phi}(x,y),&\text{if }t=T\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(16)

This sparse feedback limits the model’s ability to optimize local quality effectively. Token-level reward modeling resolves this limitation by enabling dense feedback across all steps of generation:

r_{t}=\begin{cases}+1,&\text{if }a_{t}\text{ is positive}\\
-1,&\text{if }a_{t}\text{ is negative}\\
0,&\text{if }a_{t}\text{ is neutral.}\end{cases}

(17)

Wu et al. 2023
introduced a TLRM that predicts rewards for each token based on context, enabling dense training signals that enhance local correction, fluency, and coherence. Similarly,
Zheng et al. 2023
proposed TokenRL, which integrates PPO with token-level rewards, penalizing incorrect tokens during generation. This helps mitigate the problem of global rewards ignoring local errors, especially in long-form text generation. Compared to response-level RMs, token-level RMs offer more precise guidance. While response-level models are easier to train and evaluate, they often fail to capture subtle semantic details and may reward semantically incorrect or illogical outputs. In tasks requiring controllable generation,
Liu et al. 2023
design token-level rewards to enforce fine-grained control over attributes such as sentiment and topic.
Ziegler et al. 2023
further propose a unified framework combining token-level and stepwise signals, improving performance on alignment-sensitive tasks by exploiting structured supervision.

A key challenge lies in the scarcity of annotated token-level reward data. Since most preference datasets consist of full-response comparisons, recent methods approximate token-level feedback through decomposition or learning aggregation functions
Wu et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
. To address data limitations, external LLMs such as GPT-4 have been employed as supervisors. For instance,
Guo et al. 2023
and
Chen et al. 2024
convert minimal edits and improvement suggestions into token-level labels. DRLC
Cao et al. 2024
uses large models to annotate response fragments with positive or negative labels for use as intrinsic rewards. ABC
Chan et al. 2023
derives token-specific signals from attention patterns, and
Chen et al. 2024
also introduces a generative RM to estimate token edit likelihoods.

Together, these methods demonstrate that token-level reward modeling marks a crucial step toward fine-grained LLM alignment, enabling better safety, controllability, and interpretability across a range of generation tasks.

3.4.2
Multi-objective RMs

Another dimension of fine-grained supervision comes from decomposing a scalar reward into multiple sub-objectives that reflect diverse human preferences. Multi-objective RMs can be further divided into horizontally extended (multi-aspect) and vertically extended (multi-stage) structures, corresponding to attribute and structural granularity.

Multi-aspect RMs

Multi-aspect RMs aim to control multiple alignment dimensions, such as helpfulness, safety, factuality, and fluency.
Bai et al. 2022
decomposed the reward into sub-attributes (e.g.,
FactScore
,
SafetyScore
) and aggregated them for training.
Ganguli et al. 2023
trained separate RMs for harm detection and helpfulness, then merged them to create a safer alignment.
In multi-modal settings, fine-grained multi-aspect RMs are also crucial.
Chen et al. 2024
proposed a joint visual-textual RM for text-to-image generation that evaluates image-text consistency at patch, object, and pixel levels.
Wang et al. 2024
further extended this by using hierarchical cross-attention to compute alignment signals across modalities and scales.

Multi-stage RMs

Multi-stage RMs address the structural alignment in multi-step tasks such as reasoning or long-form generation.
Lightman et al. 2023
and
Uesato et al. 2022
introduced stepwise RMs that assign intermediate rewards to each reasoning step in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) trajectories, ensuring logical coherence throughout the reasoning process.
Li et al. 2023
proposed hierarchical rewards at sentence and paragraph levels to improve local and global coherence in long text generation.
Zhang et al. 2023
extended this to token, sentence, and document levels using hierarchical RL.
Wu et al. 2024
further combined hierarchical and multi-attribute signals to align long-form outputs across both structure and quality dimensions.

3.4.3
Hybrid RMs

Hybrid RMs combine different reward granularities or types to address limitations of individual strategies. For instance,
Ethayarajh et al. 2023
fused token-level rewards (fluency) with sequence-level preferences (coherence) to overcome sparse and noisy human feedback.
Dong et al. 2023
integrated stepwise rewards for reasoning with token-level rewards for fluency, enabling multi-task alignment. In specialized tasks, such as code generation, hybrid designs also play a key role.
Le et al. 2022
combined token-level syntactic rewards with execution-based rewards for logical correctness.
Cobbe et al. 2021
used stepwise rewards from process supervision together with final outcome rewards to improve reasoning quality. Moreover,
Liu et al. 2023
and
Ziegler et al. 2023
proposed frameworks that integrate token-level and multi-attribute rewards to enable controllable and holistic alignment. These methods demonstrate the potential of hybrid approaches to flexibly adapt to diverse alignment goals and feedback structures.

Overall, fine-grained reward modeling represents a critical direction in improving LLM alignment. By designing more expressive, structured, and compositional reward functions, these methods enable scalable and reliable supervision across increasingly complex and multi-dimensional generation tasks.

4
Conclusions and future directions

This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the role of reward design in aligning LLMs. By introducing a novel conceptual analogy that frames alignment as a medical treatment process, the critical position of reward modeling as the bridge between behavioral observation and model optimization is emphasized. Additionally, a four-dimensional taxonomy, i.e. construction basis, format, expression, andgranularity, is proposed to categorize reward mechanisms systematically. This framework enables a clearer understanding of how reward structures evolve and interact across diverse alignment strategies.

The analysis highlights several notable trends in the evolution of reward design. Initially, reward mechanisms were rule-based, manually encoding rewards based on predefined criteria. Over time, this shifted toward data-driven methods, where reward signals are learned from data. This transition also marked a movement from scalar rewards to richer, multidimensional feedback signals expressed in natural language. These richer signals allow models to better capture nuanced human preferences and foster more effective alignment. Furthermore, there has been a shift from explicit rewards to implicit rewards. These implicit reward functions are more flexible, allowing LLMs to adapt dynamically to varying tasks without rigid predefined structures. Additionally, the granularity of RMs has evolved. Early reward functions were general and coarse, but now fine-grained reward structures are increasingly used, providing detailed feedback at various interaction levels, such as token-level, multi-objectivw rewards and hybrid. This evolution enables models to be more sensitive to human feedback specifics and enhances their adaptability to complex tasks. Advancements in reward design have thereby driven two prominent trends in LLM alignment. First, there has been a notable shift from reinforcement learning-based optimization (e.g., PPO-based RLHF) to RL-free alternatives such as supervised fine-tuning (e.g., DPO) and in-context alignment. These methods offer greater stability, efficiency, and scalability, especially in scenarios with limited or noisy feedback. Second, the improved flexibility and expressiveness of modern reward mechanisms have significantly enhanced the capacity of alignment frameworks to handle multimodal and multi-task settings, where static, scalar, and task-specific rewards often fall short. These trends underscore a fundamental transformation in how LLMs are optimized to reflect human values under complex, real-world conditions.

Looking beyond current trends, future research in reward design for LLM alignment is expected to catalyze a paradigm shift: from static rule-following to dynamic value co-creation. This evolution entails not merely optimizing models to follow predefined instructions or fixed preference signals, but enabling them to participate in the continuous, contextual negotiation of human values. A central research direction will likely focus on extracting and modeling values from collective human interactions, rather than relying solely on isolated annotations or expert-curated datasets. By learning from open-ended dialogues, debates, and feedback loops within communities, future reward models could begin to reflect pluralistic, evolving, and sometimes conflicting values, capturing the subtleties of human ethical reasoning. This calls for moving beyond scalar or static rewards toward distributions over preferences and context-aware value representations that can be adapted over time.

Another promising direction lies in socially interactive reward modeling, where LLMs continuously update their alignment objectives based on real-time user interactions. Instead of treating alignment as a one-off fine-tuning task, reward functions could become temporally adaptive, adjusting to shifting user expectations, norms, and goals. Such feedback might come not only from individual users but also from collaborative inputs like upvotes, comments, and corrections which is serving as implicit, fine-grained reward signals. To support generalization and adaptability, researchers are beginning to explore meta-reward learning, where the goal is not to learn a single reward model, but to endow LLMs with the capacity to learn how to learn reward models. This enables agents to quickly adapt alignment behavior in new tasks, cultural settings, or moral dilemmas, facilitating scalable deployment in diverse environments. Crucially, future reward design may also embrace a co-evolutionary perspective, positioning LLMs as not only learners but also contributors in the ongoing process of value formation. Through carefully designed interaction protocols, LLMs could surface ethical dilemmas, propose reflective questions, or generate alternative perspectives, thereby stimulating users to clarify and articulate their own preferences. In this way, alignment is no longer a one-way process, but a dynamic dialogue, one where human and machine understanding of values co-develop over time. As LLMs become integral to high-stakes applications, reward mechanisms will also need to incorporate governance constraints, institutional norms, and collective decision-making processes. This introduces challenges in multi-agent alignment, where reward models must balance the interests of multiple stakeholders under principles of fairness, accountability, and transparency. Furthermore, in the realm of embodied or multimodal AI systems, alignment will require reward signals that are grounded not only in language but in sensorimotor experiences and real-world context. This opens the door to rewards informed by spatial, temporal, and safety-critical factors, anchoring value alignment in the physical environment.

Altogether, these research directions reflect a fundamental shift in the role of reward mechanisms: from tools for task completion to instruments for social negotiation and ethical reasoning. As such, reward design will play a foundational role in shaping the next generation of trustworthy, human-centered AI systems, i.e., systems that do not merely follow static instructions, but dynamically align with the evolving values and needs of human society.
