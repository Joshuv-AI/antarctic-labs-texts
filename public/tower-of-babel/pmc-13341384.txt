Dose–Response Effect of Glucagon on Glucose, Beta‐Cell Secretion and Metabolism in Healthy Individuals
Authors: Sophie Betty Brock, Nina Lerberg Hansen, Elvar Már Sturlaugsson Jóhönnuson, Sasha Alexandra Sampson Kjeldsen, Nicolai J Wewer Albrechtsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimGlucagon is a key regulator of glucose and energy metabolism, yet the dose–response effects of glucagon on markers of metabolism in humans are not fully characterised. We investigated the dose‐dependent effects of glucagon on glucose metabolism, β‐cell secretion and markers of hepatic metabolism in healthy adults.Materials and MethodsFifteen healthy, lean adults underwent a 180‐min stepwise intravenous infusion of native human glucagon at six increasing doses (0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 10 and 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1). Plasma glucose, insulin, C‐peptide, cyclic AMP (cAMP), non‐esterified fatty acids, triglycerides, total amino acids and urea were measured repeatedly throughout the study.ResultsPlasma glucose increased in a dose‐dependent manner, with significant stepwise elevations observed from intermediate infusion rates and reaching peak levels at supraphysiological glucagon levels. Insulin and C‐peptide levels rose markedly, reflecting β‐cell activation. Plasma cAMP increased only during the highest glucagon infusion rate, despite earlier metabolic responses. Plasma non‐esterified fatty acids, triglycerides and total amino acids declined at higher glucagon doses, whilst plasma urea decreased gradually from the second infusion rate onward.ConclusionInfusion of glucagon results in dose‐dependent metabolic effects in healthy humans. The differential timing of glucose, lipid and amino acid responses relative to systemic cAMP elevations could be consistent with pathway‐specific activation thresholds rather than uniform signalling. These findings provide new insights into human glucagon physiology with potential relevance for metabolic diseases.Keywords: beta cell function, clinical physiology, clinical trial, dose‐response relationship, glucagon

Introduction

Glucagon is a peptide hormone secreted from pancreatic α‐cells in response to hypoglycaemia and protein intake. Together with insulin, glucagon plays a key role in the regulation of glucose‐ and energy metabolism, and dysregulated glucagon secretion contributes to the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases such as Type 2 diabetes and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In parallel, increasing attention has been directed towards glucagon's potential as a therapeutic target, particularly in the context of glucagon‐based co‐agonist [3, 6, 7, 8, 9].

In the liver, glucagon stimulates endogenous glucose production and regulates lipid‐ and amino acid metabolism [2, 3, 5, 10]. These actions are classically attributed to activation of the hepatic glucagon receptor, a G‐protein‐coupled receptor that primarily signals via the Gas–adenylyl cyclase–cAMP signalling pathway [5, 11]. Beyond its hepatic effects, glucagon also exerts insulinotropic actions on pancreatic β‐cells [12, 13, 14], thereby contributing to the regulation of post‐prandial glucose homeostasis and emphasising its role as an integrative metabolic hormone.

Whilst the principal metabolic actions of glucagon are well described, the dose‐dependent metabolic effects of glucagon in humans are not fully characterised. Most human studies have examined glucagon action using single‐dose infusions [12, 15, 16, 17, 18] or a limited range of infusion rates [19, 20], often focusing on specific metabolic outcomes rather than integrated metabolic responses across glucose‐, lipid‐ and amino acid metabolism [12, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Consequently, the relationships between increasing glucagon exposure, metabolic responses and activation of downstream signalling pathways remain incompletely understood.

In humans, glucagon administration has been shown to increase plasma cAMP levels [16] and plasma cAMP has been used as an indirect marker of glucagon receptor activation. However, the relationship between plasma cAMP and metabolic responses across different glucagon doses has not been systematically examined.

In the present study, we investigated the dose–response effects of stepwise intravenous glucagon infusion on glucose metabolism, β‐cell secretion and markers of hepatic metabolism in healthy individuals. Plasma cAMP was assessed as an exploratory and indirect marker of glucagon receptor signalling.

Materials and Methods

Study Approval

The study was conducted at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry, Copenhagen University Hospital Bispebjerg and Frederiksberg, Denmark. The study protocol was approved by the Scientific Ethical Committee of the Capital Region in Denmark (registration number H‐24042699) and registered with the Danish Data Protection Agency (registration number p‐2024‐17 351) and with ClinicalTrials.gov (Nr. NCT06921824). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Study participants provided both oral and written consent prior to inclusion.

Study Subjects

Participants were recruited through advertisement on the Trialtree platform. Inclusion criteria included healthy men and women between 25 and 70 years of age and a body mass index (BMI) ≤ 25 kg/m [2]. Exclusion criteria included diagnosis of diabetes (Type 1 or 2); pregnancy or breastfeeding; impaired liver or kidney function; inadequately treated high blood pressure; active or recent malignant disease; history of alcohol or substance abuse, and use of medications which could interfere with the study protocol. All participants took part in an information and screening visit prior to the study day.

Glucagon Infusions

Glucagon was administered as stepwise intravenous infusions at six predefined infusion rates: 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 10 and 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1. The infusion rates were selected to cover a broad range of glucagon exposure, from physiological to supraphysiological levels, allowing evaluation of dose–response relationships.

Glucagon stock solutions and infusion bags were prepared using stepwise dilutions in isotonic NaCl supplemented with human serum albumin to minimise peptide adsorption. Final infusion concentrations (2 ng/mL, 20 ng/mL and 1 μg/mL) were achieved by serial dilution of GlucaGen HypoKit.

The target infusion rate was achieved by adjusting the infusion pump speed using an Infusomat Space infusion pump (B. Braun Infusomat Space, B. Braun Melsungen AG, Germany). All glucagon solutions were prepared using GlucaGen HypoKit (Novo Nordisk A/S) dissolved in isotonic NaCl (9 mg/mL; B. Braun) with human serum albumin (5%; CSL Behring), which was added to minimise adsorption of glucagon to plastic surfaces.

Experimental Design

Each participant participated in one study day. Each participant served as their own control, with baseline measurements obtained prior to glucagon infusion. Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise and alcohol consumption for 48 h prior to the study day. Participants arrived between 08:00 and 08:30 at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry after an overnight (> 10 h) fast. Upon arrival, two peripheral catheters were inserted into the antecubital veins, one in each arm. One catheter was used for repeated blood sampling, and the other for intravenous glucagon infusion.

The glucagon infusion protocol was initiated shortly after arrival. After collection of a baseline blood sample (t = 0 min), the first glucagon infusion rate was initiated (0.01 ng × kg−1 × min−1, t = 0–30 min) (Figure 1). The glucagon infusion rate was subsequently increased in a stepwise manner every 30 min to 0.1, 0.5, 1, 10 and finally 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 at t = 30, 60, 90, 120 and 150 min, respectively. The glucagon infusion lasted a total of 180 min.

Biochemical Analysis

Plasma concentrations of glucose, insulin, and C‐peptide were measured using enzymatic and immunoassay‐based methods on a COBAS 8000 analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland).

Plasma glucagon concentrations were determined using a commercially available sandwich ELISA (Mercodia Glucagon ELISA, catalogue no. 10‐1271‐01, lot no. 36743; Mercodia AB, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All samples were analysed in duplicate, except for samples obtained at 180 min, which were analysed in singlicate due to limited plate capacity.

Plasma cAMP concentrations were measured using an ELISA (cAMP Parameter Assay Kit, catalogue no. KGE002B, lot no. P46830; Bio‐Techne Ireland Limited, Dublin 11, Ireland). Plasma total amino acid and non‐esterified fatty acid (NEFA) concentrations were determined using commercially available assays (l‐Amino Acid Assay Kit, catalogue no. ab65347, lot no. 2001013626; Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK; NEFA‐HR (2) Assay, catalogue nos. 994‐91801, 990‐91901 and 991‐91791, lot nos. 11N25, 18M24 and 15M25; FUJIFILM Wako Diagnostics, Lexington, Kentucky, USA).

All samples from each participant were analysed within the same assay run to minimise inter‐assay variability.

Statistical Methods

Data in text are shown as mean and 95% confidence interval unless stated otherwise. The sample size was based on power calculations demonstrating > 90% power to detect changes in plasma glucose. Data in tables are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data in figures are shown as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses and graphical presentation were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1. For all measured parameters (glucagon, glucose, insulin, C‐peptide, cAMP, NEFA, triglycerides, total amino acids and urea), mean values during each infusion rate period and at individual sampling time were compared with baseline (t = 0 min) and with the preceding period or sampling time using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Holm–Sidak's multiple comparisons test to correct for multiple testing. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants

Sixteen healthy participants (10 females and six males) were enrolled in the study. The mean age was 31 (27–36) years and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 21.7 (20.9–22.6) kg × m−2. All participants had normal HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose levels of 33.5 (31.6–35.4) mmol/mol and 5.1 (4.8–5.3) mmol/L, respectively. They had no chronic conditions. One participant was excluded on the morning of the study day due to unsuccessful catheter insertion, resulting in a total of 15 participants (nine females and six males) completing the study. No adverse events were observed, and no participants experienced side effects related to the glucagon infusion. Baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1.

Glucagon

Baseline plasma glucagon concentration was 6.7 (4.6–8.8) pmol/L. The 0.5 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate resulted in a significant increase in plasma glucagon to 9.4 (7.3–11.4) pmol/L compared with baseline (Figure 2A,B) (p < 0.01). Plasma glucagon concentrations continued to rise with increasing infusion rates, reaching 85.6 (74.6–96.5) pmol/L at 150 min during the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period, and peaking at 354 (321–386) pmol/L during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period. From an infusion rate of 0.5 ng × kg−1 × min−1 and onwards, plasma glucagon concentrations increased significantly in the first sample following each stepwise increase in infusion rate (all p < 0.01). Concentrations subsequently plateaued and reached steady‐state during the final 15 min of each infusion period.

Plasma Glucose

Plasma glucose increased in a dose–dependent manner (Figure 2C,D). Baseline plasma glucose concentration was 5.1 (4.9–5.3) mmol/L. During the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period, plasma glucose increased significantly to 6.1 (5.8–6.5) mmol/L and further to 7.5 (6.9–8.0) mmol/L during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period (both p < 0.01). This corresponded to a 21 (15–26) % increase at 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 and a 47 (35–59) % increase at 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 relative to baseline. Comparing individual sampling times to the preceding time point showed stepwise increases from 120 to 135 min (to 5.9 (5.5–6.2) mmol/L), from 135 to 150 min (to 6.4 (6.0–6.9) mmol/L) and from 150 to 165 min (to 7.5 (7.0–8.0) mmol/L) (all p < 0.01), with no further increase observed between 165 and 180 min.

Insulin and C‐Peptide

Glucagon infusion induced a marked rise in both insulin and C‐peptide levels, consistent with glucagon‐stimulated β‐cell activation and with the elevated plasma glucose concentrations (Figure 2E–H). Plasma insulin and C‐peptide increased significantly at and above the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate compared with baseline (both p < 0.01), and this was further enhanced during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period relative to the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period (both p < 0.01).

Plasma insulin was 45 (35–56) pmol/L at baseline and rose to 97 (76–118) pmol/L during the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period (Figure 2E,F) (p < 0.01). Plasma insulin further increased to 300 (246–354) pmol/L during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period (p < 0.01). Comparing each sample time with the previous sample showed significant increases in insulin from 120 to 165 min (all p < 0.01), with no further change thereafter.

Plasma C‐peptide was 513 (426–600) pmol/L at baseline and increased to 770 (636–904) pmol/L and 1600 (1395–1806) pmol/L during the 10 and 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate periods, respectively (Figure 2G,H) (both p < 0.01). This corresponded to a 50 (43–58) % and 222 (190–253) % increase relative to baseline. In contrast to insulin, C‐peptide continued to rise throughout the entire 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period, increasing sequentially at each sampling time and reaching a peak value of 1731 (1530–1931) pmol/L at 180 min.

The C‐peptide: insulin ratio was calculated as an indirect marker of insulin clearance (Figure S1). The ratio decreased significantly at and above the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate compared with baseline and declined further during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period compared with the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 period (all p < 0.01). No significant changes were observed at lower infusion rates.

Plasma cAMP

As an indicator of glucagon receptor signalling, plasma cAMP levels remained unchanged during all lower glucagon infusion rates and first increased during the highest infusion rate period (50 ng × kg−1 × min−1) (Figure 3A,B). Plasma cAMP was 57 (43–72) pmol/mL at baseline and increased significantly to 118 (78–157) pmol/mL during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period (p < 0.05). Plasma concentrations of cAMP showed no significant difference from baseline values at any infusion rate from the lowest dose through to the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period. Comparing each sample time with the preceding sample time showed a significant increase from 150 to 165 min (p < 0.05), corresponding to the initiation of the highest infusion rate.

Plasma Non‐Esterified Fatty Acids

Plasma non‐esterified fatty acid (NEFA) concentrations decreased during glucagon infusion (Figure 4A). Baseline NEFA levels were 0.39 (0.28–0.5) mmol/L. A significant decrease was first observed at 150 min (to 0.28 (0.2–0.37) mmol/L) compared with baseline (p < 0.05), corresponding to 30 min into the 10 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period. NEFA levels declined further to 0.17 (0.12–0.22) mmol/L during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period. Comparing each sample time with the preceding sample time showed significant declines from 135 to 150, 150 to 165 and 165 to 180 min (all p < 0.01).

Plasma Triglycerides

Glucagon infusion resulted in a modest but significant reduction in plasma triglycerides (Figure 4B). Plasma triglyceride concentrations decreased significantly during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period compared with baseline (p < 0.01). Triglycerides were 0.7 (0.5–0.8) mmol/L at baseline and decreased to 0.5 (0.4–0.7) mmol/L during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period. Relative to baseline, concentrations were significantly reduced from 165 min onwards (all p < 0.01).

Total Plasma Amino Acids

Total plasma amino acid concentrations decreased during glucagon infusion (Figure 4C). Total amino acid concentrations were significantly reduced during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period compared with baseline (p < 0.01). Total amino acids had a baseline concentration of 1104 (1016–1193) μmol/L, which decreased to 903 (805–1001) μmol/L during the 50 ng × kg−1 × min−1 infusion rate period. Relative to mean baseline values, total amino acids were significantly lower from 165 min and onwards (all p < 0.01). No significant changes were detected between consecutive sampling times.

Plasma Urea

Plasma urea decreased during glucagon infusion (Figure 4D). Baseline urea concentration was 4.7 (4.1–5.3) mmol/L. Levels remained unchanged during the initial infusion rate but decreased significantly at 0.1 ng × kg−1 × min−1 (p < 0.05) and at all higher infusion rates (all p < 0.01). When each sampling time was compared with baseline, urea was significantly reduced from 75 min onward (all p < 0.05). No significant changes were detected between consecutive sampling times.

Discussion

In this study, stepwise intravenous glucagon infusion in healthy individuals induced dose–dependent increases in plasma glucose, insulin and C‐peptide, whereas plasma cAMP increased significantly only at the highest infusion rate. In parallel, markers of hepatic metabolism, including total amino acids, urea, NEFA and triglycerides, declined following glucagon infusion. Collectively, these findings could indicate that glucagon's metabolic actions in humans involve distinct, dose‐dependent physiological responses. Importantly, this conclusion is based on functional metabolic observations rather than direct measurements of intracellular signalling pathways and therefore describes physiological responses rather than definitive differences in intracellular signalling mechanisms. The present study was designed to assess integrated physiological responses to glucagon in vivo rather than isolate individual molecular pathways. Whilst in vitro approaches may provide mechanistic insights, they do not capture the complex endocrine interactions present in humans. The observed plateau in plasma glucagon concentrations within each infusion step likely reflects rapid attainment of steady state, consistent with the short circulating half‐life of glucagon.

To interpret these dose‐dependent physiological responses, it is important to consider the limitations of plasma cAMP as an indirect marker of intracellular glucagon signalling. Glucagon is well known to stimulate intracellular cAMP production [5] and intravenous glucagon administration has previously been shown to increase plasma cAMP concentrations in humans [16]. However, plasma cAMP is an extracellular measure and does not necessarily reflect intracellular cAMP concentrations or signalling dynamics in hepatocytes. Thus, the absence of a measurable increase in plasma cAMP at lower glucagon infusion rates does not exclude cAMP‐mediated signalling in the liver. Local and transient cAMP production within hepatocytes may be compartmentalised and therefore not necessarily reflected in circulating cAMP levels at the temporal resolution applied in the present study.

Plasma glucose increased in a dose‐dependent manner, with modest elevations at physiological glucagon concentrations and more pronounced increases at supraphysiological concentrations. The early glucose response at lower infusion rates may primarily reflect increased hepatic glycogenolysis rather than gluconeogenesis, as these processes may differ in their sensitivity to glucagon [1, 21]. At higher infusion rates, the concomitant rise in insulin likely limited further glucose elevations, highlighting the counter‐regulatory relationship between glucagon and insulin in glucose homeostasis [3, 4, 11]. Accordingly, the absence of further increases in plasma at the highest glucagon doses may reflect a physiological ceiling effect rather than a lack of continued glucagon action.

Notably, plasma glucose increased before any detectable rise in plasma cAMP (Figure S2A). This temporal dissociation suggests that early glucagon‐induced stimulation of hepatic glucose production does not result in a measurable spillover of cAMP into the systemic circulation. Thus, the lack of a systemic cAMP response does not preclude hepatic Gas signalling but may also reflect the contribution of non‐cAMP‐dependent signalling. Circulating cAMP may also reflect inter‐organ signalling, with previous studies suggesting a hepatorenal axis whereby hepatic cAMP is released into circulation and influences renal function.

In our study, NEFA levels declined following glucagon infusion, preceding any detectable increase in plasma cAMP (Figure S2B). This reduction is most likely explained by the concomitant rise in insulin and the subsequent suppression of peripheral lipolysis [22], rather than a direct effect of glucagon on adipose tissue. This interpretation is consistent with previous human studies showing limited glucagon receptor expression in adipocytes [23]. Moreover, the presence of concomitant hyperinsulinemia during human infusion studies complicates the interpretation of potential direct glucagon effects on lipid metabolism.

In contrast to glucose and NEFA, total plasma amino acids declined only at higher glucagon infusion rates and in parallel with the increase in plasma cAMP (Figure S2C). This pattern is consistent with established cAMP‐dependent actions of glucagon on hepatic amino acid uptake and ureagenesis [2, 5]. The requirement for higher glucagon doses to reduce circulating amino acids is consistent with observations from previous experimental and human studies of the liver‐α‐cell axis, suggesting that amino acid metabolism may be less sensitive to glucagon stimulation than glucose metabolism [2, 24, 25].

The gradual decline in plasma urea observed during glucagon infusion is consistent with previous human studies, which similarly report no acute rise in urea [25]. In contrast, rodent studies report rapid increases in plasma urea, reflecting hepatic amino acid catabolism [26]. Together, these findings indicate that plasma urea is not a reliable marker of acute amino acid catabolism in humans, and that accurate assessment likely requires dedicated protocols to quantify urea production rates. This decrease may reflect the combined effects of insulin‐mediated peripheral amino acid uptake and potential changes in renal urea clearance.

Collectively, the differential timing and dose–dependency of glucose, lipid and amino acid responses suggests that glucagon's metabolic effects are not mediated by a single uniform signalling mechanism, but rather by pathway‐specific signalling requirements with distinct activation thresholds.

An improved understanding of pathway‐specific glucagon signalling is particularly relevant considering the growing interest in glucagon‐based co‐agonists. Glucagon/GLP‐1 dual agonists and glucagon/GLP‐1/GIP triple agonists have shown promise in the treatment of metabolic diseases such as Type 2 diabetes, obesity and MASLD [6, 7, 9]. Whilst some dual agonists have advanced to Phase III clinical trials [27], clinical outcomes vary substantially between compounds, with differences in efficacy on weight loss or glycemic control and some compounds being discontinued due to adverse effects [8].

One potential way to explain such differences is biassed signalling, which refers to preferential activation of specific downstream signalling pathways by certain ligands [28]. In the present study, biassed signalling is considered only as a conceptual framework and not as a directly demonstrated mechanism. We did not assess intracellular signalling bias, β‐arrestin recruitment or pathway‐specific activation at the glucagon receptor. Rather, the relevance of this concept is that increasing glucagon receptor stimulation may, in principle, engage downstream signalling outputs to different extents, including canonical Gαs–cAMP signalling, compartmentalised cAMP signalling, β‐arrestin‐related pathways or other downstream mechanisms. Thus, the observed dissociation between metabolic responses and circulating cAMP should be interpreted as compatible with pathway‐selective glucagon action, but not as direct evidence of biassed signalling. Glucagon infusion induced a marked increase in both insulin and C‐peptide. Whilst plasma insulin reached a plateau during the highest infusion rate, C‐peptide continued to rise, indicating sustained β‐cell secretory activity. The decline in C‐peptide‐to‐insulin ratio observed at the highest infusion rates may be compatible with a decrease in insulin clearance; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and would require dedicated studies accounting for concomitant changes in glucose levels.

Notably, the increase in C‐peptide was disproportionate to the rise in plasma glucose, supporting a direct insulinotropic effect of glucagon that is not solely driven by prevailing glucose concentrations. Potential mechanisms include direct stimulation of pancreatic β‐cells via glucagon receptors or indirect activation through GLP‐1‐related signalling [29].

A key strength of this study is the stepwise, broad‐range glucagon infusion protocol, enabling detailed characterisation of dose–response relationships across multiple metabolic pathways within the same individuals. Limitations include the relatively small sample size, restriction to healthy lean adults, the short‐term exposure and the indirect assessment of intracellular signalling using plasma cAMP. A further limitation is the sample size, which precluded subgroup analyses such as sex‐specific comparisons. Glucagon receptor antagonists have been used in preclinical studies to explore glucagon physiology; however, such approaches are not currently available for clinical investigation in humans. The study was conducted under fasting conditions, and responses may differ in postprandial settings where insulin and nutrient flux are altered. Differences between expected and measured circulating glucagon concentrations may reflect variability in peptide stability, preparation and assay characteristics, as previously reported [30]. Inter‐individual variability was limited, although minor temporal differences in response kinetics were observed. Additional clinically relevant endpoints such as appetite measures, energy expenditure and renal electrolyte handling were not assessed.

Collectively, these findings provide new insights into human glucagon physiology and could indicate that glucagon's metabolic actions involve pathway‐specific, dose–dependent physiological responses, with potential relevance for diseases characterised by dysregulated glucagon secretion, including Type 2 diabetes and MASLD.
