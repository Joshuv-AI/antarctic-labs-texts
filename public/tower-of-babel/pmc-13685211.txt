Investigating long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) HOTAIR expression changes in ischemic stroke patients
Authors: Alia Saberi, Niloufar Nabavi, Amirreza Ghayeghran, Zivar Salehi, Yaser Moaddabi, Mohammad Ebrahim Ghaffari, Haniyeh Haghanipor, Sara Khoshdooz, Ali Bonyad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke (IS), a leading global health problem, is caused by interrupted blood flow in the brain, often due to clotting or artery constriction. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), particularly HOX transcript antisense intergenic RNA (HOTAIR), are pivotal in stroke pathophysiology.AimThis study focused on the expression levels of HOTAIR lncRNA in ischemic stroke patients.Methods and materials: This comparative cross-sectional study with 100 participants: 50 IS patients and 50 patients without IS serving as controls. The study gathered demographic data, disease specifics, and medication history. Blood samples were analyzed for HOTAIR lncRNA expression levels.ResultsThis study showed significantly higher expression levels of HOTAIR lncRNA among IS patients compared to the control group (5.73 ± 0.75 vs. 2.35 ± 0.29; P < 0.001), with an average increase of 3.19 units compared to the control group (P < 0.001) after adjusting the independent variables. Additionally, participants with hypertension had an average increase of 0.36 units in of HOTAIR lncRNA expression compared to the control group (P = 0.041).ConclusionOur study found that HOTAIR lncRNA levels are significantly higher in IS patients and may be associated with a history of hypertension. diseases. This suggests that HOTAIR lncRNA could be a useful biomarker for stroke and related conditions.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Cerebrovascular disease, Long non-coding RNA HOTAIR

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS), a leading global health concern, is the most prevalent category of stroke and constitutes a significant cause of long-term disability and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. It occurs when there is an interruption to cerebral blood flow, frequently due to obstructions such as blood clots or the narrowing of arteries from atherosclerosis [3]. The primary causes of stroke in the adult population consist of established cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and smoking [4]. However, the global number of IS deaths has increased over the past three decades, it is expected to rise to 4.9 million by 2030 [5].

The pathophysiology of IS involves a cascade of molecular and cellular events precipitated by reduced blood flow and oxygen supply to the brain, which leads to a depletion of ATP, causing failure of ion pumps in the neuronal cell membrane and subsequent ionic imbalance. The buildup of intracellular calcium, in particular, triggers a series of deleterious pathways including excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammation, which culminate in neuronal death through necrosis or apoptosis [6]. The expression, function, and mechanism of Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), typically longer than 200 nucleotides, and microRNAs (miRNAs) in IS have garnered significant attention in recent years. Numerous lncRNAs with aberrant expression have been found in both animal models of stroke and human patients [7, 8].

lncRNAs are increasingly recognized for their role in gene expression regulation at the transcriptional level, including chromatin remodeling and modification, as well as post-transcriptional processes such as RNA degradation [9]. Reports showed that ischemia may cause extensive changes in lncRNA in the pathophysiological process of stroke and has an active role in stroke; It has also been shown that these factors have a huge impact on the development of ischemic brain damage [10]. HOX antisense intergenic RNA (HOTAIR), a particular lncRNA discovered in 2007 and located on chromosome 12, is implicated in gene silencing through its interaction with the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2), influencing various physiological processes [11]. Previous studies revealed that elevated HOTAIR levels correlate with worse outcomes in ischemic events. Also, researchers indicated that HOTAIR upregulation in IS models contributes to increased neuronal apoptosis, infarct size, and neurological deficits [12].

Additionally, HOTAIR’s interaction with miR-148a-3p and KLF6 suggests a complex regulatory network affecting cell viability, inflammation, and immune responses, which are critical in stroke pathogenesis [13]. HOTAIR’s role in maintaining blood-brain barrier integrity and its association with EZH2, a histone methyltransferase involved in ischemia/reperfusion injury, further supports its significance [14]. By analyzing the expression patterns of HOTAIR lncRNA, this research aims to elucidate its correlation with IS. Understanding these expression changes may unlock new insights into the gene’s role in the disease, potentially paving the way for its application in both diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Methods

Study design

This comparative case-control study was conducted at Poursina Hospital, an academic stroke center in the North of Iran. We meticulously evaluated two distinct groups: one comprising 50 patients with a recent diagnosis of IS, and a control group of 50 individuals without IS visiting the Neurology Clinic for routine examinations. Both groups included 50 individuals each, ensuring a balanced comparison in age and gender, and sufficient representation of both groups in the study.

Inclusion criteria

The patient who experienced IS (case) group was stringently chosen from individuals over the age of 18 admitted to the center, with confirmed diagnoses of IS by board-certified neurologists based on comprehensive clinical assessments and imaging studies such as CT scans and MRIs based on the American Heart Association (AHA)/American Stroke Association (ASA) guidelines. A thorough process ensured that only patients meeting specific inclusion criteria were considered. For controls recruited individuals who were matched with cases for age and gender.

Exclusion criteria

Those with conditions such as stroke history, autoimmune diseases, vasculitic diseases, angiopathies, drug use or abuse, or vascular dissection were excluded.

Informed consent was obtained from each patient or their legal proxies, guaranteeing their understanding and agreement to participate in the study.

For all cases and control groups, data collection was conducted systematically using a structured questionnaire to compile comprehensive demographic and clinical data, including information on underlying diseases and medication history, carefully recorded from the patient’s medical files. Also, 2 ml of venous blood of each subject was collected, and centrifuged for further use.

RNA extraction, and real-time polymerase chain reaction

The RNA extraction process was conducted immediately in the laboratory using fresh blood samples. For this purpose, we employed the High Pure miRNA Isolation Kit produced by Roche, Germany. Following extraction, the concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a Nanodrop device.

The design of the primers necessary for our experiments was accomplished using Oligo7 software. Subsequently, we synthesized cDNA from the extracted RNA. This step was crucial for preparing the samples for the next phase of our analysis.

The primary objective was to measure the expression rate of HOTAIR lncRNA, which was performed using a Real-time PCR system. For the purpose of ensuring accuracy and reliability in our measurements, we selected the U48 gene as a reference gene. The expression levels of HOTAIR lncRNA were compared and normalized based on the expression of the U48 gene.

Statistical analysis

In our study, we used frequencies and percentages to describe qualitative data and means with standard deviations for quantitative data. Before data analysis, we checked statistical assumptions to align with our research goals. The Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests were applied to check for normality and homogeneity of variances, respectively. Depending on these results, we used independent t-tests or the Mann-Whitney test for quantitative data and the chi-square for qualitative data. Covariance analysis and linear regression were performed to adjust for confounders, with data transformations applied when assumptions were not met. We conducted all analyses with SPSS version 26, maintaining a significance level of P < 0.05.

Discussion

Our case-control study evaluated the expression levels of HOTAIR lncRNA in patients who experienced IS compared to individuals without IS and it revealed that the HOTAIR lncRNA expression was significantly higher in IS patients and the difference remained significant across various demographics and clinical subgroups. Additionally, hypertension was associated with an increase in HOTAIR expression, while other conditions and medication use did not significantly affect expression levels, suggesting HOTAIR’s specific role in stroke pathology.

Previous studies demonstrated that HOTAIR expression was significantly positively correlated with the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, indicating that higher HOTAIR levels may worsen neurological deficits and functional activities in cerebrovascular stroke (CVS) patients. The study also found that HOTAIR was positively correlated with systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP), suggesting a possible role in hypertension-related vascular changes [15]. However, our study expands on this knowledge by specifically investigating HOTAIR expression changes in ischemic stroke patients in Iranian compared to a control group, assessing its association with hypertension. Another study was conducted on changes in the expression of lncRNA in the blood of humans following IS compared to healthy controls (HCs) and patients with transient ischemic attack (TIA). The study found that different lncRNAs altered their expression levels over time and that these changes, as well as the timing of these changes in IS patients, varied between men and women. Notably, some of the lncRNAs with altered expression were located near or within genomic regions previously associated with stroke risk, suggesting they might play a regulatory role in these loci [16]. Other research by Huang et al., reported the effects of lncRNA HOTAIR in the context of IS, utilizing both in vivo and in vitro models for its investigation. Results revealed that HOTAIR expression significantly rises in mice with permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO) following ischemic incidents. This increased expression of HOTAIR correlates with larger areas of cerebral infarction, more extensive apoptosis of neural cells, and deterioration in neurological functions. Importantly, the study found that reducing HOTAIR levels leads to notable improvements in neurological function, as well as decreases in cerebral infarction, cell apoptosis, and inflammation. The mechanism involves HOTAIR’s interaction with KLF6, as it competes for binding with miR-148a-3p, which affects the survival of cells subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) [12]. Our study corroborates previous findings by demonstrating elevated HOTAIR levels in IS patients, yet lacks pre-stroke samples to establish a baseline, underscoring the importance of longitudinal studies in understanding HOTAIR’s role in cerebrovascular events.

Also, The HOTAIR is potentially implicated in the development of hypertension through several mechanisms. It may affect the function of vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells, key players in maintaining vascular tone and blood pressure regulation. Dysregulated HOTAIR expression can lead to vascular dysfunction, contributing to increased peripheral resistance and endothelial dysfunction [17]. HOTAIR is also related to inflammatory pathways. Elevated expression of HOTAIR might promote inflammation in the vascular system, leading to damage and stiffness that can raise blood pressure [18]. Moreover, HOTAIR interacts with microRNAs, some of which are involved in blood pressure regulation. This interaction could indirectly affect vascular tone and blood pressure [19].

While our study indicated that the coexistence of diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular conditions did not affect HOTAIR expression, prior research focusing on these specific diseases reported diverse outcomes. Sargazi et al. identified that three HOTAIR gene polymorphisms (rs920778, rs12826786, and rs4759314) increased the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), while one (rs1899663) decreased it. These genetic variations also correlated with cholesterol and blood sugar level changes in T2DM patients, with haplotype analysis indicating that specific polymorphism combinations might increase T2DM risk [20]. Another study found that HOTAIR levels in the serum are higher in patients with type 2 diabetes than in healthy individuals, suggesting its potential as a non-invasive marker for diabetes and its complications [21].

Additional support for these findings came from a study in China. Their study linked IS to lncRNA’s influence on the adipocytokine signaling pathway, leading to acute stroke events [22]. Lei Yang’s 2016 study further identified the role of HOTAIR in the development of ischemic infarcts, particularly examining its relationship with NOX2 levels under hypoxia-induced conditions. Using an ischemic infarct mouse model and hypoxia-treated HT22 cells, the research revealed a significant increase in HOTAIR expression, which corresponded with elevated NOX2 production. The findings showed that silencing HOTAIR not only improved cerebral function in mice with ischemic infarcts but also led to a marked reduction in NOX2 production. The study concludes that heightened HOTAIR expression aggravates hypoxia-induced ischemic infarcts through the regulation of NOX2, shedding light on the molecular dynamics of ischemic infarct development [23].

Another study offered new insights for stroke treatment and pharmacotherapy development investigated the role of HOTAIR in the context of neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) and its impact on human brain microvascular endothelial cells (hBMVECs) during oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) injury. It found that HOTAIR levels were markedly increased in neonatal HIE patients and in an OGD/R in vitro model. Knockdown of HOTAIR in hBMVECs led to decreased cell viability, increased cell permeability and apoptosis, and reduced tight junction protein expression. However, these effects were reversed by overexpressing EZH2, a protein associated with HOTAIR [24].

Our study indicated no significant disparity in the comorbidity of diseases such as diabetes history, hypertension history, hyperlipidemia history, heart failure, seizure (epilepsy) and dementia between the two groups examined, which could influence gene expression. The study revealed that the HOTAIR lncRNA expression levels did not show a significant correlation with these comorbidities, suggesting that HOTAIR may not be involved in the pathophysiology of these diseases. The inconsistency might be attributed to variations in sample size and genetic sequencing methods, with the sample size being appropriate for the study’s focused aim on HOTAIR RNA and these diseases. Notably, our research focuses on gene expression analysis, while some previous studies have investigated polymorphisms in this context.

This study encountered several limitations that may have influenced its results. One of the limitations of our study is that we did not separately analyze whether comorbid conditions such as Diabetes, Hypertension, Hyperlipidemia, Heart Failure, Epilepsy, and Dementia could influence HOTAIR expression in the control group before evaluating their effects in IS patients. While our study shows that HOTAIR expression is significantly elevated in IS patients compared to controls, the study design does not allow us to conclude whether age, gender, or comorbid conditions independently influence HOTAIR expression within each group. Another limitation is the lack of analysis on whether therapeutic interventions influence HOTAIR expression. Although we found no significant effect of antihypertensive or cardiac medications,

This key limitation highlights the need for future studies with larger cohorts and longitudinal designs is required to validate HOTAIR’s role in IS diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic response. Also, they should focus on using HOTAIR lncRNA expression levels in blood samples as a supplementary diagnostic tool for IS. Prospective studies are needed to assess the predictive value of HOTAIR lncRNA in stroke incidence. Further areas of study include longitudinal tracking of HOTAIR lncRNA expression in at-risk individuals, exploring interactions with other genetic and environmental stroke risk factors, and examining the potential of HOTAIR lncRNA as a target for stroke treatment and prevention. Population-based studies would also be beneficial to understand how HOTAIR lncRNA expression varies across different groups and its impact on stroke susceptibility. These research efforts can advance our understanding of HOTAIR lncRNA in IS, leading to improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

Our study indicated that HOTAIR lncRNA expression levels are significantly elevated in patients with IS and may be associated with a history of hypertension. These findings suggest the potential of HOTAIR lncRNA as a biomarker for IS and possibly other related diseases. Further research should assess HOTAIR lncRNA’s utility in stroke diagnosis and risk prediction, as well as its potential as a treatment target, with a focus on diverse populations to enhance stroke care.
