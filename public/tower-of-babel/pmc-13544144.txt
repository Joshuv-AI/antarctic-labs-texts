Newcastle Disease Virus – Induced Interferons Regulate PD-L1 and MICA Expression in Human Tumor and Normal Cells
Authors: Teridah Ernala Ginting, Young Othiwi Larasati, Vina Cornelia, Nur Rahmaniah Hidayat, Salomo Christian, Kamaluddin Zarkasie, Irawan Yusuf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore whether NDV and/or NDV-induced IFNs regulate programmed death-1 ligand (PD-L1) expression and modulate the balance between inhibitory and stimulatory immune signals.Materials and MethodsHuman non-malignant (NB1RGB and HEK293) and tumor (A549 and U87MG) cell lines were used. Cells were either infected with Newcastle disease virus (NDV) LaSota strain or treated with interferons (IFNs). PD-L1 expression was analyzed by flow cytometry, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence. The relationship between PD-L1, as an immune inhibitor, and major histocompatibility complex class I polypeptide-related sequence A (MICA), as an immune activator, was evaluated using duplex chromogenic in situ hybridization. To investigate whether the NDV-induced shift in the PD-L1/MICA axis was sufficient to activate natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) receptor-possessing cells within peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), IFN-γ production was measured in PBMCs following 24 hours of co-culture with tumor and normal cell lines previously infected with NDV or treated with IFN-λ.ResultsNDV infection and IFN treatment resulted in a reduction of PD-L1 expressions in specific cell types. This decrease was accompanied by a concurrent increase in MICA expression, as demonstrated by in situ hybridization. The shift in balance between PD-L1 and MICA correlated with enhanced activation of NKG2D receptor-expressing immune cells, evidenced by increased IFN-γ secretion.ConclusionThese findings suggest that NDV may exert antitumor effects by modulating the balance between inhibitory checkpoint molecules and stimulatory ligands, specifically through downregulation of PD-L1 and upregulation of MICA. This mechanism highlights the potential of NDV to enhance cytotoxic immune responses and supports its further development as a novel strategy in cancer immunotherapy.Keywords: Newcastle disease virus, PD-L1, MICA, NKG2D, interferons, oncolytic virotherapy

Introduction

The effectiveness of anticancer immunotherapy is determined by its potential to initiate a tumor-targeting immune response, its ability to improve an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and its sensitivity to immune effector mechanisms.1 These factors have been identified as essential in determining the success of cancer treatment. One promising cancer therapy approach is oncolytic viruses, which selectively replicate, kill, and spread within tumor cells.2,3

Newcastle disease virus, a bird pathogen, has been extensively studied for decades as a potent antitumor agent.4 The mechanism of NDV oncolytic can be direct killing or indirect through stimulation of antitumor pro-inflammatory mediators and antigen-independent or dependent lysis of virus-infected cells by natural killer (NK) and cluster of differentiation 8 positive T (CD8+ T) cells.5–7 Upon infection, NDV as RNA virus induces different types of interferons (IFNs) strongly through the upregulation of interferon regulatory factors (IRF)-1 and 3.8,9 Previous studies have shown that NDV induced distinct IFN production in tumors and normal cells. In tumor cells, non-lytic NDV induced high levels of IFN type I (IFN-α, β) and type III (IFN-λ),10 while normal cells mainly produced IFN-λ. The redundant IFNs effect led to higher apoptosis in tumor cells.11 These interferon-mediated effects are of particular relevance to anti-tumor immunity, as IFNs are known to modulate the expression of both inhibitory and activating immune ligands on the surface of infected cells, thereby influencing the responsiveness of immune effector cells.12

The immune system recognizes cancer cells as foreign bodies that induce the antitumor population, mainly cytotoxic T cells or CD8+ and NK cells, to infiltrate, bind, and kill tumor cells. Natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) receptor is expressed in NK cells and many T cells, such as natural killer T (NKT), CD8+ T, and gamma delta T (γδT) cells. While NKG2D functions as a direct cytotoxicity-mediating receptor on NK cells, it acts as a co-stimulatory receptor in T cells. NKG2D ligands (NKG2DL) activate NK cells upon receptor binding, thereby promoting tumor cell killing.13 One prominent NKG2DL is Major Histocompatibility Complex class I polypeptide-related sequence A (MICA) gene, encoded by a highly polymorphic gene and induced by cellular stress, damage, or malignant transformation of cells, acting as a “kill me” signal to immune effector cells. Therefore, tumor cells with upregulated MICA are more susceptible to NKG2D-mediated cytotoxic immune attack.14–18

In a physiological condition, there is a regulation to balance the immune response sufficiently to minimize damage caused by the response, known as an immune checkpoint.19 Programmed death-1 receptor (PD-1) is one of the immune checkpoint molecules found on cells’ surface, and its interaction with ligands on others, such as PD-L1 (B7-H1) or PD-L2 (B7-DC), causes T cell inactivation. Some tumor cell types express high PD-L1 molecules and are seen as a mechanism to escape immune surveillance.20–25 Therefore, the intervention of immune checkpoint molecules interaction with their ligands has enhanced antitumor responses.26–28 This has provided the rationale for combining immune checkpoint inhibitors with other antitumor strategies, to further potentiate immune-mediated tumor killing.

Oncolytic viruses activate innate or adaptive antitumor immune effector cells,29–31 and their combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been shown to potentiate T-cell response against tumor. However, checkpoint inhibitors combined with oncolytic virotherapy can have the added effect of repairing antiviral T-cell responses, which normally act to restrict viral replication, representing a competing effect that warrants consideration. The extent of PD-L1 modulation appears to be virus-dependent; oncolytic reovirus treatment alone did not result in significant PD-L1 upregulation,28 whereas NDV infection led to the upregulation of PD-L1 and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) in tumor-grafted mice.29,30 Notably, combination therapy of NDV with checkpoint molecule inhibitors resulted in a marked enhancement of the antitumor immune effect, leading to the rejection of local and abscopal tumors, highlighting the therapeutic potential of combining oncolytic NDV with checkpoint blockade.29,30 Consistently, cytotoxic immune response driven by CD8+ T and NK cells has been shown to play an important role in the early phase of oncolytic treatment, as demonstrated in studies using oncolysis reovirus and adenovirus treatment in patients.32–35

While prior studies report NDV-mediated upregulation of PD-L1 in tumor-bearing mice,30 it remains unknown whether NDV-induced IFNs simultaneously regulate MICA expression, and whether the balance between these inhibitory and stimulatory ligands determines NKG2D-mediated immune activation. To our knowledge, no published study has examined the coordinated regulation of PD-L1 and MICA by NDV or NDV-induced type III interferon (IFN-λ) in tumor and normal cell lines. Here, we hypothesize that NDV and its IFNs shift the PD-L1/MICA balance in a cell-type-specific manner. We further hypothesize that this balance determines NKG2D receptor activation in cells possessing NKG2D receptors in PBMCs. This work provides an early fundament of NDV as a dual-checkpoint modulation strategy and identifies the PD-L1/MICA ratio as a potential biomarker for oncolytic immunotherapy response. Different tumors and normal types of cell lines are used to narrow individual systemic influences in the in vivo study.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human non-malignant cell lines, specifically NB1RGB (skin fibroblast) and HEK293 (transformed embryonic kidney), in addition to tumor cell lines, ie, A549 (non-small lung carcinoma) and U87MG (glioblastoma multiforme), were purchased from RIKEN BioResource Center – Cell Bank (Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan) or American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). Subsequently, the mentioned cell lines were cultured in MEMα, DMEM, F-12 Kaighn’s, and DMEM high glucose, as well as supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Our previous studies reported that tumor cells A549 and U87MG tumor cell lines expressed high IFN-α, -β, and -λ, while NB1RGB, HEK293 non-malignant lines expressed low IFN-α, -β, and high IFN-λ after NDV infection. All media and supplements utilized in this research were provided by Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA).10

Virus

Lentogenic NDV LaSota strain was obtained from PT. IPB-Shigeta Animal Pharmaceuticals, Bogor, Indonesia. Virus was then inoculated in the allantoic cavity of 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs. After 4 days, the allantoic fluid was collected and cleared from debris at 5000 x g, 4°C, for 30 minutes. To determine the virus amount for a uniform multiplicity of infection (MOI) across the cell lines being infected in the study, titration of infectious particles was confirmed by endpoint dilution assay tissue culture infective dose 50 (TCID50) in A549 cells according to our previous method.10,11 All experiments involving a live virus and cell culture were conducted in biosafety level-2 containment of Mochtar Riady Institute for Nanotechnology (MRIN).

NDV Infection

Confluent normal and tumor cells were prepared in 6- or 12-well plates or coverslips, as appropriate for the assay. Plates were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Gibco) and infected with NDV at the MOI 0.01 or 0.001 for 60 minutes at 37°C to allow virus adsorption. Subsequently, the excess virus was removed, and cells were washed twice with PBS. Following viral adsorption, cells were maintained in maintenance medium (identical to growth medium except FBS was replaced with 0.2% BSA from Gibco) to prevent viral neutralization and serum-mediated survival signaling, and to maintain cell monolayer integrity for 72 hours.

PD-L1 Expression After NDV Infection by Flow Cytometry and Western Blot

To determine whether PD-L1 protein was expressed on the surface of normal (NB1RGB and HEK293) and tumor (A549 and U87MG), and to understand the effect of NDV infection on PD-L1 expression, cell lines were infected with NDV at 0.01 and 0.001 MOI as described in the infection method and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C. Mock-infected cells were prepared for negative infection control. Cells were then washed twice with corresponding media without FBS and then detached with Accutase (Gibco). The infectious virus was inactivated with 10% formalin, and to detect PD-L1 protein on the surface, cells were incubated with human anti-PD-L1 alexa fluor 488-conjugated (Novus Biologicals, LLC, Littleton, CO, USA) in PBS (1:1000) for 60 minutes in the dark. Similar independent procedure was performed to collect the cell lysate from HEK293 and U87MG cell lines for Western blot, as the representatives of normal and tumor cells, respectively.

After we obtained the status of PD-L1 expression, we assessed the kinetics of cell viability in comparison of PD-L1 by using flow cytometry. NB1RGB, A549 and U87MG were infected with NDV at 0.01 and 0.001 MOI for 72 hours. Mock-infected cells were prepared as negative infection control. All groups were prepared in triplicate. Cells were collected at 24, 48, and 72 hours and assessed against PD-L1 and propidium iodide (PI) by using flow cytometry. PI is a membrane-impermeable fluorescent dye that binds to DNA and selectively enters non-viable cells with compromised plasma membrane integrity; therefore PI level is an indicator of cell death.36 After cells were incubated with human anti-PD-L1 alexa fluor 488-conjugated (Novus Biologicals) in PBS (1:1000) for 60 minutes in the dark, cells were incubated with PI (1 μg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 minutes at room temperature prior to acquisition, and PI-positive cells were considered non-viable. The level of PD-L1 expression was analyzed using flow cytometer BD Accuri (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) in the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) green stain FL1 channel, while PI expression was analyzed in phycoerythrin (PE) blue stain FL2 channel. Meanwhile, the raw data were collected using BD CSampler software 1.0.264.21 (Becton Dickinson) and analyzed with FlowJo 10.5.3 (BD Bioscience). Protein expression of PD-L1 was tested by Western blot using polyclonal goat immunoglobulin G (IgG) anti-human PD-L1/B1-H1 (R&D Systems, MN, USA, cat. no. AF156) as primary antibody and donkey anti-goat IgG (IRDye 800CW, Lincoln, Nebraska, US, cat. no. 925–32214) as secondary antibody. House-keeping protein on both cells, β-actin, was detected using monoclonal mouse IgG anti β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. A5316-2ML) as primary antibody and goat anti-mouse IgG (IRDye 800CW) as secondary antibody. Densitometric analysis of Western blot bands was performed in Fiji Software (ImageJ, NIH). All blot images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, processed with a single Despeckle filter to reduce speckle noise. Band intensities were quantified by measuring the area under each densitometric peak. Protein concentrations were normalized against β-actin, and then were represented as fold change relative to mock (Mock=1).

In situ Hybridization

Chromogenic in situ hybridization was performed using RNAscope™ 2.5 HD Duplex Assay, including control, preamplifier, and amplifier probes (Advanced Cell Diagnostics/ACDBio, Hayward, CA). Label probes used were human CD274 (cat. no. 600861) and MICA (cat. no. 571231) (ACDBio). The target genes and probed regions are listed in Table S1.

Approximately 1×105 of normal and tumor cells were cultured on sterile 2×2 cm pre-coated poly-L-lysine coverslips a day before treatment. Coverslips were washed and infected with NDV at 0.01 MOI or treated with a mixture of IFNs-α, β, and λ (1000 U each). Three coverslips were prepared per independent cell group (mock or NDV infection or IFNs treatment) as biological replicates. The use of IFNs mixture was determined from our previous publication that showed NDV to be a strong type I (of IFNs-α, β) and III (IFN-λ) IFN inducers in tumor cells, while IFN-λ was the main antiviral IFN in normal cells.10,11 Meanwhile, IFN-α and λ were purchased from PBL Assay Science, Piscataway, NJ, USA, while IFN-β was obtained from R&D System, MN, USA. Infection was performed by NDV incubation at 37°C for 60 minutes, washing the unbound virus, and culturing the cells in a maintenance medium. IFNs treated group was prepared by treating the washed cells with IFNs at 37°C for 60 minutes, then adding the maintenance medium, without washing the IFNs, before culturing at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 24 hours. The mock-treated and assay control coverslips were also cultured in a maintenance medium without any treatment.

Subsequently, the medium was removed, and cells were fixed in 10% formalin (Gibco) for 30 minutes; coverslips were attached to microscope slide glass, followed by protease digestion (2.5 μg/mL) at room temperature. Cells were incubated with mixed target probes (PD-L1 and MICA) or control positive probes against peptidylprolyl isomerase B (PPIB) (green control) and RNA polymerase II subunit A (POLR2A) (red control) genes or negative control probe against bacterial dihydrodipicolinate reductase (dapB) gene, at 40°C for 2 hours. Coverslips were then stored in 5x saline-sodium citrate buffer overnight in the dark at room temperature. Moreover, the amplification steps were performed the following day with the provided probes according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Coverslips were hybridized with Amp1 to Amp6 probes consecutively at 40°C, followed by the detection of a red signal with fast red solution. The process continued with the hybridization of Amp7 to Amp8 at 40°C and Amp9 to Amp10 probes at room temperature, then detecting a green signal with the provided solution. The coverslips were washed between each step, counterstained with 50% hematoxylin (Sigma Aldrich) staining solution, mounted with VectaMount mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Inc., Burlingame, CA, USA), and visualized under the light microscope at 400x magnification and captured using ZEN 2012 software. A semi-quantitative scoring was conducted by calculating the average of red and green punctate dots (score 0 represented no staining or less than 1 dot in every 10 cells; score 1 for 1–3 dots/cell; score 2 for 4–9 dots/cell; score 3 for 10–15 dots/cell and score 4 for >15 dots/cell), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Semi-quantitative ISH scores were performed on three biological replicates per treatment group and calculated as the average of two independent observers blinded to treatment conditions.

Immunofluorescence Assay

A total of 1×105 normal or tumor cells were seeded in 2×2 cm sterile coverslips a day before treatment. Subsequently, the cells were infected with 0.01 MOI NDV, or treated with 3000 IU IFN-λ, and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C. Unlike the method used in situ hybridization, in this experiment, the concentration of IFN-λ was increased to 3000 IU, three times the concentration used in ISH experiment to compensate the omission of IFN-α and IFN-β. Type I IFNs excluded to reflect the interferon secretion profile of normal cells, which predominantly secrete type III IFN (IFN-λ) following NDV infection,10,11 thereby excluding exogenous type I IFN as a confounding variable in normal cell conditions. Coverslips were washed 3 times with PBS and fixed in 10% formalin for 30 minutes, followed by PBS wash and incubation with human anti-PD-L1 alexa fluor 488-conjugated (Novus Biologicals) in PBS (1:1000) for 60 minutes. Furthermore, they were washed with PBS and incubated in 4′6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) to achieve a final concentration of 1 µg/mL at room temperature for 5 minutes. The coverslips were then washed with PBS and observed under the fluorescent microscope and captured using ZEN 2012 software. PD-L1 expression was visualized in the FITC channel, while the counterstaining of the nucleus was observed in the DAPI channel.

Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMC) Culture

PBMC was isolated from whole blood of a normal donor using Ficol Paque solution (Amersham Bioscience, Buckinghamshire, UK) through differential centrifugation. It was then incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C with 5% CO2 in an LGM-3 medium (Lonza, MD, USA). Adherent cells, including monocytes and platelets, were removed, and the floating cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in LGM-3 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 0.5 ng/mL interleukin-2 (IL-2) at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 3 days. The PBMC was collected by centrifugation, washed with medium to remove the serum and IL-2, and replaced with fresh LGM-3 before being counted for the assay. Each assay collected fresh blood from the same donor to culture the PBMC.

IFN-γ Induction Assay

Confluent tumor and normal cells were prepared in 12-well plate with corresponding growth medium. Cells were washed and infected by NDV at 0.01 MOI or treated with 3000 IU IFN-λ (PBL Assay) for 24 hours in a maintenance medium. Then, the cells were washed to remove the virus and IFN-λ and co-cultured with 2×105 PBMC in LGM-3 for 72 hours. Mock-infected cells were prepared as a negative control (without NDV and IFNs). Medium was collected every 24 hours and measured for IFN-γ by Human Interferon Gamma (IFNg) ELISA Kit (Abbexa, USA), according to the protocol of the manufacturer.

Interferon Signaling Assay

Confluent normal (NB1RGB and HEK293) and tumor (A549 and U87MG) cells were cultured in a 12-well plate. The plates were rinsed with PBS and infected with NDV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) 0.01 and 0.001 for 60 minutes at 37°C. Subsequently, the virus was removed, and cells were rewashed with PBS. Cultures were then overlaid with a maintenance medium similar to the growth medium, but FBS was replaced with 0.3% BSA Fraction V (Gibco). The cells were collected 24 hours post-infection and treated with freeze–thaw cycles of 5 and 10 minutes at −80°C and 37°C in cold lysis buffer. The lysates were then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant, and protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford test. Cell lines were treated with IFN-α, β, and λ at a concentration of 1000 IU/mL in the maintenance medium for 24 hours at a temperature of 37°C to determine the effect of IFNs induced by NDV on the modulation of IRF-1 and IRF-3. After 24 hours, the cells were lysed using the mentioned method, and the proteins were collected. Proteins in the lysate were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. Meanwhile, immunoblotting was performed for IRF-1 (Cell Signaling, USA), IRF-3 (Cell Signaling), and phosphorylated IRF-3 (Cell Signaling) at a 1:1000 dilution. Beta-actin was employed as a housekeeping standard to normalize the detected proteins, which were visualized using WesternBright Quantum HRP substrate (Advansta, USA) and captured using Quantity One 4.5.2 software. Densitometric analysis of Western blot bands was performed in Fiji Software (ImageJ). All blot images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, processed with a single Despeckle filter to reduce speckle noise. Band intensities were quantified by measuring the area under each densitometric peak. Protein concentrations were normalized against β-actin and then were represented as fold change relative to mock (Mock=1).

Statistics

All flow cytometry and ELISA experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated on at least two independent occasion using independently prepared cell cultures and virus stocks. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to test significant differences among treatment groups. Prior to analysis, normality of data distribution was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variance was verified using Levene’s test. All parametric assumptions were met. Following ANOVA, Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used to compare each NDV infection against the mock-infected control, which controls multiple comparisons. Error bars represent standard deviation (n=3). The p-values of ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p ≤ 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Results

PD-L1 Expressions are Modulated by MOI-Dependent NDV Infection

As an initial investigation, this study aimed to examine the status of PD-L1 and the effect of NDV lentogenic infection on the regulation of PD-L1 in normal and tumor cell lines. Normal NBIRGB and HEK293 cells, along with tumor A549 and U87MG cells, were infected with 0.01 and 0.001 MOI for 24 hours and subjected to analysis of PD-L1 expression using flow cytometry (Figures 1A and S1) with gating strategy is provided in Figure S3. Flow cytometry data were normalized by subtracting the mean unstained control fluorescence for each cell type to account for background fluorescence variability (Figure 1A). Normalized data (comprising three treatment groups: Mock-infected control, NDV 0.001 MOI, and NDV 0.01 MOI) were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to assess differences in PD-L1 expression across infection groups. Following ANOVA, Dunnett’s post-hoc test was performed to compare each NDV-infected treatment directly against the mock-infected control, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons. The flow cytometry results showed that mock-infected tumor cells (A549 and U87MG) expressed greater PD-L1 than normal cells (NB1RGB and HEK293). Furthermore, NDV infection decreased the PD-L1 expression on A549 and U87MG, with the effect being MOI-dependent. Contrary, the infection increased PD-L1 on both NB1RGB and HEK293 normal cell lines, demonstrating cell-type-specific immunoregulation. Further assay with Western blot in HEK293 and U87MG confirmed the flow cytometry result (Figure 1B and C).

More Prolonged NDV Infection Leads to Different PD-L1 and PI Expression Kinetics

Since our previous results in similar cell lines showed a kinetic of NDV cytotoxicity until 72 hours post-infection,10 the current study aimed to examine the consistency of PD-L1 modulation in tumor cells after prolonged incubation and its correlation with cell death. It focused on PD-L1 rather than PD-L2, based on reports indicating its heightened likelihood of upregulation in inflamed cells, in contrast to PD-L2, that is expressed by antigen-presenting cells.37 The infection experiment was repeated from 24 hours until 72 hours using normal NB1RGB, tumor A549, and tumor U87MG cell lines, as shown in Figure 2, with flow cytometry samples collected to evaluate PD-L1 and PI expression. The results showed a trend of reduction in PD-L1 expression of NDV-infected compared to mock-infected in all cell types, with statistically significant reductions observed in U87MG and NB1RGB at 48 and 72 hours a post-infection (Figure 2A).

Upon analysis of PI expression indicative of cytotoxicity, all cells demonstrated a trend of elevated PI levels after NDV infection compared to the mock infection within 24 hours. NDV cytotoxicity continued to increase for U87MG at 48 hours, consistent with the findings reported in the previous study.10 At 72 hours post-infection, the PI level was not different from the mock infection, showing the weak replication of NDV LaSota in these types of cells (Figure 2B).11

NDV Infection and IFNs Modulate PD-L1 and MICA mRNA Expression

Our previous publication showed that NDV infection in tumor cells produced IFN-α, β, and λ.10 To observe the NDV infection and IFNs effect on the innate and adaptive antitumor immune response, an examination of PD-L1 and MICA transcription levels was conducted through duplex chromogenic ISH against human PD-L1 and MICA probes.

Microscopic images are presented in Figure 3A–T which PD-L1 expression shows as a blue dot and MICA as a red dot. At a glance, MICA expression was increased after IFNs and NDV treatment in tumor cells compared to basal, contrary to the expression of PD-L1 which decreased in tumor cells after being treated with IFNs and NDV. In order to have a better understanding of the result, we did a semi-quantitative analysis of the picture and presented it in Figure 3U–X.

As presented in Figure 3U–X, in general, NDV infection within 24 hours decreased the ISH score of PD-L1 transcripts, although the basal PD-L1 expression was found to be relative to cell types. Greater PD-L1 reduction was observed in tumor cells compared to the normal cells group. In terms of MICA expression, almost all cell types expressed a low level of MICA before NDV infection, and the level was increased upon NDV infection.

The combination of IFN-α, β, λ treatment in normal cells did not have any remarkable effect on PD-L1 expression in NB1RGB (blue dot in Figure 3B), with a slight increase observed in HEK293 cells (blue dot in Figure 3G), which was similar to flow cytometry data (Figure 1). In contrast, the marked reduction of PD-L1 by IFNs treatment was observed in the tumor cells group (blue dot in Figure 3L and Q), suggesting the higher sensitivity of tumor cells.

Regarding MICA expression, IFNs treatment in normal cells showed no response in NB1RGB (red dot in Figure 3B) and down-regulation to HEK293 cells (red dot in Figure 3G). Notably, IFNs treatment caused up-regulation of MICA transcript in tumor cells, supporting the evidence of IFN sensitivity in tumor cells in this study. When compared to IFNs treatment, NDV infection induced more robust PD-L1 transcripts reduction (Figure 3U and V). These findings suggested that tumor cell types were more sensitive to the combination of IFN-α, β, and λ treatment in down-regulating PD-L1 than normal cells.

This result showed a balance of PD-L1 and MICA in different cell types induced by NDV infection and combined IFN-α, β, and λ treatment, where there was a negative feedback loop of PD-L1 and MICA regulation.

PD-L1 Surface Protein Immunofluorescence Assay After NDV and IFN-λ Exposure

As IFN-λ the major IFN secreted by all types of cells after NDV infection, we next sought to demonstrate if IFN-λ alone was enough to regulate PD-L1 surface protein. We treated cells with IFN-λ at 3000 IU for 24 hours. Sets of cells infected with NDV and mock infected were prepared for the comparison. The expression of PD-L1 on the cell surface was observed with mouse monoclonal anti-human PD-L1 alexa fluor-488-conjugated with DAPI as nucleus counterstaining. In Figure 4, we observed that both IFN-λ and NDV modulated PD-L1 expression (in green) in all cell types, and the most noticeable reduction was observed in tumor A549 (Figure 4C) and U87MG (Figure 4D) cells. In the normal cells group, mock-treated NB1RGB (Figure 4A) expressed PD-L1 molecules, unlike HEK293 (Figure 4B). There was slight increase in PD-L1 signal in NB1GB and HEK293 cells in groups that received IFN-λ. From this result, we showed that IFN-λ alone was enough to regulate PD-L1 and the level varied among cells.

Activation of NKG2D Receptor-Possessing Cells by NDV and IFN-λ Is Dependent on PD-L1 and MICA Balance

To investigate the impact of PD-L1 and MICA balance on the activation of NKG2D receptors, normal and tumor cells were treated with NDV infection and IFN-λ for 24 hours. Following the treatment, the cells were washed, replaced with fresh medium, and co-cultured with PBMC. We intended to pretreat the cells with NDV and IFN-λ to alter PD-L1/MICA expression; thus, hypothetically would lead to the activation of PBMC indicated by IFN-γ secretion. PBMC was prepared by only culturing the floating cells and leaving the adherent cells with IL-2 supplementation to induce the proliferation of lymphocyte subsets and NK cells. The co-culture medium was collected every 24 hours for 72 hours and tested for the presence of IFN-γ. The absolute expression of IFN-γ is shown in Figure 5A. As shown in Figure 5A and B, IFN-γ secretion in PBMC-tumor/normal cell co-cultures was elevated in a time-dependent manner in cells pre-treated with IFN-λ, with statistically significant increase observed in U87MG cells at 48 hours (p < 0.05). Discussion

Extensive studies have been conducted on oncolytic NDV therapy, demonstrating the potential for reducing tumor burden and eliciting anti-tumor immune responses. However, the likelihood of concomitant upregulation of immunosuppressive checkpoint molecules, such as PD-L1 in some studies, warrants careful consideration.29,30 Several studies have linked PD-L1 upregulation following oncolytic virus treatment to NDV-induced type I IFN, as well as to CD8+ T cell activity and IFN-γ secretion.29,30,38 While the PD-L1/PD-1 axis constitutes an inhibitory immune checkpoint that suppresses anti-tumor immunity, the NKG2DL (including MICA/MICB, expressed on tumor cells)/NKG2D (expressed on NK and T cells) axis serves as a stimulatory counterpart. Therefore, in addition to strategies targeting PD-L1 suppression, stimulation of NKG2D-activating ligands may represent a complementary approach to antitumor immunotherapy.37,39

Building on this rationale, our previous studies demonstrated that while lentogenic NDV LaSota strain was too weak to replicate productively in human cell lines, it strongly induced both type I (IFN-α/β) and type III (IFN-λ) IFNs. Notably, a higher degree of type I IFN induction was observed in tumor cells, whilst normal cells predominantly responded with type III IFN induction.10 The cumulative level of NDV-induced IFNs, rather than direct viral cytotoxicity, appeared to be the principal contributor to the higher apoptosis in tumor cell lines being used.10,11 The present study therefore employed exogenous IFN-α/β and IFN-λ treatment to investigate whether NDV-induced IFNs are responsible for the observed modulation of immune checkpoint and immune-activating ligand expression.

Flow cytometry, Western blot analyses, in situ hybridization assay, and immunofluorescence assay demonstrated that NDV infection and IFN treatment reduced PD-L1 protein expression in tumor cell lines (A549 and U87MG), which exhibit higher intrinsic PD-L1 expression compared to normal cell lines (NB1RGB and HEK293). This reduction was observed in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Figures 1–4). We acknowledge that the elevated basal PD-L1 expression observed in A549 and U87MG cell lines may not fully reflect the complexity of the in vivo tumor microenvironment or clinical disease status. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that these cell lines provide a reproducible and tractable in vitro model for investigating PD-L1 surface regulation and immune checkpoint modulation in the context of oncolytic virus and interferon-based therapies. Reduced PD-L1 expression was accompanied by increased cell death as measured by PI intensity in flow cytometry, suggesting that NDV-induced IFNs not only exert PD-L1 modulation but also cytotoxicity effects. This phenomenon appears to be cell-type specific, as our findings contrast with previous reports demonstrating enhanced expression of other natural cytotoxicity receptor ligands, excluding NKG2D ligands, in human carcinoma and melanoma cells following NDV infection.40

Duplex ISH confirmed that PD-L1 downregulation coincided with concomitant upregulation of MICA mRNA expression, suggesting a coordinated regulation of these opposing immune signals at the transcriptional level (Figure 3). As PD-L1 functions as an immune inhibitory ligand and MICA serves as an immune activating ligand for the NKG2D receptor, this inverse relationship may reflect a potential immune system homeostatic mechanism mediated through the PD-L1/MICA axis. A similar balance has also been reported in lung cancer cells, where repetitive radiation was reported to upregulate PD-L1 while downregulating NKG2D ligands, influencing resistance to NK cell cytotoxicity,21 suggesting the existence of a regulatory feedback loop between PD-L1 and NKG2D in modulating the activation of NKG2D receptor-bearing cells.

To investigate whether the observed PD-L1/MICA shifts were functionally relevant to the activation to NKG2D-mediated immune activation, NDV-infected and IFN-λ-treated cell lines were co-cultured with PBMCs, and IFN-γ secretion was measured as a surrogate marker of NKG2D-bearing cell activation. Our initial hypothesis was that NDV-induced MICA upregulation would be sufficient to activate NK2GD receptors-bearing cells, such as NK, CD8, and γδT cells, leading to the secretion of IFN-γ. However, IFN-γ production did not always occur concurrently with increased MICA expression (Figure 5), suggesting that MICA upregulation alone is insufficient to activate cells possessing NK2GD receptors while PD-L1 remains expressed at elevated level. Furthermore, MICA induction appeared blunted or attenuated under conditions of high PD-L1 expression, as observed in A549 cells. Taken together, these results imply that NDV infection and its induced type I (IFN-α/β) and type III (IFN-λ) interferons contribute to the functional outcome on NKG2D receptor-bearing immune cells, determined by the balance between the inhibitory ligand PD-L1 and the activating NKG2D ligand MICA, rather than by changes in either molecule independently. A stronger shift in the PD-L1/MICA balance below a critical PD-L1 threshold may be required to mount effective NKG2D-mediated cytotoxic activation, as shown in Figure 7. These findings are consistent with numerous reports demonstrating NK cells and CD8⁺ T cell activation following NDV oncolytic treatment40,41 and further support a model in which IFN-γ production is determined not by MICA or PD-L1 levels independently, but by their coordinated balance.

In the context of molecular mechanisms, NDV infection and IFN-α/β/λ treatment appeared to regulate IRF-1 and 3 expression and extensively implicated in innate antiviral immune response and IFN production.8,9 However, further experiments are required to clarify the precise roles of IRF-1 and IRF-3 in the MICA/PD-L1 regulatory axis. It is noteworthy that NK cells may contribute to PD-L1 upregulation through IFN-γ secretion,42 which could partially explain reports of increased PD-L1 expression following NDV infection. Conversely, overexpression of tumor PD-L1 has been suggested to impair IFN-γ secretion by CD8+ CTLs, indicating a possible negative feedback regulation mechanism.25 While this focused on type I and type III IFN signaling as they are most directly associated with NDV infection, other pathways involved in PD-L1 regulation, including phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) as a negative regulator and IFN-α, β, and γ as up regulators,43 the potential distinction between intrinsic and adaptive immune-induced PD-L1 expression should be considered in future investigations.

The present study has several limitations. First, Western blot analysis of PD-L1 protein expression in Figure 1 was performed in HEK293 and U87MG cells only, as extension to NB1RGB and A549 was not technically feasible within the current study period. Second, flow cytometry analysis to assess PD-L1 expression and cell viability kinetics in Figure 2 was not performed in HEK293 due to an unforeseen loss of cell stocks during the revision period, precluding a complete comparative analysis of NDV-induced cytotoxicity across all four cell lines. Despite these constraints, the cell lines included in the analyses represent both normal and tumor cell types, providing a biologically meaningful comparison within the scope of the current study, although employing primary normal cell cultures or patient-derived cells, and in vivo validation would more closely reflect physiological conditions and tumor microenvironment thus strengthen the translational relevance of these findings. Third, this study employed lentogenic NDV LaSota strain. Future studies should incorporate additional pathotypes, including mesogenic (eg, Komarov) and velogenic genotype VII strains, to comparatively evaluate strain-specific differences in IFN induction, cytotoxicity, and immune checkpoint regulation relevant to NDV-based oncolytic immunotherapy.44,45 Furthermore, cytotoxicity assay would more directly validate IFN-γ-mediated NKG2D bearing cells activation in PBMCs; future studies should investigate the specific cellular source of IFN-γ within PBMC subsets, including CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, and NKT cells, to delineate the specific immune effector populations activated by NDV-induced IFN signaling.

Since several strategies are being pursued to counter the inhibitory effect of negative regulatory mechanisms as a strategy for cancer immunotherapy, such as antibodies against PD-1 or PD-L1,46–48 the present study represents an early attempt to explore the potential link between NDV-induced type I and III IFN and cancer immune regulation. Moreover, possible functional distinctions between tumors with intrinsic PD-L1 expression and those induced through adaptive immune cells, which should be considered in subsequent studies. The immune activation threshold represents the level of immunogenic stimulation to elicit an effective immune response; it is a critical concept in this context: stimulation below this threshold may activate cells but remains insufficient to mount an effective response.49 Our findings suggest that NDV infection may contribute to a favorable shift in the inhibitory/activating ligand balance by simultaneously downregulating PD-L1 and upregulating MICA, potentially surpassing this threshold and thereby creating conditions conducive to NKG2D-mediated anti-tumor immune activation.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study provides evidence that NDV infection and NDV-induced IFNs modulate the balance between inhibitory and activating immune ligands in a cell-type-specific manner, reducing PD-L1 expression whilst concomitantly upregulating MICA in tumor cell lines. These findings suggest that the coordinated shift in the PD-L1/MICA axis, rather than changes in either molecule independently, may be a critical determinant of NKG2D receptor-bearing cell activation and IFN-γ production. While these observations are encouraging, definitive mechanistic conclusions regarding the regulatory pathways involved await validation through further experimentation, including the use of knock-out cell lines, pathway inhibitors, and in vivo models. Future studies should also address the specific contributions of type I and type III IFNs independently, the role of IRF-1 and IRF-3 as potential regulatory mediators, and the cellular sources of IFN-γ within PBMC subsets. Taken together, these findings represent an early step toward understanding the immunomodulatory potential of NDV-based oncolytic therapy in the context of immune checkpoint regulation and may offer a basis for future combination strategies with existing checkpoint inhibitor therapies in tumors where PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression is a key feature.
