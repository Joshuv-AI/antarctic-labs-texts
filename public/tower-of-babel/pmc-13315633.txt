Understanding Patient Preferences and Their Impact on Adherence to Glaucoma Therapy: A Multicenter Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Tomás Hernández, Sara Labay, Susana Duch, Carlos Arciniegas, Elena Avila, Cristina Vendrell, Shirin Djavanmardi, Nuris Nuria Gabarró, Naiara Relaño, Alegria Amram, Elena Millá
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlaucoma is a progressive, irreversible optic neuropathy that leads to the loss of retinal ganglion cells, vision loss, and blindness. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) is the only modifiable risk factor, and topical hypotensive eye drops remain the mainstay of treatment, reducing disease progression by up to 60%. However, patient adherence to therapy remains a significant challenge since this disease is usually asymptomatic in its early stages. Patient preferences and individual characteristics play a key role in adherence. This study aimed to obtain further knowledge about patient preferences regarding glaucoma treatment to better understand factors influencing adherence.MethodsA cross-sectional, multicenter survey was conducted in Spain between March and December 2024 among patients with glaucoma receiving topical treatment for more than 6 months. A questionnaire was designed to collect patients’ information about their beliefs, preferences, and adherence-related factors.ResultsOf 200 participants, 169 valid responses were analyzed. Most patients were aged 51–74 years, 60.2% were women, and 71.2% had been on treatment for more than 12 months. Although 93.5% recognized the importance of preservatives, only 36.0% reported having received information about them from their ophthalmologist. Preferences between multidose bottles (MDBs, 42.2%) and single-dose units (SDUs, 37.0%) were similar, although younger women (18–30 years) showed a preference for SDUs (p < 0.05). Perceived ease of handling was strongly influenced by prior experience. Most patients considered SDUs as more hygienic, whereas MDBs were perceived as more environmentally friendly; nearly 79% valued ecological impact. Self-reported adherence to therapy was moderate, with 57.6% of participants denying missed doses. Nonadherent patients were more likely to favor reminder systems (74.5%, p = 0.029). Treatment efficacy was ranked as the most important factor, followed by side effects, ease of use, cost, and environmental impact.ConclusionsThis study highlights substantial variability in patient preferences and underscores the importance of personalized, patient-centered glaucoma care. Enhanced education on preservatives, improved communication, and sustainable packaging could improve adherence and satisfaction. Larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and inform long-term adherence strategies.

Introduction

Glaucoma encompasses a group of progressive and irreversible optic neuropathies characterized by the loss of retinal ganglion cells and characteristic structural changes of the optic nerve head, leading to irreversible vision loss and, in some cases, blindness. This damage is frequently, though not invariably, associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) [1, 2].

Lowering IOP is the only modifiable risk factor that has been shown in randomized clinical trials to slow glaucoma progression [1]. Despite advances in laser and surgical therapies [3, 4], topical hypotensive medications remain the mainstay of treatment for most early and moderate forms of glaucoma [5].

First-line therapy typically consists of topical ocular hypotensive eye drops. These medications comprise several major pharmacological classes with distinct mechanisms of action and tolerability profiles; therefore, treatment selection requires balancing IOP-lowering efficacy, side effects, and treatment burden [6]. Ocular hypotensive therapy has been shown to reduce glaucoma progression by up to 60% [6].

However, patient adherence to topical glaucoma therapy remains a significant challenge. Because glaucoma is usually asymptomatic in its early stages, patients often do not perceive an immediate benefit of being adherent to their medications, which they often find onerous. Adherence is a complex behavior [7] influenced by multiple interrelated factors. [8, 9].

Patient knowledge, beliefs, and education play a crucial role in shaping treatment preferences and adherence. Trust in clinicians and clear communication are essential for informed decision-making and patient engagement [10–12]. Furthermore, patient satisfaction and, consequently, adherence are strongly associated with treatment tolerability, ease of use, and side effect profile. Ocular side effects, particularly irritation and conjunctival hyperemia, negatively affect both adherence and quality of life [13–15]. Preservative-free medications have been associated with improved ocular comfort and higher satisfaction, which may enhance adherence [14, 16–18].

Patients generally prefer convenient regimens with fewer daily instillations, and higher satisfaction has been reported with once‑daily dosing or single‑bottle fixed combinations compared with multiple separate bottles. In a large survey, overall treatment satisfaction approached 80%, with a clear preference for simplified regimens [19].

Ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and other demographic factors also influence patient priorities and treatment acceptance [20–22]. Differences in health outcome preferences and willingness to engage with specific therapies highlight the importance of culturally sensitive and individualized treatment approaches [21].

From the patient’s perspective, maintaining vision, independence, and functional abilities, such as driving, are often prioritized. Quality-of-life considerations may outweigh clinical parameters such as IOP alone [23–26]. Social context and the ability to integrate treatment into daily life are therefore critical determinants of treatment success [24].

Identifying barriers and facilitators to treatment acceptance is essential for improving adherence. Fear, cost, and insufficient information are among the main barriers [20, 22, 27], whereas trust in clinicians, perceived treatment efficacy, and tailored patient education act as key facilitators. Addressing misconceptions and providing support can enhance uptake [10, 28]. Commonly reported obstacles to adherence include forgetfulness, side effects, difficulty with eye drop administration, and the need for multiple doses a day [29].

In summary, personality traits, beliefs, cultural background, motivation, age, and health literacy significantly influence adherence, as do treatment-related factors such as drop-related blurriness, redness, or stinging, and the frequency or complexity of the dosing regimen. In addition, physical limitations related to comorbidities affecting cognition or manual dexterity may impair patients’ ability to self-administer eye drops [12, 26].

Taken together, these findings suggest that implementing personalized treatment strategies incorporating patient preferences and expectations may improve adherence. To explore these factors, a national survey was conducted in Spain to assess patient preferences regarding hypotensive eye drops, with the aim of better understanding patient needs, raising ophthalmologists’ awareness of the importance of individualized prescriptions, and providing valuable information for the development of future treatment strategies.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the preferences, beliefs, and adherence-related factors of patients with glaucoma receiving topical hypotensive therapy at two specialist centers in Spain. Secondary objectives included assessing the relationship between patient preferences and self-reported treatment adherence, exploring the influence of packaging format (SDU versus MDB) on patient experience, and identifying gaps in patient education regarding preservatives. These findings are intended to inform more individualized, patient-centered prescribing strategies in glaucoma care.

Methods

A cross-sectional, prospective, observational multicenter study was conducted in Spain between March and December 2024. Patients were recruited from dedicated glaucoma outpatient clinics at Hospital Clinic de Barcelona and Instituto VERTE (Barcelona). Both institutions are part of a comprehensive health system that covers more than 540,000 people.

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Hospital Clínic de Barcelona Ethics Review Board (HCB/2024/0234). The Ethics Committee of Hospital Clínic, both in its composition and in its procedures, complies with the standards of EMA/CHMP/ICH/135/1995 and with the current legislation that regulates its operation. The need for written informed consent was waived by the ethics review board, as no personally identifiable information was collected.

The study was conducted as part of routine clinical care, without any modification to standard medical care.

Patients of any age with a diagnosis of glaucoma who had been receiving topical medical treatment for more than 6 months were invited to participate. Patients who were unable to understand or complete the questionnaire were excluded.

The principal investigators designed the questionnaire, which was distributed to participating physicians in paper format. It was offered and explained to all eligible patients, and those who agreed to participate completed it in the waiting room.

The questionnaire included: range of ages (18–30, 31–50, 51–74, > 75 years), sex, current treatment modality (single-dose unit [SDU], multidose bottle [MDB], or both [SDU + MDB]), and duration of treatment (≤ 12 months or > 12 months). It also included four yes/no questions assessing prior experience with single-dose units, prior experience with multidose bottles, patients’ beliefs regarding the importance of preservatives, and whether information about preservatives had been provided by their ophthalmologists. In addition, 12 statements assessed patient preferences using a five-point Likert scale (strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, and strongly agree), with an additional “don’t know” option.

Finally, patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either rank treatment attributes from 1 (most important) to 6 (least important) or rank each factor from 1 (most important) to 5 (least important), depending on the questionnaire version received.

All questionnaires were centralized and analyzed by the principal investigators.

Statistical analyses were performed using the software XLSTAT version 2025 1.3 1429 (XLSTAT statistical and data analysis solution, New York, NY, USA. https://www.xlstat.com/es).

For the overall study population, qualitative variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. To facilitate interpretation, responses of “strongly disagree” and “disagree” were combined, as well as “agree” and “strongly agree.” Comparisons between subgroups for qualitative and categorical variables were performed using the chi-squared test. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 200 patients completed the survey. Among the respondents, 31 patients (15%) did not report their current treatment and were therefore excluded from the analysis. Consequently, the final study population comprised 169 patients.

Nearly 60% of participants were in the age range of 51–74 years old, 60.2% were women, and 104 patients (71.2%) had been receiving topical glaucoma treatment for more than 12 months. Overall, 93.5% of patients expressed the importance of preservatives, but only 36.0% of them were informed by their ophthalmologist about their impact on the treatment. Regarding treatment modality, 46.7% of patients used MDBs, 27.2% SDUs, and the remainder used both formats. Almost 70% of patients reported prior experience with SDUs, and more than 88% with MDBs. Regarding patients’ preferences, they did not show a significant preference in favor of SDU or MDB; 42.2% of patients preferred MDBs, while 37% preferred SDUs. These preferences were not significantly influenced by sex, age group, or prior treatment experience, except for women aged 18–30 years, who showed a significant preference for SDUs (p < 0.05).

Regarding ease of handling, 39.1% of patients reported that MDB eye drops were easier to use than SDUs, whereas 32.0% considered SDUs easier to handle. Perceived ease of handling was significantly influenced by prior treatment experience: patients previously treated with MDBs more frequently reported MDB drops as easier to use (p = 0.006), whereas those with prior SDU experience reported SDU drops as easier to handle (p < 0.001). No other variables, such as sex or previous treatment, significantly influenced these responses. Concerning the timing of instillation, more patients preferred applying their eye drops in the evening rather than in the morning (35.4% versus 11.0%, respectively), while 45.7% expressed a neutral preference.

Adherence to Treatment

A total of 57.6% of patients disagreed with the statement, “Sometimes I forget to use my eye drops,” suggesting moderate self-reported adherence. No significant differences were found between adherent and nonadherent patients associated with other factors such as age range or the perceived importance of preservatives.

There was a significant difference between adherent and nonadherent patients regarding a reminder system for eye drop instillation; 74.5% of nonadherent patients were in favor versus 43.6% of adherent patients (p = 0.029).

Ranking of Treatment Attributes

Among patients randomized to rank treatment attributes by importance (1–5), treatment efficacy was consistently ranked as the most important factor. This was followed by side effects, ease of handling, frequency and timing of instillation, treatment cost, and, lastly, environmental impact.

Similar results were observed among patients who ranked all treatment characteristics from 1 (most important) to 6 (least important), with efficacy again identified as the primary determinant of treatment preference, followed by side effects, ease of handling, frequency and timing of instillation, treatment cost, and finally environmental impact.

Discussion

The management of glaucoma relies primarily on long-term daily instillation of topical eye drops, a regimen that may be burdensome and may negatively affect patient adherence. Understanding patient preferences, beliefs, barriers, and knowledge regarding glaucoma treatment is therefore essential for optimizing therapeutic outcomes. This was the primary objective of the present survey.

Overall, our findings are consistent with those of previous studies assessing patient preferences in glaucoma therapy. The distribution of preferences between multidose bottles (MDBs) and single-dose units (SDUs) in our cohort (42.2% MDBs versus 37% SDUs) was comparable to the findings reported in a French multicenter study (32% vs 37%) [30]. However, a slightly higher proportion of patients favored MDBs in our cohort.

In our survey, one subgroup (women aged 18–30 years) showed a significant preference for SDUs (p < 0.05). This difference has not been consistently reported in previous studies; this could be explained by factors such as portability, more convenient for on-the-go use, which is a common lifestyle need for younger adults, precise dosing, or ease of handling. A 2010 study found a statistically significant difference in dispensing experiences based on gender. While men reported that traditional MDBs were easier to open, women found the SDUs easier to handle. This suggests that the design of SDUs may be more intuitive for many women [31]. The same study showed that women generally rated the overall performance of SDUs higher than conventional MDBs. This includes observing the contents of the dispenser and the ease of administration. Hypothetically, another contributing factor could be the potential appeal for specific eye conditions, such as contact lens use; young adults, particularly women, are frequent contact lens wearers. SDUs are often preservative-free, making them ideal for rewetting lenses throughout the day without irritation. Women also tend to wear contact lenses for longer periods, increasing their potential for dry eye symptoms, which can be exacerbated by factors such as contact lens use or hormonal fluctuations. For frequent, daily use, preservative-free single-dose vials are often recommended to avoid cumulative irritation from preservatives. These factors may partly explain the observed preference, although further investigation is needed.

In this study, more than 93.5% of patients expressed the importance of preservatives, but just 36.0% of patients were informed by their ophthalmologist, which is consistent with the French finding [30]. This lack of information from their ophthalmologist reinforces the importance of medical education and early recognition of symptoms associated with preservatives.

The findings regarding ease of handling (39.1% saying MDBs are easier, 32.0% saying SDUs are easier; prior experience influencing perception) highlight the importance of usability and previous experience. A study investigated the usability (dose accuracy, reproducibility, and user preference) of multidose preservative-free devices. This study underscores that device design (ease of opening and dose accuracy) may influence patient acceptance and adherence [32]. Our survey expands on usability considerations by showing that prior experience influences perceived ease of use, which is consistent with the importance of device design identified in literature.

Our survey asked patients about environmental impact and hygiene but not directly about cost-effectiveness. Interestingly, 63.5% of patients believed single-dose drops are more hygienic, while the same proportion believed that multidose drops could be environmentally friendly, suggesting that patients may weigh hygiene against environmental impact when considering packing preferences. One study found that switching from multidose bottles to single-use minims substantially increased costs and concluded that, under routine conditions, multidose bottles may be more cost‐effective [33]. Our findings align with this, showing that patients do consider environmental and packaging concerns, reflecting the cost/environment trade-offs discussed in literature, although our data come from the patient perspective rather than an institutional cost analysis.

Usability studies of single-dose packaging found high ease of use of a delivery aid for single-dose units. This aligns with our finding that patients with prior SDU experience found SDUs easier to handle, and this was similar for MDB users. These results support the idea that patient familiarity influences perceived ease of use, reinforcing our survey’s suggestion that prior experience affects handling preferences [34].

A literature review discussed how preservative-free and packaging design issues influence choice. Our survey addressed preservative awareness and packaging/environment concerns; the literature pointed to growing emphasis on packaging/sterility issues [35]. Our data reflect trends highlighted in the literature (patient desire for preservative-free, better packaging) and emphasize that many patients are aware (or concerned) about packaging/environment, even if not fully informed about preservatives.

Adherence to Treatment

Adherence to glaucoma treatment is critical because it directly prevents vision loss and blindness by keeping IOP at a safe level. Poor adherence has been associated with faster disease progression, higher healthcare costs owing to complications, and increased need for surgical intervention [6, 36].

In this study, 57.6% of patients reported adherence to treatment. No significant differences in adherence were observed according to factors such as age, sex, or the perceived importance of preservatives, in contrast to the French study, where nonadherence was associated with younger age and lack of awareness of preservatives [30]. A literature review notes that adherence (and persistence) in glaucoma treatment “ranges anywhere from 5 to 80%” globally, reflecting a very large variation [5]. Some countries have reported higher adherence or persistence rates, including some studies quoting 72.1% in Canada [37], and 72.7% in the Netherlands [38], possibly reflecting differences in healthcare access, insurance coverage, and follow-up systems. However, several studies showed a high proportion of noncompliant patients [39–47]. Observed country differences arise from a mix of measurement choices [48–50], health‑system regulation and reimbursement, availability and delivery of patient education/support, and population health and psychosocial stressors rather than a single universal cause [45, 48, 49, 51, 52].

Although 74.5% of nonadherent patients expressed support for a reminder system for eye drop instillation, previous studies using electronic monitoring devices have shown that such systems often reveal that patients missed their doses, showing lower objective adherence rates than patient self-report [45, 46].

Ranking Survey of Treatment Attributes

A French multicenter study similarly asked patients to rank treatment criteria: Efficacy was first, followed by side effects, ease of use, cost, and environmental impact [30]. In our results, patients likewise ranked efficacy as first, followed by side effects, ease of handling, cost, and environment. Although environmental impact is gaining attention, with a high proportion of surveyed patients citing it as a factor in treatment selection, this factor remained a lower priority in therapeutic decisions compared with clinical effectiveness and tolerability.

Limitations

The study included a sample of patients that was relatively small, and patients were recruited from a limited geographic area, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Future studies including larger populations from different areas, and even other countries in our surroundings, would provide us with more comprehensive insights into patient preferences and support broader personalization of glaucoma treatment.

Most of the participants were between 51 years and 74 years old. Consequently, the results obtained in other age ranges correspond to a minimal number of patients, which may have introduced age-related bias and need confirmation of these findings in subsequent studies with more age-balanced cohorts.

Patients answered the questionnaire without direct supervision, which may have led to inaccuracies in some responses, such as the treatment modality. Furthermore, 32 patients were not included in the analysis because they did not report their treatment type, representing a relatively high exclusion rate that may have introduced selection bias.

Although the survey was designed to be easy for patients to complete, responses depended on individual levels of health literacy and familiarity with certain medical terminology. As a result, some questions may have been misunderstood or left unanswered. This could be an opportunity to establish educational initiatives aimed at improving patient knowledge and disease awareness.

The low proportion of patients who reported being informed by their ophthalmologist about the importance of preservatives suggests a potential gap in physician–patient communication. Educational efforts targeting ophthalmologists may help to encourage them to advise on the harmful effects of preservatives, to ensure that patients are adequately informed.

Adherence was assessed using self-reported measures, which are known to overestimate true adherence. Some questions, such as the one about forgetting to instill the drops, were interpreted as a lack of adherence when the answer was agree or strongly agree, although patients who answered other options may also have been nonadherent. Previous studies have demonstrated that objective adherence measured by electronic monitoring is often substantially lower than self-reported adherence [53]. Although our results on adherence are in line with other studies in similar settings, the true prevalence of nonadherent patients may have been underestimated. Our suggestion, given the wide variability in adherence across different studies, is that further research is needed, adopting a standardized definition of adherence and incorporating the factors described in the literature as associated with it. This would allow us to establish measures to improve adherence.

Finally, there were also no questions about treatment persistence sustained over time; therefore, our findings cannot be extrapolated to treatment persistence. More focused studies are needed to explore patients’ preferences regarding sustained long-term use.

Conclusions

This study highlights the substantial variability in preferences among patients with glaucoma and emphasizes the importance of incorporating patient perspectives into treatment decisions. Understanding these preferences is key to personalize therapy and may contribute to improved adherence and treatment outcomes.

The limited information provided about preservatives highlights the need for educational initiatives to raise awareness, which may also improve adherence.

The growing patient interest in aspects related to the ecological impact of glaucoma treatments underscores the need to implement measures related to the sustainable use of medications, alongside clinical effectiveness and tolerability.

Despite advances in glaucoma management, nonadherence remains a major barrier to successful long-term treatment. As shown by various surveys conducted across different European countries, there is a clear need to implement patient-centered adherence-improvement strategies that focus on a detailed understanding of patient preferences and expectations.

Overall, our findings contribute to the ongoing European discussion on patient-centered glaucoma care and provide practical insight to inform more individualized and sustainable treatment approaches.
