Hydrochemical evolution and formation mechanisms of groundwater in the Daihai Lake Basin plain under ongoing ecological water replenishment
Authors: Juanjuan Zhang, Lewei He, Tengfei Ma, Haozhong Yuan, Wenxin Liu, Liqun Sun, Zihao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Daihai Lake, a typical closed inland lake in the arid and semi-arid region of Inner Mongolia, has been subject to two consecutive years of ecological water replenishment to mitigate its severe ecological degradation. While the lake water level has risen and wetland ecosystems have been gradually restored, existing studies have predominantly focused on changes in lake water quality, leaving a critical research gap regarding the hydrochemical evolution, formation mechanisms, and drinking water safety risks of groundwater in the plain area of the Daihai Lake Basin under the dynamic conditions of ongoing water replenishment. To fill this gap, this study systematically analyzed the hydrochemical characteristics and formation mechanisms of groundwater in the study area, and conducted a comprehensive groundwater quality assessment. A suite of representative groundwater samples were collected from the study area after two years of ecological water replenishment, and analyzed using an integrated set of methods including Self-Organizing Map (SOM) clustering, hydrochemical graphical analysis, ion ratio analysis, multivariate statistical analysis, and the Entropy-weighted Water Quality Index (EWQI) method. The results show that: (1) Groundwater is divided into three clusters via SOM, with distinct ion sources from carbonate/silicate weathering and halite dissolution across clusters; some samples have excess SO₄2− and HCO₃−, requiring additional cations for charge balance. (2) Groundwater evolution is jointly controlled by water-rock interaction and evaporative concentration, with limited influence from atmospheric precipitation. (3) Three high-fluoride enrichment mechanisms are identified: mineral dissolution under weakly alkaline conditions, evaporative concentration-driven F⁻ enrichment, and accelerated dissolution of fluorine-bearing minerals induced by acidic mining wastewater. (4) 89% of Cluster-3 groundwater meets drinking standards (EWQI < 50), while 70% of groundwater samples from Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 are of poor quality, mainly distributed in the southwestern lakeshore. This study systematically elucidates the hydrochemical characteristics and formation mechanisms of groundwater in the Daihai Lake Basin under continuous ecological water replenishment, identifies key risk zones for groundwater quality, and provides a solid scientific basis for the protection and sustainable utilization of regional groundwater resources, as well as the optimization of ecological water replenishment strategies in similar arid and semi-arid inland lake basins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50698-6.

Introduction

Characterizing groundwater hydrochemical evolution is a cornerstone of sustainable water resource management in arid and semi-arid regions1. Located in the Daihai Basin, a typical closed inland lake basin in northern China, the shallow groundwater system within the alluvial-proluvial and lacustrine plain is characterized by a shallow water table and sluggish runoff. Hydrochemical evolution in this region is jointly shaped by natural processes and anthropogenic activities, leading to pronounced spatial heterogeneity in groundwater chemistry2.

Over recent decades, the Daihai Basin has experienced a severe and ongoing ecological crisis. Driven by the coupled impacts of climate change and intensive anthropogenic activities, the surface area of Daihai Lake shrank drastically from 115.49 km2 in 1989 to 54 km2 in 2017. By September 2022, immediately before the launch of the ecological water replenishment project, the lake area had further contracted to 45.9 km2, corresponding to a total shrinkage of 69.65% alongside severe salinization of the lake water.

To mitigate this escalating ecological crisis, an emergency ecological water diversion project was officially launched in September 2022. As of September 2024, a cumulative total of 39.47 million m3 of water had been diverted into the lake3. The replenishment pathway enters Liangcheng County west of Daihai and flows into the lake via the Gongba River. This diversion constitutes a major, ongoing hydrological perturbation that may significantly affect the groundwater environment along the route, demanding a concurrent evaluation of the subsurface system.

Previous systematic investigations into groundwater hydrochemistry across arid and semi-arid regions have established several foundational insights. First, with respect to pollution sources, industrial wastewater discharge, domestic sewage leakage, and agricultural non-point source pollution have been identified as the dominant contributors to elevated nitrogen compound concentrations in groundwater systems4–7. Second, regarding the controls on hydrochemical composition, in addition to conventional water-rock interactions, solute inputs from primary geological environments also exert a critical influence8,9. Evaporation, geogenic mineral dissolution, anthropogenic activities, agricultural practices, and groundwater recharge are widely recognized as the primary drivers of groundwater quality evolution, with severe seawater intrusion also imposing substantial impacts on coastal aquifer systems10,11. Third, in terms of groundwater-surface water interactions, inland lake systems in arid regions are predominantly sustained by precipitation, surface runoff, and groundwater discharge, with these water bodies exhibiting complex bidirectional hydrological exchange12 . Finally, with regard to the ecological impacts of water replenishment projects, the evolutionary trajectory of lake salinity varies markedly under different water replenishment regimes, while such ecological diversion measures generally deliver a beneficial effect on lake water quality13,14.

However, notable research gaps and limitations persist in existing studies, and the practical application relevance of related research remains insufficient. From the perspective of livelihood security, our hydrogeological field survey found that a portion of rural residents in the Daihai Lake Basin still directly extract groundwater as their source of domestic drinking water. Although this population accounts for a low proportion of the total population in the basin, and no systematic and accurate quantitative statistics are available to date, groundwater quality is directly related to the drinking water safety and physical health of these residents. There is thus an urgent need for systematic research on the hydrochemical characteristics and water quality risk assessment of groundwater in this region. From the perspective of scientific research, on the one hand, existing studies have mostly focused on the variation characteristics of lake water quality after ecological water replenishment, with insufficient attention paid to the hydrochemical response processes and evolution laws of groundwater along the water conveyance route and in the surrounding areas. On the other hand, systematic research on groundwater hydrochemistry in the Daihai Basin is still relatively scarce, especially under the dynamic hydrological conditions of the ongoing ecological water diversion at present, relevant research is even more inadequate. The spatial differentiation patterns of regional groundwater hydrochemical characteristics and their formation mechanisms are in urgent need of further clarification.

Therefore, this study addresses these gaps through systematic groundwater sample collection and integrated analysis using methods including the Self-Organizing Map (SOM), hydrochemical graphical analysis, ion ratio analysis, and multivariate statistics. The explicit and specific research objectives of this study are: (1) To reveal the spatial distribution characteristics of groundwater chemical composition in the context of ongoing ecological water replenishment; (2) To identify the dominant natural and anthropogenic processes controlling hydrochemical evolution; (3) To assess groundwater quality and delineate risk-based zones for regional groundwater resource management. The findings will provide a scientific basis for the rational development, utilization, and protection of groundwater resources in the Daihai Basin, and will serve as a reference for similar arid and semi-arid regions.

Materials and methods

Study area

Daihai Lake is located in the central part of Liangcheng County, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China, in the transitional zone between the Loess Plateau and the Inner Mongolia Plateau. 95% of the drainage area is distributed within Liangcheng County, Ulanqab City. The geographical coordinates of the study area are between 112°21’ and 112°54’ east longitude and 40°20 ’and 40°50’ north latitude (Fig. 1). The study area is bordered by Zhuozhi County to the north, Xinrong County, Zuoyun County and Youyu County of Shanxi Province to the south, Fengzhen City to the east and Yongxing Town of Liangcheng County to the west. The study area belongs to the southern extension of the Yinshan Mountain Range, and its landform presents a typical basin-mountain pattern. Surrounded by mountains on all sides, the northern Manhan Mountain, Jiushe Mountain and Wulahada Mountain are composed of Archean granite and gneiss. The southern Ma Tou Mountain is a basalt plateau, sloping from south to north. The eastern part belongs to the Fengzhen Hills, while the western part has an open terrain. The central part is an alluvial and lacustrine plain. The Daihai Lake in the center of the basin is the lowest base level of erosion in the region (about 1,200 meters above sea level), which is a typical inland lake. It relies on more than 20 surrounding rivers (such as the Gongba River, Wuhao River, Tiancheng River) and middle-level groundwater replenishment, and there is no surface water outlet channel.Fig. 1The distribution of sampling points in the study area.

The distribution of sampling points in the study area.

The hydrogeological system in the study area exhibits distinct zonal characteristics. The northern mountainous area is dominated by bedrock fissure water, while the central plain area is primarily characterized by pore phreatic water in Quaternary unconsolidated sediments. Groundwater runoff in the basin is generally slow, with a consistent flow direction toward the central Daihai Lake. The primary recharge pathways include precipitation infiltration, lateral mountain-front recharge, and gully flow infiltration during the wet season; the dominant discharge modes are phreatic evaporation and artificial extraction. Aquifer architecture is mainly controlled by alluvial and lacustrine processes, with sediment grain size gradually fining from the basin margin to the center. Groundwater in the study area is classified into three categories based on the characteristics of its occurrence medium. The Quaternary unconsolidated rock pore water is the dominant water-bearing type in the area, hosted in the Quaternary sand, sandy gravel and gravel beds of the alluvial-lacustrine and piedmont plains, with a maximum aquifer thickness of up to 800 m in the basin’s central hinterland. This aquifer features favorable permeability and sufficient recharge, with wide variability in water yield capacity. Next is the Cretaceous–Neogene pore-fissure water, which is only locally distributed. Bedrock in the study area is sporadically exposed, with only a small amount of bedrock fissure water developed. Surface water and groundwater in the basin have a complex transformation relationship, and the river network forms a close hydraulic connection with the aquifer system, which together maintain the water balance of Daihai Lake. As a closed inland hydrological system with no surface outflow, Daihai Lake has undergone severe areal shrinkage and progressive salinization over the past 50 years. As reported by Zhang15, the average total dissolved solids (TDS) of the lake water reached 11.59 g/L in 2018, with a mean pH of 9.47, representing a 0.77 increase from the pH value of 8.7 recorded in 1989. This long-term salinization and continuous lake retreat have driven substantial enrichment of soluble salts in the vadose zone around the lake basin. Notably, this salt-enriched vadose zone is located along the main flow path of groundwater discharging into Daihai Lake, and thus acts as a key source of Na+ and Cl− in the surrounding groundwater.

The Daihai Basin, as the main part of the basin, spreads in a northeastward direction between the Manhan Mountain Range in the north and the Matou Mountain Range in the south. It has formed a cutting-type faulted basin. The Maihutu borehole in the northeast of the basin has revealed sedimentary strata over 240 meters, among which the strata below 141 meters are from the Early Pleistocene. Above 141 meters are Middle Pleistocene strata and Late Pleistocene loess deposits. The aquifer is composed of sandy pebbles, medium-coarse sand and silt, and the particles gradually become finer from the edge of the basin towards the lakeside. The groundwater depth gradually changes from 50 meters to less than 2 meters towards the lakeside, transitioning from phreatic water to confined water. The lakeside silty clay layer serves as an excellent impermeable layer, and in some parts of the lakeside area, it forms a self-flowing water. On the mountain front alluvial fan in the Qingfudian - Xiamaoqing Sumu area in the northeastern part of the basin, the upper-middle aquifer is composed of gravel and pebbles. There are 2 to 3 aquifers at a depth of 60 meters. The groundwater is not confined, and the water level burial depth is generally 15 to 40 meters, with abundant water volume. Combined with the long-term observation data of regional groundwater level and lake water level in the study area, the groundwater level is generally higher than the water level of Daihai Lake, with a stable hydraulic gradient pointing to the lake. Overall, the groundwater presents a unidirectional recharge characteristic to Daihai Lake, and groundwater is an important stable recharge source of Daihai Lake. Detailed descriptions of the topography, geological structure and hydrogeological characteristics of the Daihai Lake Basin are provided in Zhang15.

Water sample collection and testing

From October to November 2024, a total of 37 groundwater samples, 9 lake water samples, and 3 river water samples were collected in the alluvial and lacustrine plain area of the Daihai Lake Basin (the distribution of sampling points is shown in Fig. 1). The sampling design considered regional hydrogeological conditions and the spatial distribution of aquifer groups to ensure regional representativeness. All procedures for sample collection, preservation, and transportation strictly adhered to the Chinese national standards “Quality Standard for Groundwater” (GB/T 14848—2024) and “Methods for Analysis of Groundwater Quality - Part 2: Collection and Preservation of Water Samples” (DZ 0064.2—2021). Field measurements of pH and total dissolved solids (TDS) were conducted using a YSI ProQuatro multiparameter water quality meter (USA). The meter has a pH measurement accuracy of ±0.05 pH units, and a TDS measurement accuracy of ±0.5% full scale (FS). Calibration of pH and TDS was completed using standard calibration solutions supplied with the instrument prior to testing, to guarantee the precision of in-situ measurement data. The remaining parameters were analyzed in the Water Resources and Environment Laboratory of Hebei GEO University.

Laboratory analysis employs advanced analytical methods. Anions such as Cl−, SO₄2−, NO₃−, and F− are determined using the Mantong IC940 ion chromatograph (IC), with detection limits ranging from 0.01 to 0.1 mg/L. The determination of cations K+, Na+, Ca2+ and Mg2+ was carried out by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP—OES), and the detection limit of the method was 0.001–0.01. CO₃2− and HCO₃− were determined by the traditional HCl titration method. To ensure the high precision and reliability of the test data, blank tests were conducted in the laboratory for each batch of samples. The test results were all below the detection limit of the method (Table 1), and the anion and cation charge balance tests were carried out on the laboratory test results. The main ions K+, Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, SO₄2−, CO₃2−, and HCO₃− were selected for calculation. Research methods

Self-organizing map

The Self-Organizing Map (SOM) is an unsupervised learning algorithm that maps input data onto a two-dimensional grid through competitive learning, where each neuron represents a weight vector corresponding to the input data. The SOM preserves the topological structure of the data, ensuring that similar data points are positioned adjacent to one another on the grid. Combining K-means clustering with SOM allows for more refined cluster analysis16. By first applying K-means clustering, the SOM can initiate learning from more reasonable starting points, thereby enhancing both training efficiency and outcomes. This hybrid approach optimizes clustering performance while effectively reducing computational costs. Furthermore, the SOM provides visualization through component planes, which display the distribution of each feature dimension. The color gradients within these component planes reflect weight variations among different neurons, offering an intuitive representation of the intrinsic structure of the data. Prior to training the SOM, it is necessary to determine the grid size and the number of neurons. In this study, the dimension of the dataset is known, so the number of neurons is 30 and the grid size is set to 5×6.

Evaluation of groundwater quality

The Entropy Weighted Water Quality Index (EWQI) method is an evaluation method based on the combination of entropy weight method and water quality index, which is used to comprehensively evaluate the quality of water18. The entropy weight method mainly determines the weight of each water quality indicator by calculating the information entropy of each indicator, and obtains the final water quality score by integrating these indicators. This method avoids the deviation of artificially specifying weights, making the evaluation results more objective and scientific19. The calculation steps of the entropy weight water quality index method are as follows: First, standardize the data. Standardization can convert the data to a unified dimensional range, and then the entropy weight method is used to determine the weights. The greater the entropy, the more uniform the information distribution of the indicator. Then, the weight is calculated based on the entropy value. Results and analysis

Spatial characteristics of hydrochemical parameters

The spatial distribution of groundwater ion concentrations in the study area exhibits significant regional heterogeneity (see Fig. 2a–l). As shown in the figures, the distributions of TDS and the concentrations of Cl−, SO₄2−, HCO₃−, Na+, and Mg2+ in the study area are relatively similar. with high-value areas primarily concentrated near Daihai Lake in the Liusumu Township, with secondary high-concentration zones extending to Yongxing Town. This distribution pattern is jointly controlled by hydrogeological conditions and human activities. The presence of a dense network of small lakes and rivers in the southwest of Daihai Lake promotes the migration of dissolved salts into the groundwater to a certain extent. Furthermore, coupled with the area’s flat topography and high population density20 agricultural irrigation return flow and domestic wastewater discharge further exacerbate ion enrichment. Areas with relatively high Ca2+ concentrations are located in Tiancheng Township and the southern part of Maihutu Town (Fig. 2g), with secondary high values extending into Liusumu Town and Yongxing Town. The concentration of K+ is generally below 10 mg/L (Fig. 2j), and its spatial variability has a negligible influence on TDS. Fluoride (F−) concentrations are significantly higher in the northern part of Daihai Lake compared to the southern region. Elevated nitrate (NO₃−) levels are mainly distributed in the southern area adjacent to Daihai Lake.Fig. 2Map of spatial distribution of groundwater ion concentrations.

Self-organizing clustering results

We determined the optimal neuron number as 30 in accordance with the standard empirical formula. By analyzing the component planes, the clustering trends of the data, as well as the similarities and differences among neurons, can be effectively visualized. As shown in (Fig. 3), yellow and blue colors represent areas of high and low ion concentrations in groundwater samples within the neuron map, respectively. The concentration distributions of Na++K+, Cl−, HCO₃−, and TDS exhibit similar spatial trends, characterized by a gradual increase from top to bottom. The distributions of Ca2+ and NO₃− are relatively consistent, showing a decrease in concentration from the lower-left to the lower-right corner. The color gradient pattern of F− ions show low similarity with other indicators, suggesting a distinct origin, potentially derived from evaporative concentration or the dissolution of fluoride-bearing minerals21,22, which warrants further analysis based on mineral saturation indices.Fig. 3Spatial distribution map of groundwater ion concentrations.

Spatial distribution map of groundwater ion concentrations.

Based on significant spatial variations in neuron weights, the 37 groundwater samples were classified into three clusters using the K-means algorithm combined with the SOM: Cluster-1 comprises 4 samples, predominantly located in the lower-left region of the topology map; Cluster-2 includes 6 samples, concentrated in the lower-right region; and Cluster-3 contains 27 samples, mainly distributed in the central area. This clustering result reflects the spatial heterogeneity in hydrochemical characteristics among different samples.

Discussion

The main factors influencing water chemistry

Analysis of water chemistry control factors

The Gibbs diagram plots the equivalent concentration ratios of major ions, specifically γNa+/γ(Na+ + Ca2+) or γCl−/γ(Cl− + HCO₃−), against total dissolved solids (TDS), thereby helping to discern the contributions of different controlling factors to hydrochemical evolution9,23.

Analysis of groundwater hydrochemistry in the study area (Fig. 5) reveals that all samples from Cluster-3 fall within the rock weathering dominance zone, indicating that water–rock interaction is the primary mechanism controlling their composition. Samples from Cluster-1 and Cluster-2 are distributed across both the rock weathering and evaporation dominance zones, suggesting that both water–rock interaction and evaporation concentration collectively influence their hydrochemical characteristics. All samples are located far from the atmospheric precipitation end-member, indicating that the influence of atmospheric precipitation on the groundwater chemical composition in the study area is relatively weak.Fig. 5The Gibbs map of surface water and groundwater in the study area.

The Gibbs map of surface water and groundwater in the study area.

The average TDS concentration of Dai Hai Lake water is 14,003.2 mg/L, significantly higher than that of other water bodies. Both the γNa+/γ(Na+ + Ca2+) and γCl−/γ(Cl− + HCO₃−) ratios approach 1.0. Combined with its position in the Gibbs diagram, which is far from the atmospheric precipitation end-member, indicating that the hydrochemical evolution of the lake is predominantly controlled by evaporation concentration, which is consistent with the strong evaporation and salt accumulation process under the arid and semi-arid climate conditions in the region24.

Source analysis of chemical ions in water

The use of ion ratios can further clarify the types of mineral sources influencing the hydrochemical characteristics25. Dissolution of halite releases Na+ and Cl−. When the equivalent concentration ratio of Na+ to Cl⁻ is approximately 1:1, it indicates that halite dissolution dominates the chemical composition of the water26. The enrichment mechanism of high-fluoride groundwater

Groundwater with a fluoride concentration exceeding 1 mg/L is widely classified as high-fluoride groundwater36. Our preceding analysis identified fluoride-exceeding samples in both Cluster-2 and Cluster-3, with fluoride enrichment in these samples driven by the combined effects of hydrochemical conditions, climatic setting, and hydrogeological characteristics. Groundwater in the study area has a pH range of 7.4–8.6, corresponding to a neutral to weakly alkaline environment that is geochemically favorable for fluoride enrichment (Fig. 8a). First, fluoride readily substitutes for hydroxyl groups (OH−) in mineral lattices, given their nearly identical ionic radii and charge, which drives the release of F− into groundwater37. Second, neutral to weakly alkaline conditions reduce the aqueous activity of Ca2+, thereby inhibiting the precipitation of fluorite (CaF₂)38. Additionally, F− mass concentration shows a significant positive trend with increasing HCO₃− concentration (Fig. 8b), indicating that competitive adsorption between HCO₃− and F− on mineral surfaces further promotes fluoride desorption39.Fig. 8Diagram of groundwater pH, γHCO₃−, SI and TDS versus γF−.

Diagram of groundwater pH, γHCO₃−, SI and TDS versus γF−.

Calculated fluorite SI are predominantly strongly negative, ranging from −3.88 to 0.06 with a mean value of −1.7. Only one sampling point yields an SI > 0, indicating fluorite saturation; for all other samples, SI values are < 0, signifying that groundwater is undersaturated with respect to fluorite. Furthermore, F− mass concentration exhibits a significant positive correlation with fluorite solubility (Fig. 8c), confirming that continuous congruent dissolution of fluorite is the dominant source of F− enrichment in groundwater across the study area. Notably, the Daihai Lake Basin is situated within Ulanqab City, which holds a retained fluorite resource reserve of 15.342 million tons; fluorite mining, processing, and related deep industries are mainly concentrated in Fengzhen City and Siziwang Banner within the municipality. Groundwater in the eastern Daihai Basin receives predominant lateral recharge from the upstream Fengzhen City area. F− released via leaching of fluorine-bearing minerals in this upstream region can be transported to the lake periphery along regional groundwater flow paths, representing an additional potential source of fluoride in groundwater in the eastern study area.

The arid and semi-arid climatic characteristics of the Daihai Lake Basin further intensify the enrichment of F⁻ via intense evaporation and concentration40. Particularly in the downstream area of the Gongba River adjacent to the lakeshore zone (the distribution area of Cluster-2), two outlier samples were detected, with a maximum F− concentration of 6.88 mg/L. Groundwater in this area is dominated by HCO₃−-Na+ type water, with relatively high contents of Na+ and HCO₃−, which show no significant correlation with the total dissolved solids (TDS) concentration of groundwater (Fig. 8d). The common ion effect further inhibits the precipitation of fluorine-bearing minerals41. Spatially, high-fluoride groundwater is mainly distributed in the lakeshore retention zone and the fine-grained sedimentary zone at the front of the alluvial-proluvial fan (a fan-shaped sedimentary body formed by mountain flood flowing out of the mountain pass). Long-term water-rock interaction and ion exchange by clay minerals in these areas jointly prolong the retention time of F− in groundwater.

The response mechanism of ecological water replenishment to groundwater chemical characteristics

The Daihai Ecological Water Replenishment Project was officially launched in September 2022 alongside the “Yellow River to Daihai” ecological emergency water replenishment project. As of September 2024, a total of 39.47 million cubic meters of water have been transferred from the Yellow River and injected into Daihai Lake, effectively curbing the shrinking trend of Daihai Lake. According to monitoring data from the Yellow River Conservancy Commission under the Ministry of Water Resources, the water quality of the Yellow River’s Inner Mongolia section has consistently met Class II or higher standards for five consecutive years up to the end of 2024. The water from the Yellow River has low suspended sediment content and a salinity level between 0.43–0.63 g/L, classifying it as low-salinity freshwater.

The ecological water replenishment project has exerted preliminary impacts on the groundwater environment of Daihai Lake Basin, though its mechanisms remain complex. This complexity stems from the combined effects and competition among multiple hydrogeological processes, ultimately leading to differential changes in ion concentrations. Due to the lack of systematic baseline monitoring data prior to the replenishment, this study utilized sampling data from two previous studies conducted in August 2019 and January 2020 around Daihai Lake as background reference values. These included 17 sets of groundwater samples and 3 sets of lake water samples collected in 2019, along with 29 sets of shallow groundwater samples and 8 sets of lake water samples collected in 2020. The mean ion concentrations from these samples were then compared and analyzed.

The results (Table 5) indicate that compared with pre-replenishment levels, the average concentrations of K+, Na+, Mg2+, Cl−, SO42−, HCO3−, and TDS in shallow groundwater increased after water replenishment, while Ca2+ and NO3− concentrations decreased. Specifically, TDS rose from 386 mg/L to 625.18 mg/L, with Na+ and Cl− on concentrations continuing to increase. The Na/Cl molar ratio improved from 0.83 to 1.32, approaching the typical lake water ratio range of 0.62–0.69. Spatial analysis of ion concentrations reveals that the western Daihai Lake area (Liangcheng County) experienced significant increases in TDS, Na− and Cl− concentrations post-replenishment. The elevated salinity (Na+ and Cl−) in groundwater after ecological water replenishment is mainly controlled by the coupled leaching-concentration mechanism: water level rise driven by water replenishment enhances the leaching of accumulated salts in the vadose zone into groundwater, while intense evaporation in the arid and semi-arid region further promotes the concentration of dissolved ions in shallow groundwater, ultimately leading to the increase in salinity. Field monitoring data of vadose zone soil water in the study area15 further confirm this mechanism: the lakeside shallow vadose zone has accumulated a large amount of Na+ and Cl− (up to 1239 mg/kg and 1950 mg/kg, respectively), and the salt concentration is positively correlated with soil water content. This indicates that water level rise driven by ecological replenishment will promote the leaching of accumulated salts in the vadose zone into groundwater, which is the core source of elevated salinity in shallow groundwater.

On the other hand, NO3- is a typical characteristic pollution indicator input by anthropogenic agricultural activities, and its spatial distribution has a significant corresponding relationship with the land use types of the basin. The western part of the Daihai Lake Basin is the core agricultural planting area of the basin, where the land use type is dominated by contiguous cultivated land, serving as the core input area of agricultural non-point source nitrogen in the basin; scattered agricultural land is distributed around the lakeside zone, which also has the input risk of agricultural non-point source pollution. Corresponding to the spatial distribution characteristics, the groundwater NO₃− concentrations in the lakeside zone and the core agricultural area in the western part of the basin generally showed a downward trend. This phenomenon is most likely attributed to the dilution effect of ecological water replenishment via Yellow River water diversion, indicating that the ecological water replenishment has a significant dilution and purification effect on exogenous anthropogenic pollutants such as NO₃− from agricultural non-point sources. However, the above inference of the mechanism still needs further verification. The changes in groundwater ion concentrations may also be regulated by a combination of factors, including concurrent agricultural irrigation activities during the water replenishment period (e.g., field application of potassium and sodium-containing waste materials), interference from other human activities, and fluctuations in natural hydrological conditions. Limited by the lack of long-term continuous in-situ monitoring data and more precise hydrochemical end-member information, the individual contribution rate of ecological water replenishment to the changes in groundwater NO₃− concentrations in the agricultural area, as well as the influence weight of different anthropogenic activities and natural factors on the evolution of groundwater quality, still need to be further clarified through subsequent long-term positioning observation and quantitative simulation studies. In addition to agricultural non-point source inputs, improper disposal of domestic sewage, human waste and rural domestic garbage is another non-negligible anthropogenic source of nitrate in regional groundwater9. Most rural residents in the study area lack centralized domestic sewage treatment facilities, and the leakage of untreated domestic sewage and leaching of human waste will continuously input nitrate into the shallow aquifer, which has a non-negligible potential impact on the spatial distribution of groundwater nitrate in the lakeshore zone and western agricultural area.

The influence of human activities

Groundwater chemical composition is generally influenced by both natural processes and human activities. Human activities such as agricultural production and mineral mining will input a large number of SO42−, Cl−, NO3−, Na+ ions into the environment, which will significantly change the chemical composition of groundwater. Specifically, mining activities often lead to the increase of SO42− content, while Cl- and NO3-concentrations are important indicators of agricultural influence9,30. The correlation between γ(Cl−)/γ(Na+) and γ(NO₃−)/γ(Na+) can usually be used to assess the impact of human activities on the NO₃−component in groundwater. Nitrogen fertilizers applied across widely distributed farmland in the basin plains undergo microbial transformation and subsequently enter the aquifer through irrigation return flow and precipitation leaching, leading to an increase in NO3−concentration (Fig. 9). Furthermore, fluoride concentrations in both Cluster-2 and Cluster-3 exceed the groundwater quality standard, with values as high as 6 mg/L. The enrichment mechanisms exhibit spatial variability. To further clarify the influence of human activities, as shown in the relationship diagram (Fig. 8b), the ratios γ(NO₃−)/γ(Ca2+) and γ(SO₄2−)/γ(Ca2+) can be used to evaluate impacts from mining and agricultural activities. The results of this study show that the hydrochemical characteristics of Cluster-1 show the contribution of agricultural activities and domestic sewage to groundwater components, while Cluster-2 shows the characteristics related to mining activities. Spatial interpolation of EWQI values (Fig. 10b) demonstrated that zones with high-quality groundwater were mainly distributed along the northeastern shore of Daihai Lake, whereas zones with degraded water quality were concentrated at the piedmont alluvial fan front along the southwestern lakeshore. This distinct spatial pattern of groundwater quality is jointly governed by natural hydrogeological processes and anthropogenic activities. The northeastern region benefits from consistent lateral recharge from mountain front runoff and favorable hydrodynamic conditions, which maintain good groundwater quality. In contrast, groundwater quality in the southwestern region has undergone significant degradation driven by intense evaporative concentration coupled with the impacts of intensive agricultural activities. A large proportion of the study area is occupied by cultivated land; the extensive application of chemical fertilizers and pesticides introduces contaminants that can infiltrate shallow groundwater via surface runoff and soil leaching, leading to elevated concentrations of nitrate, nitrite and pesticide residues in groundwater. This not only degrades groundwater quality, but also exerts a secondary impact on lake water quality through groundwater-surface water exchange. Notably, although ecological water replenishment via the Gongba River has significantly elevated the groundwater level in the southwestern region and achieved initial water volume recovery targets, its ameliorative effect on groundwater quality along the conveyance route has been relatively limited and lagged.

The groundwater circulation characteristics clarified in this study play a key regulatory role in the water balance and water-salt balance of Daihai Lake. In terms of water balance, groundwater is an important stable recharge source of Daihai Lake. The core Quaternary pore aquifer and the main runoff areas in the western and northern parts of the basin identified in this study are the core foundation for maintaining the stability of lake water volume, while artificial groundwater exploitation will reduce the lake recharge and aggravate lake shrinkage. In terms of water-salt balance, the closed Daihai Lake has no salt discharge channel. Groundwater is continuously enriched with salt during the runoff process to the lake, and is input into the lake with lateral runoff, which is an important factor driving the long-term salinization of the lake. The main groundwater runoff areas identified in this study are also the core channels for salt input into the lake.

Conclusions

Against the backdrop of the ongoing ecological water replenishment in the Daihai Lake Basin, this study addressed the pre-identified research gaps by systematically characterizing the spatial heterogeneity of groundwater hydrochemistry, deciphering the dominant evolutionary mechanisms, and delineating risk zones for water resource management. The integrated findings not only fully fulfill the three proposed research objectives, but also provide actionable insights for regional ecological restoration and livelihood security. The core conclusions are synthesized as follows:

First, the hydrochemical evolution of groundwater in the basin is governed by a coupled "natural-anthropogenic" multi-process control mechanism, with distinct spatial differentiation.

Specifically, for the wide alluvial-pluvial plain area (Cluster-3), water-rock interaction (including coupled dissolution of carbonate, silicate and evaporite minerals) dominates the hydrochemical composition, with limited evaporation influence, forming the stable freshwater background of the basin. For the lakeshore zone (Cluster-1 and Cluster-2), the evolution is co-controlled by water-rock interaction and evaporative concentration: the long-term closed-basin arid climate drives continuous salt accumulation in the shallow vadose zone, while widespread cation exchange processes further modify the ion balance to drive the evolution from freshwater to brackish water. Notably, the ongoing ecological water replenishment project has triggered a unique "dilution-migration-rebalancing" evolutionary process with an approximately 1-year lagged response: while the external low-salinity freshwater has effectively diluted anthropogenic pollutants such as agricultural nitrate, it has also intensified local evaporation under the high water table condition, leading to a gradual increase in background salinity (shallow groundwater TDS increased from 386 mg/L in 2019 to 625.18 mg/L in 2024) and dynamic adjustment of mineral saturation states. Furthermore, human activities have superimposed additional targeted controls: agricultural non-point source pollution has elevated nitrate levels in the southwestern lakeshore, while mining activities in the northwest have accelerated fluoride dissolution via acidic wastewater discharge, exacerbating the natural geogenic fluoride enrichment. This coupled mechanism clarifies the complex hydrochemical response to the ongoing ecological intervention, filling the critical knowledge gap of groundwater evolution under dynamic replenishment conditions that was neglected in previous studies.

Second, the groundwater quality risk exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity, posing targeted threats to the drinking water safety of rural residents in specific zones.

Our EWQI assessment reveals that the northeastern alluvial plain (Cluster-3) hosts high-quality groundwater, with 89% of samples meeting the direct drinking water standard (EWQI < 50), which can safely support long-term domestic water use. In contrast, the southwestern and northwestern lakeshore zones (Cluster-1 and Cluster-2) are identified as the primary high-risk areas: 70% of groundwater samples here are classified as Class V inferior water, with severe exceedances of multiple health-related indicators. Specifically, the northwestern lakeshore suffers from extreme high-fluoride groundwater, with the maximum F⁻ concentration reaching 6.88 mg/L (5.88 times the national drinking water standard limit), which will induce endemic fluorosis such as dental fluorosis and skeletal fluorosis for residents with long-term intake. The southwestern core agricultural zone faces dual risks of high nitrate (maximum 96 mg/L, 3.8 times the standard) and high salinity, which not only threatens the health of infants and pregnant women (causing methemoglobinemia) but also restricts the safety of local agricultural irrigation. Critically, these high-risk zones are exactly the areas where local rural residents still directly extract shallow groundwater as their main domestic water source, without effective water quality treatment, meaning these residents are exposed to long-term unrecognized health risks. This risk delineation addresses the previously neglected livelihood security issue in the basin, providing a clear target for public health intervention.

Third, the findings provide clear core considerations for the optimization of water resource management in the Daihai Lake Basin, breaking the limitation of traditional single-objective lake water level restoration.

Zoned groundwater resource management

The northeastern high-quality groundwater zone should be designated as the core drinking water source protection area, strictly restricting industrial and agricultural pollution discharge to maintain its stable water quality. For the southwestern and northwestern high-risk zones, direct extraction of shallow groundwater for drinking should be prohibited, and centralized water supply projects should be implemented to replace the current decentralized self-supplied water, to fundamentally eliminate residents’ health exposure risks.

Optimization of ecological water replenishment strategy

The current replenishment mode should be adjusted from "only focusing on lake volume restoration" to "coordinated regulation of water volume and water quality". Considering the 1-year lagged response of groundwater to replenishment, long-term continuous monitoring of groundwater level and quality along the water conveyance route should be strengthened, to avoid the potential risk of secondary salinization of shallow groundwater caused by excessive evaporation under high water level conditions. Meanwhile, the dilution effect of replenishment on agricultural non-point source pollutants can be fully utilized to moderately improve the water environment of the southwestern agricultural zone.

Long-term dynamic monitoring system

A joint surface water-groundwater monitoring network should be established, to track the long-term evolution of hydrochemical characteristics under the continuous ecological replenishment, to provide real-time data support for the adaptive adjustment of management strategies.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the hydrochemical evolution mechanism and risk distribution of groundwater in the Daihai Basin under the ongoing ecological water replenishment, which not only provides a solid scientific basis for the sustainable management of regional water resources, but also offers a valuable reference for the ecological restoration and water resource regulation of similar arid and semi-arid inland lake basins.
