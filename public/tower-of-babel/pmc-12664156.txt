Research on eggplant pest and disease detection in complex environments based on improved RT-DETR
Authors: Hui Sun, Rui Fu, Zhijun Zhang, Hao Sun, Yangkwon Jeong, Jinlong Lyu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate detection of pests and diseases in eggplant under real-field conditions remains challenging due to large variations in target scales, complex background clutter, and the frequent presence of small and occluded objects. To address these issues, this paper proposes Eggplant-DETR, an improved detection model based on the RT-DETR architecture. The model introduces a collaborative mechanism that integrates multi-scale feature enhancement, semantic fusion, and frequency perception. The key components of this mechanism include: (1) CPSE module is designed in the shallow layers of the backbone to enhance detailed features of small pest targets, providing more discriminative low-level features for subsequent processing and mitigating detail loss of small objects; (2) The CHSFPN module effectively integrates detail-enhanced features from CPSE and global semantic information from AIFI, generating multi-scale and semantically rich feature maps for the subsequent WTMANet module; (3) at the end of the encoder integrating the WTMANet module refines the multi-scale features from CHSFPN and significantly improves perception capability for irregularly shaped targets (e.g., FruitRot) and small objects (e.g., MelonThrips). Extensive experiments on a public eggplant pest and disease dataset demonstrate that the proposed method achieves mAP50 of 77.8%, parameters reduces to 15.77M. The overall performance surpasses that of the other 11 mainstream comparison models. The framework provides an effective solution for smart agricultural pest and disease detection, while also offering valuable technical insights into the application of frequency-domain feature representation in this field.

Introduction

Agriculture remains a vital economic sector in developing countries today, as the primary sources of economic and employment income in these nations largely depend on agricultural production [1, 2]. However, the agricultural sector faces numerous challenges, such as diseases, pests, and variable weather conditions. These factors can accelerate the spread of diseases and lead to food safety issues [3]. Plant diseases have become one of the significant barriers to agricultural production. According to reports from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), losses in food production caused by plant pests and diseases account for 20–40% of the global food output [4, 5]. Eggplant (Solanum melongena L.), one of the economically most valuable vegetable crops cultivated and consumed globally, is predominantly produced in Asia, which accounts for approximately 94% of the total production (FAOSTAT 2020). Among these countries, China has the highest eggplant yield, at 35.5 million tons (FAOSTAT 2020). As an important economic crop in Asia, diseases and pests pose a worldwide obstacle to eggplant production. If not detected in a timely manner, eggplant plants may be subject to various pests and diseases, ranging from mild symptom expression to the devastation of entire plantations, ultimately severely affecting the economic income of plantations and the enthusiasm of growers for production [6]. Therefore, timely detection of pests and diseases is crucial for preventing their spread in eggplant production and avoiding significant economic losses. Thus, continuous and accurate monitoring of pests and diseases is essential for rapid identification and prevention of their spread. Traditional manual detection of pests and diseases relies on visual inspection by agricultural practitioners, which is not only inefficient and labor-intensive but also prone to low reliability. These drawbacks render it incapable of meeting the precise identification requirements of modern large-scale agricultural production. To enhance the accuracy and efficiency of pest and disease detection in precision agriculture, researchers have been actively exploring various detection methods. With the advancement of computer vision and image processing algorithms, as well as the improvement of computer performance, image processing detection methods that simulate human vision using computer vision have become the mainstream approach. Target detection based on deep learning has also emerged as the predominant method for pest and disease detection in precision agriculture, a key technology for assisting agricultural safety early warning, and a research hotspot in related fields [7]. In summary, research on agricultural pest and disease detection, as a branch of the object detection field, has achieved rapid development and made significant progress. However, the main shortcomings in the research are as follows: the existing YOLO series perform poorly in recognizing small-sized, densely clustered pest targets; complex scenarios such as leaf occlusion,bright reflection, and lighting changes affect the quality of feature extraction; the lack of representative, high-quality annotated complex scene eggplant pest and disease datasets restricts model training and generalization ability verification; and the current mainstream detection models based on RT-DETR are difficult to implement high-precision, low-latency detection on edge devices. Related work

In recent years, deep learning models, especially Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers, have demonstrated significant potential [1, 14] and achieved remarkable research progress [2] in the field of plant disease detection in agriculture. The progress includes one-stage and two-stage object detection methods as well as object detection methods based on transformers, each with its own characteristics.

Common object detection algorithms

One-stage object detection algorithms such as SSD [15], the YOLO series, and RetinaNet [16] have been widely applied due to their real-time performance. Since YOLOv1, YOLO-based models have enabled fast detection, and numerous improvements have targeted agricultural pest and disease detection. For instance, Nasution et al. used YOLOv4 for real-time eggplant disease detection with camera images [17], Xie et al. proposed a lightweight YOLOv5s-BiPCNext model with superior performance on natural eggplant leaf images [18], Huang et al. developed YOLOv8-E by refining Backbone, Neck, and Loss to reduce model size while maintaining high mAP and F1 score [19], and Li et al. optimized feature fusion for small pests using an improved YOLOv10n [20]. Despite high speed and good accuracy, single-stage methods struggle with complex backgrounds, occlusion, and lack global context modeling.

Two-stage detectors, including SPP-Net [21], Fast R-CNN [22], Faster R-CNN [23], Cascade R-CNN [24], and Sparse R-CNN [9], improve accuracy via candidate region generation and classification but incur high computational cost and slow inference, limiting their applicability on resource-constrained devices [25].

End-to-end crop disease detectors

Recently, DETR [26] based on transformer is the first end-to-end object detection framework that applies transformer to the field of object detection. It has attracted research attention in the academic community due to its simplified architecture and the elimination of manually constructed components. Salamai, Abdullah Ali et al. proposed a rice disease perception vision transformer that accurately and reliably detects rice leaf diseases by identifying discriminative disease features [27]. DHLC-DETR proposed by YANG H et al. increased the mAP value of small targets in rice disease detection by 9.5% [28]. GM-DETR proposed by Xiao et al. introduced GAM (Global Attention Mechanism) to enhance the capture of defect edges and textures [29]. Although DETR simplified the traditional two-stage method, it still relies on pre-generated candidate regions to perform classification and localization tasks. DETR has a large number of parameters, high computational cost, and cannot meet the requirements of real-time detection.

RT-DETR, a novel transformer-based detection framework, overcomes a major limitation of the YOLO family of models, namely their reliance on the Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) post-processing step [30]. There are several studies that apply RT-DETR or its improved models to pest detection. For example, Chen et al. [27] proposed RTDETR-DRA, an improved model based on RT-DETR. The method was designed to address the high computational cost and low deployment efficiency of existing grape leaf disease detection models on resource-constrained devices. The improved model achieved higher disease recognition accuracy while maintaining a lightweight structure (with a 15.5% reduction in parameters). To enable selective harvesting of cucumbers under complex conditions such as varying illumination, DONG Shi et al. [31] proposed the RT-DETR-EV model. Experimental results showed that the model exhibits robustness and generalization across harvesting scenes with different lighting. It also meets the real-time detection requirements for cucumber fruits in complex growth environments. Improved RT-DETR proposed by Wu et al. reduced the model parameters and computational complexity while improving detection accuracy in fruit ripeness detection [32]. Meanwhile, the improved RT-DETR model significantly reduced the false-negative rate and improved detection accuracy compared with the baseline model and YOLOv8 in pest detection.

RT-DETR baseline model

RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer) is an end-to-end real-time object detection model based on the transformer architecture, proposed by the Baidu Paddle team in 2023. Its performance surpassed the state-of-the-art YOLOv8 detector at the time of release. The architecture of RT-DETR combines the strengths of CNNs and Transformers. Unlike the CNN-based YOLO detection head (decoupled convolutional head), the RT-DETR detection head employs a Dynamic Deformable Transformer Decoder [33]. Through sparse sampling in the attention module, the computational complexity is reduced from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( N^2\right)$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O\left( N\bullet K\right)$$\end{document}. Compared with DETR, RT-DETR is based on the parallel computing mechanism of transformer. By optimizing the network structure and introducing efficient components, it significantly improves real-time detection performance. The RT-DETR architecture shown in Fig. 2 mainly consists of three key components: a CNN-based Backbone, an Efficient Hybrid Encoder, and a Transformer Decoder with an auxiliary prediction head. The hybrid encoder is a key innovation of RT-DETR, including AIFI (Attention-based Intra-scale Feature Interaction) and CCFF (CNN-based Cross-scale Feature Fusion). AIFI processes high-level features from the backbone, and CCFF further fuses low-level and high-level features from the Backbone and AIFI. The fused features enter the decoder part. The model uses an uncertainty minimizing query selection mechanism to generate initial queries from the features and performs feature map interaction through self-attention and cross-attention mechanisms. Methodology

Overall architecture of eggplant-DETR

Eggplant-DETR is shown in Fig. 3. Its design is to further enhance the performance of RT-DETR in detecting eggplant fruit diseases in complex environments. The Eggplant-DETR based on RT-DETR r18. The implementation process of the model is as follows: The backbone network of the model is based on a lightweight design improvement of the ResNet-18 [35] algorithm. After extracting image features in the CPSE(as shown in Fig. 3-CPSE), the three stages of features with strides of 8, 16 and 32, namely S3,S4 and S5 are output to the Efficient Hybrid Encoder(as shown in Fig. 3). The encoder includes AIFI (as shown in Fig. 3-AIFI) and an improved cross-scale feature fusion module (CHSFPN) (as shown in Fig. 3-CHSFPN). The CHSFPN module is improved based on the feature pyramid design with high-level screening functions. After the encoder converts multi-scale features into a set of image features, they are fed into the self-developed module WTMANet(as shown in Fig. 3-WTMANet) to enhance the model’s gradient flow and improve the multi-scale expression of feature hierarchy. Subsequently, the uncertainty minimizing query selection method (IOU) is employed to select a fixed number of encoder features as the initial query objects for the decoder. Finally, the decoder iteratively optimizes the feature queries to generate pest and disease category detection and bounding boxes.

CHSFPN: cross-level hierarchical selective feature pyramid network

Accurate detection of pests and diseases in eggplant fruits remains challenging due to substantial scale variations between the fruits and the anomalies. Targets such as FruitBorers are particularly small and exhibit subtle features, while FruitRot and MelonThrips often display irregular morphologies. These characteristics complicate reliable detection by deep learning models. Furthermore, multi-level feature transmission suffers from progressive information attenuation. The limited channel capacity of high-level features (e.g., S5) further restricts cross-scale semantic fusion. Compared to the PAFPN architecture (which integrates FPA [36] and PAN [37]) and dense prediction mechanisms used in YOLOv8 models of similar scale, the pure transformer-based interaction in RT-DETR demonstrates limited adaptability for dense small object detection, especially for pests and diseases smaller than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$32^2$$\end{document} pixels. This constraint considerably limits the feature representation capacity in pest detection tasks.

To address these challenges, this paper introduces a Cross-level Hierarchical Selective Feature Pyramid Network (CHSFPN, see Fig. 3), an efficient cross-layer interaction module based on an explicit semantic feedback mechanism. The proposed module aims to reconstruct detection details, process multi-scale information, and enhance feature representation. It consists of two core components: a feature selection module and a feature fusion module.

Unlike conventional FPN and attention-based FPN variants, CHSFPN is specifically optimized for agricultural multi-scale pest detection. In contrast to HS-FPN designed for medical imaging applications such as white blood cell detection, our approach does not incorporate shallow features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_1$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_2$$\end{document}. These low-level features exhibit weak semantic expressiveness, limited receptive fields, and high sensitivity to noise interference. Instead, we initiate feature integration from level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_3$$\end{document} and above, as these features maintain spatial resolution while offering stronger semantic structural representation.

During the feature fusion stage, the Selective Feature Fusion (SFF) submodule (Fig. 7-SFF) performs precise feature integration through the following operations:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{att} = \text {BilinearUpsample}\left( T_{conv}(F_h)\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{out} = F_l \otimes \text {CA}(F_{att}) + F_{att} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Feature selection module

As illustrated in Fig. 7-Feature Selection, the feature selection module receives shallow feature maps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_3$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_4$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_5$$\end{document}, and the channel-aligned high-level feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_5$$\end{document} from Fig. 3-AIFI. These features are processed by a Channel Attention (CA) mechanism, detailed in Fig. 7-CA. The primary objective of this module is to extract discriminative features strongly associated with key regions such as lesions and pests along the channel dimension. This module aims to extract discriminative feature information highly correlated with key target regions such as lesions and pests in the channel dimension.

Its distinctive design simultaneously utilizes both global average pooling and global max pooling operations to extract salient features. Channel weights are generated through a shared-weight multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a Sigmoid function:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_c = \sigma \left( \text {MLP}\left( \text {GAP}(F) \oplus \text {GMP}(F)\right) \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

This design enhances discriminative features highly correlated with pest and disease regions while effectively suppressing irrelevant background interference. These weights reflect the relative importance of each channel in the current pest and disease detection task. Finally, by weighting the original feature maps through the channels, key features are enhanced and redundant information is suppressed, resulting in weighted filtered feature maps.

To achieve unified processing of multi-scale features, after processing by the CA module, all feature maps of different scales are compressed to 256 channels through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolution operation, providing dimensionally consistent input for subsequent feature fusion.

Select feature fusion

This paper employs a simplified Top-down Feature Fusion structure, as shown in Fig. 7-Top-down Feature Fusion, which includes three layers of features (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_5$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_4$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_3$$\end{document}). In each layer, the Select Feature Fusion (SFF) module is introduced to filter low-level features with high-level features, offering greater flexibility than traditional methods. It integrates semantic features from higher levels with spatial features from the current level. This design is similar to the traditional Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) [36], but it addresses the issue of information loss in traditional FPN by emphasizing the layer-by-layer transmission of information from high semantics (low resolution) to low semantics (high resolution). Compared to the four-layer fusion HS-FPN, the three-layer fusion design is more suitable for scenarios with limited computational resources and high real-time requirements.

As shown in Fig. 7-SFF, the SFF module fuses high-level and low-level features. The high-level feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_h \in \mathbb {R}^{c \times h \times w}$$\end{document} provides semantic cues but has low spatial resolution. The low-level feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_l \in \mathbb {R}^{c \times h_l \times w_l}$$\end{document} offers fine spatial details but weaker semantics. First, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_h$$\end{document} is expanded using transpose2d and bilinear upsampling to obtain:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{att} = BL(T_{conv}-Conv(F_h)). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Then, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{att}$$\end{document} enters the Channel Attention (CA) module to generate attention weights. These weights are multiplied with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_l$$\end{document}, enhancing target localization. Finally, the weighted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_l$$\end{document} is combined with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{att}$$\end{document}, producing the fused output:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{out} = F_l \times CA(F_{att}) + F_{att}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

This process enhances feature representation with simple operations and improves detection accuracy.

WTMANet

As shown in Fig. 8, we propose WTMANet, a multi-branch feature enhancement structure designed to address feature vanishing or inflation issues in deep networks. While ELAN [38] improves feature aggregation through stacked CBS (Conv-BN-SiLU) modules, it increases computational and parametric costs. Based on ELAN, our WTMA-Net incorporates depthwise separable convolution (DWConv), wavelet transform convolution (WTConv2d), and multi-scale bottleneck structures (WTBottleneck). By regulating short and long gradient paths, the network captures richer features, enhances multi-frequency information encoding, and improves robustness to scale variation and fine-grained details. This results in higher accuracy for detecting occluded and overlapping targets.Fig. 8WTMANet architecture diagram

WTMANet architecture diagram

Wavelet transform convolution

Wavelet transform provides an effective approach in signal processing to expand the convolutional receptive field. By decomposing an image into low-frequency and high-frequency components, it achieves multi-resolution representation and supplies rich multi-scale information. This operation not only enhances the receptive field of CNNs, enabling effective capture of multi-scale features, but also mitigates over-parameterization, thereby reducing model complexity and computational overhead.

As shown in Fig. 8-WTConv2d, the Wavelet Transform Convolution (WTConv2d) is based on the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) and depthwise separable convolution. Haar wavelet is adopted for its efficiency and simplicity. The method decomposes the input feature map into multi-level frequency bands, where depthwise convolution is applied. The decomposed features are then reconstructed through the Inverse Wavelet Transform (IWT), which effectively enlarges the receptive field. For an input feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X \in \mathbb {R}^{H \times W \times C}$$\end{document}, L levels of decomposition are performed, generating one low-frequency band \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{LL}^{\ell }$$\end{document} and three high-frequency bands \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{LH}^{\ell }, X_{HL}^{\ell }, X_{HH}^{\ell }$$\end{document}. After convolution, the bands are upsampled and linearly combined as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {IWT}_{\ell }(X_{\ell }) = &\text {Upsample}(X_{LL}^{\ell }) + \text {Conv2D}^{T}(X_{LH}^{\ell }) \\&+ \text {Conv2D}^{T}(X_{HL}^{\ell }) + \text {Conv2D}^{T}(X_{HH}^{\ell }). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The final output is the sum of all levels. This design allows exponential receptive field growth with relatively few parameters.

WTBottleneck architecture

As shown in Fig. 8-WTBottleneck, the WTBottleneck is the basic unit of WTMANet. It consists of two sequential WTConv2d layers with a residual connection. This design enhances information retention and stabilizes gradient propagation.

Each WTConv2d layer performs wavelet transform convolution, capturing both low-frequency structures and high-frequency details. The output feature Y is defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Y = X + WTConv2d(WTConv2d(X)). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Here, the input feature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X = \{x_{h,w,c} \,|\, 1 \le h \le H,\, 1 \le w \le W,\, 1 \le c \le C \}$$\end{document}, where H, W, and C denote height, width, and channels. The residual connection adds the original input to the transformed features, overlaying high-frequency details and edges. This design preserves semantic information while improving edge response and texture representation.

Split branch strategy and multi-path information fusion

WTMANet adopts a three-branch structure to process different channel features. The main branch applies depthwise convolution (DWConv) to capture spatial and channel information while reducing parameters and improving efficiency [39]. The bypass branch introduces the WTBottleneck to enhance high-order feature expression, combining context perception and detail retention. Outputs from the three branches are concatenated along the channel dimension, followed by a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 1$$\end{document} convolution for compression and mixing. BatchNorm and ReLU are then applied to improve stability and feature expression.

In summary, WTMANet integrates a multi-branch design with wavelet-based frequency modeling. This enables strong semantic retention, texture response, and scale generalization. Such properties make it well-suited for detection tasks in complex backgrounds. Experiments in Chapter 4 further confirm that WTMANet improves recognition accuracy for small and occluded targets in pest and disease detection.

Experimental results and analysis

Eggplant-DETR(OURS) achieves state-of-the-art performance on the public eggplant pest and disease dataset, with Precision, Recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 reaching 85.1%, 71.1%, 77.8%, and 49.1%, respectively. Compared to the baseline model, the number of parameters and computational requirements are reduced by 21.2% and 18.4%, respectively. Its high mAP and high precision make it particularly suitable for the complex environment compared to 16 other mainstream detection models. On the eggplant dataset with four categories:FruitRot, FruitBorer, Healthy, and MelonThrips,our model’s mAP50 is increased by 4%, 2.3%, 7%, and 5.8% respectively compared to the baseline model. Our model demonstrated its advantages in complex scene detection. Such as: (1) The comparative experiments under complex background conditions demonstrate that the Eggplant–DETR(OURS) model outperforms existing mainstream models in terms of bounding box localization, classification accuracy, and confidence stability. Particularly for the small-target category Melon Thrips;Eggplant–DETR model effectively suppresses background interference, reduces false positives and false negatives, and exhibits superior detection robustness. These results provide strong evidence that the introduced modules significantly enhance small-object detection and adaptability to complex agricultural environments. In the occlusion contrast experiment, other comparative models exhibited multiple healthy detection boxes and suffered from multi-target confusion; (2) In the comparative experiments under severe occlusion by stems and leaves, the improved Eggplant-DETR(OURS) demonstrates significant advantages. Unlike existing mainstream models that suffer from redundant detections, false positives, and boundary drift under occlusion, Eggplant-DETR(OURS) maintains robust detection performance with more accurate and concise bounding boxes. Particularly, it achieves higher reliability in small-object recognition even under challenging conditions. These results confirm that the proposed improvements (CPSE + CHSFPN + WTMANet) significantly enhance feature extraction and robustness to occlusion in complex agricultural environments.

Although our model has achieved a significant improvement in overall detection performance, with a notable increase in the mAP50 value, there are still several areas that require further enhancement and optimization. Notably, the detection of the MelonThrips category remains a critical area for improvement. Specifically, the increase in mAP50 for MelonThrips is the lowest among the four detection categories, indicating that the model’s ability to identify this particular target is still relatively limited. This finding suggests that, despite the overall superior performance of the proposed method in detection, there are still deficiencies in handling small targets, weak features, or high background interference in the MelonThrips category.

Conclutions

Based on the RT-DETR r18 architecture, this paper proposes Eggplant-DETR, an enhanced object detection model tailored for eggplant pest and disease identification. Extensive ablation and comparative experiments confirm that Eggplant-DETR attains an effective balance between detection accuracy, computational efficiency, and adaptability to challenging environments. While Eggplant-DETR surpasses 11 state-of-the-art models in overall performance, certain limitations remain. Addressing the limitations in detecting small targets of MelonThrips,generalization capability under few-shot conditions requires further improvement. Future research will emphasize few-shot learning and synthetic data augmentation to improve detection accuracy and robustness for categories with limited samples, thereby offering a more targeted approach for small object recognition in complex agricultural settings. Moreover, given the constraints that model complexity may impose on practical deployment, subsequent studies will incorporate advanced lightweight techniques to reduce parameter size and computational overhead while maintaining detection precision, thus facilitating real-world application.

In summary, Eggplant-DETR provides an efficient and reliable solution for intelligent pest and disease detection in eggplants under complex conditions, and establishes a solid theoretical and practical foundation for extending RT-DETR applications within smart agriculture. As object detection technologies continue to evolve and become more widespread, pest and disease detection technologies will also continue to develop and update.
