Radiology-based artificial intelligence for predicting targeted therapy response in pan-cancer: a comprehensive review
Authors: Bo Yang, Silin Chen, Yunze Wang, Huiran Wang, Jiaqi Deng, Yufei Liu, Jiayi Ran, Yishu Deng, Tailin Li, Xiaohan Zhang, Lian Wang, Xiaochen Zhang, Yue Wang, Huaqiong Huang, David C. Hay, Ava Khamseh, Syed Ahmar Shah, Canrong Long, Shuifang Chen, Bing Xia, Jian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTargeted therapy is central to precision oncology, but identifying patients who will benefit remains challenging. Conventional molecular testing, though the current standard, provides limited predictive value. With recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and the widespread availability of imaging data, radiology-based AI models have emerged as valuable non-invasive tools for treatment response assessment.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive review of 112 studies that developed radiology-based AI models for predicting responses to targeted therapy across various cancer types. The reviewed models were classified into direct prediction approaches, which use end-to-end imaging-based modeling to estimate therapeutic response, and indirect prediction approaches, which infer molecular biomarkers from imaging features to indirectly assess therapeutic sensitivity.ResultsAcross the identified literature, computed tomography (CT) was the most frequently used imaging modality, followed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and ultrasound (US). Lung and breast cancers were the most commonly studied diseases, though work has also expanded into gastric, colorectal, liver, kidney, brain, and ovarian cancers. Both machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) frameworks have been applied, with ML remaining dominant but DL gaining increasing attention in recent years, likely because ML offers interpretability and suitability for smaller datasets, whereas DL excels in handling complex, high-dimensional data. Collectively, these studies demonstrate promising performance in predicting response to targeted therapy, while also highlighting the diversity of cancer contexts and methodological designs.ConclusionRadiology-based AI offers a non-invasive approach to guide treatment selection and monitoring in targeted therapy. This review summarizes current progress, highlights strengths and limitations of direct and indirect prediction strategies, and discusses future directions. To support accessibility, we also provide a continuously updated interactive website of included resources.

Introduction

In recent years, the paradigm of precision oncology has increasingly emphasized the role of targeted therapies in the management of diverse malignancies [1, 2]. By selectively inhibiting oncogenic driver mutations or aberrant molecular pathways, targeted agents have markedly improved survival and quality of life in specific patient subgroups [1, 3]. Nevertheless, stratifying patients solely on the basis of tissue-based molecular testing is associated with notable limitations. Molecular assays typically require invasive biopsies, and their accuracy may be compromised by tumor heterogeneity and sampling bias, thereby failing to capture the complete genomic landscape of disease [4–6]. Furthermore, the presence of a molecular alteration does not necessarily translate into therapeutic sensitivity; treatment efficacy is influenced by mutation subtype, co-occurring genomic alterations, resistance mechanisms, and tumor–microenvironment interactions [1, 3]. These challenges highlight the need for complementary tools to refine patient selection for targeted therapies.

Against this backdrop, medical imaging has emerged as a promising modality for therapy prediction. Unlike tissue-based assays, imaging offers non-invasive, pan-cancer, and repeatable characterization, capturing not only macroscopic morphology but also spatial heterogeneity and phenotypic features that may reflect tumor biology and microenvironmental states [5–8]. With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI), imaging analysis has evolved from qualitative visual inspection to quantitative, automated, and predictive modeling [9]. This paradigm shift has created opportunities to incorporate imaging-derived information into frameworks for targeted therapy response prediction, thereby complementing molecular diagnostics [7, 8].

AI-based imaging approaches for targeted therapy prediction can be broadly categorized into two methodological paradigms. Direct prediction strategies establish an association between imaging-derived representations and clinical outcomes, aiming to predict whether a patient will benefit from a given targeted therapy. These studies typically leverage AI models to learn latent phenotypic signatures that reflect drug sensitivity, capturing morphological, textural, or temporal dynamics from CT, MRI, or PET images. By contrast, indirect prediction strategies focus on inferring the presence of actionable molecular alterations—such as EGFR mutations, ALK rearrangements, or other mutations—from imaging features, with these alterations then serving as biological surrogates for therapeutic eligibility. This approach integrates radiogenomics and imaging biomarkers, providing a non-invasive means to approximate molecular testing [5, 7]. Both direct and indirect paradigms can be implemented using diverse AI methodologies, ranging from conventional machine learning (ML) algorithms applied to handcrafted radiomic or habitat features [10, 11] to advanced deep learning (DL) architectures capable of learning hierarchical representations directly from raw imaging data. The selection of methodology is typically guided by data scale, study design, endpoint definition, and requirements for interpretability or generalizability, rather than by any inherent superiority of a particular paradigm.

Taken together, AI-driven imaging represents a promising frontier for predicting targeted therapy response, extending the scope of molecular diagnostics and supporting individualized clinical decision-making [1, 7]. This review systematically summarizes the current landscape of imaging-based AI models in pan-cancer targeted therapy, organizing studies into direct and indirect paradigms and, within each, further distinguishing ML- and DL-based approaches. By outlining methodological advances, practical challenges, and potential solutions, the review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of this emerging field [7, 9]. To further enhance accessibility, we have also developed a companion website that provides an interactive overview of the reviewed studies and resources, which will be continuously updated to reflect new advances in the field.

Artificial intelligence foundations in radiology

The rapid development of AI has transformed medical practice, with applications extending beyond diagnostic imaging to areas such as surgical education and procedural skill assessment [12, 13]. In oncology, particularly in predicting targeted therapy response, AI provides critical tools for uncovering latent imaging patterns and supporting clinical decision-making [7, 9]. Two major methodological pathways are commonly adopted: ML and DL. ML approaches are typically embedded within the radiomics framework, where CT, MRI, or PET images undergo preprocessing to derive handcrafted features that characterize tumor morphology, texture, intensity distribution, and spatial heterogeneity [5, 8, 10]. Beyond conventional radiomics, habitat imaging has been introduced to partition tumors into subregions with distinct radiological phenotypes, thereby enhancing the capture of intratumoral heterogeneity [6]. These features are subjected to reproducibility and stability assessments, followed by feature selection or dimensionality reduction, and subsequently modeled using algorithms such as regression methods, kernel-based classifiers, or ensemble techniques [14–16]. DL approaches, by contrast, employ multi-layer neural networks to automatically learn hierarchical representations directly from raw imaging data, obviating the need for handcrafted features [17–19]. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been widely used to analyze tumor morphology and treatment-related changes; Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for sequential or longitudinal imaging data; and more recent innovations such as transformers and graph neural networks (GNNs) enable modeling of long-range dependencies, spatial relationships, and relational structures [20–22]. Increasingly, multimodal DL frameworks integrate imaging with clinical, molecular, or genomic data, constructing more comprehensive predictive models [7].

Despite methodological diversity, AI applications in medical imaging typically follow a structured workflow as shown in Fig.1. The first stage is data curation and harmonization, including voxel resampling, intensity normalization, artifact control, and region of interest (ROI) delineation by manual, semi-automated, or automated segmentation [10, 19, 23]. The second stage is representation construction: ML focuses on deriving radiomic and habitat-based descriptors refined through robustness and stability analysis [11], while DL employs neural encoders, often initialized via transfer learning or self-supervised pretraining, to learn high-level features [24]. The third stage is task-specific modeling and optimization, wherein representations are mapped to clinical endpoints such as classification, regression, or survival outcomes through predictive heads, with hyperparameter tuning, loss function selection, regularization, class-imbalance correction, and data augmentation as essential components; in DL, encoders and prediction heads are frequently optimized end-to-end [9]. The fourth stage involves validation and evaluation, combining internal cross-validation, independent hold-out cohorts, and external multicenter testing, with metrics including AUROC, AUPRC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, C-index and time-dependent AUC, supplemented by calibration and decision-curve analysis [25–28]. The final stage is interpretation and clinical readiness: ML models often provide interpretability through feature weights or SHAP analyses, while DL models rely on visualization methods such as saliency maps, CAMs, and attention heatmaps, increasingly enhanced by explainable AI (XAI) [29, 30]. Radiology-based biomarkers for targeted therapy

Building upon the methodological overview of AI in radiology presented in Section “Artificial Intelligence Foundations in Radiology”, we propose a conceptual framework that categorizes radiology-based AI approaches for predicting targeted therapy response into two paradigms: (i) direct prediction, which learns clinical endpoints (e.g., treatment response, progression-free survival, overall survival) directly from imaging; and (ii) indirect prediction, which infers molecular biomarkers from imaging that subsequently inform therapeutic benefit. Direct prediction is clinically intuitive, aligns closely with treatment outcome, and offers straightforward translational potential, but it is often limited by small, retrospective cohorts, treatment-selection confounding, and heterogeneous outcome definitions. Indirect prediction provides biological interpretability and scalability by linking imaging phenotypes to molecular biomarkers and leveraging larger biomarker-labeled datasets, but it depends on surrogate validity, is sensitive to assay variability, and requires an additional evidentiary link to clinical benefit.

This taxonomy offers several advantages: (1) it separates clinical endpoints from molecular surrogates, which avoids conflating heterogeneous study designs and allows meaningful cross-cancer comparison—for instance, lung cancer studies often emphasize biomarker prediction whereas gastrointestinal cancers focus more on outcome prediction, and our taxonomy provides a unified lens to synthesize both; (2) it clarifies evidentiary and regulatory pathways, since direct prediction can be validated directly against clinical endpoints and thus aligns with clinical-based standards, whereas indirect prediction requires a two-step chain of evidence comprising the imaging-to-biomarker accuracy and the established biomarker-to-outcome validity.

Indirect prediction through radiology

* Single AUC values indicate that validation/test results were not reported in the original study

To ensure comprehensiveness and transparency, we selected the articles based on the following inclusion criteria: only peer-reviewed articles were considered; eligible studies involved radiological imaging (CT, MRI, PET, or ultrasound) combined with AI methods (ML, or DL); only studies directly relevant to targeted therapy response prediction were included; larger studies with independent validation were prioritized, although small but novel or rare-cancer studies were also retained; and all included studies explicitly reported the AI model type, imaging modality, and defined outcome measures.

Furthermore, we standardized the reporting and comparison of performance metrics across studies according to the nature of the prediction task. For classification tasks, such as predicting therapeutic response (responder vs. non-responder) or molecular biomarker status (e.g., EGFR, HER2, KRAS mutations), AUC was adopted as the primary metric because it reflects threshold-independent discrimination and overall model robustness [139]. Complementary indicators including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were reported when available in the original studies. For regression or survival prediction tasks, such as estimating PFS, OS, or C-index was used to evaluate the consistency between predicted and observed event rankings [140]. In cases where both discrimination and survival metrics were available, AUC was prioritized for cross-study comparison given its superior stability and interpretability [140]. Rather than setting fixed thresholds for “acceptable” performance, we focused on comparative trends across models, emphasizing methodological improvements and translational potential as indicators of progress in the field.

Direct prediction methods

Among the 36 studies identified, most used computed tomography (CT, 80%, n = 29), followed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, 16.7%, n = 6) and ultrasound (US, 2.8%, n = 1). The predominant cancer types investigated were lung (36%, n = 13), liver (19.4%, n = 7), colorectal (13.9%, n = 5), renal (11.1%, n = 4), breast (11%, n = 4), gastric (5.6%, n = 2), and skin (2.8%, n = 1). As illustrated in Fig. 2 (inner circle), these distributions highlight the concentration of direct prediction studies in CT-based radiomics for lung and liver cancers, reflecting both clinical prevalence and data availability. Two modeling strategies were observed: (i) ML-based radiomics for direct prediction of prognosis or treatment response (58.3%, n = 21), and (ii) DL-based pipelines for the same tasks (41.7%, n = 15).

Lung cancer

Lung cancer is the second most common malignancy, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for 76% of cases. Epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) are the standard first-line treatment for EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients [141]. Recent work in DL-based radiomics has moved beyond conventional CNNs toward more diverse architectures and multimodal frameworks aimed at improving prognostic performance in NSCLC patients receiving EGFR-TKIs. Hou et al. applied transfer learning with 3D-CNNs and achieved AUC of 0.744–0.794 for progression-free survival (PFS) prediction in EGFR-mutant patients [35]. Integrating radiomic with clinical data further improved performance: a DeepSurv-based combined model reached an AUC of 0.86 for 24-month PFS, compared with 0.67 for radiomics alone in stage IIIB–IV patients [36]. Similarly, Wang et al. incorporated a prognostic index and clinical data into the CoxCC model, yielding an AUC of 0.87 for nine-month PFS in advanced NSCLC patients with brain metastases, compared to 0.70 for clinical data alone [142]. These findings highlight the added value of clinical variables—particularly smoking status—when integrated with radiomic features.

Beyond CNNs, newer architectures such as EfficientNetV2 have shown promise by improving accuracy while reducing parameter complexity. Deng et al. developed an EfficientNetV2-based survival benefit prognosis (ESBP) system, which improved diagnostic accuracy of junior clinicians (from 47.9% to 66.3%) and mid-level clinicians (from 53.1% to 61.4%) in NSCLC prognosis [34]. However, most studies still required manual tumor segmentation and ROI selection, processes prone to subjective bias and reduced reproducibility [143].

To overcome these limitations, recent research has focused on DL-based semantic feature extraction to reduce manual intervention. For example, large-scale bidirectional generative adversarial networks (BigBiGANs) can automatically extract high-dimensional semantic features. Song et al. employed a BigBiGAN framework to predict EGFR-TKI efficacy in stage IV EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients without manual delineation, thereby improving efficiency and applicability [38]. Similarly, Wang et al. introduced a fully automated AI system (FAIS) that analyzed raw CT scans to predict EGFR mutation subtypes (AUC = 0.813) and individualized PFS across lung cancer subtypes [39].

While DL methods dominate recent research, ML remains especially valuable in lung cancer contexts where data are limited or heterogeneous. Zhu et al. reported that logistic regression achieved the highest AUC (0.797) among tested models [40], while Yang et al. showed random forest (RF) models reached an AUC of 0.746 for response prediction [41]. Zhang et al. extended RF survival models with intra- and peritumoral temporal features, attaining an AUC of 0.96 at 12 months in lung adenocarcinoma patients treated with EGFR-TKIs [42]. ML approaches have also been tested in clinically challenging subgroups. In patients with acquired T790M mutations treated with osimertinib, stepwise regression achieved robust PFS prediction (C-index = 0.75) [43]. However, subsequent studies revealed limitations: Yang et al. found ML-based radiomics offered limited survival prediction in this subgroup due to small cohorts, clinical heterogeneity, and the inability of baseline CT features to capture dynamic resistance biology [44]. Similar issues were noted in ALK-positive NSCLC: Huang et al. showed a general radiomic signature had little predictive utility in TKI-treated patients, retaining value only in those receiving non-targeted therapy [45].

Breast cancer

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide [144]. Anti-HER2 therapies have significantly improved the survival rates of patients with HER2-positive breast cancer, which accounts for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancer cases [145, 146]. Recently, emerging evidence highlighted the transformative potential of radiomics integrated with DL and ML techniques in forecasting therapeutic efficacy and survival endpoints. A landmark study by Zhang et al. developed DeepTEPP, a DL framework combining preoperative breast MRI radiomics with clinical–pathological parameters (e.g., menstruation status, surgery information, tumor status, and molecular markers). This model, validated in one of the largest HER2-positive cohorts to date, achieved high accuracy in predicting both PFS and OS for patients undergoing single or dual HER2-targeted therapy [46]. Extending beyond MRI, He et al. evaluated six ML radiomics models using contrast-enhanced CT in metastatic breast cancer, identifying the portal venous phase ROI as most predictive, with SVM achieving the best performance (AUC = 0.865) [49]. In the neoadjuvant setting, ML-based radiomics models applied to breast MRI have also predicted pathologic complete response (pCR), including in patients receiving HER2-targeted regimens, with reported an AUC around 0.77 [47, 48].

Kidney cancer

The global mortality rate of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is estimated to approach 175,000 annually, with incidence rates projected to rise over the next decade [147]. Targeting the VEGF pathway has become the standard first-line therapy for advanced or metastatic RCC (mRCC) [148]. However, up to 30% of patients exhibit intrinsic resistance to TKIs, leading to early treatment failure [149].

Radiomics offers opportunities to identify patients more likely to benefit from TKIs. Baseline CT-derived features have been used to build ML models predicting resistance: one study using 3D tumor ROIs from enhanced CT achieved an AUC of 0.81 for early VEGFR-TKI resistance in metastatic clear cell RCC (mccRCC) [50]. Building on such approaches, Chen et al. developed a delta-radiomics model that accurately predicted short-term TKI response (AUC = 0.916) in advanced RCC [51], while Negreros et al. showed that adding radiomics to clinical models improved prediction, increasing AUC from 0.90 to 0.94 [52].

Despite these advances, reliance on handcrafted features limits robustness. To overcome this, DL models have been explored: Yu et al. applied CNN-based frameworks capable of automatic feature extraction, achieving more than 90% accuracy in expected lifespan prediction across RCC cohorts [53]. These results highlight a transition from traditional ML-based handcrafted radiomics toward DL models with greater potential for reproducibility and clinical translation.

Colorectal cancer

CRC is the second deadliest cancer and the third most common malignancy worldwide. The therapeutic landscape of CRC has been reshaped by targeted agents, with EGFR- and VEGF-directed monoclonal antibodies forming the backbone of systemic therapy, and HER2 emerging as a novel actionable target [150]. For EGFR-targeted therapy, Dercle et al. applied a RF model to predict early sensitivity, achieving an AUC of up to 0.80 [54]. For VEGF-directed therapy, Lu et al. designed a hybrid CNN–RNN architecture that outperformed size-based networks in predicting responses to aflibercept monotherapy in the VELOUR trial [55, 151]. In the context of first-line bevacizumab plus chemotherapy (BBC), Maaref et al. proposed a fully automated DCNN framework using baseline CT, with the best model reaching an AUC of 0.88 [56]. Building on this, Chui et al. demonstrated that ResNet models could further improve response prediction, correctly identifying patients as responsive who were misclassified as non-responders by radiologists [57]. For HER2-positive CRC, Giannini et al. developed a ML model for patients with liver metastases, achieving per-patient sensitivity and specificity of 92 and 86%, respectively, in predicting response to HER2-targeted therapy [58].

Liver cancer

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has a poor prognosis and a 5-year survival rate of 18%, making it one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide [110]. TKIs such as lenvatinib and sorafenib have been approved as first-line therapies for advanced HCC [152]. For example, Bo et al. developed a RF-based radiomics model to predict the efficacy of lenvatinib monotherapy in unresectable HCC, achieving an AUC of 0.96 [61]. Given the limited efficacy of TKIs alone, and with the rise of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), combination regimens are increasingly becoming the standard of care for advanced HCC [153, 154]. Accordingly, radiomics and AI models have been applied to predict response to TKI-based combinations. Among these, one study incorporating 20 ultrasound features achieved robust discrimination for predicting response to TKI plus anti–PD-1 therapy, with an AUC of 0.83 in the independent validation cohort (n = 41) [62–65]. Similarly, Liao et al. combined multi-phase CT with DL-generated heatmaps to predict outcomes of lenvatinib plus ICI therapy (CLICI), achieving an AUC of 0.802 [67]. Beyond ICIs, other novel strategies are being explored. For instance, sorafenib has been shown to augment NK cell–based adoptive immunotherapy (NK-ATI) [155], and MRI radiomics combined with an SVM algorithm achieved 96% accuracy in early evaluation of this combination [66].

Gastric cancer

GC is the fifth most common malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death [66]. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) overexpression can be detected in GC patients up to 30.5%, which is an important prognostic factor for GC [156]. Anti-HER2 targeted therapy has also been shown to be a practical treatment for GC [157]. Although the researches on predicting the prognosis of GC directly based on radiomics and AI are limited, there is still some potential. He et al., based on Transformer architecture, built a DL model to predict the OS of anti-HER2-targeted therapy with an AUC of 0.894 [59]. Another study showed favorable accuracy for the prediction of OS and PFS in patients treated with trastuzumab [60].

Ovarian cancer

Bevacizumab has been proven can prolong PFS and OS of patients with ovarian cancer in a phase 3 trial [158]. Huang et al., based on CT images and developed a ResNet18 DL model to predict the PFS of patients with ovarian and treated with bevacizumab. The model achieved a Harrell’s C-index of 0.73 in the internal test set, demonstrating its noteworthy predictive accuracy [121].

Skin cancer

Blocking the RAF-RAS-MEK signaling pathway with BRAF inhibitors or combined with MEK inhibitors has been established recently as a target treatment for patients with melanoma and has been proven to prolong the PFS and OS [159, 160]. However, recent data indicate that the available literature on the use of ML and radiomics in the diagnosis of melanoma is still very limited and the existing AI-based predictive model showed limited specificity. The ML-based model constructed by Peisen et al. showed no additional predictive value of CT radiomics for best-response prediction, PFS for six months, or six-month and twelve-month OS prediction for stage IV melanoma patients receiving first-line targeted therapy (BRAF inhibitor monotherapy or in combination with a MEK inhibitor) [161].

Indirect prediction methods

A total of 76 studies applied indirect prediction, using CT (42%, n = 32), MRI (27.6%, n = 21), PET-CT (25%, n = 19), ultrasound (US, 6.6%, n = 5), and automated breast volume scanning (ABVS, 1.3%, n = 1). The most frequently studied cancers were lung (29%, n = 22), breast (14.5%, n = 11), kidney (10.5%, n = 8), colorectal (9.2%, n = 7), brain (9.2%, n = 7), ovarian (7.9%, n = 6), stomach (6.6%, n = 5), bladder (6.6%, n = 5), skin (4%, n = 3), and liver (2.6%, n = 2). As shown in Fig. 2 (outer circle), indirect biomarker prediction spans a broader range of cancer types than direct prediction, reflecting the diversity of available biomarker-driven therapies. ML dominated this group (73.7%, n = 56), while DL accounted for a smaller but growing proportion (26.3%, n = 20).

Lung cancer

As previously noted in “Direct Biomarker” section, EGFR mutations (primarily exon 19 deletion and exon 21 L858R, 90% of cases) are well-established drivers of TKI response, while secondary T790M mutations confer resistance and necessitate third-generation EGFR-TKIs such as osimertinib [162–164]. Consequently, noninvasive prediction of EGFR mutation subtypes has become a major focus of AI-based radiomics.

DL approaches, especially CNN variants, have achieved strong predictive performance. In DL applications, CNNs have demonstrated exceptional performance. Zhao et al. developed a DL model based on 3D CNNs to automatically predict EGFR-mutated LUAD in CT images and achieved promising performance [68]. Similarly, SiMu et al., using a 2D small-residual-convolutional network (SResCNN) developed a DL model to predict EGFR mutation with an AUC of 0.86 [69]. In predicting EGFR mutations, a DL-based framework can advance CNN variants and ensemble strategies and model integration strategies. Xiao et al. achieved an accuracy of 86.25% in predicting EGFR mutation based on the EfficientNet-V2 model [70]. Yin et al. developed SECT and SEPET models through the Squeeze-and-Excitation Residual Network (SE-ResNet) and the model performance was improved by stacked generalization (AUC reached 0.84 from 0.72 and 0.74) [71]. Chen et al. developed a stacked PET-CT model with an AUC of 0.85 and a ResNet PET model with an AUC of 0.790.82 [72]. Notably, Zhang et al. achieved the highest performance with a SE-CNN (AUC = 0.91), outperforming conventional CNNs such as VGG16/19 [73].

ML approaches also demonstrated strong utility across EGFR subtypes and modalities. Jiang et al. combined CT imaging and an SVM classifier, achieving an AUC of 0.7413, while Yang et al. achieved an AUC greater than 0.8 in predicting wild-type EGFR and mutations with radiomic analysis based on 18F-FDG PET-CT [74, 75]. In comparison, RF models have shown superior performance in CT, PET, and PET-CT imaging radiomics analysis [165]. Tang et al. and Li et al. used RF models for the prediction of the T790M mutation, the highest AUC reaches 0.886, and Yang et al. achieved an AUC of 0.82 for EGFR 19/21 mutations [76, 77]. Liu et al. used the XGBoost algorithm to predict EGFR exon 21 L858R missense, exon 19 deletion, and overall EGFR mutation-positive cases, achieving AUC of 0.92, 0.77, and 0.87, respectively [78]. Zuo et al. applied the LGBM model to predict EGFR mutation status, achieving an AUC of 0.8, indicating the model’s optimal performance in EGFR mutation prediction [79]. Other studies have also demonstrated that LGBM and XGBoost classifiers outperform LR and RF models in terms of performance [80, 166]. Overall, DL methods demonstrated higher upper bounds in predictive accuracy (AUC often exceeding 0.9), whereas ML models, by leveraging explicit radiomic features, provided greater interpretability and showed flexibility across different imaging modalities (CT, PET, PET-CT).

For predicting the efficacy of ALK-TKIs, both DL and ML models have demonstrated high accuracy. Song et al. developed a CNN-based model to predict ALK fusion status, achieving an AUC of 0.8540 [109]. Song et al. used LR and decision tree (DT) models to develop a conventional CT and radiomic feature-based (also named morphological or semantic CT features) to predict ALK mutations, with an AUC of 0.83 [81]. The important clinicopathological information, such as the patient’s age, sex, smoking history, and pathological type of tumor (e.g., adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma), helps to enhance the performance of the CT-based AI model for ALK prediction. Chang et al. combined radiomic methods CT PET and two clinical characteristics, burrs and pleural effusion were significantly correlated with ALK mutation status of tumors, which are independent predictors of ALK mutation and the prediction accuracy of this model is significantly better than that of the model using clinical features alone, with an AUC of 0.88, which is significantly improved compared to 0.74 relying on the clinical model alone and previous similar models [82, 83]. In a similar study, the DL model built by Song et al. showed better prediction performance of ALK fusion than the simple radiomic model by combining usual clinicopathological information and radiomic characteristics (AUC 0.8046 vs 0.8540) [109]. These results highlight that for ALK mutation prediction, multi-source data integration may be more important than model architecture alone.

KRAS mutation prediction has been less studied but also shows promise. Le et al. employed an XGBoost classifier and achieved an AUC of 0.86, [84] while Shiri et al.’s RF-based model achieved an AUC of 0.93 after combat harmonization, significantly enhancing consistency across datasets and better prediction accuracy [85, 167]. Although fewer studies exist compared with EGFR and ALK, these findings suggest that AI-based radiomics can serve as a valuable noninvasive tool for identifying KRAS mutation status.

Breast cancer

HER2 has long been recognized as a critical predictor of therapeutic outcomes in breast cancer [168]. Since the development of HER2-targeted therapies, especially HER2-blockade agent trastuzumab, the PFS and OS of HER2-positive breast cancer subtypes have significantly improved [169]. More recently, novel antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), such as DS-8201a, have also shown benefits in patients with HER2-low expression [170], further emphasizing the clinical importance of accurately identifying HER2 status and stratifying patients into HER2-positive, HER2-negative, and HER2-low groups.

Various imaging techniques, including mammography (MG), CT, US, PET, and MRI, play critical roles in breast cancer evaluation. PET-CT-based texture analysis has revealed marked differences between HER2-positive and HER2-negative tumors [171], supporting its value as a radiomics input. Building on this, Chen et al. developed ML models from PET-CT radiomics, with the best-performing XGBoost classifier achieving an AUC of 0.76 [172].

US, as a radiation-free and widely accessible modality, has also been actively studied for HER2 prediction. Studies have shown that both DL and ML methods efficiently predict HER2 status using US imaging. An ML model using the SVC-RBF algorithm demonstrated superior predictive value for HER2-low breast cancer patients, achieving an AUC of 0.89 [87]. Xu et al. constructed a DenseNet-based DL model that achieved an AUC of 0.87 [88]. Quan et al. constructed a hybrid DL-ML radiomics (DLR) model, incorporating YOLO v5 for automated tumor detection and feature extraction, with an XGBoost and LR ensemble achieving a specificity of 91.7% and an AUC of 0.8 in the test cohort [89]. Beyond conventional 2D US, 3D approaches such as the automatic breast volume scanner (ABVS) provide more comprehensive tumor characterization, with studies showing improved HER2 prediction compared with standard US (AUC 0.826 vs. 0.786) [90, 91].

MRI, as a routine modality for breast cancer staging and treatment planning, has been the focus of numerous AI-based HER2 prediction studies. Guo et al. developed the first deep-learning radiomics model using MRI to predict HER2 status and successfully distinguished HER2-negative from HER2-overexpressing cases, achieving an AUC of 0.868. It also demonstrated promise in predicting disease-free survival (DFS) for HER2-low-positive patients [92]. Luo et al. extracted a large number of radiomic features (n = 4506) from multisequence MRI to develop predictive models based on various ML algorithms, all of which show good performance in predicting HER2 status in invasive ductal breast carcinoma (IDC) [93]. Similarly, multiparametric MRI combined with SVM classifiers has repeatedly demonstrated robust results, with an AUC around 0.86 [94, 124].

Other imaging modalities have been less studied but still provide insights. MDCT, though not a primary modality, provides useful HER2-related radiomic features. A model based on the recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithm showed good performance in predicting HER2 status [95]. MG has historically offered limited sensitivity of 75% to 85% in predicting preoperative HER2 status in breast cancer, which decreases sharply in those with dense breast tissue [173, 174]. However, a LF-based ML model constructed by Zhou et al. for HER2 status prediction achieved an AUC of 0.846, offering new hope for MG’s future clinical application [96].

Kidney cancer

Molecular subclassifications may affect response to vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) directed therapy [96]. Mutations in PBRM1 and BAP1 have been shown to affect the response to VEGF-targeted treatments in advanced or metastatic clear cell RCC (ccRCC). Specifically, BAP1 mutations are associated with significantly worsened overall survival (OS), while PBRM1 mutations correlate with longer time to treatment failure (TTF) and a trend toward improved OS under VEGF-targeted therapies [175]. Moreover, PBRM1-deficient tumors appear more sensitive to immune checkpoint blockade rather than targeted agents, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of distinguishing these subclasses [176].

Early studies used two-dimensional (2D) CT radiomics for BAP1 mutation prediction. Kocak et al. used 2D manual segmentation to contract unenhanced CT texture and the RF-based predictive model demonstrated an AUC of 0.897 for correctly classifying BAP1 mutations [98, 175]. Ghosh et al. observed that the nephrographic study, the contrast-enhanced CT of the Kidney, had a higher predictive potential when compared to non-contrast CT [99]. Shinagare et al. also noted that BAP1 mutation was influenced by the unclear margins and the presence of calcification [177]. These studies demonstrated that the contracted CT has the potential to have high sensitivity and specificity to identify the characteristic gene signature of ccRCC. A RF model based on contrasted CT scans achieved an accuracy of 0.83 [97].

However, 2D imaging has limitations in assessing complex structures, such as the three-dimensional (3D) shape of a tumor and its relationship to surrounding tissues. 3D imaging techniques can provide more comprehensive anatomical information, helping to more accurately assess tumor morphology, volume, and relationship to surrounding tissue. Ghosh et al. correlated 3D intra-tumor heterogeneity features from contrast-enhanced CT images with BACA status and the predictive model presented a high degree of sensitivity and specificity [99].

ML models demonstrate high sensitivity in predicting PBRM1 mutations and PBRM1-deficient tumors, which are also highly sensitive to certain cell cycle inhibitors, such as CDK1 inhibitors [178]. The PBRM1 gene was predicted with the highest AUC with the SVM classifier in a CT-based radiomics model constructed by Hedyeh et al. [100]. Kocak’s models based on high-dimensional quantitative CT texture analysis, using artificial neural networks (ANN) and RF algorithms, demonstrated high classification accuracy of PBRM1 prediction, with AUC of 0.925 and 0.987, respectively [37]. Wang et al. used 68 Ga-labeled prostate-specific membrane antigen-11 PET-CT to predict PBRM1 mutation. Molecular imaging has also been incorporated: Wang et al. used 68Ga-PSMA-11 PET-CT, where attenuation values, SUVmax, and tumor size served as robust predictors of PBRM1-silent tumors, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 70% [101].

Most existing studies benchmark individual classifiers, selecting the best-performing model for prognostic prediction. To further enhance accuracy, ensemble learning strategies have been tested. Chen et al. combined multiple classifiers (SVM, Naive Bayes (NB), LR, KNN, DT, and DA) into the framework, achieving an AUC of 0.97 [102]. Chen et al. combined multiple ML classifiers, including SVM, NB LR, KNN, DT and DA to build the MCMO model, achieving an AUC of 0.97 [102].

Colorectal cancer

Studies have demonstrated that RAS (KRAS and NRAS) and BRAF mutations, which lead to constitutive activation of the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, render CRC patients unresponsive to anti-EGFR therapies such as cetuximab and panitumumab [179, 180]. Accordingly, imaging-based AI models have been developed to non-invasively predict RAS and BRAF mutation status, thereby indirectly informing the efficacy of anti-EGFR treatments.

Early studies primarily applied ML classifiers to PET and CT radiomics. Zhao et al. utilized 18F-FDG PET imaging with a SVM classifier to predict KRAS/NRAS/BRAF mutations, achieving an AUC of 0.759 in the training cohort [103]. CT-based radiomics has also demonstrated potential for predicting KRAS mutations in CRC. González-Castro et al. and Porto-Álvarez et al. tested multiple ML models, finding that NNET achieved the highest accuracy of predicting KARS mutation at 83% [104, 105]. The studies developed a ML-based CT model based on SVM, showing great prediction for KRAS mutation diagnosis with an AUC of 0.82 to 0.862 [106, 107].

Building on these foundations, more advanced models have demonstrated substantial performance gains. Shi et al. employed seven ML algorithms and an ANN to predict RAS and BRAF mutations, achieving an AUC of 0.95. Their ANN method, incorporating radiomics and semantic features, consistently outperformed all seven evaluated ML algorithms [108]. Notably, most DL models typically address tasks independently, such as image segmentation and mutation prediction. Notably, instead of focusing on single-task predictions, recent DL models have embraced multi-task learning. Song et al. developed a multi-scale attention model (SMSAM), which simultaneously performs both segmentation and mutation prediction tasks, substantially outperforming existing methods [181].

Liver cancer

A recent study reported that HCC patients with high EGFR expression were more likely to develop resistance to lenvatinib [182], highlighting the value of imaging-based prediction of EGFR status as an indirect marker of therapeutic response. Ma et al. developed an US radiomics model across five ML algorithms, among which the RF model achieved the best performance (AUC = 0.929). Incorporating clinical baseline features further improved the RF-based model, raising the AUC to 0.937 [110]. Similarly, c-Met overexpression, observed in 20–40% of advanced HCC patients, is associated with resistance to sorafenib and serves as a potential biomarker for prognosis and treatment response [183]. Gu et al. constructed a three-phase CT fusion model that incorporated arterial, portal venous, and delayed-phase radiomics features, achieving an AUC of 0.828 in predicting c-Met expression [111].

Stomach cancer

HER2 overexpression is an important oncogenic driver in GC and is associated with poor prognosis in advanced disease [184, 185]. Accordingly, accurate evaluation of HER2 status is critical for predicting response to HER2-targeted therapy. Early AI efforts focused on conventional ML applied to CT or PET-CT radiomics. Jiao et al. constructed three ML models, with LR and RF achieving AUC of 0.809 and 0.861, respectively, for identifying HER2-positive patients [112]. Chen et al. similarly reported strong performance of an LR-based model (AUC = 0.84, accuracy = 0.86) [113], while Liu et al. employed an Adaboost classifier on 18F-FDG PET-CT radiomics, with a more modest AUC of 0.72 [114]. Building on these foundations, more sophisticated DL frameworks have demonstrated substantial performance gains. Guan et al. developed ResNet50-SVM and Inte-SVM hybrid models, achieving AUC of 0.8955 and 0.9055, respectively [115]. They later introduced PredictionNet, an end-to-end DL framework specifically for HER2 prediction. Unlike their earlier ResNet50-SVM hybrid models, PredictionNet directly learned high-level feature representations from imaging data and incorporated a multi-stage training strategy, which substantially improved performance (AUC = 0.9721 in an external validation cohort and 0.9995 in a prospective cohort). Notably, they also deployed this model as a web-based tool for clinical use [116]. However, the near-perfect AUC should be interpreted cautiously, as the study used slice-level data from 389 patients with limited external cohorts, which may have inflated apparent performance.

Ovarian cancer

In ovarian cancer, patients with BRCA mutations exhibit high sensitivity to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi), such as olaparib, niraparib, talazoparib, and pamiparib, which has been well-documented in clinical practice [186, 187]. To support treatment stratification, several AI-based imaging models have been proposed. Avesani et al. developed an ML model based on CT radiomics that achieved an AUC of 0.74 for BRCA status prediction [117], while Mingzhu et al. reported that both 2D and 3D CT radiomics yielded favorable performance in predicting BRCA mutations [188].

Histologically, epithelial ovarian carcinomas are classified into two distinct types: type I and type II. Type II tumors are typically characterized by TP53 and BRCA mutations, making them more responsive to PARPi compared with type I tumors, which commonly harbor KRAS and BRAF mutations [189, 190]. Accordingly, AI models have also been applied to subtype classification. Wei et al. demonstrated that a DL model based on T2-weighted MRI differentiated between the two subtypes with an AUC of 0.888 [118]. Similarly, multi-sequence MRI radiomics (T2WI, DWI, ADC, CE-T1WI) achieved high accuracy in subtype prediction, as shown by Zhang et al. and Jian et al. [191, 192].

While radiomics-based models offer high accuracy and non-invasiveness, they cannot fully replace traditional clinical predictors. Qian et al. demonstrated that a model incorporating clinical features such as age, Cancer Antigen 125 (CA125) levels, and lesion texture achieved superior performance compared with CT radiomics alone (AUC 0.96 vs. 0.81) [89]. The integration of clinical and imaging data further enhances predictive power. Li et al. constructed an ML model combining CT radiomics with clinical variables, yielding AUC of 0.900 and 0.934 in training and test cohorts, respectively [120]. Similarly, Huang et al. proposed a hybrid LightGBM model integrating CA125 and risk scores from a ResNet18-based CT radiomics system, which improved patient stratification and accurately predicted progression risk under bevacizumab therapy [121].

Beyond ovarian cancer, similar strategies have been explored in endometrial cancer. PTEN loss, leading to PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway activation, occurs in 32–83% of endometrioid-type endometrial carcinomas (EC) [193]. Rapamycin analogs have shown clinical benefit across multiple tumor types [193]. Sun et al. integrated multiparametric MRI radiomics with clinicopathological features to develop an ML model that achieved an AUC of 0.829, highlighting its potential for predicting PTEN, PIK3CA, and mTOR expression in EC patients [122].

Brain cancer

Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations are central to glioma biology and clinical stratification, making IDH an essential molecular biomarker for diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making. With selective IDH inhibitors now in clinical trials, they represent the most advanced targeted strategy for a subset of IDH-mutant gliomas [194]. Correspondingly, AI-based imaging studies on IDH prediction are the most extensive. Multiparametric MRI radiomics has become the predominant approach, and DL models such as CNNs have achieved high accuracy. For example, Chang et al. reported 94% accuracy [123], and Choi et al. developed a fully automated framework with accuracies ranging from 78.8% to 93.8% and AUC of 0.86–0.96 [125]. ML methods have also shown strong predictive power: Zhou et al. reported an AUC of 0.86 using RF [126], and Sakai et al. achieved over 90% accuracy with XGBoost [127]. Integrating imaging with structured radiological descriptors can further enhance performance; Cao et al. combined Visually Accessible Rembrandt Images (VASARI) features with MRI radiomics, improving an AUC to 0.879 in lower-grade gliomas [128].

Compared with IDH, BRAF alterations occur less frequently but have important clinical implications. Patients with BRAF V600E mutations—particularly those with concurrent CDKN2A deletion—carry a higher risk of progression, and MAPK or BRAF V600E inhibitors are under active clinical investigation [195]. In this context, Wagner et al. validated a RF-based radiomic model that predicted BRAF fusions and V600E mutations in pediatric low-grade gliomas with an AUC of 0.85 [129], suggesting the potential of radiomics to support therapeutic stratification in these rarer subgroups.

EGFR amplification or overexpression is highly prevalent in glioblastoma, occurring in 50–60% of cases [196]. However, EGFR inhibitors have so far demonstrated limited clinical benefit in gliomas. [197]. AI-based imaging models are beginning to address EGFR status prediction: Verduin et al. developed an MRI radiomics model that achieved an AUC of 0.707 [130], indicating potential but highlighting the need for further refinement.

Skin cancer

The V600E BRAF mutation is the most prevalent genetic alteration in melanomas, occurring in approximately 50% of cases [198]. The presence of this mutation has significant therapeutic implications, as it confers sensitivity to targeted therapies, including BRAF inhibitors used alone or in combination with MEK inhibitors [159]. Saadani et al. combined conventional radiomics 18F-FDG PET-CT features with ML algorithms (LR and RF) to predict BRAF mutation. However, their results indicated that conventional PET features such as SUV metrics were not associated with BRAF V600E mutation status, yielding only modest performance (AUC = 0.62) [131]. In contrast, MRI-based radiomics has shown more promise, likely because MRI captures phenotypic correlates of tumor cellularity, vascularity, and heterogeneity that are influenced by BRAF-driven biology but not reflected in routine PET metrics. Fanizzi et al. and Meißner et al. constructed SVM-based MRI models, achieving AUC of 0.78 and 0.92, respectively [132, 133].

Bladder cancer

Bladder cancer is the third most common cancer associated with HER2 overexpression, which plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis and is strongly linked to increased malignancy and poor clinical outcomes [199, 200]. HER2 status, therefore, serves as a critical biomarker for prognosis and treatment strategies in this malignancy. Disitamab vedotin, the novel HER2 targeted therapy, has been proven to have good efficacy and manageable safety and is approved for the treatment of advanced urothelial carcinoma. The ongoing exploration of HER2-targeted therapies highlights the need for precise diagnostic methods to identify patients who may benefit most from these treatments [201, 202]. MRI-based approaches have provided encouraging results. Yu et al. constructed an ML-based model combining 22 MRI features, which achieved an AUC of 0.712 in the testing cohort [66]. Building on this, Luo et al. incorporated the Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS) score into their radiomics model, resulting in improved performance with an AUC of 0.889 [134].

Above results reflect MRIs particular strengths in bladder cancer: high soft-tissue contrast and multiparametric techniques (T2WI, DWI, and DCE-MRI) enable accurate differentiation of muscle-invasive versus non-muscle-invasive disease, while functional imaging captures tumor cellularity and vascularity—biological processes that correlate with HER2 overexpression [203]. CT-based approaches provide complementary strengths in this setting. Unlike MRI, CT is the standard modality for routine staging and surveillance in bladder cancer, offering reproducibility across centers and integration into clinical workflows. Multi-phase contrast-enhanced CT (non-contrast, arterial, venous) can further capture perfusion heterogeneity associated with HER2-driven angiogenesis. Wei et al. extracted 11 CT features for ML modeling, with RF achieving the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.815) [135]. Peng et al. and Zhang et al. incorporated clinical variables alongside multi-phase CT radiomic features, reaching AUC of 0.857 and 0.83, respectively [136, 137]. DL applications have also emerged. Jiao et al. employed a multilayer perceptron (MLP) using CT radiomic features, achieving an AUC of 0.73, outperforming LR and RF models (AUC of 0.61 and 0.59, respectively) [138].

Discussion

This review evaluates current radiology-based AI approaches, including both ML and DL, for predicting the efficacy of targeted therapies across cancer types. These methods can be applied either directly, by modeling clinical endpoints such as PFS or pathological complete response (pCR), or indirectly, by predicting the status of therapeutic targets from imaging features. By enabling large-scale analysis of high-throughput imaging data, AI facilitates the identification of prognostic features that are difficult to capture through conventional assessment. Our survey reveals a notable growth in studies combining radiology and AI for targeted therapy prediction, with 102 articles published between 2020 and 2024. Despite these advances, important limitations remain, both in relation to the biological and clinical complexity of cancer and in the methodological and technical aspects of AI model development. For clarity, we structure the discussion around these two domains of challenges and, building on this analysis, outline future research directions aimed at strengthening biological integration, enhancing robustness and generalizability, improving interpretability, and advancing clinical validation and regulatory frameworks.

Biological and clinical limitations

Indirect Biomarkers Indirect prediction strategies attempt to approximate molecular or genetic profiles—such as EGFR, HER2, ALK, KRAS, or BAP1 status—through imaging features that serve as phenotypic surrogates. However, the biological link between genotype and phenotype is often indirect. The presence of an actionable mutation does not necessarily indicate therapeutic benefit, as response is further influenced by biological complexities like mutation subtype, co-occurring alterations, and pathway cross-talk [204, 205]. For example, epigenetic modifications like DNA methylation—such as EGFR promoter silencing—can alter treatment sensitivity without changing the mutation status itself [206], leading to clinical outcomes that deviate from predictions based solely on imaging surrogates.

Direct Biomarkers Direct prediction strategies bypass molecular intermediates and estimate therapeutic efficacy, such as PFS or OS, directly from imaging features. However, a smaller subset of studies relies on short-term efficacy measures, such as pCR or PR, which may not consistently correlate with durable benefit and thus capture only post-treatment response [207].

Beyond endpoint selection, direct prediction frameworks also face biological and temporal limitations. Biologically, most current models rely on single-modality imaging data, which limits their capacity to capture the full complexity of tumor biology. Temporally, pre-treatment imaging provides only a static snapshot of tumor biology, while clonal adaptation and resistance mutations (e.g., EGFR T790M emergence during therapy [208]) reshape disease trajectories over time. Without longitudinal imaging or delta-radiomics, such frameworks capture only a single snapshot of a dynamic process, constraining their ability to predict long-term benefit.

Methodological and technical limitations

Although radiology-based AI models have demonstrated considerable potential in predicting the efficacy of targeted therapies, several methodological and technical limitations hinder their reliable application in clinical practice. These challenges primarily involve data availability and representativeness, model generalizability, interpretability and clinical trust, system deployment and scalability, as well as clinical translation and regulatory barriers.

Data Limitations The performance of AI models is highly dependent on the quantity and quality of training data. However, most existing studies rely on retrospective, single-center cohorts with limited sample sizes and incomplete outcome information, restricting the robustness of training and validation. Moreover, the lack of population diversity reduces representativeness; for instance, public datasets are often dominated by specific ethnic groups, while underrepresentation of minorities may compromise fairness across patient cohorts [209, 210]. Class imbalance, such as uneven distributions between responders and non-responders, as well as disparities among imaging modalities, further undermine prediction stability [211, 212]. Differences in imaging equipment and in data governance across countries further exacerbate variability, hindering large-scale data sharing [213]. These issues underscore the importance of large, diverse, and well-curated datasets as the foundation for reliable AI modeling [214]. Recent initiatives, including open-access datasets (e.g., TCIA, TCGA) and harmonization standards such as IBSI, have improved data transparency across institutions, yet prospective, multicenter, and demographically balanced datasets remain scarce [214].

Model Generalizability While many AI models achieve strong performance on training or internal validation cohorts, their performance often declines substantially on external datasets, reflecting limited generalizability. ML models are sensitive to variations in feature extraction pipelines and parameter settings [215], whereas DL models are prone to capturing scanner- or institution-specific artifacts, resulting in domain dependency. Techniques such as ComBat harmonization and transfer learning, which respectively reduce scanner-related variability, enable knowledge adaptation across datasets, showing promise for improving cross-domain robustness but remain constrained by heterogeneous imaging protocols and lack of prospective validation [215]. This lack of robustness constrains the utility of models in multicenter, cross-institutional, and real-world settings, highlighting the necessity of systematic external validation and strategies to ensure cross-domain adaptability.

Model Interpretability In clinical decision-making, AI models are expected not only to provide accurate predictions but also to offer interpretable outputs that clinicians can understand and trust. Traditional ML approaches, such as radiomics-based models, offer higher interpretability through explicit feature extraction but often rely on hand-crafted features that may lack biological specificity or generalizability across cohorts. In contrast, DL models automatically learn high-dimensional representations from raw data but are frequently criticized as “black boxes,” making it difficult to trace predictions back to meaningful imaging or biological correlates [9, 216]. Current interpretability tools—such as saliency maps, class activation maps (CAM), and SHAP value analyses—partially enhance transparency by visualizing regions or features most influential to model decisions [29, 30]. Nevertheless, these explanations often suffer from limited stability, reproducibility, and biological validity, thereby constraining clinical confidence and hindering real-world adoption.

System Scalability Most AI models require substantial computational resources, including high-performance hardware and large storage capacities, which limit their applicability in resource-constrained healthcare settings. Recent efforts, such as compact network architectures and cloud-based deployment, have shown technical feasibility for reducing hardware dependence, yet they remain limited by data security concerns, regulatory uncertainty, and heterogeneous clinical infrastructures. Clinical workflows further demand real-time and reliable predictions, yet existing models often struggle with computational efficiency, responsiveness, and interoperability with hospital information systems (HIS). These constraints continue to hinder large-scale dissemination and highlight the need for scalable and clinically adaptable AI systems [10, 215].

Clinical Translation Despite promising research results, the transition of AI models into clinical practice remains constrained by regulatory and ethical barriers. The lack of large-scale, prospective, multicenter validation undermines evidence for real-world applicability. At the same time, international regulatory frameworks for medical AI remain under development, with no universally accepted standards for evaluating safety, efficacy, accountability, or traceability [217]. Current approval pathways, such as the FDAs Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) framework in the United States [218] and the European MDR/CE process [219], provide partial guidance but remain incomplete for AI-driven imaging biomarkers. Concerns regarding patient privacy and data security add further complexity, as sensitive data must comply with strict ethical and legal requirements [220, 221]. Ethical concerns including bias, explainability, and accountability, also remain major obstacles to physician and patient acceptance [222]. Recent initiatives, such as algorithmic auditing frameworks, model registration systems, and regulatory sandboxes, have begun to address issues of transparency, traceability, and oversight, yet these mechanisms remain fragmented and lack harmonization across jurisdictions. Variability in physician and patient acceptance also poses obstacles, especially when models lack transparency or robustness. These challenges emphasize that successful clinical translation requires not only technological advancement but also regulatory clarity, ethical safeguards, and stakeholder trust.

Future directions

Looking forward, advancing radiology-based AI for targeted therapy prediction requires strategies that directly respond to the biological, clinical, and methodological challenges outlined above. On the biological side, closer integration with tumor biology and longitudinal monitoring will be critical to capture resistance mechanisms and dynamic disease evolution. On the data side, the establishment of large-scale, diverse, and multimodal datasets is essential to ensure representativeness and reproducibility. Methodologically, improving model robustness across domains and enhancing interpretability will help build trust and usability in clinical settings. Finally, rigorous clinical validation and regulatory frameworks are needed to translate these advances into safe, effective, and ethically responsible tools. The following subsections elaborate on these future directions in detail.

Biological Integration Predicting the presence of actionable targets alone does not fully reflect therapeutic efficacy, as response is shaped by mutation subtypes, resistance mechanisms, and microenvironmental dynamics. To overcome this, radiology-based AI models should be combined with multi-omics approaches, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, to capture tumor evolution and therapy-induced remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, longitudinal imaging follow-up in conjunction with liquid biopsy and spatial omics technologies could provide complementary perspectives for monitoring tumor adaptation and resistance in real time [223–225].

Dataset Expansion To overcome the limitations of small, single-center datasets and the lack of population diversity, future efforts should prioritize international multicenter collaborations to establish high-quality, large-scale datasets that capture diverse populations, cancer subtypes, and therapeutic contexts. Standardization of acquisition, reconstruction, and segmentation protocols is essential for ensuring reproducibility and comparability of imaging features across institutions. Furthermore, integration of multimodal data—including CT, MRI, PET, clinical variables, and molecular biomarkers—will provide a more comprehensive representation of tumor biology, strengthening the data foundation of the roadmap [7, 10].

Model Robustness From a methodological standpoint, future AI research should focus on strategies that enhance robustness and cross-domain generalization. Approaches such as domain generalization, federated learning, and foundation models may help models adapt to variability across scanners and institutions. In parallel, ensemble frameworks such as the Super Learner, which integrates multiple algorithms through internal cross-validation, can mitigate model-specific bias and improve predictive stability across heterogeneous datasets, thereby indirectly enhancing robustness [226]. Equally important is systematic external validation across independent cohorts, ensuring that models perform consistently across diverse clinical environments. Robustness against domain shifts will be indispensable for achieving clinical-grade reliability [9, 23].

Model Interpretability Complementary to robustness, interpretability remains a key methodological frontier for developing clinically trustworthy AI. Future systems should move beyond post hoc visualization toward generating transparent, biologically grounded explanations. More advanced XAI techniques, such as concept-based models that relate learned features to human-understandable concepts, counterfactual or prototype-based reasoning that contrasts alternative outcomes, and causal inference frameworks that uncover feature–phenotype–outcome pathways, offer new avenues to elucidate the reasoning process of AI models [227] [228]. Additionally, integrating multimodal or multi-omics data (e.g., transcriptomics, proteomics, and spatial profiling) enables biological validation of model-derived representations, linking computational interpretability with molecular and cellular mechanisms. Together, these advances can enhance methodological transparency, foster clinician trust, and accelerate safe clinical deployment.

Model Scalability In addition, AI models should be optimized for clinical usability, with lightweight architectures and efficient inference to enable real-time deployment in routine workflows, ensuring seamless integration into clinical decision-making [216, 227]. In parallel, cloud-based or hybrid edge–cloud deployment frameworks can extend accessibility by allowing hospitals with limited local computing resources to securely access AI services while maintaining data protection within institutional boundaries [229].

Clinical Translation The ultimate goal of radiology-based AI models is clinical translation. To this end, prospective, multicenter, and real-world studies will be essential for establishing safety, efficacy, and clinical impact. At the same time, the development of harmonized regulatory frameworks is required to define standards for performance evaluation, reporting, accountability, and data compliance. The EU AI Act (2024) exemplifies this effort, introducing a comprehensive regulatory approach that designates predictive healthcare AI as high-risk and mandates strong governance, transparency, and human oversight to ensure accountable and interpretable decision-making [230]. Complementary to these regulatory advances, initiatives such as SPIRIT-AI and CONSORT-AI provide practical guidance for trial design and reporting [231], though further adaptation is needed to address AI-specific issues such as algorithmic drift, continuous learning, and post-market monitoring. Patient privacy and data security must be addressed within these frameworks to ensure ethical deployment [220, 221]. Ethical safeguards should focus on mitigating bias, improving explainability, and establishing accountability to strengthen stakeholder trust [222]. To further reinforce this trust, future trials should incorporate uncertainty quantification using conformal prediction, which provides calibrated confidence intervals for individual predictions and communicates the reliability of model outputs to both clinicians and patients, thereby enhancing safety and regulatory transparency [232]. Establishing transparent validation pipelines and regulatory clarity will be the final step of the roadmap, transforming AI-based prediction from research innovation into routine clinical practice [64, 233–235].

Conclusion

Radiology-based AI models are emerging as a transformative approach for predicting therapeutic responses to targeted treatments across diverse cancer types. By harnessing quantitative imaging features, these models operate under two complementary paradigms: direct prediction, which maps imaging-derived representations to clinical treatment outcomes, and indirect prediction, which infers molecular alterations serving as imaging surrogates of treatment sensitivity. Such frameworks underscore the unique potential of medical imaging to noninvasively capture tumor phenotype and spatial heterogeneity beyond the reach of biopsy-based assays. Despite these advances, major challenges remain, including the absence of standardized imaging and feature extraction protocols, limited robustness and generalizability across multi-center cohorts, insufficient interpretability to ensure clinical trust, and a paucity of prospective validation. Addressing these limitations through sustained interdisciplinary collaboration will be critical for translating AI-based imaging biomarkers into clinical practice and advancing the realization of precision oncology.
