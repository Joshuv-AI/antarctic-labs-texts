Inclusive school practices for all youth: A European Cross‐National Study of general, sexual orientation, and gender identity‐based bullying
Authors: Haoran Meng, Alberto Amadori, S Henry Sherwood, Salvatore Ioverno, Stephen T Russell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBullying based on sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI‐based bullying) is prevalent across Europe. This study examined the associations among student‐level and school‐level inclusive school practices and general and SOGI‐based bullying across LGBTQ+ students and heterosexual cisgender students. Data were drawn from 13 European countries, with a final analytic sample of n = 6730. Participants had a mean age of 16.37 years (SD = 1.46), and 58% identified as LGBTQ+. Results from multivariate multilevel models indicated that SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies at the student level and positive LGBTQ+ representation at both the school and student levels were associated with lower rates of bullying, whereas SOGI‐inclusive sex education was not significantly associated with bullying outcomes. Further analyses showed that these inclusive school practices were negatively associated with general and SOGI‐based bullying to the same extent. More importantly, such associations were similar between LGBTQ+ students and heterosexual cisgender students. Overall, the findings highlight that inclusive school practices could be protective against both SOGI‐based and general bullying for all students, emphasizing the need for school interventions that explicitly address stigma toward LGBTQ+ identities to foster safer and more supportive school environments.Keywords: identity‐based bullying, inclusive school practices, sexual and gender minority youth

INTRODUCTION

Globally, one in three students experienced bullying at school (UNESCO, 2019). In addition, bullying based on minoritized sexual orientation and gender identity (i.e., SOGI‐based bullying) is pervasive, particularly among students who identify as LGBTQ+ (lesbian, gay, bisexual, queer, or questioning their sexual orientation and/or transgender, gender diverse, or questioning their gender identity). In Europe, SOGI‐based bullying remains a pressing concern. A recent report from the European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights (2024) revealed that nearly two‐thirds of LGBTQ+ students experienced harassment, insults, or threats at school because of their sexual orientation or gender identity. However, only 7% of respondents reported that their school education addressed LGBTQ+ issues in a positive way. This stark contrast highlights a substantial gap between the high prevalence of SOGI‐based bullying and the limited implementation of inclusive school interventions, underscoring the urgent need to examine the effectiveness of inclusive school practices in the European context. Accordingly, the current study examines how inclusive school practices, specifically SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies, positive LGBTQ+ representation, and SOGI‐inclusive sex education are associated with general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying across 13 European countries.

General bullying and SOGI‐based bullying

Bullying refers to intentional, repeated aggressive behaviors that involve a power imbalance and can cause harm to victims (Gladden et al., 2014). Depending on whether the bullying is driven by stigma toward individuals' social identity, bullying can be categorized as stigma‐based (or bias‐based) bullying and nonstigma‐based bullying (Earnshaw et al., 2018). In this study, we refer to nonstigma‐based bullying that does not target individuals' social identity as general bullying, and stigma‐based bullying that specifically targets individuals' actual or perceived sexual and gender identity as SOGI‐based bullying.

LGBTQ+ youth report higher levels of both general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying than their heterosexual cisgender peers, largely due to their divergence from heteronormativity and cisnormativity (Espino et al., 2024; Myers et al., 2020). For example, in a longitudinal study of 5037 students from the United Kingdom, Xu and Rahman (2025) found that LGB youth reported a greater increase in bullying victimization experiences compared to heterosexual youth. Similarly, Day et al. (2020) found that, among 1067 youth from the United States, transgender youth had three times higher odds of being bullied based on their gender identity than cisgender peers. At the same time, it is important to note that SOGI‐based bullying can also be directed at heterosexual cisgender youth, including youth who may deviate from traditional gender norms or are perceived to be LGBTQ+ by their peers (Martin‐Storey & Benner, 2023; Tucker et al., 2016). For instance, in a sample of 2552 Spanish youth, Ojeda et al. (2024) found that cisgender heterosexual youth with gender‐nonconforming expressions were more likely to be victims of both general and SOGI‐based bullying than cisgender heterosexual youth whose gender expression aligned with social expectations.

All forms of bullying are concerning; however, research suggests that SOGI‐based bullying is particularly harmful for youth compared to general bullying (Russell et al., 2012). Specifically, SOGI‐based bullying has been linked to poorer psychosocial well‐being, including internalizing problems (Eisenberg et al., 2024; Elipe et al., 2022), suicidal ideation (Meng et al., 2025), substance use (Gower, Watson, et al., 2025), disrupted sleep patterns (Real et al., 2025), lower self‐esteem (Lawrence et al., 2024), and poorer school adjustments (Lessard et al., 2020). Moreover, the effects of bullying are not uniform across youth. For example, Martin‐Storey and Benner (2023) found that the adverse impacts of SOGI‐based bullying were stronger among sexual minority youth compared with their heterosexual peers. These findings may help explain the persistent mental health disparities documented between LGBTQ+ and heterosexual cisgender youth (Eisenberg et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2025; Real et al., 2025). More importantly, these disparities often emerge early in development and, in many cases, persist throughout adolescence (Fish et al., 2023), highlighting the urgent need for early interventions that directly address SOGI‐based bullying.

Inclusive school practices targeting general and SOGI‐based bullying

School is the frontline for addressing general and SOGI‐based bullying, as youth spend a substantial portion of their time in this context interacting with peers. Although school‐level interventions targeting general bullying have been extensively researched and evaluated over the years, distinct strategies are needed to effectively address bullying that stems from stigma‐related factors such as social dominance, stereotypes, and prejudice, as in the case of SOGI‐based bullying (Earnshaw et al., 2018). In recent years, SOGI‐inclusive practices have been implemented and evaluated in school settings (Russell et al., 2021; Van Vliet et al., 2025). The current study focuses on three key forms of inclusive school practices: SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies, LGBTQ+ representation, and SOGI‐inclusive sex education.

The stigma underlying SOGI‐bullying manifests across the structural/institutional and interpersonal levels. Accordingly, school interventions must adopt multilevel approaches to effectively address SOGI‐based bullying (Earnshaw et al., 2018). One critical structural intervention is SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies, which explicitly enumerate regulatory rules against bullying based on actual or perceived sexual orientation and gender identity (Black et al., 2012). Such policies contribute to safer and more supportive school environments for LGBTQ+ and all students by explicitly prohibiting bullying behaviors, mandating that teachers and administrators report and investigate bullying incidents, and promoting positive, respectful peer interactions (Hall, 2017). A growing body of research has established the effectiveness of SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies in reducing SOGI‐based victimization (Day et al., 2020; Gower et al., 2018; Ioverno, 2023). Recent meta‐analyses and systematic reviews have identified SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies as among the most frequently investigated school practices, consistently linking them to lower rates of general and SOGI‐based bullying and higher levels of perceived school safety (Espino et al., 2024; Van Vliet et al., 2025).

As an upstream intervention, SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies can also reduce bullying by providing a foundation for downstream practices (Hall, 2017), such as promoting positive LGBTQ+ representation within schools. Creating such representation, through classroom discussions of LGBTQ+ individuals' achievements and contributions, learning about key milestones in LGBTQ+ history, and incorporating literature that features LGBTQ+ characters and experiences, can effectively challenge stereotypes and prejudices that fuel discrimination (Schlief et al., 2023; Van Vliet et al., 2025). Positive LGBTQ+ representation not only provides affirmation and validation for LGBTQ+ students but also offers opportunities for all students to understand experiences different from their own, which, in turn, can reduce stigma and increase awareness toward LGBTQ+ identities and foster a safer, more inclusive school climate (Snapp et al., 2015). Indeed, empirical studies have consistently shown that positive LGBTQ+ representation is associated with better mental health (Ioverno et al., 2025; Sousa & Gato, 2025), greater perceptions of school safety (Snapp et al., 2015), and lower rates of both general and SOGI‐based bullying among LGBTQ+ and all students (Eisenberg et al., 2022; Gower et al., 2018).

Similar to positive LGBTQ+ representation, SOGI‐inclusive sex education is another critical pathway through which students can understand and appreciate sexual and gender diversity (Goldfarb & Lieberman, 2021). Broadly conceptualized as comprehensive sex education, SOGI‐inclusive sex education provides age‐appropriate and medically accurate information about human anatomy and physiology, sexual health, gender identity and expression, sexual orientation, and interpersonal relationships (SIECUS, 2018). This approach explicitly incorporates LGBTQ+ topics and creates opportunities for students to learn about and discuss LGBTQ+ issues (Baams et al., 2017). Empirical studies have shown that SOGI‐inclusive sex education is linked to lower risks of mental health problems and lower prevalence of both general and SOGI‐based bullying victimization (Baams et al., 2017; Proulx et al., 2019; Snapp et al., 2015). Notably, a recent review found that SOGI‐inclusive sex education not only improves disease prevention but also reduces dating violence, fosters the development of healthy relationships, enhances appreciation for sexual and gender diversity, and reduces SOGI‐based bullying (Goldfarb & Lieberman, 2021).

Inclusive school practices in the European context

Across Europe, the implementation of school‐based practices aimed at reducing SOGI‐based bullying varies substantially in both scope and depth. Although many countries have adopted broad anti‐discrimination frameworks applicable to education, only a subset have translated these into concrete policy action plans, systematically embedded inclusive content within national curricula, or provided comprehensive training for educators (IGLYO, 2022). Moreover, the inclusion and representation of LGBTQ+ identities in curricula remain uneven (Ferbežar et al., 2025), and structured efforts such as SOGI‐inclusive sex education are often optional, locally implemented, or inconsistently delivered across educational systems (Ketting et al., 2021). Together, these gaps point to a broader disconnect between formal policy adoption and effective implementation in practice.

Importantly, this variability reflects broader regional and national differences in policy climates across Europe. Western European countries are generally more likely to implement comprehensive, inclusive measures, whereas Southern, Central, and Eastern European contexts often exhibit partial or uneven implementation, and in some cases, restrictive environments that limit LGBTQ‐related content in schools (IGLYO, 2022). This cross‐national heterogeneity underscores the importance of examining how school‐level inclusive practices are associated with both general and SOGI‐based bullying, and whether these associations are consistent across multiple European contexts.

The current study

Although existing research in Europe has established a link between inclusive school practices and lower levels of SOGI‐based bullying, several gaps remain. First, most studies in Europe focus on a single country (Espino et al., 2024), yet there is substantial social, economic, and political diversity across European countries, including with regard to attitudes toward LGBTQ+ populations (IGLYO, 2022; Ioverno, 2023). Thus, a cross‐national approach is necessary to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Second, prior research has primarily examined student‐level associations, overlooking the potential differences in impact between school‐level practices and students' experiences and perceptions of such practices (Earnshaw et al., 2018; Snapp et al., 2015). Finally, to our knowledge, no studies have explicitly and simultaneously compared whether inclusive school practices are effective for addressing both general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying. Such understanding is important, as these inclusive practices are expected to reduce all forms of bullying among all students, including LGBTQ+ as well as heterosexual cisgender youth (Russell et al., 2021).

To address these gaps, the present study examines the associations between inclusive school practices (i.e., anti‐bullying policies, LGBTQ+ representation, and sex education) and both general and SOGI‐based bullying across 13 European countries with varying socioeconomic conditions and national climates for LGBTQ+ populations. Specifically, the study investigates: (1) What are the associations between students' perceived inclusive school practices and their experiences of general and SOGI‐based bullying? (2) What are the associations between school‐level inclusive school practices and general and SOGI‐based bullying? (3) Do inclusive school practices have similar or different associations with general bullying compared to SOGI‐based bullying? (4) Do the associations between inclusive school practices and both forms of bullying exist for both LGBTQ+ youth and heterosexual cisgender youth?

METHODS

Participants and procedures

Data were drawn from the FREE (Fostering the Right to Education in Europe) Project, collected from 2020 to 2022 through online surveys distributed across 13 countries in different areas of Europe: The Southern area (i.e., Greece, Italy, Spain and Portugal), the Western area (i.e., Austria, Belgium, France, Ireland, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom), the Central and Eastern area (i.e., Croatia, Slovenia) and the Baltic area1 (N = 35,804). Data collection involved working with local collaborators and nongovernmental organizations through distributing surveys via schools' online educational platforms, targeted social media advertisements, and websites. By focusing recruitment on minority stress experiences related to LGBTQ+ identities in schools and collaborating with LGBTQ+ NGOs, this targeted recruitment strategy oversampled LGBTQ+ students, allowing us to include a larger proportion of LGBTQ+ participants than would be expected in the general student population.

The surveys were translated from English into each country's language using back‐translation procedures. Parental consent was waived to protect participants from inadvertent disclosure of a sexual or gender minority identity. This approach adhered to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) framework in Europe, which permits research conducted in the public interest to enhance knowledge for the benefit of society (Gefenas et al., 2022). The study did not collect data on participants' racial or ethnic identity due to varying legal restrictions and cultural sensitivities across European Member States. In many countries, such questions are discouraged, and researchers are advised to use alternative descriptors such as geographical or cultural origin. Additionally, the classification of ethnic and racial categories differs significantly across Europe, making standardized data collection challenging (Farkas, 2017). The study was approved by the University of Ghent, Belgium, Institutional Review Board.

Due to the multilevel structure of the analyses, only participants who reported a valid school identifier were retained (n = 26,963). Responses flagged as potentially mischievous were excluded (n = 563; 2.1%). Participants younger than 14 years old were also removed (n = 1489; 5.5%), consistent with the minimum age of consent for study participation. To support multilevel estimation, participants from schools with fewer than five respondents were excluded (n = 17,149; 63.6%). Finally, additional data quality checks were conducted to ensure sufficient information for multilevel multiple imputation. Specifically, schools with no valid data on one or more study variables were excluded, resulting in the removal of 1032 participants (3.8%).

A minimum of five respondents per school was retained to ensure that each school‐level cluster contributed sufficient within‐school information while preserving as much of the available data as possible. Although larger within‐school sample sizes are generally desirable, simulation study results suggest that the number of higher‐level units (i.e., schools) is more consequential for obtaining reliable estimates than the number of individuals within each cluster (Maas & Hox, 2005). Given the large number of schools in this study (n = 706), smaller within‐school sample sizes are unlikely to substantially compromise the estimation of fixed effects. Moreover, the current analyses focus on random‐intercept models with fixed effects only, which further reduces the demands on within‐school sample size (McNeish & Stapleton, 2016).

The final analytic sample included n = 6730 students across 706 schools. Participants had a mean age of 16.37 years with a range from 14 to 25 (SD = 1.46). Most participants were not immigrants (93.2%). In terms of sexual orientation, 32.6% identified as heterosexual, 14.1% as lesbian or gay, 22.6% as bisexual, 3.9% as questioning, 9.8% as another sexual orientation, and 17.0% did not report their sexual orientation. Regarding gender identity, 54.1% identified as cisgender women, 20.8% as cisgender men, 4.0% as transgender, 7.1% as questioning, 10.5% as another gender identity, and 3.4% did not report their gender identity. In total, 58% identified as LGBTQ+, and 32% identified as heterosexual.

Researcher reflexivity

The research team includes scholars at different career stages whose work focuses on LGBTQ+ youth, school contexts, mental health, intersectionality, and inclusive educational practices. As members of the LGBTQ+ community, our lived experiences provided partial insider perspectives that sensitized us to issues of identity‐based marginalization and school inclusion. At the same time, differences in nationality, race, professional status, and familiarity with European educational contexts may have shaped our perspectives and biases. Several authors were trained primarily in US research contexts, raising the possibility of interpreting European school practices and LGBTQ+ youths' experiences through US‐based assumptions. Team members with lived and professional experience in European contexts provided important contextual knowledge, while we recognized that no individual researcher could represent the experiences of youth across all participating countries and social positions.

To minimize potential bias and reduce the influence of any single cultural or academic perspective, we developed interpretations collaboratively across disciplinary, national, and cultural perspectives. We considered alternative explanations for cross‐national patterns, questioned assumptions about what constitutes inclusive school practice across contexts, and drew on the expertise of investigators familiar with the participating countries and broader project. These discussions helped identify potential blind spots, limited the influence of any single perspective, and supported more context‐sensitive interpretations. In addition, investigators and organizations from each participating country contributed to the wording and contextual adaptation of the survey, providing important local and cultural knowledge. Nevertheless, we recognize that our interpretations remain shaped by our collective positionalities and by the inherent power imbalance between researchers and the young people whose experiences are represented in the data.

Measures

Anti‐bullying policy

To measure the presence of anti‐bullying policies, participants were asked, “Does your school have a policy or a procedure for reporting incidents of harassment or assault in school?” (yes, no, I don't know). If they answered yes, they were presented with two follow‐up questions: “Does this policy specifically mention sexual orientation?” and “Does this policy specifically mention gender identity and/or expression?” (yes, no, I don't know). The variable was coded such that 0 = No anti‐bullying policy, 1 = General anti‐bullying policy (answered “yes” to the main question but “no” or “I don't know” to both follow‐up questions), and 2 = SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policy (answered “yes” to at least one of the two follow‐up questions). Similar questions were used in previous studies (Day et al., 2020).

LGBTQ+ representation

To measure LGBTQ+ representation in school, participants were asked, “In the last school year, were any of the following topics addressed in the classroom during a lesson or a discussion?” (yes, no). Two items followed: one on “transgender people or issues” and another on “lesbian, gay, or bisexual people or issues.” Participants who answered yes to either item were then asked, “How positive or negative were representations of these topics in class?” (mostly not supportive, neutral/mixed, mostly supportive). Responses were recoded into four categories: 0 = No LGBTQ+ representation: “No” to both main questions; 1 = Negative representation: “Yes” to at least one main question and “mostly not supportive” on the follow‐up (including combinations of “mostly not supportive” and “neutral/mixed”); 2 = Mixed representation: “Yes” to at least one main question and “neutral/mixed” on the follow‐up(s); and 3 = Positive representation: “Yes” to at least one main question and “mostly supportive” on the follow‐up (including combinations of “mostly supportive” and “neutral/mixed”). Similar questions were used in previous studies (Austin et al., 2015).

Sex education

To measure students' experience of sex education at schools, participants were asked, “Have you received any sex education in your school?” (yes, no). If they answered yes, they were also asked, “Was the sex education that you received inclusive of different sexual orientations?” (yes, no). Responses were recoded such that 0 = No sex education, 1 = General sex education, 2 = SOGI‐inclusive sex education. Similar questions were used in previous studies (Cruz et al., 2023).

General bullying

The California Bullying Victimization Scale (Felix et al., 2011) was used to measure general bullying. The scale has 7 items asking participants how often they experienced bullying over the past 30 days at school (e.g., “been teased or called names in a mean or hurtful way?”). The response options include 0 = Never, 1 = Not in the past month but it happened before, 2 = Once, 3 = Two to three times, 4 = About once a week, and 5 = Several times a week. These responses were averaged, with higher scores indicating more general bullying. The scale demonstrated good reliability and validity in LGBTQ+ youth samples (Bauman et al., 2023). The Cronbach's α in the current sample was .83.

Sexual orientation‐ and gender identity‐based bullying (SOGI‐based bullying)

A 9‐item measure was used to assess bias‐based bullying (Austin et al., 2015). The scale asked participants, “Over the last 30 days you were on school property, how often were you harassed or bullied for any of the following reasons?” The 4 items related to SOGI were used to construct the SOGI‐based bullying variable: minoritized sexual orientation (“Because you are gay, lesbian, bisexual or queer or someone thought you were”), gender identity (“Because of your gender”), transgender identity (“Because you are transgender”), and gender expression (“Because you aren't as “masculine” as other guys or because you aren't as “feminine” as other girls”). The response options include 0 = Never, 1 = Not in the past month but it happened before, 2 = Once, 3 = Two to three times, 4 = About once a week, and 5 = Several times a week. An average score across the 4 items was used to construct SOGI‐based bullying. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in LGBTQ+ youth samples (Baams et al., 2019). The Cronbach's α in the current sample was .75.

Demographic covariates

Demographic variables, including age in years, sexual orientation, gender identity, immigration status, and socioeconomic status, were included as covariates for analysis, given consistent evidence suggesting their association with general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying (Espelage et al., 2019; Espino et al., 2024).

Sexual orientation was measured with the question, “Which of the following best describes your current sexual orientation?” (heterosexual, gay/lesbian, bisexual, questioning, pansexual, asexual, and other). For analysis, the last three responses were combined into a single category for another sexual orientation due to small cell sizes.

Gender identity was assessed using a two‐step method. Participants were asked “What sex were you assigned at birth, on your original birth certificate?” (male, female) and “If you had to choose only one of the following terms, which best describes your current gender?” (man, woman, nonbinary, queer, transgender woman, transgender man, intersex, questioning, and other). Youth whose gender identity aligned with their sex assigned at birth were coded as cisgender girls or cisgender boys, while those whose gender identity differed or chose transgender identity were categorized as transgender girls or transgender boys. Due to small cell sizes, responses including transgender woman and transgender man were combined into a category for transgender, whereas nonbinary, queer, and other were combined into a category for another gender identity.

Immigration status was determined by asking participants whether they were born in the country where the survey was distributed. The variable was recoded such that 0 = Not immigrant, 1 = Immigrant.

Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed with a 4‐item revised version of the Family Affluence Scale (Hobza et al., 2017). Sample items included “How many computers (PCs, Macs, or laptops) does your family own?” and “How many times did you travel abroad for a holiday/vacation the year before the coronavirus pandemic?” (0, 1, 2, 3, or more). A sum score was used for the analysis, with higher scores indicating higher SES.

Analytic approach

The logistic regression results suggest that missingness is not completely random but is systematically associated with certain demographic characteristics. Specifically, older participants were slightly more likely to have missing data (b = 0.06, p = .041). In addition, gender identity was a consistent predictor of missingness: compared to cisgender men, participants identifying as cisgender women, transgender, questioning, or other gender identities showed significantly higher odds of missingness (bs = 0.33–0.43, ps < .05). These findings indicate that the data are unlikely to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), but the observed associations with measured demographic variables are consistent with a Missing at Random (MAR) mechanism. Little's MCAR test further confirmed that the data were not MCAR, χ2 (438) = 1555, p < .001. This finding aligns with prior work, which notes that MCAR is a strong yet often unrealistic assumption, particularly in large‐scale social surveys (Enders, 2022). Taken together, we proceeded under the assumption that the data were MAR.

Multiple imputation was conducted using the R package rblimp that integrates Blimp in R (Keller & Enders, 2023). Blimp is a stand‐alone program that specializes in missing data analysis, which accommodates the multilevel structure of the analysis model in this study. Multilevel multiple imputation via fully conditional specification was used for the missing data including all the study variables. Recommendations suggest that the number of imputations should be at least as large as the proportion of missing data (van Buuren, 2018). In the present study, although average missingness was 20%, the number of imputations was set to m = 30 to further improve the precision and stability of the estimates. Due to the convergence consideration, the missing responses from sexual orientation and gender identity measures were collapsed into the “another sexual orientation” and “another gender identity” categories. The parameter estimates of the imputation model converged, as indicated by the potential scale reduction factor from the final check of the burn‐in period, which was less than 1.05 (Gelman et al., 2013), when the number of burn‐in iterations was set to 80,000 and the number of iterations was set to 50,000. The statistics of interest were estimated within each imputed dataset and subsequently pooled using Rubin's rules (Rubin, 2004).

Substantive analyses

We estimated three‐level multivariate multilevel models (MVMM), with students nested within schools and schools nested within countries. To implement the multivariate structure, outcomes were stacked in long format, and outcome indicators were included in both the fixed and random parts of the model (Pituch & Stevens, 2016). This approach accounts for the intercorrelation between outcomes and enables direct comparison of whether the effects of key predictors (i.e., inclusive school practices) differ across outcomes (i.e., general versus SOGI‐based bullying).

Three MVMMs were specified. Model 1 was an empty means model with random intercepts, allowing outcome‐specific intercepts (and their covariance) at the school level nested within country, along with an unstructured residual covariance at the student level to account for within‐student dependence between outcomes. Model 2 extended Model 1 by including demographic covariates and students' perceptions of school practices (entered as group‐mean‐centered variables) at the student level, as well as aggregated school practices (group means) at the school level. Model 3 further allowed the effects of predictors and covariates to vary by outcome to test whether inclusive school practices and demographics were differentially associated with general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying. Nested model comparisons were conducted using likelihood‐ratio‐based F tests to evaluate improvements in model fit across imputations.

Because the number of participating students per school varied substantially, ranging from 5 to 168 students, with a median of 7, aggregated school‐level scores may be less representative of actual school practices in schools with fewer respondents. Although this limitation cannot be fully resolved with the current data, we calculated within‐school agreement proportions to assess whether students within the same school reported similar practices. In the current sample, mean within‐school proportion agreement was high for school policy items (M = .83), indicating that, on average, 83% of students within the same school selected the same policy response. Agreement was lower but still moderate for sex education (M = .70) and LGBTQ+ representation (M = .62). Thus, although small within‐school sample sizes remain a limitation, the moderate to high levels of agreement suggest that this concern may be less consequential than it would be under conditions of low within‐school agreement.

RESULTS

Descriptive results

As shown in Figure 1 and Table S1, levels of general and SOGI‐based bullying varied meaningfully across countries. The United Kingdom showed the highest levels of both general and SOGI‐based bullying, whereas the Netherlands showed the lowest levels. Greece and the Baltic state showed relatively elevated bullying, while Austria, Portugal, Spain, and Italy showed lower levels, particularly for SOGI‐based bullying. School practices also varied across countries. Reports of anti‐bullying policies were generally high, although SOGI‐inclusive policies were less common than general policies. LGBTQ+ representation showed especially pronounced variation, with higher levels of positive representation in some countries, such as Austria and France, and more frequent reports of no representation in others, such as the Baltic state and the Netherlands. Inclusive sex education was similarly uneven, with higher levels in some countries, such as the Netherlands, and much lower levels in others, such as Greece.

Bullying (Z score) and prevalence of school practices across countries.

Importantly, the descriptive patterns did not suggest a uniform country‐level correspondence between higher reported inclusive practices and lower bullying. Some countries appeared to reflect a “high‐inclusion, low‐risk” pattern, such as the Netherlands and Austria, where more reports of certain inclusive practices coincided with lower bullying. Others showed a “low‐inclusion, high‐risk” pattern, such as the Baltic state, where lower reported inclusion aligned with higher bullying risk. Still others reflected an “inclusion gap and high‐victimization” pattern, most notably the United Kingdom, where relatively high reports of some inclusive practices co‐occurred with the highest bullying levels. These mixed patterns underscore the importance of accounting for country‐level clustering and considering country contexts when interpreting the associations between school practices and bullying.

Substantive analyses results

Based on the empty means, random‐intercept model, ICCs at the school level were .052 and .048 for general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying, respectively, meaning that 5.2% of the variance in general bullying and 4.8% of the variance in SOGI‐based bullying was due to school‐level factors.

Using likelihood‐ratio‐based F tests to compare the goodness of fit of nested models, the results showed that Model 2 fit the data better than Model 1: F (25, 68) = 18.35, p < .001, suggesting that adding demographic covariates and students' perceptions of school practices at the student level, along with aggregated school practices at the school level, significantly improved the model fit. Similarly, Model 3 fit better than Model 2: F (25, 157) = 9.40, p < .001, suggesting that allowing the associations of key predictors and demographics to differ between the two dependent variables further improved the model. Taken together, Model 3 was retained, suggesting that, overall, the school practices and demographic characteristics had distinct associations with general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying.

However, when examining the results of Model 3 (see Table S2), school practices, along with age, SES, and immigration status, had similar coefficients across the two outcomes. At the same time, the effects of sexual and gender identity variables showed greater distinction. This pattern suggests that the heterogeneity in coefficients between general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying outcomes primarily stems from the experiences related to sexual orientation and gender identity rather than school practices or other demographics.

To verify whether the associations of school practices and sexual orientation and gender identity variables differed between general and SOGI‐based bullying, additional nested models were estimated. Built on Model 2, where all predictors had the same effects on general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying outcomes, Model 4 allowed the effects of school‐practice variables to vary across outcomes, and Model 5 allowed the effects of the sexual orientation and gender identity variables to vary across outcomes. Results showed that Model 4 did not have a better fit than Model 2: F (14, 63) = 1.16, p = .327. In contrast, Model 5 fit the data better than Model 2: F (8, 382) = 30.13, p < .001. Furthermore, Model 3, in which all predictors had varying effects on two outcomes, did not outperform Model 5: F (17, 71) = 1.05, p = .420. Taken together, Model 5 was retained, showing that sexual orientation and gender identity differed in their associations with general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying but not school practices or other demographic characteristics.

The results for Model 5 are presented in Table 2. Compared to schools without anti‐bullying policies, the presence of general anti‐bullying policies was not significantly associated with bullying at the school level, but was significantly associated with lower rates of both general and SOGI‐based bullying at the student level (b = −0.24, p < .001). Similarly, SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies were associated with lower rates of both forms of bullying at the student level (b = −0.20, p = .003), though the school‐level effect was nonsignificant.

For sexual and gender identities, the general pattern showed that sexual or gender minority youth reported higher rates of both general and SOGI‐based bullying compared to their heterosexual or cisgender peers. Moreover, these associations were consistently stronger for SOGI‐based bullying than for general bullying. For instance, compared to cisgender students, students who identified as transgender reported significantly higher rates of both forms of bullying, with the effect size for SOGI‐based bullying (b = 0.79, p < .001) being almost double that for general bullying (b = 0.41, p < .001).

Sensitivity analyses results

To further examine whether the associations between school practices and bullying outcomes differed between LGBTQ+ and cisgender heterosexual students, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing two nested multivariate multilevel models. The constrained model (see Table S3) estimated the effects of school‐practice variables on general bullying and SOGI‐based bullying. The group‐difference model (see Table S4) additionally included interaction terms between LGBTQ+ group membership and each school‐practice variable, allowing the effects of school practices to vary across LGBTQ+ and non‐LGBTQ+ students. The pooled model comparison indicated that allowing school‐practice effects to differ by LGBTQ+ group membership did not significantly improve model fit, F(14, 167) = 1.12, p = .348. Thus, there was no evidence that the associations between school practices and bullying outcomes differed between LGBTQ+ and cisgender heterosexual students.

DISCUSSION

The current study examined the associations between SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies, positive LGBTQ+ representation, and SOGI‐inclusive sex education with both general and SOGI‐based bullying using a large and diverse cross‐national sample from 13 European countries. By adopting a multilevel perspective (i.e., school‐level practices versus students' individual experiences and perceptions of such practices), the findings provide a more comprehensive examination of how inclusive school practices relate to two forms of bullying, general and SOGI‐based, among both LGBTQ+ and heterosexual cisgender students in the European context.

The associations between inclusive school practices and bullying

The current study found that both general and SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies were associated with lower rates of general and SOGI‐based bullying compared to schools without any anti‐bullying policy. However, these associations of general and SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies emerged only at the student level. This finding suggests that for anti‐bullying policies to be effective in reducing bullying, students must perceive them. School administrators must ensure these policies are implemented as intended through teacher training, consistent enforcement, and supportive school leadership (Hall, 2017; Schlief et al., 2023). Effective implementation allows bullying incidents to be appropriately monitored and addressed, enabling students to perceive and experience the actual benefits of the policy (i.e., student‐level perception) rather than merely the presence of such policies with poor enactment at the school level. These results underscore the importance of distinguishing between school‐level practices and students' lived experiences of those practices, as their impacts on student outcomes may differ (Snapp et al., 2015).

The findings also highlight the critical role of LGBTQ+ representation in schools. Specifically, compared to the absence of LGBTQ+ representation, negative representation, at both the student and school levels, was associated with higher rates of both general and SOGI‐based bullying. This suggests that negative remarks and deficit‐ or risk‐oriented narratives about LGBTQ+ individuals and issues may reinforce biases against the LGBTQ+ community, thereby increasing bullying targeting LGBTQ+ students. In contrast, positive LGBTQ+ representation was linked to lower rates of general and SOGI‐based bullying at both the student and school levels. These findings align with previous research showing that positive and affirming LGBTQ+ representation can enhance awareness, understanding, and acceptance of LGBTQ+ individuals, foster equitable learning environments and supportive school climates, encourage students to intervene in bullying situations, and ultimately lower the prevalence of bullying (Ioverno et al., 2022; Schlief et al., 2023; Snapp et al., 2015).

Contrary to evidence from prior studies (Baams et al., 2017; Proulx et al., 2019; Snapp et al., 2015), the current study did not find significant associations between SOGI‐inclusive sex education and bullying at either the student or school level. One explanation is its conceptual and empirical overlap with broader LGBTQ+ representation, which captures inclusive curricular content more generally (Russell et al., 2021). This shared variance may make it difficult to detect the unique contribution of inclusive sex education when modeled alongside other school practices. In addition, the measure of SOGI‐inclusive sex education in the current study is relatively broad and does not capture key aspects such as frequency, depth, or quality of implementation. Given substantial cross‐national variability in sex education across European contexts (Ketting et al., 2021), this measure may not fully reflect meaningful differences in students' experiences. Accordingly, these null findings should be interpreted with caution. Rather than indicating that SOGI‐inclusive sex education is unimportant, the findings highlight the need for more nuanced measures and further research on implementation quality.

Another key finding of this study is that inclusive school practices, particularly SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies and positive LGBTQ+ representation, were significantly associated with lower rates of both general and SOGI‐based bullying to the same extent. These results not only align with evidence from the United States showing that inclusive school practices are effective in reducing bullying broadly, regardless of whether it is specifically targeted at LGBTQ+ identity (Gower et al., 2018; Russell et al., 2021), but also extend this evidence by showing that their associations are equally salient for both general and SOGI‐based bullying.

Importantly, sensitivity analyses showed no significant differences in the associations between school practices and bullying outcomes between LGBTQ+ and cisgender and heterosexual students. This finding suggests that inclusive school practices, particularly SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies and positive LGBTQ+ representation, may be protective for both LGBTQ+ and cisgender and heterosexual students. These results align with prior US‐based research showing that SOGI‐inclusive school practices can benefit all students, not only those who identify as LGBTQ+ (Gower et al., 2018; Snapp et al., 2015). This can be explained by the fact that SOGI‐inclusive practices are intended to promote a safer and more supportive school environment for all students, not only those who identify as LGBTQ+ (Russell et al., 2021). First, all students, regardless of their SOGI identities, may experience bullying, including SOGI‐based bullying based on actual or perceived identities (Martin‐Storey & Benner, 2023). Second, SOGI‐inclusive practices can improve the broader school climate by establishing norms against discrimination and increasing awareness of different forms of bullying. For example, inclusive anti‐bullying policies have been associated with a safer school climate (Van Vliet et al., 2025), and positive LGBTQ+ representation may foster greater acceptance of LGBTQ+ individuals while promoting broader values of respect for diversity and opposition to prejudice (Ioverno et al., 2025). Together, these findings reinforce the importance of implementing inclusive school practices as part of broader school‐based bullying prevention efforts.

The importance of the country context and global research

This study extends the predominantly single‐country literature on bias‐based bullying by examining these processes across 13 European countries representing diverse social, cultural, political, and educational contexts. Despite cross‐national variation in both bullying and school practices, associations between SOGI‐inclusive school practices and bullying were evident across multiple European contexts. At the same time, this variation highlights the importance of considering national context rather than assuming that findings from one country generalize uniformly to others.

The cross‐national variation observed in SOGI‐based bullying underscores the importance of examining SOGI‐based bullying from a broader global and structural perspective. SOGI‐based bullying reflects the intersection of interpersonal aggression and broader systems of structural discrimination tied to sexual orientation and gender identity (Earnshaw et al., 2018). Differences in social climates, cultural norms, and policy environments linked to structural stigma may therefore shape the prevalence and visibility of SOGI‐based bullying across countries. Thus, global and comparative research can help identify how these contextual factors shape the prevalence, awareness, and consequences of SOGI‐based bullying across countries.

Inclusive school practices also varied substantially across countries, and their descriptive correspondence with bullying was not uniform. Some countries showed relatively higher levels of inclusive practices and lower bullying, whereas others showed relatively high reports of some inclusive practices alongside elevated bullying. These inconsistencies suggest that the meaning, implementation, and consequences of inclusive school practices may depend on broader cultural norms, policy environments, and social attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people (Ioverno, 2023). Global and comparative research is therefore needed not only to assess whether inclusive practices are protective but also to understand the contextual conditions under which they are most likely to promote safer school environments.

Although the current study advances cross‐national research, the participating countries were all European and therefore represent only a subset of global contexts. Future research should extend these comparisons to more diverse regions, particularly Majority World contexts, to examine the generalizability of existing frameworks and identify culturally sensitive approaches to addressing bias‐based bullying.

Limitations, future directions, and implications

Despite the important findings, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference and prevents examination of change over time. Second, although the study used a cross‐national sample, country‐level effects were not explicitly modeled. Because SOGI‐based bullying operates across multiple ecological levels (Earnshaw et al., 2018), future research should incorporate country‐level predictors and examine how national contexts intersect with school‐level practices to shape bullying. Prior research suggests that inclusive school practices may buffer LGBTQ+ youth from bullying even in less supportive national climates (Sherwood et al., 2025), indicating that although national policies can shape the broader context for inclusion, local school practices may be more proximal and meaningful to students' daily experiences.

In addition, the sampling strategy and some methodological decisions may limit the generalizability of the findings. Recruitment through LGBTQ+ organizations, networks, and online spaces likely contributed to an overrepresentation of LGBTQ+ youth and an underrepresentation of cisgender heterosexual youth relative to the broader student population. As a result, estimates of bullying prevalence and subgroup comparisons should not be interpreted as population‐representative. In addition, the study included only youth currently enrolled in school. Because out‐of‐school youth may experience greater marginalization and discrimination, bullying prevalence in the present sample may be underestimated (Gower, Houghtaling, et al., 2025; Russell et al., 2014). Finally, due to sample size constraints, some gender identity and sexual orientation categories were collapsed, which may have obscured important within‐group differences and intersectional experiences.

Importantly, missingness on school‐practice variables warrants caution. Missing responses may reflect either the absence of practices or limited awareness of existing practices. If missingness reflects absence, the analytic sample may overrepresent schools with more visible or formalized inclusive practices. Alternatively, if it reflects uncertainty, the findings may be most applicable to schools where practices are clearly communicated to students, which is itself an important dimension of school climate. Survey design may also have contributed to missingness, as school‐practice items appeared near the end of a long questionnaire, increasing the possibility of respondent fatigue or disengagement. Because these mechanisms cannot be distinguished, findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Finally, school‐level inclusive practices were aggregated from student reports rather than measured through objective school records. Accordingly, school‐level associations may partly capture broader perceptions of school climate in addition to formal policy or practice implementation. In addition, because both bullying experiences and school practices were reported by students, associations between perceived school practices and bullying may be inflated by common reporter effects or broader perception bias. Future studies should incorporate reports from administrators, staff, or policy documents to better assess actual implementation and distinguish subjective perceptions from objective school‐level practices.

The findings carry important implications for educational policy and practice across Europe. Given the cross‐national scope of this study, our results highlight the need for consistent, SOGI‐inclusive frameworks across European education systems that explicitly prohibit bullying and promote positive LGBTQ+ representation. Although countries differ substantially in how they address school bullying (Ioverno, 2023), our results suggest that anti‐bullying efforts enumerating sexual orientation and gender identity offer distinct protective value, particularly when their implementation is visible to both LGBTQ+ and all students. These results also align with the principles of a whole‐school, multi‐pronged approach that engages all members of the school community and employs multiple strategies, ranging from upstream SOGI‐inclusive anti‐bullying policies to downstream practices, such as promoting positive LGBTQ+ representation (Ioverno, 2023; Pearce et al., 2024). Educational stakeholders could strengthen existing anti‐bullying policies by explicitly incorporating SOGI‐related provisions, enhancing LGBTQ+ visibility, and formalizing institutional support for LGBTQ+ students. Furthermore, the protective role of positive LGBTQ+ representation underscores the importance of curricular inclusion beyond sex education—embedding diverse identities across subjects and grade levels to normalize difference and foster belonging. This work could inform frameworks such as the forthcoming EU Strategy for LGBTQI Equality (2026–2030) and the European Education Area (European Commission, 2024) to support the harmonization of inclusive educational standards and resource‐sharing among Member States.
