Experimental assessment with data-driven machine learning-based prediction of compressive strength of waste natural fiber-reinforced sustainable concrete
Authors: Prince Paul, Md Kawsarul Islam Kabbo, Rajarshi Das Gupta, Md Habibur Rahman Sobuz, Prosanjit Baral, Mohammed Jameel, Sani Aliyu Abubakar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a hybrid approach combining experimental evaluation and machine learning modeling for the compressive strength (CS) estimation of natural fiber-reinforced concrete (NFRC), which utilizes jute, coir, and bamboo fibers. A dataset of 444 concrete mix designs was compiled from the literature using seven input variables. Six advanced machine learning (ML) algorithms: Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors were trained and tested with proper hyperparameter optimization. Among these, the LGBM model demonstrated superior accuracy in prediction with an R2 of 0.8637 and the lowest root mean square error of 4.19 MPa on the testing sets. To ensure interpretability and mix design optimization, SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) analyses were incorporated. The dominant predictors of CS were found to be cement content, water, coarse aggregate, and supplementary cementitious materials, while the curing period and fiber content also showed a small but meaningful effect. To further validate the modeling outcomes, experimental investigations were conducted by developing 10 mix combinations with varying percentages of coir fiber, which were then subjected to compressive strength and scanning electron microscopy tests. The outcomes also validated that the incorporation of natural fiber up to 0.75% gradually increased the CS to a maximum of 41.3% after 28 days of curing, while further addition reduced performance. The dual-approach-based (ML and experimental) outcomes could assist in the sustainable advancement of the infrastructure industry as a potential solution for cost-effective, large-scale production of NFRC.Keywords: Supervised machine learning, Natural fiber, Microstructural assessment, Compressive strength, Parametric optimizationSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Due to its capabilities and being easy to use, concrete continues to be one of the most popular materials globally, playing an essential role in the building of residential infrastructure and cities1. With rising global demands, experts are predicting that the world will produce 5.5 billion tons of concrete in a year by 20502. Such considerable usage not only demonstrates the adaptability and durability of concrete but also the importance of seeking innovative and sustainable production and improved material performance3. Concrete is inherently fragile because of its brittle tensile strength and strain capacity4,5. Moreover, studies have shown that concrete suffers from low tensile strength, high porosity, cracking sensitivity, minimal impact resistance, water permeability, and low chemical durability6,7. To address this issue, several studies have shown that concrete includes fibers such as steel, carbon, jute, coir, palm, and cellulose to improve its efficiency as a stabilizing and reinforcing element8–11. Several studies reveal that natural fibers are a more sustainable alternative to synthetic fibers, improving the compressive and tensile strength, crack resistance, fracture tolerance, and stress adaptability of concrete12–15. Past research has demonstrated that cellulose fiber-reinforced concrete increases strength, toughness, and durability, with the optimal content being 0.9 kg/m³ in extreme environmental conditions16. A study of bamboo fiber-reinforced concrete indicates enhanced tensile, flexural, impact, and ductile properties, with an optimum fiber content of 1.2% by weight of cement17. One of the studies shows that the incorporation of the 0.25% jute fiber improves compressive and flexural strength18. An investigation revealed that 1% Kenaf fiber reinforcement in high-strength concrete composites enhances tensile strength and toughness; however, compressive strength is reduced19.

Experts agree that sometimes natural fibers, though useful in concrete reinforcement, reduce concrete strength due to a lack of hydration and fewer pozzolanic reactions20,21. To resolve this problem, researchers have employed Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCM) like fly ash, slag, silica fume, and metakaolin to enhance durability, porosity, and fiber-matrix bond in the fiber-reinforced concrete22–26. Al-Naghi et al.27 revealed that the addition of wheat straw ash at 10%, silica fume at 7%, and coconut fiber at 1.5% in a recycled aggregate concrete produced 30.7 MPa of compressive strength and 3.89 MPa of tensile strength. Subedi et al.28 showed that the addition of 1% Himalayan Giant Nettle fiber with SCM, such as 20% fly ash and 20% rice husk ash, enhanced compressive (16.2%), flexural (33.3%), and tensile strength (36.9%). The fibers have an exceptionally good ability to enhance strength by reducing the development of cracks and evenly distributing stresses in the concrete29. To understand the mechanical behavior of different fiber-reinforced concrete incorporating different SCM, robust studies and tests are needed. But concrete production has environmental consequences because it releases considerable quantities of carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere, which causes climate change24,30,31. These limitations justify the necessity of more adaptable and robust test techniques in sustainable concrete systems.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) algorithms have been used to solve these limitations by applying them to predictive modeling of structural systems32–37. Along with ML modeling, numerical simulation also has a significant application to reduce the time-consuming laboratory trials38–40. Pal et al.41 revealed that K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) forecasted the tensile strength of fiber-reinforced rubberized recycled aggregate concrete (FR3C) with an R2 of 0.944, and it was shown that various synthetic fiber content, W/C ratio, and rubber content played a critical role in determining the mechanical properties. Manan et al.42 developed an AI-based model to predict axial load-deflection behavior of recycled concrete powder and steel fiber reinforced columns with high accuracy (R2 = 0.95), using 196 datasets. Ray et al.43 evaluated the effectiveness of Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) to predict the compressive and splitting tensile strength of ceramic waste-based and nylon fiber-based concrete on a dataset of 162 records, and GBM (R2 = 0.981) achieved higher results than SVM. Also, considerable research has been conducted on developing supervised ML models to predict the individual effect of either SCM with synthetic fiber, including marble dust (MD) with glass fiber (GF)44, Volcanic Pumice Powder (VPP) with Nylon Fiber (NyF)45, ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) with glass fiber (GF)46, hybrid fiber such as steel with polyvinyl alcohol fiber (PVAF)47, and silica fume (SF) with polypropylene fiber (PPF)48, on mechanical properties. Few studies have been done about ML applications on natural fibers and SCM49–51. For instance, Kashyap et al.52 employed several ML models, such as Random Forest (RF), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), and SVM, to predict the strength of coconut fiber-reinforced concrete, where the RF model (R2 = 0.9841) outperformed all models. A study by Abdrahman et al.53 employed Gaussian Progress (GP), Linear Regression (LR), ANN, and SVM to predict the strength of sisal fiber-reinforced concrete composites, with ANN showing higher accuracy than the other models. Cakiroglu et al.54 developed predictive models, including Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) for tensile strength of natural-fiber-reinforced recycled aggregate concrete, XGB achieving the highest R2 = 0.95. Table 1 shows various AI techniques applied to predict the properties of SCM and fiber-reinforced concrete, showing that most studies relied on single ML models. Although previous research has evaluated the effect of fiber and SCM materials in concrete and the use of machine learning in predicting material performance, there remains a limitation in understanding the combined impacts of natural fibers and SCM materials on structural components. Additionally, the use of machine learning to optimize their mix design remains challenging due to the non-linear nature of the dataset and the need for large datasets.

To address these limitations, the current study proposes six supervised ML-based models to predict the compressive strengths of natural fiber-reinforced concrete (NFRC) incorporating supplementary cementitious materials (SCM), as well as investigating their performance through comparative evaluation. Here, jute, bamboo, and coir fibers are utilized as natural fibers, and different SCMs of similar properties are considered for the natural fiber-reinforced concrete (NFRC). The proposed study enhances the efficiency and sustainability of concrete research and development by reducing the necessity for trial-and-error mix designs. In this research, a significantly large dataset of 444 data points acquired from previous literature is utilized, and seven key input parameters are employed. Furthermore, the incorporation of SHAP (SHapley Additive explanations) and Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) facilitated an extensive examination of parameter impact, providing critical insights for optimizing mixture ratios. These insights can inform the design of concrete mixes that attain the desired compressive strength with fewer experimental trials.

Research methodology

Machine learning algorithms

In this investigation, six models (RF, GB, XGB, LGBM, DT, and KNN) were employed due to their ability to handle complex nonlinear data interactions effectively while providing a strong balance among interpretability and prediction accuracy. These models offer high efficiency and robustness for tabular data with limited samples and serve as a clear baseline to demonstrate the impact of variables. In contrast, hybrid machine learning approaches require many computational resources and a robust dataset, which are not always feasible in practical field conditions.

Random forest (RF) regressor

In the world of tree-based ensemble models, Random Forest is a widely favored method that’s frequently applied in many data analysis and machine learning tasks. The Random Forest algorithm works by using a collection of decision trees that have been trained using the bagging approach55. The model trains several randomly created decision trees at the same time, allowing for parallel processing. The technique provides configuration options to fine-tune the design, scale, and randomness level of every individual tree. RF improves the accuracy of the model because it chooses a few of the features in each tree, and aids in preventing overfitting. A good feature is selected at each node of the decision tree in such a manner that the data is separated into the left and right descendant subtrees. This is critical in the enhancement of the generalization ability of the model.

The training process of RF happens in two main stages: first, the decision trees are built, and then their predictions are combined. Bootstrap sampling is used to generate random, same-sized subsets of the data for training each tree individually. The next stage involves combining the outputs of the individual trees using bagging. Gradient boosting (GB) regressor

Gradient Boosting is a frequent type of forward-stage ensemble learning, frequently applied in machine learning to both classification and regression problems57. This method builds a powerful model by integrating multiple decision trees, known as GB regression trees. Unlike random trees, GB regression trees are not generated randomly; instead, an alternative approach they adopt is strict dictionary pruning. Similar to AdaBoost, the GB model adds predictors to the aggregate sequentially, with each successive predictor trying to resolve the mistakes of the preceding predictors. However, unlike AdaBoost, GB learns from the residual errors left by the prior predictors, refining its model based on these mistakes. The method of the GB model has been found to combine several weak learners (usually regression trees) into one strong model. It trains step by step, each time focusing on the errors made before, so predictions keep getting better.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGB) model

The XGBoost algorithm is an optimized version of the Gradient Boosting (GB) algorithm58. As a supervised machine learning method, XGBoost has attracted considerable interest and widespread use in recent times due to its high accuracy, efficiency, and scalability.

XGB is built on the boosting framework, which works by consecutively adding weak learners to create a stronger overall model55. Through repeated iterations, XGB refines the model by learning from the errors of previous weak learners, ultimately producing a highly accurate prediction model (shown in Fig. 1). The XGBoost employs the second-order Taylor series of the loss function in order to achieve accuracy improvement in prediction, including the first and second derivatives in every iteration. The method shall be figuring out the negative gradient of the loss function, and updating the model sequentially to fix the model and reach the desired accuracy.

Light gradient boosting machine (LGBM) model

LGBM is a strong machine learning algorithm, which is an integration of decision trees and gradient boosting towards improved outcome of model prediction60,61. However, due to its high accuracy as well as capacity to scale with datasets of larger size via a histogram-based data discretization approach to continuous data, LGBM is particularly applicable in regression tasks. A key advantage of the LGBM algorithm is its rapidity in training and prediction. The technique is distinguished by its rapid training, elevated prediction accuracy, little memory use, and capacity for parallel processing, rendering it optimal for extensive datasets60,61. LGBM employs a leaf-wise growth strategy, where the data is split at the leaf level, focusing on the most significant data points, shown in Fig. 2. This allows the algorithm to construct trees more efficiently, improving model performance with each iteration. The model’s final predictions are determined by taking a weighted average of the outputs from each decision tree, boosting its overall performance and reliability.

Decision tree (DT) regressor

A Decision Tree (DT) is a hierarchical model that works by applying a series of decision rules to split input variables into increasingly uniform groups systematically62. Its primary objective is to identify a group of rules that can easily predict an outcome based on specific input data. Depending on the nature of the target variable, DTs are classified as either regression trees (for continuous outputs) or classification trees (for categorical outputs).

By deploying DTs as predictive and classificatory machine learning methods, many authors have been able to use DTs in a variety of real-world situations. One essential benefit of DT models is that they have an excellent ability to extract and model interactions among input variables. DTs have several advantages over the others, since they can process both quantitative and qualitative ordinal data without assuming a strong distribution of the data. In addition, DT models are easy to train and easy to interpret, which makes them attractive for applications requiring model interpretability.

K-nearest neighbors (KNN) model

The K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm is generally a model of low efficiency in machine learning, although it retains all the points it views and afterwards classifies and estimates another point in such a way that the new point is the nearest to the training point63. The main purpose of this model is to predict a numerical result by finding the mean of the values of the k-nearest neighbors to the observed value. To compute nearest neighbors, KNN uses an inverse distance weighted average, where closer neighbors are weighted most.

SHapley additive explanations (SHAP)

This model is an explanation method grounded in game theory, designed to evaluate predictions made by machine learning algorithms. It employs Shapley values, which are rooted in mathematical theory and originally developed for cooperative games, to determine the contribution of each participant (in this case, the input features) to the outcome, or “payout”, which is the model’s prediction60,64. The fundamental goal of SHAP is to evaluate the influence of each input variable on the model’s predicted outcome by observing how predictions change depending on the inclusion or removal of a feature from the model’s input set. This approach assigns a specific value to each feature, indicating its impact on the prediction. However, SHAP represents the model explanation as a combination of feature contributions, allowing for a transparent view of how each input affects the model’s behavior59,65–67.

Partial dependence plot (PDP) analysis

It is a convenient instrument to visualize the interrelation of a series of input variables to the machine learning model output prediction. It may be helpful to demonstrate the variations of the model’s predictions as input parameters are varied (a more intuitive knowledge of how input parameters interact with the model)68. The PDPs are especially useful since they also reveal the average influence of each parameter on the predictions, which is needed to interpret complex models. PDPs are an interpretable method in a global scope, which reveals a global scenario of how each feature affects the predictions of the model58. With these plots, we can look at how the output depends on one of the features when all other features are fixed. For example, one-dimensional PDPs (PDPs-1D) can be used to evaluate the impact of a single feature on the predicted results69,70. This makes them especially useful for investigating straightforward, direct relationships between a feature and the model’s predicted value. In practice, PDPs can reveal non-linear relationships between predictors, interactions, or threshold effects that would otherwise remain hidden.

Performance evaluation indicators

Four common statistical indicators were carefully observed in the three phases of training, validation, and testing to measure how the prediction model performed. The mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), root mean square error (RMSE), the mean absolute error (MAE), and the determination coefficient (R2) were the measurements to check the performance of the prediction models. Based on these different measures, the accuracy and living value of the model could be well measured in a variety of aspects. The RMSE was specifically used to estimate the mean error of the observed and the predicted values of the observations. Another measure, which was used on the other hand, is the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) to measure percentage errors of the prediction, and to inform about the overall accuracy of the model in relation to the actual values. Cumulatively, such measures offered a good baseline in order to determine the effectiveness of the model at various stages of testing and validation71–74. Results and discussion

Analytical overview of dataset attributes

In the context of the present investigation, a comprehensive dataset of about 444 data points was obtained from prior research papers. The dataset emphasizes concrete mix compositions and comprises critical input parameters, including water, natural fiber, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), sand, coarse aggregate, and curing period. The data sample for the concrete includes 444 samples of mix proportions, with 8 data attributes, among which compressive strength is regarded as the response variable. Additionally, an overview of the input and output datasets and their statistics can be found in Table 3. Each parameter’s statistical data is outlined in Table 3, accompanied by its minimum and maximum values, kurtosis, skewness, and standard deviation (std). Most values accumulate around the mean in cases of low standard deviation, such as for variables like Natural Fiber, SCM, and Compressive strength. However, the values get more dispersed as the std for Cement, Sand, and Coarse aggregate rises. Skewness, which can be zero, positive, negative, or undefined, assesses the imbalance in the probability distribution of a variable about the average value75. Positive skewness values suggest data density in Fig. 4e for Compressive strength and SCM. When skewness numbers are negative, it means that there is more data on the right side of Fig. 4a. Kurtosis measures the sharpness or flatness of a data distribution relative to the normal distribution. It helps assess whether the data has more or fewer extreme values, with typical values falling between − 10 and + 1076. Table 3 reports a higher kurtosis value for Curing Period (days), due to a small sample size. The presence of skewness and kurtosis in the dataset indicates non-normality in several input variables. This non-normality affects performance metrics (R², RMSE, MAPE, and MAE) and requires normalization techniques. However, since the data is non-normal, non-parametric models have been employed for this study, which can handle such data without relying on normality assumptions.

SHapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis

Data on SHAP values have been used to estimate the impact of input features on the model prediction, as shown in Fig. 10. The mean SHAP value denotes the average contribution of each feature in all data samples. Feature importance was evaluated using the LGBM model due to its strong performance in previous phases. Among all input variables, cement content emerged as the most influential factor, with an average SHAP value of 5.73, highlighting its dominant role in enhancing compressive strength (CS). This aligns with its known effect on strength gain through hydration and binding of other mix components. Water content and coarse aggregate followed in importance, with water playing a more critical role due to its influence on hydration, workability, and durability, while coarse aggregate enhances load distribution and reduces shrinkage. SCM ranked next, contributing to strength by improving the concrete’s microstructure and supporting secondary C-S-H formation. Fine aggregate also showed a notable impact by ensuring mix cohesion and density. Finally, fiber content and curing age, though lower in SHAP value, played distinct roles in refining the material’s strength development over time.

Experimental validation

Materials and experimentation

To further evaluate the impact of natural fiber on concrete’s mechanical properties, this study carried out an experimental program using coir fiber (CF). The CFs were collected from local coconut processing units located in the Khulna region, Bangladesh. After washing, the fibers were air-dried for 24 h. To enhance the interfacial bond between the hydrophilic coir fibers and the cement matrix, a chemical treatment was performed using a 5% NaOH solution. The fibers were soaked in the solution for 24 h, then rinsed with distilled water to neutralize excess alkali, and finally dried under ambient conditions. The treated fibers were then cut into 25 mm lengths before being added to the concrete mix. Furthermore, a total of 10 experimental mixes were designed with varying percentages of CF (0.25%, 0.50%, 0.75% and 1%) with constant 5% silica fume (SF) as SCM. Such behavior indicates the need to optimize the amount of natural fiber so that the benefits of both fiber and SCM are realized during concrete mix design.

Microstructural assessment

The microstructural evaluation of NFRC was carried out to better understand the role of natural fibers in modifying the inner matrix of the cementitious composite. Figure 17a and b show natural fibers under secondary electrons (SE) and backscattered electrons (BSE) imaging modes, respectively. The fibers exhibit elongated rough external surfaces, which are essential for mechanical interlocking within the cementitious matrix. High-magnification views in Fig. 17c and d reveal surface microgrooves and irregularities. These irregular surface characteristics, ensuring better adhesion and limiting fiber pull-out under stress conditions, contribute to the overall mechanical performance by enhancing bond strength and reducing the risk of failure underloading. Furthermore, SEM images of the control mix matrix in Fig. 17e and f show a relatively dense matrix with a visible interfacial transition zone (ITZ), where aggregate and cement paste are well integrated. In contrast, Fig. 17g and h show the fiber-concrete composite samples. The presence of bridging fibers spanning across microcracks demonstrates the fibers’ ability to arrest crack propagation and delay failure. Additionally, fiber pull-out marks and gaps around embedded fibers indicate partial debonding, which contributes to energy dissipation under loading. These microstructural features are consistent with the predictive modeling results, where fibers were found to be significant predictors of compressive strength, especially in terms of improving crack resistance and limiting stress propagation. The microcracks appear finer and more tortuous in the fiber-reinforced concrete mixes, which suggests that the inclusion of natural fibers contributes to enhanced crack resistance and improved post-cracking behavior. This observation correlates with the machine learning model’s prediction, where fiber content was found to improve strength up to an optimal point, aligning with experimental findings. Studies conducted by Sobuz, Aayaz82 using hybrid fiber combinations in concrete composites reported similar findings, where the incorporation of fibers within the matrix significantly enhanced crack-bridging capacity and improved the post-cracking toughness of the material, which is well captured by the predictive model in these studies.

Conclusions

Machine learning is transforming civil engineering with the potential to optimize and predict concrete properties and data-driven innovations of construction processes. Machine learning models were applied to the research in the prediction of the CS of concrete. This study aimed to contrast the predictive accuracy and effectiveness of six models that predict CS using basic input parameters. The following conclusions are drawn based on the results of our study on the prediction of compressive strength of NFRC mixes:

The regression analysis indicates that the LGBM model performs best and is the most reliable model among the other five machine learning models. Of them, LGBM obtained the largest R² score of 0.864 in the testing period and R2 score of 0.923 in the training stage. This suggests solid predictive accuracy and generalization capability in estimating strength and low overfitting of the dataset. The XGB, GB, RF, and DT outperformed in the training phase, with the R2 scores of 0.956, 0.949, 0.938, and 0.949, respectively, indicating low overfitting ability. However, their R2 scores decreased to 0.829, 0.833, 0.788, and 0.721 in the testing phase, indicating lower predictive accuracy and generalization capability.

The proposed technique provides a reliable and efficient method of predicting the compressive strength of NFRC using machine learning models. It can greatly limit the amount of time and expense involved in traditional lab testing. Moreover, the knowledge learned from this study may help in the mix design optimization and promote sustainable large-scale production of fiber-reinforced concrete in the construction industry.

Limitations and further studies

Certain limitations of this study can be addressed in future investigations. Due to the availability of materials and laboratory facilities, time and cost limitations, the experimental validation was done only for coir fiber, which limits the validation for jute and bamboo fibers. In this sense, there is yet a lack of generalizability of the results for all types of NFRCs. The data set was collated from a variety of literature sources, which introduced heterogeneity in mix proportions and testing conditions that may impact predictive performance. In addition, the scope was limited to compressive strength, without considering other mechanical and durability properties, and no field-scale validation was carried out. To address this issue, future investigation can be done by including a hybrid fiber system, durability and other mechanical properties, field-scale validation, and hybrid supervised AI techniques. This will make it possible to find out a more reliable predictive model for the infrastructure industry.
