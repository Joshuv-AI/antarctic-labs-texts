On the expressivity of sparse maxout networks
Moritz Grillo Max Planck Institute for Mathematics in the Sciences moritz.grillo@mis.mpg.de Tobias Hofmann TU Berlin tobias.hfm@icloud.com

Abstract
Abstract. We study the expressivity of sparse maxout networks, where each neuron takes a fixed number of inputs from the previous layer and employs a, possibly multi-argument, maxout activation. This setting captures key characteristics of convolutional or graph neural networks. We establish a duality between functions computable by such networks and a class of virtual polytopes, linking their geometry to questions of network expressivity. In particular, we derive a tight bound on the dimension of the associated polytopes, which serves as the central tool for our analysis. Building on this, we construct a sequence of depth hierarchies. While sufficiently deep sparse maxout networks are universal, we prove that if the required depth is not reached, width alone cannot compensate for the sparsity of a fixed indegree constraint. Keywords. Expressivity of neural networks, maxout networks, polyhedral geometry, virtual polytopes MSC 2020. 68T07, 52B05, 14T99

Introduction

Despite the impressive progress that has been made in the practical use of neural networks, our theoretical understanding of their inner mechanisms and why they generalize so well is still far from being complete.
Universal approximation theorems, such as those of
Cybenko 1989
,
Hornik et al. 1990
, or
Leshno et al. 1993
marked major steps towards explaining the expressivity of neural networks.
Yet, making precise what neural networks can or cannot do goes beyond approximation results and requires addressing questions of exact representability.
That is, given a specific neural network architecture, what is the exact class of functions that can be represented by that architecture?
Recent work, for example,
Telgarsky 2016
,
Arora et al. 2018
, or
Hertrich et al. 2021
, have provided new insights into how architectural parameters such as depth and width influence expressivity.

In contrast, this article investigates how network sparsity affects expressivity and how it interacts with architectural parameters such as depth and width.
Beyond that, we are interested in the effects of using multi-argument maxout activation functions, which can be seen as generalized rectified linear units, as introduced by
Goodfellow et al. 2013
.
We call a network sparse, or more specifically indegree-
d
-constrained, if each of its neurons depends on a fixed number
d
of outputs from the previous layer. This framework interpolates between fully connected architectures (
d
equal to the previous layer’s width) and extremely sparse ones (
d=2
).
A precise formalization of the architecture we are concerned with follows in Section
2
.
Our considerations are motivated by two aspects.

First, one might find it interesting that explicit attempts to construct neural networks of minimum depth, such as those by
Arora et al. 2018
or the recent improvement by
Bakaev et al. 2025
are nonetheless sparse.
In other words, when not restricting the width of the neural network, sparse networks can compute any continuous piecewise linear function with logarithmic depth.

Second, sparsity is a characteristic property of many practically relevant network types.
For example, a convolutional layer involving a typical
3\times 3
kernel is indegree-
9
-constrained.
Another example are graph neural networks, which, depending on the specific variant, often come with indegree constraints stemming from their localized feature update rules.
The preceding examples also provide relevant network types in which indegree-constrained operations are used together with pooling steps. In our model, this is reflected by incorporating multi-argument maxout activations.

Note also that the architecture we investigate allows for varying indegree constraints across layers.
The only exception is the first and last layer, where indegree constraints are not imposed.
Thus, our model interpolates between fully connected networks on those in which each neuron depends on only two inputs from the previous layer, apart from the first and last one.
Not enforcing indegree constraints in the first and last layer has the following reasons.

From a practical perspective, even networks that otherwise incorporate sparse operations, such as convolutional or graph neural networks often bundle information via fully connected layers into one or few neurons in the final layer.
See
Krizhevsky et al. 2012
for a prominent example.
Allowing the first layer to be fully connected can be seen as allowing a general feature transformation or preprocessing step, for instance, in which an image is patched or graph nodes are embedded into a suitable feature space.

Another reason is that our theoretical interest lies more on structural effects of sparsity across the layers of a network, rather than on the information-theoretic limitations imposed by a restricted receptive field in the first layer.
For example, given an input with
n
pixels and a network in which each neuron, including those in the first layer, depend on at most two inputs from the preceding layer, it is clear that a network with depth smaller than
\log_{2}(n)
is insufficient to capture every aspect of the input.
But this says little about structural effects within the network, but merely reflects the restrictive reception in the beginning of information processing chain.

Our Contributions.

Using a duality between positively homogeneous convex continuous piecewise linear functions and polytopes, we establish an isomorphism between functions computed by sparse maxout networks and a class of
virtual polytopes
(
Theorem

7
), which are formal differences of polytopes.
We then study the dimensions of these virtual polytopes in
Section

4
, where
Theorem

11
provides an upper bound and
Theorem

12
shows that this bound is attained.
These results form the foundation of our analysis of how sparsity affects expressivity in
Section

5
.

While our general framework, introduced in Section
2
, allows for varying indegree and rank constraints across layers, and thus involves vector-valued parameters, the following outline of our main results suppresses this notation for simplicity. For any indegree constraint
d\in\mathbb{N}
, number of arguments
r\in\mathbb{N}
in the maxout activation, and input dimension
n
, let
\mathcal{N}_{n}({d,r,\ell})
denote the class of functions representable by such networks with
\ell
hidden layers. Our main results are as follows:

•

For any
d,r
, and
\ell
, there exist an input dimension
n
and a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
computable by a fully connected network with two hidden layers and width
O(n)
that is not contained in
\mathcal{N}_{n}({d,r,\ell})
(
Theorem

18
).
This, in particular, implies that sparse shallow networks cannot represent all continuous piecewise linear functions, regardless of their width.

•

For
d=r=2
, we fully characterize the class of functions
\mathcal{N}_{n}({2,2,\ell})
(
Theorem

19
), showing a sharp separation for each
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,\lceil\log_{2}(n+1)\rceil\}
.

In words, when allowing arbitrary input dimension, for any fixed depth and indegree constraint, the resulting sparsity cannot be compensated by increasing width.
This, in particular, also implies that for a fixed indegree, there is no depth that fully compensates for sparsity.
Overall, our results demonstrate that sparsity has a decisive impact on the expressivity of neural networks.

Theorem 1

(

)
.

Every function

can be written as

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}\max\{a_{i,1}^{\top}x,\ldots,a_{i,n+1}^{\top}x\}

Building on this representation,

The following result is a refinement of their result, implying that deep indegree-constrained networks are as expressive as networks that are not indegree-constrained.

Theorem 2

(C.f.
Arora et al. 2018
)
.

For given
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}

\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{r}})=\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})=\mathcal{C}_{n}.

By definition, every function in

The inner linear functions

Finally, the

3
Virtual polytopes and support functions

The goal of this section is to establish a duality between specific virtual polytopes and the functions computable by related sparse maxout networks. For on overview of virtual polytopes, we refer to
Panina and Streinu 2015
.

The Minkowski sum of two sets
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
X+Y=\{x+y:x\in X,y\in Y\}
.
By
\spn(X)
we denote the smallest linear subspace containing
X
, by
\aff(X)
we denote the smallest affine subspace containing
X
, and by
\conv(X)
we denote the convex hull of
X
.
A
polytope

P
is the convex hull of finitely many points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the set of all such polytopes.
The
dimension
of a polytope
P
is the dimension of its affine hull
\dim(\aff(P))
.
Note that
P+Q+(-Q)\neq P
for
-Q\coloneqq\{-q:q\in Q\}
and hence naive subtraction is not the inverse of Minkowski addition.
But there is a canonical way to introduce a formal Minkowski difference.
The set of polytopes together with Minkowski addition forms a commutative semigroup
S
, having the polytope that consists only of the zero vector as neutral element.
For any polytopes
P,Q,R
in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, there holds the cancellation law, which says that if
P+Q=R+Q
, then
P=R
.
This allows to construct the Grothendieck group of
S
by
\mathcal{V}_{n}\coloneqq\{(P,Q):P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}/\sim
where the equivalence relation
\sim
is defined by

(P_{1},Q_{1})\sim(P_{2},Q_{2})\iff P_{1}+Q_{2}=P_{2}+Q_{1}.

The equivalence class represented by
(P,Q)
is denoted by
P-Q
and called
virtual polytope
.
Furthermore, we denote
\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{V}_{n}
.
As a virtual polytope is not a point set, further definitions have to be adjusted appropriately.
For virtual polytopes
P_{1}-Q_{1}
and
P_{2}-Q_{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, one defines the operations

\displaystyle\lambda(P_{1}-Q_{1})

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\lambda P_{1}-\lambda Q_{1}),

\displaystyle(P_{1}-Q_{1})+(P_{2}-Q_{2})

\displaystyle\coloneqq(P_{1}+P_{2})-(Q_{1}+Q_{2}),

\displaystyle\conv(P_{1}-Q_{1},P_{2}-Q_{2})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\conv(P_{1}+Q_{2},P_{2}+Q_{1})-(Q_{1}+Q_{2}).

For more details about virtual polytopes, we refer to the survey of
Panina and Streinu 2015
.
The
support function

f_{P}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of a polytope
P=\conv\{a_{j}:j\in[p]\}
is given by

f_{P}(x)\coloneqq\max\{a_{j}^{\top}x:j\in[p]\}.

Note that support functions depend only on the extreme points of a given polytope.
So they are independent of a specific representation.
More precisely, if
\conv\{a_{j}:j\in[p]\}
and
\conv\{b_{k}:k\in[q]\}
describe the same polytope, then the corresponding support functions are identical.
In tropical geometry, functions of the form
\max\{a_{j}^{\top}x:j\in[p]\}
are also called
tropical polynomial
.
As for classical polynomials, one defines the
Newton polytope

P(f)
of
f(x)=\max\{a_{j}^{\top}x:j\in[p]\}\in\mathcal{C}^{+}_{n}
as

P(f)\coloneqq\conv\{a_{j}:j\in[p]\}.

The following theorem shows a duality between the functions in
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{n}
and their Newton polytopes.

Theorem 3

(
Schneider 2013
)
.

The map
\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\mathcal{C}^{+}_{n},P\mapsto f_{P}
is a bijection and for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\lambda\geq 0
, it holds that

(i)

f_{P+Q}=f_{P}+f_{Q}
,

(ii)

f_{\lambda P}=\lambda f_{P}
, and

(iii)

f_{\conv(P\cup Q)}=\max\{f_{P},f_{Q}\}
.

For our investigation it is essential to extend the preceding relationships to virtual polytopes and corresponding functions in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
Given the support functions
f_{P}
and
f_{Q}
of two polytopes
P
and
Q
, the
support function of the virtual polytope

P-Q
is defined as
f_{P-Q}\coloneqq f_{P}-f_{Q}
.
We also build on the following well-known fact.

Lemma 4

(
Melzer 1986
)
.

Every positively homogeneous continuous piecewise linear function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be written as
f=g-h
where
g
and
h
are convex positively homogeneous continuous piecewise linear functions.

The following Lemma is a direct application of
Theorem

3
and
Lemma

4
and the fact that virtual polytopes are constructed as the Grothdendieck group of polyoptes
(
Panina and Streinu 2015
)
.

Lemma 5
.

The map
\mathcal{V}_{n}\to\mathcal{C}_{n},P-Q\mapsto f_{P-Q}
is a bijection and satisfies

(i)

f_{(P-Q)+(R-S)}=f_{P-Q}+f_{R-S}
,

(ii)

f_{\lambda(P-Q)}=\lambda f_{P-Q}
,

(iii)

f_{\conv(P\cup Q)}=\max\{f_{P},f_{Q}\}
.

Proof.

To check that the given map is well-defined, note first that support functions are positively homogeneous and continuous piecewise linear.
So
f_{P-Q}=f_{P}-f_{Q}
is also positively homogeneous and continuous piecewise linear, for any polytopes
P
and
Q
.
We yet have to show that
f_{P-Q}
is independent of the representation of
P-Q
.
Given equivalent virtual polytopes
P-Q
and
R-S
, we obtain
P+S=R+Q
and hence
f_{P-Q}(x)=f_{P}(x)+f_{S}(x)=f_{R}(x)+f_{Q}(x)=f_{R-S}(x)
.

To see that the map
P-Q\mapsto f_{P}(x)-f_{Q}(x)
is injective, let
f_{P-Q}(x)=f_{R-S}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Rearranging gives
f_{P}(x)+f_{S}(x)=f_{R}(x)+f_{Q}(x)
.
Since the support function uniquely determines a polytope, it follows that
P+S=R+Q
.
Thus, the virtual polytopes
P-Q
and
R-S
are the same, as is to be shown.

To verify that the map
P-Q\mapsto f_{P}(x)-f_{Q}(x)
is surjective, consider an arbitrary positively homogeneous continuous piecewise linear function
f(x)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By Lemma
4
, we can assume that
f
is of the form
\max\{a_{j}^{\top}x:j\in[p]\}-\max\{b_{k}^{\top}x:k\in[q]\}
, for finitely many points
a_{j}
,
j\in[p]
, and
b_{k}
,
k\in[q]
.
Defining the polytopes
P=\conv\{a_{j}:j\in[p]\}
and
Q=\conv\{b_{k}:k\in[q]\}
, we obtain
f(x)=f_{P}(x)-f_{Q}(x)
and hence every positively homogeneous continuous piecewise linear function is the image of the map, establishing surjectivity.

To verify Relation
(i)
, consider two arbitrary virtual polytopes
P-Q
and
R-S
and check that

\displaystyle f_{(P-Q)+(R-S)}

\displaystyle=f_{(P+R)-(Q+S)}=f_{P+R}-f_{Q+S}=(f_{P}+f_{R})-(f_{Q}+f_{S})

\displaystyle=(f_{P}-f_{Q})+(f_{R}-f_{S})=f_{P-Q}+f_{R-S}.

For Relation
(ii)
, take an any virtual polytope
P-Q
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
There holds

\displaystyle f_{\lambda(P-Q)}

\displaystyle=f_{\lambda P-\lambda Q}=f_{\lambda P}-f_{\lambda Q}=\lambda f_{P}-\lambda f_{Q}=\lambda(f_{P}-f_{Q})=\lambda f_{P-Q}.

For
(iii)
, consider arbitrary virtual polytopes
P-Q
and
R-S
. We find that

\displaystyle f_{\conv(P-Q,\mkern 1.0muR-S)}

\displaystyle=f_{\conv((P+S)\cup(R+Q))-(Q+S)}

\displaystyle=f_{\conv((P+S)\cup(R+Q))}-f_{Q+S}

\displaystyle=\max\{f_{P+S},f_{R+Q}\}-f_{Q+S}

\displaystyle=\max\{f_{P}+f_{S},f_{R}+f_{Q}\}-(f_{Q}+f_{S})

\displaystyle=\max\{f_{P}+f_{S}-(f_{Q}+f_{S}),f_{R}+f_{Q}-(f_{Q}+f_{S})\}

\displaystyle=\max\{f_{P}-f_{Q},f_{R}-f_{S}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{f_{P-Q},f_{R-S}\}.\qed

Having established a general duality between virtual polytopes and corresponding functions in
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, our next goal is to focus on those virtual polytopes which correspond to the functions computable by sparse maxout networks.

Definition 6
.

Let be given the parameters
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1}=n,d_{2},\ldots,d_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, and
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
.
We define recursively

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({0,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\{v\}:v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\{\conv\left\{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{ij}V_{j}:i\in[r_{\ell}]\right\}\;:\penalty\ \alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\penalty\ V_{j}\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\big\}.

Furthermore, let us denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\big\{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}V_{i}\penalty\ :\penalty\ k\in\mathbb{N},\penalty\ \alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\penalty\ V_{i}\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\big\},

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{V}}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\text{, and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}}).

In
Hertrich et al. 2021
, it is shown that the above recursive definition of virtual polytopes precisely corresponds to the Newton polytopes of the function computed by networks with
\ell
layers. The following theorem, carrying this to the indegree-constrained setting, is an immediate consequence

Theorem 7
.

The map
V\mapsto f_{V}
is a bijection between
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
and between
\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.

Proof.

Note that
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{n}
.
Likewise,
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{n}
.
Since Lemma
5
says that
V\mapsto f_{V}
is a bijection between
\mathcal{V}_{n}
and
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we certainly know that its restrictions to
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
or
\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
are injective.
It remains to be shown that the image of
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
is
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
From there, Lemma
5
, Part
(i)
, gives directly that
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
is the image of
\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
. The point is that the functions in
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
are exactly the linear combinations of those in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, just as the virtual polytopes in
\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
are precisely the linear combinations of those in
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.

Let us begin with the case where
\ell=0
.
By definition,
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({0,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})=\{\{v\}:v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. The corresponding support functions are the linear functions from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}
, which are precisely the functions in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({0,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.

We proceed inductively, assuming that the image of
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}}))
is
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
Any
V\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, by Definition
6
, is of the form

V=\conv\left\{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{ij}V_{j}:i\in[r_{\ell}]\right\}

for certain
\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
and
V_{j}\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
By Lemma
5
, Parts
(i)
to
(iii)
,

f_{V}(x)=\max\limits_{i\in[r_{\ell}]}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{ij}f_{V_{j}}(x).

This is precisely what is computed at a neuron of a rank-
r_{\ell}
-maxout layer that is indegree-
d_{\ell}
-constrained.
Since, by our induction hypothesis, each
f_{V_{j}}
is realized by a network from
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, it follows that
f_{V}
is in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
Conversely, every function in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
is of the same form as
f_{V}
.
With the
f_{V_{j}}
lying in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, we know by induction that the corresponding
V_{j}
belong to
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
These serve to construct
V=\conv\left\{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{ij}V_{j}:i\in[r_{\ell}]\right\}
, which shows that every function in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
is indeed the image of a suitable virtual polytope in
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
∎

Note that the relations
(i)
to
(iii)
of Lemma
5
remain valid when restricting the map
V\mapsto f_{V}
.
In particular, this map is not only a bijection between
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
and
\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, as stated in Theorem
7
, but also a vector space isomorphism and a semigroup isomorphism, the latter with respect to building the convex hull and taking the maximum.

4
The dimension of polytopes arising from sparse networks

Having discussed which virtual polytopes are dual to functions computable by networks in
\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, we now aim to bound the dimension of these polytopes.
Eventually, this will link the geometry of the polytopes to the expressivity of corresponding networks.

Definition 8
.

The
dimension
of a virtual polytope
V\in\mathcal{V}
is defined by

\dim(V)=\min\{\dim(P+Q)\mid V=P-Q\}.

The proof of this section’s main result, Theorem
11
, builds on the following two lemmas.

Lemma 9
.

For subspaces
U_{1},\ldots,U_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there holds

\aff\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+U_{i})\big)=x_{1}+\spn\big\{x_{2}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}-x_{1}\big\}+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\spn(U_{i}).

Proof.

By the definition of the affine hull,

\aff\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+U_{i})\big)=x_{1}+\spn\big\{s-x_{1}:s\in\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+U_{i})\big\}.

If
s\in x_{1}+U_{1}
, then
s=x_{1}+u_{1}
for any
u_{1}\in U_{1}
and
s-x_{1}=u_{1}
.
For
i\in\{2,\ldots,k\}
, if
s\in x_{i}+U_{i}
, then
s=x_{i}+u_{i}
for some
u_{i}\in U_{i}
and

s-x_{1}=(x_{i}-x_{1})+u_{i}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\{s-x_{1}:s\in\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+U_{i})\}

\displaystyle=U_{1}\cup\textstyle\bigcup_{i=2}^{k}\{(x_{i}-x_{1})+u_{i}:u_{i}\in U_{i}\},

which implies our statement, because

\displaystyle\aff\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(x_{i}+U_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\spn\bigl(U_{1}\cup\textstyle\bigcup_{i=2}^{k}\{(x_{i}-x_{1})+u_{i}:u_{i}\in U_{i}\}\big)

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\spn\bigl(U_{1}\cup\big\{x_{i}-x_{1}:i\in\{2,\ldots,k\}\big\}+\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{k}U_{i}\bigr)

\displaystyle=x_{1}+\spn\big\{x_{i}-x_{1}:i\in\{2,\ldots,k\}\big\}+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}.\qed

Lemma 10
.

For integers
d,r\in\mathbb{N}
and virtual polytopes
V_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, there holds

\dim\big(\conv\big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{1i}V_{i},\ldots,\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ri}V_{i}\big)\big)\leq\big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\dim V_{i}\big)+r-1,

for any coefficients
\alpha_{1i},\ldots,\alpha_{ri}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

For
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, let
V_{i}=P_{i}-Q_{i}
be represented by
P_{i}\subseteq x_{i}+U_{i}
and
Q_{i}\subseteq y_{i}+W_{i}
such that
\dim(V_{i})=\dim(P_{i}+Q_{i})=\dim(U_{i}+W_{i})
.
For
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we use the shorthands

\displaystyle P^{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ji}P_{i},

\displaystyle Q^{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ji}Q_{i},

\displaystyle x^{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ji}x_{i},

\displaystyle y^{j}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ji}y_{i},

\displaystyle z^{j}\coloneqq x^{j}+\textstyle\sum_{k=1,k\neq j}^{r}y^{k}

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i},

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}W_{i}.

and observe that

P^{j}\subseteq x^{j}+U\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{j}\subseteq y^{j}+W.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}\dim\big(\conv\big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{1i}V_{i},\ldots,\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ri}V_{i}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\dim\big(\conv\big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{1i}(P_{i}-Q_{i}),\ldots,\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{ri}(P_{i}-Q_{i})\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\dim\big(\conv\big(P^{1}-Q^{1},\ldots,P^{r}-Q^{r}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\dim\big(\conv\big(P^{1}+\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{r}Q^{j}\cup\ldots\cup P^{r}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}Q^{j}\big)-\big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}Q^{j}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\dim\big(\conv\big(x^{1}+\textstyle\sum_{j=2}^{r}y^{j}+U+W\cup\ldots\cup x^{r}+\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}y^{j}+U+W\big)\hskip-0.56905pt+\hskip-0.56905pt\big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}y^{j}+W\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\dim\big(\aff\big(z^{1}+U+W\cup\ldots\cup z^{r}+U+W\big)\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(\hskip-0.7113pt*\hskip-0.7113pt)}{=}\dim\big(z^{1}+\spn\{z^{2}-z^{1},\ldots,z^{r}-z^{1}\}+U+W\big)

\displaystyle\leq\dim(U+W)+r-1=\dim(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i}+\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}W_{i})+r-1

\displaystyle\leq\big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\dim(U_{i}+W_{i})\big)+r-1=\big(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\dim(V_{i})\big)+r-1,

using Lemma
9
at
(\hskip-0.7113pt*\hskip-0.7113pt)
.
∎

Theorem 11
.

For parameters
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{\ell})
, and
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, any virtual polytope
V\in\check{\mathcal{V}}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
satisfies

\dim(V)\leq\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}.

Proof.

For
\ell=0
, both sides of the inequality are zero.
We proceed by induction on
\ell\geq 1
, assuming our claim to be true for virtual polytopes in
\check{\mathcal{V}}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
Now, consider an arbitrary virtual polytope
V\in\check{\mathcal{V}}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
It can be expressed as

V=\conv\big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{1j}V_{j},\ldots,\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{r_{\ell}j}V_{j}\big),

for suitable virtual polytopes
V_{j}\in\check{\mathcal{V}}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
and coefficients
\alpha_{1j},\ldots,\alpha_{r_{\ell}j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Invoking Lemma
10
, there holds

\displaystyle\dim(V)

\displaystyle=\dim\big(\conv\big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{1j}V_{j},\ldots,\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\alpha_{r_{\ell}j}V_{j}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\dim V_{j}\big)+r_{\ell}-1,

\displaystyle\leq\big(\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\ell}}\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell-1}d_{i}\big)+r_{\ell}-1,

\displaystyle=\big(d_{\ell}\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-1}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell-1}d_{i}\big)+r_{\ell}-1,

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}.\qed

The given dimension bound is tight, as is shown next.

Theorem 12
.

Given parameters
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{\ell})
, and
\boldsymbol{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
with
d_{i}\geq r_{i}
for
i\in[\ell]
, if
n\geq\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}
, then there exists a polytope
P\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
such that

\dim(P)=\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}.

Proof.

Denoting by
\{e_{i}\}_{i}^{n}
the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we prove the following statement by induction on
\ell
.

Claim.

Let
n\geq\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}\eqqcolon m_{\ell}
. Then, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
I\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=m_{\ell}
there is a polytope
P\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
such that
\aff(P)=v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\}
.

For
\ell=0
, there is
m_{\ell}=0
and thus
I=\emptyset
. In this case, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can just take
P=\{v\}\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({0,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
whose affine hull is
v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\}=\{v\}
.

For
\ell\geq 1
, we have that
m_{\ell}=d_{\ell}\mkern 1.0mum_{\ell-1}+(r_{\ell}-1)
.
Let us take some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m_{\ell}}\}\subseteq[n]
be an arbitrary index set of cardinality
m_{\ell}
.
We split

I=I_{1}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}I_{2}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\ldots\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}I_{d_{\ell}}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}J

such that
|I_{k}|=m_{\ell-1}
for
k\in[d_{\ell}]
,
|J|=r_{\ell}-1
, and denote
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r_{\ell}-1}\}
.
By induction, for each
k\in[d_{\ell}]
, there exists a polytope

P_{k}\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell-1,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\quad\text{such that}\quad\aff(P_{k})=t_{k}+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I_{k}\},

where the reference points
t_{k}
are free to be chosen.
Let us pick

t_{r_{\ell}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{r_{\ell}}\bigg(v-\sum\limits_{s=1}^{r_{\ell}-1}e_{j_{s}}\bigg)\quad\text{and}\quad t_{i}\coloneqq t_{r_{\ell}}+e_{j_{i}}\quad\text{for }i\in[r_{\ell}-1]

and set
t_{k}\coloneqq 0
for
k>r_{\ell}
.
Note that the points are chosen such that

\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}}t_{k}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_{\ell}-1}(t_{r_{\ell}}+e_{j_{k}})+t_{r_{\ell}}=r_{\ell}t_{r_{\ell}}+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_{\ell}-1}e_{j_{k}}=v.

Furthermore, for
i\in[r_{\ell}-1]
, we define the polytopes

Q_{i}\coloneqq P_{i}+\!\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1,\;k\neq r_{\ell}\end{subarray}}^{d_{\ell}}\!\!\!\!\!\!P_{k},\quad Q_{r_{\ell}}\coloneqq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}}P_{k},\quad\text{and}\quad P\coloneqq\conv\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}Q_{i}\right).

By construction,
P\in\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
It remains to be shown that the affine hull of
P
is
\aff(P)=v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\}
.
We compute the affine hulls of the
Q_{i}
first.
For
i\in[r_{\ell}-1]
, we have

\displaystyle\aff(Q_{i})

\displaystyle=\aff\big(P_{i}+\textstyle\sum_{k=1,\,k\neq r_{\ell}}^{d_{\ell}}P_{k}\big)

\displaystyle=t_{i}-t_{r_{\ell}}+\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}}t_{k}+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\setminus(J\cup I_{r_{\ell}})\}\big\}

\displaystyle=e_{j_{i}}+v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\setminus(J\cup I_{r_{\ell}})\}

and similarly,
\aff(Q_{r_{\ell}})=v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\setminus J\}
. The affine hull of
P
is

\displaystyle\aff(P)

\displaystyle=\aff\big(\conv\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}Q_{i}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\aff\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}Q_{i}\big)

\displaystyle=\aff\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}}\aff(Q_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\aff\big(\textstyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{r_{\ell}-1}(e_{j_{i}}+v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\setminus(J\cup I_{r_{\ell}})\})

\displaystyle\hskip 36.98857pt\cup\aff(v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\setminus J\})\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(\hskip-0.7113pt*\hskip-0.7113pt)}{=}v+\spn\{e_{j_{1}},\ldots,e_{j_{r_{\ell}-1}}\}+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\setminus J\}

\displaystyle=v+\spn\{e_{i}:i\in I\},

where Lemma
9
is used at
(\hskip-0.7113pt*\hskip-0.7113pt)
.
This concludes our induction.
∎

5
Consequences for the expressivity of sparse networks

Knowing which dimensions can be realized by virtual polytopes from sparse networks, we now explore consequences for the expressivity of corresponding network classes.
The results developed to that point yield the following hierarchy.

Theorem 13
.

For parameters
\boldsymbol{d}\geq\boldsymbol{r}
, there holds

\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({0,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq\check{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}}),

where
\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
is the depth for which
n\geq m_{\ell^{\prime}}\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell^{\prime}}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell^{\prime}}d_{i}
, but
n<m_{\ell^{\prime}+1}
.

Proof.

The inclusions follow from the recursion in Definition
6
.
The depth
\ell^{\prime}
is the largest index for which the dimension bound, Theorem
11
, and its attainment, Theorem
12
, apply.
This ensures strictness up to that level.
∎

Corollary 14
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
13
, there holds

\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({0,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq\check{\mathcal{N}}_{n}({\ell^{\prime},\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}}).

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem
13
and the duality given by Theorem
7
.
∎

Our next goal is to show that every indegree constraint is a real constraint and cannot be compensated by width or depth. For this, we aim to show that
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(m_{\ell}+1)
for a suitable
m_{\ell}
that does not depend on the input dimension
n
. We then proceed by showing that for every input dimension
n
there is a function
f\in\mathcal{N}_{n}({2,\mathbf{2}})\setminus\mathcal{M}_{n}(n)
. Hence, for every
\ell,\mathbf{r},\mathbf{d}
, not all functions representable with a fully-connected network that has only two hidden layers, is in
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
For the first part, we build on the fact that every polytope admits a triangulation into simplices.
For example, this is discussed by
Lee and Santos 2017
. Lemma 9 in
Bakaev et al. 2025
implies the following.

Lemma 15
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a polytope of dimension
d
.
Then
P
is a linear combination of simplices of dimension at most
d
.

Theorem 16
.

Given parameters
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, if
m_{\ell}\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}
, then

\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(m_{\ell}+1).

Proof.

Let be given an arbitrary function
f\in\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
By Theorem
7
, its Newton polytope
V(f)
is in
\mathcal{V}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
.
The dimension bound, Theorem
Theorem

11
, implies that
V(f)
is a linear combination of virtual polytopes of dimension at most
m_{\ell}
.
Together with Lemma
Lemma

15
, this says that
V(f)
is a linear combination of simplices of dimension at most
m_{\ell}
.
In other words,
V(f)
decomposes into convex hulls of at most
m_{\ell}+1
affinely independent points.
By
Lemma

5
, it follows that
f\in M_{n}(m_{\ell}+1)
, proving the claim.
∎

Our argumentation relies on the following result by
Hertrich et al. 2021
.
Given a polyhedral complex
\mathcal{P}
, the set of all
k
-dimensional polyhedra in
\mathcal{P}
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}^{k}
.

Lemma 17
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex continuous piecewise linear function and let
\mathcal{P}
be its underlying polyhedral complex.
If there exists a hyperplane
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the set

T\coloneqq\!\!\bigcup\limits_{F\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}:F\subseteq H}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!F

is nonempty and contains no line, then
f
is not contained in
\mathcal{M}_{n}(n)
.

Theorem 18
.

There exists a function
f\in\mathcal{N}_{n}({2,\mathbf{2}})\setminus\mathcal{M}_{n}(n)
.
In particular, for
\ell\geq 2
,
\boldsymbol{d}\geq\mathbf{2}
,
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
, and
n\geq m_{\ell}+1
,
m_{\ell}\coloneqq\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(r_{k}-1)\textstyle\prod_{i=k+1}^{\ell}d_{i}
, there holds

\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subsetneq\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{r}}).

Proof.

By construction, the function

f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad f(x)=\max\bigg\{\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\max\{0,x_{j}\},\,\max\{x_{n-1},x_{n}\}\bigg\}

is contained in
\mathcal{N}_{n}({2,\mathbf{2}})
, which in turn is a subset of
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{r}})
for
\ell\geq 2
and
\boldsymbol{d}\geq\mathbf{2}
.

The inclusion
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{r}})
holds by definition.
It is strict if
n\geq m_{\ell}+1
and
f\notin\mathcal{M}_{n}(n)\supseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(m_{\ell}+1)\supseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{r}})
, where the latter inclusion is by Theorem
16
.

It remains to be shown that
f\notin\mathcal{M}_{n}(n)
.
We prove that by applying Lemma
17
to
f
and the hyperplane
H\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n-1}=x_{n}\}
.
Certainly,
f
is convex and continuous piecewise linear.
Also, we may rewrite it as

f(x)=\max\{S(x),\,x_{n-1},\,x_{n}\},\quad\text{where }S(x)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\max\{0,x_{j}\}.

Furthermore, denote by
\mathcal{P}
the polyhedral complex underlying
f
.
To use Lemma
17
, we have to verify that
T\coloneqq\textstyle\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}:F\subseteq H}F
is nonempty and contains no line.

First, we show that there is exactly one
F\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}
with
F\subseteq H
, the polyhedron
F^{\star}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n-1}=x_{n}\geq S(x)\}
.

To see what is in
\mathcal{P}^{n-1}
, let us list all elements of
\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
The maximal regions on which
S(x)
is affine are the sign chambers
\Pi_{J}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{j}\geq 0\text{ for }j\in J,\,x_{j}\leq 0\text{ for }j\notin J\}
,
J\subseteq[n-2]
, where
S(x)=\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}
.
So,
f
is affine on

\displaystyle C_{J}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:S(x)\geq x_{n-1},\,S(x)\geq x_{n}\}\cap\Pi_{J}\quad\text{for }J\subseteq[n-2],

\displaystyle C_{n-1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n-1}\geq x_{n},\,x_{n-1}\geq S(x)\},

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}\geq x_{n-1},\,x_{n}\geq S(x)\}.

The polyhedra in
\mathcal{P}^{n-1}
are intersections of two distinct polyhedra from
\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
We obtain

C_{n-1}\cap C_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n-1}=x_{n}\geq S(x)\}=F^{\star}.

So
F^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}^{n-1}
and certainly also
F^{\star}\subseteq H
, thus
F^{\star}\subseteq T
.

Any other intersection of polyhedra from
\mathcal{P}^{n}
imposes further equality constraints.
If different
C_{J_{1}}
,
C_{J_{2}}
intersect, then some
x_{j}
,
j\in[n-2]
, has to be zero.
If some
C_{J}
intersects with
C_{n-1}
, then
S(x)=\textstyle\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}=x_{n-1}
, and likewise if some
C_{J}
intersects
C_{n}
.
With the additional constraint
x_{n-1}=x_{n}
enforced by
F\subseteq H
, any such intersection has dimension at most
n-2
.
Therefore
F^{\star}=T=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n-1}=x_{n}\geq S(x)\}
.

It remains to be shown that
T
contains no line.
Suppose, for a contradiction, that
L=\{y+\lambda v:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}\subseteq T
for some
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Setting
t\coloneqq v_{n-1}=v_{n}
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\max\{0,y_{j}+\lambda v_{j}\}=S(y+\lambda v)\leq y_{n-1}+\lambda v_{n-1}=y_{n}+\lambda v_{n}=y_{n}+\lambda t

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Dividing both sides by
\lambda>0
yields

\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\max\{0,\frac{y_{j}}{\lambda}+v_{j}\}\leq\frac{y_{n}}{\lambda}+t

Taking the limit
\lambda\to\infty
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\max\{0,v_{j}\}\leq t.

Analogously, dividing by
\lambda<0
and taking the limit
\lambda\to-\infty
yields

\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\max\{0,-v_{j}\}\leq-t.

Adding up those inequalities gives

\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}|v_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\big(\max\{0,v_{j}\}+\max\{0,-v_{j}\}\big)\leq 0.

Thus
v_{j}=0
for
j\in[n-2]
.
With this,
S(y+\lambda v)=S(y)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
The inequality
S(y)\leq y_{n}+\lambda t
must then hold for all
\lambda
, hence letting
\lambda\to\pm\infty
forces
t=0
and thus
v=0
.
In other words, there is no line in
T
, concluding the proof.
∎

For the particular setting
\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{r}=\mathbf{2}
, we obtain the following characterization.
Beyond this strict hierarchy, the class
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})
can be characterized succinctly by relating it to the class
\mathcal{M}_{n}(m)
.

Theorem 19
.

For all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there holds

\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})=\mathcal{M}_{n}(2^{\ell}).

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(2^{\ell})
follows by Theorem
16
.
Just note that
m_{\ell}+1=2^{\ell}
for
\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{r}=\mathbf{2}
.

For the inclusion
\mathcal{M}_{n}(2^{\ell})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})
, consider an arbitrary
f\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(2^{\ell})
.
Such a function is of the form

f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\beta_{i}\max\limits_{j\in[2^{\ell}]}\{a_{ij}^{\top}x\}

for some
p\in\mathbb{N},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R},a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
To check that
f
is in
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})
, let us take a look at Figure
1
.
Recall that the first layer of networks in
\mathcal{N}_{n}({\ell,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})
is not indegree constrained.
This allows to realize the linear terms
a_{ij}^{\top}x
until the first activation.
The following
\ell
hidden layers of rank-
2
-maxout units compute
\max_{j\in[2^{\ell}]}\{a_{ij}^{\top}x\}
for
i\in[p]
.
Those terms can be combined linearly, with coefficients
\beta_{i}
, in the final layer, as desired.
∎

\mathcal{M}_{n}(k)\subsetneq\mathcal{M}_{n}(k+1)

Hertrich et al. 2021

Koutschan et al. 2023

for all

Corollary 20
.

\mathcal{N}_{n}({0,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq\mathcal{N}_{n}({\lceil\log_{2}(n+1)\rceil,\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2}})=\mathcal{C}_{n}.

Discussion

All in all, our work shows that the virtual polytopes associated with the functions computable by sparse maxout networks can help understanding them.
We have seen that our bound on their dimension provides a useful tool for analyzing expressivity, yielding various depth hierarchies and showing that width cannot fully compensate for given indegree constraints. The
lineality space
of a positively homogeneous continuous piecewise linear function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the vector space
\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)=f(v+x)\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
. The codimension of the lineality space of a function
f
equals the dimension of the virtual polytope
V_{f}
. The codimension of the lineality space was also used by
Koutschan et al. 2023
to find the smallest
k
such that the function
f
is contained in the vector space
\mathcal{M}_{n}(k)
. Consequently, by tracking the lineality space of a function computed by a sparse network, one could alternatively derive Theorem
16
. However, formulating Lemma
10
directly in terms of the lineality space, rather than in the dual setting of virtual polytopes, seems to be more involved.
