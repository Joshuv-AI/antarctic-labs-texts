Prediction of preterm birth using machine learning: a comprehensive analysis based on large-scale preschool children survey data in Shenzhen of China
Authors: Liwen Ding, Xiaona Yin, Guomin Wen, Dengli Sun, Danxia Xian, Yafen Zhao, Maolin Zhang, Weikang Yang, Weiqing Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm birth (PTB) is a significant cause of neonatal mortality and long-term health issues. Accurate prediction and timely prevention of PTB are essential for reducing associated child mortality and morbidity. Traditional predictive methods face challenges due to heterogeneous risk factors and their interaction effects. This study aims to develop and evaluate six machine learning (ML) models to predict PTB using large-scale children survey data from Shenzhen, China, and to identify key predictors through Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis.MethodsData from 84,050 mother–child pairs, collected in 2021 and 2022, were processed and divided into training, validation, and test sets. Six ML models were tested: L1-Regularised Logistic Regression, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Naive Bayes, Random Forests, Support Vector Machine, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Model performance was evaluated based on discrimination, calibration and clinical utility. SHAP analysis was used to interpret the importance and impact of individual features on PTB prediction.ResultsThe XGBoost model demonstrated the best overall performance, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) scores of 0.752 and 0.757 in the validation and test sets, respectively, along with favorable calibration and clinical utility. Key predictors identified were multiple pregnancies, threatened abortion, and maternal age of conception. SHAP analysis highlighted the positive impacts of multiple pregnancies and threatened abortion, as well as the negative impact of micronutrient supplementation on PTB.ConclusionOur study found that ML models, particularly XGBoost, show promise in accurately predicting PTB and identifying key risk factors. These findings provide the potential of ML for enhancing clinical interventions, personalizing prenatal care, and informing public health initiatives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-06980-4.Keywords: Preterm birth, Machine learning, Prediction model, SHAP, Multiple pregnancies, Threatened abortion

Background

Preterm birth (PTB), defined as the birth of an infant before 37 weeks of gestation, remains a global health challenge. From 2010 to 2020, the global annual rate of PTB increased by approximately 0.14%, reaching 9.9% (13.4 million) in 2020 [1]. In China, as many as 6.1% of newborns are preterm, a rate that has persisted over the past decade [1]. However, in most countries, the rate of PTB may continue to rise [2, 3]. PTB complications were the leading cause of death in children under five, responsible for nearly 0.94 million deaths worldwide in 2019 [4]. In China, PTB complications were the second leading cause of child mortality, resulting in 30,900 deaths in 2015 [5]. PTB was associated not only with neonatal mortality but also short- and long-term physical, neurodevelopmental, and socio-economic impacts, such as growth retardation, psychiatric disorders, and increased risk of chronic diseases in early to mid-adulthood [6–9]. Therefore, it is crucial to accurately predict and then timely prevent PTB to reduce child mortality and morbidity associated with PTB.

Maternal conditions during pregnancy were the most significant risk factors for PTB, which had a direct long-term impact on fetal development and future health [10, 11]. Common risk factors included maternal demographic characteristics (e.g., age at conception, pregnancy mode, parity), pregnancy complications, nutritional and metabolic environment, social determinants (e.g., smoking), and fetal conditions (e.g., sex, intrauterine growth retardation) [12–15]. However, existing researches presented inconsistent findings. For example, some studies identified advanced maternal age as a risk factor, while others highlight young maternal age, particularly under 18 years, as associated with higher PTB risk [12, 15, 16]. Another study noted a bimodal distribution of the relationship between PTB and maternal age [17, 18]. Pre-pregnancy Body mass index (BMI) was also related to PTB, but effects on different types of PTB varied among different studies [15, 19, 20]. Additionally, there was controversy over whether socioeconomic factors influence PTB [15, 21, 22]. These inconsistent findings might stem from small sample sizes, confounding biases and interaction effects, making it difficult to predict PTB by traditional methods [15, 22, 23].

Machine learning (ML), as a part of artificial intelligence, enables computers to simulate human learning behavior, using big data to acquire new knowledge and improve performance [24, 25]. Compared with traditional statistical methods, ML can effectively handle dynamic, large-scale and complex data with interactions between factors [26, 27]. Therefore, ML has been widely applied in medicine, from cancer detection to the prediction of chronic diseases [28–30], and is becoming an advanced tool for prenatal diagnosis [31]. A few studies have applied ML to PTB prediction, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) ranging from 0.62 to 0.78, depending on the number of predictors and models used [32–35]. A hospital study demonstrated high predictive performance but low precision relatively, making it more suitable for early screening rather than precise prediction [36]. Similarly, Fergus et al.'s machine learning model demonstrated promising predictive power (AUC 95%), but large-scale use of Electrohysterography is impractical [37]. Meanwhile, most studies focused on developed Western countries, with limited research on Asian populations. Consequently, there is significant potential to improve PTB prediction models for Asian populations with comprehensive predictive performance.

Our study aims to develop and identify the best PTB predictive model from six ML models using large-scale children survey data, and to identify key predictors through Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis.

Methods

Participants

This study conducted large-scale children surveys enrolled in 235 kindergartens in Longhua District, Shenzhen, China, in 2021 and 2022. After exclusion of cases with no identity record (n = 1,605), missing gestational age (n = 1,305), a total of 66,728 participants from the 2021 survey were included as the training and internal validation set. In 2022, exclusions were made for cases with no identity record (n = 137) and missing gestational age (n = 8), resulting in a final sample of 17,322 participants, used as the test set (see Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health, Sun Yat-sen University, and informed consent was obtained from all children's primary guardians.

ANB, average net benefit; AUC, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; L1-Logistic, L1-regularised logistic regression; LightGBM, Light Gradient Boosting Machine; NB, Naive Bayes; RF, Random Forests; SHAP, Shapley Additive Explanations; SVM, Support Vector Machine; XGBoost, Extreme Gradient Boosting

Data acquirement

The data was collected through a self-administered online structured questionnaire, completed under the supervision of child care practitioners and teachers. The questionnaire contained demographic characteristics, maternal condition during pregnancy (medical history, pregnancy complications, behaviors and habits, etc.), and neonatal birth characteristics (gestational age, birth weight, mode of delivery, etc.). Based on the question of what week was the child's gestational age at birth, PTB was defined by a mother's response of < 37 weeks as the outcome. According to previous researches [36, 38–41], we considered 22 potential predictors, with definitions and coding detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Each feature had an average missing rate of 5.6% (range: 0.5–10.8%), addressed using multiple imputations [42]. The Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was introduced to solve the data imbalance [43]. Continuous features, such as maternal age of conception, were z-score normalized, and multi-categorical variables were processed as dummy variables.

Model construction, evaluation and interpretation

Feature screening

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression was applied to analyze the relationship between features and PTB, selecting variables with marginal significance (p < 0.10). Highly correlated variables (absolute correlation coefficient > 0.8) and those with multicollinearity (Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) > 10) were identified and eliminated. The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) was then applied to finalize the variable selection [44].

Model construction and evaluation

The 2021 survey data were split into an 80% training set and a 20% internal validation set, with tenfold cross-validation resampling [45]. The 2022 survey data were used as the test set. Six machine learning models were fitted: L1-Regularised Logistic Regression (L1-Logistic), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Naive Bayes (NB), Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [35, 38, 46–49]. Each model was optimized through tenfold cross-validation to fine-tune hyperparameters, detailed in Supplementary Table 2. To further improve predictive performance, stacking ensembles were implemented by combining the three best-performing models in pairs, as well as all three together, to generate multiple meta-models [50].

The confusion matrix was employed to present model classification. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves was constructed, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was computed to evaluate discrimination [51]. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Brier score [52]. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was plotted to demonstrate clinical utility, with average net benefit (ANB) quantified [53]. Other parameters including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1- score were used to complement the model evaluation.

Model interpretation

The importance of individual features was explained by coefficient magnitudes in the L1-Logistic model, conditional probabilities in the NB model, the weight vector of the hyperplane in the SVM model, and mean decrease Gini (MDG) in the LightGBM, RF, and XGBoost models [54, 55]. For the best model, feature significance and contribution were interpreted using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP), through the absolute values and signs of SHAP values [56]. In addition, we applied SHAP analysis to offer detailed, individualized explanations of how the model predicts PTB risk for each specific patient. To complement this, we developed a nomogram based on a basic logistic regression model using the screened predictors to measure overall risk [57].

Statistical analysis

All variables were compared among sets by ANOVA and t-test for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables. Classifications in confusion matrix were compared using McNemar’s test, and trained models were compared with Wilcoxon signed-rank test for pairwise comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Statistical analyses above were performed via R version 4.2.3, with the "glmnet" v4.1.7, "randomForest" v 4.7.1.1, "e1071" v 1.7.13, "xgboost" v 1.7.7.1 and "lightgbm" v 4.3.0 packages for model construction.

Screening of key variables

Univariate and multivariate analyses identified 19 independent risk factors for PTB (Supplementary Table 4). Correlation tests (Supplementary Fig. 1) and VIF analysis of these 19 factors (Supplementary Table 5), revealed no multicollinearity among these factors. After conversion to 23 dummy variables, LASSO was then applied to these factors through ten-fold cross-validation, resulting in 14 features with nonzero coefficients (Fig. 2): pregnancy mode, threatened abortion (TA), gestational hypertension (GH), pre-eclampsia (PE), intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), folic acid supplementation, calcium supplementation, iron supplementation, pre-pregnancy BMI (BMI ≥ 24), parity, multiple pregnancy, child’s sex, birth season (winter), and maternal age of conception.

Based on the strong performance of L1-Logistic, RF, and XGBoost, four meta-models were created: L1-Logistic + RF, L1-Logistic + XGBoost, RF + XGBoost, and L1-Logistic + RF + XGBoost. In the training set, all of these stacking ensemble models consistently demonstrated better overall performance compared to the individual models. However, in the validation and test sets, the individual XGBoost model outperformed the stacking ensembles. Additionally, the stacking ensemble combining all three models did not show better performance compared to the ones combining only two models (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

In our study, we developed and evaluated six models for predicting PTB using data from the 2021 and 2022 children surveys, which included 84,050 mother–child dyads. Our results indicated a PTB prevalence of 7.8%, and 14 features were included in the final models. The XGBoost model showed the best overall performance, with high AUC scores in both validation (0.752) and test (0.757) sets, along with favorable calibration and clinical utility. SHAP analysis identified multiple pregnancies, TA and maternal age of conception as the key predictors. Compared to the sample with TB, PTB had a higher SHAP prediction score, with the lack of iron and folic acid supplementation as positive predictors.

According to the ROC curves, the RF model was high performance in the training set but lower performance in the validation and test set, consistent with other studies, likely due to overfitting [36, 58]. Instead, the XGBoost model performed consistently and effectively across sets, because of its ensemble of weighted decision trees and an iteratively optimized gradient boosting framework [59]. This performance matches the results reported in previous research [49, 56]. What’s more, we find that the XGBoost model also exhibits outstanding performance in predicting other diseases such as diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and rheumatoid arthritis, indicating its potential for broader applications [60–62]. In calibration curve analyses and clinical utility analyses, we note that the L1-Logistic and LightGBM show good performance as well, which aligns with the results of other researches [35, 49, 63]. Although ensemble models performed well in the training set, they did not surpass the XGBoost model in the validation and test sets, and increasing the number of stacked ensemble models did not improve performance. This outcome, also noted in other studies, may be attributed to overfitting and model redundancy [64–66]. This suggests that complex ML models do not always outperform traditional statistical methods and simpler models, offering more flexible options for predictive models.

Multiple pregnancies were the top PTB risk factor in our research, with a consensus in other studies [34, 38, 40]. Multiple pregnancies are often associated with excess amniotic fluid and high uterine load, leading to uterine overstretching, which increases PTB risk [67, 68]. Uterine stretching may upregulate contraction-associated proteins and inflammatory cytokines, triggering an inflammatory response in the myometrium that can induce PTB [68–71]. Additionally, uterine overstretching exacerbates cervical shortening, further contributing to PTB risk [72]. It was found that multiple pregnancies are diagnosed with cervical shortening earlier, with a mean cervical length shorter in multiple pregnancies (23.5 mm) compared to singleton pregnancies (32.1 mm) [73]. Studies confirmed that women with multiple pregnancies account for 15–20% of all PTBs, with over 50% experiencing PTB—a rate significantly higher than that of singleton pregnancies. [74, 75].

Maternal age of conception is another common predictor of PTB [34, 35, 38, 40, 63]. Our SHAP analysis revealed a bimodal distribution of PTB risk with conception age, showing higher risk in women under 18 and over 35, with the risk increasing further after 35 [12, 18]. Age-related placental issues, such as placental insufficiency and placental abruption, may increase PTB risk [76]. At the same time, older pregnant women also have a higher prevalence of underlying maternal diseases and pregnancy complications, such as gestational hypertension and pre-eclampsia, which interact to further increase the risk of PTB [77, 78]. Advanced maternal age poses significant risks not only for the fetus but also for the mother, increasing the likelihood of adverse maternal outcomes, even maternal death [79].

Threatened abortion, as a type of hemorrhage during pregnancy, has proven to be a significant predictor of PTB [38, 40]. Pregnant women with threatened abortion generally have an unstable intrauterine environment, and they are more prone to placental or fetal membrane abnormalities, leading to abnormal fetal development and preterm delivery [80, 81]. These conditions are often accompanied by infections and inflammation, which indicate an unhealthy uterine lining and increased uterine contractility, thus increasing the risk of PTB [82, 83]. Additionally, threatened abortion is associated with other adverse birth outcomes, such as miscarriage, low birth weight, and other complications [84]. The importance of gestational hypertension in other models should not be overlooked, as evidenced by other studies [34–36, 40, 85], mainly because of its effect on placental perfusion [86]. Micronutrients, which support fetal skeletal development, neural tube formation, and uterine muscle balance, have a protective effect against preterm birth [87–89].

Most existing PTB prediction methods rely on ultrasonography and laboratory tests, which are not feasible for wide-ranging screening [90]. Our study demonstrated the potential of ML in PTB prediction due to its non-invasiveness and broad applicability. We apply SHAP to enhance the predictability and interpretability of the XGBoost model, helping clinicians to better identify key predictive factors [28]. This allows for timely and targeted interventions, such as closer monitoring, lifestyle modifications, and medical treatments aimed at mitigating risk factors. Furthermore, accurately predicting PTB allows for more personalized prenatal care plans. Pregnant women identified as high-risk can receive individualized care plans that address their specific risk factors, including tailored nutritional supplementation and management of pregnancy complications. Additionally, public health initiatives can inform educational campaigns and preventive strategies, such as raising awareness about the importance of micronutrients supplementation during pregnancy and promoting regular maternal check-ups for pregnant women of advanced age.

There are potential limitations to this study. First, the generalisability of the results may be limited by the sample being restricted to mother–child dyads in Shenzhen, China. Second, the use of a structured questionnaire to collect data may be affected by participant self-report bias, such as recall bias. The questionnaires also have limited predictors without other relevant factors, such as genetic information. In addition, although SHAP values enhanced model interpretability, machine learning models are more focused on prediction rather than causal inference. Therefore, further researches with more diverse populations and additional relevant factors are needed to enhance predictive accuracy and generalizability, as well as to determine the exact causal relationships between factors and PTB.

Conclusion

In summary, our study developed and evaluated six machine learning models to predict PTB using large-scale preschool children survey data from Shenzhen of China. The XGBoost model performed best, with high AUC scores in validation (0.752) and test (0.757) sets, along with good calibration and clinical utility. SHAP analysis identified key predictors of PTB, included multiple pregnancies, threatened abortion, and maternal age at conception. These findings highlight the potential of ML for enhancing clinical interventions, personalizing prenatal care, and informing public health initiatives aimed at reducing PTB-related morbidity and mortality. Despite the promising results, future population-based studies with broader factor categories are needed to determine the exact causal relationships between these factors and PTB.
