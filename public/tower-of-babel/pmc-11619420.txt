Correlation between MRI findings and functional outcomes in patients with calf muscle strain injuries: a retrospective study on 78 patients
Authors: Koray Kaya Kilic, Murat Yuncu, Fırat Dogruoz, Volkan Buyukarslan, Mehmet Baris Ertan, Ozkan Kose
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCalf muscle strain injuries (CMSI), also known as “tennis leg,” are frequently observed in middle-aged and physically active people, including non-professional athletes. While magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is frequently used to assess the extent of these injuries, the relationship between MRI findings and long-term functional outcomes in non-athletic populations remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the correlation between MRI-detected injury severity and functional outcomes using the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) and the Tegner Activity Scale (TAS).MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on 78 non-athletic patients diagnosed with CMSI and were followed for an average of 25.6 ± 16.1 months. Injury severity was classified into three grades: Grade 1 (edema without architectural disruption), Grade 2 (partial muscle disruption with hematoma), and Grade 3 (complete muscle disruption or tendon detachment) based on MRI examination. The functional outcomes were assessed using the ATRS and TAS. A simple linear regression analysis was conducted to assess the impact of demographic variables and MRI findings on ATRS.ResultsThe mean ATRS at the final follow-up was 92.9 ± 10.6, with 75.6% of patients achieving an excellent outcome. Despite the excellent ATRS scores, a notable reduction in TAS was evident from the pre-injury assessment (4.4 ± 1.2) to the final follow-up (3.8 ± 1.3; p < 0.001). Moreover, 44.9% of patients did not resume their pre-injury activity levels. The regression analysis demonstrated no statistically significant correlation between MRI-detected injury severity, demographic factors (age, gender, BMI), and ATRS outcomes (R² = 0.094, adjusted R² = -0.011).ConclusionMRI-detected injury severity does not significantly predict long-term functional outcomes in patients with CMSI, even though most patients achieved excellent ATRS scores. However, nearly half of the patients did not return to their pre-injury activity levels. These findings suggest that additional factors might influence recovery, and further research is needed to elucidate these factors in non-athletic patients.Level of evidenceLevel IV, Retrospective case series.

Introduction

Calf muscle strain injuries (CMSI) are common musculoskeletal injuries that can occur in individuals of all ages, fitness levels, and competitive backgrounds. However, in the general population, the group at highest risk consists of middle-aged, poorly conditioned men, typically in their 40s, engaging in recreational activities. Millar et al. evaluated 720 patients with CMSI who were admitted to a sports injury treatment center over a 12-year years. The findings indicated that these injuries occurred in a wide age range, from 15 to 60 years old, with the majority of patients falling within the 30 to 45 age group, representing 53% of the total number of patients [1]. While calf injuries are commonly associated with participation in sports, these injuries may also manifest in the non-athletic population or the elderly during low-energy activities of daily living, such as walking and getting out of a car or occupational tasks [2, 3]. These injuries typically result from an acute overstretching or sudden contraction of the calf muscles, primarily affecting the gastrocnemius, soleus, and plantaris muscles. This condition, also called tennis leg, often presents with sudden, acute pain in the calf, which limits mobility and causes significant functional impairment. The diagnosis of tennis leg is straightforward in most cases. Typical history and symptoms and physical examination findings, including localized pain, swelling, ecchymosis, and a palpable gap over the medial gastrocnemius musculotendinous junction, are usually sufficient for diagnosis [4, 5]. Nevertheless, an imaging examination is often performed to verify the clinical diagnosis, assess the severity and extent of the lesion, and rule out the other conditions that might mimic tennis leg with a similar clinical picture, such as thrombophlebitis, deep vein thrombosis or Baker cyst rupture [6, 7]. Ultrasonography (USG) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are efficient imaging modalities for this purpose. However, MRI provides a detailed visualization of muscle tears, fluid collections, and other soft tissue abnormalities due to high contrast resolution and multiplanar capability. Previous studies have highlighted the competency of MRI in evaluating the extent of the injuries [6–10].

A tennis leg is typically managed with a conservative approach unless there is evidence of compartment syndrome due to significant bleeding [11]. The initial approach includes rest, ice application, leg elevation, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) to reduce pain and inflammation. Compression therapy, often using elastic bandages or neoprene sleeves, is recommended to minimize bleeding and swelling and promote faster recovery [12, 13]. Once the acute symptoms have resolved, early ambulation and a gradual stretching program are instituted. Generally, patients can expect full recovery with proper rehabilitation within a few weeks to months. However, surgery might occasionally be required for indications such as persistent pain, loss of strength, joint contractures, and heterotopic ossification [14, 15]. Despite the effectiveness of these treatments, the current literature lacks studies that report detailed functional outcomes in these patients. Most studies report a diagnostic approach with either USG or MRI but fail to report clinical and functional outcomes late after injury [6–10]. Moreover, most of these studies are conducted on elite athletes, even though CMSI affects both athletic and non-athletic populations [16–22]. Besides accurate diagnosis, assessing the injury’s severity is crucial for developing an effective treatment plan and predicting patient outcomes.

While MRI is a valuable tool for diagnosing these injuries, its ability to predict long-term functional outcomes remains uncertain. Although several studies have explored the correlation between MRI findings and return-to-play outcomes in elite athletes [17, 19, 20, 22], there is a notable lack of research examining this relationship in more diverse populations, including recreational athletes and non-athletes. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically investigated the correlation between MRI-detected injury severity and long-term functional outcomes in the general population. Understanding the correlation between MRI findings and outcomes is crucial for developing effective rehabilitation strategies that can enhance recovery and minimize the risk of long-term disability. This study aims to bridge this gap by analyzing the relationship between MRI-detected injury severity and the clinical outcomes of patients diagnosed with CMSI, using validated measures such as the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) and Tegner Activity Scale (TAS). We hypothesize that high-grade injuries will result in poorer functional outcomes and a decreased likelihood of returning to previous activity levels. By focusing on a more diverse population, including both athletes and non-athletes, we aim to provide more generalizable insights to the broader population affected by this condition.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

A retrospective review was conducted on the institutional digital database to identify patients diagnosed and treated for tennis leg between January 2018 and December 2023. The review spanned a period of six years. All patients were treated at a public university-affiliated training and research hospital. The study population comprised adult male and female patients between 18 and 74 years, including non-athletes and recreational or professional athletes. Patients lacking a proper MRI examination, with a follow-up period of less than six months, incomplete medical records, or those unwilling to participate were excluded from the study. The search was primarily based on the clinical information and MRI reports with the following keywords: ‘GCM head injury,’ ‘GCM head rupture,’ ‘GCM head edema,’ ‘Soleus injury,’ ‘Soleus rupture,’ ‘Soleus edema,’ ‘Plantaris rupture,’ ‘Plantaris injury,’ ‘GCL head injury,’ ‘calf muscle injury,’ ‘calf muscle starin,’ and ‘tennis leg.’ This search identified a total of 163 patients. Of these, 62 were excluded because MRI did not detect CMSI. The remaining 101 patients were further evaluated. An additional 23 patients were excluded for the following reasons: five patients declined to participate, 11 patients were lost to follow-up, five patients only had knee MRI scans (with an insufficient field of view), and two patients had a follow-up period of less than 6 months. Ultimately, 78 patients were included in the study for final analysis (Fig. 1). Antalya Training and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee approved the study protocol, and informed consent was obtained from all participants (Approval date and issue: 2024/6–16). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and followed the STROBE guidelines.

MRI imaging and evaluation

All patients underwent standard lower leg MRI on two MRI units: 1.5 T MRI Achieva DS Advance (Philips Healthcare, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) or 3.0 T MRI Ingenia (Philips Healthcare, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) using 16 Channel Sense XL Torso Coil with the patient in a supine position. Sequences followed a standard protocol from a clinical routine comprising a T1w turbo spin echo (TSE) and T2w SPAIR sequences. All images were transferred to a client-server-based PACS digital workstation (Sectra IDS7, Ver. 18.2., Sectra AB, Sweden). A radiologist experienced in musculoskeletal imaging (Senior author: KKK) retrospectively assessed the MRI scans to ascertain the degree of injury and categorize the extent of muscle damage. The radiologist was blinded to the patients’ clinical history to avoid bias in evaluating the MRI findings. The grading of muscle injuries was based on the classification proposed by Dai et al., which divides muscle injuries into three groups [8]. Grade 1 is characterized by edema with no architectural disruption or macroscopic tear. Grade 2 is characterized by partial muscle disruption with hematoma or local disruption of muscle architecture. Grade 3 is characterized by a complete muscle disruption with local hematoma covering the tear cavity or tendon detachment (Fig. 2). The calf muscles, the medial head of the gastrocnemius, the lateral head of the gastrocnemius, the plantaris, and the soleus were evaluated separately.

Treatment and follow-up

The standard treatment for calf muscle strain injuries is conservative management, and this approach was primarily applied at our institution. Thus, all patients were treated with a conservative approach in this series. In the acute phase of conservative treatment, a multimodal approach was employed, comprising elastic bandaging, local and oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), ice application, and the use of crutches. Patients were permitted to bear as much weight as they were able to. Once pain control was achieved, the patients were advised to follow a structured home-based rehabilitation program. This included gradual strengthening and stretching exercises, focusing on calf muscle flexibility and strength. No additional formal physical therapy sessions were prescribed, but patients were encouraged to gradually increase their activity level based on tolerance.

Outcome assessments

All patients were interviewed via telephone. The interviews with patients were conducted at the final follow-up, averaging 25.6 ± 16.1 months after the initial injury. The Achilles tendon rupture score (ATRS) was employed to assess functional outcomes [23]. This patient-based and injury-specific scoring system comprises ten questions, each scored on a Likert scale from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating better function and fewer symptoms. The Tegner Activity Scale was employed to ascertain the activity level of the subjects prior to the injury and at the end of the follow-up period [24]. Additionally, the time to cease pain and return to symptom-free activities of daily living was documented.

Statistical analysis

SPSS Statistics Base v23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for all statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov-Smirnoff test. Paired sample t-tests were used to compare pre-injury and post-injury Tegner Activity Scale scores to evaluate the impact of the injury on patients’ activity levels. To investigate the associations between ATRS and demographic factors (age, gender, BMI), as well as injury severity in the gastrocnemius medial head, gastrocnemius lateral head, soleus, and plantaris muscles, a simple linear regression analysis was performed. The statistical significance of the regression coefficients was evaluated with t-tests, and the model’s overall fit was determined using R-squared and adjusted R-squared values. A p-value of less than 0.05 was accepted as indicative of a statistically significant result. There were no instances of missing data in this study; therefore, no imputation or data-handling techniques were required.

Results

A total of 78 patients (23.1% female, 76.9% male) were included in the study, with a mean age of 44.5 ± 10.2 years. The average BMI was 27.8 ± 4.4 kg/m². Sports-related injuries were the most common etiology, accounting for 38 (48.7%) of the cases, with football being the most frequent sport involved (26.9%), followed by tennis (10.3%). Of the 38 patients presenting with sports-related injuries, only four were professional athletes. These included a soccer player, soccer referee, tennis coach, and personal fitness trainer. The remaining 34 patients were engaged in recreational sports activities. The remaining injuries were related to occupational or daily activities in non-athletic individuals (40 cases, 51.3%). Most patients (89.5%) were admitted to the hospital within the first week of the injury. Demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients were presented in Table 1.

The mean follow-up duration was 25.6 ± 16.1 months. At the final follow-up, the mean ATRS was 92.9 ± 10.6. The majority of patients (75.6%) achieved an excellent outcome based on the ATRS categorical evaluation, while 7.7% had a poor outcome. The mean pre-injury Tegner Activity Scale (TAS) score was 4.4 ± 1.2, which significantly decreased to 3.8 ± 1.3 at the final follow-up (p < 0.001). Additionally, 55.1% of the patients were able to return to their previous level of activity, while 44.9% did not achieve their pre-injury activity level (Table 3). None of the patients reported loss of either knee or ankle range of motion. Six patients were seen and examined at the final follow-up. All had superior retraction of the gastrocnemius medial head and varying degrees of calf atrophy (Fig. 4). One patient developed deep vein thrombosis (DVT) six weeks after the injury, which was treated with thrombolytic medication and subsequent recovery.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the correlation between MRI-detected injury severity in calf muscle strain injuries (CMSI) and long-term functional outcomes, measured primarily by the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) and Tegner Activity Scale (TAS). The key findings revealed that despite the high proportion of patients achieving excellent ATRS outcomes (75.6%), nearly half (44.9%) did not return to their pre-injury activity levels, as indicated by the significant decrease in TAS scores from pre-injury to final follow-up. The results also showed that MRI-detected injury severity, including the grading of gastrocnemius medial and lateral heads, soleus, and plantaris muscles, was not significantly associated with ATRS outcomes. Demographic factors such as age, gender, and BMI were also not significant predictors of functional outcomes, suggesting that other factors may influence recovery and return to activity.

In contrast to the findings of the present study, several previous studies have underscored the role of MRI in guiding return-to-play (RTP) decisions, particularly in elite athletes. The differences between our findings and those of previous studies likely arise from the complex nature of calf muscle strain injuries (CMSI) and the variability in patient populations, study designs, and outcome measures. Prior research has predominantly focused on elite athletes with specific physical demands and access to advanced rehabilitation, leading to findings that may not be generalizable to broader populations [19–21]. For instance, Prakash et al. found that the severity of connective tissue involvement, particularly higher-grade injuries, was strongly correlated with longer recovery times [19]. Similarly, Waterworth et al. demonstrated that specific MRI features, such as multiple muscle involvement, musculotendinous junction strains, deep strain locations, and intramuscular tendon tears, were associated with missing games in Australian Football League players [20]. Together, these studies highlight that connective tissue integrity and specific injury characteristics on MRI are key factors influencing recovery and RTP in athletes. In contrast, our study included a more diverse cohort, encompassing both athletes, recreational athletes and non-athletes, which likely influenced the correlation between MRI findings and functional outcomes [22]. The variability in recovery trajectories and return-to-activity expectations across these groups may explain why our study observed a weaker correlation between MRI-detected injury severity and functional outcomes [16].

Furthermore, while previous studies focused on return-to-play (RTP) as the primary outcome, particularly for athletes [19–21], our study utilized the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) [23] and Tegner Activity Scale (TAS) [24], which assess general function but may not fully capture the specific performance demands of athletes [9]. The significant drop in TAS scores observed in our study suggests that, despite achieving good general function, patients may not return to their pre-injury activity levels, especially in less physically demanding contexts [22]. This relatively low rate of return to the previous level of activity may be attributed to several factors. First, the psychological impact of the injury, including fear of re-injury, may deter patients from fully resuming their previous activity levels. Second, chronic pain or persistent muscle weakness, despite achieving high ATRS scores, could limit performance in sports requiring explosive strength or agility. Lastly, the differences in rehabilitation approaches—more individualized and closely monitored in elite sports settings versus more generalized in our study—could contribute to the weaker correlations between MRI findings and functional outcomes [12, 16].

Our findings also suggest that MRI-detected injury severity may not fully capture long-term structural changes and functional impairments, as highlighted by previous research [8]. Chronic alterations in muscle architecture and aponeurosis structure, which we did not specifically evaluate, could significantly impact long-term outcomes, particularly in the ability to return to pre-injury activity levels [22]. This indicates that MRI alone may be insufficient for predicting long-term recovery, underscoring the need for a comprehensive approach that integrates both structural and functional aspects of recovery [10, 17]. These factors emphasize the importance of further research to better understand the relationship between injury characteristics, treatment approaches, and functional recovery, ultimately leading to more tailored rehabilitation strategies [9, 12].

This study possesses several strengths that contribute to the robustness of its findings. Its comprehensive approach to evaluating the relationship between MRI-detected injury severity and long-term functional outcomes in patients with calf muscle strain injuries (CMSI) is a key strength. The study utilized a well-defined cohort of 78 patients, ensuring a sufficient sample size for meaningful analyses. The use of validated functional outcome measures, such as the Achilles Tendon Rupture Score (ATRS) and Tegner Activity Scale (TAS), provides reliable and relevant assessments of patient recovery and activity levels. Additionally, the relatively long follow-up period (mean of 25.6 months) allows for a thorough evaluation of long-term outcomes, offering insights into the persistent effects of CMSI. The inclusion of a wide range of injury severities and muscle involvement enhances the study’s ability to explore the correlation between different grades of injury and functional outcomes. However, the study also has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces risks of selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Reliance on self-reported functional outcomes, such as ATRS and TAS, introduces potential recall bias and subjective interpretation, which could affect the accuracy of the results. While the sample size is adequate for the primary analysis, it may still be insufficient to detect small but clinically significant effects, particularly in subgroups defined by injury severity or demographic characteristics.

In conclusion, while MRI remains a valuable tool for diagnosing CMSI, our findings suggest that MRI-detected injury severity does not significantly predict long-term functional outcomes in patients with calf muscle strain injuries, particularly in a population consisting primarily of recreational athletes and non-athletes. Despite the majority of patients achieving excellent functional scores, nearly half could not resume their previous activity levels. These findings indicate that factors other than MRI-detected injury severity may be of significant importance in the recovery process. Further research should examine additional clinical, biomechanical, and patient-related factors affecting the ability to resume previous activity levels to understand the recovery process following calf muscle strain injuries.
