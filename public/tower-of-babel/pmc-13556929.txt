GJB2, a novel transcription target of HSF4, confers tumorigenic and metastatic phenotypes and sustains mitochondrial homeostasis in lung adenocarcinoma via the PI3K/AKT pathway
Authors: Qing Hu, Renfan Zhang, Yefeng Zhou, Yang Yang, Hao Zhou, Xin Han, Shengrong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe ion channel gene GJB2 emerges as a therapeutic target for LUAD with significant prognostic value. Herein, the potential mechanisms of GJB2 were investigated. GJB2 expression was analyzed by bioinformatics, RT-qPCR and western blotting. In vitro functional assays and A549 xenografts detected the role of GJB2. The upstream mechanism of GJB2 was explored. GJB2 was up-regulated and associated with the poor overall survival of LUAD patients. GJB2 knockdown inhibited cell malignancy and impaired mitochondrial homeostasis. HSF4 enhanced GJB2 transcription. GJB2 knockdown lowered PI3K/AKT activity. SC79 reversed the in vitro impacts of GJB2 inadequacy and promoted tumor growth when HSF4 was up-regulated and GJB2 was down-regulated simultaneously. GJB2, transcriptionally activated by HSF4, activates PI3K/AKT pathway to support LUAD tumorigenesis and development.KEYWORDS: GJB2, HSF4, LUAD, mitochondrial function, PI3K/AKT pathway

Introduction

Lung cancer is a lethal malignancy that arises from the tunica bronchial mucosa or glands [1]. According to the latest global cancer statistics report, lung cancer annually contributes to top-ranking global instances and death rates, resulting in serious universal health issues and substantial societal burdens [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a predominant variant of lung cancer encompassing three primary histological categories: adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma [3]. The latest World Health Organization (WHO) classification posits that lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tumors develop in a typical sequence: atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH) to adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS) and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA) to invasive adenocarcinoma [4]. Currently, therapy for LUAD has transitioned from traditional regimens composed of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery into a novel treatment paradigm redefined by molecular targeted therapy and immunotherapy [5]. However, a considerable number of patients reflect an unfavorable outlook [6]. Against this backdrop, the discovery of sensitive and specific tumor biomarkers may be a breakthrough in the clinical treatment of LUAD.

Gap junctions, assembled from connexin subunits, are clusters of intercellular channels between adjacent cells that allow for electrical and metabolic communication known as gap junctional intercellular communication (GJIC) [7]. In addition, connexins can function non-canonically as single membrane channels (hemichannels) or as signaling hubs adjacent to any organelle and/or plasma membrane. Defective GJIC has been demonstrated to contribute to the loss of homeostasis and the ‘rebellious’ characteristics of malignant cells, including unregulated growth, loss of differentiated characteristics, invasion, and metastasis [8,9]. Gap junction protein beta 2 (GJB2), also known as connexin 26 (Cx26), is predominantly expressed in the connexin subunit. Across multiple malignancies, aberrant GJB2 expression can disrupt GJIC to affect tumor growth, immune microenvironment, chemoresistance, and glycolysis [10–12]. Contemporary literature has characterized GJB2 as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target, and its link with cell stemness, growth, and metastasis in LUAD [13–16].

Heat shock factors (HSFs), both individually and cooperatively, regulate transcriptional programs that function as molecular chaperones in response to proteotoxic stress conditions and physiological changes by preventing abnormal protein folding and aggregation to maintain intracellular protein homeostasis [17]. Heat shock factor 4 (HSF4) is a histone H3 variation in centromeric nucleosomes that is necessary for normal chromosomal segregation assembly. In addition to governing lens development and homeostasis, emerging evidence has proposed an association between HSF4 and cancer pathogenesis [18,19]. Notably, recent reports have revealed that HSF4 can transcriptionally modulate the expression of oncogenes and functionally intersect with key oncogenic signaling cascades, including the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby influencing tumor cell communication and malignancy [19,20]. In the context of LUAD, however, whether and how HSF4 contributes to tumor progression by regulating GJB2 remains entirely unexplored, which prompts us to investigate the potential HSF4–GJB2 regulatory axis.

The phosphoinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) constitutes a key ubiquitous and recurrently deregulated intracellular signaling cascade that is critical for vital physiological activities implicated in tumor formation and metastasis, including apoptosis, metabolism, angiogenesis, and cell cycle [21,22]. Several PI3K/AKT inhibitors have been developed for clinical trials in NSCLC [23]. Moreover, GJB2 co-expressed genes are enriched in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in tumors [24] and previously published data have suggested that GJB2 functionally interacts with PI3K/AKT signaling to increase epithelial-mesenchymal transition and gefitinib resistance in NSCLC cells [25].

Along these lines, this work hints toward the upstream HSF4 and downstream PI3K/AKT pathways of GJB2 in the context of LUAD.

Methods

Cell culture and infection

Normal human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) and LUAD cells (A549 and NCI-H358) purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China) were incubated in DMEM (Invitrogen; #10569–010) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen; #A5256701) and 1% double antibiotic (penicillin–streptomycin; Invitrogen; #15140122) under standard conditions (37°C, 95% humid air, and 5% CO2). All cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling.

Shanghai Integrated Biotech Solutions Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) designed and synthesized lentiviral particles containing shRNA sequences targeting GJB2 and full-length human HSF4, GLIS3, or HSF1. LUAD cells were transfected with sh-GJB2, sh-NC, pc-HSF4, pc-GLIS3, pc-HSF1, and pc-DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen; #L3000008). The sequences of shRNAs were listed as follows: sh-GJB2#1: ATTCTATGCCTGTCTTAATTT; sh-GJB2#2: GTTTAACGCATTGCCCAGTTG; sh-GJB2#1: CTGTAGGGGTTATTGGTGTAA; shRNA: CCGGTTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTCTCGAGACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAATTTTTG.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the Fastagen200 kit (Fastagen Biotech, Shanghai, China; 220,050), which was then synthesized into cDNA using Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen; #28025013). qPCR was then performed using SsoFast EvaGreen Supermix (Bio-Rad, 172–5204) within the ViiATM 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The thermal cycling process was as follows: denaturation (95°C, 3 min), 40 cycles of denaturation (95°C, 3 s), and elongation (60°C, 30 s). Relative transcript levels of GJB2 were normalized to that of GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt. The primers were listed as follows: GJB2 forward, 5’-CCTCAGGACGGTTCCATCAG-3’ and reverse, 5’-GGTTTGCTCACGCTTCTCAA-3’; GAPDH forward, 5’- ACAACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG-3’ and reverse, 5’- GCCATCACGCCACAGTTTC-3.’

Western blotting

LUAD cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, P0013B) supplementing with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitors. The homogenate was homogenized in an ice bath, shaken at 4°C for 2 h, and then centrifuged (12,000 rpm) at 4°C for 20 min. The supernatant was then taken and stored at −80°C. The protein concentration was quantified using a Coomassie Plus (Bradford) Assay Kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, P0006) and then added with 5x loading buffer in a water bath at 100°C for 10 min. Equal quality protein separated by SDS-PAGE was transferred onto PVDF membranes. After 1 h of blocking in 5% defatted milk powder, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with diluted primary antibodies against GJB2 (16960–1-AP; 1/1000; Proteintech), MFN1 (13798–1-AP; 1/2000; Proteintech), MFN2 (12186–1-AP; 1/5000; Proteintech), DRP1 (12957–1-AP; 1/2000; Proteintech), HSF4 (18797–1-AP; 1/1000; Proteintech), HSF1 (51034–1-AP; 1/1000; Proteintech), GLIS3 (12678–1-AP; 1/200; Proteintech), PI3K (ab191606; 1/1000; Abcam), p-PI3K (ab278545; 1/1000; Abcam), AKT (ab8805; 1/1000; Abcam), p-AKT (ab38449; 1/1000; Abcam) and GAPDH (ab181602; 1/1000; Abcam), followed by incubation with fluorescently labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab97051; 1/2000; Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature. The protein abundance was viewed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, P0018S), and the grayscale data were ascertained using the Gel-Pro Analyzer software.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

LUAD cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. After 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h of incubation, the cells were incubated with ten microliters of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, C0037) for 2 h. The absorbance OD value at 450 nm was then measured with a Synergy HT Multi-Mode Microtiter Plate Instrument.

Cell scratch assay

LUAD cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well. After the cells were adhered to the wall, a vertical line was drawn with a 200 ul tip. The scratch area was captured at 0 and 24 h in four random fields using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Transwell assay

Matrigel matrix (BD Science, Sparks, MD, United States) in 100 μL of serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:8 was applied to an 8.0 μm filter membrane for invasive assays. A total of 1 × 105 LUAD cells were seeded in the upper chamber of Transwell inserts containing 1% FBS medium, and 600-μL of medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After an overnight incubation period, the cells remaining in the upper chamber were carefully scraped off with a cotton-tipped swab. After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, cells that were transferred to the lower surface were visualized using 0.1% crystal violet. Five random different fields of each stained transwell inserts were photographed under a microscope (Nikon Eclipse TE-2000·E, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow cytometry

The cells were dissociated with EDTA-free trypsin. After centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 5 min, the cells were suspended in 300 μl of 1 × binding buffer and incubated with annexin V-FITC and PI (5 µL each) (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, C1062S) in dark for 15 min at room temperature. Apoptotic cells were sorted using FACS Calibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). The results were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 7.6.1; Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR, USA).

JC-1 probe

LUAD cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well. JC-1 (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, C2006) was diluted to 10 μg/mL, respectively, in serum-free medium and added to the wells, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS 3 times and observed with a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71, Tokyo, Japan). The ratio of the JC-1 monomer of the dye (green fluorescence at 530 nm) to the JC-1 polymer (red fluorescence at 590 nm) of the dye was calculated.

DCFH-DA probe

LUAD cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. The cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, S0033S) in the dark at 37°C for 30 min and rinsed three times with PBS to remove residual DCFH-DA. Then, 0.5 mL serum-free medium was added to the cells. Levels of ROS were then measured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71, Tokyo, Japan).

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) biochemiluminescence kit

Cellular ATP levels were quantified using an enhanced luminescence-based ATP detection kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, S0026). Cells or homogenized tissues were lysed in ATP detection lysis buffer on ice. After lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4°C for 5 min, the cell supernatants were collected. ATP concentrations were measured by mixing 20 µl of supernatant with 100 µl of ATP detection working solution (prepared by diluting ATP detection reagent 1:9 with ATP detection reagent diluent) in a luminometer tube. Luminescence signals were recorded with a multifunctional microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Protein content was assessed by measuring absorbance at 280 nm, and ATP levels were normalized to total protein concentration.

Measurement of oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

Cells were seeded into XFe96 cell culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured overnight in complete medium at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Prior to the assay, the medium was replaced with XF DMEM base medium (103344–100, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and cells were incubated in a CO2-free incubator at 37°C for 1 h. After sequential injections of 1.5 µM oligomycin, 1 µM FCCP, and 0.5 µM rotenone/antimycin A, OCR was then measured using a Seahorse XFe96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The consensus fragments of transcription factors within the promoter region were cloned into the pGL3-basic vector (OMEGA Engineering Inc., Norwalk, CT, USA). LUAD cells were then seeded at a density of 1.25 × 105 cells/well in 12-well plates. The following day, the above plasmids were co-transfected with pc-HSF4, pc-GLIS3, pc-HSF1, and pc-DNA into LUAD cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. The luciferase activity was normalized to pRL-TK activity and measured using a dual-luciferase assay kit (OMEGA Engineering Inc.).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The cells were cross-linked for 10 min with 1% formaldehyde at 37°C and then incubated for 5 min with glycine at 25°C. Cells were lysed in SDS lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing 1 mM PMSF for 10 min on ice. Genomic DNA was fragmented into 200 to 1000 bp by sonication. ChIP samples were used for the immunoprecipitation with anti-HSF4 and nonspecific IgG, and 20 µL of the ChIP sample was used as the input. Protein A + G agarose was added to each immunoprecipitation and incubated at 4°C for 2 h with rotation. The immunoprecipitated DNA was then amplified by real-time PCR. ChIP primer for the GJB2 promoter: 5’-CCTCATCCCTCTCATGCTGT-3’ (forward) and 5’-CATCCTGCTGAATGTCACTGA-3’ (reverse).

In vivo tumorigenicity

Fifteen male nude mice (BALB/c nude, 5-week-old) were fed with free water and food intake in a standardized environment (room temperature: 23 ± 2°C; relative humidity: 55 ± 5%; light-dark schedule: lights on from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m.). All mice were divided into the pc-DNA group, pc-HSF4, pc-HSF4+sh-NC group, pc-HSF4+sh-GJB2 group and pc-HSF4+sh-GJB2+SC79 group at random by means of drawing lots (n = 3), and a total of 100 µl A549 cell suspension (5 × 106) carrying pcDNA, pc-HSF4 with or without transfection of sh-nc or sh-GJB2 were injected subcutaneously into the right axillary region of the mice with or without treatment with SC79 (4 μg/g body weight, intraperitoneal injection once a day, from the 7th day after the tumor cells were inoculated until the end of the experiment). The tumor weight was monitored over a 4-week period. Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. Tumor size was measured every 4 days using a Vernier caliper according to the modified ellipsoidal formula: volume = ½ (length × width2). Tumor xenograft studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, and animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc. (No. JSAB24011C). Due to the limitations of our hospital’s animal experimentation facilities and long waiting time for appointments, we chose Jiangsu Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc. to conduct the animal experiments and obtained approval from the Ethics Committee.

H&E assay

After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded with wax, the tissues were divided into 5 µm-thick sections. After being dewaxed with xylene and ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, absolute), the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and eosin for 2 min (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, C0105S). After sealing with neutral resin, micrographs were obtained.

Immunohistochemical assay

After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded with wax, the tissues were divided into 5 µm-thick sections. After being dewaxed with xylene and ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, absolute), the sections were heated in citrate buffer to unmask antigenic sites and inactivated with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After 1 h of blocking in 5% normal goat serum, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (1:100; #AF0198, Affinity) and a biotinylated secondary antibody (IgG; 1:1,000; #ab6721, Abcam). Positive signals in the tissue samples were detected using 3, 3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen solution. After counterstaining with hematoxylin and sealing with neutral resin, the micrographs were obtained.

Statistical study

GraphPad Prism version 8 was used for data analysis. The assays were repeated a minimum of three times. All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare the mean differences among various groups. The significance level was set at p < .05.

Data mining

The UALCAN Database (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) was used to analyze the differential gene expression patterns and prognostic value of GJB2 in LUAD. Combining UCSC (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) and JARSPAR (http://jaspar.genereg.net/), the putative upstream transcriptional factors of GJB2 and their consensus sequences within the promoter region were identified.

Results

Elevated GJB2 expression in LUAD and its correlation with poor prognosis of LUAD patients

To screen for genes potentially involved in LUAD, the top 25 upregulated genes were retrieved from the UALCAN database. Among these candidates, GJB2 attracted our attention due to its emerging oncogenic roles in multiple malignancies (Figure 1A). As shown in Figure 1B-C, GJB2 mRNA levels were markedly higher in LUAD tissues compared with normal lung tissues. Kaplan–Meier analysis further revealed that high GJB2 expression was significantly associated with shorter overall survival in LUAD patients. Compared with normal bronchial BEAS-2B cells, GJB2 expression was significantly elevated in LUAD cells (A549 and NCI-H358) at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure 1D-E), confirming the aberrant upregulation of GJB2 in LUAD.

Elevated GJB2 expression in LUAD and its correlation with poor prognosis of LUAD patients. A. Heatmap of 25 up-regulated genes in LUAD. B. Prediction of GJB2 expression in LUAD by UALCAN. C. Assessment of the prognostic value of GJB2 in UALCAN. D. RT-qPCR analysis of GJB2 in LUAD cells. E. Western blotting analysis of GJB2 in LUAD cells. *** p < .001, by one-way ANOVA (D-E) with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Depleted GJB2 weakens cellular proliferation, metastasis and induces apoptosis in LUAD

To investigate the functional significance of GJB2 upregulation, GJB2 was silenced in A549 and NCI-H358 cells using specific shRNAs. As verified by RT-qPCR and western blotting, GJB2 mRNA level and protein level were significantly depleted after shRNA transfection (Figure 2A-B). When GJB2 expression was depleted, the CCK-8 curve showed that the activities of both A549 and NCI-H358 cells were strongly reduced, with a time-dependent decrease in absorbance compared to control cells (Figure 2C). Flow cytometry data indicated that the apoptosis of LUAD cells was promoted by the absence of GJB2 (Figure 2D). Scratch and transwell assays demonstrated that in response to GJB2 silencing, the migration and invasion of LUAD cells were inhibited (Figure 2E-F). These results indicate that GJB2 is essential for maintaining the aggressive phenotype of LUAD cells.

Cell activity curve in CCK-8 assay. D. The distribution of apoptotic cells in flow cytometric assay. E. Scratch assay for cellular migration. F. Transwell assay for cellular invasion. *** p < .001, by Student’s t-test.

Depleted GJB2 disrupts the mitochondrial homeostasis in LUAD cells

Given that connexin proteins have been detected in mitochondria and implicated in mitochondrial function [26], we next assessed whether GJB2 affected mitochondrial homeostasis. As illustrated by the JC-1 probe, the red/green fluorescence ratio was decreased when GJB2 was downregulated, indicating that the mitochondrial membrane potential in LUAD cells was depolarized (Figure 3A). Measurement of intracellular ROS using a fluorescent probe showed that GJB2 deficiency reduced ROS formation (Figure 3B). In addition, when GJB2 was disrupted, the expression of mitochondrial fusion proteins, including MFN1 and MFN2, was reduced, whereas the expression of mitochondrial fission protein DRP1 was increased (Figure 3C). In addition, in line with the mitochondrial dysfunction, ATP synthesis was inhibited in LUAD cells by GJB2 knockdown (Figure 3D). Collectively, these data suggest that GJB2 is required for maintaining mitochondrial function and dynamics in LUAD cells.

Western blotting analysis of mitochondrial fission and fusion proteins. D. ATP assay kit for ATP synthesis. ** p < .01 and *** p < .001, by Student’s t-test.

GJB2 is transcriptionally targeted by HSF4

To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanism of GJB2 overexpression, the potential transcription factors were explored. Based on the UALCAN tool, HSF4, HSF1, and GLIS3 are probable transcription factors binding to the GJB2 promoter region (Figure 4A). The motifs of their binding sites in the GJB2 promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database (Figure 4B). Then each candidate transcription factor was overexpressed individually. After infection with pc-HSF4, pc-HSF1, pc-GLIS3, HSF4, HSF1, and GLIS3 were found to be overexpressed (Figure 4C). . The luciferase reporter assay revealed that the luciferase activity of the GJB2 promoter was remarkably increased in LUAD cells (Figure 4D), suggesting transcriptional activation. Interestingly, the luciferase activity of the GJB2 promoter was increased by upregulation of HSF4, but not HSF1 or GLIS3 (Figure 4E). ChIP assay also demonstrated the enrichment of GJB2 promoter was increased in the anti-HSF4 group (Figure 4F). Moreover, GJB2 expression was increased in response to HSF4 overexpression (Figure 4G-H). These findings establish HSF4 as a direct transcriptional activator of GJB2 in LUAD cells.

SC79 reverses the impacts of GJB2 knockdown on the aggressive behaviors and mitochondrial homeostasis in LUAD

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is critically involved in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis, cell survival, and metabolism in lung cancer [27,28]. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that GJB2 can activate the PI3K/AKT pathway in ovarian cancer and pancreatic cancer [11,24]. We therefore hypothesized that GJB2 might exert its effects on mitochondrial function and malignant phenotypes through this pathway. To test this, we first examined the phosphorylation status of PI3K and AKT. Upon ablation of GJB2, p-PI3K and p-AKT expressions were unexpectedly repressed in LUAD cells (Figure 5A), indicating that GJB2 sustains PI3K/AKT signaling. At the same time, the expression of p-AKT, rather than p-PI3K, was increased by supplementation with the small-molecule AKT activator SC79 (Figure 5B). As expected, p-AKT expression was decreased by LY294002, the major inhibitor of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, similar to GJB2 knockdown. Under the condition, GJB2 silencing was unable to further decrease p-AKT expression (Fig. S1A). Moreover, SC79 expedited the activity of LUAD cells which was attenuated by GJB2 deficiency (Figure 5C). Despite that LY294002 or interference with GJB2 also impaired cell viability, combined LY294002 and sh-GJB2 did not show superimposed effects (Fig. S1B). In contrast, the loss of GJB2 or treatment with LY294002 strengthened the apoptotic capability of LUAD cells. However, the impacts of sh-GJB2 were offset by SC79, which could not be further aggravated by LY294002 (Figure 5D; Figure S1C).The number of migrated and invaded cells was decreased by GJB2 interference, and was then elevated again by SC79 (Figure 5E-F). LY294002 also impaired cell migration and invasion. However, there were no significant difference between the sh-GJB2 + LY294002 group and sh-GJB2 group or sh-NC+LY294002 group (Fig. S1D-E). In addition, GJB2 downregulation lowered mitochondrial activity, which was reversed by SC79 treatment (Figure 5G). SC79 facilitated ROS formation which was previously suppressed by GJB2 knockdown (Figure 5H). When exposed to SC79, MFN1 and MFN2 expression was elevated, and DRP1 expression in GJB2-depleting LUAD cells was reduced (Figure 5I). In addition, GJB2 deficiency suppressed ATP synthesis in LUAD cells, which was increased again by SC79 (Figure 5J). As demonstrated in Figure 5K, the OCR, a measure of mitochondrial respiration, of LUAD cells was declined when GJB2 was silenced, which was then enhanced by SC79 (Figure 5K). Together, these data indicate that the PI3K/AKT pathway serves as a key downstream mediator of GJB2 in regulating both the malignant behaviors and mitochondrial homeostasis of LUAD cells.

HSF4 drives tumor erosion via transcriptionally up-regulating GJB2 and stimulating the PI3K/AKT pathway

To validate the HSF4/GJB2/PI3K/AKT axis in vivo, a xenograft tumor model was established. After HSF4 was overexpressed, the tumor size and tumor weight were increased, which were both decreased by interference with GJB2 (Figure 6A-C). As analyzed by western blotting, p-PI3K and p-AKT expression was increased by HSF4 elevation. Furthermore, similar to HSF4 overexpression, SC79 also increased p-PI3K and p-AKT expression, both of which were inhibited by GJB2 silencing in the tumor tissues of HSF4-overexpressing mice (Figure 6D). In addition, the loss of GJB2 lowered Ki67 expression, which was previously enhanced by HSF4. Under these conditions, Ki67 expression was increased by SC79 treatment (Figure 6E). H&E staining revealed that HSF4 aggravated pulmonary damage, which was efficiently mitigated by GJB2 ablation and aggravated again in the presence of SC79 (Figure 6F). These in vivo findings demonstrate that the HSF4/GJB2 axis promotes tumor growth and pulmonary damage through activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway.

Western blotting analysis of PI3K/AKT pathway activity. E. Immunohistochemical assay for Ki67. F. H&E staining of tumor sections. *** p < .001, by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Discussion

Dysregulation of GJIC or the hemichannels formed by GJB2 is directly associated with oncogenesis [9,29]. In lung cancer, connexins are involved in brain metastasis [30]. Previous studies have shown that GJIC expression is decreased in lung carcinoma and mediates tissue homeostasis and phenotypic regulation [31]. In various malignancies affecting the hepatic, ovary, cervical and pancreatic regions [10,12,32,33], GJB2 has been increasingly reported to be highly expressed and linked with worse outcome. In this study, by comparing clinical data and experimental cells, we observed a notable upregulation of GJB2 within LUAD tissues and cells and its correlation with worse overall survival of LUAD patients, in concordance with previous studies [13–16,34,35].

GJB2 has been recognized as a multifaceted player in tumor biology. Functionally, overexpression of GJB2 increases glycolysis, proliferation, migration, and stemness, protects against apoptosis in LUAD [15,16,36] and induces the migration and invasive potential of NSCLC cells [35]. Consistently, our current work substantiated that deficiency of GJB2 suppressed LUAD cell viability, migration, invasion, and induced cell apoptosis.

Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles that are the main cellular power generators, providing most of their energy in the form of ATP to meet the energetic and anapleurotic demands of malignant transformation, unregulated proliferation, and tumor progression [37]. Two opposing processes, fission and fusion, can continuously reshape the dynamic balance of form, function, and number of mitochondria, the switch between which is essential for maintaining cellular physiological activity [38]. Dysfunctional mitochondrial dynamics are involved in the initiation and progression of malignant tumors [39]. DRP1 dynamically regulates mitochondrial morphology, and its recruitment to the outer mitochondrial membrane is critical for mitochondrial fission [40]. Fusion of the outer mitochondrial membrane is mediated by MFN1 and MFN2, which form homodimers or heterodimers on two closely adjacent mitochondria [40]. Increased fusion and impaired fission are observed in NSCLC [41]. In particular, mitochondrial DRP1 is associated with the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of LUAD cells [42]. Connexin proteins are abundant in mitochondria and participate in mitochondrial function [43]. Our findings showed that after GJB2 silencing, the mitochondrial membrane potential was impaired, ROS formation was suppressed, MFN1 and MFN2 expression was downregulated, DRP1 expression was upregulated, and ATP synthesis was restrained. We acknowledge that our assessment of ROS levels in the present study relied on a general fluorescent probe (such as DCFH-DA, Figure 3B), which reflects total intracellular ROS but does not differentiate among individual species, such as superoxide (O2•−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), or hydroxyl radical (•OH). Given that we observed concurrent mitochondrial membrane potential dissipation and altered mitochondrial dynamics, it is plausible that mitochondrially derived superoxide represents a major contributing species. Nevertheless, the specific ROS culprit(s) driving the observed phenotypes remain to be identified. Future investigations employing species-specific probes, genetically encoded redox sensors, or antioxidant panels will be necessary to dissect the contributions of distinct ROS types and to further refine the mechanistic model. Despite this limitation, the total ROS data, in conjunction with the mitochondrial functional assays, provide sufficient evidence to support the involvement of oxidative stress in GJB2-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis disruption.

A recent review summarized that GJB2 modifications can also participate in the central hub of post-transcriptional regulation [44]. In our ongoing investigation, HSF4, HSF1, and GLIS3 were predicted as probable transcription factors upstream of GJB2. Meanwhile, the luciferase activity of the GJB2 promoter increased in LUAD cells, suggesting a transcriptional mechanism of GJB2. HSF4 transactivates c-MET as a transcription factor in colorectal cancer [45]. Herein, only HSF4 overexpression increased the luciferase activity of the GJB2 promoter and upregulated GJB2 expression. In particular, HSF4 has been acknowledged as an oncogene in colon carcinoma [45,46]. Our in vivo studies substantiated the tumor-promoting role of HSF4 in LUAD, as evidenced by the elevated tumor size, tumor weight, Ki67 expression, and aggravated pulmonary injury, which were all partially reversed after GJB2 knockdown.

Previous studies have suggested that the biological role of GJB2 in tumors is partly dependent on PI3K/AKT signaling [24]. Yang et al. showed that GJB2 functionally interacts with PI3K/AKT signaling to increase epithelial-mesenchymal transition and gefitinib resistance in NSCLC cells [25]. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is frequently altered in NSCLC, which in turn affects tumor advancement and therapy resistance [23,47]. Importantly, targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway can orchestrate mitochondrial homeostasis in lung cancer [48,49]. Through recruiting AKT to the cell membrane, AKT phosphorylation is promoted by PI3K, which activates downstream transduction molecules to activate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [50]. Our study showed that GJB2 ablation lowered both p-PI3K and p-AKT expression in LUAD cells, and p-AKT expression was nullified after administering SC-79. Furthermore, upon exposure to SC79, the suppressive effects of GJB2 insufficiency on the malignant process and mitochondrial function in LUAD cells were partially compensated. In both HSF4-overexpressing and GJB2-depleting tumor mice, SC79 also boosted tumor growth, upregulated p-AKT and Ki67 expression, and impaired lung morphology. However, in the presence of LY294002, further GJB2 knockdown did not show superimposed effects.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that the HSF4/GJB2/PI3K/AKT axis mediates the malignant process and mitochondrial homeostasis in LUAD. This finding has introduced an undiscovered physiological role for GJB2 in mitochondrial homeostasis and a novel upstream and downstream regulatory mechanism of GJB2 in LUAD, underscoring the significant implications of GJB2 for molecular-targeted therapy for LUAD. However, a limitation of this work is the absence of direct clinical validation using patient-derived tissues. Nevertheless, published clinical data showing that high GJB2, HSF4 expression and PI3K/AKT pathway activation are independently correlated with adverse clinicopathological features [19,51–56] provide indirect support for the clinical relevance of this axis. Further validation in annotated clinical cohorts will be essential to translate these findings into prognostic or therapeutic applications.
