Association of exposure to organophosphate pesticides with reproductive lifespan
Authors: Xianli Zhou, Wanting Yu, Jinying Xie, Senyuan Huang, Yadou Zhan, Lina Cao, Xin Chen, Xuelan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrganophosphate pesticides (OPs) have been found to have reproductive toxicity with overt endocrine disruption potential, yet the relationships between OPs with reproductive lifespan remain unexplored.ObjectiveWe aimed to investigate associations between urinary OP concentrations with reproductive lifespan.MethodThis study included 1,356 postmenopausal women who participated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 1999 to 2018 to evaluate the relationship between OP exposure and reproductive lifespan. Urine was analysed using gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry for concentrations of six dialkyl phosphate (DAP) metabolites of OPs.ResultMultivariate linear regression and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) models revealed that total urinary DAP metabolite concentrations (β = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.03–0.17, p = 0.006) were positively associated with age at menopause. Urinary dimethyl phosphate (DMP) concentration was positively associated with age at menopause (β = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.30–1.03, p = 0.001) and reproductive lifespan (β = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.26–1.02, p = 0.001). Furthermore, BKMR and weighted quantile sum models identified DMP as the primary risk contributor amongst the six detected DAP metabolites (weight = 0.573 for age at menopause and weight = 0.634 for reproductive lifespan).ConclusionFor the mixed analysis, the overall exposure to DAP metabolites was associated with delayed age at menopause. For the individual analysis, DMP exposure was associated with delayed age at menopause and longer reproductive lifespan, and DMP was identified as the major risk factor amongst the six detected DAP metabolites. Future research should prioritise DMP to further elucidate impacts of OPs exposure on female reproductive health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04455-6.Keywords: Organophosphate pesticides (OPs), Dialkyl phosphates (DAPs), Menopause, Reproductive lifespan

Introduction

Organophosphate pesticides (OPs) are a class of pesticides widely used in household and agricultural practices to kill broad-spectrum insects and prevent insect-borne diseases [1]. Despite stringent regulatory actions by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency restricting the use of certain OPs due to their irreversible neurotoxicological effects, human exposure to these compounds remains prevalent in the general population [2–4]. This persistent exposure primarily arises from multifaceted environmental sources and occupational hazards underscoring the continued public health concern [5].

Accumulating evidence revealed that OPs are potential endocrine disrupting chemicals. Some studies indicated that exposure to OPs disrupted the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis and thus interfered with oestrogen secretion, inhibited follicle growth and oocyte maturation and damaged sperm quality [6–12]. In addition, a study by Zhang et al. showed that exposure to OPs can disrupt the regularity of the menstrual cycle [13]. However, this study on the association between OP exposure and menstrual cycle regularity is cross-sectional, making it difficult to reflect the impact of OP exposure on women’s reproductive lifespan over a longer period.

Reproductive lifespan, referring to the interval from menarche to menopause, both of which are highly hormonally regulated processes, reflects the female fertility period [14, 15]. The average female reproductive lifespan has been statistically determined to be 35.85 years [16]. A shorter reproductive lifespan, particularly less than 33 years, is associated with fewer opportunities for initiating pregnancies and an increased risk of chronic conditions, such as osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease [17, 18]. Conversely, a longer reproductive lifespan does not necessarily correlate with better pregnancy outcomes because age-related comorbidities, such as diabetes and hypertension, can negatively impact reproductive health and also increase the risk of oestrogen-related cancers, including endometrial and breast cancers [14, 19]. These findings underscore the importance of the length of reproductive lifespan when examining long-term health outcomes in women.

Given that reproductive lifespan is a more comprehensive and valuable indicator of women’s reproductive health, we aimed to explore the associations of OP exposure on the entire reproductive lifespan of women who participated in National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study population

Our study included data of female from eight NHANES cycles: 1999–2000 (n = 5,082), 2001–2002 (n = 5,708), 2003–2004 (n = 5,152), 2005–2006 (n = 5,268), 2007–2008 (n = 5,053), 2011–2012 (n = 4,900), 2015–2016 (n = 5,079) and 2017–2018 (n = 4,697). The cycles of 2009–2010 and 2013–2014 were excluded because they did not include DAP metabolite testing. NHANES is a large comprehensive survey project conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) at the U.S. Center of Disease Control and Prevention, which periodically collects the data of demographic, health status, behaviour, environmental risk factors and biosamples from participants. NHANES has obtained written consent from all participants, as well as approval from the NCHS Ethics Review Board.

For the current analysis, 40,939 female participants were initially obtained. After excluding women with missing data on age at menarche or last menstruation, as well as participants whose answer to the question ‘Reason for not having a period in the past 12 months’ was ‘pregnancy, breastfeeding or undergoing ovariectomy or hysterectomy before or at the time of their last menstruation’, 5,644 females were preliminarily left. After excluding participants without DAP metabolites measurements and covariates, a total of 1,356 women were ultimately included in the study population for the analysis of reproductive lifespan (Fig. 1).

Flow of eligible participants selection

Measurement of urinary DAP metabolites

Urinary DAPs, which are nonspecific metabolites of OPs, serve as proxies for assessing OP exposure levels [20]. In this study, OP exposure was evaluated by measuring six common DAP metabolites in urine samples, including dimethyl phosphate (DMP), diethyl phosphate (DEP), dimethyl thiophosphate (DMTP), diethyl thiophosphate (DETP), dimethyl dithiophosphate (DMDTP) and diethyl dithiophosphate (DEDTP). Although these metabolites are not specific to pesticides, they are still widely applied as the biomarkers of OPs exposure [20, 21].

At the mobile examination centres, urinary DAP metabolites samples were collected and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Then, the concentrations of six metabolites were measured by solid phase extraction (SPE) coupled with isotope dilution–ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC)–tandem mass spectrometry. Specifically, the method relies on an enzymatic hydrolysis of urinary conjugates, automated off–line SPE to preconcentrate the target compounds while minimising urine matrix potential interferences to increase the overall sensitivity and specificity. The deconjugated target analytes in the urine extract are separated on an UHPLC system with reversed phase chromatography and quantified by isotope dilution tandem mass spectrometry. The lower limits of detection (LOD) varied throughout the different cycles. Urinary DAP metabolites measurements below LOD were calculated as the LOD divided by the square root of 2. In addition, urinary creatinine was measured by the modified kinetic Jaff ´e method [22].

Assessment of reproductive endpoint

Information on age at menarche and natural menopause was based on self-report. The age at menarche was determined by asking participants the age at their first menstrual period. The age at menopause was determined by asking the age at menopause of participants who had experienced natural menopause. Reproductive lifespan was calculated as age at menopause minus age at menarche.

Covariates

Our study included the following covariates: age at screening, race/ethnicity, education, body mass index (BMI), smoking, alcohol use, and parity. Age was expressed as continuous data. Race/ethnicity was categorised into non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican Americans, other Hispanic and other race. Educational attainment was divided into three levels: under high school, high school graduation and above high school. BMI was calculated by dividing weight in kilograms by measured height in meters squared. Parity was determined by asking participants the pregnancies resulting in live births. Participants who had less than 12 alcohol drinks a year were defined as nondrinkers while those who had more than 12 alcohol drinks were defined as drinkers. Participants who smoked less than 100 cigarettes in the lifetime were defined as nonsmokers, while those who smoked more than 100 cigarettes were defined as smokers [23].

To address potential confounding and justify covariate selection, we developed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Fig. S1) based on established causal inference frameworks. The DAG illustrated the hypothesised pathways between covariates (e.g. age, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, smoking, alcohol use, parity), exposure (urinary DAP metabolites) and outcome (reproductive lifespan). It clarifies that covariates were selected as common causes of exposure and outcome, ensuring they met the criteria for confounders without introducing collider bias.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented in terms as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), whereas proportions were used for categorical variable. To assess potential selection bias, we compared the key baseline characteristics between the final analytical sample and naturally postmenopausal women excluded due to missing data on urinary DAP metabolite measurements and covariates. The population characteristics of the study population in different groups were compared by a chi-square test for categorical variables, and a two-tail student t-test for continuous variables. Moreover, to account for urine dilution, urinary DAP metabolites were firstly adjusted by urinary creatinine levels (DAP concentrations [µg/L] divided by creatinine [mg/dL]). All concentrations of the target chemicals presented a right-skewed distribution, so they were then logarithmic transformed. Geometric means, arithmetic means and percentiles of urinary DAP metabolite concentrations were all presented. Pairwise Pearson correlations were also applied to assess the correlations between six DAP metabolites.

Multivariate linear regression model was applied to assess the relationship between DAP metabolites with age at menarche, age at menopause and reproductive lifespan. We adjusted for age at screening, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, smoking, alcohol use, and parity. The coefficients estimated by the multivariate linear regression model were reported as β values and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A p-value < 0.05 was reported to be statistically significant.

To account for the increased risk of type-I error due to multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied [24, 25].​ Specifically, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to adjust the p-value derived from the significance tests of the seven independent variables (six individual DAP metabolites and total DAP metabolites) across the three reproductive outcomes (a total of 21 statistical tests). The adjusted p-value, reported as q-value, were interpreted to control the expected proportion of false discoveries at a level of 0.05 [24]. Associations with a q–value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant after FDR correction, in addition to the unadjusted p-value < 0.05 [24].

To address the limitations of multivariate linear regression, which assumes linearity and additivity and is unable to capture complex mixture effects or interactions, we employed the Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) model. This approach accommodates nonlinear dose–response relationships, interactions amongst DAP metabolites and overall mixture effects [26, 27]. The BKMR model was implemented to evaluate the combined associations of urinary log-transformed DAP metabolites with reproductive lifespan, adjusted for age at screening, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, smoking, alcohol use, and parity. This model utilises kernel functions to map exposure variables into a high-dimensional feature space, thereby capturing nonlinear associations and interactions amongst variables. A Gaussian predictive approach was applied to enhance model fitting efficiency [28].

The BKMR model showed the exposure–response surfaces through several approaches. Specifically, we estimated the cumulative effect of the DAP mixture by comparing outcomes when all metabolites were fixed at a given quantile. Single–metabolite effects were assessed by varying one metabolite while holding others at certain quantiles. Dose–response relationships for each DAP metabolite with reproductive outcomes (age at menarche, age at menopause, reproductive lifespan) were plotted with other metabolites fixed at their 50th percentiles. The relative importance of each DAP metabolite was quantified using the posterior inclusion probability (PIP), where a PIP > 0.5 generally indicates a meaningful contribution [29]. A high PIP value indicates a substantial influence of the individual DAP metabolite on outcomes. Finally, pairwise chemical interactions were examined while fixing remaining metabolites at their median concentrations [30].

In addition, the weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression was applied to validate the BKMR results and quantify relative contributions of each DAP to the mixture effect [31]. We adjusted for age at screening, race/ethnicity, education, BMI, smoking, alcohol use, and parity. WQS generates a single weighted index (WQS_index) representing the overall mixture burden. Its constraint (∑w = 1, w ≥ 0) ensures weights reflect relative importance within the mixture. A higher weight value indicates a greater influence of the individual DAP metabolite on outcomes.

Multivariate linear regression was conducted in SPSS v25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). BKMR (bkmr package) was performed using R package ‘bkmr’ (R version 4.4.3) and the quantifiable and visualised results of BKMR model were plotted with package ‘ggplot’. WQS model was fitted by R package ‘gWQS’ (R version 4.4.3).

Associations between urinary DAP metabolites and reproductive lifespan analysed by multivariate linear regression model

The results of the multivariable linear regression analyses, presenting the p-values and the FDR-adjusted q-values, are shown in the Table 3. After adjusting for the relevant covariates, multivariable linear regression analysis revealed no statistically significant associations between any DAP metabolite and age at menarche. Uncorrected analyses suggested that several DAP metabolites were significantly associated with a delayed age at menopause and a longer reproductive lifespan. Notably, urinary DMP (β = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.30–1.03, p = 0.001), DMTP (β = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.12–0.77, p = 0.008) and total urinary DAP metabolites (β = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.03–0.17, p = 0.006) concentrations were positively associated with age at menopause. Urinary DMP (β = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.26–1.02, p = 0.001), DMTP (β = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.08–0.76, p = 0.016) and total urinary DAP metabolites (β = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.02–0.16, p = 0.017) concentrations were positively associated with reproductive lifespan. Similarly, urinary DAP metabolite concentrations, including DEP, DETP, DMDTP and DEDTP, showed nominally significant associations (p < 0.05) with at least one of these two outcomes. However, after rigorous FDR correction, only positive associations between DMP and DMTP exposure with delayed age at menopause and longer reproductive lifespan remained statistically significant (q = 0.011, 0.028, 0.024, 0.011, 0.034, 0.035 respectively).

Discussion

Our study evaluated associations of six DAP metabolite exposure in urine and their mixtures with age at menarche, age at menopause and reproductive lifespan in women from NHANES survey. For the mixed analysis, the overall exposure to DAP metabolites was associated with delayed age at menopause. For the individual analysis, DMP exposure was positively associated with delayed age at menopause and longer reproductive lifespan. Additionally, BKMR and WQS models consistently identified DMP as the most influential factor in overall effect.

We found that DMP presented the highest detection frequency (80.09%) amongst the six metabolites of DAP metabolites, consistent with the previous research results of Aguilar-Garduño (81%) and Blanco-Munoz (92.7%) [32, 33]. We also revealed positive associations between DMP exposure with age at menopause and reproductive lifespan. Some studies have shown that urinary DMP levels were associated with the decreased serum-free androgen index levels in females, the decreased inhibin B levels in male, the decreased FSH levels and the decreased testosterone/ oestradiol ratio in foetal and chromosomal abnormalities in male sperm [9, 32, 34]. Additionally, a previous study has also presented that urinary DMP level was associated with reduced chance of successful implantation, clinical pregnancy and live birth [35]. Based on these studies, more attention should be paid on the impact of DMP on human reproductive health in future research.

Our study revealed positive correlations between DMP concentration with age at menopause and reproductive lifespan. Animal studies and in vitro studies have found that OP exposure can disrupt the expression and biosynthesis of the gonadotrophin releasing hormone (GnRH) gene, thereby interfering with the HPG axis and ultimately leading to a decrease in oestradiol levels [7, 36]. OPs presented an estrogenic action in the feedback negative regulation of pituitary follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) releasing, which can lead to a decrease in FSH levels [7]. Moreover, previous studies showed that women with reduced oestradiol levels and decreased FSH levels tend to enter menopause later [37]. Therefore, these findings demonstrated that exposure to DAP metabolites may lead to delayed age at menopause and increased reproductive lifespan by hindering the biosynthesis of oestradiol and FSH. Although a priori, extending the reproductive lifespan may appear advantageous for increasing pregnancy opportunities, and studies have shown that extending the reproductive lifespan is associated with adverse outcomes [14, 38–41].

Farr et al. found that exposure to OPs was significantly associated with prolonged menstrual cycles, missed periods and an increased risk of intermenstrual bleeding [42]. These findings suggested that OPs may disrupt the dynamic balance between oestrogen and FSH by interfering with the HPG axis or exerting direct ovarian toxicity, thereby leading to menstrual irregularities [7, 43]. Specifically, OPs may delay follicular depletion and the onset of menopause by inhibiting oestrogen synthesis or interfering with FSH secretion [7]. As a sensitive indicator of female reproductive health, menstrual disturbances often reflect early endocrine dysfunction and may foreshadow subsequent changes in age at menopause. Furthermore, this finding highlights the cumulative impact of OP exposure on the female reproductive axis, spanning from short-term menstrual regulation to the long-term process of menopausal transition. Compared with menstrual cycle regularity, which only reflects short-term fluctuations, reproductive lifespan more comprehensively captures the long-term impact of OPs exposure on women’s reproductive health. We are the first study to investigate the association between Ops exposure and reproductive lifespan.

Our research has several advantages. Firstly, the main strengths of our study are the larger sample size, which providing high statistical power to identify modest associations, and the inclusion of ethnically diverse populations thereby enhanced the generalisability of our findings. Secondly, our study employed data from NHANES with high-quality control over the sampling procedures and laboratory measurements of urinary DAP metabolites levels. Finally, to draw a robust and strong conclusion, we adopted BKMR and WQS models to evaluate systematically the relationship between single and mixed OPs exposure with reproductive lifespan.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the association between OP exposure and reproductive lifespan. Second, potential selection bias cannot be ruled out. Comparisons showed that the analytical sample differed from participants excluded due to missing DAP or covariate data in terms of BMI, race and education, indicating that missingness may not be completely at random and could affect sample representativeness. Third, the mixture analyses (BKMR and WQS) did not incorporate the complex survey design and sampling weights of NHANES. While this is unlikely to reverse the direction of the observed associations, it may influence the precision of the estimates and limit the generalizability of the mixture-specific results to the national population. Fourth, a key limitation is the temporal misalignment between exposure assessment and reproductive events. Urinary DAP metabolites reflect recent exposure, whereas menarche occurred decades earlier. Thus, the null finding for age at menarche does not preclude a potential effect of peripubertal OP exposure, for which a single spot urinary biomarker serves as a suboptimal proxy. Fifth, despite adjustment for key covariates, residual confounding by unmeasured factors remains possible. Sixth, although FDR correction was applied to address bias from multiple comparisons in the several linear regression models, the residual risk of type-I errors cannot be fully ruled out, particularly given the exploratory nature of analysing multiple metabolites. Therefore, further validation through independent cohort studies or experimental research is needed to validate the conclusions. Finally, the use of single-spot urine measurements to assess exposure to rapidly metabolized OPs is a major constraint. Such measurements capture recent exposure rather than long-term cumulative burden, potentially leading to exposure misclassification in studies of outcomes that develop over decades, such as age at menopause or reproductive lifespan [23, 44]. Future longitudinal studies with repeated biospecimen collection across critical reproductive windows (e.g., perimenarche, perimenopause) are warranted to better characterize the cumulative effects of OPs on reproductive health.

Conclusion

In a national representative population, for the mixed analysis, we found that the overall DAP metabolite concentrations were associated with delayed age at menopause. For individual analysis, urinary DMP concentration was positively associated with delayed age at menopause and longer reproductive lifespan. Notably, DMP emerged as the most influential risk factor amongst the six DAP metabolites analysed. Our findings provide preliminary epidemiological evidence on the associations between OPs and female reproductive health, underscoring the necessity of prioritising DMP in subsequent investigations to clarify the health impacts of OPs exposure. Future longitudinal studies with repeated exposure assessments are warranted to establish temporal relationships and confirm these observations.
