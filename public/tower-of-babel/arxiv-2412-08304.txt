Robust entanglement measure for mixed quantum states
Dharmaraj Ramachandran, Aditya Dubey, Subrahmanyam S. G. Mantha, Radhika Vathsan

Abstract
We introduce an entanglement measure, the Modified Bloch Norm ( M ​ B ​ N \displaystyle MBN ), for finite-dimensional bipartite mixed states, based on the improved Bloch matrix criteria [ 1 ] . M ​ B ​ N \displaystyle MBN is demonstrated to be effective in analyzing the dynamics of bound entanglement—a valuable resource for quantum protocols where free entanglement may not be available. Through examples, we illustrate the applications of M ​ B ​ N \displaystyle MBN in accurately estimating the Entanglement Sudden Death (ESD) time and detecting behavior such as the freezing of entanglement. Additionally, we show that the error rate for entanglement measured using state estimation from a limited number of measurement copies is significantly lower when using M ​ B ​ N \displaystyle MBN compared to negativity. This demonstrates the robustness of M ​ B ​ N \displaystyle MBN under practical constraints.



I
Introduction

Entanglement is recognized as one of the most significant features of quantum systems and serves as a valuable resource contributing to the quantum advantage in various information-theoretic protocols
[
2
]
. This importance underscores the need to quantify the entanglement of a quantum state. Although numerous entanglement measures exist for pure states, it has been conclusively proven that determining the entanglement of an arbitrary mixed quantum state is an NP-hard problem
[
3
,
4
]
. Given the computational intractability of a complete solution, substantial research has focused on making entanglement quantification feasible under certain assumptions or constraints.

In this work, we introduce an entanglement measure that addresses two practical challenges in the entanglement quantification of mixed states: measuring bound entanglement, and estimating entanglement from measurements on a finite number of copies of the state.

I.1
Dynamics of bound entanglement

A finite-dimensional separable bipartite quantum state on a Hilbert space
\displaystyle H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
is represented by a density matrix that is a linear combination of separable density matrices
[
5
]
:

\rho_{\small AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{A,i}\otimes\rho_{B,i}.

(1)

A bipartite quantum state that cannot be expressed in the form given in Eq. (
1
) is entangled.

Entanglement in a quantum state is considered distillable or free if using
\displaystyle n
copies of the state, one can obtain
\displaystyle k\leq n
copies of maximally entangled states, using Local Operations and Classical Communications (LOCC)
[
6
]
. Entangled states that possess non-distillable entanglement are known as bound entangled states
[
7
]
, which are also useful across a range of applications
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. For instance, in 2006, Masanes
et al.
showed that all entangled bipartite states, whether bound or distillable, can be used in teleportation protocols
[
16
]
.

While the dynamics of distillable entanglement under various noisy channels have been studied extensively
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
, very little is known about the dynamics of bound entanglement
[
21
,
22
]
. In this work, we propose an entanglement measure suitable for studying the dynamics of both free and bound entangled states.

I.2
Entanglement quantification using finite copies of quantum state

In practice, an unknown quantum state is estimated from the expectation values of observables measured on a number of copies of the state. Error in estimation is inherent to the finite sample size taken for measurement. The number of observables needed to characterize an
\displaystyle N
level quantum system scales as
\displaystyle N^{2}-1
, leading to an exponential increase in the sample size requirement. Consequently, the error in estimation is expected to increase. Therefore, experimenters are constrained to quantify properties of quantum states, such as entanglement, using inherently imprecise expectation values
[
23
]
.

The simplest technique to estimate an
\displaystyle N
-dimensional quantum state is Linear Inversion (LI)
[
24
]
using the standard representation of a state in the basis of identity and the generators of
\displaystyle SU(N)
,
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
:

\rho_{\text{est}}=\frac{1}{N}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{N}+x_{i}\sigma_{i}\right].

(2)

Here
\displaystyle x_{i}
’s are estimated expectation values of the
\displaystyle\sigma_{i}
’s, which are traceless, Hermitian, orthogonal
\displaystyle N\times N
operators, the natural generalizations of the Pauli operators to
\displaystyle N
dimensions
[
25
]
.
Though computationally less demanding due to its simplicity, LI has a major limitation:
\displaystyle\rho_{\text{est}}
could be negative, violating a fundamental condition of a density operator.

Other standard approaches to estimate density operators rely on methods such as Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
and Bayesian Inference (BI)
[
29
,
30
]
. However, both MLE and BI are computationally intensive for arbitrary quantum states. Alternative methods, such as randomized measurement, seek to quantify entanglement without requiring full state tomography
[
31
,
32
]
.
There has been promising success in quantum state estimation using machine learning
[
33
,
34
]
, but a generalized model to faithfully estimate finite-dimensional quantum states remains a challenging task. The entanglement measure introduced in this work exhibits low error rates when based on inaccurate estimations of quantum states.

II
Existing Entanglement measures

There exist several entanglement measures for pure states, such as entanglement entropy
[
35
]
and geometric measures based on Bures distance
[
36
]
, which are straightforward to compute and satisfy the properties of an entanglement monotone. However, their extensions to mixed states are based on convex roof construction, which is computationally demanding.

Concurrence, an entanglement measure derived from entanglement of formation, has a closed-form expression for two-qubit systems
[
37
]
. For multipartite pure states, concurrence is generalized as tangle
[
38
]
, which quantifies only the residual entanglement in the system
[
39
,
40
]
. Beyond convex-roof construction and some other numerical approximation techniques
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
, there is no closed-form expression for calculating concurrence in arbitrary-dimensional mixed states.

The Positive Partial Transpose (PPT) criterion is a well-known separability criterion that asserts all density matrices with a negative partial transpose are entangled
[
45
,
46
]
. However, for systems of dimensions higher than
\displaystyle 2\otimes 3
, the PPT criterion is only necessary but not sufficient. This limitation leads to the existence of PPT entangled states, also known as bound entangled states, as first reported by Horodecki
et al.

[
47
]
.

A well known measure constructed based on PPT criterion is negativity which is defined as
[
48
]
,

\mathcal{N}(\rho):=\sum_{a_{i}<0}a_{i}

where
\displaystyle a_{i}
’s are the eigenvalues of the partial transposed density matrix.

Although negativity is a widely used entanglement measure, it cannot detect bound entangled states due to its basis in the PPT criterion. Several criteria, such as the Realignment Criterion
[
49
]
, the Computable Cross Norm Criterion (CCNR)
[
50
,
51
]
, and the Improved Bloch Matrix (IBM) criterion, which forms a family of entanglement detection criteria, have been proposed to detect bound entanglement. Among these, the Improved Bloch Matrix criterion stands out due to its strong relevance to experimentally measurable quantities and its flexibility. In this work, we adopt the Improved Bloch Matrix criterion to construct an entanglement measure capable of addressing this gap.

III
Separability based on Bloch matrix picture

Consider a bipartite composite system Hilbert space
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, where
\displaystyle\dim[\mathcal{H}_{A}]=N,\dim[\mathcal{H}_{B}]=M
. In terms of the generators
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}
of
\displaystyle SU(N)
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{j}
of
\displaystyle SU(M)

\rho_{AB}=\frac{1}{NM}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}r_{i}\lambda_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\right.\\
\left.\sum_{j=1}^{M^{2}-1}s_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\tilde{\lambda}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M^{2}-1}t_{ij}\lambda_{i}\otimes\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\right),

(3)

where
\displaystyle\lambda
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}
are traceless Hermitian orthogonal matrices
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
, commonly taken to be the
generalized Gell-Mann matrices
[
25

.
The coefficients
\displaystyle\{r_{1},r_{2}...r_{M^{2}-1}\}
and
\displaystyle\{s_{1},s_{2}...s_{N^{2}-1}\}
represent the local Bloch vectors
\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{t}

and
\displaystyle\mathbf{s}^{t}
of A and B subsystems respectively, while
\displaystyle T=[t_{ij}]
is the correlation matrix between the two subsystems.
The local density operators are represented in terms of the Bloch vectors:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Tr_{B}(\rho_{AB})=\rho_{A}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{N}+\sum_{i}r_{i}\lambda_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Tr_{A}(\rho_{AB})=\rho_{B}=\frac{1}{M}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\sum_{i}s_{j}\tilde{\lambda}_{i}\right).

The Bloch matrix representation of the state
\displaystyle\rho_{AB}
is then written as
[
54
]

B_{\rho_{AB}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\mathbf{s}^{t}\\
\mathbf{r}&T\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

This representation facilitates two well known separability criteria: the Correlation Matrix (CM) criterion
[
54
]
and Generalized Correlation Matrix (GCM) criterion
[
55
]
. Later, Shen
et al
gave improved separability criteria based on a modification of the Bloch matrix. We refer to this as the Improved Bloch Matrix (IBM) criteria, which prove to be more discriminant than the CM and GCM criteria
[
1
]
.
Their modified Bloch matrix is constructed by prepending
\displaystyle m

scaled copies of the first row and column of the Bloch matrix:

S_{m,a,b}(\rho)=\begin{bmatrix}abE_{m\times m}&bw_{m}(\mathbf{s})^{t}\\
aw_{m}(\mathbf{r})&T\end{bmatrix}

(5)

where
\displaystyle E_{m\times m}
is the
\displaystyle m\times m
matrix of ones,

\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
are positive real scale factors.
\displaystyle w_{m}(\mathbf{x})
for an
\displaystyle n
-dimensional column vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
is

w_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
.\\
.\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
.\\
.\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}&\begin{matrix}.\\
.\\
.\\
.\\
.\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}.\\
.\\
.\\
.\\
.\end{matrix}&\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
.\\
.\\
x_{n}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{m columns}}.

The IBM criteria for separability of
\displaystyle\rho_{AB}
states that
[
1
]

|S_{m,a,b}(\rho)|_{\text{tr}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(2ma^{2}+N^{2}-N)(2mb^{2}+M^{2}-M)},

(6)

where
\displaystyle|\;|_{tr}
denotes trace norm of the matrix.

The IBM criteria are necessary conditions for separability of mixed bipartite quantum states for all
\displaystyle a,b\in[0,\infty)
and
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

However the authors
[
1
]
have shown that discriminance of IBM always improves when m increases by fixing
\displaystyle a\text{ and }b
as
\displaystyle a=\sqrt{{2}/{M(M-1)}},\;b=\sqrt{{2}/{N(N-1)}}
. Thus, for all examples in this manuscript, we have fixed
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle b
to the above mentioned values and
\displaystyle m
to be
\displaystyle 4
, which was sufficient to detect entanglement in the chosen states.

It is worth noting that Eq-(
6
) reduces to the CM criterion at
\displaystyle a=b=0
and to GCM criterion at
\displaystyle m=a=b=1
.

Pure states:

Modified Bloch matrix
\displaystyle S_{m,a,b}^{s}
of a separable pure bipartite state
\displaystyle\rho_{s}
can always be expressed in terms of Bloch vectors of the subsystems as

S_{m,a,b}^{s}=\begin{bmatrix}bE_{m\times 1}\\
r\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}aE_{\small 1\times m}&s^{t}\end{bmatrix}

By properties of matrix norms, the trace norm of
\displaystyle S^{s}_{m,a,b}
is

|S^{s}_{m,a,b}|_{tr}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(2ma^{2}+N^{2}-N)(2mb^{2}+M^{2}-M)}.

Thus IBM criteria become both necessary and sufficient conditions for separability for all finite dimensional pure bipartite quantum states
[
1
]
.

IV
Bipartite Measure

We quantify the violation of the IBM criterion exhibited by a bipartite quantum state
\displaystyle\rho
by the quantity

V_{\rho}=|S_{m,a,b}|_{\text{tr}}-c,

(7)

where
\displaystyle c=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(2ma^{2}+N^{2}-N)(2mb^{2}+M^{2}-M)}
, the right hand side of the inequality Eq.(
6
).
We propose a bipartite measure of entanglement, the Modified Bloch matrix Norm (
\displaystyle MBN
) of a bipartite quantum state on
\displaystyle H^{N}\otimes H^{M}
as

\displaystyle\displaystyle MBN(\rho)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\max(0,V_{\rho})/V(\rho_{m}),

(8)

where the normalisation constant
\displaystyle V(\rho_{m})
of the maximally entangled state
\displaystyle\rho_{m}
of the given bipartition. This is a family of measures depending on the parameters
\displaystyle a,b
and
\displaystyle m
.

Properties of
\displaystyle MBN

We now verify that
\displaystyle MBN
satisfies all the requisite properties of a valid entanglement measure.

Discriminance:

An entanglement measure must be zero for a separable state. This property is known as weak discriminance:

E(\rho)=0\quad\forall\quad\rho\in\Omega_{s},

where
\displaystyle\Omega_{s}
denotes the set of separable states. The measure is strongly discriminant if the converse is also true: if
\displaystyle E(\rho)\neq 0
for all entangled states.

The IBM criterion Eq.(
6
) is not violated by separable states. Therefore the derived
\displaystyle MBN
is inherently weakly discriminant.

On the other hand,
\displaystyle IBM
is violated by all entangled pure states but not all entangled states
as counterexamples can show. Thus
\displaystyle MBN
is strongly discriminant for only pure states and weakly discriminant in general.

While
\displaystyle MBN
can detect various bound entangled states it can neither detect all bound entangled nor freely entangled states.
Now the separability problem itself is NP-hard
[
3
]
, and proving a criterion to be strongly discriminant is therefore also hard. In fact, formulating a completely discriminant and computable entanglement measure for higher-dimensional mixed states is expected to be extremely challenging, if not infeasible.

Convexity:

Let
\displaystyle\rho
be a convex combination of two density matrices
\displaystyle\rho_{1}
and
\displaystyle\rho_{2}
,
\displaystyle\rho=p\rho_{1}+(1-p)\rho_{2}
. A positive function
\displaystyle E(\rho)
is convex if

E(\rho)\leq pE(\rho_{1})+(1-p)E(\rho_{2}).

(9)

In terms of the modified Bloch matrices
\displaystyle S({\rho_{1}})
and
\displaystyle S({\rho_{2}})
, for any value of
\displaystyle a,b
and
\displaystyle m
, the modified Bloch matrix of the convex combination
\displaystyle\rho
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle S({\rho})=pS({\rho_{1}})+(1-p)S({\rho_{2}}).

This is true due to the linearity of the Bloch matrix representation.
The matrix norm is a convex function:

|S({\rho})|\leq p|S({\rho_{1}})|+(1-p)|S({\rho_{2}})|.

(10)

Therefore
\displaystyle MBN
is convex.

Invariant under Local Unitary operations:

Let us consider a bipartite density matrix
\displaystyle\rho_{AB}
. From Eq: (
3
) we know that
\displaystyle\rho_{AB}
can always be expressed in terms of generators
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda_{j}}
of
\displaystyle SU(N)
and
\displaystyle SU(M)
as

\rho_{AB}=\frac{1}{NM}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}r_{i}\lambda_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\right.\\
\left.\sum_{j=1}^{M^{2}-1}s_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\tilde{\lambda}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M^{2}-1}t_{ij}\lambda_{i}\otimes\tilde{\lambda}_{j}\right),

(11)

When
\displaystyle\rho_{AB}
undergoes a Local Unitary(LU) transformation by
\displaystyle U=U_{A}\otimes U_{B}
then the LU transformed density matrix
\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{AB}
is expressed as

\rho^{\prime}_{AB}=\frac{1}{NM}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}r_{i}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{M}+\right.\\
\left.\sum_{j=1}^{M^{2}-1}s_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{N}\otimes\tilde{\lambda}^{\prime}_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{N^{2}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M^{2}-1}t_{ij}\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\otimes\tilde{\lambda}^{\prime}_{j}\right),

(12)

where

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{AB}=U\rho_{AB}U^{\dagger},\;\;\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=U_{A}\lambda_{i}U_{A}^{\dagger},\;\;\tilde{\lambda}^{\prime}_{j}=U_{B}\lambda_{i}U_{B}^{\dagger}
. By definition
\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{\prime}
are generators of
\displaystyle SU(N)
and
\displaystyle SU(M)
respectively.

From Eq: (
11
) and (
12
) it is straightforward to see that the modified Bloch Matrix of
\displaystyle\rho
and
\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}
are exactly the same except for the change in set of generators.
Thus
\displaystyle MBN(\rho)
is invariant under LU transformations.

Entanglement monotone:

Entanglement monotones are positive functions that do not increase under probabilistic Local Operations and Classical Communications (LOCC). It is considered to be the most important property for an entanglement measure
[
2
]
.

W. Dür
et. al
in
[
56
]
have discussed about constructing entanglement monotones based on Stoachastic Local Operations and Classical Communications (SLOCC) invariants. Later, C. Eltschka
et al
extended this discussion and proved that a positive homogeneous function of pure bipartite states invariant under determinant-1 SLOCC operations defines an entanglement monotone if and only if
the homogeneous degree is not larger than 4
[
57
]
.

In order to prove that
\displaystyle MBN
is an entanglement monotone, we initially highlight that the trace norm of the modified Bloch matrix
\displaystyle S_{m,a,b}
is invariant under determinant-1 SLOCC operations.

Jeng
et al.

[
58
]
have shown that the eigen values of the Bloch matrix is invariant under determinant-1 SLOCC operations. Below we summarize major results of
[
58
]
.

When an SLOCC symmetry
\displaystyle g=A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\in G=SL(d_{1})\otimes SL(d_{2})
acts on the state
space
\displaystyle H=H_{1}\otimes H_{2}
, this action
\displaystyle\rho\rightarrow g\rho g^{\dagger}=(A_{1}\otimes A_{2})\rho(A^{\dagger}_{1}\otimes A^{\dagger}_{2})

can be represented by a tensor
\displaystyle C_{k}
such that

A_{k}\lambda_{i}^{(k)}A_{k}^{\dagger}=\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}_{k}}C_{k}^{ij}\lambda_{i}^{(k)}

(13)

The Bloch matrix under the action of
\displaystyle g
can then be represented as

B^{\prime}=(C_{1}\otimes C_{2})B

The characteristic polynomial
\displaystyle f(\Lambda)
of
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\text{det}((C_{1}\otimes C_{2})(I_{n}\Lambda-B))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\text{det}(I_{n}\Lambda-B)

This conclusively proves that the charecteristic polynomial and all its coefficients of the Bloch matrix are invariant under determinant-1 SLOCC operations.

Following from these results we see that the procedure of modification of a Bloch matrix shown in Eq: (
5
) does not change the set of generators used in the actual Bloch matrix. Hence Eq: (
13
) holds true for modified Bloch matrices as well. Thus the eigen values of modified Bloch matrix are invariant under determinant-1 SLOCC operations.

Now we shall show that the linear homogeneous degree of
\displaystyle MBN
is less than 4.

A function
\displaystyle E()
that takes pure quantum state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}
as input is a linear homogeneous function of degree
\displaystyle\eta
iff

E(c\ket{\psi})=c^{\eta}E(\ket{\psi})

where
\displaystyle\eta,c>0
.
For a quantum state
\displaystyle\ket{\psi}
, let its modified Bloch matrix be given as
\displaystyle S_{\psi}
. Then for a quantum state
\displaystyle c\ket{\psi}
its modified Bloch matrix is represented as,

S_{c\psi}=c^{2}S_{\psi}

This implies that

|S_{c\psi}|=c^{2}|S_{\psi}|

Since
\displaystyle MBN(\rho)
has a linear homogeneous degree of
\displaystyle\eta=2
, it qualifies as an entanglement monotone for pure states.

Any quantum operation on a pure state can be expressed as convex combination of density matrices
[
59
]
. Since
\displaystyle MBN
is a convex function that is non-increasing under probabilitic LOCC for pure states it directly implies that
\displaystyle MBN
is non-increasing under probablistic LOCC even for mixed states.

Normalizability:

For any finite-dimensional bipartite system, a maximally entangled state can be readily expressed in the Schmidt form. This guarantees that
\displaystyle MBN
is normalizable for all finite dimensions.

Thus
\displaystyle MBN
satisfies all the essential properties of a valid entanglement measure.

\displaystyle a=\sqrt{{2}/{M(M-1)}},\;b=\sqrt{{2}/{N(N-1)}}

[

\displaystyle m=4
.)

Conversion of free entanglement into bound entanglement through mixing is demonstrated in this example. We consider a 2-qutrit state

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\left(\ket{01}\bra{01}+\ket{12}\bra{12}+\ket{20}\bra{20}\right),

VI
Robustness under inaccurate estimation of quantum state

In the introduction, we discussed the need for and various approaches to estimate entanglement using finite copies of quantum states.

We would like to ask an important question in this regard:

Do all entanglement measures suffer equally from inaccuracies in state estimation using Linear Inversion(LI)?

In attempting to answer this question, it is important to note that no analytical solution exists. The probabilistic nature of measurement outcomes makes the associated errors inherently random. Thus, the only meaningful approach is to generate a large sample of random quantum states and address the question quantitatively.

The notion of Maximal Entanglement (ME) for multi-qubit systems has been defined by G. Gour
et al

[
74
]
.
Such states for two qubits, three qubits, and four qubits in the computational basis are expressed as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{\psi_2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{00}+\ket{11},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{\psi_3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{000}+\ket{111}\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ket{\psi_4}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ket{0000}+\ket{1100}+\ket{0011}-\ket{1111}\right).

We calculate entanglement between the first qubit and the rest of the system using negativity (
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
) and
\displaystyle MBN
which we denote as
\displaystyle E_{\text{true}}
.
We then simulate state estimation through LI using n copies of the state under consideration to obtain an estimated Hermitian matrix
\displaystyle\rho_{\text{est}}
. We calculate the entanglement of
\displaystyle\rho_{\text{est}}
which is represented as
\displaystyle E_{\text{expt}}
.
To ensure that our results were not biased by the choice of any particular state, we generated 1,000 LU-equivalent states for each of the above states. The estimated entanglement from this process is
\displaystyle E_{\text{expt}}
, and the error in entanglement quantification is measured as

\Delta=\frac{|E_{\text{true}}-E_{\text{expt}}|}{E_{\text{true}}}.

In Fig.
4
, we present the frequency distribution of error
\displaystyle\Delta
. For the two-qubit case, the frequency distribution of error is nearly identical for
\displaystyle MBN
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
when 100 copies of the state are used for characterization. However, for three- and four-qubit systems, the frequency distribution of
\displaystyle\Delta
for
\displaystyle MBN
is centered around significantly lower values compared to
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
. This clearly indicates that, when finite copies of a quantum state are used to quantify entanglement via Linear Inversion (LI),
\displaystyle MBN
exhibits lower error rates than
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
, with this trend becoming more pronounced as the number of qubits increases.

These results are justified by the following.
As mentioned in the introduction, linear inversion is a straightforward technique for estimating quantum states. However, it may yield Hermitian matrices with negative eigenvalues, that are therefore not valid density operators.

Since negativity quantifies entanglement based on the negative eigenvalues of partially transposed density operators, the inaccuracies in the LI-estimated density matrix propagate to the computed negativity, often resulting in significant errors.

In contrast,
\displaystyle MBN
relies on the deviation from a norm value characteristic of separable states. This measure is inherently more robust, thereby reducing the impact of errors on the entanglement estimate.

The difference in the frequency distribution of errors in
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
and
\displaystyle MBN
naturally increases with system size, as the number of negative eigenvalues will also increase with system size
[
75
]
.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a bipartite entanglement measure, the Modified Bloch Norm (
\displaystyle MBN
) derived from the Improved Bloch Matrix (IBM) separability criterion. We show that this measure satisfies the desirable properties for effective entanglement quantification. Through practical examples, we illustrate how
\displaystyle MBN
can estimate the Entanglement Sudden Death (ESD) time with greater accuracy than the widely used entanglement measure Negativity
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}
. This improvement could enable the study of bound entanglement dynamics in complex environments, such as those involving non-Markovian noise, potentially revealing unobserved aspects of bound entanglement.

We further demonstrate that
\displaystyle MBN
achieves significantly lower error rates than Negativity when the quantum state is estimated using Linear Inversion (LI). For experimental scenarios where accurate entanglement estimation is needed and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) or Bayesian Inference (BI) are computationally intensive,
\displaystyle MBN
offers a more efficient alternative for estimating entanglement reliably.
