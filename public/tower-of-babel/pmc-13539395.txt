Bilateral detection, prediction, and lateralization of seizures using white matter
Authors: Nrupen Pakalapati, Chia‐Chu Chiang, Siddharth Lokam, Dominique M Durand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine how the corpus callosum handles interhemispheric seizure propagation and to assess its potential as a strategic recording locus for state‐dependent biomarkers.MethodsEpileptiform activity was induced in vitro and in vivo using 4‐aminopyridine. Propagation dynamics were examined using cross‐correlation analysis. Signal features were quantified using root mean square amplitudes, Shannon entropy, and spectral coherence. A previously unreported callosal evoked response, the corpus callosum evoked echo, was characterized pharmacologically (hypocalcemia, hypoxia) and anatomically (callosal disconnection) to determine synaptic dependence and cortical origin. The echo amplitude was evaluated as a predictive biomarker using logistic regression.ResultsEpileptiform discharges propagated through the corpus callosum with delays consistent with polysynaptic cellular transmission (24.81 ± 4.09 ms in vitro), and directionality was resolved in vivo. Although the propagation attenuated the seizure signal power, the spectral content of seizures was largely preserved within the corpus callosum. Following epileptogenic induction (pre‐seizure), the corpus callosum signal's root mean square increased significantly, supporting its value as a physiological marker of propagated seizure activity. The corpus callosum‐evoked echoes were temporally distinct from compound action potentials, were eliminated by synaptic blockade and callosal disconnection, and were selectively vulnerable to metabolic compromise, supporting their dependence on a cortico‐callosal feedback loop. The echo amplitude increased during the pre‐ictal period, achieving an area under the curve of 0.77 for seizure prediction in vitro.SignificanceThese findings identify the corpus callosum as a physiologically active component of epileptic networks rather than a passive transmission pathway. By revealing a previously unrecognized cortico‐callosal response and demonstrating its predictive potential, this work supports a network‐level framework for epilepsy in which white matter structures contribute directly to seizure generation and may provide targets for physiological monitoring and intervention.Plain Language SummaryWhite matter tracts, such as the corpus callosum, have long been dismissed as passive “wires” that merely route seizure activity. Furthermore, we uncovered a novel network response called the corpus callosum evoked echo (ccEE), which shows promise as a biomarker for seizure onset. INTRODUCTION

The pathology of epilepsy involves abnormally synchronized neuronal discharges, which propagate rapidly from the seizure onset zone (SOZ) to recruit distant cortical and subcortical regions. White matter tracts, the brain's primary conduits for long‐range communication, are functionally essential for this pathological spread. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

The corpus callosum (cc) is the largest white matter commissure that facilitates the transfer of information between the cerebral hemispheres. Its role in epilepsy is well documented, particularly in secondary generalization and the formation of mirror foci in bilateral epilepsies. 5 , 6 , 7 However, this established involvement has not translated into its therapeutic targeting. When the cc is addressed, it is typically via a non‐specific corpus callosotomy aimed at blocking propagation after the seizure has already initiated. Crucially, the cc is often viewed as a passive conduit rather than an active monitoring site. Consequently, it has been largely overlooked in epilepsy management strategies like neuromodulation.

This study challenges this paradigm by demonstrating that the cc provides unique advantages for epilepsy management. We show that a single cc electrode can record epileptic activity simultaneously in both hemispheres and that the recorded activity propagates directionally. We show that this directional propagation can be used to identify the primary seizure focus. Furthermore, we introduce the corpus callosum Evoked Echo (ccEE), a novel evoked response recorded within the cc that is distinct from axonal compound action potential. Importantly, we show that the amplitude of ccEE increases significantly immediately prior to seizure onset, demonstrating the ccEE as a powerful measure of network excitability.

Our findings advocate including the corpus callosum as a vital target for future electrophysiological monitoring and as a promising site for the implantation of responsive neurostimulation devices to prevent the generation and spread of seizures and for pre‐surgical planning and localization of the SOZ.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Coronal brain slice preparation

Coronal slices (400 μm) were prepared from CD‐1 mice (P20‐30; both sexes) using a vibrating microtome (Leica VTS1000S) per IACUC guidelines. Slices were harvested in chilled oxygenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) sucrose‐rich artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) and recovered for 60 min in normal ACSF. Sucrose‐rich ACSF (mmol/L): 220 sucrose, 2 KCL, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 2 MgSO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2, and 10 D‐glucose (pH 7.45). Normal ACSF: 124 NaCl, 3.75 KCl, 1.25 KH2PO4, 2 MgSO4, 26 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2, and 10 D‐glucose. 8

In vitro electrical recording & induction of seizures

Extracellular signals were recorded from M1 (layers II‐III) and the dorsal corpus callosum (d.cc) using glass micropipettes (150 mM NaCl). Signals were amplified (Axoclamp‐2A), filtered to 5 kHz (FLA‐01, Cygnus Technology), and digitized at 20 kHz (PowerLab, AD Instruments).

Generalized epileptiform activity was induced by bath application of 100 μM 4‐aminopyridine (4‐AP) in modified low‐Mg2+ low‐Ca2+ ACSF (1.2 mM MgSO4, 1.7 mM CaCl2). Focal activity was induced by placement of a glass micropipette filled with 30 mM 4‐AP into the M1 cortex.

Stimulation‐evoked responses were generated by stimulating the ipsilateral dorsal corpus callosum (d.cc) with a tungsten electrode and recording:

Cortico‐callosal Evoked Potentials (CcEPs): Recorded in M1 cortex. 8

Cortico‐callosal Evoked Potentials (CcEPs): Recorded in M1 cortex. 8

Compound Action Potentials (CAPs: N1, N2): Recorded in d.cc (2 mm distance from stimulation).

Compound Action Potentials (CAPs: N1, N2): Recorded in d.cc (2 mm distance from stimulation).

Corpus callosum Evoked Echoes (ccEEs): Recorded in d.cc at <0.05 mm from stimulation.

Corpus callosum Evoked Echoes (ccEEs): Recorded in d.cc at <0.05 mm from stimulation.

Stimulus artifacts from stimulation were eliminated using an automated template subtraction framework. For each sample, an artifact template was generated by averaging the highly consistent, short‐width electrical transients. This master artifact template was then mathematically subtracted from each individual evoked recording trace.

In vivo surgical procedure

Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (150–300 g) were implanted with stainless steel electrodes under isoflurane anesthesia (1–3%). Coordinates (mm): M1 (AP 1.0, ML ± 1.8, DV 1.4) and cc (AP 0.24, ML ± 0.8, DV 2.1). Focal seizures were induced via 1 μL injection of 30 mM 4‐AP into the right M1 under anesthesia, following established protocols. 9 Seizures were identified via Teager‐Kaiser Energy (TKE) values >2 × baseline for ≥ 20s, using similar metrics as studies in the literature. 9 , 10 , 11

Data and statistical analysis

Data were bandpass filtered (2–500 Hz) and down‐sampled (2 kHz) in MATLAB. Cross‐correlation analysis (X‐Corr) was performed on all channel pairs for both in vivo and in vitro data. For in vivo recordings, an X‐Corr R coefficient greater than 0.6 was used to identify highly similar signals for analysis. Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as mean ± SD, along with sample size (n) and p‐value. Statistical significance was determined using paired t‐tests, two‐sample t‐tests, analysis of variance (ANOVA), or repeated measures ANOVA (Figure 1).

Recording electrodes within the cortex (cx, blue) and the corpus callosum (cc, red) both captured short‐duration bursting events. (B) In the in vivo model, 4‐AP injection into the cortex induced epileptic activity across all four recording locations: Focus (ipsilateral cortex), corpus callosum ipsilaterally, corpus callosum contralaterally, and the mirror focus (contralateral cortex). RESULTS

Directional propagation of epileptiform activity across corpus callosum pathways can lateralize event origins and the focus

We investigated epileptic activity propagation through the corpus callosum (cc) in vitro and in vivo. In vitro event‐level cross‐correlation (X‐Corr) of 251 events (R > 0.6) showed a significant mean delay from cortical focus to cc (24.81 ± 4.09 ms; p = 5.07 × 10−9). A subsequent slice‐level t‐test confirmed this significant delay (24.93 ± 18.06 ms; p = 0.03), supporting directional axonal propagation and ruling out volume conduction (Figure 2A).

Propagation of focal epileptic activity from the cortex into the corpus callosum. (A) Cross‐correlation analysis (X‐Corr) of 251 epileptic events from the focus to the corpus callosum (cc) in the in vitro model yielded a highly significant mean pooled delay of 24.81 ± 4.09 ms (p = 5.07 × 10−9). This measurable time lag, confirmed by the mean slice delay (24.93 ± 18.06 ms, p = 0.03), strongly indicates cellular‐mediated and axonal propagation rather than volume conduction (expected delay of 0 ms). (B) Lateralization of the 4‐AP injection site was tested on unsorted events (grand mean and individual samples, n = 6). The Focus‐cc(C) pair successfully predicted the focus hemisphere (testing μ delay >0) in five out of six samples. The cc(I)–cc(C) pair succeeded in four out of six. Both the Focus‐cc(C) and cc(I)–cc(C) electrode pairs successfully predicted the hemisphere containing the focus at the grand mean level. (C) White‐matter pathway tracking resolves primary focus driving force where standard cortical correlation fails. (Left) Schematic showing the conditioning protocol: Network events are sorted binarily based on their cortical Focus‐Mirror delay segregating focus versus mirror‐driven states. (Middle) Unconditioned cortical event‐level delays demonstrating the apparent synchrony and overlapping distributions that typically limit clinical focus lateralization. (Right) Network‐wide white matter propagation delays averaged by subject (n = 6 animals). While standard cortical metrics show ambiguity, conditioning white matter pathway segments on event origin unmasks a robust network driving effect (Two‐Way RM‐ANOVA main effect of Event Origin, p = 0.0076). Significant post‐hoc directional shifts isolate clear driving dynamics along the Focus‐cc(I) (p = 0.0072), cc(I)‐Mirror (p = 0.0147), and Focus‐cc(C) (p = 0.0352) pathways.

Next, we evaluated whether directional spread to and within the cc could lateralize the 4‐AP injection site in vivo despite contralateral mirror foci independently generating activity. Outlier events outside ± 5 SD were removed. An ANOVA framework on the grand means across six channel pairs followed by planned one‐sided directional contrasts (Ha: u delay > 0) showed that Focus‐cc(C) and cc(I)–cc(C) trended greater than zero (Figure 2B, left panel), though neither survived multiple‐comparison correction (p = 0.961 and p = 0.965, respectively). The grand mean delays and unadjusted contrast p‐values were as follows: Focus‐cc(I): 1.04 ± 4.23 (0.3); Focus‐cc(C): 7.11 ± 6.75 (0.039); Focus‐Mirror: 1.71 ± 2.44 (0.68); cc(I)–cc(C): 5.99 ± 5.80 (0.034); cc(I)‐Mirror: 1.32 ± 9.58 (0.43); cc(C)‐Mirror: −5.06 ± 9.16 (0.84). Subject‐specific t‐tests mirrored these trends (Figure 2B, right panel); significant positive delays (p < 0.05) occurred in most subjects for Focus‐cc(C) (5/6) and cc(I)‐cc(C) (4/6), confirming that the primary focus drives propagation.

We investigated whether incorporating white matter pathway recordings could resolve propagation directionality. Unconditioned, pooled, event‐level cross‐correlations between the cortical Focus and cortical Mirror contacts initially demonstrated overlapping delay distributions centered near zero (Figure 2B, left panel, Figure 2C, middle panel).

To resolve network dynamics at an individual event level, events within each animal were classified binarily based on Focus‐Mirror delay (delay > 0 ms vs. < 0 ms) to isolate focus‐ and mirror‐originating events (Figure 2C). Event delays were averaged within each animal, and a Two‐Way Repeated‐Measures ANOVA (n = 6 animals) revealed a highly significant main effect for Event Origin (F [1,5] = 18.64, p = 0.0076), indicating network‐wide delays differ based on the driving focus. Post‐hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed significant directional differences along three pathways: Focus‐cc(I): 8.62 ms vs. −3.08 ms, (p = 0.0072), cc(I)‐Mirror: 9.38 ms vs. −18.75 (p = 0.014), Focus‐cc(C): 17.17 ms vs. 0.33 ms (p = 0.035). Delays were indistinguishable along cc(C)‐Mirror: −0.51 ms vs. −16.02 ms (p = 0.175) and cc(I)–cc(C): 8.39 ms vs. −1.84 ms (p = 0.218), likely due to high axonal velocity and close contact spacing.

In summary, standard cortical cross‐correlations yield near‐zero delays that obscure the primary focus. However, conditioning white matter pathway recordings on event directionality explicitly unmasks the driving network dynamics.

RMS increase in the corpus callosum can be used as a metric to enable closed‐loop stimulation

Root Mean Square (RMS) in the cc was investigated as a metric for predicting seizures. In vitro (Figure 3A, left), RMS was analyzed in the cc and the cortex (Cx) over time after focus induction. The mean cc RMS (red) rose from a baseline of 0.0055 ± 0.0018 over the subsequent 30s after the induction of a focus (RMS (mV) = 0.0007 t(s) + 0.034; R 2 = 0.51). Cortical RMS (blue) also exhibited a similar trend, showing a significant increase (RMS (mV) = 0.0045 t(s) + 0.023; R 2 = 0.97) (Table S1A). Analysis of the RMS over a 60‐s window further confirmed this increase. The cc (red) exhibited an RMS of 0.039 ± 0.02 (p = 0.03), whereas the cortex (blue) showed a higher RMS of 0.12 ± 0.08 (p = 0.02).

Increased signal RMS in the corpus callosum (cc) in the pre‐seizure period indicates increased cortical excitability. (A) Time‐series analysis showed a rapid and significant increase in RMS activity in the cc and cortex (cx) immediately upon focus induction. The mean cc RMS (red) rose significantly from a baseline of 0.0055 ± 0.0018 mV to 0.025 ± 0.008 mV at 5 s (p = 0.0019). This increase continued, reaching 0.15 ± 0.14 at 30s (p = 0.055). Overall analysis over a 60s window confirmed a robust increase in cc RMS (0.40 ± 0.03 mV, p = 0.03), providing a rapid and clear signal suitable for intervention shortly after seizure initiation. (B) RMS, normalized to baseline (= 1), was tracked in 2‐min intervals post 4‐AP injection. The Focus (pink) and ipsilateral cc (cc(I)) (blue) both showed significant RMS increases starting at 2 min (Focus: 1.32 ± 0.18, p = 0.023; cc(I): 1.29 ± 0.13, p = 0.013) and remained elevated throughout the 10‐min period. This confirmed the rapid recruitment of the CC into the epileptic network, and these sustained and rapid RMS elevations in the cc validate it as a reliable, quantifiable metric for triggering reactive closed‐loop neuromodulation within the cc.

RMS normalized to baseline (baseline = 1) showed a significant increase in activity in all channels post‐4‐AP injection in vivo (Figure 3B, Table S1B) starting at 2 min and maintained throughout the next 10 min. While the mirror focus (orange) showed elevated RMS, this increase was not statistically significant relative to baseline.

This confirmed the rapid recruitment of the cc into the epileptic network and validates that the increase in RMS within the cc provides a rapid and quantifiable metric for indicating an increase in epileptiform activity.

Seizures recorded in the corpus callosum lose power, but the frequency content observed in the focus is preserved

We analyzed RMS, line length, Shannon entropy, spectral content, and coherence of 45 seizures recorded from 6 rats. RMS was significantly lower at the cc (cc(I): 0.085 ± 0.017 mV, p = 0.08; cc(C): 0.075 ± 0.003 mV, p = 0.045) and the mirror focus (0.086 ± 0.019 mV, p = 0.048) than at the primary focus (0.20 ± 0.13 mV; Figure 4B).

Despite a loss of power, seizures in the corpus callosum (cc) retain key spectral information. (A) Representative traces showing seizures recorded in the cc and mirror focus sometimes exhibit lower power and altered frequency components compared to the high‐amplitude activity recorded at the cortical focus. (B) Both Root Mean Square (RMS) and Line Length were significantly attenuated in the cc channels compared to the Focus (Focus RMS: 0.20 ± 0.13 mV; cc(I) RMS: 0.085 ± 0.017 mV). This confirmed the quantitative loss of signal power in the white matter. Crucially, the Shannon Entropy (information content) was preserved across all sites (Focus: 5.36 ± 0.38 bits; cc(I): 5.65 ± 0.39 bits), with no significant difference found, suggesting that the underlying complexity and signal structure are conserved during propagation. (C) Although RMS and total power were highest at the focus, normalizing power in each frequency band to the event's total power revealed that the normalized spectral content was generally preserved in the CC and mirror focus. (D) Spectral coherence analysis demonstrated strong functional connectivity between all recorded electrode pairs (p < 0.0001 for all values) across the Delta, Theta, Alpha, and Beta bands, indicating that the primary focus, the propagating CC, and the mirror focus are all highly coupled and synchronized during the epileptic event.

Line length was significantly reduced in the cc (cc(I): 275.6 ± 118 mV/s, p = 0.0017; cc(C): 235.9 ± 91.3 mV/s, p = 0.00022) relative to the focus (528.6 ± 320 mV/s). The mirror (428.9 ± 203 mV/s, p = 0.42) did not show a significant reduction in line length.

Shannon Entropy was preserved across all recording locations. Entropy in the cc (cc(I): 5.65 ± 0.39 bits, p = 0.12; cc(C): 5.54 ± 0.34 bits, p = 0.41) and the mirror focus (5.31 ± 0.41 bits, p = 0.89) was not significantly different from the focus (5.36 ± 0.38 bits). This suggests that the underlying complexity and randomness of the signal structure are conserved during propagation.

PSD was highest at the focus, consistent with the RMS analysis. To allow direct frequency comparisons, the power in each frequency band was normalized to the event's total power (Figure 4C). The normalized power across all channels was generally preserved, suggesting that seizures in the cc and the mirror focus share the same frequency information, just delivered at lower overall power. Statistical testing was performed using repeated measures ANOVA and a modified Tukey Test (Table S1C).

Spectral coherence across all brainwave bands demonstrated strong functional connectivity between all recorded electrode pairs (Figure 4D). Coherence values between all channel pairs were significantly greater than zero across the Delta, Theta, Alpha, and Beta bands (Table S1D, μ > 0, p < 0.0001 for all values). Indicating that, despite a reduction in signal power, the focus, the cc, and the mirror are all highly coupled during the epileptic events.

Pinging the cortex from the cc generates an echo in the cc

We placed a stimulating electrode in the dorsal cc (d.cc) of one hemisphere and a recording electrode in the contralateral hemisphere and varied the distance between them. When the inter‐electrode distance was reduced to less than 0.05 mm, a novel response component (corpus callosum evoked echo, ccEE) (Figure 5A) that was distinct from the typical N1 and N2 compound action potentials (CAPs) was observed.

Corpus callosum evoked echoes (ccEEs) are generated by recording and stimulating the corpus callosum (cc) and are a predictor of seizure onset. (A) The cc's response to stimulation was monitored as the inter‐electrode distance was varied, and a new response was observed when the distance between the stimulation and recording electrodes was less than 0.05 mm. At these distances, the experimental delays for the standard axonal components, N1 and N2, collapsed to zero (0 ± 0 ms), consistent with near‐instantaneous detection of the standard Compound Action Potentials (CAPs). In contrast, the novel response component, corpus callosum evoked echoes (ccEE), exhibited a significant and sustained delay of 3.01 ± 0.27 ms at D < 0.05 mm (p < 0.0001 relative to N1/N2 delays). This delay is notably slower than the theoretical CAP conduction velocities, suggesting that ccEE is a distinct physiological process unrelated to standard axonal conduction. (B) To investigate the predictive potential of ccEEs, their amplitudes were monitored (0.5 Hz stimulation frequency) for 45 s preceding seizure onset (n = 10 slices). The percent difference in ccEE amplitude relative to the −45 s baseline demonstrated a consistent and progressive increase in white matter excitability before seizures. The amplitude change became statistically significant at −25 s (17.86 ± 23.41%, p = 0.003) and peaked at seizure onset (0 s) at 34.98 ± 24.46% (p = 0.000011). (C) A simple logistic regression model was trained using changes in ccEE amplitude to forecast seizure probability, defining the 20s preceding a seizure as the pre‐seizure window. The model was trained on 83 individual seizures across 6 slices, yielding coefficients β0 = −1.12 ± 0.15 and β1 = 0.045 ± 0.0043. When tested on the remaining 4 slices (holdout validation), the model achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.77 on the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. This result confirms that the change in ccEE amplitude is a sufficiently reliable and quantifiable predictor of seizure onset.

We compared the experimental delays of the standard N1 and N2 responses with those of the novel response component across five inter‐electrode distances. Experimental delays (ms) for N1 (1.31 ± 0.15, 1.14 ± 0.15, 0.8 ± 0.19, 0 ± 0.08, 0 ± 0) and N2 (3.60 ± 0.3, 2.81 ± 0.25, 2.1 ± 0.25, 0.52 ± 0.6, 0 ± 0) were measured at 2, 1.5, 1, 0.5, and <0.05 mm.

At the shortest distance (<0.05 mm), the characteristic N1 and N2 delays effectively collapsed to zero (0 ± 0 ms), reflecting near‐instantaneous detection of the stimulation artifact and the onset of the CAPs. In contrast, the novel response component exhibited a significant measurable delay of 3.01 ± 0.27 ms at this same distance.

This measured delay for the novel response was significantly different from the N1 and N2 delays at <0.05 mm (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, this delay is notably slower than the theoretical conduction velocities for N1 (1.53 ± 0.1 m/s) and N2 (0.52 ± 0.03 m/s), suggesting it represents an underlying process distinct from standard CAPs.

Characterizing corpus callosum evoked echoes (ccEEs)

We characterized the novel response, named corpus callosum Evoked Echoes (ccEEs), to determine its origin and dependence on axonal vs. synaptic activity.

The mean duration of the ccEE was measured to be 3.25 ± 0.75 ms (n = 35 slices) (Figure 6A).

The ccEEs showed the fastest and most severe degradation, becoming effectively eliminated by 120 s. The dependence of the ccEE on stimulation intensity was assessed by comparing the normalized current saturation curves of the ccEEs, the Cortico‐callosal Evoked Potentials (CcEPs), and the N1 component of Compound Action Potentials (CAPs) across a 0.1 to 1 mA range (n = 8) (Figure 6B). All responses were normalized to their amplitude at 1 mA.

A significant difference between ccEEs and N1 was observed at low to moderate stimulation currents. This suggests that the population generating the ccEE requires a higher activation threshold or recruits more gradually than the fast N1‐component axons. No significant differences were found between the ccEEs and the CcEPs across the entire stimulation range (Table S2A).

We monitored the responses to 4 min (in 30s intervals) of induced hypoxia to test for differential metabolic sensitivity (n = 5) (Figure 6C; Table S2B). Data were normalized to pre‐hypoxia baseline levels. ccEEs were effectively eliminated (~0) by 120 s of hypoxia while N1 and N2 showed greater resistance, suggesting that ccEEs rely more on metabolically demanding processes, such as synapses. Significant differences were observed between the ccEEs and N1 at 60 s (p = 0.009) and 90 s (p = 0.004) seconds, and between ccEEs and N2 at 30 s (p = 0.04), 60 s (p = 0.04), and 90 s (p = 0.03).

We induced hypocalcemia for 20 min (n = 10) to block calcium‐dependent neurotransmitter release and impede synapses (Figure 6D; Table S2C). ccEEs significantly decreased in amplitude across the 20‐min period (p < 0.0001 comparing 0 min to 20 min). Conversely, the N1 and N2 components showed a non‐significant increase over the 20 min (p = 0.07, p = 0.063). Significant differences between ccEEs and N1 and N2 were observed after 10 min: (p = 0.004, p = 0.013), 15 min: (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001), and 20 min: (p = 0.0002, p = 0.0002). This opposite response to hypocalcemia strongly indicates that the ccEE is synaptically dependent, unlike the pure axonal CAPs.

Severing the connections between the cortex and the cc reduced both the ccEE (90.8 ± 10.6%, p < 0.0001) and CcEP (99.8 ± 0.6%, p < 0.0001) amplitudes while the purely axonal N1 component remained unchanged (13.3 ± 19.8%, p = 0.075) (Figure 6E; n = 10). The near elimination of ccEEs confirms that it is primarily generated by an efferent loop involving the cortical gray matter. Specifically, the activation of local cortical circuits that project back to the cc, rather than being solely generated by activity within the cc fiber bundle itself.

The amplitude of the ccEEs increases before seizures appear and can be used to predict incoming seizures

ccEEs were evoked continuously at 0.5 Hz, and their amplitudes were monitored for the 45 s preceding seizure onset (Figure 5B). Only seizures preceded by 45 s of stable baseline activity were included to ensure reliable comparison.

The percent difference in ccEE amplitude relative to the baseline at −45 s was computed and averaged across the first 10 seizures in each sample. We observed a significant increase in ccEE amplitude beginning at −25 s and continuing until seizure onset (0 s). A one‐tailed t‐test comparing the change at −40 s (which was close to baseline) to subsequent time points revealed: −45 s: 0 ± 0 (0.17), −40 s: 2.71 ± 14.9, −35 s: 1.13 ± 18.27 (0.35), −30 s: 4.41 ± 16.98 (0.64), −25 s: 17.86 ± 23.41 (0.003), −20 s: 26.22 ± 20.67 (0.000063), −15 s: 29.21 ± 24.12 (0.00019), −10 s: 31.48 ± 26.63 (0.00013), −5 s: 33.70 ± 25.13 (0.000066), 0 s: 34.98 ± 24.46 (0.000011). This consistent, progressive increase in ccEE amplitude demonstrates that changes in white matter excitability precede the clinical onset of seizures.

To calculate the probability of an incoming seizure, we utilized a simple logistic regression model: PYij=1=expβ0+β1X%Diff.ccEE Amplitude,ij+μi1+expβ0+β1X%Diff.ccEE Amplitude,ij+μi; PYij=1= probability ofaseizure,i=sampleID,j=seizure observation, μi=random intercept variable. We defined the 20 s preceding a seizure as the pre‐seizure zone for training (Figure 5C). Using holdout validation, 6 out of 10 brain slices (comprising 83 individual seizures) were used to train the model (β 0 : −1.12 ± 0.15, β 1 : 0.045 ± 0.0043, and μ i = 0.076 ± 0.28 [means ± SE]). The fitted model was then tested on the remaining 4 brain slices.

The model's performance in predicting seizures based on the ccEE amplitude achieved an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.77 on the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. This demonstrates promising predictive capability, serving as a proof‐of‐concept for white matter‐based seizure forecasting.

DISCUSSION

White matter has been heavily implicated in the spread of seizures from the seizure onset zone (SOZ) to other regions in humans. 3 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 However, because these structural tracts do not autonomously generate epileptic activity on their own, they have historically been neglected as functional targets for epilepsy monitoring and treatment. Consequently, while previous studies using white matter stereo‐electroencephalography (SEEG) successfully captured neural information along these pathways, the functional significance and directional dynamics of these recordings have remained fundamentally unclear.

While the spatial and temporal network dynamics observed here offer insights, important translation limitations must be considered. Both the in vitro and in vivo protocols utilized an acute 4‐aminopyridine (4‐AP) model of epileptiform activity. This chemoconvulsant approach reliably models hyper‐synchronous network propagation and acute seizure dynamics, but it does not capture the complex behavioral pathogenesis, structural remodeling, or spontaneous chronicity characteristic of clinical epilepsy.

Epilepsy can be monitored within the cc, and it is a result of propagation via axonal conduction

The primary finding of this study is that combining cortical tracking with localized white matter pathway recording successfully lateralizes the primary seizure focus, even in the presence of an active, independent mirror focus. Our in vitro data explicitly rule out volume conduction and confirm transmission across neuronal elements, likely involving synapses and action potential conduction along axons in the cc. 2 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

Translating this in vivo, grand‐mean analyses initially revealed a critical statistical hurdle: while directional trends emerged toward the contralateral hemisphere, these macro‐level relationships failed to survive strict multiple‐comparison corrections. However, evaluating independent subjects individually revealed that a clear majority (up to 5/6 animals) consistently exhibited significant positive delays along the Focus‐cc(C) and cc(I)–cc(C) pathways, confirming that the primary focus maintains a dominant baseline drive.

To resolve this network at a finer resolution, event‐level conditioning broke the apparent synchrony of unconditioned cortical metrics. Sorting individual callosal delays by their baseline directionality unmasked a highly significant network‐wide driving force (p = 0.0076) led by the primary focus. Integrating white matter pathway tracking with event‐level conditioning provides a quantifiable biomarker for seizure localization. This approach helps resolve network dynamics in cases where standard cortical EEG and ECoG metrics remain ambiguous.

Closed‐loop neuromodulation techniques can be applied to seizures observed in the corpus callosum

Within in‐vitro and in‐vivo (recording under acute anesthesia) models, the RMS in the cc increased rapidly immediately after seizure induction (Figure 3); however, not to the same extent as the focus. The in vitro results were particularly clear, with a highly significant increase in RMS in the cc within the first 5 s (Figure 3A, p = 0.0019). This rapid response is critical for closed‐loop systems, which rely on detecting seizure onset or propagation early enough to deliver interventions. RMS is a computationally simple measure, making it ideal for real‐time implementation in implantable devices. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 This finding highlights the utility of the cc as a strategic recording target for closed‐loop neuromodulation, demonstrating its capacity to capture bilateral network activity originating from distinct cortical foci.

Change in power and frequency spectra of seizures propagating into the corpus callosum

While seizures recorded in the cc and mirror focus suffer significant attenuation of signal power (RMS), they largely preserve the frequency composition and signal complexity, both of which are common methods for detecting and analyzing seizures. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 The strong spectral coherence observed between the focus, cc, and mirror focus across all brainwave bands further emphasizes that the cc serves as an integral, highly coupled link within the generalized cortical epileptic network. 35 Preserving frequency information is crucial, as specific frequency bands are often associated with distinct seizure dynamics. 36 , 37 , 38 This suggests that the filtering effect of the white matter pathway primarily acts as an amplitude dampener, which leaves the underlying structure and complexity of the epileptic rhythm unchanged.

Discovery and physiological origin of the corpus callosum evoked Echo (ccEE)

Our electrophysiological analysis revealed a novel response in the cc termed the corpus callosum Evoked Echo (ccEE) that is fundamentally distinct from the classical axonal Compound Action Potentials (CAPs).

The fast (N1) and slow (N2) components exhibit near‐zero delays when the recording distance approaches zero 39 , 40 , 41 (Figure 5A). However, under these same conditions, we observed ccEEs with a significant delay of 3.01 ± 0.27 ms. This long delay indicates that the signal arises from a process electrically distinct and temporally slower than CAPs, and we speculate on these underlying mechanisms:

Local Synaptic or Circuitry Activation: The response may be generated from evoked responses within adjacent cortical neurons, which then transmit the signal into the corpus callosum or are picked up via volume conduction and are visible when the CAP is minimized.

Distinct physiological requirements and local circuit dependence

Crucial evidence for the ccEE's origin came from pharmacological and metabolic manipulations:

Synaptic Dependence: The induction of hypocalcemia, which blocks Ca2+ dependent neurotransmitter release, caused a progressive decrease in ccEE amplitude. Conversely, the axonal N1 and N2 CAPs remained unchanged. This starkly opposite response is the clearest indicator that the ccEE depends on synaptic transmission, distinguishing it from the purely axonal process of CAPs. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47

These findings confirm that the ccEE is distinct from classic CAPs and depends on synaptic transmission. Rather than originating intrinsically within the main white matter tract, the ccEE relies on a functional efferent loop, where initial stimulation activates cortical elements that subsequently project a delayed, synaptically driven potential back into the corpus callosum.

Although our pharmacological, metabolic, and surgical lesion data provide strong converging evidence that the ccEE is a synaptically driven potential requiring a functional cortico‐callosal efferent loop, these methods remain indirect.

To precisely map the physiological origin of the ccEE, future studies must employ direct cellular and molecular interventions. Specifically, targeted pharmacological receptor blockade experiments (e.g., using AMPA/NMDA or GABA receptor antagonists) are required to confirm classic synaptic neurotransmission. Additionally, cell‐type‐specific patch‐clamp recordings or localized silencing of callosal projection neurons versus localized astrocytic networks will be essential to definitively differentiate neuronal synaptic feedback from slow glial activation.

Predictive capability as a seizure biomarker

The characterization of the ccEE as a synaptically driven potential provides a mechanistic basis for its predictive power. We observed a progressive increase in ccEE amplitude starting approximately 25 s before seizure onset. This amplitude increase, which reflects either increased synchrony or enhanced excitability of the cc‐cortical projection neurons, strongly suggests that the ccEE undergoes a measurable change in excitability during the pre‐ictal state. This was verified by a simple logistic regression model based solely on ccEE amplitude, which achieved an AUC of 0.77 in vitro. While this represents moderate performance for a clinical biomarker, it serves as a foundational proof‐of‐concept demonstrating that white matter tracking can detect the early onset stages of a seizure to provide an early intervention window.

CONCLUSIONS

This study establishes the cc as a physiologically active component of seizure networks. We demonstrate that epileptic transmission through the cc reflects time‐dependent cellular propagation rather than volume conduction, and we identify a novel synaptically driven field response—the corpus callosum evoked echo (ccEE)—that is distinct from axonal compound action potentials and shows promise as a predictor for seizure onset.

Together, these findings support a new framework in which the corpus callosum serves not only as a transmission pathway but also as an anatomically precise and computationally efficient substrate for seizure detection, forecasting, and intervention. This work motivates the incorporation of white‐matter targets into future epilepsy‐mapping strategies and suggests that closed‐loop neuromodulation systems may benefit from exploiting the intrinsic physiology of callosal circuits rather than relying solely on cortical focus localization.
