Gradient bounds and Liouville property for a class of hypoelliptic diffusion via coupling Thanks: Sponsored by the NSF of China (No. 11671076)
Bin Qian Beibei Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we obtain the reverse Bakry-Émery type estimates for a class of hypoelliptic diffusion operator by coupling method. The (right and reverse) Poincaré inequalities and the (right and reverse) logarithmic Sobolev inequalities are presented as consequences of such estimates. Wang-Harnack inequality, Hamilton’s gradient estimate and Liouville property are also presented by reverse logarithmic Sobolev inequality.



Key Words:
Coupling; Gradient bounds; Liouville property; Hypoelliptic diffusion.

60J60; 35H10

Introduction

We consider the second order differential operator (Kolmogorov type operator)

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}div(AD)+\langle x,BD\rangle,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

(1.1)

where
D=\left(\partial_{x_{1}},\cdots,\partial_{x_{N}}\right)
,
div
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote, respectively, the gradient, the divergence and the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The matrix
A=(a_{ij})
and
B=(b_{ij})
are
N\times N
real constant matrices in the form

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{0}&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ B=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&B_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&B_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&B_{r}\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{array}\right)

(1.2)

where
A_{0}
is a
m_{0}\times m_{0}
symmetric and positive semidefinite and
B_{k}
is a
m_{k-1}\times m_{k}
matrix of rank
m_{k},k=1,2,\ldots,r
, with

m_{0}\geq m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{r}\geq 1\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \sum_{k=0}^{r}m_{k}=N.

(
1.1
) arises in the description of wide classes of stochastic processes and kinetic models and in mathematical finance, see
[
17
]
etc.
By Proposition 2.1 and Proposition 2.2 of
[
20
]
. Hypothesis (
1.2
) implies that the operator
L
verifies the classical Hörmander condition
[
14
]
. Define, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
E(t)=\exp(-tB^{*})
(
B^{*}
is the transpose matrix of
B
) and

\ \mathcal{C}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}E(s)AE^{*}(s)ds

(1.3)

Under the assumption (
1.2
), we have
\mathcal{C}(t)>0
for every
t>0
, it means that
\mathcal{C}(t)>0
is positive definite, see
[
20
]
,
[
17
]
etc. By Hörmander’s theorem
[
14
]
, the operator
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
1.1
) is hypoelliptic and generators a Markov process
\textbf{X}_{t}
. The transition probability density of the processs
\textbf{X}_{t}
is Gaussian with respect to the Lebesgue measure
dx
, see e.g
[
14
, Page 148]
,
[
16
, Theorem 1 and Theorem 4]
or
[
20
, Page 30]
for explicit expression of the transition probability:

h(x,t;\xi,\tau)=p(x-E(t-\tau)\xi,t-\tau)

0\leq\tau<t
, where
p(x,t;0,0)=0
if
t\leq 0
and

p(x,t)=\frac{(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}}{\sqrt{\det\mathcal{C}(t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)x,x\rangle\right),\ t>0.

We can express the Markov process
\textbf{X}_{t}=(X_{1}(t),\cdots,X_{r+1}(t))
by the following stochastic differential equation

\begin{cases}dX_{1}(t)&=\sigma dB(t),\\
dX_{2}(t)&=B_{1}^{*}X_{1}(t)dt\\
&\vdots\\
dX_{r+1}(t)&=B_{r}^{*}X_{r}(t)dt\end{cases}

(1.4)

where
\sigma
is a
m_{0}\times m_{0}
constant matrix satisfying
\sigma\sigma^{*}=A_{0}
, for
1\leq i\leq r
,
B(t)
is a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
and
X_{i+1}(t)
is a
\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}-
valued process (
1\leq i\leq r
).

In particular,
r=1
and
A_{0}=B_{1}=1
, (
1.1
) reduces to the Kolmogorov operator initially introduced by A. N. Kolmogorov in
[
19
]
, which is the simplest example of a hypoelliptic seconde order differential operator, see
[
14
]
. In the case of

and

, Baudoin et al
[

]
and the very recent paper
[
7
]
obtained gradient bounds and other functional inequalities including reverse Poincaré inequality, reverse logarithmic Sobolev inequality by generalized
\Gamma-
calculus, see
[
3
,
2
]
for Bakry-Émery
\Gamma-
calculus in Riemannian manifolds. The authors
[
23
]
generalized the results to the iterated Kolmogorov diffusion operator (

) by a similar modified
\Gamma-
calculus. The main observation of
[
23
]
is the reverse Bakry-Émery type inequalities, which generates the reverse Poincaré inequality and reverse logarithmic Sobolev inequality. For non-Gaussian degenerate diffusions, Baudoin et el.
[
9
]
studied Wang-Harnack inequality (by reverse logarithmic Sobolev inequality) and the quasi-invariance property for infinite dimensional Kolmogorov diffusions. See

,

,
5

On the other side, the Li-Yau gradient estimates for the positive solutions of

(1.5)

by the original variational argument due to Li and Yau

Bakry-Émery type inequality

2.1
Bakry-Émery type inequality:
r=1
.

To illustrate the method, we begin with the case of
r=1
. Now the matrix
B
in (
1.2
) has a simple form
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&B_{1}\\
0&0\end{array}\right).
The associated Markov process
{\bf{X}}_{t}=(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t))
can be described as

\begin{cases}dX_{1}(t)&=\sigma dB(t),\\
dX_{2}(t)&=B_{1}^{*}X_{1}(t)dt\end{cases}

We have the following Bakry-Émery type estimate for the semigroup
P_{t}f(x)=\mathbb{E}(f({\bf X}_{t}))(x)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f\in C^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\right)
with bounded second derivatives. Then for any fixed
t\geq 0
,
x^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}},x^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}
and any constant
\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left\|\nabla^{{(1)}}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\leq P_{t}\left(\left\|\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}).

(2.1)

Proof.

Consider two copies of hypoelliptic diffusions

\begin{split}\mathbf{X}_{t}&=(X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t))=\left(x^{(1)}+\sigma B(t),x^{(2)}+tB_{1}^{*}x^{(1)}+\int_{0}^{t}B_{1}^{*}\sigma B(s)ds\right),\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}&=(\widetilde{X}_{1}(t),\widetilde{X}_{2}(t))=\left(\widetilde{x}^{(1)}+\sigma\widetilde{B}(t),\widetilde{x}^{(2)}+tB_{1}^{*}\widetilde{x}^{(1)}+\int_{0}^{t}B_{1}^{*}\sigma\widetilde{B}(s)ds\right),\end{split}

(2.2)

where
B(t)
and
\widetilde{B}(t)
are two Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
started at
0
.
\mathbf{X}_{t}
starts at
(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
starts at
(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)})
. Now let us synchronously couple
(B(t),\widetilde{B}(t))
for all the time
t
(
B(t)=\widetilde{B}(t),\forall t
) and take

x^{(1)}=\widetilde{x}^{(1)}+\varepsilon v,\ x^{(2)}=\widetilde{x}^{(2)}+\alpha_{2}\varepsilon B^{*}_{1}v

(2.3)

for any real constant
\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and some constant vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
so that, for any
t>0
,

{X}_{1}(t)-\widetilde{{X}}_{1}(t)=\varepsilon v,\ \ X_{2}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{2}(t)=(\alpha_{2}+t)\varepsilon B^{*}_{1}v.

(2.4)

By using an estimate on the remainder
R
of Taylor’s approximation to
f
and the assumption that
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}})
has bounded second derivatives, there exists a positive constant
C_{f}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{X}_{t})-f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})|

\displaystyle=\left|\nabla^{(1)}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})({X}_{1}(t)-\widetilde{{X}}_{1}(t))+\nabla^{(2)}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})({X}_{2}(t)-\widetilde{{X}}_{2}(t))+R\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\left|\left(\nabla^{(1)}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right)\cdot v\right|+\varepsilon^{2}C_{f}\left(1+|\alpha_{2}|+t\right)^{2}\|B_{1}B_{1}^{*}\|_{HS}^{2}\|v\|^{2},

where
\|B_{1}B_{1}^{*}\|_{HS}
is the Hilbert Schmidt norm of the
m_{0}\times m_{0}
matrix
B_{1}B_{1}^{*}
. Using this estimate and Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}^{((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)}))}[f(\mathbf{X}_{t})-f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)}))}\left|f(\mathbf{X}_{t})-f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}^{((x^{(1)},x^{(2)}),(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)}))}\left|\left(\nabla^{(1)}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right)\cdot v\right|+\varepsilon^{2}C_{f}\left(1+|\alpha_{2}|+t\right)^{2}\|B_{1}B_{1}^{*}\|_{HS}^{2}\|v\|^{2}.

Dividing out by
\varepsilon
and taking
\varepsilon\to 0
(hence
\widetilde{x}^{(1)}\to x^{(1)}
and
\widetilde{x}^{(2)}\to x^{(2)}
since
\widetilde{x}^{(2)}-x^{(2)}=\alpha_{2}\varepsilon B^{*}_{1}v
), we have that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\left|P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)})\right|}{\varepsilon}\leq P_{t}\left(\left|\left(\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\right)\cdot v\right|\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}).

Meanwhile, we can see that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\left|P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)})\right|}{\varepsilon}=\left|\left(\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right)\cdot v\right|

thus

\left|\left(\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right)\cdot v\right|\leq P_{t}\left|\left(\nabla^{(1)}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right)\cdot v\right|.

Notice that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right\|_{A_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sigma^{*}\left(\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\left|\left\langle\sigma^{*}\left(\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right),v\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\left|\left\langle\left(\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right),\sigma v\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|v\|=1}P_{t}\left(\left|\left\langle\left(\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\right),\sigma v\right\rangle\right|\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|v\|=1}P_{t}\left(\left|\sigma^{*}\left\langle\left(\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\right),v\right\rangle\right|\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}).

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle P_{t}

\displaystyle\left(\left|\left\langle\sigma^{*}\left(\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\right),v\right\rangle\right|\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|\cdot P_{t}\left(\left\|\sigma^{*}\left(\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\right)\right\|\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|\cdot P_{t}\left(\|\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}).

Hence we have

\left\|\nabla^{(1)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})+\alpha_{2}B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}P_{t}f(x^{(1)},x^{(2)})\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\leq P_{t}\left(\|\nabla^{(1)}f+(\alpha_{2}+t)B_{1}\nabla^{(2)}f\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\right)(x^{(1)},x^{(2)}).

Thus we complete the proof.
∎

Let

(2.5)

Proposition 2.5

(Bakry-Émery type estimate)
.

Let
f\in C^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}\right)
with bounded second derivatives. Then for any fixed
t\geq 0
,
x=(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(r+1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
and any constants
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}\ (2\leq k\leq r+1)
,

\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}P_{t}f(x)\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\leq P_{t}\left(\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}f\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\right)(x),

(2.7)

where
\alpha_{1}=1
.

Proof.

Consider two copies of hypoelliptic diffusions

\mathbf{X}_{t}=\left(\begin{matrix}&X_{1}(t)\\
&X_{2}(t)\\
&\cdots\\
&X_{r+1}(t)\end{matrix}\right),\ \ \widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}=\left(\begin{matrix}&\widetilde{X}_{1}(t)\\
&\widetilde{X}_{2}(t)\\
&\cdots\\
&\widetilde{X}_{r+1}(t)\end{matrix}\right)

with
X_{1}(t)=x^{(1)}+\sigma B(t),\ \widetilde{X}_{1}(t)=\widetilde{x}^{(1)}+\sigma\widetilde{B}(t),
and for
2\leq k\leq r+1
,

\begin{split}X_{k}(t)&=x^{(k)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i}B_{k-j}^{*}x^{(k-i)}+\int_{0}^{t}dt_{k-1}\int_{0}^{t_{k-1}}dt_{k-2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}B_{k-j}^{*}\sigma B(t_{1})dt_{1},\\
\widetilde{X}_{k}(t)&=\widetilde{x}^{(k)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i}B_{k-j}^{*}\widetilde{x}^{(k-i)}+\int_{0}^{t}dt_{k-1}\int_{0}^{t_{k-1}}dt_{k-2}\cdots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}B_{k-j}^{*}\sigma\widetilde{B}(t_{1})dt_{1},\end{split}

(2.8)

where
B(t)
and
\widetilde{B}(t)
are two Brownian motions in
\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
started at
0
.
\mathbf{X}_{t}
starts at
(x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\cdots,x^{(r+1)})
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
starts at
(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\widetilde{x}^{(2)},\cdots,\widetilde{x}^{(r+1)})
. Now let us synchronously couple
(B(t),\widetilde{B}(t))
for all the time
t
(
B(t)=\widetilde{B}(t),\forall t
) and take

x^{(1)}=\widetilde{x}^{(1)}+\varepsilon\alpha_{1}v\mbox{ and }x^{(k)}=\widetilde{x}^{(k)}+\alpha_{k}\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}B_{k-j}^{*}v,2\leq k\leq r+1

(2.9)

for any constants
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R},2\leq j\leq r+1
with
\alpha_{1}=1
and some constant vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
so that, for any
t>0
,
2\leq k\leq r+1
,

X_{1}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{1}(t)=\varepsilon\alpha_{1}v,\ \ X_{k}(t)-\widetilde{X}_{k}(t)=\varepsilon\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{k-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}B^{*}_{k-j}v

Similar to the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\left|P_{t}f(x)-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x})\right|\leq\varepsilon\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\cdot v\right|+\varepsilon^{2}C_{f}C_{B,\alpha,t}\|v\|^{2}

where
C_{B,\alpha,t}=\sum_{k=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\alpha_{k-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\right)^{2}\left\|\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}B_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}B^{*}_{k-j}\right\|_{HS}^{2}
. Dividing out by
\varepsilon
and taking
\varepsilon\to 0
(hence
\widetilde{x}_{k}\to x_{k}
for all
1\leq k\leq r+1
), we have that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\left|P_{t}f(x)-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x})\right|}{\varepsilon}\leq P_{t}\left(\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}f\cdot v\right|\right)(x).

On the other hand, we have

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\left|P_{t}f(x)-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x})\right|}{\varepsilon}=\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}P_{t}f(x)\cdot v\right|

thus

\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}P_{t}f(x)\cdot v\right|\leq P_{t}\left(\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}f\cdot v\right|\right)(x).

(2.10)

Hence we have

\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}P_{t}f(x)\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\leq P_{t}\left(\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\right)(x).

Thus we complete the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.7

(2.11)

\left\langle E(t)AE^{*}(t)\nabla P_{t}f(x),\nabla P_{t}f(x)\right\rangle\leq 2P_{t}\left(\Gamma(f)\right)(x).

(2.12)

Proof.

(2\leq k\leq r+1)

(2.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\frac{t^{k_{1}+k_{2}}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!}\left\langle B_{k_{2}}^{*}\cdots B_{1}^{*}A_{0}B_{1}\cdots B_{k_{1}}\nabla^{(k_{1}+1)}f,\nabla^{(k_{2}+1)}f\right\rangle,

and meanwhile

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\left(E(-t)AE^{*}(-t)\right)_{k_{2}+1,k_{1}+1}\nabla^{(k_{1}+1)}f,\nabla^{(k_{2}+1)}f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{1}=0}^{r}\sum_{k_{2}=0}^{r}\frac{t^{k_{1}+k_{2}}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!}\left\langle B_{k_{2}}^{*}\cdots B_{1}^{*}A_{0}B_{1}\cdots B_{k_{1}}\nabla^{(k_{1}+1)}f,\nabla^{(k_{2}+1)}f\right\rangle,

where we apply the identity (3.6) in

]

means the block at the position

(2.14)

Combining with (
2.13
) and (
2.14

2.11

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
f\in C^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}\right)
be a positive, globally Lipschitz and bounded function. Then for any fixed
t\geq 0
,
x=(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(r+1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
and any constants
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}(2\leq k\leq r+1)
,

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x)\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\ln P_{t}f(x)\right\|_{A_{0}}^{2}\leq P_{t}\left(f\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\ln f\right\|^{2}_{A_{0}}\right)(x),

(2.15)

where
\alpha_{1}=1
.

Proof.

As before, we assume
f\in C^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}\right)
is a positive and bounded function with bounded first and second derivatives. Without loss of any generality, we can assume
f\geq\delta>0
, otherwise we consider
f+\delta
instead, and then
\delta\to 0
.
Let us consider the same hypoelliptic diffusions
\mathbf{X}_{t},\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t}
in the proof of Proposition
2.5
with the start points
x=(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(r+1)})
and
\widetilde{x}=(\widetilde{x}^{(1)},\cdots,\widetilde{x}^{(r+1)})
with
x^{(1)}=\widetilde{x}^{(1)}+\varepsilon v
,
x^{(k)}=\widetilde{x}^{(k)}+\alpha_{k}\varepsilon\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}B_{k-j}^{*}v
for any
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}(2\leq k\leq r+1)
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}
.

For any positive function
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}})
with bounded first and second derivatives, there exists a positive constant
C_{f}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle|\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})-\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})|\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t))\cdot v\right|+\varepsilon^{2}C_{f}C_{B,\alpha,t}\|v\|^{2},

where
\alpha_{1}=1
and
C_{B,\alpha,t}
is defined in the proof of Proposition
2.5
. It follows by Minkowski’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})-\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\left(\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t))\cdot v\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}C_{f}C_{B,\alpha,t}\|v\|^{2}.

Thus

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{P_{t}f}(x)-\sqrt{P_{t}f}(\widetilde{x})\right|\left(\sqrt{P_{t}f}(x)+\sqrt{P_{t}f}(\widetilde{x})\right)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|P_{t}f(x)-P_{t}f(\widetilde{x})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}[f(\mathbf{X}_{t})-f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|f(\mathbf{X}_{t})-f(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\left(\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})-\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right)\left(\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})+\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})-\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})+\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon\left(\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}(t))\cdot v\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}C_{f}C_{B,\alpha,t}\|v\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})+\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

where we use the Hölder inequality in the second last inequality. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}^{(x,\widetilde{x})}\left|\sqrt{f}(\mathbf{X}_{t})+\sqrt{f}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{t})\right|^{2}=4P_{t}\left(f\right)(x).

Dividing out
\varepsilon\left(\sqrt{P_{t}f}(x)+\sqrt{P_{t}f}(\widetilde{x})\right)
in the both side of (
2.16
) and letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, it yields

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{P_{t}f}(x)\cdot v\right|

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\left|\sqrt{P_{t}f}(x)-\sqrt{P_{t}f}(\widetilde{x})\right|}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\left(P_{t}\left|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{f}\cdot v\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(x).

Similar to the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{P_{t}f}(x)\right\|_{A_{0}}\leq\left(P_{t}\left\|\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{r+1}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{i^{\prime}-1}\alpha_{i^{\prime}-i}\frac{t^{i}}{i!}\prod_{j=1}^{i^{\prime}-1}B_{j}\right)\nabla^{(i^{\prime})}\sqrt{f}\right\|^{2}_{A_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}(x).

The desired result follows immediately.
∎

3
Applications to functional inequalities and Liouville property

Theorem 3.1

(Poincaré inequality and reverse Poincaré inequality)
.

Let
f\in C^{1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}\right)
be a globally Lipschitz bounded function. Then for any
t\geq 0
,
x=(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(r+1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-(P_{t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq P_{t}\left(\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}E(-(t-s))AE^{*}(-(t-s))ds\nabla f,\nabla f\right\rangle\right).

(3.1)

For
t>0
, the following reverse Poincaré inequality holds for any bounded function
f
:

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-(P_{t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla P_{t}f,\nabla P_{t}f\right\rangle.

(3.2)

Where
E(t)=\exp(-tB^{*})
and
\mathcal{C}(t)
is defined in (
1.3
).

Proof.

It follow from (
2.11
),

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-(P_{t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\Gamma(P_{t-s}f)ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}P_{t}\left(\left\langle E(-(t-s))AE^{*}(-(t-s))\nabla f,\nabla f\right\rangle\right)ds

\displaystyle=P_{t}\left(\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}E(-(t-s))AE^{*}(-(t-s))ds\nabla f,\nabla f\right\rangle\right).

where the first identity follows from
[
2
, Page 207]
. Hence (
3.1
) follows. Now we use the reverse Bakry-Émery type inequality (
2.12
),

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-(P_{t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle E(s)AE^{*}(s)\nabla P_{t}f,\nabla P_{t}f\right\rangle ds

\displaystyle=\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}E(s)AE^{*}(s)ds\nabla P_{t}f,\nabla P_{t}f\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla P_{t}f,\nabla P_{t}f\right\rangle.

Hence we complete the proof.
∎

We can rewrite respectively (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) as

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-(P_{t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{r}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(k!)^{2}(2k+1)}P_{t}\left(\left\|\prod_{j=1}^{k}B_{j}\nabla^{(k+1)}f\right\|_{A_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{0\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq r}\frac{t^{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!(k_{1}+k_{2}+1)}P_{t}\left(\left\langle A_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{k_{1}}B_{j}\nabla^{(k_{1}+1)}f,\prod_{j=1}^{k_{2}}B_{j}\nabla^{(k_{2}+1)}f\right\rangle\right).

and

\displaystyle P_{t}(f^{2})-(P_{t}f)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=0}^{r}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{(k!)^{2}(2k+1)}\left\|\prod_{j=1}^{k}B_{j}\nabla^{(k+1)}P_{t}f\right\|_{A_{0}}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{0\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq r}\frac{(-1)^{k_{1}+k_{2}}t^{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!(k_{1}+k_{2}+1)}\left\langle A_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{k_{1}}B_{j}\nabla^{(k_{1}+1)}P_{t}f,\prod_{j=1}^{k_{2}}B_{j}\nabla^{(k_{2}+1)}P_{t}f\right\rangle.

Theorem 3.2

(Logarithmic Sobolev inequality and reverse Logarithmic Sobolev inequality)
.

Let
f
be a positive, globally Lipschitz and bounded
C^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}\right)
function. Then for any fixed
t\geq 0
,
x=(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(r+1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
, we have

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptP_{t}(f\ln f)-(P_{t}f)\ln P_{t}f

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}P_{t}\left(f\left\langle\int_{0}^{t}E(-(t-s))AE^{*}(-(t-s))ds\nabla\ln f,\nabla\ln f\right\rangle\right).

(3.3)

For
t>0
, the following reverse Logarithmic Sobolev inequality holds for any positive bounded function
f
:

\displaystyle P_{t}(f\ln f)-P_{t}f\ln P_{t}f

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}P_{t}f\left\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla\ln P_{t}f,\nabla\ln P_{t}f\right\rangle,

(3.4)

where
\mathcal{C}(t)
defined in (
1.3
) is a positive matrix.

Proof.

It follow from (
2.17
),

\displaystyle P_{t}(f\ln f)-(P_{t}f)\ln P_{t}f

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\left(P_{t-s}f\Gamma(\ln P_{t-s}f)\right)ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}P_{t}\left(f\left\langle E(-(t-s))AE^{*}(-(t-s))\nabla\ln f,\nabla\ln f\right\rangle\right)ds,

where the first identity follows from
[
2
, Proposition 5.5.3]
. Hence (
3.3
) follows.

For the reverse Logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
3.4
), we only need to use the reverse inequality (
2.18
),

\displaystyle P_{t}(f\ln f)

\displaystyle-(P_{t}f)\ln P_{t}f=\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\left(P_{t-s}f\Gamma(\ln P_{t-s}f)\right)ds

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}P_{t}f\int_{0}^{t}\langle E(t-s)AE^{*}(t-s)ds\nabla P_{t}f,\nabla P_{t}f\rangle ds.

(
3.4
) follows easily.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f
be a non-negative Borel bounded function on
\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
.
Then for every
t>0,x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
and
\alpha>1
, we have

\left(P_{t}f\right)^{\alpha}(x)\leq C_{\alpha}(t,x,y)P_{t}f^{\alpha}(y)

with
C_{\alpha}=\exp\left(\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\left\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\right\rangle\right)
,
\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)
denotes the inverse matrix of the positive definite matrix
\mathcal{C}(t)
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of Theorem 3.11 in
[
23
]
. We prove it here for readers’ convenience. Without loss of any generality, we can assume
f
is non-negative and rapidly decreasing. Let
t
be fixed. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}\times\cdots\times m_{r}}
, consider the curve

\gamma(s)=x+s(y-x)

such that
\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y
and
\beta(s)=1+(\alpha-1)s,0\leq s\leq 1
. Let

\phi(s):=\frac{\alpha}{\beta(s)}\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)}(\gamma(s)),\ 0\leq s\leq 1.

Differentiating with respect to
s
and using (
3.4
) yields

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{\beta^{2}(s)}\frac{P_{t}(f^{\beta(s)}\ln f^{\beta(s)})-(P_{t}f^{\beta(s)})\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)}}{P_{t}f^{\beta(s)}}-\frac{\alpha}{\beta(s)}\langle\nabla\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)},y-x\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2\beta^{2}(s)}\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)},\nabla\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)}\rangle-\frac{\alpha}{\beta(s)}\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)},\nabla\ln P_{t}f^{\beta(s)}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\rangle}

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\rangle,

where we use the inequality
\langle x,y\rangle\leq\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}(t)x,x\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)y,y\rangle}
for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}+\cdots+m_{r}}
and for any positive matrix
\mathcal{C}(t)
in the second last inequality and use the elementary inequality
ax^{2}-bx\geq-\frac{b^{2}}{4a}
for
a>0
in the last one.

Integrating
s
from
0
to
1
yields

\ln P_{t}f^{\alpha}(y)-\ln(P_{t}f)^{\alpha}(x)\geq-\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\rangle.

The desired result follows.
∎

We also have the following Hamilton’s elliptic gradient estimate by the reverse logarithmic Sobolev inequality.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
u
be the positive bounded solution to the hypoelliptic heat equation
\mathcal{L}u(x,t)=\partial_{t}u(x,t)
with the initial value
0\leq f\leq C
(
C
is a constant), we have for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
and
t>0
,

\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla\ln u,\nabla\ln u\rangle\leq\ln\frac{C}{u}.

(3.5)

Proof.

It is known that
u(x,t)=P_{t}f(x)
. Then by the reverse logarithmic Sobolev inequality (
3.4
),

\frac{1}{2}u\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla\ln u,\nabla\ln u\rangle\leq u\ln(C)-u\ln u.

The desired result follows.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Under the assumption of Proposition
3.4
, we have for
t>0
,
\alpha>1
and any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{m_{r}}
,

u^{\alpha}(x,t)\leq u(y,t)C^{\alpha-1}e^{\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\rangle}.

Proof.

Let
t
be fixed. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}+\cdots+m_{r}}
, consider the curve

\gamma(s)=x+s(y-x),\ 0\leq s\leq 1

such that
\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(1)=y
.
We have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\ln\frac{C}{u}(y,t)}-\sqrt{\ln\frac{C}{u}(x,t)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\sqrt{\ln\frac{C}{u}(\gamma(s),t)}ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\nabla\ln u(\gamma(s),t)\cdot\dot{\gamma}(s)}{2\sqrt{\ln\frac{C}{u}(\gamma(s),t)}}ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}(t)\nabla\ln u,\nabla\ln u\rangle}\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\rangle}}{2\sqrt{\ln\frac{C}{u}(\gamma(s),t)}}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)(y-x),y-x\rangle},

where we apply the Hamilton’s gradient estimate (
3.5
) in the last inequality and in the second last inequality we apply the elementary inequality
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\leq\langle\mathcal{C}(t)v_{1},v_{1}\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(t)v_{2},v_{2}\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}}
for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{0}+\cdots+m_{r}}
and the positive definite matrix
\mathcal{C}(t)
.
Thus for any
\alpha>1
, applying the elementary inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq\alpha a^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}b^{2}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\ln\frac{C}{u}(y,t)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sqrt{\ln\frac{C}{u}(x,t)}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(y-x),y-x\rangle}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\ln\frac{C}{u(x,t)}+\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha-1)}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}(y-x),y-x\rangle.

The desired result follows.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
3.4
, the following Liouville property holds.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
u
be the positive bounded solution to the hypoelliptic Laplacian equation
\mathcal{L}u(x)=0
. Then
u
must be a constant.

Proof.

Assume
C
is the upper bound of
u
, we have
u<C+1
. Define the dilations group on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

\delta_{\lambda}=\text{diag}\left(\lambda I_{m_{0}},\lambda^{3}I_{m_{1}},\cdots,\lambda^{2r+1}I_{m_{r}}\right),\lambda>0

where
I_{m_{k}}
is the
m_{k}\times m_{k}
identity matrix. We can express
\mathcal{C}(t)
as

\mathcal{C}(t)=\delta_{\sqrt{t}}\mathcal{C}(1)\delta_{\sqrt{t}},

(3.6)

see Proposition 2.3 in
[
20
]
. Then we have by (
3.5
)

\displaystyle\ln\frac{C+1}{u}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}(1)\delta_{\sqrt{t}}\nabla\ln u,\delta_{\sqrt{t}}\nabla\ln u\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\text{min}}\frac{1}{u^{2}}\left\|\delta_{\sqrt{t}}\nabla u\right\|^{2},

where
\lambda_{\text{min}}
is the minimal eigenvalue of the positive matrix
\mathcal{C}(1)
. Since
u
is independent of
t
, letting
t\to\infty
in the above inequality, we obtain
\nabla u\equiv 0
. Hence
u
must be a constant.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The above Liouville property can
not
follow by the Li-Yau type inequality (see Proposition 4.2 in
[
21
]
).
