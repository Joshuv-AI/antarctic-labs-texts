Performance enhancement of a low-energy humidification dehumidification desalination system using the M-cycle
Authors: Mohammad Ali Bakhtiari, Mohamad Sadeq Karimi, Bahar Firoozabadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Water scarcity remains a pressing global issue, particularly in arid and coastal regions, necessitating the development of energy-efficient and sustainable desalination technologies. This study presents a novel humidification-dehumidification desalination system enhanced by the M-Cycle, which utilizes ambient thermal energy to minimize power consumption and eliminate reliance on fossil fuels. A coupled computational approach involving CFD simulations and PSO was employed to model and optimize system performance. The CFD analysis captured detailed temperature and humidity distributions across the heat and mass exchanger, while the PSO algorithm optimized three key parameters: air velocity, heat exchanger width, and dry air split ratio. Notably, the optimized configuration—an airflow velocity of 6.89 m/s, a device width of 0.63 m, and a 35.97% dry air split—resulted in a freshwater production rate of 3.04 kg per kWh of electricity consumed, 360.5 L per day, and an outlet relative humidity of 99%, with total power consumption of 4.94 kW. Compared to previous M-Cycle-based desalination systems, the proposed configuration demonstrates over a threefold improvement in energy efficiency, as measured by the GOR, highlighting its superior thermodynamic performance. Additionally, the study uniquely resolved the spatial distribution of relative humidity and evaporation rates, without relying on simplifications such as assuming saturated air at the outlet. The results confirm the potential of the proposed M-Cycle HDH system as a highly efficient, scalable, and eco-friendly desalination technology suitable for decentralized and off-grid applications.

Introduction

The Earth’s oceans hold about 97% of the planet’s water, a vast resource that is crucial for many processes, yet it is largely unusable for human consumption due to its high salinity1. Despite its abundance, seawater is unsuitable for direct use in human activities such as drinking, agriculture, and industry, because of the high salt concentration, which is typically between 30,000 and 40,000 parts per million (ppm) of total dissolved solids (TDS). While seawater is abundant, it remains an untapped resource for providing the necessary freshwater needed to sustain human populations. At present, more than 3.4 billion people around the world are struggling with water scarcity, highlighting the critical importance of developing and improving desalination technologies to convert seawater into usable freshwater2–4.

Desalination technologies have thus become a central focus for governments and researchers worldwide5,6. The process of desalination involves removing salts and minerals from seawater to make it suitable for human use7–9. Research in this field primarily focuses on two main categories of desalination methods: thermal processes3 and membrane-based processes10. Thermal desalination methods are based on heating seawater to evaporate the water and then condensing the vapor to separate freshwater from saltwater. Examples of thermal processes include multi-effect distillation, vapor-compression distillation, and multi-stage flash distillation11–13. On the other hand, membrane-based processes use specially designed filters, or membranes, to allow water molecules to pass through while blocking salts and other impurities. Reverse osmosis, electrodialysis, and capacitive deionization are some of the common membrane-based methods14–17.

Despite the diversity of desalination technologies, they all face similar challenges. One of the most significant obstacles is maximizing the rate of freshwater production while minimizing the amount of energy required to operate the systems. Desalination is an energy-intensive process, and high energy consumption remains a key factor that affects the economic feasibility of these systems. Reducing the operational and maintenance costs of desalination plants, while also decreasing the amount of salt residue left after the desalination process, is another area of focus. Therefore, the development of energy-efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable desalination technologies is an urgent priority for researchers and engineers18–20.

Among the promising solutions for desalination is the Humidification-Dehumidification (HDH) process. The HDH process has gained attention due to its ability to be powered by clean, renewable energy sources, such as solar power, and its relatively low energy requirements compared to other desalination methods21–26. This makes it particularly suitable for use in remote areas or locations that are off the electrical grid, where traditional desalination technologies may not be feasible. The process itself works by using air as a carrier medium to transfer water vapor. In the humidifier, seawater is heated to produce vapor, which is absorbed by the air. This humidified air is then passed through a dehumidifier, where the water vapor condenses into liquid freshwater, ready for use3,27,28.

One of the key advantages of the HDH process is its ability to operate at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, which reduces the need for complex equipment and lowers maintenance costs29–33. Additionally, the process can be optimized by using a variety of heat sources, such as waste heat from industrial processes, solar energy, or geothermal energy, to enhance the performance of the humidifier34–36. By preheating the air and the saline water, these energy sources can improve the overall efficiency of the system. Furthermore, the system’s performance can be further optimized by increasing the temperature difference between the humidified air and the cooling surface in the dehumidifier. This temperature gradient enhances the condensation of water vapor, leading to greater freshwater recovery37,38.

Although the HDH process holds great promise, there are still challenges in optimizing the humidification process to increase the amount of freshwater recovered39. One of the most promising innovations in this area is the Maisotsenko Cycle (M-Cycle), a thermodynamic process that captures latent heat from water evaporation40–42. The M-Cycle has been widely used in evaporative cooling systems, as it is capable of cooling air below its wet-bulb temperature, approaching the dew point. This feature makes the M-Cycle a promising approach for enhancing desalination performance by enabling efficient heat recovery and facilitating high-rate moisture transfer43–45. The M-Cycle also has applications in waste heat recovery systems, where it can be integrated with heat exchangers to improve the overall efficiency of energy use in industrial processes46–49.

Recent studies have focused on integrating the M-Cycle with various desalination and heat recovery systems to enhance performance. For instance, researchers such as Saghafifar and colleagues46 have analyzed the use of M-Cycle-based air saturators in humid air turbines for waste heat recovery. By integrating the M-Cycle with heat exchangers, these systems can recover energy from waste heat and improve the efficiency of both desalination and cooling processes49. Additionally, Tariq and his team have developed a novel design for an M-Cycle-based air saturator that has demonstrated a 30% increase in efficiency and an 11% improvement in the gain output ratio (GOR) compared to conventional HDH systems5. This innovation also reduces operational costs by 14% and the carbon footprint by 7%, making it both economically and environmentally beneficial5.

Building on these advances, this project proposes a new desalination system that utilizes the M-Cycle. The proposed system takes advantage of the cooling effects of water evaporation to create two distinct air streams. One air stream is cooled without humidification, losing a significant amount of sensible heat. The second air stream, known as the “wet stream,” absorbs the moisture from evaporated water, ensuring efficient humidification and stable system operation. This wet stream, which becomes fully saturated with moisture, is then passed through the dehumidification system to produce freshwater. The design of this system is particularly beneficial as it allows the air temperature to drop to its dew point, a feat that conventional evaporative cooling systems cannot achieve.

To evaluate the performance of the new system, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) is used to simulate the temperature and humidity distribution within the heat exchanger50–54. CFD simulations are essential for optimizing the design of the system, ensuring that the temperature and humidity profiles are balanced to maximize freshwater recovery. Additionally, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is employed to identify the optimal design parameters for the heat exchanger. This optimization technique allows for the fine-tuning of system components to achieve the best possible performance, and the results are compared with those from previous research to assess improvements in efficiency.

Despite the increasing interest in integrating the M-Cycle with HDH systems for sustainable desalination, several critical gaps remain unaddressed in the literature. Previous studies, including those by Shaikh et al.55–57, have primarily focused on system-level energy assessments, experimental validation of M-cycle performance, and hybrid solar desalination configurations. However, most of these works either rely on idealized assumptions—such as fully saturated air or uniform evaporation—or lack high-resolution numerical modeling of the coupled heat and mass transfer phenomena within the heat exchanger structure. Moreover, systematic optimization of geometric and operational parameters using advanced algorithms remains largely unexplored.

To address these limitations, the present study proposes a comprehensive computational framework for analyzing and optimizing an M-Cycle-based HDH desalination system. The novelty of this work lies in three main contributions: (1) the development of a high-fidelity CFD model capturing spatial distributions of temperature, humidity, and evaporation without relying on simplifying assumptions; (2) integration of PSO for simultaneous tuning of airflow velocity, heat exchanger width, and air-split ratio to maximize water production per unit energy; and (3) inclusion of a sensitivity analysis and practical feasibility assessment to bridge the gap between numerical modeling and real-world implementation. These contributions not only offer a more realistic understanding of system behavior but also provide actionable insights for the design of next-generation off-grid desalination technologies.

Method

Assumptions

In this study, several simplifying assumptions were made to facilitate the analysis and modeling of the M-cycle HDH desalination process. Heat losses to the surrounding environment were considered negligible, ensuring that all thermal energy remains effectively within the system. Both air and water flows were assumed to be steady-state and incompressible, with the airflow treated as an ideal mixture of dry air and water vapor. The system walls were assumed impermeable, preventing any fluid penetration. Molecular diffusion of water vapor within the air, as well as thermal conduction along the walls and within the liquid phase in the flow direction, were neglected due to their minimal impact on overall transport phenomena.

Considering the thin water film, its temperature distribution across the thickness was assumed uniform and not explicitly modeled. Furthermore, owing to the high convective heat transfer coefficient between the water film and the air, the wall temperature at any location was taken to be equal to that of the water film. Evaporation was assumed to occur exclusively at the air–water interface. Given the high heat capacity of water, heat transfer between the water film and the airflow was presumed sufficient to maintain a constant water film temperature throughout the system. Finally, airflow in all channels was considered fully developed from the inlet, with a uniform velocity profile across all wet channels.

System description

The proposed desalination system is a novel integration of the M-Cycle into a HDH framework, designed to maximize thermal efficiency and freshwater production without relying on external thermal energy sources (Fig. 1). The system comprises four main components: a brackish water storage tank, a circulating pump, a Heat and Mass Exchanger (HMX) based on the M-Cycle, and a dehumidifier unit. Together, these components facilitate efficient simultaneous heat and mass transfer, enabling continuous freshwater generation.Fig. 1Schematic of the proposed humidification-dehumidification desalination system utilizing an innovative M-Cycle-based air saturator (Heat and Mass exchanger) for enhanced energy efficiency.

Initially, brackish water is drawn from the storage tank and distributed as a thin film over the surface of the wet channels within the HMX using a pump. This crossflow configuration enables the recovery of latent heat of evaporation and allows the product air to cool down to its dew point temperature—beyond the capabilities of conventional evaporative cooling systems. The saturated humid air then flows into the dehumidifier, typically composed of finned-tube heat exchangers, where it is further cooled and condensed into liquid water, which is collected in the freshwater tank.

The dry air stream exits the system after completing its role in thermal energy exchange. The airflow paths, pressure distribution, and channel geometries are optimized to ensure maximum energy recovery, efficient evaporation, and low power consumption. Electrical energy is the only external input, primarily used for the operation of the pump and fans, making the system ideal for off-grid or remote applications.

Overall, by leveraging the thermodynamic advantages of the M-Cycle and the operational simplicity of HDH, this configuration provides a scalable, energy-efficient, and cost-effective solution for freshwater production under diverse environmental conditions.

Implementation

In this study, the PSO method will be employed for system design and optimization. The optimization criterion will be defined as the ratio of water production to thermal power consumption, which directly indicates system efficiency. Results of optimization

The optimization process aimed to maximize freshwater production per unit of energy by adjusting air velocity, flow distribution ratio, and device width. After 334 iterations, the optimal configuration was determined: an air velocity of 6.89 m/s, a flow distribution ratio of 35.97%, and a device width of 0.63 m. Under these conditions, the system achieved a freshwater production rate of 3.04 kg per kWh, producing 360.5 L of water per day with an average outlet relative humidity of 99% and power consumption of 4.94 kW. Compared to previous M-Cycle-based desalination systems5, the proposed configuration demonstrates over a threefold improvement in energy efficiency, as measured by the GOR, highlighting its superior thermodynamic performance.

To evaluate the robustness and practical feasibility of the optimized configuration, a parametric sensitivity analysis was conducted. Each of the three optimized parameters—air velocity, flow distribution ratio, and device width—was individually perturbed by ± 10%, while the other parameters were held constant. The analysis revealed that system performance, measured in terms of water production per kWh (WPPE), is most sensitive to variations in the air velocity, where a ± 10% deviation caused an average ~ 8.4% change in WPPE. In comparison, device width and flow distribution ratio showed moderate sensitivities, with ~ 3.7% and ~ 2.9% variations in WPPE, respectively. These findings indicate that while all parameters influence performance, the air velocity plays a more dominant role in thermodynamic efficiency and freshwater yield.

Furthermore, practical constraints were considered regarding the implementation of the optimal airflow velocity (6.89 m/s). Achieving this airflow in real-world systems would require appropriately sized ducts and high-efficiency fans capable of maintaining airflow against system pressure drops. Based on ASHRAE duct sizing guidelines and empirical fan performance curves69, sustaining this velocity in a 0.63-m-wide channel would require an estimated fan power of 4–5.5 kW, assuming a pressure drop of approximately 180–210 Pa. This aligns closely with the total electrical power consumption reported for the system (4.94 kW), confirming the design’s feasibility for practical deployment. Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated a novel desalination system integrating the M-Cycle with a HDH process, aimed at enhancing thermodynamic efficiency and freshwater yield without dependence on external thermal sources. Unlike conventional thermal desalination technologies that rely on direct heating and high energy inputs, the proposed configuration leverages ambient thermal energy through indirect evaporative cooling, facilitating simultaneous heat and mass transfer in a low-energy framework.

Numerical modeling techniques have proven to be powerful tools70–75 in analyzing and optimizing complex thermal-fluid systems, particularly in early-stage design and performance evaluation. A comprehensive CFD model was employed to resolve the coupled transport phenomena within the system’s heat and mass exchanger. The model captured spatially resolved air and water temperature profiles, relative humidity distributions, and local evaporation rates across the dry and wet channels. Importantly, the modeling framework eliminated oversimplified assumptions—such as uniform evaporation or fully saturated outlet conditions—thus enabling a more accurate assessment of system performance.

Optimization of operational and geometric parameters was performed via PSO, targeting a maximized ratio of water production to energy input. The resulting optimal configuration—air velocity of 6.89 m/s, exchanger width of 0.63 m, and 35.97% dry air split—achieved a water production rate of 3.04 kg per kWh of electricity consumed. This corresponds to a GOR approximately 3.2 times higher than that reported in previous studies, such as the M-Cycle-based system by Tariq et al.5, which achieved a GOR of ~ 0.8 under comparable conditions.

In addition to its superior energy performance, the proposed system offers operational simplicity, avoids the complexity of solar-thermal integration, and is readily adaptable to off-grid settings. These results highlight the system’s potential as a sustainable, scalable desalination solution. Future work may explore hybridization with renewable electricity sources and modular design adaptations to enhance deployment in diverse environmental contexts.
