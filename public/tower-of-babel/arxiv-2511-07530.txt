Abstract
. We study Penrose tilings of the plane ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} and nonperiodic infinite frieze patterns from the point of view of Cohen–Macaulay representation theory: Triangulations of the completed ∞ \infty -gon correspond to subcategories of the Frobenius category 𝒞 2 = CM ℤ ​ ( ℂ ⁡ [ x , y ] / ( x 2 ) ) \mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathrm{CM}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^{2})) , the singularity category of the curve singularity of type A ∞ A_{\infty} . We relate Penrose tilings to certain triangulations of the completed ∞ \infty -gon, and thus to the corresponding subcategories of 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}_{2} . We then extend the cluster character of Paquette and Yıldırım for a triangulated category modelling said triangulations to our setting. This allows us to define nonperiodic infinite friezes patterns coming from triangulations of the completed ∞ \infty -gon and in particular from Penrose tilings.

1.
Introduction

Penrose tilings are aperiodic tilings of the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which were investigated by the renowned physicist Roger Penrose in the 1970s
[
Pen74
,
Pen79
]
. They appear everywhere from medieval Islamic geometric patterns to the study of quasicrystals which earned Shechtman the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry
[
Fer18
]
.

Frieze patterns were introduced by Coxeter in
[
Cox71
]
motivated by Gauss’s
pentagramma mirificum
. They are grids of positive integers satisfying a determinant rule and together with Conway, Coxeter showed that there is a one-to-one correspondence between frieze patterns and triangulated polygons
[
CC73a
,
CC73b
]
. These friezes have subsequently appeared in the representation theory of finite dimensional algebras in the last two decades and they are a topic of active research. We refer to
[
MG15
,
Bau21
,
Pre23
,
Fab25
]
for surveys on frieze patterns.

By the
A_{\infty}
-singularity, we mean the commutative ring
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^{2})
. This is a nonreduced hypersurface ring of Krull dimension one - hence it has a nonisolated singularity - and
\mathrm{Spec}(S)
is a plane curve singularity of type
A_{\infty}
. It is referred to as the
A_{\infty}
-singularity as it can be seen (in some sense) as a limit of
A_{n}
-singularities given by
\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^{2}+y^{n+1})
, see
[
Sie83
]
.

Our goal in this paper is to draw connections between these classical and beautiful objects using the representation theory and the combinatorics associated to the commutative ring
S
. To start with, we equip
S
with a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading and consider the category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded maximal Cohen-Macaulay
S
-modules. In
[
ACF+23
]
, the authors studied this Frobenius category as the Grassmannian category of infinite rank associated to an infinite version of the Grassmannian of planes and they classified cluster tilting subcategories of it. In particular, they showed that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between cluster tilting subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and fountain triangulations of the completed
\infty
-gon. We are going to recall the necessary background in Section
2
.
Let us note that there are several other approaches to categorify the combinatorics of
\infty
-gons, for example
[
HJ12
,
Fis16
,
PY21
,
IT15
,
ÇKP25
]
, cf. the discussion in the introduction of
[
ACF+23
]
where some of these approaches are compared.

One of the fundamental tools in the representation theory of cluster algebras and cluster categories is the notion of mutation. In the finite case, the rules of mutation are well-defined and well-understood. In particular, for cluster algebras of type
A
, mutation can be described combinatorially as flips of diagonals in a triangulated polygon. In the infinite case, there are simply too many triangulations of an
\infty
-gon. There are many pairs of triangulations which may not be connected via a series of finitely many mutations. To attack this problem, Baur and Gratz introduced in
[
BG18
]
strong mutation equivalence and transfinite mutation equivalence. They proved that any two triangulations of the completed
\infty
-gon are transfinitely mutation equivalent. This is where our motivation for our main theorem comes from: finite mutation equivalence gives us a very wild behaviour and transfinite mutation equivalence gives us only one equivalence class. Can we find a new type of mutation equivalence which lives somewhere between these two extremes? Our answer is affirmative. We introduce an equivalence relation
\sim
on the set of cluster tilting subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and a special class of cluster tilting subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
which allows us to prove our first main theorem.

Theorem A
.

Let
2^{\mathbb{N}}
denote the set of all sequences on
\{0,1\}
and
\mathcal{P}
denote the space of Penrose tilings.

(1)

There exists a surjection

\displaystyle\psi\colon\{\text{Cluster tilting subcategories of }\mathcal{C}_{2}\}\twoheadrightarrow 2^{\mathbb{N}}\times 2^{\mathbb{N}}\ .

(2)

For two cluster tilting subcategories
\mathsf{S}_{1},\mathsf{S}_{2}
of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, we have
\mathsf{S}_{1}\sim\mathsf{S}_{2}
if and only if
\psi(\mathsf{S}_{1})
and
\psi(\mathsf{S}_{2})
differ only in finitely many places.

(3)

There exists a one-to-one correspondence

\displaystyle\frac{\{\text{Special cluster tilting subcategories of }\mathcal{C}_{2}\}}{\sim}\longleftrightarrow\frac{\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}}{\text{isometry}}\ .

Our Section
3
is devoted to defining what these special cluster tilting subcategories are and to proving this theorem. In Section
4
, we move to infinite friezes. The representation theoretic link between friezes and cluster algebras has been well-established since the beginning of 2000s, starting with the work of Caldero and Chapoton
[
CC06
]
. The main tool here is a function called
cluster character
. However, this map often requires some finiteness conditions. Recently,
[
PY21
, Section 6]
, went through the technical details and defined a cluster character
\chi_{\mathcal{T}}
on a triangulated category associated to the
\infty
-gon where
\mathcal{T}
is a cluster tilting subcategory of this triangulated category. Utilising their methods, given a cluster tilting subcategory
\mathsf{S}
of the Frobenius category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, we define a cluster character
X^{\mathsf{S}}
on (a certain subcategory of)
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Our second main theorem is as follows.

Theorem B

(see Theorem
4.13
and Definition
4.10
)
.

Let
\mathsf{S}
be a cluster tilting subcategory of the Frobenius category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Then, the cluster character
X^{\mathsf{S}}
yields an infinite fountain frieze
\mathcal{F}
with coefficients.

We devote Section
5
to examples, in particular, we compute a fountain frieze and a frieze coming from a leapfrog triangulation of the
\infty
-gon.

2.1.
Maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules over the
A_{\infty}
-singularity

Given a commutative ring
S
, a finitely generated
S
-module
M
is
maximal Cohen–Macaulay
if
0pt_{S}M_{\mathfrak{p}}=\dim M_{\mathfrak{p}}=\dim S_{\mathfrak{p}}
for every prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
of
S
. When
S
is a commutative Gorenstein ring, this condition is equivalent to requiring that
\mathrm{Ext}_{S}^{i}(M,S)=0
for all
i>0
and maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules coincide with Gorenstein-projective modules, see e.g.
[
Buc21
, Chapter 4]
. By virtue of the Depth Lemma, the category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules
\mathrm{CM}(S)
is an exact category and in the Gorenstein case the above Ext-vanishing gives us a Frobenius structure. In this special case, the category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules modulo projectives, i.e.
the stable category
of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules
\underline{\mathrm{CM}}(S)
, has the natural structure of a triangulated category with the shift functor
\Omega^{-1}
(the coszygy functor) and is equivalent (as triangulated category) to the singularity category of
S
which is defined as the Verdier quotient of the bounded derived category of
S
-modules by the subcategory of perfect complexes by the celebrated work of Buchweitz
[
Buc21
]
.

Next, we continue with another celebrated work from the 1980’s: Eisenbud’s matrix factorizations
[
Eis80
]
. Over a local hypersurface ring
\mathbb{C}\llbracket x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\rrbracket/(f)
given by a polynomial
f
, every maximal Cohen–Macaulay module has a two-periodic minimal projective resolution. Moreover, the ranks of the free modules appearing in this resolution are all equal, say
l>0
. This gives us a pair of square matrices
A
and
B
with entries in
\mathbb{C}\llbracket x_{0},\ldots,x_{d}\rrbracket
such that
AB=BA=f\mathbb{I}_{l}
. The pair
(A,B)
is called a
matrix factorization
of
f
. In fact, the assignment
(A,B)\mapsto\mathrm{coker}A
yields an equivalence of categories between the category of matrix factorizations and maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules. This equivalence reduces to an equivalence of triangulated categories between the stable category of maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules and the homotopy category of matrix factorizations.

One can also make these definitions in the graded setting albeit with a little extra care.

As mentioned in the introduction, we are interested in maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules over the ring
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y]/(x^{2})
. We will consider
S
as a graded ring with
\deg(x)=1
and
\deg(y)=-1
and we will consider the category
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathrm{CM}_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
of graded maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules. This is a Frobenius category and it has the Krull–Remak–Schmidt property.

The ring
S
has countable Cohen–Macaulay type. That is, there are countably many indecomposable maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules up to isomorphism, see, for instance
[
LW12
, Chapter 14]
. In the local case, the indecomposable maximal Cohen–Macaulay
S
-modules were classified by Buchweitz–Greuel–Schreyer
[
BGS87
, Proposition 4.1]
and all these modules are gradable. So, one has the following families of indecomposable objects in the category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, see
[
ACF+23
, Proposition 2.1]
:

(1)

the shifts of projective module
S
given by the matrix factorization

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[x,y](-2)\xrightarrow{1}\mathbb{C}[x,y](-2)\xrightarrow{x^{2}}\mathbb{C}[x,y],

(2)

the shifts of the module
\mathbb{C}[y]\cong S/(x)
given by the matrix factorization

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[x,y](-2)\xrightarrow{x}\mathbb{C}[x,y](-1)\xrightarrow{x}\mathbb{C}[x,y],

(3)

the shifts of the family of the ideals:
(x,y^{k})
with
k\geq 1
given by the matrix factorization

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[x,y](-2)\oplus\mathbb{C}[x,y](k-1)\xrightarrow{B}\mathbb{C}[x,y](-1)\oplus\mathbb{C}[x,y](k)\xrightarrow{A}\mathbb{C}[x,y]\oplus\mathbb{C}[x,y](k+1)

where

\displaystyle A=B=\begin{bmatrix}x&y^{k}\\
0&-x\end{bmatrix}.

2.2.
The combinatorics

We can visualise the indecomposable graded maximal Cohen–Macaulay
S
-modules as arcs in a (completed)
\infty
-gon. By an
\infty
-gon
, we mean a disc with a discrete set of marked points on the boundary indexed by the integers. By the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, these marked points need to have at least one accumulation point. Our assumption is that there is a unique accumulation point which we call
\infty
. By adding
\infty
to the set of the marked points, we talk about the
completed

\infty
-gon.

By an
arc
in the completed
\infty
-gon, we mean a pair
(a,b)
in
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
with
a<b
. We say that an arc is a
finite arc
if
a<b<\infty
and an
infinite arc
if
a<b=\infty
. To the shifts of the regular module
S
, we associate the
boundary arcs
: the projective module
S(j)
is matched with the arc
(-j,1-j)
for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. For the ideal
(x,y^{k})(j)
with
k\geq 1
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have the finite arc
(-j-k,1-j)
. Finally, for the modules
\mathbb{C}[y](j)
, we have the corresponding arcs
(-j,\infty)
.

Conversely, an arc
(a,b)
with
a<b
is matched with the ideal
(x,y^{b-a-1})(1-b)
, and in particular, a boundary arc
(a,a+1)
corresponds to
S(-a)
. The infinite arc
(a,\infty)
corresponds to the module
\mathbb{C}[y](-a)
. This gives us a bijection between the arcs on the
\infty
-gon and the set of isomorphism classes of graded maximal Cohen–Macaulay
S
-modules.

We say that two arcs
(a,b)
and
(c,d)

cross
if
a<c<b<d
or
c<a<d<b
. Equivalently, we have that
\mathrm{Ext}^{1}
between the corresponding modules in
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\mathrm{CM}_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is
1
-dimensional. We will often use the combinatorial terminology when discussing properties of the category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. A
triangulation
of the (completed)
\infty
-gon is a maximal set of noncrossing arcs. The triangulations of the
\infty
-gon were described in
[
HJ12
, Lemma 3.3]
and for the completed
\infty
-gon in
[
BG18
]
.

A set of arcs
\{(n,x_{i})\colon i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is called a
right fountain
if
x_{i}
is an increasing sequence. We call
n
, in this case, the
fountain point
of the right fountain. Left fountains are defined similarly. A
fountain
is a union of a left fountain and a right fountain with the same fountain point. If we are working with the completed
\infty
-gon, we also consider the infinite arc at the fountain point. There exists a one-to-one correspondence between cluster tilting subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and fountain triangulations of the completed
\infty
-gon, i.e., triangulations of the completed
\infty
-gon containing a fountain
[

, Theorem 4.11]
. For a schematic picture, see Fig.

Next we recall the notion of
mutation
of a triangulation: Let

be a triangulation of the (completed)
\infty
-gon. Then an arc
\gamma\in T
is mutable if and only if there exists an arc
\gamma^{\prime}\neq\gamma
in the (completed)
\infty

\mu_{\gamma}(T)=(T\backslash\{\gamma\})\cup\{\gamma^{\prime}\}

BD

and

2.3.
Penrose tilings

There are several books and survey articles introducing and discussing Penrose tilings both for a general audience and the working mathematician
[
GS87
,
Gar97
,
D’A23
]
. We will refer to the recent book
[
D’A23
, Chapter 6]
by D’Andrea for the preliminaries that we outline below.

Penrose tilings are parametrised by sequences on the two element set
\{0,1\}
with the property that there are no consecutive
1
’s. More precisely we have a bijection

\displaystyle\frac{\{\text{Penrose tilings}\}}{\text{isometry}}\longleftrightarrow\frac{\{\text{sequences on }\{0,1\}\text{ with no consecutive $1$'s}\}}{\text{tail equivalence}}\ .

Here, tail equivalence means that we declare two sequences equivalent if and only if they agree on all but finitely many places. The set of all sequences on
\{0,1\}
, equipped with Tychonoff topology, is homeomorphic to the Cantor set. Moreover, the map that replaces a
1
with a
10
is a homeomorphism from the set of all sequences on
\{0,1\}
to the subspace consisting of sequences with no consecutive 1s. Moreover, the tail equivalence is dense on this space giving that the quotient space on the right hand side of our bijection above has the trivial topology. When a quotient space has “bad topology”, one can instead take a groupoid approach and study them using
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-algebras and
K
-theory. This is what Connes did with his noncommutative geometry and attached a noncommutative algebraic object to the space of Penrose tilings
[
Con94
]
.

In the decades following Connes’ influential book on noncommutative geometry, there have also been different approaches to establish a noncommutative version of algebraic geometry. One approach is to use Serre’s equivalence
[
Ser55
]
between modules and quasicoherent sheaves. Indeed, one can reconstruct a scheme (up to isomorphism) from the abelian category of quasicoherent sheaves on it
[
Gab62
,
Ros98a
,
Ros98b
]
. To this end, given a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded noncommutative ring
A
, one considers the
noncommutative space
whose category of quasi-coherent sheaves is given by the quotient
\mathrm{Qgr}A:=\mathrm{Mod}^{\mathbb{Z}}A/\mathrm{Fdim}A
of the category of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded left
A
-modules by the Serre subcategory of modules that are unions of their finite dimensional submodules. By definition, the objects of
\mathrm{Qgr}A
is the same as objects of
\mathrm{Mod}^{\mathbb{Z}}A
but there are more isomorphisms in this category: in particular, two modules
M,N
are isomorphic when
M_{n}\cong N_{n}
for all but finitely many
n
.

Using this philosophy, in
[
Smi14
]
Smith showed that the space of Penrose tilings can be thought of as a noncommutative algebraic space. Precisely, he established a categorical equivalence between
\mathrm{Qgr}B
and the category of modules over Connes’ ring where
B
is the graded noncommutative algebra
\mathbb{C}\langle x,y\rangle/(x^{2})
with
x
and
y
both living in degree 1. Moreover, he gave a correspondence between
point modules
over
B
and Penrose tilings using the sequences on
\{0,1\}
. This was a motivating factor for us given that Smith’s ring is a noncommutative version of the
A_{\infty}
-singularity.

3.
Penrose tilings and friezes from triangulations of the
\infty

In this section, we will study the relation between Penrose tilings and the Frobenius category

of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules over the

-singularity. We will also see how one can go from a frieze to a Penrose tiling. Following Remark

, we will start with looking at how to get a Penrose tiling from a triangulation of natural numbers.

3.1.
Penrose tilings from right fountains

Let
R
be a triangulation with a right fountain of the
\infty
-gon. We call such a triangulation
R
a
right fountain triangulation
.
Assume that it has fountain point
0
so that we restrict ourselves to triangulations of natural numbers. Associated to
R
, there are two natural special types of arcs in
R
.

Definition 3.1
.

An arc
(a,b)
in
R
is a
fountain arc
if
a=0
. We denote the set of fountain arcs of
R
by
A_{1}(R)
.

Note that, by definition, the set of fountain arcs of
R
is (countably) infinite. Hence, it naturally defines a sequence
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}
on the two element set
\{0,1\}
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{R}_{n}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if }(0,n+1)\in R\\
0\quad\text{else.}\end{cases}

Note that the sequence starts by checking whether
(0,2)
is in
R
as
(0,1)
is contained in any triangulation. The second sequence
\boldsymbol{y}^{R}
given by the set of fountain arcs of
R
is a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers and it is defined by the rule

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n}=\text{the }(n+1)\text{st positive integer }\ell\text{ such that }(0,\ell)\in R.

Remark 3.2
.

A fountain arc
(0,n)
corresponds to the graded module
(x,y^{n-1})(1-n)
in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
under our identification. Hence, the smallest power of
y
which lies in this module lives in degree zero. In fact, the converse is also true. If the smallest power of
y
which lies in a module of the form
(x,y^{k})(j)
lives in degree zero, then the corresponding arc is a fountain arc. Note that this works only when we take 0 to be our fountain point.

Lemma 3.5

For any

Proof.

We know that the arc

∎

The assertion of Lemma

By virtue of these three lemmas, we propose the following equivalence relation.

Definition 3.14
.

We say that two right fountain triangulations
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are equivalent and write
R_{1}\sim R_{2}
if
R_{1}
can be mutated into
R_{2}
by using only finitely many mutations at arcs of the form
(0,\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n})
or
(\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n+1})
and possibly infinitely many mutations at other arcs.

This definition gives us the language to state the following theorem which follows from the preceding lemmas.

Theorem 3.15
.

Two right fountain triangulations
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are equivalent if and only if the sequences
\boldsymbol{x}^{R_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{R_{2}}
differ in finitely many places.

The following lemma follows from the definitions of our sequences.

Lemma 3.16
.

Given a right fountain triangulation
R
. For any
n\geq 1
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

We have
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}_{n}=\boldsymbol{x}^{R}_{n+1}=1
.

(2)

We have
\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n}=1
.

(3)

The arc
(\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}^{R}_{n+1})
is a boundary arc.

Definition 3.17
.

We say that a right fountain triangulation
R
is
special
if there exists a right fountain triangulation
R^{\prime}
such that
R\sim R^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{R^{\prime}}_{n+1}-\boldsymbol{y}^{R^{\prime}}_{n}\geq 2
for all
n\geq 1
. In other words, there does not exist any boundary arcs in
A_{2}(R^{\prime})
.

The following proposition is just another characterisation of special right fountain triangulations.

Proposition 3.18
.

A right fountain triangulation
R
is special if and only if it contains only finitely many boundary arcs in
A_{2}(R)
.

Combining all this discussion, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.19
.

The map
\boldsymbol{x}
that sends a special right fountain
R
to the sequence
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}
induces a bijection

\displaystyle\{\emph{specialrightfountaintriangulations}\}/\sim\quad

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\frac{\{\emph{sequenceson}\{0,1\}\emph{withnoconsecutive1^{\prime}s}\}}{\emph{tailequivalence}}

which, in turn, gives us a bijection

\displaystyle\{\emph{specialrightfountaintriangulations}\}/\sim\quad

\displaystyle\longleftrightarrow\frac{\{\textup{Penrose tilings}\}}{\textup{isometry}}\ .

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
A

We know from Section
2.2
, that cluster tilting subcategories of the Frobenius category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are in bijection with fountain triangulations of the completed
\infty
-gon. On the other hand, we have established in Section
3.1
a relation between right fountain triangulations and 01-sequences. Hence, now we have all the ingredients to understand the relation between Penrose tilings and cluster tilting subcategories of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

A fountain triangulation consists of a left fountain and a right fountain at a point
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. We might assume, up to shifts, that the fountain point is
a=0
. As all the results of Section
3.1
hold true also for left fountains, given a cluster tilting subcategory
\mathsf{S}
of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
(or an appropriate shift so that the fountain point is 0), we obtain a pair of sequences
(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf{S},<0},\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf{S},>0})
where the first component is given by the left fountain associated to
\mathsf{S}
and the second by the corresponding right fountain. It is clear that the assignment

\displaystyle\mathsf{S}\mapsto(\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf{S},<0},\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf{S},>0})

is surjective. This proves the first part of Theorem
A
from the Introduction. The second part of Theorem
A
follows from Theorem
3.15
and the third by Corollary
3.19
combined with the discussion below.

We conclude the proof by translating the language of special right/left fountains to the categorical setting. Recall that
R
is said to be a special right fountain triangulation if it is equivalent to a right fountain triangulation
R^{\prime}
so that
A_{2}(R^{\prime})
does not contain a boundary arc. distinguished classes
A_{1}(R)
and
A_{2}(R)
of arcs in
R
correspond to distinguished full subcategories of a cluster tilting subcategory
\mathsf{S}
. Moreover, the boundary arcs correspond to projective objects in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Hence, with all the identifications we have made so far, for a given cluster tilting subcategory
\mathsf{S}
up to shift, it is special if it is equivalent to a cluster tilting subcategory
\mathsf{S}^{\prime}
such that the corresponding special subcategory
A_{2}(\mathsf{S}^{\prime})
is
stable
in the sense that it does not contain any projective objects. ∎

3.3.
Friezes and Penrose Tilings

Using the bijection from Section
3.1
, we relate Penrose tilings to certain arrays, which we call
half-friezes
: following Conway and Coxeter, we want to associate a quiddity sequence to a fountain triangulation of the
\infty
-gon and construct a frieze from that sequence. For simplicity assume that
0
is the fountain point. Here we quickly run into problems. There are infinitely many arcs starting at
0
, which would give us as quiddity
a_{0}=\infty
. Consequently, any arc crossing
0
, that is, any arc of the form
(-i,j)
, with
i,j>0
, would have a non-integer value. However, all other elements of the quiddity sequence and the remaining arcs are well-defined integers. Therefore we make the following

Definition 3.20
.

An
infinite right half-frieze
at
r\in\mathbb{Z}
is a set of elements
m_{a,b}
in a commutative ring
R
with unit
1
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
r\leq a\leq b
,
which satisfies the following rules:

(1)

m_{a,a}=0
for any
a\geq r
.

(2)

m_{a,a+1}=1
for any
a\geq r
.

(3)

m_{a,b}\neq 0
for any pair
(a,b)
with
r\leq a<b
.

(4)

m_{a,b}\;m_{a+1,b+1}-m_{a+1,b}\;m_{a,b+1}=1
for any pair
a,b
with
r\leq a<b
.

Similarly, one can define an
infinite left half-frieze
as the set of non-zero elements
m_{a,b}
with constant
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, where the following hold:

(1’)

m_{a,a}=0
for any
a\leq l
.

(2’)

m_{a-1,a}=1
for any
a\leq l
.

(3’)

m_{a,b}\neq 0
for any pair
(a,b)
with
a<b\leq l
.

(4’)

m_{a-1,b-1}\;m_{a,b}-m_{a-1,b}\;m_{a,b-1}=1
for any pair
a,b
with
a<b\leq l
.

We define an
infinite half-frieze
or
fountain frieze
at
r=l
to be the union of the left half frieze at
r
and the right half frieze at
r
.

In the following we will consider the entries
m_{a,b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
b>a
, that is, we will only look at
integral
(right/left) half-friezes.
When it is clear from the context, we will just use the term frieze without the adjectives.
The sequence
m_{a,a+2}
with
a\neq r-1
(
a\geq r
resp.
a+2\leq l
) is called the
quiddity sequence
of the (right/left) half-frieze.

It is clear from our rules (and it is easy to see when visualised on an array where
m_{a,b}
lives in the
a
th row and
b
th column) that a quiddity sequence determines the frieze. We refer to
[
BPT16
]
for more on infinite friezes and their quiddity sequences.

Definition 3.21
.

Given a right fountain triangulation
R
of the completed
\infty
-gon (with fountain point

), we can define a

by the quiddity sequence

\displaystyle m_{a,a+2}=\text{the number of triangles in }R\text{ that the vertex }a+1\text{ is adjacent to}.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any finite arc
(a,b)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
which is not a boundary arc (i.e.

) the short exact sequence

(4.1.1)

\displaystyle 0\to(a-1,b-1)\to(a-1,b)\oplus(a,b-1)\to(a,b)\to 0

is an almost split sequence (i.e., the AR-sequence) ending at
(a,b)
. Note that the AR-translate of
(a,b)
is
\tau((a,b))=(a-1,b-1)
.

The corresponding exchange sequence is

(4.1.2)

0\to(a,b)\to(a-1,a)\oplus(b-1,b)\to(a-1,b-1)\to 0

Similarly, the short exact sequence

(4.1.3)

\displaystyle 0\to(a,b)\to(a+1,b)\oplus(a,b+1)\to(a+1,b+1)\to 0

is an almost split sequence (i.e., the AR-sequence) starting at
(a,b)
.

For an infinite arc
(a,\infty)
there does not exist an almost split sequence starting or ending with it.

Proof.

The claims for finite arcs can be readily verified by looking at the description of extensions above and computing the minimal ones. The statement for infinite arcs can be also be verified by direct computation by using morphisms from
[
ACF+23
]
and extensions listed above.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(a,\infty)
,
(b,\infty)
be infinite arcs in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. A homomorphism

. In other words: the only irreducible maps between infinite arcs are
(a-1,\infty)\xrightarrow{}(a,\infty)

Moreover, any homomorphism from a finite arc

to
(a,\infty)
factors, and any homomorphism from
(a,\infty)
to a finite arc
(c,d)
factors. In other words: there are no irreducible maps between infinite arcs and finite arcs.

Proof.

This is again verified by direct computation using the description of the morphisms from

∎

4.2.
Paquette–Yıldırım cluster character for
\mathcal{C}

Paquette–Yıldırım define a cluster character on
\mathcal{C}
with respect to a cluster tilting subcategory
\mathcal{T}
for any

via the formula

(4.5.4)

\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(M)=x^{-\mathsf{coind}(\varphi(M))}\sum_{d}\chi(\mathrm{Gr}_{d}(\varphi(M)))x^{\mathsf{coind}(d)-\mathsf{ind}(d)}\ .

Here

is the covariant functor that sends

to
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(-,M)
and

runs over dimension vectors of finitely presented submodules of

, see
[
PY21
, Section 5]
. Index and Co-index are defined in
[
PY21
, Section 6.2]
.

Note that the variables

are in the homogeneous coordinate ring of the infinite Grassmannian
Gr(2,\infty)
with the frozen variables
x_{i,i+1}
set to
1
, namely
\mathbb{C}[x_{ij}:i,j\in\mathbb{Z}]/(
Plücker relations
)
, where
\deg(x_{ij})=1
and
i<j-1
. See
[
GG14
, Appendix]
for more details. Choosing the cluster tilting subcategory
\mathcal{T}
means to choose a fountain triangulation in the
\infty
-gon. Thus, we have variables
x_{ab}
, where
(a,b)
corresponds to an arc in
\mathcal{T}
(we may assume here that
a<b
). For any
T\in\mathcal{T}
we denote by
[T]
the corresponding class in
K_{0}(\mathcal{C})
, and if
[T]=\sum_{i\in I}m_{i}[T_{i}]
, then
T_{i}
is an indecomposable corresponding to an arc
(a_{i},b_{i})
in
\mathcal{T}
. Thus here we mean with
x^{[T]}=\prod_{i\in I}x_{a_{i}b_{i}}^{m_{i}}
. Note that
\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(M)
is an element of
\mathbb{C}\llbracket x_{a,b}^{\pm}:(a,b)\in\mathcal{T}\rrbracket
.

In
[
PY21
, Prop. 6.9]
it is shown that
\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(M)
satisfies the product formula, that is,

\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(M_{1}\oplus M_{2})=\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(M_{1})\cdot\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(M_{2})

for any
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
. However, since
\mathcal{C}
is not
2
-CY, the exchange formula for the cluster character only holds on the subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
corresponding to the finite arcs in the completed
\infty
-gon, see
[
PY21
, Theorem 6.13]
.

4.3.
Cluster character for
\mathcal{C}_{2}

Now we switch to the Frobenius category
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and show that there is a cluster character too, see Def.
4.6
. In Section
4.4
we will then show that our cluster character satisfies the generalised frieze relations, matching up with (infinite) frieze patterns with coefficients in the sense of Cuntz, Holm, and Jørgensen
[
CHJ20
]
, see Theorem
4.13
.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mathsf{S}
be a cluster tilting subcategory of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and let
M\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
an object. Then the cluster character is

(4.6.1)

X^{\mathsf{S}}(M):=x^{\mathsf{ind}_{\mathsf{S}}(M)}\sum_{e}\chi\left(\mathrm{Gr}_{e}(\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}(-,M))\right)x^{\mathsf{coind}_{\mathsf{S}}(e)-\mathsf{ind}_{\mathsf{S}}(e)}\ ,

where the sum runs again over all finitely presented dimension vectors
e
in
\mathrm{Ext}^{1}(-,M)
. Note that
X^{\mathsf{S}}(M)\in\mathbb{C}\llbracket x_{a,b}^{\pm}:(a,b)\in\mathsf{S}\rrbracket=:R_{\mathsf{S}}
. Furthermore, since all the boundary arcs
(n,n+1)
have to be contained in
\mathsf{S}
, we have that
x_{n,n+1}
are variables in
R_{\mathsf{S}}
and
X^{\mathsf{S}}((n,n+1))=x_{n,n+1}
(cf. Lemma
4.9
) are precisely the frozen variables in the homogeneous coordinate ring of
Gr(2,\infty)
.

Our cluster character follows the general principle of defining a cluster character for a 2-Calabi-Yau extriangulated category equipped with a cluster tilting object as outlined in
[
WWZ24
, Section 4.3]
. The only and the main obstacle is that our cluster tilting subcategories are not additively generated by a single object, they have infinitely many indecomposable objects up to isomorphisms. This situation, in the triangulated case, is treated carefully by Paquette and Yıldırım. We note the isomorphisms

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ext}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}^{1}(X,M)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\underline{\mathcal{C}_{2}}}(\Omega X,M)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\underline{\mathcal{C}_{2}}}(X,\Omega^{-1}M)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\pi X,\pi M[1])

where
\pi
denotes the composition

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}\to\underline{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{C}.

Hence, the Grassmannian in our cluster character is meaningful: it agrees with the Grassmannian defined by Paquette and Yıldırım when the object
M
is not projective. When
M
is projective, its image in the stable category is isomorphic to the zero object and so is its shift. So, the Euler characteristic returns zero. As every projective object belongs to the cluster tilting subcategory
\mathsf{S}
, this aligns with Lemma
4.9
(a) below.

Secondly, we note that there is an equality
\mathsf{ind}_{S}(M)=-\mathsf{coind}_{S}(\Omega^{-1}M)
which also proves that for a non-projective object
M
we have an equality (cf.
[
FK10
, Theorem 3.3(b)]
and
[
WWZ24
, Proposition 4.5]
)

\displaystyle X^{\mathsf{S}}(M)=\chi_{\pi S}(\pi M[1])\ ,

where
\pi:\mathcal{C}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is the projection on the stable category.
From these two remarks, it follows that our formula does indeed give a cluster character, when restricted to generically free maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules. Here we note that the subcategory of generically free maximal Cohen–Macaulay modules in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is indeed stably
2
-CY, see
[
ACF+24
, Prop. 3.12]
.

.

\mathcal{T}
in

in Proposition

5.1.
Fountain triangulation

For ease of comparison with the computations in

, Example 6.14]
, we use the same notation. First we will compute the fountain frieze consisting of a left and a right half frieze for the fountain triangulation

\mathsf{S}=\{(0,n):|n|\geq 2\}\cup\{(n,n+1):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\{(0,\infty)\}

in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. In the notation of Paquette–Yıldırım, this means that the triangulation consists of arcs
\alpha_{i}=(0,n)
and
\beta_{i}=(-n,0)
for
n\geq 2
. We will use our cluster character
X^{\mathsf{S}}
and restrict the variables
x_{a,b}
for
(a,b)\in\mathsf{S}
to
1
. Then we get that
X^{\mathsf{S}}(\alpha_{i})=X^{\mathsf{S}}(\beta_{i})=1
, as well as the cluster characters of the boundary arcs are all
1
. The calculations are the same as for the Paquette–Yıldırım-cluster character for the cluster tilting subcategory
\mathcal{T}=\mathsf{S}\setminus\{(n,n+1):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
of
\mathcal{C}
, see
[
PY21
, Examples 6.14 and 6.15]
, except that
X^{\mathsf{S}}(\alpha)=\chi_{\mathcal{T}}(\alpha[1])
for any
\alpha
in
\mathcal{C}
. The beginning of the frieze looks as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|cccccccccc}&0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8&\ldots\\

Now the entries
(i,j)
with either
0\leq i<j

can be computed with Lemma
4.9
, that is, the half frieze coming from the fountain triangulation. Here is a picture of the first part of it

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|cccccccccc}&0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8&\ldots\\

5.2.
Leapfrog triangulation

Here we work in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
with

\mathsf{S}=\{(-n,n-1):n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}\}\cup\{(-n,,n):n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\}\cup\{(n,n+1):n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\ .

\mathsf{S}
is not cluster tilting in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, it is still maximally rigid, see
[

. Note that if we consider the projection of
\mathsf{S}
to the Holm–Jørgensen category
\mathcal{D}
, then this is indeed cluster tilting. Hence we can compute the cluster character

for all generically free indecomposable modules in
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, that is, all finite arcs. Setting

for any

, we thus obtain a non-periodic infinite frieze, since we can compute all entries in the frieze (
5.2
) with Lemma
4.9
. Note that the quiddity sequence is

\{a_{i}\}_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }i=0\\
2\text{ if }i=1\\
3\text{ else}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|cccccccccc}&-4&-3&-2&-1&0&1&2&3&4&\ldots\\
\hline\cr-4&0&1&3&8&21&13&4&2&1&\ldots\\

One sees that the elements of
\mathsf{S}
are ordered in the shape of a zigzag. Moreover, all entries of the frieze are Fibonacci numbers.

Here indeed we find that
\mathsf{S}
is equivalent to a triangulation giving the Penrose tiling with index sequence
(0,0,\ldots)
: first we choose as fountain point

. Then the sequence
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}=(0,0,\ldots)
since there are no arcs of the form

in the triangulation corresponding to
\mathsf{S}
. Choosing any other fountain point

will give us a sequence
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}
that is equivalent to
(0,0,\ldots)

, then the
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}
-sequence is
(0,0,\ldots)
, if

, then after finitely many mutations (only one mutation at

is sufficient), the triangulation also yields the
\boldsymbol{x}^{R}
-sequence
(0,0,\ldots)
and thus by Theorem
3.15
the two fountains are equivalent.
