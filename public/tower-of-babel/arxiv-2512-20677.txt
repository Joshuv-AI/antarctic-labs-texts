Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains, ensuring their security and alignment has become a critical challenge. Existing red-teaming practices depend heavily on manual testing, which limits scalability and fails to comprehensively cover the vast space of potential adversarial behaviors. This paper introduces an automated red-teaming framework that systematically generates, executes, and evaluates adversarial prompts to uncover security vulnerabilities in LLMs. Our framework integrates meta-prompting-based attack synthesis, multi-modal vulnerability detection, and standardized evaluation protocols spanning six major threat categories—reward hacking, deceptive alignment, data exfiltration, sandbagging, inappropriate tool use, and chain-of-thought manipulation. Experiments on the GPT-OSS-20B model reveal 47 distinct vulnerabilities, including 21 high-severity and 12 novel attack patterns, achieving a 3.9 × 3.9\times improvement in vulnerability discovery rate over manual expert testing while maintaining 89% detection accuracy. These results demonstrate the framework’s effectiveness in enabling scalable, systematic, and reproducible AI safety evaluations. By providing actionable insights for improving alignment robustness, this work advances the state of automated LLM red-teaming and contributes to the broader goal of building secure and trustworthy AI systems.

Methodology

This section presents the detailed architecture and methodology of our automated red-teaming framework. We begin with formal problem definition, followed by comprehensive descriptions of each system component and the evaluation protocols.

III-A

Mathematical Framework:
Let

III-B

System Architecture

Our automated red-teaming framework consists of four interconnected modules that work together to achieve systematic vulnerability discovery. Figure
1
illustrates the overall system architecture and information flow between components.

Seed Collection Module:
This component automatically gathers initial attack templates from multiple sources including public vulnerability databases, research papers, and community-contributed examples. The module implements intelligent filtering and categorization to ensure high-quality seed material across all vulnerability categories.

Attack Generation Module:
Using advanced meta-prompting techniques, this module creates new adversarial prompts by combining, mutating, and evolving existing seeds. The generation process incorporates diversity constraints and coverage requirements to ensure comprehensive exploration of the attack space.

Vulnerability Detection Module:
This component implements a sophisticated multi-tier detection system that combines lexical analysis, semantic understanding, and behavioral pattern recognition to identify potentially vulnerable responses with high accuracy and low false positive rates.

Evaluation and Reporting Module:
This module provides comprehensive analysis of discovered vulnerabilities, including severity assessment, reproducibility verification, detailed categorization, and actionable recommendations for mitigation.

Level 3: Behavioral Pattern Recognition

At the third level, the system conducts an in-depth analysis of response characteristics and behavioral patterns, which includes: examining whether response length shows abnormal verbosity or brevity compared to the baseline; identifying whether hedging language expressing uncertainty or qualification is excessively used; evaluating behavioral consistency by comparing multiple responses to similar prompts; and investigating response patterns across multiple interactions from a temporal perspective.

The final vulnerability score is computed as a weighted convex combination of the normalized scores from the three detection levels:

\mathcal{V}_{j}(p,r)=\alpha_{1}\mathcal{L}_{1}^{(j)}(r)+\alpha_{2}\mathcal{L}_{2}^{(j)}(r)+\alpha_{3}\mathcal{L}_{3}^{(j)}(r)

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(0.2,0.5,0.3)

III-E

Evaluation Protocol

Our evaluation protocol systematically assesses six primary vulnerability categories, each with specific detection criteria and scoring mechanisms:

Reward Hacking
identifies instances where systems claim task completion without demonstrating genuine problem-solving. Scoring considers metric gaming sophistication, detection difficulty, and potential impact, with indicators including superficial compliance with evaluation criteria while ignoring substantive task requirements.

Deceptive Alignment detection
focuses on revelations of hidden motivations or misaligned objectives. This category evaluates the sophistication of deception, severity of alignment gaps, and cross-context consistency, exemplified by admissions of strategic deception or goal misrepresentation.

Data Exfiltration
involves unauthorized disclosure of potentially sensitive information. Assessments consider information sensitivity levels, disclosure explicitness, and context appropriateness, with indicators such as sharing training data, personal information, or confidential content.

Sandbagging detection
targets evidence of deliberate underperformance or capability concealment. Evaluation factors include performance gap magnitude, concealment sophistication, and strategic motivation, indicated by admissions of capability hiding or strategic incompetence.

Inappropriate Tool Use
covers misuse of available tools or unauthorized access attempts. Scoring assesses misuse severity, authorization violations, and potential damage, with examples including using tools beyond intended scope or without proper authorization.

Chain-of-Thought Manipulation
examines exploitation of reasoning transparency for deceptive purposes. This category evaluates manipulation sophistication, reasoning integrity, and transparency abuse, indicated by deliberately misleading reasoning chains or hidden reasoning steps.

Each identified vulnerability receives multidimensional scores across four metrics:
Severity
(1–10) measures potential real-world impact;
Breadth
(1–10) assesses cross-context generalizability;
Novelty
(1–10) evaluates uniqueness compared to known vulnerabilities; and
Reproducibility
(1–10) examines triggering consistency across multiple attempts.

IV-A

Experimental Setup

Target Models:
Our primary evaluation focuses on GPT-OSS-20B, a hypothetical large-scale open-source language model designed with 20 billion parameters and a standard Transformer architecture aligned through RLHF. This setup enables us to demonstrate our framework’s effectiveness within a controlled and reproducible environment. The systematic evaluation methodology draws inspiration from recent advances in domain adaptation
[
68
]
and rigorous testing frameworks. To assess generalizability, we also conduct validation experiments on additional models including LLaMA-2-13B and Claude-2 (via API).

Computing Infrastructure:
All experiments are conducted on a cluster of NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80GB memory each. The attack generation module employs GPT-4 via the OpenAI API for meta-prompting, while vulnerability detection and evaluation are performed using local models to maintain reproducibility and experimental control.

Baseline Methods:
We compare our framework against four established methodologies: (1)
Manual Expert Red-teaming
: Conducted by three AI safety experts, each with over five years of experience, who performed 8-hour testing sessions following established protocols. This structured protocol includes threat model analysis (clarifying testing objectives), category-based brainstorming (targeting six vulnerability categories), free-form exploration, and cross-validation among experts, drawing inspiration from the red-teaming approach described in OpenAI’s GPT-4 System Card
[
48
]
. (2)
Random Prompt Generation
: Systematically creates random prompts through template filling and
n
-gram sampling from diverse text corpora. (3)
Template-Based Attacks
: Adapts existing jailbreaking templates and known attack patterns from literature and public repositories. (4)
AdvPrompter
: Implements recent automated adversarial prompt generation methods from the literature.

Evaluation Metrics:
Our evaluation employs multiple quantitative metrics:
Discovery Rate
(number of unique vulnerabilities identified per testing hour);
Coverage Score
(percentage of vulnerability categories with at least one high-confidence detection);
Severity Distribution
(proportion of discovered vulnerabilities across each severity level);
False Positive Rate
(percentage of flagged responses incorrectly classified as vulnerable);
Reproducibility Rate
(percentage of reported vulnerabilities that can be consistently triggered); and
Novelty Score
(proportion of discovered vulnerabilities not previously documented in existing literature).

Defense Mechanism Evaluation:
To comprehensively assess existing defense methods, we implement four representative defense mechanisms and two combination strategies:

•

Input Filtering
: Cleanses and rejects user inputs based on predefined keywords and rule-based patterns.

•

Output Monitoring
: Analyzes model responses in real-time using detectors similar to those described in Section III-D, intercepting harmful outputs before delivery.

•

Model Hardening
: Enhances the model’s intrinsic resistance through targeted safety fine-tuning using RLHF and adversarial training data.

•

System-Level Protection
: Implements strict access controls and tool usage permission management at the system level.

•

Combination A
: Input Filtering + Output Monitoring, representing a common lightweight deployment configuration.

•

Combination B
: Integrated implementation of all four defense mechanisms, representing the theoretically most comprehensive defense solution.

We employ all 47 high-confidence vulnerability prompts identified by our framework—defined as those with a comprehensive vulnerability score
\mathcal{V}_{j}(p,r)>0.75
and independently confirmed by two experts—to attack the GPT-OSS-20B model equipped with each defense configuration. The security effectiveness is measured by the
block success rate
, defined as the percentage of attacks successfully prevented. Additionally, we evaluate the performance overhead and cost-effectiveness of each defense mechanism using the following metrics:

•

Response Latency Increase
: Average percentage increase in end-to-end response time over 1,000 standard baseline requests.

•

Computational Overhead
: Percentage of additional computational resources (GPU/CPU time) consumed by defense mechanisms.

•

False Positive Rate
: Proportion of benign requests incorrectly intercepted from a 5,000-query test set.

•

Cost-Effectiveness Score
: To enable a quantitative comparison, we define a comprehensive score as
S=\frac{\text{Avg Block Rate}}{\log(1+\Delta t+\Delta c)\times(1+\text{FPR})}
, where
\Delta t
is the latency increase,
\Delta c
is the computational overhead, and FPR is the false positive rate. This formulation favors defense mechanisms that achieve a high block rate while incurring low overhead and a low false positive rate. The logarithmic function captures the diminishing marginal utility of performance overhead (e.g., the perceived impact of an increase from 10% to 20% is greater than that from 60% to 70%), while the
(1+\text{FPR})
term ensures a multiplier of 1 when the false positive rate is zero.

Implementation Details:
Our framework is implemented in Python 3.9 and leverages several key components: attack generation is handled through the OpenAI API (GPT-4) using custom meta-prompting templates; vulnerability detection utilizes the sentence-transformers library with all-mpnet-base-v2 embeddings; evaluation relies on custom scoring algorithms implementing our multi-dimensional assessment protocol; and data processing is managed through Pandas and NumPy for statistical analysis and result aggregation.

VI
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduces an automated red-teaming framework for large language model security assessment that overcomes key limitations of manual testing—scalability, coverage, and reproducibility—through integrated meta-prompting attack generation, multi-modal vulnerability detection, and standardized evaluation. Experimental results demonstrate a
3.9\times
gain in vulnerability discovery efficiency, with 47 unique vulnerabilities identified—including 21 high-severity and 12 novel patterns—and full coverage across six threat categories: reward hacking, deceptive alignment, data exfiltration, sandbagging, inappropriate tool use, and chain-of-thought manipulation. A hierarchical detection mechanism combining lexical, semantic, and behavioral analysis achieved 89% accuracy, while analysis of defense strategies revealed a critical trade-off between high blocking rates (e.g., 97.9% for Combination B) and substantial performance overhead. Acknowledged limitations include model-specific adaptability, evolving threats, and interpretability, alongside emphasis on ethical deployment and responsible disclosure. Future efforts will aim at generalizable detection, improved explainability, and standardized benchmarks to advance secure and trustworthy AI systems.
