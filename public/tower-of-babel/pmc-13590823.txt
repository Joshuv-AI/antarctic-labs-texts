Multi‐omics insights into salinity tolerance in African bermudagrass
Authors: Prabha Adhikari, Shuhao Yu, Mingying Xiang, Yanqi Wu, Felipe Cevallos, Charles Fontanier, Justin Moss, Dennis Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfrican bermudagrass (Cynodon transvaalensis Burtt‐Davy) is a key parent species used to produce interspecific hybrid bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon × C. transvaalensis). Due to the increasing use of non‐potable water for landscape irrigation, salt stress has become a major concern in the turfgrass industry. While variation in salt tolerance is known within bermudagrass, the molecular basis of this trait is not well understood, particularly in African bermudagrass. In this study, we aimed to identify the molecular mechanisms associated with salt tolerance in African bermudagrass. The first‐generation selfed (S1) population derived from African bermudagrass genotype OKC1163 was evaluated for salt stress response in controlled‐environment conditions in 2024 and 2025. Phenotypic data for visual leaf firing and percent green cover were collected weekly. Using a preexisting high‐density linkage map, 15 quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with salt response were identified on seven linkage groups. Four QTLs were consistently identified as associated with salt response across years. A total of 1261 upregulated genes, 150 upregulated metabolites, and 60 upregulated lipids were identified under salt stress using the genotype OKC1163. Combining genomic and omics analyses, several candidate genes were identified to be involved in the synthesis of abscisic acid and maltose, alteration of the salicylic acid signaling pathway, and the ABC (ATP‐binding cassette) transporter pathway. The results of this study elucidated the molecular mechanisms of African bermudagrass response to salt stress and provide a foundation for future marker‐assisted selection and gene function validation.

INTRODUCTION

African bermudagrass (Cynodon transvaalensis Burtt‐Davy) (2n = 2x = 18) is indigenous to the southwestern Transvaal, Orange Free State, and the northern part of the central Cape Province of South Africa (Harlan et al., 1970). African bermudagrass spreads by stolons, rhizomes, and seeds, forming low‐growing, dense sod that tolerates low mowing heights. Besides use as turfgrass, African bermudagrass is extensively used as a parent crossing with tetraploid or hexaploid common bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon var. dactylon Pers.) to produce interspecific hybrid bermudagrass (C. dactylon × C. transvaalensis) cultivars that are commonly used on golf courses, sports fields, home lawns, and municipal parks (Burton, 1991; Taliaferro, 1992).

Salt stress is one of the growing concerns in the turf industry (B. Huang et al., 2014). Rapid urbanization is converting vast areas of previously nonirrigated land into irrigated lawns and landscapes. This expanding demand exacerbates potable water scarcity, leading to widespread government‐mandated water restrictions on using reclaimed water for irrigation, which is typically high in total dissolved salts, further worsening soil salinization issues, which already affect over 45 million ha of irrigated land worldwide (Carillo et al., 2011; Marcum & Pessarakli, 2006). Salt stress reduces the plant's ability to take up water and essential ions, eventually injuring cells in the transpiring leaves after entering the transpiration stream that disrupts normal growth and development (Munns, 2005; Parida & Das, 2005). Bermudagrass is considered a salt‐tolerant species. However, when subjected to higher salt concentration, bermudagrass gradually turns darker green with reduced leaf sizes, stiffer and narrower leaf blades, and eventual wilting (Lunt et al., 1961; Noor et al., 2023). Shoot growth of turfgrasses typically decreases with increasing salt concentration, whereas salinity can increase root growth at moderate salt concentrations prior to reducing root growth at higher concentrations (Dudeck et al., 1983). A soil salinity of 15 dS m−1 increased root biomass by 59% and 29% in Tifway and Riley's Super Sport (Celebration) bermudagrass, respectively, despite reducing turfgrass quality compared to the control (2.5 dS m−1) (Chavarria et al., 2019). Xiang et al. (2017, 2018) found that variations existed in salt tolerance among bermudagrasses, with some genotypes maintaining acceptable turfgrass quality after exposure to water having a salinity of 15 to 30 dS m−1. The natural variations in salt tolerance in bermudagrass provide a foundation to discern the mechanisms contributing to tolerance for the goal of developing salt‐tolerant bermudagrass cultivars.

After sensing salt stress, plants trigger a series of physiological and biochemical responses that are activated by transcriptional regulations to alleviate the adverse stimuli (Gupta & Huang, 2014; Munns, 2005). One important response is the maintenance of ion homeostasis. The uptake and transport of sodium (Na+) is involved in transporters, such as HKT1 (high‐affinity K+ transporter), which regulates the transportation of Na+ and K+ to prevent excess accumulation of Na+ in leaves through the salt‐overly‐sensitive pathway (Tester & Davenport, 2003). Salt‐tolerant bermudagrass can maintain a relatively low Na+/K+ ratio by excreting Na+ through salt glands (Chavarria et al., 2020; L. Hu et al., 2012). Because Na+ transport is an energy‐demanding process that depends upon the activity of H‐ATPase, salt‐tolerant plants show higher expression of vacuolar and plasma membrane H‐ATPase to generate electrochemical gradients for Na+ exclusion (Fan et al., 2018). The accumulation of compatible osmolytes, such as proline, glycine betaine, and sugar, can maintain osmotic balance that are synthesized from transcriptional regulations (Gupta & Huang, 2014). After exposure to salt stress, plants activate hormone signaling cascades, which can further activate other regulators like transcription factors (TFs). For instance, the increase in abscisic acid (ABA) synthesis in shoots and roots under osmotic stress activates expression of OsNAC5 in Oryza sativa and CdWRKY50 in common bermudagrass. This interaction may regulate the synthesis of proline, sugar, and late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins that play an essential role in salt tolerance (He & Cramer, 1996; X. Huang et al., 2021; Song et al., 2011).

Transcriptome analysis provides a functional perspective on the molecular pathways governing plant salt tolerance (Hussain et al., 2021). In common bermudagrass, studies have identified key transcriptional responses to salinity stress. For instance, L. Hu, Li, et al. (2015) reported the differential expression of TF families (e.g., MYB, bHLH, WRKY) associated with stress responses and growth regulation, alongside genes involved in cell wall modification. Furthermore, the transcriptional response is dynamic. Shao et al. (2021) observed a time‐specific pattern of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with those involved in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (SMs) being exclusively upregulated after 24 h of treatment. Additional work by Z. Fang et al. (2022) has highlighted the differential expression of ABA pathway genes, and Z. Hu et al. (2018) reported 43 differentially expressed microRNAs implicated in carbohydrate metabolism, protein transport, and stress defense under salt stress. However, despite these insights into common bermudagrass, the transcriptomic response of African bermudagrass to salt stress remains uncharacterized.

Metabolomics is a powerful tool that provides a comprehensive profile of low molecular weight metabolites, including amino acids, triglycerides, organic acids, aldehydes, ketone bodies, and hormones, which serve as initiators, intermediates, and end products of critical physiological and biochemical pathways (Kumari & Parida, 2018). By capturing these dynamic molecular changes, metabolomics can elucidate complex signaling circuits and metabolic reactions activated in response to single or combined stresses (Kumari et al., 2024). Recent studies have examined salt tolerance of turfgrass species. For instance, L. Hu, Chen, et al. (2015) investigated metabolomics profiling and found different patterns in roots and leaves of common bermudagrass that involved carbon and nitrogen metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle under salinity. Beyond bermudagrass, the metabolomic response to salt stress has also been investigated in species such as creeping bentgrass (Agrostis stolonifera L.) and seashore paspalum (Paspalum vaginatum Sw.). Studies have partially elucidated that the accumulation of sugars and amino acids contributes to salt tolerance in these species (K. Chen et al., 2020; Pan et al., 2023).

Lipids serve as fundamental structural components of plant cell membranes and play a crucial role in growth and development, environmental stress responses, and metabolic regulation (Hou et al., 2016; Okazaki & Saito, 2014). Phospholipids and glycolipids are two basic lipids in plant cell membranes and function to maintain membrane structure and regulate plant salt stress tolerance (Mansour, 2013). In plant cells, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are continuously generated during normal processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, and photorespiration (Dat et al., 2000). Under salt stress, ROS production increases several‐fold and can function as a signaling molecule to activate defense responses (Cruz de Carvalho, 2008). However, excessive ROS accumulation, if not efficiently scavenged, leads to membrane lipid peroxidation and alterations in membrane lipid content and composition (Gamper & Shapiro, 2007). In rice (O. sativa L.), the accumulation of phosphatidic acid and phosphatidylinositol bisphosphate was detected under salt stress, which acts as salt stress‐signaling lipids (Darwish et al., 2009). As described by Q. Guo et al. (2019), glycolipids, sterols, and lipid fatty acids (FAs) have been found to be altered under salt stress. Zuo et al. (2023) found that lipid remodeling through choline metabolism plays an important role in the salt tolerance mechanism of Kentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis L.).

With advancing sequencing and liquid chromatography combined with tandem mass spectrometry techniques, omics studies, especially integrated transcriptomics, metabolomics, and lipidomic analyses, have successfully provided many new insights into the development and stress response in bermudagrass (Ma et al., 2024; Seelam and Jespersen, 2025; Zhang et al., 2017). However, information provided from omics analyses is massive and difficult to selectively use in breeding and genetic engineering. Genomic analysis enables the elucidation of the genetic basis underlying complex traits. By identifying quantitative trait loci (QTLs) through biparental populations or genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) in diverse populations, these methods directly link genomic regions to phenotypic variation. This not only narrows down global regulatory pathways to specific, causal genomic regions but also captures allelic variations that can be directly utilized in marker‐assisted breeding programs to accelerate crop improvement. So far, only H. Wang et al. (2024) have reported QTL and identified candidate genes associated with salt tolerance in common bermudagrass. In this study, we aim to identify the QTL linked to salt tolerance in African bermudagrass and integrate transcriptomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic analyses to reveal the transcriptional regulation and biosynthesis of bioactive molecules in African bermudagrass under salt stress. We hypothesized that integrated genomic and multi‐omics analyses would identify molecular mechanisms and candidate genes underlying variable salinity tolerance in the OKC1163 S1 population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and establishment

The first‐generation selfed (S1) mapping population derived from African bermudagrass genotype OKC1163 was used for this study (Yu et al., 2021). Parent OKC1163 and 104 progeny were collected from the field in August 2022, planted into pots (10.2 cm × 10.2 cm × 12.7 cm) filled with BM2 media (Berger), and maintained in a greenhouse thereafter. In May of 2023, the population was vegetatively propagated into three replications by planting five stolons per pot. The experimental design was a randomized complete block. Irrigation was applied daily during the establishment period to avoid any drought stress. Plants were fertilized every 2 weeks using a slow‐release fertilizer (Shake and Feed NPK All‐Purpose Plant Food, 12‐4‐8 N‐P2O5‐K2O, The Scotts Miracle‐Gro Co.) at 1 g per pot. Plants were trimmed at 3.8 cm every week. Pesticides were sprayed routinely by rotating four chemicals: abamectin (Avid 0.15EC, Syngenta Crop Protection, Inc.), bifenthrin (Talstar Insecticide, FMC Agricultural Solutions), imidacloprid (Mallet 2F T&O Nufarm), and Spirotetramat (Kontos Miticide Insecticide, Bayer) at label rates to prevent insect infestation in greenhouses.

Salt response screening

The salt response screening was conducted in the Ridge Road Greenhouse at Oklahoma State University, Stillwater, Oklahoma. The first run of the experiment was conducted from March 3 to May 4, 2024. Plants were subsequently allowed to recover under optimal growing conditions until the second run of the experiment was conducted from January 26 to March 18, 2025. Greenhouse conditions were monitored using a mini‐weather station (Watchdog Mini Station 2475, Spectrum Technologies, Inc.) throughout the study. During the first run, the photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) ranged from 239 to 561 µmol m− 2 s− 1. Relative humidity (RH) ranged from 45.8% to 77.4%, and temperatures ranged from 26.3°C to 30.5°C. In the second run, the PPFD ranged from 237.2 to 586.5 µmol m− 2 s− 1. RH ranged from 37.2% to 68.3%, while temperatures ranged from 25.4°C to 32.6°C.

For salt response screening, plants were submerged twice a week in totes (132 cm × 71.1 cm × 12.7 cm) containing a saline solution for 25 min during each submergence event. The saline solution was prepared by mixing tap water with a water‐soluble fertilizer (13‐2‐13 + 6Ca + 3Mg) at a rate of 0.52 g L− 1 (Peters Excel 13‐2‐13 Plug & Bedding Plant Special, ICL Specialty Fertilizer), MgSO4 at 0.04 g L− 1, and Instant Ocean Synthetic Sea Salt mix (Aquarium Systems, Inc.) to achieve specific salt levels. Around 40 L of saline solution was prepared every week, and the electrical conductivity of the solution was measured using a portable conductivity meter (Accumet AP85 Waterproof Conductivity Meter, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to confirm the solution maintained the desired salinity before each submergence. To prevent acute salinity shock, the salinity was maintained at 5 dS m−1 on the first day and then increased daily by 5 dS m−1 until reaching 25 dS m−1. Salinity was held at 25 dS m−1 for the next 2 weeks before each dipping. Due to no visible stress symptoms, the salinity was then increased to and maintained at 30 dS m−1 for an additional 3 weeks in 2024. To avoid the accumulation of salt on the leaf surface, an overhead flush was performed using 200 mL of the saline solution once a week. In 2024, plants were only trimmed once (after 5 weeks of salt stress) at 3.8 cm height of cut. In 2025, the salt response screening followed the same protocol, except that the final salt level of 30 dS m−1 was achieved on the sixth day and maintained for 4 weeks as the grass exhibited less variation. In 2025, due to more vigorous growth, trimming was done three times (second, third, and fourth week after salt stress). Screening was terminated when some genotypes showed severe stress with leaf firing (LF) below 2 by irrigating with tap water to initiate grass recovery.

Phenotypic data collection and statistical analysis

LF visual rating was recorded on the scale of 1–9 based on National Turfgrass Evaluation Program criteria, where 1 means completely brown or dead leaf tissue, and 9 means completely green leaf tissue (Morris & Shearman, 2000) (Figure S1). Percent green cover (PGC) derived from digital image analysis was measured each week in both years. Images of each genotype were captured using a Canon PowerShot G15 digital camera (Canon Inc.) mounted on a portable light box with a consistent light source. These images were cropped using FastStone Photo Resizer software (FastStone Corporation) and then analyzed by using Turf Analyzer Academic‐1.0.4 software to determine the PGC (Karcher et al., 2017).

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using PROC MIXED in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.) for both phenotypic parameters collected over multiple dates under salt stress as repeated measurements. The effects of genotype, date, replication, genotype‐by‐date interaction, and genotype‐by‐replication interaction were treated as random effects (Yu et al., 2024). The analysis was performed using the MIXED procedure in SAS with the Type III method and a variance‐components covariance structure. F‐tests were conducted based on the expected mean‐square structure and the corresponding denominator error terms. Denominator degrees of freedom were determined using the Kenward–Roger method. The best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) of LF and PGC were calculated using the META‐R software (Alvarado et al., 2020). Pearson's correlation analysis was performed among the measured variables to evaluate pairwise linear relationships using R (R Core Team, 2025). A correlation matrix was computed using the ggcorrplot packages in R (Kassambara & Kassambara, 2019).

QTL analysis

The BLUEs were subjected to QTL analysis using MapQTL6.0 (Kyazma) (Van Ooijen, 2006). The chromosome‐wide threshold value for the logarithm of odds (LOD) at a p‐value of 0.05 was determined by using a permutation test with 1000 iterations. Then, the data were first analyzed by an interval mapping (IM) method with default settings. The putative QTL identified by IM was then again subjected to multiple QTL mapping (MQM). Cofactors for MQM were selected manually based on high LOD value and proximity of the marker to the position. Consistent cofactors from MQM were then selected as the QTL region. The graphical representation of the detected QTL was conducted using Mapchart2.4 (Voorrips, 2002).

Transcriptome analysis

During the first experimental run, leaf samples of OKC1163 were collected from treated pots 6 days after reaching 25 dS m−1 salinity and an untreated control, immediately put into liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. RNA was extracted from 100 mg frozen leaves (pooled from three biological replicates and sequenced as one technical replicate) using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the instructions standardized by the manufacturer. RNA quality and concentration were evaluated using NanoDrop and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies Inc.) techniques. Highly pure total RNA samples that passed quality control were used for sequencing. Library preparation was performed by Novogene (Novogene Corporation Inc.). These libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq System (Illumina Inc.) to produce 150‐bp paired‐end libraries. Raw Illumina reads were assessed for quality using FastQC (S. Chen, 2023), and filtered for low quality or adaptor trimming using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014).

Trimmed high‐quality RNA‐seq reads were mapped to the African bermudagrass reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5 (Cui et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2015). Mapped reads of each library were quantified and assembled in a reference‐based approach using StringTie, version v1.3.3b (Pertea et al., 2015). Differential gene expression analysis between treated and control samples was performed using the edgeR package in R (Robinson et al., 2010). Genes were considered differentially expressed based on 5% false discovery rate (FDR), p‐value ≤ 0.05, and log2 fold change (FC) = 2. Gene Ontology (GO) categories, including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) were selected for enrichment analysis. ShinyGO 0.77 graphical tool was used to test the statistical enrichment of DEGs (Ge et al., 2020). GO enrichment bubble plots were generated using the graphical interface SRplot (Tang et al., 2023). DEGs were further annotated in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database using the foxtail millet (Setaria italica L.) database because it is the species with higher genome similarity and closer evolutional history compared with bermudagrass (T. Fang et al., 2020; Kanehisa & Goto, 2000; X. Wang et al., 2015).

Targeted metabolomics

A total of 50 mg of pooled leaf tissue from three biological replicates was collected separately for the treated and control OKC1163. The pooled samples were lyophilized using a vacuum freeze‐dryer and then ground in a ball mill grinder (MM 400, Retsch) at 60 Hz for 1.5 min. Then the ground samples were mixed with 1200 µL of −20°C precooled 70% methanol for extraction. The mixture was mixed by vortex for 30 s every 30 min for a total of six times, then centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 3 min, 4°C). The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22‐µm filter membrane.

Metabolite separation was performed on an ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) (ExionLC AD, Sciex) with an Agilent SB‐C18 Column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 µm) and a tandem mass spectrometry (MS) system by Novogene (Novogene Corporation Inc.). The mobile phase A was ultrapure water, and phase B was acetonitrile, both with 0.1% formic acid added. The gradient elution started with 5% phase B at 0 min, then the proportion of phase B increased linearly to 95% within 9 min and remained at 95% for 1 min. Between 10 and 11.1 min, the proportion of phase B decreased to 5% and balanced at 5% up to 14 min. The UPLC setup was as follows: 0.35 mL/min for flow rate, 40°C for column temperature, and 2 µL for injection volume. For targeted metabolites detection and quantification, a Triple quadrupole linear ion trap mass spectrometer (QTRAP6500+, https://sciex.com/) was used with a multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) model. The running conditions of electrospray ionization were 550°C for source temperature, and ion spray voltage was 5500 V for positive ion mode and −4500 V for negative ion mode. The ion source gas I, gas II, and curtain gas were set to 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively.

Once detected, metabolites matched the database, software MultiQuant was used to analyze mass spectrum data, and the relative abundance of each metabolite was characterized by the peak area of its corresponding chromatographic peak. Mass spectrum peak of each metabolite in different samples was corrected based on retention time and peak distribution information to ensure the accuracy of qualitative and quantitative analysis. The data normalization and analysis were conducted using the R package MetaboAnalyst (Chong & Xia, 2018). The metabolites with FC ≥ 2 and FC ≤ 0.5 were differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs). Metabolites will be further annotated using the KEGG database for functional annotation (Kanehisa & Goto, 2000).

Targeted lipidomics

For the treated and control OKC1163 groups, leaf tissue from three biological replicates was pooled separately. A total of 20 mg of each pooled, freeze‐dried sample was transferred to a 2‐mL centrifuge tube containing one 4‐mm‐diameter steel ball and ground in a ball mill grinder (MM 400, Retsch) at 60 Hz for 1.5 min. The powders were mixed with 1 mL lipid extraction solution (methyl tert‐butyl ether:methanol = 3:1, V/V, with internal standard mixture) for 30 min. Then, 300 µL of ultrapure water was added to the centrifuge tube and mixed for 1 min, then incubated at 4°C for 10 min. After centrifuging at 12000 rpm for 3 min at 4°C, 400 µL of the supernatant was pipetted into a 1.5‐mL centrifuge tube and dried at room temperature. The dried sample was reconstituted by adding 200 µL of lipid reconstitution solution (acetonitrile:isopropanol = 1:1, V/V), vortexed for 3 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, 4°C, for 10 min. Then 120 µL of supernatant was aspirated and kept frozen before measurement.

Lipid samples were separated on a UPLC (Nexera LC‐40, Shimadzu) and MS/MS (Triple Quad 6500+, https://sciex.com/) by Novogene (Novogene Corporation Inc.). The Nexera LC‐40 UPLC was equipped with a Thermo AccucoreC30 (2.6 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) chromatographic column. The mobile phase A was acetonitrile and water mix (6:4, V/V), and phase B was acetonitrile and Isopropyl alcohol (1:9, V/V), both with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mmol/L ammonium formate added. The gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min, starting with 8:2 (Va/Vb) (volume ration of phase A and phase B elution) at 0 min, 7:3 (V/V) at 2 min, 4:6 (V/V) at 4 min, 15:85 (V/V) at 9 min, 1:9 (V/V) at 14 min, 5:95 (V/V) at 15.5 min, 5:95 (V/V) at 17.3 min, 8:2 (V/V) at 17.5 min, 8:2 (V/V) at 20 min. The column temperature was set at 45°C. The lipidomic profiling was conducted using a Triple quadrupole linear ion trap mass spectrometer (QTRAP6500+, https://sciex.com/) with an MRM model. The running conditions of electrospray ionization were 500°C for source temperature, and ion spray voltage was 5500 V for positive ion mode and −4500 V for negative ion mode. The ion source gas I, gas II, and curtain gas were set to 45, 55, and 35 psi, respectively.

Software Lipid Search was used to identify lipid species based on MS/MS match, which contains more than 30 lipid classes and more than 1,500,000 fragment ions in the database. Once detected lipids match the database, The lipid content in the sample was calculated by the following formula: X = 0.001 × R × c × F × V/m, where X represents content of lipids in the sample (nmol/g), R represents the ratio of the peak area of the substance to be measured to the peak area of the internal standard (area ratio), F represents internal standard correction factors for different types of substances, c represents concentration of internal (µmol/L), V represents extraction solution for samples (µL), and m represents weighed sample size (g). Results were processed by multivariate statistical analysis. The data normalization and analysis were conducted using R package MetaboAnalyst (Chong & Xia, 2018). The lipids with FC ≥ 2 and FC ≤ 0.5 were differentially accumulated lipids (DALs). Lipids were annotated using the KEGG database (Kanehisa & Goto, 2000) for functional annotation.

RESULTS

Phenotypic variation and heritability

The basic statistics and frequency distribution of LF and PGC for each evaluation date are given in Table S1 and Figure S2, and these data show visible stress symptom progression in the population. ANOVA revealed significant genotype, genotype × rep, and date effects among the OKC1163 S1 African bermudagrass population in both years (Table 1). In 2024, the broad‐sense heritability (H 2) of LF and PGC was 0.33 and 0.35, respectively. In 2025, the genotype effect remained significant, with slightly increased broad‐sense heritability for LF (H 2 = 0.40) and PGC (H 2 = 0.53). QTL analysis

Altogether, 15 QTLs were identified associated with salt tolerance on linkage groups (LGs) 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 (Table 2, Figure 1) and named numerically based on the LGs and their genetic position. Among them, five QTLs were detected on LG4. Two QTLs were detected on LGs1, 2, 5, and 6, respectively. One QTL was detected on LGs3 and 7. The phenotypic variation explained (PVE) by each QTL ranged from 8.8% to 21.1%, with peak LOD between 3.34 and 7.34. QCTST3 on LG2 at the peak, ranging from 36.71 to 37.67 cM, explained the highest amount of phenotypic variations up to 21.1%. Four QTLs, QCTST2, QCTST8, QCTST10, and QCTST13, were consistently detected across years, indicating their stable effects on salt tolerance. QCTST2 was mapped on LG1 between 57.03 and 66.89 cM and explained up to 17.3% of the phenotypic variation. QCTST8 was identified on LG4 between 37.16 and 44.78 cM, accounting for up to 11.5% of the phenotypic variation. QCTST10 was also located on LG4, with a peak interval spanning 81.21–84.78 cM, and explained up to 14.6% of the phenotypic variation. QCTST13 was mapped to LG6 between 31.30 and 35.75 cM and accounted for up to 15.5% of the phenotypic variation. There are several QTLs detected multiple times but not consistently across years. For example, QCTST1 on LG1, QCTST3 on LG2, QCTST5 on LG3, QCTST9 on LG4, and QCTST11 and QCTST12 on LG5 explaining up to 17.3% phenotypic variations. Five QTLs, QCTST4, QCTST6, QCTST7, QCTST14, and QCTST15, were reported only once on LGs2, 4, 6, and 7.

Transcriptome analysis

For the treated samples, Illumina sequencing yielded an average of 22,970,523 raw read sequences, and an average of 22,345,896 raw sequences for the control samples. After removing low‐quality bases and adapter sequences, the dataset was refined, resulting in a filtered set of 20,836,652 and 20,300,742 read sequences for treatment and control samples, respectively.

Genome‐guided transcriptome assembly was used to examine the gene expression profile of treatment versus control plants of OKC1163 under salinity stress. The alignment rates in average across the treatment and the control samples were 88.19% and 87.97%, respectively. Based on the number of mapped reads, a total of 21,092 transcripts were assembled in a single master transcriptome from all the libraries, including control and treatment libraries. The number of transcripts only in control, only in treatment, and observed both in control and treatment were 3179, 1219, and 16,694, respectively (Figure 2a).

(b) Volcano plot showing the number and distribution of DEGs between control and treated African bermudagrass OKC1163. Top significantly enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms in the set of upregulated DEGs (c) and downregulated DEGs (d) between treated versus control in African bermudagrass OKC1163 under salinity stress (BP: biological process, CC: cellular component, MF: molecular function). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs (e) and downregulated DEGs (f) between treated versus control in African bermudagrass OKC1163 under salinity stress.

DEGs were identified by comparing the control versus treated samples. After filtering the adjusted p‐value < 0.05 and log2 FC > 2, the number of identified and annotated DEGs was 5075. A total of 3814 and 1261 genes were downregulated and upregulated, respectively, for the plants exposed to salinity stress compared to the control (Table S2), and the volcano plot illustrates the statistical significance of the expression changes on the DEGs in plants exposed to salinity stress (Figure 2b).

DEGs were categorized into enriched GO terms to describe functional annotations for their MF, CC, and BP. For the upregulated genes, a total of 62 and 52 significant GO terms were designated for BP and MF, respectively. The most significant categories for BP and MF were transmembrane transport (GO:0055085) and transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0022857), respectively (Figure 2c). On the other hand, for the downregulated genes, a total of 421, 88, and 120 significant GO terms were designated for BP, CC, and MF, respectively. The most significant categories for BP, CC, and MF were protein phosphorylation (GO:0006468), cell wall (GO:0005618), and protein kinase activity (GO:0004672), respectively (Figure 2d). More detail of the GO enrichment analysis is described in Table S2. The top three pathways from KEGG enrichment analysis of upregulated genes were tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloid biosynthesis, isoquinoline alkaloid biosynthesis, and nitrogen metabolism (Figure 2e). The top three pathways from KEGG enrichment analysis of downregulated genes were motor proteins, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction (Figure 2f).

Metabolomic changes under salinity stress

According to targeted metabolomic profiling, a total of 1748 metabolites were detected from both control and treatment samples. Differentially accumulated metabolites were identified by comparing the control versus treated samples. After filtering FC ≥ 2 and FC ≤ 0.5, the number of identified DAMs was 323, including 173 downregulated and 150 upregulated DAMs (Table S3). Among DAMs, 10 metabolite categories were identified. Flavonoids accounted for the largest number of DAMs (23.46%), while Tannins accounted for the least number of DAMs (0.4%) (Figure 3a). Among all DAMs, diferuloylcadaverine was the most upregulated (log2 FC = 5.61) and 2′‐deoxyinosine‐5′‐monophosphate was the most downregulated (log2 FC = −7.34) (Figure 3b). Based on the KEGG classification, 73.1% of DAMs were involved in metabolism, biosynthesis of SMs (36.59%), and ABC transporters (18.3%) pathways (Figure 3c). According to the KEGG enrichment pathway (Figure 3d), highly enriched DAMs were involved in d‐amino acid metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, and ABC transporters.

Lipidomic changes under salinity stress

A total of 549 lipids from five lipid classes were identified from both control and treated plants, including 33 lipids in FA, 306 lipids in glycerolipids, 133 lipids in glycerophospholipids, 3 lipids in polyprenyl, and 74 lipids in sphingolipids. Among 29 lipid subclasses, triglyceride (TG) had the largest number (192), while diacylglyceryl glucuronide and lipopolysaccharides had the least number (1) (Figure 4a). The total lipid content of ceramide (Cer) was the highest, followed by ceramide transport protein (Cert). Although TG had the largest number, the total content of TG only ranked fifth among 29 lipid subclasses (Figure 4b). Compared to control, the contents of phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylethanolamine, and lysophosphatidylethanolamine were increased, while the content of Cer, Cert, monoacylglycerol, phosphatidylserine (PS), and TG were decreased. Among 549 identified lipids, 112 lipids were differentially accumulated, including 60 upregulated and 52 downregulated DALs (Table S4). The most upregulated DAL was TG (12:0/14:0/14:0) (log2 FC = 3.31), while the most downregulated DAL was DGTS (14:0_18:4) (log2 FC = −4.03) (Figure 4c). Based on the KEGG classification, most of the DALs were involved in metabolic pathways and glycerolipid metabolism (Figure 4d). According to KEGG enrichment analysis, highly enriched DALs were involved in glycerolipid metabolism and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (Figure 4e).

Combined genomic and omics analyses

Due to a substantial number of DEGs, DAMs, and DALs identified under salt stress in African bermudagrass, it is difficult to identify the regulatory pathways that truly explain the phenotypic response. The QTL mapping ensures the identification of genomic regions that can explain phenotypic variations to salt stress within the population. Therefore, combining genomic and omics analysis narrows down genomic region and focuses on the stress response regulatory pathways in the targeted regions. Genomic regions of four consistent QTLs, between 1.0 and 5.5 Mb (QCTST10),and 19.0 and 24.0 Mb (QCTST8) on chromosome 1, between 11.5 and 19.0 Mb (QCTST2) on chromosome 4, and between 10.0 and 16.5 Mb on chromosome 9 (QCTST13), were selected for identified DEGs and establish the molecular regulatory pathways of metabolites and lipids response to salinity stress. A total of 23 DEGs, from consistent QTL regions, showing upregulation effects were involved in nine pathways according to the KEGG database (Table S5). For example, the upregulation of evm.TU.LG01.2183 (log2 FC = 3.2, annotated as 9‐cis‐epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase, KEGG ID 1.12.11.51) was involved in the synthesis of abscisate (ABA) (carotenoid biosynthesis pathway) (Figure S4). The metabolomics analysis clearly found the upregulation of abscisate (log2 FC = 1.07). In the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway, the upregulation of evm.TU.LG01.312 (log2 FC = 2.51, annotated as beta‐amylase, KEGG ID 3.2.1.2) was involved in the increased concentration of maltose (log2 FC = 1.71) (Figure S5). evm.TU.LG01.2284 was involved in salicylic acid (SA) signal transduction (Figure S6). The upregulation of evm.TU.LG01.2284 (log2 FC = 2.55, annotated as a BTB domain‐containing protein, KEGG ID NPR1) is likely to promote regulation of SA‐mediated signaling in defense response, with clear evidence of detection of SA. Two DEGs, ABCG2 (evm.TU.LG01.396) and PDR5 (evm.TU.LG01.2010), transporters involved in ABC transporter pathways, were upregulated (Figure S7). There were three pathways identified that have FA involved. They are FA metabolism, FA elongation, and biosynthesis of unsaturated FAs that had evm.TU.LG09.1032 involved. However, no direct links between DEGs and DALs were observed. We expanded the search to other QTL identified in the first run since samples for omics analyses were collected from the first run. No DEGs in QTL regions were involved in lipid regulatory pathways (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

Although genotype effects were significant for both LF and PGC, low to moderate heritability showed that environmental factors strongly influence salt tolerance in this African bermudagrass population. Moderate heritability for salt tolerance in African bermudagrass is consistent with other genetic analyses of salt tolerance traits in rice and soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] with heritability ranging from 0.33 to 0.59 (Lee et al., 2004; Ray & Islam, 2008). Higher heritability in 2025 compared to 2024 was due to higher genotype variance and smaller genotype‐by‐date interaction variance. With the observation that less stress symptoms in 2025, it is likely that stress memory may have developed in this population. Stress memory can shape plant responses across years. Plants can retain molecular and physiological imprints of earlier stress exposure, which modify gene expression and physiological processes during subsequent stress events. As a result, genotypes may not respond in a uniform way across years, even when environmental conditions are replicated (Kambona et al., 2023; McGowan et al., 2021).

Previous studies have reported salt tolerance as a highly polygenic trait in various crops such as Arabidopsis thaliana (Katori et al., 2010), rice (Geng et al., 2024), maize (Zea mays L.) (H. Fang et al., 2024), wheat (Triticum aestivum) (Pasam et al., 2023), soybean (Pruthi et al., 2024), and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) (Yasir et al., 2019). Our study also demonstrated salt tolerance traits as a polygenic trait in African bermudagrass controlled by multiple genetic loci and influenced by environmental factors. A total of 15 QTLs were found linked to LF and PGC under salinity stress in this study. The relatively small size of the mapping population and the moderate broad‐sense heritability estimates for LF and PGC may have reduced the power to detect QTL with small effects and overestimated the PVE explained. In this study, four QTLs, QCTST2, QCTST8, QCTST10, and QCTST13, were consistently reported across years, showing reliable associations with salt response in African bermudagrass even with low to moderate heritability. Few QTL mapping studies have been conducted in turfgrass species. H. Guo et al. (2014) also reported two major QTLs for LF and one QTL for reduced shoot clipping weight in zoysiagrass under salinity stress, and Eudy (2016) reported two QTLs on LG4 and LG9 associated with sodium accumulation, potassium accumulation, and K+/Na+ ratio in green leaf fractions under salinity stress in seashore paspalum. Recently, a GWAS was conducted in common bermudagrass, which reported candidate genes associated with salt tolerance (H. Wang et al., 2024). A consistent QTL, QCTST2, aligned to common bermudagrass chromosome 1A between 29.7 and 32.4 Mb, overlapping with a DEG (Chr1A_Cda14673) under salt stress on chromosome 1A (31.5 Mb) that was annotated as DOWNY MILDEW RESISTANCE 6 protein. Another QTL, QCTST12, aligned to common bermudagrass chromosome 9A at 26.5 Mb, close to a DEG (Chr9A_Cda28223) as on chromosome 9A at 26.7 Mb under salinity stress (H. Wang et al., 2024). This concordance provided additional support for the functional relevance of QCTST2 and QCTST12, highlighting their potential as a target for further functional validation.

Several QTLs associated with salinity tolerance detected in our study were colocalized when compared to the QTL identified for morphological, adaptive, and other stress response traits in the previous study done using the same African bermudagrass population by Yu et al. (2021, 2022, 2024). For instance, QCTST8 was found to be overlapped with QCTSG5, QCTWK3, QCTIP5, QCTLBW3, and QCTLBL3 on LG4, peaking between 36.04 and 40.41 cM. The co‐localization of these QTLs within a small genomic interval suggests the presence of a common genomic region or cluster of genes that may simultaneously influence multiple physiological or morphological traits (Kochevenko et al., 2018). This region may therefore represent a key hotspot for stress‐response mechanisms or growth‐related processes in bermudagrass. Another QTL, QCTST2, was found to overlap with QCTER1 on LG1 as reported by Yu et al. (2021), demonstrating pleiotropic effects of this region. Co‐localized QTL has been reported in other crops, such as rice, for salinity tolerance and shoot length, reflecting the underlying genetic relationship between visible plant growth and physiological resilience under salt stress (Rahman et al., 2017).

Although QTL associated with salt tolerance was identified, salt‐tolerance functional mechanism in African bermudagrass still remains unknown. To elucidate the knowledge gap of salt response in African bermudagrass, we conducted multi‐omics analyses to investigate the transcriptional regulations and biochemical biosynthesis in OKC1163. Among 1219 upregulated genes, many were involved in significantly enriched SMs biosynthesis pathways, including alkaloid and flavonoid biosynthesis. Unlike primary metabolites that are essential for plant growth and metabolism, SMs are generally dispensable but play crucial roles in plant adaptation to biotic and abiotic stresses (Bennett & Wallsgrove, 1994). In the metabolomic analysis, the accumulation of diferuloylcadaverine and N‐feruloyl‐cadaverine (cadaverine‐derived conjugates) was highly upregulated. It is reported that cadaverine is often accumulated in the tissues of several plant species in response to a wide variety of environmental stimuli (Jancewicz et al., 2016). In Vicia faba leaves, cadaverine showed evidence to inhibit the effect on stomatal opening to against water‐deficient conditions, which may play a similar role in African bermudagrass under salt stress (Liu et al., 2000). In addition, cadaverine is also known to influence the accumulation of putrescine‐derived polyamines in plant tissues (Liu et al., 2014). In this study, we observed highly upregulated accumulated N, N′‐diferuloylputrescine. N, N′‐Diferuloylputrescine is known for its strong antioxidant activities as radical scavengers against ROS and for inhibiting tyrosinase activity, and has been found highly upregulated under salt stress in cotton in response to exogenous melatonin application (Iwai et al., 2004; Ren et al., 2022). The upregulation of these previously mentioned cadaverine‐derived and putrescine‐derived polyamines corresponds to the upregulation of evm.TU.LG02.1664 (log2 FC = 3.1) that is annotated as amine oxidase (KEGG enzyme ID 1.4.3.21) in the direct downstream oxidative deamination of cadaverine and putrescine. In addition, N‐acetyl‐5‐hydroxytryptamine (Log2 FC = 1.1) was involved in melatonin synthesis, controlled by upregulation of evm.TU.LG07.399 (log2 FC = 3.8) that annotated as aromatic‐l‐amino‐acid decarboxylase (KEGG enzyme ID 4.1.1.28). Melatonin enhances plant resistance to salt stress through two mechanisms: directly by scavenging ROS, and indirectly by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, photosynthetic efficiency, metabolite accumulation, and the expression of stress‐related TFs (Li et al., 2019).

Another two pathways, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism and arginine and proline metabolism, were significantly enriched. In the metabolomic analysis, l‐ascorbic acid (Log2 FC = 1.1) was differentially accumulated. The biosynthesis of l‐ascorbic acid is directly regulated by evm.TU.LG04.944 (log2 FC = 1.8, l‐gulonolactone oxidase), evm.TU.LG08.665 (log2 FC = 1.2, glutathione dehydrogenase/transferase), evm.TU.LG09.1588 (Log2 FC = 1.7), evm.TU.LG04.3520 (log2 FC = 1.4), and evm.TU.LG07.2262 (log2 FC = 1.4) that were all annotated as monodehydroascorbate reductase (data not shown). Although these genes are not considered as DEG using log2 FC = 2 as the threshold, the upregulation in expression and observed differential accumulation of l‐ascorbic acid confirmed the transcriptional regulation and biochemical response under salt stress in African bermudagrass. l‐Ascorbic acid is a major antioxidant playing a vital role in the mitigation of excessive ROS activity through enzymatic as well as nonenzymatic detoxification (Mittler, 2002). Studies showed that increased production of l‐ascorbic acid demonstrated enhanced salt tolerance in transgenic potato, tomato, and tobacco (W. H. Sun, Duan, et al., 2010; Y. Wang et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2011). Proline plays crucial roles in plant stress responses. In this study, l‐proline was found upregulated accumulated (log2 FC = 3.1). Similar to l‐ascorbic acid, the biosynthesis of l‐proline was upregulated by evm.TU.LG03.5042 (log2 FC = 1.6, annotated as pyrroline‐5‐carboxylate reductase). The function of proline under salt stress is in both ROS scavenging and stimulating the antioxidant system (Ben Rejeb et al., 2014).

Lipid metabolism underpins plant salt tolerance by regulating membrane integrity, permeability, and fluidity, and by modulating cellular signaling (Chalbi et al., 2015; Narayanan et al., 2016). Lipidomic analysis in this study clearly showed that the total content of lipids decreased under salt stress (Figure 4b), indicating that salt stress inhibits lipid synthesis or enhances degradation. However, the content of PG was highly upregulated among detected lipid classes. As the primary phospholipid in thylakoid membranes and an integral component of photosystem complexes, PG is critical for photosystem structure and function (Kobayashi et al., 2017). Y. L. Sun et al. (2010) demonstrated that overexpression of a glycerol‐3‐phosphate acyltransferase gene in tomato increased PG content. This, in turn, alleviated PSII photoinhibition under salt stress by accelerating D1 protein repair and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity in chloroplasts. In our study, the upregulation of PG may be due to upregulation of evm.TU.LG01.2673 (log2 FC = 2.33) that annotated as glycerol‐3‐phosphate 2‐O‐acyltransferase (Table S2). Total TG content decreased under salt stress in OKC1163. However, the most upregulated DALs was TG (12:0_14:0_14:0). TG could serve as carbon/energy reserves or buffer lipid remodeling under abiotic stress (Yu et al., 2019). The overall downregulation of TG has not been reported in other plant species, indicating the uniqueness of lipid response in African bermudagrass.

The combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses provide insight in African bermudagrass salt stress response. However, the genome‐wide distribution of DEGs presented a challenge for resolving the key genes and pathways underlying salt tolerance variation. This lack of resolution has limited our ability to translate these findings into understanding molecular mechanisms in African bermudagrass salt response variations and identify target genes for future breeding. To address this, we combined genomic and multi‐omics analyses to identify the salt stress response pathways that represent phenotypic variations. By focusing on four consistent QTL regions, the upregulation of ABA was due to the upregulation of evm.TU.LG01.2183 located with QCTST8 region. Meanwhile, the upregulation of the ABCG2 transporter was identified in the QCTST10 region. ABCG‐type transporters are known to be engaged in ABA transport, and may facilitate the movement of ABA from the plastid to the cytosol during its biosynthesis (K. Chen et al., 2020). In addition, the SA was identified unchanged between treatment and control. However, the upregulated evm.TU.LG01.2284 may promote the SA signaling pathway in response to salt stress. ABA and SA are both stress‐responsive hormones that mitigate salt stress primarily by enhancing antioxidant defenses, as evidenced by increased superoxide dismutase and peroxidase activities (Borsani et al., 2001). The finding in this study generally agrees that ABA is antagonistic to SA to develop systemic acquired resistance; the upregulated gene in the signaling pathway may involve cross talks with ROS, mediate of Na+ uptake and sequestration, and membrane transporters controlling K+ homeostasis (Jayakannan et al., 2015). However, plant hormone signal transduction was significantly enriched as a downregulated pathway in KEGG enrichment analysis by only focusing on transcriptomic analysis. Similarly, ABC transporters and starch and sucrose metabolism that led to the upregulation of maltose by upregulated genes in the consistent QTL region were all as enriched as downregulated pathway. On the contrary, the top GO termed was all involved in membrane transport for upregulated genes, suggesting GO enrichment may be more accurate to summarize the transcriptional regulations under salt stress. Similar findings are also reported by Cao et al. (2024) that membrane transport and ABC transporters were significantly correlated salt tolerance in perennial ryegrass (Lolium perenne L.). The different findings by incorporating genomic information indicated that many loci were homozygous in OKC1163 and no segregation in the population. Therefore, these loci cannot be used for future breeding through traditional breeding. Gene editing provides an alternative approach to utilize these conservative and lack of variation loci in molecular breeding.

The integration of genomic and omics analyses has greatly advanced our understanding of transcriptional regulation and biochemical regulation under salt stress in African bermudagrass. Salt stress response is a complex procedure in African bermudagrass, controlled by multiple genes. Previous studies have reported that TFs, such as WRKY, BHLH, MYB, NAC, and ERF, have clear roles in growth and developmental programs in turfgrass species under salt stress (Amombo et al., 2018; Bushman et al., 2016; Cao et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2022; L. Hu, Li, et al., 2015; Shao et al., 2021; J. Wang et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). Similarly, in African bermudagrass, several TFs have been found differentially expressed under salt stress, especially those located within consistent QTL regions (Table S2). It is likely that these TFs play critical roles in activating candidate genes in response to salt stress. In addition, evm.TU.LG01.620 (Log2 FC = 8.3) was annotated as an LEA protein. LEA proteins, which confer salt tolerance in rice (Xu et al., 1996) and cold tolerance in zoysiagrass (Brown et al., 2023), are known to play a key role in membrane stabilization and the prevention of protein aggregation under stress conditions (Chinnusamy et al., 2003). In the future, combining proteomic analysis would be ideal to confirm the function of protein in response to salt stress.

CONCLUSION

Salt tolerance is a critical trait in developing new turf‐type bermudagrass cultivars. In this study, we screened the salt tolerance of the OKC1163 S1 population, and significant genotypic variance was observed, and moderate broad‐sense heritability was estimated. A total of 15 QTLs were identified for salt tolerance. Combining multi‐omics analyses, we built the connection between transcriptional regulation and biochemical accumulation under salt stress. Among them, genes involved in alkaloid and flavonoid, ascorbate and aldarate, and arginine and proline biosynthesis pathways were highly upregulated, while the corresponding metabolites, including diferuloylcadaverine, ascorbate, and proline, consistently accumulated at higher levels, revealing molecular mechanisms of African bermudagrass under salt stress. These genes could be used in genetic engineering to improve salt tolerance as natural variation may not exist in this population. Combining consistent QTL with multi‐omics data, we identified candidate genes involved in ABA and maltose biosynthesis, SA signaling, and membrane transporters that showed natural variation that can be used in hybridization for improving salt tolerance in African bermudagrass and developing molecular markers to increase selection accuracy and efficiency.
