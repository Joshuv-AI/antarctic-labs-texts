Tailoring copper content in high-entropy oxides for enhanced water oxidation catalysis
Authors: Seyedsaeed Mehrabi-Kalajahi, Seyed Amir Hossein Vasigh, Hassan Yousefi Bavili, Behrouz Shaabani, Maryam Badri, Shadab Akbarpour, Sama Maghoul, Alexei Vagov, Ahmad Ostovari Moghaddam, Mikhail A. Varfolomeev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-entropy oxides (HEOs) have recently gained considerable attention as next-generation electrocatalysts owing to their exceptional compositional flexibility, structural stability, and synergistic multimetal effects. In this study, a series of Cu-modified high-entropy oxide catalysts (HEO–Cu) with different Cu loadings (20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60%) supported on nickel foam (NF) were synthesized via a facile co-precipitation method and comprehensively characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses. The structural results confirmed the formation of a homogeneous single-phase oxide with uniform elemental distribution. Electrochemical measurements revealed that the incorporation of Cu significantly improved the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performance. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves showed a remarkable decrease in overpotential from 570 mV for bare NF to 470, 376, 371, 262, and 252 mV at 10 mA/cm for 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60% Cu loadings, respectively. The HEO–Cu 40%/NF electrode exhibited the lowest overpotential (252 mV) and a Tafel slope of 64.5 mV/dec, indicating the most favorable OER kinetics among all compositions. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) confirmed a significant reduction in charge transfer resistance (Rct) from 212 Ω (NF) to 82 Ω (HEO–Cu 40%/NF), while chronoamperometric stability tests demonstrated that the optimized electrode maintained over 95% of its initial current density after 36,000 s of continuous operation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50442-0.

Introduction

Water oxidation, the four-electron process that generates oxygen from water, is a serious half-reaction in artificial photosynthesis process and electrochemical energy conversion systems, including hydrogen production and renewable fuel generation1. Despite extensive investigation, the reaction is hindered by slow kinetics and elevated overpotentials, which underscores the need for the creation of highly effective and stable catalysts2. Conventional metal oxides like RuO2 and IrO2 demonstrate remarkable catalytic performance; however, their high cost and limited availability drive the quest for alternative materials that are abundant in the earth’s crust3. Transition metal oxides have surfaced as highly promising candidates because of their adjustable electronic structures, redox properties, and structural adaptability, presenting potential avenues for attaining superior water oxidation efficiency at a reduced cost4–6.

High-entropy oxides (HEOs) have recently attracted considerable interest in the realm of catalysis owing to their distinctive structural and compositional properties7–9. Characterized as multi-component metal oxides that incorporate five or more cations in nearly equimolar proportions, HEOs demonstrate elevated configurational entropy, which helps to maintain stable single-phase structures even in complex compositions8,10–12. The intrinsic structural stability, along with adjustable electronic characteristics, facilitates the formation of numerous active sites and promotes synergistic interactions between various metal cations, consequently improving catalytic efficiency13–15. Recent research has shown the capabilities of HEOs in various oxidation processes, encompassing both water oxidation and the catalytic breakdown of organic substances, underscoring their adaptability as catalysts for the next generation16–19.

Among the constituent metals in HEOs, copper plays a particularly important role due to its accessible redox states (Cu+/Cu2+) and strong interaction with oxygen species. Incorporating copper into HEOs has been shown to significantly influence their electronic structure, surface reactivity, and catalytic behavior20,21. Prior research concerning the oxidation of water and organic compounds suggests that modifying the copper content can result in increased catalytic activity, enhanced stability, and improved regulation of the reaction pathway22–24. However, in spite of these developments, a comprehensive understanding of how different copper fractions influence the structure–activity relationship of HEOs in water oxidation is still absent. Specifically, the interaction between copper concentration, the local electronic environment, and the generation of active oxygen species has yet to be thoroughly investigated.

The Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER) represents a critical bottleneck in electrochemical water splitting, demanding significant overpotential due to its complex multi-electron transfer pathway. Efficient OER catalysis is paramount for sustainable energy technologies, yet overcoming the inherent kinetic and thermodynamic hurdles remains a substantial challenge. Addressing this knowledge gap, the present work focuses on the rational design and synthesis of copper-tuned high-entropy oxides (HEOs), specifically Cu-modified high-entropy oxide (HEO–Cu) electrocatalysts with tunable Cu content. This approach introduces a novelty in the systematic tuning of copper within the HEO framework for water oxidation catalysis. By systematically varying the copper content, we aim to elucidate the correlation between composition, structural features, and catalytic performance. Detailed characterization techniques will be employed to investigate the impact of copper on the phase stability, electronic structure, and surface properties of these HEOs, providing mechanistic insights into their catalytic behavior. This study demonstrates that the synergistic multimetal interactions within the HEO structure significantly enhance OER activity, achieving exceptionally low overpotential and high stability compared to conventional oxide catalysts. The ultimate goal is to establish a composition–activity relationship that can guide the development of optimized high-entropy oxide catalysts for efficient and sustainable water oxidation. In summary, this study leverages the intrinsic advantages of HEOs and the tunable redox properties of copper to address current limitations in water oxidation catalysis. By combining compositional engineering with systematic catalytic evaluation, we aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of copper-dependent activity in high-entropy oxides, paving the way for the design of next-generation catalysts characterized by superior performance and stability.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and precursors

Analytical grade metal sulfate precursors, including cobalt (II) sulfate heptahydrate (CoSO4·7H2O), nickel (II) sulfate hexahydrate (NiSO4·6H2O), iron (III) sulfate hydrate (Fe2 (SO4)3·9H2O), manganese(II) sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4·H2O), and copper (II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O), were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. The desired molar ratios of the metal salts were dissolved in deionized water under constant stirring to form a homogeneous aqueous solution. In addition, aqueous solutions varying molar ratios of Cu (20, 30, 40, 50, and 60%) were prepared and stirred to ensure homogeneity. Aqueous ammonia (NH4OH, 25%) was then slowly added dropwise to the solution under vigorous stirring until the pH reached ~ 10–11, leading to the co-precipitation of metal hydroxides. The resulting precipitate was aged at room temperature for several hours, then filtered, washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol to remove residual ions, and dried at 80 °C overnight. The dried powder was subsequently calcined at 800 °C temperatures for 5 h to obtain the final HEO products (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis process.

Structural and physicochemical characterization

Powder samples were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan), employing Cu-Kα radiation with a wavelength of 0.15406 Å. Raman spectroscopy measurements were performed using a UniDron-A system (Andor Technology, South Korea). The specific surface area of the synthesized catalysts was evaluated by nitrogen adsorption at − 196 °C using a BET Mini II analyzer (Belsorp, Japan), assuming a nitrogen molecular cross-sectional area of 0.162 nm2. Prior to measurement, the samples were degassed at 300 °C until the residual pressure reached 0.013 Pa. Microstructural evaluation was carried out via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a JEOL JSM7001F microscope (Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector (Oxford INCA Xmax 80, UK). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was performed on a Hitachi HT7700 Exalens operating at 120 kV. Inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) analyses were performed using an iCAP Qc ICP–MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Prior to measurement, the instrument was calibrated with multi-element standard solutions prepared at concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 ppm to ensure analytical accuracy and precision. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed under ultra-high vacuum conditions with a base pressure of approximately 5 × 10−10 mbar. The spectra were acquired using a Phoibos 150 hemispherical energy analyzer (SPECS GmbH) and an Mg Kα X-ray source operated at 12.5 kV and 250 W.

Electrochemical measurements

The synthesized HEOs electrodes were fabricated via electrodeposition onto nickel foam (NF) substrates (0.5 × 0.5 cm2). Prior to deposition, the NF was cleaned in a 3 M HCl solution using ultrasonic treatment for 15 min to remove surface oxides, followed by sequential sonication in distilled water, ethanol, and acetone (10 min) to ensure thorough cleaning.

For each sample high entropy oxide with varying Cu content (0.2–0.6), 5 mg of catalyst was dispersed in 5 mL ethanol and ultrasonicated for 1 h. Electrodeposition was carried out at a constant voltage of 2 V for 1800s, using the NF as the working electrode (placed 1 cm from a platinum wire counter electrode). After deposition, electrodes were dried at room temperature for 24 h.

Electrochemical characterization was performed at room temperature using an Autolab PGSTAT100 system and an EG&G PAR Model 273A potentiostat in a standard three-electrode setup. The working electrode was the catalyst-coated NF; a commercial Ag/AgCl electrode (saturated KCl) served as the reference, and a platinum plate as the counter. Oxygen evolution reaction (OER) activity was assessed by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) at 50 mV/s in 0.1 M KOH (pH ≈ 13), with potentials referenced to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). At this scan rate, while the double-layer capacitance contribution increases relative to the time for Faradaic reactions, the inherently fast kinetics of our catalytic system ensure a strong and observable Faradaic response. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted in the same electrolyte over a frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 100 kHz.

Results and discussion

Figure 1a shows the XRD patterns of the (CoNiFeMnCu)3O4 HEO samples with varying Cu contents (20–60%) reveal the structural evolution with increasing Cu incorporation. All samples exhibit the characteristic diffraction peaks of a single-phase high-entropy oxide (HEO) with a cubic spinel-like structure, as indicated by the reflections marked with ◆. At lower Cu contents (20–30%), only the HEO phase is clearly observed, confirming the successful incorporation of Cu into the lattice without formation of secondary phases9. However, when the Cu content exceeds 30%, weak additional peaks (marked with *) corresponding to CuO appear, suggesting that the lattice solubility of Cu in the HEO matrix is limited. The intensity of the CuO peaks becomes more pronounced in the 40–60% Cu samples, indicating the gradual segregation of excess Cu as a separate CuO phase as the secondary phase. This trend highlights that while moderate Cu substitution is well accommodated within the HEO lattice, excessive Cu loading leads to phase separation and crystallization of CuO25. The Raman spectra of (CoNiFeMnCu)3O4 with varying Cu content (20–60 at.%) reveal a systematic red shift of the characteristic spinel modes, particularly the A1g and Eg bands, accompanied by progressive line broadening9. This behavior arises from the incorporation of Cu2+ into the spinel lattice, where longer and weaker Cu–O bonds reduce the effective force constants of the metal–oxygen vibrations. In addition, local Jahn–Teller distortions associated with octahedral Cu2+ increase structural disorder, further broadening the phonon modes26,27. At higher Cu levels, weak additional features emerge that may be assigned to CuO or Cu2O, suggesting incipient phase segregation. Overall, the observed compositional dependence of Raman shifts and linewidths confirms the sensitivity of the vibrational modes to Cu substitution and provides direct evidence for the evolving local structure within the high-entropy oxide.Fig. 1(a) XRD patterns, (b) Raman spectra, (c) nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of HEO‒Cu 20‒60%, and (d) atomic ratio from the ICP-MS of HEO‒Cu 40%.

The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms provide direct evidence of the effect of Cu loading on the porous structure of the synthesized samples. The sample containing 40% Cu exhibits the highest nitrogen uptake (≈11 cm3 (STP)/g at p/p₀ → 1), corresponding to a BET surface area of 5.96 m2/g, which reflects the most developed porous structure among the series. The 50% Cu sample shows the next highest surface area (5.43 m2/g), followed by the 60% Cu material with a surface area of 4.96 m2/g. The 30% Cu specimen displays intermediate adsorption behavior with a surface area of 4.74 m2/g, while the 20% Cu sample presents the lowest nitrogen uptake, yielding a surface area of only 4.21 m2/g. Taken together, these results indicate that a Cu content of 40% provides the optimal balance for maximizing pore volume and surface area, whereas shifting to either lower or higher Cu levels results in less favorable textural properties. In addition, Fig. 1d presents the atomic ratio percentages of the metal elements in all HEO samples obtained from ICP‑MS analysis. For instance, in the 40% Cu HEO sample, the atomic ratios of Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu are 14.9%, 14.7%, 15.3%, 15.7%, and 39.4%, respectively. The complete quantitative ICP‑MS data for all synthesized catalysts are also provided in the Supplementary Information (Table S1) for comprehensive reference.

Figure 2a–c presents the SEM micrographs of high-entropy oxide (HEO) samples with Cu loadings of 20%, 40%, and 60%, respectively. All samples display agglomerated particles with irregular polyhedral morphologies in the sub-micrometer range. Increasing the Cu content leads to a broader particle size distribution and denser crystallite packing, most evident in the 60% Cu sample. This trend suggests that Cu incorporation modifies the nucleation and growth dynamics of the oxide phase, consistent with the formation of a secondary phase as confirmed by XRD analysis. The corresponding EDS elemental mapping of the 40% Cu HEO sample is shown in Fig. 2d. The uniform spatial distribution of Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu indicates successful incorporation of all constituent elements into the HEO matrix without noticeable segregation. Such compositional homogeneity reflects the high-entropy effect, which stabilizes the multicomponent solid solution. AFM analysis was used to evaluate the thickness of the HEO–Cu 40% sample. As shown in Fig. S1, the AFM image reveals a highly irregular surface with sharp peaks and valleys, indicating a rough and heterogeneous morphology28. This nanoscale roughness likely arises from the high-energy synthesis process and contributes to an increased surface area with more exposed active sites, which is beneficial for catalytic activity. The observed height variations also suggest non-uniform grain growth and partial aggregation of oxide nanoparticles, consistent with the formation of a high-energy surface. Further structural insights are provided by the TEM image of the 40% Cu sample (Fig. 2e). TEM analysis reveals nanoscale crystallites interconnected within larger agglomerates, in agreement with the SEM observations. The XPS analysis of the Cu-rich HEO (40% Cu) reveals the coexistence of multiple oxidation states across all constituent cations, highlighting the chemical complexity of the system. The Cu 2p spectrum exhibits intense peaks at 933.8 and 953.6 eV, confirming Cu2+ as the predominant oxidation state, with an additional contribution from Cu+ at around 932.2 eV. The coexistence of these mixed-valence states is particularly advantageous in the Cu-enriched HEO structure, as it enhances redox flexibility. This flexibility likely promotes efficient electron transfer and intermediate stabilization during OER, contributing to the observed improved catalytic performance29. Similarly, Ni 2p (855.7/873.5 eV) and Co 2p (780.3/796.1 eV) spectra demonstrate Ni2+/Ni3+ and Co2+/Co3+ states, while Mn 2p (641.7/653.4 eV) reveals Mn3+/Mn4+, and Fe 2p (711.2/724.6 eV) is dominated by Fe3+ with minor Fe2+ contributions30–32. The O 1s spectrum is deconvoluted into lattice oxygen (529.5 eV), adsorbed oxygen/hydroxyl groups (531.2 eV), and surface-adsorbed water (532.7 eV). Importantly, the relatively high proportion of adsorbed oxygen species points to a considerable concentration of oxygen vacancies33–35. These vacancies act as active sites for surface reactions, facilitate oxygen mobility, and accelerate charge-transfer processes36. In combination with the high Cu content, which stabilizes multiple oxidation states, the enriched oxygen-vacancy environment is expected to significantly enhance the catalytic reactivity and durability of the HEO‒Cu 40% compared with other compositions (Fig. 3).Fig. 3High-resolution XPS spectra of HEO‒Cu 40%: (a) Mn 2p, (b) Fe 2p, (c) Co 2p, (d) Ni 2p, (e) Cu 2p, (f) O 1s.

The electrocatalytic performance of high-entropy oxide (HEO) catalysts with different copper contents (20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60%) supported on nickel foam (NF) was evaluated using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in a 0.1 M KOH alkaline electrolyte. As illustrated in the LSV profiles at a scan rate of 50 mV/sec (Fig. 4a), the overpotential values for the NF, HEO‒Cu 20%/NF, HEO‒Cu 30%/NF, HEO‒Cu 60%/NF, HEO‒Cu 50%/NF, and HEO‒Cu 40%/NF electrodes at a current density of 10 mA/cm2 are approximately 570 mV, 470 mV, 376 mV, 371 mV, 262 mV, and 252 mV, respectively. It can be seen that, the onset potential for the HEO‒Cu 40% catalyst was also lower compared to the others, demonstrating improved reaction kinetics. Increasing the copper content from 20 to 40% enhanced the catalytic activity, likely due to optimized active sites and favorable electronic interactions within the high-entropy matrix37. However, further increasing Cu content beyond 40% (to 50% and 60%) resulted in a slight decrease in activity, possibly due to aggregation or electronic effects that reduce active site accessibility38. The bare NF substrate exhibited significantly lower current densities under the same conditions, confirming that the catalytic enhancement is primarily due to the HEO‒Cu catalysts. These results suggest that a copper content of around 40% in the HEO structure provides an optimal balance between active site availability and electronic conductivity, leading to superior electrocatalytic performance39. Also, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) kinetics of the synthesized high-entropy oxide catalysts with different Cu contents supported on nickel foam were evaluated using Tafel slope analysis. The obtained Tafel plots (Fig. 4b) reveal a clear dependence of the reaction kinetics on the Cu content. The calculated Tafel slope values are 134.4 mV/dec for bare NF, 100.4 mV/dec for HEO–Cu 20%/NF, 91.8 mV/dec for HEO–Cu 30%/NF, 64.5 mV/dec for HEO–Cu 40%/NF, 72.3 mV/dec for HEO–Cu 50%/NF, and 76.9 mV/dec for HEO–Cu 60%/NF. The significant decrease in the Tafel slope compared to bare NF demonstrates the strong catalytic enhancement provided by the HEO structure. The introduction of Cu into the high-entropy oxide framework effectively improves the electrical conductivity and optimizes the adsorption energy of oxygen intermediates, thus accelerating the OER kinetics39,40. Among all the catalysts, HEO–Cu 40%/NF shows the smallest Tafel slope (64.5 mV/dec), indicating the most efficient electron transfer and the lowest energy barrier for the OER process. However, a further increase in Cu content to 60% slightly raises the Tafel slope to 76.9 mV/dec, suggesting that excessive Cu may lead to the partial blocking of active sites or structural distortion, thereby reducing the catalytic performance. Overall, these results confirm that a balanced multicomponent configuration in the HEO system—particularly at 40% Cu—creates a synergistic interaction among the constituent metal cations, optimizing the intrinsic activity and stability of the catalyst for oxygen evolution41. The HEO–Cu 40%/NF electrocatalyst shows enhanced electrocatalytic performance in alkaline media for water electrooxidation compared to other reported electrocatalysts, as detailed in Table S2. This superior performance is attributed to its exceptionally low Tafel slope, which signifies a faster electron transfer rate. The long-term chronoamperometry test (~ 36,000 s) demonstrates markedly improved and stable OER activity for all electrodes compared with bare NF. Averaged steady-state current densities extracted from the traces are approximately 15 mA/cm for NF, ~ 22 mA/cm for HEO–Cu 20%/NF, ~ 28 mA/cm for HEO–Cu 30%/NF, ~ 44 mA/cm for HEO–Cu 40%/NF, ~ 35 mA/cm for HEO–Cu 50%/NF, and ~ 31 mA/cm for HEO–Cu 60%/NF. In addition to higher absolute currents, all HEO–Cu samples show excellent durability: the current traces remain essentially flat over the entire 36,000 s interval with negligible decay (< ≈ 5%), indicating stable catalytic performance and robust electrode integrity under prolonged operation. The HEO–Cu 40%/NF sample gives the highest and most stable current, suggesting that ~ 40% Cu in the HEO matrix provides an optimal balance of active-site density, conductivity, and structural stability for sustained OER, whereas either lower (20–30%) or excessive (60%) Cu content results in lower steady-state activity.Fig. 4(a) LSV polarization curves, (b) Tafel slope plots, (c) CA curves, and (d) Nyquist plots of the EIS spectra obtained from the HEO–Cu/NF electrodes.

The Nyquist plots obtained from the EIS measurements (Fig. 4d) illustrate the influence of Cu content on the charge transfer resistance (Rct) of HEO–Cu catalysts supported on nickel foam (NF). The bare NF electrode shows the largest semicircle, corresponding to an Rct value of approximately 212 Ω, indicating poor charge transfer efficiency (Table S3). With the incorporation of HEO–Cu, the semicircle diameter gradually decreases, confirming enhanced electrical conductivity and faster interfacial electron transport. In the study of copper-tuned high-entropy oxides (HEOs) on nickel foam (NF) for the Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER), charge transfer resistance (Rct) values were measured for HEO–Cu compositions of 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60%, yielding approximately 177 Ω, 158 Ω, 82 Ω, 114 Ω, and 119 Ω, respectively. The HEO–Cu 40%/NF electrode exhibited the lowest Rct (around 82 Ω), evidenced by the smallest semicircle in the impedance data, signifying the most efficient charge transfer kinetics and superior electrocatalytic performance. An equivalent circuit model fitted to the data revealed two time constants, associated with double-layer charging and Faradaic charge transfer. This high catalytic activity at 40 wt% Cu content suggests a favorable reaction pathway involving enhanced charge transfer dynamics, likely facilitated by copper’s presence which increases conductivity, as directly supported by the reduced Rct. The findings collectively indicate that increasing copper content up to 40 wt% significantly enhances the conductivity and catalytic activity of the HEO–Cu/NF electrode, addressing both performance metrics and the underlying OER mechanisms.

Conclusions

The combined structural and electrochemical analyses reveal that the enhanced OER performance of HEO–Cu/NF catalysts originates from the synergistic effects between multiple metal cations and the optimized Cu incorporation level. The XRD and XPS results verified strong lattice distortion and multivalent cationic states, promoting efficient electron transfer and active site generation. Among the investigated samples, the HEO–Cu 40%/NF electrode exhibited the best balance between conductivity, surface area, and stability, achieving the lowest Rct (82 Ω), the smallest Tafel slope (64.5 mV/dec), and the highest current retention (~ 95%). Increasing Cu beyond this optimal level (≥ 50%) slightly reduced catalytic efficiency, possibly due to partial Cu agglomeration and decreased active site exposure. The equivalent circuit fitting indicated two relaxation processes corresponding to charge transfer and surface adsorption phenomena. Overall, the results confirm that the rational compositional engineering of high-entropy oxides, particularly through optimized Cu incorporation, offers an efficient strategy for designing stable and high-performance OER electrocatalysts suitable for practical energy conversion systems such as water electrolysis and metal–air batteries.
