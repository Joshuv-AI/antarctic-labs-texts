Blue Light Exposure During Sleep in Type 1 Diabetes: Impacts on Glycemic Control and Psychosocial Health
Authors: Seok Jin Hong, Sarida Pratuangtham, Pamela Martyn‐Nemeth, Alana D Steffen, Chang Gi Park, Sirimon Reutrakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLight exposure at night is linked to increased risk for obesity and insulin resistance in the general population. While sleep health is recognized as a key component of diabetes management, there has been less emphasis on nighttime light exposure. This study aimed to investigate the effects of blue and white light exposure immediately prior to (1 h) and during sleep (LEDS) on glycemic control and psychosocial wellbeing in adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D). Using baseline data from a randomized controlled trial testing a sleep intervention (NCT 04506151), we analysed the following data from 170 adults with T1D: light exposure pattern and sleep from one week of wrist‐worn actigraphy, glucose levels from one week of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), and A1C and self‐reported psychosocial measures. Generalized linear models, adjusting for covariates, were employed to examine the relationship between log‐transformed light variables and glycemic/psychosocial outcomes. The results revealed that greater blue LEDS was significantly associated with higher glucose levels from CGM including less % time spent in glucose range 70–180 mg/dL (B = −7.30; p = 0.022) and greater % time spent above glucose range 180 mg/dL (B = 6.95; p = 0.041), along with greater depressive (B = 4.41; p = 0.016) and anxiety symptoms (B = 2.25; p = 0.008), and worse diabetes quality of life (B = −5.81; p = 0.031). Light exposure one hour prior to sleep was related to increased fatigue. In conclusion, increased blue LEDS is linked to hyperglycemia and worse psychosocial outcomes in T1D. Thus, blue LEDS warrants further investigation as a modifiable risk factor. Introduction

Light exposure at night has been shown to be detrimental to health. Light exposure at night can disrupt sleep and circadian system function, leading to adverse metabolic and cardiovascular health (Fonken and Nelson 2014). An experimental study revealed that acute light exposure during sleep (LEDS) in healthy volunteers led to increased insulin resistance the next morning (Mason et al. 2022). Several population‐based studies have confirmed the findings. For example, in a population study conducted in the elderly Japanese population, light at night exposure (≥ 3 lx) was associated with higher odds of having obesity (odds ratio, OR, 1.89) and dyslipidemia (OR 1.72) (Obayashi et al. 2013), as well as higher incidence of diabetes (incidence rate ratio 2.74) (Obayashi et al. 2020). Another study in community‐dwelling adults in the U.S. showed that light at night exposure was associated with a higher prevalence of obesity (OR 1.82), diabetes (OR 2.00), and hypertension (OR 1.74) (Kim et al. 2023). Evening blue light exposure prior to sleep onset was also found to predict elevated fasting glucose in pregnant women (Izci Balserak et al. 2022). Furthermore, the harmful effect of light at night extends to psychological outcomes including an increased risk of depression (Obayashi et al. 2018) as well as mood and anxiety disorders (Paksarian et al. 2020). While these data suggest the adverse effects of light exposure at night in general population, the data in people with diabetes is lacking.

Besides light at night, sleep health deserves consideration, as it is recognized as a critical component in care for people with diabetes. The American Diabetes Association includes the importance of sleep with other lifestyle behaviours (e.g., diet, exercise) (American Diabetes Association Professional Practice Committee. 2024) as sleep disturbances are associated with poor glycemic control in people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes (Reutrakul and Van Cauter 2018). Furthermore, sleep is recognized as a part of Life's Essential 8 by the American Heart Association (AHA) due to its effect on cardiovascular health (Lloyd‐Jones et al. 2022). Sleep health consists of multiple dimensions (i.e., duration, efficiency, timing/regularity, and satisfaction), and the AHA recently placed the emphasis of multidimensional sleep health on cardiometabolic health (St‐Onge et al. 2025). Furthermore, in people with diabetes, sleep has a bidirectional relationship with psychological health, which can in turn affect their metabolic health (Zhu et al. 2021).

Evidence suggests that sleep disturbances and poor psychological health (e.g., depressive symptoms, diabetes distress) are common in people with T1D (Ni et al. 2024). However, the gap of knowledge remains if light exposure at night affects glycemic control and psychological outcomes in people with T1D beyond those of sleep disturbances alone. Therefore, the aims of this study were to explore the association of light exposure at night (before and during sleep) on glycemic control, as assessed by A1C and glucose parameters from continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and psychological symptoms. The results could potentially provide insights into these relationships and inform further interventional studies.

Methods

This was a secondary analysis of baseline data from 170 participants with T1D who enrolled in a randomized controlled trial (RCT) using a sleep intervention to improve sleep regularity and sleep duration (NCT 04506151)(Martyn‐Nemeth et al. 2022). Potential participants were eligible if their self‐reported sleep duration was < 6.5 h/night (workdays) or had sleep timing irregularity ≥ 1 h. The exclusion criteria included sleep disorders (severe insomnia symptoms determined by the Insomnia Severity Index questionnaire (ISI) with a score ≥ 15), high risk for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) assessed by snoring, tiredness during daytime, observed apneas, pressure (high blood pressure), (STOP) questionnaire [with a score ≥ 2], history of severe hypoglycemia in the past six months, significant depressive symptoms, A1C level > 10%, shift work, use of sleep aid medications, significant medical comorbidities (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, active treatment for cancer, congestive heart failure), neurological deficits, breastfeeding, pregnancy, or planning pregnancy. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Illinois Chicago (Martyn‐Nemeth et al. 2022). Participants gave written informed consent, and the study was conducted remotely in participants' free‐living environments across the United States.

Measures

Demographic and Diabetes Characteristics

Questionnaires were used to collect baseline demographic data including age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, marital status, and diabetes history, such as T1D duration and diabetes treatment methods. The treatment methods include multiple daily injection (MDI) alone, MDI with continuous glucose monitor (CGM), or CGM with an insulin pump.

Glycemic Parameters

Participants wore CGM (FreeStyle Libre Pro or Dexcom) for 7 days which captured interstitial glucose levels every minute and recorded the data every 5 to 15 min. The following variables were derived: percentage of time spent in the glucose range 70–180 mg/dL (TIR), percentage of time spent above range > 180 mg/dL (TAR), percentage of time spent below range < 70 mg/dL (TBR), mean glucose (mg/dL), and coefficient of variation (CV%). In addition, haemoglobin A1C (A1C) was assessed by A1C dried blood spot (A1C; Everly Corp).

Sleep and Light Parameters

Sleep duration, sleep efficiency, and sleep regularity were obtained from the Actiwatch Spectrum Plus (Phillips Respironics Inc) which participants wore on their non‐dominant wrists for 7 days, along with completing a daily sleep log. Bedtime and wake time were determined by the researchers using the previously described algorithm (Patel et al. 2015). Sleep duration, efficiency, sleep onset and offset were generated by the Actiware Software 6.3.0. Sleep regularity was calculated as the standard deviation (SD) of sleep midpoint (halfway time point between sleep onset and offset) as this has previously been shown to be related to glycemic outcomes in people with T1D (Reutrakul et al. 2023).

Light exposure (lux) was captured by a light sensor on the Actiwatch. The following parameters were derived (using R program) from average values over the recording period for each participant: blue and white light exposure during sleep periods (LEDS) and one hour before sleep onset.

Psychological Measures

Psychosocial data were collected through self‐reported questionnaires at baseline. Fatigue was measured with the PROMIS Short Form 8a (Cella et al. 2016), reported as T‐scores (mean of 50, SD of 10) for the US general population and higher scores indicating greater fatigue. Depressive mood was measured with the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES‐D) (Radloff 1977), with scores ranging from 0–60 and higher scores indicating greater depressive symptoms. Anxiety was measured using the General Anxiety Disorder 7‐item (GAD‐7) scale (Jordan et al. 2017), with scores ranging from 0–21 and higher scores indicating more severe anxiety symptoms. Diabetes self‐management was measured using the Diabetes Self‐Management Questionnaire‐Revised (DSMQ‐R) (Schmitt et al. 2021), with scores ranging from 0–10 and higher scores indicating better self‐care. Lastly, diabetes quality of life was measured with the Diabetes Quality of Life Instrument (DQOL) (The DCCT Research Group. 1988) with scores interpreted on a 100 point scale (Jacobson et al. 1994) and higher scores indicating better perceived quality of life.

Statistical Analyses

Data are described as mean (SD), median (interquartile range, IQR) or frequencies (%). Univariate linear regression models were performed with each covariate entered individually for each glycemic outcome. Generalized linear models (GLMs) were then employed to examine the relationship between light variables, which were transformed using log (1 + x) as they were not normally distributed, and glycemic or psychosocial variables of interest. All GLMs were controlled for the following covariates: sex, ethnicity, duration of T1D, CGM use, type of insulin treatment (MDI vs. insulin pump), sleep efficiency, sleep duration, and sleep regularity. To evaluate whether associations between LEDS and outcomes were independent of sleep characteristics, sensitivity analyses were conducted comparing models with and without adjustments for sleep variables. Statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team. 2025).

Figure 2 shows associations between light parameters and glycemic parameters, using GLMs adjusting for covariates (Table S2). The results revealed that greater blue LEDS was significantly associated with less %TIR (B = −7.30, 95% confidence interval (CI) −13.48, −1.12; p = 0.022), Figure S1, and greater %TAR (B = 6.95, 95% CI 0.33, 13.57; p = 0.041), Figure S2. Figure 3 shows the associations between light parameters and psychosocial outcomes (Table S3). The result revealed that greater blue LEDS was significantly associated with greater depressive symptoms (B = 4.41, 95% CI 0.88, 7.95; p = 0.016), greater anxiety symptoms (B = 2.25, 95% CI 0.60, 3.89; p = 0.008), and worse diabetes quality of life (B = −5.81, 95% CI −11.04, −0.57; p = 0.031), while white LEDS was not related to these outcomes. Discussion

Using baseline data from our sleep intervention RCT, we demonstrated for the first time that nighttime light exposure negatively impacts glucose metabolism and psychosocial wellbeing in people with T1D, after adjusting for sleep parameters. Specifically, increased blue LEDS was significantly associated with less time spent in range and more time spent above range of glucose values from CGM, while the amount of white LEDS before or during sleep had no significant effects on any glycemic parameters. Furthermore, greater blue LEDS was significantly linked to more severe depressive and anxiety symptoms. The association with diabetes‐related quality of life, however, was reversed after adjustment for sleep parameters and thus the relationship may be mediated by sleep. Interestingly, there was a significant relationship between light exposure (blue and white) 1 h before, but not during, sleep on increased perceived fatigue. Collectively, these data suggest the adverse impacts of light exposure at night on metabolic and psychological health of people with T1D and raise the possibility that reduction in light exposure at night could be a targeted intervention to improve health in people with T1D.

In our study, increased LEDS, particularly blue light, was linked to hyperglycemia and impaired glycemic control in T1D, independently of sleep characteristics. Light exposure at night has been shown to alter core circadian clock rhythms in the suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN), resulting in circadian disruption (Fonken et al. 2013). It is well known that circadian disruption can result in increased insulin resistance and elevate cardiometabolic risks (Scheer et al. 2009). Moreover, intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) located in the inner retina are particularly sensitive to short‐wavelength light (around 480 nm) contained within the spectrum of blue light (wavelength 400–495 nm) (Hatori et al. 2017). The ipRGCs express the photopigment melanopsin and play a crucial role in synchronizing the synthesis of melatonin (Hatori et al. 2017), a neurohormone involved in glucose metabolism (Peschke and Muhlbauer 2010). Exposure to short‐wave enriched polychromatic light was shown to suppress melatonin secretion (Brainard et al. 2015), a known risk for diabetes development (McMullan et al. 2013). In addition, experimental acute light exposure at night in healthy volunteers resulted in increased sympathovagal balance, which correlated with insulin levels and insulin resistance the next morning (Mason et al. 2022). Light at night can lead to sleep disturbances, which are risks for insulin resistance and incident diabetes (Reutrakul and Van Cauter 2018). Collectively, these mechanisms likely explain our findings of the relationship between increased blue LEDS and less TIR/greater TAR in our T1D cohort. While light at night exposure has recently been shown to increase T2D risks in several large population‐based studies (Kim et al. 2023; Salomon et al. 2025), the data in T1D is scarce. Therefore, our study further extends the knowledge of the adverse metabolic effects of light exposure during sleep in people with T1D.

Further, several key psychosocial variables were also negatively linked specifically with blue LEDS. Significantly worse diabetes‐related quality of life, as well as more severe depressive and anxiety symptoms, were seen with higher blue LEDS. These findings agree with the data from the general population (Deprato et al. 2025). Likely partly through circadian and sleep disruption, light exposure at night was linked to increased prevalence of mental health disorders, including depression (OR 1.18) and anxiety (OR 1.10) (Deprato et al. 2025). These psychological domains critically influence T1D management beyond metabolic changes. Given the demanding regimen of glycemic monitoring, insulin therapy, and dietary restrictions, these psychosocial factors can work together to substantially add to the already high burden of disease in T1D. Higher depression symptom severity has been associated with worse diet and medication adherence in diabetes (Ciechanowski et al. 2000), and both depression and anxiety have also been linked with worse quality of life in this population (de Ornelas Maia et al. 2013). The latter relationship is also bidirectional—anxiety and depression symptoms predicted worse health‐related quality of life, and declines in quality of life longitudinally exacerbated these mood symptoms as well (Liu et al. 2020); not only do these factors independently hinder effective treatment, but they can also build on each other to further worsen negative outcomes in T1D. We posit that blue LEDS may be one of the common denominators in this complex, reciprocal interplay between mental health and sleep health (Bacaro et al. 2024) and therefore could possibly be an important target for intervention specifically in T1D.

Previous studies have explored the benefits of light at night reduction and health outcomes, although none were performed in people with T1D. In a pilot study performed in six healthy Japanese adults, blue‐light shield glasses worn 2–3 h before bedtime for one month led to a reduction in fasting glucose levels (mean glucose baseline 84.5 mg/dL vs. post‐intervention 81.5 mg/dL), along with a trend reduction in insulin levels and a significant reduction in fasting insulin resistance (Nagai et al. 2019). There was an improvement in self‐reported sleep quality and sleep efficiency (Nagai et al. 2019). A systematic review found that evening blue light blocking glasses could improve sleep onset latency in patients with sleep disorders or variable shift work schedules and possibly improve symptoms in patients with mania (Hester et al. 2021). Use of eye masks at night has also been shown to improve subjective sleep quality in patients undergoing cardiac surgery (Dagcan Sahin et al. 2024). Our results raise the question if blue LEDS specifically warrants further investigation as a modifiable risk factor in people with T1D to improve metabolic and psychological outcomes. For example, changing our environment to include nighttime blackout curtains or blue light‐blocking devices may have a place in the management of T1D. It is important to note that a general sleep hygiene recommendation suggests limiting light exposure at night (National Sleep Foundation. 2025). Effective strategies in achieving this goal and whether they improve health in T1D remain to be explored.

Our study has several strengths. It provided an objective way to quantify how different types of light exposure at night affects both glycemic control and specific psychosocial variables in people with T1D. Through the carefully selected sleep metrics used as controls in our GLMs, we were able to focus on light at night as the driving factor independently of its mediating effects on sleep. Further, we collected data remotely in participants' personal living arrangements, allowing us to explore complex relationships among key variables within subjects' natural circadian routines, not in an artificially manipulated lab setting. However, the study has some limitations. Our cohort had relatively good glycemic control and only included participants with self‐reported irregular or short sleep; thus, the results might not be generalizable. The screening for sleep apnea was performed using questionnaires and not an objective assessment. The use of wrist worn actigraphy for gathering nighttime environmental light exposure may not be ideal as compared to the neck‐worn light sensors which can capture light exposure closer to the level of the eyes. Overall, our study sample had minimal light exposure before and during sleep. In addition, there is no agreement on how to best capture light exposure (e.g., timing, units: lux vs. melanoptic EDI). In this study, we did not see a significant relationship between light exposure and A1C, which could be partly due to the relatively low A1C in our cohort. Lastly, we understand that environmental light is complex, with communities of lower socioeconomic status likely being exposed to higher amounts of light pollution, especially ambient outdoor light; nighttime light is not always just a straightforward matter of improving sleep hygiene. In the future, we could consider looking further into seasonal changes and address differences in zip code between participants. However, there are ways to control the amount of light one's eyes are directly exposed around at nighttime—from blue light blocking glasses to blackout curtains, the technology and strategies are constantly evolving before us.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we were able to identify that blue light exposure during sleep was linked to hyperglycemia and worsened psychosocial wellbeing in T1D after adjusting for sleep parameters. These results further underscore the importance of good sleep hygiene and maintaining a properly dark environment before and during sleep. Inappropriately timed nighttime light is not just harmful for sleep; this is likely an issue of circadian dysregulation with clinically significant and wide‐reaching downstream effects on metabolism and mental health. For this reason, we propose further investigation of blue LEDS as a key future target for intervention in T1D.
