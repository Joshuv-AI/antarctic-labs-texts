Trifid mandibular condyle: An outlier from normal physiology with review
Authors: Ajit Mishra, Arkaprava Banerjee, Rudra P Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A complicated skeletal structure, the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), is necessary for the jaw to function normally. The mandibular condyle and its associated tissues can grow abnormally unilaterally or bilaterally for a variety of reasons. A rare and unusual anatomical variant known as trifid mandibular condyle (TMC) occurs when the condyle’s head is divided into three separate heads by grooves of varying depths. The bifid variety, which is distinguished by the duplication of the mandibular condyle, is the subject of the majority of the work now in publication. We present a unique case of this rare morphological variation of the trifid mandibular condyle and review the present literature. It can be unilateral, with a predominance on the left side, or a less common bilateral form. Three separate heads make up the trifid mandibular condyle. Some facial asymmetry is caused by the trifid mandibular condyle; however, TMJ function may be almost normal.

INTRODUCTION

A complicated skeletal structure, the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), is necessary for the jaw to function normally. The mandibular condyle and its associated tissues can grow abnormally unilaterally or bilaterally for a variety of reasons. Trifidity and aplasia are two distinct abnormalities affecting the mandibular condyle. Duplicity of the mandibular condyle is a characteristic of bifidity and trifidity, which are very rare conditions.[1]

A rare and unusual anatomical variant known as trifid mandibular condyle (TMC) occurs when the condyle’s head is divided into three separate heads by grooves of varying depths. The bifid variety (BMC), which is distinguished by the duplication of the mandibular condyle, is the subject of the majority of the work now in publication. The literature indicates that the prevalence of BMC in the general population varies between 0.018% and 1.82%. It can be unilateral, with a predominance on the left side, or a less common bilateral form. Three separate heads make up the trifid mandibular condyle. A CT scan is necessary for a conclusive diagnosis, even when the condyle seems aberrant on a panoramic radiograph. Some facial asymmetry is caused by the trifid mandibular condyle; however, TMJ function may be almost normal.[2]

We present a unique case of this rare morphological variation of the trifid mandibular condyle and review the present literature.

CASE REPORT

A 13-year-old healthy boy [Figure 1] reported to the Department of Oral Medicine and Radiology complaining about maligned teeth and occasional pain in his upper right back teeth region. The pain first appeared three years ago. It felt like a dull non-continuous pain which resolved spontaneously. The patient also complained of a clicking noise arising from the right jaw and a self-resolving open lock of the right temporomandibular joint (TMJ). The patient was asked whether he had any previous trauma or accident involving her jaw or face. The patient recalled an accident that occurred when he was four years old, he fell on his face from the roof-top, his parents recalled facial pain and restriction of jaw movements that lasted only for a few days and as far as they could remember it resolved with some pharmacological management.

Extraoral picture showing bilateral asymmetry

His face was bilaterally asymmetrical upon extraoral inspection, on further examination, the right TMJ made a clicking sound. A displacement of the jaw towards the left side was seen during mouth opening. His condyles were able to move laterally, protrusively, and rotate normally and painlessly.

Intraoral examination revealed Angle’s Class 2 molar relation with crossbite in posterior segments and crowding in the anterior segments [Figure 2]. The tongue movement was restricted as he was having a short lingual frenum. There was presence of fluorosis over all teeth and tooth number 16, 36, and 46 was carious. No other gross abnormality was found in the oral cavity.

Intraoral picture showing malocclusion and fluorosis

We advised the patient for panoramic imaging (orthopantomogram) for further radiological investigations. OPG showed non-uniform condylar head and reduced articular space on the right side with normal appearances on the left side [Figure 3]. We decided to go for an advanced radiographic analysis; cone-beam computed tomography scan for better visualisation of the condyles and the temporomandibular joint as a whole. To our surprise, the right condyle had three distinct heads, making it an extremely rare morphological variation. The condylar head was viewed in the axial, coronal, and sagittal sections simultaneously, and all of them showed three distinct condylar heads rising from a single neck on the right side concomitantly along with a deep fissure-like indentation suggesting of previous fracture. The left condyle did not show any abnormality [Figure 4].

Review of Trifid Mandibular Condyle cases upto the present cases

The first case of TMC was reported by Artvinli and Kansu in the year 2003, while the last one was reported by Zoabi et al. in 2022. One unique tetrafid condyle case was reported by Sahman H. et al. in the year 2012. Definition criteria for bifid mandibular condyle have evolved since 1941, when Hrdlicka defined it as a condylar split or groove of variable depth. The definition was changed in 1998 by Stefanou et al., describing it as the formation of two distinct condyles with a single separate neck, irrespective of whether the heads are oriented mediolaterally or antero-posteriorly. In 2008, Dennison et al. claimed that ‘true BMC’ must have an antero-posterior orientation. However, in 2010, Lopez-Lopez et al. opposed the statement of orientation and suggested that to call BMC a true BMC, the two heads must emerge from the condylar neck.[1]

The actual etiopathogenesis of these illnesses is uncertain. Condylar fractures from trauma, endocrinological anomalies, infections, teratogenic embryopathy, and surgery are among the suspected causes. Condylectomy as well as dietary deficiencies can be other causes. Three of the documented cases developed bifid condyles during the condylar fracture healing phase, which leads to the notion that these illnesses had a traumatic genesis. According to Stadnicki’s description of a paediatric kid delivered with forceps, same problems have also been documented in patients who had instrumentation during labour. According to Moffett, proponents of the developmental theory believe that connective tissue septa are retained throughout foetal life. However, the bifid mandibular condyle is typically asymptomatic and discovered by accident in the majority of instances.[3]

There are two primary hypotheses on the potential aetiology of BMC. The first is trauma; this theory is predicated on the observation that the lateral pterygoid muscle’s action causes the condyle to shift anteromedially following a fracture to the condylar neck. The original displaced condylar head then undergoes resorption, while a new condylar head is produced at the proper anatomical position by the metaplasia of local fibroblasts in the condylar neck. Because of this action, the injured joint will contain two or more condyles for a period of time the new created condyle, which is the functioning one, and the original displaced condyle, which is regarded as a non-functioning condyle—until the original condyle is resorbed. Experimental studies, which were conducted on animals, demonstrated a high capacity of regeneration and remodelling of the condyle after trauma. For example, lizards sharing the same tool box of genes as humans have the ability to form a new tail after an autotomy.[2]

Condylar development begins with the ectomesenchymal condensation of condylar blastemas at seven to eight weeks of intrauterine life, and definitive condylar cartilage appears at 10 weeks. Therefore, if any condyle defect occurred as a developmental malformation, it most likely occurred before the 10th week post-conception, when the mandibular condylar cartilage would have begun to develop. The manifestation and severity of the congenital abnormality may depend on changes in gene expression profiles, so signalling proteins, like Indian hedgehog (Ihh), fibroblastic growth factor (FGF), bone morphogenic proteins, Spry1 and Spry2 (sprouty genes), Shh, Runx2, and parathormone-related protein, are essential for condylar development (e.g., the absence of Ihh causes condylar growth deficiency).

It has been discovered that TMJ development depends critically on N-sulfotransferase (Ndst1)-dependent heparan sulfation. A severely impacted Ndst1 mutation results in the absence of condylar formation by affecting its beginning at early stages of embryogenesis. The condylar development plate is defective in weakly impacted mutations, exhibiting ectopic ossification along the condylar lateral boundaries and a broader distribution of Ihh activity. Therefore, we can presume that the trifid condyle on the left side may be due to ectopic ossification. But in this instance, the Ndst1 mutation was not examined. In healthy people, muscles are constantly active to preserve joint stability. The condylar blastema tissue, to which the lateral pterygoid muscle is linked, directs the conical condyle to form the TMJ throughout the eight- to ten-week intrauterine period. Trifid condyles may have resulted from these muscle fibres’ compensatory orientation to preserve the tripod effect for joint stability. According to Gundlach et al., when muscle fibres are misdirected, bone spikes form in the place where the fibres join, and these spikes eventually grow into the bifid/trifid condyle.[1]

According to Loh F. and Yeo J., 67% of BMC cases were asymptomatic. In their retrospective analysis, Cho b. and Chung y. reported 49% of asymptomatic patients. In their systematic review, Fererres J. et al. found that 40.6% of patients were asymptomatic. The most commonly reported signs and symptoms in symptomatic cases were ankylosis, arthralgia, hypomobility, and articular noise. Three months following a fall while running, a six-year-old kid had only mandibular first molar contact and a significant open bite, according to Isik et al. Some writers proposed that osteoarthritis may occur in bifid condyles as a result of traumatic aetiology, and that arthritic alterations were evident in many of these cases. Axial open bite, mandibular deviation, jaw weakness, mandibular hypoplasia, restricted mouth opening, snoring during sleep, noises, Frey’s syndrome, facial asymmetry, swelling of the masseteric region, and incisal midline shift are additional signs and symptoms that have been reported in TMC cases. According to Antoniades K. et al., a 15-year-old kid with TMC who reported trauma at age 5 had bilaterally Angle Class II division 1 skeletal relationships, mandibular hypoplasia, restricted mouth opening, and snoring during sleep.[2]

The symptomatology associated with bifid or trifid condyle is diverse. It ranges from articular noises to pain, restriction of mandibular opening, inflammation, facial asymmetry, jaw deviation during mouth opening, among others.[4]

There is no necessity for the treatment in asymptomatic cases. In symptomatic cases, the treatment is usually conservative and analogous to the treatment of TMJ pain dysfunction syndrome (analgesics and anti-inflammatory agents, muscle relaxants, physiotherapy, and/or splints). Surgical intervention was considered in cases with limited mouth opening and ankylosis.[5]

These symptoms and indicators point to the possibility that TMC may result in a number of issues, including TMJ development, occlusal issues, and temporomandibular disorders. There are insufficient data to determine if these symptoms and indicators were caused by TMC or if they were unrelated to it. Furthermore, there are not enough studies on how TMC affects the TMJ over the long run; the only data available come from case studies that have been written up in the literature.

CONCLUSION

Despite being a rare anatomical condition, trifid mandibular condyle is becoming more frequently identified as more sophisticated imaging methods—particularly CT, CBCT, and MRI—are developed in the dental sector. Uncertainty surrounds the aetiology, pathophysiology, potential developmental variations, and connection to TMD of TMC. This anatomic variance and potential connections with local disorders may be better understood with additional research and follow-up. The key takeaways are that the majority of TMC cases included a history of childhood trauma, emphasising the value of obtaining a comprehensive medical and dental history, including enquiries about prior facial and jaw trauma, as well as the significance of performing a thorough extraoral and intraoral examination.
