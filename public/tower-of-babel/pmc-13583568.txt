Clinically actionable genomic alterations in breast cancer brain metastases

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer brain metastases (BCBMs) represent a critical unmet clinical need in metastatic breast cancer (MBC) and the identification of novel therapeutic targets is urgently needed in this context. In this study, we describe clinically actionable targets in BCBMs using comprehensive genomic profiling.Patients and methodsGenomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded archival BCBM samples and analyzed using the commercially available Agilent SureSelect V6 whole exome sequencing (WES) kit and an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Pathogenic alterations were classified as actionable alterations (AAs) if they met the updated MBC or tumor-agnostic ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) I or II criteria of the ESCAT scale.ResultsWES data from 56 BCBM samples were available [33.9% hormone receptor (HR)-negative/human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER)2-negative; 25.0% HR-positive/HER2-negative; and 38% HER2-positive]. ESCAT I/II AAs were detected in 76.8% (n = 43) of all BCBMs and the most frequently detected AAs were in genes involved in the homologous recombination repair pathway (BRCA1/BRCA2/PALB2; 53.6% overall). Biallelic inactivation of BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2 was observed in 19.6% of samples, with higher rates in HER2-negative BCBMs (26% in HR-negative /HER2-negative and 21% in HR-positive/HER2-negative). ESCAT I/II PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN pathway alterations were present in 48.2% of samples and, in particular, in 50% of HR-positive/HER2-negative BCBMs. No ESR1 mutation was detected in HR-positive/HER2-negative BCBMs. The prognostic impact of previously described AAs was evaluated overall and according to breast cancer subtype. Twenty-three BCBMs (41%) were classified as HER2-positive; among these, 3 (13%) presented a hotspot PIK3CA mutation and 7 (30%) presented a PTEN deletion. Among patients with HER2-positive BCBMs, the identification of a hotspot PIK3CA mutation was significantly associated with worse prognosis.ConclusionsESCAT I/II actionable genomic alterations are frequent in BCBMs, highlighting the potential for genomically targeted treatments in this setting.

Introduction

Brain metastases (BMs) represent a common complication of metastatic breast cancer (BC), as ∼30% of patients with metastatic BC will eventually develop central nervous system (CNS) involvement during their clinical history. Moreover, the incidence is increasing due to improved survival of metastatic BC patients and wider use of more sensitive imaging techniques.1,2

In this context, BCBMs represent one of the major unmet medical needs for patients with BC, negatively affecting survival and quality of life and representing one of the main causes of death. However, over the last years, treatment strategies have greatly evolved and targeted therapies with intracranial activity have become increasingly available, translating into substantial clinical benefit for specific patient subgroups.3

In the era of precision medicine, genomic analyses play a relevant role in guiding the personalization of therapeutic decision-making and tumor molecular profiling can open new therapeutic opportunities for patients with metastatic BC cancer.4 However, the reliable identification of molecular alterations that are both actionable for treatment selection and supported by robust evidence of therapeutic relevance represent a major challenge. In this context, the ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) provides a framework for prioritizing genomic alterations based on evidence of clinical benefit. Actionable targets are classified into tiers, with levels I and II identifying alterations with the highest clinical benefit.5 Latest ESMO recommendations for the use of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has identified several ESCAT I/II genomic alterations in advanced BC, endorsing the use of tumor NGS in patients with hormone receptor (HR)-positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER)2-negative (HR-positive/HER2-negative) advanced BC as standard of care. Furthermore, tumor-agnostic genomic alterations further widen the spectrum of targetable biomarkers.6

However, it is well known that relevant biological heterogeneity may emerge during the metastatic process of BC and that the biological characterization of primary BC cannot be completely trusted to guide treatment in the metastatic setting. In fact, even for fundamental biomarkers, such as HR and HER2, a discordancy rate of ∼21% and ∼10%, respectively, between primary BC and matched BM has been reported in previous studies.7 Moreover, it has been previously described that assessment of cell-free circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) derived from plasma samples cannot efficiently recapitulate genomic alterations of brain tumors, including the detection of clinically actionable alterations (AAs).8 In this context, the present study aims to assess the genomic profile of BCBMs through comprehensive genomic profiling and describe clinically actionable targets according to ESCAT classification.

Methods

Patients and samples

Patients with BC who underwent neurosurgery at three institutions between 2003 and 2019 were retrospectively identified. Archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) BM samples were centralized for analysis at the University of Padova. Among these, this work has benefited from 450 white slides and the expertise of Professor Valérie Rigau responsible for the collections ‘Neurology’ and ‘CEREMET-LR’ of the Biological Resource Center of Montpellier University Hospital [http://www.chu-montpellier.fr (BB-0033-00031)].9

Clinicopathological data were retrospectively collected from medical charts. HR and HER2 statuses evaluated on BCBM were used to classify samples according to BC biological subtypes. HR positivity was defined as staining for estrogen receptor in at least 1% of tumor cells. HER2 positivity was defined according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)–College of American Pathologists (CAP) guideline as HER2 score 3+ by immunohistochemistry or HER2 gene amplification by fluorescence or chromogenic in situ hybridization in cases scored as 2+ by immunohistochemistry.10

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time interval from the date of diagnosis of BCBM to death from any cause or last follow-up. Patients alive without event at last follow-up were censored at date of last follow-up.

This study was approved by involved institutional review boards and/or ethics committees when needed according to the involved site legislation and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from participants when needed according to the involved site legislation.

Genomic analysis

FFPE samples were reviewed for tumor tissue quality and quantity (all samples presented >30% of tumor cells). Genomic DNA was extracted from samples with adequate tumor tissue quality and quantity using the MagCore® Genomic DNA FFPE One-Step Kit (RBC Bioscience, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Concentration and quality of samples were assessed using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay on Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts).

Adequate genomic DNA samples were then analyzed using the Agilent SureSelect V6 whole exome sequencing (WES) kit (Agilent Technologies, California) and an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, California; Supplementary Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108551), to identify genomic alterations, including base substitutions, indels, and copy number alterations including amplifications and gene deletions.

Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in relevant genes (level I/II genomic alterations according to ESCAT; Table 1) were prioritized among passing filters coding variants with variant allele frequency (VAF) of ≥0.05. Pathogenic alterations were classified as AAs if they meet updated 2024 ESCAT I or II criteria for advanced BC or for tumor-agnostic indications (Table 1).11 For technical limitations, gene fusions (NTRK1/2/3, RET, and FGFR1/2/3) and microsatellite instability-high status were not assessed. ERBB2 amplifications were not reported as they were already assessed by clinical practice using ASCO–CAP guidelines.10

For gene involved in the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway (i.e. BRCA1/2 and PALB2), biallelic alterations were defined as mutations with loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of the wild-type allele, homozygous deletion, or two or more alterations in a same gene in a same sample. Because of the retrospective nature of this study, the results of the WES analysis did not inform clinical decision-making and none of the patients included in this cohort received treatment based on these results.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS, version 29 (IBM), and R software, version 4.3.3 (R Foundation). Descriptive statistics were used to present baseline demographic, clinical, pathologic, and treatment variables. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square test.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to calculate estimated survival curves and the log-rank test was used to compare two groups. Hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated using univariate Cox models. A P value of <0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

The median age at BC and BM diagnoses was 46 (range 25-77) years and 52 (range 23-76) years, respectively. The median time from BC diagnosis to BM diagnosis was 32.1 (range 0-172.3) months.

As expected, most patients presented a single BM (83.9%, n = 47) and around one-third (32.1%, n = 18) did not present extracranial disease at time of first BCBM diagnosis. Moreover, CNS involvement was present at first diagnosis of stage IV BC in 38 patients (67.9%). As a result, median time from BC diagnosis to BM diagnosis was 0 (range 0-123.2) months, without significant differences across the three BC subtypes (P = 0.963; Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108551).

As expected, most patients received systemic (73.3%) and radiotherapy (75.0%) after neurosurgery. BCBM subtyping according to immunohistochemical staining was available for all 56 samples: 19 (33.9%) were triple negative (HR-negative/HER2-negative), 14 (25.0%) were HR-positive/HER2-negative, and 23 (38%) were HER2-positive.

At a median follow-up from BCBM diagnosis of 46.9 months, the median OS in the overall study cohort was 28.6 months (95% CI 16.8-40.4 months). BCBM subtype was significantly associated with prognosis from BCBM diagnosis, with patients with HER2-positive BC showing the longest median OS (53.0 months, 95% CI 29.4-76.6 months), followed by patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative BC (33.4 months, 95% CI 20.2-46.6), and with patients with triple-negative BC showing the shortest median OS (9.4 months, 95% CI 7.0-11.8). Other factors, including number of BMs, Karnofsky performance status at BM diagnosis, and extracranial disease status at BM diagnosis, were not significantly associated with OS (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108551).

The most frequently detected AAs were in genes involved in the HRR pathway with 53.6% of samples (n = 30) presenting at least a heterozygous alteration in BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2 (Figure 1). Alterations in genes of the PI3K/AKT/PTEN pathway were also frequently observed, with 48.2% of samples (n = 28) presenting at least a heterozygous alteration in PIK3CA or PTEN. No AAs in AKT1 were detected in this study cohort (Figure 1). In particular, ESCAT I/II hotspot PIK3CA mutations and PTEN mutations/deletions were frequently observed across all BC subgroups and were identified in 53%, 50%, and 43% of HR-negative/HER2-negative, HR-positive/HER2-negative, and of HER2-positive BCBMs, respectively (Figure 2B). Most of these alterations were mutations or deletions of the PTEN gene, which were identified in 42%, 43%, and 30% of HR-negative/HER2-negative, HR-positive/HER2-negative, and of HER2-positive BCBMs, respectively (Figure 2B). No AA of ESR1, hotspot mutations in ERBB2, or mutations in BRAF or FGFR1/2/3 (tumor-agnostic alterations) were detected in this study cohort.

Tumor mutational burden

Median tumor mutational burden (TMB) of all BMs was 8.8 (range 3.0-23.4) mut/Mb. Median TMB was 7.0 mut/Mb for HR-negative/HER-negative BMs, 8.48 mut/Mb for HR-positive/HER2-negative, and 8.55 mut/Mb for HER2-positive, with no significant differences between subgroups (P = 0.215). Overall, 28.6% of patients presented a high TMB (≥10 mut/Mb), including 43.4% of patients with HER2-positive BCBMs (Figure 2C).

Prognostic impact of genomic alterations

The prognostic impact of previously described genomic alterations was evaluated in the overall population and according to BC subtype. BRCA1/BRCA2/PALB2 alterations, PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN pathway alterations, and high TMB (≥10 mut/Mb) were not associated with significant differences in OS from BM diagnosis overall and in each BC subtype separately (Supplementary Figures S3-S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108551). However, alterations in PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN were associated with a trend toward worse prognosis in patients with HER2-positive BCBMs. This observation was probably guided by a significant association between hotspot PIK3CA mutations and worse OS from BM diagnosis in patients with HER2-positive BCBMs (Supplementary Figure S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108551).

Discussion

The clinical implementation of precision medicine has represented a paradigm shift in oncology, moving from a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach to tailored strategies based on the molecular and genetic profile of individual tumors. Brain metastatization is a highly complex process during which tumor cells undergo extensive molecular and phenotypic reprogramming, favoring their adaptation to the brain metastatic niche and the subsequent formation of clinically detectable macrometastases. Previous analyses of matched primary BCs and paired BMs have shown that BCBMs frequently exhibit changes in HR and HER2 expression,7 substantial modifications in gene expression profiles,16 and the acquisition of frequently recurrent clinically actionable genetic alterations that could influence targeted therapy choices if identified.17

In this retrospective study, we aimed to explore the genomic profiling of BMBC focusing on actionable molecular alterations ranked as ESCAT I/II according to the latest ESMO Precision Medicine Working Group classification. This comprehensive genomic analysis revealed a high prevalence of actionable molecular alterations, with 76.8% of samples harboring ESCAT I/II tier alterations.

HRR pathway gene alterations were among the most frequently detected genomic aberrations in our cohort. Notably, biallelic inactivation of BRCA1, BRCA2, or PALB2, the biological alteration typically associated with canonical HRR deficiency-related genomic scars and high sensitivity to PARP inhibitors,12, 13, 14 was observed predominantly in HER2-negative BCBMs, with 26% of HR-negative/HER2-negative and 21% of HR-positive/HER2-negative tumors harboring such events. The presence of biallelic HRR gene alterations in our cohort aligns with recent data reporting recurrent mutations in DNA repair genes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, and CHEK2 in BMs.18,19 These alterations may represent acquired events during metastatic dissemination or adaptation to the brain microenvironment, as mutational signatures associated with HRD have been described to be significantly enriched in BCBMs compared with matched primary BCs.19 On the contrary, a higher incidence of BMs has also been described in BRCA1/2 germline mutation carriers in some reports, thus supporting the hypothesis that, when present, these alterations may play a role in CNS dissemination.20, 21, 22

Overall, these findings suggest a potentially targetable vulnerability to treatment with PARP inhibitors with adequate CNS penetration in a wide proportion of BCBMs. However, the CNS activity of currently approved PARP inhibitors remains underexplored. A retrospective subgroup analysis of 63 germline BRCA1/2 carriers with a history of CNS metastases (patients with active BMs excluded) included in the EMBRACA trial showed an intracranial overall response rate of 18.2% with talazoparib (versus 20.0% for treatment of physician’s choice arm), with a median OS of 12.9 months.23 In the OlympiAD trial, among gBRCAm patients with HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer (MBC) who had brain/CNS metastases, treatment with olaparib was associated with a higher overall response rate as compared with treatment of physician’s choice (64.7% versus 20.0%) and with a 49% reduction in the risk of disease progression or death. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, due to limited sample size (n = 26). Although the strongest clinical evidence supporting PARP inhibitor efficacy has been generated in patients with germline BRCA1/2 mutations, more recent data have demonstrated clinically meaningful activity also in patients with somatic BRCA1/2 mutations and germline PALB2 pathogenic variants,24 supporting the inclusion of these variations as a ESCAT IIB biomarker in metastatic BC and their recognition as a predictive biomarker by the current ESMO guidelines.6,25 In this context, the identification of pathogenic biallelic BRCA1/2 alterations in BCBMs may provide a strong biological rationale for PARP inhibitor treatment, particularly when CNS disease represents the predominant site driving patient prognosis. Nevertheless, prospective evidence specifically evaluating PARP inhibitors in patients selected according to BM-specific HRR alterations is lacking. Our findings therefore provide additional rationale for the expanded clinical investigation of PARP inhibitors, especially those with adequate CNS penetration, in this setting.

In this study, PIK3CA mutations and PTEN mutations/deletions were frequently observed across all BC subgroups, in line with previous evidence highlighting the central role of PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation in the biology of BCBMs. Indeed, PIK3CA and PTEN alterations have been associated with a higher risk of developing BCBMs across all BC subtypes.26, 27, 28 Although PTEN loss-of-function alterations or loss of nuclear PTEN expression are infrequently detected in primary BCs, they occur at a significantly higher frequency in matched BMs, suggesting a context-dependent role in disease progression and metastatic dissemination.26,29, 30, 31 Experimental models have additionally shown that PTEN loss can alter tumor–microenvironment crosstalk, facilitating astrocyte recruitment and promoting tumor cell migration and invasion toward the brain.32 Importantly, the high prevalence of PTEN alterations compared with PIK3CA mutations supports the biological relevance of downstream PI3K/AKT pathway activation, highlighting AKT inhibition as a particularly relevant therapeutic strategy in this setting. Furthermore, the development of novel agents with improved CNS penetration, such as brain-permeable AKT inhibitors, may help overcome pharmacokinetic barriers posed by the blood–brain barrier.33

The presence of PIK3CA mutations in HER2-positive MBC also represent a potentially relevant target. Indeed, constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade results in sustained downstream signaling independent of HER2 modulation, thereby contributing to reduced sensitivity and therapeutic resistance to anti-HER2 agents. Interestingly, in our cohort, hotspot PIK3CA mutations were associated with poorer prognosis in patients with HER2-positive BCBMs, supporting the rationale for the combination of CNS-penetrant PI3K inhibitors (currently under clinical development) and anti-HER2 agents in HER2-positive BC, including in patients with BMs.

It should however be highlighted that the presence of an AA does not necessarily translate into immediate clinical benefit in patients with BCBMs, as this also depends on the ability of the corresponding agents to achieve adequate intracranial drug exposure, which varies substantially across drug classes and individual compounds. Therefore, while HRR and PI3K/AKT pathway alterations represent biologically compelling therapeutic targets, the development of CNS-penetrant targeted agents (e.g. novel CNS-penetrant PI3K inhibitors, such as GDC-0084) and prospective clinical evaluation of their intracranial efficacy remain essential to translate genomic findings into real clinical benefit.

When evaluating TMB, our study highlighted a substantial proportion of BCBMs (28.6%) showing a high TMB (≥10 mut/Mb), including 43.4% of patients with HER2-positive BCBMs. While this percentage is higher than that reported in previous reports, a retrospective analysis of a large real-world genomic dataset reported a higher proportion of TMB-high samples in BCBMs compared with primary BCs across all three BC subtypes.34 These findings may potentially allow eligibility for immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment, based on Food and Drug Administration tumor-agnostic approval. However, whether TMB-high status translates into clinical benefit from immune checkpoint inhibition in patients with BCBMs remains uncertain, given the unique immune microenvironment of BMs, which is enriched in macrophagic components compared with primary BC, together with the limited evidence supporting the intracranial activity of immunotherapy in BC.35,36

In our study, no actionable ESR1 mutation or hotspot mutations in ERBB2 was detected, in contrast with what is generally known about the mutational landscape of metastatic HR-positive/HER2-negative BC. However, this result should be interpreted with caution as only a limited number of patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative BC (n = 14) was included in the study. Moreover, as BMs generally occur later in the clinical history of HR-positive/HER2-negative BC,37 having selected patients undergoing neurosurgery might constitute a relevant bias in this BC subtype, potentially selecting a subgroup HR-positive/HER2-negative BCs with specific biological features, less addicted to endocrine pathways.38

Overall, our results highlight that comprehensive molecular profiling of BMs may identify clinically actionable genomic alterations that might not fully be captured by analyses of the primary tumor or extracranial metastatic sites. Recent comparative genomic studies further support this concept. A very large study comparing unmatched BCBMs and extracranial metastases reported enrichment of several clinically AAs in BCBMs, including ERBB2, PTEN, CDKN2A/CDKN2B, and HRR gene alterations, whereas ESR1 alterations were more frequent in extracranial metastases.39 Smaller patient-matched studies have similarly shown that clinically relevant alterations may be acquired during CNS dissemination, when compared with extracranial metastases.40 However, adequately powered patient-matched analyses remain lacking. Together, these findings support the molecular characterization of BCBMs whenever tissue is available for molecular profiling. How to obtain this biological information when neurosurgery is not clinically indicated remains however an open challenge. ctDNA in the cerebrospinal fluid has been shown to be more representative of somatic mutations present in CNS lesions compared with plasma ctDNA.8,41 However, its potential use is limited by the invasive nature of this procedure, which make it not easily acceptable by patients. While radiomics, an emerging technique that extracts mineable high-dimensional data from clinical images and applies artificial intelligence algorithms to identify those correlated with clinical outcome or genetic/molecular characteristics, has shown promise to noninvasively identify relevant biological features (e.g. epidermal growth factor receptor mutations in non-small-cell lung cancer) in other solid tumors, its potential application to BCBMs still remains underexplored.41,42 The development of an accurate, noninvasive method to identify therapeutically relevant biological targets in BCBMs represents a relevant field for potential improvement for the clinical management of these patients.

Our study presents some relevant limitations. Firstly, the retrospective nature of our cohort and the limited number of patients warrant validation of the results in independent and larger cohorts of BCBMs. In particular, the relatively small sample size and limited number of events within individual subgroups in addition to the potential influence of multiple confounding factors severely impacts the robustness of survival analyses, which should therefore be considered as exploratory, warranting evaluation in larger independent cohorts with correction for standard prognostic factors. Secondly, a clear selection bias is necessarily present because only patients undergoing neurosurgery could be included, leading to an enrichment in patients with favorable performance status, single BM, and diagnosis of CNS involvement early in their metastatic disease course (most patients diagnosed with BMs at time of first stage IV diagnosis). These characteristics might potentially present an association with specific tumor biology features, thus limiting the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, the number of brain lesions is also a well-known independent prognostic factor and strongly influences treatment strategies, thus potentially impacting the results of survival analyses. Thirdly, gene fusions and microsatellite instability were not assessed due to technical constraints. An additional limitation relates to the use of archival FFPE samples. The samples showed varying proportions of G > A/T > C transitions, suggesting that at least a fraction of them may stem from deamination effects. While fixation artifacts are well-documented to occur at lower frequencies (typically with a VAF of <5%),43 our bioinformatic analysis explicitly filtered for variants exceeding a 5% VAF threshold, minimizing the likelihood of such artifacts confounding our results. Moreover, alterations in BMs were not compared with those in the primary tumor or subsequent extra-CNS metastases. Accordingly, the objective was not to identify AAs acquired during CNS dissemination but rather to characterize the AA landscape of BCBMs. Finally, the inability to distinguish between germline and somatic mutations did not allow to assess the relative relevance of germline and somatic alterations in BRCA1/BRCA2/PALB2. However, this limitation is unlikely to have materially affected the estimated prevalence of clinically AAs in the present study, as both germline and somatic BRCA1/BRCA2 AAs are classified as ESCAT I/II according to the updated 2024 criteria, while PALB2 AAs were rare, and all cases involved also harbored AAs in other genes.

Nevertheless, this study also presents notable strengths. To our knowledge, it represents one of the largest series to date evaluating genomic alterations in BCBMs. Furthermore, the use of the ESCAT classification allowed to focus on clinically AAs, thereby enhancing the translational relevance of our results. Finally, the availability of detailed clinical data enabled the assessment of the association between genomic findings and survival.

Conclusions

Overall, our retrospective study highlighted a high prevalence of clinically actionable genomic alterations in BCMCs, particularly in gene involved in HRR and PIK3/AKT/PTEN pathway. These findings suggest the potential therapeutic relevance of molecular profiling BM samples, when tissue is available in clinical practice, as this might identify CNS-specific, evolution-driven alterations, not detected in primary or extracranial samples, which might be clinically actionable. These results also emphasize the need for future studies prospectively including larger cohorts of patients to confirm these findings and explor the therapeutic efficacy of targeted strategies in this population.
