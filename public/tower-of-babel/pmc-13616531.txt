Unmasking AL Cardiac Amyloidosis During Familial Cardiomyopathy Screening
Authors: Giuseppe Barberis Barcia, Inés Toranzo-Nieto, Verónica González-Calle, Javier Maillo Seco, Leyre Álvarez Rodríguez, Jose C. Cañadas Salazar, Mónica García Monsalvo, Jose A. Muñoz León, Cristina Agullo Roca, Rocío Eiros Bachiller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmyloid light-chain (AL) amyloidosis is a life-threatening plasma cell disorder in which prognosis is largely determined by cardiac involvement. Early diagnosis remains uncommon.Case SummaryA 65-year-old asymptomatic man was referred for familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy screening. Echocardiography showed severe left ventricular hypertrophy, and cardiac magnetic resonance revealed biventricular hypertrophy with normal extracellular volume, along with late gadolinium enhancement in the interventricular septum and both atria, raising suspicion for infiltrative cardiomyopathy. Bone scintigraphy showed no pathologic cardiac tracer uptake (Perugini grade 0). An abnormal κ/λ ratio prompted hematologic work-up, and endomyocardial biopsy confirmed AL cardiac amyloidosis. Treatment with daratumumab, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone was initiated.DiscussionThis case highlights the diagnostic value of multimodality imaging and systematic monoclonal protein screening in unexplained ventricular hypertrophy.Take-Home MessageA high index of suspicion is required to avoid missing early AL cardiac amyloidosis, particularly in patients with unexplained ventricular hypertrophy.

History of Presentation

A 65-year-old man was referred to the inherited cardiomyopathy clinic for screening after a brother was diagnosed with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). The patient was completely asymptomatic from a cardiovascular standpoint. Physical examination showed no evidence of congestive heart failure.

There was no history of carpal tunnel syndrome, lumbar spinal stenosis, unexplained bruising, or spontaneous petechiae. Initial laboratory evaluation showed normal cardiac biomarkers, a normal complete blood count, and a mild renal impairment based on estimated glomerular filtration rate.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of dyslipidemia, tobacco use, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. In addition, he was treated surgically for basal cell carcinoma.

Family history was notable for a brother with HCM with positive genetics for MYBPC3. There was no family history of sudden cardiac death.

Given the marked hypertrophy on echocardiography and the positive family history, further evaluation was undertaken to exclude phenocopies, including genetic testing and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). CMR confirmed severe concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, with a maximum wall thickness of 17 mm at the basal anteroseptum (Figure 2A). There was no evidence of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve apparatus, or myocardial crypts. In addition, right ventricular free wall hypertrophy was present. Biventricular systolic function was preserved. Native T1 values and extracellular volume (ECV) (22%) were within normal limits (Figures 2C and 2D). However, subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) was observed in the interventricular septum, corresponding to the segments with the greatest wall thickness, as well as in both atria (Figure 2B); the remaining parameters were unremarkable. Because CMR findings were atypical for HCM, particularly the presence of biatrial LGE and right ventricular hypertrophy, the diagnostic evaluation was expanded to investigate alternative phenocopies. As cardiac amyloidosis (CA) was the main diagnostic consideration, further testing included bone scintigraphy and serum and urine studies for monoclonal proteins. Bone scintigraphy (Figure 1B) demonstrated no pathologic cardiac tracer uptake (Perugini grade 0).

Meanwhile, serum and urine studies revealed a monoclonal immunoglobulin G (IgG) λ plasma cell dyscrasia, with markedly elevated free λ light chains (138.75 mg/L) and a suppressed κ/λ ratio (0.1). Serum protein electrophoresis identified a monoclonal protein of 0.65 g/dL, and serum and urine immunofixation were positive for monoclonal IgG λ and λ light chains. Evidence of renal involvement was present, with 24-hour proteinuria of 1.99 g, albuminuria of 1,077 mg/24 h, and mild renal impairment, with estimated glomerular filtration rate of 79 mL/min/1.73 m2. Further, cardiac biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin I and N-terminal pro B–type natriuretic peptide) were not elevated.

To confirm the diagnosis, endomyocardial biopsy was performed. Histopathologic examination demonstrated amyloid deposition (Figures 3A and 3B) with amorphous deposits showing characteristic red-orange staining on Congo red stain (Figures 3C and 3D) and apple-green birefringence under polarized light (Figure 3E). Management

Following diagnosis, first-line therapy was initiated with daratumumab, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone, which the patient tolerated adequately. Given the absence of symptoms and preserved cardiac function, no heart failure therapy was required.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient has continued to be active and to engage in physical activity. During follow-up, cardiac biomarkers remained stable, proteinuria decreased (from 1.99 g/24 h to 1.07 g/24 h), and urine monoclonal λ light chain and monoclonal IgG lambda became negative, showing partial hematologic response. Diagnosis occurred at Mayo 2012 cardiac stage I, European modified cardiac stage I, and Palladini renal stage I, enabling treatment before irreversible cardiac damage developed.

Discussion

AL amyloidosis is a rare but life-threatening disorder caused by deposition of misfolded immunoglobulin light chains in tissues. Cardiac involvement occurs in up to 70% of patients and represents the main determinant of prognosis.1 Despite advances in diagnostic strategies, delays in diagnosis remain common, frequently exceeding 6 to 12 months from symptom onset. Early detection before the development of overt heart failure is therefore crucial, as it is associated with improved survival.1,2

This case highlights key aspects of the contemporary diagnostic pathway for CA in patients with unexplained hypertrophy and illustrates a range of clinical and imaging features in the differential diagnosis of HCM and CA (Table 1). First, multimodality cardiac imaging2,3 plays a central role in identifying infiltrative cardiomyopathies. The initial echocardiogram demonstrated severe biventricular hypertrophy. Although this patient did not show other prominent echocardiographic abnormalities, echocardiography remains a key tool in the evaluation of phenocopies, particularly through the assessment of diastolic function, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, and longitudinal strain, all of which may raise suspicion for CA when abnormal. In this case, CMR provided the most relevant diagnostic clues; in particular, LGE involving both atria represents a finding that strongly suggests an infiltrative myocardial process. Atrial LGE is rarely observed in HCM or hypertensive heart disease, but has been increasingly described in both AL amyloidosis and transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR) with cardiac involvement. LGE is highly prevalent (96% to 100%) in CA and has diagnostic value (sensitivity of 85% and a specificity of 92%). There are, however, important differences between the 2 subtypes that should be considered: Whereas ATTR more commonly shows transmural involvement, and AL amyloidosis more often demonstrates subendocardial involvement, this pattern alone does not reliably distinguish between the 2 subtypes. Additional sites of LGE may include the right ventricular free wall, interatrial septum, and atria, with atrial involvement being a particularly suggestive feature of CA (sensitivity 76% and specificity 94%). Beyond functional assessment, CMR provides important tissue characterization parameters, including native T1 and ECV, both of which are typically elevated in amyloid infiltration; ECV >40% is highly suggestive of CA. Second, bone scintigraphy is a fundamental component of the differential diagnosis of CA and should be performed alongside serum and urine monoclonal protein testing, as the current diagnostic protocol indicates. Positive cardiac tracer uptake strongly supports the noninvasive diagnosis of ATTR; however, interpretation requires greater caution when AL amyloidosis is a possibility, as false-positive uptake may occur. Therefore, when monoclonal protein studies are abnormal, definitive diagnosis should be established with tissue confirmation through invasive biopsy.2,6

Third, assessment for monoclonal proteins is a critical component of the diagnostic work-up for suspected CA and should be performed systematically in this setting, as failure to do so may lead to misclassification of AL amyloidosis as ATTR. By contrast, systematic monoclonal protein screening is not routinely indicated in patients with HCM or with other causes of secondary ventricular hypertrophy, such as chronic kidney disease or hypertensive heart disease, unless there are clinical or imaging features suggestive of amyloidosis. This case underscores the importance of ordering and interpreting diagnostic tests within the appropriate clinical context, as monoclonal protein studies were pursued only after CMR revealed findings suspicious for amyloidosis. Interpretation of abnormal monoclonal protein studies and subsequent management should be undertaken within a multidisciplinary team that includes a hematologist, particularly given the possibility of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance. In our patient, the presence of renal involvement, reflected by significant proteinuria, and cardiac involvement further supported the need for invasive diagnosis by endomyocardial biopsy.6,7

Notably, the diagnosis in this patient was established before the onset of cardiac symptoms, which is uncommon in AL amyloidosis. The incidental detection during familial cardiomyopathy screening highlights the value of comprehensive evaluation in patients referred to inherited cardiomyopathy assessment, even when genetic testing is negative. Such screening programs and protocols may therefore provide an opportunity for early identification of infiltrative cardiomyopathies before the development of clinical heart failure.6,8

From a therapeutic perspective, the incorporation of daratumumab into cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone–based regimens has significantly improved hematologic response rates and survival in AL amyloidosis. Achieving deep hematologic responses is strongly associated with cardiac improvement and better clinical outcomes.1

Conclusions

AL cardiac amyloidosis may manifest subclinically and mimic HCM. Multimodality imaging combined with monoclonal protein testing, along with a high index of suspicion and greater disease awareness, is essential for enabling early diagnosis in patients with unexplained ventricular hypertrophy. Early-stage detection enables prompt initiation of targeted therapy and may significantly improve prognosis.Take-Home Messages•In patients with unexplained ventricular hypertrophy, biatrial LGE and negative bone scintigraphy should prompt systematic evaluation for AL amyloidosis with monoclonal protein testing.•Early recognition of subclinical AL cardiac amyloidosis enables timely initiation of plasma cell–directed therapy before overt heart failure develops.
