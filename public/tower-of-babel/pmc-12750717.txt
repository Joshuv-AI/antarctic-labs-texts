Surgical treatment of rib fractures more than one month after injury: a single-center experience
Authors: Kaile Jiang, Ruonan Li, Weiwei He, Yi Yang, Lei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) has gained widespread acceptance, with a prevailing consensus that the procedure should be performed as promptly as possible. However, it remains uncertain whether patients with rib fractures sustained for more than one month experience significant benefits from undergoing SSRF. This study primarily investigates the effectiveness and safety of SSRF in patients who have suffered rib fractures for over one month.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on the clinical data of patients with rib fractures who were hospitalized between January 2017 and March 2023, specifically those who had sustained injuries for more than one month. All patients included in this study underwent SSRF. The analysis primarily focused on evaluating improvements in chest wall deformity, alleviation of chronic chest pain, and the incidence of postoperative complications following SSRF.ResultsThis cohort consisted of 22 cases, among which 16 patients underwent SSRF within 1–3 months following their injuries, while the remaining 6 patients had SSRF performed after a period exceeding 3 months. In the early intervention group (1–3 months), comprising 16 patients, 12 (75%) presented with chest wall deformities, all of which demonstrated significant improvement postoperatively. The average preoperative pain score was recorded at 7.31 ± 0.68, in contrast to a postoperative score of 1.25 ± 0.43 (p < 0.05). Post-surgical complications included one case of chest wall numbness and another reporting a foreign body sensation in the chest wall.In the group consisting of 6 patients who underwent surgery more than 3 months post-injury, all demonstrated significant improvement in chest wall deformities following SSRF. The average preoperative pain score for this group was recorded at 5.17 ± 1.46, while the postoperative score indicated a notable reduction to 1.33 ± 0.47 (p < 0.05).ConclusionsFor patients with rib fractures who have not undergone SSRF within one month and continue to experience significant pain and/or chest wall deformities, it is recommended to consider a minimally invasive SSRF procedure via a small incision. This approach can improve the correction of chest wall deformity, alleviate chronic chest pain, and is associated with minimal postoperative complications.

Background

The safety of SSRF in patients with rib fractures has been substantiated by numerous studies, indicating that many patients can derive significant benefits from this intervention [1–3]. Early surgical fixation of rib fractures has been associated with improved in-hospital outcomes [4–7]. However, for various reasons, a subset of patients still does not receive timely internal fixation surgery for rib fractures. For example, some patients—and even certain thoracic surgeons—maintain the belief that surgical intervention is unnecessary for rib fractures. Consequently, some individuals continue to experience chest wall deformities, pain, and other complications 1 month post-injury. Given the considerations surrounding surgical efficacy and postoperative complications, it remains uncertain whether SSRF is both safe and advantageous for patients with rib fractures who are more than one month post-injury.

This study performed a retrospective analysis of clinical data from patients admitted to the Department of Thoracic Surgery at Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital between January 2017 and March 2023. The focus was on cases in which the interval from injury to surgery exceeded one month, with the objective of summarizing and exploring the associated risks and benefits of SSRF within this patient population.

Methods

This study was a retrospective analysis approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (No. 2024-ky-526). All patient privacy information was anonymized, and the ethics committee determined that informed consent from patients was not required.

This article retrospectively examined data from patients admitted to our department between January 2017 and March 2023 who underwent surgical intervention more than one month following their injury (Table 1). It is widely recognized that fractures which have not healed after 3 months can be classified as nonunion. Consequently, we categorized patients undergoing SSRF more than 3 months post-injury into a distinct group for statistical analysis. All patients received treatment with Ni–Ti alloy claw plates and Synthes rib fixation plates. The data were obtained from the Chest Wall Trauma Database of our department. We analyzed the fundamental clinical characteristics of the patients, including chest wall deformities, pain scores, and postoperative complications (Table 1). Basic characteristics of patients and postoperative follow-up

Computed tomography (CT) is the primary examination method for assessing chest wall deformities [8]. However, there is currently no standardized approach to evaluate the severity of chest wall deformities resulting from trauma. In this study, we utilized patients’ satisfaction with their chest wall appearance as an evaluation metric. Satisfaction levels regarding chest wall deformities were categorized into two groups: satisfied and unsatisfied. Pain scores were statistically analyzed using a pain assessment scale.

All patients were monitored by a dedicated personnel over a 6-month follow-up period. The data collected were subjected to statistical analysis, and differences in satisfaction with chest wall appearance before and after surgery were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Additionally, variations in pain scores pre- and post-surgery were analyzed utilizing a t-test, with P < 0.05 deemed statistically significant.

Results

Sixteen patients underwent SSRF within 1–3 months following their injuries. The basic clinical data are presented in Table 1. Among the participants, there were 7 males and 9 females, with a mean age of 58 ± 13.45 years. All 16 patients reported experiencing pain, and 12 (75%) exhibited chest wall deformities. Additionally, other complications included atelectasis (n = 3), pleural effusion (n = 8), and pneumothorax (n = 2). The average duration from injury to surgery for all patients was recorded as 36 ± 8.81 days, with an average of 6.6 ± 2.69 fracture fixation points per patient. No postoperative complications such as hemothorax or perioperative mortality were observed.

At the 6-month follow-up, the relief rate for chest wall deformities among the 12 affected patients was found to be an impressive 100%. Preoperative pain levels were assessed at an average score of 7.31 ± 0.68; conversely, the mean pain score recorded 15 days postoperatively decreased significantly to just 1.25 ± 0.43, yielding a t-test result with p < 0.05 that indicates statistical significance. Postoperatively, one patient experienced numbness in the chest wall while another reported sensations consistent with foreign body presence.

Since patients with rib fractures persisting for more than 3 months may experience nonunion, we conducted a subgroup analysis. The study included six patients with rib fractures lasting over 3 months, comprising four males and two females, with an average age of 58 ± 15.33 years. All six patients presented with chest pain and deformities of the chest wall; additionally, four exhibited pseudoarthrosis. Other complications noted were atelectasis (n = 1), pleural effusion (n = 2), and pneumothorax (n = 1). The mean duration from injury to surgery was 151 ± 71.40 days, while the average number of fracture fixation points was 2.7 ± 1.49. No short-term complications such as hemothorax or perioperative mortality were reported.

After a follow-up period of 6 months, all 6 patients demonstrated a 100% satisfaction rate regarding the correction of their chest wall deformities. Preoperative pain levels were assessed at 5.17 ± 1.46, whereas the average pain score recorded 15 days postoperatively decreased significantly to 1.33 ± 0.47 (p < 0.05), indicating statistical significance in the t-test results. One patient experienced postoperative numbness in the chest wall; however, no other complications were observed during this period.

Discussion

Surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) has emerged as a viable therapeutic option for patients suffering from rib fractures. However, due to various factors, some patients are unable to undergo SSRF in a timely manner. The safety and efficacy of SSRF for patients with multiple rib fractures who have sustained their injuries for more than one month remain uncertain.

Severe rib fractures, characterized by displaced ribs that compress the intercostal nerves, represent a significant source of pain [9] and may also lead to deformities of the chest wall. Surgical intervention is the only effective method for repositioning these displaced ribs. All patients in this study sought medical attention due to pain and/or chest wall deformities. Each patient underwent surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF), resulting in substantial relief from pain, with statistically significant differences observed in pain scores before and after surgery.

After more than one month, notable callus formation and fibrous tissue proliferation can obscure the boundaries surrounding the intercostal nerves. The surgical separation of soft tissue at fracture sites may inadvertently damage these nerves, potentially leading to long-term postoperative pain and localized numbness in the chest wall. Therefore, meticulous surgical separation is essential. Our experience indicates that dislocated fractured ends should be fully exposed while ensuring careful separation close to the rib cortex; partial removal of periosteum may be necessary if required. It is crucial to avoid unnecessary dissection while exposing sufficient length for fixation purposes. Excessive proliferative tissue can be judiciously excised; however, caution must be exercised to prevent injury to the intercostal nerves.

Chest wall deformity is a primary reason that prompts patients with rib fractures older than one month to seek medical treatment. In a cohort of 22 evaluated patients, 18 (82%) demonstrated significant deformities that could potentially reduce thoracic cavity volume, compromise compliance, and even impair lung function. Surgical repositioning remains the sole effective method for restoring normal chest shape. Notably, among the 18 patients presenting with chest wall deformities in this group, there was a remarkable 100% satisfaction rate regarding postoperative recovery of chest contour. The successful separation and fixation of all displaced rib fracture ends are critical factors contributing to favorable surgical outcomes.

A fracture that remains ununited 9 months post-injury, or one in which there is a lack of progression toward union over the preceding 3 months, can be classified as a nonunion [10]. Due to the physiological characteristics associated with respiratory movement, some scholars propose that rib fractures persisting without healing for more than 3 months should also be diagnosed as nonunion [11, 12]. Numerous studies have confirmed that SSRF is the primary method for treating nonunion [13–15].

In this study, six patients who had sustained injuries for more than 3 months exhibited symptoms of nonunion, including chronic pain and pseudoarthrosis, as well as pronounced chest deformity. During the surgical intervention, it was observed that most cases involved soft tissue entrapment at the fracture sites or severe displacement contributing to nonunion (Fig. 1), with pseudoarthrosis resulting from surrounding callus formation. Following the surgical removal of the entrapped soft tissue and subsequent repositioning and fixation of the bones, these patients experienced significant relief from their symptoms. Surgical incisions can cause damage to nerves and muscles, resulting in postoperative pain and chest discomfort. We minimized the size of the incision based on the location of the rib fracture as well as the anatomical characteristics of the chest wall’s nerves and muscles. By avoiding nerve structures during dissection and separating along muscle fiber orientations whenever possible, we aimed to reduce muscle cutting, thereby achieving minimally invasive rib fixation. This approach is a key factor contributing to the low complication rate observed in this patient cohort.

Since the surgical procedure necessitates the separation of severely dislocated ribs and the excision of soft tissue embedded in the fracture ends, there is a potential for significant postoperative exudation. It is crucial to routinely place drains within the surgical field (preferably utilizing negative pressure drainage), as effective drainage plays a vital role in minimizing or preventing local effusion and postoperative infections. Due to these inherent risks, it is common practice to insert a thoracostomy tube following surgery [16].

Although there remains a possibility of experiencing some secondary discomfort following the surgery, we are confident that the procedure is relatively safe.

Limitations

The number of patients included in this study was relatively small, resulting in a limited sample size. Consequently, despite the observation of clinically meaningful trends, the statistical power of the study was insufficient to detect potential intergroup differences. Furthermore, as a single-center investigation, larger sample sizes will be necessary in future research to draw more definitive conclusions.

Additionally, this study is retrospective in nature, which inevitably introduces selection bias. Moreover, the absence of an appropriate control group precludes a clear determination of the effects associated with continued conservative treatment. Nevertheless, the benefits observed among the patients identified in this study are evident.

Conclusions

In summary, for patients with multiple rib fractures who have been injured for more than one month and continue to experience chest wall deformities or pain, surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) should be considered. Postoperatively, symptoms can be alleviated with a low incidence of complications, rendering the procedure both safe and reliable.

Particularly for patients with rib fractures persisting beyond 3 months, there is a risk of nonunion; therefore, we strongly recommend SSRF to correct chest wall deformities, alleviate chronic chest pain, and facilitate a prompt return to normal life and work activities.
