Nasal colonization by potentially pathogenic bacteria, their antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, and risk factors for colonization among cancer patients at Dessie comprehensive specialized hospital, Northeast Ethiopia

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNasal colonisation by bacteria occurs more frequently in immunocompromised patients than in healthy individuals. Background

Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide, with a significant impact on underdeveloped nations. In fact, more than half of all cancer cases and deaths occur in developing countries. Nasal bacterial colonization in cancer patients refers to the presence of bacteria in the nasal cavity without causing an infection or symptoms [1]. This condition is particularly concerning for cancer patients because their immune systems are often weakened due to the disease or treatments like chemotherapy, making them more susceptible to infections [1, 2]. The most common gram positive bacteria found in the nasal cavity of cancer patients are Staphylococcus aureus including methicillin resistant S. aureus (MRSA) [3], coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), Streptococcus pneumoniae, and enterococci [4]. Among Gram-negative bacteria, frequently identified species include Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified antimicrobial resistance (AMR) as one of the greatest threats to human health, and it becomes challenging in immunocompromised populations such as cancer patients [6]. Antimicrobial resistance can result in chronic infection and increased mortality [7]. The unregulated sale of antimicrobials leads to the emergence and rapid dissemination of resistant bacterial strains which is a growing problem in developing countries and in Ethiopia [8].

Approximately 30% of the population is asymptomatically colonized in the nose, with healthcare settings showing higher rates [1]. Although it is often asymptomatic, nasal S. aureus colonization can result in serious infections, including surgical site infections and pneumonia, contributing to increased morbidity and healthcare costs [9]. Factors associated with nasal bacterial colonization among cancer patients have been reported to include the disease and chemotherapy, as well as age, sex, use of steroids and antibiotics, and surgical procedures [10–12]. Prolonged hospital stays and invasive procedures, aging, and individuals with chronic illnesses increase the risk of colonization [9]. Chemotherapy and radiation therapy can induce lymphopenia, further heightening infection risk [13, 14].

Infections pose a major obstacle to the success of cancer care, and the global rise of antimicrobial resistance further complicates matters [15]. Oncology patients’ microbiomes have a significant impact in the colonization and subsequent infection by bacterial pathogens, particularly multidrug-resistant organisms [16]. These infections are caused by a variety of bacteria [17]. Nasal colonisation stands out as a significant concern as it can serve as a reservoir for opportunistic pathogens, potentially leading to severe infections that can complicate cancer treatment, prolong hospital stays, and increase healthcare costs [18]. A 2019 study from India of oral cancer patients found that prior to receiving chemotherapy 35.2% were colonised by MRSA and prior to receiving radiotherapy 25% were colonised by MRSA in their noses, but this increased to 50% after chemotherapy and 33.3% after radiotherapy [19]. In lung cancer patients from Iran in 2022, the rate of carriage of S. aureus was 16.4% of which 55.5% were MRSA [20]. An Ethiopian study published in 2025 found carriage rates of MRSA of 11.1% in a group of mixed cancer patients, as well as 2.8% of patienjts being colonised by vancomycin-resistant enterococci [21]. In this latter study, the colonisation was linked to low neutrophil counts and prior invasive proedures.

Despite the critical importance of this issue, there is a lack of comprehensive data on the prevalence of nasal bacterial colonization among cancer patients. Moreover, the antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of these colonizing bacteria remain under-explored, complicating the development of effective infection control measures. Identifying the factors associated with bacterial colonization in this vulnerable population is essential to tailor preventive strategies and optimize patient care.

Methods and materials

Study area, design and period

Hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among cancer patients, from March 01 to June 30 2024, in Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DCSH) that is found in Dessie town, south Wollo zone of Amhara Regional state, North East Ethiopia. The town has a latitude and longitude of 11°8′N39°38′ with an elevation between 2,470 and 2,550 m above sea level. The town has a total area of 15.08 km2 and is 401 km from the capital city of the county, Addis Ababa and, 481 km far from Bahir Dar, the capital city of Amhara regional state [22]. In Dessie town there is one Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, one district hospital, eight health Centers, four general hospitals (private), and one public health institute. DCSH serves more than 7 million people living in its catchment area (south and north Wollo zones, Oromo zone, Waghimra zone and partials Afar region), and has on average a daily patient flow of 600 people. It has 600 beds, 8 wards, and different Outpatient departments (OPDs) like adult OPD, Paediatric OPD, Emergency OPD, and others. Also, the hospital provides different service like, laboratory, pharmacy, imaging (X-ray, ultrasound, city scan) and others. The hospital has a 14-bed oncology ward with a male and female OPD, inpatient department, a preparation room and, an oncology pharmacy.

Population

Patients who were critically ill or presented with active nasal bleeding at the time of data collection were excluded from the study to ensure the safety of participants and the integrity of the nasal swab samples. The source population for this study included all adult cancer patients aged 15 years and above with histologically confirmed solid tumours who were receiving chemotherapy at the oncology unit of Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DCSH) during the study period. It is important to note that DCSH does not provide chemotherapy services for pediatric patients under the age of 15 or for patients with hematologic malignancies such as leukemia; therefore, these groups were not considered in the study population. The oncology ward manages both inpatient and outpatient cancer cases, with an average of 6 to 8 patients admitted daily and 20 to 30 patients seen in the oncology outpatient department. During the study period, approximately 806 adult patients with solid tumours were undergoing chemotherapy, forming the accessible population from which study participants were drawn.

Operational and standard definition

Colonization

Was defined as the presence and multiplication of a microorganism in the host without any clinical findings and immune reaction at the time of isolation [23].

Multidrug resistance

Refers to bacteria that are resistant to three or more antibiotics from different classes [24].

Solid cancer also known as solid tumours cancer, refers to an abnormal mass of tissue that usually does not contain cysts or liquid areas [25].

Neutropenia

Was defined as an absolute neutrophil count < 500/mm [26].

Data collection procedures

Data related to socio-demographic characteristics and clinical variables of cancer patients were collected by face -to- face interviews using pre-tested structured questionnaires and reviews of patient cards (Supplementary file 1). The questionnaires were checked for consistency and completeness. A swab sample was collected from the nose of each study participant using sterile cotton swabs wet with sterile normal saline. Each nasal swab was obtained by rotating 2–3 times in the anterior nares of patients [27], and all collected samples were labelled and transported to Amhara public health institute Dessie branch microbiology laboratory within 1 h by placing the swabs in to the sterile test tubes having 0.5 ml of sterile normal saline solution.

Isolation and identification of bacterial isolates

Following transportation, the swab was cultured on blood agar plate (BAP), chocolate agar plate (CAP), MacConkey agar (MAC), and mannitol salt agar (MSA) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. The grown bacterial colonies were detected and identified using gram stain, and confirmed by standard biochemical tests such as catalase, coagulase, optochin, indole production, Simmons citrate, urease, motility test, lysine, gas and hydrogen sulfide(H2S) production, glucose, and lactose fermentation [28].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method as per the Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute 2024 (CLSI) guidelines [29]. The bacterial inoculate was prepared by suspending recently grown bacteria in 3–5 ml of normal saline. The turbidity of the bacteria was adjusted to meet the 0.5 McFarland standards. A sterile cotton swab was immersed and rotated multiple times, and then pressed against the wall of the test tube. After this, the swab was moved across the entire surface of the Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA). Antimicrobial disks were placed on the plate. Gram-negative bacteria were tested for susceptibility to: Amikacin (30 µg), Ampicillin (30 µg), Cefepime (30 µg), Cefotaxime (30 µg), Ceftazidime (30 µg), Ciprofloxacin (5 µg), Amoxicillin-clavulanate (20/10 µg), piperacillin-tazobactam (100/10µg), Gentamycin (10 µg), Meropenem (10 µg), Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), Tobramycin (10 µg), Cefuroxime (30 µg), Chloramphenicol (30 µg), and Tetracycline (30 µg). Gram-positive bacteria were tested for susceptibility to: Gentamycin (10 µg), Linezolid (30 µg), Chloramphenicol (30 µg), Cefoxitin (30 µg), Ciprofloxacin (5 µg), Azithromycin (15 µg), Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), Clindamycin (2 µg), and Tetracycline (30 µg). The resistance and sensitivity were interpreted in accordance with the CLSI 2024 guidelines [29]. Reference strains were used, quality of the culture media was checked by inoculating known strains (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)) of P. aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), E. coli (ATCC 25922), and S. aureus (ATCC 25923) species. Temperatures of the incubator and the refrigerator were regularly monitored.

Quality assurance

Data quality was ensured during various activities of the study by following a prepared standard operating procedure (SOP). Questionnaires were prepared in a clear and precise way and translated into the local language and back-translated to English to ensure the consistency of the questionnaires. The prepared questionnaires were pre-tested on 5% of patients (13 patients) at Debre Berhan comprehensive specialized hospital before full data collection.

Data management and analysis

After the collection of socio-demographic characteristics, associated factors, and laboratory data using a structured questionnaire, and reviewing patient cards, data were edited, cleaned, entered, and analysed using Epi-data software version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. The descriptive statistics were presented with tables and graphs. Logistic regression was used to show the association of different variables with the dependent variable. Univariate and multi-variate analysis was computed to identify factors that are independently influence the occurrence of dependent variables. Odds ratio and 95% confidence interval was calculated to measure the strength of the association. P value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Nasal carriage rate of bacteria

A total of 112 bacteria (43.1%, 95% CI 37.7–49.2) were isolated from nasal swab samples. Among these isolates, S. aureus was the predominant bacterial species, accounting for 27 cases (24.1%), followed by CoNS in 24 cases (21.4%), and S. pneumoniae in 16 cases (14.3%). P. aeruginosa was found in 12 cases (10.7%), and N. meningitidis was present in 9 cases (8.0%) (Fig. 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility pattern of isolated bacteria

Among the S. aureus isolates, 59.3% were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, 55.6% were resistant to tetracycline and, 48.15% were resistant to cefoxitin. MRSA colonization was 48.1%. Most S. aureus isolates were sensitive to linezolid (96.3%). Among the S. pneumoniae isolates, 87.5% were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, 56.3% to tetracycline, and 37.5% to azithromycin. However, all S. pneumoniae isolates were sensitive to linezolid, levofloxacin, and chloramphenicol.

In the case of gram-negative bacteria, all K. pneumoniae isolates were sensitive to chloramphenicol, amikacin, cefepime, cefotaxime and, cefuroxime. In the case of P. aeruginosa, 58.3% were resistant to ciprofloxacin and 50% were resistant to piperacillin-tazobactam.

The nasal swab isolates exhibited the highest resistance percentages, with tetracycline resistance at 100% for K. ozaenae and Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole resistance at 100%, 88%, 87.5%, 83.3%, and 75% for K. ozaenae, Acinetobacter species, S. pneumoniae, K. pneumoniae, and P. mirabilis, respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate nasal bacterial colonization, the antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of bacteria, and the associated risk factors among cancer patients at DCSH in Northeast Ethiopia. Understanding the prevalence of bacterial colonization and resistance to common antibiotics is crucial for improving infection control and treatment strategies in immunocompromised patients, particularly cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy. Additionally, the study identifies key risk factors contributing to bacterial colonization, including hospital admission history, which can guide better clinical management and reduce infection-related complications.

The prevalence of bacterial colonization among cancer patients who were receiving chemotherapy from nasal swab culture in the present study was 43.1% (95% CI 37.7–49.2).This surpasses the prevalence reported in other studies conducted in Gondar, Ethiopia, which indicated a rate of 27% [18]. Conversely, other research at the University of Texas has documented a prevalence of 57% [30]. A different study involving primary lung cancer patients and individuals without cancer development found a statistically significant difference in nasal bacterial colonization, showing a higher rate of colonization compared to the current study [31]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the type of specimens, study period, population and methodology.

Approximately ten bacterial species were isolated from nasal swab samples, with S. aureus being the predominant species. A study in Dessie town similarly reported high rates of nasal colonization of S. aureus among immune-compromised individuals, such as HIV patients [32]. This species is well known for colonizing the nasal area of cancer patients, increasing the likelihood of carriage leading to infection [3]. High bacterial carriage rates, including S. aureus, have been documented in cancer patients who developed nasal vestibulitis due to targeted therapies [2]. Infections in cancer patients vary by anatomical location, source, and duration of cancer treatment, with patients with solid tumors commonly harboring infections caused by organisms reflecting their resident microbiota at the specific infection site [33]. In non-hospitalized and non-diseased individuals, nasal carriage of S. aureus is a risk factor for developing skin and soft tissue infections [34]. Additionally, there is evidence of bloodstream infections caused by MRSA in patients who exhibit nasal carriage of the isolate [1]. A study conducted approximately 25 years ago revealed that the nasal mucosa was the most commonly colonized site by CoNS, often serving as the primary source of CoNS bacteremia in cancer patients [35]. Consistent with these findings, CoNS was identified as the second most prevalent isolate in the current study.

P. aeruginosa was the most frequently isolated gram-negative bacterium. Similarly, a study in Pakistan provided evidence that this species was the most common Gram-negative species isolated from the bloodstream of cancer patients [36]. A study conducted in Poland indicated a significantly higher frequency of S. aureus and Gram-negative bacilli colonization in the nares of chronic lymphocytic leukemia patients [11].

The nasal swab isolates showed high levels of resistance, with K. ozaenae demonstrating 100% resistance to both tetracycline and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. This resistance rate is similar to the 68.5% resistance observed in a study conducted at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital [37]. In the current report, 88% of Acinetobacter species were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, which is similar to the 100% resistance observed in a study conducted at King Edward Memorial Hospital in Mumbai [38]. The resistance pattern of S. pneumoniae to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole in the study was 83.3%, which is similar to the 96% resistance reported in a study conducted in Malawi [39]. In the case of K. pneumoniae, 87.5% of isolates were resistant to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, which is higher than the 63.4% resistance reported in a study conducted at Wenzhou Medical University in China [40].

The data presented in the current study highlight the alarming prevalence of MDR in bacterial species commonly found in the nasal area.The rise in antibiotic resistance among bacteria can be attributed to factors such as the misuse and overuse of antibiotics, inadequate infection prevention and control measures, and poor personal and environmental hygiene [41]. Additionally, the common practice of empirical treatment, along with infrequent bacterial identification and a lack of susceptibility testing, has contributed to the growing problem of antimicrobial resistance. This escalating resistance limits the available options for clinicians, leaving them with fewer effective drugs to treat bacterial infections [42].

A high level of MDR from nasal swabs isolates was observed (45.5%) in bacteria such as Acinetobacter spp., which showed the highest MDR at 80%, consistent with findings that indicate a significant presence of MDR in hospital settings of Littoral Region, Cameroon [43]. P. mirabilis and K. pneumoniae also exhibited high resistance rates, with 75% and 66.7% respectively, reflecting trends observed in various studies on Gram-negative bacteria [44, 45].

The high rate of MDR may be attributed to the repeated, inappropriate, and incorrect use of antimicrobial agents in empirical treatment, along with poor infection control strategies, which contribute to the rise of resistant microorganisms. Additionally, variations in MDR rates could be due to differences in antibiotic selection, MDR definitions, study periods, specimen types, and study populations. However, the increased proportion of MDR observed in this study is concerning, as it limits available treatment options for infections. Therefore, implementing robust infection control strategies is essential to reducing the MDR burden.

The bacteria isolated from nasal swabs in the current study showed a significant rate of overall MDR. Notably, the resistance pattern of S. aureus to cefoxitin, a key marker for MRSA, was also a prominent finding. When comparing this study to a similar one conducted at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, which aimed to determine the MRSA blood stream infection rate among cancer patients, the latter reported a lower rate of MRSA [46]. The rate of MRSA from blood stream specimen among cancer patients in a systematic review and meta-analysis report around the globe [47] is in line with the result in the present study. Cancer patients undergoing active chemotherapy are at a higher risk of mortality due to bloodstream infections caused by MRSA isolates. These infections can lead to severe complications, increasing the likelihood of death in suspected cases [48].

Significant resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and ciprofloxacin was shown by N. meningitides isolates obtained from nasal swabs of cancer patients; however, the isolate’s MDR rate was modest in the current investigation. Parallel to this, resistance to the two antibiotics used to treat the isolate was also found in a study conducted in Gonder primary school students in an effort to ascertain the nasal carriage rate of the species [49]. The emergence of novel meningococcal strains with declining susceptibility (particularly for the two drugs) during the past ten years warrants careful observation and on-going epidemiological surveys, since this could pose a concern in the future [50].

The current study found a strong correlation between nasal bacterial colonization and a history of hospitalization in cancer patients (AOR 3.921, 95% CI 1.97–7.78, P-value 0.000). This finding aligns with similar research conducted in the United States [1]. The association between hospital admission and bacterial colonization highlights the importance of implementing stringent infection control measures in healthcare settings, particularly for immunocompromised patients such as those with cancer. Additionally, the present study found that history of smoking was significantly associated with nasal bacterial colonization in cancer patients (AOR 3.105, 95% CI 1.41–6.82, P-value 0.005). This finding is consistent with a study conducted in New York, USA [51]. It highlights that smoking can increase colonization by potentially pathogenic bacteria.

According to the current study, cancer patients who don’t maintain the hygiene of their noses had a considerably greater nasal carriage of bacterial isolates (AOR 2.649, 95% CI 1.44–4.85, P-Value 0.002), similar to studies carried out in Vilnius, Lithuania [52],. Maintaining nasal hygiene can help in managing the nasal microbiota and preventing colonization by opportunistic pathogens. It emphasizes the role of regular nasal washing in maintaining respiratory health.

Strengths and limitations of the study.

A key strength is that it is the first of its kind conducted in the country, providing valuable insights for oncologists to consider potential bacterial infections and antimicrobial resistance arising from normal commensal bacteria. Additionally, the findings can inform future research and support policymakers in developing targeted interventions.

On the other hand, the study has some limitations. Sterile normal saline was used as a transport medium, which may not effectively preserve fastidious organisms such as Neisseria meningitidis and Streptococcus pneumoniae, possibly leading to an underestimation of their prevalence. Furthermore, the study was conducted at a single health facility, limiting the generalizability of the results. Children and patients with bloodstream cancers were excluded due to the absence of chemotherapy services for these groups at the study hospital, potentially omitting important patient populations from the findings.

Conclusion

The rate of nasal bacterial colonization in the present study was high. The most frequently isolated bacteria from nasal swabs of cancer patients were S. aureus, followed by CoNS, S. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa. Antibiotic resistance levels varied among isolates, highlighting the need for targeted therapy based on susceptibility patterns. This study found significant antibiotic resistance, with K. pneumoniae, S. pneumoniae, and Acinetobacter species having high MDR levels. Ongoing surveillance of antibiotic susceptibility is essential for updating treatment protocols. Additionally, a history of hospital admission, smoking, and lack of nasal washing were significantly associated with higher bacterial carriage. Promoting regular nasal washing, particularly for cancer patients, could serve as an important preventive measure to help reduce bacterial colonization and potentially decrease the risk of infection.
