What if Deception Cannot be Detected? A Cross-Linguistic Study on the Limits of Deception Detection from Text
Aswathy Velutharambath 1,2, Kai Sassenberg 3, Roman Klinger 2

Abstract
Can deception be detected solely from written text? Cues of deceptive communication are inherently subtle, and even more so in text-only communication. Yet, prior studies have reported considerable success in automatic deception detection. In this study, we hypothesize that such findings are largely driven by artifacts introduced during data collection and do not generalize beyond specific datasets. We revisit this foundational assumption by introducing a belief-based deception framework, which defines deception as a misalignment between an author’s claims and their true beliefs, irrespective of factual accuracy, allowing deception cues to be studied in isolation. Based on this framework, we construct three corpora—collectively referred to as DeFaBel —including a German-language corpus of deceptive and non-deceptive arguments and a multilingual version in German and English, each collected under different conditions to account for potential belief change and enable cross-linguistic analysis. Using these corpora, we evaluate commonly reported linguistic cues of deception. Across all three DeFaBel variants, we find that these cues exhibit negligible and statistically insignificant correlations with deception labels, contrary to prior work that treats such cues as reliable indicators. We further benchmark against other English deception datasets, that follow similar data collection protocols. While some of these show statistically significant correlations, the effect sizes remain low and, critically, the set of predictive cues is inconsistent across datasets. We also evaluate deception detection using feature-based models, pre-trained language models, and instruction-tuned large language models. While some of these models perform well on established deception datasets, they consistently perform near chance on DeFaBel . Our findings challenge the core assumption that deception can be reliably inferred from linguistic cues and call for a rethinking how deception is studied and modeled in Natural Language Processing.

Introduction

Deception is not fundamentally about factual inaccuracy, but about the intention of the author to deceive
Mahon 2016
. A statement can be objectively true and still be deceptive if the author does not believe it. Conversely, a factually inaccurate statement may be asserted with full sincerity and thus not constitute a lie. For instance, consider the statement:
“The Colosseum in Rome is an iconic ancient amphitheater. My family owns a secret underground passage that leads directly to it.”
While the first sentence is factually accurate—i.e., verifiable based on objective evidence—the second is likely fabricated. As a whole, the text is deceptive given the author’s intent, even though it contains elements that are objectively true. This disconnect between truth and intent presents a fundamental challenge to deception detection in Natural Language Processing (NLP): factual correctness and deceptive intent are
mutually independent
, and systems that conflate the two risk mischaracterizing both.

The vast majority of NLP research on deception has focused on detecting linguistic markers of deceptive intent, without considering the factual accuracy of the statements or the speaker’s underlying beliefs
(
Ott et al. 2011
;
Pérez-Rosas and Mihalcea 2014
;
Salvetti, Lowe, and Martin 2016
;
Capuozzo et al. 2020
)
. These approaches typically assume that deception manifests in observable language patterns, overlooking the epistemic dimension of deception, namely, the misalignment between a speaker’s beliefs and their claims. This issue becomes even more pertinent with the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) trained on extensive corpora of world knowledge, which could be biased towards factual accuracy. However, despite their vast informational foundations, these models exhibit mixed performance in detecting factual inconsistencies and may inadvertently align with prevalent misconceptions
(
Lin, Hilton, and Evans 2022
;
Augenstein et al. 2024
)
. This makes it even more ill-equipped in detecting
genuine
deception, especially when the deception takes the form of an objectively correct statement written with deceptive intent.

To disentangle deception from factuality, we introduce a belief-based framework, where a statement is considered deceptive when it misrepresents the author’s
belief
, not when it misrepresents the world. For example, the sentence
“The Earth revolves around the Sun”
is scientifically accurate. Yet if someone who believes otherwise writes it—to appear informed or persuasive—the statement becomes deceptive, despite its factual correctness. Here, the deception lies not in the content, but in the divergence between private belief and public expression. This distinction is foundational to our approach.

Using this framework, we construct three corpora in which deceptive intent is operationalized as a misalignment between participants’ self-reported beliefs and their written arguments.
DeFaBel_V1_De
is a German-language corpus in which participants are asked to argue in favor of a given statement, followed by a post-task report of their true belief. In
DeFaBel_V2_De
, we address the possibility that participants may shift their beliefs during argumentation, which could undermine the reliability of deception labels. To account for this, participants report their belief both before and after writing. Finally,
DeFaBel_V2_En
extends this design to English, enabling cross-linguistic comparison of deception phenomena within the same belief-based framework.

We conduct a comparative analysis of linguistic cues across
DeFaBel
datasets and other deception datasets that adopt comparable data collection methods. This analysis examines whether markers of deceptive intent remain consistent across languages and data collection settings. Further, to assess the robustness of current deception detection methods, we evaluate a diverse set of models, including feature-based classifiers, pre-trained language models, and instruction-tuned large language models, on these datasets.

Our experiments show that, while many of these models perform well on other datasets, their performance drops to near-chance levels on all versions of
DeFaBel
. This systematic failure suggests that prior model success is largely driven by linguistic patterns that are likely dataset-specific artifacts, rather than by features that reliably reflect deceptive intent. Supporting this conclusion, we find that linguistic indicators commonly associated with deception do not generalize to belief-based deception data: they fail to correlate significantly with deception labels when lies are embedded within factually accurate contexts. As hypothesized, in the case of LLMs, we observe a pronounced bias, wherein models tend to classify most instances as truthful (or non-deceptive)

This challenges the assumption that deception reliably manifests in text and points to the limitations of treating it as a purely linguistic phenomenon.
This calls for a fundamental rethinking of how deceptive intent is conceptualized, detected, and modeled within NLP.

Related Work

In everyday life, deception manifests on a spectrum, ranging from harmless white lies to more deliberate and self-serving falsehoods
Turner, Edgley, and Olmstead 1975
. It has been widely studied across disciplines, leading to a myriad of definitions and interpretations.
Mahon 2016
examines the various definitions proposed in the literature and the key conditions that must be met for a statement or an interaction to be considered deceptive. While there is no universally accepted definition,
intentionality
is often regarded as a fundamental component. In communication, deception is typically understood as the deliberate act of misrepresenting the truth to create or maintain a false belief in another person
Zuckerman, DePaulo, and Rosenthal 1981
;
Mahon 2016
.

2.1
Linguistic Cues of Deception and Their Theoretical Foundations

Deception detection has been extensively studied through multiple theoretical lenses, each of which predicts specific linguistic cues that can be used to distinguish truthful from deceptive communication.

Cognitive Load

Deceptive and non-deceptive texts exhibit distinct linguistic markers, as lying is a cognitively more demanding task than truth-telling
Zuckerman, DePaulo, and Rosenthal 1981
;
DePaulo et al. 2003
. Fabricating information requires additional mental effort, making deception more complex to sustain
Vrij et al. 2006
;
Vrij et al. 2010
. While this effect is more pronounced in speech than in written text, certain linguistic cues can been identified in both modalities. Deceptive statements tend to have simpler structures, using fewer complex words and sentences, as brevity helps liars manage contradictions and reduce cognitive strain
Burgoon et al. 2003
;
DePaulo et al. 2003
. Additionally, liars use fewer exclusive words such as “but”, “except” and “without” as these words are often used to contrast information which is cognitively more demanding
Newman et al. 2003
. In contrast, deceptive texts often contain more motion words like “walk”, “move”, and “go”, as these words represent straightforward actions that require less cognitive effort to integrate into a narrative
Newman et al. 2003
.

Self-preservation perspective

Individuals often engage in verbal deception as a way to protect themselves from negative consequences, such as social disapproval, punishment, or psychological discomfort. This need to safeguard one’s image or avoid conflict can lead to the use of indirect language, hedging, and rationalizations in an attempt to maintain self-protection
(
DePaulo et al. 2003
)
. One key linguistic adaptation involves pronoun use: liars often reduce their use of first-person pronouns (e.g., I, me, my) to psychologically distance themselves from the lie and minimize accountability
(
Pennebaker and Chung 2001
)
. Conversely, they may increase their use of third-person pronouns (e.g., he, she, they) to shift focus onto others and create plausible deniability.

Reality Monitoring

Johnson and Raye 1981
differentiates between memories derived from real experiences and those that are fabricated. According to this theory, truthful statements are richer in sensory, spatial, and temporal details, whereas deceptive accounts lack such vivid descriptions
Sporer 1997
;
Sporer 2004
;
Jaume Masip and Herrero 2005
. Hence in deceptive communication, liars may struggle to replicate concrete details, leading to simpler descriptions and fewer sensory references. Additionally, deceptive texts often contain fewer temporal markers compared to truthful references.

Verifiability Approach

Truthful statements are more likely to contain verifiable details, such as specific names, locations, and temporal markers, which can be independently checked for accuracy. In contrast, deceptive statements tend to lack these elements, as liars often avoid providing concrete details that could expose inconsistencies or be easily falsified
Nahari, Vrij, and Fisher 2014
. Previous research also suggests that liars compensate by using vague language, general claims, and irrelevant details to create the illusion of credibility without increasing their risk of detection
Nahari and Vrij 2019
.

Information Manipulation Theory

Deception often involves strategic violations of conversational norms related to quantity, quality, relevance, and manner of information
McCornack 1992
. Liars may omit details, provide false information, introduce irrelevant content, or use vague language to mislead without outright fabrication
McCornack, Baxter, and Braithwaite 2008
. In text-based deception, these violations appear as generalizations, ambiguity, and a lack of concrete details, helping deceivers manage their narratives while avoiding contradictions.

2.2
Deception Datasets

Humans perform subpar at deception detection owing to a pronounced truth bias
Levine 2014
, which undermines their reliability as annotators of deception
Bond Jr. and DePaulo 2006
;
Vrij et al. 2010
. This necessitates reliance on self-disclosure by authors of texts or identification of deceptive communication based on external knowledge, for data collection. Moreover, deception is an inherently subtle phenomenon, with its cues closely tied to the stakes involved: in high-stakes face-to-face interactions, deceptive signals are more pronounced due to the immediate consequences of lying, whereas in online or written communication, the lower stakes typically result in more subtle cues
(
Ten Brinke and Porter 2012
;
Salvetti, Lowe, and Martin 2016
)
. Deception datasets are often categorized into high-stakes and low-stakes types, with high-stakes data being particularly scarce, leading much of the research in NLP to focus predominantly on low-stakes scenarios.

High vs. Low Stake

High-stake deception involves scenarios where individuals have strong incentives to lie, such as legal interrogations, fraud investigations, or crisis communications. High-stake datasets often contain real-world deception cases, making them valuable for studying authentic deceptive behavior, which is difficult to collect due to ethical concerns, legal restrictions, and the sensitive nature of the contexts involved. Additionally, the logistical challenges of recording and labeling such behavior, along with the rarity of high-stakes deceptive interactions, further contribute to the limited availability of these datasets.
Pérez-Rosas et al. 2015
is one of the few high stakes datasets used in NLP research and it contains video collected from public court trials, where the transcriptions are labeled as deceptive or non-deceptive based on the evidences and verdict.

Low-stake deception occurs in experimental or everyday conversational settings, where the consequences of lying are minimal. Such datasets are often collected within controlled environments, enabling researchers to systematically investigate deceptive behavior. One prevalent method involves soliciting individuals’ opinions on controversial topics, such as abortion, death penalty, euthanasia, and gay marriage
Mihalcea and Strapparava 2009
;
Pérez-Rosas and Mihalcea 2014
;
Capuozzo et al. 2020
. This approach provides balanced datasets, as participants provide statements that are both aligned with and contrary to their opinions. Instead of focusing on opinions,
Velutharambath, Wuehrl, and Klinger 2024
collects arguments supporting factual or non-factual statements, categorizing them as deceptive if they contradict the participants’ beliefs, and as truthful if they align with their beliefs.

Pérez-Rosas and
Mihalcea 2015
collected equal numbers of truthful and deceptive statements across freely chosen topics, instructing participants specifically to focus on the plausibility of the statements. Another common strategy involves eliciting product or hotel reviews, where some datasets encompass both truthful and deceptive reviews solicited explicitly
Salvetti, Lowe, and Martin 2016
, while others acquire truthful reviews from genuine sources and solicit deceptive ones separately to maintain clear distinction
Ott et al. 2011
;
Ott, Cardie, and Hancock 2013
;
Fornaciari and Poesio 2014
.
In addition to opinion/belief-based statements and reviews, low-stake deception datasets may also include autobiographical narratives or personal stories to examine deception from a more personally involved perspective.
Loconte et al. 2023
uses the Hippocorpus dataset
Sap et al. 2020
, which was initially developed to investigate the differences between recollection and imagination rather than studying deception.
Loconte et al. 2023
labels genuine recollections as truthful, and imagined or fabricated experiences as deceptive. Similarly,
Kleinberg and Verschuere 2021
contains truthful and deceptive narratives of autobiographical activities.

Another category of datasets, characterized by moderate stakes but distinct from typical experimental datasets, involves strategic deception within interactive game scenarios. These datasets are primarily derived from dialogue based games conducted online, such as
Mafiascum
3
3

3

https://www.mafiascum.net/
or
Diplomacy

de Ruiter and Kachergis 2018
;
Peskov et al. 2020
, or from television and radio programs like
Box of Lies
and
Wait Wait… Don’t Tell Me!

(
Soldner, Pérez-Rosas, and Mihalcea 2019
;
Skalicky, Duran, and Crossley 2020
)
. In these contexts, deception labels are explicitly determined by the rules inherent to each game or show, allowing for systematic annotation of deceptive interactions. In these scenarios, stakes are attributed primarily to competitive incentives, such as winning the game, social reputation, or entertainment value.

In this study, we build on and extend our previously published work
Velutharambath, Wuehrl, and Klinger 2024
, which introduced the belief-based deception framework, by incorporating experimental design enhancements aimed at amplifying deception cues.

2.3
Automatic Deception Detection

The task of automatically detecting deception from textual data has gained considerable prominence within Natural Language Processing (NLP), driven by both theoretical interests and practical applications such as forensic data analysis and detecting deceptive instances in online communication
Ott et al. 2011
;
Vrij et al. 2010
;
Pérez-Rosas et al. 2015
. Automatic deception detection involves classifying truthful and deceptive content by analyzing linguistic patterns embedded in text. Given the complex cognitive and psychological processes underlying deceptive behavior, NLP-based deception detection typically draws from psychological theories that characterize linguistic and cognitive differences between truthful and deceptive communication
DePaulo et al. 2003
;
Newman et al. 2003
. Early methods predominantly relied on manually crafted linguistic features derived from lexical resources and readability metrics, while recent advancements have embraced deep neural architectures capable of capturing subtle semantic and contextual signals indicative of deception
Peskov et al. 2020
;
Velutharambath and
Klinger 2023
. However, due to the inherently subtle nature of deception and variation across contexts, cultures, and communication modalities, automatic deception detection remains a challenging research problem within NLP, requiring continuous exploration of robust methods and reliable linguistic cues.

Linguistic Cues as Features

Automatic deception detection methods typically employ linguistic cues drawn from established psychological theories on deceptive behavior, such as Cognitive Load Theory, Self-preservation Theory, Reality Monitoring, the Verifiability approach, and Information Manipulation Theory
DePaulo et al. 2003
;
Newman et al. 2003
;
McCornack, Baxter, and Braithwaite 2008
;
Vrij et al. 2010
. These linguistic cues are commonly operationalized through feature extraction frameworks, with the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) being the most widely employed lexicon-based resource
Pérez-Rosas and Mihalcea 2014
;
Yao et al. 2017
. LIWC categorizes words into psychologically meaningful classes, including cognitive processes, affective expressions, self-references, exclusivity terms, and negations.

Previous deception detection research has extensively replied on linguistic markers such as reduced lexical diversity, decreased use of complex sentence structures, fewer exclusive terms (e.g., “but”, “except”), diminished use of self-referential pronouns (“I”,“my”), increased use of hedging and indirect speech, and limited sensory or temporal details in deceptive narratives
(
Newman et al. 2003
;
Fornaciari and Poesio 2014
;
Hauch et al. 2015
)
. Readability scores, including Flesch-Kincaid
Kincaid et al. 1975
and Gunning-Fog indices
Kincaid et al. 1975
, have also been employed to quantify linguistic complexity or simplicity, reflecting the increased cognitive demands experienced by deceivers.

Despite their prevalence, these linguistic cues demonstrate considerable variability and limited cross-domain consistency
(
Pérez-Rosas and
Mihalcea 2015
;
Velutharambath and
Klinger 2023
)
. This reflects the challenge of developing robust deception detection systems based solely on linguistic features and the need for careful consideration regarding dataset characteristics and contextual factors during feature selection and modeling.

Deception Detection Methods

Numerous studies have investigated automatic deception detection from textual data, employing various computational approaches. Early research predominantly utilized feature-based classification methods with linguistic cues, including support vector machines
Ott et al. 2011
;
Pérez-Rosas and Mihalcea 2014
;
Fornaciari and Poesio 2014
, logistic regression
de Ruiter and Kachergis 2018
, decision trees
Pérez-Rosas and
Mihalcea 2015
, and random forests
Soldner, Pérez-Rosas, and Mihalcea 2019
;
Pérez-Rosas and
Mihalcea 2015
. These methods commonly depend on manually engineered linguistic features, which, despite their interpretability, often exhibit limited generalizability across domains
Hernández-Castañeda
et al. 2016
;
Velutharambath and
Klinger 2023
.

More recent approaches have shifted toward contextual embeddings, utilizing deep neural models like recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and transformer-based architectures. RNNs, specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models, have effectively captured contextual nuances relevant to deception detection tasks
Peskov et al. 2020
. Transformer-based models, such as BERT and RoBERTa, have also been widely explored
Capuozzo et al. 2020
;
Peskov et al. 2020
;
Fornaciari et al. 2021
;
Velutharambath and
Klinger 2023
. Although transformers frequently outperform traditional methods, they are not universally superior; for instance, BERT’s performance is comparable to LSTM’s in certain conversational datasets
Peskov et al. 2020
, whereas transformer architectures with additional attention mechanisms has shown further improvements
Fornaciari et al. 2021
.

Advancements in large language models (LLMs) have introduced strategies like zero-shot prompting and cue extraction. Recent studies demonstrated that LLMs, particularly FLAN-T5, achieve state-of-the-art performance, especially when scaling up the model size and fine-tuning on targeted deception datasets
Loconte et al. 2023
. More recently,
Hazra and Majumder 2024
applied zero-shot prompting to identify various deceptive cues and employed discriminator models to aggregate predictions, emphasizing the potential of utilizing generative models for deception detection.

Challenges

Despite promising advances, several challenges continue to limit the robustness and applicability of automatic deception detection methods:

•

Dataset Size and Domain Sensitivity:
Deception datasets are often relatively small and highly sensitive to domain-specific factors. This limits the generalizability of models across different contexts or conversational settings
(
Velutharambath and
Klinger 2023
;
Hernández-Castañeda
et al. 2016
)
.

•

Inconsistency of Linguistic Cues:
Linguistic features indicative of deception are not consistently reliable across datasets or experimental scenarios. The inconsistency can stem from variability in individual strategies for deception, the nature of the topic, or subtle contextual differences.
(
Newman et al. 2003
;
Kleinberg et al. 2019
)
.

•

Lack of Detailed Data Collection Descriptions:
Frequently, the precise conditions under which data were collected are inadequately documented. Since even subtle environmental or contextual changes can significantly impact deceptive behaviors, the omission of detailed descriptions complicates the replication and interpretation of results.

Addressing these methodological concerns remains essential for future research, particularly when aiming for generalizable and reproducible deception detection systems.

3
Belief-based Deception

Belief-based deception, as originally introduced in
Velutharambath, Wührl, and
Klinger 2024a
, refers to the annotation of deception based on the alignment between an individual’s self-reported beliefs and the arguments they articulate, rather than the factual accuracy of those claims. The core premise is that deception arises when individuals knowingly present arguments that contradict their own beliefs. This framework’s defining feature is its emphasis on belief consistency as the marker of deception, shifting the focus away from factuality.

Figure
1(b)
illustrates this concept with an example: Person A genuinely believes the Earth is flat and argues accordingly. Under this framework, their argument is labeled as
non-deceptive
despite being objectively incorrect. In contrast, Person B personally believes the Earth is round, yet presents the same argument (“Earth is flat”), leading to their argument being labeled as
deceptive
due to the contradiction with their true belief.

Belief-based deception annotation thus provides a robust methodology for collecting deceptive data, by assessing an individual’s genuine beliefs and then encouraging the participant to argue for positions that may contradict these established beliefs. Any subsequent arguments contradicting their personal convictions are inherently considered deceptive. This method facilitates controlled data collection of deception without relying exclusively on the objective truthfulness of statements, thus creating more precise datasets to study deception from a psychological and linguistic perspective.

For each of the 30 selected statements, we reformulate the yes/no belief probes into imperative instructions suitable for eliciting persuasive writing (e.g.,
Convince me that eating watermelon seeds cause indigestion
). Each participant is instructed to write an argumentative text in favor of the given statement, regardless of their personal stance. Only one version of a statement—either factually correct or incorrect—is used per item to avoid introducing stimulus-related confounds.

After completing the argument, participants are asked to report their belief about the target statement. In addition, we collect two auxiliary annotations: their familiarity with the topic, and their confidence in the persuasiveness of the argument they wrote. These annotations help contextualize the responses and enable later analysis of how topic knowledge and self-perceived writing strength may influence deceptive expression. A deception label is assigned based on the comparison between the participant’s reported belief and the stance expressed in their argument. If a participant argues in favor of a statement they do not believe, the response is labeled as deceptive. If their belief and argument align, the response is labeled as non-deceptive.

4.2
Corpus II: Accounting for Belief Change (German)

Our original data collection protocol assumed that participants’ reported beliefs after the task reflected their genuine attitudes. In revisiting this assumption, we consider the possibility that belief expression may be influenced by the argumentation process itself. To address this, we introduce refinements to the data collection setup intended to better capture participants’ actual beliefs, increase the relevance of the task, and reduce potential confounds in the labeling of deceptive intent.

Stimuli Design

In psychological experiments, the design of stimuli critically influences participant responses and the validity of conclusions. In our previous data collection process, we use a unidirectional stimulus design, where participants are always required to argue in favor of a given statement, regardless of their belief. This approach limits the natural variation in deception and truthful argumentation, as it does not reflect how individuals naturally construct arguments.

By incorporating bidirectional stimuli, where participants argue both for and against a claim, we can more accurately assess how deception manifests across different argumentation strategies, rather than merely measuring compliance with a given prompt. Additionally, using different formulations of a statement—factual and non-factual versions of a claim (e.g.,
Camels store water in their hump
vs.
Camels store fat in their hump
)—prevents artificial stimulus bias that could otherwise influence response patterns. This variation ensures that differences in deception markers arise from cognitive and linguistic processes rather than the rigidity of the stimulus itself.

In the unidirectional stimuli design, participants are randomly assigned a statement and asked to argue in favor of it. This approach led to two key artifacts in the resulting data: first, the dataset became highly imbalanced, as participants are only required to argue for one side of the statement; second, participants are often forced to engage with topics they likely did not have strong personal beliefs about, which may have influenced the authenticity of their responses.

We re-use the 30 statements described in Section
4.1
, which contained either factually accurate or inaccurate versions of one statement (e.g.,
Camels store fat in their hump.
or
Eating watermelon seeds can cause indigestion
). In the revised version, we reformulated the original statements into a factual version and non-factual version (e.g.,
Camels store fat in their hump
and
Camels store water in their hump
resp.). This resulted in a set of
30 statement pairs which we use as stimuli.

Belief Change

In the belief-based deception framework, we collect participants’ personal beliefs after they have generated argumentative texts. However, according to Cognitive Dissonance Theory
Festinger 1957
, individuals experience psychological discomfort when their actions contradict their pre-existing attitudes, often leading them to adjust their beliefs to align with their expressed arguments. This post-task belief reporting may, therefore, fail to capture participants’ genuine pre-existing beliefs, instead reflecting a rationalized stance developed as a result of the argumentation process
Elliot and Devine 1994
. Prior research suggests that when individuals are asked to advocate a position that contradicts their initial beliefs, they may reinterpret their beliefs to reduce dissonance, particularly if they perceive that they freely chose to engage in the task
Harmon-Jones 2000
.

A more methodologically sound approach would involve securing belief commitment before the argument generation phase, ensuring that belief measurement remains consistent throughout the task. Research has shown that prior commitment to an attitude strengthens resistance to subsequent change, as individuals become more certain of their stance after articulating it, reducing susceptibility to persuasion
(
Tormala and Petty 2002
;
Tormala and Petty 2004
)
. Implementing these safeguards can help mitigate the potential distortion of belief measurement caused by cognitive dissonance effects and enhance the reliability of collected data.

Personal Consequences or Stakes

Our goal is to investigate whether deceptive intent can be discerned independently of the factual accuracy of statements. To achieve this, we selected statements where factuality could be objectively determined. However, this approach inherently led to the selection of statements with minimal personal consequences, unlike politically or morally charged topics where individuals hold subjective opinions. The lack of personal stakes in the statements used may have reduced the salience of deception cues in argumentation tasks.

When stakes are low, individuals invest less effort in maintaining belief integrity, reducing
the motivation to align arguments with their beliefs
(
DePaulo et al. 2003
)
. In contrast,
high-stakes deception induces greater cognitive and emotional demands, amplifying physiological and linguistic deception cues
(
Vrij 2008
;
Levine 2014
)
.
One way to increase the stakes without altering the stimuli themselves is modifying the task context. Introducing social accountability—for example, requiring
participants to justify their arguments in a public forum where their responses are open to scrutiny—can increase personal investment
DeAndrea et al. 2012
.

Survey Design

Out of the 30 statement pairs, each participant is presented with a randomly selected set of five pairs, totaling ten statements. Rather than making a direct choice between the two statements within each pair, participants are instructed to select the single statement from the entire set that most strongly aligns with their personal convictions. This design allows for a more natural expression of belief by offering a diverse set of statements while avoiding the constraints of forced binary choices.

For example, a participant might be shown pairs containing statements about food, health, and policy, such as “
Gluten-free bread has health benefits
” versus “
Gluten-free bread is only better if you are gluten intolerant
”. Instead of picking between them, they evaluate all ten statements they are presented with and select the one that resonates most with their views. By structuring the task this way, the approach ensures that the participant’s choice reflects the strength of their belief across different topics rather than being unduly influenced by how statements are framed.

In the next step, we reconfirm this choice by asking the participant whether they strongly believe in the selected statement. Only upon confirming this belief are they allowed to proceed; if they do not confirm, they are redirected back to the statement selection phase where they can choose an alternative. Further, to reinforce belief commitment before any further task engagement, in the next step, participants are prompted to provide the reasoning behind their chosen belief. This helps in reducing the likelihood of post-hoc rationalization as described by cognitive dissonance theory.

Participants are then directed to the argumentation task. Similar to the survey in
DeFaBel
, they are informed that a bonus will be awarded for writing convincing arguments. To increase the stakes further, the instructions explicitly state that the responses will be displayed in a public forum, thereby increasing the personal relevance of the task. To ensure a balanced dataset, both deceptive and truthful arguments on the same topic are collected. A truthful argumentation is elicited by asking participants to argue in favor of the statement they strongly believe in, whereas a deceptive argumentation is obtained by asking them to argue in favor of the opposite statement. To mitigate any order effects, the sequence is randomized so that participants might begin by writing an argument in support of their belief or against it.

4.3
Corpus III: Cross-Linguistic Extension (English)

To examine whether the patterns observed in our belief-based deception framework generalize across languages, we extend our revised data collection protocol to English. This version mirrors the design used in the updated German corpus, including belief elicitation before and after argumentation, the use of bidirectional stimuli, and the incorporation of task elements intended to increase the personal relevance of the argumentation.

By aligning task design across both languages, we enable direct comparison of deceptive and truthful argumentation strategies in German and English. This setup allows us to explore potential language-specific variation in how deception is expressed, as well as the extent to which linguistic cues to deception are shared or divergent across typologically distinct languages.

The English corpus thereby serves both as a replication of our methodological improvements and as a testbed for cross-linguistic analysis within the belief-based deception framework.

5.2
Are Linguistic Cues Reliable Indicators of Deception?

To examine the reliability of linguistic cues commonly associated with deception and to establish a foundation for assessing their predictive utility, we conduct a correlation analysis. We aim to quantify the strength and significance of relationships between individual linguistic cues and deception labels.

To identify linguistic features correlated with deception, we compute point-biserial correlations

Glass and Hopkins 1996
)
between each linguistic feature and the binary deception labels for each dataset. The features in all datasets are standardized using z-score normalization (
\mu=0

Bonferroni 1936

0.05

We conduct the analysis using the statistical programming language R
(

5.3
How Accurately Can Models Detect Deception?

To systematically evaluate the predictive value of linguistic features in deception detection, we conduct experiments using (1) traditional machine learning models trained on linguistic features, (2) transformer-based language models fine-tuned for classification, and (3) instruction-tuned large language models applied in a zero-shot setting. This setup allows for a comparison between models that rely on predefined linguistic cues, those that learn representations from raw text, and models that follow task instructions without additional supervision.

5.3.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets and Splits

Our experiments are conducted on the selected set of deception datasets discussed in Section
5.1
, which includes
DeFaBel_V1_De
and
DeFaBel_V2_De
in German, and
DeFaBel_V2_En
,
Cross_Cult_De
,
Dec_Op

Open_Domain
,
Intention
in English. For each of the datasets, we follow a consistent topic-disjoint data partitioning strategy, splitting the data into a training set for model development and a holdout set reserved for final evaluation. This ensures that no overlapping topics appear in both subsets, thereby evaluating models’ generalization to unseen instances. See Appendix Table
10
for distribution of labels across the different splits of data.

Linguistic Feature-based Classification

To assess the predictive value of linguistic cues as features, we train feature-based classifiers for the task of deception detection. As features we included all of them used for the correlation analysis. They span a range of theoretically motivated dimensions, including syntactic patterns, affective and cognitive expressions, lexical diversity, readability, and referential strategies (see Appendix B, Table
7
).

We implement four feature-based classifiers—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Classifier (SVC), and Gradient Boosting—chosen for their diversity in learning approaches, from simple linear models to complex ensemble methods and non-linear classifiers. This variety ensures reliable evaluation across different data patterns. All features undergo z-score normalization to ensure comparability across dimensions. To evaluate model robustness and reduce sensitivity to data splits, we conduct stratified 10-fold cross-validation, preserving class distributions. After cross-validation, each classifier is retrained on the entire training set and evaluated on the topic-disjoint holdout set to assess generalization beyond memorized content. We use the
scikit-learn

To evaluate the effectiveness of transformer-based models in deception detection task, we

Training is conducted for up to eight epochs with a batch size of 8, reflecting computational constraints and available data.

Model performance is evaluated through 10-fold cross-validation on the development set. All transformer-based experiments are implemented using the
transformers

5.4
How Do LLMs Justify Misclassifications?

Instruction-tuned language models offer not only label predictions but also free-text justifications, making them uniquely suited for post hoc interpretability analysis. In this section, we investigate the internal reasoning patterns that underlie incorrect predictions by large language models in the context of deception detection. Our focus is on understanding the types of explanations LLMs provide when they misclassify inputs, particularly when deceptive content is labeled as truthful or vice versa. By analyzing the generated justifications, we aim to identify systematic biases in model reasoning and evaluate whether these align with linguistic or pragmatic cues relevant to deception.

5.4.1
Experimental Setup

As discussed in Section
5.3.1
, our LLM-based classification experiments involve prompting instruction-tuned models to perform deception detection. Given an input text, each model is asked to output (1) a binary label indicating whether the author is lying or telling the truth, (2) a short justification summarizing the rationale for its decision (referred to as the
reason title
), and (3) a longer, more detailed explanation of the decision process.

Figure
8
shows the precision, recall and macro
F_{1}
for each LLM evaluated on the full dataset. On reviewing the results, we observe a consistent pattern across all LLMs: a strong bias toward predicting inputs as truthful. We see that recall for the truthful class is consistently high, indicating that the models correctly identify most truthful instances. However, this comes at the cost of lower precision for the same class, suggesting that many deceptive inputs are incorrectly labeled as truthful. In contrast, for the deceptive class, precision is relatively high—when the model does predict deception, it is often correct—but recall is substantially lower, meaning the majority of deceptive instances are not detected. This imbalance reveals a systematic tendency to favor the truthful label unless there are clear and strong cues suggesting deception. To better understand the models’ internal reasoning processes and sources of error, we conduct a detailed analysis of the misclassified instances.

Although our experiments include multiple large language models, we center this error analysis on the outputs of the Mistral model as it consistently achieves stronger performance across datasets, both in terms of macro-averaged
F_{1}
score and recall for deceptive instances. These properties make it the most informative model for analyzing misclassification behavior, particularly in examining how deceptive content is interpreted and justified. We focus our analysis on the
reason titles
, treating them as concise, high-level representations of the models’ decision rationales.

Each model prediction is compared to the corresponding ground truth label, and the outcomes are categorized into four standard evaluation classes: true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). In our evaluation, as the task is deception detection, we treat the
deceptive
class as the positive class. In this context, a false positive denotes a truthful input incorrectly classified as deceptive, while a false negative refers to a deceptive input wrongly labeled as truthful.

To investigate the reasoning patterns underlying model misclassifications, we focus on the
reason titles
associated with false positives and false negatives, as these errors offer the most direct insight into model failures. Each reason title is embedded using the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
model from the SentenceTransformers
15
15

15

https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
library, which maps the short justifications into dense semantic vectors in a high-dimensional space. We then apply KMeans clustering to group together semantically similar justifications within each error type. The number of clusters is determined dynamically for each subset, with an upper bound of 20 and a lower bound of 2, depending on the number of available samples. This allows us to maintain a balance between interpretability and resolution, while adapting to the size of each group. To visualize the resulting clusters, we reduce the embedding dimensions to two using t-SNE. The perplexity parameter for t-SNE is set adaptively based on subset size, capped at 30 and floored at 5, to ensure stability across datasets. The resulting two-dimensional coordinates are used to analyze and interpret the distribution of reasoning patterns across error types.

After clustering, we manually reviewed each group of reason titles within the false positive and false negative subsets. For each cluster, we examined representative examples and assigned a descriptive label summarizing its dominant reasoning pattern. In cases where multiple clusters reflected closely related themes, we merged them to avoid redundancy and improve interpretability. This qualitative analysis allowed us to identify recurring justification strategies used by the model when it misclassified deceptive or truthful instances.

Overall, the model appears to conflate factual correctness with honesty and often attempts to justify its predictions by referencing linguistic correlates of deceptive communication, even in cases where these features are not contextually warranted.

6
Conclusion & Future Work

Can deception be detected purely from linguistic cues in text? This question has long guided research in computational linguistics, showing promising results across a variety of datasets. Previous work often relies on the assumption that deception creates cognitive strain, which in turn reveals itself through linguistic markers, suggesting that surface-level textual features can reliably signal deceptive intent. In this paper, we critically reassess these assumptions through the belief-based deception framework.

By introducing the
DeFaBel
corpus—a multilingual dataset grounded in belief-based deception annotations—we present an alternative framework that isolates deceptive intent from factual correctness. This shift in operationalization allows us to examine deception as a misalignment between belief and expression, irrespective of factual accuracy. Across all variants of
DeFaBel
, we find that linguistic cues traditionally associated with deception show no significant correlation with deception labels. In similar datasets, these cues exhibit weak and inconsistent correlations. Models that perform well on existing deception datasets, including feature-based classifiers, transformer models, and instruction-tuned large language models, consistently fail under our belief-based paradigm. Instruction-tuned models in particular display a pronounced truth-bias, conflating plausibility with honesty and largely ignoring intent. These findings suggest that prior successes in deception detection may stem more from dataset-specific artifacts than from any stable, generalizable signals of deception, which also invite broader reflection on the nature of deception in language and discourse.

Open Question

Why do deception cues vanish in the
DEFABEL
setting? We hypothesize that the argumentative nature of the task plays a critical role. Deceptive arguments, especially in low-stakes experimental settings, are not spontaneous lies but carefully constructed rhetorical acts. They do not necessarily impose heightened cognitive load, nor do they evoke emotional discomfort; instead, they may rely on the same linguistic strategies used in persuasive writing. As a result, deception in this context may not disrupt language, instead may refine it. This blurs the line between deception and persuasion: the liar and the debater may be linguistically indistinguishable, not because deception leaves no trace, but because argumentation offers tools to mask it.

Our findings thus call for a broader rethinking of deception detection in computational linguistics. Rather than treating deception as a fixed linguistic pattern, we propose viewing it as a context-sensitive, pragmatically constructed phenomenon, rooted in speaker intent and communicative setting. In doing so, we shift the analytical focus from linguistic features to epistemic alignment and communicative goals. A closely related perspective comes from
Li and Santos 2012
, one of the few studies to explicitly model deception in structured, argumentative settings. Their work simulates social interactions between agents, capturing both the internal reasoning behind deceptive arguments and the communicative strategies used to present them. Deception, in their model, emerges through the intentional construction of arguments designed to align with the expectations of others. Crucially, they find that deception leaves behind detectable patterns in belief manipulation and argument structure. Our findings present a contrast: in real-world rhetorical contexts, such as those represented in our corpus, these patterns may be masked rather than revealed. When deception is embedded in persuasive, fluent argumentation, it may not disrupt linguistic or structural norms at all. This divergence points to a key question for future work: under what conditions do belief-based signals of deception survive, and when are they erased by the demands of genre and discourse?

What if deception cannot be detected?

This question, posed in the title of our paper, serves as a final reflection. If deception, specifically in low-stakes contexts, cannot be reliably detected through linguistic cues alone, then the widespread deployment of automated deception detection systems must be approached with extreme caution. Our findings challenge the foundational assumption that textual features can capture the intent to deceive, especially when separated from factual accuracy. This casts doubt on the reliability and ethical defensibility of systems that claim to detect deception in online-communication.

More broadly, our results urge the community to reconsider the framing of deception detection as a solvable classification task. If deception is inherently contextual, strategic, and shaped by interpersonal and cultural dynamics, then purely text-based models, no matter how advanced, may always fall short. Future work must grapple with these limitations, exploring richer representations of belief, context, and communicative intent, or perhaps acknowledging the limits of what such systems can responsibly infer.

Future work

Our findings point toward a deeper reconceptualization of deception in structured discourse. In argumentative settings, which reward coherence, fluency, and persuasiveness, deception may not manifest through disruption or leakage, but through conformity. Rather than breaking with linguistic norms, deceptive statements may align closely with the rhetorical expectations of the genre, effectively camouflaging intent behind structured language. This echoes Goffman’s notion of performance in social interaction, where the speaker’s presentation of self is always strategically constructed to fit the frame of the situation
(
Goffman 1959
)
. Similarly, in the context of mediated communication, Turkle
(
Turkle 1995
)
noted that text-based environments allow for the construction of idealized selves, where messages can be carefully ”
edited
,
deleted
, and
retouched
“. Walther’s hyperpersonal model
(
Walther 1996
)
likewise emphasizes how low-cue, asynchronous settings enable users to curate their personas in ways that obscure intent. In these contexts, deception becomes less a linguistic anomaly and more a stylized act, indistinguishable from persuasive stance-taking. Taken together, these insights suggest that the absence of detectable cues in our study may not reflect a shortcoming of computational models, but rather the success of deceivers in adapting to the communicative affordances and rhetorical conventions of mediated, structured discourse.

This shift opens up several directions for future research. One is to investigate how deception behaves across different communicative genres—for instance, comparing structured argumentation with more spontaneous, less rhetorically constrained settings. Another is to examine whether belief-expression misalignment becomes more salient under higher-stakes or emotionally charged conditions. Finally, while our results suggest that linguistic cues are weak signals in rhetorical contexts, future work may explore whether deeper models of reasoning and audience design can recover latent traces of deceptive intent. Understanding when, where, and how deception becomes visible remains an open challenge that lies at the intersection of language, intent, and context.

Limitations

This study investigates linguistic cues of deception in low-stakes scenarios, across German and English. While we analyze how previously reported cues behave on selected datasets and evaluate the performance of existing state-of-the-art models, our primary focus is not on optimizing model accuracy. Our aim is to understand how deception manifests linguistically rather than to build the most effective classifier. We acknowledge that tailored models could yield improved performance for specific usecases. However, our study intentionally prioritizes the linguistic exploration of deception over model refinement, leaving further optimization for future work.

Additionally, we discuss in the paper that deceptive language in argumentative contexts may differ from other forms of deception. However, we do not explore or contrast linguistic patterns of deception in argumentative contexts with those in other low-stakes scenarios, as this is beyond the scope of the current study. This is an area that warrants further exploration, particularly in understanding how deception patterns vary across different discourse genres and communicative goals.

Ethical Considerations

This study involves the elicitation and analysis of deceptive arguments in a controlled, low-stakes setting. Participants were asked to argue against their own beliefs, with all prompts carefully curated to avoid sensitive or emotionally charged content. These prompts reflect common misconceptions rather than deeply held values, minimizing potential discomfort.

All participants were informed of the nature and goals of the study in advance and gave explicit consent prior to taking part. They were also reminded of their right to withdraw at any time, without consequence. While we collected demographic information to support analysis, we did not collect or publish any personally identifying information. All data used in our analyses remains fully anonymized.

We are aware of the broader ethical implications of deception detection research. While our research aims to deepen the scientific understanding of deceptive intent through linguistic and computational analysis, such models could be misused, for example, to unfairly profile individuals or generate more convincing disinformation. We therefore caution against any deployment of such models in consequential settings, particularly where individual judgment or profiling is involved.

At present, automatic deception detection models should not be relied upon for high-stakes decisions. The performance of such systems remains insufficient for practical deployment, and their use in real-world scenarios risks producing incorrect or unjust outcomes. We advocate for the responsible and transparent use of such models, and stress that any deployment should be accompanied by appropriate ethical oversight.

In Figure
22
, we visualize a cross-corpus comparison based on bert-based deception classifiers. It shows the representations learned by the base model and multiple fine-tuned RoBERTa models using t-SNE projections of sentence embeddings. Each subplot shows the separation between deceptive and truthful texts across the English datasets, enabling comparison of how well different models capture deception-related distinctions. Rows correspond to models (base and fine-tuned), while columns represent the datasets being evaluated.

DeFaBel_V2_En
. This visualized the model’s difficulty to discern truthful and deceptive instances in belief-based dataset.
