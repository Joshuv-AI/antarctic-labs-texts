Impact of hypotension burden on survival after in-hospital cardiac arrest: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Abdullah Bakhsh, Sulafah Bahamdan, Rafah Ghazi, Hanaa Bokhary, Saleh Binmahfooz, Mohamed Hegazy, Hadeel Alsufyani, Abdulaziz Boker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimal post-cardiac arrest blood pressure targets remain uncertain, and fixed MAP thresholds may not capture the prognostic importance of dynamic hemodynamics after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), particularly after in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult IHCA events (January 2010-January 2020) at a single academic center. Patients with sustained ROSC (≥ 20 min) were included. The primary exposure was post-ROSC hypotension burden during 0–8 h, defined as the hourly time-weighted proportion of time with MAP ≤ 65 mmHg (piecewise-constant assumption between hourly readings). The primary outcome was 24-hour survival; secondary outcomes were survival to discharge and favorable neurologic outcome at discharge (CPC 1–2). The prespecified primary analysis used multivariable logistic regression (per 10% increase in burden) adjusting for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Frailty index, Pre-arrest MAP, initial rhythm, arrest location, CPR duration, and time to epinephrine (splines for continuous covariates). CPC models used ridge-penalized logistic regression. Exploratory unadjusted analyses evaluated MAP variability and MAP trajectories. Sensitivity analyses included exclusion of deaths ≤ 2 h, vasopressor-parameter adjustment, and an 8-hour landmark analysis (8–24 h survival).ResultsOf 1,543 IHCA events screened, 434 patients achieved sustained ROSC and comprised the analytic cohort; 164/434 (37.8%) survived to 24 h. Twenty-four hour survival declined across hypotension-burden categories from 59.4% (0–25%) to 9.4% (76–100%). Higher hypotension burden was associated with lower odds of 24-hour survival in univariable and multivariable models. Associations with survival to discharge and CPC 1–2 were directionally worse but imprecise. In the 8-hour landmark cohort (n = 380), survival from 8 to 24 h decreased stepwise across burden categories (60.0% to 13.3%), with directionally similar adjusted associations.ConclusionsIn adults with IHCA achieving sustained ROSC, greater early post-ROSC hypotension burden (0–8 h) was consistently associated with lower odds of 24-hour survival, including landmark and treatment-intensity sensitivity analyses. Longer-term neurologic outcomes were directionally worse but imprecise, supporting the need for prospective studies.

Introduction

Survival after cardiac arrest depends not only on successful resuscitation but also on the quality of post-ROSC care. Optimizing blood pressure is critical for maintaining cerebral and myocardial perfusion [1], yet the ideal systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) targets remain uncertain [2]. Current guidelines recommend a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, a threshold extrapolated from sepsis management rather than cardiac arrest-specific evidence [3, 4]. Recent trials have yielded conflicting results, and most have focused on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), leaving important gaps in understanding outcomes after in-hospital cardiac arrest, where patient profiles and arrest circumstances differ [5–8].

Importantly, prior studies have primarily evaluated fixed MAP targets in comatose survivors of OHCA [7–14]. These approaches overlook the dynamic hemodynamic fluctuations that occur in the immediate post-ROSC period, when patients are most vulnerable to recurrent arrest and circulatory collapse. Measures such as the cumulative burden of hypotension, depth and duration of time below critical threshold, variability in blood pressure, and trajectory patterns over time may provide a more comprehensive assessment of post-ROSC hemodynamic stability [15].

However, these dynamic metrics have not been systematically studied in IHCA populations.

While long-term neurological outcomes are the gold standard, 24-hour survival is a clinically meaningful milestone. The first day after ROSC is the period of greatest vulnerability to recurrent arrest and hemodynamic collapse, and early survival strongly correlates with longer-term outcomes [16]. It therefore serves as a pragmatic and robust endpoint for evaluating the effectiveness of immediate post-resuscitation care.

Our study aimed to determine the association between post-ROSC hypotension burden (0–8 h) and 24-hour survival following IHCA. Secondary outcomes were survival to discharge and favorable neurologic outcome at discharge with cerebral performance category (CPC) 1–2. Exploratory analyses evaluated associations of MAP variability and MAP trajectory classes with these outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study population and eligibility

We screened consecutive in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) events recorded in the institutional cardiac arrest database during the study period (January 2010 – January 2020). Eligible patients were adults (> 18 years) who achieved sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC ≥ 20 min) following IHCA occurring in the emergency department, intensive care unit, or inpatient wards. We excluded pediatric patients (< 18 years), Do-Not-Resuscitate (DNR)/withdrawal of care, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, operating room arrests, traumatic arrests, pregnant patients, and cases with missing exposure or outcome data required for analysis. For patients with multiple arrests during the same hospitalization, only the first event resulting in sustained ROSC was included.

Cardiac arrest management and standards of care

All arrests were managed according to institutional protocols aligned with American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines for Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) [17–19] in effect at the time of each event. Standard measures included high-quality chest compressions, rapid defibrillation for shockable rhythms, guideline-directed administration of epinephrine, airway management with endotracheal intubation as indicated, and capnography.

At our institution, arrests were managed by trained multidisciplinary teams per institutional ACLS protocols.

Because this investigation spanned a 10-year period, institutional practice was supported by our Life Support Training Center and the hospital’s CPR Committee, which ensured that all providers received regular training and updates consistent with the most current AHA ACLS recommendations as they evolved over time.

Post-ROSC management followed a standardized bundle of care: endotracheal intubation (if not done during arrest), titration of inspired oxygen to maintain SpO2 ≥ 93% saturation, adjustment of minute ventilation to target PCO2 35–45 mmHg, initiation of vasopressor support to maintain mean arterial pressure MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, fever prevention < 38.0 C, avoidance of hypoglycemia (glucose < 70 mg/dL) or hyperglycemia (glucose > 180 mg/dL), and early consideration for suspected ischemic etiology.

Hemodynamic data collection and definitions

Blood pressure was recorded from non-invasive oscillometric devices (GE CARESCAPEtm, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) immediately after ROSC and then at hourly intervals up to 8-hours. At each time point, three consecutive measurements were obtained and averaged. In patients with arterial catheters, invasive arterial pressures from the GE CARESCAPE monitoring system were used. For exposure derivation, MAP values were taken from the standardized hourly timepoints for all patients, regardless of monitoring modality, to avoid bias from differential measurement frequency.

Reporting and ethics

The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. All data were extracted retrospectively from the institutional cardiac arrest registry and electronic health records. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Units of Biomedical Ethics (ref. 297 − 23) of the Unit of Biomedical Ethics at King Abdulaziz University, Faculty of Medicine, with a waiver of informed consent due to its retrospective design and use of de-identified data. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 and its later amendments.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median (IQR) and categorical variables as n (%). Baseline and arrest characteristics were summarized across hourly time-weighted hypotension-burden categories (0–25%, 26–50%, 51–75%, 76–100% of 0–8 h with MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) and assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs).

To characterize post-ROSC hemodynamics over the first 8-hours, we derived the primary exposure, hypotension burden, defined as the time-weighted proportion of observed time with MAP ≤ 65 mmHg using hourly MAP measurements, assuming piecewise constant between consecutive readings (last observation carried forward). Prespecified secondary/exploratory exposures included MAP variability (within-patient SD of MAP values during 0–8 h) and MAP trajectory classes identified using group-based trajectory modeling; solutions with 2–5 classes were evaluated, and the 4-class solution was selected based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), minimum class size ≥ 10%, and average posterior probability ≥ 0.70, with class assignment based on the highest posterior probability.

The prespecified primary (confirmatory) analysis evaluated the association between hypotension burden (modeled continuously per 10% increase) and 24-hour survival using multivariable logistic regression. Models adjusted a priori for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Rockwood frailty index, Pre-arrest MAP, initial rhythm (shockable vs. non-shockable), arrest location (ED/ICU/ward), CPR duration, and time to first epinephrine. Continuous covariates (age, CPR duration, time to epinephrine) were modeled using restricted cubic splines (3 knots). Secondary outcomes were survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurologic outcome at discharge (CPC 1–2); for CPC 1–2, adjusted estimates were obtained using ridge-penalized logistic regression due to low event counts, with the penalty parameter selected by cross-validation. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Secondary analyses evaluating MAP variability and trajectory class associations with outcomes were considered exploratory and are presented as unadjusted ORs with 95% CIs. The primary analysis was interpreted at a two-sided 0.05 level; secondary/exploratory analyses were interpreted cautiously with emphasis on effect estimates and CIs given multiple comparisons.

Sensitivity analyses (i) excluding patients who died within 2 h post-ROSC; (ii) incorporating vasopressor parameters (0–2 h initiation, time to first vasopressor, and escalation [0/1/≥2 agents]) as proxies for treatment intensity; and (iii) an 8-hour landmark analysis restricted to patients alive at 8-hours post-ROSC evaluating survival from 8 to 24 h. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and calibration using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Brier score; optimism was assessed using bootstrap resampling (1,000 resamples). Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study cohort

During the study period, 1,543 consecutive IHCA events were screened. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1,069 patients met eligibility criteria, of whom 434 achieved sustained ROSC for at least 20 min and comprised the analytic cohort (Fig. 1). Overall, 164/434 patients (37.8%) survived to 24 h. Of the 270 patients who did not survive to 24 h, 54 died within the first 8-hours after ROSC and 216 died between 8 and 24 h.

Baseline and arrest characteristics

Patients were stratified by hourly time-weighted post-ROSC hypotension burden (0–8 h; MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) into four ordered exposure categories (0–25%, 26–50%, 51–75%, and 76–100%) (Table 1). Baseline and arrest characteristics were generally comparable across categories, with most SMDs < 0.10. Notable between-group differences were observed for pre-arrest MAP (progressively lower MAP with higher burden; SMD 0.28) and overall illness burden measures (Charlson Comorbidity Index and Rockwood frailty, SMDs 0.18 and 0.16, respectively). Markers of resuscitation intensity also varied across strata, including sodium bicarbonate use (SMD 0.20) and CPR duration (SMD 0.15), whereas arrest location and initial rhythm showed only modest imbalance (Table 1).

Primary analysis: hypotension burden and outcomes

A clear exposure–response gradient was observed between higher post-ROSC hypotension burden (0–8 h; MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) and lower 24-hour survival (Fig. 2), which declined from 59.4% in the lowest burden category (0–25%) to 9.4% in the highest (76–100%). In univariable and multivariable logistic regression, hypotension burden (per 10% increase) was associated with lower odds of 24-hour survival (Table 2). The primary multivariable model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.82) with acceptable overall accuracy (Brier score 0.17). Bootstrap validation (1,000 resamples) suggested modest optimism in discrimination (optimism 0.02), yielding an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.80. Calibration was acceptable on visual inspection of the calibration curve. In exploratory secondary outcome models, higher hypotension burden showed directionally lower odds of survival to discharge and favorable neurologic outcome (CPC 1–2), although confidence intervals crossed the null (Table 2).

Trajectory analyses showed a more pronounced gradient for the primary outcome. Compared with the stable-high MAP trajectory (reference), membership in the moderate-rising, low-stable, and low-falling trajectories was associated with progressively lower odds of 24-hour survival. In contrast, trajectory class membership was not clearly associated with survival to discharge or CPC 1–2, with imprecise estimates consistent with limited event counts for these secondary outcomes (Table 3).

Vasopressor use and sensitivity analyses

Among the 434 patients, 378 (87.1%) received at least one vasopressor infusion during the first 8-hours post-ROSC. Among those treated with vasopressors, the median time to initiation was 38 min (IQR 15–82) and was progressively shorter across increasing hypotension-burden strata: 55 min (25–105) in the 0–25% group, 40 min (15–85) in the 26–50% group, 30 min (12–70) in the 51–75% group, and 20 min (8–55) in the 76–100% category. Early vasopressor initiation within 0–2 h occurred in 260 patients (59.9%), and escalation to ≥ concurrent vasopressor agents occurred in 110 (25.3%). After excluding patients who died within < 2 h post-ROSC (n = 24), the restricted cohort included 410 patients (164 survivors and 246 non-survivors), and the association between hypotension burden and 24-hour survival was directionally similar (aOR per 10% increase 0.85, 95% CI 0.73–0.99). In sensitivity models adding early initiation, time to first vasopressor, and escalation (0/1/≥2) agents) to the primary adjustment set, hypotension burden remained inversely associated with 24-hour survival (aOR) per 10% increase 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98).

Landmark analysis: 8-hour landmark (exposure 0–8 h burden; outcome 8–24 h survival)

To mitigate bias from incomplete exposure ascertainment among early deaths, we performed an 8-hour landmark analysis restricting the cohort to patients alive at 8-hours post-ROSC, ensuring that hypotension burden during 0–8 h was fully observable for all included individuals. Of the 434 patients in the primary cohort, 380 (87.6%) were alive at 8-hours and comprised the landmark cohort. Among these 380 patients, 164 (43.2%) survived from 8 to 24 h corresponding to 216 deaths occurring between 8 and 24 h.

Within the landmark cohort, increasing hourly time-weighted hypotension burden (0–8 h; MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) remained associated with lower subsequent survival from 8 to 24 h. Landmark Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated clear, graded separation across burden categories over the 8–24 h interval, with the lowest-burden group maintaining the highest survival probability and the highest-burden group exhibiting the steepest early decline (Fig. 3). Consistent with this pattern, survival decreased stepwise across hypotension-burden categories: 60.0% (90/150) in the 0–25% group, 45.5% (50/110) in 26–50%, 24.0% (18/75) in 51–75%, and 13.3% (6/45) in 76–100%. In univariable logistic regression, each 10% increase in hypotension burden was associated with lower odds of survival from 8 to 24 h (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96). After adjustment using the prespecified covariate set (age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Rockwood frailty index, pre-arrest MAP, initial rhythm, arrest location, CPR duration, and time to first epinephrine), the association was directionally consistent but modestly attenuated (aOR per 10% increase 0.90, 95% CI 0.81–1.00). Overall, these findings suggest that the primary association is not solely attributable to differential exposure ascertainment due to early death.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of adult patients with in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) who achieved sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), greater early post-ROSC hypotension burden during the first 8-hours was associated with lower odds of 24-hour survival. This association persisted after multivariable adjustment and remained directionally consistent across sensitivity analyses that incorporated vasopressor features as proxies for treatment intensity. Our findings align with the broader “dose/duration” hypothesis shown in a large OHCA prehospital analysis by Smida et al. [20], where greater severity and duration of post-ROSC hypotension – quantified using average SBP, lower SBP, and a hypotension dose (mmHgꞏmin) – was independently associated with worse outcomes (survival to home), while hypotension management strategy was not associated with improved survival or faster resolution [17].

Although hypotension burden showed a clear association with 24-hour survival in our cohort, associations with survival to discharge and favorable neurologic outcome (CPC 1–2) were directionally worse with higher burden but imprecise, likely reflecting fewer events and the growing influence of downstream ICU complications, treatment decisions and goals-of-care practices on longer term outcomes. This pattern differs in strength, but not direction, from prior work in larger cohorts evaluating more distal outcomes: in the International Cardiac Arrest Registry (adult OHCA) [21], circulatory shock as hospital admission (SBP < 90 mmHg and/or need for supportive measures) was independently associated with worse neurologic outcome at discharge (lower odds of good CPC 1–2; aOR 0.60 in those without preexisting cardiovascular disease). More recently [22], an explainable machine-learning study using hourly MAP measurements during the first 24-hours after ROSC found that MAP was strongly predictive of favorable neurologic outcome, especially in the first 6 h and suggested an optimal MAP around 80 mmHg. Notably, that study excluded patients who died within 24 hours, focusing neurologic analyses on a more selected cohort. Additional observational studies align with the broader concept that early blood pressure patterns after ROSC matter: prehospital post-ROSC hypotension was associated with poorer neurologic outcomes in adult OHCA cohort [23], and time-weighted average MAP above 70 mmHg has been associated with better neurologic outcome in postarrest patients [24]. Taken together, these data support the biologic plausibility that early hemodynamics matter for neurologic recovery, while differences in populations (IHCA vs. OHCA), exposure definitions (time-weighted hypotension burden vs. admission shock vs. MAP optimization), and outcome ascertainment (including/excluding early deaths) likely contribute to why our discharge/CPC estimates are less definitive despite early-survival signal.

In exploratory analyses, higher MAP variability and worse trajectory class membership were associated with lower odds of 24-hour survival, suggesting that hemodynamic instability and temporal MAP patterns may carry additional prognostic information beyond cumulative hypotension. However, these secondary analyses were explicitly hypothesis-generating and were not consistently associated with discharge to survival or favorable neurologic outcome, with wide confidence intervals for CPC reflecting sparse events. This pattern is directionally consistent with the prehospital phenotyping work by Smida et al. [25], who applied unsupervised k-clustering to post-ROSC vital-sign extremes and trends and identified five phenotypes; a cluster characterized by hypotension with tachycardia and clusters marked by hypoxemia with dynamics SBP changes had substantially higher mortality (and greater odds of rearrest) than a hypertensive reference cluster. Additionally, a study by Lin et al. [26], which used hourly intra-arterial MAP to quantify blood pressure variability over the first 24–48 h and found that higher variability was independently associated with greater 30-day mortality and poor neurologic outcomes at discharge. Notably they restricted inclusion to patients who survived > 24 h, focusing on longer-term outcomes in a selected cohort with invasive monitoring, whereas our variability/trajectory analyses were anchored to the 0–8 h window and prioritized early survival. This supports the concept that blood pressure over time, not only single-threshold hypotension, are informative for risk stratification after ROSC.

A major concern in studies that derive exposures over time is bias from incomplete exposure ascertainment among patients who die early. To address this, we performed an 8-hour landmark analysis, restricting to patients alive at 8-hours and evaluating subsequent survival from 8 to 24 h. The burden-survival association was directionally consistent and only modestly attenuated, supporting that the primary finding is not solely attributable to differential exposure measurement time due to early death.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability, and residual confounding remains likely despite adjustment for key arrest factors. Second, blood pressure measurements were obtained from routine clinical care; even with hourly sampling, intermittent measurements may miss transient hypotensive episodes, and differences in monitoring modality (arterial line vs. non-invasive) were not consistently captured, introducing potential measurement bias. Third, vasopressor administration is both a marker of shock severity and a treatment that can modify observed hypotension although our sensitivity models incorporating timing and escalation did not materially change the primary estimate, unmeasured aspects of treatment intensity (dose, titration strategy, concurrent fluid resuscitation, sedation) may still confound associations. Fourth, patient-specific MAP targets were not reliably documented in selected subgroups. Fifth, neurologic outcome (CPC 1–2) events were relatively infrequent, requiring penalized modeling and resulting in imprecise estimates; conclusions regarding neurologic outcomes should therefore be cautious. Finally, trajectory classes were derived using an unsupervised, model-dependent approach; while standard selection criteria were applied, alternative modeling choices could yield different class structures. Given multiple exploratory analyses, some findings may represent Type I error and should be confirmed in independent cohorts.

Conclusions

In adults with IHCA achieving sustained ROSC, greater hypotension burden during the first 8-hours post-ROSC was consistently associated with lower 24-hour survival, including sensitivity analyses addressing vasopressor use and early-death exposure ascertainment. These findings motivate prospective studies that integrate standardized hemodynamic measurement, treatment intensity data, and longer-term functional outcomes to clarify optimal post-arrest blood pressure strategies.
