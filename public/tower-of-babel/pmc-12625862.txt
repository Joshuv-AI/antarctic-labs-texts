Improving care for patients with severe eating disorders in a university hospital without a formal eating disorder service
Authors: Anne M Doherty, Caroline Flynn, Carol Goulding, Lynn Spooner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEating disorders affect 1%–4% of the population and anorexia nervosa has the highest mortality rate of all mental disorders. Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders guidelines guide the management of severely medically unwell patients with anorexia nervosa who require medical admission. Locally, incidents had raised concerns around deviations in care locally from guideline-based care, and we aimed to improve care and seek full compliance with guidelines.The management of patients with severe eating disorders in the past year at our hospital was evaluated, and the Hospital Information System was interrogated for patients with eating disorders who might not have been identified clinically. In consultation with staff, a pathway was agreed to optimise identification and treatment of eating disorders in patients presenting to hospital. We developed a resource pack to guide care and delivered education to relevant clinical areas: emergency department, general medicine and psychiatry, including using simulation-based training where appropriate.Concordance with guideline-based care rose from 27% prior to this intervention to 100% afterwards. Each new patient with a suspected eating disorder represented a new plan-do-see-act cycle, allowing development of the project with each episode of patient care. There was an improvement in overall confidence in the identification and management of eating disorder in the hospital.This process highlighted that implementing a small change in patient care requires extensive consultation. The patient story was key to engaging stakeholders as it illustrated the impact on the individual patient. At 6-month follow-up, six patients have been treated via the pathway. These numbers may seem small, but this is a rare condition with a high mortality rate, and there is no room for error. The project has improved the management of patients with severe eating disorders in the general hospital setting.

Problem

Patients with eating disorders who require medical treatment rarely present to the emergency department (ED), but when they do, they are frequently severely ill. It is essential that they are recognised and treated appropriately, as this condition carries a high mortality rate.

People with anorexia nervosa (AN) have a high mortality rate and may present to hospital critically ill requiring medical admission, although they frequently appear ‘deceptively well’.1 2 Hospitalisation may be indicated due to electrolyte derangements, ECG changes or perhaps a very low body mass index (BMI) requiring urgent refeeding under supervision (to avoid the development of a refeeding syndrome, and its complications, which can include Wernicke’s encephalopathy).3 4 The Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders (MEED) guidelines have replaced the earlier MAnagement of Really SIck Patients with Anorexia Nervosa (MARSIPAN) and Junior MARSIPAN guidelines and are freely downloadable and guide management of these critical situations and allow seriously ill patients (BMI of <15 kg/m2) to be treated promptly and appropriately.5

The need for a well-defined and robust pathway was identified by the case of a near-miss event (patient A). Despite being reviewed by both medicine and psychiatry out of hours, this patient was not identified as having an eating disorder, on no occasion was she weighed, and her critically ill and high-risk status was not identified during these out of hours presentations. Her sixth presentation in 3 weeks was with collapse due to a community-acquired pneumonia and electrolyte disturbance, requiring admission to the intensive care unit. This demonstrated the need to have a well-defined pathway for the identification and management of critically underweight and ill patients with eating disorders. It also raised the possibility that other patients were presenting whose high-risk status was not being identified.

Galway University Hospitals (GUH) comprises a tertiary university hospital in the west of Ireland, with 800 beds over two sites. It has a busy ED and has on-call psychiatry, obstetric and paediatric services in addition to medicine and surgery.

Background

Eating disorders affect 1%–4% of the population—this figure includes AN, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder and other eating disorders. AN has a prevalence of 0.3% in young women (peak age of onset 15–19 years) and is the deadliest of all mental disorders with a crude mortality rate of 5.1 deaths/1000 person years.6

The diagnostic criteria for AN are well established in the diagnostic schedules: the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) of the American Psychiatric Association (APA) and International Classification of Disease (ICD-10) of the World Health Organisation (WHO).7 8 There is agreement on the basic criteria: low body weight <17.5 kg/m2, fear of fatness and body image disturbance. People with AN have a high mortality rate and may present to hospital critically ill requiring medical admission.1 2 The MEED guidelines outline the emergency management of critically ill patients (BMI of <15 kg/m2). Many hospitals will have a local variant of the guidelines, and in general either a consultant gastroenterologist (GC) or endocrinologist will lead on the care, in partnership with a consultant liaison psychiatrist (or eating disorder specialist if available). Input from dietetics and nursing is essential according to the MEED guidelines, which also highlight the importance of constant supervision to minimise any behaviours, which may threaten or undermine treatment gains, and the importance of team communication. However, most doctors and other healthcare professionals receive very little education about this high-lethality condition at any point in their training.9

The MEED guidelines clarify the appropriateness of using mental health or capacity legislation where indicated and appropriate, bearing in mind the differing mental health legislations, which exist even within the UK. MEED reiterates that a severe eating disorder is a life-threatening mental disorder, for which treatment is weight restoration in the immediate term, that is, feeding under careful supervision. Patients may require transfer to a psychiatric ward (preferably a specialist eating disorder unit) as soon as they can be safely discharged from a medical ward to avail of specialist expertise in management of symptomatic behaviours. Less acute medical issues such as derangements of thyroid or liver function may be followed up on an outpatient basis.1 2 10

In Ireland, despite the recent publication of the Eating Disorder Services Model of Care for Ireland,11 in the vast majority of the country, there are no specialist eating disorder services available to those without the means to seek private treatment: the situation is not dissimilar to ‘developing countries’ in this regard.12

The SMART (i.e. Specfic, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Timebound) aim of this study was improvement from 25% (baseline) to 100% compliance with the MARSIPAN-based (now MEED) Bundle of Care from September 2019 to September 2020. Outcome measures

Our primary outcome measure was compliance with the Bundle, congruent with our smart aim of improvement from 27% to 100% compliance with the Bundle of Care from September 2019 to September 2020. The Bundle of Care consisted of the key elements of care required by the MEED Guidelines (at the time of the intervention, Marsipan) for the management of critically ill people with AN.

Balancing measures

We needed to consider the balancing measures, in particular, increased activity—as we started to identify more people with eating disorders at GUH, we needed to provide a service for them. There is no specialist provision for the treatment of eating disorders in the West of Ireland, although this is planned in the recently published Model of Care for Eating Disorders. Increased case finding will increase the workload for hospital teams, including the Liaison Psychiatry team, which at GUH is resourced at 30% of the national minimum standard.13 We believed that early intervention in these patients’ management would improve their overall outcomes and ultimately reduce their need for inpatient admission or, where required, the duration of inpatient secondary care.

Baseline assessment

In order to establish a baseline level of need, n>50 000 presentations to the ED or Acute Medical Unit (AMU) in 2019 were screened by searching the electronic systems for the terms “eating disorder”, “anorexia”, “bulimia”, “low weight” and “vomiting”. Of these 33 (0.07% of total emergency/urgent presentations to GUH) presentations of n=17 individual patients were flagged as possible eating disorder presentations and examined. Of these, there were 22 (0.048% of total) eating disorder-related presentations (n=8 patients), and 13 of these presentations (59.1%) resulted in a medical admission. Regarding gender, 5 (62.5%) were female and 3 (37.5%) were male. The mean age was 28.1 (SD 12.8) years with a median of 26.5 years (range 14–44). Three (37.5%) patients were under 18 years old.

A mean number of 5.6 (SD 2.1; median 6, range 1–8) items from the Bundle were completed. The majority had documentation of vital signs (96%), and approximately 80% had bloods and ECGs performed. Only one-quarter, 6 (27.3%), had BMI documented. Over three-quarters (n=18, 81.8%) were referred to liaison psychiatry, and only 32% (n=7) were reviewed by a dietician.

As severely ill patients present infrequently, we decided to consider each individual patient presentation as a plan-do-see-act (PDSA) cycle, to allow us to learn from each individual instance of care.

Design

The project team comprised a GP based in the ED (LS), an ED nurse manager (CF), a consultant liaison psychiatrist (AMD) and a consultant gastroenterologist (CG).

We set up a series of meetings with key stakeholders including ED medical and nursing staff, consultant GCs, consultants from liaison psychiatry and child and adolescent psychiatry, hospital management and dietetics. These discussions allowed us to establish four priority areas to achieve the SMART aim of 100% compliance with the Bundle of Care.

Improving the process of identifying people with eating disorders

At the stakeholder meetings, it was apparent that there was no formal process for screening people for eating disorder. The baseline work and the highlighted cases where the diagnosis of eating disorder had not been identified, indicated that it was important to increase the index of suspicion for eating disorders in ED among acute medical and psychiatry teams alike. It was agreed that the simplest way to ensure the identification of high-risk patients was to provide education on eating disorders and the importance of obtaining a weight and/or BMI if there was any suspicion.

Developing a pathway for people with eating disorders

In consultation with stakeholders, a pathway was developed to optimise identification and treatment of eating disorders in patients presenting to hospital. Once patients are identified, staff need an identified pathway to ensure high-quality standardised treatment. The Bundle of Care was agreed, which included the following core measures from the Marsipan (now MEED) guidelines: BMI; vital signs; key bloods including glucose, electrolytes (incl Mg2+), full blood count; ECG; liaison psychiatry consult and dietician input. We planned to identify a primary ward where people with eating disorders would be admitted, to allow staff to build expertise.

This empowered staff to ask difficult questions, knowing that they have a potential treatment plan and a team ready and willing to engage in the treatment. We established a pathway for shared care to facilitate clear communication with clear roles and responsibilities for each team member. This clarified treatment goals and the relationships established between medical and psychiatry teams, shared communication to patients and families and avoiding inconsistent messages to patients and their families.

Developing an educational programme for acute hospital staff including psychiatry

Educational materials were developed and delivered to key clinical areas: the ED, general medicine and psychiatry. Cases were presented to demonstrate the importance of this case at the Case Conference meetings of both psychiatry and internal medicine.

A simulation-based training module was developed and delivered in an informal in-situ format in the ED, and as a more formal simulation module in the Simulation Lab of the Irish Centre for Applied Patient Safety and Simulation for both ED and psychiatry staff. It was identified that ED nursing staff are key to the success of this endeavour, so this cohort of patients receives the highest standard of care. The ED has a high volume and turnover of staff; therefore, continuous education of staff is crucial. Triage nurses were identified as key staff to involve, as they are the first clinicians to encounter patients presenting to the acute services.

Ensuring consistency of care out of hours by developing a resource based on the MARSIPAN (now MEED) Guidelines, which may be downloaded by staff out of hours

A resource pack or ‘pop up pack’ was provided to guide care along this pathway, and to ensure appropriate information was available to clinical staff, especially out of hours. This clear guidance for each team member was easily downloadable and comprised a detailed condensed version of the relevant parts of the Marsipan (now MEED) guidelines available in ED and the AMU for staff at all times for reference and guidance on patient care. Each section details what each team member should aim for in providing safe and quality care to the eating disorder patients, a guide for nursing, medical, dietician, health care assistants (HCA) and catering staff on how to manage each patient.

These resources were updated post-2022 to be consistent with the new MEED guidelines.

Strategy

After implementing the bundle, we agreed to assess future practice on a case by case basis—considering each patients’ presentation to be a PDSA cycle (table 2). We chose this approach as this is a relatively rare presentation, it would be difficult to gather timely data to generate learning unless each patients’ presentation was assessed as an individual PDSA cycle. This allowed for changes to be made to the pathway in real time and any other elements of the intervention where this was required, consistent with the relationships between the relevant areas of change as laid out in our driver diagram (figure 1). It provided a valuable moment of reflection on the progress of the project.

Results

Our Bundle of Care was based on the core requirements of the MARSIPAN (now MEED) guidelines. At baseline, on one occasion only were all components of the full bundle delivered within 24 hours. After implementing the bundle, we agreed to assess future practice on a case by case basis—considering each patient presentation to be a PDSA cycle (table 1).

With the implementation of the bundle, supported by the educational and training initiatives, and the availability of the ‘pop up pack’ to guide care, compliance with the bundle improved from 27% to 100%, in the six presentations since implementation. Embarking on a project like this has been challenging in many ways but a very enriching experience, which has improved the patients experience and empowered staff to make better judgements, informed decisions and facilitate smoother admission policy for staffing.

Lessons and limitations

From the initial stakeholder meetings, it became clear that we would encounter many barriers, due to the complexity of these cases and previous difficult experiences of physicians and nurses alike. Lack of staffing and resources was a common theme in resistance to change, particularly from the dietetics department who had significant resource issues. The overall impact on the institution has been small in terms of patient numbers but their quality of care has been improved greatly.

We identified in our qualitative work that many physicians and general hospital staff feel anxiety in managing the care of people with severe AN: it is a rare and challenging condition to treat. Physicians reported feeling ill-equipped, resourced or skilled to deal with these individuals with complex medical needs and often fraught family members. Furthermore, the legal process, capacity and potential for requiring court orders are huge barriers for general hospital physicians in taking care of these patients: most physicians are not accustomed to treating patients against their will and may find this especially distressing.

The biggest impact was on the core staff dealing with these patients, from registration at triage, better identification of ‘at risk’ patients and knowing where to find the resources required to facilitate appropriate care. It is essential to empower staff to ask difficult questions, knowing that they have a potential treatment plan or team ready and willing to engage in the treatment. Establishing a pathway for shared care is key, focussing on clear communication and clear roles and responsibilities for each team member. This clarified treatment goals and the relationships, established between medical and psychiatry teams, shared communication to patients and families and avoiding mixed messages to patients. This clear guidance for each team member was contained in the readily downloadable ‘pop up pack’: a detailed condensed version of the Marsipan guidelines available in ED and AMU for staff at all times for reference and guidance on patient care. Marsipan Guidelines were the active guidelines at the time of this work and have been more recently updated to MEED guidelines in 2022. The measures in our bundle are key measures in both the Marsipan and MEED guidelines, so this remains relevant despite the change in guidelines. Each section details what each team member should aim for in providing safe and quality care to the eating disorder patients, a nursing, medical, dietician, HCA, catering staff guide on how to manage each patient.

This work is applicable and reproducible to other similar sites but tailored for local service level arrangements. This hospital was particularly complex in that all children are only treated as children by paediatrics until the age of 14. All persons aged 14 and over were treated on adult wards. Thus, the added complexity here with teenagers, as a 14-year old, could potentially be admitted for refeeding to an adult medical ward.

Telling the patient stories at our educational meetings and sharing stories of previous patients’ experiences certainly motivated all stakeholders to engage in a meaningful way and strive to improve the story for future patients. Allowing frustrations to be shared in a trusting open forum of collaborative professionals chaired well over a number of meetings allowed us to move forward with this project. However, the dedication of our extended team members and willingness of staff was key.

The qualitative data gave us insights into how staff felt about engaging with this group of patients, and the challenges they felt in caring for this patient group. Fear of conflict, of lack of solutions, fear due to lack of knowledge and lack of prior experience in dealing with these patients were common themes. The educational sessions and simulation-based training collaboratively delivered to psychiatry and ED staff were highly rated and both teams learnt valuable insights from each other.

The establishment of a pathway for shared care with improvement in clear communication and clear roles and responsibilities for each team member clarified treatment goals and the relationships established between medical and psychiatry teams, shared communication to patients and families and avoiding mixed messages to patients. This comprised a detailed condensed version of the Marsipan (subsequently updated to MEED) guidelines available in ED and AMU for staff at all times for reference and guidance on patient care. Each section details what each team member should aim for in providing safe and quality care to the eating disorder patients: a nursing, medical, dietician, healthcare assistant and catering staff guide on how to manage each patient.

The main limitation of this project was the fact that we were examining outcomes in a relatively rare condition, less than 1 in 2000 presentations to our ED. As a result, we described change on a case-by-case basis, rather than having run charts of hundreds. The advantage of this approach is that it is truly patient-centred and allowed us to consider individual needs and incorporate personalised medicine into the pathway.

Conclusion

Eating disorders, especially AN, carry the highest mortality rates of all psychiatric illnesses.14 Patients are at greatest risk at times of physical decompensation and when at risk of refeeding, and need joint medical and psychiatric care. This may pose challenges and can even give rise to interspeciality conflict if the absence of a clear pathway.

This project achieved its aims in improving the care of this group of patients, at high risk of poor outcomes. We demonstrated that care can be improved by utilising a multifaceted approach including clear pathways, bundles of care, guidance packs and education, all underpinned by a spirit of collaboration and concern for optimal care and that there is ongoing teaching and training about this vulnerable and high-risk patient group.

This will require ongoing education for medical and nursing staff from the ED, acute medicine and psychiatry.12 15 The ‘pop up’ resource based on MEED Guidelines will need to be promoted, that staff are aware it is available to staff who need its guidance day or night.

We recommend that all acute hospitals find a way of embedding a similar pathway to ensure patients with severe eating disorders are identified and receive the appropriate treatment.
