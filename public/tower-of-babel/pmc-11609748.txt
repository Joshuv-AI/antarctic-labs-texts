Qualitative analysis of blood from patients engaging in deliberate self‐harm: Differences between prescribed and detected drugs
Authors: Masato Masuda, Brian Waters, Leo Gotoh, Yoshihiko Nakamura, Yoshifumi Kato, Shigeki Nabeshima, Shin‐ichi Kubo, Nobuaki Eto, Hiroaki Kawasaki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile drugs are sometimes taken during deliberate self‐harm (DSH), no study has attempted to analyze drugs in the blood of DSH patients and compare them with prescribed medications or other drugs. In this study, drugs were analyzed from the blood of DSH patients, and the detected, prescribed, and suspected drugs were documented.MethodsPatients who practiced DSH and were transferred to the emergency sites of Fukuoka University Hospital between April 2021 and September 2022 participated in the study. Psychiatrists assessed information such as the history of psychiatric treatment and recent methods of DSH, as well as prescribed drugs within 1 month of presenting to the hospital. Blood samples were analyzed using LC–MS/MS. Participants were divided into groups according to whether or not they were prescribed psychotropics within 1 month.ResultsFifty‐five patients were enrolled in the study. Forty had been prescribed psychotropics within 1 month of hospital admission. However, non‐prescribed drugs (NPD) were detected in 42 of the 55 participants (76%). The detection of NPD was significantly high among patients with overdose of medications and OTC drugs (p = 0.036), but NPD were also detected in patients who engaged in other methods (n = 14), and in patients without prescribed medication (n = 10).DiscussionThis is the first study focused on the drug analysis of blood from patients engaging in DSH. Approximately 80% of the DSH patients in this study had taken NPD, revealing a large discrepancy between prescribed medications and those detected in the blood.

INTRODUCTION

There are two consistent challenges confronting suicide prevention: overdosing and the use of medications with other lethal methods of suicide. Overdosing with drugs is a common method of suicide attempt in Japan 1 and is an increasing trend worldwide. 2 Especially in the field of emergency psychiatry, there have been numerous reports on the detection of psychotropic drugs in overdose cases, including antipsychotics, 3 antidepressants, 4 and benzodiazepines, 5 among others. Overdosing on psychotropics may lead to serious physical problems, such as arrhythmias and disorders of consciousness. 6 , 7 Therefore, detecting these drugs from the blood is very important for medical care.

A critical question arising from these reports is whether or not the drugs used in overdoses are prescribed medications, particularly those which were recently prescribed. Research by Okumura et al. in Japan revealed that current sedative‐hypnotic use was associated with an increased risk of overdose relative to general control non‐users. Additionally, the report showed that more than 77% of potentially problematic medications were prescribed by psychiatrists. 8 Reneflot et al. demonstrated in a Norwegian study that the dispensing of prescription medications, including psychotropic medications, is common prior to suicide. 9 While these studies using large‐scale data analysis suggest a link between prescribed drugs and overdose, they do not show a direct relationship at the individual level. In addition, overdoses of over‐the‐counter (OTC) drugs, primarily in young people, have recently increased. 10 Some OTC drugs have been increasingly used for overdose recently, 11 and this has become a global concern. Therefore, relying on information about prescribed medications alone is insufficient.

Another question arises regarding the use of drugs in suicide or self‐harm methods other than overdose, such as jumping, hanging, or stabbing/cutting. Hirokawa et al. reported that among those who completed suicide with a history of psychiatric treatment within the last year before their suicide, 47.4% had sought consultation within a month before their death. Additionally, it was common for these individuals to overdose on prescribed drugs as a supplementary means of suicide, along with engaging in self‐harming behavior before death. 12 Based on these findings, it is possible that those who chose more lethal methods like jumping or hanging may have also had access and considered using recently prescribed antipsychotics. To our knowledge, however, there have been no reports of drugs detected in suicide attempters who engaged in methods other than overdosing. No studies have involved examining the relationship between the medications prescribed or suspected to have been taken by the patient and those actually detected in the patient's blood.

In clinical practice, when patients are hospitalized for suicide attempts, emergency medical staff often review the most recently prescribed medications. They gather information by interviewing the patients and/or the patient's parents, and by examining circumstantial evidence to help determine the drugs ingested. Unfortunately, the drugs that patients are suspected to have taken are usually not extensively investigated, except in cases of overdose. Also, these drugs are rarely analyzed in the blood owing to time and financial constraints in the emergency department. Therefore, the drugs that the patients have actually taken are unclear. There is a need to investigate whether the prescribed medications were taken by those who attempted suicide to better understand the relationship between the use of psychotropic medications and suicidal or self‐harm behavior.

It is important to establish a range of “suicidal behavior” in our study. A suicide attempt obviously signals suicidal intent; however, there have been studies that investigated whether self‐harm without suicidal intent can be classified as suicidal behavior. 13 , 14 In clinical cases, particularly at emergency sites, distinguishing between these two behaviors is usually challenging. Therefore, the term “deliberate self‐harm (DSH)” has been established as the act of intentionally damaging one's body, regardless of suicidal intention. 15 DSH is reportedly 10–20 times more common than suicide attempts 15 and is repeated in short periods. 16 , 17 , 18 To prevent suicide, it is crucial to offer life‐saving care to patients involved in DSH, regardless of whether suicidal thoughts are present. Interventions by psychiatrists play a vital role in this process. 19 It is imperative to broaden the scope to encompass all forms of DSH, instead of limiting interventions to individuals who have attempted suicide or exhibit suicidal thoughts, or concentrating on specific DSH methods like overdose.

Fukuoka University Hospital is located in Fukuoka, a city in southwestern Japan with a population of 1.6 million. Our emergency medical service has two transport sites: the tertiary emergency unit and the secondary emergency unit. Approximately 3000 patients are transported to the two sites yearly; of these, 2%–3% are engaging in DSH. Since 2006, our hospital has implemented a psychiatric liaison intervention for all patients engaging in DSH who are brought to the emergency unit. This initiative aims to prevent suicide through the psychiatric evaluation of these individuals. 20

The aim of our study was to evaluate whether drugs other than prescribed medications were used in suicide attempts and DSH incidents. By comparing the drugs detected in blood samples from patients engaging in DSH with their prescribed medications, if any, or drugs that they were suspected to have taken based on interviews with emergency physicians, we attempted to draw a direct relationship between prescribed medications and the drugs detected from the blood of the individuals. We anticipated that, especially in overdose cases, there may be a discrepancy between prescription and detected drugs, and that NPD may be detected in methods other than overdose.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Outcomes

The primary objective of this research was to test the blood of patients who were admitted for DSH to see what drugs or other substances were in their system at the time of admission. The secondary objective of this research was to compare the results of the blood tests to the medications that were prescribed, if any, to the patients in their recent therapeutic history. By doing this, it could serve to evaluate a pattern or a correlation with the patients’ behavior and their prescribed medication history, to evaluate whether drugs other than those prescribed to them were used in suicide attempts and DSH incidents.

Patients

This study was performed between April 1, 2021, and September 30, 2022. Of the patients who were transported to either the tertiary emergency unit or secondary emergency unit of Fukuoka University Hospital, psychiatrists diagnosed those who were engaged in DSH. The participants of the study were selected according to the following criteria: (1) if the patient was aged >18 years, consent to participate in the study was obtained; (2) if the patient was aged <18 years and/or had impaired consciousness, consent was obtained from their parents or guardians; and (3) if the patient recovered from the disturbance of consciousness, the psychiatrist obtained consent from the patient. Patients who did not consent to participate in the study or died before the psychiatrist's interview were excluded. This study was approved by the Fukuoka University Medical Ethics Committee (U20‐802) and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Drug analysis procedure

Blood collection and sample storage

Blood samples were collected during a general emergency examination. A nurse collected the blood in polypropylene tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Subsequent manipulation and storage procedures were performed at the two transport sites as follows: (1) for patients transported to the tertiary emergency unit, fresh blood was immediately centrifuged at 3000g for 10 min at 25°C, and the resulting plasma samples were stored in a freezer at −80°C; (2) for patients transported to the secondary emergency unit, fresh blood was initially stored in a refrigerator at 4°C and within 4 days was centrifuged at 2500g for 20 min at 18°C producing plasma samples that were stored in a freezer at −4°C. These plasma samples were delivered frozen to the Department of Forensic Medicine and stored at −30°C until analysis within 30 days of collection.

Drug analysis

Frozen plasma samples were thawed and extracted using a micro‐volume QuEChERS method. Plasma (0.1 mL) was mixed with 0.3 mL acetonitrile, 0.2 mL deionized water, 80 mg magnesium sulfate, and 20 mg sodium acetate in a 2‐mL Eppendorf tube. After vortexing and centrifugation, 0.1 mL of the supernatant was removed and transferred to a clean glass test tube. The extract was mixed with 0.02 mL of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in acetonitrile and evaporated to dryness at 42°C under a nitrogen gas stream. Subsequently, the dried residue was reconstituted in methanol (0.5 mL) and transferred to an autosampler vial.

The extract was analyzed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) on a Shimadzu LCMS8045 using a Shim‐pack Velox SP‐C18 column held at 40°C. The mobile phase comprised a gradient of 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (mobile phase B). The samples were screened using the Shimadzu Rapid Toxicology Screening System (Ver. 3). 21 The database contains 231 compounds, including drugs of abuse, psychoneurotic drugs, and sedatives. Other compounds unsuitable for LC–MS/MS analysis (e.g., valproic acid) were analyzed using gas chromatography‐tandem mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Alcohol was not analyzed in this study, but alcohol use was confirmed during the interview with the patients or the patients' family members/guardians.

Evaluation

The psychiatrists interviewed the patients within 1 week of admission, depending on the treatment process. For participants who remained unconscious, the psychiatrists interviewed family members and/or guardians.

The psychiatrists assessed the categories based on various factors, including the medical and psychosocial variables described below.

Sociodemographic information

The sex and age of the participants were confirmed.

Information on medical care

The medical information included the participant's history of psychiatric treatment, psychiatric diagnosis, history of DSH, and family history of psychiatric treatment. The psychiatric diagnosis was discussed and agreed upon by two expert psychiatrists at the hospital. The patient was diagnosed according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition (The ICD‐10 Classification of Mental and Behavioral Disorders). 22

Information on recent DSH

The DSH method was confirmed according to a textbook 23 and was classified into the following categories: overdose of medications, including OTC drugs; poisoning by other substances (household cleaners and pesticides); jumping; stabbing and cutting; hanging; and burning. The overdose and poisoning cases were categorized as nonviolent, and all others as violent. 24 In addition, alcohol use immediately before or during the recent DSH was confirmed by interview or circumstantial evidence.

Confirming drug information

Similar to previous studies, 7 , 8 this study focused only on psychotropic drugs among the prescribed and OTC drugs. The following drug categories were included: antipsychotics, antidepressants, benzodiazepines and benzodiazepine receptor agonists (BZD), mood stabilizers, sleep medications (other than BZD), anti‐attention deficit and hyperactive disorder (ADHD) medications, other psychotropic medications, and OTC drugs. As OTC drugs often contain multiple components, we used the name of the component for specificity.

Statistical analysis

To demonstrate the primary objective, we categorized the drugs as follows: (1) “Detected Drugs”: Drugs that were actually detected from the blood according to the specified procedure; (2) “Prescribed Drugs”: Drugs that were prescribed within 1 month prior to transport to our hospital. These prescriptions were confirmed through the medical information letters by the prescribing doctors or the patient's electronic medical records; (3) “Suspected Drugs”: Drugs that were suspected to have been taken by the patient during or before the recent DSH, as determined by emergency medical staff. These were confirmed through the patient's electronic medical records. This category includes drugs that may not have been prescribed recently, but were suspected to have been taken by those doing the questioning during intake. This could and probably does include older drugs that might still be in their possession. All drugs according to the above definitions were listed and counted.

To address the secondary objective, several groups were established for statistical analysis, following the criteria outlined above for the primary objective. Patients are divided into two major groups based on whether or not they had been prescribed drugs (psychotropics) within 1 month of presenting to the emergency sites.

The “non‐prescribed drugs detected” (NPD detected) group includes patients who had drugs other than their prescribed medications (if any) detected in their plasma. This includes patients who had no drugs prescribed within the last month, but still had drugs detected in their plasma. The “prescribed drugs only” (PD only) group includes patients with only prescribed medications detected. The “None” group includes patients with no drugs detected.

Statistical analyses were carried out between males and females in the patient group, and “NPD detected” and “PD only” in two major groups based on whether or not they had been prescribed psychotropics, including unknown samples. For statistical analysis, the T‐test was performed for age, and Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test were performed for other items. P‐values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 12.2.0.

RESULTS

Although the processing and storage of blood samples differed between tertiary and secondary emergency units, we used the same analysis method for drug detection in the blood.

During the study period, a total of 2900 patients (1532 males and 1368 females) were transported to either the tertiary emergency unit or secondary emergency unit of Fukuoka University Hospital. Of these, 82 patients were diagnosed with DSH. These patients included those who had attempted suicide and those who had practiced self‐harm but did not exhibit suicidal intent. Following the criteria mentioned in the method, 27 patients were excluded (12 dead on arrival, 12 incapable of giving consent, and 3 who declined to provide samples) from the study, leaving 55 total patients (20 males and 35 females) (Figure 1, Table 1).

Summary of all DSH cases

The assessment categories other than drugs for all participants were summarized in Table 1. Twenty of the 55 participants were males, with a mean age of 46.5 ± 16.4 years. The mean age for females was 24.9 ± 9.4 years, significantly younger than that of males (p < 0.0001). Forty‐five of the patients had a history of psychiatric treatment, and 40 had been prescribed psychotropics within the last month. The most common diagnosis according to ICD‐10 classification was F3 (Mood (affective) disorders) in 16 patients, followed by F8 (Disorders of psychological development) in 9, and F1 (Mental and behavioral disorders due to psychoactive substance use) and F2 (Schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders) in 7. Thirty‐nine patients had a history of DSH, with females having a significantly higher rate of past DSH (p = 0.004). Nine had a family history of psychiatric treatment. The most common method of DSH was overdose (of medications and OTC drugs), which accounted for 28 cases, followed by jumping (15 cases) and poisoning (through other substances) (5 cases). Alcohol was consumed by 13 patients. There were no significant differences between males and females on diagnosis, family history of psychiatric treatment, method of recent DSH, violent methods, and alcohol use.

DISCUSSION

This study is novel in that it involved detecting drugs in the blood of patients with any method of DSH. To our knowledge, no studies have attempted to analyze drugs in the blood of patients with DSH, including DSH methods other than overdoses, and we believe that this is a valuable study for understanding the actual status of medication intake in patients who engage in DSH.

The key finding in our study is that drugs that differed from those prescribed (including those with no prescriptions) were detected in 76.4% (42 out of 55) of the participants (Table 3). It is usually clinically unclear whether prescribed drugs are taken correctly, and this study revealed a large discrepancy between them. A significantly higher percentage of NPD was detected in overdoses (of medications and OTC drugs) than in other methods. Hirokawa et al. point out that over half of those who successfully committed suicide and were also in treatment overdosed on psychotropic drugs that were prescribed by a psychiatrist, and that the prescription was part of their treatment at the time of death. 12 In our study, many patients were taking prescribed drugs for overdose as well, but it is noteworthy that 92% (23 out of 25) of them were taking drugs other than those prescribed. The data confirmed the common experience of overdoses involving currently prescribed, previously prescribed, psychotropic drugs obtained from different sources (internet, etc.), and OTC drugs.

It is noteworthy that NPDs were also detected in cases other than overdose in our study, and also in patients without current prescription. Many individuals who have committed suicide have a history of overdose of prescribed drugs 12 and psychotropic medications may lower the threshold for more lethal methods than overdose, 12 , 25 such as jumping. In our study, among the violent DSH methods, the proportion of NPD detected was higher (50.0%) in jumping cases, which might support the findings of previous studies. Borges et al. report that the number of substances used is clearly more important than the type of substance in predicting the suicide attempt. 26 Considering our results with these previous studies, it might be possible to say that the use of NPD may lead to higher possibility of DSH. Accurately ascertaining whether drugs are being used, regardless of the DSH method, may be therapeutically necessary to prevent DSH recurrence.

We identified the differences between the drugs actually taken and those prescribed or suspected, by comparing all detected drugs with those prescribed and suspected (Table 2). BZD, antipsychotics, and antidepressants were the most commonly detected drugs, a trend similar to that of previous studies limited to overdoses. 27 Similarly, the differences between the detected and suspected drugs should be considered. Particularly in overdose cases, accurately identifying the drug types actually taken is challenging for physicians in emergency departments. After an overdose, patients may experience impaired consciousness, 7 making it difficult for medical staff to interview the patient and determine the self‐administered drug. In this study, the difference between detected and suspected drugs was larger than that between detected and prescribed drugs, indicating that accurate identification of medications is challenging in emergency clinical practice.

BZD were the most frequently detected drugs in this study. They are the most commonly used overdose drugs in Japan 27 and a similar trend was observed in this study, as overdose (of medications and OTC drugs) was the most common method of DSH. Furthermore, this study demonstrated this by actually analyzing the drugs in the blood. Notably, a large discrepancy was observed between the detected and prescribed BZD (Table 2). BZD has been reported to be prescribed in over 60% of pre‐admission prescriptions for patients who overdose. 28 In clinical practice, several types of BZD were prescribed in the past, and it also can be prescribed by departments other than psychiatry to many patients.

Patients with family history of psychiatric treatment are also of our interest. Indicators of anxiety and depression in suicide attempters reportedly correlate with those of family members. 29 Family members with high anxiety or depressive traits are considered more likely to visit psychiatrists. Easy access to family medications may contribute to their use in DSH. One case in our study was confirmed to have been using medications prescribed to family members for DSH. This finding suggests the importance of understanding the psychiatric treatment of the family member as well as that of the patient.

A unique feature of this study is the inclusion of OTC drugs. OTC drugs are used as part of self‐medication 30 , 31 and should be actively used for lowering the cost of healthcare. However, some OTC drugs have been increasingly used for overdose recently, 11 and this has become a global concern. A survey in Japan reported that OTC drugs account for 15.7% of drug abuse, second only to methamphetamine and sleeping pills/anxiolytics, with an increase between 2012 and 2020. 32 The 2014 revision of Japan's Pharmaceutical Affairs Law, which allowed the online purchase of OTC drugs, may have contributed to this trend.

Notably, components from OTC drugs (excluding caffeine) were detected in more than 50% of all cases in this study; however, not all OTC drugs were used for overdoses. It is also important to note that NPD was detected in 10 (66.7%) of the prescription drug‐naïve cases, from that 8 of which OTC drugs were detected from the blood. In our study, the physicians in emergency departments assumed 10 (33%) participants as OTC substance users but could not evaluate the actual usage of OTC drugs in 19 (67%). It is reported that many patients underestimate the effects of OTC substances. 33 Hence, patients may not report the use of OTC drugs to the emergency medical staff. Similarly, medical personnel may not ask for OTC medications and might be unaware of their intake by patients who engage in DSH. In daily clinical practice, it is assumed that patients are not interviewed about OTC substances unless they are used for an overdose.

OTC drugs can increase the severity of side effects when used together by increasing the number of interactions because they contain several components in a single drug. Cases requiring hospitalization and treatment owing to interactions between OTC and prescribed drugs have been reported. 34 In 2024, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare in Japan is trying to prevent overdose of OTC drugs by expanding the range of drugs that have a risk of abuse and by tightening the handling of such drugs in terms of sales methods. 35 From the clinical point of view, in particular, physicians in Emergency department and psychiatrists should be aware of OTC drug abuse in their daily clinical practice, asking whether patients are using OTC drugs repeatedly.

This study has several limitations, one of which is the small sample size. The study was limited to patients practicing DSH transported to a single facility for emergency care, and there was a period during which the number of transports was lower than those in previous years owing to the COVID‐19 pandemic during the study period. Another limitation involves handling the specimens. Although there was a significant difference between the ages of males and females in this study, the limited sample size makes it difficult to determine if this had any impact on the findings. Further research is required to clarify the relationship. In this study, we attempted to promptly perform blood tests at the beginning of emergency medical care; however, there were some variations owing to the course of treatment and the timing of obtaining consent. To eliminate the effects of drug metabolism during treatment, all drugs used for emergency care were extensively examined. However, ascertaining the entire process of drug metabolism was impossible. Another aspect of limitation is related to 27 patients that were excluded from the study, accounting up to 1/3 of the DSH‐diagnosed patients. The 12 that were dead on arrival included 8 jumping and 4 hanging cases; 4 stabbing/cutting cases did not give study consent. These exclusions may have influenced the results of detected drugs in violent methods of DSH. In this study, the use of alcohol was evaluated by interview as opposed to actually testing each sample for the presence of ethanol. Although it did not statistically affect whether NPD were detected or not from the patients, we realize that the consumption of alcohol could have an influence on the ingestion of drugs during DSH, so that relationship should be investigated through actual analysis in future studies.

Despite some limitations, this study revealed medications not prescribed by physicians are used for DSH, including OTC drugs. Therefore, establishing a testing system to accurately identify the medications that are actually taken is essential. This also suggests that the medications the patients are currently taking should be checked regularly in daily clinical practice. From these findings, psychiatrists need to be aware that drugs other than those they have prescribed might be used for DSH, and that physicians in emergency departments need to be aware of the possibility of drugs being used even though the patients were not prescribed medications recently.
