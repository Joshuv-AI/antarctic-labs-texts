Systemic impact of intramuscular snake envenomation and intravenous immunoglobulin‐based antivenom treatment on murine plasma proteome and peptidome
Authors: Bianca C. S. C. de Barros, Alison F. A. Chaves, Mariana S. L. C. Morone, Ana T. A. Sachetto, Marcelo L. Santoro, Oliver Schilling, Solange M. T. Serrano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSnake envenomation remains a critical global public health challenge. In Brazil, the genus Bothrops is responsible for most accidents, typically resulting in coagulopathy, hemorrhage, and renal failure. Despite its clinical prevalence, the molecular mechanisms driving plasma protein disturbances remain poorly understood. In this study, we applied mass spectrometry to characterize the plasma proteome and peptidome of mice following a sub‐lethal dose of Bothrops jararaca venom and subsequent single dose antivenom treatment at timepoints 3, 6 and 24 h. From a total of 781 identified proteins, quantification of 590 revealed systemic pathological signatures such as the lower abundance of key coagulation and complement proteins, indicating coagulopathy and immune disruption. Simultaneously, an increase in tissue damage markers, innate immune response proteins, and Damage‐Associated Molecular Patterns was detected. In the acute phase, the biological response was highly conserved regardless of antivenom administration, while by 24 h the group treated only with venom exhibited prolonged inflammation and metabolic stress, whereas the venom + antivenom group transitioned to cellular recovery and detoxification. The peptidomic analysis identified a large degradome of 1964 unique peptides, showing proteolysis of fibrinogen, apolipoprotein A‐I, complement factors and protease inhibitors, among others. The analysis of cleavage sites implicated the activity of cysteine‐, metallo‐, and serine proteases in this process. Our results demonstrate that B. jararaca venom induced a rapid, dramatic systemic imbalance and proteostasis disruption that was only partially mitigated by antivenom. These findings underscore the necessity of developing complementary therapies that target both the primary toxins and secondary host‐driven pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Snakebite envenomation is an important public health problem around the world, especially in rural and poor areas in developing countries, resulting in 81,000–138,000 deaths each year and three times as many cases of amputation or other permanent dysfunction (Gutiérrez et al., 2017). As such, it is classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a neglected tropical disease (WHO, 2019). To better understand the epidemiology of envenomation, in 2019, the WHO initiated a management efforts program aiming to achieve preventative measures, advancements in treatments, and accessibility to healthcare, with the goal of reducing snakebite‐related deaths and disabilities by 50% by 2030 (Munshi & Gajbhiye, 2024).

In Brazil, the most prevalent and the main genus of medical importance is Bothrops spp., which is responsible for approximately 90% of envenomation cases occurring in the country. Envenomation by Bothrops snakes results in local and systemic clinical manifestations, which include local edema, inflammation, myonecrosis, local and systemic hemorrhage, coagulopathy, vascular disorders, hypotension, shock, and renal failure (França et al., 2003; Pinho et al., 2008; Rosenfeld, 1971; Santoro et al., 2008).

Patients bitten by Bothrops snakes typically suffer from a bleeding tendency, illustrated by thrombocytopenia and consumption coagulopathy, a hemostatic disorder that comprises coagulant, fibrinolytic, and anticoagulant effects, which are resultant of the action of a potent blend of bioactive toxins present in snake venoms (Santoro et al., 2008; Wellmann et al., 1874). The consumption coagulopathy triggered by Bothrops venoms mainly involves the activation of the coagulation cascade by venom proteases leading to the consumption of coagulation factors. The diversity of toxins found in Bothrops venoms is mainly characterized by snake venom metalloproteases (SVMP), snake venom serine proteases (SVSP), phospholipases A2 (PLA2), and L‐amino acid oxidases (LAAO), which comprise the most abundant enzymatic venom components, in addition to C‐type lectin‐like toxins (CTL) and disintegrins, all of them targeting prey hemostasis (Andrade‐Silva et al., 2016; Larréché et al., 2021).

Bothrops venom toxins can also affect the innate immune system by activating the complement cascade through the classical, alternative, or lectin pathway and generating anaphylatoxins, which contribute to the inflammatory process observed in patients upon envenomation (Pidde‐Queiroz et al., 2010). Triggering the complement system mainly involves the direct cleavage of complement components by SVMPs and SVSPs (Leonel et al., 2022).

The blood plasma contains significant biological and clinical markers due to its circulation and irrigation of the whole organism. Plasma from peripheral blood holds proteins and peptides secreted by the organs and derived from endothelial glycocalyx and cells, thus it dynamically illustrates systemic alterations that may occur in disease states. Therefore, exploring the blood proteome and peptidome may underscore pathological changes occurring systemically upon snake envenomation. This study aimed to elucidate the changes in the blood plasma of mice following Bothrops jararaca venom injection in the thigh muscle. Furthermore, the effects of Bothrops antivenom administration on the envenomation effects were assessed within this experimental model. By clarifying the molecular mechanisms underlying venom‐induced pathophysiological changes and evaluating the effects of an antivenom, this investigation contributes valuable insights to the understanding and management of snakebite envenomation.

RESULTS

Experimental strategy

We aimed to investigate the systemic impact of B. jararaca venom and Bothrops antivenom in a murine model in a time‐resolved manner. To this end, we applied experimental strategies of proteomics and peptidomics to evaluate the mouse plasma response to the injection of venom in the thigh muscle and, after 1 h, antivenom in the caudal vein, at three timepoints (3, 6 and 24 h) (Figure 1).

Schematic representation of the in vivo experimental design. Groups of four male Swiss mice were injected with 50 μL 0.15 M NaCl or venom solution (1.6 mg/kg) in the gastrocnemius muscle. One hour later, animals were injected with 50 μL of 0.15 M NaCl or antivenom solution (1.6 mg/kg) in the tail vein. After 3, 6, and 24 h, blood was collected in 0.38% sodium citrate and centrifuged to obtain plasma for proteomic and peptidomic analyses. Assigned sample groups: saline, antivenom, venom, and venom + antivenom.

Under the experimental conditions of this study, with the injection of venom (1.6 mg/kg animal body weight), antivenom (1.6 mg/kg animal body weight) or venom + antivenom, no macroscopic alterations were detected in the collected blood and plasma up to 24 h (data not shown).

Murine plasma proteome coverage

Given the analytical challenges posed by the extended dynamic range of plasma proteins, we implemented a strategy combining immunodepletion with data‐independent acquisition (DIA). To avoid the masking of abundance differences among low‐abundance proteins associated with dynamic range compression, we employed immunodepletion of albumin, IgG, and transferrin combined with DIA to minimize data sparsity. For label‐free, quantitative proteomic analysis, 48 blood plasma samples from all animal groups were individually subjected to depletion of albumin, IgG, and transferrin using affinity chromatography. Proteins present in the non‐bound fractions were submitted to the SP3 protocol for clean‐up, trypsin digestion and LC–MS/MS, using DIA and label‐free quantitation as described in Section 2.

Data acquisition quality was high, as demonstrated by total ion chromatogram (Figure S1) and ion profile (Figure S2) reconstitutions. Sample #2 from the “saline 3 h” group was removed because of a shifted ion chromatogram profile. Plasma proteomic analysis identified an average of 4646 (range 4066–5230) tryptic peptides per sample (Figure S3a) from which 781 proteins were inferred (Figure S3b). After removing sample #2, the protein abundance matrix showed 21.9% missing values (Figure S4). Peptides with poorly quantifiable features were removed using QuantUMS empirical and protein group quality scores (≥0.75) (Figure S5), eliminating peptides with poor agreement among quantitative features (Moschem et al., 2025) yielding a protein abundance matrix with robust quantitative properties.

The plasma proteome alterations induced by the venom injection in the thigh muscle were analyzed over time up to 24 h. The coefficients of variation (CV) were analyzed to estimate the residual technical variability. Except for the samples of group “venom + antivenom 6 h”, all others showed median CV values under 30% per group, which conferred high reliability and precision to the relative quantification (Figure 2a). Supervised clustering (Partial Least‐Squares Discriminant Analysis; PLS‐DA) (Figure 2c) resolved the major experimental groups representing venom or saline injection at the first variate followed by the time‐dependent alterations at the second variate (venom 24 h and venom + antivenom 24 h). Overall, supervised PLS‐DA clearly distinguished the plasma proteome profiles of venom‐injected animals from those of saline‐ and antivenom‐treated animals. However, the effects of antivenom administration were not clearly resolved by this analysis (Figure 2c). We extracted the top 30 loads from the PLS‐DA to assess the proteins most influential in the separation of the second variate (Figures 2d and S6). These proteins were primarily responsible for clustering the 24 h venom and 24 h venom + antivenom samples, distinguishing them from the remaining experimental groups. The proteins with the highest loading values were associated with biological processes related to acute inflammation (Gsdmd, Saa1, Saa2, and Osmr), coagulation and fibrinolysis (F13b, Cpb2, Adamts13, and Serpina10), extracellular matrix remodeling (Fn1, Fbln2, and Svep1), proteolysis (Capn1, Serpina3n, and Psma5), and metabolic adaptation (Pfkm and Aldoa) (Figure 2d). Although these proteins contributed to the discrimination of both groups, their relative contributions suggest distinct biological signatures associated with venom and venom + antivenom treatments, particularly involving inflammatory, hemostatic, proteolytic, and metabolic responses (Figure 2d).

From the 781 unique proteins identified across all plasma samples, 590 protein groups were successfully quantified after filtering by QuantUMS scores (Table S1) (Moschem et al., 2025). The overall results showed that changes in the plasma proteome were evident in all analyzed timepoints, with dozens of proteins identified with higher or lower abundance. The numbers of proteins identified with differential abundance were similar over time up to 24 h in animals that received B. jararaca venom injection (venom group) and in those that received antivenom administration after venom injection (venom + antivenom group) when compared to the group of animals that received only saline (Figure 3a).

Interestingly, although in lower numbers, animals only treated with antivenom also showed proteins identified in higher and lower abundance in comparison to the saline group. The generalized additive model (GAM) applied to the average protein abundance relation to log2FC indicated that, on average, the most abundant proteins were decreased, while the least abundant proteins in plasma were generally increased. The results underscored differences in plasma proteome of animals injected with venom compared to saline group regardless of the antivenom administration.

The numbers of statistically significant differentially abundant proteins were similar between the comparisons, except for the antivenom versus saline groups, which suggests only a subtle effect of the antivenom on the plasma proteome. To dissect these subtle changes, we tested the overall correlation of the direction of changes in protein abundance to address independently the antivenom administration and the time course variables. We observed strong correlation of proteome alterations (>75% of proteins) upon venom or venom + antivenom at 3 h or at 24 h (Figure 3b,c). The correlation of comparisons venom versus saline against the venom + antivenom versus saline at a fixed time (3 or 24 h) showed that the proportion of proteins with opposite trend was negligible (<1%) and the proportion of mismatched pattern was low (15%–20%) (Figure 3b,c), once again indicating a minimal impact of the antivenom on the proteome alterations. The proportion of proteins matching the direction of changes in comparisons venom versus saline at 3 and 24 h or venom + antivenom versus saline at 3 and 24 h was low (Figure 3d), suggesting that the overall trend in proteostasis was disrupted in a time dependent manner regardless of the antivenom treatment (Figure 3e). In line with this, the proportion of proteins with opposite (3.6%) or mismatch (36%–37%) trends was not negligible in time course correlation. Altogether, these results pointed to a time‐dependent proteostasis disruption in the plasma proteome of animals injected with B. jararaca venom that could not be resolved by the antivenom dose applied in the present study.

Venom yields decreased abundance of coagulation and complement proteins

Proteins detected with altered abundance upon venom injection included plasma and tissue components. Figure 4 shows selected coagulation proteins based on biological and statistical significance of differential abundance variation data. In agreement with the systemic pathology occurring in Bothrops human envenomation (França et al., 2003; Pinho et al., 2008; Rosenfeld, 1971; Santoro et al., 2008), the injection of B. jararaca venom affected hemostasis by promoting consistent changes in the abundance of blood coagulation proteins, such as factor VIII (F8), factor XIII A and B chains (F13a1 and F13b), fibrinogen alpha, beta and gamma chains (Fga, Fgb, and Fgg), plasma kallikrein (Klkb1), and von Willebrand factor (Vwf), all detected in lower abundance. In the case of factor VIII, after 24 h it showed the lowest relative abundance; however, the antivenom neutralized this effect, restoring abundance to baseline level. On the other hand, fibrinogen levels were significantly reduced up to 6 h, but recovered to baseline values by 24 h, regardless of antivenom administration.

Besides alterations in these proteins, some components of the complement system (C1s2, C3, C5, C8a, and Cfd) showed lower abundance in animals injected with venom (Table S1) thus affecting the progress of the three complement activation pathways.

Venom yields increased abundance of proteins derived from tissue leakage, inflammation and innate immune response

As a consequence of local and systemic tissue damage reported in Bothrops envenomation (Bittenbinder et al., 2024; Cavalcante et al., 2023; Gutiérrez et al., 2018; Pinho et al., 2008), proteins of diverse functions derived from tissue leakage, such as creatine kinase M‐type (Ckm), filamin‐2 (Flnc), four and a half LIM domains protein (Fhl1), glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh), heat shock 70 kDa protein (Hspa13), lactate dehydrogenase A and B chains (Ldha and Ldhb), malate dehydrogenase (Mdh1), and titin (Ttn), were identified in higher abundance in animals injected with venom and most of them remained that way despite antivenom treatment (Figure 5a).

Reflecting the local and systemic responses and consistent with the extensive tissue damage induced by venom injection, proteins classified as damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) were also detected in higher abundance in the plasma of envenomed animals, including histone H4 (H4c1), heat shock proteins HSP 90 alpha (Hsp90aa1) and beta (Hsp90ab1), heat shock 70 kDa protein 13 (Hspa13) and 60 kDa heat shock protein (Hspd1) (Figure 5b). The release of these endogenous danger signals was accompanied by increased abundance of various inflammation‐ and innate immune response‐related proteins, and 20 of them are depicted in Figure 5c (serum amyloid P component, Apcs; alpha‐2‐macroglobulin‐P, A2m; apolipoprotein C‐III, Apoc3; BPI fold containing family A, member 2, Bpfia2; carbonic anhydrase 3, Ca3; beta enolase, Eno3; gasdermin‐D, gsdmd; fatty acid synthase, Fasn; inter‐α‐trypsin inhibitor heavy chain 4, itih4; neutrophil gelatinase‐associated lipocalin, Lcn2; legumain, Lgmn; leukotriene A4 hydrolase, Lta4h; polymeric immunoglobulin receptor, Pigr; serum amyloid A1 and A2, Saa1 and Saa2; serine protease inhibitor A3N, serpina3n; serine protease inhibitor A3M, serpina3m; corticosteroid‐binding globulin, serpina6; plasminogen activator inhibitor 1, serpine1; and alpha‐2‐antiplasmin, serpinf2). Overall, the antivenom did not revert the increased abundance of these proteins.

Over‐representation analysis of biological processes affected by the venom injection over time

To characterize the temporal evolution of the physiological response to envenomation and treatment, we performed an over‐representation analysis (ORA) of biological processes comparing protein abundance variation at 3 versus 24 h (Figures 6 and S7; Table S2). In the acute phase (3 h vs. 24 h), the biological response was highly conserved regardless of antivenom administration. Both the venom‐only (Figure 6a) and venom + antivenom (Figure 6c) groups exhibited significant enrichment for hemostatic processes, with “blood coagulation”, “fibrin clot formation”, and “wound healing” showing the highest fold enrichment. However, by 24 h (24 h vs. 3 h), the proteomic profiles diverged significantly between the groups. The venom‐only group showed a marked shift toward energy metabolism, characterized by the upregulation of “canonical glycolysis” and “glucose catabolic process,” alongside “acute inflammatory response” (Figure 6b). In contrast, the venom + antivenom group at 24 h was dominated by cellular recovery mechanisms, specifically pathways involved in lipid metabolism and stress management, such as “cellular oxidant detoxification” and “response to oxidative stress” (Figure 6d).

Venom‐promoted changes in the murine plasma peptidome/degradome

As B. jararaca venom is rich in proteases (Henriques et al., 1958; Serrano et al., 2014), it is essential to analyze the peptides resultant of protein cleavage to better understand the systemic effects of envenomation. To this end, the peptidomic analysis of murine plasma was performed as described in Section 2, revealing in vivo substrates of venom proteases, as well as from host proteases activated in the envenomation process. In parallel, the analysis resulted in the identification of the plasma basal peptidome in animals injected with saline, illustrating the plasma intrinsic proteolysis.

Overall, the ion profiles (Figure S8) and mobilograms (Figure S9) indicated sufficient precursor identification across samples, with +1 ion mobility precursors increasing identifications and dataset completeness. Peptides ranging from 7 to 56 amino acids showed similar length distributions across conditions (not shown). A total of 1964 peptides were unique to venom‐injected animals, and all identified peptides are listed in Table S3. Figure S10 summarizes the numbers of identified peptides and their corresponding proteins across groups. Although low peptide identification in five replicates indicated inter‐replicate variability, likely reflecting biological or methodological variation, the analysis broadly covered the murine peptidome. Fewer peptides were identified in all four venom‐treated animals at 24 h, suggesting further degradation of endogenous peptides or venom‐generated proteolytic products over time.

Proteins detected as cleaved in the plasma and proteases acting in the plasma

The top 25 proteins containing the highest number of exclusive identified peptides in the plasma of animals injected with venom and venom + antivenom indicate prominent cleavage events (Figure S11). They include typical plasma proteins such as fibrinogen alpha chain (Fga), serum amyloid A1 (Saa1), various apolipoproteins, complement C3 (C3), and inter‐alpha‐trypsin inhibitor heavy chain 4 (Itih4), as well as tissue proteins like creatine kinase (Ckm), collagen (Col1a2), myosin‐4 (Myh4), and titin (Ttn). The similar profiles comprising the most cleaved proteins in both animal groups (venom and venom + antivenom) indicate that the antivenom did not mitigate venom‐promoted proteolysis.

Figure 7 shows the amino acid sequence of fibrinogen chains indicating the location of peptides identified in the plasma of animals injected with either venom or venom + antivenom, comprising a total of 204 unique peptides mapping across the fibrinogen chains. The identification of such a high diversity of fragments provides direct evidence of extensive in vivo fibrinogen degradation within the plasma environment. Notably, the alpha chain was extensively cleaved at the N‐terminal and central positions, while the beta and gamma chains were cleaved in regions closer to the N‐ and C‐termini.

Among the proteins cleaved in plasma of animals injected with venom, apolipoprotein A1 (Apoa1) and serum amyloid (Saa1) stood out due to the extensive degradation they underwent as can be observed in Figures S12 and S13, showing nearly complete coverage of their amino acid sequences by venom‐promoted proteolysis.

Furthermore, components of the complement system were targets of proteolysis as we identified a total of 57 proteolytic products derived from eight proteins, most of which, however, were from component C3 (31 peptides) and complement factor B (12 peptides) (Table S3).

Peptides identified only in the plasma of animals injected with venom or venom + antivenom were analyzed using the Proteomic Identification of protease Cleavage Sites (PICS) (Schilling et al., 2011). The sequence logos that emerged from the analysis showed similar profiles between the groups treated with antivenom or not (Figure S14). Moreover, the most frequent residues at the P1 position included Arg and Lys, indicating the potential activity of SVSPs or any activated plasma serine protease (Dobó et al., 2022; Pathak et al., 2013; Zelanis et al., 2015). Also, Leu and Ala were represented at P1, which points to the likely activity of a neutrophil protease, including elastase or cathepsin G (Birk et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2018). Leu was similarly found as relatively frequent at P1′, suggesting the direct activity of SVMPs (Paes Leme et al., 2011; Zelanis et al., 2019).

To further characterize the proteolytic signature suggested by the sequence logos, we developed a python tool that takes the peptide sequences, maps the cleavage sites using protein sequences and matches these observed motifs against MEROPS database specificity curated profiles using a scoring algorithm that rewards matches with known protease specificity and assigns each cleavage to the most likely protease family with a confidence score. While the residue analysis suggested the activity of serine and metalloproteases, the aggregate analysis of catalytic types (Figure S15) revealed that cysteine (C) proteases were associated with the highest number of cleavages (2836), followed by unknown proteases (1830) and metallo (M) proteases (1608). Serine (S) proteases, although significant (1130 cleavages), ranked fourth in overall frequency.

The temporal dynamics of proteolysis were further analyzed by stratifying the cleavage counts per experimental group (Figure S15) showing that the dominance of cysteine and metallo protease signatures was consistent across all time points (3, 6 and 24 h). Overall, the administration of antivenom did not abolish proteolytic activity as the venom + antivenom groups exhibited cleavage counts comparable to, or in some cases higher than, the venom‐only groups, particularly at the 3 and 24 h timepoints.

The complexity of the proteolysis events occurring in plasma can be visualized in chord diagrams, which map the catalytic types to specific protein substrates (identified by UniProt accession numbers), demonstrating a dense network of cleavages particularly at the 3 h timepoint (Figure 8). Moreover, the proteolysis analysis highlighted the specific plasma proteins most susceptible to this cleavage (Figure S15). Considering all samples from groups venom and venom + antivenom, the top 15 substrates comprised plasma and tissue proteins, with emphasis on apolipoprotein A‐I (Apoa1) with 1087 cleavages, followed by serum amyloid A‐1 protein (Saa1) (698 cleavages) and fibrinogen alpha chain (Fga) (632 cleavages).

Predicted biological activity of newly generated peptides in plasma

Peptides identified exclusively in the plasma of animals injected with venom, totaling 1964, were analyzed for their predicted putative biological activities according to PepNet (Han et al., 2024), and classified as anti‐inflammatory (1530) or toxic (238) (Table S4). Figure S16 shows only those identified with prediction score >0.85 (69 anti‐inflammatory and 238 toxic peptides).

Among the identified bioactive peptides, those derived from dentin matrix acidic phosphoprotein 1 (SRSQESQSEQDSRSEEDSDSQDSSRSKEESNSTGSASSSEEDIRPK) and plasminogen (CNRVEYLNN) exhibited the highest predicted anti‐inflammatory activity scores of 0.94 and 0.93, respectively. Conversely, peptides derived from albumin (LDTCCKAADKDTCFS) and complement C3 (ITQGENCIKAFIDCCNHIT) showed the highest scores (1.0) for putative toxicity.

DISCUSSION

Bothrops venoms promote systemic effects, including coagulopathy, complement activation, and kidney pathology in human envenomation and in different animal models (Cavalcante et al., 2023; Chaves et al., 2025; Larréché et al., 2021; Leonel et al., 2022; Sachetto et al., 2022). Here we explored the plasma‐related effects elicited by B. jararaca venom in a mouse model, by the intramuscular injection of B. jararaca venom in the thigh, followed by the intravenous injection of anti‐Bothrops antivenom. The venom induced clear alterations in the plasma proteome and peptidome, which were detected at distinct levels of complexity at 3, 6, and 24 h after injection.

Considering the severe local effects of Bothrops envenomation and the stress induced by the disturbances in hemostasis, complement and cardiovascular systems, animals injected with venom alone were expected to show different profiles of plasma protein abundance compared to animals which received only saline or antivenom. Indeed, plasma proteomics showed a clear pattern of imbalance of true plasma proteins, of proteins originated from tissue damage, and emerging innate immune response proteins up to 24 h after the injection of a sub‐lethal dose of venom. These results clearly reflect the remarkable systemic changes occurring in a mammalian organism upon the challenge of the injection of a concentrated cocktail of toxins.

Certainly, the possibility cannot be ruled out that variations in plasma protein abundance are also indirectly affected by (i) the secondary effects of the presence of inflammatory markers in increased concentration, in a feedback process (Cavalcante et al., 2023; Petricevich et al., 2000), and (ii) by the kidney tissue injury induced by the venom that is reflected in altered blood filtration and protein clearance (Gois et al., 2017; Pinho et al., 2008).

The abundance reduction in key proteins involved in blood coagulation, especially factor VIII, factor XIII, fibrinogen, plasma kallikrein, and von Willebrand factor, reflects the systemic coagulopathy observed in human Bothrops envenomation. Nevertheless, the antivenom showed targeted efficacy in restoring factor VIII levels to baseline by 24 h. In contrast, the recovery of fibrinogen levels by 24 h occurred regardless of antivenom treatment, suggesting an endogenous compensatory synthesis that is independent of neutralization. Indeed, previous transcriptomic data provide a mechanistic basis for this observation: Bothrops jararaca envenomation acts as a powerful trigger for the hepatic acute‐phase response, inducing a massive and rapid upregulation of mRNA expression for all three fibrinogen chains (Fga, Fgb, and Fgg), peaking prominently at 3 and 6 h (Sachetto et al., 2020). This synchronized hepatic effort is likely mediated by the simultaneous activation and upregulation of Stat3, a well‐established transcriptional regulator of acute‐phase proteins. Under basal conditions, the steady‐state synthesis of fibrinogen is insufficient to rapidly counter the massive consumption induced by snake venoms. However, this early, venom‐induced genetic drive in the liver ensures that a substantial pool of newly synthesized fibrinogen is actively being produced. Consequently, while antivenom remains vital to break ongoing enzymatic consumption by neutralizing circulating toxins, the actual structural regeneration of fibrinogen levels relies heavily on this pre‐programmed, independent hepatic machinery. Recognizing this autonomous physiological response deepens our understanding of the clinical recovery timeline in snakebite victims and underscores why fibrinogen levels can display an intrinsic tendency to restore over time.

The persistent presence of tissue‐derived proteins (creatine kinase M‐type, titin, and lactate dehydrogenase) in the plasma up to 24 h indicated ongoing or unresolved tissue damage. This was intensified by a robust inflammatory signature, characterized by the elevation of acute‐phase proteins like serum amyloid A1 and A2 and DAMPs (such as histone H4 and various HSPs). The failure of antivenom to attenuate these markers suggests that while the venom toxins might have been neutralized, the secondary inflammatory processes they initiated kept significant kinetic strength (Bickler, 2020; Chaves et al., 2025; Voronov et al., 1999).

Our over‐representation analysis highlighted a critical temporal checkpoint at 24 h, characterized by a progressive transition toward a severely altered metabolic state. This phenotype was heavily dominated by the upregulation of canonical glycolysis and the persistence of sustained acute inflammation, reflecting an intense systemic tissue response and bioenergetic stress triggered by the envenomation (Ryan et al., 2021; Vivas‐Ruiz et al., 2023). Conversely, the administration of the antivenom triggered a distinct functional pivot. Instead of a continuation of metabolic stress, the antivenom‐treated group showed a marked shift toward cellular oxidant detoxification pathways and lipid metabolism, which operated in parallel with systemic acute‐phase response mechanisms. This pronounced pathway divergence indicates that while the antivenom therapy was insufficient to completely normalize the proteomic profile to baseline levels, it effectively redirected the systemic pathology (Cavalcante et al., 2023). Specifically, it intercepted the trajectory of active metabolic exhaustion and channeled cellular resources into pathways dedicated to oxidative stress control, antioxidant defense networks, and cellular recovery (Dong et al., 2020).

B. jararaca venom contains abundant SVMPs and SVSPs that can directly promote protein limited proteolysis or degradation, in vitro and in vivo, generating proteolytic products of different sizes (Henriques et al., 1958; Serrano et al., 2014). In vivo, these newly generated polypeptide chains may potentially participate in the envenomation process by acting synergistically or antagonistically with the activity of the diverse toxins present in the snake venom. This aligns with recent in vivo peptidomic data demonstrating that venom‐induced pathodegradomes contain bioactive host peptides that directly inhibit collagen‐induced platelet aggregation, thereby actively contributing to the impairment of host hemostasis and accelerating local tissue damage (Asega et al., 2023). The overall pattern of protein cleavage in the plasma was complex and pointed to the activity of a mix of proteases likely including those released from circulating leukocytes or leaked from vascular endothelial cells. Our results revealed a clear perturbation of the plasma proteolysis homeostasis in the plasma of mice injected with venom and venom + antivenom.

Fibrinogen is a key protein in the blood coagulation cascade that is targeted by venom proteolytic enzymes (Lu et al., 2005; Santoro et al., 2008; Wellmann et al., 1874). It can either undergo limited proteolysis by some thrombin‐like SVSPs promoting coagulation or degradation by both SVMPs and SVSPs, leading to fibrinogen consumption coagulopathy (Bourke et al., 2022; Oliveira et al., 2010; Paes Leme et al., 2008; Serrano et al., 2000; Swenson & Markland Jr., 2005).

Here, fibrinogen has been extensively degraded in the plasma of animals injected with venom. Thrombin‐like SVSPs are known to be capable of cleaving the human fibrinogen at the R16‐G17 peptide bond in the alpha chain to release fibrinopeptide A (FPA) or the R14‐G15 peptide bond in the beta chain to release fibrinopeptide B (FPB) (Markland, 1998; Serrano & Maroun, 2005). Notably, the cleavage of murine fibrinogen alpha and beta chains at the N‐terminal region resulted in peptides of variable lengths; however, none of them corresponded to fibrinopeptides A and B. Although the presence of these peptides in the plasma of animals injected with venom cannot be ruled out, here, the sequences and extension of the identified degradation products indicate the unfeasibility of the generation of FPA and FPB.

Also prominent was the selected degradation occurring in the central region of the fibrinogen alpha chain, while the rest of the polypeptide chain was left untouched. In the same chain, peptides derived from the cleavage of the peptide bond Lys101–Asp102, usually cleaved by plasmin on fibrin, were identified, although the other peptide bond targeted by plasmin, Phe124–Asp 125, was not detected as hydrolyzed. In the beta chain the degradation of the N‐terminal region in animals injected with venom would interfere with the chemoattractant property of peptide beta1–42 toward neutrophils, and of peptide beta15–42 to inhibit platelet aggregation and fibrinogen binding to activated platelets (Chen et al., 1988; Skogen et al., 1988). Moreover, cleavage occurred in the N‐terminal region of the beta chain likely compromise the interactions between the sequence 15–42 and vascular endothelial cadherin, affecting capillary tube formation and angiogenesis (Bach et al., 1998). In the gamma chain the degradation was located at the region corresponding to amino acids 90–110 and at the C‐terminus and consequently would impair fibrin polymerization and clot stability (Mosesson, 2005).

Overall, the venom‐promoted degradation of fibrinogen has implications in hemostasis and inflammation. Besides the unviability of bona fide clot formation, it would impair the binding of fibrinogen to the platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (integrin alpha2b beta 3) in platelets, which is essential for incorporating platelets into an emerging thrombus (Podolnikova et al., 2014). Likewise, the interaction of fibrinogen with integrin alpha M beta2 on neutrophils might be prevented, with consequent impaired fibrinogen‐mediated pro‐inflammatory response involving the move of leukocytes out of the bloodstream and into damaged tissue to assist in the healing process (Yakovlev et al., 2005).

Besides coagulation‐related proteins, snake venom toxins effectively compromised the complement system by promoting the degradation of specific components, hijacking a defensive immune response and likely worsening the pathology of the envenomation. The simultaneous cleavage of various complement components (C1s2, C3, C5, C8a, and Cfd) further highlights a multi‐attack on plasma proteolytic cascades, potentially impairing the animal innate immune signaling (Leonel et al., 2022; Trevisan‐Silva et al., 2023; Voronov et al., 1999). The injection of antivenom significantly increased the complexity and number of total cleavages across almost all catalytic types (proteases), suggesting that the venom‐antivenom interaction may involve or trigger further proteolytic activity within the complement system.

Among plasma proteins cleaved in animals injected with venom, protease inhibitors, such as alpha‐1‐antitrypsin and alpha‐2‐macroglobulin were targeted. By degrading these inhibitors, which are meant to control protease activity, the venom effectively disarms the organism's natural defenses to facilitate its systemic effects.

Our findings revealed a complex and dynamic proteolytic scenario in the plasma following envenomation, characterized by a mix of direct venom activity and a significant systemic host response. The integration of PICS analysis with our custom MEROPS‐based mapping tool provided a high‐resolution view of these events, highlighting that the systemic damage persisted upon time, despite the administration of antivenom.

Notably, the initial P1 and P1′ residue analysis pointed toward the activity of SVMPs and SVSPs. The presence of Arg and Lys at P1 is a classic hallmark of SVSP activity or the activation of the host coagulation and complement cascade proteases. However, our aggregate analysis indicated the dominance of cysteine proteases, suggesting that envenomation triggers a massive release of endogenous lysosomal proteases, likely from damaged tissues or activated immune cells.

Perhaps the most striking finding was that antivenom administration did not mitigate proteolytic activity. In fact, cleavage counts in the venom + antivenom groups were comparable to, or exceeded, the venom‐only groups at the 3 and 24 h timepoints. The hypotheses to explain these findings could be the following: (i) once the venom initiated endogenous proteolytic cascades (like the coagulation, complement or kallikrein‐kinin systems), neutralizing the venom itself might have been insufficient to stop the process; (ii) the formation of venom‐antivenom immune complexes might lead to further inflammatory activation or different patterns of protease activation; (iii) the antivenom may effectively have neutralized toxins in circulation but failed to reach those already sequestered in tissues or bound to substrates.

There is also the possibility that the proteolytic events happen within a feed‐forward mechanism in which once the venom initiates the first wave of cleavages, the resulting peptide fragments and activated enzymes might act as molecular “kindling” to keep the fire burning, in a retro‐alimentation mode. This would be achieved by the activation of protease zymogens, by the shedding of membrane‐bound enzymes, or by the generation of peptides that would act as danger signals recognized by the innate immune system.

The plasma proteolytic signature remained remarkably consistent across the experimental 24 h window. The high density of cleavages at the 3 h timepoint suggested a “proteolytic storm” that occurred shortly after envenomation. The fact that proteolysis, under the conditions of this study, did not significantly dissipate by 24 h, also in the presence of antivenom, underscores the necessity of longer‐term monitoring and possibly adjunctive therapies (like protease inhibitors) alongside antivenom in a mouse model.

Overall, the peptidomics findings indicate a rearrangement in the functional peptidome in the plasma induced by venom injection and suggest that these newly generated peptides with putative anti‐inflammatory or toxic activity may play a secondary role in the complex response of the murine organism to the venom infliction. We have shown that the injection of HF3, a hemorrhagic SVMP of B. jararaca venom, in the mouse skin promoted proteolysis and the generation of peptides capable of inhibiting collagen‐induced platelet aggregation (Asega et al., 2023). In the present study, the peptides with potential anti‐inflammatory or toxic activity serve as a source of bioactive molecules for future studies on systemic innate immune response.

The application of proteomics and peptidomics approaches provided robust data on the systemic activities of B. jararaca venom and Bothrops antivenom in a murine model. After injection in the thigh muscle, the venom promoted various host reactions in response to its local and systemic effects, inducing the rearrangement of blood plasma proteome and inducing proteolysis of various types of proteins.

In this study, the fact that only one dose of venom and antivenom was used, and the animals were analyzed at three timepoints up to 24 h limits our interpretation of the organismal response to these conditions and precludes the analysis of the consequences of the venom toxins' direct and indirect activities upon longer periods. Nevertheless, the venom effects on a large range of proteins, including true plasma proteins and others originated from tissue/cell leaking into the blood vessels, whether identified as protein abundance variation or cleavage, point out to a dramatic and fast organismal innate immune response to the venom infliction, also including the proteolysis products generated by the direct and indirect activity of venom proteases.

Investigating the altered pathways associated with the systemic response to snake envenomation in the blood plasma and finding protein targets from the proteome and degradome underscore the direct and indirect mechanisms of toxin activity that should be considered in the search for alternative therapies.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we applied proteomic and peptidomic approaches to provide a high‐resolution map of the systemic disruptions triggered by B. jararaca envenomation in the murine plasma. Within a 24‐h window, the venom induces a rapid “proteolytic storm” and a profound innate immune response, characterized by extensive degradation of coagulation and complement cascades components, particularly fibrinogen, alongside severe metabolic stress and ongoing tissue damage.

Our findings highlight clear nuances in antivenom efficacy. While the antivenom successfully restores specific clotting factors like Factor VIII and pivots the systemic pathology from metabolic exhaustion toward cellular recovery pathways, it fails to halt the self‐sustaining, endogenous proteolysis and secondary inflammation initiated by the toxins. Furthermore, the structural regeneration of fibrinogen is driven by an autonomous, venom‐induced hepatic response rather than antivenom neutralization.

Finally, the persistence of host‐driven proteolysis despite antivenom administration underscores the need for prolonged monitoring and suggests that adjunctive therapies, such as protease inhibitors, are vital to explore for improving snakebite outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bothrops jararaca venom and Bothrops antivenom

Lyophilized venom from B. jararaca, composed of different snake adult samples, was kindly provided by the Herpetology Laboratory from Butantan Institute (São Paulo, Brazil). Bothrops antivenom was provided by Butantan Institute (protein concentration 5 mg/mL; batch 210020) and is composed of the heterologous F(ab′)2 fraction of horse immunoglobulins.

Animal experimental procedure

Experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Butantan Institute (CEUAIB no. 9991131219). For plasma proteomics and peptidomics, an in vivo experiment was carried out using 48 male Swiss mice weighing 24–31 g grouped in 12 groups of four animals each and injected with venom and antivenom as previously described (Chaves et al., 2025). Briefly, animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% for induction and 2.5% for maintenance of general anesthesia), then received an injection of 50 μL of venom solution (1.6 mg/kg) or 0.15 M NaCl in the right gastrocnemius muscle. A dose of 1.6 mg/kg was selected to reproduce the characteristic kinetic profile of hemostatic alterations (e.g., hypofibrinogenemia and platelet variations) observed clinically in envenomed patients at the Vital Brazil Hospital (Butantan Institute) (Sachetto et al., 2018).

After 1 h, animals received an injection of 50 μL 0.15 M NaCl or Bothrops antivenom solution (1.6 mg/kg) in the tail vein. To replicate the clinical standard of care, antivenom was delivered intravenously. The dose of 0.32 mL/kg corresponds to 1.6 mg/kg of neutralized venom based on a stated neutralization potency of 1 mL per 5 mg of venom. After 3, 6, and 24 h, blood was collected from each animal from the caudal vena cava using a 1.0‐mL plastic syringe wetted in 3.8% trisodium citrate, immediately dispensed in 1.5‐mL centrifugation tubes containing the same anticoagulant (0.38% trisodium citrate, final concentration), and centrifuged at 1900 × g for 10 min at 4°C prior to plasma collection and storage at −80°C for proteomic and peptidomic analyses.

Plasma processing for proteomic analysis

For proteomic analysis, 30 μL of each plasma sample were diluted in 70 μL of MARS buffer A (Agilent) and filtered in a 0.22 μm filter for removing any particles. The three most abundant proteins (albumin, IgG, and transferrin) from mouse plasma were depleted using the multiple affinity removal column Mouse 3 (MARS‐Mouse3 Agilent) installed in an ÄKTA system containing MARS buffers A and B (Agilent). The non‐bound fraction containing the proteins of interest was collected and submitted to reductive alkylation with 5 mM Tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine and 20 mM 2‐chloroacetamide for 30 min, at room temperature, in the dark, followed by the single‐pot, solid‐phase‐enhanced sample preparation (SP3) protocol (Hughes et al., 2019). Digestion was performed using trypsin (SERVA) at a 1:100 enzyme:substrate ratio for 16 h at 37 °C and 1000 rpm. Tryptic peptides were dried by vacuum centrifugation and stored at −80 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis.

Plasma processing for peptidomic analysis

For peptidomic analysis, 30 μL of 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) were added to 30 μL of each plasma sample for protein precipitation, followed by incubation for 16 h at 4 °C. Afterwards, samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 × g, 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants containing peptides were subjected to reductive alkylation with 5 mM Tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine and 20 mM 2‐chloroacetamide during 30 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by desalting using the StageTips protocol (Rappsilber et al., 2003) and three SDB‐RPS membranes (Empore). The eluates were dried by vacuum centrifugation and stored at −80 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC–MS/MS analysis of plasma samples

For proteomic analysis, tryptic peptides were resuspended in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid solution, sonicated for 10 min, mixed with 400 fmol of indexed Retention Time peptides—iRT (Biognosys) and injected into an EASY‐nLC 1200 system equipped with μPAC C18 trapping column (5 μm pillar diameter, 2.5 μm interpillar distance, 2 cm bed length) and a μPAC C18 nano‐LC analytical column (5 μm pillar diameter, 2.5 μm inter‐pillar distance, 18 μm pillar length, 200 cm bed length, 100–200 Å pore sizes). For elution, a 120 min gradient composed of 8%–10% buffer B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) in buffer A (0.1% formic acid) at 700 nL/min for 2 min, 10–20% buffer B at 350 nL/min for 46 min, 20%–40% buffer B at 350 nL/min for 10 min, 55%–100% buffer B at 350 nL/min for 2 min, then 100% buffer B for 38 min, was applied. The LC system was coupled to a Q‐Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) via NanoSpray Flex Ion Source. The instrument was operated in data independent acquisition (DIA) mode with a 24 m/z isolation window covering 385–1015 m/z. The first experiment was set to Full MS–SIM at resolution of 70,000 FWHM with AGC target set to 3e6 and 50 ms maximum injection time. The second experiment was set to DIA at a resolution of 17,500 FWHM acquiring in mode centroid with AGC target set to 1e6 and 80 ms maximum injection time. The default charge state was set to 2, rejecting ions unassigned or single charge state. Stepped collisional energy was active with normalized collision energy set to 25% and 30%.

For peptidomic analysis, plasma peptides were resuspended in 20 μL of 0.1% formic acid solution, sonicated for 10 min, and loaded onto Evotips for analysis in an Evosep One chromatographic system (Evosep) coupled to a timsToF fleX mass spectrometer (Bruker) operating in positive mode and acquiring DDA data in a scan range of 300–1350 m/z and 0.6–1.6 1/k 0 with a resolution of 45,000 FWHM. The chromatographic length was set to 30 SPD using a 44 min gradient. Mass tolerance for precursor and fragment ions was 0.025 Da at MS1 and MS2.

Data analysis

For proteomic analysis, the raw files were converted to mzML format using MSConvert with peak picking filter. The files were searched using DIA‐NN version 2.1.0 with UniProtKB canonical sequences of Mus musculus and cRAP contaminants. The spectral library was predicted from FASTA file in independent search without mzML files. The FDR was set to 1% and the parameters were as follows: ‐‐met‐excision ‐‐cut K*,R* ‐‐missed‐cleavages 1 ‐‐unimod4 ‐‐var‐mods 2 ‐‐var‐mod UniMod:35,15.994915,M ‐‐var‐mod UniMod:1,42.010565,*n ‐‐proteoforms ‐‐reanalyze ‐‐rt‐profiling. The report.parquet file was loaded in positron R session using the filters Lib.PG.Q.Value <= 0.01, Lib.Q.Value <= 0.01, and PG.Q.Value <= 0.01. The matrix of protein abundance was extracted using diann R package with Genes.MaxLFQ.Unique metrics and filtering by PG.MaxLFQ.Quality >0.75 and Empirical. Quality >0.75. Only proteotypic peptides were used for quantification. Multivariate analyses (PCA and PLS‐DA) were performed using factoextra and mixOmics R packages, respectively. Cosine similarity was calculated using lsa R package. Statistics for differential abundance was conducted using limma R package. Before proceeding to statistics for differential abundance, we used a custom function to perform imputation of missing protein abundances in the matrix using a strategy based on group‐wise minimum values and a defined shift. Our strategy first detects proteins that are completely missing within specific groups, preserving the biological interpretation of proteins not detected. This method identifies the lowest detected value for each protein (globally or group‐wise), applies a shifted imputation using the minimum value plus a −1.5 shift, and adds noise from normal distribution to avoid identical imputed values. Our algorithm tracks which proteins were handled during the pre‐imputation phase, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. After the stepwise imputation, the protein matrix was normalized using the method scale (Median Absolute Deviation) defined in limma R package. The statistics for differential abundance was conducted using limma R package with ordinary least squares methods with trend = TRUE to account for heteroscedasticity. Proteins were considered differentially abundant when the adjusted p‐value <0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.58 (meaning 1.5‐fold magnitude).

To characterize the temporal proteomic changes, an over‐representation analysis (ORA) of biological processes was carried out. Four distinct gene sets were established based on proteins showing differential abundance between the 3 and 24 h timepoints in mice injected with either venom or venom + antivenom. These lists, comprising proteins increased at either 3 or 24 h for each experimental group, were filtered from the limma results using a |log2FC| > 0.58 and an adjusted p‐value of less than 0.05. Functional interpretation was performed via Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for Biological Processes (BP) using clusterProfiler R library. This ORA utilized a hypergeometric test with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) multiple testing correction, considering terms significant at an adjusted p‐value and q‐value of less than 0.05.

For peptidomic analysis, the raw files were converted to mzML format using MSConvert with peak picking filter. Then, data were searched using FragPipe version 23.0 with Mus musculus FASTA file and unspecific search. Carbamidomethylation of Cys was set as fixed modifications. Methionine oxidation (+15.99), N‐terminal acetylation (+42.01), loss of ammonia (−17.02), loss of water (−18.01), and C‐terminal amidation (−0.98) were set as variable modifications. Three maximum variable modifications were accepted per peptide. Peptide length was set to a minimum of 7 and a maximum of 65. False discovery rate was set to 1%, precursor and fragment mass tolerance were set to 20 ppm. Data analysis was performed loading the psm.tsv files and filtering by peptideProphet probability >0.99. Contaminant sequences (cRAP) were removed and raw intensity information for PSMs was recovered from mzML source files using left_join strategy. For networking analysis, Cytoscape was used with stringDB plugin and KEGG terms for pathway annotation. Anti‐inflammatory and toxic bioactivity of peptides were predicted using PepNet algorithm (Han et al., 2024).

For the identification of protease families responsible for observed cleavages in plasma, a novel procedure was developed. MeroPy is a Python tool that analyzes peptidomics data to identify proteases using MEROPS release 12.5 curated specificity data. For each peptide, meroPy extracts P4‐P1 and P1′‐P4′ cleavage motifs and matches them against MEROPS substrate databases using a frequency‐based scoring algorithm. The algorithm calculates position‐specific amino acid frequencies from MEROPS experimental cleavages, applies 2× weighting to critical P1|P1′ positions, penalizes mismatches (−0.2), and normalizes scores to [0,1] confidence intervals. Each cleavage is assigned to the highest‐scoring protease family with quantitative confidence metrics (Appendix S1).
