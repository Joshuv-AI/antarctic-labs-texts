Can a volunteer-based service create an effective, locally appropriate and scalable model of prehospital care for traffic injury victims on the national highways of Bangladesh? Operational outcomes after 10 years of TraumaLink
Authors: Jon Scott Moussally, Arup Chandra Saha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRoad traffic injuries continue to be a leading killer worldwide, but many developing countries lack formal prehospital services. This leaves victims without access to care when it can be most effective in preventing death and disability and contributes to the disproportionate burden of deaths and injuries on the roads in low-income and middle-income countries.MethodsTo address the lack of prehospital care in Bangladesh, we developed TraumaLink. The service uses an emergency hotline number and 24-hour call centre with local first responders trained in basic trauma first aid, provided with essential medical supplies and dispatched through mobile phone text messages to provide free care at the crash scene. Introduction

The crisis of road traffic injuries (RTIs) remains a top priority in global health. In 2021, there were an estimated 1.19 million fatalities on the roads, with low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) accounting for 92% of these preventable deaths.1 Lack of rapid access to prehospital care and transportation to the hospital contributes to the disproportionate burden borne by developing nations.2,7 LMICs face different sets of challenges in delivering prehospital care, and systems designed for higher-income countries do not translate well to countries with fewer available resources.8,11 In 2005, the WHO thus advocated for layperson first responders as a way to provide basic prehospital trauma care in areas that cannot support more costly ambulance-based emergency medical services.12

To address the lack of formal prehospital care in Bangladesh, we created TraumaLink, a community-based first responder system providing free on-scene care for traffic injury victims.13 It uses an emergency hotline number and 24-hour call centre with local first responders who are trained in basic trauma first aid and mass casualty triage, provided with essential medical supplies, and dispatched to crash scenes through mobile phone text message notifications.

We performed a retrospective observational analysis/programme evaluation focusing on key performance indicators from the first decade of TraumaLink’s implementation to assess its utility as an effective, locally appropriate and scalable model for providing free prehospital trauma care in a resource-limited country. We also describe the programme’s more recent evolution into a public–private partnership with the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and WHO to create a government post-crash response programme.

Materials and methods

TraumaLink service model

On 23 November 2014, we launched a service model in which local community members are recruited and trained to act as layperson volunteer first responders (VFRs) treating traffic injury victims at the crash scene, free of charge. Volunteers are trained in crash scene management, basic trauma first aid and mass casualty triage and are provided with essential medical supplies. Paid, full-time employees of TraumaLink (Area Coordinators) supervise VFRs, provide local support and quality control, and act as a link between the central office in the capital Dhaka and local officials, volunteers and community members. In its first decade, the programme expanded from a 14 km pilot to a total of 249 km on 3 major national highways, focusing on more rural areas with less access to trauma services.

The call centre, available through a dedicated emergency hotline number (16465), uses a custom-designed graphic user interface (GUI) to rapidly connect injured victims with prehospital resources. The GUI features interactive and searchable maps of the catchment areas densely populated with local landmarks. Before beginning operations in a new service area, community members help TraumaLink staff identify prominent local landmarks along the highway and the sometimes multiple names that are commonly in use. Staff collect exact GPS coordinates for each site on a smartphone and enter them, along with all the local names, into the GUI. This allows for easy identification of the crash location even when the caller is not familiar with the community.

Before launching the service on a new stretch of highway, we organise multiple events in the community to raise awareness about RTIs and recruit potential volunteers. We also work with local religious and civic leaders, as well as the family members of those interested in volunteering, to answer questions and address cultural and religious barriers that might discourage women from participating. Those expressing an interest in becoming VFRs are interviewed and vetted by TraumaLink Area Coordinators. Candidates are selected based on factors including where they live and/or work, personal experiences with RTIs, prior efforts to treat or transport injured victims, motivations for wanting to serve and feedback from other community members. VFRs must be at least 18 years old, but there are no minimum literacy or educational requirements.

VFR candidates receive one full day of training on basic trauma first aid and mass casualty triage from a Bangladeshi physician-trainer employed by TraumaLink, and other staff members assist those with limited literacy to ensure full participation. We designed the training curriculum to teach simple lifesaving skills that people with any level of education and no prior medical background can learn and perform. Classes are conducted locally and combine didactics with a strong emphasis on hands-on training, with class size limited to 15–20 students to ensure individual attention for all participants. To reinforce their learning, we also developed a professional-quality training video in Bengali that is provided to trainees in a high-definition version and a condensed format that can be downloaded and then viewed without an internet connection on a mobile phone. No students have failed the training course, but Area Coordinators provide additional instruction and practice time for those who need it. Graduates receive a printed version of the training curriculum, the training video, a personal photo ID badge and a reflective fluorescent vest featuring the TraumaLink logo.

For each expansion of the service, we hold a local launching ceremony featuring distinguished guests, community leaders and the new VFRs. At these events, all VFRs publicly take an oath to provide care to anyone in need and to refuse any financial or other type of compensation for their work. However, TraumaLink fully reimburses VFRs and Area Coordinators for any local travel expenses incurred while responding to crashes and transporting patients. To stay active in the programme, volunteers are required to undergo a half-day of retraining every 6–12 months.

Operators receiving a call first collect information on where the crash occurred and how many patients have been injured. As soon as this information is entered into the GUI, the software uses an embedded algorithm to dispatch the closest volunteers through text messages to their mobile phones. Because this process is automated, it leaves the operator free to notify police, fire services and receiving hospitals as needed.

Service areas on the national highways are divided into operational zones 1–2 km in length, with 4 VFRs per kilometre of highway. All VFRs are assigned to a specific zone based on where they live and/or work and are dispatched to crashes on a rotating basis. For larger mass casualty events, volunteers are also recruited from adjacent zones. TraumaLink provides and maintains locally sourced first aid supplies and stores them in locations that can be accessed 24 hours a day. At the request of the Highway Police, who have jurisdiction over the national highways, hotline operators routinely notify them of all crashes that might require their presence. The police provide scene management, protection and legitimacy for the volunteers, and sometimes transportation of victims to the hospital. Fire services are also activated as necessary to assist with rescue operations and ambulance transport.

Volunteers triage patients using guidelines developed for the programme (online supplemental appendix 1) and provide treatment at the crash scene. Patients who are not sent home and require further care at a hospital are rapidly transported using local networks that include fire services, police, vehicles for hire and bystanders. All patients travelling to the hospital are accompanied there, most commonly by volunteers, fire services or family members. We default to sending patients to public hospitals to avoid potential delays in care resulting from private hospitals refusing them admission and to protect VFRs from having to make potentially financially consequential decisions for incapacitated patients.

For quality monitoring and evaluation of the programme, operators routinely follow up with the volunteers within a few hours of the completion of all calls to collect any missing information, and complete data were available for all crash responses.

Evolution of TraumaLink into a government post-crash response programme

Because of the numerous factors contributing to unsafe roads in Bangladesh, there are multiple government and non-governmental organisations across a variety of sectors focused on different aspects of road safety. These organisations often work independently, making it more difficult to create comprehensive and enduring programmes to address unsafe roads. Since the earliest days of TraumaLink’s development, one goal has been to bring together these different organisations to encourage communication, education and collaboration across different sectors. Our efforts have succeeded in moving these various stakeholders toward a broader recognition and understanding of issues affecting road safety, raised awareness of our work in prehospital care, and culminated in what is now a broad national coalition with a unified and well-defined focus on improving post-crash response in Bangladesh.14

As part of the growing movement to decrease road traffic deaths in Bangladesh, in 2019, the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, United Nations, WHO and World Bank began organising a series of multistakeholder meetings on post-crash response involving senior officials from government agencies and non-governmental organisations involved in road safety. In addition to the organising agencies, participants also included the Bangladesh Roads and Transportation Authority, the Roads and Highways Department, Police, and Fire Services and nongovernmental organisations including the Centre for the Rehabilitation of the Paralysed and International Red Crescent Society, among others. Among the recommendations stemming from these meetings were calls to expand TraumaLink’s model of post-crash services nationwide.

In January 2020, a memorandum of understanding was signed between TraumaLink and the Non-communicable Disease Control programme of the Bangladesh Directorate General of Health Services, the implementing arm of the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare (NCDC-DGHS), to facilitate post-crash care for RTI victims in Bangladesh. In early 2022, through a grant from the United Nations Road Safety Fund (UNRSF), a formal partnership was created between the WHO country office, the NCDC-DGHS and TraumaLink to begin establishing a national post-crash response programme in Bangladesh. The programme closely adheres to the TraumaLink service model and uses its call centre, emergency hotline number and staff. It is supervised by the NCDC-DGHS, with the WHO providing financial and technical support, and TraumaLink acting as the implementing partner. We first refined and updated the operational protocols and training manual as a collaborative effort with a technical committee organised by the WHO and composed of experts from more than 20 government and non-governmental organisations.

In the next phase of the project, with additional funding from the UNRSF, in February 2023, we launched a pilot programme as a proof of concept for the creation of a public–private partnership as a means of achieving a broader expansion of post-crash services in Bangladesh. The pilot was established on a 10 km section of the Dhaka-Chattogram Highway in the Gazaria upazila (subdistrict), an area where TraumaLink’s services had been in place since 2018. The Dhaka-Chattogram Highway is the busiest and most dangerous road in Bangladesh, and Gazaria is 30 km away from Dhaka—close enough for officials from the NCDC-DGHS and WHO to be able to travel to the project area without significant difficulty.

All pre-existing volunteers were retrained, and the same first aid training was also newly provided to local police, fire services and public hospital staff. All participants and community leaders were educated on the structure and goals of the NCDC-DGHS post-crash response programme. For the community-at-large, there were no changes or disruptions in the service offering as primary oversight was transitioned between TraumaLink and the NCDC-DGHS. The remainder of our service areas continued to be run independently by TraumaLink.

The pilot has run continuously since its launch, with ongoing funding from the WHO supporting refresher training and the purchase of supplies to restock first aid kits. All calls to the hotline number have been answered with an emergency response and all key performance indicators have been consistent with TraumaLink’s prior performance. The pilot has had strong and ongoing support from members of the community and all other participating stakeholders. The NCDC-DGHS post-crash response programme and the heroic work of the volunteers were recognised in the UNRSF’s 2023 #moments2live4 campaign.

Based in part on the successful implementation of this pilot, in July 2024, a national post-crash response programme was included in the new 5-year operational plan, which defines priorities for the national budget. The service continues to be run by the NCDC-DGHS, with TraumaLink acting as the implementing partner and the WHO providing financial and technical support. In October 2024, TraumaLink signed an exclusive renewable 2-year contract with the WHO to begin the next phase in the rollout of this programme, extending post-crash services east along the Dhaka-Chattogram Highway.

Data analysis

During the first aid training sessions for the volunteers, we emphasise the importance of data collection for the ongoing success of the programme and our expectations that they will be active participants in this process. We obtain their consent to use these data for research, and to formalise this understanding, this is included in the consent form they sign at the launching ceremony when they publicly take their oath of service.

We chose our data analyses to reflect the reliability and efficiency of operations as proxies for improved clinical outcomes, since baseline data on RTI victim outcomes in our service areas was not available for comparison, a randomised controlled trial would be neither feasible nor ethical, and obtaining detailed follow-up information on all treated patients was beyond the scope of our available resources. However, the benefits of prehospital trauma care are well established.215,18

Complete data were available for all crash responses and information was stored in a Google Sheets document that was available only to the two study authors, JM (TraumaLink’s primary founder) and ACS (executive director), and two full-time TraumaLink call centre operators responsible for collecting data for quality monitoring and evaluation. Information for the current study was assembled and checked for accuracy and completeness by local Bangladeshi staff. Results were then translated into English and the deidentified dataset was analysed to determine the percentage of calls receiving a response, the intervals between the crash and arrival of a first responder, the percentage of patients for whom transportation to the hospital could be found, the intervals between the crash and arrival at the hospital, and the alignment of prehospital injury severity assessments with patient dispositions. We also examined volunteer retention as a measure of community engagement and sustainability and analysed patient demographics to better understand the broader societal impacts of the programme. Because there were no dedicated prehospital first aid services in place before the intervention, improvements in the provision of care were attributed to TraumaLink’s implementation. We used the Standards for QUality Improvement Reporting Excellence reporting guidelines in the creation of this article.19

Patient and public involvement

The public was actively involved from the programme’s inception and participated in the initial needs assessment and stakeholder discussions, development and refinement of the operational model and choice of service areas. Based on stakeholder feedback, we focused on sustainably delivering rapid and reliable care at the crash scene, which guided priorities for data collection and analysis. In each geographic expansion of the programme, community leaders help to suggest and vet potential volunteers and remain actively involved throughout implementation and after the service launch. We regularly provide compiled data, including maps of crash locations, to the Highway Police and local policy-makers.

Results

Emergency response

During the 10-year study period from 23 November 2014 to 22 November 2024, TraumaLink’s services ran continuously and we provided free care to 5966 patients involved in 3330 crashes. Every call to the hotline received an emergency response, and volunteer arrival times to the incident scene were 5 min or less from the time of the crash in 2991 (90%) incidents (figure 1). Patient injury assessments

Responding volunteers and call centre operators make basic prehospital injury severity assessments for each patient to help prioritise them for first aid treatment and transport to the hospital (online supplemental appendix 1). Among our patients, 2349 (39%) were assessed with light injuries, 2199 (37%) with moderate injuries and 1418 (24%) with severe injuries. Overall, 2502 (42%) patients were treated at the scene and sent home, 2050 (34%) were treated at the scene and then sent to the hospital for further care and 1414 (24%) patients were immediately taken to the hospital without receiving treatment at the scene. Most of these 1414 were victims with severe injuries who were stabilised en route; however, some were victims who had less serious injuries but were involved in mass casualty incidents where providing treatment to every patient at the scene was not feasible. Of the 3464 patients transported to the hospital, 1851 were discharged home after treatment and 1613 were admitted or transferred to a higher level trauma centre for further care.

Injury severity assessments correlated closely with patient dispositions, reflecting the accuracy of on-scene triage decisions (figure 3). Of the 2502 patients treated at the scene and sent home, 2224 (89%) were determined to have light injuries. Among 1851 patients transported to the hospital but discharged home after treatment, 1726 (93%) had moderate injuries, and severely injured patients accounted for 1418 (88%) of the 1613 victims admitted to the hospital. No patients assessed with light injuries were admitted to the hospital, and no one determined to have severe injuries was discharged home.

Patient transport

First responders found transportation for all 3464 victims requiring transfer to the hospital. The most, 772 (22%), were taken by CNGs—motorised 3-wheel vehicles for hire that are widely used for local travel, whereas 533 (15%) were taken in a fire service ambulance and 368 (11%) travelled there by rickshaw. Volunteer engagement

For the first 10 years of operations, a total of 1055 volunteers (988 men and 67 women) passed the training course and were registered as VFRs. There were no instances of community members harassing volunteers or interfering with their work, and 988 VFRs (930 men and 58 women) were still actively working at the end of the study period. Of the 67 people leaving the service, 41 moved out of the area for a new job and/or marriage, 14 died, 10 resigned (all during the 18-month pilot) because they had not been called for any incidents, and 2 became too busy to continue.

Discussion

After its first decade of operations, TraumaLink’s key performance indicators suggest that a community-based VFR programme can be an effective, locally appropriate and scalable model for providing prehospital care for RTI victims in the resource-limited setting of Bangladesh. The consistently high level of community and stakeholder engagement since the programme’s inception has been crucial to our ability to weather a number of challenges, including the COVID-19 pandemic, with no interruptions in service. It is also important to note that because we involved so many different stakeholders, bringing all their different experiences, perspectives and expertise to the development of the service model, and worked deliberately and diligently to address the realities we faced, rather than planning for ideal conditions, the model was largely ‘battle-tested’ before we went live. Even 10 years later, very few modifications have been needed for either the operational model or the GUI that we developed in parallel with it.

After more than 3300 crash responses, our VFRs continue to maintain a 100% response rate, with rapid arrival times and, for all victims requiring it, they have been able to provide transportation to the hospital using locally available vehicles. The dense coverage of the volunteers, their close proximity to the highways and their dominant role in initiating emergency responses have enabled these rapid response times. The VFRs also become well known in their communities and are frequently called first by witnesses to a crash. They then notify operators on their way to the scene.

These post-crash services are particularly important because most victims are young and otherwise healthy men—often family breadwinners—and the majority of RTI fatalities in Bangladesh are related to uncontrolled haemorrhage prior to arrival at the hospital. Thus, the rapid provision of simple first aid treatment is often life-saving, allowing these patients to return to healthy and productive lives. The programme decreases the burden on the country’s healthcare system and social services and also serves to reduce healthcare disparities by providing rapid access to first aid in more rural areas with fewer available resources for the management of traumatic injuries.

TraumaLink has had very low volunteer attrition, only 6.4% over the first decade, and the experience and expertise of these first responders is evident in the close alignment of prehospital injury severity assessments and patient dispositions that reflects the accuracy of their triage decisions. We have been fortunate in having consistently more community members offering to work as volunteers than we have had positions available, and we have placed a strong emphasis on carefully selecting and vetting our first responders. We also make considerable efforts to maintain close communication with, and to recognise the work of, the volunteers through social media, phone calls and in-person visits to our service areas. Our first responders are generally deeply rooted and stable members of the community, such as small business owners and teachers. However, we have also found that a number of the volunteers take leaves of absence to travel abroad for work but return to their duties after returning home.

As TraumaLink evolved, it became increasingly clear that a broader scale-up of the service would be difficult or impossible without support from larger and better-resourced organisations. We, therefore, pivoted from trying to achieve national expansion through our own funding generation to a strategy of focusing on demonstrating the efficacy, sustainability and scalability of the model in such a way that it could be adopted and expanded by larger donors with more resources. By consistently working to create collaboration and communication between different stakeholders invested in road safety, we have been successful in weaving together a network of relationships within Bangladesh to achieve a broad and cohesive coalition of government and non-governmental organisations now working toward scaling up post-crash services to a national level. This has led to the adoption of the TraumaLink model in the establishment of an official government post-crash response programme under the supervision of the NCDC-DGHS, with TraumaLink acting as the implementing partner and the WHO providing financial and technical support. Creating this programme under the umbrella of the NCDC-DGHS will allow the government to also engage with other major donors in a way that maintains consistency in the service model and facilitates integration with police, fire services and the national emergency response programme.

While there have been a number of other Tier-1 layperson first responder programmes developed by nongovernmental organisations around the globe,1720,27 we are not aware of any other programmes that have subsequently been adopted by the government for a national post-crash response programme. This service, therefore, offers not only a model for other LMICs seeking to better address the public health crisis of RTIs, but also insights into the vital importance of the collaborative process in creating broad support for the development and sustenance of such national programmes.

Limitations

In this retrospective analysis of operational data, we lacked detailed clinical information on individual patients and did not have baseline data or a control group available for comparison. These limitations make it difficult to accurately quantify the programme’s clinical and cost-effectiveness, and more rigorous evaluation of both is needed as we move forward.

Most of our patients are taken first to the nearest available facility, usually subdistrict level public hospitals that lack the resources to treat seriously injured patients, and there can be significant delays in subsequently getting more badly injured patients to higher level trauma centres. This has proven difficult for us to address with our available resources, but we hope that by more formally integrating our programme with government services, we can advance our capacity to provide rapid and affordable ambulance transfer, thereby improving outcomes.

We have been encouraged by the participation of women in the programme, although we continue to struggle with achieving a more balanced gender representation in our pool of VFRs. However, some of these women are among our longest serving and most active volunteers, and our women VFRs have consistently been treated with trust, respect and appreciation by their communities. Of the nine who left the service during our first ten years, six moved away for work and/or marriage and three died. Importantly, none have left because of harassment, lack of family or community support or dissatisfaction with the work.

Conclusions

In its first decade of operations, TraumaLink’s key performance indicators supported the programme’s utility as an effective, locally appropriate and scalable model for providing free post-crash care in the resource-limited setting of Bangladesh. The service model has now been adopted as an official government programme and is in the early stages of a national expansion through a public–private partnership with the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare and the WHO, providing an example for other LMICs facing similar challenges with traffic injury victims.
