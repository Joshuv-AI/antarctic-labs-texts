Probing the responses of thirty-six ajwain (Trachyspermum Ammi L.) ecotypes by phytochemical, morphological, and yield characteristics under drought stress
Authors: Ghaffar Kiani, Azadeh Karimi Afshar, Seyyed Jaber Hosseini, Mansour Ghorbanpour, Masoumeh Khalvandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ajwain is a significant medicinal plant known for its antimicrobial, antispasmodic, and digestive properties. Its high resistance to drought allows it to be cultivated in areas with limited water resources. Interestingly, drought stress can increase the production of its active compounds, making the identification of resistant ecotypes crucial for developing sustainable cultivation practices that meet pharmaceutical and industrial demands. This study explored the impact of drought stress on 36 ajwain ecotypes regarding their growth, yield, and phytochemical properties. The findings revealed that under normal conditions, the E-NAJ (80 cm), F-SEP (79 cm), and Y-YAZ (79 cm) ecotypes exhibited the tallest plant heights, while K-RAF (1708 kg ha− 1) and S-B-ZAB (1601 kg ha− 1) demonstrated the highest seed yields (SY). Under drought stress, F-SEP had the best growth at 68 cm, while S-B-ZAB produced the highest yield at 1322 kg ha− 1. In terms of essential oil compounds, the study found that thymol content increased by an average of 28% under stress conditions. The role of monoterpenoid phenols in creating stress tolerance in superior ecotypes such as S-B-ZAB was very prominent. The K-JOO (4.40%) and R-KH-CHE (4.35%) ecotypes yielded the highest essential oil. Cluster analysis classified the ecotypes into four distinct groups, with the third and fourth groups achieving the highest SY (800–816 kg ha− 1) and essential oil production (34–37 kg ha− 1) under stress conditions. An analysis of the synthesis pathway indicated that α-terpinene positively influenced thymol production, while β-phellandrene had a negative effect. Overall, the S-B-ZAB, S-B-ZAH, and K-MAH ecotypes were identified as drought-tolerant varieties with stable both SY and EOY. These findings can inform ajwain breeding programs aimed at cultivation in arid regions. Moreover, the increased essential oil content (EOC) under drought stress presents an opportunity for the targeted production of valuable medicinal metabolites.

Introduction

Medicinal plants are considered one of the most valuable natural resources for humanity, playing a fundamental role in the treatment of diseases as well as the prevention and promotion of health [1]. These plants contain active compounds such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and terpenoids, which exhibit anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and antioxidant properties [2]. Nowadays, due to the side effects associated with chemical drugs, the demand for herbal medicines is increasing [1]. Additionally, medicinal plants are widely used in the food, cosmetic, and health industries [3–5]. The cultivation of these plants also creates job opportunities and promotes sustainable agricultural development. Thus, the preservation and study of these natural resources are vital for the future of medicine and the global economy [6].

Ajwain (Trachyspermum ammi L.), a medicinal plant from the Apiaceae family, is an annual, drought-resistant species that grows as a shrub in arid and semi-arid regions of various countries [7]. Ajwain possesses remarkable therapeutic properties, with its essential oil primarily containing thymol, p-cymene, and γ-terpinene. These compounds exhibit antimicrobial, antispasmodic, digestive, and anti-flatulent properties [8]. It is commonly used in traditional medicine to treat indigestion, abdominal pain, and respiratory infections, and it is also prevalent in the food and pharmaceutical industries [8, 9]. Its drought resistance makes it an ideal candidate for cultivation in water-scarce areas [7, 8]. Drought stress is one of the most significant factors limiting plant growth and performance, especially in arid and semi-arid regions [10]. By reducing a plant’s access to water, this stress disrupts essential physiological processes such as photosynthesis, cell division, and nutrient uptake [11]. In response to drought stress, plants activate various defense mechanisms, including stomatal closure, reduction of leaf area, and increased production of protective compounds such as proline and antioxidants [10, 11]. Drought stress typically results in decreased plant height, branch number, and leaf size [12]. At the molecular level, drought conditions lead to increased production of heat shock proteins, which help prevent cell damage [13]. Additionally, drought affects the production of secondary metabolites such as phenols, terpenoids, and alkaloids, which can increase the levels of active pharmaceutical ingredients in medicinal plants [14]. However, severe drought stress ultimately leads to reduced SY, impaired pollination, and cell death [15]. Therefore, it is essential to identify and cultivate drought-tolerant varieties and implement optimal irrigation management practices to maintain productivity under water deficit conditions [10].

The SY is a complex quantitative trait influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Direct selection for improvement under drought stress is challenging due to issues such as low heritability, epistatic effects, and genotype × environment interactions [16]. Therefore, it is necessary to employ indirect selection methods that focus on traits affecting yield, including yield components like the number of umbels per plant (NOU), thousand-seed weight (TSW), and number of seeds per umbel (NOS). Research indicates that in plants of the Apiaceae family, such as cumin [17] and anise [18], the traits of the NOU and the NOS significantly impact SY. Drought stress not only affects plant growth and yield but can also lead to changes in secondary compounds, such as essential oils and medicinal metabolites [14]. Generally, plants respond to environmental stresses by increasing the production of defense compounds, including phenols and terpenoids [1]. Studies have shown that drought stress enhances the content of ajwain essential oil, particularly compounds like thymol [19]. However, the response to drought stress may vary among different ecotypes, making it essential to investigate phytochemical changes under stress conditions to identify high-quality ecotypes [10]. Given the rising demand for medicinal plants and the scarcity of water resources, it is crucial to identify drought-tolerant ecotypes and examine the effects of drought stress on the growth and yield characteristics of ajwain. Ajwain is widely distributed in Iran and exhibits significant ecotypic diversity, suggesting that some ecotypes may be better adapted to drought conditions.

However, there has been limited research on the impact of drought stress on the growth, performance, and phytochemical traits of ajwain. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the responses of different ajwain ecotypes to drought stress and to identify resistant ecotypes with desirable yield and essential oil quality. The findings from this research could help identify superior ajwain ecotypes that tolerate drought and possess high essential oil quality, forming a foundation for breeding programs aimed at this valuable medicinal plant in arid and semi-arid regions. Additionally, with the increasing demand for medicinal plants and decreasing water availability, the cultivation of drought-resistant ajwain ecotypes can contribute to the sustainable development of agriculture in water-scarce areas.

Materials and methods

Site characteristics and experimental procedure

A total of 36 ecotypes of ajwain were identified (by Prof. M. Ghorbanpour, Arak University, Iran), and the voucher specimen codes were deposited at Sari Agricultural and Natural Resources University, Sari, Iran. The samples were collected from their natural habitats in nine different provinces of Iran using valid botanical keys (Table 1). To investigate the level of drought tolerance, experimental ajwain ecotypes were cultivated in split plots in a randomized complete block design including two factors, different irrigation regime as the main factor and 36 ecotypes as the secondary factor with four replications in the field of the Agricultural and Natural Resources Research Center of Kerman Province during two crop years 2018 and 2019. The study site is situated at 30°5′ N, 57°28′ E with an elevation of 1,969 m above sea level.

Irrigation of the field was done similarly in both normal and drought stress irrigation conditions up to the 5% flowering stage. From the 5% flowering stage onwards, irrigation was done after 60 mm of evaporation from the Class A pan in normal conditions and after 120 mm of evaporation from the Class A pan in drought stress conditions, so that the irrigation period continued until harvest every 7 days for normal conditions and every 14 days for drought stress conditions.

Land preparation operations were carried out in both years, including plowing, discing, and leveling before planting. Planting was carried out in both years in January in plots 1.5 m long and 20 cm between rows (4 rows, with the first and fourth rows considered as margins) and in the form of ridge cultivation. After emergence, the plants were thinned out, keeping a distance of 5 cm between rows. Agricultural operations, including irrigation and weed control, were carried out during the seedling stage.

Trait measurement

For phenotypic studies, 15 plants were randomly selected from each ecotype in each treatment and the traits of NOU (total fertile umbels per plant), NOS (number of seeds counted per plant, considering the number of umbels per plant), and plant height (from the ground level and near the crown to under the terminal umbel using meters) were measured. The measurements of the traits of TSW, biological yield (BY) (obtained from weighing the aerial part of the plants after drying at room temperature), SY (obtained from weighing the seeds after drying at room temperature), and EOY were carried out accurately. The essential oil was extracted from 20 g of dried and ground seeds using a Clevenger apparatus for 4 h and then dehydrated with sodium sulfate. The EOY was calculated by multiplying the EOC by the SY.

Phytochemical evaluation

The analysis and identification of essential oil compounds were carried out by GC and GC/MS. First, the essential oils were injected into the GC device, and then the percentage of each compound was calculated according to its area under the curve in the chromatogram spectrum by normalizing the area of ​​the curve without calculating the correction factor. After finding the appropriate column thermal program for complete separation of the essential oil compounds and determining the percentage and retention time of each compound, the essential oils were injected into a gas chromatography device connected to a GC/MS spectrometer, and the mass spectrum of the compounds was determined.

The GC/MS instrument was an Agilent model with a capillary column HB-5MS with a length of 30 m, an internal diameter of 0.25 mm, and an internal layer thickness of 0.25 mm. The column temperature program was set as follows: initial oven temperature of 50 °C, a hold at this temperature of 5 min, final oven temperature of 260 °C, a hold at this temperature of 3 min, and a thermal gradient of 4 °C/min. The injection chamber temperature was 290 °C, and helium gas was used as the carrier gas with a flow rate of 0.8 mm/min. The thermal program of two similar instruments was selected. The detector electrical capacity was eVIE 70. The spectra were identified using their inhibition index and by injecting normal hydrocarbons (C7-C25) under the same conditions and comparing them with mass spectra suggested by the GC/MS instrument library.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the experiment were first evaluated for normality based on the Kolmogorov-Smirnov method. Then, combine analysis was performed using SAS 9.1 software. Python was used to draw mean comparison heatmaps. Also, cluster analysis based on UPGMA and Euclidean distance method was used using Python (v3.11.4) to group different ecotypes. Biplot resulting from principal component analysis was drawn based on the first and second components. Heatmap was performed to group ecotypes considering phytochemical compounds. Path analysis was performed to find the role of phytochemical compounds in the synthesis of the most dominant compound. In this method, the regression coefficients of the paths were determined and the comparison of the behavior of phytochemical compounds was interpreted based on these coefficients.

Results

Plant height

In the present experiment, various growth characteristics of ajowan ecotypes were investigated under drought stress. The results of variance analysis showed that the plant height was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype (Table 2). The average plant height in the second year was 65.70 cm, which was an 8.91% increase compared to the average height in the first year (60.32 cm). The results showed that in non-stress conditions, E-NAJ (80 cm), F-SEP (79 cm), and Y-YAZ (79 cm) ecotypes had higher plant height compared to other ecotypes, but these three ecotypes did not have statistically significant differences with a large number of ecotypes (Fig. 1). In contrast, the lowest plant height in non-stress conditions was observed in K-JOO (64 cm) ecotype. The highest plant height in drought stress conditions was observed in F-SEP (68 cm), K-MAH (60 cm), K-RAF (60 cm), Y-MEI (60 cm), E-NAJ (58 cm) and M-ARA (58 cm) ecotypes; In contrast, the lowest plant height was observed in K-RAJ (41 cm), K-RAV (46 cm), and S-KH-GHA (45 cm) ecotypes (Fig. 1).

Number of umbels per plant (NOU)

The results of variance analysis showed that the NOU was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype and year × stress (Table 2). The interaction of drought stress on the year factor showed that the highest NOU by 35.45 was related to the no drought stress treatment in the second year, and the lowest value of this trait by 17.84 was observed in the drought stress treatment in the first year. The results of the present experiment showed that the NOU value in the second year was 30.27, which was an increase of 28.24% compared to the first year (23.6).

Based on the results obtained under non-stress conditions, it was observed that the highest NOU were related to R-KH-MAS (50), S-KH-BAS (50), R-KH-ZAV (47), R-KH-CHE (40), S-KH-GHA (44), Y-ARD (40) and Y-SHA (44) ecotypes (Fig. 1). In contrast, M-KHO (18), R-KH-MOH (22) and F-MAR (22) ecotypes had the lowest NOU in the non-stress treatment. In the drought stress treatment, the lowest NOU were observed in K-JOO (14), Y-MEI (14) and R-KH-MOH (13) ecotypes, and the highest value of this trait were related to S-KH-BAS (39), R-KH-MAS (23), S-KH-GHA (35), F-SEP (29) and Y-SHA (31) ecotypes (Fig. 1).

Among the ecotypes, K-JOO (75%), K-BAR (84%), K-MAH (87%), Y-MEI (102%), Y-SAD (90%), N-KH-GAR (115%), M-ARA (115%), R-KH-ZAV (116%), and R-KH-MAS (115%) showed the highest reduction in the NOU compared to the control treatment. While the lowest reduction in the NOU compared to the control treatment were related to the F-SEP (2%), S-KH-FER (25%), and M-KHO (11%) ecotypes (Fig. 1).

Thousand seed weight (TSW)

The results showed that the TSW was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype and year × stress (Table 2). Based on the results obtained from the interaction of drought stress with the year factor, it was observed that the non-drought stress treatment in the second year had the highest TSW (1.67 g), while the drought stress treatment in the first year (0.85 g) had the lowest value of this trait. The average TSW trait in the second year was 1.29 g, which was an increase of 17.44% compared to the first year (1.1 g).

The results of the comparison between ecotypes in non-stress conditions showed that S-B-ZAH (2.04 g) ecotype had the highest TSW and did not differ significantly from many ecotypes (Fig. 1). In contrast, the lowest TSW in the non-stress treatment was related to K-KOO (0.8 g) ecotype. In the drought stress treatment, S-B-ZAH (1.16 g) ecotype had the highest TSW, and R-KH-GON (0.35 g), K-KOO (0.5 g), and M-ARA (0.55 g) ecotypes had the lowest TSW (Fig. 1).

Drought stress caused a severe decrease in TSW in K-BAR (108%), K-RAJ (115%), Y-SHA (139%), Y-ARD (149%), F-SAR (95%), N-KH-GAR (96%), S-KH-SAR (107%), S-KH-BAS (118%), M-ARA (209%), R-KH-ZAV (192%), and R-KH-GON (385%) ecotypes. On the other hand, K-MAH (2%), E-SEM (6%), S-KH-BIR (8%), and R-KH-MOH (10%) ecotypes had a very small decrease in TSW when faced with drought stress (Fig. 1).

Number of seeds per umbel (NOS)

Analysis of variance showed that the NOS was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype (Table 2). The results showed that the average of NOS trait in the second year was 70.58, which was an increase of 10.43% compared to the first year (63.92). The highest NOS under non-stress conditions were observed in M-SHA (106), M-ARA (103), R-KH-CHE (98), R-KH-MAS (101), E-NAJ (92) and K-RAF (96) ecotypes; while the lowest value of this trait was related to R-KH-MOH (54) ecotype (Fig. 2). Under stress conditions, the highest NOS was observed in R-KH-MAS (90), R-KH-CHE (82), S-KH-BAS (82) and S-KH-GHA (78) ecotypes. In contrast, under stress conditions the lowest NOS were observed in R-KH-MOH (45), K-JOO (45) and K-RAV (44) ecotypes (Fig. 2). The K-RAV (61%), K-RAF (55%), F-SAR (62%), F-SEP (53%), N-KH-BOJ (52%), S-KH-BIR (54%), M-SHA (55%), and M-ARA (60%) ecotypes had the highest reduction in the NOS compared to the non-stress treatment, and the lowest reduction in the NOS were observed in S-B-ZAH (2%), S-KH-GHA (3%), and S-KH-BAS (6%) ecotypes (Fig. 2).

Biological yield (BY)

The results of variance analysis showed that the BY was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype (Table 2). Based on the results, it was observed that the BY trait rate in the second year was 2910.21 kg ha− 1, which was higher compared to the first year (2845.12 kg ha− 1). Under non-stress conditions, S-B-ZAB (3884 kg ha− 1) and N-KH-BOJ (3702 kg ha− 1) ecotypes had the highest BY, and the lowest value of this trait was observed in E-FAL (2440 kg ha− 1) and R-KH-MAS (2488 kg ha− 1) ecotypes (Fig. 2). On the other hand, under stress conditions, S-B-ZAB (3434 kg ha− 1) ecotype had the highest BY, and the lowest value of this trait were observed in E-FAL (2003 kg ha− 1) and F-SEP (2104 kg ha− 1) ecotypes (Fig. 2). Based on the results, it was observed that K-RAF (52%), Y-SAD (64%), and R-KH-GON (56%) ecotypes had the highest reduction in BY compared to the non-stress treatment, and the lowest reduction in this trait was related to K-BAR (2%), Y-YAZ (8%), Y-ARD (4%), F-MAR (9%), S-KH-FER (6%), S-KH-GHA (7%), M-KHO (7%), and R-KH-MOH (7%) ecotypes (Fig. 2).

Seed yield (SY)

The SY was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype (Table 2). The results of the mean comparison showed that the value of the SY trait in the second year was 957.60 kg ha− 1, which was an increase of 3.82% compared to the first year (922.40 kg ha− 1) (Table 2). The results of the present experiment under non-stress conditions showed that K-RAF (1708 kg ha− 1) and S-B-ZAB (1601 kg ha− 1) ecotypes had the highest SY, and the lowest value of this trait was observed in K-RAV (655 kg ha− 1) ecotype (Fig. 2). In addition, under salinity stress conditions, S-B-ZAB (1322 kg ha− 1) ecotype had the highest SY; in contrast, the lowest value of this trait was observed in N-KH-BOJ (360 kg ha− 1), S-KH-BIR (385 kg ha− 1), and E-FAL (401 kg ha− 1) ecotypes (Fig. 2). The highest reduction in SY compared to the non-stress treatment was observed in K-RAF (130%), Y-SAD (97%), E-FAL (76%), N-KH-BOJ (318%), S-KH-GHA (168%), S-KH-BAS (155%), S-KH-BIR (220%), and M-SHA (134%) ecotypes, and the lowest reduction for this trait was observed in K-JOO (1%) and K-MAH (11%) ecotypes (Fig. 2).

Essential oil content (EOC)

The results showed that the essential oil content was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype and year × stress (Table 2). The results of the interaction of drought stress on the year factor showed that the drought stress treatment in the second year had the highest EOC with 4.07%, and the lowest value of this trait was observed in the non-drought stress treatment in the first year with 2.89%. The results showed that in the second year, the EOC trait value was 3.63%, which was an increase of 19.45% compared to the first year (3.04%). The amount of EOC increased under the influence of drought stress. The results in non-stress conditions showed that R-KH-CHE (3.69%) and S-KH-FER (3.50%) ecotypes had the highest amount of EOC and the lowest amount of this trait was observed in Y-MEI (2.35%), F-MAR (2.40%) and N-KH-BOJ (2.40%) ecotypes (Fig. 3). On the other hand, under stress conditions, K-JOO (4.40%) and R-KH-CHE (4.35%) ecotypes had the highest EOC; in contrast, N-KH-BOJ (3.05%) and E-FAL (3.10%) ecotypes had the lowest EOC. Based on the results of the comparison between drought stress and non-stress levels, it was observed that the highest increase in EOC was related to K-JOO (30%), Y-MEI (42%) and F-MAR (33%) ecotypes, and the lowest increase in this trait was observed in Y-SAD (8%), F-SEP (9%), S-KH-FER (6%) and S-KH-GHA (1%) ecotypes (Fig. 3).

Essential oil yield (EOY)

The results of variance analysis showed that the EOY was significantly affected by the year, stress, ecotype and the interaction of stress × ecotype (Table 2). A comparison of the averages between the two years showed that in the second year the EOY value was 34.21 kg ha− 1, which was an increase of 10.77% compared to the first year (30.88 kg ha− 1) (Table 2). Under non-drought stress conditions, the highest EOY was observed in S-B-ZAH (44 kg ha− 1), S-KH-FER (43 kg ha− 1), E-NAJ (43 kg ha− 1) and R-KH-ZAV (42 kg ha− 1) ecotypes, and the lowest value of this trait was observed in Y-ARD (24 kg ha− 1), S-KH-GHA (25 kg ha− 1) and K-RAV (26 kg ha− 1) ecotypes (Fig. 3). On the other hand, under drought stress conditions, the highest EOY was observed in the S-B-ZAH (34 kg ha− 1), S-KH-FER (34 kg ha− 1), R-KH-ZAV (34 kg ha− 1), and E-NAJ (37 kg ha− 1) ecotypes, and the lowest value of this trait was observed in the Y-ARD (20 kg ha− 1), M-SHA (20 kg ha− 1), and M-ARA (22 kg ha− 1) ecotypes (Fig. 3). Comparison between non-stress and drought stress levels showed that the highest amount of EOY reduction was observed in K-KOO (45%), Y-SAD (46%), E-FAL (43%), and S-KH-BIR (66%) ecotypes, and the lowest amount of yield reduction was observed in F-SAR (9%) and S-KH-GHA (10%) ecotypes (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Drought stress is a major factor that limits plant growth and significantly affects the morphological and yield characteristics of ajowan plants. This stress can lead to reduced growth, disruptions in physiological processes, and decreases in yield and its components [10]. In contrast, medicinal plants like ajowan may exhibit changes in secondary metabolites, including essential oil compounds, in response to drought stress, which can influence their quality and therapeutic properties [12]. It seems that in the second year of cultivation of different ecotypes of ajowan, environmental and climatic conditions were significantly more favorable, which led to a significant improvement in the studied traits in ecotypes. This increase in morphological and physiological traits was due to the simultaneous effect of favorable environmental factors, including temperature and appropriate humidity during the growing season.

The results of our experiment indicated that drought stress reduced plant height in most ajowan ecotypes. This reduction is attributed to decreased photosynthesis and assimilate production (such as sugars), which occurs because drought stress limits water absorption and causes stomatal closure [20]. Consequently, there is a lack of energy and materials necessary for cell growth and division [21]. Additionally, drought stress hinders the production of growth hormones, including auxin and gibberellin, while increasing inhibitory hormones like abscisic acid [22]. This hormonal imbalance restricts cell division and elongation, ultimately leading to a decrease in plant height. Bayati et al. investigated the effects of drought stress on black cumin (Nigella sativa) and observed a similar decline in plant height, which aligns with our findings [23]. In our experiment, we also noted a reduction in both the NOU and the NOS under drought stress. This can be explained by the decreased energy available for flowering and umbel formation due to limited photosynthesis, nutrient uptake, and disrupted plant metabolism [24, 25]. Drought stress may also lead to flower drop, infertility of pollen, and reduced cell division, all of which contribute to the decline in the NOU and NOS [12, 15, 26].

Numerous studies on fennel (Foeniculum vulgare Mill.) by Peymaei et al. [1] and cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) by Archangi et al. [27] have shown that drought stress leads to a decrease in both the NOU and the NOS. These findings align with the results of our experiment. In our study, we observed that drought stress caused a reduction in the TSW across most ecotypes. This decrease can be attributed to several mechanisms. Firstly, the limitation of photosynthesis due to stomatal closure and a reduction in leaf area disrupts the production of carbohydrates and proteins essential for seed filling [28–30]. Additionally, water deficiency affects the activity of key enzymes involved in synthesizing storage materials such as starch and oil, resulting in smaller, lighter seeds [31–33]. Furthermore, drought stress shortens the seed filling period and accelerates ripening, thereby reducing the accumulation of assimilates [21]. Increased photorespiration and loss of energy reserves, along with disruptions in the absorption and transport of vital nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus, also contribute significantly to this issue [20]. Ultimately, these conditions lead to a notable decrease in TSW. The study by Irik et al. on black cumin supports our findings, showing that drought stress leads to a reduction in TSW as well [34]. Our experiment revealed that drought stress diminished both BY and SY in most ecotypes. Since SY is influenced by various components, it appears that the reductions in the NOS, NOU, TSW, and plant height (each affected by drought) contributed to the overall decrease in SY and BY. Similar observations were made by Archangi et al. who found that drought stress reduced SY and BY in cumin [27]. Likewise, Peymaei et al. demonstrated that drought stress negatively affected SY and BY in fennel, which is consistent with our results [12].

In the present experiment, we observed that under drought stress, most ecotypes showed an increase in EOC but a decrease in EOY. This suggests that the plants responded to drought stress by producing secondary metabolites, such as essential oil compounds, as a defense mechanism [35]. However, the decline in EOY can be attributed to limited plant growth and reduced biomass production under drought conditions. As a result, although EOC increased, the significant reduction in SY and EOY ultimately lowered the total EOC [36]. In a study of Ocimum species, it was also found that drought stress decreased EOY, which aligns with our findings [37]. Additionally, Peymaei et al. reported increased EOC in fennel under drought stress [12]. Our experiment’s principal component analysis biplot revealed that under non-drought conditions, plant height did not strongly correlate with SY or BY traits. In contrast, under drought stress, plant height emerged as a significant factor influencing both SY and BY. It appears that under non-stress conditions, adequate water and nutrient resources allow the plant to focus its energy on producing lateral branches and umbels, making factors like the NOU and NOS more critical for yield. Conversely, under drought stress, plant height becomes a crucial indicator. Taller plants typically develop deeper root systems, enabling them to access water from lower soil layers [38–40]. Furthermore, greater height reflects the plant’s enhanced ability to allocate limited resources to growth. The positive correlation between plant height and SY under drought conditions indicates that taller plants are more efficient in utilizing constrained resources [41, 42]. These finding highlights that under stress conditions, drought tolerance criteria such as plant height become particularly important for determining yield, whereas in normal conditions, other growth factors play a more dominant role.

The results of the present experiment demonstrated that the synthesis of phytochemical compounds was influenced by drought stress, leading to some compounds increasing while others decreased. Drought stress affects phytochemicals in medicinal plants differently, depending on the ecological roles and biosynthetic pathways of each compound [43]. Certain phytochemicals tend to increase under drought stress, as plants produce these secondary metabolites as part of their defense mechanisms [44]. Drought induces oxidative stress, elevating the production of reactive oxygen species. In response, plants synthesize non-enzymatic antioxidants such as phenolic compounds and terpenes to neutralize these free radicals [45]. Additionally, drought stress enhances the biosynthesis of these compounds by stimulating hormonal signaling pathways and activating key enzymes, such as terpene synthases and phenylalanine ammonia lyase [46, 47]. Conversely, some phytochemicals may be reduced because, under drought conditions, the plant directs its limited energy and carbon resources toward producing compounds that directly contribute to stress tolerance [48]. Moreover, certain metabolic pathways are inhibited during drought stress to conserve resources for synthesizing more vital compounds [46].

Consequently, the response of phytochemicals to drought represents a complex balance between the needs for defense and metabolic constraints, resulting in some compounds increasing while others decrease. This phenomenon is evident in medicinal plants and highlights their intelligent adaptation to environmental conditions [14, 49, 50]. It seems that the achievement of high SY in S-B-ZAB under drought stress is related to the amount of monoterpenoid phenols compounds in the essential oil of this ecotype (67.61%), because ecotypes with higher thymol and carvacrol (phenols) content are often superior, both in terms of stress adaptation and in terms of the medicinal quality of the essential oil. Mirniyam et al. [19] showed in a study on plant populations that the compounds α-thujene, α-terpinene, β-thujone, carvacrol, β-pinene increased in most populations, did not change significantly in others, and decreased in some, which was consistent with the results of our experiment. These researchers also stated that the compound thymol also behaved differently under the influence of stress in different populations, so that it increased in some and had no significant effect in other populations, which was consistent with the results of our experiment [19].

A key aspect of the present study was evaluating different ecotypes of ajowan. The results indicated that these ecotypes exhibited varying responses to stress regarding growth, yield characteristics, and phytochemical compounds; specifically, these traits increased in some ecotypes and decreased in others. Notably, some ajowan ecotypes did not show significant differences in certain traits under stress conditions. The differences in how ajowan ecotypes respond to drought stress can largely be attributed to their inherent genetic diversity. Each ecotype possesses a unique genetic potential for regulating metabolic pathways and expressing genes related to stress tolerance [51, 52]. Some ecotypes were able to maintain or even enhance their growth and phytochemical characteristics by efficiently activating defense pathways, such as the production of antioxidants and secondary metabolites. In contrast, sensitive ecotypes lacked the necessary genetic mechanisms for stress adaptation [1, 53, 54]. Indifferent ecotypes may have already developed physiological adaptations that provide resistance to various stresses. These differences underscore the importance of genetic diversity in responding to environmental challenges [16, 55].

Conclusion

This study thoroughly examined the impact of drought stress on the growth, yield, and phytochemical characteristics of 36 ajwain ecotypes. The results indicated that drought stress significantly reduced plant height, NOU, TSW, and both SY and EOY. However, drought stress also led to an increase in EOC across various ecotypes. Under non-stress conditions, the K-RAF and S-B-ZAB ecotypes demonstrated superior SY. In terms of EOC, the R-KH-CHE and S-KH-FER ecotypes had the highest levels, while S-B-ZAH exhibited the highest EOY. When subjected to drought stress, S-B-ZAB emerged as the most drought-resistant ecotype in terms of SY, showing the least reduction in yield under stress conditions. The role of monoterpenoid phenols in creating stress tolerance in superior ecotypes such as S-B-ZAB was very prominent. The K-JOO and R-KH-CHE ecotypes experienced the greatest increase in EOC, while S-B-ZAH and E-NAJ maintained the highest EOY during stress. These findings suggest that certain ecotypes, such as S-B-ZAB and S-B-ZAH, remain stable regarding SY and EOY under both stressed and non-stressed conditions. Additionally, multivariate analyses (PCA and cluster analysis) confirmed that drought-tolerant ecotypes—such as K-MAH, F-SEP, and S-B-ZAB—are associated with desirable traits such as high yield, high EOC, and minimal reductions in height and yield components. Furthermore, an analysis of the synthesis pathway for phytochemical compounds revealed that drought stress alters the metabolic pathways for thymol (the major compound in the essential oil) production. In this pathway, some compounds like α-terpinene have a positive effect, while others, such as β-pinene, have a negative impact. Overall, the ecotypes S-B-ZAB (noted for SY and BY), S-B-ZAH (for EOY), and K-MAH (for drought tolerance) are recommended as superior choices for cultivation in arid regions and breeding programs. This study emphasizes the significance of selecting drought-tolerant ecotypes to enhance the sustainability of ajwain production under challenging climatic conditions.
