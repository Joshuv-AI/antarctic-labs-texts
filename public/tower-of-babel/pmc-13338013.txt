The application of deep learning in tactical analysis of football matches
Authors: Wuyu Huang, Sihang Wang, Pei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a deep learning-based framework for tactical analysis in football, focusing on modeling structured tracking data rather than attempting full end-to-end perception. We introduce two modular components: Tactiformer, a transformer-based encoder that captures spatiotemporal player dynamics with role- and zone-aware attention, and StratGaze, a segment-level reasoning module that identifies recurring tactical motifs using contrastive sequence modeling. Tactiformer learns representations of coordinated multi-agent behavior at the player level, while StratGaze operates at the segment level to extract interpretable tactical abstractions over time. Our framework is fully data-driven, modular, and compatible with standard football analytics pipelines. We evaluate the approach on benchmark datasets including SoccerNet and PASS, demonstrating consistent improvements in event prediction accuracy, trajectory forecasting, and motif clustering quality over existing baselines. In addition to quantitative results, we provide qualitative visualizations of model attention maps and inferred tactical timelines, supporting the interpretability and usability of the system in real-world scenarios. Rather than solving the entire pipeline from detection to tactical inference, this work targets the reasoning layer and shows how structured spatiotemporal representations, combined with inductive priors, can support scalable, interpretable, and data-efficient football analytics tools suitable for analysts and coaching staff.Keywords: Causal reasoning, Context-aware modeling, Deep learning, Football analytics, Interpretability, Multi-agent modeling, Spatiotemporal representation, Tactical motif discovery, Transformer architecturesSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The tactical analysis of football matches has become crucial in modern sports science, allowing teams to refine strategies and enhance player performance1. Traditional analysis methods, relying on expert observation and handcrafted features, often suffer from limited scalability and precision2. With the rapid advancement in data acquisition technologies, the availability of match data , including player trajectories, ball movement, and event logs , has grown significantly3. This explosion of data calls for intelligent, automated, and robust analytical tools capable of uncovering complex patterns within spatiotemporal data.

Early computational approaches were based on logic-driven models and spatial rule encoding4,5, offering structured but inflexible representations. As these methods struggled to adapt to the dynamic nature of football, statistical techniques emerged6,7 to model player distances, possession sequences, and spatial distributions. While more adaptive, these still relied heavily on manual feature engineering, limiting generalizability8. Recent advances introduced neural networks , such as CNNs, LSTMs, and Transformers9, to extract multi-scale dynamics from raw data10. These methods significantly improved formation recognition, event prediction, and tactical transition analysis11.

While existing approaches have made progress in football analytics, several specific limitations remain unaddressed. Rule-based and statistical models, although interpretable, struggle to adapt to dynamic and multi-agent environments due to their rigid structure and reliance on pre-defined templates. Early deep learning methods often treat players as isolated agents, neglecting inter-player dependencies and tactical coherence. Moreover, many models lack the capability to incorporate hierarchical reasoning across multiple levels of abstraction , from individual motion to team strategies. They also fail to incorporate role-specific priors and contextual game semantics, limiting their interpretability and transferability across teams and formations. Our work addresses these gaps by introducing a unified framework that integrates spatial interaction modeling, temporal dynamics, and tactical semantics in an end-to-end and interpretable manner.

To address limitations such as rigidity, dependency on handcrafted features, and poor temporal-spatial integration, we propose a novel deep learning framework for football tactical analysis. Our method leverages a convolutional backbone to extract spatial features and employs Transformer-based temporal encoding to capture long-range dependencies and tactical transitions. The model is end-to-end trainable, requires minimal manual design, and leverages multimodal input data , including tracking, event logs, and video , to achieve a holistic understanding of match dynamics.

Despite these advantages, practical deployment of deep learning models in football analytics still faces several challenges. Real-time application requires models to be both computationally efficient and latency-aware, especially when integrated into live coaching or broadcast systems. The availability and quality of structured tracking data in lower-tier leagues or youth competitions may be limited, making it difficult to directly apply data-hungry models trained on elite-level datasets. These challenges motivate the need for flexible, lightweight architectures and semi-supervised extensions, which we consider in our future work and experimental design.

Related work

Player tracking and pose estimation

Recent advances in deep learning have significantly improved the granularity and accuracy of player tracking and pose estimation in football matches12. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and Transformer-based architectures have been applied to extract spatial and temporal features from multi-camera broadcast or panoramic video data13. Deep learning models like OpenPose, HRNet, and PoseNet have demonstrated effectiveness in capturing player skeletons and body postures in real-time, which are crucial for analyzing player orientation, speed, and biomechanical behaviors during different phases of the game14. These models are frequently trained on domain-adapted datasets to mitigate challenges such as occlusion, motion blur, and varying lighting conditions15. Beyond individual player analysis, multi-person tracking techniques have been enhanced by deep learning frameworks like DeepSORT and FairMOT, which integrate detection and reification into unified pipelines16. These methods have been instrumental in tracking all 22 players simultaneously and preserving identity across frames. Temporal coherence and inter-player relationships are further modeled using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) or Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (ST-GCNs), which provide insights into team formations and transitions17.

Event detection and action recognition

Automated detection of significant football events, such as passes, shots, tackles, and fouls, has become a focal point in tactical analysis18. Deep learning models have outperformed traditional rule-based and machine learning approaches by learning complex spatio-temporal patterns directly from raw data. End-to-end neural architectures like 3D CNNs, Temporal Segment Networks (TSNs), and Transformer-based models are employed to recognize temporal sequences corresponding to high-level match events19. Recent general-purpose video understanding models, such as SlowFast, TimesFormer, VideoSwin, ViViT, and MMAction2, have achieved strong performance in generic human action recognition benchmarks. However, their applicability to football-specific analytics remains limited for several reasons. SlowFast is optimized for short-window motion recognition and lacks mechanisms for modeling structured team interactions or long-term dependencies. TimesFormer and ViViT introduce temporal attention via factorized transformers, but operate on raw frame patches without incorporating structured spatial roles or team semantics. VideoSwin improves efficiency and local modeling, yet remains constrained to relatively shallow temporal reasoning and does not support cross-agent interaction modeling. MMAction2, while modular and comprehensive, primarily serves as an evaluation toolkit rather than a domain-specialized system. These models are effective in recognizing individual actions but do not explicitly account for inter-player dependencies, formation dynamics, or role-zone constraints that are essential in team sports like football. Moreover, their input representations are often image-based, limiting interpretability and scalability when applied to structured tracking data. By contrast, our framework is tailored to football-specific requirements by combining structured spatiotemporal encodings with role- and zone-aware attention, enabling interpretable tactical motif discovery across long sequences. Supervised learning approaches require annotated datasets such as SoccerNet and PASSTA, which provide frame-level or segment-level labels20. These models are trained to distinguish subtle differences between similar-looking actions, such as a short pass versus a through ball, by considering contextual information such as ball trajectory, player positions, and interaction zones21. Recent work incorporates multimodal data, combining video, audio commentary, and positional data, to improve event classification accuracy. Moreover, unsupervised and self-supervised learning approaches are being explored to discover novel event patterns without manual annotations22. These methods include contrastive learning and autoencoder-based anomaly detection, which can reveal team-specific strategies or identify deviations from typical playstyles23. The integration of action recognition into tactical frameworks allows for fine-grained assessments of team performance, decision-making patterns, and game momentum shifts24.

Strategy modeling and game intelligence

Modeling team strategies and understanding game intelligence involves higher-level reasoning that combines spatial-temporal data with domain-specific tactical knowledge25. Deep learning plays a central role in uncovering latent structures in team behavior through representation learning26. Techniques such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Long Short-Term Memory networks (LSTMs), and Graph Attention Networks (GATs) are utilized to model play sequences and predict future movements or tactical decisions27. Predictive models trained on positional tracking data aim to anticipate player movements, evaluate passing options, and simulate potential outcomes of different tactical choices28. Reinforcement learning and imitation learning frameworks are also being adopted to emulate expert-level decision-making and optimize team tactics under varying game scenarios. These models often integrate rules of the game, domain constraints, and historical performance metrics to refine predictions29. Explainability and interpretability remain crucial challenges, as tactical decisions must be understood and trusted by coaches and analysts. Therefore, interpretable deep models are gaining traction, providing visual heatmaps, decision boundaries, and counterfactual explanations to elucidate why a certain tactical recommendation was made30. This direction is not only pushing the frontier of AI in sports but also bridging the gap between data science and on-field coaching strategies.

Recent research in basketball analytics has also demonstrated the effectiveness of data mining techniques in uncovering tactical and performance-related patterns. Iatropoulos et al. proposed a quarter-level analysis framework for identifying temporal player performance patterns in NBA games31, while Sarlis et al. explored clutch-time behavior using interpretable models and game dynamics features32. In a related line of work, Sarlis et al. employed association rule mining to reveal latent patterns concerning recovery and economic factors33. Papageorgiou et al. further extended this direction by forecasting player performance and optimizing line-ups in daily fantasy basketball through advanced statistical modeling34. These studies highlight the value of interpretable, data-driven approaches in sports analytics, which inspire our efforts to develop tactically-aware deep learning models for football.

Method

Overview

To provide a clear understanding of the methodological novelty introduced in this work, we summarize the essential contributions of our architecture before diving into implementation details. Compared to existing deep learning frameworks for football analytics, our Tactiformer model introduces a dual-path spatiotemporal attention mechanism that disentangles inter-agent spatial interactions from intra-agent temporal continuity. This design improves interpretability and alignment with tactical structures in football. Furthermore, the Role and Zone Encoding module incorporates domain-specific priors, enabling role-aware and context-sensitive modeling, which is largely absent in prior end-to-end models. On a higher abstraction level, our StratGaze module offers a new perspective by combining attention-based segment reasoning with prototype-based motif discovery and symbolic reasoning. Unlike earlier approaches that treat temporal segments independently, StratGaze leverages inter-segment attention and contrastive clustering to extract interpretable tactical motifs. It also supports strategy forecasting and motif transition modeling through a lightweight symbolic reasoning layer , a component rarely seen in prior football-related work. These design choices collectively define the novel aspects of our proposed framework.

In this section, we present an overview of our proposed framework for understanding and modeling football matches through computational means. Our method is designed to address the inherent spatiotemporal complexity and semantic richness of football games, which combine structured strategies, dynamic multi-agent interactions, and continuous motion. The modeling of football matches presents several unique challenges compared to conventional video understanding tasks. The environment is dynamic and multi-agent, involving 22 players, referees, and the ball, all moving continuously in a large spatial field Second, the semantics of the game are deeply structured, involving formations, roles, transitions, and events such as passes, shots, tackles, and goals. The visual data is often noisy and incomplete, due to camera motion, occlusions, and variations in broadcast quality. Finally, understanding football requires both local perception and global reasoning.

To address these challenges, our approach begins by establishing a formal spatiotemporal representation of the match. In Sect. Preliminaries, we introduce a symbolic framework for modeling player trajectories, ball dynamics, and field geometry. This includes definitions for spatial zones, temporal windows, and semantic actions, all of which are fundamental to the remainder of the pipeline. We also introduce the concept of an interaction graph, where each node represents an agent (player or ball) and edges encode physical proximity, visual context, or tactical coordination. Building upon this foundation, Sect. Tactiformer introducesTactiformer, our core model that leverages a spatial-temporal transformer architecture tailored for football. The model is designed to capture hierarchical representations across three levels: low-level player and ball movements; mid-level group behaviors such as coordinated pressing or off-ball runs; and high-level team strategies and formations. To encode these levels, Tactiformer utilizes a multi-branch attention mechanism that selectively aggregates temporal features at different granularities, with position-aware embeddings based on pitch zones and game time context. Furthermore, Tactiformer is augmented with a set of structured encodings based on player roles and field positions. For example, we inject role-based inductive bias by conditioning attention weights on predefined tactical roles. This enables the model to generalize across teams and matches with differing formations but similar role semantics. We introduce a field-aware alignment module that projects the player positions into a canonical coordinate frame, mitigating the effects of camera distortions and inconsistent viewpoints. Section StratGaze elaborates on StratGaze, our strategy-aware knowledge extraction and inference scheme. While Tactiformer focuses on learning from the visual-temporal data, StratGaze operates at a higher abstraction level. It is designed to extract interpretable tactical motifs, such as build-up patterns, pressing schemes, and counter-attacks. These motifs are encoded as structured attention maps and symbolic action chains, allowing the system to support downstream tasks such as match summarization, tactical comparison between teams, and even counterfactual analysis.

To support real-time deployment in practical sports analytics scenarios , such as live match broadcasting, coaching decision tools, or on-field tactical assistance , our architecture can be adapted using several strategies. First, the model supports sliding-window inference, allowing it to operate on a stream of incoming data frames with low latency. During inference, we apply short overlapping windows (2-3 seconds) to ensure continuity while avoiding significant delays. Second, the architecture is designed with modularity and scalability in mind. Key components such as the spatial encoder and Transformer blocks can be pruned or quantized using model compression techniques to meet runtime constraints without substantial performance degradation. Our earlier analysis (Sect. 3.5) also showed that a Lite variant of the model achieves 86.55% F1 score with 50% higher inference throughput, making it suitable for deployment on edge devices or within cloud-streaming pipelines. Furthermore, the Role and Zone Encoding serves as a valuable inductive bias that can help maintain robust predictions even under low-resolution or incomplete tracking, which is common in live feeds. For implementation, we envision a pipeline where player tracking is performed using fast pose estimation models (OpenPose or BlazePose), followed by batched input to our model, enabling frame-by-frame tactical feedback with minimal latency (under 100ms). These considerations make the proposed system adaptable to various deployment scenarios, from professional team analysis environments to real-time streaming-based fan engagement platforms.

Our architectural choices particularly the dual-path attention mechanism and the Role & Zone Encoding , are motivated by the unique structure and demands of football match analysis. The dual-path attention separates spatial and temporal reasoning, which is critical in football due to the asymmetric importance of player-to-player interactions versus player self-dynamics. While standard self-attention mechanisms entangle all tokens uniformly, our design allows the model to explicitly encode intra-agent consistency over time and inter-agent influence within each frame. This improves interpretability and aligns with how coaches and analysts think about tactics , distinguishing movement patterns from team formations. The Role & Zone Encoding module introduces semantic structure into the model, guiding attention based on player roles and field locations. We chose additive positional embeddings (instead of one-hot roles or raw coordinates) to maintain differentiability and allow soft alignment across formations. This encoding injects domain priors (left backs operate in outer zones; center-backs coordinate more with goalkeepers) which improve generalization across different tactical schemes. Our ablation results show that removing either component results in consistent performance degradation across datasets, validating their complementary contribution. Compared to simpler alternatives , such as flat attention or no role conditioning , our approach offers both superior accuracy and better tactical interpretability, as further demonstrated in our visualizations and motif discovery module.

Tactiformer

Tactiformer is designed to encode multi-level semantics ranging from raw trajectories to tactical coordination, leveraging spatiotemporal attention, role-aware embedding, and contextual aggregation(As shown in Fig. 1).

The system takes raw player tracking data as input, applies preprocessing to generate structured trajectory sequences, and passes them through two hierarchical modules: Tactiformer and StratGaze. Tactiformer captures fine-grained spatiotemporal dynamics and role-specific behaviors, while StratGaze performs high-level tactical reasoning over temporal segments. The final outputs include event predictions, trajectory forecasting, and tactical motif inference.

Through this mechanism, the model captures rich patterns such as asymmetric fullback expansion, zonal traps, or synchronized positional shifts, and maps them into compact motif identities that can be reused across matches, generalized to unseen teams, or retrieved for tactical comparison in analytic workflows.

Experiments

Task definition

This study formulates tactical pattern recognition in soccer as a supervised multi-object tracking (MOT) problem. The input X is a sequence of synchronized soccer video frames captured from a fixed full-pitch broadcast camera or a calibrated multi-view system, where each frame contains all visible players, referees, and the ball. From each frame, the model extracts object-level state variables including spatial coordinates and motion cues derived from consecutive frames. The output Y consists of frame-wise bounding boxes for every player and the ball together with temporally consistent identity trajectories across the entire video sequence. The learning paradigm is supervised detection and tracking, where the model jointly optimizes localization accuracy and identity association through end-to-end training. The objective is to learn a mapping that produces precise spatial localization and stable identity preservation under occlusion, fast motion, and inter-player interactions.

The supervision signals are obtained from manually annotated benchmark datasets designed for soccer multi-object tracking. SoccerNet-Tracking35 and SoccerTrack v236 provide frame-level bounding box annotations and persistent identity tracklets for players, referees, and the ball across complete match segments. These annotations are generated through expert-guided manual labeling and refined by quality control procedures to ensure temporal consistency. The training objective integrates detection supervision based on bounding box regression and classification losses, identity supervision derived from ground-truth track IDs, temporal smoothness regularization to stabilize embeddings across adjacent frames, and contrastive alignment constraints to enhance inter-instance discriminability. All supervisory signals are explicitly defined by human-annotated ground truth and dataset-provided identity trajectories, ensuring fully supervised optimization of spatial accuracy and identity consistency.

Dataset and data preprocessing

Datasets

We conduct experiments on two publicly available soccer multi-object tracking benchmarks. The first dataset is SoccerNet-Tracking, released as an open benchmark for soccer MOT. It contains 200 short video clips of 30 seconds each together with 45-minute full half-match recordings. All frames are annotated with bounding boxes and persistent identity tracklets for players, referees, and the ball. The annotations are manually produced by trained annotators following a unified protocol that enforces consistent identity assignment across frames and precise spatial localization for each visible object. We remove frames with severe motion blur or incomplete pitch coverage and discard tracklets shorter than five consecutive frames to ensure stable temporal supervision. The dataset is divided using an official split into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1, where match segments are temporally separated to avoid overlap across splits.

The second dataset is SoccerTrack v2, a publicly released full-pitch multi-view soccer dataset containing 10 complete matches recorded in 4K panoramic resolution. It provides frame-level 2D coordinates, bounding boxes, persistent track IDs, player roles, team labels, and ball annotations across entire matches. Annotations are manually verified with identity consistency checks and role alignment rules to guarantee structural correctness. We filter inactive frames without ball visibility and remove identity trajectories shorter than ten frames to reduce noise in temporal modeling. Data are partitioned by match into training, validation, and test subsets with a ratio of 7:1:2, ensuring that no match appears in more than one subset and preventing temporal leakage across splits.

Data preprocessing

All video sequences from SoccerNet-Tracking and SoccerTrack v2 are first cleaned to ensure annotation consistency and temporal validity. Duplicate frames caused by broadcast replay segments are removed through hash-based frame comparison. Frames with missing bounding boxes or broken identity indices are discarded. Bounding boxes with zero area, extreme aspect ratios outside the range [0.1, 10], or coordinates exceeding image boundaries are treated as annotation anomalies and removed. Identity trajectories shorter than five consecutive frames in SoccerNet-Tracking and ten frames in SoccerTrack v2 are filtered out to eliminate unstable supervision. After cleaning, spatial coordinates are normalized by image width and height to map bounding boxes into the [0, 1] range. Player positions are further aligned to a unified pitch coordinate system through homography transformation for multi-view consistency. Temporal alignment is enforced by resampling all videos to 25 frames per second. Identity alignment is applied to guarantee globally unique track IDs within each match and consistent role indices for structural encoding.

For sequence modeling, we adopt a sliding window strategy with a fixed length of 32 frames and a stride of 16 frames to construct training samples. Sequences shorter than 32 frames after filtering are discarded. To improve robustness against appearance variation, we apply data augmentation including horizontal flipping with pitch-side label remapping, random scaling within [0.9, 1.1], small-angle rotation within , and color jittering in brightness and contrast. No external feature extractor is employed; all spatial, temporal, role, and regional features are learned end-to-end from normalized bounding box coordinates and motion-derived velocity representations.

Evaluation metrics and baseline

Metrics definition

The primary evaluation follows standard multi-object tracking criteria. We report Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA), which aggregates false positives, false negatives, and identity switches into a unified accuracy measure. Identity F1 score (IDF1) is used to quantify identity consistency by computing the harmonic mean of identity precision and identity recall over matched trajectories. Multiple Object Tracking Precision (MOTP) evaluates localization quality by measuring the average alignment between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes. In addition, we report detection Recall and Precision to reflect object coverage and false alarm rates. As auxiliary efficiency metrics, we measure the total number of learnable parameters (Params) and the computational complexity in floating point operations (FLOPs). These efficiency indicators quantify model capacity and computational cost, enabling a balanced assessment of tracking accuracy and resource consumption.

Evaluation protocol

All evaluations are conducted under a frame-level multi-object tracking protocol. A predicted bounding box is considered a true positive if its Intersection over Union with the corresponding ground-truth box exceeds 0.5. Identity matching is performed using one-to-one Hungarian assignment based on IoU scores within each frame, followed by temporal association for track-level evaluation. The evaluation is conducted in an offline setting, where the full video sequence is available during inference and temporal attention modules utilize complete history within each sliding window. Dataset splits follow predefined temporal partitions to prevent overlap between training, validation, and test matches. The evaluation strictly uses the held-out test set, and no cross-match identity information is shared across splits. All metrics are computed over complete sequences and averaged across matches to ensure consistent comparison.

Statistical settings

To ensure statistical reliability, each model is trained and evaluated with five independent runs using different random seeds. For every metric, we report the mean value and standard deviation across the five runs in the format mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance between the proposed method and each baseline is assessed using a two-sided paired t-test computed on per-sequence metric scores. A result is considered statistically significant when the p-value is lower than 0.05. This protocol ensures that performance improvements are not attributable to random initialization or data ordering effects. Efficiency metrics including Params and FLOPs are computed once per model configuration and reported together with accuracy results for comprehensive comparison.

Baseline

We compare the proposed framework against six representative multi-object tracking baselines. SORT is included as a classical tracking-by-detection method based on Kalman filtering and Hungarian assignment. DeepSORT extends SORT by incorporating deep appearance embeddings for improved identity association. ByteTrack represents a strong contemporary tracking-by-detection approach that leverages low-confidence detections for robust data association. FairMOT is a unified detection and re-identification framework that jointly learns object localization and identity features. OC-Tracker is selected as a lightweight tracking model emphasizing computational efficiency with reduced parameter complexity. GTR-T is included as a state-of-the-art transformer-based tracker that models long-range temporal dependencies. These baselines cover traditional methods, mainstream deep learning approaches, state-of-the-art transformer models, and lightweight designs, providing a comprehensive benchmark for comparison.

Implementation details

All experiments are conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon Silver 4314 CPU, 128 GB RAM, and a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB memory, running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. The implementation is developed in Python 3.10 using PyTorch 2.1.0 with CUDA 12.1 and cuDNN 8.9. Core dependencies include torchvision 0.16.0, numpy 1.24.4, and scipy 1.11.3. The model is trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 8 sliding-window sequences. We use the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-4 and a weight decay of 1e-4. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduler is applied over the full training schedule to ensure stable convergence. Gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 5.0 is used to prevent exploding gradients in temporal attention modules. All experiments are initialized with a fixed random seed of 42 to ensure reproducibility across runs. Model checkpoints are selected based on the best validation IDF1 score. The model configuration follows the structured design described in the method summary. Each player state vector consists of normalized position and velocity concatenated with role and region embeddings. The initial feature embedding dimension is set to 128, produced by a two-layer MLP with ReLU activation. The spatial attention module contains 2 transformer layers with 4 attention heads and a hidden dimension of 128. The temporal attention module also contains 2 layers with 4 heads and identical embedding size to maintain dimensional consistency. The dual-path fusion gate is implemented as a learnable scalar parameter constrained by a sigmoid function. Role and region encodings are represented using 32-dimensional embeddings and added to the state representation. The segment aggregation module operates on windows of 32 frames and outputs a 256-dimensional segment embedding. The prototype-based motivation module maintains 8 learnable prototypes and employs a single-layer GRU with hidden size 128 for temporal summarization. The final prediction head is a two-layer MLP mapping the 256-dimensional global representation to detection refinement and identity association outputs. For fair comparison, all baseline models are trained and evaluated under the same data splits, preprocessing pipeline, hardware configuration, and optimization schedule. Hyperparameters of each baseline are tuned on the validation set while strictly keeping the test set unseen during model selection.

Scalability and real-time roadmap

To realize the full potential of tactical AI in practical football analytics, we outline a concrete roadmap for scaling our framework to support diverse league environments and real-time deployment. Different leagues (professional vs youth, European vs South American) exhibit varying playing styles, tactical norms, and data availability. To address this, we propose domain-adaptive pretraining strategies using league-specific statistical priors and contrastive learning objectives. Role & Zone Encoding modules can be re-parameterized with weak supervision to adapt to different formations and semantics, while motif clustering can be fine-tuned using small annotated subsets from each target league. To support frame-by-frame tactical inference during live matches, we plan to optimize inference latency through model pruning, ONNX-based acceleration, and batch-stream hybrid processing. The Tactiformer module already supports sliding-window inference over short temporal windows, which can be coupled with a streaming data buffer to minimize lag. For edge deployment, a Lite variant of the model (as shown in Table X) achieves real-time throughput (>70 FPS) with competitive accuracy. We envision integrating the model within a larger tactical assistant system including real-time player detection (via YOLO or BlazePose), data preprocessing pipelines, and user interfaces for coaching staff. Visualization modules (heatmaps, motif timelines) can provide interpretable overlays, while the backend operates continuously on match feed input. This roadmap provides a practical and modular path toward scalable and deployable tactical reasoning systems across competitive contexts.

Discussion

While the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance and interpretability, several limitations remain. First, the model assumes access to structured and temporally synchronized tracking data, which may not be available in lower-tier leagues, youth competitions, or amateur settings. Although we demonstrate robustness to moderate noise and occlusion, performance may degrade significantly under extreme data sparsity, which calls for further work in semi-supervised learning or domain adaptation. Second, while our role-aware encoding provides a useful inductive bias, it currently relies on static role definitions that may not generalize well to teams with highly fluid tactical systems. Future extensions could explore dynamic role inference or data-driven spatial semantics. Third, our current design focuses on pattern recognition and symbolic reasoning, but does not account for causal relationships in tactical outcomes. For instance, while we can identify a high-pressing motif, the model does not reason about its effectiveness or link it to possession gain. Integrating causal inference mechanisms or counterfactual analysis remains a promising but underexplored direction. Lastly, the model has been tested predominantly on professional-level datasets; its transferability to less structured or more diverse football cultures (5-a-side, youth leagues) remains an open question. Addressing these limitations would enhance the model’s utility across broader real-world scenarios and deployment environments.

Interpretability remains a fundamental challenge in applying deep learning to tactical sports analysis, especially when models are expected to assist human decision-makers such as coaches or analysts. While our model incorporates certain interpretable elements , such as role-aware embeddings and tactical motif discovery , several limitations persist. First, the transformer-based attention mechanisms, although powerful, are inherently difficult to interpret at scale. Attention weights are often diffuse and context-dependent, making it hard to draw deterministic explanations for specific outputs. While we visualize aggregated zone-level attention maps, these do not always correspond directly to tactical reasoning processes used by practitioners. Second, the motif clustering generated by StratGaze offers symbolic summaries of gameplay patterns, but the abstraction level is not always human-aligned. For instance, a discovered cluster may correspond to a tactical transition without matching any well-defined coaching terminology. Bridging this gap requires integrating expert knowledge or performing post-hoc motif labeling. Third, our model currently lacks mechanisms for counterfactual analysis , i.e., explaining why a prediction changed or what would happen if a player had moved differently. Such reasoning is critical for interpretability in tactical feedback systems and remains an open challenge. Despite these limitations, we believe our role-aware design, modularity, and intermediate motif representation provide a useful foundation for future work on explainable tactical AI. Future directions include the integration of causal attention, expert-in-the-loop labeling, and symbolic reasoning engines to enable richer interpretive layers.

The application of AI in football analytics raises important ethical and societal considerations. First, the use of player tracking data, especially from professional athletes, must adhere to data protection regulations such as GDPR. While the datasets used in this study are publicly available and anonymized, real-world deployments must ensure informed consent, secure data storage, and proper data handling protocols. Second, algorithmic bias remains a risk. Models trained on elite-level European leagues may not generalize fairly to teams in youth leagues, women’s football, or underrepresented regions. This can lead to uneven performance, misinterpretation of tactics, or the propagation of data-driven disadvantages. We encourage future work to consider fairness auditing across demographic and geographic lines, and to explore transfer learning methods that improve equity in predictive accuracy. Third, the increasing use of tactical AI may affect human decision-making dynamics. While our model is designed as an assistive tool for analysts and coaches, overreliance on algorithmic recommendations could diminish human agency or reduce diverse tactical creativity. We believe that AI systems should serve as transparent, interpretable support mechanisms, not as prescriptive decision-makers. Addressing these ethical dimensions is essential for responsible deployment. We advocate for interdisciplinary collaboration among data scientists, ethicists, and practitioners to ensure that football analytics tools enhance, not constrain, human insight and sporting fairness.

An important theoretical perspective that complements our data-driven framework is game theoretic strategy construction based on Nash Equilibrium (NE). Prior studies have explored equilibrium based modeling for collective strategic reasoning in soccer, baseball, and American football43, where team behaviors are formulated as strategic choices within predefined payoff structures and equilibrium solutions characterize stable competitive configurations44. This line of work provides a normative view of strategy: given an explicit utility definition, strategies are derived as rational best responses to opponents under competitive interaction45. Compared with equilibrium based approaches, our framework follows a different paradigm. NE-based models are prescriptive in that they assume explicit payoff definitions and rationality assumptions to derive theoretically optimal strategies. In contrast, Tactiformer and StratGaze adopt a descriptive and predictive representation learning approach: the model learns latent tactical embeddings directly from spatiotemporal tracking data, capturing empirical regularities, interaction patterns, and recurring motifs without requiring a manually specified utility function. As a result, our framework targets scalable tactical representation and motif discovery, rather than explicit equilibrium construction. Nevertheless, the two paradigms are complementary and can be integrated. Within our framework, the prototype based motif representations learned by StratGaze (Eq. 25-27) define a discrete latent strategy space, where each prototype corresponds to a recurring high level tactical configuration. If payoff signals, such as expected goal probability, territorial progression, possession stability, or defensive recovery success, are associated with interactions between opposing motifs, it becomes feasible to construct a motif-level payoff matrix over the learned motif indices. Nash equilibrium solutions could then be computed in this learned interaction space, enabling a hybrid system that combines empirical tactical motif discovery with equilibrium stability analysis. The opponent aware contrastive objective in Eq. (29) already encourages strategic differentiation between competing teams within the same segment, implicitly modeling adversarial tension and competitive divergence in latent space. Extending this design to an explicit best response formulation, where future segment embeddings are predicted conditionally on opponent embeddings, would allow dynamic equilibrium trajectory modeling and counterfactual tactical simulation. Such a direction could enable analysts to evaluate how alternative motif selections might alter downstream match evolution. Therefore, while the present work does not explicitly solve for NE, the modular design of Tactiformer and StratGaze is structurally compatible with equilibrium-based integration, offering a promising pathway to unify representation learning with game theoretic reasoning in competitive team sports.

Conclusions and future work

In this study, we explored how deep learning technologies, particularly transformer-based models, can be effectively applied to the tactical analysis of football matches. The motivation behind this research is to enhance the understanding of complex, dynamic, and multi-agent behaviors inherent in team sports. We introduced two novel models, Tactiformer and StratGaze, each targeting different layers of tactical reasoning. Tactiformer utilizes a hierarchical transformer architecture that efficiently encodes spatiotemporal features, enabling the modeling of player trajectories, team structures, and evolving strategies. StratGaze builds upon this foundation by incorporating a strategic attention mechanism, allowing the model to infer high-level tactical motifs and anticipate game developments. Our experimental results demonstrated that these models provide rich tactical interpretations and predictive capabilities that surpass traditional statistical approaches, delivering valuable tools for analysts, coaches, and decision-makers within the football community.

Despite the promising results, several limitations remain that point to concrete directions for future work. First, our performance degrades modestly on out-of-distribution and noisy tracking data, suggesting the need for lightweight adaptation mechanisms and semi-supervised learning techniques that can better handle low-resolution or partially missing inputs. Second, although StratGaze outputs interpretable motif clusters, the mapping between these patterns and established tactical concepts remains indirect. Future extensions could incorporate expert-in-the-loop labeling or hierarchical symbolic annotation layers to improve human alignment. Third, the current model operates as a descriptive pattern recognizer, without understanding causal relationships between tactical choices and match outcomes. Integrating causal reasoning or counterfactual analysis would enable more actionable and trustable tactical insights. Finally, real-time deployment considerations such as frame-by-frame latency optimization and model pruning strategies deserve further investigation to ensure practical usability in coaching environments. By grounding these future directions in the specific limitations observed during experimentation, we aim to develop more robust, interpretable, and context-aware tactical AI systems for real-world football applications.
