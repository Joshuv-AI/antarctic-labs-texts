Investigating acute-to-subacute neuroanatomical outcomes and the role of sex and age in adolescent concussion: A PedCARE+MRI substudy
Authors: KR Simas, AB Dodd, V Sicard, N Abdeen, KO Yeates, R Zemek, AA Ledoux, for the Pediatric Emergency Research Canada (PERC) PedCARE Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited research has examined acute-to-subacute neuroanatomical trajectories following adolescent concussion. To investigate acute-to-subacute longitudinal changes in cortical thickness and subcortical volume following adolescent concussion, we compared neuroanatomical trajectories relative to orthopedic injury (OI) from 72 h to 4 weeks post-injury, and explored moderation by age and sex.MethodsYouth aged 10–17 years with an acute concussion or OI were recruited from a pediatric emergency department and completed acute and subacute T1-weighted MRI scans. Sixty-seven youth with concussion and 31 youth with OI were included in analyses.ResultsTime did not moderate group differences in cortical thickness and volume. No main effects of group were found across cortical regions, with no moderating effects of age or sex. Youth with concussion demonstrated persistently reduced hippocampal volume relative to OI across time. Age, but not sex, further moderated group differences in hippocampal volume, with similar trajectories below 12 years of age, but progressively greater hippocampal volume in OI relative to concussion around 13 years of age and older. Increasing age was associated with cortical thinning across cortical regions, except the middle frontal gyrus, irrespective of group.ConclusionThese findings suggest cortical structures may not be affected within the first 4 weeks post-injury. Hippocampal volume was reduced in youth with concussion aged approximately 13 years and older from 72 h through 4 weeks post-injury, suggesting deviation from the positive age–hippocampal volume relationship in OI. This age-dependent pattern may suggest an acute-to-subacute neurodevelopmental vulnerability of the hippocampus during early adolescence following concussion.Keywords: Brain concussion, Mild traumatic brain injury, Adolescence, MRI, Neuroimaging

Introduction

Concussion, a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), accounts for upwards of 93% of all TBIs, with an average annual incidence rate of 7.91 per 1000 individuals (Jones et al., 2026). Recent estimates suggest 5.85 in 1000 youth aged 5-14, and 8.88 in 1000 older adolescents experience a concussion annually within the general population (Jones et al., 2026). Nevertheless, the pathophysiology underpinning concussion recovery within the developing brain is not fully understood (Choe et al., 2012); advanced neuroimaging metrics may have prognostic utility for concussion in adolescence, including cortical thickness and gray matter volumetric indices. The neuroanatomical consequences of concussion in adolescence remain poorly understood. Existing longitudinal work examining cortical thickness and subcortical volume spans heterogeneous post-injury timelines—subacute-to-chronic (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2015, 2023), chronic (Mac Donald et al., 2019), or acute/subacute-to-chronic (Ware et al., 2023)—and cross-sectional studies have focused on isolated subacute (Mac Donald et al., 2018; Ware et al., 2020) or chronic (Urban et al., 2017; Zivanovic et al., 2021; Bigler et al., 2018) phases. To our knowledge, no studies to date have characterized neuroanatomical changes across the critical acute (24–72 h) to the subacute (∼4 weeks) window of recovery post-injury.

Although limited, evidence suggests that concussion in adolescence may alter both cortical and subcortical structures, with findings varying in timeline, magnitude, and regional specificity. Trajectories of region-specific alterations have been reported as early as 1–3 weeks (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2015, 2023; Ware et al., 2020), and up to 4–12 months post-injury (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2015, 2023; Urban et al., 2017), whereas other studies report no persistent differences at 6-month to 2.5 years (Mac Donald et al., 2019; Zivanovic et al., 2021; Bigler et al., 2018). Despite the limited studies, alterations have been corroborated in several specific regions, such as the hippocampus (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2023; Mac Donald et al., 2018), right angular gyrus (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Urban et al., 2017), and right middle frontal gyrus (Mayer et al., 2015, 2023). Moreover, studies have reported opposing effects on cortical thickness within the same regions (right superior frontal gyrus: increased (Nathaniel et al., 2025) vs decreased (Mayer et al., 2015); left postcentral gyrus: increased (Ware et al., 2020) vs decreased (Mayer et al., 2015)), both relative to control groups and across similar time periods. However, cortical thickness and volumetric studies have some methodological shortcomings, such as small sample sizes (Mayer et al., 2015) or cross-sectional methodologies (Mac Donald et al., 2019; Ware et al., 2020; Zivanovic et al., 2021; Bigler et al., 2018), which contribute to overall less robust-evidence.

Heterogeneity may further arise from developmental factors, as adolescent girls report slower symptom recovery than boys (Ledoux et al., 2019a). Neuroanatomical studies exploring sex and age in concussion are sparse. However, a large cohort study found female youth with concussion to have reduced cortical thickness relative to those with an orthopedic injury (OI) at 6-month post-injury within the posterior orbital gyrus, subcallosal area, and basal forebrain, but not at 1-month or 3-month post-injury (Ware et al., 2023). These findings may support the notion that prolonged symptomatic recovery trajectories in female youth (Ledoux et al., 2019a) are identifiable at the neuroanatomical level. Similarly, age has sometimes been reported as a moderator of neuroanatomical outcomes (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Ware et al., 2023), though some cross-sectional work found no moderation (Ware et al., 2020; Urban et al., 2017) and reported typical cortical thinning across both concussion and OI groups (Ware et al., 2020). Collectively, the inconsistency and lack of replication, particularly in the early post-injury period, highlight key gaps in understanding adolescent neuroanatomical recovery trajectories after concussion.

In the present study, we investigated whether time moderates group differences in cortical thickness and subcortical volume between youth with concussion and OI from within 72 h of injury to 4-week post-injury. Regions of interest (ROI) were selected based on prior adolescent and pediatric concussion literature. We hypothesized that the groups would differ on initial (72-h) regional cortical thickness and volume metrics, but not at 4-week post-injury. We additionally explored whether age or sex moderated neuroanatomical trajectories.

Methods

Study design

This study is a planned secondary analysis of the randomized clinical trial (RCT) “Pediatric Concussion Assessment of Rest and Exertion + MRI (PedCARE + MRI), NCT02893969)” completed at the Children's Hospital of Eastern Ontario (CHEO) from May 2018 to February 2020, as an adjunct study to the PedCARE study within Pediatric Emergency Research Canada (Ledoux et al., 2019b, 2022a). CHEO's Research Ethics Board approved this study. During recruitment from the emergency department (ED), youth with concussion were randomized to either early physical activity resumption or rest until asymptomatic before resuming physical activity. Prior studies have shown no difference in time spent performing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity throughout the first two weeks of recovery between both treatment groups in this cohort (Ledoux et al., 2022a). As such, both groups were combined. OI youth were recruited as a comparison group for the neuroimaging component of the study but not assigned to a physical activity treatment group.

Participants

Adolescent concussion group

Youth with concussion aged 10 up to 17 years of age (inclusive) presenting to the ED within 48 h of a head injury and diagnosed with a concussion according to a modified version of the ACRM definition (Mild Traumatic Brain Injury Committee et al., 1993) were recruited (please seeLedoux et al., 2019band Ledoux et al., 2022a). Exclusion criteria were as follows: Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13 or less; abnormality on brain imaging if completed for clinical purposes; neurosurgical intervention, intubation, or intensive care unit admission; multisystem injury requiring hospitalization; absence of trauma history as primary event; or previous enrollment.

Adolescent OI group

Youth with OI aged 10 up to 17 years of age (inclusive) presenting to the ED within 48 h of an isolated upper extremity injury due to blunt force or physical trauma (e.g., sprain, fracture, strain) that potentially required immobilization were recruited. Exclusion criteria included those with a self-reported history of diagnosed concussion or traumatic brain injury within the last year or a diagnosed concussion during the present ED visit, or any injury requiring closed reduction, procedural sedation, or surgical management, were excluded.

Shared exclusion criteria

The following exclusion criteria applied to both groups: Severe chronic neurological developmental delay resulting in communication difficulties; history of neurological or neurodevelopmental disorder such as epilepsy, intellectual disability/mental retardation, autism (history of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, learning disability, or Tourette's syndrome was not an exclusion); intoxication at the time of ED or MRI presentation as per clinician or RA judgment; previous hospitalization for psychiatric disorders; inability to obtain a proper written informed consent/assent (language barrier, absence of parental authority, developmental delay, patient too confused to consent, etc.); legal guardian not present; any sedation medication administered prior to ED visit or during ED visit; inability to be present at the 72 h (±48 h) or the 4 weeks (±5 days) MRI follow-up; or any contraindications to MRI (e.g., metal, braces).

Demographic and clinical data were collected at the time of enrolment in the ED, except for psychiatric history which was collected at 72 h.

Outcome measures

All eligible participants completed an initial MRI scan at 72 ± 48 h and a follow-up scan at 4 weeks ± 5 days post-injury.

Data acquisition

A 3-T Siemens Biograph molecular Magnetic Resonance (mMR) scanner equipped with a 12-channel head coil was used. Anatomical scanning was performed using a 3D T1-weighted Multi-Echo Magnetization Prepared Rapid Acquisition Gradient Echo (MEMPRAGE) (van der Kouwe et al., 2008) sequence. T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained with the following parameters: Voxel size: 0.9 x 0.9 × 1.0 mm, slice thickness: 1.0 mm, SNR: 1, TI: 1160 msec, TE: 2.21; 4.09; 5.97; 7.85 msec, TR: 2300 msec, flip angle (FA) = 8°, 176 transversal slices with FOV 230 × 230 mm, 256 × 256 pixel matrix, 0.9 × 0.9 mm in-plane resolution, with bandwidth = 650 Hz per pixel for all 4 contrasts.

MRI data processing

Quality assurance

Initial visual quality assurance of raw T1-weighted anatomical images was conducted according to methods presented by (Backhausen et al., 2016). Anatomical images received a grade of pass, check, or fail based on a visual assessment of image sharpness, ringing artifacts, contrast-to-noise ratio for subcortical structure identification, and gray and white matter differentiation. Images with a grade of “check” were subsequently reviewed by an independent trained analyst who determined if the image was suitable for inclusion into analyses. Scans were also reviewed for significant incidental findings and excluded if deemed as clinically relevant or 4+ white matter hyperintensities were identified.

Image processing

Cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation was performed with the FreeSurfer image analysis suite (Fischl, 2012), version 7.3.2. The recon-all processing stream with default parameters was applied. The preprocessing included resampling of the T1-weighted image from 0.9 x 0.9 × 1.0 mm to a standardized 1 mm isotropic resolution within the native anatomical space, along with standard surface and volume-based preprocessing steps of cortical and subcortical reconstruction. Individual cortical models were registered onto the FreeSurfer average template (fsaverage) and the cerebral cortex parcellated into units with respect to gyral and sulcal structure following the probabilistic sulco-gyral Destrieux atlas (Destrieux et al., 2010). Cortical thickness estimates were obtained for a total of 74 cortical regions and volumetric estimates for total intracranial volume and 6 subcortical gray matter regions, per hemisphere. Cortical thickness estimates reflect the distance between the pial and white matter surfaces per cortical region averaged across all vertices, whereas subcortical volumetric estimates reflect total voxel counts within a subcortical region. These measures were extracted as regional estimates for external statistical analyses.

Primary cortical and subcortical ROI examined were selected according to prior literature findings and limited to parcellated regions within the Destrieux atlas (Destrieux et al., 2010) following the standard FreeSurfer processing pipeline. Primary parcellated cortical ROI included in analyses were the superior frontal gyrus (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2015) (anatomically encompasses the medial frontal gyrus (Mayer et al., 2015) within the Destrieux atlas (Destrieux et al., 2010)), middle temporal gyrus (Mayer et al., 2015), postcentral gyrus (Mayer et al., 2015; Ware et al., 2020), middle occipital gyrus (Mayer et al., 2015), orbital gyrus (anatomically encompasses the posterior orbital gyrus (Ware et al., 2023)), cuneus (Mayer et al., 2015), precuneus (Ware et al., 2020), middle frontal gyrus (Mayer et al., 2015, 2023) (anatomically encompasses majority of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Urban et al., 2017)), superior parietal lobule (Ware et al., 2020), inferior parietal cortex (anatomically encompassed by the supramarginal (Mayer et al., 2015; Ware et al., 2020; Urban et al., 2017) and angular gyri (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2015; Ware et al., 2020, 2023; Urban et al., 2017)), and subcallosal area (Ware et al., 2023), whereas the hippocampus (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2023; Mac Donald et al., 2018) was the primary parcellated subcortical ROI included (Fig. 1). Excluded from analyses was the basal forebrain (Ware et al., 2023) which follows a separate FreeSurfer processing pipeline for limbic structures, despite being reported within the adolescent and pediatric concussion literature.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2019) in RStudio version 2025.05.0.496 (Posit team, 2024). Frequencies and descriptive statistics were computed and reported using the gtsummary package (Sjoberg et al., 2021) to summarize characteristics of both cohorts. Linear mixed effect random intercept models using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom approximation for p-value calculation were carried out using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017). All omnibus tests were grouped by neuroanatomical region (12 cortical, 1 subcortical), with a false discovery rate (FDR) (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) correction applied for multiple comparisons within cortical and subcortical regions, respectively. Primary analyses investigated two-way interactions of group (Concussion, OI) by linear time postinjury (72-h, 4-week), while adjusting for age (time invariant) and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV; time-varying), and fit with a random intercept for subject and for hemisphere nested within subject (eq. (1)). Secondary analyses were used to investigate three-way interactions of group by time by age and group by time by sex (Male, Female) while adjusting for their lower order interaction terms, eTIV, and the same random effects structure as Equation (1) (eq. (2)). Intraclass Correlation Coefficients were calculated for intercept-only null models across ROI and suggested a random intercept for subject and for hemisphere nested within subject was justified for the data. In preliminary omnibus analyses, FDR-corrected group differences were not moderated by hemisphere, thus hemisphere was modeled as a covariate in all final models, with model estimates averaged across hemisphere. Sex was not modeled as a covariate given eTIV was used to control for allometry variation across male and female adolescents (Lenroot and Giedd, 2010) in addition to general whole brain differences between subjects, which also allowed us to reduce the risk of model overfitting. Age and eTIV were grand mean centred as continuous covariates given the primary interest in between group differences over time; this also increases coefficient estimate interpretability, reduces estimation instability associated with multicollinearity (Afshartous and Preston, 2011), and reduces significant effect interpretation errors (Robinson and Schumacker, 2009), while also retaining between-subject variability within our time-varying covariate of eTIV.

Where we did not find a time-moderating effect in primary analyses, we collapsed time into a covariate and further investigated for time invariant group differences for primary analyses (eq. (3)), and time invariant moderating effects of group by age (eq. (4)), and group by sex while controlling for age (eq. (5)), in which all estimates were averaged estimates across time and hemisphere. Lower order main effects of age and sex were assessed from reduced models in the absence of a group by age/sex interaction for our secondary analyses. In this case, all estimates were averaged estimates across group, time and hemisphere.

Post-hoc contrasts were calculated using model-estimated marginal means or slopes using the emmeans package (Lenth et al, 2025) to assess significant moderating effects on group, with the Kenward-Roger method used to calculate degrees of freedom. Quantile-based contrasts (25th, 50th, 75th) were performed for significant continuous (age) by group (by time, if applicable) interactions to further decompose group differences. Contrasts were not corrected for multiple comparisons given the conservative nature of our omnibus testing correction.

The statistical significance threshold used was p = 0.05. Given the complexity of variance partitioning in linear mixed models (Rights and Sterba, 2019), there is no consensus to calculating standardized effect sizes for main effects or interactions per individual model. Thus, unstandardized effect sizes were reported when possible, which is also in keeping with general effect size reporting recommendations (Pek and Flora, 2018).

Results

Participants

Sixty-seven youth with a concussion (median age [IQR] = 12.96 [2.58] years, 46.27% female) and 31 with OI (median age [IQR] = 12.33 [2.82] years, 38.71% female) were included in analyses (Fig. 2). Table 1 presents personal and injury characteristics. The concussion and OI groups did not differ in age, sex, mechanism of injury, history of previous concussion, prevalence of co-morbid learning, sleeping, developmental and psychiatric disorders, or eTIV at 72-h and 4-week (Table 1). However, the proportion of youth experiencing a headache at enrollment was greater in the concussion group (91.04%) relative to the OI group (16.13%; p < 0.001), and time from injury to the first MRI was shorter for the concussion group (median days [Q1, Q3] = 3.10 [2.20, 3.70]) relative to the OI group (median days [Q1, Q3] = 4.55 [2.95, 5.05]; p = 0.004). The groups did not differ in time from injury to the second MRI (p = 0.9).

Group differences

Moderating effect of time and group on cortical and subcortical gray matter macrostructure

Time did not moderate group differences in mean cortical thickness or volume across all a priori cortical and subcortical regions (Table 2). Group did not moderate mean cortical thickness differences across all cortical regions (Table 3). Differences in hippocampal volume were moderated by group (β = −144.95 [95% CI: −279.75, −10.32], p = 0.039; Table 3). Youth with concussion had reduced hippocampal volume (EMM = 4600.55 [95% CI: 4524.12, 4676.98]) relative to the OI group (EMM = 4745.5 [95% CI: 4631.8, 4859.19]; Fig. 3; Supp Table 1).

Moderating effect of sex and age on cortical and subcortical gray matter macrostructure

Age and sex did not moderate group differences in mean cortical thickness across all a priori cortical regions (Table 4; Table 5). Age moderated group differences in hippocampal volume (β = −94.52 [95% CI: −161.64, −27.35], p = 0.008; Table 4). Specifically, we found a significant positive relationship between hippocampal volume and age for OI youth (β = 95.35 [95% CI: 36.65, 154.05]) but consistent hippocampal volumes across age for youth with concussion (β = 0.83 [95% CI: −35.91, 37.57]; Fig. 4; Supp Table 2). Quantile-based age contrasts revealed no significant differences between youth with concussion and OI in hippocampal volume at the 25th percentile (25th Age percentile = 11.39; β = −13.75 [95% CI: −178.22, 150.72], p = 0.868). However, youth with concussion had significantly lower hippocampal volume than OI at the 50th percentile (50th Age percentile = 12.79; β = −146.56 [95% CI: −280.54, −12.57], p = 0.032) and 75th percentile (75th percentile for Age = 14.34; β = −293.30 [95% CI: −465.87, −120.74], p = 0.001; Table 6). Sex did not moderate group differences in hippocampal volume (Table 4). Across groups, age was found to be significantly negatively associated with cortical thickness across all cortical ROIs except the Middle Frontal Gyrus (FDR corrected p = 0.905; Supp Table 3). No significant main effect of sex was found across groups for cortical and subcortical regions following FDR-correction (Supp Table 4).

Discussion

In this prospective longitudinal study, youth with concussion demonstrated reduced mean hippocampal volume relative to those with OI, with age moderating this effect. Hippocampal volumes were comparable between groups at younger ages but diverged around early adolescence, such that youth with concussion showed lower hippocampal volume from 72 h through 4 weeks post-injury. In contrast, no group differences were observed in cortical thickness over time or as a function of age or sex, nor were persistent cortical alterations evident at 4 weeks post-injury in regions previously implicated in adolescent and pediatric concussion.

Hippocampal volume and age

Our hippocampal findings align with prior concussion studies in adolescents reporting subacute (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2023) and persistent (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2023; Mac Donald et al., 2018) (1 month up to 1 year) reductions in hippocampal volume, extending this literature by identifying a developmentally specific association during early adolescence within the acute-to-subacute phase post-concussion. Further, previous work has described a negative age-hippocampal volume relationship in symptomatically stratified adolescent concussion groups versus a positive age-hippocampal volume relationship in healthy controls, averaged across longer recovery periods, with age algorithmically defined as estimated “brain age” from T1-weighted images (Nathaniel et al., 2025). While the present data demonstrates an association between adolescent concussion and reduced hippocampus volume relative to OI within the first month post-injury, this association is driven by an age-dependent difference in hippocampal maturation. Specifically, hippocampal volume remained similar across groups before the age of 12 years, but afterwards volume displays a positive relationship with age for the OI group whereas a null association with age was seen in youth with concussion, with notable differences between groups seen at approximately 13 years of age and older. This may suggest a disruption of normative developmental trajectories rather than delayed recovery per se, with more pronounced associations as adolescent age at injury increases. These findings may point to early adolescence as a potential window of heightened vulnerability of the hippocampus to concussion. Moreover, the previous study reporting a negative relationship between age and hippocampal volume was an averaged effect over a longer time period spanning up to 1 year (Nathaniel et al., 2025); therefore, it may be possible that hippocampal atrophy as a function of older adolescent age following concussion may be more apparent within the latter phases (i.e., 4 months, 1-year) post-injury rather than within the timeframe examined in our study (1 month), potentially preceded by a period of disrupted hippocampus volume development at older adolescent ages at time of injury. No prior study has identified a similar age-moderated effect on hippocampal volume apparent at approximately 13-years of age and older within an acute-to-sub-acute recovery timeline, and relative to an OI group. An important consideration in interpreting these findings is whether the acute reductions in hippocampus volume truly represent atrophy or instead reflect a transient mechanism. For example, the apparent acute reduction in hippocampus volume may result from transient compression of the hippocampus secondary to acute neuroinflammation in surrounding regions. Preclinical mild to moderate head injury models have identified neuronal apoptotic cells most abundant within the hippocampus as early as 24-48 h and upwards of 5 days following controlled cortical impact injury (Akamatsu and Hanafy, 2020). Given our acute scans reflect a 1-5 day (median 3.1 days) period post injury, acute programmed cell death within the hippocampus represents one plausible mechanism underlying the observed age-moderated findings averaged across time. Another potential mechanistic explanation for the age moderated findings leading to persistent volumetric reductions seen at 4-weeks may relate to hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation following brain injury (Tapp et al., 2019), potentially leading to an altered stress response and affecting hippocampus volume longer term – particularly considering that older adolescents are more prone to stress-provoking disorders such as anxiety, depressive, and emotional disorders relative to younger adolescents (WHO, 2025), and the overall greater risk of mental health issues in adolescents following a concussion (Ledoux et al., 2022b).

Cortical thickness and age

Contrary to our hypotheses, concussion was not associated with alterations in cortical thickness during the acute orsubacute period, nor were cortical outcomes by group moderated by age. This absence of early cortical effects is consistent with prior suggestions of null group differences during early recovery (Ware et al., 2023), and may support that detectable cortical alterations may emerge later in the chronic phase rather than within the first month post-injury (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Urban et al., 2017). However, other studies have reported findings of cortical alterations detectable within 4-weeks of injury that persist into the latter chronic phases relative to control groups (Nathaniel et al., 2025; Mayer et al., 2015, 2023). Taken together, our findings highlight the heterogeneity in the timing of cortical changes after concussion and the need for better characterization of the onset of when such cortical alterations emerge and how they relate to symptom trajectories. Importantly, the observed rates of age-related cortical thinning were comparable to established neurotypical developmental trajectories (Ducharme et al., 2016; Shaw et al., 2008) and did not differ between groups, suggesting that early cortical maturation processes may proceed largely uninterrupted following adolescent concussion. This result has been previously supported by work reporting similar patterns of neurotypical age-related cortical thinning between youth with concussion and OI within a subacute time frame (Ware et al., 2020).

Moderating effects of sex

Sex-based differences in neuroanatomical metrics following adolescent concussion are sparse. Sex did not moderate group differences in hippocampal volume or cortical thickness. Although girls have been shown to experience more prolonged symptom recovery relative to boys (Ledoux et al., 2019a), prior studies focusing on cortical thickness report mixed findings (Ware et al., 2020, 2023). Our results align with one study that showed no sex-based group differences in cortical thickness during the acute-to-subacute post-injury period (Ware et al., 2020), while another study reported female-specific cortical alterations emerging at only later time points (i.e. 6 months post-injury) but not at earlier time points (1 and 3 months) (Ware et al., 2023). This pattern suggests that sex-related neuroanatomical differences, if present, may reflect delayed or compensatory processes rather than acute injury effects. Our null findings for main effects of sex are consistent with normative neurodevelopmental studies showing minimal overall sex differences in cortical thickness and subcortical volume maturation (Muftuler et al., 2011; Zhou et al., 2015).

Limitations

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to characterize neuroanatomical changes across the critical acute (24–72 h) to subacute (4 weeks) window post-injury. While our findings add to the literature on acute and subacute cortical changes following adolescent concussion and corroborate findings, several limitations warrant consideration. The concussion cohort stems from a randomized controlled trial involving physical activity interventions. Although no allocation effects were observed for cortical thickness or subcortical volume, residual influences of physical activity cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, our sample was recruited from the ED which may not be representative of the broader pediatric concussion population. While OI controls were included to account for injury-related stress and healthcare exposure (Mathias et al., 2013), they may not fully approximate uninjured neurotypical development (Wilde et al., 2019). Given the OI exclusion criteria consisted of self-reported history of concussion or TBI within the past 1 year from the participant or parent directly, the information may have been subject to recall bias and misclassification. Thus, this limitation associated with this enrollment criterion for OI participants must be acknowledged. However, it should be noted that the modelling approach used in the analysis in theory accounts for any between subject variation (such as prior history of TBI or concussion) that could account for partial baseline differences in hippocampus volume. The sample size, particularly in the OI group, may have limited power to detect interaction effects. This reduced power also yields limitations in our ability to generalize our results. Further, sensitivity analyses revealed that influential observations contributed to the observed main effects of group on hippocampal volume differences, however they did not contribute to the significant moderating effect of age on group differences in hippocampal volume. Importantly, detailed neuroimaging data quality control and case-level review did not identify these observations as artifactual or erroneous, suggesting they likely reflect true inter-individual variability rather than measurement bias. Such variability may be clinically meaningful and highlight the heterogeneity inherent in pediatric concussion, which larger samples will be better positioned to characterize more robustly. The lack of pre-injury volumetric data also limits our ability to assume any directionality that concussion may lead to the volumetric difference in the hippocampus observed within our acute-to-subacute timeframe. The template used to standardize participant T1-images varies across studies, with very few that have been supported in the literature as appropriate for the pediatric brain. In the present study, images were preprocessed in FreeSurfer (Fischl, 2012) version 7.3.2 and standardized to the fsaverage template. While no gold standard exists in processing methods, FreeSurfer has been reported as reliable for cortical segmentation, with robust thickness derivation and subcortical volume calculation in children as young as 5-years old (Pulli et al., 2022). Further, the use of an average cortical region-based analysis may be limited to detecting larger overall differences in cortical thickness relative to a vertex-wise analysis, which is more sensitive to detecting smaller clusters of cortical alterations. Thus, this may limit our ability to detect true cortical thickness differences following adolescent concussion and may explain the lack of significant differences found in cortical thickness following correction.

Future directions

Given the growing evidence linking adolescent concussion to alterations in hippocampal volume, further research is needed to characterize the longitudinal trajectory of the hippocampus following injury. It remains unclear whether the acute volumetric reductions observed after concussion persist into the chronic stages of recovery or instead reflect transient processes that subsequently contribute to the reduced hippocampus volumes at longer follow-up reported in the broader literature. Future studies should examine longitudinal trajectories of the hippocampus across a continuous recovery period, from the acute stage through 1 year following concussion, while incorporating pre-injury neuroimaging data whenever feasible. Moreover, beyond the structural differences identified in the hippocampus, our study did not directly evaluate associations with clinically relevant outcomes such as memory or cognitive performance. Future work should evaluate the functional significance of hippocampus volumetric alterations through longitudinal assessments of memory, learning, spatial navigation, and broader cognitive outcomes. These studies may help determine whether post-concussion hippocampus alterations may serve as a biomarker of recovery and whether early therapeutic interventions targeting hippocampus health are associated with improved symptom resolution and clinical outcomes in adolescents over time.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest the hippocampus may experience volumetric changes as early as 72 h post-concussion that persist at up to 4 weeks in youth aged 13 years and older relative to OI, with more pronounced changes for older aged adolescents at time of injury. Cortical thickness may not be altered within the first 4 weeks following adolescent concussion, with no explicit associations with sex or age beyond normal neurotypical development. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to determine if clinical memory deficits are associated with changes in hippocampal volume from the acute-to-chronic phases post-injury.
