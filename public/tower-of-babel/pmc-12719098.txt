NADPH oxidase isoform NOX-2 deficiency affects mitochondrial oxygen consumption and metabolic flexibility
Authors: Kannathasan Thetchinamoorthy, Diana Wierzbicka, Emilia Waraksa-Zasada, Michalina Kazek, Adrian Konopko, Mariusz Z Ratajczak, Magdalena Kucia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur recent study showed that the intracellular pattern recognition receptor Nlrp3 inflammasome, activated by reactive oxygen species (ROS), regulates metabolism in hematopoietic cells by maintaining proper “tonic activation” of the electron transport chain (ETC) in mitochondria. Therefore, we asked whether a deficiency in the expression of NADPH oxidase isoform NOX-2, a primary source of ROS in hematopoietic cells, would similarly affect mitochondrial function and metabolic adaptation to stress. In this study, we examined how NOX-2 influences ETC, redox balance, and glycolytic adaptation in lineage-negative Sca-1+c-Kit+ (SKL) bone marrow stem cells from wild-type (WT) and NOX-2 knockout (NOX2-KO) mice. Metabolic testing revealed that NOX2-KO cells have impaired mitochondrial respiration, lower ATP production, and reduced spare respiratory capacity. When exposed to hydrogen peroxide, NOX2-KO cells failed to activate a mitochondrial stress response and instead relied more on anaerobic glycolysis. Treatment with extracellular ATP (eATP), the most abundant signaling alarmin, increased ROS levels in WT cells but not in NOX2-KO cells, emphasizing eATP-NOX-2's role in redox regulation during stress conditions. Proteomic analysis identified proteins differentially expressed related to hypoxia, glycolysis, and oxidative stress response between WT and NOX2-KO SKL cells. These results demonstrate NOX-2 as a key regulator of mitochondrial function and metabolic flexibility in hematopoietic stem cells, highlighting its importance in maintaining redox balance during stress conditions.Keywords: NOX-2, Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), Redox signaling, Mitochondrial metabolism, Oxidative stress, Reactive oxygen species (ROS), Purinergic signaling

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are chemically reactive molecules derived from molecular oxygen that play a dual role in cellular biology: at low to moderate levels, they act as essential second messengers regulating proliferation, differentiation, and survival [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. However, excessive ROS accumulation causes oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, leading to cellular senescence, apoptosis, and cancer development [3,4]. Recently, we reported that NOX-2-deficient hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) exhibit i) defective migration toward the gradient of the main bone marrow (BM) chemoattractants, ii) impaired intracellular activation of the Nlrp3 inflammasome, and iii) impaired membrane lipid raft (MLR) formation, which is crucial for an appropriate chemotactic response to BM homing factors [5,6]. Nonetheless, the mechanisms behind these effects—particularly those related to cellular bioenergetics and mitochondrial homeostasis—remain largely unexplored. Mitochondria represent an essential intracellular ROS generator in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, contributing superoxide and hydrogen peroxide under both basal and stress conditions.

In eukaryotic cells, ROS are primarily produced by NADPH oxidases (NOXs), a family of membrane-bound enzymes that facilitate the reduction of O2 to superoxide anion (O2•-). The dismutation of O2•- by superoxide dismutases (SODs) yields hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a diffusible and relatively stable oxidant that can engage in redox signaling or be broken down by catalase and glutathione peroxidases [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Among the NOX isoforms, NOX-2 (also known as gp91phox) is a main source of ROS in hematopoietic and immune cells [2,4]. Originally identified for its role in microbial killing by phagocytes, NOX-2 is now increasingly recognized for its regulatory role in non-phagocytic cells, including hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) [3,5,6]. It is expressed as a plasma membrane-bound NADPH oxidase and is also found in mitochondrial membranes, where it contributes to ROS production. In NOX2-deficient mice, gp91phox is absent from plasma membrane–associated and mitochondrial microdomains, thereby removing both sources of NOX2-derived ROS. NOX-2-derived ROS influence signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, mTOR, and HIF-1α, which are closely linked to metabolic reprogramming and cell fate decisions [1,7].

HSPCs reside in hypoxic niches within the bone marrow that favor anaerobic glycolysis over mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [11,12]. This metabolic profile protects stem cells from excessive ROS production and helps maintain quiescence and self-renewal capacity. However, dynamic changes in redox status are necessary during cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation. Notably, HSPCs have limited antioxidant defenses and reduced DNA repair abilities compared to differentiated cells, making them highly vulnerable to oxidative damage [[12], [13], [14], [15]].

Despite increasing recognition of the role of ROS in stem cell biology, the specific contribution of NOX-2 to redox regulation, mitochondrial function, and metabolic adaptation in HSPCs remains not fully understood. In this study, we combine functional metabolic assays, high-resolution proteomics, and ROS quantification to examine NOX-2's role in controlling mitochondrial respiration, glycolytic compensation, and redox adaptation in murine bone marrow–derived Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+ (SKL) stem cells. Using NOX-2 knockout mice, we show that NOX-2 deficiency causes mild mitochondrial dysfunction, evidenced by decreased oxygen consumption rate, reduced spare respiratory capacity, and impaired adaptation to oxidative stress. Additionally, we demonstrate that extracellular ATP (eATP), a primary danger signaling alarm and purinergic signaling activator [16], increases ROS levels and alters proteomic responses in a NOX-2-dependent manner. Our findings support NOX-2 as a key regulator of redox-metabolic balance in hematopoietic stem cells and offer mechanistic insights into how redox signaling interacts with mitochondrial function to maintain stem cell integrity under both normal and stress conditions.

Materials and methods

Lineage-negative cells depletion

Lin− cells were isolated from the bone marrow of WT and Nox-2-KO mice using a Direct Lineage Cell Depletion Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Lin− cells represent immature hematopoietic populations depleted of lineage-defining surface markers and enriched for early progenitor compartments. Briefly, cells were incubated for 10 min at 4 °C with MicroBeads conjugated to monoclonal antibodies against CD5, CD11b, CD45R (B220), Anti-Gr-1 (Ly-6G/C), 7-4, and Ter-119 (Direct Lineage Cell Depletion Kit, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). After incubation, the cells were washed with 3 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at 304×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of PBS, then applied to an LC column (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) that had been rinsed with 3 ml of PBS and placed in a magnetic field (Manual Cell Separator, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). The column was then washed with 3 ml of PBS to elute and collect the purified Lin− cells [17].

Isolation of SKL cells

SKL cells were isolated from the bone marrow of C57BL/6 wild-type and NOX-2 knockout mice. SKL (Lin−Sca-1+c-Kit+) cells enrich for metabolically distinct hematopoietic stem/progenitor subsets and represent a functionally relevant population for metabolic and redox studies. Briefly, the bone marrow was flushed from the femurs, and the total nucleated cell population was obtained after lysing red blood cells (RBCs) with 1 × BD Pharm Lyse buffer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). The cells were then stained with lineage marker-specific antibodies (all from BD Biosciences): PE–anti-TCR γδ, clone GL3; PE–anti-CD11b, clone M1/70; PE–anti-TCR β-chain, clone H57–597; PE–anti-CD45R/B220, clone RA3-6B2; PE–anti-TER-119/erythroid cells, clone TER-119; PE–anti-Ly-6G and -Ly-6C (Gr1), clone RB6-8C5; PE–Cy5–anti-Ly-6A/E (Sca-1), clone D7; FITC–anti-CD117 (c-Kit), clone 2B8 for 30 min in medium containing 2 % fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were then washed, resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium, and sorted using a Moflo XDP cell sorter (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN, USA) into populations of SKL (Sca-1+ c-Kit + Lin−) cells [18].

Cell lysates preparation and protein digestion

A total of 30,000 SKL cells were isolated for each independent biological sample (n = 6). The cell pellets were lysed using RIPA buffer without adding protease or phosphatase inhibitors to avoid nonspecific peaks in the chromatogram. Subsequently, the proteins were heat-denatured at 98 °C for 1 h, followed by ultrasound treatment on ice for 1 h. Proteins were precipitated with ice-cold LC-MS grade acetonitrile (Merck Millipore, USA), centrifuged at 18,000×g for 30 min at −10 °C, and dried for 10 min at room temperature using an Eppendorf Concentrator Plus (Eppendorf, Germany). The proteins were then re-dissolved in 40 mM ammonium bicarbonate, reduced with 20 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT), and alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). In-solution trypsin digestion was performed overnight at 37 °C with shaking, and the digestion was stopped by adding 0.1 % formic acid [6].

LC-MS proteome analysis

LC-MS analysis was performed using a nanoUHPLC system (nanoElute, Bruker) coupled with a Captive Spray ion source (Bruker) connected to an ESI-Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Compact, Bruker). A two-column separation method was employed, consisting of a pre-column (300 μm × 5 mm, C18 PepMap 100, 5 μm, 100 Å, Thermo Scientific) and a Bruker fifteen separation column with CSI fitting (75 μm × 150 mm, C18 1.9 μm). The gradient elution ranged from 2 % to 35 % of mobile phase B over 90 min, with a flow rate of 300 nL/min. The mobile phases used were: A – 0.1 % formic acid in water; B – 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile. Sample ionization was performed at a gas flow rate of 3.0 L/min, a temperature of 150 °C, and a capillary voltage of 1600 V. The quadrupole energy was set to 5.0 eV, and the collision chamber energy to 7.0 eV, with an ion transfer time of 90 μs. Ions were analyzed in positive mode within a mass range of 150–2200 m/z, with an acquisition frequency of 4 Hz per spectrum and using the autoMS/MS system. The spectra were analyzed and calibrated with DataAnalysis software (Bruker) using sodium formate, and after peak list extraction, proteins were identified via ProteinScape (Bruker) utilizing the MASCOT server. Protein identification was performed against the SwissProt and NCBI_prot databases, with annotation and biological significance assessed using Reactome.org and String.org [19].

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurements

Bone marrow stem cells were isolated from WT and Nox-2-KO mice. Lin− cells were purified using an LC column as previously described. The cells were treated with 10 μM H2O2 for 15 min and washed with phenol-red-free RPMI media. The oxygen consumption rate was measured at 37 °C using a Seahorse XF HS MINI (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, United States) with the Cell Mito Stress Test Kit, following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 400,000 cells per well in 180 μl of phenol-red-free RPMI media were added to a Seahorse XFp PDL miniplate and incubated at 37 °C without CO2 for 1 h. OCR measurements were taken from a 90 % confluent monolayer culture. A protocol was used to assess mitochondrial function indices, involving sequential injections of oligomycin, FCCP, and a mixture of rotenone (RO) and antimycin A (AA) through the ports of Seahorse Flux Pak cartridges. The final concentrations of these compounds were 1.5 μM, 2 μM, and 0.5 μM, respectively, enabling the determination of basal, maximal respiratory, spare respiratory capacity, and ATP production. These parameters were calculated, including non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption correction, using the following formulas [17].

Basal Respiratory = OCR initial – OCR RO/AA.

Maximum Respiratory = OCR FCCP – OCR RO/AA.

Spare Respiratory Capacity = OCR FCCP – OCR initial.

ATP production = OCR initial – OCR oligomycin.

Glycolytic proton efflux rate (glycoPER) measurement

Following the manufacturer's protocol, the glycoPER was measured at 37 °C using a Seahorse XF HS MINI (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, United States) with the Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit. Briefly, 400,000 cells per well in 180 μl of phenol-red-free RPMI media were added to a Seahorse XFp PDL miniplate and incubated at 37 °C without CO2 for 1 h. GlycoPER measurements were obtained from a 90 % confluent monolayer culture. A protocol was followed for the glycolytic rate assay, involving sequential injections of a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A and 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG) through the ports of Seahorse Flux Pak cartridges. The final concentrations of these compounds were 0.5 μM and 50 mM, respectively, enabling the assessment of basal and compensatory glycolysis [20].

Total level of ROS (CM-H2DCFDA fluorescence)

The CM-H2DCFDA (chloromethyl derivative of 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. C6827) was used to measure ROS levels following the standard procedure. Briefly, cells (10,000 per 100 μl media) were pre-treated with two concentrations of eATP (1 nM and 10 μM) for 16 h. The cells were then centrifuged at 304×g for 10 min, and the resulting cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml of PBS with CM-H2DCFDA (final concentration 5 μM) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were centrifuged again at 304×g for 10 min and resuspended in 200 μl of PBS. The fluorescence of the cells was recorded at λex = 488 nm and λem = 523 nm using SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Readers (Molecular Devices). ROS levels were quantified by measuring CM-DCF fluorescence intensity and expressed as a percentage of the control (non-treated cells) [20,21].

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) level (ROS glow assay luminescence)

The ROS-Glo H2O2 AssayTM (Promega, Cat. No. G8820) was used to measure hydrogen peroxide levels following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells (10,000 per 100 μl media) were pre-treated with two concentrations of eATP (1 nM and 10 μM) for 16 h. The cells were then centrifuged at 304×g for 10 min, and the resulting cell pellets were resuspended in PBS. Next, 20,000 cells were added to each well of a 96-well plate. Subsequently, 20 μl of the H2O2 substrate was added directly to the cells (final concentration 25 μM). The cells were incubated in the dark in a humidified incubator (5 % CO2, 37 °C) for 3 h. After incubation, 100 μl of the ROS-Glo detection reagent was added to the cells, and an additional incubation in darkness was carried out for 20 min at room temperature. Total luminescence was measured using SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Readers (Molecular Devices). H2O2 levels were quantified based on luminescence intensity and expressed as a percentage of the control (non-treated cells) [20,21].

Real-time analysis of expression of mRNA for electron transport chain complexes (ETCs) in WT and NOX2-KO cells

Bone marrow Lin− stem cells were isolated from WT and NOX2-KO mice. The cells (40,000) were transferred into 200 μl of RLT lysis buffer (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and either frozen at −80 °C or directly subjected to RNA isolation using a RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. Reverse transcription was performed using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (BioRad, CA, USA), and gene expression was analyzed with the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad) along with the primers listed in Table 1 on a CFX Connect real-time cycler (BioRad). The expression of a specific gene in each sample was normalized to the expression of the GAPDH housekeeping gene [20,21].

Total ATP production

ATP production in Lin− cells from NOX2-KO mice BM was measured using the CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA; Catalog No. G9241), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, Lin− cells (40,000 cells per 100 μl of phenol red-free media) were pre-treated with H2O2 (10 μM, 15 min) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Control group without H2O2 pre-treatment was also evaluated. After incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 304×g for 10 min, resuspended in an appropriate volume of phenol red-free RPMI medium, and plated into a 96-well plate (100 μl cells/well). Next, 100 μl of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well and mixed gently for 3 min to ensure cell membrane lysis and ATP release. The cells were then incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Luminescence intensity, which indicates ATP levels, was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, CA, USA) at 10, 20, and 30 min post-incubation [21].

Lactate production measurements

Lactate production in Lin− cells isolated from WT and Nox2-KO BM cells was measured using the Lactate-Glo® Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA, Catalog No. J5021) following the manufacturer's instructions. Control cells (untreated) and cells treated with H2O2 (10 μM) for 15 min were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5 % CO2. After incubation, the cells were harvested, and 40,000 cells were seeded per well in a 96-well plate. Lactate-Glo® Reagent (50 μl/well) was added, and the plate was gently mixed for 45 s. The cells were then incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Luminescence was measured using a SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, CA, USA) [20,21].

Statistical analysis

At least six independent replicates were conducted for all experiments, and data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7 software (GraphPad, USA). The significance of differences between experimental groups was assessed with Student's t-test, considering a P value below 0.05 as significant. All statistical analyses were performed with R, and visualization was generated using the ggplot2R [22] package [version 3.4.4]. UMAP analysis of biological processes was carried out using the Appyters bioinformatic pipeline [23]. All primary cells were processed and analyzed under ambient 21 % O2 conditions. We acknowledge that these values exceed the physiological O2 levels of the bone marrow niche; this limitation is addressed in the Discussion.

Results

NOX2 deficiency impairs mitochondrial Respiration and restricts adaptive responses to H2O2-Induced oxidative stress

We assessed mitochondrial respiration in Lin− BM cells from WT and NOX2-KO mice using Seahorse analysis. At steady state, NOX2-KO cells exhibited significantly lower basal and maximal oxygen consumption rates (OCR), ATP production, and spare respiratory capacity (SRC), indicating mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 1A–D). This finding was also partially shown in our previous publication as Supplementary Figure [5]. Additionally, this defect is supported by a decreased total basic ATP production in Nox2-KO cells (Fig. 1G).

To expand on this initial observation, we exposed cells to 10 μM hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to induce mild oxidative stress that further decreased mitochondrial respiration in both genotypes. However, only WT cells maintained a partial SRC response (SRC >0), while NOX2-KO cells showed a complete loss of adaptability (SRC <0) (Fig. 1B–C and E–F). These data indicate that NOX-2 is essential not only for baseline mitochondrial health but also for the ability to adapt to oxidative insults involving oxidative phosphorylation.

Since impaired oxidative phosphorylation can shift ATP production toward glycolysis [24], we aimed to further investigate the energy metabolism of WT and NOX2-KO cells by measuring their glycolytic rates (Fig. 2). First, to examine acidification from glycolysis, we initially treated the cells with a combination of rotenone and antimycin A to inhibit mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I and III, effectively blocking mitochondrial oxygen consumption and proton production. Then, we exposed cells to 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG), a well-known inhibitor of glycolysis that competitively binds to glucose hexokinase, the first enzyme in the glycolytic pathway. This allowed us to assess compensatory glycolysis [21].

Fig. 2A and D shows that Lin− BM cells isolated from NOX2-KO mice under steady-state conditions have reduced basal and compensatory glycolysis compared to WT cells. Notably, after exposure to mild oxidative stress caused by 10 μM H2O2, both WT and NOX2-KO cells displayed decreased glycolytic parameters. However, the reduction was more pronounced in WT cells than in NOX2-KO cells (Fig. 2B and C and 2E–F). Supporting this, we observed an increase in lactate production in Nox2-KO cells using the Lactate-Glo® Assay (Fig. 1H).

Based on the results of our Mito Stress test, which show that oxidative phosphorylation is impaired in NOX2-KO cells under oxidative stress, while it remains functional in WT cells, NOX2-KO cells may rely more on glycolysis as an alternative energy source. Additionally, these findings suggest that the mitochondria in NOX2-KO cells are more sensitive to low levels of extracellular H2O2 than those in WT cells. This data aligns with our recent report on the ROS downstream target Nlrp3 inflammasome [5,20,21].

Nevertheless, unlike Nlrp3-KO cells, which showed a decrease in mRNA expression for the ETC complex III component CYTB [20], we observed no changes in mRNA expression for components of all five ETC complexes (Supplementary Fig. 1).

ROS Basic Expression and production in WT and NOX2-KO stem cells

Given the known role of ROS in stem cell activation and stress response, we measured total ROS levels using the CM-H2DCFDA fluorescence assay under steady-state conditions and found higher levels of total ROS in NOX2-KO cells (Fig. 3A). Next, cells were stimulated with adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which, when secreted into the extracellular space (extracellular ATP - eATP), acts as a key alarmin involved in stress responses. We exposed cells to eATP (1 nM or 10 μM) and observed an increase in total ROS levels in WT cells after exposure to 10 μM of eATP. In contrast, total ROS levels did not change significantly in NOX2-KO cells (Fig. 3B).

(B) Total ROS after eATP (1 nM and 10 μM) stimulation. (C) H2O2 levels measured using the ROS-Glo assay. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n ≥ 3). ∗p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗p ≤ 0.01, ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001.

To further explore the effects of eATP, we measured H2O2 levels in WT and Nlrp3-KO cells using the commercially available ROS-Glo™ H2O2 Assay (Fig. 3C). Unexpectedly, there were no significant differences in H2O2 levels between WT and NOX2-KO cells under steady-state conditions or after eATP treatment (Fig. 3C). This indicates that other types of ROS, such as hydroxyl radicals (HO•), peroxyl radicals (HOO•), or lipid peroxyl radicals (LOO•), might be involved. Further research is needed to confirm this and to measure SOD, which is responsible for H2O2 production.

Interestingly, in our previous paper, we observed similar upregulation of total ROS expression in Nlrp3-KO HSPCs compared to WT HSPCs, with no difference in H2O2 levels measured by ROS-Glo™ H2O2 Assay [5,20,21]. This suggests that the NOX-2-Nlrp3 inflammasome axis mainly triggers other types of ROS.

Mass spectrometry–based proteomic analysis identified 5913 proteins shared between WT and NOX2-KO SKL cells, and 5675 proteins common to WT-eATP and NOX-eATP groups (Supplementary Fig. 2). Unsupervised clustering and UMAP analysis revealed distinct proteomic signatures (biological process) among the experimental groups, with enrichment of proteins involved in oxidative stress, metabolism, hypoxia response, and glycolytic processes (Fig. 4A).

Analysis of proteins related to oxidative stress showed that NOX2-KO SKL cells had higher levels of TXND3 and HSP72, indicating a redox imbalance. Conversely, WT cells exhibited increased amounts of cytoprotective proteins such as HSP7E, HSF2, and ZNRF4 (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that NOX2 is essential for maintaining proteomic stability across redox and metabolic pathways in BM stem cells. In broader metabolic processes, NOX2-KO cells increased INSRR and IRS1, while WT cells showed higher levels of MTOR, a key regulator of energy sensing and protein synthesis (Fig. 4C).

Furthermore, in the hypoxia response pathway, NOX2-KO cells showed higher expression of HIF1N, while WT SKL cells had greater levels of IFIH1, LOXL4, LOXL1, and MMP19 (Fig. 4D). Next, we focused on pathways related to redox and metabolic adaptation. Notably, SKL cells from NOX2-KO mice exhibited increased levels of proteins involved in glucose metabolism, including SHKB1, IRS1, INSR, and HIF1N, indicating compensatory metabolic reprogramming. In contrast, WT cells displayed higher levels of PGAM5, ITPI1, and ATG2B, reflecting a balanced glycolytic flux and mitophagy (Fig. 4E).

eATP induces distinct proteomic changes in glycolytic, hypoxic, and redox pathways in WT and NOX2-KO SKL cells

To further explore NOX-2–dependent signaling, we examined how eATP stimulation impacts SKL cell proteomes. Redox-related protein profiling revealed higher G6PD levels in WT cells exposed to eATP, indicating activation of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and increased NADPH-dependent antioxidant capacity. No significant differences were observed in HSP76, HSF4, or ZNRF4 levels (Fig. 5A). In metabolic processes, PPARA, ATG2B and CPT1C were enriched in NOX2-KO cells exposed to eATP, while INSRR was downregulated in NOX2-KO cells stimulated with eATP (Fig. 5B). Concerning hypoxia-related pathways, NOX2-KO cells responded to eATP with increased expression of COX10, CUL2, and KRBX1, whereas control NOX2-KO cells exposed to eATP downregulated MMP16 and ENOA (Fig. 5C). Among glycolysis-related proteins, NOX2-KO SKL cells after eATP exposure showed increased levels of SHKB1, ATG2B, PEKAM, FBP1L, and PDK2 while NOX2-KO cells exposed to eATP had lower amounts of KHK, G6PD, MYCB2, GCKR, KLC3, and INSRR (Fig. 5D). Additionally, our RQ-PCR analysis confirmed that NOX2-KO cells increase mRNA expression of PFKB3 and G6PD enzymes, which regulate glycolysis and the pentose phosphate cycle, respectively (Fig. 5E). Overall, these results indicate that eATP regulates all the pathways evaluated in this paper in NOX-2-KO cells.

Discussion

NOX2 is an enzyme attached to the plasma membrane and contributes to regulating mitochondrial function and ROS production [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In this report, we provide additional evidence that NOX2 deficiency impacts mitochondrial function and the capacity of murine HSPCs to adapt to oxidative stress [5,12]. The dual localization of NOX2 within plasma membrane domains and mitochondrial microdomains suggests that distinct ROS microenvironments operate in HSPCs. Plasma membrane–derived ROS are typically linked to receptor-proximal signaling processes, whereas mitochondrial NOX2 pools may influence mitochondrial redox tone, electron transport chain stability, and metabolic stress responses. The absence of both these NOX2 pools in NOX2-deficient HSPCs likely disrupts the fine spatial regulation of ROS signaling, contributing to the impaired mitochondrial responses observed in this study.

We observed that NOX2-KO cells showed reduced basal and maximal oxygen consumption rates (OCR), lower ATP production, and decreased spare respiratory capacity (SRC), indicating mitochondrial dysfunction. When exposed to hydrogen peroxide, NOX2-KO cells did not trigger a mitochondrial stress response and instead became more reliant on anaerobic glycolysis. In response to eATP, the most abundant signaling alarmin, ROS levels increased in WT cells but not in NOX2-KO cells, emphasizing the role of this oxidase in redox regulation during stress conditions. Additionally, proteomic analysis identified proteins that were differentially expressed related to hypoxia, glycolysis, and oxidative stress response between WT and NOX2-KO HSPC cells.

ROS plays a vital role in cell biology. It activates in the cytoplasm and functions as a key part of innate immunity, specifically as a pattern recognition receptor called the Nlrp3 inflammasome [6,25,26]. ROS are crucial signaling molecules that alter the activity of various enzymes, transcription factors, histones, and structural proteins by oxidizing cysteine and methionine residues in proteins. For example, protein targets for this modification include AKT kinases, NRF2, HIF-1α, FOXOs, AP-1, PTEN, and SIRT1 [1,3]. Changes in the signaling pathways of these transcription factors can influence the expression of many intracellular and extracellular enzymes. Moreover, ROS released in a NOX2-dependent manner in mitochondria can activate NOX2 through a positive feedback loop, creating a feed-forward mechanism that may contribute to overall cellular oxidative stress and potential mitochondrial damage.

In this report, we show that HSPCs lacking NOX2 have OCR-related and metabolic defects. This study expands our understanding of NOX-2 as an essential regulator of redox balance in HSPCs. NOX-2 acts as a signaling hub that manages mitochondrial reserve capacity, redox buffering, and energy metabolism regulation at the transcriptional level. The proteomic signatures identified in NOX2-deficient cells—marked by reduced levels of respiratory chain complexes and redox-sensitive enzymes—support the Seahorse and ROS results, demonstrating a consistent mechanistic pattern.

This data aligns with a recent report showing that chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) cells, in response to NOX2 silencing, increase glycolysis, produce more mitochondrial ROS, and display mitochondrial dysfunction [27]. Interestingly, as reported, NOX2 and another oxidase, NOX4, can also interact in the context of mitochondrial function [27]. For example, NOX4 can produce hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which can then activate NOX2, amplifying the increased mitochondrial ROS production. The compensation of NOX2 by NOX4 may explain why NOX2-deficient cells show similar yet somewhat different mitochondrial phenotypes. To support this, NOX4 expression in CML cells was upregulated upon NOX2 silencing, and this was necessary to alter mitochondrial function and NOX2 [26]. Through the regulation of NOX4 expression, they controlled metabolism and mitochondrial function in these cells.

While our study measured overall ROS levels using MitoSOX and CM-H2DCFDA, these probes do not precisely differentiate between specific ROS species such as superoxide (O2−•), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), or peroxynitrite (ONOO−) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. We acknowledge this limitation and recognize that our measurements mainly reflect general oxidative activity rather than individual molecular species. In future research, using ROS species–specific assays—like Amplex Red for H2O2 and DHE or specialized fluorescence sensors for superoxide and peroxynitrite—will be necessary to more accurately identify the oxidative signature related to NOX2 activity. As mentioned, we observed a similar pattern of ROS expression in Nlrp3 inflammasome–deficient HSPCs [20].

The unexpected decrease in ROS levels after eATP stimulation in NOX2-deficient HSPCs calls for further investigation. One possible reason is impaired activation of purinergic P2X receptors, such as P2X7, which are known to trigger NOX-dependent ROS production and affect calcium signaling [28,29]. Without NOX2, this pathway may be disrupted, preventing the typical ROS burst seen in WT cells. Alternatively, increased antioxidant responses or NADPH-independent sources of ROS, like mitochondrial reverse electron transport, could explain this surprising finding. More research into receptor activity, calcium fluxes, and mitochondrial redox buffering is needed to fully understand the redox responses to purinergic stimuli when NOX2 is absent.

Our data indicating that NOX2-KO cells are in a state of chronic mild oxidative stress, with elevated basal ROS and impaired mitochondrial function, raise important questions about their long-term viability and regenerative capacity. This has direct implications for bone marrow transplantation, aging, and hematopoietic disorders, where redox imbalance and mitochondrial problems are common. This chronic condition may explain, on one hand, why the number of HSPCs increases in NOX2-KO mice [5], and on the other hand, how this persistent activation of hematopoiesis under stress conditions may contribute to malignant transformation and leukemia. Therefore, adjusting NOX-2 activity could be a strategic way to optimize redox balance in HSPCs aimed at improving HSPC health, particularly in older patient groups. An important methodological consideration is that all experiments were performed under ambient oxygen (∼21 % O2), which is substantially higher than the physiological oxygen tension of the bone marrow niche (1–5 % O2). Exposure of HSPCs to supraphysiologic oxygen levels is known to alter ROS balance, mitochondrial metabolism, and differentiation trajectories. Thus, although necessary for technical reasons, normoxic handling may accentuate some oxidative phenotypes. We address this limitation and note that future low-oxygen studies will clarify how NOX2 regulates HSPC behavior under more physiological conditions.”

Despite mitochondrial deficits, NOX2-KO cells exhibit strong compensatory glycolysis when facing oxidative stress, indicating a shift toward a more glycolytic profile. This change aligns with metabolic adaptations observed in other stem cell populations under stress, where glycolysis offers a more ROS-tolerant energy source. Our proteomic data support this, with NOX2-KO SKL cells showing increased levels of IRS1, INSR, and glycolytic effectors such as SHKB1 and HIF1N. Similarly, we observed elevated mRNA levels for PFKFB3 (6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3), a key enzyme regulating glycolysis and influencing cell proliferation [30,31]. These results reflect a “Warburg-like” adaptation and suggest that NOX-2 influences not only mitochondrial bioenergetics but also the selection of metabolic pathways. Interestingly, at steady state, NOX2-KO cells showed lower basal glycolytic activity compared to WT cells, possibly indicating a reduced ability to engage glycolysis unless under stress. This implies that the metabolic phenotype of NOX2-KO HSPCs is not inherently glycolytic but shifts to that state in response to energy demands. Such dynamic metabolic changes are likely controlled by redox-sensitive transcriptional and post-translational factors like HIFs, FOXOs, and sirtuins.

These findings highlight the loss of ROS-dependent eustress signaling in NOX2-deficient cells. As emphasized in the classical ROS biology framework, low-to-moderate ROS levels serve as essential second messengers that control metabolic adaptation, transcriptional activation, and stress tolerance. This aligns with the concept of a hormetic response, where low and high doses of a potential stressor have different effects [25,32,33]. The blunted ROS response to eATP stimulation observed in NOX2-KO cells suggests that the absence of NOX2 disrupts these eustress-driven pathways, leading to an impaired ability to mount appropriate metabolic and mitochondrial stress responses. Future studies using targeted ROS scavenger approaches—such as NAC, mitoTEMPO, or catalase mimetics—will help clarify the relative contribution of mitochondrial versus NOX-derived ROS to these phenotypes. These experiments will be especially useful for distinguishing signaling-specific ROS functions from general oxidative damage responses.

Conclusions

NOX-2 is a key regulator of mitochondrial function, redox balance, and metabolic adaptation in HSPCs. These findings expand our understanding of redox biology within stem cell compartment and highlight the essential role of NOX-2 in maintaining stem cell resilience during stress [5,12,31,34]. Future research should investigate the downstream signaling pathways affected by NOX-2, its interactions with purinergic signaling and inflammatory pathways, and the potential for targeting NOX-2 activity in regenerative treatments and hematological disorders. Additional studies will also focus on the importance of NOX-2 downregulation in the hematopoietic microenvironment and stem cell niches.
