eSolubilized amniotic membrane ECM as a promising biological surface treatment approach for 3D-printed bone tissue engineering scaffolds
Authors: Shima Shorouei, Latifeh Karimzadeh Bardeei, Mousa Kehtari, Negin Vaziri, Afshin Taheriazam, Farzad Moradikhah, Ali Moghadas Jafari, Ehsan Seyedjafari, Jochen Salber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, the fused deposition modeling method was used to fabricate 3D-printed polycaprolactone (PCL). This study introduces a cost-effective post-fabrication treatment by using a Solubilized Amniotic Membrane (SAM) extracellular matrix (ECM) as a biological surface modifier. The employment of SAM provides an easy approach to overcoming the current challenges in the way of using fresh, cryopreserved, or dehydrated tissue. Four groups were included in this study, including neat PCL, oxygen plasma-treated PCL, SAM 0.001%w/v (SAM1), and SAM 0.005%w/v (SAM5). According to the SEM images, the diameter of each 3D-printed filament and filament distance were around 573 μm and 372 μm, respectively. The FTIR-ATR spectra confirmed the presence of amide groups in specimen, containing SAM. A higher weight loss rate was obtained for oxygen plasma-treated PCL and SAM-containing samples than neat PCL. The results of in vitro studies revealed that the optimized content of SAM (SAM 5) could promote the osteogenesis potency of Wharton-Jelly Mesenchymal Stem Cells (WJ-MSCs), cultured on the 3D-printed scaffolds in terms of alkaline phosphatase activity, calcium deposition and real-time PCR assessment of alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, and osteonectin. Also, in vivo, the collagen content in the control group and SAM 5 was 30.89 ± 1.73 and 44.24 ± 2.91, respectively. According to the Micro-CT assessment, the bone volume fraction was remarkably improved in the presence of SAM5 as it increased from 36.52 ± 1.56% in the control group to 42.66 ± 2.17% in SAM5. The results of the present study provide a promising surface modification approach by employing SAM for the future of bone tissue engineering scaffolds.

Introduction

The design of tissue engineering scaffolds with adjustable architecture is one of the main goals of tissue engineers1. Traditional fabrication methods show limited precision in producing the architecture with the absolutely essential pore interconnectivity1,2. 3D printing is one of the advanced fabrication approaches, enabling accurate control of the pore structure and architecture that overcomes most of the limitations of common fabrication techniques3. Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), Stereolithography (SLA), Selective Laser Sintering (SLS), Particle Binding (PB), Inkjet Printing (IP), and Direct Ink Writing (DIW) are the most frequently employed 3D printing approaches4. FDM is a cost-effective, safe, and efficient operating method of scaffold fabrication with desirable accuracy to produce samples with complex geometries5.

Among the different biomaterials, utilized in the fabrication of bone tissue engineering scaffolds, synthetic biomaterials are more favorable due to their precise chemistry, ease of processing and ability to be tailored for specific applications, allowing a tight control over the micro and macrostructures production. Polycaprolactone (PCL) is a polyester, FDA-approved synthetic polymer, enabling desirable machinability at normal temperatures6. A concern about PCL is its hydrophobic nature, restricting the biomedical application of this polymer7. To this end, some physical and chemical surface treatment techniques have been developed8. Alkaline hydrolysis9, plasma treatments10, and coating with biological materials (e.g. collagen, cellulose)11 are the most used approaches. Although the bulk characteristics of scaffolds can be preserved through plasma treatment, the surface chemistry is altered10. As the natural ECM is a complex of biomolecules with hierarchical topographical features in the scale range from micrometer to nanometer, the coating of scaffolds with biological agents can be an advantage12.

The Amniotic Membrane (AM) is the inner part of the placenta, that directly contacts the fetus13. It is a translucent avascular membrane, providing water-soluble molecules (e.g., growth factors and cytokines, etc.) penetration14. Over the last recent years, the application of AM has evolved from a plain sheet for topical usages such as skin and corneal regeneration into advanced forms that can be applied for cytoskeleton regeneration purposes13. From the bone tissue engineering point of view, acellular AM has been used as a scaffold for bone tissue engineering applications. Tang et al. showed that acellular AM provides desirable biocompatibility, osteoinductivity, and angiogenesis potency15. In a study, AM was loaded with calcium and phosphate through the double diffusion method, providing a mineralized membrane with bone regeneration capacity16. Also, it has been introduced that AM extract has the ability to improve osteogenic differentiation. Recently, solubilized amniotic membrane (SAM) was introduced as a potent wound-healing biomaterial in the form of hydrogel17.

In the present study, to the best of our knowledge, SAM was utilized as a promising biological surface modifier agent for the first time. The employment of SAM can overcome the current challenges in the way of using fresh, cryopreserved, or dehydrated tissue by enabling an easy approach to delivering SAM. For this purpose, we proposed an easy post-fabrication modification approach to improve the physicochemical properties of 3D-printed PCL scaffolds, produced by FDM. At first, SAM was prepared as a biological additive modifier of the prepared scaffolds. The fabricated PCL-based scaffolds were categorized into four groups including pure PCL, oxygen plasma-treated PCL, and two surface-treated scaffolds, i.e., SAM (%0.001 w/v), and SAM (%0.005 w/v)) coated scaffolds. We hypothesized that the SAM-coated scaffolds could improve the osteogenic differentiation potency of Wharton-jelly mesenchymal stem cells (WJ-MSCs), cultured on the 3D-printed scaffolds. The osteogenic differentiation capacity of WJ-MSCs was evaluated in vitro by determining alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, calcium deposition content, and bone-related gene expression. Also, in vivo studies were performed on Wistar rats as the animal model in terms of histological assessment, bone formation analyses by micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), and gene expression of bone-related genes.

Materials and methods

Materials

Polycaprolactone (Mn = 80 000, Sigma-Aldrich) was used as the 3D printer feed. Pepsin (Sigma-Aldrich), HCl (Merck), and NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich) were utilized for the SAM preparation step. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, Gibco), and penicillin-streptomycin (pen-strep) were used for the cell culture medium preparation. Phosphate Buffered Saline (1x PBS) and 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl) 2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ascorbic acid, dexamethasone, and β-glycerolphosphate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and were used for preparing of osteogenic differentiation medium.

Methods

Preparation of amniotic membrane extraction

SAM was obtained according to the previously mentioned method17. Briefly, for this purpose, the AM (SinaCell company, Iran) was washed with sterile saline and cut into approximately 5 × 5 cm2 pieces. Then, they were washed with 100 ml sterile saline (five times) and demineralized sterile water. The obtained pieces were transferred to a 50 ml tube, kept at −80 °C for 24 h, and lyophilized for 48–72 h (Operon, Korea). The freeze-dried AMs were ground for 3 cycles of 5 min/5min cooling/milling cycle. A combination of 220 mg of amnion and 22 mg of pepsin were transferred in a 15 ml tube and exposed to gamma irradiation (1 h and 1 mega rad). Then, 10 ml of HCl solution (0.01 N) was added to the mixture and kept at 37 °C for 48 h for digestion before centrifuging (4500 rpm and 10 min). The supernatant solution was collected, neutralized with a dropwise addition of NaOH, and lyophilized for 48 h to obtain SAM.

Scaffold fabrication by FDM method

The FDM method was used for the fabrication of 3D porous scaffolds. For this purpose, the PCL pellets were introduced to a 3D printer (Omid Afarinan Mohandesi Ayande, BioFabX2, Iran) and melted at 75 °C to obtain square scaffolds layer-by-layer in a lay-down pattern of 0/90°, enabling square-shaped pores, in disc shape (9 mm x 1 mm) for in-vitro use and cylinder shape (2.5 mm x 5 mm) for in-vivo experiments. Also, the flow rate and nozzle diameter were selected at 2 mm/s and 300 μm, respectively.

Due to the hydrophobic nature of PCL, the fabricated scaffolds were exposed to an oxygen plasma condition (Diener Electronics, Nagold, Germany) at 0.4 mbar pressure for 3 min (named plasma-treated PCL) prior to the SAM coating. The biological surface modification of fabricated 3D-printed scaffolds was performed by immersing the samples in a uniform suspension of SAM solutions (0.001 and 0.005%w/v in deionized water (DI), labeled as SAM1 and SAM5, respectively) overnight before washing with DI water. For the in vitro and in vivo studies, the sterilization process was performed by immersion of the scaffolds in ethanol solution (% 70 v/v in water) for 30 min. After complete drying of the ethanol solution, the samples were exposed to UV irradiation for 20 min on each side.

Characterization of scaffolds

A scanning electron microscope (SEM, Seron Technology, South Korea, AIS2100) was utilized to evaluate the morphology of 3D-printed scaffolds. The surface chemical properties of pure PCL, plasma-treated PCL, and SAM-coated PCL scaffold were evaluated by an attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy (AVATAR, USA) in transmittance mode at room temperature from 400 to 4000 cm− 1 with a resolution of 4 cm− 1.

To evaluate the hydrolytic degradation of 3D-printed scaffolds, firstly the samples were weighted (W0) and incubated in PBS solution for 8 weeks at 37 °C. Every week, the samples were removed from the PBS solution and Wd was measured after drying at room temperature for 24 h. To investigate the bulk hydrophilicity, the samples, immersed in PBS were taken out from the medium, and weighed (Ws) after wiping with filter paper after 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h. In vitro cell studies

Cytocompatibility assessment

To determine whether the fabricated scaffolds have the desirable cell attachment and cytocompatibility, WJ-MSCs (passage 2, purchased from Royan Institute, Tehran, Iran) were seeded on the plasma-treated PCL, SAM1, and SAM5 scaffolds with a concentration of 3 × 104 cells/cm2 and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2 with normal media (DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 100U/ml pen-strep). On days 1, 3, and 5, the culture media were substituted by an MTT solution and incubated at 37 °C. After 4 h, the media was removed and samples were soaked in DMSO to dissolve the formed dark-blue formazan crystals. An ELISA reader (Biotek Instrument) was employed to measure the absorption of the obtained solution at 570 nm.

Osteogenic differentiation assay

Determination of ALP activity and calcium deposition can be accounted as the indicator of the potency of fabricated scaffolds for osteogenic differentiation. The utilized WJ-MSCs were seeded on plasma-treated PCL, SAM1, and SAM5 with similar conditions to the Cytocompatibility assessment Sect. (2.2.3.1). After 24 h, the normal media were substituted by the osteogenic differentiation media, containing 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10− 8M dexamethasone, and 10mM β-glycerolphosphate in addition to 10% v/v FBS and 100U/ml pen-strep in DMEM. At three time points (7, 14, and 21 days), the total protein content of cells, seeded on the scaffolds was obtained by substituting the osteogenic medium with 200 µL of radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer. After the elimination of cell debris with a centrifuge at 4 °C (15 000 g for 15 min), the supernatant was gathered. The ALP activity of scaffolds was evaluated by an ALP assay kit (Pars Azmoon, Iran).

To evaluate the deposited calcium on 3D-printed scaffolds after 7, 14, and 21 days, the samples were washed with PBS solution, immersed in 0.6 N HCl solution, and shaken for 1 h at 4 °C. The extracted secreted calcium was evaluated by using a calcium content kit protocol (Pars Azmoon, Iran). A microplate reader (Biotek Instruments) was employed to determine the optical density of samples at a wavelength of 570 nm.

The expression of three bone-related genes, including ALP, osteonectin, and osteocalcin was evaluated by a real-time PCR method on days 7, 14, and 21 at the transcript level. According to the manufacturer’s guidelines, an extraction buffer RNA (Maxspin Super plus RNA extraction kit, Iran) was utilized to extract the RNAs from samples. A cDNA synthesis kit (Bonbiotech, Iran) was employed for cDNA obtaining. Quantification of the real-time PCR process was performed via the standard curve method using RESTRG software. The β2-microglobulin (β2M) gene was the internal control for the normalization. Table 1 indicates the primer sequences, employed in qRT-PCR.

In vivo studies

Animals

Eighteen Adult male Wistar rats, weighing approximately 200 ± 20 g, were employed for the in vivo studies. The animals were maintained in special cages with free access to water, dry food, and a cycle of 12 h/12 h (light/darkness). The ambient temperature and humidity were set to 22 °C ± 2 and 50%, respectively. All animal experiments comply with the ARRIVE guidelines (PLoS Bio 8(6), e1000412,2010​) and carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals. All procedures were approved by the Farhikhtegan Hospital ethics committee (Approval ID: IR.IAU.FARHIKHTEGANH.REC.1403.007).

Surgical procedure

After anesthetization of animals with a 10% ketamine/5% pentobarbital mixture (80 mg and 1 g per kg body weight, respectively), a round channel with a depth of 5 mm was created from the medial to the lateral distal femur by a 2.5-mm drill. Then, the channels were rinsed with a physiological saline solution. Shape-fitted Plasma-treated PCL and SAM5 scaffolds were inserted into the 2.5 mm x 5 mm-holes, while in the control group the defect was left unfilled. The dimensions were selected as the critical size defect according to18.

In vivo assessments

On day 30 after surgery, accordimg to American Veterinary Medical Association​ (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020)​, the animals were euthanized using CO2 gas inhalation and the distal part of femur tissues were harvested for further assessments. The histological evaluation of bone was performed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining. Also, an X-ray micro-CT scanner (LOTUS inVivo, Behin Negareh Co., Tehran, Iran) was utilized to evaluate the new bone regeneration. The instrument was equipped with a cone beam micro-focus X-ray source and a flat panel detector. The voltage and current of the employed X-ray were selected at 40 kV and 150 µA, respectively with a 2-second frame exposure time (magnification = 4.4) and a total scan duration of 49 min. A standard Feldkamp, Davis, Kress (FDK) algorithm was utilized for rendering reconstructed 3D images. The % bone volume fraction value was calculated by the division of the volume of trabecular bone to the volume of the region of interest (2.5 mm in diameter and 5 mm in length), multiplied by 100. Moreover, the gene expression level of the bone-related genes in the harvested tissues was evaluated by real-time PCR, similar to Sect. 2.2.3.2.

Statistical analysis

Data were reported as mean ± SD. Each experiment was performed three times or more. GraphPad Prism 10.2.1.395 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com) was employed for the evaluation of the results. One-way analysis of variances (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed to compare the results. the P-value < 0.05 was assumed as the level of significance.

Results

Scaffold characterization

Field emission scanning electron microscopy

The morphology of pure PCL, plasma-treated PCL, SAM1, and SAM5 was examined by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 2 A). The diameter of each filament and filament distance were around 573 μm and 372 μm, respectively. The macropores larger than or equal to 300 μm support the oxygen and nutrients transportation, vital for cell survival19. The architecture of 3D-printed samples was in a lay-down pattern of 0/90°, forming square-shaped pores. The pure PCL scaffold without any treatment showed a smooth surface. However, the oxygen plasma exposure formed micropores on the surface of filaments. According to the SEM images, there is a smooth coverage of SAM on the SAM-containing samples. Also, the coating of plasma-treated PCL scaffolds with SAM did not change the architecture of macropores.

Attenuated total reflectance-fourier transform infrared

Attenuated Total Reflectance-Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) diagrams of pure PCL, plasma-treated PCL, and SAM5-coated 3D-printed scaffolds are shown in Fig. 2B. The peaks are represented as follows:

PCL scaffold

The peaks at 2850–2960 cm− 1 are related to the stretching of the − CH2 groups in the PCL structure, respectively. The sharp peak at 1730 cm− 1 refers to the stretching vibrations of C = O groups. The peaks at 1290 and 1242 cm− 1 show the asymmetric stretching vibrations of the C − O−C groups. Also, the absorption peak at 1170 cm− 1 is related to the symmetric stretching vibrations of C − O−C groups.

Plasma-treated PCL scaffold

There was no remarkable sign of the oxygen plasma treatment on the PCL scaffolds.

SAM scaffold

The absorption peaks around 1510–1560 cm− 1, 1600–1700 cm− 1, and 3310–3300 cm− 1 are attributed to amid I, amid II, amid A, and C-H groups, respectively20.

The results of the water uptake assessment (Fig. 1C) represented that the PCL, plasma-treated PCL, SAM1, and SAM5 had a weight add-on of around 3.2%, 5.8%, 6.5%, and 7.1%, respectively after 1 h of immersion, respectively. At the end of the experiment (48 h), these values were increased to 4.2%, 6.9%, 7.4%, and 8.4% for PCL, plasma-treated PCL, SAM1, and SAM5, respectively.

Fig. 1D shows the hydrolytic degradation of PCL-based scaffolds. The hydrolytic degradation of plasma-treated samples was higher than the pure PCL sample. At the beginning, SAM samples exhibited a higher rate of weight loss. The % weight loss gradually increased from the first week and reached the maximum level at the end of the test period (8 weeks). There was a significant difference between plasma-treated PCL and SAM5 from the second week until the last time point.

Cytotoxicity assay

MTT assessment was used to investigate the cytocompatibility of 3D-printed porous scaffolds (Fig. 2A). The plasma-treated PCL scaffold was considered as the control group. There was no remarkable difference between the SAM groups and the control group on day 1. According to the MTT results of days 3 and 5, the surface modification of 3D-printed scaffolds with SAM5 could significantly promote the cell viability of WJ-MSCs.

Osteogenic differentiation evaluation

ALP activity and calcium deposition assessments

Figure 2B depicts the ALP activity of WJ-MSCs, cultured on 3D-printed scaffolds after 7, 14, and 21 days. Although there were no substantial differences between SAM-coated scaffolds and the control group on days 7 and 21, on day 14, SAM5 significantly improved the ALP activity of WJ-MSCs.

The calcium content of the WJ-MSCs cultured on the PCL-based scaffolds was assessed for plasma-treated PCL, SAM1, and SAM5 (see FIG. 2C). After 7 days of the osteogenic cell culture substitution, there was no remarkable difference between the calcium content of SAM-coated scaffolds with plasma-treated PCL. On day 14, this trend remained similar to day 7. In the third week, both SAM-coated 3D-printed scaffolds represented a substantial difference compared to plasma-treated PCL as the control group.

Gene expression analysis

To further clarify whether the fabricated scaffolds have the potency to provide a desirable osteogenic differentiation, the expression levels of three mRNA markers including ALP, osteonectin, and osteocalcin were evaluated by employing a real-time PCR assessment on days 7, 14, and 21 (Fig. 2D-F). ALP as an early and medium marker of osteogenesis hydrolyzes monophosphate esters at a high pH level21. In contrast, osteocalcin and osteonectin can be considered as the late osteogenic markers that play a crucial role in the mineralization and initial nucleation of hydroxyapatite22. Also, osteonectin regulates the initial levels of crystal growth23 by binding to Ca2+. As depicted in Fig. 2E, there was a statistically remarkable increment at the mRNA level of both osteonectin and osteocalcin in SAM5 samples on day 21 and ALP on day 14. In contrast, SAM1 did not provide significant mRNA level promotion at any time point.

Among SAM-coated scaffolds, SAM 5 as the optimized one was selected for this step. On day 30, the animals were sacrificed and the mentioned analyses were performed. Discussion

3D printing, a promising approach for manufacturing scaffolds for bone tissue engineering, is a suitable method for the temporary repair of larger bone defects with regenerative potential. The cost-effectiveness and easy workflow are the main origins that make it a desirable approach24. PCL is one of the most usable biodegradable and biocompatible synthetic polyesters that has been utilized for the fabrication of bone tissue engineering scaffolds25. Physical and chemical surface modification of biomaterials can promote cell-biomaterial interaction24. The positive impact of AM-based products on the improvement of cell viability and osteogenesis has been shown earlier15,16. The ECM of the AM serves as a reservoir for various growth factors and cytokines, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) that can stimulate osteogenesis26. SAM also contains a range of growth factors, such as bone morphogenetic protein 5 (BMP5), bone morphogenetic protein 7 (BMP7), fibroblast growth factor 4 (FGF4), Insulin-like growth factor binding protein (IGFBP1–6), transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFβ1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), which are involved in bone regeneration via various pathways17,27. In this study, we used SAM as a potent biological surface modifier to mimic the physicochemical behavior of natural ECM and as a promoter of osteogenesis. The osteoconductivity potential of SAM on WJ-MSCs was investigated through in vitro and in vivo studies. However, SAM coating method and stability could be refined in further studies, using EDC/NHS, glutaraldehyde, or Genipin crosslinking methods or covalent surface bonding methods, such as Surface functionalization of the scaffold with polydop-amine28.

Sangbae Park et al. showed that the oxygen plasma treatment made micropores on the surface of PCL scaffolds29.The formation of these micropores can improve the stability of SAM coating on the scaffold surface. Although, the ATR-FTIR results showed no remarkable peak associated with the oxygen plasma treatment on the pure PCL scaffolds. It may originate in the fact that only the superficial layers of the scaffolds are modified by plasma treatment while the penetration depth of ATR-FTIR is remarkably larger (around 600 nm)30. According to the water uptake assessment results, oxygen plasma exposure could improve the hydrophilicity of fabricated scaffolds. Therefore, oxygen plasma treatment can be an effective approach for modifying the hydrophobic nature of pure PCL31. Through hydrolytic degradation, the ester bonds in PCL chains are cleaved which leads to the formation of water-soluble degradation products such as 6-hydroxycaproic acid32. The hydrolytic degradation of PCL is performed via surface or bulk-degradation pathways33. Plasma-treated samples demonstrated higher hydrolytic degradation than the pure PCL sample mainly due to the hydrophilic modification of PCL and micropores formation (see Fig. 2). The slow rate of PCL hydrolysis can be accelerated by physical or chemical modification of PCL as a result of the higher penetration rate of water molecules34. In several studies, it was shown that the presence of hydrophilic agents could enhance the degradation rate of scaffolds35,36. The higher weight loss rate at the beginning for SAM samples can be rooted in the partial detachment of surface coating. Majidnia et al. showed that the weight loss rate in AM-coated PCL scaffolds was higher than in other groups because of coating removal within the first three days of immersion37.

According to MTT results, the SAM coating made no disruption in natural cell attachment. Although, the employment of higher dosages of SAM (0.01 and 0.05%) as surface coating may lead to a decrease in cell attachment and cell proliferation37. This clarifies the importance of optimization of the coating process. Furthermore, There are three steps in the osteogenic differentiation process including cell proliferation, ECM secretion, and mineralization38. Each stage of this process can be identified by determined markers. Early osteoblastogenesis and detection of immature osteoblasts can be investigated by ALP activity assay38,39. The content of deposited calcium is an effective indicator for the determination of osteogenesis complementation. The biomineralization occurs in the late stages of osteogenesis8. The suitable osteogenic differentiation capacity of WJ-MSCs has been proven in previous studies40,41. Moreover, these cells provide high proliferative capacity and little immune response after transplantation42. The obtained results of ALP activity and calcium deposition assessments revealed that the surface treatment of 3D-printed scaffolds with SAM can significantly improve the process of osteogenic differentiation. The result of ALP activity and calcium deposition assays were in accordance with previous pertinent works and substantiated that the amniotic membrane products can provide desirable osteoconductivity15,16. The cellular procedures, happening during osteogenesis can be followed by gene expression investigations. The results of real-time PCR assessment revealed the importance of optimization of the coating process. To the best of our knowledge, the present study introduces SAM as a promising osteoconductive candidate for the first time for bone tissue engineering purposes that can pave the way for future studies although it was previously applied for wound healing improvement17.

According to the H&E staining results, there are significant increase in the osteoblast numbers of SAM5 group compared to the control group. The results of the quantitative evaluation of Masson’s trichrome staining through image analysis showed that the presence of the biological surface modifier could substantially promote bone formation in SAM5. The results of micro-CT showed that ossification can be promoted in the presence of the biological surface modifier. In a study, ε-poly-L-lysine (EPL) scaffolds coated with polycaprolactone/hydroxyapatite (PCL/HA) showed enhanced bone repair capacity when it was transplanted into rabbit calvarial bone defects at 4 and 8 weeks43. Similarly, HA and bioglass (BG) bioceramics coating on 3D PCL scaffolds showed improved calvarial bone regeneration over 30 days in vivo assessments44. Ebrahimi et al. also reported that surface modified PCL scaffolds with HA and collagen type I (COL) promoted osteogenic differentiation31. The results of our study proved the potential of SAM5 scaffolds in bone tissue engineering applications. Furthermore, the results of real-time PCR of in vivo assessments were consistent with in vitro analyses. Therefore, the introduced cost-effective, post-fabrication surface treatment approach in this study could successfully improve the osteogenesis properties of fabricated scaffolds and can be a promising approach for future studies in the field of bone tissue engineering.

Conclusion

The introduction of cost-effective, post-fabrication treatment of 3D-printed PCL scaffolds as a potential hydrophobic biodegradable synthetic polymer for biomedical applications is a real challenge. In this study, we proposed a post-fabrication approach to address this issue. For this purpose, SAM was prepared and employed as the biological surface modifier of FDM-based 3D-printed bone tissue engineering scaffolds. The results of in vitro studies showed that the optimized content of SAM could improve the osteogenesis potency of fabricated scaffolds. Also, the results of in vivo studies in terms of histological, micro-CT, and real-time PCR assessments showed that the SAM5-coated samples promote ossification in the animal models. The results of the present study provide a promising solution for the future of bone tissue engineering scaffolds.
