Multi-source domain generalization with few-shot fine-tuning (MSDG-FT) for cross-dataset EEG mental workload classification
Authors: Abinaya G, Dinakaran K
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEEG-based mental workload (MWL) classifiers consistently achieve high within-dataset accuracy but collapse when applied across datasets recorded under different paradigms or hardware. This cross-domain generalisation gap limits real-world deployment of passive brain-computer interfaces. We evaluate transfer strategies across three publicly available EEG-MWL datasets — CogBCI (29 subjects, 3 sessions), Neuro2021 (15 subjects), and STEW — revealing a mean within-domain accuracy of 78.8% versus cross-domain accuracy of only 44.0%, a gap of 34.8 percentage points. We propose Multi-Source Domain Generalisation with Few-Shot Fine-Tuning (MSDG-FT), which reduces this gap to 6.6 percentage points using as few as 50 labelled calibration samples. Cross-session drift on CogBCI is further characterised across all six session-pair directions, showing near-chance baseline accuracy (36.0%) that recovers to 51.6% with minimal calibration.•A 3 × 3 cross-domain transfer matrix quantifies generalisation failure across three heterogeneous EEG-MWL datasets and establishes a reproducible benchmark for future methods.•Multi-source pre-training combined with few-shot target-domain fine-tuning (MSDG-FT) closes the 34.8% transfer gap to 6.6% using only 50 labelled samples from the target domain.•Random calibration (20 samples) matches sophisticated confidence-weighted selection (p = 0.28), demonstrating simple baselines suffice. Cross-session benefits vary by dataset: CogBCI +15.6%, Neuro2021 +3.5%, indicating task-dependent effectiveness.Keywords: Mental workload, EEG, Cross-domain transfer, Domain adaptation, Multi-source learning, Brain-computer interface, Few-shot fine-tuning

Background

Mental workload (MWL) — defined as the portion of an operator's information-processing capacity demanded by a task — is a critical variable in safety-sensitive environments including aviation, automotive control, healthcare, and industrial monitoring [[1], [2]]. Electroencephalography provides a non-invasive, high-temporal-resolution window into MWL-related neural dynamics, with spectral signatures in the theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–13 Hz) bands serving as established neurophysiological correlates [[3], [4], [5]]. EEG-based passive brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) offer continuous, non-intrusive monitoring of cognitive states without requiring active user engagement, making them particularly suited for real-world deployment [6].

Despite impressive within-dataset classification accuracies reported in the literature — frequently exceeding 70–90% — EEG-MWL classifiers are rarely evaluated across datasets. When they are, performance collapses dramatically. This cross-domain gap arises from at least three compounding sources of variance: (1) electrode configuration and amplifier hardware differences that alter signal amplitude, impedance, and frequency response; (2) task paradigm differences that engage distinct cognitive sub-processes and evoke non-overlapping spatial activation patterns; and (3) inter-subject neurophysiological variability that interacts differently with each dataset's demographic composition [7].

Existing solutions largely address cross-subject or cross-session transfer within a single dataset through domain adaptation techniques [8] such as Euclidean alignment [9,10], covariate shift correction [11], and adversarial training [12]. Cross-dataset transfer has received comparatively little systematic attention, and the few published studies rarely benchmark more than two datasets or report ablations on calibration sample requirements [13].

This methods article addresses this gap. We quantify the cross-domain transfer problem across three diverse EEG-MWL datasets using a comprehensive transfer matrix, evaluate multi-source pre-training as a domain-generalization strategy, and characterize the sample efficiency of few-shot fine-tuning. Our results provide the first 3 × 3 cross-domain transfer matrix for EEG-MWL classification and a reproducible protocol for benchmarking future generalization methods.

Method details

Datasets

Neuro2021 [14]: A controlled EEG dataset comprising 15 subjects performing a binary cognitive load task. After feature extraction and standardization, each recording is represented as a 2080-dimensional feature vector. The within-domain classification task is binary (high vs. low workload), with 8940 high-workload and 4470 low-workload samples. The dataset exhibits high intra-dataset separability, achieving a within-domain MLP accuracy of 74.2%, and near-zero feature sparsity indicating dense, informative representations.

CogBCI [15]: A large-scale, longitudinal EEG-MWL dataset with 29 subjects each completing three recording sessions (ses-S1, ses-S2, ses-S3) of an n-back task at three load levels. The task is 3-class mental workload (low, medium, high), yielding 164,128 total samples with near-equal class distribution (54,378 / 54,773 / 54,977). Session sizes ranged from 53,806 to 56,023 samples. The multi-session structure makes this dataset uniquely suited for cross-session drift analysis alongside the cross-domain experiments.

STEW (Simultaneous Task EEG Workload) [16]: A binary EEG-MWL dataset comprising 13,320 samples with a balanced 50/50 class split, recorded during a simultaneous multitasking paradigm. STEW features exhibit 95.8% sparsity (compared to near-zero sparsity in Neuro2021 and CogBCI), and the within-domain classification accuracy reaches 92.6% — substantially higher than the other two datasets — suggesting that the multitasking paradigm yields highly separable neural signatures. This sparsity also contributes to asymmetric transfer behaviour in the cross-domain matrix.

Feature standardization and preprocessing

All three datasets were harmonized to a common 2080-dimensional feature representation. For STEW, which originally contained 88 features, zero-padding was applied to reach the target dimensionality. Per-domain z-score standardization was applied independently — scaling parameters (mean, standard deviation) were computed exclusively on each domain's training split and applied to the validation and test splits. Missing values were imputed using a 5-sample median filter followed by forward fill (maximum gap cap of 16 samples). This strict no-leakage protocol prevents any information from future time points or test-set statistics from influencing the model.

Classifier architecture

We employ a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with the following architecture: Input(2080) → Dense(512, BatchNorm, ReLU, Dropout 0.5) → Dense(256, BatchNorm, ReLU, Dropout 0.5) → Dense(128, ReLU) → Output(C, Softmax), where C is the number of classes. Training uses the Adam optimiser (learning rate 1 × 10⁻³, batch size 64, up to 100 epochs) with early stopping on validation loss (patience 15). An architecture depth ablation (2-layer: 79.7%, 3-layer: 79.4%, 4-layer: 79.8%) confirmed no meaningful difference across depths; the 3-layer configuration was used for all reported experiments. A dropout sweep confirmed 0.5 as optimal (77.6% vs. 77.0% at 0.3 and 75.6% at 0.7).

Baseline comparisons were conducted against Random Forest, XGBoost, EEGNet [17], ShallowConvNet [10], DeepConvNet [10], and compact convolutional architectures [18]. The MLP matched or exceeded all baselines on Neuro2021 (74.2% vs. XGBoost 67.7%, Random Forest 65.8%). EEGNet (33.3%) and DeepConvNet (50.1%) underperformed due to the flattened feature representation not preserving the spatiotemporal 2D EEG structure required by convolutional architectures.

MSDG-FT algorithm

The proposed MSDG-FT method proceeds in three phases: (1) standardized preprocessing of all source and target domains independently; (2) equal-weighted multi-source pre-training on a combined 30,000-sample dataset; and (3) optional few-shot fine-tuning of the final layers using K labelled target samples. The full procedure is formalized in Algorithm 1.

Cross-Domain transfer matrix protocol

We construct a 3 × 3 transfer matrix by training an MLP on the full training split of each source dataset and evaluating directly (zero fine-tuning, K = 0) on the test split of each target dataset. All datasets use the same 2080-dimensional standardized features and identical MLP architecture. Within-domain entries (diagonal) reflect standard held-out test accuracy; off-diagonal entries reflect zero-shot cross-domain transfer accuracy. This matrix serves as the primary baseline for quantifying the transfer gap and is visualized as a heatmap in Fig. 1 (left).

t-SNE visualization of the 2080-dimensional EEG feature space sampled from all three datasets. Left: points are colored by domain (Neuro2021 = blue, CogBCI = orange, STEW = green), revealing three clearly separated clusters and confirming substantial inter-domain distributional shift. Right: the same projection colored by binary workload level (low = blue, high = red) shows that workload classes are distributed across all domain clusters and cannot be separated by a domain-specific boundary, explaining the failure of zero-shot cross-domain transfer.

Multi-Source sampling strategies

Three sampling strategies were evaluated for constructing the combined training set: equal sampling (10,000 samples per domain, N = 30,000 total), proportional sampling (samples proportional to domain size), and weighted sampling (samples weighted inversely proportional to within-domain baseline accuracy). Equal sampling achieved the best average cross-domain accuracy (66.9% average across three targets; 74.3% average within-domain) and is used as the MSDG baseline throughout.

Cross-Session drift analysis

Cross-session drift on CogBCI was characterized across all six directed session pairs (S1→S2, S1→S3, S2→S1, S2→S3, S3→S1, S3→S2) for n = 29 subjects. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed significant distributional shift in 100% of the 2080 features between sessions (p < 0.05), with a mean inter-session feature mean difference of 0.383 (S1 mean: 0.159, S2 mean: −0.224), confirming that drift is a genuine signal-level phenomenon. Random calibration (20 labelled target-session samples, fine-tuning as per Algorithm 1 Phase 3) was evaluated as a minimal-intervention baseline.

Method validation

t-SNE visualization of domain separation

Fig. 1 presents a t-SNE projection of 2080-dimensional features from all three domains. The left panel, colored by domain, shows three well-separated clusters confirming strong inter-domain distributional shift — a direct visual explanation of the 34.8 percentage-point transfer gap. The right panel, colored by workload class, reveals that low and high workload samples are interleaved across all three domain clusters. This domain-class entanglement means that a classifier trained to separate workload levels in one domain learns domain-specific discriminative directions that do not generalize to other domains. Together, the two panels motivate the need for domain-invariant representation learning as a prerequisite for cross-dataset MWL generalization.

Baseline method comparison

Fig. 2 compares within-domain accuracy on Neuro2021 across all evaluated methods. The SimpleMLP baseline achieves 74.2%, outperforming all classical ML methods (XGBoost: 67.7%, Random Forest: 65.8%) and all deep CNN architectures (ShallowConvNet: 68.0%, DeepConvNet: 50.1%, EEGNet: 33.3%). The multi-source and MSDG-FT variants achieve 67.0% on Neuro2021, a reduction of 7.2 percentage points from the within-domain baseline, representing the acceptable cost of domain generalization. CNN-based methods underperform because the feature vectors used in these experiments are pre-extracted and flattened; the 2D spatiotemporal structure required by convolutional layers is not preserved in this representation.

MSDG-FT performance

Table 2 presents the full method comparison. Multi-source training (equal sampling) reduces the transfer gap from 34.8% to 6.7%, achieving consistent cross-domain accuracy of approximately 66–67% across all three target datasets. This comes at a cost of 4.9 percentage points of average within-domain accuracy (78.6% → 73.7%), an inherent trade-off of domain-generalization approaches. MSDG-FT (50 samples) further closes the gap marginally to 6.6% while matching multi-source cross-domain performance. Critically, multi-source training transforms the two most severe failure cases: CogBCI→Neuro improves from 34.2% to 66.5% and STEW→Neuro improves from 36.2% to 65.5% — gains of over 30 percentage points in both cases.

Ablation studies

Fig. 3 presents five ablation studies examining key design choices. Ablation 1 (number of source domains) shows that combining all three source domains does not always outperform a single strong source — STEW alone yields 44.9% cross-domain accuracy to CogBCI, while adding Neuro2021 reduces this to 33.3%, suggesting that domain combination can introduce competing gradients when source domains are highly dissimilar. Ablation 2 (fine-tuning sample size) reveals the steep gain from 0 to 10 samples (+15.4%) followed by diminishing returns after 50 samples, confirming the sample efficiency of the fine-tuning phase. Ablation 3 shows that equal sampling (74.3% average within-domain) outperforms proportional (70.3%) and weighted (73.2%) strategies. Ablations 4 and 5 confirm that architecture depth (79.4–79.8%) and dropout rate (77.0–77.6% across 0.3–0.9) have negligible effects on performance, validating the robustness of the design choices.

Negative result: confidence-weighted calibration

An Adaptive Confidence-Weighted Calibration (ACWC) strategy was evaluated that preferentially selects medium-confidence target samples (confidence ∈ [0.5, 0.8]) for fine-tuning, weighted inversely by prediction confidence. The hypothesis was that selecting informative, non-trivial samples would outperform random selection. Across 29 subjects and calibration sizes of 10, 20, and 30, ACWC did not consistently outperform random sampling. At calibration size 20, ACWC achieved 44.8% ± 5.7% versus random calibration at 48.8% ± 7.2% (t(28) = −4.15, p = 0.0003, random significantly better). A hyperparameter sweep over confidence ranges and weight ratios found no configuration that reliably surpassed random selection. This negative result suggests that distributional shift in EEG-MWL is sufficiently severe that model confidence is not a reliable proxy for sample informativeness during calibration. Simple random sampling is thus recommended as the more robust and computationally cheaper calibration strategy for practical deployment.

Three factors likely explain ACWC's failure. First, medium-confidence samples (0.5–0.8) in severely shifted domains may represent outliers rather than true decision-boundary cases—the source model's confidence estimates are unreliable when the domain gap is large (baseline cross-session accuracy ∼35%). Second, the 20-sample budget is insufficient to reliably identify boundary regions; confidence-based selection requires larger calibration sets (K ≥ 100) to overcome sampling variance. Third, random sampling provides more uniform coverage of the target distribution, preventing over-concentration on potentially unrepresentative medium-confidence clusters.

Practical implication: For small-budget scenarios (K < 50), practitioners should prefer random calibration over sophisticated selection strategies. This finding simplifies implementation (no confidence thresholding, no iterative selection) while maintaining or improving performance. Random calibration also proved robust across all six CogBCI session pairs (Table 4), achieving consistent +12–23% improvements with minimal implementation complexity.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, cross-domain experiments used a fixed MLP architecture; Riemannian geometry-based classifiers or transformer models may exhibit different transfer characteristics and are recommended for future comparison. Second, alignment-based domain adaptation methods (CORAL, DANN) were not included as explicit baselines; these represent important future comparisons. Third, STEW's extreme feature sparsity (95.8%) creates an atypical within-domain advantage that may not reflect typical EEG recording conditions; results on this dataset should be interpreted with this caveat. Fourth, subjects 17–24 in CogBCI showed near-identical per-subject statistics in some conditions, warranting verification of data integrity in those recordings. Fifth, statistical power of cross-domain comparisons is limited by the three-dataset design; replication with additional datasets is required to draw general conclusions.

Ethics statements

This study used only pre-existing, publicly available EEG datasets (CogBCI, Neuro2021, STEW). No new human subjects data were collected. All datasets were used in accordance with their respective terms of access. No personally identifiable information was processed or stored.
