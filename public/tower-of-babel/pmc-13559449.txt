A drainage-analgesia integrated chest tube for pain control after thoracoscopic lung resection
Authors: Junichi Murakami
Published: 2026

Pain control after thoracic surgery is not just about comfort. It affects a patient’s ability to take deep breaths, clear secretions, and get out of bed, which directly impacts whether the recovery outlined by enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways actually occurs. While minimally invasive approaches have lessened incision trauma, one source of pain has persisted through every technical improvement: the chest drain. The trial reported by Tang and colleagues in a recent issue of Journal of Thoracic Disease (1) attempts to treat that source of pain directly.

The drain is often overlooked as an analgesic target because it is common. However, it lies against the wound, the parietal pleura, and the intercostal bundles, and could contribute to both resting and dynamic pain. Refai and colleagues (2) observed that pain scores decreased by about 40% and forced expiratory volume increased by roughly 13% after tube removal. That study was observational and evaluated patients once they had recovered enough to remove the drain, so it can’t fully separate the effects of removal from the passage of time; however, current ERAS guidance for lung surgery (3,4) reflects the same concern, recommending a small-bore tube, avoiding routine suction, and prompt removal.

These recommendations all function by limiting exposure to the tube, either by using a smaller drain or by omitting the drain altogether in selected patients (5,6). The complementary approach is to treat the pain where it occurs while the tube remains in place, by administering local anesthetic into the pleural space. This is an old idea whose history warrants more attention than it usually receives. Intrapleural analgesia was thoroughly studied in the late 1980s and 1990s and then quietly dropped from routine practice. It is often assumed that it failed due to logistical reasons: a second catheter, a separate pump, one more thing to dislodge. The contemporary literature suggests a less convenient explanation: efficacy varied widely between studies, drug distribution proved unpredictable, and plasma concentrations after interpleural injection were among the highest of any regional technique (7,8). Individual trials were positive (9-11), but the principle was never consistently established enough to make delivery the only remaining obstacle.

This distinction determines how the current trial should be interpreted. If the barrier is workflow, an engineering fix would be enough. If it’s pharmacological, meaning whether an effective concentration can be reached at the relevant neural structures, then no elegant design can solve it, and a trial that doesn’t measure drug exposure cannot determine which is the case.

Tang and colleagues (1) report a prospective, single-center, open-label randomized controlled trial of a device designed for this problem: a drainage-analgesia integrated (DAI) chest tube. Instead of adding a second device, they redesigned the drain. A sealed infusion lumen runs within the tube wall alongside three fan-shaped drainage channels; local anesthetic exits through microporous side holes, and a sealing plug at the tip directs it away from the tube end. Targeted delivery and closed negative-pressure drainage coexist within one tube, inserted through the usual observation port.

Forty-seven patients undergoing uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) lung resection were randomly assigned to receive either a DAI tube (n=22) or a standard drainage (STD) tube (n=25). Both groups received identical multimodal systemic analgesia, including intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with a continuous background infusion of sufentanil at 2 µg/h. The DAI group also received a programmed intrapleural infusion of 100 mg of ropivacaine and 400 mg of lidocaine diluted to 300 mL, administered as a 20-mL bolus followed by 20 mL every four hours. The primary endpoint was the visual analog scale (VAS) score at 24 hours. The DAI group exhibited lower VAS scores at 24 and 48 hours, both at rest and on coughing, fewer PCA activations (0.1±0.4 vs. 0.9±1.1; P=0.004), and higher scores on the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) at 48 hours and at the time of tube removal.

Negative findings should be considered equally important. Total sufentanil consumption was similar at 24 and 48 hours (P=0.85 and P=0.49); similarly, total drainage, time to tube removal, length of stay, rescue analgesic use, and adverse events showed no significant differences. QoR-15 scores at 24 hours, the primary endpoint, did not differ (P=0.46). VAS scores also showed no difference at 1, 6, or 12 hours, or at the time of tube removal. All endpoints that distinguished the groups were reported by the patients; every measurable endpoint independent of the patient was null. In an unblinded trial, this pattern could indicate either a true effect too small to influence behavior or an expectation effect.

Before addressing bias, however, there is a fundamental question that determines how the results should be interpreted: was the administered dose capable of producing the effect attributed to it?

The prescribed solution contains 100 mg of ropivacaine and 400 mg of lidocaine in 300 mL, which means ropivacaine 0.033% and lidocaine 0.13%. Each 20 mL pulse therefore delivers approximately 6.7 mg of ropivacaine and 26.7 mg of lidocaine into the pleural cavity. In comparison, the regimens on which the principle is based used bupivacaine 0.25% (9), which is an order of magnitude more concentrated, and a single intercostal nerve block typically involves 15–25 mg of ropivacaine per level. The DAI protocol administers less than half the dose of one intercostal level, spread across the entire pleural surface, into a cavity under continuous suction.

The proposed mechanism makes this arithmetic hard to reconcile. Local anesthetic in the pleural space will affect parietal pleural nociceptors, which could plausibly relieve pain caused by contact between the tube and the pleura. However, somatic pain from the port wound and intercostal neuralgia originate away from the pleural surface, and relieving them requires retrograde diffusion to multiple intercostal nerves, producing the multilevel or paravertebral-like effect described in older literature (7,12). These descriptions are based on studies using concentrated agents in undrained pleural spaces. Whether 6.7 mg of ropivacaine, immediately exposed to suction, reaches an effective perineural concentration is not just unproven; it appears unlikely.

This also reverses the usual safety concern. At these concentrations and infusion rates, roughly 1.7 mg/h of ropivacaine and 6.9 mg/h of lidocaine, systemic toxicity remains a theoretical rather than a practical concern. The pharmacokinetic sampling question is not whether plasma levels were dangerously high, but whether pleural concentrations ever became significant (8). Dermatomal sensory testing, plasma sampling, or imaging of drug distribution would have clarified this. None was performed.

A related and more specific challenge concerns fluid balance. Under the protocol, the DAI group receives about 265 mL of solution by 48 hours and, at the average time to tube removal of 71 hours, approximately 365 mL, exceeding the 300 mL prepared. This suggests either the infusion was stopped early or the reservoir was replaced, neither of which is explained. Nonetheless, total drainage was nearly identical between groups: 196.4±15.6 vs. 194.6±13.0 mL (P=0.68). Several hundred milliliters of instilled fluid are unaccounted for. If the infused volume was subtracted from the recorded output, the method is not specified; if it was not, then nearly all of the instillate was absorbed, which directly affects systemic exposure and the duration of pleural drug contact. This is not a minor detail: the fate of the infused volume is crucial.

A third unreported variable might be as important as the drug itself. The DAI tube is a multi-lumen device with three fan-shaped drainage channels and an embedded infusion lumen; the comparator is a conventional drain. The external diameter, wall stiffness, and side-hole configuration of either tube are not reported. Since reducing tube-related pain with smaller-caliber drains is one of the better-supported findings in this field and is included in ERAS guidance (3,4), a difference in tube dimensions between groups alone could explain the observed pain difference, with no pharmacological effect at all.

Against this background, the design limitations the authors openly acknowledge become more significant. The trial was single-center, open-label, and small. The tubes are visibly different, so patients, surgeons, anesthesiologists, and nurses all knew the allocation, and the endpoints that distinguished the groups were precisely those most influenced by expectation. The control group received nothing in the pleural space, not even saline, so the comparison is not device versus device but tube plus drug plus pump plus a four-hourly staff interaction versus tube alone. Five authors, including corresponding authors, hold a utility-model patent on the device. Together, these features set the limits on what the results can support.

The statistical presentation complicates this further. The primary endpoint is the mean VAS at 24 hours, but the protocol does not specify whether this score was measured at rest or on coughing; both are reported, so the primary comparison is effectively duplicated. The sample size calculation specifies alpha, power, and a dropout rate, but it does not include an assumed difference, standard deviation, or resulting target number, making verification impossible. Results are presented as P values throughout without showing mean differences or confidence intervals, which makes it difficult to determine whether the VAS difference approaches a minimal clinically important difference of roughly 1.3–2.0 points, or whether the QoR-15 difference reaches its threshold of 6–8 points. Secondary comparisons are made without adjustments for multiple testing. The PCA outcome, which carries significant interpretive weight, is heavily zero-inflated: 14 of 25 control patients never pressed the button, and the difference amounts to fewer than 1 additional press over 2 days.

The authors’ interpretation of the opioid data is correct and can be refined. Total sufentanil use was the same because both groups received the same continuous background infusion, which provides opioids regardless of demand. However, the assertion that total opioid exposure remained unchanged cannot be confirmed: rescue paracetamol-dihydrocodeine was only recorded as a categorical variable and was not converted into morphine equivalents. It was used by 1 of 22 DAI patients compared to 6 of 25 controls (P=0.10). Therefore, total opioid exposure was not accurately measured and should be reported in morphine milligram equivalents.

Safety warrants a similarly cautious review. With 22 patients exposed, the absence of device-related events only rules out complication rates above about 13%. This is a conduit open from the exterior into the pleural space for three days, yet no infectious endpoint is reported: not fever, leukocyte count, empyema, or pleural culture. What can be said is that no safety signal appeared, not that safety was proven.

There is also a structural tension in the device’s clinical positioning. Intrapleural analgesia through a drain can only work while the drain is in place, and ERAS pathways are moving in the opposite direction: toward a single small tube removed on the first postoperative day or no tube at all (5,6). Here, the drains stayed in for a mean of 71 hours with a standard deviation of only 7.4 hours, a distribution suggesting removal on a fixed institutional schedule rather than in response to air leak or output. The positive result was therefore obtained under a drainage practice that the ERAS guidance cited in support of the device would regard as unnecessarily prolonged, and the more successful ERAS drain management becomes, the narrower this device’s therapeutic window. One might argue that its natural home is the patient whose drain must stay in for a prolonged air leak, but that is precisely the setting in which pleural air turnover is greatest and drug distribution is least predictable. This is a constraint on the concept, not merely on the trial.

None of this diminishes what the authors have accomplished, which is an engineering contribution rather than a pharmacological one. Every previous attempt to deliver analgesics into the pleural space required a second catheter or a dedicated pump, with the attendant handling, cost, and risk of dislodgement (9-11). By integrating the infusion channel into the drain wall, Tang and colleagues have eliminated that friction entirely: the tube is placed exactly as before, and the drug port is a standard Luer connector that ward staff can use without training. A technique that fits an existing workflow is far more likely to be adopted than one that adds steps. The device is a good solution to the delivery problem. The open question is whether that problem was ever the binding constraint.

The same logic applies to the proposed secondary uses. The authors note that the channel could function as a port for pleurodesis or for agents targeting prolonged air leaks. However, the design suggests otherwise: microporous side holes are not ideal for viscous sclerosants, and pleurodesis requires the drain to be clamped so the agent stays in place, which is the opposite of the continuous drainage the device provides.

What should the next trial look like? Its first goal is to separate the device from belief in it, and that requires more than just a two-arm comparison. A three-arm design, comparing the DAI tube with the study drug, the DAI tube with an identical volume of saline, and STD, would help distinguish the pharmacological effect from the effect of the instilled volume and the effect of knowing one is receiving something. Assessors should be blinded, the study conducted across multiple centers, and the tubes in the two DAI arms should be identical in size and construction.

Second, the trial should measure what this one inferred: dermatomal sensory levels, plasma and pleural drug concentrations, and pre-specified reporting of infused and drained volumes. Given how far the concentrations used here sit below those in the literature on which the concept rests, a dose-finding step beforehand would be reasonable. Effect sizes should then be reported with confidence intervals and interpreted against minimal clinically important differences.

Finally, the device should be tested against its peers. Contemporary thoracic analgesia increasingly relies on regional techniques such as intercostal, paravertebral, serratus anterior plane, and erector spinae plane blocks (13,14). Comparing it only to systemic opioids provides limited insight into whether the device offers additional benefits within a modern opioid-sparing approach. Its role will depend on whether it complements a regional block or can take over part of its function. Follow-up should be long enough to determine if early pain control at its source influences the development of chronic post-thoracotomy pain (15), and the analysis should also consider cost.

Tang and colleagues have taken an old idea and given it a much better delivery system. The engineering is elegant, the trial is registered and prospective, the negative findings are reported honestly, and the authors position their device with appropriate restraint. What they have not yet shown, and what this design could not show, is that the drug reaches an effective concentration, or that the observed differences in patient-reported pain reflect anything more than a visibly different tube in an unblinded study. Read as a feasibility and safety report, the trial does its job well. Read as evidence of analgesic efficacy, it is not yet persuasive. Whether the DAI tube becomes part of thoracic ERAS or remains an ingenious answer to a question that was not the limiting one will be settled by a blinded, sham-controlled, adequately dosed trial. That trial is worth doing.
