Precision Nanodelivery Enables Spatiotemporal Control of NAD⁺ Metabolism in Ageing and Disease
Authors: Zerong Pei, Xuemei Wang, Fengni Liang, Minyong Zhong, Qingxiang Jiang, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeclining nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) represents a conserved metabolic hallmark of ageing, organ dysfunction and numerous acute and chronic disorders. NAD⁺ metabolism exhibits pronounced spatial, cell-type and subcellular compartmentalization in pathological tissues, orchestrating DNA repair, redox homeostasis, inflammatory cascades and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Such compartment heterogeneity accounts for the inconsistent therapeutic outcomes of systemic NAD⁺ precursor supplementation, which suffers from uneven tissue distribution and insufficient subcellular metabolite restoration. While conventional precursor replenishment lays a fundamental foundation for NAD⁺ intervention, its therapeutic potency is hampered by an inability to achieve localized, time-tunable metabolic modulation. Herein, we advocate a hierarchical precision nano-delivery framework to advance NAD⁺ therapeutics beyond passive systemic supplementation toward spatiotemporally programmable metabolic regulation. Nanodelivery platforms integrate tissue penetration, cellular uptake, organelle targeting and stimulus-triggered cargo release to overcome biological barriers and match NAD⁺ modulation to defined pathological disease windows. This Review systematically summarizes cutting-edge nanodelivery strategies for tissue-, cell- and organelle-specific delivery of NAD⁺, precursors and metabolic modulators across degenerative, inflammatory and metabolic illnesses. We further discuss key translational bottlenecks, including compartment-specific biomarkers, stratified safety evaluation, rational dosage regimen, scalable production and individualized patient stratification. We argue that the next generation of NAD⁺ therapeutics will not be defined by enhanced bulk supplementation alone, but by the capacity to orchestrate precise NAD⁺ manipulation across biological space and time.Keywords: precision nanodelivery, NAD⁺ homeostasis, aging intervention, subcellular delivery, stimuli-responsive nanosystems, hierarchical multiscale delivery, mitochondrial NAD⁺ restoration

Introduction

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) functions as a central metabolic hub coordinating cellular energy metabolism, stress adaptation and repair.1,2 Through its roles as a redox cofactor and substrate for multiple NAD⁺-consuming enzymes, NAD⁺ governs diverse physiological processes ranging from mitochondrial homeostasis to inflammatory signalling.3,4 Declining NAD⁺ availability has emerged as a recurrent metabolic feature of ageing and age-related disease, and has been linked to neurodegeneration, cardiovascular injury, metabolic dysfunction, immune dysregulation and impaired tissue repair.5 This broad disease relevance has made NAD⁺ restoration therapeutically attractive.6 Yet as the biological rationale for NAD⁺ intervention has strengthened, its translational limitations have become increasingly apparent: NAD⁺ functions through multiple tissue, cellular and subcellular pools that are neither uniformly accessible nor functionally interchangeable.7,8

A major limitation lies in the mismatch between conventional NAD⁺-raising strategies and the spatially organized biology they are intended to correct.9 Direct NAD⁺ administration, precursor supplementation with nicotinamide mononucleotide or nicotinamide riboside, and pharmacological modulation of NAD⁺ biosynthesis or consumption can increase NAD⁺ availability in selected contexts.10 However, systemic intervention does not ensure restoration in the tissues, cell populations or intracellular compartments in which NAD⁺ loss drives pathology.11,12 Diseased organs contain heterogeneous vascular barriers, inflammatory microenvironments, stromal and immune cell states, and divergent NAD⁺ pools in the nucleus, cytosol and mitochondria.13 Moreover, the consequences of NAD⁺ restoration are context dependent: enhancing NAD⁺ metabolism may support repair in degenerative or injured tissues, but could also sustain unwanted cell survival, tumour metabolism or maladaptive immune activity in certain settings.14 These considerations shift the central question from whether NAD⁺ can be increased to how NAD⁺ metabolism should be controlled across tissue location, cellular identity, subcellular compartment and disease stage.15 Given these context-dependent limitations, a critical question naturally arises: how can we precisely tune NAD⁺ levels in a spatiotemporally matched manner to overcome these biological barriers? For a labile and highly polar metabolite whose function depends on where and when it becomes bioavailable, delivery is not a secondary pharmaceutical consideration but a determinant of therapeutic efficacy.16

Precision nanodelivery can address this delivery problem, but only when carrier design is matched to the biological barrier that limits therapeutic action.17,18 Nanocarriers may protect labile NAD⁺-related cargoes, tune pharmacokinetics and redirect tissue distribution.19,20 More importantly, they can be engineered to navigate sequential barriers—including vascular access, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, intracellular trafficking, organelle localization and stimulus-responsive release—to ensure that cargo reaches its intended site of action.21 For NAD⁺ therapeutics, such multiscale control is not merely a formulation advantage; it determines whether increased NAD⁺ availability is converted into a functional metabolic effect.22,23 A carrier that accumulates in the correct organ but fails to release its cargo at the right intracellular location or therapeutic time window may yield little benefit despite favourable biodistribution.24 Delivery success should therefore be evaluated by functional NAD⁺ restoration in the relevant biological compartment, rather than by circulation time, encapsulation efficiency or bulk tissue accumulation alone.

We therefore propose a hierarchical precision delivery framework for NAD⁺ nanomedicine. Rather than treating nanocarriers as interchangeable platforms, this framework organizes NAD⁺ delivery as a cascade of linked decisions across tissue, cellular, subcellular and temporal scales.25,26 At the tissue level, delivery systems must negotiate anatomical barriers and heterogeneous organ distribution.27 At the cellular level, selective uptake is needed to direct NAD⁺ modulation towards disease-relevant effector cells while limiting effects in cells where NAD⁺ enhancement may be undesirable.28 At the subcellular level, intracellular trafficking and organelle-directed strategies are required to reach NAD⁺ pools that regulate mitochondrial bioenergetics, nuclear repair responses or cytosolic redox balance.29,30 At the temporal level, programmed and microenvironment-responsive systems may align NAD⁺ modulation with dynamic disease windows—defined as the specific temporal phases during disease progression when NAD⁺ metabolic intervention is most likely to be therapeutically effective, such as the acute inflammatory phase of tissue injury or the early degenerative stage before irreversible damage occurs.31 In this framework, delivery success cannot be reduced to any single design variable; rather, it emerges from the alignment of carrier properties, biological barriers and therapeutic objectives across scales.

This is therefore not organized as a simple inventory of nanocarrier platforms or NAD⁺ precursors.32,33 Unlike previous reviews that primarily describe NAD⁺ biosynthesis pathways, precursor pharmacology or nanocarrier categories, this work focuses on the delivery logic required to translate NAD⁺ biology into therapeutic precision.34 We first summarize the biological basis of NAD⁺ decline and the major classes of NAD⁺-related therapeutic cargoes. We then examine nanodelivery strategies across tissue, cellular and organelle scales, emphasizing the barrier addressed, the biological rationale and the limitations of each approach.35 Finally, we discuss the translational requirements for NAD⁺ nanodelivery, including compartment-resolved biomarkers, rational dose design, context-specific safety, scalable manufacturing and patient selection. We argue that the field must move from generalized supplementation towards controlled NAD⁺ metabolic intervention across biological space and time (Figure 1).

Biological Basis of NAD⁺ Decline and the Limits of Conventional Replenishment

NAD⁺ Homeostasis is Governed by Synthesis, Consumption and Compartment-Specific Availability

NAD⁺ homeostasis is not a static biochemical parameter, but a dynamically regulated property of cellular metabolism.36,37 Cellular NAD⁺ pools are maintained through the de novo pathway from tryptophan, the Preiss–Handler pathway from nicotinic acid and salvage pathways that recycle nicotinamide and related precursors, including nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN).38,39 In many mammalian tissues, the salvage pathway provides a major route for NAD⁺ maintenance, with nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT) catalysing a rate-limiting step in nicotinamide recycling.40 However, the relative contribution of these pathways varies across tissues, cell states and pathological contexts, a feature that partly explains why systemic precursor administration produces heterogeneous biological responses.

This biosynthetic network is counterbalanced by continuous NAD⁺ consumption.41 Sirtuins, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs), CD38, SARM1 and other NAD⁺-dependent enzymes cleave or consume NAD⁺ to coordinate chromatin regulation, DNA repair, calcium signalling, immune activation, axonal degeneration and metabolic adaptation.42–45 As a result, NAD⁺ pools are shaped not only by precursor availability, but also by enzymatic demand, stress intensity, inflammatory tone and tissue-specific metabolic state.46 NAD⁺ homeostasis therefore reflects a dynamic equilibrium between synthesis and depletion rather than a simple measure of metabolite abundance.

A further layer of complexity arises from subcellular compartmentalization. Distinct NAD⁺ pools exist in the nucleus, cytosol and mitochondria, where they support non-identical metabolic and signalling functions.47 These pools are maintained by compartment-specific enzymes, transport processes and local precursor availability, and they are not freely interchangeable.48 For example, nuclear, cytosolic and mitochondrial NAD⁺ pools are supported by distinct NMNAT isoforms and are differentially consumed by compartment-localized enzymes such as PARPs in the nucleus and sirtuins in mitochondria; mitochondrial NAD⁺ availability additionally depends on dedicated transport mechanisms.49,50 Consequently, restoration of total cellular NAD⁺ does not necessarily imply rescue of the NAD⁺ pool that is functionally limiting in a given disease context.51 This point is central to therapeutic design because many pathological processes are driven by local, rather than global, NAD⁺ insufficiency.

NAD⁺ decline during ageing and disease is mechanistically multifactorial.52 Reduced biosynthetic capacity is one contributor: NAMPT expression or activity decreases in several tissues during ageing and metabolic stress, weakening the efficiency of nicotinamide salvage.53 Mitochondrial dysfunction, nutrient imbalance, circadian disruption and chronic inflammation can further compromise biosynthetic flux and reduce the capacity of cells to sustain NAD⁺ pools. At the same time, NAD⁺ consumption often increases under pathological stress.51 DNA damage activates PARPs, and sustained PARP activity can accelerate NAD⁺ depletion during repair responses. Sterile inflammation and immune activation induce CD38 in multiple cell types, thereby lowering intra- and extracellular NAD⁺ availability.54 In the nervous system, SARM1-dependent NAD⁺ cleavage contributes to axonal degeneration after injury or stress. SARM1 differs fundamentally from PARPs and CD38: it remains catalytically quiescent under basal conditions but undergoes robust activation upon axonal damage, resulting in rapid, near-complete local NAD⁺ breakdown within injured neurites. Since SARM1-mediated NAD⁺ depletion occurs specifically within spatially confined axonal compartments, systemic NAD⁺ precursor supplementation often cannot reverse local metabolic collapse, emphasizing the demand for neuro-targeted delivery systems capable of achieving sub-axonal NAD⁺ restoration. Beyond CD38 and SARM1, other NAD⁺ glycohydrolases such as BST1 (CD157) also participate in extracellular and cell-surface NAD⁺ turnover; BST1 exhibits distinct tissue expression profiles and substrate preferences, adding further complexity to tissue- and cell-type-specific NAD⁺ metabolism. These processes often reinforce one another: DNA damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress and inflammatory signalling create a metabolic environment in which NAD⁺ demand rises while replenishment becomes progressively less efficient.

The consequences of NAD⁺ decline extend beyond redox imbalance. NAD⁺ insufficiency impairs mitochondrial respiration, weakens adaptive stress responses, reduces sirtuin activity, compromises DNA repair and promotes inflammatory and degenerative phenotypes. In this sense, NAD⁺ depletion is not merely a biomarker of biological ageing, but a mechanism through which mitochondrial failure, impaired repair, inflammatory activation and tissue degeneration become functionally linked. This centrality explains the therapeutic interest in NAD⁺ restoration, but it also underscores why systemic supplementation alone may be inadequate for complex disease states.

Conventional NAD⁺ Replenishment Strategies: Strong Rationale, Limited Translational Performance

Given the broad biological significance of NAD⁺ decline, therapeutic replenishment has been pursued through direct NAD⁺ administration, precursor supplementation and pharmacological modulation of NAD⁺ biosynthesis or consumption. Commonly used precursors include nicotinamide, NR and NMN, whereas emerging strategies include reduced precursors and inhibitors of NAD⁺-consuming enzymes.55 These approaches are attractive because they exploit endogenous metabolic pathways and, in selected experimental settings, can elevate tissue NAD⁺ levels and improve physiological readouts. They have therefore established NAD⁺ restoration as a rational intervention strategy in preclinical and early translational studies (Table 1).

However, the translational performance of these approaches has remained uneven. Early-phase human clinical trials investigating oral NR and NMN have yielded encouraging yet heterogeneous results across metabolic, cardiovascular and neurodegenerative indications. In metabolic research, supplementation can elevate circulating NAD⁺ metabolites and improve markers of insulin sensitivity in some aged or prediabetic cohorts, but responses vary substantially between individuals.56,57 Cardiovascular studies demonstrate modest improvements in endothelial function in older adults, though consistent myocardial NAD⁺ restoration is difficult to verify. Trials targeting neurodegeneration remain preliminary: while precursor administration raises peripheral NAD⁺ levels, robust evidence demonstrating sustained NAD⁺ elevation within the central nervous system or clear symptomatic benefit is still limited. Collectively, available human data reveal a recurring constraint: systemic supplementation achieves bulk metabolite elevation in blood without reliable control over tissue, cellular or subcellular NAD⁺ pools.

Many of these inconsistent clinical outcomes stem from fundamental physicochemical and biological barriers confronting free NAD⁺-related molecules. Direct NAD⁺ administration is limited by poor membrane permeability, limited stability and rapid extracellular degradation. Precursor-based supplementation partly circumvents these barriers, but introduces additional uncertainties related to absorption, enzymatic conversion, systemic distribution and tissue-specific utilization.58 The relationship between precursor exposure and intracellular NAD⁺ restoration is neither linear nor uniform across organs or disease states.59 Different tissues exhibit distinct precursor preferences, enzyme expression profiles and transporter activities (Table 2), and the mechanisms driving NAD⁺ depletion differ across neurodegeneration, cardiovascular injury, metabolic disease, acute organ damage and inflammatory disorders. Under these conditions, increasing systemic precursor supply does not guarantee meaningful NAD⁺ rescue in the relevant cells or intracellular compartments. The therapeutic problem is therefore not solely one of insufficient dose, but of insufficient delivery precision.

These limitations are often described in pharmacokinetic terms, including instability, rapid clearance, low bioavailability and poor tissue penetration. Although valid, this description does not fully capture the central obstacle facing NAD⁺ therapeutics. Conventional replenishment remains pharmacologically blunt relative to the biological architecture of NAD⁺ metabolism. NAD⁺ decline is heterogeneous across tissues, evolves over time and may be restricted to specific cell populations or subcellular pools.67 In many disease settings, the therapeutic objective is not maximal systemic NAD⁺ elevation, but restoration of the metabolically limiting pool within the relevant pathological microenvironment. Generalized precursor exposure may therefore be inefficient, and in some contexts mechanistically misaligned with the underlying disease process.

This mismatch becomes particularly important when therapeutic benefit depends on crossing serial biological barriers, including vascular endothelium, inflamed tissue interfaces, cellular membranes, endosomal compartments and, in some cases, organelle membranes. In diseases involving the brain, heart, kidney, retina or inflamed solid tissues, these barriers can restrict the access of free NAD⁺-related molecules to therapeutically relevant sites of action. Thus, the apparent simplicity of metabolite supplementation masks a complex delivery challenge. Current evidence supports a clear conclusion: NAD⁺ metabolism is a compelling therapeutic axis, but effective intervention cannot be reduced to systemic metabolite replacement. Because NAD⁺ homeostasis is dynamic, compartmentalized and disease-context dependent, successful intervention requires control over cargo stability, circulation behaviour, tissue accumulation, cellular uptake, intracellular trafficking and, in selected cases, organelle-specific localisation. This realization reframes the therapeutic problem. The key question is no longer whether NAD⁺ levels can be increased in principle, but whether NAD⁺ metabolism can be restored or modulated with sufficient spatial and temporal precision to produce durable biological benefit. This requirement provides the rationale for nanomedicine. Nanocarrier Platforms for NAD⁺ Delivery

If the central limitation of conventional NAD⁺ replenishment lies in inadequate delivery precision, the choice of nanocarrier becomes more than a formulation decision; it becomes a determinant of therapeutic logic.68 Different nanoplatforms vary substantially in their ability to encapsulate hydrophilic cargoes, protect labile molecules, prolong circulation, cross tissue barriers, promote intracellular uptake and support controlled or organelle-directed release.69 For NAD⁺ and related metabolites, these distinctions are particularly important because high polarity, limited membrane permeability and susceptibility to degradation impose stringent demands on carrier design. Because dedicated NAD⁺ nanodelivery studies remain less extensive than the broader nanomedicine literature, the discussion below distinguishes between platforms with direct NAD⁺-related evidence and those whose relevance is inferred from their ability to solve analogous delivery barriers70 (Figure 3). Accordingly, an effective NAD⁺ nanodelivery platform should be evaluated not only by loading efficiency, but also by its alignment with the biological requirements of target tissues, disease states and subcellular destinations. Comparative Features of Major Nanocarrier Platforms for NAD⁺-Related Delivery

Lipid-Based Nanocarriers

Lipid-based nanocarriers are among the most established delivery systems in nanomedicine and are attractive for NAD⁺-related cargoes because of their biocompatibility, structural versatility and clinical familiarity. Liposomes, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), nanoemulsions and solid lipid nanoparticles can provide protected microenvironments for labile molecules and can be engineered to tune circulation time, serum stability, biodistribution and cellular uptake.71 For highly polar NAD⁺-related agents, aqueous core-containing systems such as liposomes are particularly relevant because they can physically entrap water-soluble metabolites that otherwise show poor membrane permeation and rapid extracellular loss. Formulation parameters can be adjusted to balance stability, release and tissue access.

These features make lipid-based systems a pragmatic starting point when the objective is to improve cargo stability, systemic exposure and tissue-level NAD⁺ restoration rather than to achieve deep subcellular precision.72 Their clinical familiarity and scalable manufacturing are additional advantages. However, lipid-based carriers also face important constraints for NAD⁺ delivery. Encapsulation efficiency for small, highly hydrophilic metabolites can be low, payload density may be limited and burst release or leakage during storage and circulation can compromise effective delivery. Uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system may bias biodistribution towards the liver and spleen, and post‑internalisation trafficking may still limit cytosolic or organelle-level bioavailability. Thus, lipid-based nanocarriers are adaptable and translationally attractive, but their performance depends strongly on loading strategy, leakage control and intracellular fate.

Polymeric Nanocarriers

Polymeric nanocarriers provide a structurally programmable platform for NAD⁺ delivery, particularly when control over release kinetics, surface chemistry or intracellular trafficking is required.73 This category includes polymeric nanoparticles, nanocapsules, micelles, dendrimers, hydrogels and polymer conjugates. Compared with many lipid-based systems, polymeric carriers offer broader control over architecture, degradability, charge and stimulus responsiveness. For NAD⁺-related therapeutics, hydrophilic core–shell structures, electrostatic complexation strategies and crosslinked polymer matrices may improve retention of water-soluble metabolites and reduce premature leakage. Surface modification can be used to tune protein adsorption, circulation behaviour and receptor-mediated uptake, whereas charge-adaptive or pH-responsive polymers may promote endosomal escape when cytosolic delivery is required.

This programmability makes polymeric systems attractive for applications in which NAD⁺ restoration must be coupled to controlled release, sequential barrier navigation or co-delivery of agents that modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction or DNA damage responses. However, the same diversity also complicates translation.74 Polymer composition, degradation behaviour and batch‑to‑batch reproducibility can strongly influence safety and performance. Some synthetic polymers raise concerns regarding incomplete degradation or long-term tissue exposure, and highly engineered designs can be difficult to compare, standardize and manufacture at scale. Polymeric nanocarriers are therefore most compelling when their added engineering complexity is necessary for biological efficacy, rather than when simple cargo protection is sufficient.

Inorganic and Hybrid Nanoplatforms

Inorganic and hybrid nanoplatforms, including mesoporous silica nanoparticles, calcium phosphate particles, layered materials, metal–organic frameworks and composite constructs, offer advantages when structural robustness, high surface area, tunable porosity or multifunctional integration is required.75 For hydrophilic metabolites such as NAD⁺ and NMN, porous or surface‑functionalised inorganic carriers may support loading through adsorption, confinement, ionic interactions or protective mineralisation. Their surface chemistry can be engineered to modulate colloidal stability, cellular interactions and release in response to disease‑associated cues such as pH, redox state or enzyme activity.76 Bio-derived carbon dots derived from phytochemicals have also demonstrated intrinsic SOD-like nanozyme activity, linking redox-modulating nanomaterials to NAD⁺-associated oxidative stress. These carbon dots reprogram the oxidative stress state through antioxidant activity.77 Hybrid designs further expand this design space by combining inorganic cores that provide stability, imaging contrast or loading capacity with lipid or polymeric shells that improve colloidal behaviour and biological compatibility.

These properties make inorganic and hybrid platforms attractive for mechanistically demanding NAD⁺ applications, such as localised delivery, triggered release, imaging‑guided therapy or organelle‑oriented strategies.78 However, their translational barriers are substantial. Long‑term biocompatibility, degradation fate, tissue persistence and protein corona formation are often more difficult to predict than for simpler lipid‑based systems. Manufacturing complexity and regulatory uncertainty increase further when multiple functional components are combined. Thus, inorganic and hybrid systems should not be viewed as default carriers for NAD⁺ interventions. Their value is greatest when the required functionality justifies the additional safety, reproducibility and regulatory burdens.

Biomimetic and Vesicle-Based Delivery Systems

Biomimetic and vesicle-based systems include extracellular vesicles, engineered extracellular vesicles, cell membrane‑coated nanoparticles, lipoprotein‑inspired particles and other bioinspired constructs.79 Their appeal lies in the possibility of using biological recognition mechanisms to improve compatibility, circulation behaviour and cell‑selective interactions. For NAD⁺ delivery, vesicle‑like architectures are relevant because they can carry hydrophilic molecules within aqueous interiors while presenting membrane components that may influence tissue tropism, immune recognition and cellular uptake. Cell‑derived vesicles or membrane‑coated systems may be particularly useful in inflammatory, vascular or neural contexts in which synthetic carriers face rapid clearance, poor barrier negotiation or limited cell‑type selectivity.

These systems also provide a route to combine endogenous recognition with synthetic engineering, for example by loading NAD⁺‑related cargoes into vesicles or coating engineered cores with cell‑derived membranes. Such approaches are attractive for hierarchical delivery strategies in which organ‑level homing, cell‑specific uptake and intracellular release must be coordinated.80 However, biomimetic platforms face major practical challenges. Composition is often heterogeneous, cargo loading can be inefficient or variable, and purification, storage and quality control are difficult to standardize. Source‑dependent safety, endogenous cargo contamination, batch‑to‑batch reproducibility and large‑scale manufacturing remain unresolved. Mechanistic understanding of vesicle biodistribution and intracellular fate is also incomplete. Biomimetic systems are therefore attractive for precision NAD⁺ nanomedicine, but their translational value will depend on whether biological complexity can be converted into reproducible and controllable product attributes.

Comparative Considerations

Platform selection for NAD⁺ delivery should be guided by comparative fit rather than material preference. No single material system is inherently optimal across all applications. If the main objective is to improve circulation stability and broad tissue exposure for a relatively accessible target, lipid‑based systems may offer an appropriate balance of simplicity, biocompatibility and translational readiness. If efficacy depends on controlled release, endosomal escape or complex intracellular trafficking, polymeric carriers may be more suitable If triggered release, imaging integration or multifunctional therapy is required, inorganic or hybrid systems may provide additional capabilities. If immune compatibility, biological barrier negotiation or cell‑type selectivity is central, biomimetic platforms may be preferable despite their manufacturing complexity.

Importantly, the optimal platform is not necessarily the one with the highest nominal loading efficiency. Colloidal stability, circulation half‑life, tissue selectivity, intracellular accessibility, organelle‑level localisation, immunological tolerance and manufacturing reproducibility may be equally or more important. This is particularly true for NAD⁺ therapeutics, which often aim to restore function in restricted biological compartments rather than maximise systemic metabolite concentration. Platform evaluation should therefore ask not only whether a carrier increases NAD⁺ levels, but where the increase occurs, how durable it is, whether it restores relevant metabolic or signalling phenotypes, and whether the system can be manufactured reproducibly within acceptable safety margins. Overall, nanocarrier platforms provide a broad toolbox for addressing the limitations of conventional NAD⁺ supplementation. The next section examines how these platforms can be engineered to achieve spatiotemporally controlled NAD⁺ delivery — not as a laundry list of techniques, but as a hierarchical design framework.

Engineering Strategies for Precision NAD⁺ Nanodelivery

If nanocarrier platforms define the structural basis of NAD⁺ delivery, engineering strategies determine whether that potential can be converted into biologically meaningful intervention.81 For NAD⁺-related therapeutics, this distinction is especially important. Because the cargo is highly hydrophilic, membrane-impermeable or poorly permeable, and often vulnerable to extracellular degradation or inefficient intracellular access, successful delivery depends less on carrier presence alone than on how the carrier is designed to control encapsulation, circulation, barrier penetration, cellular internalization, subcellular trafficking, and release.82 In this sense, the performance of NAD⁺ nanomedicine is ultimately an engineering problem. Engineering Strategies for Precision NAD⁺ Nanodelivery Across Biological Scales

Encapsulation Optimization for Highly Hydrophilic and Labile NAD⁺-Related Cargoes

A primary engineering challenge in NAD⁺ nanodelivery lies in the physicochemical mismatch between the cargo and many conventional carrier systems. NAD⁺, NMN, and related metabolites are strongly hydrophilic, relatively small, and prone to premature loss if not effectively confined within a protected microenvironment.83 As a result, delivery success begins with encapsulation design rather than downstream targeting alone. Several engineering strategies are particularly relevant. Aqueous-core architectures, including liposomes and vesicular systems, are naturally suited for water-soluble metabolites because they can physically isolate cargo from the external environment while preserving molecular integrity. However, passive loading alone may yield low payload efficiency and leakage during storage or circulation. To address this, more advanced strategies such as ion pairing, electrostatic complexation, polymer-network confinement, and multilayer core-shell assembly can be used to improve cargo retention and reduce burst release. Core-shell architectures are especially attractive because they allow the inner compartment to be optimized for loading and stabilization, while the outer shell is independently tuned for colloidal stability, circulation, and targeting.84 Recent advances in microfluidics-enabled core/shell nanostructure assembly further suggest that fabrication-level control may become increasingly important for improving encapsulation reproducibility and understanding the relationship between particle structure and loading behavior. Encapsulation design also influences what kind of therapeutic logic a system can support. A formulation intended for broad systemic NAD⁺ replenishment may prioritize high loading and slow leakage, whereas a system designed for localized rescue in a diseased microenvironment may favor conditional release over maximal retention. Similarly, carriers intended for intracellular or organelle-directed delivery must balance cargo stability with timely liberation after cellular entry.85 Thus, encapsulation should not be regarded as a purely technical preprocessing step, but as a central determinant of how the therapeutic payload behaves in vivo. For NAD⁺ nanomedicine, an ideal encapsulation strategy should satisfy at least three requirements: protection against premature degradation, adequate loading efficiency for hydrophilic cargoes, and compatibility with subsequent trafficking and release. The challenge is not only to package NAD⁺-related metabolites, but to do so in a form that preserves downstream delivery function.

Surface Functionalization and Circulation Engineering

Once cargoes are stably incorporated, the next engineering challenge is to ensure that nanocarriers remain in circulation long enough to reach their biological targets without being rapidly cleared or nonspecifically sequestered. Surface engineering is therefore a foundational component of NAD⁺ nanodelivery, particularly because many target tissues affected by aging and chronic disease are not immediately accessible after systemic administration.86 Surface chemistry governs multiple aspects of in vivo behavior, including colloidal stability, serum protein adsorption, immune recognition, vascular interactions, and biodistribution. Traditional stealth strategies such as PEGylation can reduce opsonization and prolong circulation, although their effectiveness depends on density, chain length, and the surrounding material context. More broadly, hydrophilic polymer coatings, zwitterionic interfaces, and membrane-mimetic surfaces can all be used to improve blood compatibility and reduce premature uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system.

Beyond passive circulation control, surface engineering also enables more dynamic forms of delivery regulation.87 Charge-adaptive systems are particularly relevant in this context. Nanocarriers that remain near-neutral or weakly shielded in circulation may minimize nonspecific interactions, yet become positively charged or otherwise activated after encountering specific intracellular or pathological conditions, thereby enhancing cellular uptake or endosomal escape. Such strategies are especially attractive for NAD⁺ therapeutics because they help reconcile two otherwise competing objectives: prolonged blood persistence and efficient intracellular access. Ligand functionalization adds another level of precision. Peptides, antibodies, sugars, receptor-binding motifs, or biomimetic membrane components can be introduced to improve interaction with selected tissues or cell populations.88 In principle, such targeting elements can be used to direct NAD⁺ cargoes toward metabolically vulnerable sites, including inflamed vasculature, injured myocardium, degenerating neural tissue, activated immune compartments, or fibrotic lesions. However, ligand density, orientation, accessibility, and stability must all be optimized; excessive or poorly presented ligands may increase clearance, reduce stealth, or compromise manufacturability. For NAD⁺ delivery, the purpose of surface engineering is not merely to prolong circulation in a generic sense. Rather, it is to maximize the probability that carriers encounter the right biological interface in the right functional state. This makes circulation engineering inseparable from targeting strategy.

Overcoming Biological Barriers and Controlling Intracellular Trafficking

Even when nanocarriers reach diseased tissues, therapeutic success is far from guaranteed. In most clinically relevant settings, NAD⁺ delivery requires the carrier to traverse multiple serial barriers, including vascular endothelium, extracellular matrix, inflamed or fibrotic tissue interfaces, cellular membranes, endosomal compartments, and, in some cases, organelle membranes.89 Failure at any one of these steps can sharply reduce effective delivery. Barrier navigation therefore represents a central engineering challenge. Particle size, deformability, shape, and surface charge all influence extravasation, tissue penetration, and interstitial mobility. Smaller particles may diffuse more effectively through dense matrices but can also be more vulnerable to rapid clearance.90 Larger or more rigid constructs may carry higher payloads yet penetrate poorly. These trade-offs mean that optimal design depends on the anatomical and pathological context rather than on universal size rules. After tissue entry, intracellular trafficking becomes equally critical. Endocytosed carriers are often directed toward endosomal and lysosomal pathways that may degrade the payload or prevent its access to the cytosol and other functional compartments. For NAD⁺-related cargoes, this issue is particularly important because therapeutic benefit may depend on restoring metabolite availability where signaling and metabolic enzymes actually operate.91 Accordingly, engineering strategies that facilitate endosomal escape—such as proton-buffering polymers, membrane-disruptive moieties, charge-switching materials, or pH-responsive destabilization—can have outsized effects on efficacy. At the same time, efficient escape is not universally desirable if the therapeutic design depends on controlled endosomal processing or delayed intracellular release. The relevant question is therefore not whether a carrier can disrupt trafficking pathways, but whether it can route cargo through the intracellular itinerary most compatible with the intended biological outcome. This is a major reason why NAD⁺ nanodelivery should be viewed as a trafficking problem as much as a targeting problem.

Tissue-Specific Targeting and Disease-Context Delivery

Because NAD⁺ decline is heterogeneous across organs and pathological states, a uniform delivery strategy is unlikely to be optimal. Tissue-specific targeting is therefore not a luxury add-on, but a biologically motivated engineering objective.92 The design principles governing effective targeting differ substantially depending on whether the therapeutic goal is to rescue neurons, cardiomyocytes, renal tubular cells, hepatocytes, retinal cells, inflamed immune niches, or aged stem-cell microenvironments. In some settings, targeting may be achieved through passive enrichment arising from altered vascular permeability or phagocytic activity. In others, active strategies are more important, including receptor-mediated binding, disease-microenvironment recognition, enzyme-triggered activation, or biomimetic homing. The key engineering point is that targeting should be based on the dominant barrier and pathological signature of the tissue in question, rather than on the assumption that all damaged tissues behave similarly.93 This issue is especially relevant for NAD⁺ therapeutics because metabolic insufficiency often coexists with inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and fibrosis.94 These pathophysiological features create both opportunities and constraints for carrier design. For example, inflamed tissues may offer adhesion targets or redox cues for selective accumulation, while neurodegenerative contexts may require designs capable of negotiating more restrictive barriers and minimizing immune activation. In ischemic or degenerative tissues, retention and local release kinetics may matter as much as initial homing efficiency. From a translational standpoint, tissue targeting also shapes dose economy and safety. If NAD⁺-related cargoes can be preferentially delivered to the sites of highest metabolic vulnerability, effective rescue may be achieved at lower systemic exposure, thereby reducing off-target distribution and potentially improving therapeutic index. Thus, targeting should be understood not simply as a localization strategy, but as a way to increase the biological efficiency of NAD⁺ restoration.

Organelle-Targeted Delivery and Subcellular Precision

For many NAD⁺-dependent processes, the functionally relevant therapeutic target is not merely a tissue or a cell, but a specific intracellular compartment. Distinct NAD⁺ pools in the nucleus, cytosol, and mitochondria support different signaling and metabolic programs, and pathological depletion may be strongly compartment-biased.95 This means that successful restoration of total cellular NAD⁺ does not necessarily equate to restoration of the pool that is biologically limiting. Organelle-targeted delivery is therefore emerging as an important frontier in NAD⁺ nanomedicine. Mitochondria are particularly compelling targets because mitochondrial dysfunction is both a cause and consequence of NAD⁺ decline across aging and multiple degenerative diseases.96 Engineering approaches for mitochondrial delivery include lipophilic cation-based targeting, mitochondria-affinitive peptides, membrane-potential-sensitive motifs, and hybrid nanoplatforms capable of releasing cargo after cytosolic entry. Similar logic applies to nuclear-directed strategies in contexts where DNA repair, chromatin regulation, or PARP-associated NAD⁺ consumption is central to pathology.97 However, organelle targeting introduces additional layers of complexity. A carrier must first survive circulation, accumulate in target tissue, enter the correct cell population, escape inappropriate intracellular sequestration, and only then localize to the intended organelle. This sequential requirement means that organelle targeting cannot be designed in isolation. Instead, it must be integrated into a hierarchical delivery architecture in which each prior stage enables the next. For NAD⁺ therapeutics, organelle-level precision may be especially valuable when disease mechanisms are dominated by compartment-specific failure rather than global metabolic insufficiency. In such cases, targeted subcellular rescue may outperform indiscriminate metabolite elevation, even if total delivered dose is lower. This principle reinforces a broader point: the therapeutic value of NAD⁺ delivery depends not only on quantity, but also on intracellular destination.

Stimuli-Responsive and Adaptive Release Systems

One of the most promising directions in NAD⁺ nanomedicine is the development of delivery systems that respond dynamically to pathological cues. Stimuli-responsive carriers can exploit pH, redox state, reactive oxygen species, enzymes, hypoxia, light, heat, ultrasound, or other endogenous and exogenous triggers to regulate when and where cargo is released.98 For NAD⁺-related therapeutics, this capability is highly attractive because metabolic failure is often spatially restricted and temporally dynamic. Conventional sustained-release logic is not always sufficient in this context. In some diseases, continuous low-level exposure may be beneficial; in others, therapeutic efficacy may depend on burst release in response to acute stress, ischemia, inflammatory activation, or mitochondrial dysfunction.99 Stimuli-responsive designs allow these possibilities to be encoded into the carrier. Such systems may improve therapeutic specificity by minimizing premature leakage during circulation while concentrating release in tissues where metabolic restoration is most urgently needed. Adaptive delivery systems can extend this concept further. Rather than responding to a single trigger, they may integrate multiple environmental inputs or undergo stage-specific transformations as they move through biological barriers.100 A carrier may, for example, remain shielded in blood, activate for tissue binding within diseased vasculature, switch charge after cellular internalization, and finally release cargo in response to endosomal acidification or mitochondrial redox conditions. This multistage behavior is particularly compatible with the demands of NAD⁺ delivery, where meaningful rescue often requires navigation across distinct physiological compartments. In parallel, a carrier-free self-assembled hydrogel formed through molecular co-assembly has been reported. This system achieves programmable release without synthetic carriers through π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding-driven self-assembly, offering a simplified translational pathway for microenvironment-responsive delivery.101 The challenge, however, lies in balancing sophistication with robustness. Excessively complex responsive systems may be difficult to characterize, manufacture, or standardize, and their in vivo behavior may become difficult to predict. For translationally realistic NAD⁺ nanomedicine, the most valuable adaptive designs are likely to be those that achieve clear biological gains with the fewest necessary layers of complexity.

Temporal Programming: Engineering Release Kinetics for Dynamic Disease Windows

Beyond spatial precision, the therapeutic efficacy of NAD⁺ nanomedicine depends critically on when and for how long the cargo becomes bioavailable. Acute pathological conditions—such as sepsis, acute kidney injury, or ischemia-reperfusion injury—often require rapid, high-intensity NAD⁺ restoration within a narrow therapeutic window to counteract sudden metabolic collapse. In these contexts, carriers designed for burst release or rapid responsiveness to disease cues may be preferred, provided that premature leakage during circulation is minimized. Conversely, chronic degenerative diseases—including osteoarthritis, neurodegenerative disorders, and age-related metabolic decline—may benefit from sustained or pulsatile release over extended periods, matching the prolonged timeline of disease progression. Between these extremes, conditions with episodic flares, such as inflammatory bowel disease or recurrent gout, may require on-demand release triggered by disease activity markers or external stimuli. Engineering strategies for temporal programming include the use of biodegradable polymers with erosion-controlled release, stimuli-responsive materials that release cargo in response to pH, redox state, or enzyme activity, and externally triggered systems activated by light, ultrasound, or magnetic fields. More advanced designs integrate multiple temporal programs within a single carrier: a system might provide an initial burst release to address acute metabolic crisis, followed by sustained release to support tissue repair, and finally a responsive phase that adapts to recurrent disease activity. Such multi-stage temporal programming is conceptually aligned with the hierarchical framework proposed here, but it also introduces substantial engineering complexity. The key design question is not whether a system can achieve a particular release profile, but whether the chosen temporal program is biologically matched to the disease dynamics and the pharmacokinetic requirements of the NAD⁺-related cargo.

Toward Hierarchical Precision Nanodelivery

The engineering strategies discussed above are often presented as separate modules, but their true value emerges when they are integrated into a unified delivery hierarchy.102 For NAD⁺ therapeutics, effective intervention may require coordinated control at multiple biological levels: protection in circulation, selective tissue accumulation, cell-specific uptake, intracellular trafficking, and organelle-directed release. A weakness at any stage can negate success at subsequent stages. This multilevel perspective supports a hierarchical precision delivery framework. In such a framework, encapsulation design protects the cargo and defines early pharmacokinetic behavior; surface engineering governs circulation and tissue interface recognition; targeting strategies determine organ and cell selectivity; trafficking control dictates intracellular accessibility; and release design resolves where and when the metabolite becomes functionally available.103 Delivery success is therefore not the sum of isolated optimizations, but the product of alignment across scales. This framework also helps explain why apparently similar nanocarriers may perform very differently in vivo. Two systems with comparable loading efficiency may diverge dramatically in therapeutic effect if one is rapidly cleared, poorly internalized, trapped in endosomes, or unable to release cargo at the relevant subcellular site. Conversely, a system with only moderate payload capacity may achieve superior efficacy if it restores NAD⁺ precisely within the compartment most relevant to disease progression. For this reason, engineering evaluation should prioritize functional localization and biological rescue over nominal formulation metrics alone.104 In the context of aging and chronic disease, hierarchical precision nanodelivery may offer a way to convert broad NAD⁺ biology into context-aware therapy. Rather than indiscriminately increasing systemic metabolite levels, it enables the design of interventions that restore NAD⁺ where depletion is mechanistically meaningful and therapeutically actionable. This shift—from generalized supplementation to multiscale precision restoration—may represent the most important engineering advance in the field.

Realising Hierarchical Precision: Applications of NAD⁺ Nanodelivery Systems

The hierarchical engineering principles discussed above are increasingly being translated into therapeutic strategies that address specific delivery bottlenecks in NAD⁺ restoration.105 Rather than viewing NAD⁺ nanodelivery applications solely through a disease-based lens, a more informative approach is to examine how each nanosystem achieves precision across biological scales.106 In this framework, delivery progresses from organ- and tissue-level accumulation, to cell-population selectivity, to subcellular compartmental rescue, and ultimately to spatiotemporally programmed intervention. This design-centred perspective helps clarify why different nanoplatforms are suited to different pathological contexts and how their structural features are matched to specific pharmacological barriers.107 In the following sections, representative studies are discussed according to this hierarchical logic. Organ- and tissue-level targeting addresses the problem of heterogeneous biodistribution and insufficient lesion-site exposure. Cellular-level precision further refines delivery toward disease-driving cell populations, which is particularly important given the context-dependent effects of NAD⁺ metabolism. Subcellular targeting aims to restore NAD⁺-related function within compartments such as mitochondria, where metabolic failure may be most consequential.108 Finally, spatiotemporally programmable systems integrate spatial selectivity with dynamic release or stage-specific targeting, enabling NAD⁺-based interventions to respond to evolving disease microenvironments.109 Together, these levels provide a practical framework for evaluating how nanodelivery may convert NAD⁺ replenishment from systemic metabolite elevation into context-aware metabolic restoration (Figure 5).

Organ- and Tissue-Level Targeting: Overcoming Distribution Heterogeneity

The first step beyond systemic NAD⁺ supplementation is to achieve preferential accumulation in diseased organs or tissues. This level of targeting addresses a major limitation of conventional NAD⁺ precursors: systemic administration does not guarantee efficient delivery to tissues with the greatest metabolic demand, and circulating precursors may be metabolised or sequestered by competing organs before reaching the intended site. Nanodelivery can partially overcome this limitation by exploiting organ-specific anatomy, pathological vascular changes, tissue retention mechanisms or disease-associated microenvironmental cues.

A representative example is the development of ultra-small polyphenol–NAD⁺ nanoparticles for acute kidney injury (AKI)110 (Figure 6A–C). This design is aligned with renal physiology because particles smaller than approximately 50 nm can take advantage of the glomerular filtration barrier to promote renal accumulation. Compared with free NAD⁺, which is rapidly metabolised and shows limited renal retention, these nanoparticles exhibited enhanced enrichment in the tubulointerstitial region, with reported targeting efficiency approximately 6.2-fold higher than that of free NAD⁺. Once accumulated in the injured kidney, the particles responded to the pathological microenvironment, including acidic pH and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), enabling disassembly and cargo release. Beyond synthetic carriers, mulberry-derived carbon dots have recently been developed as a bio-derived nanoplatform for kidney fibrosis therapy. These carbon dots achieve renal accumulation through size-dependent glomerular filtration and directly inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway to attenuate fibrotic responses.111 By exploiting size-dependent glomerular filtration—a passive targeting mechanism—the ultrasmall nanoparticles overcome the first major delivery bottleneck, namely insufficient renal accumulation of free NAD⁺ following systemic administration. The sequential design of size-driven organ accumulation followed by microenvironment-responsive cargo release demonstrates how nanocarriers can coordinate tissue-level targeting with disease-cue responsiveness, converting systemic administration into locally restricted metabolic restoration. Moreover, the polyphenol carrier contributes intrinsic ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammatory activity, illustrating a principle that extends beyond NAD⁺ delivery: carrier materials can be selected not only for their drug-delivery properties but also for therapeutic functions that complement the primary cargo.

Similar principles apply to other organs with strong delivery barriers. In neurological diseases, the blood–brain barrier remains a major obstacle for systemic NAD⁺-related therapies. Nanocarriers functionalised with ligands for receptor-mediated transcytosis, such as transferrin receptor- or glucose transporter-associated pathways, may provide a strategy to enhance brain entry of NAD⁺ precursors or related cargoes. However, for NAD⁺ nanodelivery, such approaches should be interpreted cautiously unless direct evidence is available, because successful BBB engagement does not necessarily ensure adequate neuronal uptake, intracellular release or compartment-specific NAD⁺ restoration. Nevertheless, these strategies illustrate how organ-level targeting can be designed around dominant anatomical barriers.

Organ-targeted NAD⁺ nanodelivery has also been explored in systemic inflammatory conditions such as sepsis. In one strategy, NAD(H)-loaded nanoparticles enhanced intracellular NAD(H) levels by improving interactions with cell membranes, thereby supporting energy metabolism and immune homeostasis across injured organs112 (Figure 6D–F). The liposomal carrier protects labile NAD⁺ from extracellular degradation—a limitation that severely compromises free NAD⁺ administration—while facilitating uptake by inflammatory immune cells. By delivering NAD⁺ directly to activated neutrophils, monocytes, and T-cell subsets, this approach addresses the challenge of cell-type selectivity: systemic precursor supplementation cannot distinguish between immune cells that would benefit from NAD⁺ restoration and those in which NAD⁺ elevation might be undesirable. Notably, this cellular discrimination is achieved not through active targeting ligands, but through the natural phagocytic propensity of inflammatory leukocytes, demonstrating that even passive mechanisms can achieve meaningful cell-type selectivity when carrier design matches pathological cell behavior. Another approach used acid-responsive hollow mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles co-loaded with NAD⁺ and calcium ion chelators112 (Figure 6G–I). Through inflammation-targeting peptide modification, these nanoparticles showed enhanced enrichment in inflammatory organs such as the lung and liver, with reported accumulation up to 5.9-fold higher than non-targeted controls. Their acid-responsive release, together with the ROS-scavenging capacity of the carrier, enabled simultaneous modulation of metabolic dysfunction, oxidative stress and inflammatory signalling. This design combines tissue-level active targeting with dual-responsive cargo release to address the complex pathophysiology of sepsis. The inflammation-targeting peptide modification overcomes the problem of non-specific biodistribution that plagues systemic NAD⁺ precursor administration, while the hollow mesoporous polydopamine carrier serves dual therapeutic functions: pH-responsive NAD⁺ release and intrinsic ROS-scavenging capacity. Such co-delivery illustrates a key principle of hierarchical design: when disease pathology involves multiple interconnected pathways (NAD⁺ depletion, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling), nanocarriers can be engineered to address them simultaneously rather than relying on NAD⁺ restoration alone. These examples demonstrate the first layer of hierarchical precision: controlling where NAD⁺-related cargoes accumulate. By using size-dependent filtration, disease-associated vascular permeability, receptor-mediated transport, inflammation-targeting ligands or microenvironment-responsive release, nanosystems can improve organ-level exposure and reduce the inefficiency of systemic precursor distribution. Even within this first layer, successful tissue-level targeting is not monolithic; it can be achieved through passive mechanisms (size-dependent renal filtration), active mechanisms (inflammation-targeting peptides), carrier-intrinsic properties (phagocyte uptake), or combinations thereof. The choice among these strategies should be guided by the dominant barrier in each target organ and the specific pathological features of the disease, rather than by a one-size-fits-all approach.

Cellular-Level Precision: Engaging Specific Pathological Cell Populations

Precision delivery must extend beyond organ accumulation because diseased tissues contain heterogeneous cell populations with distinct, and sometimes opposing, responses to NAD⁺ restoration. A non-selective increase in NAD⁺ availability may benefit metabolically compromised cells, but in some contexts it could also support undesirable processes, including pathological immune activation, senescent-cell persistence or tumour-cell metabolism. Cellular-level targeting is therefore essential when therapeutic benefit depends on engaging specific disease-driving cell populations while limiting exposure of bystander cells.

A representative example is the biomimetic Cu-NAD@CM nanosystem designed to target senescent cells113 (Figure 7A–D). In this platform, NAD⁺-carrying copper nanosheets are cloaked with membranes derived from M2 macrophages. The membrane coating provides a complex surface receptor profile, including CCR2 and RAGE, which can interact with chemokines such as CCL2 and advanced glycation end products enriched around senescent cells. This enables affinity-driven accumulation in senescent-cell-rich microenvironments, with high binding efficiency reported in vitro. After cellular internalisation, the copper core contributes additional therapeutic activity by exhibiting NADH oxidase-like function, promoting local conversion of NADH to NAD⁺ while also scavenging H2O2. This system addresses two fundamental challenges in senescent cell targeting. The M2 macrophage membrane coating overcomes the lack of specific surface markers that has historically hindered senescent cell identification, while the copper nanosheet core goes beyond merely delivering exogenous NAD⁺ by enabling catalytic conversion of NADH to NAD⁺ in situ—an important design principle for aged tissues where endogenous salvage pathways are impaired. This catalytic regeneration represents a departure from conventional NAD⁺ delivery: rather than transiently increasing NAD⁺ levels through exogenous supply, it actively recycles the existing NAD(H) pool, potentially achieving more sustained metabolic restoration with lower cargo requirements.

Cellular discrimination is also important in vascular repair after injury. Restenosis therapy requires differential regulation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and endothelial cells (ECs): VSMC migration and proliferation should be suppressed, whereas endothelial repair should be promoted. Cell-membrane-coated NAD⁺ nanoparticles provide one strategy for addressing this challenge114 (Figure 7E and f). Platelet membrane coating can guide particles toward exposed subendothelial collagen at vascular injury sites, whereas endothelial cell membrane coating can enhance interaction with damaged ECs. Although the delivered cargo is NAD⁺, cell-type-specific intracellular signalling may generate divergent therapeutic outcomes. In VSMCs, NAD⁺-mediated activation of Sirtuin-3 has been associated with reduced migration and proliferation, whereas in ECs, activation of the Akt/eNOS pathway may support survival and migration. This system illustrates how cellular-level targeting can generate divergent therapeutic outcomes from the same metabolic cargo through cell-type-specific signaling networks. The platelet membrane coating exploits the natural injury-response machinery of platelets for biomimetic homing, while the differential intracellular responses to NAD⁺—Sirtuin-3 in VSMCs versus Akt/eNOS in ECs—enable the same delivered metabolite to produce opposing, yet therapeutically desirable, effects in distinct cell populations within the same tissue. This example highlights that delivery precision is not limited to physical localization; by engaging cell-specific signaling pathways, nanocarriers can achieve context-appropriate outcomes even when the cargo itself is a ubiquitous metabolite.

In age-related osteoarthritis, a biomimetic hydrogel microsphere system, NAD@NPs@HM, has been developed to target both chondrocytes and synovial macrophages115 (Figure 7G and H). In this system, NAD⁺-loaded liposomes modified with lactoferrin achieved enhanced chondrocyte targeting, with a reported 4.3-fold increase in targeting efficiency. Hydrogel encapsulation enabled sustained release for up to 10 days. Functionally, the system reduced chondrocyte senescence markers through NAD⁺-mediated activation of the Sirtuin-1 pathway and promoted synovial macrophage polarisation toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. In addition, the hydrogel component provided mechanical lubrication, reducing the joint friction coefficient and creating a more favourable microenvironment for cartilage repair. This design addresses cellular-level delivery in a mechanically challenging tissue environment where both metabolic and physical pathology coexist. The lactoferrin modification achieves active chondrocyte targeting, while hydrogel encapsulation provides sustained NAD⁺ release and—critically—mechanical lubrication that reduces joint friction. This dual functionality illustrates an important principle for degenerative diseases: when the target tissue presents both metabolic dysfunction and structural degradation, nanocarriers can be engineered to address both dimensions simultaneously. Functionally, the system achieves cell-type-specific NAD⁺ restoration that simultaneously targets chondrocyte senescence (via Sirtuin-1) and inflammatory macrophage polarization (toward M2), demonstrating that cellular-level precision can coordinate therapeutic effects across multiple cell populations within a single diseased tissue.

These studies illustrate the second layer of hierarchical precision: determining which cells receive NAD⁺-related intervention. Cellular-level targeting can improve therapeutic index by concentrating NAD⁺ restoration in pathological cell populations while reducing non-specific exposure, and by leveraging cell-type-specific biology to produce context-appropriate therapeutic outcomes. Critically, cellular precision is not limited to physical delivery to the correct cell type; it also encompasses the ability to engage cell-specific signaling networks that determine how the delivered NAD⁺ is converted into functional effects. The examples discussed here reveal that successful cellular targeting can be achieved through multiple mechanisms: biomimetic membrane coatings that exploit natural receptor–ligand interactions, injury-homing strategies that leverage pathological tissue features, and ligand-modified carriers that recognize cell-surface markers. The optimal strategy depends on the specific cell population to be targeted, the accessibility of targetable surface features, and the intracellular signaling pathways that mediate the desired therapeutic response.

Subcellular-Level Precision: Restoring Mitochondrial Homeostasis

For many NAD⁺-dependent processes, the most relevant therapeutic site is not only a tissue or cell type, but a specific intracellular compartment. Mitochondria are particularly important because mitochondrial NAD⁺ availability influences oxidative phosphorylation, redox balance, stress responses and cell survival. In ageing and degenerative diseases, mitochondrial dysfunction can both result from and amplify NAD⁺ decline. Therefore, mitochondria-oriented NAD⁺ restoration has become an important direction for precision nanodelivery.

One common strategy is to incorporate mitochondriotropic motifs such as triphenylphosphonium (TPP⁺), which accumulates in mitochondria in response to the negative mitochondrial membrane potential. In osteoarthritis, TPP-conjugated hyaluronic acid has been integrated with hollow MnO2 nanoparticles loaded with NMN.116 The TPP⁺ moiety provides mitochondrial affinity, whereas hyaluronic acid can support interaction with CD44-expressing chondrocytes. After cellular uptake, mitochondrial localisation of the carrier improves the likelihood that released NMN or NAD⁺-related metabolites contribute to mitochondrial metabolic repair. The MnO2 core further decomposes H2O2 in the inflammatory joint microenvironment, generating oxygen and reducing oxidative stress. In this design, NAD⁺ precursor delivery and oxygen-generating antioxidant activity are combined to support mitochondrial function and restore ATP production and redox balance in damaged chondrocytes.

Beyond delivering NAD⁺ precursors, some systems aim to enhance local NAD⁺ regeneration. The Cu-NAD@CM platform described above provides such an example.113 Its copper nanosheet core exhibits NADH oxidase-like activity, enabling local conversion of NADH to NAD⁺ within targeted pathological cells. Although this strategy does not necessarily prove selective mitochondrial matrix delivery, it reflects an important design concept: NAD⁺ restoration can be achieved not only by transporting exogenous NAD⁺ or precursors, but also by installing catalytic capacity for in situ NAD⁺ regeneration. This may be particularly relevant in aged tissues where endogenous salvage pathways are impaired.

A more integrated cascade-targeting strategy has been reported for acute lung injury using a biomimetic nanoparticle system referred to as TNF@MB.117 The nanoparticles are coated with pulmonary epithelial cell membranes to enhance lung targeting. After cellular uptake, the Fe3⁺-containing core facilitates mitochondrial accumulation, while the acidic lesion microenvironment triggers dissociation of the TA–NAD⁺–Fe3⁺ complex and release of NAD⁺. This system was reported to restore alveolar barrier function through combined NAD⁺ replenishment, ROS scavenging and ferroptosis inhibition. Importantly, this example illustrates a multilevel targeting strategy in which tissue homing, cellular uptake and mitochondrial-oriented release are integrated within a single platform. Subcellular targeting therefore addresses the question of which compartment should be restored. For NAD⁺ therapeutics, this distinction is critical because global increases in cellular NAD⁺ may not correct compartment-specific deficits. Mitochondria-oriented delivery or regeneration may improve biochemical efficiency by directing NAD⁺ restoration toward the site where metabolic dysfunction is most consequential.

Spatiotemporally Programmable Delivery: Integrating Location, Timing and Disease Cues

The most advanced level of hierarchical precision integrates spatial targeting with temporally controlled activation. Such systems are designed to respond to disease-stage biomarkers, microenvironmental signals or selected external triggers, allowing NAD⁺ delivery to be aligned with dynamic biological needs. This is particularly important in conditions where pathology evolves over time, such as tissue regeneration, acute inflammation, sepsis or age-related degeneration.

A representative example is a spatiotemporally adaptive NAD⁺ nanodelivery system, termed NZM, developed for promoting regeneration in aged tissues118 (Figure 8A–D). This system was designed around the sequential phases of tissue repair, including early inflammation and later stem cell-mediated regeneration. NZM particles are cloaked with a hybrid membrane derived from macrophages and stem cells. During the inflammatory phase, surface glucose moieties promote interaction with GLUT1-expressing, metabolically active pro-inflammatory macrophages. As the microenvironment shifts toward repair, stem-cell membrane markers facilitate interaction with recruited but functionally impaired mesenchymal stem cells. This phase-adaptive targeting enables a single system to deliver NAD⁺ first to immune cells, supporting inflammatory resolution and pro-repair polarisation, and subsequently to stem cells, where NAD⁺ restoration may reverse senescence-associated dysfunction and improve regenerative capacity. This design embodies the most sophisticated layer of the hierarchical framework: spatiotemporal programming. Unlike static targeting strategies that assume a fixed therapeutic target, the NZM system adapts its cellular preference in response to the evolving wound microenvironment. The hybrid membrane design—incorporating both macrophage and stem cell surface markers—enables a single carrier to perform sequential functions that would otherwise require multiple administrations of different formulations. This phase-adaptive approach addresses a fundamental limitation of conventional supplementation: the inability to match NAD⁺ availability to the changing metabolic demands of different cell populations across the repair timeline. By delivering NAD⁺ to immune cells during the inflammatory phase and to stem cells during the regenerative phase, the system ensures that metabolic support is provided precisely when each cell type requires it most.

Spatiotemporally responsive delivery is also relevant in systemic acute inflammatory diseases such as sepsis. The MSe-NAD⁺/Nes nanozyme system provides an example of using neutrophils as living carriers119 (Figure 8E–H). In this design, mesoporous selenium nanozymes loaded with NAD⁺ are introduced into neutrophils, which naturally migrate toward disseminated inflammatory foci. The neutrophil carrier provides active inflammatory-site homing, while the high ROS burden at diseased sites triggers NAD⁺ release. At the same time, the selenium nanozyme scavenges ROS and inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Consistent with these functional benefits, NAD⁺ delivery via this platform restored ATP levels in multiple vital organs—including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney—indicating improved systemic energy homeostasis (Figure 8I). This system represents a self-guided spatiotemporally programmable strategy that leverages living cells as autonomous delivery vehicles. By exploiting the intrinsic chemotactic machinery of neutrophils, the design eliminates the need for exogenous targeting ligands—the carrier itself (the neutrophil) responds to pathological cues and transports cargo to sites of active disease. The ROS-responsive release mechanism ensures that NAD⁺ is liberated specifically within the inflammatory microenvironment, while the selenium nanozyme simultaneously addresses oxidative stress and inflammasome activation. This integration of four complementary functions—inflammatory chemotaxis, ROS-triggered cargo release, antioxidant activity, and NAD⁺ replenishment—within a single living-cell carrier system illustrates a key principle: when the delivery vehicle possesses intrinsic homing and responsiveness, the overall system architecture can be simplified, potentially reducing the translational burden associated with multicomponent synthetic carriers.

Spatiotemporally programmable systems therefore integrate control over where, when and under what pathological conditions NAD⁺ cargoes are released. Their potential advantage lies in matching NAD⁺ restoration to dynamic disease states rather than delivering a fixed systemic dose. Importantly, temporal control is not merely an additional feature layered onto spatial targeting; it is equally essential because metabolic failure unfolds over time and the optimal window for intervention may be narrow. The examples discussed here demonstrate two distinct approaches to temporal programming: one based on phase-adaptive targeting that responds to changes in the tissue microenvironment over time (NZM), and another based on real-time responsiveness to acute pathological cues (neutrophil-mediated delivery). Both strategies share the common goal of aligning NAD⁺ availability with the evolving needs of diseased tissues, moving beyond the static supplementation paradigm. However, these systems also introduce substantial translational challenges. Multicomponent designs, living cell carriers, hybrid membranes and responsive nanozymes may be difficult to manufacture reproducibly, characterise mechanistically and regulate clinically. Therefore, the key question is not simply whether a system is more sophisticated, but whether each added layer of responsiveness produces a measurable biological advantage that justifies the added complexity. This consideration is particularly pertinent for spatiotemporally programmable systems, where the translational burden increases with each additional functional layer, and where clear evidence of stage-specific benefit is needed to justify the developmental investment.

Translational Challenges and Future Directions

The spatiotemporal hierarchical precision framework outlined above provides a conceptual roadmap for NAD⁺ nanomedicine, yet its real-world clinical translation faces multi-layered practical constraints that impose stringent manufacturing, pharmacodynamic and safety requirements. Clinical products must demonstrate not only that NAD⁺-related cargoes can be delivered, but also that functional restoration occurs within the intended biological compartment, at a therapeutically appropriate magnitude and duration, with acceptable long-term safety and reproducible product quality. Many proof-of-concept studies have demonstrated short-term efficacy in animal models, yet few have addressed how these effects could be measured, dosed, controlled, manufactured, and interpreted in patients. We therefore reorganise the principal translational requirements into three interconnected thematic clusters: pharmacodynamics and patient stratification; safety and dose optimization; and manufacturing, standardization, and regulatory strategy. Within each cluster, we outline near-term, actionable approaches that may help move the field from proof-of-concept studies towards clinically viable products (Figure 9).

Pharmacodynamics and Patient Stratification: Defining Target Engagement and Identifying Responders

A fundamental obstacle is the lack of clinically applicable tools that report NAD⁺ restoration with cellular or subcellular resolution. Current clinical assays usually measure NAD⁺ and related metabolites in blood, or occasionally in tissue homogenates. These bulk measurements cannot distinguish nuclear, cytosolic and mitochondrial pools, nor can they define which cell population within a diseased tissue has been restored. This distinction is crucial for NAD⁺ nanomedicine. For example, a carrier that increases total renal NAD⁺ but fails to restore mitochondrial NAD⁺-dependent function in proximal tubular cells may show limited therapeutic benefit despite encouraging tissue pharmacokinetics. Future development will require pharmacodynamic readouts that connect carrier localisation, cargo release and functional NAD⁺ pathway engagement.

Near-term solutions should prioritize surrogate pharmacodynamic panels that do not require invasive tissue sampling. These may include: (1) NAD⁺/NADH ratios and NAD⁺ metabolomic profiles in accessible patient samples (blood, urine, or cerebrospinal fluid), which can provide indirect readouts of systemic NAD⁺ flux; (2) SIRT1- or SIRT3-associated deacetylation signatures as markers of NAD⁺-dependent enzymatic activity; (3) PARP activity assays to assess DNA repair pathway engagement; (4) mitochondrial respiration assays in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as a surrogate for systemic mitochondrial function; and (5) transcriptomic or proteomic signatures of NAD⁺-dependent pathway impairment, which may help identify patients with compartment-specific NAD⁺ deficiency. Isotopically labelled nicotinamide, NMN or related tracers could also help track tissue distribution and metabolic flux, although they are unlikely to provide subcellular resolution in patients. These markers are imperfect and should not be interpreted as direct measures of compartmental NAD⁺. However, when integrated with biodistribution and efficacy data, they may help distinguish nominal NAD⁺ elevation from functional restoration of disease-relevant pathways.

Not all patients with low systemic NAD⁺ levels are likely to benefit from precision NAD⁺ delivery. Some may already have adequate NAD⁺ availability in the disease-relevant compartment, whereas others may have defects downstream of NAD⁺, such as impaired sirtuin expression, mitochondrial electron transport chain dysfunction, irreversible tissue loss or dominant inflammatory drivers that are not corrected by NAD⁺ restoration. Without appropriate patient selection, a biologically effective NAD⁺ nanomedicine could fail because it is tested in an unselected population. Patient stratification should therefore be based on accessible biomarkers that reflect the delivery barrier and metabolic defect relevant to each indication. In acute kidney injury, urinary NAD⁺ metabolites or markers of impaired salvage pathway activity may help identify patients with tubular metabolic vulnerability. In neurodegenerative disease, cerebrospinal fluid NAD⁺-related metabolites, neuroimaging signatures or peripheral mitochondrial functional assays could help enrich for patients with metabolic dysfunction. In inflammatory diseases, circulating immune-cell metabolism or inflammatory-metabolic signatures may identify patients most likely to benefit from targeted NAD⁺ restoration. Future trials should incorporate pre-specified enrichment strategies using companion or complementary diagnostics, with the goal of matching delivery logic to disease mechanism.

Safety and Dose Optimisation: Balancing Biological Benefit Against Context-Specific Risks

The safety of NAD⁺ nanomedicines must be considered at two levels: the biological consequences of NAD⁺ restoration and the material-specific risks of the carrier. Short-term studies of NAD⁺ precursors suggest acceptable tolerability in many settings, but these data do not fully address chronic, tissue-selective or compartment-directed NAD⁺ elevation. NAD⁺ supports DNA repair, mitochondrial function and cell survival, which may be beneficial in degenerative or inflammatory disease but potentially problematic in contexts of uncontrolled proliferation, pre-malignant transformation or tumour metabolism. Although clinical evidence has not established that NAD⁺ supplementation increases cancer risk, dysregulation of PARPs, sirtuins, CD38 and other NAD⁺-associated pathways in malignancy warrants careful evaluation. Other potential risks include prolonged survival of damaged or senescent cells, altered immune-cell function, excessive suppression or activation of inflammatory pathways, redox imbalance and tissue-specific metabolic rewiring. These risks are likely to depend on disease context, target tissue, dosing schedule and the cell populations exposed to increased NAD⁺ availability. Therefore, safety assessment should not treat NAD⁺ restoration as uniformly beneficial, but should define where and for how long NAD⁺ availability is increased.

Carrier-related risks require separate evaluation. Nanocarriers may induce complement activation, protein-corona-dependent biodistribution shifts, immunogenicity, long-term material persistence, unexpected degradation products or off-target accumulation in liver, spleen and bone marrow. Biomimetic systems such as extracellular vesicles or membrane-coated particles add further concerns related to donor variability, residual endogenous cargoes, pathogen safety and immune recognition. For chronic or repeated administration, these issues may be as important as the NAD⁺ cargo itself.

Mitigation strategies include cell-type-specific targeting, biodegradable materials, intermittent rather than continuous dosing, and indication-specific toxicology studies. Critically, dose design for NAD⁺ nanomedicines cannot simply follow the maximum tolerated dose paradigm used for many cytotoxic or anti-infective agents. NAD⁺ is a ubiquitous metabolic cofactor, not a conventional receptor-targeted drug. Raising systemic NAD⁺ availability beyond a certain threshold may not increase therapeutic benefit and could perturb NAD⁺-dependent processes in non-target tissues. The relevant dosing question is therefore not how much NAD⁺ can be delivered, but how much restoration is sufficient to normalise the limiting pathway without disrupting other NAD⁺-dependent networks. For each indication, preclinical studies should define the disease-relevant NAD⁺ pool, the minimum effective restoration threshold, and the duration of restoration required for phenotypic benefit. Temporal dosing also deserves greater attention: acute injury may require short, high-intensity restoration during a defined therapeutic window, whereas chronic degenerative disease may benefit from intermittent or pulsatile regimens that avoid continuous metabolic stimulation. Long-term carcinogenicity and immunotoxicity assessments may be particularly important when targeting tissues with high proliferative turnover, such as gut, bone marrow and skin. For first-in-class NAD⁺ nanomedicines, post-approval registries or long-term follow-up may be needed to detect rare delayed adverse events.

Manufacturing, Standardization, and Regulatory Strategy: From Complex Prototypes to Clinically Viable Products

Many NAD⁺ nanocarriers reported in preclinical studies are assembled using multi-step protocols that are difficult to scale, standardize or validate under good manufacturing practice conditions. This is particularly important for NAD⁺-related cargoes, which may be hydrophilic, labile, redox-active or prone to leakage during processing and storage. Organic solvents, sonication, high-shear mixing, sterilisation procedures or prolonged aqueous exposure can alter cargo integrity or release behaviour. Even clinically mature platforms such as liposomes can show limited and variable loading of small hydrophilic molecules without active-loading strategies. Biomimetic systems pose additional manufacturing challenges: extracellular vesicles, cell-membrane-coated particles and living cell-based carriers may contain endogenous nucleic acids, proteins, lipids and metabolites that vary by donor, cell source, culture condition and isolation method. These features may contribute to biological performance, but they also complicate definition of critical quality attributes, potency assays, sterility testing and batch release criteria.

For early clinical development, priority should be given to platforms with scalable and controllable manufacturing routes. Specific near-term solutions include: (1) microfluidic or ethanol-injection methods for lipid nanoparticles, enabling precise control over particle size and encapsulation efficiency; (2) controlled nanoprecipitation for polymeric nanoparticles, facilitating batch-to-batch reproducibility; (3) tangential-flow filtration for purification and concentration, reducing product loss and improving yield; (4) continuous manufacturing workflows coupled with in-line quality monitoring, which provide real-time control over critical process parameters and reduce batch-to-batch variability; and (5) high-pressure homogenisation where appropriate for lipid-based formulations.

A quantitative quality-control strategy should include particle size distribution, surface charge, morphology, cargo identity and integrity, encapsulation efficiency, release kinetics, residual solvents or reagents, endotoxin, sterility, stability and potency-related assays. Biomimetic or multicomponent systems should be advanced only when their added biological selectivity cannot be achieved by simpler synthetic alternatives. Their manufacturing strategy should be developed with regulatory input early, rather than after proof-of-concept efficacy has already been established.

Regulatory and clinical-trial design also require clearer strategies. NAD⁺ nanomedicines occupy a complex space between metabolic therapeutics, nanomedicines, biologics and, in some cases, cell-derived or biomimetic products. Their clinical evaluation cannot rely solely on the regulatory logic used for dietary supplements or conventional small molecules. Product-specific critical quality attributes, mechanism-linked potency assays and clinically meaningful pharmacodynamic endpoints will be required. Trial design should be matched to the indication and delivery mechanism. Acute indications such as acute kidney injury, acute lung injury or sepsis may offer measurable short-term organ-function endpoints, but they also involve heterogeneous patient populations and narrow therapeutic windows. Chronic ageing-related diseases may allow longer treatment periods, but endpoints can be slower, more variable and more susceptible to placebo effects or comorbidities. Broad “anti-ageing” claims are unlikely to be clinically or regulatorily tractable; early development may be more feasible in well-defined diseases with measurable compartmental metabolic dysfunction and objective functional endpoints. Comparator selection is also important: depending on the cargo and indication, trials may need to compare nanodelivery against free NAD⁺, NMN, NR, standard-of-care therapy or carrier-only controls. Without appropriate comparators, it will be difficult to determine whether therapeutic benefit comes from NAD⁺ restoration, carrier bioactivity, improved biodistribution or non-specific anti-inflammatory effects. Early engagement with regulators will be important to define acceptable endpoints, quality attributes, safety packages and risk-mitigation strategies.

Conclusion

NAD⁺ biology has moved from a classical metabolic concept to a therapeutic axis with broad relevance to ageing, inflammation, tissue injury and degenerative disease. However, the therapeutic translation of NAD⁺ restoration remains constrained by a central paradox: NAD⁺ is ubiquitous, yet its disease-modifying effects are likely to be highly context-, cell- and compartment-dependent. Systemic supplementation with NAD⁺ precursors can increase circulating or bulk tissue NAD⁺ levels, but such changes do not necessarily indicate functional restoration in the disease-relevant cellular compartment. This distinction is particularly important because NAD⁺-dependent processes are distributed across the nucleus, cytosol and mitochondria, and are governed by spatially restricted enzymes, transport mechanisms and metabolic fluxes.

In this Review, we propose that NAD⁺ nanomedicine should be understood not simply as a delivery strategy, but as a hierarchical precision framework. At the first level, nanocarriers can improve pharmacokinetics, protect labile cargoes and enhance tissue accumulation. At the second level, they can direct NAD⁺-related interventions towards specific diseased organs or pathological microenvironments. At the third and most demanding level, they should enable functional restoration of NAD⁺-dependent pathways in the relevant cell type and subcellular compartment. This hierarchy provides a useful lens for interpreting current preclinical studies: therapeutic benefit should not be inferred solely from increased NAD⁺ abundance, but from evidence that the limiting NAD⁺-dependent pathway has been restored at the site where disease is driven.

Spatial precision alone, however, is unlikely to be sufficient. Temporal control must complement tissue, cellular and organelle targeting by coordinating cargo release with disease stage, fluctuating metabolic demand and the therapeutic window in which NAD⁺ restoration is beneficial. Accordingly, the ultimate objective is not sustained or indiscriminate NAD⁺ elevation, but the delivery of an appropriate intervention to the relevant site, at an appropriate dose and for an appropriate duration. Such spatiotemporal control may be particularly important in dynamic pathological settings—including acute injury, episodic inflammation and progressive degeneration—in which the biological consequences of NAD⁺ elevation may change over time. Despite encouraging proof-of-concept studies, the field remains at an early translational stage. Several barriers must be addressed before NAD⁺ nanomedicines can progress from elegant prototypes to clinically credible products. First, pharmacodynamic tools capable of distinguishing nominal NAD⁺ elevation from functional pathway restoration are urgently needed. Second, dose design should move beyond maximum tolerated dose paradigms and instead define the minimum effective restoration required for each disease context. Third, long-term safety evaluation must consider both the biological consequences of sustained or compartment-selective NAD⁺ elevation and the material-specific risks of repeated nanocarrier exposure. Fourth, manufacturability, reproducibility and potency testing should be incorporated early in platform development rather than treated as downstream technical issues. Finally, clinical studies will require rational patient stratification, appropriate comparators and mechanism-linked endpoints to determine whether therapeutic effects arise from NAD⁺ restoration, carrier bioactivity or improved biodistribution.

Emerging technologies may help address these challenges and accelerate translation. Artificial intelligence and machine-learning approaches could support the multi-parameter optimization of nanocarrier composition, targeting ligands, release kinetics and dosing regimens. Organ-on-a-chip systems, patient-derived organoids and other human-relevant models may enable more predictive evaluation of biological barriers, tissue-specific efficacy and long-term toxicity than conventional preclinical models alone. In parallel, single-cell and spatial multi-omics, advanced metabolic imaging and isotope-tracing approaches could identify disease-relevant NAD⁺ deficits and verify pathway restoration with cellular and subcellular resolution. The development of standardized, mechanism-linked potency assays will also be essential for ensuring batch comparability, supporting regulatory evaluation and connecting product attributes to clinically relevant biological activity. The future of this field will therefore depend on a shift from “raising NAD⁺” to “restoring NAD⁺ function with spatial, temporal and disease specificity”. This transition will require closer integration of NAD⁺ metabolism, nanomedicine engineering, systems pharmacology, biomarker science and regulatory strategy. If these challenges are addressed, precision NAD⁺ nanomedicine could provide a new class of metabolism-directed therapeutics for diseases in which conventional systemic supplementation is insufficient. More broadly, the principles emerging from this field may establish a general framework for converting ubiquitous metabolites, metabolic cofactors and other broadly distributed biological mediators into spatiotemporally controlled, compartment-resolved and mechanism-guided therapies.
