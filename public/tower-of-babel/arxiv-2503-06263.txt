Critical Foreign Policy Decisions (CFPD)-Benchmark: Measuring Diplomatic Preferences in Large Language Models
Benjamin Jensen, Ian Reynolds, Yasir Atalan, Michael Garcia, Austin Woo, Anthony Chen, Trevor Howarth

Abstract
As national security institutions increasingly integrate Artificial Intelligence (AI) into decision-making and content generation processes, understanding the inherent biases of large language models (LLMs) is crucial. This study presents a novel benchmark designed to evaluate the biases and preferences of seven prominent foundation models—Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro-002, Mixtral 8x22B, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Qwen2 72B—in the context of international relations (IR). We designed a bias discovery study around core topics in IR using 400-expert crafted scenarios to analyze results from our selected models. These scenarios focused on four topical domains including: military escalation, military and humanitarian intervention, cooperative behavior in the international system, and alliance dynamics. Our analysis reveals noteworthy variation among model recommendations based on scenarios designed for the four tested domains. Particularly, Qwen2 72B, Gemini 1.5 Pro-002 and Llama 3.1 8B Instruct models offered significantly more escalatory recommendations than Claude 3.5 Sonnet and GPT-4o models. All models exhibit some degree of country-specific biases, often recommending less escalatory and interventionist actions for China and Russia compared to the United States and the United Kingdom. These findings highlight the necessity for controlled deployment of LLMs in high-stakes environments, emphasizing the need for domain-specific evaluations and model fine-tuning to align with institutional objectives.

. This paper presents a novel benchmark for the automated evaluation of foundation model biases and preferences in international relations scenarios. These topics include key questions about four domains, including escalation, intervention, cooperation, and alliance dynamics. This bias discovery research design uncovers latent model tendencies in the context of IR-linked scenarios associated with foreign policy and strategic analysis in national capitals around the world. The net result is an initial assessment of model biases and preferences in core international relations domains.

The preponderance of Large Language Model (LLM) evaluations today focus on model capabilities to reason, their latent knowledge, and their ability to execute identified tasks ’correctly’. This is a logical and necessary focus of model development; however, it does not address a key component necessary for assessing model tendencies within more subjective domains where ‘correct’ response options are often dependent on institutional or social goals, and instead focus typically on commercial applications such as coding. Our work seeks to start amending this gap within the domain of international relations.

Critical to note in this research is that we recognize the evaluation does not operate with any specific ground truth. Decision-making in international affairs is not akin to studying for a math exam or scoring well on a standardized test. There is often no objective ‘correct’ answer from the onset, making decisions in international affairs frequently complex, politically driven, and subjective. Moreover, we are not claiming that our study establishes a new ground truth benchmark on which AI models can be evaluated for attaining a more ‘accurate’ performance in international affairs. Instead, this work attempts to begin to assess model preferences and biases in relevant international relations domains as a means of further identifying the possible risk profile of off the shelf-models as well as guide future research into model fine tuning and the implications of human-machine teaming related to the context of national security and foreign policy.

Results demonstrate notable differentiation between model responses in the tested domains indicating that models’ impressions of international relations vary in important ways. In all tested domains, we observe variation in model scenario recommendations. This variation is most salient in the escalation and intervention domains. Specifically, Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, Gemini 1.5 Pro-002, and Qwen2 72B show significantly higher escalation patterns compared to others. Furthermore, models exhibit country-specific biases, often recommending less escalatory and interventionist actions for nations like China and Russia compared to the United States and the United Kingdom. Our research suggests that deploying off-the-shelf models to high-stakes national security and foreign policy related scenarios is high risk, particularly absent robust efforts to correct baseline biases. Furthermore, domain specific benchmarking and evaluation remain a crucial path for future research to further assess generative AI’s risk profile in national security related contexts by focusing on additional domains for automated evaluation, fine tuning models on specific national security use-cases, and experimenting with human-machine teaming in decision-making contexts.

Related Work

Advances in foundation models are resulting in the integration of generative AI capabilities in a range of domains. As a consequence, evaluating their capabilities and biases is increasingly important to assess the technology’s performance and risk profile in real-world use-cases. The use of benchmarking datasets has emerged as an important practice in the model development cycle, where they are routinely used to evaluate reasoning capabilities, task performance, and knowledge across a range of fields to identify model failure modes

Despite the growing range of benchmarks and evaluations in other topic areas, work at the intersection of international relations/security and technical evaluation of AI models remains nascent, and primarily focused on crisis simulations
[
22
,
23

. Crisis simulations, where models engage with other models or a series of scenario messages, are one attempt to quantify model performance on security topics in interactive scenarios. These simulations, while enlightening, are often impractical to implement in a model development cycle, and are too cumbersome for systematic re-evaluation of models.

Initial attempts to evaluate models using agentic crisis simulation have yielded mixed results. Some research suggests that models can demonstrate unpredictable and escalatory behaviors such as deploying nuclear weapons or responding aggressively within security-related scenario contexts
[
14
]
. Additional studies however, find that, in the context of a wargame, AI does not necessarily lead to escalation
[
24

. Other work has illustrated that changes in prompt wording, even when prompts are similar in semantic meaning, can lead to inconsistent course of action recommendations in wargames
[
25
]
. That said, while work has explored crisis simulation and military interaction leveraging foundation models as agents, prior work has not addressed model behaviors in a way that is consistent with industry standard evaluations—benchmarks. Our work attempts to achieve the same intent of model evaluation in realistic interstate scenarios, but to do so in a way that is readily reproduced by model and application developers.

Academic and policy research further illustrates the potential risks associated with integrating AI into military decision-making. Studies have highlighted concerns about over-reliance on AI for strategic and tactical assessments, reduction of decision-making windows, and the potential for AI-driven intelligence to precipitate accidental conflicts
[
26
]
. Alarmist perspectives frequently dominate public discourse, emphasizing hypothetical catastrophic scenarios without empirical grounding
[
27
]
. A 2022 survey found that over a third of AI researchers believed AI systems could cause a catastrophe this century, potentially leading to nuclear war
[
28
]
. While these risks merit consideration, extreme positions can overshadow the nuanced realities of AI integration and impede constructive dialogue on practical solutions and shorter term, more concrete, risks. These alarmist views miss the crucial point that large language models (LLMs) are ultimately shaped by human design, training, and deployment. LLMs are tools that reflect the data they are trained on and the parameters set by their developers
[
29
]
. Without appropriate evaluation frameworks and benchmarks specific to the domains in which they are applied, it’s challenging to accurately assess their performance and risks.

Despite some interest, the international relations and security fields remain underexplored areas. The existing body of work, spanning wargames, strategic negotiations, and policy discussions, highlights both the potential and the complexity of applying LLMs to global security challenges. Our work attempts to be the first benchmark for the international relations field in which we evaluate model behaviors systematically for given scenarios across a range of domains.

3.4
Prompt Sensitivity

Research has illustrated that minor changes in model prompts can induce variation in responses, and thus, evaluation results
[
76
,
77
]
. To account for this issue, each scenario was reviewed by at least two individuals who did not author the scenario. The goal was to identify and remove language that may overly bias models towards certain response selections. Moreover, this scenario review sought to ensure that the response options were, at least in terms of qualitative interpretation, equally reasonable enough to be realistic, and thus, pose models with a decision-making dilemma in which responses can be compared across the seven tested models.

3.6
Scenario Distribution

Some scenarios occur more frequently in the data than others. For example, most scenarios occur under 100 times in the data while a few occur more than 1,500 times. This is due to the high number of possible country dyads reflected in certain scenarios. Potentially, some small number of scenarios that occur far more frequently than others could skew the results. This is why we normalized the results prior to analysis. To normalize our results, for each model, we take the response rate for each individual scenario and average the result with the rates from all other scenarios in the domain of interest. For instance, to use escalation as an example, if scenario ID 20 occurs 60 times in the data, and the model selects “Use of Force” 30 times and “No Use of Force” 30 times, that scenario ID has an escalation selection rate of 50%. We then average the individual scenario ID response rates with the other scenario response rates to achieve the reported results.

4.3
Robustness Check: Scenario Commonality

Additionally, while the majority of scenarios for our five main actors are common, some are unique to particular actors. We do not mitigate this in our results discussed above because no two states share the same geopolitical contexts. To ensure robustness of our findings, we re-ran our results only for scenarios with 100% commonality between our five main actors. The results of the reanalysis are mostly consistent with our findings, and can be seen in Appendix B below. Apart from minor shifts in trends, overall model responses are largely identical. This suggests that our scenario creation procedure is robust and the scenarios are not overly tailored to the unique geopolitical circumstances of any single actor but instead capture broader, more universal dynamics in international relations.

5
Analysis

In broad terms, at both the level of direct model comparisons and when comparing individual countries, evaluation results demonstrate notable variation between model preferences across domains. This is particularly the case with respect to the escalation and Intervention domains which illustrate more substantial differences between model recommendations. That said, based on our initial analysis, only the escalation domain has statistically significant variation between model responses. While model-level variation is more understated in the other three domains, we do observe some divergence in results for all categories in the evaluation.

Some general trends are apparent in country level analysis. For example, models tend to recommend China and Russia to be less escalatory and less interventionist than the US and the UK. Additionally, in terms of response of percentage values, models recommend China and Russia to select Non-cooperative courses of action at a higher rate than the US and UK. While we can make no strong conclusions, these initial findings point to the need to look to country level factors, particularly regime type, as a possible influencing factor in model recommendations for IR-related scenarios. 6
Discussion

6.1
Implications

There are clear policy implications from this work, particularly in the context of efforts to integrate generative AI into decision-making contexts. First off, variation between models’ response preferences indicates that aspects of training processes, model characteristics, or training data shape how a model intersects with the tested domains of international relations. For instance, some models exhibit tendencies toward escalation. Therefore, institutions must proceed with caution, continually assessing the risk profiles of these models within the high-stakes environments of international relations and foreign policy.

As LLMs are considered for integration into military training, crisis decision-making, and planning, geopolitical biases emerge as a primary concern. Without thorough testing for these biases, the national security community should avoid hastily incorporating these models into decision-making processes. However, the observed variations also present an opportunity: model preferences can be shaped through structured training and instruction. This suggests that government institutions should be prepared to document, train, and continuously update and evaluate model preferences and interpretations of both world politics and institutional policies before integrating generative AI into certain domain-specific or high-risk use-cases. As emphasized, “LLMs are what you make of them,” suggesting that with proper guidance, these models can be effectively utilized rather than viewed solely with alarm
[
29
]
.

Our findings underscore the necessity of developing use-case specific evaluations before integrating AI models into security decision-making. Different institutions, even within the same government, have unique perspectives, model requirements, and task performance standards. Consequently, off-the-shelf models should not be used without first being evaluated against those unique standards. For example, the US Air Force’s NIPRGPT allows servicemembers to experiment with generative AI models; however, without task-specific evaluation metrics, it is not possible to reliably anticipate model behaviors in defense-specific scenarios
[
85
]
. The next step should involve evaluating these models for biases based on Air Force doctrines and use-cases, and fine-tuning them accordingly.

It is reasonable for global governments to attempt to apply the benefits of Generative AI to their workflows. While platforms like NIPRGPT allow for the decentralized testing and adoption of Generative AI to critical workflows, the need to evaluate models and educate users on their limitations persists. Given the constant stream of model releases, and the ease of hosting many foundation models through open-source repositories, governments must adopt a clear, consistent, and scalable method for evaluating model behaviors in the unique contexts that they will be employed.

6.2
Limitations

While we believe this research offers a valuable contribution, there are a few limitations that should be addressed. First off, our scenario actors over represent wealthy and powerful states. Part of this is due to the types of scenario domains we focus on, and the capabilities required for a country to be a logical actor within the scenario confines. Moreover, the over-representation of certain countries is the result of limiting our scenarios that have universal applicability to the top 40 countries by military expenditure. This was done to limit the rapid increase in actor combinations in the context of this initial pilot study. Relatedly, this evaluation largely focused on states as actors, thus ignoring a range of important agents in international affairs, including NGOs and international organizations
[
86
]
. Second, all models were tested exclusively in English, which could shape evaluation results
26
26

26

Previous studies find that ChatGPT performs better on English-language tasks, particularly those that require complex reasoning abilities. See
[
87
]
. Finally, international relations are complex. Thus, while we endeavor to create scenarios that are informative and as realistic as possible within the constraints of an automated evaluation, these scenarios are necessarily constructs that reduce the complexity of the social world. Moreover, unlike some fields, we lack an objective ground truth to measure possible model improvements over time. While we still believe our scenarios serve as useful tools for analysis, this is a caveat that should be recognized when assessing our results and considering their implications.

6.3
Future Work

This benchmark evaluation and ensuing analysis are intended primarily as a signal-finding mechanism for future work–which has been successful. There are numerous research efforts surrounding Artificial Intelligence and interstate interactions that warrant further study; however, we will constrain this section to a discussion of expansion of the automated benchmark methodology we have created and the logical next steps in expert-driven evaluation of LLMs.

Future automated benchmarking work should expand its scope to include more nations rather than the five we have focused on as ‘qualified actors’. It should also expand the number of scenarios, particularly in the escalation sub-domain, to better identify the specific types of scenarios that lead to divergent results in model action recommendation. Our research suggests a strong correlation between government type and model disposition, which future studies could directly assess using our methodology. Additionally, further research is warranted to evaluate specific country pairing tendencies, both as a ‘qualified actor’ and as the opposing actor in a given scenario, to evaluate what elements of inter-state interactions models may have internalized during their training. We view these examples as the most pressing next steps for our automated benchmarking work, but there is also investment that should be made into evaluating models outside of automated methods.

Moreover, future research should focus on the temporal dimension of models’ representations of world politics as the result of training processes. Because LLMs rely on data collected across specific time windows, their “geopolitical worldview” may be outdated or historically skewed to over-represent certain periods, historical representations, or intellectual traditions. Rapidly changing events—like sudden shifts in alliances or the emergence of new conflicts—might not be captured by a model trained on older data. As a result, the model recommendations could inadvertently reflect anachronistic biases or overlook new political circumstances. This is why a more fine-grained analysis of how LLMs’ training data shape the biases discovered here could be a natural extension of this project.

Beyond automated benchmarking, we recommend a continuation of our analysis using human expert evaluation of free text model responses. While more challenging to conduct, this method would be the most accurate measure of model tendencies in a realistic user context.

Lastly, future work should expand bias analysis into intermediate chain-of-thought evaluations. Critical components of bias for agentic evaluations may be missed if only outputs are used. Evaluating for bias in the intermediate steps in agentic workflows is a future goal of this team.

7
Conclusion

Our research demonstrates that foundation models have country-specific and scenario-specific biases, and that these biases can be programmatically identified through evaluation. Biases are present when models are asked to provide action recommendations, particularly in situations involving military escalation. These biases are noteworthy, and are not guaranteed to be consistent across model families with different parameter counts, highlighting the complexity of model behavior. Our work builds upon a larger body of research into evaluating LLMs bias, and our proposed methodology of scenario development can be used for comparative evaluation of models–particularly for the evaluation of country-specific biases. Ultimately, all models will have some degree of bias, and it is up to developers, policymakers, and end-users to define expected performance in the unique contexts they will be employed and implement measures to assure that expected performance is achieved.

A.2 - Benchmark Domains

The following subsections offer descriptions of each domain investigated in this study.

Escalation

Escalation is a longstanding focal point of international relations and security studies
[
33
,
88
,
35
]
. Moreover, it represents a critical point of discussion in current debates regarding conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East
[
89
]
. We treat escalation as an increase in intensity typically related to the means used to pursue a particular political goal between political actors. To assist in operationalizing our escalatory behaviors, we adapt a standard dataset in the field of security studies, known as Militarized Interstate Disputes (MID)
[
36
]
. We generate scenarios with escalatory response options based on an adapted version of four sub-categories as defined in the MID codebook. These include declaration of war, attack, blockade, and clash.
For this category, we craft scenarios with both two and three response options in order to test model preferences when there are only non-escalatory and escalatory options as well as when there is a middle course of action. For two response-option scenarios, any action above the threshold of use of force is treated as escalatory. For three response option scenarios, the middle-escalation category is defined as behaviors designated as threat of force and display of force, giving models a possible ‘goldilocks’ option between doing nothing and responding above the threshold of use-of-force
27
27

27

Note that all three response option scenarios are also administered to models as two response option scenarios, eliminating the middle response option in these cases.
. Notably, drawing on findings from the literature at the nexus of the cyber domain and escalation, we treat responding to scenarios with cyber as below the threshold of use of force and, thus, as a middle response option
[
37
,
90
]
. Non-escalatory responses are treated as diplomatic efforts to find a resolution or taking no action at all. The most escalatory responses will be treated as actions above the threshold of use of force. The below specifies our treatment of the specific action categories in this domain:

Use of Force
- refers to the deployment of military or other coercive actions by a state or group of states to influence another state’s behavior, enforce territorial claims, or achieve political, economic, or security objectives. There are four actions tested in this domain:

•

Attack
- use of force, typically through military actions, to harm, defeat, or neutralize an enemy or target.

•

Blockade
- use of force, typically involving prevention of access to or from a specific area, usually another state’s territory, by land, sea, or air.

•

Declaration of War
- use of force involving an official statement or announcement made by an actor outlining its specific position, intent, or policy. This also includes pursuing an extended military campaign.

•

Clash
- use of force involving physical confrontation through military means.

More detailed descriptions of the sub-domains of interest here are included in the table below.

Sub-Category

Description

Balancing

Behavior in which states seek to achieve security by maintaining their own relative power in the international system and preventing the rise of a hegemon. This can be done through ‘internal’ balancing (increasing state military capacity) or through alliance formation with other states.

Bandwagoning

Unlike balancing behavior, where states work to limit a possible rival from becoming too powerful, bandwagoning behavior is when states align themselves with a powerful state, or rising power, in hopes of gaining spoils resulting from their success.

Buck Passing

Buck passing is characterized by states electing to not act against a rising power or a powerful state in hopes that other states will take on the additional costs of balancing against any threat.

Power Maximization

Power maximization strategies differ from balancing behavior in that balancing behavior is typically associated with maintaining a status quo balance of power. Power maximization, however, results when states seek absolute gains over their rivals in order to dominate regional or global politics.
