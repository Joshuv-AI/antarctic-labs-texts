Multivariate analysis of morphological variables in tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.)
Authors: Ali Khadivi, Farhad Mirheidari, Abdolvahid Saeidifar, Younes Moradi, Yazgan Tunç
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTamarind (Tamarindus indica L.) is a tree belonging to the Fabaceae family. This tree grows in subtropical and semi-arid tropical regions, and it is particularly common in India, Africa, and Southeast Asia. In this study, the morphological variations of 55 tamarind accessions collected from two areas of Sistan-va-Baluchestan province in Iran were evaluated using correlation matrix, principal component, and hierarchical cluster analyses based on 61 variables.ResultsOne-way ANOVA (p < 0.05) showed significant differences among the examined accessions. The coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 8.50% (lateral petal width) to 110.44% (flesh adherence to skin). 45 out of 61 traits (73.77% in total) had a CV greater than 20.00%. This value indicated that there was a high degree of variation among the examined accessions. Leaf length ranged from 35.38 to 97.68 mm, leaf width from 17.98 to 33.65 mm, fruit length ranged from 33.23 to 127.24 mm, and fruit seed weight from 0.65 to 9.79 g. Tree growth vigor, tree height, and branch flexibility were dominant with high values (28, 33, and 29 accessions, respectively). Peak of fruiting season was classified as late-April (15 accessions) and early-May (40 accessions). The obtained correlation data indicated significant relationships between leaf length and leaf width (r = 0.646**), fruit length and fruit weight (r = 0.710**), as well as fruit length and fruit seed weight (r = 0.599**). According to the principal component analysis, the first 17 principal components explained 83.28% of the total variation. The accessions are distributed across the four regions of the scatter plot. There were 15 accessions in cluster 1, 11 accessions in cluster 2, 18 accessions in cluster 3, and 11 accessions in cluster 4. According to the hierarchical cluster analysis, four clusters were formed, with the highest number of accessions in the B2 subgroup (34 accessions). The first 20 accessions were determined based on their individual quantitative trait performances, with the best results listed as follows: Bahoukalat-37, Bahoukalat-36, Bahoukalat-31, Bahoukalat-33, Tis-10, Bahoukalat-32, Bahoukalat-2, Bahoukalat-16, Bahoukalat-1, Tis-8, Bahoukalat-4, Bahoukalat-10, Tis-7, Bahoukalat-6, Bahoukalat-3, Bahoukalat-34, Tis-1, Bahoukalat-5, and Bahoukalat-29, respectively.ConclusionsThis study addresses key gaps in tamarind research, offering important insights into its genetic diversity and morphological traits. The results are expected to be a valuable resource for plant breeding programs, supporting the development of more adaptable and sustainable tamarind varieties. Further studies on accessions from diverse ecological conditions could deepen our understanding of its genetic diversity and aid future breeding efforts.

Introduction

Tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.), belonging to the Caesalpinioideae subfamily of the Fabaceae family, has a chromosome structure of 2n = 24 [21]. Tamarind is a slow-growing, evergreen tree with multiple uses that has naturalized in subtropical and semi-arid tropical regions worldwide [25]. This species, which is native to Africa, has spread to Australia, Asia, and America due to human migrations [39]. Almost every part of the tree (fruit pulp, seeds, flowers, leaves, wood, bark) has particular uses, including human and animal consumption, trade, construction, fuel wood, traditional medicine, and industrial processing [10]. Studies have categorized tamarind based on its biochemical content into three groups: 1) Sweet tamarind, which has a low acid content and high sugar content, is rich in minerals and vitamins, and is used in making jellies, chocolates, jams, and candies. 2) Sour tamarind, which has a high acid content and low sugar content, constitutes the largest population and is the richest natural source of acidifiers and tartaric acid used in food preparation due to its sour pulp. 3) Red tamarind, which has a high anthocyanin content, is used in pharmaceuticals, food processing, confectionery, and brewing. Red tamarind helps replace inorganic colorants and carcinogenic substances with significant commercial volume [35]. Additionally, these three characteristics make it one of the most affordable sources of multivitamins and minerals [16]. Tamarind is a large, long-lived tree characterized by its spreading crown. It exhibits high drought resistance, favors sunny conditions, and grows well in open spaces. Its thick, drooping branches offer ample shade and act as an efficient windbreak. The tree has a robust root system and is recognized for its ability to withstand hurricanes [9].

The wide geological distribution of the species, greater cross-pollination, and adaptability to various climatic zones contribute to the high level of diversity within a population, resulting in a large number of genotypes [11]. Since tamarinds are often propagated from seeds, various provenances and accessions with a wide range of morphological variability have developed, which can be utilized in breeding programs [8]. This characteristic has led to the identification of valuable accessions in Thailand [14], India [5, 31], Mali [39], Uganda [29], and Ecuador [2]. Despite the recognized significance of tamarind, there is a substantial gap in research concerning its diverse physical characteristics and the exploration of its potential economic applications. This lack of information obstructs the development of sustainable strategies needed to fully realize its economic and ecological benefits, limiting its broader adoption and hindering the optimization of resource allocation and conservation efforts. Involving farming communities in participatory plant breeding, ensuring access to a variety of high-quality seeds and planting materials, practicing ecological agriculture for healthier crops and environments, developing and marketing agricultural products, and strengthening the capacity of smallholder farmers to engage with formal research and extension services all facilitate and promote the equitable sharing of benefits [33].

An effective approach to conserving plant diversity is to explore and analyze genetic diversity, starting with the creation of germplasm collections [38]. This study aims to describe the population structure of tamarind accessions growing in their natural habitat by morphological characteristics (tree, leaf, flower, fruit, seed characteristics, etc.) and to provide information on the necessary genetic structure. Thus, this study will fill a significant gap in the literature. The findings obtained from the study will guide relevant researchers, and the accessions can be used in breeding programs based on specific needs.

Material and methods

Plant material

Morphological variability of 55 accessions belonging to tamarind collected from two areas of Sistan-va-Baluchestan province, Iran, including Bahoukalat (40 accessions) and Tis (15 accessions) was evaluated. The Bahoukalat area is located in Dashtyari county at 25˚42′08"N latitude, 61˚25′24"E longitude, and 38 m height above sea level. The Tis area is located in Chabahar county at 25˚21′32"N latitude, 60˚37′15"E longitude, and 17 m height above sea level. For correct sampling, a proper distance of at least 200 m between the accessions of each area was regarded so that the clone samples were not collected. The formal identification of the specimens was performed by Prof. Dr. Ali Khadivi. A herbarium voucher specimen with sediment number TI-2533 has been donated to the public available herbarium of the Faculty of Agriculture and Natural Resources of Arak University, Iran.

For this purpose, 50 adult leaves and 50 mature fruits were randomly selected and harvested from each accession. Statistical analysis

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using the GLM (General Linear Model) procedure in SAS software (SAS® Procedures) [34] was used to determine the phenotypic variation among accessions based on the recorded traits. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to determine the relationship between the recorded quantitative traits using SPSS software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) [27]. To identify the most important influencing traits in the grouping of accessions, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied using SPSS software. Hierarchical cluster analysis based on Ward's method and Euclidean distance coefficients using PAST software [18] was used to classify genotypes. The first and second principal components (PC1/PC2) were used to draw a two-dimensional plot by determining the distribution of genotypes, using PAST software.

Results and discussion

Pomological and morphological variation of accessions

To determine the phenotypic variations of the examined traits, minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV%) values were calculated and presented in Table 1. Accordingly, one-way ANOVA (p < 0.05) showed significant differences among the examined accessions. The CV ranged from 8.50% (lateral petal width) to 110.44% (flesh adherence to skin). Lateral petal width was followed by bracteole color (85.09%), fruit seed surface (82.75%), fruit seed color (75.47%), petal ground color (73.67%), and fruit flesh color (71.25%) respectively. 45 out of 61 traits (73.77% in total) had a CVs greater than 20.00%. This value indicated that there was a high degree of variation among the examined accessions [22].

Leaf length ranged from 35.38 to 97.68 mm, leaf width varied between 17.98 and 33.65 mm, petiole length changed between 2.84 and 8.80 mm, petiole width ranged from 0.48 to 1.08 mm, leaflet length varied between 11.33 and 19.77 mm, and leaflet width changed between 4.08 and 7.48 mm. In a study conducted in India, it was determined that leaf length ranged from 39.8 to 59.3 mm, and leaflet length varied between 15.7 and 25.0 mm [19]. In a study conducted in Colombia, petiole length was reported to range from 3 to 10 mm [30]. Our findings about the leaf are partially similar to the findings of the researchers. Leaves can vary depending on climate and genetic diversity [14].

Fruit length ranged from 33.23 to 127.24 mm, fruit width from 14.48 to 31.14 mm, and fruit thickness from 10.63 to 20.07 mm. Fruit stalk length varied between 8.65 and 21.38 mm, while fruit stalk diameter changed between 1.32 and 3.81 mm. Fruit weight varied between 3.22 to 26.02 g, fruit skin thickness changed between 0.50 and 1.16 mm, and fruit skin weight ranged from 0.92 to 6.10 g. Number of seeds in fruit ranged from 2 to 12, fruit seed weight varied between 0.65 and 9.79 g, fruit seed length changed between 8.65 and 15.27 mm, fruit seed width ranged from 5.24 to 13.12 mm, and fruit seed thickness varied between 3.32 and 7.81 mm. In the study conducted by Divakara [9], fruit weight changed between 4.23 and 51.33 g, fruit thickness varied between 13.8 and 22.5 mm, and fruit width ranged from 18.0 to 54.8 mm. Van den Bilcke et al. [39] reported fruit length to be between 97.8 and 127.0 mm, fruit width ranging from 15.6 to 18.7 mm, fruit thickness between 13.2 and 14.2 mm, and number of seeds in fruit between 5 and 7. Mayavel et al. [26] detected in their study carried out in India that fruit length ranged from 49.6 to 120.2 mm, fruit width from 13.2 to 31.5 mm, number of seeds in fruit from 3 to 8, and fruit seed weight from 1.93 to 7.86 g. In a study performed in Uganda, it was determined that fruit width ranged from 11.0 to 30.0 mm, fruit weight varied between 0.19 and 22.0 g, and number of seeds in fruit changed between 1 and 17 [28]. Osorio et al. [30] found fruit length to range from 42.4 to 148.8 mm, fruit width changed between 8.0 and 33.7 mm, fruit weight varied between 1.97 and 15.27 g, number of seeds in fruit ranged from 1 to 8, fruit seed weight changed between 0.46 and 6.6 g, fruit seed length varied between 7.4 and 23.5 mm, and fruit seed width ranged from 6.8 to 19.3 mm. In a study carried out in India, it was determined that fruit thickness ranged from 11.70 to 23.40 mm, fruit weight varied between 3.11 and 18.86 g, fruit skin weight changed between 1.82 and 10.22 g, number of seeds in fruit ranged from 3 to 14, and fruit seed weight varied between 2.52 and 14.89 g [3]. Kanupriya et al. [21] detected fruit length as ranging from 45.0 to 259.2 mm and fruit width from 8.2 to 39.6 mm. Our findings are generally in line with the findings of the researchers, and it is thought that the small differences that emerged may be due to the different accessions and different ecological conditions used in the study. Fruit characteristics are key quantitative hereditary traits that influence the likelihood of consumer acceptance [7].

The frequency distribution for the measured qualitative morphological characteristics in the studied tamarind accessions is given in detail in Table 2. Tree growth habit was classified into four categories: weeping (10 accessions), spreading (21 accessions), open (12 accessions), and semi-erect (12 accessions). Tree growth vigor, tree height, and branch flexibility were dominant with high values (28, 33, and 29 accessions, respectively). Leaf density was classified as low for 2 accessions, moderate for 19 accessions, and high for 34 accessions. As a result, high leaf density was observed in the accessions. High leaf density provides a greater surface area, allowing the plant to utilize sunlight more effectively, which increases the rate of photosynthesis. It can also enhance the plant's capacity to retain water vapor and preserve humidity, thereby increasing resistance to drought conditions. This allows for more stomata to open for gas exchange, which supports photosynthesis by increasing CO₂ intake. Leaves help cool the plant's surface. A dense leaf canopy prevents direct sunlight from reaching other parts of the plant, maintaining temperature balance. A high leaf structure can reduce soil erosion, aiding in the better development of the root system. It allows the plant to utilize water and nutrients more efficiently, supporting growth and development. High leaf density can protect the plant from pests and diseases and can also repel some pests [4, 15, 17, 37]. Crown diameter (25 accessions), canopy density (30 accessions), branching (30 accessions), branch density (31 accessions), trunk diameter (29 accessions), and fruit density (25 accessions) were categorized as moderate. Mature stem color and fruit flesh color were predominantly light brown (52 accessions and 24 accessions, respectively). Leaf color (42 accessions) and bracteole color (26 accessions) were classified as Light green. Leaflet shape was determined as narrowly oblong (30 accessions) and oblong (25 accessions). Leaflet apex shape was divided into three groups: 29 accessions (rounded), 22 accessions (truncate), and 4 accessions (emarginate). Leaflet base shape was classified as acute (3 accessions), Round (38 accessions), and Wide round (14 accessions). Location of inflorescence was categorized as along new branches (44 accessions) and along old branches (11 accessions). Sepal color was identified as white (41 accessions), cream (12 accessions), and light yellow (2 accessions). Sepal shape was predominantly Ovate (23 accessions). Petal ground color varied between white and light red, with cream being dominant (18 accessions). Petal streaks color was reported as purple (16 accessions), red (14 accessions), and orange (25 accessions). The variation in color of reproductive organs is believed to primarily result from a climate with mild temperatures [6]. The coloration of reproductive organs can serve as a morphological trait in progeny testing programs and can also enhance fruit set by attracting pollinators [36]. Lateral petal shape was detected as oblanceolate (45 accessions) and obovate (10 accessions). Peak of fruiting season was classified as late-April (15 accessions) and early-May (40 accessions). Phenological variations are closely associated with climatic factors, including high rainfall, land use types, as well as abiotic and biotic stress, all of which influence the vegetative and reproductive growth of genotypes [13]. Fruit axile was predominantly straight (27 accessions). Fruit skin surface was categorized into four types: flat (14 accessions), wrinkled-low (25 accessions), wrinkled-moderate (14 accessions), and wrinkled-high (2 accessions). Fruit skin color was identified as light brown (19 accessions), brown (30 accessions), and dark brown (6 accessions). Flesh adherence to skin was observed as high (1 accession) and absent/low (21 accessions). High flesh adherence to skin can be undesirable for some consumers, as it may make it difficult to separate the flesh from the skin while eating the fruit. Fruit seed shape was determined as ellipsoid (2), obovoid (5), orbicular (11), rhomboid (27), and triangular (10). Fruit seed color varied between brown (1) and brown–red (26). Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a statistical technique aimed at summarizing variables in a dataset into a smaller number of new variables (principal components). PCA reduces data dimensionality, facilitating analysis and helping to reveal the underlying structure in complex data. Additionally, it groups related variables together, allowing for the identification of components that explain the most significant variance in the dataset. This method is particularly useful in studies with many variables, as it enhances visualization and interpretation [20].

Eigenvalues of the principal component axes from the PCA of the morphological characters in the studied tamarind accessions are given in Table 4. Accordingly, the first seventeen principal components (PCs), all having an Eigenvalue greater than 1.0, explained 83.28% of the total variation. Bold values indicate the characteristics that most influence each PC (eigenvalues ≥ 0.55). Hierarchical cluster analysis

Ward method is a statistical method commonly used for hierarchical clustering. This analysis is applied to group similar objects or observations. Ward method clusters by minimizing the variance within each cluster. Specifically, it aims to minimize the total sum of squares error (SSE) at each step. Its advantages include the creation of homogeneous groups due to the minimization of variance within clusters, and the ability to visually present analysis results through a dendrogram [12]. Accordingly, the accessions were divided into two groups, A and B. Subsequently, each group was further divided into two subgroups: A1, A2 and B1, B2. The subgroups A1 and A2 each contained 4 accessions, while subgroup B1 had 13 accessions and subgroup B2 included 34 accessions (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Ward cluster analysis of the studied tamarind accessions based on morphological traits using Euclidean distances

Conclusion

This study highlights the extensive genetic and morphological diversity of tamarind accessions, offering critical insights for breeding and conservation. One-way ANOVA (p < 0.05) revealed significant differences among accessions, with coefficients of variation (CVs) spanning 8.50% (lateral petal width) to 110.44% (flesh adherence to skin). Notably, 73.77% of traits (45 out of 61) had CVs greater than 20%, indicating high variability. Key measurements included leaf length (35.38–97.68 mm), fruit length (33.23–127.24 mm), and fruit seed weight (0.65–9.79 g).

Correlation analysis identified significant relationships, such as between leaf length and width (r = 0.646**), fruit length and weight (r = 0.710**), and fruit length and seed weight (r = 0.599**), suggesting interactions that influence adaptation and performance. Denser branches were linked to increased leaf density, highlighting traits enhancing photosynthesis and water retention.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identified 17 components explaining 83.28% of total variation, with fruit and leaf traits as key contributors. Hierarchical cluster analysis classified accessions into four clusters, with the largest group (B2) containing 34 accessions.

The first 20 accessions were determined based on their individual quantitative trait performances, with the best results listed as follows: Bahoukalat-37, Bahoukalat-36, Bahoukalat-31, Bahoukalat-33, Tis-10, Bahoukalat-32, Bahoukalat-2, Bahoukalat-16, Bahoukalat-1, Tis-8, Bahoukalat-4, Bahoukalat-10, Tis-7, Bahoukalat-6, Bahoukalat-3, Bahoukalat-34, Tis-1, Bahoukalat-5, and Bahoukalat-29, respectively.

These accessions serve as valuable candidates for breeding and cultivation, providing a robust foundation for future research and the development of high-performing, adaptable cultivars.
