Diagnostic performance of clinical symptom combinations for Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever in emergency department patients with reported arthropod bites, stings, or exposure
Authors: Yasin Bilgin, Fatih Mehmet Sari, Cemalettin Sakal, Erdem Yakup Cimen, Orhan Tanrıverdi, Abdullah Emre Yurttutan, Umut Devrim Binay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is a potentially fatal tick-borne viral disease that remains an important public health problem in endemic regions. Early recognition of CCHF in emergency departments is challenging because initial clinical manifestations are often nonspecific. This study aimed to evaluate the demographic, clinical, and exposure-related characteristics of adult patients presenting to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact and to investigate clinical predictors associated with CCHF.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included adult patients presenting to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact between October 1, 2023, and October 1, 2024. A total of 526 patients were screened; 24 patients with incomplete data and 2 patients younger than 18 years were excluded. Demographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, and history of tick exposure were recorded. Clinical findings associated with CCHF were analyzed using univariable and multivariable statistical methods. The diagnostic performance of symptom combinations with tick exposure was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, likelihood ratios, odds ratios, and relative risks.ResultsA total of 500 adult patients were included, and CCHF was confirmed in 38 patients (7.6%). The mean age was 49.48 ± 15.91 years, and 62.8% of the patients were male. Fever, headache, diffuse body aches, malaise, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, rash, ecchymosis, and history of tick exposure were significantly more frequent in CCHF-positive patients (all p < 0.05). CCHF positivity was significantly higher among patients with a history of tick exposure than among those without tick exposure (11.9% vs 3.8%; OR: 3.45, p = 0.001). Among symptom combinations evaluated with tick exposure, diffuse body aches showed the highest specificity (100.0%) and a very high positive likelihood ratio after continuity correction; however, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously because no CCHF-negative patient had this combination. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, malaise (adjusted OR: 117.29, 95% CI: 28.23–487.33, p < 0.001) and fever (adjusted OR: 6.41, 95% CI: 1.87–21.94, p = 0.003) were independently associated with CCHF.ConclusionSimple clinical findings and symptom combinations may provide valuable early diagnostic clues for CCHF in endemic regions, even before laboratory confirmation. In particular, malaise and diffuse body aches in patients with a history of tick exposure showed high diagnostic performance and may contribute to early recognition and appropriate management of CCHF in emergency department settings.

Background

Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is a tick-borne zoonotic viral hemorrhagic infection caused by an RNA virus belonging to the genus Orthonairovirus within the family Nairoviridae. The disease is distributed across a wide geographical region encompassing Africa, Asia, the Middle East, and Southeastern Europe and is considered one of the most widespread tick-borne viral infections worldwide [1–4].

CCHF virus is primarily transmitted to humans through the bite of infected ticks. Transmission may also occur through contact with the blood or tissues of viremic animals or through exposure to body fluids of infected individuals. Ticks belonging to the genus Hyalomma, particularly Hyalomma marginatum, constitute the principal vectors of the disease and play a critical epidemiological role [4–7]. A documented history of tick exposure may be absent in a substantial proportion of CCHF patients, and transmission may occur through unrecognized tick contact or other epidemiological routes, particularly in endemic settings [4, 5 ].

From a One Health perspective, the epidemiology of CCHF is shaped by interactions among humans, livestock and wildlife hosts, tick vectors, and environmental conditions. Climatic and ecological changes, together with livestock movement, land-use patterns, and migratory bird routes, may influence the dispersal and potential establishment of Hyalomma ticks beyond their traditional range. Recent European surveillance and systematic reviews have documented detections of Hyalomma ticks in regions outside their established distribution, including northern parts of Europe, although such detections do not necessarily indicate established local populations. These changes underscore the importance of integrated human, animal, vector, and environmental surveillance for CCHF and other tick-borne diseases [8].

In Türkiye, Hyalomma species, particularly Hyalomma marginatum, are regarded as the principal tick vectors associated with CCHFV transmission. However, other tick genera may also participate in the ecological cycle of CCHFV, and Hyalomma ticks can carry additional bacterial and protozoal pathogens relevant to human and veterinary health. Because no entomological or taxonomic investigation of the suspected arthropods was performed in the present study, the species distribution of vectors in the study area could not be evaluated [8].

The clinical spectrum of CCHF ranges from asymptomatic infection to fulminant hemorrhagic disease. Patients commonly present with fever, malaise, diffuse myalgia/arthralgia, headache, nausea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal symptoms, whereas petechiae, ecchymosis, mucosal bleeding, hepatic dysfunction, shock, and multiorgan failure may develop during the later stages of the disease [1, 9–11 –].

Because the early clinical manifestations of the disease are often nonspecific, the diagnostic process may be challenging. Laboratory abnormalities such as thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, lymphopenia, and elevated liver enzymes are frequently observed, and thrombocytopenia and leukopenia are considered particularly important parameters for early diagnosis [5, 12 ].

Emergency department visits following reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact constitute an important public health burden, particularly in endemic regions. Although most of these presentations follow a benign clinical course, some patients may develop CCHF, and delayed diagnosis in the early stage of the disease may lead to significant morbidity and mortality. Therefore, CCHF should be considered in patients presenting with systemic symptoms such as fever, headache, diffuse body aches, malaise, and hematological abnormalities [2, 4, 1310 ].

Early diagnosis is critical for implementing appropriate isolation measures, initiating supportive treatment without delay, and reducing potential mortality. Therefore, the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of patients presenting to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact should be evaluated in detail [10, 13 ].

The aim of this study was to retrospectively evaluate the demographic, clinical, and exposure-related characteristics of adult patients presenting to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact between October 1, 2023, and October 1, 2024, and to identify symptom and exposure combinations that may support early clinical suspicion of CCHF before laboratory confirmation.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This single-center, retrospective, observational, and analytical study was conducted in the emergency department of a tertiary-care university hospital. The study included adult patients aged 18 years or older who presented to the emergency department between October 1, 2023, and October 1, 2024, after a self-reported or clinically suspected arthropod bite, sting, or contact.

The study population reflected routine emergency department presentations in which the responsible organism was often not definitively identified. Exposure classification was based on patient history and routine emergency department documentation. Therefore, no entomological examination or taxonomic identification of the suspected arthropod was performed. The cohort included patients reporting tick exposure as well as patients reporting an unspecified bite, sting, or contact attributed to another arthropod.

During the study period, 526 patients with reported or clinically suspected arthropod exposure were identified. Twenty-four patients with incomplete clinical or demographic data and two patients younger than 18 years were excluded. The final analysis included 500 adult patients.

Data collection and definitions

Demographic, clinical, and epidemiological data were retrospectively obtained from the electronic hospital information system and available emergency department medical records. Age and sex were recorded as demographic variables.

The type of arthropod exposure was based on patient report and routine clinical documentation. A history of tick exposure was recorded when the patient, accompanying person, or treating clinician specifically documented tick attachment, tick bite, tick removal, or direct contact with a tick. Other patients were classified as having reported or clinically suspected arthropod exposure when they presented with a history of an unspecified bite, sting, or contact attributed to an arthropod, but without a documented tick exposure. Because the suspected arthropod was not routinely collected or examined, no entomological or taxonomic identification was performed.

Clinical variables recorded at emergency department presentation included fever (body temperature ≥ 38 °C), headache, diffuse body aches, malaise, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, ecchymosis, rash, and bleeding manifestations. These variables were selected because they have been reported as potential early clinical manifestations of CCHF in previous studies [4, 7, 14910 ].

Diffuse body aches were defined as a patient-reported sensation of pain affecting multiple body regions (“pain all over the body”), as documented in routine emergency department records. This variable reflected a generalized pain complaint and was recorded separately from headache and abdominal pain. Because of the retrospective design and the wording used in routine clinical documentation, diffuse body aches could not be consistently subclassified as myalgia, arthralgia, or another specific pain syndrome.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult patients aged 18 years or older who presented to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact during the specified study period were eligible for inclusion.

Definition of CCHF and CCHF-negative comparison group

At the initial emergency department visit, patients presenting after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact underwent routine clinical assessment, including a targeted skin examination for attached ticks or other visible arthropods. When an attached arthropod was identified, it was removed as part of routine clinical care. In accordance with national CCHF management recommendations and routine emergency department practice in an endemic setting, asymptomatic patients without clinical findings suggestive of CCHF were discharged without immediate laboratory testing after receiving return precautions regarding symptoms that should prompt re-evaluation.

Patients who developed symptoms compatible with possible CCHF or re-presented to the emergency department underwent further clinical and laboratory evaluation, including RT-PCR testing and/or CCHF virus-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) testing when clinically indicated. CCHF was defined as laboratory confirmation by RT-PCR and/or CCHF virus-specific IgM positivity. During the study period, 38 patients met these criteria and were classified as CCHF-positive.

The CCHF-negative comparison group comprised patients who did not receive a diagnosis of CCHF during the study period. This group included patients who remained asymptomatic after the initial visit and did not re-present for further evaluation, as well as patients who re-presented with symptoms but had no laboratory confirmation of CCHF. Therefore, the CCHF-negative group should be interpreted as a routine-care comparison cohort rather than a group in which CCHF was laboratory excluded in every patient. The comparison group included patients with and without a history of reported tick exposure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test according to data distribution. The diagnostic performance of clinical findings associated with tick exposure history was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive likelihood ratio (LR +), negative likelihood ratio (LR −), odds ratio (OR), and relative risk (RR), with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent clinical predictors associated with Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever. Clinically relevant variables were included in the regression model. Because complete separation was observed for the diffuse body aches variable, stable parameter estimation could not be achieved and this variable was excluded from the multivariable model. Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction was applied in analyses involving zero-cell frequencies. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As a sensitivity analysis, Firth penalized logistic regression was performed to assess the robustness of the multivariable findings in the setting of a limited number of CCHF-positive cases and complete separation for diffuse body aches.

Ethics statement

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Erzincan Binali Yildirim University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval date: November 7, 2024; decision no: 15/01). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Results

During the study period, a total of 526 patients presented to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact. Twenty-four patients were excluded because of incomplete clinical or demographic data, and two patients younger than 18 years were excluded because the analytic cohort was restricted to adults. The final analyses were performed on 500 adult patients.

The mean age of the study population was 49.48 ± 15.91 years, with an age range of 18–91 years. Of the participants, 314 (62.8%) were male and 186 (37.2%) were female. Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) was confirmed in 38 patients (7.6%).

The mean age was 54.00 ± 17.00 years in the CCHF-positive group and 49.10 ± 15.78 years in the CCHF-negative group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.068).

The most frequently observed clinical findings in the study population were malaise (9.8%), fever (8.6%), diffuse body aches (7.2%), headache (5.2%), and nausea/vomiting (4.6%). When malaise was present together with tick exposure, sensitivity was 65.8% (95% CI: 48.6–80.4), specificity was 96.8% (95% CI: 94.7–98.2), and the positive likelihood ratio was 20.3 (95% CI: 11.7–35.1). In the presence of fever together with tick exposure, sensitivity was 44.7% (95% CI: 28.6–61.7), specificity was 96.5% (95% CI: 94.4–98.0), and the positive likelihood ratio was 12.9 (95% CI: 7.1–23.5) (Table 2). The combination of diffuse body aches and tick exposure showed a sensitivity of 68.4% and a specificity of 100.0%; because no CCHF-negative patient had this combination, the Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction was applied for estimation of odds ratios, relative risks, and likelihood ratios (Table 2).

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, among adult patients presenting after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact, fever and malaise were independently associated with CCHF. Malaise was identified as the strongest independent predictor (adjusted OR: 117.29, 95% CI: 28.23–487.33, p < 0.001). Fever was also independently and significantly associated with CCHF (adjusted OR: 6.41, 95% CI: 1.87–21.94, p = 0.003). In contrast, a history of tick exposure did not retain independent significance in the multivariable analysis (adjusted OR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.19–3.45, p = 0.786) (Table 3). In sensitivity analyses using Firth penalized logistic regression, the main findings were broadly consistent with the conventional logistic regression model. In the model including fever, malaise, and history of tick exposure, fever (adjusted OR: 6.05, 95% CI: 1.82–20.10; p = 0.003) and malaise (adjusted OR: 90.05, 95% CI: 23.99–338.02; p < 0.001) remained independently associated with CCHF, whereas tick exposure was not independently associated with CCHF (adjusted OR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.24–3.47; p = 0.881). When diffuse body aches was additionally included, it remained strongly associated with CCHF; however, the estimates for all variables were imprecise, with very wide confidence intervals, reflecting sparse data and separation. Therefore, this extended model was interpreted as a sensitivity analysis rather than the primary multivariable model.

Presentations showed a marked seasonal pattern, with the highest number of emergency department visits occurring in June (n = 193), followed by July (n = 119) and May (n = 73). Confirmed CCHF cases were observed between April and August, peaking in June (n = 14), followed by May and July (n = 10 each). Discussion

In this study, clinical findings and exposure-related characteristics associated with CCHF were evaluated in adult patients presenting to the emergency department after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact. The principal findings of the study were that fever, malaise, headache, diffuse body aches, and a history of tick exposure were significantly more frequent among CCHF-positive patients. In particular, the combinations involving malaise and diffuse body aches demonstrated high diagnostic performance. Furthermore, multivariable analysis identified malaise and fever as independent predictors of CCHF.

Previous studies have reported that the early clinical manifestations of CCHF are predominantly composed of nonspecific symptoms. Fever, malaise, myalgia, headache, and gastrointestinal symptoms have been described in the majority of patients. Consistent with the existing literature, our study demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of fever, malaise, diffuse body aches, and nausea/vomiting among CCHF-positive patients [9, 12, 15 ].

In our study, the rate of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever positivity was significantly higher among patients with a history of tick exposure. This finding is consistent with epidemiological studies demonstrating that tick exposure represents the principal route of transmission for CCHF. However, the loss of independent significance of tick exposure history in the multivariable analysis suggests that clinical symptoms may play a more decisive role in the diagnostic process. Importantly, it should be recognized that CCHF may also develop in patients without a documented history of tick exposure, particularly in endemic regions [4, 16 ].

The monthly distribution showed that both emergency department presentations and laboratory-confirmed CCHF cases clustered during the warmer months, with confirmed cases occurring between April and August. This pattern is consistent with the seasonal activity of Hyalomma ticks and the established seasonal epidemiology of CCHF in Türkiye. Importantly, the pre-test probability of CCHF is likely to vary across the transmission season; therefore, the predictive values of symptom–exposure combinations should be interpreted in the context of local seasonal disease activity. These findings may be particularly useful during periods of increased tick activity, when early triage and prompt diagnostic evaluation are most relevant [16].

One of the notable findings of our study was the high diagnostic performance of combinations including tick exposure together with diffuse body aches and malaise. In particular, the high specificity and the markedly elevated corrected positive likelihood ratio observed for the combination of tick exposure and diffuse body aches suggest that this finding may provide a useful “rule-in” clue for possible CCHF; however, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously because no CCHF-negative patient had this combination.

In emergency department practice, early recognition of such clinical combinations may facilitate timely implementation of isolation precautions, appropriate patient management, and accelerated diagnostic evaluation. In endemic regions, these simple clinical combinations may provide valuable early diagnostic clues for CCHF even before laboratory results become available [12, 13, 15 ].

Validated CCHF severity scores, such as the Severity Grading Score and Severity Scoring Index, were developed to estimate disease severity and prognosis in patients with established or laboratory-confirmed CCHF. In contrast, the present study evaluated symptom- and exposure-based findings at the time of emergency department presentation to support pre-diagnostic clinical suspicion before laboratory confirmation. Therefore, these approaches address different stages of clinical care and should be regarded as complementary rather than competing. A simple triage-oriented approach may help identify patients who require prompt isolation, diagnostic testing, and closer clinical evaluation, whereas validated severity scores may subsequently assist in risk stratification and prognostic assessment after CCHF has been confirmed [17].

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, malaise emerged as the strongest independent predictor of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF). Fever was also found to be independently associated with CCHF. Previous studies have demonstrated that systemic symptoms such as fever, malaise, and diffuse body aches are frequently observed during the early phase of the disease. Our findings further support the role of these symptoms as important clinical indicators that may enhance early recognition and clinical suspicion of CCHF in the emergency department setting [4, 12, 13 ].

The major strengths of this study include its relatively large patient population, the use of real-world clinical data, and its focus on emergency department presentations. In addition, the evaluation of the diagnostic performance of specific clinical symptom combinations represents one of the novel aspects of the study.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and single-center design may have introduced selection bias. Because symptom variables were derived from routine records, the completeness and consistency of documentation may have varied among clinicians, particularly for subjective symptoms such as malaise and diffuse body aches. Although diffuse body aches were recorded separately from headache and abdominal pain, they could not be consistently subclassified as myalgia, arthralgia, or another specific pain syndrome. In addition, alternative final diagnoses, pregnancy status, and detailed arthropod species or bite/sting types were not consistently available; therefore, the CCHF-negative group should be interpreted as a routine-care comparison cohort rather than a clinically homogeneous diagnostic group. Second, laboratory parameters were intentionally not included because the primary aim was to evaluate a laboratory-independent, pre-diagnostic triage approach. Future studies should investigate whether incorporating readily available laboratory parameters, such as platelet count, leukocyte count, or liver enzymes, improves predictive performance. Finally, because complete separation was observed in some clinical variables and the number of confirmed CCHF cases was limited, the magnitude of the estimated odds ratios should be interpreted cautiously. The predictive values observed in this cohort should also be interpreted in the context of local CCHF prevalence, and external validation in populations with different baseline risks is needed.

Conclusion

In adult patients presenting to the emergency department in endemic settings after reported or clinically suspected arthropod bites, stings, or contact, fever, malaise, diffuse body aches, and a history of tick exposure were significantly associated with CCHF. In particular, the combinations of diffuse body aches and malaise evaluated together with tick exposure demonstrated high diagnostic performance. Multivariable analysis identified malaise and fever as independent predictors of CCHF. In endemic regions, simple clinical findings and symptom combinations may facilitate early clinical recognition of CCHF even before laboratory confirmation is available. These findings may contribute to the timely implementation of isolation precautions and appropriate patient management in emergency department practice.
