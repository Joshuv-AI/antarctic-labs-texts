Transcriptomic profiling of Kupffer cells exposed to Clonorchis sinensis extracellular vesicles: Unraveling the role of CSF3 in hepatic fibrosis
Authors: Zhuo Lan, Xue Wang, Yuxi Zhang, Lu Zhou, Xue Bai, Xinhui Zhang, Haokun Zhang, Hongyu Qiu, Junfeng Gao, Guofeng Cheng, Chunren Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clonorchiasis, caused by Clonorchis sinensis, is a significant public health issue in China and East Asia. However, understanding its pathological mechanisms underlying this disease remains limited. Here, we isolated and characterized C. sinensis extracellular vesicles (CsEVs) and evaluated their uptake by Kupffer cells (KCs) in vitro in immortalized KC (ImKC) line and in vivo in a model of C. sinensis infection, investigating genes with altered gene expression after treatment with CsEVs and during progression to liver fibrosis. CsEVs were successfully taken up by KCs to regulate gene expression. RNA-sequencing analysis identified 694 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including upregulation of those encoding colony-stimulating factor 3 (CSF3), IL1b, and others. Further validation showed increased expressions of these genes in mice infected with C. sinensis. Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analyses suggested that these DEGs were involved in pathways such as TNF signaling, and NF-κB signaling. During liver fibrosis progression, CSF3 expression positively correlated with alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) levels in the liver, both of which were higher compared with negative controls. CSF3 inhibition caused a significant decrease in α-SMA expression. This study was the first to report differential gene expression changes in ImKCs stimulated by CsEVs, with CSF3, the most significantly upregulated gene, having a potential role in liver fibrosis. These findings provide important data for the pathology of clonorchiasis and could identify new targets for treatment.

Introduction

Clonorchis sinensis (Opisthorchiidae) is an important foodborne trematode that is parasitic in the bile duct and significantly impacts the health of both humans and animals.

The life cycle of C. sinensis is intricate, including three hosts; humans, cats, dogs, and other carnivorous mammals act as the final hosts; some aquatic snails act as the first intermediate hosts; and freshwater fish or occasionally shrimps as the second intermediate hosts. In the definitive host, adult C. sinensis lives mainly in intrahepatic bile ducts and the gall bladder, and are occasionally found in the extrahepatic and pancreatic ducts. Each adult C. sinensis is capable of producing 1000–4000 eggs daily for at least six months, depending on the mammalian host species. When ingested by freshwater snails, these eggs hatch into miracidia within the gastrointestinal tract. The miracidia penetrate the intestinal wall, developing into sporocysts in the peri-intestinal tissues and organs, and subsequently transforming into redia and then cercaria. The cercariae then exit the snail and infect fish or shrimps, where they form metacercariae. When humans or other mammals consume raw fish containing metacercariae, they become infected with C. sinensis (Tang et al., 2016). An estimated 15 million people are infected with C. sinensis, primarily in China, South Korea, Vietnam, and the Far East regions of Russia (Qian et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2020). Among these countries, China has the highest number of infections, with an estimated 13 million people infected, with Heilongjiang Province one of the major endemic areas (Fang et al., 2008; Xin et al., 2021). C. sinensis infection causes significant harm to host health through mechanical obstruction involving infection-related inflammation, pathological effects from excretory secretory products (ESPs), and enhanced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), among others (Liu et al., 2022; Sawanyawisuth et al., 2021). Mild infections can lead to bile duct inflammation, hyperplasia, and fibrosis in chronically infected patients, whereas cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is also possible in those with chronic infection; however, infections are usually asymptomatic. Given the serious harm that can result from C. sinensis infection, the World Health Organization classified C. sinensis as a Class I biological carcinogen in 2009 (Bouvard et al., 2009). The recommended treatment for clonorchiasis is praziquantel. However, this treatment is often associated with mild or transient side effects such as headaches, dizziness, nausea, and abdominal discomfort, as well as concerns about drug resistance (Qian et al., 2016).

Kupffer cells (KCs), a type of liver-resident macrophages, are the most abundant immune cell population within the liver. These macrophages, which are of embryonic origin, are strategically positioned intravascularly to provide immediate immunosurveillance against blood-borne microparticles, including circulating tumor cells (Chen et al., 2025). KCs have crucical roles in maintaining immune homeostasis in the liver, particularly during pathogen infections (Dai et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023a). When liver damage occurs, KCs activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) by releasing cytokines, such as TGF-β, which strongly promote extracellular matrix, (ECM) synthesis, inhibit collagenase production, and reduce collagen degradation, ultimately leading to liver fibrosis (Heymann et al., 2015; Shek and Benyon, 2004). KCs produce inflammatory cytokines or chemokines in response to Schistosoma japonicum infection which is involved in liver fibrosis (Zhang et al., 2019). Similarly, Echinococcus multilocularis primarily causes liver damage through mechanical compression and secretion of bioactive molecules. As reported by Pu et al., qRT-PCR analysis revealed that mmu-miR-374b-5p was significantly upregulated in primary KCs from infected mice. The authors further demonstrated that mmu-miR-374b-5p binds to the 3′-UTR of the C/EBPβ gene, suppressing its expression and consequently downregulating NF-κB signaling—a pathway that plays a critical role in E. multilocularis-induced liver injury. (Pu et al., 2024). Given that there is no effective treatment for liver fibrosis caused by C. sinensis infection, identifying genes involved in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis in KCs could help develop novel therapeutic strategies for clonorchiasis.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a heterogeneous group of cell-derived membranous structures comprising exosomes and microvesicles. They are considered an additional mechanism for intercellular communication, allowing cells to exchange proteins, lipids, and genetic material (van Niel et al., 2018). Once flukes, such as C. sinensis, Fasciola, and others, parasitize in the host, the adult worms release ESPs, which contains EVs. Research showed that CsEVs activated M1-like hepatic macrophage and contributed to injuries by targeting the Socs1- and Clec7a-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway (Yan et al., 2021). Dicrocoelium dendriticum infects its host in a similar manner to C. sinensis, and D. dendriticum EVs have a pro-inflammatory effect on HSCs. In contrast, Fasciola hepatica migrates through the liver parenchyma and its EVs exert a more anti-inflammatory effect on hepatocytes (Sánchez-López et al., 2023; Sánchez-López et al., 2024). This supports the hypothesis that flukes that reside in the bile ducts and do not migrate through liver secrete pro-inflammatory factors. In addition, EVs secreted by Opistorchis viverrini have been associated with cholangiocyte proliferation and induction of inflammatory cytokines secretion (Chaiyadet et al., 2015).

However, the differential expression of genes associated with EVs from C. sinensis in KCs remains under investigation. C. sinensis and its EVs contribute to severe liver damage in its final hosts. During liver fibrosis progression, KCs, as an important subset of immune cells are essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis and initiating immune responses. Therefore, using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), this study characterized changes in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in KCs after CsEVs stimulation, and identified such genes through in vivo experiments. Furthermore, we aimed to predict functionally relevant genes, analyze their associated functions, and investigate signaling pathways, providing new insights for treating liver fibrosis caused by C. sinensis infection.

Materials and methods

Metacercariae collection and animal model establishment

All experiments involving mice and rabbits were carried out based on the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Management Committee and the Science and Technology Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang Bayi Agriculture University, China (No. DWKJXY2023102).

Metacercariae of C. sinensis were obtained from positive Pseudorasbora parva (a second intermediate fish host) in Daqing, Heilongjiang Province, China. Fish were ground and digested in artificial gastric juice for 4 h (0.4 % pepsin, pH 1.0; Yuanye Biochemicals Co., Shanghai, China). Then, the mixture was filtered using a 1-mm-diameter mesh sieve. The filtrate was collected and water was added. After standing for 30 min, the supernatant was discarded. This step was repeated several times until the liquid was clear. To avoid other species of metacercariae being included, C. sinensis metacercariae were identified under a dissecting microscope and placed in Alsever's Solution containing Penicillin-Streptomycin-Amphotericin B Solution (100 U/ml) (Solarbio, China) at 4 °C until use (Dai et al., 2024).

Japanese white rabbits and 6-week-old female mice (BALB/c) were purchased from the Animal Centre of Harbin Medical University (Harbin, China), and fed in a specific pathogen-free animal laboratory. Each rabbit (n = 10) was fed of C. sinensis 500 metacercariae artificially. Each positive mouse (n = 27) was orally infected with 50C. sinensis metacercariae. A control group was established with mice (n = 6) given PBS orally. Three mice were injected intravenously with labeled exosomes. All mice and rabbits were fed an appropriate amount of sterilized commercial diet and water freely without further treatment. Three mice from the C. sinensis positive group (n = 3) and from the control group were used to isolate primary KCs for qPCR after 8 weeks of infection. Another C. sinensis positive group (n = 24) and another control group (n = 3) model were established following the same protocol; livers were extracted in each week (0–8 w) for histological analysis and qPCR. Livers at 8 weeks post-infection were also used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis.

CsEVs collection and characterization

CsEVs were collected as described previously (Yan et al., 2021). Briefly, rabbits (n = 10) fed with C. sinensis at eight weeks postinfection were euthanized under deep ether anesthesia, and adult worms were collected from the bile ducts and carefully washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Coolaber, China) containing 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C. Adult worms were then cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA). The supernatant was collected every 2 h and more RPMI-1640 medium was added. CsEVs were isolated by differential centrifugation. The culture solution was pooled and centrifuged for 30 min at 1500 ×g, and 3500 ×g for 30 min to remove any cellular debris, worm eggs, and sperm. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for a further 30 min and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22-μm filter (Steriflip; Millipore, USA). The samples were subjected to further centrifugation at 20,000 ×g for 1 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was carefully removed. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, and then centrifuged at 120,000 ×g for 1 h 45 min using an Optima L-100XP tabletop ultracentrifuge equipped with a Ti60 rotor (Beckman Coulter, USA). This washing step with PBS was repeated twice. After discarding the supernatant, the final pellet containing CsEVs was resuspended in 50 μl of PBS.

Using the method of Glamočlija et al. (2025), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to analyze the morphology and estimate the size range of the CsEVs. CsEVs (10 μl) were placed on a mesh in thin layers for 1 min, using filter paper to absorb any floating liquid. A 2 % aqueous solution of uranyl acetate was applied to the sample mesh, which was then allowed to dry overnight. Subsequently, TEM was performed using a Hitachi TEM instrument (Hitachi, Japan) operated at 100 kV to visualize the morphology of CsEVs. Determination of size and concentration analysis of CsEVs was done using a ZetaView PMX-120 (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany) nanoparticle tracking instrument. Specifically, a 5-μl aliquot of the resuspended CsEV pellet was diluted in 30 μl of ddH2O and loaded into the sample chamber of the nanoparticle tracking analysis system. The system was set to detect samples at 11 different positions within the sample chamber, with two cycles of measurement performed for each position. The data obtained were analyzed using ZetaView software version 8.02.28 (Particle Metrix, Germany) to determine the diameter size and particle concentration of CsEVs.

Isolation and identification of Kupffer cells

Primary KCs were isolated followed the methodology of Aparicio-Vergara et al. (2017). In brief, mice were anesthetized with ether and the liver was perfused with 20 ml digestive buffer. The digestive buffer was prepared by diluting collagenase IV (0.5 mg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) in Hanks' solution to a final concentration of 5 %. The extracted liver was then cut into small pieces and transferred into the digestive buffer (diluted collagenase IV with Hanks to a concentration of 5 %) for 20 min of shaking at 190 rpm. The cells were filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged for 3 min at 100 ×g. The supernatants were collected by centrifuging at a speed of 400 ×g for 8 min. The cells were resuspended with 10 ml D-Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) and transferred to isolate macrophages using 35 % Percoll separation and 65 % Percoll separation by centrifuging at 1400 ×g for 30 min. The cells were carefully collected and resuspended in KC media (RPMI-1640) and plated at a density of 6.8 × 106 cells for 3 h. The media was then discarded, with the remaining adherent cells being primary KCs.

The primary KCs isolated from the mice were identified by flow cytometry by detecting the KC markers F4/80 (Biolegend, USA, 123119) and CD11b (Biolegend, USA). Fc receptors were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 (Biolegend, USA), F4/80, and CD11b for 30 min at 4 °C and then centrifugated at 3000 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded and the cells were washed with 500 μl PBS twice. Cells were analyzed using a MACSQuant flow cytometer and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.8.1 software. All of the above steps were performed in the dark.

CsEVs taken up by Kupffer cells

To verify whether CsEVs could be taken up by KCs, the concentration of CsEVs was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and labeled with PKH67 membrane dye (5 μM) using a PKH67 green fluorescent labeling kit (Sigma-Aldich, Germany), followed by 100,000 ×g ultracentrifugation for 70 min. Labeled exosomes (50 μg) were resuspended in PBS (100 μl) and injected intravenously into mice (n = 3). The primary KC isolated from mice were extracted at 24 h post injection. Mice were anesthetized by ether and their livers were immediately perfused through the portal vein with 20 ml of HBSS liquid, followed by 5 ml (1.5 ml/min) perfusion with HBSS containing collagenase IV (0.5 mg/ml, Sigma, Aldrich). Livers were cut into small pieces, removed, and digested at 190 rpm for 10 min at 37 °C under HBSS containing collagenase IV. The samples were centrifuged at 100 ×g for 3 min. The supernatant was then collected and centrifuged at 400 ×g for 8 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in 2 ml of RPMI-1640 medium and then mixed with a gradient of Percoll separation solution, comprising 50 % and 25 % solutions. This mixture was then centrifuged at 400 ×g for 20 min. The white membrane layer, containing the cells of interest, was carefully extracted. These cells were resuspended in 8–10 ml of HBSS and further centrifuged at 400 ×g for 8 min to collect the cell pellet. The supernatant was discarded, leaving the final cell pellet ready for subsequent analysis. Remaining red blood cells were removed from the pellet by adding red blood cell lysis buffer (Coolaber, China). After a washing step, the remaining cells were placed in an incubator for 4 h and any cells that adhered to the wall of the incubator were KCs. The immunofluorescent staining was used to detect F4/80, which is a marker of KCs.

For in vitro experiments, ImKC (NASH-associated mouse KC lines) was purchased from the BeNa Culture Collection (BNCC340733; China). The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Clark, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. When the density of cells in the 6-well plate reached ∼80 %, labeled CsEVs (10 μg/ml) were added and then incubated with KC cells in the RPMI-1640 medium containing 1 % FBS at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. Immunofluorescence analysis was then performed for both the primary KCs and ImKCs to identify whether CsEVs had been taken up by KCs. In addition to PKH67, a membrane dye used for labeling CsEVs, the marker of KCs, F4/80, was also used for detection, as described below.

The cell-climbing sheets with primary KCs isolated from mice and ImKCs were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 3 min, and then blocked with 1 % BSA for 1 h. To identify whether primary KCs had been isolated successfully and ImKCs had been successfully used, both primary KCs and ImKCs were incubated with primary antibodies against F4/80 (1:200 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with CY3-labeled secondary antibodies (1:400 dilution, Boster, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained using DAPI (10 μg/ml) (Coolaber, China) for 10 min. Cell sections were then observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan), and images were acquired by Image J software.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

ImKCs were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Clark, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2. When the density of cells in the 6-well plate reached ∼80 %, CsEVs (5 μg/ml) were added and incubated with ImKCs in RPMI-1640 medium containing 1 % FBS at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 for 24 h. ImKCs had been added to which the same volume of PBS as the volume of CsEVs was used as a control group. Total RNAs were isolated from treated ImKC and control cells using the QIAzol lysis reagent (Qiagen, Germany) and sent to BGI, Personal Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for RNA quality control and RNA-seq analysis.

Total RNA (≥1 μg) was used for library construction with the NEB Next Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina or NEB Next Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Poly(A)-tailed mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation via divalent cation-mediated enzymatic cleavage (RNA Fragmentation Reagents, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Fragmented mRNA served as the template for first-strand cDNA synthesis using random hexamer primers. After double-stranded cDNA synthesis, purification was performed using the provided purification beads. The cDNA underwent end repair, 3′-end adenylation, and adapter ligation. Libraries were size-selected (400–500 bp) using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), PCR-amplified, and further purified with AMPure XP beads. Library quality was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

The clean reads for subsequent analysis were obtained after raw data filtering, sequencing error rate checking, and GC content distribution checking. The clean reads were then assembled into the genome or transcriptome via Hisat2 software. Read counts mapped to each gene were calculated as raw expression values using HTSeq (v0.9.1). The DEGseq R package was used to analyze DEGs based on the conditions of a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 and Q-values≤0.001 (Jiang et al., 2021). A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was created based on the STRING database (https://string-db.org/cgi/input.pl) (Li et al., 2023b), with a minimum required interaction score set at 0.7 (high confidence). A volcano plot was created using the ggplot2 package based on the Student's t-test to display the statistical significance (expressed as -log10) and gene expression (expressed as log2 fold-change). Heatmap was established using the pheatmap package based on the log2 fold-change in gene expression. The topGO was used to perform a Gene Ontology (GO) function enrichment analysis. The cluster profile was used to conduct Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses.

Histological analysis

The collected livers were fixed overnight in 4 % paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, transparentized, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm for histological analysis. Histological analysis, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson's staining, and Picro Sirius Red (PSR) staining, was performed to diagnose liver fibrosis. Both Masson's staining and PSR staining were utilized to provide a comprehensive assessment of hepatic fibrosis. Masson's staining provided a clear visualization of collagen fibers and overall fibrotic architecture, whereas PSR provided high specificity for collagen fibers. In addition, PSR staining allowed for precise quantitative analysis of fibrosis through digital image analysis. The combination of these two methods enhanced the accuracy and detail of fibrosis assessment, providing valuable insights into the pathological mechanisms of hepatic fibrosis.

H&E staining was used to observe the tissue structure. The deparaffinized slides were treated with Mayer's Hematoxylin (Powerful Biology, Wuhan, China) for 5 min, followed by rinsing under running water for 10 min. Subsequently, the slides were stained with eosin (Powerful Biology) for 2 min before being passed through ascending concentrations of ethanol and xylene. Finally, the slides were covered.

Masson's staining was used to detect collagen fibers. The paraffin sections were sequentially treated with xylene I for 8 min, xylene II for 8 min, anhydrous ethanol I for 6 min, anhydrous ethanol II for 6 min, 95 % ethanol for 6 min, 85 % ethanol for 6 min, and 75 % ethanol for 5 min, followed by rinsing with running water. The sections were then stained with Weigert's Iron Hematoxylin Staining Solution for 5 min, washed thoroughly with water, and blued using Masson's bluing solution before another wash with water. Next, the sections were stained with Masson Lichun red acidic magenta solution for 5 min, rinsed with running water, treated with phosphomolybdic acid solution for 5 min, and then the excess liquid was removed. They were subsequently stained with aniline blue solution for 3 min, followed by rinsing with 1 % glacial acetic acid aqueous solution until no blue colour remained. Finally, the sections were briefly washed with 95 % ethanol, dehydrated with anhydrous ethanol for 3 min, and cleared in xylene III for 5 min, xylene II for 5 min, and xylene I for 5 min before being mounted with neutral gum.

PSR staining was also used to detect collagen fibers. A stain kit (Polyscience, USA) was used, whereby phosphomolybdic acid was applied to the deparaffinized slides for 2 min, followed by PSR staining for 60 min, and 0.1 N Hydrochloride Acid for 2 min. The slides were transferred into 70 % ethanol, and then through ascending concentrations of ethanol and xylene, before being covered. All images were obtained using CaseViewer (Sysmex, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry

Serial sections of embedded tissue from mice were used for IHC staining of CSF3 and to develop an index of liver fibrosis, including alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and collagen I. In brief, the liver tissue was deparaffinized and hydrated. The slices were put into a 3 % hydrogen peroxide solution and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 25 min. The slides were then placed in PBS for washing it on a decolorization shaker, heated in citric acid buffer at 95 °C for 15 min, and then blocked with 5 % BSA for 30 min. The slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary anti-CSF3 (1:400, bs-1023R; Bioss, Beijing, China), anti-α-SMA (1:1000, ab32575; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-collagen I (1:500, ab21286, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). This was followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 45 min (1:2000, ab205718, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP). After washing with PBS, DAB (1:200, ZSGB–BIO, Beijing, China) was added as an enzyme. Images of the tissues were taken using an Olympus BX51 microscope (Japan). Image analysis from the stained slides was done using ImageJ and the Immunohistochemistry Image Analysis Toolbox. The images were converted into 8-bit files. Five high-power fields (×100 magnifications, Olympus) were randomly selected from each stained section. CSF3, α-SMA, and collagen I positive expression integrated optical density IOD was calculated by ImageJ software, followed by the average optical density (AOD = IOD/Area), with a higher AOD value indicating stronger positive expression.

Stimulation of Kupffer cells and their co-culture with hepatic stellate cells

To explore the relationship between CSF3 and liver fibrosis, a co-culture system of KCs and HSCs was established, based on the methodology of Zhang et al. (2018). Initially, ImKCs were treated with CsEVs at a concentration of 10 μg/ml or CSF3 siRNA at a concentration of 20 pmol/μl for 24 h. In another group, ImKCs were first treated with CsEVs (10 μg/ml) and then subsequently cultured with CSF3 short interfering (si)RNA (20 pmol/μl) for an additional 24 h. CSF3 siRNA was obtained from JTSBIO Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China), wih the sequences detailed in Table S1. After siRNA treatment, the differentiated ImKCs were washed with fresh RPMI-1640 medium and cultured in 2 ml of RPMI-1640 medium for an additional 24 h. To produce conditioned media from ImKCs, all CsEVs and CSF3 siRNA were thoroughly removed by washing, and the cells were further cultured in 2 ml of fresh RPMI-1640 medium. An insert containing HSCs (Cell line, CL018, Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was placed on top of the ImKCs (in a 6-well plate) for 24 h. The conditioned media containing HSCs were then collected for RNA analysis. To assess the effect of HSCs on KCs, a separate control group was treated with CsEVs and CSF3 siRNA as described above, but without coculturing with HSCs. The ImKCs in this group were collected for RNA analysis for further investigation.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNAs were isolated from primary KCs, ImKCs, HSCs, and whole livers infected by C. sinensis for different lengths of time (0–8 weeks) with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The quality of the isolated RNAs was analyzed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh) by measuring absorbance at both 260 nm and 280 nm. The cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription using a DNA synthesis kit HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China) for qPCR. We added 2 μl template cDNA (20 ng), 12.5 μl TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara, Japan), 1 μl forward/reverse primer (0.2 μM each), and 8.5 μl H2O mix into 96-well PCR plates for reactions (25 μl). Thermal cycling comprised of 30 s at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The qPCR was then carried out using a SYBR green master mix on a MyiQTM2 (Bio-Rad, USA). The primers were synthesized by Tianyi Huiyuan Biotechnology (Beijing, China). The sequences of all primers are listed in Table S1. The gene encoding glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GADPH) was used as an internal control. The relative changes in gene expression were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Statistical analysis

The correlation between CSF3 and α-SMA expression levels was analyzed by GraphPad Prism (10.1.2). Normality tests were conducted using the “Normality and Lognormality Tests” in the software, resulting in a Normal QQ plot. Based on this analysis, if the results followed a normal distribution, they were analyzed using Pearson's correlation analysis. Otherwise, Spearman's rank correlation analysis was used. The extent of correlation was analyzed based on the correlation coefficient r and the resulting P value.

RNA-seq analysis of DEGs

In total, 16,264 genes were identified in ImKCs stimulated with CsEVs. Among these, 413 genes showed significant upregulation and 281 genes showed downregulation (P < 0.05). Importantly, CSF3 showed the greatest upregulation (Additional file 1). Compared wit the control group (negative), the genes in ImKCs incubated with CsEVs (positive) showed differential expression and were split into different branches, indicating that genes on the same branch participated in the same metabolic process or regulated the same signaling pathway (Fig. 2A). Moreover, a cohort of upregulated (red) and downregulated (blue) DEGs was enriched according to their log10 (Q value). There were more upregulated genes than the downregulated genes, with the former showing more obvious changes in expression (Fig. 2B). Discussion

Although CsEVs have been reported to induce proinflammatory macrophage activation (Yan et al., 2021), the DEGs resulting from the uptake of CsEVs by target cells and/or organs have remained unclear. In the present study, we investigated to reveal the regulatory mechanism of CsEVs involved in liver fibrosis. We demonstrated for the first time that CSF3 was significantly increased during C. sinensis infection in response to the secretion of CsEVs, which could have an important role in the process of liver fibrosis following C. sinensis infection.

C. sinensis exosomes could be involved in host pathological reactions. Yan et al. (2021) found that, when injected into mice, CsEVs altered biliary structures and induced massive inflammatory infiltration, increasing the level of Ki67 in cholangiocyte-like cells in the liver, suggesting that CsEVs can lead to liver damage. Recent studies documented that parasite-derived EVs could participate in pathological tumor progression (de la Torre-Escudero et al., 2016; El Andaloussi et al., 2013), while Yan et al. (2021) found that labeled CsEVs were internalized by RAW264.7 cells.

In the present study, we investigated the effect of CsEVs on DEGs in target cells both in vitro and in vivo. CsEVs were taken up by KCs and potentially participate in the process of liver fibrosis through KCs. Similar effects of parasite-derived EVs have also been reported. For example, EVs released from S. japonicum are taken up primarily by macrophages, and other host peripheral blood immune cells, and their miRNA cargo, such as miR-125b and bantam miRNAs, is transferred into recipient cells. These two miRNAs increase macrophage and TNF production, which enables S. japonicum to survive and aggravate damage to the host (Liu et al., 2019). During infection with E. multilocularis, KCs secret a large amount of the fibroblast stimulating factor TGF-β1, which promotes activation of HSCs and further promotes liver fibrosis (Tian, 2020). When stimulated by exosomes, KCs exert their moderation effect on liver microenvironments via the secretion of cytokines and chemokines (Costa-Silva et al., 2015; Shao et al., 2018). In the current study, we analyzed the changes in cytokines and chemokines secreted by KCs when stimulated with CsEVs.

Colony-stimulating factors (CSFs), including macrophage CSF (M − CSF), granulocyte-macrophage CSF (GM-CSF), granulocyte CSF (G-CSF), and multipotential CSF (multi-CSF) are produced by variety of cell types, such as fibroblasts and lymphocytes, and are widely distributed throughout the body. They are important in controlling the numbers and function of macrophage lineage populations (Fleetwood et al., 2007). CSF1 is a secreted cytokine with an important role in the immune-suppressive function of macrophages in inflammatory diseases (Xiang et al., 2023). CSF2, a hematopoietic growth factor and immunomodulator, was named for its in vivo induction of the production of granulocyte and macrophage colonies from bone marrow precursors (Chen et al., 2024; Greter et al., 2012). Macrophages can be activated to produce CSF2 under inflammatory or pathological conditions and participate in pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Fleetwood et al., 2007). G-CSF (CSF3) is a growth factor belonging to the IL-6 superfamily, which encodes cytokines and is often used to stimulate bone marrow to produce more granulocytes and stem cells and release them into the bloodstream (Suzuki et al., 2023). Notably, CSF1, CSF2, and CSF3 were all upregulated in the C. sinensis infection group in the current study, with CSF3 showing the most significant upregulation (P < 0.05, see Additional file 1). CSFs are widely studied in medical research, including in colorectal cancer (CRC) and liver cancer (Lv et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2017). For example, Ortega-Ribera et al. (2024) used alcohol to construct murine liver injury models and found that CSF3 showed great upregulation in mice with liver injury. The author concluded that CSF3 can promote liver damage, accompanied by inflammatory bodies that activate and increase neuroinflammation (Ortega-Ribera et al., 2024). This is similar to our study, where once the CSF3 was inhibited, α-SMA as a biomarker of the HSCs, showed lower expression. The high expression of α-SMA was related to liver fibrosis; similarly, a previous report also indicated that CSF3 was expressed more highly in CRC liver metastasis than in normal liver (Jiang et al., 2021). However, it is unknown whether CSF3 is related to liver fibrosis caused by parasitic infection. Our study identified for the first time that CSF3 was obviously upregulated in response to C. sinensis infection similarly to that seen with CSF3 expression in CRC. In our study, all histological analyses including H&E staining, Masson, and PSR, indicated that livers from C. sinensis-infected mice showed varying degrees of damage, with more severe liver injury observed as the infection duration increased. Moreover, in the liver of mice infected with C. sinensis for different lengths of time (1–8 weeks), there was higher CSF3 expression than in healthy liver, companied by higher expression of α-SMA. The expression level of α-SMA decline after the 5th week of infection despite a progressive increase in liver fibrosis in this study; this might be because the liver entered a repair phase during Week 5, and that some HSCs underwent apoptosis, leading to a reduction in their numbers and a decrease in α-SMA expression. Iredale et al. (1998) established a liver fibrosis model by injecting CCl4 into rats; the authors noted downregulation of α-SMA expression at different stages of liver damage (0, 3, 7, 28d). They demonstrated that during the repair phase following liver injury in rats, activated HSCs undergo apoptosis, leading to a reduction in their numbers and a decrease in α-SMA expression. Although the established animal models of liver fibrosis differ, in the animal models used in our study, C. sinensis caused continuous damage to the liver, gradually aggravating liver injury; however, during this process, the activated HSCs in the liver could still undergo apoptosis, as also reported by Iredale et al. (1998). Thus, based on current research and our findings, we hypothesize that CSF3 has an important role in promoting liver fibrosis caused by C. sinensis infection.

Recent research has highlighted cell signal transduction pathways related to liver fibrosis, including JAK/STAT, NF-κB, MAPK pathway, and the TGFβ1/Smad, IL-17 signaling pathway. For example, Kang et al. (2020) found that a gene encoding the MF6p/HDM of C. sinensis (CsMF6p/HDM) induced a pro-inflammatory response mediated via the NF-κB-dependent MAPK signaling pathway, characterized by the secretion of IL-6 and TNF in RAW 264.7 macrophage. Moreover, it has been reported that the IL-17 signaling pathway is related to alcoholic liver disease, which is mainly associated with inflammation and lipid metabolism (Shen et al., 2024). The IL-17 signaling pathway has an important role in colon cancer, pulmonary fibrosis, NASH and related fibrosis, and liver cancer pathogenesis (Gomes et al., 2016; Liao et al., 2013). It also activates the STAT3, NF-κB, and P38 pathways to stimulate HSCs production, which is vital in liver fibrosis (Meng et al., 2012; Tan et al., 2013; Caligiuri et al., 2021). Moreover, Fan et al. (2018) revealed that CSF3 promotes tumor metastasis by activating the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway, which is also involved in liver fibrosis. In this study, the TNF, NF-κB, IL-17, and MAPK pathways were enriched, which were related to liver fibrosis; however, some pathways, such as the JAK/STAT pathway, were enriched but did not occur in the top 20 pathways (see Additional file 1). As the most upregulated gene, CSF3 was enriched in two signaling pathways, IL-17 and JAK/STAT, which were related to liver fibrosis in the KEGG analysis in our study. Overall, upregulated CSF3 in KCs could have a crucial role in C. sinensis infection, producing a marked effect in the progress of liver fibrosis via the IL-17 and JAK/STAT pathways. Although we identified that CSF3 was upregulated significantly in mice infected with C. sinensis, the specific mechanisms involved in activating the IL-17 and JAK/STAT pathways to promote liver fibrosis remain unclear, and thus, futher work is required.

Conclusion

This research was the first to report changes in the expression of genes in KCs following CsEVs stimulation. As a potential functional gene in KCs, CSF3 showed the highest degree of upregulation during C. sinensis infection. Given that, CSF3 could have profound roles in the progress of liver fibrosis, these results provided not only a scientific reference for the study of the pathogenic mechanism of C. sinensis, but also novel potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of liver fibrosis.
