Complexity of Constructing Minimal Faithful Permutation Representations for Fitting-free Groups
Michael Levet, Pranjal Srivastava, Dhara Thakkar

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the complexity of computing minimal faithful permutation representations for groups without abelian normal subgroups (a.k.a. Fitting-free groups). When our groups are given as quotients of permutation groups, we exhibit a polynomial-time algorithm for constructing such representations. Furthermore, in the setting of permutation groups, we obtain an NC procedure for computing the minimal faithful permutation degree, and a randomized NC ( RNC ) algorithm for computing a minimal faithful permutation representation. This improves upon the work of Das and Thakkar (STOC 2024, SIAM J. Comput. 2026), who established a Las Vegas polynomial-time algorithm for computing the minimal faithful permutation degree for this class in the setting of permutation groups.

Techniques for Computing Minimal Faithful Permutation Representation.
While our work on computing
\mu(\textbf{G})
, building on the work of Das and Thakkar
[
23

socle

\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
is the direct product of the minimal normal subgroups of

is of the form

, for some non-abelian simple group

S_{1}
be one such non-abelian simple direct factor of
N
. Let

be the almost-simple subgroup induced by the conjugation action of
N_{\textbf{G}}(S_{1})
on
S_{1}
. Our first step is to build a minimal faithful permutation representation of

. For computing
\mu(\textbf{G})
, we only require constructive recognition of finite simple groups. However, in order to compute an embedding
\varphi:A\to\text{Sym}(\mu(A))
, we require minimal faithful permutation representations for each non-abelian simple group
S
.

If
|S|\in\poly(n)
, then we can write down the multiplication table for
S
. As
S
is simple, we have from
[
41
]
that there exists a maximal subgroup
P\leq S
such that the regular action of
S
on the right cosets of

is a minimal faithful permutation representation. Kantor
[
45
]
established that if

is an exceptional group of Lie type, then
|S|<n^{9}
(recalled as Theorem
2.9
). Note that there are
26
sporadic groups, and so these all have bounded order. Thus, for the exceptional groups of Lie type and the sporadic groups, we may construct a minimal faithful permutation degree in this way (see Lemma
2.12
).

In the case when
|S|\geq n^{9}
, then

is either an alternating group or a classical simple group. We do not require detailed knowledge of the minimal faithful permutation representation of the alternating groups. However, we crucially utilize the fact that, except in a few small cases, a classical simple group of dimension
m
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
admits a minimal faithful permutation representation over an appropriate orbit of
1

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}

(see in particular, Mark’s thesis
[

) shows that if

is a classical simple group of order

, that their procedure for constructive recognition of finite simple groups returns a minimal faithful permutation representation detailing the action of

on the corresponding orbit of
1

, we will need to do so (Lemma

Now if

is a classical or exceptional simple group of Lie type (excluding

can be decomposed in terms of inner, diagonal, field, and graph automorphisms

37

. For these cases, we will need to build permutations for the inner, field, diagonal, and graph and triality automorphisms. The key technical challenge is to build permutation representations for the graph and triality automorphisms in the cases where

is a classical simple group. While classical simple groups act naturally on orbits of

Remark 1.5
.

It remains an intriguing open question whether our polynomial-time bounds for quotients (Theorem
1.2
(a)) can be improved to
NC
. In the setting of polynomial-time computation, Kantor and Luks
[
44
]
conjectured a Quotient Group Thesis, stating:

“If a problem for quotient groups
G/K
of permutation groups has a polynomial-time solution when
K=1
, then it has a polynomial-time solution in general.”

In the same paper, Kantor and Luks gave considerable supporting evidence for their Quotient Group Thesis by exhibiting a number of polynomial-time algorithms for quotient groups. There has been considerable work on quotient groups since, strengthening the case for the Quotient Group Thesis
[
71
,
12
,
50
,
51
,
24
]
. In some cases, such as computing direct product decompositions, access to quotients appears necessary
[
71
]
.

It is worth noting that while the polynomial-time library for quotients is similar to that of permutation groups, the techniques are often quite different. In the setting of permutation groups, algorithmic techniques often crucially leverage the action of the group on the permutation domain. For quotient groups, such an action is not available
[
58
]
, and so the techniques rely more deeply on the theory of the underlying group.

The setting of
NC
highlights this distinction. We note that in order to obtain
NC
bounds, we are limited to polylogarithmic iterations. However, at each iteration, we can perform a “polynomial amount of work” in parallel (see Section
2.2
for a precise formulation of
NC
). Consider the problem of computing the centralizer
C_{G}(H)
, provided
G
normalizes
H
. Babai, Luks, and Seress
[
3
]
exhibited an
NC
algorithm for this problem in the setting of permutation groups, crucially taking advantage of the action of
G
and
H
on the underlying permutation domain. In the setting of quotient groups, Kantor and Luks
[
43
]
exhibited a polynomial-time algorithm for this problem by utilizing polynomial-time subroutines to compute Sylow subgroups
[
43
,
54
]
, as well as the intersection
G\cap P
between an arbitrary permutation group
G
and a
p
-group
P

[
52
,
51
]
.

While Sylow subgroups of permutation groups can be computed in
NC

[
43
,
54
]
, obtaining such bounds for the intersection problem remains elusive. Such procedures for computing

employ a divide-and-conquer strategy, processing the orbits sequentially and then breaking the orbits up into blocks of imprimitivity. Note that there exist permutation groups of degree
n
with a linear number of orbits (take, for instance, the action of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
on
2n
points, which yields

orbits of size
2
). Thus, the key bottleneck lies in the sequential processing of the orbits. Overcoming this obstacle appears to require new techniques, and so improving the bounds on Theorem

(a) from polynomial-time to

is non-trivial. It is an intriguing open question as to whether the Quotient Group Thesis of Kantor and Luks holds in the setting of

2.1
Groups

All groups are assumed to be finite. Let
\text{Sym}(n)
denote the symmetric group on
n

of a subset

that contains

of a group

G

decomposes uniquely as the direct product of non-abelian simple factors. The
solvable radical

\text{Rad}(G)
is the unique maximal solvable normal subgroup of

\text{Rad}(G)
by the quotient
G/\text{Rad}(G)
, the latter of which has no abelian normal subgroups. We refer to the class of groups without abelian normal subgroups as
Fitting-free
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be two groups acting on

and
\Gamma

. Let

be the set of functions from

(i.e.,
\mathrm{Fun}(\Gamma,G_{1})
). It is easy to see that

forms a group with binary operation composition on two functions (see e.g.,

, Section 2.6]
). Then

is isomorphic to the direct product of
|\Gamma|
copies of
K
via isomorphism
f\mapsto(f(\gamma_{1}),\ldots,f(\gamma_{m}))
. Let
\Omega
be the set of all functions from
\Gamma
to
\Delta
. Then the

of

by

with respect to the set

, denoted by

via

. Clearly,
|G_{1}\wr G_{2}|=|G_{1}|^{m}|G_{2}|
.

Definition 2.1
.

Fix
k\geq 0
. We say that a language
L
belongs to (uniform)
\textsf{NC}^{k}
if there exist a (uniform) family of circuits
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
over the
\textsf{AND},\textsf{OR},\textsf{NOT}
gates such that the following hold:

•

The
AND
and
OR
gates take exactly
2
inputs. That is, they have fan-in
2
.

•

C_{n}
has depth
O(\log^{k}n)
and uses (has size)
n^{O(1)}
gates. Here, the implicit constants in the circuit depth and size depend only on
L
.

•

x\in L
if and only if
C_{|x|}(x)=1
.

Now the complexity class
NC
is defined as:

\textsf{NC}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\textsf{NC}^{k}.

We also allow circuits to compute functions by using multiple output gates.

Definition 2.2
.

Fix
k\geq 0
. We say that a language
L
belongs to (uniform)
\textsf{RNC}^{k}
if there exists a polynomial
p_{L}(n)
depending only on
L
, and a language
L^{\prime}
in (uniform)
\textsf{NC}^{k}
such that the following hold. Let
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a (uniform) family of circuits witnessing
L^{\prime}
belonging to
\textsf{NC}^{k}
.

•

If
x\in L
, then:

\text{Pr}_{y\in\{0,1\}^{\leq p(|x|)}}[C_{n}((x,y))=1]\geq 1/2,\text{ and }

•

If
x\not\in L
, then for all
y\in\{0,1\}^{\leq p_{L}(|x|)}
,
C_{n}((x,y))=0
.

Now define:

\textsf{RNC}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\textsf{RNC}^{k}.

2.3
Permutation Group Algorithms

In this paper, we will consider groups specified succinctly by generating sequences. The first model we will consider is that of permutation groups. Here, a group
G
is specified by a sequence
S
of permutations from
\text{Sym}(n)
.

Alternatively, we will consider the more general quotients of permutation groups model, which we will refer to as the
quotients model
. To the best of our knowledge, this model was first introduced by Kantor and Luks
[
44
]
. Here, we will be given
K\trianglelefteq G\leq\text{Sym}(n)
, represented in the following way:

•

G,K
are both specified by a generating sequence of permutations in
\text{Sym}(n)
, and

•

Each element of
\textbf{G}:=G/K
is specified by a single coset representative.

The computational complexity for both the permutation group and the quotients models will be measured in terms of
n
. We recall a standard suite of problems with known
NC
solutions in the setting of permutation groups.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
G
be a permutation group. The following problems are in
NC
:

(a)

Compute the order of
G
.

(b)

Whether a given permutation
\sigma
is in
G
; and if so, exhibit a word
\omega
such that
\sigma=\omega(S)
.

(c)

Find the kernel of any action of
G
.

(d)

Find the pointwise stabilizer of
B\subseteq[m]
.

(e)

Find the normal closure of any subset of
G
.

(f)

For
H\leq\text{Sym}(n)
such that
G
normalizes
H
, compute
C_{G}(H)
.

(g)

For
H\leq\text{Sym}(n)
such that
G
normalizes
H
, compute
G\cap H
.

We easily extend a subset of these problems to the setting of quotients.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\textbf{G}=G/K
be a quotient of a permutation group. The following problems are in
NC
:

(a)

Compute the order of
G
.

(b)

Given a permutation
\sigma
, test whether
\sigma K
is in
G
; and if so, exhibit a word
\omega
that evaluates to
\sigma K
.

Proof.

We proceed as follows.

(a)

As
G,K
are permutation groups, we can compute
|G|,|K|
in
NC

[
3
]
.

(b)

We note that
\sigma K\in\textbf{G}
if and only if
\sigma\in G
. So we use the
NC
constructive membership test from
[
3
]
to test whether
\sigma\in G
. ∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
c>0
. Let
K\trianglelefteq G\leq\text{Sym}(n)
, and let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
. If
|\textbf{G}|\leq n^{c}
, then we can list
G
in
NC
.

Proof.

We begin by computing
|\textbf{G}|
in
NC
(Lemma
2.4
). Now let
S
be the set of generators we are given for
G
. Wolf
[
73
]
showed that we can write down
\langle S\rangle
in
O(\log|\textbf{G}|)
rounds. We recall Wolf’s procedure here. Let
T_{0}=S\cup\{1\}
. Now for
i\geq 0
, we construct
T_{i+1}
by computing
xy
, for each
x,y\in T_{i}
. Our procedure terminates when
|T_{i}|=|\textbf{G}|
.

We note that Wolf is considering quasigroups given by their multiplication tables, in which case we can multiply two elements in
\textsf{AC}^{0}
. However, we are dealing with quotients of permutation groups. In particular, any element of
G
is represented as a permutation in
\text{Sym}(n)
. Note that multiplying two permutations is
FL
-computable
[
21
]
. Thus, given
T_{i}
, we can compute
T_{i+1}
in
FL
. The result follows.
∎

2.4
Background on Simple Groups

In this section, we examine classical simple groups, exceptional simple groups of Lie type, and other essential concepts related to simple groups. For a comprehensive treatment of the relevant details concerning simple groups, the reader is referred to
[
65
, Section 2.2]
. For a more in-depth exploration, further references include
[
18
]
,
[
47
]
, and
[
72
]
.

Classical Simple Groups.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
d
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=p^{e}
. We write
\mathrm{GL}(d,q)
to denote the
general linear group
, which is the group of all invertible linear transformations from
V
to
V
. The
special linear group
(denoted by
\mathrm{SL}(d,q)
) is a subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(d,q)
containing all invertible linear transformations with determinant
1
. The center
Z({\rm{GL}}(d,q))
consists of all transformation of the form
T(x)=\beta x
for
\beta(\neq 0)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The factor group
\mathrm{SL}(d,q)/Z(\mathrm{SL}(d,q)
is called the
projective special linear group
and is denoted by
\PSL(d,q)
. It is known that
\PSL(d,q)
is a simple group with few exceptions
[
18
,
72
]
.

Now we define the simple group
\mathrm{PSp}(2d,q)
. Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
2d
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=p^{e}
. The symplectic classical group
\mathrm{Sp}(2d,q)
is the set of invertible linear transformations of a
2d
-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
which preserves a non-degenerate, alternating, skew-symmetric bilinear form (see, e.g.,
[
47
, Page 13]
). It is known that any non-degenerate, skew-symmetric, bilinear form can be represented with respect to some basis by the matrix

X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&&&&&&&\\
-1&0&&&&&\bf{0}&&\\
&&0&1&&&&&\\
&&-1&0&&&&&\\
&\bf{0}&&&&\ddots&&&\\
&&&&&&&0&1\\
&&&&&&&-1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Let
T
be the matrix corresponding to a linear transformation
\tau
. We have that
\tau\in{\rm{Sp}}(2d,q)
if and only if
T^{t}XT=X
, where
T^{t}
is the transpose of
T
. The center
Z({\rm{Sp}}(2d,q))
consists of the linear transformations
f(x)=\beta x
where
\beta=\pm 1
. The factor group
{\mathrm{Sp}}(2d,q)/Z({\rm{Sp}}(2d,q))
is called
projective symplectic group

\mathrm{PSp}(2d,q)
. The group
\mathrm{PSp}(2d,q)
is a simple provided
{d}\geq 2
except when
{\rm PSp}(4,2)
. In the case when
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is of characteristic
2
, we have
\mathrm{PSp}(2d,q)={\mathrm{Sp}}(2d,q)
.

Let us now define the simple group
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
. Let
V
be vector space of dimension
m\geq 2
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=p^{e}
and
p
odd. Let
Q
be a quadratic form whose associated bilinear form is
\mathfrak{f}_{Q}
. Then, the isometries of
V
are called orthogonal linear transformations; they comprise the orthogonal group
{\rm{O}}(V,Q)

[
36
,
72
]
. Thus

{\rm{O}}(V,Q)=\{\tau\in\mathrm{GL}(m,q)|Q(\tau u)=Q(u)\text{ for all }u\in V\}\leq\mathrm{GL}(m,q)

We denote by
\Omega(V,Q)
the commutator subgroup of
{\rm{O}}(V,Q)
. Let
Z({\rm{O}}(V,Q))
be the center of
{\rm{O}}(V,Q)
. Then the corresponding projective group is defined as

\mathrm{P\Omega}(V,Q)=\Omega(V,Q)/(Z({\rm{O}}(V,Q))\cap\Omega(V,Q)).

Now assume that
m=2d
. In this case, there are two non-equivalent quadratic forms on
V
, say
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
; these forms give rise to two different orthogonal groups (see, e.g., section 1.4,
[
18
]
,
[
47
]
). These quadratic forms are represented by the following matrices
X
and
X^{\prime}
, respectively,

X=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{d}\\
I_{d}&0\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }X^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{d-1}&&&I_{d-1}&0&0\\
&&&&\vdots&\vdots\\
I_{d-1}&&&0_{d-1}&0&0\\
0&&\ldots&0&1&0\\
0&&\ldots&0&0&-\epsilon\end{bmatrix},

where
\epsilon
is a non-square element in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The orthogonal groups corresponding to these quadratic forms are denoted by

O^{+}(2d,q)=O(V,Q)
and
O^{-}(2d,q)=O(V,Q^{\prime})
, respectively. Similarly,
\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
is the commutator subgroup of
O^{+}(2d,q)
, and
\Omega^{-}(2d,q)
is the commutator subgroup of
O^{-}(2d,q)
. The associated simple groups corresponding to
O^{+}(2d,q)
and
O^{-}(2d,q)
are denoted by
\mathrm{P\Omega}^{+}(2d,q)
and
\mathrm{P\Omega}^{-}(2d,q)
, respectively (see, e.g.,
[
18
, Pages 4-7]
). The groups
{\rm P}\Omega^{+}(2{d},q)
and
{\rm P}\Omega^{-}(2{d},q)
are simple when
{d}\geq 4
.

A subspace
W
of
V
is said to be
anisotropic
with respect to a quadratic form
Q
if the restriction of
Q
to the subspace is nondegenerate. A bilinear map
\mathfrak{f}_{Q}
is the null-form if
\mathfrak{f}_{Q}(u,v)=0
for all
u,v\in V
. A subspace
W
of
V
is said to be
isotropic
with respect to quadratic form
Q
if the restriction of
\mathfrak{f}_{Q}
to
W
is the null-form and
Q(w)=0
for all
w\in W

[
66
,
55
]
.

Now we describe various automorphisms of
\SL(d,q)
,
\Omega^{+}(2d,3)
(
d\geq 4
), or
\mathrm{Sp}(4,2^{e})
(
e\geq 2
)(see
[
18
]
for more details).

Definition 2.6
.

Let
G
be one of the following:
\SL(d,q)
,
\Omega^{+}(2d,3)
(
d\geq 4
), or
\mathrm{Sp}(4,2^{e})
(
e\geq 2
). We introduce several automorphisms.

(a)

An automorphism
\mathfrak{i}:G\to G
is called an
inner automorphism
if there exists a matrix
F\in G
such that
\mathfrak{i}(U)=F^{-1}UF
for all
U\in G
.

(b)

An automorphism
\mathfrak{d}:G\to G
is called a
diagonal automorphism
if there exists a diagonal matrix
F\in\text{GL}(d,q)
such that
\mathfrak{d}(F)=F^{-1}UF
for all
U\in G
.

(c)

Let
\sigma
be an automorphism of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. A
field automorphism

\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma}:G\to G
is defined as
\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma}([u_{ij}])=[\sigma(u_{ij})]
, where
[u_{ij}]
denotes the matrix in
G
whose
(i,j)
th entry is
u_{ij}
.

Let
q=p^{e}
. Let us consider the Frobenius automorphism
\sigma_{0}
of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
given by
a\mapsto a^{p}
. The automorphism
\sigma_{0}
is a generator of
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
of order
e
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma}=\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}
for some
1\leq t^{\prime}\leq e
.

(d)

The
graph automorphism

\mathfrak{g}:G\to G
is defined as
\mathfrak{g}(U)=(U^{t})^{-1}
for all
U\in G
. Here,
U^{t}
denotes the transpose of
U
.

A map
f:V\rightarrow V
is called
semi-linear transformation
of
V
if there is an automorphism
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
such that for all
u,v\in V
and
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
f(u+v)=f(u)+f(v)
and
f(\beta v)=\sigma(\beta)f(v)
. A semi-linear transformation
f
is invertible if
\{v\in V\mid f(v)=0\}=\{0\}
. The group of all invertible semi-linear transformations is called
general semi-linear group
, denoted by
\Gamma{\rm L}(d,q)

[
47
]
. The
projective general semi-linear group
,
{\rm P}\Gamma{\rm L}(d,q)
, is obtained by factoring out the scalars in
\Gamma{\rm L}(d,q)

However, for the current purpose the only fact that we need regarding
{\rm P}\Gamma{\rm L}(d,q)
is that it can be regarded as a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\SL(d,q))
and automorphisms of
\SL(d,q)
that contain the graph automorphisms
\mathfrak{g}
in its decomposition are exactly the elements in
\mathrm{Aut}(\SL(d,q))\setminus{\rm P}\Gamma{\rm L}(d,q)

[
47
,
18
]
.

Exceptional Groups of Lie Type.

In this section, we define the Chevalley generators for
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\in\{G_{2}(3^{e}),F_{4}(2^{e}),E_{6}(q)\}
. Unlike the classical simple groups, the exceptional simple groups of Lie type do not have matrix representations. Thus, we opt for this Chevalley presentation. Additionally, the Chevalley generator framework provides a well-established method for studying the automorphisms of these groups. Let us now define the Chevalley generators of the simple group
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
from
[
18
, Chapter 3-4, 7]
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lie algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. Let
\Phi
be a root system corresponding to
\mathcal{L}
and
\Pi\subsetneq\Phi
be a fundamental system of roots. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the subset of
\mathcal{L}
of all linear combinations of basis elements with coefficients in the ring
\mathbb{Z}
of integers. Define
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
using the tensor product as follows:
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}=\mathbb{F}_{q}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Z}}
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Section 4.4, Page 62]
). The automorphisms of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
are the Chevalley generators of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. For each
r\in\Phi
and
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
x_{r}(\beta)
is an automorphisms of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. The action of
x_{r}(\beta)
on the generating set of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
has been explicitly defined in
[
18
, Chapters 3-4, 7]
for the comprehensive details.

We have
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=\langle\{x_{r}(\beta)\mid r\in\Phi\text{ and }\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\rangle
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Section 4.4, Page 64]
). E.g., let
\mathcal{L}=G_{2}
, then
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=G_{2}(q)
is the exceptional group of Lie type obtained from
\mathcal{L}
. Also, we have
G_{2}(q)=\langle\{x_{r}(\beta)\mid r\in\Phi\text{ and }\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}\rangle
and
|\Phi|=12
. Similarly, we can define the Chevalley generators for
F_{4}(2^{e}),E_{6}(q)
. In case of
F_{4}(2^{e})
,
|\Phi|=2^{7}3^{2}
, and in case of
E_{6}(q)
, we have
|\Phi|=2^{7}3^{4}5
(see
[
18
, Page 43]
). Therefore, the size of the Chevalley generators is polynomial in
q
. Additionally, note that, since
|\Phi|
is bounded by a constant, the size of a generating set
\{h_{r},r\in\Pi,e_{r},r\in\Phi\}
of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
is also bounded by a constant.

Let us now describe the automorphisms of the Chevalley group
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Chapter 12]
). Notice that it is enough to define the automorphism at the Chevalley generators of the group
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

There are four types of automorphism of a finite simple group: the inner automorphism, the diagonal automorphism, the field automorphism, and the graph automorphism. Any automorphism of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F})
can be decomposed as a product of these four automorphisms
[
18
]
. We first define the inner and diagonal automorphisms of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.

Let
P=\mathbb{Z}\Phi
be the set of all linear combinations of elements of
\Phi
with rational integer coefficients.
P
is the additive group generated by the roots of
\mathcal{L}
. A homomorphism from
P
into the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}^{*}_{q}
is called
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-character of
P
. The
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-character of
P
forms a multiplicative group and each
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-character
\chi
gives rise to an automorphism
h(\chi)
of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. The automorphisms
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
of the form
h(\chi)
form a subgroup
\hat{H}
of the full automorphism group of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Section 7.1, Page 98]
). Let
H
be the subgroup of
\hat{H}
generated by the automorphisms
h_{r}(\beta)
for all
r\in\Phi
,
\beta\in\mathbb{F}^{*}_{q}
. Then
G=\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
normalized by
\hat{H}
in the group of all automorphisms of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Thus, if
h(\chi)\in\hat{H}
and
g\in G
, then a map
g\rightarrow h(\chi)gh(\chi)^{-1}
is an automorphism of
G
. If
h(\chi)\in\hat{H}\setminus H
, then the automorphism is called a
diagonal automorphism
of
G
. If
h(\chi)\in H
, then the automorphism is called an
inner automorphism
of
G
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Section 12.2, Page 200]
).

Any automorphism
\sigma_{0}
of the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
induces a field automorphism, say
\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma_{0}}
, of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
It is defined in the following manner:
\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma_{0}}:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
\mathfrak{f}_{\sigma_{0}}(x_{r}(\beta))=x_{r}(\sigma_{0}(\beta))
for
r\in\Phi
. The set of field automorphisms of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a cyclic group isomorphic to
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Section 12.2, Page 200]
).

The graph automorphisms of the Chevalley group
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
arise from the symmetries of the Dynkin diagram. A symmetry of the Dynkin diagram of
\mathcal{L}
is a permutation
\varrho
of the nodes of the diagram such that the number of bonds joining nodes
i.j
is the same as the number of bonds joining
\varrho(i),\varrho(j)
for any
i\neq j
. Let
r\in\Phi
,
\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
r\rightarrow{\bar{r}}
be a map of
\Phi
into itself arising from a symmetry of the Dynkin diagram of
\mathcal{L}
(see e.g.,
[
18
, Sections 12.2-12.4]
and
[
22
, Section 2]
). The graph automorphisms
\mathfrak{g}
of the Chevalley groups
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\in\{G_{2}(3^{e}),F_{4}(2^{e}),E_{6}(q)\}
are defined in the following ways (
[
18
]
):

1.

if
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=G_{2}(3^{e})
,

\mathfrak{g}(x_{r}(\beta))=x_{\bar{r}}(\beta^{\lambda(\bar{r})}),

where
\lambda(\bar{r_{i}})=1
if
r
is short root and
3
if
r
is long root.

2.

\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=F_{4}(2^{e})
,

\mathfrak{g}(x_{r}(\beta))=x_{\bar{r}}(\beta^{\lambda(\bar{r})}),

where
\lambda(\bar{r_{i}})=1
if
r
is short root and
2
if
r
is long root.

3.

\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{F}_{q})=E_{6}(q)
,

\mathfrak{g}(x_{r}(\beta))=x_{\bar{r}}(\gamma_{r}z),

where
\gamma_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}
can be chosen so that
\gamma_{r}=1
if
r\in\Pi
,
\gamma_{r}=-1
if
-r\in\Pi
.

Theorem 2.7
.

(cf.
[
18
, Theorem 12.5.1, p. 211]
)
Let
G
be a classical simple group or an exceptional simple group of Lie type defined over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Then every automorphisms of
G
can be decomposed as
\mathfrak{idgf}
, where
\mathfrak{i}
,
\mathfrak{d}
, and
\mathfrak{f}
are inner, diagonal, field automorphisms respectively, and
\mathfrak{g}
is the graph automorphism.

Remark 2.8
.

Note that every automorphism of
G
can also be decomposed as
\mathfrak{idfg}
as described in
[
1
, Section 10]
.

2.5
The Minimal Faithful Permutation degree of Simple and Almost simple groups

In this section, we will examine how to construct minimal faithful permutation representations of finite simple groups. We will crucially use the following result of Kantor.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
45
]
)
.

If
G\leq\text{Sym}(\Omega)
, with
|G|\geq|\Omega|^{9}
, then
G
and
\Omega
are (up to a permutation isomorphism):

•

G=\text{Alt}(r)
for some
r
, and there exists some
k
such that
\Omega
is the set of all
k
-element subsets of
[r]
;

•

G=\text{Alt}(r)
for some
r
, and
\Omega
is the set of partitions of the underlying set
[r]
into blocks of size
k
for some
k
; or

•

G
is a classical group, and
\Omega
is an orbit of subspaces of an underlying vector space.

In our setting, Theorem
2.9
essentially allows us to focus on classical simple groups
G
, where the underlying vector space
V
satisfies
\text{dim}(V)>8
(see e.g.,
[
45
,
43
]
). Thus, we are able to avoid technicalities when the dimension of the underlying vector space is

. Additionally, if the simple group in question has order at most
n^{9}
, then we may write down its multiplication table in
NC
, using Lemma
2.5
.

We now recall key facts concerning minimal faithful permutation representations of certain classical simple groups. Let
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, and let
\overline{V}
be an (orbit of)
1
-spaces of
V
. The
natural action
of a classical group
G
on

if the only

are
{0}
and
V
. Let

and

.

Definition 2.15

Let

be a group. We say that two representations

and

are

such that

for all

. We write

.

Lemma 2.16

(Schur’s Lemma o
[
63
]
)
.

Let
\vartheta:G\rightarrow{\rm{GL}}(V)
and

be irreducible representations of a group

be a linear map such that

is invertible or

. Moreover, the only non-trivial linear maps

are identity or scalar multiples of identity.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let

Lemma 3.3

(

]
)

If

, then

is Fitting-free and
\text{Soc}(N)=\text{Soc}(G)\cap N
.

Proof.

Every subgroup of

\text{Soc}(N)
cannot contain any non-minimal normal subgroups of

. And since
\text{Soc}(N)
is characteristic in

In the opposite direction, suppose

is a minimal normal subgroup of

. Then

is a direct product of non-abelian simple groups since

is Fitting-free. As

(see also

is a normal subgroup of

N_{i}
which is normal in
G
, we have that
\text{Soc}(N_{i})
is also normal in
G
. Hence
\text{Soc}(N_{i})
is also normal in
\text{Soc}(N_{i+1})
. Since all these socles are direct products of non-abelian simple groups, there is some
S\leq G
that is a direct product of non-abelian simple groups such that
\text{Soc}(N_{i+1})=\text{Soc}(N_{i})\times S
. By the definition of
M_{i+1}
, it is then necessary and sufficient to show
N_{i+1}\cap C_{G}(M_{i})=S
.

As
S
centralizes
\text{Soc}(N_{i})=M_{i}
, we have that
S
is contained in both
N_{i+1}
and
C_{G}(M_{i})
. Therefore
S\leq N_{i+1}\cap C_{G}(M_{i})
.

In the opposite direction, suppose
g\in N_{i+1}\cap C_{G}(M_{i})
; we will show that
g
must be in
S
. Since
C_{N_{i+1}}(\text{Soc}(N_{i+1}))=1
,
g
must not centralize all of
\text{Soc}(N_{i+1})
. But since
g
centralizes
M_{i}=\text{Soc}(N_{i})
,
g
must not centralize
S
. Let
N
be the normal closure of
g
in
G
. Since
N_{i+1}\unlhd G
, we have
N\leq N_{i+1}
and hence
N\unlhd N_{i+1}
. Additionally, since
M_{i}=\text{Soc}(N_{i})
, we have argued above that
M_{i}\unlhd G
, and since the centralizer of a normal subgroup is normal,
C_{G}(M_{i})
is also normal in
G
, and thus
N\leq C_{G}(M_{i})
. Since
N
is normal in
G
, it is Fitting-free, and we have
\text{Soc}(N)=N\cap\text{Soc}(G)\leq N_{i+1}\cap\text{Soc}(G)=\text{Soc}(N_{i+1})
. Since
N\leq C_{G}(M_{i})=C_{G}(\text{Soc}(N_{i}))
, we must have
\text{Soc}(N)\cap\text{Soc}(N_{i})=1
. But
\text{Soc}(N)\leq\text{Soc}(N_{i+1})=\text{Soc}(N_{i})\times S
, and
\text{Soc}(N)
is normal in
N_{i+1}
, so—since these socles are all direct products of non-abelian simple groups—we must have
\text{Soc}(N)\leq S
, and
g
does not centralize
\text{Soc}(N)
. But if
g
is not in
\text{Soc}(N)
, then
N
is not a direct product of non-abelian simple groups so we must in fact have
g\in\text{Soc}(N)\leq S
, as claimed.

Therefore
S=N_{i+1}\cap C_{G}(M_{i})
, and
M_{i+1}=M_{i}\times S=\text{Soc}(N_{i+1})
. ∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Babai, Luks, and Seress
[
3
]
established that there exists an
NC
-computable series

1=N_{0}\leq N_{1}\leq\ldots\leq N_{r}=G

of length
r\in O(\log^{c}n)
(Kantor, Luks, and Mark
[
43
]
determined that
c=2
) such that for all
i\in[r]
,
N_{i}\trianglelefteq G
(see
[
43
, Sec. 3.6]
) and each
N_{i}/N_{i-1}
is a direct product of simple groups (semisimple). In particular,
N_{i}/N_{i-1}
is either abelian or has no abelian direct factor. We crucially take advantage of both the length of the series, as well as the fact that the successive quotients are semisimple to compute
\text{Soc}(G)
in
NC
.

We proceed inductively along the series. We begin with
N_{1}
. As
G
is Fitting-free,
N_{1}
is the direct product of non-abelian simple groups. By Lemma
3.3
,
N_{1}=\text{Soc}(N_{1})\trianglelefteq\text{Soc}(G)
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\text{Soc}(G)=N_{1}\times\text{Soc}(C_{G}(N_{1}))
.

Define
M_{1}:=N_{1}(=\text{Soc}(N_{1}))
. And for
i>1
, define
M_{i}:=M_{i-1}\times(N_{i}\cap C_{G}(M_{i-1}))
. As each
N_{i}
is normal in
G
, we have that each
M_{i}
is normal in
G
. Now by Lemma
2.3
(f), we may compute
C_{G}(M_{i-1})
in
NC
, provided we are given generators for
M_{i-1}
. By Lemma
2.3
(g), we may then compute
C_{G}(M_{i-1})\cap N_{i}
in
NC
. Thus, we may compute
M_{i}
in
NC
, provided we are given generators for
M_{i-1}
. As
r\in O(\log^{2}n)
, we may compute
M_{1},\ldots,M_{r}
in
NC
. By Lemma
3.4
, we have that
M_{r}:=\text{Soc}(G)
.

Once we have generators for
\text{Soc}(G)
, we may decompose
\text{Soc}(G)
into a direct product of non-abelian simple groups in
NC
using
[
3
]
(see also the discussion following the proof of
[
4
, Proposition 2.1]
). The result now follows.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In the setting of both permutation groups and their quotients, the socle is polynomial-time computable
[
44
]
. However, obtaining
NC
bounds is a long-standing open problem. Extending Proposition
3.1
to the setting of quotients is also non-trivial. We rely crucially on the
NC
algorithm of Babai, Luks, and Seress
[
3
]
to compute the centralizer of a normal subgroup
H
in
NC
. Kantor and Luks
[
44
, Section 6]
note that the techniques for computing
C_{G}(H)
in the setting of permutation groups appear not to work for quotients. Instead, Kantor and Luks bring to bear the extensive machinery for computing Sylow subgroups of permutation groups in order to compute cores of quotients. Kantor and Luks then reduce computing
C_{G}(H)
to computing an appropriate core. It is unclear how to compute cores of quotients in
NC
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
K\trianglelefteq G\leq\text{Sym}(n)
, and let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
be Fitting-free. Suppose we are given the non-abelian simple factors of
\text{Soc}(G)
. Then in
NC
, we can compute the set of minimal normal subgroups of
G
.

Proof.

The conjugation action of
G
on
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
induces a permutation action on the non-abelian factors of
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
. There are
\leq\log|G|\leq\log n!\sim n\log n
such factors. View
G
as a permutation group
G^{*}
, with the non-abelian simple factors as the permutation domain. For every given orbit
\mathcal{O}
of
G^{*}
, the members of
\mathcal{O}
are precisely the factors of a single non-abelian simple factor of a minimal normal of
G
. Furthermore, all such minimal normal subgroups of
G
are realized in this way. Now computing orbits in a permutation group is
NC
-computable
[
56
]
. The result now follows.
∎

Finally, we will show that if we are given a non-abelian simple factor
S
is a non-abelian simple factor of the socle, for a Fitting-free group
G
, we can compute
N_{\textbf{G}}(S)
in
NC
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G,K\leq\text{Sym}(n)
with
K\trianglelefteq G
. Let
\textbf{G}=G/K
. Suppose that
G
is Fitting-free, and we are given the non-abelian simple factors of
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
. Let
S
be such a simple factor. Computing
N_{\textbf{G}}(S)
is
NC
-reducible to
Pointwise Stabilizer
. In particular, we can compute
N_{\textbf{G}}(S)
in
NC
.

Proof.

The conjugation action of
G
on
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
induces a permutation action on the non-abelian simple factors of
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
. There are
\leq\log|\textbf{G}|\leq\log n!\sim n\log n
such factors. View
G
as a permutation group
G^{*}
, with the non-abelian simple factors as the permutation domain.

We claim that, in
NC
, we can decide whether an element of
G
fixes
S
setwise. Given generators for
S
and some
g\in\textbf{G}
, we can use
Membership
to test whether
\langle S\rangle=\langle S\rangle^{g}
. By Lemma
2.3
(b),
Membership
is
NC
-computable. Thus, we can decide in
NC
whether an element of
G^{*}
stabilizes
S
as a point. The point stabilizer
\text{Stab}_{G^{*}}(S)=N_{\textbf{G}}(S)
. By Lemma
2.3
(d),
Point Stabilizer
is
NC
-computable. The result follows.
∎

4
Lifting of Automorphisms

4.1
Lifting Automorphisms of
\PSL(d,q)
to Automorphisms of
\text{SL}(d,q)

In this section, we extend the work of
[
23
, Section 3]
to show that we can lift an automorphism of
\PSL(d,q)
to an automorphism of
\text{SL}(d,q)
in
NC
. Throughout this section, we will fix
n
and consider quotients of permutation groups of
\text{Sym}(n)
. We will also assume throughout this section that
d\geq 3
, and
(d,q)\neq(3,2),(4,2)
. We begin with the following observation.

Observation 4.1
.

Let
G,K\leq\text{Sym}(n)
, with
K\trianglelefteq G
. Let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
. Suppose that
\PSL(d,q)\leq\textbf{G}
. Then (i)
d\in O(\sqrt{n\log n})
, and (ii)
q\leq n
.

Proof.

We note that
|\PSL(d,q)|\in q^{O(d^{2})}\leq|\textbf{G}|\leq|G|
. It follows that
d^{2}\log q\leq\log|G|\leq\log n!\leq n\log n
, which establishes (i). However, this only provides the bound that
\log q\leq n\log n
. To see that
q\leq n
, let
q^{\prime}
be the largest prime power such that
\text{PSL}(d,q^{\prime})\leq\text{Sym}(n)
. In this case,
q^{\prime}\leq n

[
46
]
. Clearly,
q\leq q^{\prime}
, and so
q\leq n
, as desired.
∎

In light of Observation
4.1
, we may assume without loss of generality that
d,q
are given in unary. Throughout this section, let
Z\leq\text{SL}(d,q)
denote the subgroup of all nonzero scalar transformations of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. Note that
Z=Z(\text{SL}(d,q))
.

Proposition 4.2

(
[
23
, Proposition 3.1]
)
.

Let
d\geq 3
,
p
be prime, and

such that
q=p^{e}
. Suppose that
(d,q)\not\in\{(3,2),(4,2)\}
. Each coset of
\text{SL}(d,q)/Z
has at most one element of order
p
.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
25
]
)
.

\Aut(\PSL(d,q))\cong\Aut(\text{SL}(d,q))
.

Let
\Psi:\Aut(\text{SL}(d,q))\to\Aut(\PSL(d,q))
be the map that takes
\alpha\in\Aut(\text{SL}(d,q))
to the map
\overline{\alpha}:\PSL(d,q)\to\PSL(d,q)
, where
\overline{\alpha}(UZ)=\alpha(U)Z
. Das and Thakkar established
[
23
, Proposition 3.3]
that

is indeed an isomorphism.

Theorem
2.11
provides a framework to convert between the matrix and permutation representations. We take advantage of this in tandem with a prescribed generating set of matrices
[
18

to lift automorphisms of
\PSL(d,q)
to automorphisms of
\text{SL}(d,q)
.

[

. As the diagram is commutative,
\alpha(U)
is an element of the coset
VZ
. So we have that
\alpha(U),m_{2}(\alpha(U))\in VZ
. However, by
[
23
, Proposition 3.1]
, there is at most one element of
VZ
of order
p
. Hence,
\alpha(U)=m_{2}(\alpha(U))
. As
|Z|=\text{gcd}(d,q-1)\leq n
, we can in
NC
, using Lemma
2.5
, list
Z
and hence
VZ
. Thus, we can in
NC
find
\alpha(U)=m_{2}(\alpha(U))
.

Now, for each
U\in L
, we can in
NC
construct a word over the specified generating set of
\PSL(d,q)
. Here, we use the fact that
\PSL(d,q)
is given as a quotient of permutation groups. Thus, by Lemma
2.4
, we can evaluate
\lambda(m_{2}(U))
in
NC
. The corresponding word, in particular, yields a coset representative of
VZ
. As we have written down
Z
, we can identify
V
in
NC
. The result now follows.
∎

We now turn to identifying the type of a given automorphism
\alpha
of
\text{SL}(d,q)
. We follow the strategy of Das and Thakkar
[
23
]
. Let
G=\text{SL}(d,q)
and
V=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, where
q=p^{e}
for some prime
p
and some
e>0
. Consider a representation
\vartheta:\text{SL}(d,q)\to\text{GL}(d,q)
, which takes
U
to
\vartheta_{U}
, where
\vartheta_{U}:V\to V
is defined by
\vartheta_{U}(v)=U\cdot v
. Similarly, consider the representation
\psi^{(\alpha)}:\text{SL}(d,q)\to\text{GL}(d,q)
which takes
U\in\text{SL}(d,q)
to
\psi_{U}^{(\alpha)}:V\to V
defined by
\psi_{U}^{(\alpha)}(v)=\alpha(U)\cdot v
. Note that we can define
\vartheta,\psi^{(\alpha)}
analogously for
{\rm{Sp}}(4,2^{e})
(
e\geq 2
). By Lemma
2.17
, both
\vartheta_{U}
and
\psi^{(\alpha)}
are irreducible.

Das and Thakkar
[
23
, Remark 3.5]
noted that
\vartheta\sim\psi^{(\alpha)}
if and only if there exists
F\in\text{GL}(d,q)
such that for all
U\in\text{SL}(d,q)
, we have that
FUF^{-1}=\alpha(U)
.

Lemma 4.5

(cf.
[
23
, Lemma 3.6]
)
.

Let
G
be one of the following:
\SL(d,q)
(
(d,q)\not\in\{(3,2),(4,2)\})
,
{\rm{Sp}}(4,2^{e})
(
e\geq 2
). Let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
G
. Suppose that
\alpha
is specified by its action on a generating set
B
of
G
, consisting of
d\times d
matrices over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
in
G
. Furthermore, suppose that
\vartheta
and
\psi^{(\alpha)}
are irreducible. Then in
\textsf{NC}^{2}
, we can decide if
\vartheta\sim\psi^{(\alpha)}
.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

We follow the strategy of
[
23
, Lemma 3.6]
. Our goal is to find an
F\in\text{GL}(d,q)
such that for all
U\in G
, we have that
FUF^{-1}=\alpha(U)
. Let
B=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{b}\}
be a generating set for
G
. Consider the following system of linear equations:

FU_{j}=\alpha(U_{j})F,\text{ for all }j\in[b],

where the entries of
F
are the unknowns. This is a homogeneous system of
bd^{2}
equations with
d^{2}
unknowns. We can solve this system of equations in
\textsf{NC}^{2}
using Gaussian elimination
[
57
]
. If there is a unique
F=0
, then there is no invertible matrix
F
satisfying
FU=\alpha(U)F
for all
U\in G
. In this case,
\vartheta\not\sim\psi^{(\alpha)}
. Otherwise, there is a non-zero solution satisfying
F\alpha(U)=UF
. But by Lemma
2.16
, we have that
F
is invertible. So
\vartheta\sim\psi^{(\alpha)}
. The result now follows.
∎

Observation 4.6

(
[
23
, Observation 3.7]
)
.

Let
G
be one of the following:
\SL(d,q)
,
{\rm{Sp}}(4,2^{e})
(
e\geq 2
). Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(G)
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

The irreducible representations
\vartheta,\psi^{(\alpha)}
are equivalent,

(b)

there exists a diagonal matrix
F\in\mathrm{GL}(d,q)
such that
\alpha(U)=F^{-1}UF
for all
U\in G
,

(c)

\alpha
is a diagonal automorphism of
G
. Moreover, if a diagonal matrix
F\in\mathrm{GL}(d,q)
also belongs to
G
then
\alpha
is also an inner automorphism of
G
.

Proof.

We note that, if (a) holds, i.e.,
\vartheta\sim\psi^{(\alpha)}
, which implies that there exists
F\in\mathrm{GL}(d,q)
such that
F\vartheta=\psi^{(\alpha)}F
, i.e., for all
U\in G
we have
FU=\alpha(U)F
. By
Lemma

2.16
, such
F
is always diagonal, which proves (b), and also yields the implication
(b)\implies(c)
. If
\alpha
is a diagonal automorphism of
G
, then clearly
\vartheta\sim\psi^{(\alpha)}
.
∎

4.2
Lifting Automorphisms of
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
to Automorphisms of
\Omega^{+}(2d,q)

In this section, we extend the work of
[
23
, Section 7]
to show that we can lift an automorphism of of
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
to an automorphism of
\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
, in
NC
. Throughout this section, we will fix
n
and consider quotients of permutation groups of
\text{Sym}(n)
. Additionally, throughout this section, we will assume that
q\geq 3
. We begin with the following observation.

Observation 4.7
.

Let
G,K\leq\text{Sym}(n)
, with
K\trianglelefteq G
. Let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
. Suppose that
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(2d,q)\leq\textbf{G}
. Then (i)
d\in O(\sqrt[3]{n\log n})
, and (ii)
q\leq n
.

Proof.

Note that
|\Omega^{+}(2d,q)|\leq q^{c\cdot d^{3}}
for some
c\geq 1
. Thus,
d^{3}\log q\leq\log|G|\leq\log n!\leq n\log n
. Thus,
d\in O(\sqrt[3]{n\log n})
. This establishes (i).

To see that
q\leq n
, let
q^{\prime}
be the largest prime power such that
\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(2d,q)\leq\text{Sym}(n)
. In this case,
q^{\prime}\leq n

[
46
]
. Clearly,
q\leq q^{\prime}
, and so
q\leq n
as desired.
∎

In light of Observation
4.7
, we may assume without loss of generality that
d,q
are given in unary.

Proposition 4.8

(
[
23
, Proposition 7.1]
)
.

Let
d\geq 4
, and let
q=p^{e}
where
p
is prime. Then each coset of
Z=Z(\rm{O}^{+}(2d,q))\cap\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
in
\Omega^{+}(2d,q)
has at most one element of order
p
.

Proof.

Let
U,U\Lambda\in UZ
have order
p
. If
q
is even, then
p=2
. In this case,
Z(\rm{O}^{+}(2d,q))=\{I_{2d}\}
, in which case
Z=\{I_{2d}\}
and we are done. So suppose that
q
is odd. In this case,
Z=\{\pm I_{2d}\}
. Here, we have that
\text{ord}(U)=p
and
\text{ord}(\Lambda)\leq 2
. As
U,\Lambda
commute, we have that
p=\text{ord}(U\Lambda)=\text{ord}(U)\text{ord}(\Lambda)
. As
\text{ord}(U)=p
, we thus have that
\text{ord}(\Lambda)=1
. So
\Lambda=I_{2d}
. The result follows.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Note that
Z(O^{+}(2d,q))=\{I_{2d},-I_{2d}\}
(if
q
is even, then
I_{2d}=-I_{2d}
). It follows that
Z=Z(\rm{O}^{+}(2d,q))\cap\Omega^{+}(2d,q)=\{I_{2d},-I_{2d}\}.

[
47
]

Theorem 4.10

(cf.
[
23
, Theorem 4.5]
)
.

(i)

[
26
]

\mathrm{Aut}(\mathrm{P}\Omega^{+}(2d,q))\cong\mathrm{Aut}(\Omega^{+}(2d,q))
.

(ii)

Let

be the map that takes
\alpha\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Omega^{+}(2d,q))

The proof of

.

12
Conclusion

We investigated the computational complexity of
Min-Per-Deg
for Fitting-free groups. When the groups are given as quotients of two permutation groups, we established polynomial-time bounds for computing both the minimal faithful permutation degree
\mu(G)
, as well as a permutation representation
\varphi:G\to\text{Sym}(\mu(G))
. Furthermore, in the setting of permutation groups, we established an upper bound of
NC
for computing
\mu(G)
, and
RNC
for constructing a minimal faithful permutation representation. In the process, we exhibited an upper bound of
NC
for computing the socle of a Fitting-free group, in the setting of permutation groups. Our work leaves open several problems. The most natural open problem is the following.

Problem 12.1
.

In the setting of quotients, does
Min-Per-Deg
for Fitting-free groups belong to
NC
?

Resolving Problem
12.1
will likely require novel parallel solutions for much of the polynomial-time framework for quotients developed by Kantor and Luks
[
44
]
. We highlight key obstacles next.

Problem 12.2
.

Let
G,K\leq\text{Sym}(n)
with
K\trianglelefteq G
, and let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
. Let
\textbf{N}\trianglelefteq\textbf{G}
. Compute the centralizer
C_{\textbf{G}}(\textbf{N})
in
NC
.

In order to obtain a polynomial-time solution for computing
C_{\textbf{G}}(\textbf{N})
in the setting of quotients, Kantor and Luks
[
44
]
reduce to computing the intersection
G\cap P
between an arbitrary permutation group
G
and a
p
-group
P
. Thus, we ask the following.

Problem 12.3
.

Let
G,P\leq\text{Sym}(n)
with
P
a
p
-group. Can we compute
G\cap P
in
NC
?

Problem 12.4
.

Let
G,K\leq\text{Sym}(n)
with
K\trianglelefteq G
, and let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
. Suppose that
G
is Fitting-free. Compute
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
in
NC
.

More generally, we ask about computing
\text{Soc}(\textbf{G})
in
NC
when
G
is
not
assumed to be Fitting-free. This is open, even in the setting of permutation groups (
K=1
).

Theorem
1.2
(a) formalizes that much of the framework established by Cannon and Holt
[
17
]
is polynomial-time computable. In order to strengthen these results to the setting of
NC
, it is necessary but not sufficient to resolve Problem
12.1
. We also need to compute the solvable radical.

Problem 12.5
.

Let
G,K\leq\text{Sym}(n)
with
K\trianglelefteq G
, and let
\textbf{G}:=G/K
. Compute
\rad(\textbf{G})
in
NC
.

Problem
12.5
is open, even in the setting of permutation groups (
K=1
).

Finally, it remains open to derandomize Theorem
1.2
(b). It would suffice to resolve the following problem.

Problem 12.6

(
[
3
, Problem 5]
)
.

Let
H\leq G\leq\text{Sym}(n)
be permutation groups. Suppose that
[G:H]\in\poly(n)
. Can a transversal for the cosets of
H
in
G
be constructed in
NC
?
