A novel 3D region-based speller paradigm for BCI systems
Authors: Tolga Turay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study proposes and evaluates a novel three-dimensional region-based (3D-RB) speller paradigm designed to enhance classification performance. EEG data were recorded from 15 participants using 32 channels. Classification accuracy was examined across both single electrodes and predefined electrode groups. Subject-dependent analyses revealed that electrodes located in the parietal and occipital regions (e.g., Pz, P7, P8, O1, O2, Oz) achieved the highest single-channel accuracies (approximately 80–85%), whereas central electrodes (e.g., Cz, C3, C4) yielded lower accuracies (around 70–73%). Electrode grouping provided a distinct advantage; for most participants, Group 4 (Parietal + Occipital) and Group 5 (Parietal + Occipital + Central) achieved the highest performance, reaching nearly 99% accuracy. Notably, despite including fewer electrodes, Group 4 performed nearly as well as Group 5, underscoring the practical benefit of optimized electrode selection. Subject-independent (LOSO) analyses showed similar trends. Among single electrodes, P7, P8, O1, and O2 achieved the highest accuracies (approximately 78–79%), while central electrodes (e.g., Cz, Cp1, Cp2, C3, C4) remained lower (70–73%). Electrode groups again outperformed single channels, with Group 4 and Group 5 reaching approximately 89–91% accuracy. The comparable performance of Group 4, despite fewer electrodes, highlights its practical advantage for real-world applications. Grand Average ERP analyses indicated that differences between target and non-target stimuli primarily emerged within early and mid-latency time windows, with these effects being more pronounced over parietal and occipital regions. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that incorporating three-dimensional visual effects within a region-based paradigm significantly enhances classification performance by leveraging parietal–occipital activity. The proposed 3D-RB paradigm therefore offers an efficient and user-friendly approach for future BCI speller designs.

Introduction

Humans employ methods reliant on voluntary muscle control, such as speech, writing, or gestures, to communicate with their surroundings and utilize technology. However, prevalent motor neuron diseases like Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)1–3 can lead to a total loss of muscle control in patients4–6. Similarly, trauma, paralysis, and various neuromuscular disorders7–14 may impair physical mobility, rendering communication impossible. In this context, Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) systems present a vital solution. By enabling these individuals to control computers or prosthetics without relying on their muscular systems, BCI technology allows them to communicate effectively and enhances their quality of life15–17.

In BCI systems, a paradigm denotes the visual stimulus presentation protocol designed to elicit specific neural responses, and it is employed to investigate or control key aspects of system performance such as information transfer and user workload18,19. The design of paradigms plays a pivotal role in BCI practicality20. In event-related potential (ERP)-based BCIs, the most commonly utilised component is the P300, which manifests as a positive deflection in voltage approximately 300 ms after a task-relevant stimulus. The P300 reflects endogenous cognitive processes, specifically attention allocation and context updating, making it a reliable marker of user intent18,20. Various protocols utilise this potential, starting with the row–column flashing paradigm introduced by Farwell and Donchin (1988)21. However, the efficacy of a given paradigm is intrinsically linked to the cognitive processes it engages. For instance, while the row-column paradigm is effective22,23, it can induce ’adjacency distraction’ or increased cognitive load, which may dampen the P300 amplitude and reduce accuracy compared to single-character24 or region-based paradigms25,26. Therefore, understanding how specific paradigm designs influence cognitive demands such as visual attention and fatigue is essential for optimising BCI efficacy.

The most widely used ERP-based BCI application in the literature was introduced by Farwell and Donchin in (1988)21. With this protocol, a person can select among 36 alphanumeric characters arranged in a 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}6 matrix presented on a computer screen, where rows and columns are randomly flashed21. This paradigm is referred to as the row–column speller paradigm. In this system, the user focuses attention on a target character and mentally counts the number of times it is flashed within the matrix. The original study demonstrated the feasibility of online communication through the detection of the ERP response, and analyses showed that the system could operate with an accuracy corresponding to approximately 0.20 bits/sec, allowing participants to communicate about 12.0 bits, or 2.3 characters per minute under the tested conditions. Building on this seminal work, subsequent studies have aimed to optimize both usability and performance of the speller. Thulasidas et al. investigated variations such as the number of visual repetitions and the number of EEG channels, showing that classification accuracy saturated when ten EEG channels were used, and that usability improved with fewer repetitions27. Sellers et al. compared different matrix sizes, reporting that a 6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}6 speller elicited higher P300 amplitudes, while a 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}3 matrix with a 175 ms inter-stimulus interval yielded higher accuracy28. Derivative versions of the row–column paradigm have also been explored. For instance, Salvaris and Sepulveda modified visual properties such as character color, screen background, font, and size, observing that the highest accuracy was achieved with white characters on a black background, whereas the lowest accuracy occurred with very small font sizes29. More recently, Ramirez-Quintana and colleagues tested a column-only speller system instead of the classical row–column paradigm23. Based on EEG recordings from eight participants, they reported that column-based flashing produced more effective results compared to the row–column approach, attributing this advantage to the fact that column-only flashing better aligns with natural reading habits.

To further explore the influence of three-dimensional (3D) effects on BCI paradigms, several studies have investigated their impact on system performance. In one such study, Qu et al. proposed a 3D single-character visual paradigm24. EEG data were collected from twelve participants, and the findings demonstrated that this paradigm yielded an average performance improvement of 4.9% compared to the conventional two-dimensional (2D) single-character speller system. In another study, researchers examined a 3D column-flashing paradigm with ten participants. The results revealed that, when compared with the classical 2D row–column paradigm, the newly proposed 3D paradigm improved performance by 9.69%, 4.72%, and 1.73% for one, three, and fifteen flashes, respectively. One of the most critical findings of this work was that the human brain’s natural familiarity with three-dimensional concepts substantially enhanced BCI performance when a paradigm incorporated 3D effects22. Collectively, these studies provide clear evidence that the integration of three-dimensional features can significantly improve the efficiency of BCI paradigms.

Fazel Rezai and Abhari proposed a new region-based speller to eliminate human perceptual errors observed in the row–column paradigm and to achieve higher classification accuracy. Their system utilized seven visual regions, each containing seven characters. The proposed speller was designed as a two-stage system. In the first stage, the user focused on the region containing the target character, and once the region was identified, the second stage displayed the seven characters of that region in separate areas on the screen. Based on the oddball paradigm, the target character attended by the user was detected. Their findings25 showed that the region-based speller not only achieved higher classification accuracy than the row–column paradigm but also reduced the error rate by 18.4%. Ikegami et al.30 applied both a two-stage region-based speller and the row–column speller in experiments with seven patients diagnosed with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). They aimed to demonstrate the superiority of the region-based design in classification performance. The results revealed that the region-based speller yielded significantly higher classification accuracy compared to the row–column speller. Similarly, Pan et al.31 compared single-character and region-based spellers with 12 participants. Their study found that the region-based approach produced higher P300 amplitudes and superior accuracy. In addition to these studies, a recent investigation26 explored an innovative paradigm that combined audio and visual modalities within a region-based speller. While earlier research primarily focused on visual-only designs, this study introduced three variations: audio-only, visual-only, and audio–visual spellers. Participants focused on the desired character stimulus, and classification was performed using stepwise linear discriminant analysis to determine whether the ERP signal was elicited. The results showed that the audio–visual region-based speller achieved a mean classification accuracy of 90.31%. Moreover, the proposed paradigm improved accuracy by 15.69% compared to the visual-only version and by 66.99% compared to the audio-only speller. These findings highlight that the integration of multimodal stimuli can significantly enhance the performance of region-based spellers. Collectively, these studies indicate that region-based paradigms consistently demonstrate superior performance compared to traditional row–column or other speller designs.

Collectively, the reviewed studies provide compelling evidence that both the integration of three-dimensional features and the adoption of region-based paradigms enhance the efficiency and accuracy of BCI spellers. Building upon these insights, the present study introduces a novel three-dimensional region-based (3D-RB) paradigm–an approach that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously investigated. We hypothesized that combining the depth perception cues of 3D presentation with the error-reducing structure of the region-based design would yield superior classification accuracy and information transfer rates compared to standard 2D methods. By recording and analyzing EEG data within this paradigm, the performance of the BCI system was systematically evaluated, thereby offering an original contribution to the literature through the demonstration of how three-dimensional regional effects can further advance paradigm design and system efficiency.

Materials and methods

Paradigm

Figure 1 illustrates the proposed three dimensional region-based paradigm. The visual stimuli were created using Adobe Photoshop with the application of three-dimensional effects to enhance perceptual salience. The 3D animation effect was implemented through a three step sequence. First, the background character set was presented for 75ms, establishing the visual context (Fig. 1a). Second, a flashing circle appeared in the foreground for 100ms, highlighting the potential target (Fig. 1b). Finally, the background character set was displayed again for 75ms (Fig. 1c). This triplet sequence constituted one full animation cycle. The same visual logic was applied to the second level, as depicted in the bottom row of Fig. 1d–f, where individual characters are intensified. The use of a three-dimensional visual effect was intended to increase user attention and evoke stronger neural responses by leveraging the brain’s natural familiarity with depth perception. An example of the applied 3D effect can also be observed in the video available at: https://youtu.be/qx7OwZD_n_I. In this video, the animation is presented in a slowed-down version, rather than at the original 75–100ms intervals described above, in order to allow clearer visualization and better understanding of the 3D effect.Fig. 1Three-dimensional region-based speller paradigm.

Three-dimensional region-based speller paradigm.

In the region-based visual paradigm, the stimuli are presented at two distinct levels. Example images of the first level are shown in Fig. 1a–c. In this stage, four circular regions are sequentially and randomly illuminated and shaded (flashed). A single trial consists of four flashes, as exemplified in Fig. 1b. For a stimulus containing the target character, each trial is repeated eight times, resulting in a total of 32 flashes (4 x 8 = 32) per run. After completing the first-level flashing, the paradigm proceeds to the second-level stage, illustrated in Fig. 1d–f. At this level, a similar flashing procedure is applied to a set of eight possible stimuli (e.g., A–B–C–D–E–F–G–H), where a specific character pops out in 3D (e.g., the letter ’M’ in Fig. 1e). Accordingly, one trial consists of eight flashes, and a full run is completed with 64 flashes (8 x 8 = 64). For both levels, consistent with standard protocols21, each stimulus was presented for 100 ms, followed by a 75 ms inter-stimulus interval (ISI)32. Although the ISI duration was fixed, the specific sequence of illumination for regions and characters was fully randomized across trials. This randomization prevents the user from predicting the exact timing of the target stimulus, thereby minimizing the potential contamination of event-related potentials by anticipatory readiness potentials.

Data acquisition

EEG data were recorded at a sampling rate of 250Hz using an actiCHamp device (Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany) across 32 channels arranged according to the international 10–20 system. The ‘Fz’ electrode served as the reference, and the ground electrode was placed on the forehead. Impedances were maintained below \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5\,\textrm{k}\Omega$$\end{document} for all electrodes. The electrode montage is depicted in Fig. 2. All experimental sessions were conducted in a quiet, dimly lit room. Participants sat comfortably in a chair at a distance of approximately 1m from a 1920\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1080 resolution LED screen. All were right-handed and free of known neurological or mental health conditions. Sessions for each participant were scheduled at the same time each day to control for circadian effects. To minimize physiological confounds, participants were instructed not to consume caffeine-containing substances (e.g., coffee, tea, soft drinks, chocolate) for at least 8 hours before the EEG session, aligning with standard clinical recommendations. Additionally, they were asked to abstain from smoking and consuming food or beverages for at least 3 hours prior to data collection. These restrictions ensure stable baseline neural states and reduce artifacts that could affect EEG signal quality33. In total, EEG data were collected from 15 healthy participants, consisting of 8 men (mean age 20 ± 1.5 years) and 7 women (mean age 19 ± 2.1 years). In our study, a total of 60 targets were presented to each participant during the experiments, consisting of 30 target regions and 30 target characters, and each region and character was flashed 8 times in total.Fig. 2Setup of 32ch EEG electrodes.

Experimental procedure

Time-locked EEG recording plays a critical role in event-related potential (ERP) analysis. Identifying the exact millisecond at which a stimulus occurs and observing the subsequent neural response allows researchers to determine how the brain generates potentials that are specifically tied to external events. For this reason, acquiring EEG data that are precisely synchronized with stimuli is of vital importance. To ensure this precise synchronization, event triggers (markers) were transmitted directly from the stimulus presentation computer to the EEG amplifier via a standard Parallel Port (LPT) interface. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the BrainProducts actiCHamp device used in this study, in conjunction with this hardware triggering method, provides a stimulus delivery accuracy of approximately 1–2 ms, ensuring reliable temporal alignment between stimulus presentation and EEG data acquisition. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the experimental setup consisted of an EEG device, a stimulus computer, and a recording computer. The stimulus presentation computer displayed the 3D region-based paradigm and simultaneously generated event markers (e.g., indices of flashing rows or columns such as S1, S9, S5). These markers were sent to the EEG amplifier, where they were integrated with the ongoing EEG data stream. The combined data (EEG signals + event markers) were then transferred to the recording computer. This configuration ensured that both the brain responses and the precise timing of the corresponding stimuli were captured in a synchronized manner, providing high-quality time-locked data for subsequent ERP analysis.Fig. 3EEG data recording procedure.

EEG data recording procedure.

EEG data processing

This study received ethics approval from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Ataturk University (Approval No: 8-50), and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Prior to the EEG recordings, all participants signed a consent form approved by the committee, and informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). EEG data were first subjected to a pre-processing stage in which continuous recordings were band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 30 Hz. This specific frequency range was selected to prioritize classification accuracy by reducing background noise, consistent with previous BCI studies that have demonstrated effective performance using similar filtering parameters34,35. Event markers recorded simultaneously with the visual stimulation paradigm were then parsed, and each event code was converted into a numeric stimulus index to enable accurate identification of flashes at both the region and character levels. After this step, the data were epoched relative to stimulus onset to obtain time-locked trials suitable for event-related potential analysis. Each epoch spanned from -200 ms before to +1000 ms after stimulus onset, corresponding to 50 pre-stimulus and 250 post-stimulus samples at the acquisition rate of 250 Hz, for a total of 300 samples per epoch. In the paradigm, each region and each character was flashed 8 times. The extraction procedure was therefore carried out in two stages to reflect this hierarchical structure. In the first stage, region-level flashes were segmented, and those matching the predefined target region were labeled as target, while all others were labeled as non-target. Since each of the four regions was flashed 8 times, a total of 32 flashes per cycle were obtained (4 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 8 = 32). In the second stage, character-level flashes within the selected region were isolated; epochs were labeled as target if the stimulus index matched the target character and as non-target otherwise. Because each of the eight characters was flashed 8 times, this stage resulted in 64 flashes per cycle (8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 8 = 64). As a result, two separate datasets–target and non-target–were obtained, each organized in three-dimensional form (channels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 300 samples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} trials). The classification stage was then carried out on the basis of these two datasets. To correct for slow baseline drifts, all epochs underwent baseline adjustment using the pre-stimulus interval from -200 to 0 ms. The mean amplitude within this window was calculated for each channel and subtracted from the entire epoch, thereby normalizing the data around zero and improving comparability across trials. Through this systematic processing pipeline, EEG signals and stimulus markers were precisely synchronized at both the region and character levels, yielding clean, time-locked data for subsequent ERP analysis and classification.

Experimental framework

In the proposed study, classification of EEG signals was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of the region- and character-based paradigm. A total of 15 participants took part in the experiments, each of whom was presented with 30 target regions and 30 target characters, yielding a total of 60 targets per participant. Each region and character were flashed eight times in a single run, resulting in 32 region-level and 64 character-level flashes per cycle.

The experiments were conducted under two different scenarios: subject-dependent and subject-independent analysis. In the subject-dependent case, classification was performed separately for each participant, and the distinction between target and non-target trials was evaluated at both the region and character levels. To examine the effect of electrode selection, classification was carried out using individual channels as well as predefined channel groups. The individual electrode set consisted of 15 channels (C3, Cp5, Cp1, Pz, P3, P7, O1, Oz, O2, P4, P8, Cp6, Cp2, Cz, C4). Based on their anatomical locations and established neurophysiological evidence regarding visual P300 generators34,37, these electrodes were organized into five groups: Central electrodes (C3, Cz, C4, Cp1, Cp2, Cp5, Cp6), Parietal electrodes (P3, P4, P7, P8, Pz), Occipital electrodes (O1, Oz, O2), Parietal + Occipital electrodes, and Central + Parietal + Occipital electrodes. The inclusion of the combined Parietal + Occipital group (Group 4) was specifically driven by BCI literature identifying this posterior region as the most informative for visual paradigms34. In total, 15 individual electrodes were used in this study, and the groups formed from these electrodes are illustrated with different colors in Fig. 4. Importantly, when forming these groups, the ERP signals from each electrode were concatenated side by side rather than averaged, creating a new group dataset that preserved the full information from all included channels. For example, while a single channel produced a 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}300 vector, combining two channels resulted in a 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}600 vector. In this way, classification performance was assessed at both the individual and combined electrode levels.Fig. 4Electrode layout and grouping of the 15 selected EEG channels.

Electrode layout and grouping of the 15 selected EEG channels.

In the subject-independent case, all EEG segments obtained from the participants were pooled to form two global datasets: one containing all target epochs and the other containing all non-target epochs. For this setting, classification was performed using a leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) approach. In each fold, data from one subject were left out for testing, while the data from the remaining participants were used for training. This scheme ensured that the classification model generalized across participants and provided results independent of individual-specific features. Both single-channel and grouped-channel analyses were repeated in this scenario as well, allowing comparison with subject-dependent outcomes.

Performance was primarily evaluated using the classification accuracy metric, which is defined as the proportion of correctly classified epochs (target or non-target) to the total number of epochs, as shown in Eq. 3. All results are presented in terms of detection accuracy for both target regions and target characters, under subject-dependent and subject-independent conditions. An example radar plot used in this study is illustrated in Fig. 5. In these plots, the vertices (axes) correspond to individual EEG channels, arranged sequentially (e.g., from Ch1 to Ch9 in the example figure). Concentric polygons represent increasing levels of classification accuracy, starting from 50% at the innermost circle and extending to 100% at the outermost circle, with increments of 10%. Each vertex shows the classification accuracy achieved by the corresponding channel, thereby allowing direct comparison across electrodes. For instance, while Ch1 achieved a test accuracy of 60%, Ch9 reached 100% accuracy in the example figure.

In the presented results, radar plots are used not only to display the performance of single electrodes but also to represent predefined electrode groups (Group 1 to Group 5). By placing individual and group accuracies around the circumference of the plot, the reader can visually identify which channels or channel combinations contribute most effectively to classification performance. This visualization highlights the spatial distribution of discriminative neural activity, making it easier to observe the relative contribution of Central, Parietal, and Occipital regions to the decoding process.

The key advantage of radar plots lies in their ability to convey multidimensional performance metrics in a compact, intuitive manner. Instead of examining tabular accuracy scores, the radar shape itself provides a holistic view of performance differences across electrodes and regions. The emergence of distinct patterns within these plots enables researchers to immediately identify the most informative brain regions and electrode groups, as well as to evaluate consistency across participants. Consequently, radar plots serve as an effective tool for both summarizing and interpreting EEG-based classification results.

Results

For the analysis, stimulus-locked EEG segments were baseline-corrected and averaged across repetitions to obtain event-related potentials (ERPs) for each target and non-target condition at both region- and character-level flashes. These ERP representations served as the basis for classification, which was performed first on individual channels and predefined electrode groups to quantify spatial contributions. Results were initially visualized through subject-specific radar plots, providing a comparative overview of channel and group performance. Subsequently, generalization across participants was evaluated using a leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation scheme, enabling subject-independent performance assessment.

Figure 6 presents the subject-specific classification results obtained from radar plot visualizations. In these results, each vertex corresponds to either a single EEG channel or a predefined electrode group, while the radial axis indicates the classification accuracy values. This format allows the relative performance of individual channels and groups to be directly compared within the same figure. By organizing both single-channel and group-level accuracies in this way, Fig. 6 provides a clear overview of the spatial distribution of classification performance across subjects.

The results obtained from the radar plots generally indicate that, in addition to single electrodes, electrode groups provide a distinct advantage in classification performance. In particular, Group 4 (Parietal + Occipital) and Group 5 (Parietal + Occipital + Central) achieved the highest accuracy values. However, it is noteworthy that although Group 5 contains a larger number of electrodes, Group 4, with fewer electrodes, yielded a comparable level of classification accuracy. This finding suggests that the parietal and occipital regions play a critical role in target/non-target discrimination and that a higher number of electrodes does not necessarily provide additional benefits.Fig. 6Subject-specific radar plot classification results for single channels and electrode groups.

Subject-specific radar plot classification results for single channels and electrode groups.

Among single electrodes, channels located in the parietal and occipital regions, particularly Pz, P7, P8, O1, Oz, and O2 achieved the highest accuracy values. This result supports the notion that ERP responses elicited by visual stimuli are more prominent in these regions. In contrast, central areas (e.g., Cz, Cp1, Cp2, Cp5, Cp6) generally exhibited moderate performance. Therefore, it can be concluded that the best-performing single channels are those concentrated in the parietal–occipital areas.

Although similar trends were observed across participants, some individual differences emerged. For instance, in certain subjects (S2, S5, S10), the occipital regions demonstrated markedly stronger classification accuracy, while in others (S4, S5, S11), parietal channels were more dominant. Nonetheless, at the group level, consistently high performance of Group 4 and Group 5 across all participants stands out as a robust finding.Fig. 7LOSO classification accuracies across individual electrodes and predefined electrode groups.

LOSO classification accuracies across individual electrodes and predefined electrode groups.

The LOSO results (Fig. 7) demonstrate that, even under subject-independent generalization conditions, the parietal and occipital regions play a critical role in classification performance. At the single-channel level, electrodes such as P7, P8, O1, and O2 achieved the highest accuracy values (approximately 78–79%). This finding reconfirms that ERP responses elicited by visual stimuli are most prominent in these regions. In contrast, central areas (e.g., Cz, Cp1, Cp2, C3, C4) showed relatively lower accuracy values under LOSO conditions (around 70–73%), indicating that these channels provide more limited discriminative information in subject-independent generalization.

Predefined electrode groups provided a clear advantage compared to single channels. In particular, Group 4 (Parietal + Occipital) and Group 5 (Parietal + Occipital + Central) achieved the highest performance, with accuracies of approximately 89–91%. Notably, Group 4, despite including fewer electrodes, yielded nearly the same level of accuracy as Group 5. These finding highlights that the parietal–occipital combination offers highly discriminative features even under LOSO conditions. Meanwhile, Groups 1, 2, and 3 also performed better than single electrodes but remained below Groups 4 and 5.

These results indicate that, even when generalization is performed independently of individual differences, classification performance largely relies on electrodes located in the parietal and occipital regions. Moreover, while Group 5 achieved the highest overall accuracy due to its larger number of electrodes, Group 4 with fewer but strategically positioned electrodes achieved comparable accuracy, suggesting a practical advantage for electrode selection in real-world applications.

Conclusion

The present study introduced and systematically evaluated a novel three-dimensional region-based (3D-RB) speller paradigm. By integrating depth-enhanced visual effects and a hierarchical region–character structure, the paradigm was designed to strengthen user attention and elicit more discriminative neural responses. The results demonstrated that classification performance benefits not only from individual electrodes but also, and more prominently, from strategically grouped electrodes. In particular, parietal and occipital regions consistently yielded the highest accuracies, both in subject-dependent and subject-independent scenarios, highlighting their critical role in target/non-target discrimination. Notably, Group 4 (Parietal + Occipital) achieved comparable performance to Group 5 (Parietal + Occipital + Central) despite comprising fewer electrodes, suggesting that effective electrode selection may enhance both efficiency and practicality in real-world applications.

Although the proposed paradigm is inspired by visual oddball principles commonly used in P300-based BCIs, the present results indicate that condition-related differences are more prominently reflected in early and mid-latency ERP components rather than in a robust P300 response. In particular, systematic differences between target and non-target stimuli were observed in the P100 and N200 time ranges over parietal and occipital regions, whereas no statistically significant differences were detected in the classical P300 window. Accordingly, the proposed approach should be regarded as an ERP-based discrimination framework that does not rely exclusively on the presence of a strong P300 component.

Although a classical P300 component was not prominently observed in the present study, this finding is consistent with literature indicating that early and mid-latency ERP components can play an important role in visual attention and target discrimination processes, particularly under complex stimulus conditions. The P100 component has been widely associated with early sensory processing and spatial attention allocation, reflecting enhanced perceptual gain for attended stimuli43,44. Similarly, the N200 component has been linked to attentional selection, stimulus discrimination, and early cognitive evaluation processes44,45. In paradigms involving structured or spatially distributed visual stimuli, neural differentiation between target and non-target conditions may emerge more strongly in these earlier components, while the classical P300 response may be reduced or less consistent46. In this context, the present findings suggest that the proposed 3D region-based paradigm may facilitate earlier-stage perceptual and attentional processing, leading to discriminative neural responses in the P100 and N200 time windows rather than relying on a robust late P300 component.

Overall, this study provides original evidence that incorporating three-dimensional visual effects into a region-based speller paradigm supports system performance. Although the proposed paradigm introduces a structured interaction design, the present study does not include a quantitative evaluation of user experience, usability, or subjective workload. Future studies should incorporate standardized usability metrics and cognitive load assessments to better understand the user-friendly aspects of the proposed system. Although the proposed two-stage paradigm improves classification accuracy, it may introduce additional time overhead due to sequential region and character selection steps. Consequently, this structure could potentially affect the overall information transfer rate (ITR).

From a cognitive perspective, the hierarchical 3D structure may contribute to reducing perceptual interference (i.e., the crowding effect) and facilitating attentional allocation, potentially lowering the perceptual load required for target selection. Beyond methodological contributions, these findings point toward practical implications for future BCI speller designs, particularly in optimizing electrode placement and leveraging perceptual salience to achieve more efficient and user-friendly communication systems.
