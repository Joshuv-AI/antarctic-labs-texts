TRIML2 promotes malignant progression of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma via canonical Wnt signaling and tumor immune escape
Authors: Xi Luo, Yang Zhang, Yingjian Wang, Huike Wang, Dao Xin, Lulu Guan, Zhe Wang, Pei Wang, Feng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe roles and underlying mechanisms of tripartite motif family like 2 (TRIML2) in tumors, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), remain poorly characterized. This study aimed to comprehensively characterize the significance of TRIML2 in HNSC using multi-omics analyses and experimental validation.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes in HNSC were screened from TCGA and GEO datasets, and TRIML2 was identified as a hub gene for further investigation. Its expression patterns, diagnostic and prognostic value, associations with clinicopathological features, drug sensitivity, and immune infiltration were systematically analyzed. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis were performed to explore TRIML2-related signaling pathways. The biological function and molecular mechanisms of TRIML2 were further validated through in vitro and in vivo experiments.ResultsTRIML2 was significantly upregulated in HNSC, and correlated with worse overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS) and disease-free survival (DFS). High TRIML2 expression was associated with advanced tumor stage, distant metastasis, increased infiltration of immunosuppressive cells, elevated expression of immunosuppressive molecules, and activation of multiple oncogenic pathways. Silencing TRIML2 inhibited cell proliferation, migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), canonical Wnt pathway activity, immunosuppressive molecule expression and tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, whereas TRIML2 overexpression produced the opposite effects.ConclusionsTRIML2 promotes malignant progression of HNSC by activating the canonical Wnt pathway and facilitating tumor immune escape. TRIML2 represents a promising biomarker for diagnosis, immunotherapy, and prognosis in HNSC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07274-9.

Introduction

Head and neck cancers are a heterogeneous group of tumors arising from the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx [1]. In 2020, they ranked as the seventh most common cancer worldwide and imposed a substantial economic burden on patients and society [2]. Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) accounts for more than 90% of these cases [3]. Major risk factors include genetic predisposition, unhealthy lifestyle habits, and human papillomavirus infection [4–7]. Recently, multidisciplinary treatment has been recommended for advanced HNSC [8–10], yet recurrence and metastasis remain major challenges [11]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for reliable biomarkers to improve diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis in HNSC.

The tripartite motif (TRIM) family participates in diverse biological processes, and its dysregulation has been linked to tumorigenesis, immune disorders, and developmental abnormalities. Seventy-seven TRIM proteins have been identified in humans [12]. Tripartite motif family like 2 (TRIML2), a TRIM family member, plays essential roles in both physiological and pathological contexts. Under normal conditions, TRIML2 is highly expressed in placental disc, pre-implantation embryos and chorion tissues [13]. In addition, Elevated TRIML2 expression has been associated with susceptibility to Alzheimer’s disease and polycystic ovary syndrome [14, 15], has been observed in breast cancer cell lines [16]. Ubiquitinated TRIML2 was implicated in the activation of human oncogenic herpesvirus [17]. Liu et al. developed a pyroptosis-related model incorporating TRIML2 to evaluate the immune status and survival in HNSC [18]. Nevertheless, the precise role of TRIML2 as a biomarker for diagnosis, treatment and prognosis of HNSC remains unclear.

In this study, we performed multi-omics analyses and experimental validation to elucidate the significance of TRIML2 in HNSC. We found that high TRIML2 expression was correlated with adverse clinicopathological features, poor prognosis, altered drug sensitivity, and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Single‑cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed predominant TRIML2 expression in malignant epithelial cells of HNSC. Functional experiments showed that TRIML2 knockdown inhibited malignant behaviors, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), canonical Wnt pathway activation, immunosuppressive molecule expression, and tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, whereas TRIML2 overexpression produced opposite effects. Together, these findings identify TRIML2 as an oncogenic driver in HNSC and a potential biomarker for diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic targeting.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data and corresponding clinicopathological information of HNSC patients were obtained from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.com/) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The raw data were standardized prior to analysis. Single-cell RNA sequencing data (scRNA-seq, GSE188737, GSE181919) and spatial transcriptomic data (GSE208253) were also retrieved from GEO.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

TRIML2 expression was assessed across 33 tumor types and matched normal tissues using TIMER2 (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) and the Human Protein Atlas (HPA, https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median TRIML2 level. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to evaluate the prognostic value of TRIML2 in HNSC. Cox regression analysis and a nomogram model were applied to further clarify its prognostic significance.

The abundance of immune cell infiltration and immunoregulatory molecules was compared between groups using the ESTIMATE package in R (v4.4.1). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to calculate correlation coefficients. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between TRIML2-high and TRIML2-low groups were identified using the limma package in R (v4.4.1). Functional enrichment was performed with Gene Ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database (http://string-db.org/).

Single-cell transcriptome analysis

The scRNA-seq data were processed using the Seurat R package (v5.1.0). Quality control included: (a) excluding genes expressed in fewer than three cells, (b) removing cells with fewer than 50 detected genes, and (c) filtering cells with ≥5% mitochondrial gene expression. Data were normalized using the SCTransform method, and batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm (v1.2.1) [19]. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were then conducted, and results were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (Umap).

Spatial transcriptome analysis

The spatial transcriptomics data were processed and visualized using the Seurat [20, 21]. Data standardization, dimensionality reduction and clustering have been carried out. SpatialDimPlot and SpatialFeaturePlot were used together to illustrate gene expression levels and cell distributions.

Cell line and cell culture

Two human oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines (CAL27, SCC25) were selected for the subsequent experiments. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Invigentech, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin liquid (NCM Biotech, China). Cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO₂.

Cell transfection

When the cell density reached approximately 70%, CAL27 and SCC25 cells were transfected with siTRIML2 (Sangon Biotech, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blot and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR).

RT-qPCR analysis

According to the instructions of Trizol reagent (TaKaRa, Japan), we extracted the RNA from CAL27 and SCC25 cell lines. CCK-8 assay

CAL27 and SCC25 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (4 × 103 cells/well, Corning, USA). At the indicated time points, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (NCM Biotech, China) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Wound-healing assay

Cells were plated in 6-well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 90% confluence. A linear scratch was made using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, followed by treatment with serum-free DMEM. Wound closure was photographed at multiple time points under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the migrating rate was quantified.

Transwell assay

Transwell chambers were coated with 100 µL of diluted Matrigel (1:6) and seeded with 4 × 104 cells/well. DMEM medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (700 µL/well) was added to the lower chamber. After incubation, cells that invaded to the lower surface were fixed, stained, and captured under an upright microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from CAL27 and SCC25 cell 72 h after siRNA transfection. Equal amounts of protein (≥30 μg) was separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The PVDF membranes were blocked in 5% skimmed milk for 1.5 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibodies: GAPDH (1:8000, Proteintech), N-cadherin (1:2000, Affinity), TRIML2 (1:500, Proteintech), E-cadherin (1:2000, Affinity), MMP2 (1:2000, Affinity), c-Myc (1:500, ZEN-bioscience), total β-catenin (1:500, ZEN-bioscience), active β-catenin (1:1000, Abcam), Cylin D1 (1:1000, Abcam), and Axin2 (1:1000, ZEN-bioscience). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:10,000, Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL detection reagent (Affinity Biosciences, China) and imaged with the AI600 imaging system (GE Healthcare, USA).

EdU proliferation assay

The EdU assay was performed using the BeyoClick™ EdU-594 Cell Proliferation Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. CAL27 and SCC25 cells (2 × 104/well) were seeded on 15 × 15 mm coverslips and transfected with siNC or siTRIML2 for 48 h. Cells were incubated with 20 μM EdU solution for 2 h at 37 °C, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min at 37 °C. Click additive solution was applied for 30 min at 37 °C, and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min. Images were captured under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded and sectioned. Tumor sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval and blocked for endogenous peroxidase activity. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TRIML2 (1:200, Proteintech), Ki67 (1:1000, Huabio), E-cadherin (1:100, Affinity), N-cadherin (1:100, Affinity), β-catenin (1:100, ZEN-bioscience), PD-L1 (1:200, Abcam), followed by a biotin-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at 37 °C. Staining was developed using diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution and counterstained with hematoxylin (ZSGB-BIO, China). Images were acquired under an upright microscope (Olympus, Japan).

TUNEL cell apoptosis assay

TUNEL staining was performed using the TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated with 20 μg/mL proteinase K (without DNase) for 20 min at 37 °C. Sections were then treated with TUNEL working solution for 1 h at 37 °C, washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) to detect apoptotic cells.

Mouse xenograft model

CAL27 cells stably expressing shCtrl or shTRIML2 (Vector or TRIML2) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 4-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (1 × 106 cells/100 μL per mouse). Tumor size was measured at predefined intervals, and volume was calculated as (length × width2)/2. Tumors were excised 21 days after injection for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate. Data were analyzed using R software (v4.4.1) and GraphPad Prism (v10.0). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We first assessed TRIML2 expression across 33 tumor types and matched normal tissues using TIMER2. TRIML2 was significantly upregulated in multiple malignancies, including HNSC, lung squamous cell carcinoma, breast invasive carcinoma, and kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (Fig. 1B). Immunohistochemistry revealed consistently higher TRIML2 expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 1D) Moreover, receiver operator characteristic curve (ROC) analysis indicated good diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.85) (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The proportions of T cells (30.01%), fibroblasts (26.06%), and malignant cells (12.22%) were the most abundant cell types (Fig. 2B). The heat map displayed the expression levels of the top 8 markers (Fig. 2C). The distribution of 10 cellular subpopulations varied considerably between individuals, suggesting substantial heterogeneity in HNSC (Fig. 2D, E). Subsequently, we analyzed the distribution pattern of TRIML2 in different cell types. Our results demonstrated that TRIML2 was barely expressed in other cell types, with the exception of malignant epithelial cells in HNSC (Fig. 2F). Umap scatter plots revealed 13 annotated cellular subpopulations in HNSC (Fig. 2G). The distribution of the 13 cell types exhibited considerable variability among HNSC patients (Fig. 2H). The correlation between TRIML2 expression and drug efficacy

Spearman correlation analysis revealed that TRIML2 expression was positively associated with resistance to Axitinib, Navitoclax, and Vorinostat (Fig. 5C–E), while negatively correlated with the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of Docetaxel, Gefitinib, and Vinblastine (Fig. 5F–H). These results suggest that TRIML2 may be useful for guiding drug selection and personalized therapy in HNSC.

The relationship between TRIML2 expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cells

The tumor immune microenvironment plays a critical role in tumorigenesis, progression, and treatment resistance. To investigate the association between TRIML2 expression and immune regulation in HNSC, we compared tumor-infiltrating immune cell profiles between TRIML2-high and TRIML2-low expression groups using TCGA data. Patients with high TRIML2 expression showed significantly higher stromal and immune scores (Fig. 6A), but lower infiltration of several anti-tumor immune cell subsets, including CD8+ T cells, follicular helper T cells, naïve B cells, plasma cells, and mast cells (Fig. 6B). Discussion

In the era of precision medicine, the search for accurate biomarkers is important for the early diagnosis, personalized treatment and prognosis of tumors, including HNSC. A large body of research has validated the clinical value of tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability, PD-L1 levels, integrin family (ITGA3, ITGA5, ITGA6) and RNA N6-methyladenosine modification in HNSC [22–24]. The TRIM family is a group of proteins consisting of the highly conserved RING domain [12]. Dysregulation of TRIM proteins has been found to play a crucial role in several tumors. Herquel et al. found that the combination of TRIM33 knockdown and TRIM24 depletion resulted in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma [25]. Geng et al. demonstrated that TRIM59 is a critical oncoprotein in lung cancer [26]. TRIML2 is a novel subtype lacking the RING domain, and its function and exact mechanisms in HNSC remain elusive [27].

In this study, we focused on TRIML2 and demonstrated its tremendous potential in the diagnosis, personalized treatment and prognosis of HNSC. We revealed that TRIML2 was specifically expressed in malignant epithelial cells of HNSC through a comprehensive analysis of single‑cell and spatial transcriptomics data. Furthermore, we observed higher levels of TRIML2 in primary HNSC samples than in metastatic HNSC samples. Meanwhile, TRIML2 displayed outstanding diagnostic ability in HNSC. We also reported that TRIML2 levels were significantly higher in HNSC and were associated with advanced T stage, M stage, lower tumor differentiation, and unfavorable survival outcomes (OS, PFS and DFS), suggesting its leading role in the initiation and development of HNSC. In addition, we validated the independent prognostic value of TRIML2 in HNSC by using a Cox regression model analysis. In conclusion, this study has identified a unique role of TRIML2 oncogene in the diagnosis and prognosis of HNSC.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) comprises tumor cells, endothelial cells, extracellular matrix, and various immune cells, such as tumor-associated macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts [28, 29]. The composition and functional state of TME exert complex effects on tumor initiation, progression and immune response in HNSC [30, 31]. A large number of clinical trials have manifested that immunotherapy is one of the most effective anti-tumor treatments [32]. Recently, the blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis has demonstrated a well-established efficacy in treating a range of cancers, including HNSC. In the first-line treatment, a significantly longer OS was observed in the pembrolizumab group compared to the chemotherapy group (12.30 versus 10.30 months, p = 0.009) [33]. The anti-tumor activity of pembrolizumab in combination with cetuximab was confirmed in patients with recurrent or metastatic HNSC [34]. However, immunotherapy is only effective in a minority of HNSC patients. Therefore, novel biomarkers are needed to develop to identify potential populations likely to benefit from immunotherapy, as well as to provide new targets for combination immunotherapy.

In our study, we analyzed the association between TRIML2 expression levels and TME in HNSC. Our results indicated that TRIML2 expression was negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, follicular helper T cells, naïve B cells, plasma cells and mast cells, which are important components of the innate and adaptive immunity [35, 36]. Conversely, HNSC patients with high TRIML2 expression tended to have higher levels of regulatory T cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, CD274 (PD-L1), PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2), TGFB1 and IL-10. Our results demonstrated that overexpression of TRIML2 increased the expression of immunosuppressive molecules, including PD-L1, TGFB1, and IL-10, in HNSC cell lines. Conversely, TRIML2 knockdown exerted opposite effects. Taken together, our results suggest that TRIML2 plays a crucial role in promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment and tumor immune escape in HNSC. Therefore, we hypothesize that the knockdown of TRIML2 would reverse the immunosuppressive state and increase the sensitivity to immunotherapy in HNSC.

In our study, GO and GSEA analysis revealed a significant association between TRIML2 and the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, which contributes to tumor development, metastasis and drug resistance [37]. Multiple biological processes, including mesenchymal stem cell differentiation, cytokinesis, organ growth, as well as the biogenesis of ribonucleoprotein complexes were also significantly enriched [38]. DPPA2 and TRIM15 were identified by PPI network analysis and were found to be upregulated in HNSC samples compared to normal samples. DPPA2, a stem cell marker, was overexpressed in colorectal cancer and correlated with advanced tumor stage [39]. TRIM15 has been shown to drive the progression of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [40]. Our results indicated that TRIML2 collaborated with its hub genes to promote HNSC progression. The ability to sustain proliferation, invasion, and metastasis is an important hallmark of cancer [41]. TRIML2 knockdown inhibited the proliferation, migration, invasion, EMT and the activity of canonical Wnt signaling pathway in HNSC cell lines. Unsurprisingly, the tumor volume and weight were significantly decreased in the shTRIML2 group in nude mice. TRIML2 overexpression exerted the opposite effects in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, TRIML2 plays a dominant role in HNSC progression by activating the canonical Wnt pathway and facilitating immune escape.

Although the research has demonstrated the multifaceted roles of TRIML2 in HNSC, there are still some shortcomings. Firstly, the expression levels, diagnostic and prognostic value of TRIML2 in HNSC were concluded via public TCGA and GEO databases. The expression levels and prognostic value of TRIML2 in OSCC were further validated using fewer clinical samples. More clinical samples are urgently needed to improve the reliability of our findings. Secondly, the TME of HNSC was modulated by TRIML2, but the exact mechanism is not fully understood in our study. Given the complexity of TME and current time constraints, we intend to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which TRIML2 modulates TME in our future work. This reflects our high priority on this issue and the continuity of our research.

Overall, based on bulk RAN-seq, single‑cell and spatial transcriptomics data analysis and experimental validation, our results indicate that TRIML2 drives the malignant progression of HNSC through activating the canonical Wnt pathway and facilitating tumor immune escape. These findings provide the theoretical basis for the clinical applications of TRIML2 in the diagnosis, immunotherapy and prognosis of HNSC. Moreover, studies with larger clinical samples are needed to further confirm our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study reveals that TRIML2 drives the malignant progression of HNSC through activating the canonical Wnt pathway and facilitating tumor immune escape, suggesting the potential of TRIML2 in the diagnosis, immunotherapy and prognosis of HNSC. Further experiments are required to validate the intricate mechanisms of TRIML2 in HNSC.
