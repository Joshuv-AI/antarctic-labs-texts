A 4-point theorem: still another variation on an old theme
Serge Tabachnikov

Abstract




The subject of this article belongs to a “neighborhood” of the 4-vertex theorem that, in the simplest form, states that the curvature of a plane oval (a smooth closed curve with positive curvature) has at least four critical points. Since its publication by S. Mukhopadhyaya in 1909, this result and its ramifications have generated a vast literature. We give but one reference, Lecture 10 of
[
4
]
.

In what follows, we freely use basic facts of elementary differential geometry of the sphere and the hyperbolic plane; we omit references to numerous textbooks on the subject.

Our starting point is a result of W. Graustein
[
5
]
.

Theorem 1

The average curvature of a plane oval is attained at least at four points.

This implies the 4-vertex theorem: by Rolle’s theorem, there is an extremum of the curvature between every two such points.

Let
s
be the arc length parameter on the positively oriented oval
\gamma
,
k(s)
be the curvature,
R(s)=\frac{1}{k(s)}
be the curvature radius,
L
and
A
be the length and the area bounded by the curve. Then the total curvature is
\int k(s)\,ds=2\pi
, and the average curvature is
\bar{k}=\frac{2\pi}{L}
.

I shall present two proofs of Graustein’s result in the Euclidean plane, starting with his own, and then discuss the extensions of this theorem to the spherical and hyperbolic geometries.

Proof by Sturm-Hurwitz theorem
[Graustein]. Let
\alpha(s)
be the direction of the tangent line of
\gamma
. Then
\gamma^{\prime}(s)=(\cos\alpha(s),\sin\alpha(s))
and
k(s)=\frac{d\alpha(s)}{ds}
or, equivalently,
ds=R\,d\alpha
. One can use
\alpha
to parameterize
\gamma
.

Since the curve is closed,

\int_{0}^{L}(\cos\alpha(s),\sin\alpha(s))\ ds=0\ \ \ {\rm or}\ \ \ \int_{0}^{2\pi}R(\alpha)(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha(s))\ d\alpha=0.

Hence the
2\pi
-periodic function
R(\alpha)
is
L_{2}
-orthogonal to
\cos\alpha
and
\sin\alpha
.

Let
\bar{R}
be the average value of
R(\alpha)
:

\bar{R}=\frac{\int_{0}^{2\pi}R(\alpha)\ d\alpha}{2\pi}=\frac{\int_{0}^{L}dt}{2\pi}=\frac{L}{2\pi}.

Then the function
R(\alpha)-\bar{R}
is also
L_{2}
-orthogonal to constants.

To conclude the proof, invoke the Sturm-Hurwitz theorem:
The number of sign changes of a
2\pi
-periodic function is not less than the number of sign changes of the first nontrivial harmonic in its Fourier expansion
(see, e.g.,
[
7
]
, Appendix 8.1, for five different proofs). The Fourier expansion of the function
R(\alpha)-\bar{R}
is free from the constant term and the first harmonics, hence this function has at least four zeros, the number of roots of the second harmonics.

That is,
R
assumes the value
\bar{R}=\frac{L}{2\pi}
at least four times, and hence the curvature
k=\frac{1}{R}
also assumes the value
\bar{k}=\frac{2\pi}{L}
at least four times.

Proofs by wave propagation
. Consider the oval
\gamma
as a source of light, and let
\gamma_{t}
be the time-
t
wavefront,
the set of points reached by light at time
t
. We coorient
{\gamma}
in the outward direction; then, for
t>0
, every point of
{\gamma}
is moving with the unit speed along the outer normal ray to the curve, and for
t<0
, the points move along the normal lines inside the curve
{\gamma}
. The wavefronts
{\gamma}_{t}
are equidistant from
{\gamma}={\gamma}_{0}
at distance
|t|
.

For positive values of
t
, the curves
{\gamma}_{t}
remain smooth and convex, but for negative
t
they start to develop singularities, generically semicubical cusps, as can be seen in Figure
1
borrowed from
[
4
]
.

Figure 1:
Propagation of light inside an oval. Eventually, the curve inverts outside in.

We parameterize the equidistant curves
{\gamma}_{t}
by the same parameter
s
:

{\gamma}_{t}(s)={\gamma}(s)+tJ{\gamma}^{\prime}(s),

where
J
is the clockwise rotation by
90^{\circ}
and hence
J{\gamma}^{\prime}(s)
is the unit outward normal.

Let
k_{t},R_{t},L_{t},A_{t}
and
\bar{k}_{t}
be the curvature, curvature radius, perimeter, area, and the average curvature of the curve
{\gamma}_{t}
. The dependences of these quantities on
t
are as follows:

R_{t}(s)=R(s)+t,\ \ k_{t}(s)=\frac{k(s)}{1+tk(s)},\ \ L_{t}=L+2\pi t,\ \ A_{t}=A+Lt+\pi t^{2}.

(1)

The first formula in (
1
) follows from the Huygens principle, according to which the tangent elements of
{\gamma}
move along the normals and remain tangent to the wavefronts
{\gamma}_{t}
. The second formula follows from the first one.
The formulas for the length and area of
{\gamma}_{t}
are classical and due to J. Steiner
[
9
]
. For polygons, they are illustrated in Figure
2
. A proof also follows from the obvious formulas

\frac{dL_{t}}{dt}=\int k_{t}(s)\ ds=2\pi,\ \ \frac{dA_{t}}{dt}=L_{t}.

(2)

Figure 2:
Steiner’s formulas for a polygon: the total length of the black segments is
L
, and the red arcs comprise a circle of radius
t
.

For curves with cusps, the length is signed: the sign changes after traversing a cusps. Note also that the winding number of the wave fronts
{\gamma}_{t}
(the number of turns of its tangent line) is 1 for all
t
.

Remark 1

It is worth mentioning that the non-negative isoperimetric defect
L^{2}-4\pi A
remains constant in the equidistant family:

L_{t}^{2}-4\pi A_{t}=(L+2\pi t)^{2}-4\pi(A+Lt+\pi t^{2})=L^{2}-4\pi A.

The next lemma says that the points of an oval where the curvature equals the average curvature propagate in the equidistant family.

Lemma 2

If
k(s)=\bar{k}
, then
k_{t}(s)=\bar{k}_{t}
for all
t
.

Proof.

Note that

\bar{k}_{t}=\frac{2\pi}{L_{t}}=\frac{2\pi}{L+2\pi t}.

Hence if

k(s)=\frac{2\pi}{L}
, then

k_{t}(s)=\frac{k(s)}{1+tk(s)}=\frac{2\pi}{L+2\pi t}=\bar{k}_{t},

as needed.

\Box

To prove Theorem
1
, consider the wavefront
{\gamma}_{t}
with

t=-\frac{1}{\bar{k}}=-\frac{L}{2\pi}.

The curvature at a point
{\gamma}(s)
equals
\bar{k}
if and only if the curvature of the respective point of
{\gamma}_{t}(s)
is infinite, that is, this point is a cusp. Thus we need to show that
{\gamma}_{t}
has at least four cusps. Two cusps are “free”: the average curvature must be attained at least twice. Working toward a contradiction, assume that there are only two cusps.

By formulas (
1
),
L_{t}=0
. Note that the wave fronts are locally convex, that is, free from inflections: the curvature radius
R_{t}(s)=R(s)+t
is never infinite. Thus the wave front
{\gamma}_{t}
has the winding number 1 and does not have inflection points. Such a front is diffeomorphic to the one in Figure
3
and, due to the triangle inequality, its length does not vanish. This is a contradiction.

Figure 3:
This front cannot have zero length.

Perhaps the claim that the curve in Figure
3
cannot have zero signed length needs an explanation. It is consequence of the following well known result.

Lemma 3

Let
U
be a domain with piecewise smooth boundary
\Gamma
, and
\gamma
a closed convex curve strictly inside
U
.
Then the length of
\Gamma
is greater than that of
{\gamma}
.

Proof.

One way of proving this claim is to approximate
{\gamma}
by a convex polygon and use the triangle inequality, as illustrated in Figure
4
.
In this figure, one has

\left\{\;\begin{aligned} |AP|+|\Gamma_{PQ}|>|AB|+|BQ|,\\
|BQ|+|\Gamma_{QR}|>|BC|+|CR|,\\
|QR|+|\Gamma_{RS}|>|CD|+|DS|,\\
|DS|+|\Gamma_{SP}|>|DA|+|AP|.\end{aligned}\right.

Adding these inequalities and collecting terms yields
|\Gamma|>|{\gamma}|
.

\Box

The length of the curve
\Gamma
is greater than the perimeter of the polygon
ABCD
.

A more conceptual proof is by applying the Cauchy-Crofton formula; see, e.g., Lecture 19 of

In the spherical geometry
. The wave propagation argument works in this case as well. We have a (geodesically) convex curve
{\gamma}(s)
on the unit sphere, parameterized by the arc length and cooriented outwards. Such a curve lies in an open hemisphere. Consider the family of equidistant curves
{\gamma}_{t}(s)
.

We need spherical versions of the equations (
1
):

\displaystyle R_{t}(s)=R(s)+t

\displaystyle,\ \ k_{t}(s)=\cot R_{t}(s)=\cot(R(s)+t),

(3)

\displaystyle L_{t}=L\cos t+(2\pi-A)\sin t

\displaystyle,\ \ A_{t}=2\pi+L\sin t-(2\pi-A)\cos t.

The second equation, relating the curvature to the curvature radius, is a standard formula of spherical geometry. The last two equations follow from the spherical analog of equations (
2
):

\frac{dL_{t}}{dt}=\int k_{t}(s)\ ds=2\pi-A,\ \ \frac{dA_{t}}{dt}=L_{t},

where the first equation is the Gauss-Bonnet formula. See
[
1
]
for generalizations.

Similarly to Remark
1
, one has

Remark 5

The spherical isoperimetric inequality reads

L^{2}\geq A(4\pi-A).

Like in the plane, the isoperimetric defect remains constant in the equidistant family:

L_{t}^{2}-A_{t}(4\pi-A_{t})
is independent of
t
.

According to the Gauss-Bonnet formula,
\bar{k}_{t}=\frac{2\pi-A_{t}}{L_{t}}
. Formulas (
3
) imply an analog of Lemma
2
:
If
k(s)=\bar{k}
, then
k_{t}(s)=\bar{k}_{t}
for all
t
(we omit this straightforward calculation).

Consider now the equdistant curve
{\gamma}_{t}
with

t=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2\pi-A}{L}\right).

Then
A_{t}=2\pi
, and
\bar{k}_{t}=0
. We need to show that this curve has at least four spherical inflection points.

We claim that the curve
{\gamma}_{t}
is smooth and embedded. Indeed, if
{\gamma}_{t}
has a cusp, then
k_{t}(s)=\infty
, and formulas (
3
) imply that
\cot t=-k(s)
. However
\cot t=\frac{L}{2\pi-A}>0
because
{\gamma}
is contained in a hemisphere. This is a contradiction.

Likewise, if the curve
{\gamma}_{t}
has self-intersections then, for some
t_{1}\leq t
, the curve
{\gamma}_{t_{1}}
developed a self-tangency. Note that
t_{1}\leq t<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Consider Figure
5
:
d
is the spherical diameter of
{\gamma}
(a geodesic chord perpendicular to the curve on both ends), and since
{\gamma}
is contained in a hemisphere,
d<\pi
. This implies that
t_{1}>\frac{\pi}{2}
, a contradiction.

Figure 5:
One has
2t_{1}+d=2\pi
, hence
t_{1}=\pi-\frac{d}{2}>\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Finally, a coda:
a closed embedded spherical curve that bisects the area has at least four inflection points
. This is V. Arnold’s “tennis ball theorem”, see
[

]
. The name is explained by Figure

. We note, in passing, that this theorem follows from a stronger earlier result of B. Serge:

Remark 6

It would be an omission not to mention the paper
[
6
]
by S. Jackson devoted to a detailed study of the geometry of spherical curves, in particular, of their vertices. Let us mention but one result that has a similar flavor to out topic:
the total torsion of a closed spherical curve, considered as a space curve, vanishes, and if the curve is simple, then its torsion vanishes at least four times (Section 6 of

). The geodesic convexity of the curve is not needed.

In the hyperbolic plane
. The formulas of spherical geometry have analogs in the hyperbolic plane: one replaces trigonometric functions by their hyperbolic counterparts and changes signs where needed. In particular, the hyperbolic analogs of formulas (
3
) are as follows:

\displaystyle R_{t}(s)=R(s)+t

\displaystyle,\ \ k_{t}(s)=\coth R_{t}(s)=\coth(R(s)+t),

(4)

\displaystyle L_{t}=L\cosh t+(2\pi+A)\sinh t

\displaystyle,\ \ A_{t}=-2\pi+L\sinh t+(2\pi+A)\cosh t.

The last two formulas follow from the hyperbolic analog of equations (
2
):

\frac{dL_{t}}{dt}=\int k_{t}(s)\ ds=2\pi+A,\ \ \frac{dA_{t}}{dt}=L_{t}.

We note that, as in Remarks
1
and
5
, the isoperimentric defect
L_{t}^{2}-A_{t}(4\pi+A_{t})
is independent of
t
. Note also that an analog of Lemma
2
holds in the hyperbolic setting as well.

This said, formulas (
4
) work only for
horocyclically convex
curves, that is, the curves whose curvature is everywhere greater than 1.

The reason is that, in the hyperbolic plane, a curve of a constant curvature
k
is not necessarily a circle. Indeed, if
k>1
, it is a circle, if
k=1
, it is a horocycle, a circle of infinite radius tangent to the circle at infinity, and if
k<1
, this is an equdistant curve, the locus of points at a fixed distance from a geodesic. Accordingly, at every point a smooth curve is approximated by a curve of constant curvature, but this is the osculating circle only if the curvature at this point is greater than 1.

So assume that our curve
{\gamma}
is horocyclically convex. Then the wave propagation proof works in the same way as in the Euclidean plane. Consider the projective (Beltrami-Cayley-Klein) model of
H^{2}
, represented by the unit disc, whose geodesics are its chords.

Consider the equidistant curve
{\gamma}_{t}
with

t=-\coth^{-1}\left(\frac{2\pi+A}{L}\right),

implying that
L_{t}=0
.

The curve
{\gamma}_{t}
is regularly homotopic to
{\gamma}
, so its winding number is 1. It is also free from inflections – this is where we use the horocyclic convexity of
{\gamma}
. Therefore it is diffeomorphic to the wavefront in Figure
3
and cannot have zero signed length.

And a counter-example
. The next example, suggested by A. Akopyan, shows that one cannot relax the assumption of horocyclic convexity.

Consider a semicircle of radius
r
in the hyperbolic plane and smooth it by replacing the corners by curves of a very large curvature and the diameter by a curve of a very small curvature, see Figure
7
.

Figure 7:
A rounded semicircle.

The average curvature of the semicircle is calculated using formulas (
4
):

\bar{k}=\frac{2\pi+A}{L}=\frac{\pi(1+\cosh r)}{2r+\pi\sinh r},

where
L
and
A
are the length of the semicircle and the area bounded by it.
At the same time, the curvature of the circle is
\coth r
.

If
r
is sufficiently large, then
\bar{k}<\coth r
(it is enough to have
r>1.386
). If the approximation of the semicircle is fine enough,
then the average curvature
\bar{k}
is attained on the nearly flat arc replacing the diameter, and one can arrange it to happen only twice.

Let me conclude with a question:
do the above 4-point results hold in the Euclidean and spherical geometries for simple closed, but not necessarily convex, curves?
