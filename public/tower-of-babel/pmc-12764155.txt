Iranian women’s perceptions of human papillomavirus and barriers to vaccination: a qualitative study based on the health belief model
Authors: Leila Alizadeh, Zohreh Keshavarz, Ahmad Shalbaf, Sohrab Iranpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a common sexually transmitted infection (STI) and the main cause of precancerous changes in the cervix. Despite the undeniable role of HPV vaccines in preventing cervical cancer, the vaccination rate has remained low. The perception of a disease can predict preventive behaviors such as vaccination. This qualitative study aimed to explore women’s perceptions of HPV and its vaccine.MethodsIn-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted in four focus groups (FG) with 23 eligible women (aged 15–45), who had no prior history of cervical cancer, HPV infection, and vaccination. Interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using a directed content analysis approach based on the Health Belief Model (HBM).ResultsPerceived sensitivity, the risk of HPV infection, benefits and barriers of HPV vaccination, cue to action, self-care, and new category named social support were the main categories that emerged. Two main concerns for women included worries about the virus leading to cancer as well as marital and sexual relationship tension in case the husband contracts an infection. Main barriers to vaccination included informational, psychological, socio-cultural, and economic barriers. Self-protection acts, stress management, and medical care were the women’s main perceptions of self-care and they expressed a need for informational, emotional, and financial support.ConclusionThe results of our study indicate that educating women and their families, improving people’s sexual health awareness, integrating the vaccine into the national vaccination program, and establishing sexual health counselling units at healthcare centers are essential actions for encouraging women to get vaccinated and preventing HPV infection and its related adverse outcomes such as cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25583-y.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Health belief model, Papillomavirus vaccines, Cervical neoplasm, Social stigma

Background

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a DNA tumor virus which commonly infects human cells of the epithelium through skin-to-skin contact or sexual contact [1]. Each year 46,711 new cases of cancer occur in the United States, 37,000 of which being related to HPV, where cervical cancer is the most common type of HPV-related cancer in women [2]. It is the fourth most commonly detected cancer and the fourth primary reason for cancer-related mortality among women. Approximately 604,000 new cases and 342,000 deaths worldwide were estimated in 2020 [3]. Although the incidence of cervical cancer in Iran is still moderately low (2.2 per 100,000 women annually), the mortality rate is high [4], and the incidence of HPV infection is growing [5, 6].

Over 200 subtypes of HPV have been identified [6, 7] and nearly all cervical cancers are attributable to high-risk HPV types, and approximately 70% of cervical cancer cases worldwide are caused by types 16 and 18 [8]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved Quadrivalent HPV vaccine (4vHPV) for use in females and males aged 9–26 years, since 2006 and 2009 respectively. Also, Nine-valent HPV vaccine (9vHPV) was licensed in 2014 [9, 10]. Due to the highly efficient vaccination, cervical cancer is widely regarded as almost entirely preventable cancer [3]. The effectiveness of HPV vaccination in preventing this cancer, primarily caused by high-risk HPV types such as HPV 16 and HPV 18, has been well-documented. Additionally, the vaccine has shown efficacy in preventing genital warts caused by low-risk HPV types, such as HPV 6 and HPV 11 [11, 12]. Based on the most recent guidance from the World Health Organization (WHO) regarding vaccination, the suggested vaccination schedule includes a one or two-dose regimen for girls and women aged 9–20 and a two-dose regimen with a 6-month interval for women aged 21 and older [13].

Despite the approval of vaccines by the FDA for several years [14], vaccination coverage has not been complete in most countries, and it remains at a minimum level in developing countries. The average HPV vaccination coverage in high-income countries stands at 67%, while in low- and middle-income countries, it reaches 55% [15]. Although the Gardasil vaccine [9] and the bivalent HPV vaccine (16/18) produced by Iranian company [16] exist in Iran’s markets, the HPV vaccine coverage is low [17, 18].

According to previous studies, one of the main reasons for the low vaccination coverage among women is their lack of knowledge and awareness about vaccines, even among women with higher levels of education [19]. In addition, some other contributing reasons include concerns about the vaccine’s side effects, fear of needle, stigma, cost, and insurance coverage problems [18, 20]. A qualitative study conducted to examine the barriers to cervical cancer screening programs identified emotional barriers such as fear and shame, practical barriers such as lack of time, and cognitive barriers such as low perceived risk [21].

Vaccination, as with other health behaviors, can be influenced by an Individual’s perception and belief of their health [22, 23]. Health Belief Model (HBM) is the theoretical framework that focuses on people’s attitudes and beliefs in an effort to explain and predict health behaviors. It contains concepts including perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy [24]. This scale is a valid and reliable tool for evaluating women’s health beliefs for HPV and its vaccination [25–27].

Since there is a relative scarcity of qualitative studies applying theoretical models about HPV and its vaccination [28] particularly in Iran [29], we decided to conduct a qualitative study with the aim of exploring women’s perceptions of HPV and its vaccine in Ardabil, which was chosen as the study site due to its traditional and religious context, and the low level of preventive behaviors related to cervical cancer [30]. Since focus group discussion (FGD) is a form of group interview and effective technique for exploring their attitudes and needs as well as encouraging open conversation about embarrassing subjects [31] as STIs, hence we used HBM trough FGD needed information. Understanding these barriers is crucial for developing targeted interventions to improve vaccination uptake and ultimately reduce HPV-related morbidity and mortality.

Methods

Participants and setting

We conducted semi-structured interviews and discussions with 23 eligible women (aged 15–45 years old) who had no previous history of cervical cancer, HPV infection, or its vaccination, in four focus groups (FG) in Ardabil, northwest Iran (November-December 2023). Those who were unwilling to continue participation were excluded. Participants from each center’s eligible clientele were purposefully selected to ensure the inclusion of women in this study representing a variety of socioeconomic situations, ages, educational levels, and jobs. The FG conducted were:

FG1: 5 women aged 15-24 years with diploma or lower education.

FG1: 5 women aged 15-24 years with diploma or lower education.

FG2: 5 women aged 25-45 years with academic education.

FG2: 5 women aged 25-45 years with academic education.

FG3: 6 women aged 15-24 years with diploma or lower education.

FG3: 6 women aged 15-24 years with diploma or lower education.

FG4: 7 women aged 25-45 years with academic education.

FG4: 7 women aged 25-45 years with academic education.

Their level of education was taken into consideration when categorizing women based on their ages. The creation of new focus groups continued until data saturation, indicating that no new codes were identified in the data.

Procedure and materials

We conducted a qualitative study using a directed content analysis approach based on the HBM. The objective was to explain how reproductive-age women perceived the HPV and its vaccination. Pilot testing with two participants ensured clarity and cultural appropriateness of the questions. In-depth semi-structured interviews conducted by expert interviewers and FGDs were used to gather the necessary information, allowing for both individual insights and interactive group dynamics that enriched the data. All women were asked about their demographics and history of their pap smear tests (see Table 1) and prompted to share their awareness, knowledge, and attitude about HPV as well as barriers related to HPV vaccine and discus about them in FG. Additionally, they were questioned on any social, economic, and cultural influences on their behavior. According to previous studies, the Health Belief Model (HBM) has been identified as a valid and reliable tool for evaluating women’s health beliefs [25, 27]. Therefore, the interview questions were designed according to the constructs of the HBM with an inductive approach and included questions such as, “What is the first thing that comes to mind when you hear the word HPV?“”, “Do you think there are benefits of the HPV vaccine for you? " and “What reasons have prevented you from getting the vaccine?” (see Table 2 for topics). Participants were prompted to elaborate further on their answers after each question. For instance, they were asked questions such as “Could you clarify?” and “Could you explain?” to elicit more information on women’s perception on HPV and its vaccination.

Each focus group discussion lasted between 90 and 120 min, and it was performed at the private room in the health centers. At the end of discussion, the participants were asked to speak about any unmentioned challenges if any were present at the end of each interview while expressing appreciation for their participation.

The interview content was digitally recorded after participants’ permission, and the researchers listened to recordings of each focus group discussion several times after completion. Immediately, the audio-recordings and the field-notes were transcribed verbatim. The written interview content also included non-verbal categories from the interview and its conduct that were noticed. All transcripts were imported into the MAXQDA software.

Data analyses

Data were analyzed using a direct content analysis approach. Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently, with transcripts reviewed immediately after each interview to identify initial codes and relevant units. Coding was performed by two researchers, with disagreements resolved through consultation with a third researcher. A codebook was developed based on the constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM), including six theoretical components such as awareness about HPV and its risks, vaccine benefits, vaccination barriers, cues to action, and self-care. Both deductive and inductive coding approaches were employed. The initial coding framework was guided by the HBM constructs, while additional codes and categories emerging from participants’ narratives were added inductively to capture unanticipated themes and contextual factors. All codes were documented and linked to the relevant HBM constructs. Analysis continued iteratively until no new concepts emerged (data saturation).(COREQ check list attached as supplementary file).

Results

There were 23 women in 4 focus groups. Participants were aged between 15 and 45 years, 78.26% were married, and 52.17% had a university education. Their demographic characteristics and history of pap tests are reported in Table 1. After conducting directed content analysis of qualitative data, a total of 368 initial codes were extracted, which were then combined into 56 merged codes. Ultimately, 19 subcategories were developed, which were categorized into six main categories following the HBM and a new category named social support. They were perceived sensitivity about HPV, the risks of HPV infection, benefits of HPV vaccination, barriers to HPV vaccination, cue to action, self-care, and social support.

Perceived sensitivity about HPV

This category contained three subcategories: Overall awareness about HPV, stress about virus transmission, and confusion about screening tests.

Overall awareness about HPV

Their main informational challenges about virus were the symptoms of infection, types of virus, virus persistence on surfaces as well as resistance to cleaning agents and detection of virus…. For example one of the women said:

“I heard that this virus is very resistant and doesn’t go away with ordinary detergents or even alcohol, but I’m not sure!” (FG1).

And the other said with concern:

“Can a blood test detect this virus? This question is always on my mind. How do we know that we have this virus?” (FG3).

Stress about virus transmission

Most of them focused on sexual contact as the main way of transmission, and mentioned the multiple partner was the most important risk factor. In addition very few of them expressed concern about non-sexual transmission such as normal vaginal delivery, breastfeeding, underwear contaminated with a virus, using gym equipment, laser hair removal, use of swimming pools and bodybuilding equipment, and the possibility for a faster transmission when warts are present.

A participant said:

“Using a condom is only effective when a person is exclusively in a relationship with one sexual partner; otherwise, it may not be effective… Sex workers are at risk …” (FG1).

Some participants had concern about non-sexual transmission:

“I specifically bring my own towel and mat to the gym because people say we might get infected, even when we want to use the gym equipment,…” (FG2).

“I think this virus can survive on surfaces for hours, especially in public bathrooms, toilets, showers, and similar places. I’m particularly cautious, especially when using public restroom facilities.“(FG1).

Confusion about screening tests

most of them had doubt regarding the schedule of the initial screening test and the intervals between pap tests. They believed that age was a main factor for starting screening; in addition they had no visual imagery about this procedure.

“I went to the doctor four times for cancer, I didn’t have any problems, but every time someone said about cancer, I would get worried and go back to check again by pap smear……“(FG2).

The risks of HPV infection

There were considerable anxiety and phobia among most women about warts and cancer. Also, some of them had concern about damage to the persistence of the marital relationship if their husband gets HPV.

Anxiety and phobia about warts and cancer

They were concerned about warts and the carcinogenicity of viruses in the female reproductive system, including the cervix and uterus. However, only a few had information about the association of HPV viruses and other cancers. They had fear of death and morbidity due to cancer and its adverse psychological outcomes. They expressed their concerns in various ways:

“This virus has different types, some of which are highly risky and can lead to severe cancers…“(FG1).

“Anyone who gets HPV doesn’t necessarily get cancer, but if it occurs, it’s very dangerous……“(FG3).

“The skin lesions and warts are agonizing, whether they are in the genital area, anus, or mouth…” (FG4).

Concern about damage to the persistence of marital relationship

They presented their concern by talking about fear of becoming infected through a partner, worrying that their partner may cheat on them, losing trust in their partner, and getting a divorce if their partner became sick.

A participant said:

“If you’re going to live with someone and you don’t trust him, then what’s the value of such a life? Well, if you’re always in a doubt or a dilemma, it is better not to live together…” (FG4).

“Because of this infection, there is possibility of sexual connection and cheating, so I prefer to separate. I think that it is enough reason to divorce…“(FG1).

Benefits of HPV vaccination

This category includes two subcategories: prevention of genital wart and cancers, and peace of mind due to protection against cervical cancer.

Prevention of genital wart and cancers

Most women mentioned the role of the vaccine in preventing cervical cancer, but a few knew about its role in preventing other cancers. Although some of them mentioned the side effects of the vaccine, they ultimately believed that currently the vaccine is the best option for preventing genital cancer.

They said:

“…I knew that these vaccines can prevent cancer and bad lesions and I don’t think they would have serious side effects…” (FG4).

Peace of mind

Although they had not injected the vaccine, some of them mentioned peace of mind about genital cancer as an important benefit of the vaccine.

One of the participants said:

“The least benefit of vaccination is the peace of mind it provides regarding cervical cancer. This is particularly important in situations where the incidence of cancers and related mortality rates are high” (FG4).

And the other said: “The number of cancer cases in the world has increased; at least when we get a vaccine, we will be relieved about uterine cancer.“(FG4).”

Barriers to HPV vaccination

This category contained four subcategories such as informational, psychological, socio-cultural, and economic barriers.

Informational barrier

Although participants had information about the virus and its vaccination, it was often insufficient or misconceived. Of course they frequently relied on the statements of friends and social media in this regards without reliable references.

Examples of quotes from these women are provided below:

“I heard that vaccine can only fight dangerous types. It can’t kill other types. Of course, it’s still very good that it solves the malignant problems.” (FG3).

" I saw a post on Instagram that there’s an age limit for getting vaccinated, for example, they say it’s possible to get it until the age of 26, and it doesn’t suit my age anymore” (FG4).

“I personally didn’t feel any sense of danger because those who have multiple sexual partners are at risk. In addition we don’t go to high-risk places such as night parties!” (FG2).

Psychological barriers

Fear and shame were identified as significant psychological barriers for women. Some participants believed that despite their willingness to participate in vaccination, they were concerned about the vaccine’s side effects. Some women were even embarrassed to bring up the topic of vaccination with their healthcare providers or doctors.

They said:

“I really wanted to get vaccinated, but when I remembered my friends’ words about the vaccine’s side effects, I backed off…They said the vaccine could cause allergy because one of their friends fainted a few minutes after the vaccine injection and remained under the doctor’s supervision for hours…I was afraid. (FG3).

“…because of its sexuality problem, I’m embarrassed, therefore I don’t want to ask for guidance or talk to anyone about it”. (FG4).

Socio-cultural barriers

Social stigma and people’s cultural beliefs were the main barriers in this field.

The participants’ experiences revealed that sexual matters in their society are still considered taboo, and the community, and even families, refrain from addressing educational issues related to sexual diseases. They said that even health providers don’t explain about sexual issues, using various excuses.

Some women said about this problem:

“There have always been limitations, many things had to be kept hidden; their society didn’t accept it. Parents would say it’s shameful.” (FG2).

“…The national broadcasting (IRIB) doesn’t have any program on this subject, and if they are forced to talk about it, they speak so cryptically that one can’t even understand what happened finally!” (FG2).

They said about native people’s belief:

“Talking about sexual topic is considered a fault. Even parents get extremely angry when their children talk or ask questions about this issue. They usually don’t answer the children’s questions.” (FG2).

“When you want to talk about sexual diseases, it feels like you’re making a mortal sin, and if you ask a question about it, people become pessimistic about you” (FG4).

Economic barriers

Unavailability and financial inaccessibility were noted as two contributing factors in this field. Most women indicated that unavailability and financial access were the important concerns about undergoing vaccination. In this section, some stated:

“The health center doesn’t provide vaccines. In some countries vaccines are easily accessible to all people, and when everyone reaches the age of maturity, they get vaccinated. My partner lived in Germany, and he received the vaccines, like HPV, after immigration. If we had the facilities like there, we would get vaccinated too.” (FG1).

“We don’t have easy access to the vaccine. At least, the vaccination should be free for those who get newly married.” (FG1).

“We’ve heard that some people got vaccinated, but they said they brought the vaccines from neighboring countries. I haven’t done it because it’s very expensive. They say it costs 4 million tomans (common currency unit in Iran)! Moreover, we need three doses of the vaccine, which incurs exorbitant costs.” (FG2).

Cue to action

This category consists of two subcategories, advice from health care providers plus doctors and content posted on social media. Women mentioned health center and social media as main information sources.

Advice from health care providers and doctors

They said that the health care providers often recommended pap smear or vaccination, though it was not mandatory and they didn’t follow them.

“The health center doctor said that if you get vaccinated, you won’t get cervical cancer, and this is very important for your health…” (FG4).

Related content posted on social media

They mentioned social media as an available informational source about HPV and vaccine regardless of the reliability of its content. One of the women said:

“Various posts have been shared in this subject on social media, and there is a lot of information about vaccines and sexually transmitted disease. I read much content there, especially about the benefits of the HPV vaccine, particularly its role in preventing cervical cancer and genital warts, which has recently become quite prevalent " (FG1).

Self-care

Some of the women believed that self-care had a key role in preventive behaviors related to HPV infection. This category consists of STI self-protection acts, stress management, and medical care.

STI Self-Protection acts

Pre-marital testing, abstinence in the case of an inability to marry, and the use of some contraceptives such as condoms in sexual relationships were three main self-protection measures emphasized as suggested solutions by women. The participants believed that every person could prevent the sexually transmitted infections by adopting certain reliable and safe methods in sexual relationships. This matter has been mentioned:

“Abstinence until marriage plays a main role in one’s sexual health. If a person doesn’t engage in relationships with others, they definitely won’t get sick. This is the best way for individuals who don’t have the conditions and opportunities for marriage.” (FG3).

“women need to be cautious before getting married whether that person has had multiple relationships before them or not. This matter was very important to me, as I did the pre-marriage inquiry.” (FG4).

“If there is a lesion on the genital area, a condom can play a protective role. It’s also better for individuals who don’t even have a lesion but are not very confident about their sexual partner to use a condom.” (FG1).

Stress management

Although some participants were aware that this virus could lead to cervical cancer, they believed that even in case of infection, with a positive mindset, seeking ways to treat infections and eliminate genital warts, and maintaining marital relationships, they could overcome the stress caused by the virus. They said:

“If we come into contact with the virus and the type of virus is low risk, we shouldn’t be overly concerned as the body itself fights against the virus and clears it.” (FG1).

“…Well, now everything has a treatment. We shouldn’t lose our composure. Warts can be removed with various devices. When you’re affected, you should quickly look for a solution and not waste time.” (FG4).

“I won’t get anxious if my husband gets it, it means I have it too, so we shouldn’t argue and seek treatment such as herbal remedies and laser.” (FG1).

Medical checkup

Some of the participants, while mentioning the role of Pap smear such as checkup test in diagnosing of HPV and early detection of cervical cancer, emphasized its high accuracy. One of the participants said:

“Although I was always scared of the test results, I have undergone the tests several times because I had heard that it can detect cancer earlier than its development…"And she continues with a choked voice: “My mother had cervical and breast cancer which were detected late, and unfortunately died because of late diagnosis.“(FG2).

“If the cervix has a lesion or has been infected with the HPV virus, a Pap smear can accurately indicate it. Pap smear detects cancer early; they even say 5 years earlier! So If I notice any infection, secretions or anything suspicious, I quickly go for a Pap smear. “(FG2).

Social support

This category consists of informational, emotional, and financial support.

Informational support

The women believed that education of STD should start with adolescents, followed by educating couples before marriage, and providing education to young women and their husbands. This education should be provided through mass media (TV, radio…), schools, and social media.

“In my opinion, education should begin for individuals from the age of 14 and 15, when children start going to high school. It’s better if these teachings are conducted through teachers, as children are more receptive to them.“(FG3).

“Nowadays, almost all educated women have access to social media platforms such as Telegram, Instagram, WhatsApp and …. After all, each of us has a smartphone that allows us to gather useful information, provided that the information shared with people comes from reliable sources. You can’t trust all the content that is shared on these social media.“(FG3).“It’s true that health providers and midwives tell us to get a Pap smear or get vaccinated, but they never exactly explain why we should do a Pap smear or get vaccinated. They basically dictate some things, and when we ask them to explain, they don’t provide more explanation, and they say they are busy!“(FG4).

Emotional support

The participants focused on improving emotional relationships between parents and adolescents as well as providing emotional support from husbands, So they can discuss their sexual issues with their parents or husbands without fear or shame.

“When we were children, we didn’t have the courage to mention a word about sexual matters, even I couldn’t tell my mother when I started menstruating because I was afraid. That’s why I try to have a relationship with my children where they feel comfortable with me, and they can ask me their sexual questions.” (FG4).

Another woman said:

“When I tell my husband that I need to have a Pap smear or maybe get vaccinated, he says, “Wow, you’re really patient. What are these things? Are they new trends? while my sister’s husband, being in the healthcare field, himself looks for medications and treatments for my sister without her having to push him to do so. “(FG2).

Financial support

Women believe that due to financial constraints, most women, especially housewives and adolescent girls, are unable to cover the costs associated with obtaining vaccines and screening tests. These expenses should be covered by family: Furthermore, their families should accompany them to receive these services, especially when they have young children and require more family support. The same applies to adolescents; parents should also accompany them to receive the services.

They said:

“The costs related to the vaccine are truly high, and I need to discuss this matter with my spouse so that I can persuade them.“(FG4).

“Considering that this vaccine is not provided by health centers, we are compelled to provide it ourselves, which incurs expenses…“(FG1).

Discussion

The vaccination coverage remains low in our country, as evidenced by previous study and WHO reports [17, 18]. This study aimed to explore women’s perceptions of HPV and its vaccine using the Health Belief Model (HBM) as a theoretical framework to predict health behaviors such as vaccination. In addition to the six classical constructs of the HBM perceived sensitivity, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy we introduced a new construct: social support. The discussion is organized into five sections:

Perceived sensitivity about HPV and its risks

According to the HBM, adequate knowledge and perception of disease risk are crucial for improving preventive behaviors [32]. Consistent with other studies [33, 34], our findings revealed that most participants had inadequate or incorrect information about HPV, its transmission, screening methods etc. While they were aware that HPV is sexually transmitted, few knew it could also be transmitted non-sexually. Although sexual contact is the primary mode of HPV transmission [35–37], the virus can also be transmitted through non-sexual means such as skin-to-skin or skin-to-mucosa contact. Vertical transmission through amniotic fluid during natural vaginal delivery is also a potential, though not definitively confirmed, route of transmission [38]. Additionally, evidence suggests HPV can be transmitted through contaminated beauty salon tools, such as hair removal wax [39, 40]. Women expressed significant concerns about genital warts and cancer-causing potential of HPV but were less aware of non-genital related cancers such as head and neck cancers, which aligns with previous findings [41]. HPV is known to cause cancers in various organs [36, 42–44]. One of the other educational challenges was women’s inadequate information about pap smear as an important secondary prevention strategy. Studies indicated that the rate of Iranian women, who received this test at least once, approximately 28–30%, which is estimated at a low rate due to inadequate knowledge [45]. Thus, educating women about HPV transmission, adverse outcomes, and screening methods is essen

Moreover, many women were concerned about the durability of marital relationships if one partner were infected. A study by Qaderi et al. (2021) indicated that HPV-positive women felt their marital and sexual relationships were permanently altered [46]. Other studies also reported psychological and sexual problems in couples with positive HPV status [47–50]. Thus, addressing the psychological and sexual aspects of HPV infection is crucial.

Benefits and barriers to HPV vaccine

According our results, although most of participants mentioned the benefits of vaccine such as prevention of genital wart and cancers and peace of mind, similar to other study results [51], they focused more on the barriers to vaccination. Informational, psychological, socio-cultural, and economic barriers were identified. Inadequate knowledge and misconceptions about vaccination were major challenges. Consistent with other studies in Iran, even educated women in medical sciences had inadequate information about HPV and its vaccination [19, 52–54]. In traditional societies, discussing sexually transmitted diseases is a taboo, and stigma associated with contracting such diseases, including HPV, can delay prevention, diagnosis, and treatment [34, 50]. People’s shame about these infections can delay the process of prevention, diagnosis, and treatment [48, 50]. Studies have shown an inverse association between awareness of HPV and stigma or shame [55].

Cultural beliefs and myths also created significant barriers. Some participants mentioned that people in their city believed religious couples could not contract STIs and therefore did not need the vaccine. Similar misconceptions were found in other studies [56–58], emphasizing the benefits of the vaccine in enhancing sexual health and prevention both genital and non-genital cancers is crucial [51]. Educational interventions based on perceived benefits and barriers have been shown to be effective in promoting HPV vaccination [59, 60].

Another major barrier was the limited accessibility and high cost of HPV vaccine, compounded by the lack of insurance coverage and the requirement of a prescription for vaccine access. Despite the results of this study, research conducted in developed countries did not mention these barriers, as the vaccine is part of routine vaccination programs there [21]. Unfortunately, the HPV vaccine is not included in the national immunization programs of over 30% of countries worldwide [61] including our country [29], so establishing it on national vaccination program is essential.

Sources of information

Our findings indicated that health centers and social media were the main and guidance about HPV. Previous studies have confirmed the key role of healthcare providers in promoting HPV vaccination and its acceptability [62, 63]; however in this process the role of general physicians is particularly critical [64]. Social media and mobile health also play significant roles in promoting awareness and acceptance of the HPV vaccine [65, 66] if the available information is adapted from reliable sources.

Self-care

According to WHO, self-care is the ability of individuals, families, and communities to promote health and prevent disease with or without support from health workers [67]. The participants in our study mentioned three self-care methods: STI self-protection, stress management, and medical checkups. For example, they correctly identified condoms as a key factor in self-protection against HPV; although condoms cannot fully prevent HPV transmission, they reduce the risk [68]. They also mentioned the role of stress management in control of disease as a self-care. The previous studies confirmed HPV often affected sexual and romantic life in infected couples and they experienced psychological burden such as severe stress [69]. Stress management was also noted as important in disease control, with previous studies highlighting the psychological burden of HPV on infected couples [50]. Medical checkups, such as Pap smears, were mentioned as beneficial self-care practices as they are recommended by WHO as effective screening programs [70].

Social support

Our analysis identified social support as a critical factor in preventive behaviors. The participants expressed a need for informational, emotional, and financial support. They emphasized the educational need from reliable and accredited sources as well as expressed dissatisfaction with the insufficient information provided by health centers. Similar to the findings of other studies, they wanted more information on HPV transmission, the HPV vaccine, the natural history of the virus, HPV detection, treatment options, incubation period, and symptoms of HPV infection [71, 72].

They mentioned emotional and financial support from parents during adolescence and from spouses after marriage. Previous studies have confirmed the supportive role of parents in vaccine acceptance among adolescents [66] and the importance of partner support in adulthood [71]. Hence, health planners should consider educating both couples and parents to improve reproductive sexual health.

Further, they emphasized establishing a national vaccination program that should at least start at the time of marriage or before it, especially given recent changes in marriage patterns [73].

These finding reflect the attitudes and experiences of women in Ardabil and offer insights for local HPV vaccination initiatives. Caution is advised when generalizing to other populations, and further research in divers setting is warranted.

Strengths and limitations

We were careful at every stage of interviewing, transcribing, and analyzing the data. However, the results cannot be generalized to the entire community due to the small sample size. Despite this limitation, the study has provided valuable information for future research. Additionally, given the sensitive nature of the topic, some women may have withheld certain experiences or opinions due to social and cultural constraints.

Conclusion

Understanding women’s beliefs about HPV and its vaccine is crucial for developing targeted healthcare interventions. This study highlighted key factors necessary for effective cervical cancer prevention programs. Based on the findings and identified barriers, it is increasingly evident that training women and their families, along with enhancing societal awareness of sexual health to reduce the stigma around STDs, is essential. Health policymakers should consider integrating the HPV vaccine into the national vaccination program and establishing sexual health counseling units at healthcare centers in the future.
