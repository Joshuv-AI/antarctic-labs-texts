Breeding objectives, selection criteria and breeding practices of indigenous goats in Ahferom district of Central Tigray, Northern Ethiopia
Authors: Daniel Woldegebriel, Tikabo Gebremariam, Hiwot Asmelash, Teshome Begashaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSustainable utilization of farm animal genetic resources requires a comprehensive understanding of farmer breeding management and selection criteria. However, site-specific data for indigenous goats in the Ahferom district of Northern Ethiopia was lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the breeding objectives and selection practices of 147 smallholder goat farmers across midland and lowland agro-ecologies. A pretested semi-structured questionnaire was used to gather data. In addition, focus group discussions (FGDs) were held with eight to ten knowledgeable farmers and key informants. The collected data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software.ResultsThe goat flock structure (Mean ± Standard Error of the Mean = SEM; 17.35 ± 0.17) was characterized by a high proportion of breeding does. Farmers exhibited a strong market-oriented focus, prioritizing income generation (Index = 0.39) and meat production (0.21) as primary breeding objectives. While most farmers (61.2%) maintained their own breeding bucks, mating remained largely uncontrolled (83.0%). Selection for the next generation was driven by productivity and morphological traits, with litter size (0.29) and body conformation (0.29) identified as the top criteria for does and bucks, respectively. Castration, practiced by over 93% of respondents, was strategically timed (typically at 1–2 years of age) to maximize market value through improved body condition.ConclusionsIn the Ahferom district, goat breeding objectives are multi-functional but lean toward market-oriented traits. Hence, breed improvement programs must prioritize marketable body size and rapid growth rates, which are the top ranked selection criteria for bucks while focusing on mothering ability and litter size for does. Furthermore, the significantly (p < 0.05) larger flock sizes, and higher proportion of breeding does in the lowland compared to the midland suggest that improvement strategies should be tailored to the specific management scales and environmental pressures of these distinct agro-ecological zones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05549-x.Keywords: Animal genetic resources, Ethiopia, Goat flock structure, Productivity and morphological traits, Smallholder goat farmers

Introduction

Ethiopia has the largest livestock population in Africa. The goat population in Ethiopia was 46 million in 2021/22 and is distributed in different ecologies, predominantly in the lowlands [1]. Of the total goats, approximately 69.2% are females and 30.8% are males. In Ethiopia, goat meat accounts for 16.8% of the total meat supplies [2] and 16.7% of milk consumed [3]. The off-take rate for goats is 27%, and the average carcass weight of Ethiopian goats is 8 kg per goat [4]. The growth rate of the goat population over the last five years, from 2017 to 2022, in the country was 10.3% [1]. Moreover, goat meat production has increased in recent years from 2017 to 2022 by 63%, and by 2028, it is projected that the annual demand for goat meat will increase by 114.3% [5]. This is attributed to increments in demand for goat meat both in domestic and export markets [6].

Goat farming serves as an engine of growth by providing income, employment, foreign exchange, skin, manure, and better nutrition for smallholder keepers in different farming systems and agro-ecological zones of the country [7]. In addition, goats in rural areas where there are no bank services also function as a source of finance, insurance, and social status [3, 8, 9]. Smallholder farmers prefer goat production not only because of their resilience to harsh environmental circumstances and limited feed resources but also because of their low production costs, high fertility, short generation interval, faster growth rate, and suitability and greater efficiency for small-scale production systems than for large ruminant production systems [10–13].

Ethiopian goat breeds are dominated by indigenous goat breeds, which constitute 99.93% of the goat population of Ethiopia [1]. Although indigenous goats are an important part of the farming system in all agro-ecological zones [11], their ability to support the livelihoods of farmers and the national economy and their productivity in Ethiopia are low. The poor performance of this indigenous goat might be attributed to different factors, such as a lack of feasible and sustainable breeding programs, poor selection and uncontrolled mating, the absence of clear strategies to improve productivity, low genetic potential, and a traditional production system as a whole [14, 15].

Genetic improvement programs are essential to maximize and sustain the productivity of indigenous goats. However, any successful strategy requires a comprehensive understanding of the selection criteria and breeding management practices that guide smallholder farmers. At present, there is limited, up-to-date and site-specific information regarding these practices in the Ahferom district of Northern Ethiopia. The novelty of this study lies in its integrated analysis of breeding objectives across distinct midland and lowland agro-ecologies. By characterizing these local practices, this research contributes vital information necessary for designing sustainable, community-based breeding programs. Consequently, this study aimed to investigate the breeding objectives, existing strategies, and selection criteria of indigenous goats under smallholder management in the Ahferom district of the Tigray region, Northern Ethiopia providing a framework to align future genetic improvement with actual farmer preferences.

Materials and methods

Descriptions of the study area

The survey was carried out in Ahferom district which is located in the Central Zone of the Tigray region, Northern Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The altitude of the area ranges from 1500 to 3005 m above sea level [16]. The average annual temperature ranges from 22 °C (°C) to 27 °C per year. With 53,643 households and a total area of 1,339 square kilometer, the district is home to about 173,651 people. The district owns 33 administrative Tabias, the smallest administrative units, with 6 Tabias in towns and 27 Tabias in rural areas. Agro-ecologically, the district is composed of midland (45.5%), lowland (44.5%), and highland (10%).

Based on the information obtained from the Office of Agriculture and Natural Resources (OoANR), the district covers a total of 133,979 hectare (ha) lands. Of this land, 23,434 ha are cultivated lands (17.49%), 26,314 ha enclosed natural forestland (19.64%), 15,792 ha rehabilitated forestland (11.79%), 25, 651 ha non-closed forestland (19.15%). Whereas, 15,134 ha of lands are used for grazing (11.30%), 723 ha for pastures (0.54%), 16,174 ha for settlements (12.07%) and 10,755 ha are miscellaneous lands (8.03%). Mixed crop-livestock farming system is the common feature of the study district. Teff (Eragrostis tef), wheat, barley, maize, sorghum, finger millet and pulses are the major crops cultivated in the study area. Livestock rearing is a pillar component of the farming system. The total livestock population is 310,382 cattle, 110,389 goats and sheep, 3,649 equines, 255,794 chickens, 24,163 traditional bee colonies and 4,568 modern bee hives [17]. Goats are dominantly reared in midland and lowland areas.

Sample size and sampling procedures

A multi-stage stratified sampling technique was employed to select representative households across the study area. In the first stage, the district was stratified into three agro-ecological zones: lowland, midland, and highland. Subsequently, three Tabias, Sero and Laelay Migaria-Tsemri from the midland, and Betegebez from the lowland, were purposively selected based on their high goat population density and production potential. The highland zone was excluded from the study as goats are less distributed and not extensively reared in that ecology. In the final stage, a sampling frame of goat producers was established using records from the district and Tabia offices of Agriculture and Natural Resources. From this frame, 147 smallholder farmers extensively involved in goat production were chosen via simple random sampling to minimize selection bias ([16], Table 1). The final sample size was determined using the formula of Yamane (Eq. 1 [18]), at a 95% confidence interval (Table 1). Duration and methods of data collection

Primary data were collected between 7 June and 6 October 2023. A semi-structured questionnaire was used, which had been pre-tested in May 2023 with 12 goat producers from a neighboring district to ensure the clarity and logical flow of the questions. Following the pre-test, the questionnaire was refined by adjusting the terminology of breeding traits into local dialects and streamlining the ranking procedures for selection criteria to improve respondent accuracy. The final version was administered via face-to-face interviews with household heads. Additionally, three FGDs were conducted, one in each sampled Tabia. Each FGD comprised 8–10 purposively selected participants, including experienced goat farmers and key informants (development agents and Tabia administrators) to complement these findings.

The FGDs were moderated by the researchers using a thematic guide to ensure consistency across locations. Data from these sessions were recorded through field notes, which were subsequently processed using thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns in breeding practices and historical production trends. These qualitative insights were then triangulated with questionnaire results to provide a comprehensive interpretation of the farmers’ breeding objectives and selection criteria. Secondary data regarding geographic and climatic characteristics were retrieved from district agricultural reports. Secondary data were collected through reviewing documents about the geographic location and climatic data of the study area.

Data reliability and validation

To guarantee data reliability, the research instrument underwent content validation by livestock experts and a pilot pre-test with 12 non-sampled farmers to refine local breeding terminology. Methodological triangulation was achieved by cross-referencing quantitative survey results with qualitative insights from FGDs, ensuring that any discrepancies in self-reporting were clarified through field-level consensus. All data collection was led directly by the researchers to maintain consistency and minimize enumerator bias.

Data analysis procedures

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 28) [19]. Results were organized and summarized using descriptive statistics: frequency, percent and mean. Welch’s independent t-test test was run to compare the means between the agro-ecological zones for quantitative variables to account for the unequal sample sizes and potential differences in variance between the midland and lowland groups. Study protocols

Ethics statement

The study does not contain clinical studies. We used goat farmers as informants for the study. The farmers were well informed and were well aware of the research objectives. They were well informed that maintaining confidentiality and anonymity was part of the study during data collection as well as reporting. They were voluntary and agreed to participate in this research work. The agreement with the farmers was in written form. The study did not include minors. The age of the farmers who participated in this research work ranged from 27 to 60 years old with an average age of 46.28 years old [16].

Goat breeding objectives

Breeding objectives compromise between increased productivity and enhanced functional traits such as health, fertility, and feed intake capacity [21]. The breeding objective is the first step to improve the genetic potential of goats. In the study area, farmers identified several traits that they would like to improve. Farmers prioritized traits like producing marketable size goats for income generation (0.39), meat production (0.21) and saving (0.19). There was variation between the two agro-ecologies, with meat production (0.26) ranking second in the midland areas and milk production (0.19) being the second highest in the lowland areas (Table 3).

Mating practice

Most of the farmers (83.0%) practiced uncontrolled mating system, while the remaining farmers (17.0%) practiced partially controlled mating system (Table 4).

Selection criteria for breeding does

Selection of breeding does was practiced in the study area by using different criteria to be the parents of the next generation. The primary criterion for the does selection in the study area was litter size (0.29), followed by growth rate (0.23), body conformation (0.22) and age at first kidding (0.11) (Table 5). There were no variations between the two agro-ecologies in the study area.

Goat castration practices

Farmers castrate bucks for a variety of reasons. Castration is one way to prevent male animals from exhibiting undesirable qualities from mating females and increasing market value. Table 7 presents the buck castration practices and methods used for castration. In the study area, castration was practiced by the majority of the farmers (93.2%), while remaining farmers (6.8%) did not practice it. The primary selection criterion for bucks’ castration, was age (50.3%), physical confirmation (42.2%) and color (5.4%). Castration performed most frequently in the age range of one to two years by the dominant farmers (90.0%), while the remaining (10.0%) practiced it for more than two years. Burdizzo is a modern castration method used by the dominant farmers (57.1%) who have access to local veterinary facilities. The remaining farmers (42.9%) castrated their bucks using traditional methods with the locally available material “Gozomo” (Table 7).

Culling is a common practice in both agro-ecologies. Discussion

Goat flock size and structure

The finding on goat flock structure is higher than the reports for Arado goats (12.65 ± 9.9 [22], . Conversely, Gatew et al. [23] for Borana (23.08 ± 1.94) and Siti goats (44.02 ± 3.33) reported higher values than the current study. Moreover, Mekonnen et al. [22] reported considerably higher values for Begait (43.02 ± 28.1) and Hassan goats (70.29 ± 52.6) than the current study. There was a wider range of variations in goat flock size per household between agro-ecologies, with higher values in the lowland (19.73 ± 1.65) than in the midland (15.16 ± 0.75) (Table 2). The goats’ flock size ranged from 17 to 24 in the lowland and 13 to 19 in the midland. Variation in browsing area, feed supplementation, water availability and access to veterinary services could cause variation in flock size in different agro-ecologies and different parts of the country.

The overall mean female flock proportion was greater than the male proportion. This finding is in agreement with other studies [24, 25]. The does comprise a flock structure that is more prevalent than buck structures. This is mostly because bucks were castrated early to make management simpler and to gain weight to boost sales value and generate more revenue. As a result, the ratio of bucks to does was 1:5. This finding is comparable to that of a study in other parts of the country (1:5) [26]. However, qualitative insights from FGDs with experienced farmers and key informants suggest a perceived decline in this ratio over recent years. While this trend was not objectively measured through longitudinal data in the present study and should be interpreted with caution, this could primarily be because as children over the age of 18 detached from their families, they are often gifted breeding does as foundation stock, which further reduces the relative proportion of males retained in the parental flock.

Goat breeding objectives

The results of the present study on breeding objectives are in agreement with those of other studies across the country, as the primary objective of goat farmers is income generation [27–29]. In contrast, other study in Ethiopia has indicated that milk production [30, 31] was the main breeding objectives.

Source of breeding bucks

Similar to the current study, Demissie et al. [32] in East Gojjam indicated that farmers used their own breeding bucks to mate their breeding does. In contrast, goat farmers in other areas of the country such as Horro Guduru Wollega [27], Ilu Abba Bora [33], used breeding bucks from their neighbors. The buck used for breeding from the neighbor can enhance the genetic diversity within the breeding pool, which minimizes the risk of inbreeding, which leads to poor growth rates. Mating was possible year-round since the bucks were kept together with the flocks.

Mating practice

The majority of the farmers (83.0%) allowed their bucks to serve does other than their own. Moreover, farmers (91.2%) allowed does to be served randomly by any buck and could not identify the sire of a kid. Partially controlled mating is rarely practiced in the midland, as tethering was used as a system of management by the same farmers. These farmers did not completely allow their goats to browse freely; the owners follow the heat period of the doe and allocate bucks for mating. Thus, in the midland areas, farmers (22.2%) had partial control over breeding activities and had better opportunities for selecting breeding bucks. In line with the results of the present study, household members practiced controlled mating in the Shabelle zone [30] and Sheko and Shey Bench districts [34]. The remaining farmers (77.9%) practiced uncontrolled mating.

Uncontrolled or free mating was common in lowland areas (93.8%); where goats of both sexes grazed together in a free extensive grazing system. No restriction to browse and tethering was unusual; goats can mate with known or unknown bucks in the field during browsing. This type of management does not allow for the selection of productive flocks, and it may expose goats that have inferior production. The results of this study are in line with those for goats that freely mate in Oromia [35], and Illu Abba Bora [33].

Selection criteria for breeding does

Based on the discussants of FGDs, twin birth was a criterion for selecting future breeding does. This demonstrates that female goats in this study area are highly prolific. This study also agrees with the study in the Horro Gudru Wollega goat type [27], where litter size, growth rate and conformation were the criteria for the selection of does. However, this result does not agree with the findings of Mahilet [28], who reported that appearance (0.50) was considered the first reason for doe selection in the East Hararghe zone of Ethiopia.

Selection criteria for breeding bucks

Similar to the finding of Alefe [30], appearance, growth rate, and color were considered the first three reasons for busk selection in the Shabelle zone, southeastern Ethiopia. As per the FGDs with knowledgeable farmers and key informants, performance history received little attention, mainly due to the small size of the flocks used for mating in the study area. Conversely, body confirmation was given greater priority because of its phenotypic expression in offspring and its economic importance. Furthermore, gray with some red in the midland and fawn and red and gray in the lowland coat were the most preferred coat colors for selecting their buckling as a replacement. Several authors [27–29] indicated that coat color type or pattern, body conformation, growth rate, and libido are crucial selection factors for breeding bucks.

Goat castration practices

The findings of the present study on goat castration practices are consistent with the findings in the Eastern Hararghe [28] and Shabelle Zone [30], who reported that approximately 70.99% and 93.7% of the respondents, respectively, engaged in castration. Most of the farmers (73.47%) castrated their bucks to enhance body weight, which eventually increased market value. Similar results were reported throughout the country in the Bench Maji zone [34], Gojam Enebse Sar Midir district [32] and Jimma zone [36]. The implication of castration practices on breeding programs can maximize genetic quality and allow controlled mating strategies through the selection of a few breeding males and castration of the rest. These ensure that only selected bucks contribute to the gene pool. In Eastern Hararghe, Mahilet [28] reported a similar outcome on utilization of traditional methods with the locally available material for bucks’ castration. There was variation in the methods used for castration. Nevertheless, modern castration was commonly practiced in the midland areas, while traditional methods were used in the lowland areas.

Goat culling criteria

Culling the goat flock is an important management practice that makes the flock size more productive and easier to manage. The current findings on culling are similar to the findings of Ahmed [27], which was the primary criterion in the Horro Guduru Wollega Zone. The current result is different from the finding that health problems [32] were the primary reasons why farmers culled goats in the East Gojjam Zone of Ethiopia. Culling of goats is practiced in many parts of the country, although the reasons for culling differ slightly among studies [27, 31].

Conclusions

This study provides insight into the breeding objective, selection criteria and breeding system of indigenous goats in two agro-ecological zones of the Ahferom district in Northern Ethiopia. Goats mainly serve as a source of income, meat and milk for home consumption, saving and manure production. Uncontrolled mating was the dominant breeding strategy, and farmers used bucks from neighbors. Farmers did not know about sires, this may increase the risk of unintended inbreeding and nonselective breeding over successive generations. Farmers practiced different selection criteria for both bucks and does. They used criteria like conformation, growth rate, color, and horn for selection of bucks. Twinning ability, growth rate, conformation, age at first kidding, and color were used for does selection thereby for replacing the stock. When designing sustainable breed improvement strategies in the area, traits selected by farmers need to be considered.
