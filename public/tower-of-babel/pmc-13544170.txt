E-lesion or stage IV disease? An ongoing unsettled issue in pediatric Hodgkin lymphoma
Authors: Jennifer Seelisch, Christine Mauz-Koerholz, Kara M Kelly, Michael Link, Angela Punnett, Dietrich Stoevesandt, Monica Palese, Sue Kaste, Kathleen McCarten, Lars Kurch, Bradford Hoppe, Jamie E Flerlage
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Staging of E-lesions and lung disease in particular in Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) has been a longstanding and controversial challenge. In 1984, Connors and Klimo published the results of a survey in the Journal of Clinical Oncology, highlighting this issue.1 They surveyed 14 internationally recognized experts in HL in an attempt to establish how various institutions were staging patients in what they suspected would be potentially unclear scenarios. At that time, the Ann Arbor staging system was in use, and the updated Cotswold criteria had not yet been established.2 Four case vignettes were distributed, and experts designated each vignette as either stage IIE or stage IV disease. Results were highly divided for 2 of the 4 case vignettes, and Connors and Klimo1 made a call for clear guidance and group consensus for staging of E-lesions (stage IIE) and stage IV disease.

Despite updates on the HL staging systems at both the Cotswold (1989)3 and Lugano (2014)4 meetings, there remains today, >40 years later, a perceived lack of uniformity in the approach to the staging of E-lesions, and specifically of lung lesions, even among experts in the field. Historically, E-lesions (extranodal or extralymphatic extension) have been defined as involvement of extralymphatic tissue because of direct, contiguous infiltration from an adjacent lymph node mass. However, challenges remain in the interpretation of “contiguous.” Furthermore, historically, the definition of contiguous was also implied to be within the same, often large, nodal irradiation field, which, in the current era of radiation approaches, is no longer relevant or appropriate.

The lack of consistency in the initial staging and response assessment criteria in pediatric HL is an ongoing challenge and is problematic because this provides the foundation for risk-adapted treatment. Currently published staging systems are not entirely reflective of current pediatric practices nor is there consistent application of staging and response criteria across pediatric consortia. Harmonization is imperative to facilitate cross-trial comparison of clinical studies globally. To achieve this, the international Staging, Evaluation, and Response Criteria Harmonization (SEARCH) for Childhood, Adolescent, and Young Adult Hodgkin Lymphoma (CAYAHL) group was developed, with harmonization as its goal.5 SEARCH for CAYAHL is comprised of representatives from the Children’s Oncology Group (COG), European Network for Pediatric HL, Pediatric Hodgkin Consortium, and the Latin American Hemato-Oncologic Pediatric Diseases Consortium. At present, there are >60 members representing 10 countries across these consortia, with members from pediatric oncology, nuclear medicine, radiology, and radiation oncology. To date, the SEARCH for CAYAHL group has published harmonization projects for the involvement of liver, cortical bone, Waldeyer ring, E-lesions, and central nervous system involvement.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 SEARCH has also recently published a staging atlas,11 and manuscripts have been accepted on the pitfalls of imaging in pediatric HL and the use of metabolic tumor volume.

The SEARCH for CAYAHL group reviewed the most recent frontline pediatric HL trials from the Pediatric Hodgkin Consortium, European Network for Pediatric HL, and the COG and concluded that lung lesions were defined differently for each (Table 1). The main areas of deviation pertain to the number and size of lung lesions and the significance of positron emission tomography avidity. Therefore, we hypothesized that the issues described by Connors and Klimo1 around E-lesion and stage IV definitions had not been resolved after more than 40 years and despite the multiple updates to our current staging systems.

To further investigate this, the SEARCH for CAYAHL group developed a similar survey using 7 case vignettes. The survey was distributed online to 26 internationally recognized experts in pediatric HL to establish how current world experts are staging potential E-lesions, with a focus on lung lesions. Responses were collected anonymously. Of the 21 survey respondents (81% response rate), 3 were radiologists, 4 dedicated nuclear medicine radiologists, and 14 pediatric oncologists from 18 different institutions. Radiation oncologists were excluded from the survey because the case vignettes were directed at those who determine upfront staging clinically. The mean years of experience in the field was 17.5 years (median, 17.5 years [range, 7-40]). There was representation from each of the major pediatric consortium groups (14 from COG, 4 from the European Network, and 3 from the Pediatric Hodgkin Consortium). Case vignettes and survey results are available in Table 2. Although there was near consensus for 4 of the cases, several case responses remained very divided (cases 1, 6, and 7). Inconsistent responses were most common around the role of positron emission tomography avidity in lung lesions, the number and location of lung lesions, and the concept of contiguous disease. Interestingly, these discrepancies persisted not only across but also within consortium groups and within specialty roles. This phenomenon is likely reflective of definitions evolving over time and across clinical trials, further highlighting the importance of harmonizing these definitions.

In conclusion, having reviewed recent frontline HL trials and the results of our survey, the SEARCH for CAYAHL group confirms the ongoing inconsistency in E-lesions and lung staging internationally. This inconsistency affects risk stratification, which in turn alters therapy and may ultimately affect outcomes and late effects. In addition, given the heterogeneity in staging practices, concern remains about the ability to accurately compare outcomes across trials. As previously highlighted by Connors and Klimo1, there is concern that with overassignment of stage IV disease, including lower-risk patients on high-risk trials, there may be a false perception of improved outcome. Conversely, overassignment of E-lesions may inadvertently give the impression of worse outcomes through the inclusion of higher-risk patients on low-risk trials. As event-free survival rates continue to rise for patients of all ages with HL,12, 13, 14 clarity in staging practices and definitions of risk factors has never been more important than now. Today, the accuracy of definitions of staging practices is essential to allow for the assessment of clinical risk factors for underperforming groups of patients. There is an ongoing pressing need for harmonization of lung staging in pediatric HL. Forty years after the Connors and Klimo1 publication, the SEARCH for CAYAHL group intends to answer this call through the process of creating expert consensus.
