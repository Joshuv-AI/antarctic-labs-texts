A Clinical Analysis of the Association Between Nifedipine and Insulin Usage and the Increased Incidence of Colonic Polyps
Authors: Xiao‐Lin Wang, Tian Tian, Dan Zhang, Yan‐Nan Li, Chang‐Zheng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the association between the incidence of colon polyps and colon cancer and the use of clinical medications, including nifedipine and insulin, thereby providing a scientific basis for the development of chemoprevention strategies for colorectal cancer and for guiding rational clinical drug use.MethodIn a cross‐sectional study, we analyzed the clinical data of 2571 eligible patients who underwent colonoscopy in our hospital from 2014 to 2022, aiming to investigate the association between certain commonly used drugs and the incidence of benign and malignant colon polyps.ResultsThere were 1197 subjects without polyps and 1374 patients diagnosed with colon polyps, among which 1059 were low‐grade adenomatous polyps, 149 were high‐grade adenomatous polyps, 120 were inflammatory polyps, and 44 were cancerous polyps. Statistically significant differences were observed in several clinical parameters across different pathological types of colon polyps and the non‐polyp group.The prevalence of different pathological types of colon polyps across various segments of the colon was generally consistent.The association between medications and colonic polyps was analyzed as follows: (1) Significant differences were observed between nifedipine, aspirin, statins, and insulin when comparing the non‐polyp group with the adenomatous polyp group (p < 0.05). (2) Significant differences were found between nifedipine, metformin, and insulin when comparing the single benign colonic polyps to multiple polyps (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe use of nifedipine, insulin, and metformin was associated with an increased risk of developing colonic polyps, whereas statin therapy was linked to a decreased risk.

Introduction

Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) ranks as the third most frequently diagnosed cancer globally, contributing to approximately 1.9 million new cancer cases annually, which accounts for 10% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases worldwide. Notably, the incidence of CRC rises markedly with advancing age [1, 2]. Colonic polyps, particularly adenomatous polyps, are significant precancerous lesions that may progress to colorectal cancer. Numerous studies have demonstrated that risk factors associated with colorectal cancer [3] encompass both non‐modifiable elements, such as male gender [4] and advancing age, and clinical indicators including the presence of more than three polyps and a polyp diameter of 2 cm or greater. Additionally, several modifiable risk factors have been identified, such as inadequate physical activity, obesity [5, 6], cigarette smoking, excessive alcohol intake [7], a diet high in red meat, and dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. Chemoprevention of CRC has emerged as a prominent research focus in recent years. The development of CRC is a gradual process that typically spans several years, progressing from precancerous lesions to adenomas, invasive cancer, and eventually metastasis. During the precancerous/adenoma stage, early detection through screening and chemopreventive interventions can effectively halt disease progression. Numerous pharmacological agents have been shown to reduce the incidence of CRC. These include aspirin, non‐aspirin non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), statins, drugs targeting metabolic pathways, as well as vitamins and minerals.

Certain commonly prescribed medications, including nifedipine and insulin, have been suggested to potentially promote the development of colon polyps. However, research in this area remains limited to date.

This study analyzed the medication usage among patients who underwent colonoscopy during hospitalization at our institution from 2014 to 2022, examining their association with the incidence of colon polyps and CRC. Specifically, it investigated the correlation between commonly used drugs, such as nifedipine and insulin, and the occurrence of colon polyps, while also preliminarily exploring potential underlying mechanisms. The findings may provide valuable insights and a theoretical foundation for the prevention of CRC.

Research Subjects

Inclusion Criteria: All Patients Who Underwent Colonoscopy During Their Hospitalization at Our Hospital From 2014 to 2022.

Exclusion Criteria: Patients With a History of Colorectal Cancer or Colorectal Resection Prior to Their First Colonoscopy; A History of Inflammatory Bowel Disease; Patients With Ulcerative Colitis‐Associated Colorectal Cancer; Incomplete Colonoscopy or Incomplete Clinical Data.

A total of 2571 eligible subjects participated in the cross‐sectional study to investigate the association between the utilization of certain commonly used drugs and the prevalence of benign and malignant colon polyps.

Blood samples were analyzed in the clinical biochemistry laboratory of the hospital's laboratory medicine department. The reference ranges are defined as follows: TC < 5.2 mmol/L, TG < 1.7 mmol/L, and fasting blood glucose within the interval of 3.9–6.1 mmol/L.

Statistical Methods

This study utilized statistical software SPSS 25.0 for conducting statistical data analysis. Count data were analyzed using the Chi‐Square test. Measurement data were compared between groups using independent sample analysis. Data with a normal distribution were analyzed using the t‐test, while data with a nonnormal distribution were analyzed using nonparametric methods, such as the rank sum test. Multivariate analysis was carried out using the logistic regression method. A p value of < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

The Characteristics of the Study Subjects in Cross‐Sectional Research

A cross‐sectional study collected data from 2571 individuals undergoing colonoscopy. Of these, 1197 were found to have no colon polyps, while 1374 were diagnosed with colon polyps. Among the 1374 cases with polyps, 1330 were classified as benign and 44 as malignant. According to pathological reports, the 1330 benign polyps comprised 1059 cases of low‐grade adenomatous polyps, 149 cases of high‐grade villous or tubular adenomatous polyps, 120 cases of inflammatory hyperplastic polyps, 1 case of hamartomatous polyps, and 1 case with an incomplete pathology report. We selected 1197 individuals without polyps as the control group and 1330 patients with benign polyps as the case group to perform statistical analysis on various clinical parameters. The results showed that the p‐values for age, gender, weight, BMI, and hypertension were all less than 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences between the two groups (see Table 1). Compared with individuals without polyps, those with benign colon polyps tended to be older, more frequently male, and exhibited higher weight, BMI, and a greater prevalence of hypertension.We selected 1330 patients with benign polyps as the control group and 44 patients with cancerous polyps as the case group to perform statistical analysis on various clinical parameters. The results showed that the p‐values for age, blood glucose, and hypertension were all less than 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences between the two groups (see Table 1). Compared with patients with benign polyps, those with cancerous polyps exhibited higher average blood glucose levels and a greater prevalence of hypertension. In individuals younger than 40 years the incidence of cancerous polyps was significantly lower, and this difference was statistically significant when compared with the incidence of benign polyps.We performed a statistical analysis of clinical data from patients in the inflammatory polyp group, adenomatous polyp group, and cancerous polyp group. The results showed that the p‐values for age and blood glucose were less than 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences (see Table 2). In the population younger than 40 years old, the incidence of cancerous polyps was significantly lower, and this difference was statistically significant compared to the incidence of both inflammatory and adenomatous polyps. Patients with cancerous polyps also exhibited higher average blood glucose levels.

The Distribution Pattern of Colon Polyps

We categorized high‐grade adenomatous polyps, low‐grade adenomatous polyps, inflammatory polyps, and cancerous polyps according to their anatomical locations identified during colonoscopy, as presented in Table 3. The distribution of different pathological types of colon polyps across anatomical segments of the colon is relatively consistent. For example, the sigmoid colon represents the most frequently affected site for all polyp types. Specifically, among patients with high‐grade adenomatous polyps, 102 cases were located in the sigmoid colon, accounting for 27.64% of this group; among those with low‐grade adenomatous polyps, 682 cases were found in the same region, representing 31.27%; in the inflammatory polyp group, 72 cases were located there, making up 33.03%; and in the cancerous polyp group, 34 cases were identified in the sigmoid colon, accounting for 77.27% of the total.

The Correlation Between Common Medications and the Prevalence of Benign and Malignant Colon Polyps

We conducted a statistical analysis of commonly used medications, including antihypertensive and antidiabetic drugs, that patients regularly took. By comparing subjects in the non‐polyp group with those in the adenomatous polyp group, as well as those with single benign colon polyps versus multiple benign polyps, we aimed to investigate the potential associations between medication use and the prevalence of different types of colon polyps. A comparison between patients in the nonpolyp group and those in the adenomatous polyp group revealed that the p values for nifedipine, aspirin, statins, and insulin were all below 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences in medication use between the two groups (Table 4). The presence of patients concurrently using multiple medications, such as both aspirin and clopidogrel or combinations of different hypoglycemic agents, may introduce potential drug interactions that could lead to data bias. Given the relatively small proportion of this subgroup and the limited available data, we did not perform separate classification or statistical analysis for these cases. This aspect, however, holds significant research value and warrants further investigation once more clinical data are collected.

Discussion

The medical problems posed by colorectal cancer are becoming increasingly severe on a global scale. A considerable proportion of colorectal cancer emerges from benign colonic polyps. If the occurrence of colonic polyps can be mitigated, it can indirectly lower the incidence of colorectal cancer. This study conducted a comprehensive statistical analysis of the association between commonly used clinical medications and colon polyps, providing valuable clinical evidence for research on the chemical prevention of CRC.

In this study, a cross‐sectional analysis of subject characteristics revealed statistically significant differences in age, gender (male), weight, and BMI between patients with benign colon polyps and those without colon polyps. These findings are consistent with existing literature [3]. Furthermore, the prevalence of hypertension was significantly higher in the colon polyp group. Previous studies [8] have indicated that the use of antihypertensive medications may serve as a risk factor for colorectal polyps, with an increased risk associated with greater intensity of antihypertensive drug use—a result that aligns with the findings of the present study.

Patients with cancerous polyps exhibited higher average blood glucose levels and a greater prevalence of hypertension compared to those with benign polyps. In individuals younger than 40 years, the incidence of cancerous polyps was relatively low. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that patients with diabetes or metabolic syndrome are at increased risk for CRC [9], and that there is a positive association between blood glucose levels and CRC risk [10, 11].

The findings of this study are largely consistent with those reported in existing literature, suggesting that the sample selection was not subject to substantial bias and that the results are of relatively high reliability.

Several drugs have demonstrated potential chemopreventive effects against colon cancer, as evidenced by extensive epidemiological, preclinical, and clinical studies. Established chemopreventive agents for colon cancer, as reported in the literature, include nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, including aspirin), statins, agents targeting metabolic pathways, as well as vitamins and minerals. NSAIDs, including aspirin, are currently the most extensively investigated chemopreventive agents for colon cancer. In this study, a statistically significant difference was observed in the prevalence of aspirin use between the polyp‐free group and the adenomatous polyp group. The proportion of patients using aspirin was significantly higher in the polyp‐free group, suggesting a potential protective effect of aspirin against the development of colorectal polyps. Existing literature supports this observation, indicating that regular aspirin use can effectively reduce the risk of both colorectal polyps and colorectal cancer. Aspirin has been shown to effectively reduce the risk of colorectal polyps and CRC [12]. Studies indicate that long‐term aspirin use among American adults over 50 years old, irrespective of age, gender, race, BMI, and smoking status, is associated with a lower risk of CRC [13].Nonaspirin nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NA‐NSAIDs), including COX‐2 inhibitors, have been shown to reduce the number of polyps in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and provide chemoprotection against colorectal cancer. Metformin exerts its protective effects against colorectal carcinogenesis through several mechanisms. Insulin. The relationship between insulin and its associated metabolic abnormalities, such as hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance, and the development of colorectal polyps or colorectal cancer has emerged as a research focus in recent years. Elevated insulin levels are recognized as a significant risk factor for colorectal adenomas, while insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia may facilitate the progression of these precancerous lesions. This hypothesis is supported by multiple studies and is consistent with the findings of this clinical investigation.

Studies from South Korea have demonstrated that elevated insulin levels are a significant risk factor for colonic adenomas [29]. Research in China has indicated that the occurrence of colorectal polyps is associated with insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism [30]. A meta‐analysis published in the British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology [31] reported that insulin therapy significantly increases the risk of colorectal cancer, with a relative risk (RR) of 1.69 for patients receiving insulin treatment. This meta‐analysis included 12 epidemiological studies involving 7947 cases of colorectal cancer and 491,384 participants, suggesting a potential association between insulin therapy and an increased risk of colorectal cancer. A study published in JAMA Network Open [32] found that the duration of diabetes correlates with the risk of colorectal cancer, with patients having diabetes for 2–5 years exhibiting a higher risk, possibly due to the long‐term effects of hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia.

It is widely accepted that insulin may contribute to the development and progression of colorectal tumors through mechanisms such as promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis: (1) The insulin/insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) signaling pathway can activate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, thereby promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis, which may facilitate the carcinogenesis of colonic epithelial cells. Hyperinsulinemia, commonly observed in patients with diabetes and obesity, may indirectly promote tumor growth by upregulating IGF‐1 levels [33]. (2) Insulin resistance is frequently associated with chronic low‐grade inflammation and oxidative stress; inflammatory factors such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α have been linked to an increased risk of colorectal cancer [34]. (3) Insulin resistance and diabetes may alter the composition of the gut microbiota, leading to an increase in pro‐inflammatory bacteria, which can indirectly compromise the colonic mucosal barrier and elevate the risk of carcinogenesis.

Long‐term and high‐dose insulin therapy may be associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer, potentially due to a persistent state of hyperinsulinemia. However, some studies suggest that the elevated risk may be attributable to diabetes itself rather than to insulin treatment [35]. Additionally, several reports indicate that the use of long‐acting insulin analogues is not associated with an overall increase in colorectal cancer risk [36]. Therefore, the relationship between insulin therapy and the development of colorectal polyps or colorectal cancer requires further investigation.

Nifedipine and insulin are commonly prescribed medications for elderly patients in clinical practice. From the perspective of colon cancer prevention, high‐risk patients may consider adjusting their medication regimens as appropriate to reduce the risk of developing colorectal polyps and colorectal cancer. Currently, there is limited research on the potential association between nifedipine and insulin use and the incidence of colorectal neoplasms, particularly concerning nifedipine. The present study has a limited sample size and is a single‐center investigation, which may introduce selection bias. There is also a lack of long‐term, large‐sample prospective cohort studies and molecular mechanism validation. Future research should focus on conducting large‐scale, well‐designed clinical studies to investigate the relationship between nifedipine, insulin, and colorectal neoplasms, with larger sample sizes, extended follow‐up periods, and consideration of influencing factors such as dosage, duration of treatment, underlying diseases, and confounding factors like metabolic disorders.
