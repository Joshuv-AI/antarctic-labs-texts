Improved bounds for connection probabilities in random loop models
Volker Betz, Andreas Klippel, Julian Nauth

Abstract
We revisit and extend results by Ueltschi [ 19 ] on the application of reflection positivity to loop models with θ ∈ ℕ ≥ 2 \theta\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2} . By exploiting additional flexibility in the method, we prove the existence of long loops over a broader range of parameters u u and θ \theta , and establish new lower bounds for connection probabilities and the critical parameter β c \beta_{c} . Our results are compared with recent numerical simulations, providing further insight into the phase diagram of quantum spin systems.



1
Introduction

In the last decades, many physical models have been investigated using probabilistic graphical representations. A class of such models are random loop models, which represent certain quantum spin systems. For the ferromagnetic quantum Heisenberg model, such a loop
model was introduced by Powers
[
16
]
and then used by Tóth
[
18
]
to give a lower bound on the pressure. A different random loop model serves as a representation of the anti-ferromagnetic Heisenberg model, as found by Aizenman and Nachtergaele
[
1
]
. Ueltschi
[
19
]
observed that these are two instances of a one-parameter family of loop models representing XXZ-models. For the definition of this family we refer to section 2.

This family of loop models is built using two independent Poisson processes on the space
E\times[0,1)_{\text{per}}
, where
E
are nearest neighbor edges. The first process, with intensity
u\beta
, generates crosses, while the second, with intensity
(1-u)\beta
, generates double bars.
Starting from a vertex
x
at time
t
, we construct loops by moving upwards along the time axis until encountering either a cross or a double bar. Upon reaching a cross or double bar, we jump to the nearest right neighbor. If we encounter a double bar, we reverse direction along the time axis, whereas if we encounter a cross, we also change direction. We continue this process until we return to the starting point, forming a closed trajectory in
V\times[0,1)_{\text{per}}
. The connection to spin-
S
quantum systems requires an additional parameter
\theta
, which reweights the measure by a factor of
\theta^{|\mathcal{L}|}
with
\theta=2S+1
.
This introduces an additional dependency into the model, making its analysis particularly challenging.
The main question of interest is whether a phase transition occurs or not.
For graphs with a sufficiently high vertex degree, one expects a phase transition in the sense that there exists a critical (loop) parameter
\beta_{c}>0
such that:

1.

for
\beta<\beta_{c}
, only finite loops occur, and

2.

for
\beta>\beta_{c}
, infinite loops appear almost surely.

A necessary condition for (b) is the existence of an infinite percolation cluster induced by the Poisson processes.
A remarkable result by Schramm
[
17
]
shows in the case
u=1
and
\theta=1
that on the complete graph
K_{n}
, where edge rates are
\beta/n
, the existence of infinite loop and the emergence of an infinite percolation cluster are asymptotically equivalent as
n\to\infty
, implying that their critical parameters coincide. Moreover, it is shown that the normalised ordered sizes of the loops in the giant component exhibit a Poisson-Dirichlet
\operatorname{PD}(1)
structure.

For graphs with uniformly bounded degree, it was shown in
[
15
]
that the existence of infinite percolation is not a sufficient condition. In this setting, there exists a gap between critical values, for which a lower bound is given in
[
5
]
. A similar phenomenon was observed for a wide class of trees in
[
14
]
.
In general to show (b) is a challenging problem.
Nonetheless, there are some cases which are well understood.

On regular trees, Angel
[
2
]
established the existence of two distinct phases for
\theta=1
on
d
-regular trees with
d\geq 4
and showed that infinite loops appear for
\beta\in(d^{-1}+\tfrac{13}{6}d^{-2},\log(3))
when
d
is sufficiently large. Hammond
[
12
]
later proved that for
d\geq 2
, there exists a critical value
\beta_{0}
above which infinite loops emerge in the case
u=1
, a result extended by Hammond and Hegde
[
13
]
to all
u\in[0,1]
. Björnberg and Ueltschi provided an asymptotic expression for the critical parameter for loops up to second order in
d^{-1}

[
7
]
and further extended this analysis to the case
\theta\neq 1

[
8
]
. A comprehensive picture for
d
-regular trees with
d\geq 3
,
\theta=1
, and
u\in[0,1]
was established by Betz et al. in
[
4
]
, proving a (locally) sharp phase transition for infinite loops in the interval
(0,d^{-1/2})
and deriving explicit bounds on the critical parameter up to order 5 in
d^{-1}
.

A first result for (b) on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 5
in the case of
\theta=1
and
u=1
was given in
[
10
]
.
For
\theta\neq 1
and
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
, there are results proving the existence of a phase transition in dimensions three and higher using the method of reflection positivity introduced by Fröhlich, Simon, and Spencer for the classical Heisenberg model in
[
11
]
, and introduced to the quantum case by Dyson, Lieb, and Simon
[
9
]
. The latter corresponds to the quantum Heisenberg antiferromagnet, which is equivalent to the loop model with
\theta=2
and
u=0
. Ueltschi managed to rigorously adapt the method to the random loop model in
[
19
]
and demonstrated that it remains applicable for various values of
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and
u\in[0,1/2]
.

The purpose of this paper is to revisit end extend the results of
[
19
]
. We use some additional flexibility that is inherent in the method, but not exploited in
[
19
]
. This allows to prove long loops for a larger range of values
\theta
and
u
, and to obtain lower bounds on nearest-neighbor, finite-range, and long-range connections probabilities. We also give lower bounds for the critical value
\beta_{c}
and compare them with numerical studies from
[
3
]
.

Outline of the remaining sections.

In Section 2, we introduce the model and some of its basic properties. Moreover, we state our main theorem and provide its proof. In Section 3, we present all the consequences of our theorem and prove them.

2
Model and results

The random loop model is well-established and precisely defined in various papers, such as in
[
19
]
and
[
4
]
. However, we provide here a brief overview of the main objects.

The model can be defined on any graph
G
, but in this paper, we only consider the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathcal{G}_{L}=(\Lambda_{L},\mathcal{E}_{L})
with side length
L
, where
\Lambda_{L}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}^{d}/L\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is the set of vertices, and the edge set
\mathcal{E}_{L}
consists of nearest neighbors, with periodic boundary conditions.

Let
u\in[0,1]
and
\beta>0
. We attach the interval
[0,\beta)
to each edge of the graph, and will interpret
t\in[0,\beta)
as a time parameter. On the space
\mathcal{E}_{L}\times[0,\beta)
, we define two independent Poisson point processes: one with intensity
u
that produces so-called crosses, and another with intensity
1-u
that produces double bars. A link is either a cross or a double bar. The combined configuration space is denoted by
\Omega(G)
, with probability measure
\rho_{G,\beta,u}
. For a realization
\omega\in\Omega(G)
, we denote by
M
the number of links.

The crosses and double bars form loops, defined by the following rules:

•

We start at a point
(x,t)\in V\times[0,\beta)
and move in the positive time direction, thereby exploring links on edges adjacent to
x
.

•

If we reach time
\tau
and encounter a double bar at
(\{x,y\},\tau-)
, we jump to
(y,\tau)
and change time direction.

•

If we reach time
\tau
and encounter a cross at
(\{x,y\},\tau-)
, we jump to
(y,\tau)
and continue in the same time direction.

•

If, for any vertex
z
, we reach
(z,\beta)
whilst moving in the positive time direction then we jump to
(z,0)
and continue moving in the positive time direction. If we reach
(z,0)
whilst moving in the negative time direction then we jump to
(z,\beta)
and continue moving in the negative time direction.

•

If we reach
(x,t)
again, the loop is complete.

We observe that loops are closed trajectories in the space
\Lambda_{L}\times[0,\beta)
.

We denote the set of loops for a given configuration
\omega
by
\mathcal{L}(\omega)
and fix
\theta\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. We then color these loops uniformly with color
i\in[\theta]
and reweight the measure by the number of colors. The loop-weighted measure is defined by

\mathbb{P}_{G,\beta,u,\theta}(\omega)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\widetilde{Z}}\theta^{|\mathcal{L}(\omega)|}\rho_{G,\beta,u},

where
\widetilde{Z}
is the partition function of the random loop model, given by

\widetilde{Z}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega(G)}\rho_{G,\beta,u}\,\theta^{|\mathcal{L}(\omega)|}\,\mathrm{d}\omega.

We denote the measure where the underlying graph is the torus with side-length
L
by

\mathbb{P}_{L,\beta,u,\theta}.

Note that this model is typically defined more generally for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Since we are only interested in the probabilistic interpretation, we restrict ourselves to natural numbers and set the color set as
[\theta]=\{1,\ldots,\theta\}
. For an introduction that includes
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we recommend
[
19
]
.

Based on this framework, we define the concept of connection within the model. Two points
(x,0)
and
(y,t)
are said to be connected through time
t
, denoted by
E_{x,y,t}
, if there exists a loop connecting them through time
t
that adheres to the rules outlined above. This connection indicates that the two points belong to the same closed trajectory within the random loop configuration.
Furthermore, we denote the infinite volume measure by

\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}\coloneqq\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{L,\beta,u,\theta}.

The existence of these limits has been discussed in
[
6
]
.

For the statement of Theorem
2.1
below, let

c=(c_{\ell})_{0\leq\ell\leq m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
u\in[0,1]
and
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}(\alpha)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\sqrt{u\alpha+(1-u)(1-\alpha)\frac{\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\epsilon(k)}}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}\,,

(1)

with
k+\pi\coloneqq(k_{j}+\pi)_{j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}
,

\epsilon(k)\coloneqq 2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(1-\cos(k_{j})\big)

for
k\in[0,2\pi]^{d}
, and
x_{+}\coloneqq\max\{0,x\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, let

I^{u,d}_{c}\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}(\alpha)\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{I}^{d}_{c}\coloneqq\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{1}{\epsilon(k)}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}.

(2)

Note that
\tilde{I}_{c}^{d}<\infty
if and only if
either
\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}=0
or
d\geq 3
.

Our main result reads as follows:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
\theta\geq 2
,
u\in[0,1/2]
,
\beta>0
and
e\in\Lambda_{L}
be a unit vector in the lattice geometry. Let further
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{m-1},c_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\bigg)\lim_{|\Lambda|\rightarrow\infty}\bigg(\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}(E_{0,x,0})\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\theta,u}\big(E_{0,\ell e,0}\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\theta\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\theta,u}\big(E_{0,e,0}\big)}{2}}I^{u,d}_{c}-\frac{\theta}{2\beta}\tilde{I}^{d}_{c}\,.

The result of
[
19
]
covers the special cases
c=(1,0)
and
c=(0,1)
above. In these cases,
the supremum involved in the definition of
I_{c}^{u,d}
is taken at
\alpha=1
. We will discuss this below,
but first we give a proof of our theorem that starts from the relevant estimates given in
[
19
]
and
only adds those parts that need to be added and modified.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We work with the Fourier transform of the connection probabilities. Let us therefore introduce the notation

\kappa(x,t)=\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}(E_{0,x,t}).

Then the Fourier transform is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}(k,t)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\Lambda}e^{-ik\cdot x}\kappa(x,t),\quad\tilde{\kappa}(x,\tau)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\beta}\text{d}t\,e^{-i\tau t}\kappa(x,t),

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{\kappa}}(k,\tau)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\int_{0}^{\beta}\text{d}t\,e^{-i\tau t-ik\cdot x}\kappa(x,t),

where
k
belongs to the set

\Lambda^{*}=\bigg\{k\in\tfrac{2\pi}{L}\mathbb{Z}^{d}:-\pi<k_{i}\leq\pi,\,i=1,\dots,d\bigg\},

and
\tau\in\tfrac{2\pi}{\beta}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Since
\kappa(x,t)
is symmetric w.r.t.
x
and
t
, the complex oscillation

can equally be replaced by a cosine.

Let

. We will use the following two inequalities from

[
19
]
:
The first inequality in
[
19

yields

. The necessary argument needs quantum spin notation and follows the one in
[

rather closely, which is why we don’t give it here. For the convenience of the reader, it is detailed in the appendix. Inequality (5.54) of the same reference states that for

and
\theta=2,3,\dots
, we have

\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\leq\theta\sqrt{\tfrac{\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}(E_{0,e,0})}{2\epsilon(k)}}\sqrt{u\epsilon(k)\alpha^{\prime}+(1-u)(1-\alpha^{\prime})\epsilon(k+\pi)}+\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\theta}{2\epsilon(k)},

(3)

where
\alpha^{\prime}\in[0,1]
is obtained from a conditional probability introduced in

19

. The value of
\alpha^{\prime}
is not known, which is why in the statement of Theorem

2.1
the supremum over
\alpha

By definition

\displaystyle|\Lambda|\kappa(\ell e_{j},0)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\Lambda^{*}}e^{-i\ell e_{j}\cdot k}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\hat{\kappa}(0,0)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}e^{-i\ell k_{j}}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\kappa(x,0)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\cos(\ell k_{j})\,.

Multiplication with

and summation over

gives

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\right)\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\kappa(x,0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\kappa(\ell e_{j},0)-\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{m}\cos(\ell k_{j})\,.

(4)

Summation over

and multiplying with
1/d
leads to

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\right)\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\kappa(x,0)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\bigg(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\kappa(\ell e_{j},0)\bigg)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\cos(\ell k_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\kappa(\ell e,0)-\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j}),

(5)

where we used the lattice symmetry for
\kappa(\ell e_{j},0)
.
Since
\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\geq 0
, which is proven in the Appendix, taking the positive part yields an upper bound

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\right)\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\kappa(x,0)\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}c_{\ell}\kappa(\ell e,0)-\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\right)_{+}\,.

Now we use (

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\right)\frac{1}{|\Lambda|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\kappa(x,0)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}c_{\ell}\kappa(\ell e,0)-\frac{\theta}{2\beta|\Lambda|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\epsilon(k)}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\right)_{+}

\displaystyle-\frac{\theta}{|\Lambda|}\sqrt{\frac{p_{e,0}}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\Lambda^{*}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{u\alpha^{\prime}+(1-u)(1-\alpha^{\prime})\frac{\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\epsilon(k)}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\right)_{+}.

We end this section with a discussion of the quantity
{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}(\alpha)
given in (

).
We first by recall a tool introduced in

]
that can be applied for
relevant choices of

.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
u\in[0,1/2]
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
.

•

If
({\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c})^{\prime}(0)\leq 0
, then
{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}(\alpha)
takes its supremum at
\alpha=0
.

•

If
({\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c})^{\prime}(1)\geq 0
, then
{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}(\alpha)
takes its supremum at
\alpha=1
.

Proof.

We consider the function

\displaystyle f_{k}(\alpha)\coloneqq\sqrt{u\alpha+(1-u)(1-\alpha)\tfrac{\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\epsilon(k)}}\,,

covering the dependence on
\alpha
of
{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}
inside the integral. Its derivatives read as

\displaystyle f_{k}^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{u\epsilon(k)-(1-u)\epsilon(k+\pi)}{2\sqrt{\epsilon(k)}\sqrt{u\alpha\epsilon(k)+(1-u)(1-\alpha)\epsilon(k+\pi)}}\,,

\displaystyle f_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(u\epsilon(k)-(1-u)\epsilon(k+\pi))^{2}}{4\sqrt{\epsilon(k)}\sqrt{u\alpha\epsilon(k)+(1-u)(1-\alpha)\epsilon(k+\pi)}^{3}}\,.

{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}
is concave since
f^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)\leq 0
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The claims follow from standard techniques using the mean value theorem.
∎

This tool allows to compute the case
u=0
straightforwardly. We set

\displaystyle J^{d}_{c}\coloneqq{\mathcal{I}}_{c}^{0,d}(0)=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\epsilon(k)}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\right)_{+}\,.

(7)

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. If
u=0
, then
I^{u,d}_{c}=J^{d}_{c}
with

defined in Eq. (

Proof.

In the case of

, the derivative of
{\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c}(\alpha)
simplifies to

\displaystyle({\mathcal{I}}^{0,d}_{c})^{\prime}(\alpha)=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{-\epsilon(k+\pi)}{2\sqrt{(1-\alpha)\epsilon(k)\epsilon(k+\pi)}}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}\leq 0

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Applying Lemma

and computing
{\mathcal{I}}^{0,d}_{c}(0)
yields the claim.
∎

For general
u\in[0,1/2]
, this statement equally holds true under certain conditions for the coefficients
c
:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
u\in[0,1/2]
, and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let further
c\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
fulfill
c_{1}\geq 0
and
c_{\ell}=0
for all odd

. Then,
I^{u,d}_{c}={\mathcal{I}}_{c}^{u,d}(0)=\sqrt{1-u}J^{d}_{c}

Proof.

In keeping with Lemma
2.2
, we consider

\displaystyle({\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c})^{\prime}(0)=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{u\epsilon(k)-(1-u)\epsilon(k+\pi)}{2\sqrt{(1-u)\epsilon(k)\epsilon(k+\pi)}}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2(1-u)}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{\epsilon(k)-\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\sqrt{\epsilon(k)\epsilon(k+\pi)}}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}

using that

and thus

. Next, we abbreviate

. Noting that

\displaystyle({\mathcal{I}}^{u,d}_{c})^{\prime}(0)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-u)}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{-g(k)}{\sqrt{\big(1-g(k)\big)\big(1+g(k)\big)}}

\displaystyle\times\bigg(c_{1}g(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
\ell\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}\,,

(8)

We split the integral into the cases

to give

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}k\,\textbf{1}_{g(k)<0}\frac{-g(k)}{\sqrt{1-g(k)^{2}}}\bigg(c_{1}g(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}k\,\textbf{1}_{g(k)>0}\frac{g(k)}{\sqrt{1-g(k)^{2}}}\bigg(-c_{1}g(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\
\ell\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})\bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\mathrm{d}^{d}k\,\textbf{1}_{g(k)>0}\frac{g(k)}{\sqrt{1-g(k)^{2}}}\bigg(c_{1}g(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=0\\

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
c=(c_{0},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
and

c_{\infty}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}c_{\ell}
.
For

N\in\mathbb{N}
with
N>n+1
set

c(N)=(c_{0},\ldots,c_{n},0,\ldots,0,c_{\infty})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad J^{d}_{c,N}=J^{d}_{c(N)},\quad\tilde{I}^{d}_{c,N}=\tilde{I}^{d}_{c(N)}.

Then

J^{d}_{c,\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}J^{d}_{c,N}=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{2d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\epsilon(k)}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}\,,

and

\tilde{I}^{d}_{c,\infty}\coloneqq\lim_{N\to\infty}\tilde{I}^{d}_{c,N}=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{2d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{1}{\epsilon(k)}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}\,.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle J^{d}_{c(N)}=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon(\tilde{k}+\pi)}{\epsilon(\tilde{k})}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell\tilde{k}_{j})+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(N\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi N]^{d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi N)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\big(\frac{\tilde{k}}{N}+\pi\big)}{\epsilon\big(\frac{\tilde{k}}{N}\big)}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos\Big(\ell\frac{\tilde{k}_{j}}{N}\Big)+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}+2\pi k}\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi N)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\big(\frac{\tilde{k}}{N}+\pi\big)}{\epsilon\big(\frac{\tilde{k}}{N}\big)}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos\Big(\ell\frac{\tilde{k}_{j}}{N}\Big)+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi N)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\big(\frac{\tilde{k}+2\pi k}{N}+\pi\big)}{\epsilon\big(\frac{\tilde{k}+2\pi k}{N}\big)}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos\Big(\ell\frac{\tilde{k}_{j}+2\pi k_{j}}{N}\Big)+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}\,.

For
N\rightarrow\infty
, the term
\tilde{k}/N
vanishes as
\tilde{k}\in[0,2\pi]^{d}
is bounded. It should be kept in mind that the integrand does not exhibit any singularity owing to
\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}c_{\ell}+c_{\infty}=0
. The result follows from a Riemann integral as

\displaystyle J^{d}_{c(N)}=

\displaystyle\,\,\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{k\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}^{d}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\big(\frac{2\pi k}{N}+\pi\big)}{\epsilon\big(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\big)}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos\Big(\ell\frac{2\pi k_{j}}{N}\Big)+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\int_{[0,2\pi]^{2d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon(k+\pi)}{\epsilon(k)}}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}\,.

(13)

In the same manner, we find that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{I}^{d}_{c(N)}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{2d}}\frac{\text{d}^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{\text{d}^{d}\tilde{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\,\frac{1}{\epsilon(k)}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\frac{c_{\ell}}{d}\cos(\ell k_{j})+\frac{c_{\infty}}{d}\cos(\tilde{k}_{j})\Big)\bigg)_{+}\,.

(14)

We prove that
\hat{\kappa}(k,0)\geq 0
for all
k\in\Lambda^{*}
based on Eq. (3.24) in
[
19
]
. Since it involves a physical notation, we first summarize some basics.

For
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
, we set the spin
S\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
such that
\theta=2S+1
. The Hilbert space reads as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{H}_{x}\,,

where each
\mathcal{H}_{x}
is a copy of
\mathbb{C}^{2S+1}
and is spanned by
(|s\rangle)_{s\in\{-S,-S+1,\ldots,S\}}
. The spin operator
S^{3}_{x}
acts on
\mathcal{H}_{x}
as
S^{3}_{x}|s\rangle_{x}=s|s\rangle_{x}
.
To construct the random loop model, we associate crosses and double bars with operators
T_{x,y},Q_{x,y}
for
\{x,y\}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{L}
as

\displaystyle T_{x,y}|a,b\rangle\coloneqq|b,a\rangle,\quad Q_{x,y}|a,b\rangle\coloneqq\textbf{1}_{a=b}\sum_{c=-S}^{S}|c,c\rangle

(17)

on
\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
. This serves to introduce the family of Hamiltonians

\displaystyle H_{u}\coloneqq-\sum_{\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{E}}\left(uT_{x,y}+(1-u)Q_{x,y}-1\right)

(18)

for
u\in[0,1]
. Next, we let
A,B:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
be hermitian and define the partition function

Z\coloneqq\text{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta H_{u}}\right]

as well as the expectation

\displaystyle\langle A\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z}\text{Tr}\left[A\,e^{-\beta H_{u}}\right]\,,

the Schwinger function

\displaystyle\langle A;B\rangle(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{Z}\,\text{Tr}\left[A^{\dagger}e^{-tH_{u}}Be^{-(\beta-t)H_{u}}\right]\,,

and the Duhamel two-point function

\displaystyle(A,B)_{\text{Duh}}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\beta}\text{d}t\,\langle A;B\rangle(t)\,,

which is a scalar product. Theorem 3.3 in
[
19
]
states that

\displaystyle\langle S^{3}_{x};S^{3}_{x^{\prime}}\rangle(t)=\frac{1}{3}S(S+1)\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}(E_{x,x^{\prime},t})

Translation symmetry in
\Lambda
gives
\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}(E_{x,x^{\prime},t})=\mathbb{P}_{\beta,u,\theta}(E_{0,x^{\prime}-x,t})
and thus simplifies

\displaystyle\langle S^{3}_{x};S^{3}_{x^{\prime}}\rangle(t)=\frac{1}{3}S(S+1)\kappa(x^{\prime}-x,t)

along with the special case

\displaystyle\langle S^{3}_{x}S^{3}_{x^{\prime}}\rangle=\langle S^{3}_{x};S^{3}_{x^{\prime}}\rangle(0)=\frac{1}{3}S(S+1)\kappa(x^{\prime}-x,0)

(19)

Finally, we return to the proof of
\kappa(k,0)\geq 0
. The first inequality in Eq. (3.24) in [4] claims

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta}(A,A)_{\text{Duh}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\langle A^{\dagger}A+AA^{\dagger}\rangle\,.

Moreover, the Duhamel two-point function fulfills
(A,A)_{\text{Duh}}\geq 0
as a scalar product, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle A^{\dagger}A+AA^{\dagger}\rangle\geq 0\,.

To conclude the proof, we let
k\in\Lambda^{*}
and set
A\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\Lambda}e^{-ik\cdot x}S^{3}_{x}
to give

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle A^{\dagger}A+AA^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\langle S^{3}_{x}S^{3}_{x^{\prime}}\rangle\left(e^{-ik\cdot x}e^{ik\cdot x^{\prime}}+e^{ik\cdot x}e^{-ik\cdot x^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{S(S+1)}{3}\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in\Lambda}\kappa(x^{\prime}-x,0)\cos\big(k\cdot(x^{\prime}-x)\big)

\displaystyle=|\Lambda|\frac{S(S+1)}{3}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\kappa(x,0)\cos(k\cdot x)

\displaystyle=|\Lambda|\frac{S(S+1)}{3}\hat{\kappa}(k,0)

using Eq. (
