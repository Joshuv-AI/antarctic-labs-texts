Vectors from Larger Language Models Predict Human Reading Time and fMRI Data More Poorly when Dimensionality Expansion is Controlled
Yi-Chien Lin, Hongao Zhu, William Schuler

Abstract
The impressive linguistic abilities of large language models (LLMs) have recommended them as models of human sentence processing, with some conjecturing a positive ‘quality-power’ relationship Wilcox et al. 2023 , in which language models’ (LMs’) fit to psychometric data continues to improve as their ability to predict words in context increases. This is important because it suggests that elements of LLM architecture, such as veridical attention to context and a unique objective of predicting upcoming words, reflect the architecture of the human sentence processing faculty, and that any inadequacies in predicting human reading time and brain imaging data may be attributed to insufficient model complexity, which recedes as larger models become available. Recent studies ( Oh and Schuler 2023 ) have shown this scaling inverts after a point, as LMs become excessively large and accurate, when word prediction probability (as information-theoretic surprisal) is used as a predictor. Other studies propose the use of entire vectors from differently sized LLMs, still showing positive scaling Schrimpf et al. 2021 , casting doubt on the value of surprisal as a predictor, but do not control for the larger number of predictors in vectors from larger LMs. This study evaluates LLM scaling using entire LLM vectors, while controlling for the larger number of predictors in vectors from larger LLMs. Results show that inverse scaling obtains, suggesting that inadequacies in predicting human reading time and brain imaging data may be due to substantial misalignment between LLMs and human sentence processing, which worsens as larger models are used.



1
Introduction

There has been considerable interest in predicting reading times and brain imaging data using predictors from LLMs
(
Goodkind and Bicknell 2018
;
Schrimpf et al. 2021
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
)
, with some conjecturing a positive ‘quality-power’ effect of LLM word probabilities on psychometric fit
(
Wilcox et al. 2023
)
, favoring larger models trained on more data.
This is important because it suggests that elements of LLM architecture, such as veridical attention to context and a unique objective of predicting upcoming words, reflect the architecture of the human sentence processing faculty, and that any inadequacies in predicting human reading time and brain imaging data may be attributed to insufficient model complexity, which recedes as larger models become available.
Earlier results have shown effects for surprisal (negative log probability) of words in context
(
Hale 2001
;
Levy 2008
)
, but there have been questions about the linear or superlinear nature of surprisal
(
Hoover et al. 2023
)
, and these effects have been shown to attenuate as successive versions of LLMs predict words with greater accuracy
(
Oh et al. 2022
;
Oh and Schuler 2023
)
, contradicting the quality-power hypothesis.

Recent experiments
(
Schrimpf et al. 2021
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
)
have shown improved fit to reading times and brain imaging data as LLM perplexity decreases when using entire LLM vectors as predictors, supporting the quality-power hypothesis and circumventing some questions about the role of surprisal.
In particular,
Schrimpf et al. 2021
vary the perplexity of the evaluated models by varying the sizes of the models used in prediction.
However, moving away from surprisal, these experiments introduce a potential confound in that they not only vary the size (and perplexity) of the LMs in their studies, they also simultaneously vary the vector size, and thus the number of predictors, which increases the number of degrees of freedom of the regression.
Previous work has shown that large sets of predictors derived from
even random
high-dimensional models can improve regression fit
(
Maass et al. 2002
;
Zhang and Bowman 2018
)
.
A larger number of predictors gives a regression more degrees of freedom, or more opportunities to find strong predictors.
This is a basis of ‘liquid state machines’
(
Maass et al. 2002
)
in which a single neural layer is trained as a classifier using a larger untrained neural network as input.

This study attempts to control for this confound in degrees of freedom due to the use of vectors from differently sized LMs as predictors.
Experiments described in this paper first replicate the increase in model fit with increasing word probability observed by
Schrimpf et al. 2021
when using vectors from differently sized pre-trained LMs as predictors.
Each trained model is then fit against the residual error resulting from subtracting predictions of vectors from an untrained model with the same architecture.

Results no longer show positive scaling, and indeed show inverse scaling for the trained models when residualized against their untrained counterparts, suggesting that the number of predictors is indeed a confound.
When dimensionality expansion is controlled, larger LLMs are not contributing more as they get larger, beyond the effects of degrees of freedom.
Moreover, this observed trend away from improving model fit may also indicate that there may be a substantial misalignment between LLMs and human sentence processing, which worsens when larger models are used.

Experiment 1: Predictive Power of Vectors from Pre-trained LLMs

Experiment 1 examines the predictive power of vector elements from pre-trained models in three LM families, GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
, GPT-Neo
(
Black et al. 2021
;
Black et al. 2022
;
Wang and Komatsuzaki 2021
)
, and OPT
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, on reading times and brain imaging data.

2.1
Response Data

The response data considered in this study includes one self-paced reading (SPR; Item
1
), two eye-tracking (ET; Items
2
and
3
), and two functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI; Items
4
and
5
) corpora.

1.

Futrell et al. 2021
(
Natural Stories SPR
):
The Natural Stories Corpus includes SPR times from 181 participants that read 10 naturalistic English stories.
Each participant was asked to answer six comprehension questions after reading each story.

2.

Kennedy et al. 2003
(
Dundee ET
):
The Dundee Corpus includes fixation durations from 10 subjects who read 67 English newspaper articles.

3.

Luke and Christianson 2018
(
Provo ET
):
The Provo Corpus includes fixation durations from 84 subjects who read 55 short English passages.

4.

Shain et al. 2020
(
Natural Stories fMRI
):
The Natural Stories fMRI corpus includes the time series of blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals collected with fMRI from the language network
(
Fedorenko et al. 2011
)
of 78 subjects, identified using a localizer task.
The fMRI data, which includes the BOLD signals of several functional regions of interest (fROIs), was collected at a two-second fixed time interval while the subjects listen to a recording of the Natural Stories Corpus, consisting of naturalistic English stories.

5.

Pereira et al. 2018
(
Pereira fMRI
):
The Pereira fMRI corpus includes data from Experiments II and III in
Pereira et al. 2018
and the responses are per-sentence BOLD signals.
Experiment II consists of 384 sentences read by eight subjects and Experiment III consists of 243 sentences read by six subjects.
Five subjects participated in both experiments.
For both experiments, subjects were presented with one sentence at a time for four seconds and sentences were separated by a four-second interval.
Each passage was presented three times to the subjects.
To get the average activation of a seeing a sentence, we calculated a BOLD value for each sentence per subject by aggregating activation across regions.

Among the corpora analyzed in this study, Natural Stories SPR and Pereira fMRI are also analyzed in
Schrimpf et al. 2021
.

2.2
Data Preprocessing and Partitioning

Data Preprocessing.

The SPR and ET datasets were preprocessed before being divided into different partitions for regression modeling.
The preprocessing procedures we used followed recent work on these datasets
(
Oh and Schuler 2023
)
:
For Natural Stories SPR, we excluded data points (1) for sentence-initial and -final words, (2) from participants with fewer than four correct comprehension questions, and (3) outside the [100ms, 3000ms] reading time window.

For both ET datasets, again following
Oh and Schuler 2023
, we used go-past durations as a dependent variable, with data points for unfixated words, words which follow saccades that skip more than four words, sentence-initial/-final words, and document-initial/-final words omitted.
For Dundee ET
(
Kennedy et al. 2003
)
, which is accompanied by annotations of line and screen positions, data points of words at the boundaries of lines and screens were also filtered out.

Data Partitioning.

For all datasets other than Pereira fMRI, we followed the data-partitioning procedure from recent work on these datasets
(
Shain et al. 2020
;
Oh et al. 2024
)
.
After preprocessing, each dataset was partitioned into subsets before regression modeling.
The remaining data was partitioned into fit, exploratory, and held-out sets consisting of roughly 50%, 25%, and 25% of data points after preprocessing.
This resulted in 384,905, 192,772, and 192,425 data points for Natural Stories SPR; 98,115, 48,598, and 48,794 data points for Dundee ET; 52,960, 26,539, and 26,640 for Provo ET; and 100,084, 51,393, and 50,818 data points for Natural Stories fMRI.
For Pereira fMRI, due to the small amount of data, we followed
Schrimpf et al. 2021
in the procedures of data-partitioning and regression modeling (Section
2.4
), using by-subject five-fold cross-validation when fitting the regression model.
Following
Schrimpf et al. 2021
, we also treated the data from the two experiments in
Pereira et al. 2018
separately.
This resulted in roughly 308 and 76 data points respectively in the per-subject training and test folds for Experiment II from Pereira fMRI and 195 and 48 data points respectively in those for Experiment III from Pereira fMRI.

2.3
Predictors

For this first experiment, which replicates the findings of
Schrimpf et al. 2021
, we collected predictors from model variants of three autoregressive Transformer-based LM families: GPT-2
(
Radford et al. 2019
)
, GPT-Neo
(
Black et al. 2021
;
Black et al. 2022
;
Wang and Komatsuzaki 2021
)
, and OPT
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.
We used all vector elements from the final layer of those LMs as predictors because it is the layer responsible for next-word prediction.
For all SPR and ET datasets, since the response data is a reading time for each word, we obtained the word-level vector by averaging the vectors corresponding to the subword tokens.
For Natural Stories fMRI, since its response data is the time series of BOLD signals, we first collected the vectors and then applied a hemodynamic response function convolution to those vectors.
For Pereira fMRI, since its responses are per-sentence BOLD signals, we followed recent work on this dataset
(
Hosseini et al. 2024
)
by collecting the vector corresponding to the final word of each sentence.
If the final word consists of multiple subword tokens, we average across the vectors of those tokens.

2.4
Regression Modeling

Following
Schrimpf et al. 2021
, to compare the models to the human response data, for each model variant, we fit a linear regression model from the vector elements corresponding to the fit partition of the human response data.
Then, we used the regression model to generate predictions for the held-out partition.
We examined the predictive power of each model variant by measuring the Pearson correlation between its predictions and the human responses.
For Natural Stories fMRI, each regression model was fit between the BOLD signals over all fROIs in the fit partition and the predictor described in Section
2.3
.
For Pereira fMRI, as mentioned in Section
2.2
, we followed
Schrimpf et al. 2021
in the regression modeling procedure by taking the average correlation scores across all five folds of all subjects and across the two experiments.
The resulting correlation scores for Pereira fMRI were normalized with a ceiling value 0.32, which is the same value used in
Schrimpf et al. 2021
for this dataset.

Experiment 2: Predictive Power of Vectors from Untrained and Fully Trained LLMs

The previous experiment showed a positive quality-power effect when using vector elements directly from fully trained LMs as predictors.
To examine the effect of degrees of freedom of the LMs, we used Pythia models
(
Biderman et al. 2023
)
, as they provide accessibility to various checkpoints during training.
For each Pythia model variant, we collected vector elements from the model after 0 (untrained) and 143,000 (fully-trained) training steps.
We first show the predictive power of the vector elements from untrained and fully trained LMs.
These LMs were then further examined in Experiment 3 to investigate the contribution of training to the increasing predictive power of larger LMs.

In general, the procedures of this experiment follow those described in Experiment 1, except that we used the Pythia models

4
Experiment 3: Contribution of Vectors from Fully Trained LLMs Beyond Effects of Degrees of Freedom

Experiment 2 showed that for the majority of datasets being analyzed in this study, the predictive power of vector elements from LMs both without any training and with full training increases as the parameter count (model size) increases.
Experiment 3 therefore examines how much the full training process of LMs contributes beyond the effect of degrees of freedom, which is indicated by the predictive power of vector elements from untrained LMs.
We examined this by residualizing each fully trained Pythia model variant against its untrained counterpart.

4.1
Procedure

The setup of this experiment generally followed that of Experiment 2, except for the procedure of regression modeling.
For each Pythia variant, we first performed a linear regression to fit the fully trained model to the residuals (differences) between the predictions of the untrained counterpart and the human response data in the fit partition.
Predictions from the fully trained model, regressed to residualized response data from the fit partition, were then compared to the residuals (differences) between the raw human response data in the held-out partition and the predictions of the untrained counterpart, regressed to raw human response data from the fit partition.
To measure the contribution of training beyond the effect of degrees of freedom, for each variant, we examined how closely the differences (from regression model fit with
untrained
vector elements) and the predictions of the differences (from the regression model fit with
trained
vector elements) are to each other with a Pearson correlation score.
All correlation scores reported in this experiment are the raw correlation scores and were not normalized.

Compared to smaller models, if the training process of larger LMs contributes more beyond the effect of degrees of freedom, which is indicated by the predictive power of vector elements from untrained LMs, larger LMs should have higher correlation scores than those of smaller LMs.
In this case, the higher correlation scores of larger LMs would suggest that the additional training (i.e., the additional 143,000 training steps) enables vector elements from larger LMs to capture more information about human response data beyond the effect of degrees of freedom than the information captured by smaller LMs.

4.2
Results

The results of Experiment 3 are presented in Figure
3
.
Instead of increasing, the correlation scores of nearly all corpora decrease significantly as the log-transformed parameter count (model size) increases (
p
< 0.005 for Natural Stories SPR,
p
< 0.001 for Dundee ET,
p
< 0.005 for Provo ET, and
p
< 0.05 for Natural Stories fMRI).
Pereira fMRI was not significant but the relationship between parameter count and the contribution of fully trained models beyond the effect of degrees of freedom still shows a numerically negative correlation.
These results suggest that after (full) training, LLMs are not contributing more as they get larger beyond the effect of degrees of freedom.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings in the relationship between model size and predictive power when using different types of predictors from Transformer-based LMs.

Oh et al. 2022
;
Oh and Schuler 2023
;
Oh et al. 2024
, who use surprisal estimates from LMs as predictors, report an inverse scaling while
Schrimpf et al. 2021
find a positive quality-power effect when using vector elements from differently sized LMs as predictors.
The study using vectors from differently sized models introduces a potential confound in that it also simultaneously varies the number of predictors, which increases the number of degrees of freedom of the model.

This study therefore evaluates the number of predictors as a potential confound to the quality-power effect when using vector elements directly from differently sized LMs as predictors.
Experiments which residualized fully trained models against their untrained counterparts show an inverse scaling.
This suggests that the better predictive power of vector elements from larger LMs is mainly due to the effect of degrees of freedom, which is increased with the increasing number of predictors from larger LMs.
The additional training process of LMs does not contribute more as the model gets larger.
This observation suggests that there may be a substantial misalignment between LLMs and human sentence processing, which worsens when larger models are used.

Limitations

Experiments described in this study attempt to identify a confound of using vectors from differently sized LLMs as predictors to predict human sentence processing data.
LLMs evaluated in this work are trained on English text and the datasets evaluated are collected from English speakers.
Therefore, these findings may or may not be replicated in other languages.

Ethics Statement

The datasets used in this study are from published work
(
Futrell et al. 2021
;
Kennedy et al. 2003
;
Luke and Christianson 2018
;
Shain et al. 2020
;
Pereira et al. 2018
)
.
These datasets were used to examine LLMs’ ability to predict human reading times and brain imaging data.
The focus of this work is to identify a confound in the inconsistent findings in the quality-power hypothesis in previous studies.
The potential risks and harmful impacts posed by this study on the society appear to be minimal.
