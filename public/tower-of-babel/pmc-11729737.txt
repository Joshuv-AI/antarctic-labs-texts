Commentary on “Application of Double‐Tube Negative Pressure Drainage in Repair of Refractory Wounds”
Authors: Mostafa Javanian, Mohammad Barary, Majid Khalilizad, Soheil Ebrahimpour
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read the article by Sun et al., “Application of double‐tube negative pressure drainage (DTNPD) in repair of refractory wounds,” with a keen interest in the potential of this innovative approach [1]. The study offers valuable insights into the effectiveness of DTNPD in accelerating wound healing, reducing complications, and enhancing patient satisfaction. However, certain methodological aspects deserve further consideration to enhance the study's robustness and clinical relevance.

First, while the study illustrates the short‐term benefits of DTNPD, a lack of long‐term follow‐up weakens its capacity to fully assess sustained efficacy, recurrence rates, and potential complications beyond the 2 weeks. A more extended observation period is necessary to ensure a comprehensive evaluation [2]. Chronic refractory wounds typically necessitate extended observation to evaluate lasting effects.

Additionally, excluding essential variables, such as the medications used during the study (e.g., antibiotics or adjunct therapies like growth factors), make it difficult to attribute observed benefits solely to DTNPD. Further clarification regarding these variables would provide better insight into DTNPD's specific role in wound management.

Also, the study could significantly benefit from including objective biomarkers like the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), which can offer a more quantifiable assessment of inflammation and wound healing potential. Alongside clinical observations, these markers could provide a deeper and more precise understanding of DTNPD's impact on systemic responses.

Moreover, it would be beneficial to analyze the expression levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β), and platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF) to understand the biological processes underlying wound healing better. These factors are crucial in angiogenesis and tissue regeneration [3]. Previous studies have also highlighted the role of wound pH and temperature in chronic wound healing, parameters that were not addressed in this study. Additionally, the study could have explored potential venous involvement and the impact of multidrug‐resistant organisms (MDROs), which are often implicated in chronic wound infections [4].

Furthermore, the absence of peripheral vascular disease (PVD) considerations is a missed opportunity. Many patients with chronic wounds suffer from PVD, and evaluating the efficacy of DTNPD in this subgroup would be invaluable in expanding its clinical applications.

Lastly, while the study reports improvements in pain and satisfaction scores, the reliance on subjective measures underscores the need for a more standardized evaluation method that consistently captures patient outcomes.

In conclusion, Sun et al. contribute significantly to the literature on refractory wound treatment, but addressing the noted limitations could substantially enhance the study's impact. We encourage the authors to consider these aspects in future investigations to deepen the understanding of DTNPD's clinical value.
