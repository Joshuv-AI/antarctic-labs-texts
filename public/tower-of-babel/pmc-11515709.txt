Evaluation of the Steno Type 1 Risk Engine in predicting cardiovascular events in an ethnic mixed population of type 1 diabetes mellitus and its association with chronic microangiopathy complications
Authors: Isabella Cristina Paliares, Patrícia Medici Dualib, Laísa Stephane Noronha Torres, Priscila Maria Teixeira Aroucha, Bianca de Almeida-Pititto, Joao Roberto de Sa, Sérgio Atala Dib
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Steno Type 1 Risk Engine (ST1RE) was developed to aid clinical decisions in primary prevention for individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D), as existing cardiovascular (CV) risk models for the general population and type 2 diabetes tend to underestimate CV risk in T1D. However, the applicability of ST1RE in different populations remains uncertain, as prediction models developed for one population may not accurately estimate risk in another. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of the ST1RE in predicting CV events among ethnically mixed T1D individuals and its association with the progression of microangiopathy complications.MethodsA retrospective survey of 435 adults with T1D who were free of CV events at baseline was assessed by ST1RE and chronic diabetes complications at 5 and 10 years of follow-up. The estimated CV risk rates were compared with the observed rates at 5 and 10 years using statistical analyses, including Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, Hosmer-Lemeshow test, Kaplan-Meier curves analysis and Cox-regression models.ResultsAmong 435 patients (aged 25 years; interquartile range [IQR]: 21–32) with a median T1D duration of 13 years (IQR: 9–18), only 5% were categorized into the high ST1RE group. Within a median follow-up of 9.2 years (IQR 6.0-10.7), 5.5% of patients experienced a CV event (1.6%, 14.9%, and 50% from the low, moderate, and high-risk groups, respectively). The hazard ratios (HRs) for CV events were greater in the high-risk group (HR 52.02; 95% CI 18.60–145.51, p < 0.001) and in the moderate-risk group (HR 8.66; 95% CI 2.90–25.80, p < 0.001) compared to the low-risk group. The ST1RE estimated CV events were similar to the observed at 5 years (3.4% vs. 3.5%; χ2 = 10.12, p = 0.899) and 10 years (6.8% vs. 9.9%; χ2 = 14.80, p = 0.676) of follow-up. The progression of microangiopathies was greater in the high vs. low for retinopathy (p = 0.008), diabetic kidney disease (p < 0.001), peripheral neuropathy (p = 0.021), and autonomic neuropathy (p = 0.008).ConclusionsST1RE performed well in predicting CV events at 5 and 10 years of follow-up. Moreover, higher ST1RE scores were associated with the progression of microangiopathy complications in this genetically heterogeneous T1D population.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-024-02460-3.

Background

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is associated with an increased risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) compared with the age-adjusted population without diabetes, and occurs over a decade earlier than expected [1, 2]. This greater risk for cardiovascular (CV) outcomes in individuals with T1D persists even when all traditional risk factors are within the recommended target ranges, however, there is a steep association between a decrease in the number of cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) on the target and the occurrence of CV events [3].

Despite the recognized benefits of optimal glycemic control and comprehensive management of CVRFs in reducing cardiovascular mortality and morbidity in T1D individuals [4–6], substantial CVFRs undertreatment remains among these patients [7]. Notably, less than 40% of patients achieve glycemic goals, and over 80% of young adults with T1D are untreated for dyslipidemia and hypertension despite indications [7]. These findings underscore the need to enhance strategies for modifiable risk factor management in patients with T1D to reduce the risk of CVD.

Identifying individuals at elevated CV risk through stratifying tools is essential to support clinical decisions and balance the risks and benefits concerning primary prevention [8]. T1D exhibits heterogeneity, with certain individuals, particularly young ones, that may manifest a low or moderate CV risk [9, 10].

Prediction models for CVD in individuals with T1D are scarce. Among these, the Steno Type 1 Risk Engine (ST1RE) was developed to guide clinical decisions in the scope of primary prevention of patients with T1D since the CV risk models designed for the general population or for those with type 2 diabetes (T2D) have been shown to underestimate the risk of CVD in individuals with T1D [11, 12]. Based on a cohort of 4306 Danish adults with T1D, the ST1RE estimates 5- and 10-year risks of fatal or nonfatal CVD in individuals with T1D and no history of CVD [11].

In addition to its capacity to predict cardiovascular events, ST1RE has a substantial association with biomarkers indicative of preclinical atherosclerosis, including carotid plaque, arterial stiffness and coronary artery calcium score [13–17]. Furthermore, a previous study showed that the prevalence of chronic microvascular complications of diabetes, such as retinopathy and somatic or autonomic neuropathy, increases progressively across ST1RE categories [18].

However, the applicability of ST1RE in different populations remains uncertain, as it was developed and validated in a population of predominantly Danish ancestry (90% in the derivation cohort), and prediction models developed for one population may not accurately estimate risk in another population [8, 11]. Yet, when applied to an Italian population with T1D, this CV risk engine significantly overestimated the likelihood of CV events, although the limited occurrence of events (only 3) recorded during the 5-year follow-up of this study leaves a degree of uncertainty [18]. Thus, additional studies validating the role of this CV tool in different ethnic mixed populations are needed before its widespread clinical implementation. Moreover, it also remains unclear whether CVD risk could amplify the excess risk of microvascular complications in individuals with T1D.

This study aimed to evaluate the performance of the ST1RE in predicting CV events in a cohort of Brazilian patients with T1D, a population with a heterogeneous genetic background and environmental factors. Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between this cardiovascular risk score and the occurrence and progression of microvascular complications in this cohort.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational retrospective study was based on a cohort of 435 adults with T1D treated at the outpatient clinic of the Diabetes Center of the Federal University of Sao Paulo (UNIFESP) between June 2010 and June 2023.

For each participant, the risk of developing CV events at 5 and 10 years was calculated using ST1RE. Subsequently, a comparison was made between the expected and observed event rates during the respective 5- and 10-year follow-up periods. Medical records were retrospectively reviewed to ascertain the occurrence of CV events. Participants were followed from the date of their first clinical examination (basal evaluation), during which comprehensive data were available for risk score calculation, until the first cardiovascular event, death, or the end of the follow-up period. Individuals who died during the study follow-up with an unknown cause of death (1.8% of the initial cohort) were included in the analysis up until the point they were censored due to loss to follow-up.

All participants in the study fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: had a T1D duration of at least 1 year, were older than 18 years, were treated with multiple daily injections (MDIs) or continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), had complete clinical and laboratory information for calculating the risk score and were regularly followed-up for at least 5 years. The criteria for diabetes duration ≥ 1 year were established to mitigate the impact of outliers in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and lipids, which are commonly observed at the time of diagnosis. Individuals with previous CVD at their initial clinical examination (47 individuals), pregnancy or recent delivery (2 individuals), end-stage kidney disease or previous pancreas and kidney transplantation (36 individuals) were excluded. Prior to initiating this study, we performed a sample size calculation to determine the necessary number of participants. For this purpose, we used the ROC curve of the risk score model as a reference. Based on the literature, assuming a cardiovascular event incidence of 10% within 5 years and 20% within 10 years [11], we estimated that a total of 412 participants would be needed to achieve a statistical power of 90% at a 5% significance level for the 5-year analysis. For the 10-year analysis, a total of 196 participants was estimated to achieve the same power and significance level.

The research was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Research Ethics Committee Registration Number: 59598422.3.0000.5505), and informed consent was acquired from all participants. The datasets generated during and/or analyzed in the current study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Measurements and definitions

Detailed clinical and laboratory data of the participants were extracted from electronic health records during the initial clinical visit, as well as at the 5- and 10-year follow-up intervals. Similar to the Danish cohort [11], T1D was defined by the criteria of an age at diagnosis < 30 years and insulin treatment at the time of diagnosis. Alternatively, individuals aged ≥ 30 years at diagnosis or who were initially using oral hypoglycemic drugs before treatment with insulin were classified as having T1D if they exhibited low C-peptide values or tested positive for anti-GAD65, both in combination with a requirement for insulin to control blood glucose levels. Early-onset T1D was defined as a diagnosis occurring before 10 years of age [19–21].

Albuminuria was categorized based on 24-hour sterile urine collections (mg/24 h) or albumin-to-creatinine ratio from spot urine samples (mg/g) as normoalbuminuria (< 30 mg/g or mg/24 h; A1), microalbuminuria (30–299 mg/g or mg/24 h; A2), or macroalbuminuria (≥ 300 mg/g or mg/24 h; A3). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD-EPI) [22]. Diabetic kidney disease was diagnosed if the eGFR was < 60mL/min/1.73 m² or if the presence of albuminuria (≥ 30 mg/g or ≥ 30 mg/24 h) was confirmed by at least two of three consecutive measurements or the use of a renin-angiotensin-aldosterone-system inhibitors (RAAS-inhibitor) for albuminuria. Retinopathy status was assessed by fundoscopy after pupillary dilation, and the individuals were classified according to the Clinical Disease Severity Scale [23] or post-laser status. Peripheral neuropathy was diagnosed based on the presence of symptoms and/or physical examination findings performed by the medical staff from the Diabetes Center (monofilament testing, 128 Hz tuning fork, or pain/touch tests) documented in the medical records. Similarly, autonomic neuropathy was diagnosed based on symptoms and/or physical examination findings, including orthostatic hypotension, severe hypoglycemia, asymptomatic hypoglycemia, symptoms suggestive of gastroparesis, erectile dysfunction without another identifiable cause, or retrograde ejaculation. Severe hypoglycemia was defined as an episode of hypoglycemia requiring external assistance for recovery.

The progression of diabetic kidney disease was defined as the progression of albuminuria category, or a reduction in the eGFR ≥ 50%, dialysis or kidney transplantation. The progression of retinopathy was defined as the onset of changes assessed by fundoscopy, or as a worsening in severity according to the retinopathy disease severity scale [23]. Progression to peripheral and autonomic neuropathy was defined as the onset of changes noted during physical examination or clinical symptoms in individuals who previously showed no neurological abnormalities.

Hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg or treatment with antihypertensive medication. A family history of premature CVD in first-degree relatives was considered CVD prior to age 55 years for men and 65 years for women. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 100 mg/dl or the use of lipid-lowering drugs, as LDL-C goals for persons with T1D are at least < 100 mg/dl according to current guidelines [24–26].

Smoking status was categorized as currently smoking one or more cigarettes per day (yes/no), regular physical activity defined as ≥ 3.5 h/week (yes/no), and alcohol intake classified into three groups: 0, 1–20, and > 20 alcohol units/week. Participants were assessed for the use of antihypertensive treatment (yes/no), lipid-lowering treatment (yes/no), or RAAS-inhibitor treatment (yes/no). Anthropometric data, including weight, height, and waist circumference, were also collected. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing the weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Race was self-identified by the study participants.

CV risk was assessed using an online interactive ST1RE calculator (https://steno.shinyapps.io/T1RiskEngine/) based on ten variables (age, sex, diabetes duration, smoking status, HbA1c, systolic blood pressure, LDL-C, albuminuria, eGFR and physical exercise) for predicting the 5- and 10-year risk of fatal or nonfatal CVD, which was defined by any documented occurrence of ischemic heart disease, ischemic stroke, heart failure, or peripheral artery disease. Individuals were classified as having a low (10-year risk of a fatal/nonfatal CV < 10%), moderate (10-year risk of a fatal/nonfatal CV between 10 and 19.9%), or high (10-year risk of a fatal/nonfatal CV ≥ 20%) risk [11].

Standardized assays were utilized for the assessment of lipid profiles and glucose and creatinine levels. LDL-C was determined through the Friedewald formula. HbA1c was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Trinity Biotech, Premier Hb9210™). Urinary albumin concentration was measured by immunoturbidimetric assays on morning spot urine samples or 24-hour urine collections.

Statistical analysis

In the descriptive analysis, categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages, normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as means ± standard deviations (SDs), and nonnormally distributed continuous variables are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]). The Anderson-Darling test was used to evaluate the normal distribution of continuous variables. Comparisons between two groups of continuous variables were performed with parametric (t test) and nonparametric tests (Mann‒Whitney). For the analysis of continuous variables with three or more groups, ANOVA was used for normally distributed variables, while Kruskal‒Wallis test was used for nonnormal variables. Tukey’s or Dunn’s test was used for multiple pairwise comparisons. Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was performed for the comparison of categorical data between ST1RE risks categories.

Time to CV events was analysed using Kaplan-Meier curves based on the ST1RE categories, with comparisons made using the log-rank test. Associations between ST1RE categories and CV outcomes were evaluated through Cox regression analyses. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed with adjustments for variables with p-values less than 0.2 in the univariate analysis that were not included in the ST1RE calculation. The proportional hazards assumption in the Cox regression model was validated using the Schoenfeld residuals test.

The discriminative ability of the ST1RE at 5 and 10 years was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), also referred to as the C-statistic, was used as a measure of how well the ST1RE predicted CV event. The optimal cut-point value was determined using Youden’s index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for each cut-point. Model calibration was determined using the Hosmer‒Lemeshow goodness-of-fit. A sensitivity analysis, including participants under 40 years old, was conducted to assess the performance of the ST1RE in younger individuals.

Additionally, Fisher’s exact test was utilized to compare the progression of microvascular complications and logistic regression was employed to evaluate the association between baseline microvascular status and ST1RE categories.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.0 and STATA/MP 18.0 for Windows (StataCorp, 2023. College Station, TX, USA: StataCorp LLC). The significance level was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Subject characteristics

A total of 435 T1D individuals with a median age of 25 years (IQR: 21‒32) and a median duration of 13 years (IQR: 9‒18) without overt CVD were included. Overall, 20% had hypertension, and 16% were receiving statin treatment. Approximately 40% of our cohort was of nonwhite ethnicity, and 43% individuals were diagnosed with T1D before 10 years of age. Cardiovascular risk stratification

According to the ST1RE, 84% of the participants were categorized as low, 11% as moderate, and 5% as a high-risk group. As CV risk increased from low to high-risk group, there was a stepwise increase (significant, p < 0.05) in most CVRFs, diabetes duration, diabetic microvascular complications, worsening glycemic and lipid profiles, a higher level of albuminuria, and a decrease in the eGFR, as detailed in Table 1. Additionally, it is important to note the large age difference among the risk groups, with the median age being 20 years older in the moderate- and high-risk individuals compared to the low-risk group.

During the median follow-up of 9.2 years (IQR: 6.0-10.7), 24 participants (5.5%) experienced a CV event, representing an incidence rate of 6.7 (95% CI 4.5–10.0) per 1.000 person-years. After 5 and 10 years, 3.5% (15/435) and 9.9% (22/223) of patients, respectively, developed CVD. Ischemic heart disease accounted for 46% of all cardiovascular events, while ischemic stroke constituted 21%, peripheral artery disease 25%, and heart failure 8%.

Notably, participants who experienced a CV event during the follow-up period were significantly older, had a longer diabetes duration, greater waist circumference-to-height ratio, lower physical activity, higher levels of LDL-C and triglycerides, and greater prevalence of hypertension, diabetes microvascular complications as well as family history of premature CVD. The prevalence of early-onset T1D did not differ significantly between the two groups.

According to the ST1RE, only 1.6% (6/366) of individuals in the low-risk group experienced a CV event. Conversely, 14.9% (7/47) of participants in the moderate-risk group and 50% (11/22) of those in the high-risk group presented CV events. Among the individuals who did not experience a CV event, 11 (2.7%) were included in the high-risk group, 40 (9.7%) in the moderate-risk group and 360 (87.6%) in the low-risk grou

Kaplan-Meier curves analysis revealed that the incidence rates of CV events during the follow-up period were significantly higher in the moderate- and high-risk groups compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 1). At the 10 year-mark, the probability of remaining free from CV events was estimated at 0.98 (95% CI 0.96–0.99) for the low-risk group, while it was considerably lower at 0.48 (95% CI 0.26–0.67) for the high-risk group.

The hazard ratios (HRs) for CV events was greater in the high-risk group (HR 52.02; 95% CI 18.60–145.51, p < 0.001) and in the moderate-risk group (HR 8.66; 95% CI 2.90–25.80, p < 0.001) compared to the low-risk group. Similarly, the high-risk group exhibited a significantly greater CV event rate than did the moderate-risk group (HR 6.01; 95% CI 2.23–16.20, p < 0.001).

Table 2 displays the analyses assessing variables associated with the risk of CV events during the follow-up period, with main interest in the ST1RE risk variable as the exposure variable. Multivariable Cox regression including only the variables not included in the ST1RE calculation and those with p < 0.2 in univariate analysis (Supplemental Table S1), demonstrated that the high-risk group persisted independently associated after full adjustments (Initial model). After excluding non-significant variables one by one from the initial model, the risk for CVD remained higher in the moderate- and high-risk groups compared to the low-risk group (Final model). Furthermore, in this final regression model, we observed that peripheral neuropathy and increased levels of High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C) were independently associated with cardiovascular events during the follow-up.

Performance of the ST1RE stratification

The discrimination performance of ST1RE within our cohort yielded a robust C-statistic of 0.912 (95% CI 0.856–0.968) for 5-year events and 0.916 (95% CI 0.864–0.968) for 10-year cardiovascular events (Fig. 2). The Hosmer‒Lemeshow test showed good calibration for 5-year CV events (χ2 = 10.12, p = 0.899) and 10-year CV events (χ2 = 14.80, p = 0.676), indicating no significant difference between the estimated event rate by ST1RE and the observed CV event rates over 5 and 10 years.

Still regarding the performance of ST1RE at 5 and 10 years, a cutoff of 2.80 from this engine exhibited high sensitivity (93%) and moderate specificity (74%) for identifying individuals with CVD within 5 years, yielding a PPV of 11% and NPV of 99%. Similarly, a threshold of 4.75 in the ST1RE at 10 years also demonstrated high sensitivity (96%) and moderate specificity (69%), with a PPV of 25% and NPV of 99%. Furthermore, considering that 86% (375/435) of our initial cohort were considerably young (under 40 years old), we performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the performance of the ST1RE in younger individuals. Similar to the findings in the entire cohort, we observed that in this subgroup, the ST1RE also demonstrated a robust C-statistic value of 0.917 (95% CI 0.838–0.997) for 5-year CV events and 0.899 (95% CI 0.828–0.970) for 10-year CV events, with good calibration (χ2 = 10.56, p = 0.783 for 5-year and χ2 = 15.43, p = 0.493 for 10-year CV events) (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Microvascular complications

Overall, at baseline, 30.1% of participants had diabetic kidney disease, 21.2% had retinopathy, 16.4% had peripheral neuropathy, and 25.1% had autonomic neuropathy. The prevalence of retinopathy, peripheral and autonomic diabetic neuropathy, and diabetic kidney disease was significantly greater in individuals in the high-risk group (Table 1). The odds ratios (ORs) of microangiopathies at baseline were greater in the high-risk than in the low-risk group of ST1RE for retinopathy (OR 57.4; 95% CI 13.1–526.4, p < 0.001), peripheral neuropathy (OR 33.8; 95% CI 10.2–146.3, p < 0.001) and autonomic neuropathy (OR 26.9; 95% CI 7.6–146.3, p < 0.001). These findings are noteworthy, given that these three microvascular complications, other than diabetic kidney disease, are not included in the ST1RE calculation. During the follow-up period, we also observed a greater progression of microvascular complications in the high-risk group, as presented in Fig. 3.

Discussion

In our cohort of genetic and ethnic mixed populations of T1D adults without previous CVD, the ST1RE algorithm showed overall good performance in the prediction of CV events at 5 and 10 years of follow-up. These data are comparable to the internal validation in the Steno derivation cohort: a C-statistic of 0.912 (95% CI 0.856–0.968) in our cohort vs. 0.826 (95% CI 0.807–0.845) for a 5-year CVD event and a C-statistic of 0.916 (95% CI 0.864–0.968) vs. 0.818 (95% CI 0.802–0.833) for a 10-year CVD event [11]. In addition, the progression of microangiopathy complications was associated with greater categories of ST1RE.

Our study evaluated the performance of ST1RE in a different ethnic mixed population with a distinct set of genetic data since approximately 40% of our cohort was of nonwhite ethnicity, unlike the derivation cohort (predominantly of Danish ancestry) [11].

Compared with the Steno population used to develop ST1RE, our cohort was younger (mean age 28 versus 42 years old), had a shorter diabetes duration (median 13 versus 16.6 years), was younger at diabetes diagnosis (median 11 versus 20.8 years), had a slightly higher baseline HbA1c (mean 8.9% versus 8.4%), greater use of statins (16.1% versus 9.8%), and lower prevalence of smokers (6.7% versus 64.1%) (Supplemental Table S2) [11]. While in the Steno cohort, almost 1 in 3 patients were diagnosed with T1D after 30 years, in our study, almost 40% were diagnosed with T1D before 10 years of age, a previously described age range with a greater relative risk of adverse CV outcomes [20].

The incidence of CV events in our cohort was 6.7 per 1,000 person-years, similar to that reported in an Italian cohort (5 per 1,000 person-years) [27] and much lower than that reported in the Danish population (17.8 to 27.2 per 1,000 persons-years) [11]. It is important to note that our T1D cohort experienced approximately five times more CV events per year than did the general Brazilian population with a similar age (25–29 years), which presented a CV event rate of 1.32 per 1,000 person-years [28, 29].

The lower incidence of CVD between our cohort and the Steno derivation cohort despite our worse glycemic control can be at least partially explained by the decreased smoking prevalence and the difference in mean age between both populations. Consistent with this, in univariate Cox regression analysis participants aged 40 years or older had a 9.1-fold increased risk of experiencing a CV event compared to those under 40 years old (95% CI 4.0–20.4; p < 0.001). This finding is aligned with results from the Diabetes Control and Complications Trial/Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications (DCCT/EDIC) cohort with T1D, where increasing age was the most significant risk factor for clinical CVD, as in the general population, followed by the updated weighted-mean HbA1c [30].

Furthermore, the pleiotropic effects of statins may also contribute to the lower incidence of CVD in our cohort since they were more commonly used in our population, although LDL-C levels were similar across the cohorts (mean 100.6 mg/dl versus 104.4 mg/dl) [31]. Despite the greater prevalence of statin use in the moderate- and high-risk groups than in the low-risk group, we observed that even with greater statin usage among patients who experienced CVD, a substantial proportion (approximately 58%) of patients did not use statins at the beginning of the follow-up period. Although there are a limited number of trials focusing on lipid-lowering therapy in individuals with T1D and most of the literature concerning diabetes and CVD relates to T2D, an observational study found that the use of lipid-lowering therapy is associated with a 22–44% reduction in the risk of CVD and mortality among patients with T1D without a previous history of cardiovascular disease [32].

The incidence of CVD during the follow-up period was significantly associated with various risk factors. However, when considering only the variables not included in the ST1RE calculation, peripheral neuropathy and elevated HDL-C levels were associated with an increased risk of CV events during the follow-up, regardless of the ST1RE risk categories. Although we acknowledge the limitation of this finding from the Cox regression due to the low number of events, the association between peripheral neuropathy and increased CV risk in individuals with T1D and T2D has been previously demonstrated [33, 34]. However, the incremental prognostic value of adding peripheral neuropathy to the ST1RE has already been examined, and the authors found that it resulted in a slight improvement that was not statistically significant [33]. Regarding HDL-C, it has been suggested that total HDL-C might not be the better metric for assessing CVD risk in T1D individuals as alterations in the composition and function of HDL particles have been observed in this population [35, 36].

When comparing the risk categories defined by ST1RE, we observed that, in addition to the variables included in the score, other factors, such as higher triglyceride levels, family history of premature CVD, BMI, waist circumference, retinopathy, and peripheral and autonomic neuropathy, were also more prevalent in the higher-risk groups. Remarkably, all the strongest predictor factors for mortality and CV outcomes among individuals with T1D, as identified in an observational cohort from the Swedish National Diabetes Register (HbA1c, albuminuria, LDL-C, systolic blood pressure, and diabetes duration), are included in the ST1RE [37]. Notably, four of these are considered modifiable risk factors.

This finding reinforces the importance of accurate and validated tools to assess CV risk and support clinical decisions on the primary prevention of CVD in individuals with T1D. Aligned with this, the American Diabetes Association/European Association for the Study of Diabetes statement and the recently published European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend the adoption of a risk calculator suitable for people with T1D, such as ST1RE, for identifying patients who would benefit from cardioprotective treatments [ 26, 38].

Additionally, we identified two ST1RE cutoff points exhibiting a high NPV for predicting CV events within 5 and 10 years. This finding holds substantial potential in clinical practice and could aid in overcoming the prevalent glucocentric management often observed in individuals with T1D. Regarding the application of this tool in patient care, the ST1RE algorithm requires ten clinical and biochemical variables, which are usually available in primary care and through an online calculator [11].

We also assessed the performance of the ST1RE in a subgroup of individuals under 40 years of age at baseline. Consistent with the findings in the entire cohort, the ST1RE showed overall good performance in the prediction of CV events at both 5 and 10 years of follow-up in younger individuals with T1D. This aspect is particularly relevant, as current guidelines suggest individualized initiation of statins for primary prevention of CVD in individuals younger than 40 years [38], a practice that is typically delayed [39].

Another noteworthy finding in our study was the observed stepwise relationship between microvascular complications and CV risk according to the ST1RE. This observation is consistent with the findings of previous studies indicating a greater prevalence of retinopathy [13, 18] and somatic or autonomic diabetic neuropathy [18] in patients with a greater risk estimated by ST1RE, even though these variables were not included in the score equation.

Notably, no previous studies have specifically evaluated the progression of microvascular complications according to different ST1RE categories. In our cohort, the high-risk group was significantly associated with a greater risk of progression of retinopathy, diabetic kidney disease, peripheral neuropathy, and autonomic neuropathy during the follow-up period. This finding highlights the shared underlying mechanisms between micro- and macrovascular complications in the T1D population and the potential usefulness of this CV tool in identifying populations at risk for the onset and progression of chronic microvascular complications, as these conditions may be seen as part of a continuum [13]. However, when we performed a sensitivity analysis on the progression rates of retinopathy, peripheral neuropathy, and autonomic neuropathy, excluding patients with baseline diabetic kidney disease, no statistically significant differences were observed across the three ST1RE risk categories. This finding supports the notion of a ‘common soil’ underlying all microangiopathies in T1D.

Some limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, it was a single-center study. Second, despite the relatively long median duration of follow-up (9.2 years), in 49% of participants, the duration of follow-up was less than 10 years. This factor could contribute to the lower incidence of CV events in our cohort. To minimize the impact of this limitation, we conducted an attrition analysis comparing the 223 participants included in the 10-year follow-up assessment with the 212 participants who were not included in this assessment. Our analysis revealed that the proportion of individuals classified by the ST1RE into low, moderate, and high-risk categories was similar between the groups in this attrition analysis (p = 0.288) (Supplemental Table S3). During the follow-up period, 1.8% of the initial cohort died from unknown causes (all of these deaths occurred between 5 and 10 years of follow-up) and we acknowledge that, although it represents a very small proportion, this is a limitation of the study. Additionally, given the retrospective nature of this analysis, the quality of the assessment of microangiopathic complications is inherently limited. Lastly, due to the limited sample size, further studies are required to validate our findings.

The strengths of this study include the validation of the ST1RE regarding CV outcomes in Brazilian individuals with T1D, a genetically mixed population, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been performed in prior investigations. Additionally, this study externally evaluated the performance of the ST1RE at 10 years, and as mentioned in the European Society of Cardiology guidelines, there is a lack of validation [26]. Another distinctive strength, in contrast to another study published in the literature [27], to avoid an underestimation of CV events in our population, we did not consider patients with suspected asymptomatic ischemic heart disease at the initial examination if this suspicion was not confirmed during subsequent follow-up to be excluded. Additionally, this study was based on a young cohort with a median age of 25 years, including both newly diagnosed patients and those with long-standing T1D. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis to assess the performance of the ST1RE specifically in a subgroup of individuals under 40 years of age, an analysis that, to the best of our knowledge, has not yet been published in the literature.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that ST1RE performs well in predicting CV events at 5 and 10 years of follow-up in a genetically mixed population of individuals with T1D. In addition, the progression of multiple microangiopathy complications during follow-up was associated with substantially higher ST1RE categories at baseline. This finding underscores the practical utility of ST1RE as a potentially valuable tool for guiding primary prevention decisions for CVD in individuals with T1D in different ethnic and age group populations.
