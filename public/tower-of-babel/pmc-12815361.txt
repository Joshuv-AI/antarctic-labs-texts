Impact of Housing Conditions and Air Quality Awareness with Indoor PM2.5 Levels in Urban Jodhpur
Authors: Ramesh Kumar Huda, Rajnish Gupta, Pankaj Kumar, Jayvardhan Singh, Arun Kumar Sharma, G.S Toteja, Bontha V. Babu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Indoor air pollution poses a major public health concern in India, particularly among urban populations exposed to high levels of particulate matter. This study aimed to assess the influence of housing conditions and awareness on indoor particulate matter (PM2.5) levels in Jodhpur, Rajasthan. A cross-sectional study was conducted across 12 households representing slum, low-, middle-, and high-income groups, as well as commercial and industrial areas. Indoor PM2.5 concentrations were continuously monitored over a one-year period using pre-calibrated AirVisual Pro devices. Awareness regarding indoor air pollution and preventive measures was evaluated through structured questionnaires administered before and after an awareness intervention. The findings revealed that effective ventilation and good hygiene practices significantly contributed to reducing indoor PM2.5 levels. Poorly ventilated homes, such as House 1 (62 μg/m³) and House 4 (73 μg/m³), exhibited higher pollutant concentrations, while well-ventilated or air-conditioned homes, such as House 2 (46 μg/m³) and House 3 (40 μg/m³), maintained lower levels. The highest concentration (111 μg/m³) was recorded in House 5, which is located near a highway with inadequate ventilation. The awareness component showed a marked improvement in residents’ understanding of air pollution sources, health impacts, and mitigation strategies following real-time monitoring and education sessions. The study concludes that housing ventilation, seasonal behavioral factors, and real-time monitoring play a crucial role in determining indoor air quality. Strengthening building design standards and enhancing public awareness are vital for sustainable indoor air quality management in urban India.

INTRODUCTION

In India, exposure to ambient air pollution on the urban population is among the highest levels anywhere else in the world. Indoor air pollution is a critical public health concern, especially in developing countries. It is caused by harmful chemicals and materials that degrade air quality within enclosed spaces, making it up to 10 times worse than outdoor air pollution.[1] The urban landscape, comprising densely packed houses that have been built without any consideration for proper ventilation, combined with the use of biomass as cooking fuel and high atmospheric dust, contaminates the indoor air to very hazardous levels. These pollutants contribute to severe health outcomes, such as respiratory infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer, and adverse perinatal outcomes. Incriminating evidence is gradually being generated about the role of pollutants in type-2 diabetes mellitus, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and cognitive function disorders.[2]

Women and children are disproportionately affected due to their extended exposure to indoor pollutants, particularly from cooking with biomass fuels. These findings underscore the urgent need for interventions such as clean cooking technologies, improved building designs, and public education to mitigate the health impacts of indoor air pollution.[123]

A study conducted in South India assessed the levels of PM2.5 in rural households, finding significant differences based on the type of housing and ventilation available. The study revealed that houses with poor ventilation and those using biomass fuels had higher concentrations of PM2.5, posing greater health risks to residents.[3] In future, a significant proportion of households will use incense sticks, mosquito coils, and kerosene, which contribute to poor indoor air quality (IAQ). Additionally, overcrowding and inadequate ventilation were prevalent. Hence, it highlights the importance of improving housing conditions, increasing awareness about the health risks of indoor air pollution, and promoting the use of cleaner fuels to reduce the consequences may give more references to indoor air pollution.[4]

In India, cultural housing conditions, climate, and geographies vary from region to region. Hence, studies in specific geographies are needed to understand the nature and extent of indoor air pollution. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the effect of housing conditions and people’s awareness about pollution on indoor air pollution in Jodhpur, western Rajasthan, India.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study area: This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Jodhpur, a city in western Rajasthan located at 26°17’N and 73°01’E, spread over 233 km2, having a population of 9,21,476 (source: Jodhpur, India Population (2024) - Population Stat).[5] The study was conducted in residential premises, in slums, low-income, middle-income, and upper-income households, as well as commercial and industrial establishments.

Data collection procedure

Indoor air quality

Detailed methodology is provided elsewhere.[5] For IAQ assessment, two houses each were randomly selected from slums, low-income, middle-income, and upper-income households, as well as commercial and industrial establishments. Portable electricity/battery-operated pre-calibrated air quality monitors, AirVisual Pro Indoor Sensor (IQAir), were deployed in the selected households for one year. These houses were equipped with air quality monitors to measure PM2.5 levels.

Awareness assessment

A survey was carried out using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire to assess the level of awareness about air pollution among the residents of all households selected for air quality monitoring (AQM), and devices were installed.

The housing conditions in these 12 houses were analyzed using a self-structured questionnaire and observation checklist, where higher scores indicated poorer air quality and lower scores suggested better air quality in the house.

This scoring system offers valuable insights into the current state of IAQ to identify specific areas for intervention and improvement. Scores of 0 typically indicate better practices or conditions that contribute to healthier indoor environments, while scores of 1 highlight areas where corrective actions are needed to mitigate potential health risks associated with indoor air pollution [Supplementary Table 1].

Data management and analysis

All collected information was recorded digitally using an Excel sheet for further analysis.

Ethics and informed consent

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institute Ethics Committee. Written consent was acquired from each participant after providing a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study.

RESULTS

Table 1 shows air pollution awareness among 12 participants in Jodhpur. At baseline, while 100% recognized air pollution and its health risks, specific knowledge was limited: Only 16.67% knew about the air quality index (AQI), and 8.33% were aware of pollution-related deaths in India. After the intervention, awareness improved significantly. AQI knowledge rose to 100%, and 75% knew about mortality due to air pollution. Recognition of specific health effects, such as breathing problems, reached 100%. All participants reported that AQM devices enhanced their understanding, with 58.33% committed to long-term use and 41.67% reporting direct benefits from the devices.

DISCUSSION

This study examines the relationship between housing conditions, awareness, and indoor PM2.5 levels in urban Jodhpur, building on existing research on IAQ. These findings extend the work of Huda et al.,[5] who mapped ambient air quality in Jodhpur using low-cost sensors. While their study focused on outdoor pollution, the present research demonstrates that indoor air is significantly affected by both external emissions and internal housing deficiencies, highlighting the need for integrated indoor-outdoor AQM.

A positive correlation was found between housing condition scores, indicating structural and hygienic inadequacies and higher PM2.5 levels. This aligns with Vijay et al.[6] who reported spatial and temporal variations in indoor PM2.5 across Indian urban homes, attributing higher concentrations to poor ventilation and household density. Similarly, Anand and Phuleria[7] found elevated indoor PM2.5 in Delhi’s slums due to structural constraints.

Proximity to traffic significantly impacted IAQ, as seen in a house near a highway with the highest. This supports Alhajeri et al.[8] who identified traffic-related emissions as a major indoor pollutant in Kuwaiti homes. In contrast, a well-maintained commercial-area home with air conditioning recorded low PM2.5, indicating that mechanical ventilation and cleanliness can reduce outdoor infiltration. This contrasts with Ścibor et al.,[9] whose study in Poland found high indoor PM2.5 despite moderate outdoor levels, due to aging buildings and lack of filtration—highlighting that both building quality and behavioral practices are critical in urban Indian contexts.

Seasonal variation was evident, with PM2.5 rising in winter due to reduced ventilation and increased heating. This is consistent with Vijay et al.,[6] but this study adds a behavioral insight: residents restricted airflow to retain warmth, worsening indoor pollution. This thermal comfort–air quality trade-off is also noted by Niculita-Hirzel et al.[10] in studies on urban environments and respiratory health.

Although cooking with biomass was not reported, findings from Karaca et al.[11] show that even clean fuels like Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG) can generate PM2.5 spikes if kitchens lack proper ventilation. This underscores the need for kitchen-specific interventions such as exhaust fans. Papagiannis et al.[12] identified traffic, construction, and industrial emissions as major PM2.5 sources in Dushanbe, Tajikistan, a pattern mirrored in Jodhpur’s industrial and commercial zones, especially when combined with poor housing infrastructure.

Awareness improved dramatically after installing air quality monitors. Awareness of the AQI rose from 16.67% to 100%, supporting Hou et al.,[13] who found that real-time data improves health literacy in Taiwan. Al-Shidi et al.[14] reported similar gains in Oman through structured campaigns, but this study shows that device feedback alone can drive awareness, suggesting a low-effort, high-impact educational strategy. However, baseline knowledge of pollution-related mortality was very low, a gap also found by Gabel et al.[15] in Greenland, indicating a global shortfall in communicating pollution’s health burden.

A clear socioeconomic gradient was observed higher-income households having better ventilation and lower PM2.5. This reflects findings by Dwivedi et al.,[16] who noted disproportionate exposure among women in low-income homes, and Ronita Bardhan et al.,[17] who linked urban re-densification in Mumbai to deteriorated housing conditions. This study reaffirms that structural inequities place the greatest pollution burden on slum and low-income residents, who lack resources to improve their living environments.

Strengths and Limitations

The study used real-time, pre-calibrated air quality monitors (AirVisual Pro) over a 12-month period, ensuring reliable longitudinal data. The inclusion of diverse housing types—slum, low-, middle-, and high-income, as well as commercial and industrial areas—enhances the representativeness of findings for urban Jodhpur.

However, the small sample size limited statistical power and generalizability. Findings are specific to Jodhpur and may not apply to other climatic or urban settings. Only PM2.5 was measured, excluding other health-relevant pollutants such as NO₂, CO, and VOCs.

Recommendations

Public education campaigns should expand to include information on the AQI, pollution-related mortality, and health risks, using schools, health centers, and local media. Urban planning policies must enforce ventilation standards in housing, particularly in low-income settlements, to reduce indoor pollution exposure.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights that housing conditions and awareness significantly influence indoor PM2.5 levels in Jodhpur. Poor ventilation and proximity to pollution sources were linked to higher PM2.5, while better ventilation and air conditioning reduced levels. Seasonal spikes, especially in winter, worsened IAQ. Although general awareness of air pollution was high, knowledge of specific indicators like the AQI and pollution-related mortality was low. Installing air quality monitors significantly improved awareness and encouraged long-term engagement in air quality management.
