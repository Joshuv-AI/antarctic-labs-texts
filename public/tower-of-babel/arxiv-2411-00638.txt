Multi-tunneling effect of nonreciprocal Landau-Zener tunneling: Insights from DC field responses
Ibuki Terada, Sota Kitamura, Hiroshi Watanabe, Hiroaki Ikeda

Abstract
Recent advancements in laser technology have spurred growing interest in nonlinear and nonequilibrium phenomena. Here, we investigate the geometric aspects of quantum tunneling and the nonreciprocal response, particularly focusing on the shift vector, in noncentrosymmetric insulators under a strong DC electric field. In insulators under a strong electric field, electrons undergoing Bloch oscillations interfere with each other by passing through different paths via Landau-Zener tunneling. We found that the interference effect due to multi-tunneling causes the oscillating nonreciprocal response that is significantly amplified with increasing electric field intensity. We also clarified the role of the shift vector in the interference conditions through an analysis of the nonequilibrium steady state. These results will contribute significantly to advancing a systematic understanding of quantum geometric effects in the nonperturbative regime.

formalism

II.1
Quantum kinetic equation

We investigate a single electron dynamics in gapped lattice systems using the quantum kinetic equation.
Here, we consider a two-band system with a finite energy gap
\Delta_{k}=\varepsilon_{k+}-\varepsilon_{k-}>0
, for simplicity.
A DC electric field
E
is introduced using the Peierls substitution, where the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is replaced by
\mathcal{H}_{k-E(t-t_{0})}=\mathcal{H}_{k}(t)
, with
t_{0}
being the initial time.

The reduced density matrix
\rho_{k}(t)
is governed by the
k
-dependent quantum kinetic equation:

\frac{d\rho_{k}(t)}{dt}=-i[\mathcal{H}_{k}(t),\rho_{k}(t)]+\mathcal{D}_{k}(\rho_{k}(t)),

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}_{k}(t)
represents the dissipation term.
We here introduce the adiabatic basis
[
43
]
,

\displaystyle\ket{\Phi_{k\alpha}(t)}=\e^{-i\theta_{k\alpha}(t)}\ket{u_{k\alpha}(t)},

(2)

\displaystyle\theta_{k\alpha}(t)=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}[\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(t^{\prime})+EA_{k,\alpha\alpha}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime},

(3)

where
\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(t)=\varepsilon_{k-E(t-t_{0}),\alpha}
and
\ket{u_{k\alpha}(t)}=\ket{u_{k-E(t-t_0),\alpha}}
are the snapshot eigenenergies and eigenstates of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(t)
, respectively.
The phase factors
-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
and
-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}EA_{k,\alpha\alpha}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
are the dynamical phase and the Berry phase with Berry connection
A_{k,\alpha\beta}(t)=i\innerproduct{u_{k\alpha}(t)}{\partial_k u_{k\beta}(t)}
, respectively.
In this basis, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
) is transformed into the Hamiltonian in the adiabatic basis

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}(t)=\left(\begin{matrix}0&W_{k}(t)\\
W^{*}_{k}(t)&0\end{matrix}\right),

(4)

\displaystyle W_{k}(t)=E|A_{k,+-}(t)|\e^{i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}[\Delta_{k}(t^{\prime})+ER_{k}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}+i\arg A_{k,+-}(t_{0})},

(5)

where
W_{k}(t)
and
R_{k}=A_{k,++}-A_{k,--}-\partial_{k}\arg A_{k,+-}
are the transition dipole moment in the adiabatic basis and the shift vector, respectively.

The dissipation term is often determined with the relaxation time approximation (RTA).
However, as demonstrated in Ref.
[
44
]
, this approximation fails to accurately represent the conductivity of an insulator.
The DC conductivity obtained from the RTA shows unphysical linear conductivity for insulators in the linear response regime due to the inadequate treatment of the density matrix.
In Ref.
[
44
]
, we addressed this issue and proposed the dynamical phase approximation (DPA) as an alternative to the RTA, which semi-quantitatively reproduces the results of the exact solution across the entire electric field region.
Furthermore, it has been successfully extended to nonlinear response in Ref.
[
45
]
.
Based on this DPA formalism, the dissipation term in particle-hole symmetric insulating systems is given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{k}=\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{k}+\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{k}+\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{k},

(6a)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{k}]_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{[\rho_{k}(t)]_{\alpha\beta}-f_{D}(\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(t))\delta_{\alpha\beta}}{\tau},

(6b)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{k}]_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{[\mathcal{W}_{k}(t)]_{\alpha\beta}\delta f_{k}(t)}{\tau\Delta_{k}(t)}\left(1-\frac{Ez^{\alpha}_{k}(t)}{\Delta_{k}(t)}\right),

(6c)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{k}]_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\alpha|W_{k}(t)|^{2}\delta f_{k}(t)}{\tau\Delta^{2}_{k}(t)}\left(1-\frac{Ez^{+}_{k}(t)}{\Delta_{k}(t)}\right)\left(1-\frac{Ez^{-}_{k}(t)}{\Delta_{k}(t)}\right)\delta_{\alpha\beta},

(6d)

where
\tau^{-1}
denotes the damping rate,
z^{\pm}_{k}=R_{k}\pm i\partial_{k}\ln|A_{k,+-}|
and
\delta f_{k}=f_{D}(\varepsilon_{k-})-f_{D}(\varepsilon_{k+})

with
f_{D}
being the Fermi-Dirac distribution function.

\mathcal{D}^{(0)}_{k}
is the RTA term, while
\mathcal{D}^{(1)}_{k}
and
\mathcal{D}^{(2)}_{k}
are the field-induced corrections describing the relaxation from an excited state to a polarized state.

The solution of Eq. (
1
) in the adiabatic basis is formally written as

\begin{split}\rho_{k}(t)&=U(t)\rho_{k}(t_{0})U^{\dagger}(t)\e^{-(t-t_{0})/\tau}\\
&\qquad+U(t)\bigg(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}U^{\dagger}(s)\Sigma(s)U(s)\e^{-(t-s)/\tau}~ds\bigg)U^{\dagger}(t),\end{split}

(7)

where
U(t)=U(t,t_{0})
is the time evolution operator of the isolated system,

U(t_{1},t_{2})=\mathcal{T}\exp\left[-i\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}\mathcal{W}_{k}(s)ds\right]

(8)

and
\Sigma(t)=\mathcal{D}_{k}(t)+\rho_{k}(t)/\tau
.
Note that the density matrix (
7
) includes nonperturbative effects via
U(t)
, such as the contribution of the Landau-Zener tunneling.
Hereafter, we set the initial time
t_{0}=0
.

II.2
Dissipative dynamics in lattice systems

Let us consider the nonequilibrium steady state of Bloch-Zener oscillations
[
36
,
37
]
that arise when a DC electric field is applied to lattice systems. The density matrix describing the nonequilibrium steady state,
\rho^{\rm NESS}_{K}
, is obtained as the long-time limit in Eq. (
7
).
Reflecting the Bloch oscillation, the snapshot eigenenergy and eigenstates are time-periodic functions,
\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(t+T_{\!B})=\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(t)
and
\ket{u_{k\alpha}(t+T_{\!B})}=\ket{u_{k\alpha}(t)}
, respectively.
Here,
T_{\!B}=2\pi/|E|a_{0}
is the period of the Bloch oscillation and
a_{0}
is the lattice constant.
On the other hand, the adiabatic basis, which includes the change in the dynamical phase, satisfies

\ket{\Phi_{k\alpha}(t+T_{\!B})}=\e^{-i\theta_{k\alpha}(T_{\!B})}\ket{\Phi_{k\alpha}(t)}
.
Then, the Hamiltonian in the adiabatic basis follows the relation below,

\mathcal{W}_{k}(t+T_{\!B})=\mathcal{X}^{\,-1}_{\!B}\,\mathcal{W}_{k}(t)\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B},

(9)

where
[\mathcal{X}_{\!B}]_{\alpha\beta}=\e^{-i\alpha\Theta_{B}/2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
and

\displaystyle\Theta_{B}(E)=\int_{\,0}^{\,T_{\!B}}[\Delta_{k}(t^{\prime})+ER_{k}(t^{\prime})]\,dt^{\prime}.

(10)

Note that
\Theta_{B}(E)
is a gauge invariant quantity.
Consequently, we obtain the following relation for the time evolution
U(t_{1},t_{2})
,

U(t_{1}+T_{\!B},t_{2}+T_{\!B})=\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-1}\,U(t_{1},t_{2})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}.

(11)

This suggests that the time evolution at any time can be described by the time evolution operator over the time interval
[0,T_{\!B}]
.
Given that the reduced time
\tilde{t}\in[0,T_{\!B}]
and
m\in\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
, any time
t\in[mT_{\!B},(m+1)T_{\!B}]
can be written as
t=t_{m}=\tilde{t}+mT_{\!B}
. Therefore,

U(t)
can be decomposed as

\begin{split}U(t_{m})&=U\big(\,\tilde{t}+mT_{\!B},T_{\!B}\big)\,U(T_{\!B})\\
&=\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-1}\,U\big(\,\tilde{t}+(m-1)T_{\!B})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}U(T_{\!B})\\
&=\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-m}\,U(\,\tilde{t}\,)\,\big[\mathcal{X}_{\!B}\,U(T_{\!B})\big]^{\,m},\end{split}

(12)

using the relation
U(t_{1},t_{2})=U(t_{1},t_{3})\,U(t_{3},t_{2})
and Eq. (
11
).

Since our concern is the density matrix in the nonequilibrium steady state
\rho^{\rm NESS}_{K}
, let us consider the long-time limit for sufficiently large
m
below.
In this case, the first term in Eq. (
7
) can be neglected. By decomposing the integral of the second term
as
\int_{\,t_{0}=0}^{\,t_{m}}ds=\int_{\,mT_{\!B}}^{\,\tilde{t}+mT_{\!B}}ds+\int_{\,0}^{\,mT_{\!B}}ds
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(t_{m})\simeq\rho_{k0}(t_{m})\!+\!\rho_{k1}(t_{m})=\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-m}\,\Big[\,\tilde{\rho}_{k0}(\,\tilde{t}\,)\!+\!\tilde{\rho}_{k1}(\,\tilde{t}\,)\,\Big]\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,m},

(13)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k0}(\,\tilde{t}\,)=U(\,\tilde{t}\,)\left(\int_{0}^{\,\tilde{t}\,}\!\!U^{\dagger}(\,\tilde{s}\,)\,\Sigma(\,\tilde{s}\,)\,U(\,\tilde{s}\,)\e^{-(\,\tilde{t}-\,\tilde{s}\,\,)/\tau}d\tilde{s}\right)U^{\dagger}(\,\tilde{t}\,),

(14)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k1}(\,\tilde{t}\,)=\!\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,n}\,U(t_{n})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}\,\tilde{\rho}_{k0}(T_{\!B})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-1}U^{\dagger}(t_{n})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-n}\e^{-t_{n}/\tau}.

(15)

See Appendix
A
for the details.
The terms
\tilde{\rho}_{k0}(\,\tilde{t}\,)
and
\tilde{\rho}_{k1}(\,\tilde{t}\,)
correspond to the components that do not include and include the interference effect of Bloch-Zener oscillations, respectively. By using Eq. (
12
), each term in Eq. (
15
) can be written in the form
U(\,\tilde{t}\,)\,\rho U^{\dagger}(\,\tilde{t}\,)\e^{-{\tilde{t}}/\tau}
. For example, the first term and the second term, respectively, are expressed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k1}^{(1)}(\,\tilde{t}\,)

\displaystyle=U(\,\tilde{t}\,)\,\Big(\mathcal{X}_{\!B}\,\tilde{\rho}_{k0}(T_{\!B})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-1}\Big)U^{\dagger}(\,\tilde{t}\,)\e^{-{\tilde{t}}/\tau},

(16a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{k1}^{(2)}(\,\tilde{t}\,)

\displaystyle=U(\,\tilde{t}\,)\,\Big(\mathcal{X}_{\!B}\,\tilde{\rho}_{k1}^{(1)}(T_{\!B})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-1}\Big)U^{\dagger}(\,\tilde{t}\,)\e^{-{\tilde{t}}/\tau}.

(16b)

The physical meaning becomes clear by comparing these forms with the first term in Eq. (
7
). The first term in Eq. (
7
),

U(t)\rho_{k}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)\e^{-t/\tau}
, implies that the electron in state
k
undergoes Landau-Zener tunneling through the time evolution
U(t)
and decays with a damping rate
\tau^{-1}
. Therefore,
\tilde{\rho}_{k1}^{(1)}(\,\tilde{t}\,)
indicates that an electron experiencing one cycle of Bloch oscillation undergoes Landau-Zener tunneling through
U(t)
, with the resulting interference decaying at a rate of
\tau^{-1}
.

\tilde{\rho}_{k1}^{(2)}(\,\tilde{t}\,)
corresponds to the interference effect after one cycle of Bloch-Zener oscillation.

Note that Eq. (
15
) includes the decay factor
\e^{-nT_{\!B}/\tau}
.
The interference effect strongly depends on the ratio of the Bloch period
T_{\!B}
to the relaxation time
\tau
.
Thus, in
T_{B}/\tau>1
, Eq. (
16
) gives a reliable approximation of the interference term of Eq. (
15
).
In the following section, let us demonstrate that
\rho_{k}(t_{m})
for sufficiently large
m
describes the distribution at the wavevector
k-E\tilde{t}
in the nonequilibrium steady state,

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(t_{m})\simeq\rho^{\rm NESS}_{k-E\tilde{t}}=\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,-m}\,\tilde{\rho}^{\rm NESS}_{k-E\tilde{t}}\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,m}.

(17)

II.4
Current response

In this section, let us discuss the interference of the Landau-Zener tunneling in the current response, given by

J(t)=-e\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\,\Tr\,\big[v_{k}(t)\rho_{k}(t)\big]\,.

(19)

Here,
v_{k}(t)
is the velocity matrix in the adiabatic basis,

\begin{split}[v_{k}(t)]_{\alpha\beta}&=\matrixelement{\Phi_{k\alpha}(t)}{\partial_k\mathcal H_k(t)}{\Phi_{k\beta}(t)}\\

\alpha\,i\Delta_{k}(t)[\mathcal{W}_{k}(t)]_{\alpha\beta}/E&(\alpha\neq\beta).\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(20)

The integral of Eq. (
19
) is performed over the Brillouin Zone (BZ).
In the long-time limit, Eq. (

) represents the electric current in the nonequilibrium steady state,

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}(E)=J(t\rightarrow\infty)=-e\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{dK}{2\pi}\Tr[\tilde{v}_{K}\,\tilde{\rho}^{\rm NESS}_{K}\big]\,,

(21)

using the relation
\tilde{v}_{k-E\tilde{t}}=v_{k}(\tilde{t})=\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{\,m}\,v_{k}(t_{m})\,\mathcal{X}_{\!B}^{-m}

III
Geometric effects in multi-tunneling

In this section, we explore the geometric effects in multi-tunneling induced by Bloch-Zener oscillations. First, we formulate the density matrix in a nonequilibrium steady state using the adiabatic-impulse approximation (AIA)
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
and derive the condition for constructive interference (CCI). Finally, we elucidate the geometric effects of multi-tunneling on carrier occupation and current response.

III.1
Overview

For simplicity, we analyze the density matrix in a nonequilibrium steady state where
T_{B}\gg\tau
. In this case, due to the factor
\e^{-T_{B}/\tau}
, the multi-tunneling effect can be adequately evaluated up to the second tunneling event. Below, we provide an overview of the formulation of the density matrix in the nonequilibrium steady state.

First, we introduce the AIA to analytically evaluate the time evolution operator
U(\tilde{t})
. AIA is a reliable approximation for the time evolution operator of isolated systems and is widely used as a convenient method to describe interference arising from different tunneling processes, known as Landau-Zener-Stückelberg interference
[
7
,
49
]
. In AIA, it is assumed that only the transition at the point of minimum gap for a single electron is non-adiabatic, while all other processes remain adiabatic.

In the adiabatic basis, the adiabatic time evolution operator,
U_{\rm ad}
, becomes an identity matrix. Therefore, the time evolution operator for the isolated system can be approximated as follows.

U(\,\tilde{t}\,)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I&(\,\tilde{t}<t_{g})\\
T&(\,\tilde{t}>t_{g})\end{array}\right.,

(22)

where
t_{g}
denotes the time at which the tunneling process occurs, and
T
is the transfer matrix associated with the tunneling process. See Appendix
B
for the details.
The transfer matrix
T
can be determined based on the continuous model that describes the vicinity of the gap minima where the tunneling process occurs.
The low-energy excitations of the SSH model considered above can be described by the following Landau-Zener model,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}=vk\sigma_{x}+\Delta/2\sigma_{z}\,.

(23)

Here, to account for the extension to noncentrosymmetric systems, we assume that the low-energy excitations are described by the twisted Landau-Zener model given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}=vk\sigma_{x}+\eta k^{2}/2\sigma_{y}+\Delta/2\sigma_{z}\,.

(24)

In this case, the transfer matrix in the adiabatic basis is given by

T=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-P\,}\e^{-i\phi_{S}}&-\sqrt{P\,}\e^{i\delta\theta_{k}(t_{g})}\\
\sqrt{P\,}\e^{-i\delta\theta_{k}(t_{g})}&\sqrt{1-P\,}\e^{i\phi_{S}}\end{pmatrix},

(25)

where
\delta\theta_{k}(t)=\theta_{k+}(t)-\theta_{k-}(t)
and
P
denotes the tunneling probability
P=\e^{-2\pi\delta}
with

\displaystyle\delta=\frac{\Big(\Delta+\frac{E\eta}{2v}\Big)^{2}}{8v|E|}.

(26)

The phase
\phi_{S}
, known as the Stokes phase
[
46
]
, represents the phase acquired during the tunneling process and is expressed as

\displaystyle\phi_{S}=\frac{\pi}{4}+\arg\,\Gamma(1-i\delta)+\delta\,(\ln\delta-1),

(27)

where
\Gamma(x)
is the gamma function.
See Appendix
C
for the details.
For finite values of
\eta
,
\delta
exhibits nonreciprocity and demonstrates perfect tunneling at a specific electric field
E
where
\delta=0
. Furthermore, when
E
is sufficiently large,
\delta
increases proportionally with
E
, indicative of the counter-diabatic driving.

III.2
Condition for constructive interference (CCI)

Let us proceed to the evaluation of
\tilde{\rho}_{k}(\,\tilde{t}\,)
. For
T_{B}\gg\tau
, it is approximately described by Eq. (
16
). For simplicity, we evaluate them within the RTA framework to derive the interference conditions, as the DPA corrections are primarily intended to improve the gapped behavior in the low-field regime and are not essential to the present objective. In this case, the diagonal parts

[\tilde{\rho}_{k}(\,\tilde{t}\,)]_{\alpha\alpha}
is estimated as

\displaystyle[\tilde{\rho}_{k}(\,\tilde{t}\,)]_{\alpha\alpha}\simeq f_{D}(\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(\,\tilde{t}\,))+\alpha\,N(E)\,g(\,\tilde{t}\,)\e^{-(\tilde{t}-t_{g})/\tau},

(29a)

\displaystyle N_{2}(E)\simeq\bigg[-P+4P(1-P)\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\Theta_{B}(E)+2\phi_{S}}{2}\right)\bigg]\,\delta f_{k}(t_{g})\e^{-T_{B}/\tau}.

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm av}=\frac{1}{T_{B}}\int_{0}^{T_{B}}\Delta_{k}(s)ds=a_{0}\int_{\rm BZ}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\Delta_{k},

IV
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we have investigated the role of quantum geometric effects and the multi-tunneling effects in nonreciprocal Landau-Zener tunneling. We first calculated the density matrix of lattice systems based on the quantum kinetic equation, decomposing the dissipative dynamics into coherent and incoherent components to clarify their contributions. The derived density matrix characterizes the nonequilibrium steady state of insulating systems, facilitating efficient calculation of nonequilibrium steady state physical quantities with low computational cost. In noncentrosymmetric systems, we demonstrated that quantum geometric effects enhance the nonreciprocity of current induced by Bloch-Zener oscillations, allowing for controlled directionality of the current.

Bloch oscillations have been experimentally observed in optical lattice systems, suggesting that our results may also be observable.
Although observing Bloch oscillations in lattice systems is generally challenging, they have been successfully observed in semiconductor superlattices
[
51
]
and proposed in moiré materials
[
52
]
. In particular, moiré materials form a flat band near the “magic angle”, enhancing quantum geometric effects
[
53
,
54
,
55
]
.
Thus, systems such as twisted bilayer graphene offer a promising platform for exploring intriguing phenomena driven by geometric multi-tunneling.

Experimentally, generating strong electric fields may be more practical with AC fields than with DC fields.
Our findings may be potentially observed in gapped 2D Dirac systems under a circular polarized light, which can describe approximately the Rice-Mele model under the DC electric field discussed in this paper, by replacing
ka_{0}/2
to
\Omega t
and
v,\delta v
to
vE_{0}/\Omega
in Eq. (
34
).

Furthermore, it would be both significant and worthwhile to extend this study to AC electric fields. Under AC fields, the dominant process—whether multiphoton absorption or quantum tunneling—continuously changes via the Keldysh crossover.
In this context, nonreciprocal Landau-Zener tunneling and shift currents represent extreme cases of phenomena arising from the same geometric quantity, i.e. quantum tunneling and multiphoton absorption. Elucidating these relationships in terms of the Keldysh crossover could lead to a systematic understanding of quantum geometric effects in the nonperturbative regime.

Appendix B
Adiabatic impulse approximation in adiabatic basis

We introduce the adiabatic impulse approximation (AIA) in Landau-Zener problem.
In the AIA, we consider the Houston basis,
\ket{\Psi(t)}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(t)\ket{u_{\alpha}(t)}
with initial state
\ket{u_{-}(t_0)}
.
This basis differs from the adiabatic basis only in its dynamical and Berry phases.
The time evolution of
\bm{c}(t)=(c_{+}(t),c_{-}(t))^{T}
is governed by the time evolution operator in the Houston basis,

\displaystyle U^{H}(t,t_{0})=\mathcal{T}\exp[-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathcal{H}^{H}(\,\tilde{t}\,)d\,\tilde{t}\,\bigg],

(43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{H}(t)=V^{H{\dagger}}(t)\bigg[\mathcal{H}(t)-i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg]V^{H}(t),

(44)

where
V^{H}(t)=(\ket{u_{+}(t)},\ket{u_{-}(t)})
.
In the AIA, the time evolution is devided
into three cases: (i)
t<t_{g}
, (ii)
t=t_{g}
, and (iii)
t>t_{g}
, where
t_{g}
is the time at which the electron passes through the gap minima.
First, in the case (i), the time evolution of
\bm{c}(t)
is expressed as
\bm{c}(t)=U_{ad}(t,t_{0})\bm{c}(t_{0})
, where
U_{ad}(t_{1},t_{2})
is the adiabatic time evolution

\displaystyle U_{ad}(t_{1},t_{2})=\left(\begin{matrix}\e^{-i\theta_{k+}(t_{1},t_{2})}&0\\
0&\e^{-i\theta_{k-}(t_{1},t_{2})}\end{matrix}\right),

(45)

\displaystyle\theta_{k\alpha}(t_{1},t_{2})=\int_{t_{2}}^{t_{1}}[\varepsilon_{k\alpha}(\,\tilde{t}\,)+EA_{k,\alpha\alpha}(\,\tilde{t}\,)]d\,\tilde{t}\,.

(46)

Next, at (ii)
t=t_{g}
, a Landau-Zener transition is assumed to occur, imposing the relation,
\bm{c}(t_{g}+)=T^{H}\bm{c}(t_{g}-)
,
where
T^{H}
is the transfer matrix in the Houston basis.
Finally, in the case (iii),
\bm{c}(t)
undergoes adiabatic time evolution once more, resulting in
\bm{c}(t)=U_{ad}(t,t_{g})T^{H}U_{ad}(t_{g},t_{0})\bm{c}(t_{0})
.
As a result, the time evolution operator can be approximated as

U^{H}(t,t_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}U_{ad}(t,t_{0})&(t<t_{g})\\
U_{ad}(t,t_{g}+)T^{H}U_{ad}(t_{g}-,t_{0})&(t>t_{g})\end{array}\right.\,.

(47)

Using
U_{ad}(t,t_{0})
, a unitary transformation can convert the Houston basis onto the adiabatic basis. The Hamiltonian in the adiabatic basis and the time evolution are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}(t)=U^{{\dagger}}_{ad}(t,t_{0})\,\bigg(\mathcal{H}^{H}(t)-i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg)\,U_{ad}(t,t_{0}),

(48)

\displaystyle U(t,t_{0})=U^{{\dagger}}_{ad}(t,t_{0})\,U^{H}(t,t_{0}).

(49)

In this case, the time evolution operator in the adiabatic basis is expressed as

U(t,t_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I&(t<t_{g})\\
T=U^{\dagger}_{ad}(t_{g},t_{0})T^{H}U_{ad}(t_{g},t_{0})&(t>t_{g})\end{array}\right..

(50)
