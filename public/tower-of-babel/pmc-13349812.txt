Future of xenotransplantation in Korea
Authors: Ik Jin Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the chronic shortage of human donor organs becomes an undeniable clinical reality, xenotransplantation has transitioned from a theoretical concept to the threshold of clinical integration. Recent successful pig-to-human kidney trials signify a pivotal milestone, shifting the scientific focus from feasibility to long-term sustainability and systemic implementation. This review critically evaluates the current status and future trajectory of xenotransplantation, challenging the "more is better" paradox of genetic engineering. It emphasizes the minimum viable edit set and the physiological superiority of miniature pigs overgrowth hormone receptor knockout models. Furthermore, the paper outlines a Korean-style governance model, integrating advanced digital transformation and specialized infrastructure. Key proposed strategies include (1) digital monitoring: implementing a noninvasive surveillance system utilizing donor-derived cell-free DNA, digital twins, and AI-driven early warning systems; (2) blockchain integration: establishing data integrity and tokenomics-based patient compliance through a sovereign medical coin system to incentivize lifelong surveillance; (3) infrastructure innovation: introducing the Hybrid Hospital Model, featuring a sterile barrier window between porcine and human surgical suites to achieve a zero-ischemia workflow. Finally, the study stresses the necessity of a Xenotransplantation Special Act and the collaboration between public organ procurement organizations and specialized private contract research organizations. By combining genetic minimalism and a robust legal framework, South Korea can establish itself as a global standard setter, transforming xenotransplantation into a standardized, manageable precision medical service.Keywords: Minimum viable edit, Hybrid hospital

INTRODUCTION

The realization that allotransplantation can no longer meet the burgeoning global demand for organ replacement has become an undeniable clinical reality. Despite persistent efforts to optimize organ procurement and promote donation, the chronic discrepancy between the supply of and demand for human organs remains unresolved. Furthermore, reliance on living donor transplantation presents profound socioethical challenges. Beyond the concerns regarding organ trafficking, even intrafamilial donations—often viewed as the most ethically acceptable path—frequently involve complex psychological pressures and ethical dilemmas. Fundamentally, extending one's life by utilizing the life or organs of another, whether from a living or a deceased donor (donation after brain death/donation after circulatory death), carries an inescapable moral burden that remains difficult to rationalize fully through conventional medical ethics [1].

In this context, xenotransplantation has emerged not merely as a supplementary option, but as a transformative necessity. The field reached a pivotal milestone in 2003 with the birth of the first α1,3-galactosyltransferase gene knockout (GalT-KO) pigs. Since then, the advent of CRISPR/Cas9 technology has exponentially accelerated the evolution of genetic engineering, shifting the primary scientific inquiry from the feasibility of editing to the identification of the optimal genetic combinations for clinical success [2]. Guided by two decades of rigorous nonhuman primate (NHP) data, xenotransplantation has recently achieved successful transition into human clinical trials, beginning with the kidney [3]. It is now highly probable that xeno-kidney transplantation will reach clinical integration within years, followed by clinical attempts in other solid organs [4,5]. As the field moves from being a bridge to a destination therapy, it is imperative to critically evaluate the factors—ranging from genetic optimization and novel immunosuppression to specialized infrastructure—that will define the future of this discipline [6].

THE PARADOX OF GENETIC COMPLEXITY: IS "MORE" TRULY "BETTER"?

As we stand on the threshold of clinical application, the focus has shifted toward refining the porcine donor. However, the historical trajectory reveals a complex and often paradoxical relationship between the number of genetic modifications and actual graft survival.

Historical Paradox: Lessons From Early Multigene Models

In the early 2010s, pioneering research in Germany led to the development of porcine models with up to seven genetic modifications. These models were met with great anticipation; however, subsequent preclinical results were sobering. In many instances, these multigene models failed to demonstrate a statistically significant survival advantage over simpler GalT-KO models in NHP studies [7]. This outcome served as a critical turning point, leading the community to realize that simply increasing the quantity of genetic edits—an additive approach—was not a panacea for the intricate network of xenogeneic rejection [8].

The 10-Gene Era: Perfection or Branding?

A decade later, the maturation of CRISPR/Cas9 has led to the branding of the 10-gene edited pig. Much like the number 10 symbolizes perfection in various cultures, this model (typically 3 knockouts [KOs] and 7 knockins [KIs]) is now widely embraced as the ideal clinical standard [9]. Recent data from the eGenesis group demonstrated that their 69-gene edited pigs (including seven functional KIs) significantly outperform 3-KO models in NHP kidney transplants [10]. However, a critical scientific gap remains: there have been no prospective, head-to-head randomized trials comparing 3–5 KI models against 7 KI models. Although comprehensive KI models are thought to better address consumptive coagulopathy and chronic microvascular inflammation, this evidence remains largely based on indirect comparisons of isolated studies [11].

Unvetted Risks and the Need for Precision Editing

Crucially, the assumption that a "more edited" pig is a "better" donor lacks rigorous validation. Long-term NHP survival of over 1 year has been achieved using pigs with fewer than 10 modifications, suggesting that qualitative synergy may be more vital than the quantity of edits [12]. Recent clinical experiments further highlight that while complex edits offer stability, they do not yet eliminate the need for intensive immunosuppressive regimens [13]. Furthermore, the potential fitness cost of extensive genetic manipulation—including off-target effects and chromosomal instability—has not been strictly evaluated [14]. Biological reality involves complex functional redundancy and interpathway interactions that cannot be solved by simply categorizing transgenes into antirejection silos [15].

Future Strategies for Genetic Optimization: Toward Evidence-Based Precision

To overcome the current stalemate of arbitrary multigene branding, the field must transition toward a more data-driven and functionally validated approach.

Minimum viable edit sets

Identifying the minimum viable edit set for each organ will minimize unnecessary genomic instability while maintaining physiological robustness [16].

Quantitative and inducible expression

Optimization must move beyond binary insertions toward controlling expression levels through inducible promoters that respond only during rejection crises, thereby reducing the metabolic burden on the donor pig.

Refined immunomodulation

The future of genetic engineering must be paired with novel costimulatory pathway blockades (e.g., anti-CD40/CD40L) to achieve synergistic protection without the systemic toxicity of conventional regimens [17].

Organ-specific tailoring

Genetic profiles must be tailored to unique physiological needs (e.g., specific humanized coagulation factors for the liver vs. growth hormone receptor knockout for the heart) [18].

Artificial intelligence-driven predictive monitoring

Integration of digital twins and remote liquid biopsies (monitoring donor-derived cell-free DNA [dd-cfDNA]) will allow for real-time adjustments in clinical protocols, ensuring that the genetic blueprint matches the recipient’s dynamic immune status [19,20].

KEY STRATEGIES AND CRITICAL REVIEWS OF IMMUNOLOGICAL MANAGEMENT IN XENOTRANSPLANTATION

Fundamental Blockade of Rejection via Multigene Editing

The essence of genetic engineering for xenotransplantation lies in eliminating key antigens on the porcine cell surface recognized by human natural antibodies (e.g., α-Gal, Neu5Gc, SDa) to prevent hyperacute rejection. Furthermore, it involves inserting human complement regulatory proteins (e.g., CD46, CD55) and coagulation regulatory genes (e.g., TBM, EPCR) to control microthrombosis and endothelial damage, ultimately preventing graft failure [21]. Theoretically, if these modifications were perfect, xenografts would achieve full engraftment without the need for immunosuppressants; however, there is currently no foreseeable prospect of such an ideal state. While research into perfect genetic engineering must continue, the practical path toward clinical application will likely involve optimizing modifications to a level manageable with appropriate immunosuppressive regimens. Given that the number of genetic edits cannot increase indefinitely and it remains unclear which factors are most decisive among similar genes, continuous advancement and preclinical validation of genetic engineering are essential [22]. Nevertheless, this complexity may become a critical weakness in establishing standardized commercial models, a point that will remain a subject of ongoing debate.

Calcineurin Inhibitor-Free and Costimulatory Blockade-Centered Immunological Control

Standard calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) used in allotransplantation have shown significant limitations in xenotransplantation; they fail to sufficiently suppress T cell immunity and exacerbate endothelial toxicity and thrombotic microangiopathy in the xenograft. To overcome these hurdles, costimulatory blockade targeting the CD40/CD154 pathway has emerged as the new standard for CNI-free protocols, significantly improving preclinical outcomes [23]. However, the agents developed in this class thus far have not yet achieved the clinical maturity required for widespread use in terms of safety and efficacy. Future developments in biphasic antibodies with modified Fc portions—designed to reduce coagulation-related side effects while enhancing potency—are expected to provide an immunosuppressive framework that maintains vascular endothelial integrity and dramatically extends graft survival [24].

Limitations and Critical Alternatives in Biosecurity and Compatibility

Current approaches to genetic control necessitate the following critical evaluations.

Limitations of porcine endogenous retroviruses control

While porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERVs) inactivation via CRISPR/Cas9 is technologically encouraging, excessive genomic editing carries risks of off-target effects and potential reactivation [25]. Rather than relying solely on genetic perfection, a multilayered biosecurity system combining designated pathogen-free (DPF) facilities with molecular diagnostic monitoring offers a more realistic solution.

Nonphysiological nature of growth hormone receptor knockout

Growth hormone receptor knockout to suppress organ size risks distorting the metabolic pathways of porcine organs and causing premature failure. Instead of forced genetic editing, utilizing miniature pigs, whose body size is similar to humans, is more appropriate for preserving the physiological stability of organ metabolism. Continuous attention must be paid to the specific porcine strains used, with a flexible development approach that allows for a rapid transition to strains demonstrating superior performance [26].

Minimalism in genetic engineering

An excessive editing burden compromises the health of the donor animal. Technological efficiency must be emphasized by editing only the core genes essential for rejection control and supplementing deficiencies with sophisticated pharmacological therapies. We must move away from the mindset of "editing because we can" and prioritize a research attitude that explores "which edits are truly necessary" [27].

Perspectives and Realistic Alternatives for Tolerance Induction

This section examines the feasibility of immunological tolerance—eliminating the need for lifelong immunosuppression—and its alternatives. Regarding the possibilities and constraints of tolerance, while thymus transplantation or mixed chimerism are academically ideal goals, they suffer from low reproducibility in xenotransplantation due to robust humoral responses and complement activation across the high species barrier [28]. Furthermore, whether transplanting the porcine central immune system into a human to induce tolerance is ethically and biologically acceptable remains a grave concern. This is because pursuing the porcinization of the human immune system, rather than humanization of the graft, poses significant potential risks.

A realistic alternative is sustainable precision immunosuppression. If perfect tolerance induction is unfeasible, the alternative is minimized precision immunosuppression that does not compromise the recipient's quality of life. This strategy involves combining costimulatory blockades with blockchain-based real-time monitoring to detect early signs of rejection and individualize drug dosages, thereby extending graft survival to match the recipient's life expectancy.

As a concluding outlook, while tolerance remains a long-term research goal, the most practical near-term future lies in establishing a xenotransplantation model as a manageable chronic disease. This can be achieved through a combination of genetically optimized miniature pigs and side-effect-free CNI-free drug regimens.

ESTABLISHING A NONINVASIVE MONITORING SYSTEM FOR XENOTRANSPLANTATION VIA DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION

Lifelong follow-up of recipients is a mandatory prerequisite for xenotransplantation to prevent potential xenozoonosis and manage long-term graft rejection [29]. This monitoring cannot be fully realized through independent, isolated systems of individual medical institutions. Furthermore, the existing lifelong management systems for allotransplantation should be integrated into a unified registry that allows for direct performance comparisons, facilitated by active cooperation from hospitals and patients. Therefore, the construction of a digital-based smart monitoring system capable of detecting subtle anomalies early without compromising the recipient's quality of life is indispensable.

Liquid Biopsy: Real-Time Noninvasive Rejection Surveillance

While biopsy remains the most accurate diagnostic tool for assessing graft status, repeated invasive procedures are impractical. To supplement or replace this, technologies analyzing biomarkers in blood and body fluids must be implemented as core components.

Donor-derived cell-free DNA

This measures porcine-specific genomic fragments released into the blood when xenograft cells are damaged. It is a highly precise tool that can detect signs of rejection several days before traditional markers, such as creatinine, show elevation, enabling early clinical intervention [30].

Multiomics analysis

By analyzing blood-based RNA panels and metabolites, clinicians can differentiate between immunosuppressant toxicity and actual rejection. This early warning system provides the evidence base for individualized drug dosage adjustments [31].

Digital Twin and Artificial Intelligence-Based Prognostic Prediction

Forward-looking technologies that replicate a patient’s clinical status in a virtual space to simulate future organ performance should be adopted.

Virtual simulation model

By constructing a digital twin that integrates the recipient's immune status with the genetic characteristics of the donor pig, the impact of changes in immunosuppressive regimens or physiological shifts can be preemptively tested in a virtual environment [20].

Artificial intelligence early warning system

Artificial intelligence analyzes cumulative real-time biometric data to detect subtle pattern changes invisible to the naked eye, operating an intelligent surveillance system that immediately alerts medical staff to risks of rejection or infection [20].

Blockchain: Ensuring Data Integrity and Traceability

Blockchain technology is essential for ensuring data transparency and integrity, which are directly linked to public health and legal accountability [32].

Lifecycle traceability

Every step—from genetic design and pathogen-free breeding to procurement, transplantation, and lifelong follow-up—is recorded on a blockchain. This creates an environment where data tampering is fundamentally blocked, securing the trust of both regulatory bodies and the public.

Data sovereignty and incentive systems

Smart contracts are used to clarify the rights and responsibilities of both recipients and medical professionals regarding data provision. Utilizing a sovereign xenotransplantation medical coin as a means of compensation can incentivize data sharing, foster a reciprocal research environment, and ensure the sustainable growth of the xenotransplantation ecosystem.

CLINICAL INFRASTRUCTURE AND ECOSYSTEM

The Role of Specialized Organ Procurement Organizations and Contract Research Organizations

Whether a separate organ procurement organization (OPO) is necessary for xenotransplantation, and how its role would differ from conventional allotransplantation OPOs, is a complex issue. In allotransplantation, the fairness of organ donation and allocation is the highest priority; thus, the OPO must continuously manage donor recruitment and ethical allocation in addition to organ procurement. Conversely, since genetically engineered pigs can be commercially supplied and mass-produced, xenotransplantation may be relatively free from the classical ethical dilemmas surrounding donation and allocation.

However, xenografts must only be sourced from animals whose pathogen-free status and functional integrity have been strictly verified. As high-value biological assets, these organs must be supplied transparently through proper authorization procedures. Therefore, public intervention in the selection, procurement, and supply of xenografts is essential. This implies a national-level mandate to control infection risks and ensure the supply of the most functionally appropriate organs. Consequently, a realistic and efficient model would involve establishing a dedicated xenotransplantation department within an existing public OPO (such as the Korea Organ Donation Agency [KODA]), which has historically managed organ allocation and donor/recipient registries.

Furthermore, to create a credible research environment from preclinical studies to clinical trials, a specialized xenotransplantation contract research organization (CRO) is indispensable. The development and dissemination of xenotransplantation differ significantly from general medical service delivery. While it may be less controversial from a bioethical perspective compared to allotransplantation, a continuous monitoring system must remain active even after clinical trial approval and implementation, considering recipient safety and national healthcare efficiency.

In particular, due to the potential risk of xenozoonosis, lifelong surveillance of recipients is a legal and ethical prerequisite. A specialized CRO must not only manage clinical indicators within the recipient in real time but also ensure the permanence and safety of the clinical environment by continuously feeding preclinical research results back into clinical practice and reporting these outcomes to regulatory agencies. In conclusion, the combination of publicly trusted organ supply management via a dedicated OPO and comprehensive clinical/postoperative management by a specialized CRO is an essential requirement for establishing xenotransplantation as a standardized medical system beyond a mere experimental attempt.

The Hybrid Hospital Model: Ensuring Minimal Ischemia via a Sterile Barrier Window

One of the most significant hurdles in clinical xenotransplantation is the logistical impossibility of bringing a donor pig directly into a human operating theater. Although the sterility requirements for donor animals are arguably more stringent than those for human recipients, the necessity of ensuring collective biosafety precludes porcine procurement within human surgical suites. Currently, the emerging standard—particularly in the United States—involves remote procurement followed by transport using normothermic machine perfusion to minimize graft damage. However, even the most advanced perfusion technologies cannot entirely eliminate the intrinsic injury caused by the interval between procurement and transplantation [33]. In this regard, the Hybrid Hospital Model, which utilizes a sterile barrier to spatiotemporally integrate porcine and human surgical suites, represents an unparalleled infrastructure for specialized xenotransplantation.

Architecture of the hybrid hospital: the sterile barrier system

Physically located within a single facility, the hybrid hospital employs a rigorous sterile barrier that completely isolates the porcine procurement suite from the human transplantation room. These two environments are linked only by an airtight, high-level sterile transfer window. This configuration inherently eliminates the risks of contamination and ischemia-reperfusion injury associated with long-distance transport. By maintaining separate clean-room systems that function in immediate physical proximity, the model achieves a zero-ischemia workflow where the graft is transferred and reanastomosed within minutes of excision.

Structural isolation and functional integration

To ensure absolute biosafety, the facility is designed with strictly separate entrances and ventilation systems for the human hospital and the DPF animal research center. This dual-track infrastructure ensures that while the donor organ moves seamlessly through the sterile window, there is a total blockade of human-animal personnel interaction and environmental cross-contamination. By optimizing the physical distance between the donor and the recipient to its absolute minimum, the Hybrid Hospital Model provides the most robust clinical environment for maximizing graft viability and ensuring long-term success in xenotransplantation [30].

Establishing a Regulatory Ecosystem: The Necessity of a Xenotransplantation Special Act

To ensure the clinical success of xenotransplantation in South Korea, a new paradigm that transcends the existing legal framework is required. Currently, xenotransplantation is managed under the Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biopharmaceuticals Act, which merely provides a foundational basis for research. This framework is insufficient to address the unique and lifelong complexities of patient management required as xenotransplantation moves through clinical trials toward full-scale clinical application. To realize the vision of a blockchain-based, sustainable clinical ecosystem, the enactment of a dedicated Xenotransplantation Special Act is an indispensable prerequisite [34].

Legal mandate for lifelong surveillance and data integrity

Unlike allotransplantation, xenotransplantation carries the inherent risk of xenozoonosis, such as the transmission of PERVs. The current legal framework is limited in its ability to enforce lifelong follow-up if a recipient withdraws consent. This limitation must be viewed as a potential risk factor that could threaten not only the individual recipient but also the safety of the entire community. Therefore, the new Act must legally mandate a permanent surveillance system and provide the structural basis for a blockchain-based decentralized identity system. This will ensure that even if a recipient changes residency or jurisdiction, their biosafety data remains uncompromised and verifiable in an immutable state [29].

Facilitating data sovereignty and compensation models

To implement a tokenomics model for the provision of clinical data, a harmonious amendment of the Personal Information Protection Act and the Medical Service Act is necessary. Legislation should clearly define the scope of data portability (Medical MyData), establishing a pathway for patients to voluntarily provide their clinical markers (e.g., dd-cfDNA) to research consortiums in exchange for legitimate incentives [23]. This must be legally and strictly distinguished from "undue inducement" or "patient brokering," and should instead be codified as just compensation for contributing to the advancement of a global public health database.

Restructuring medical incentives and compliance

The strict antirebate regulations under the current Medical Service Act pose a significant barrier to providing fair compensation to clinicians who must meticulously manage complex xeno-clinical data [35]. The Special Act should include explicit exemptions for research contribution tokens, defining them not as payment for medical procedures, but as compensation for the administrative and investigative labor required to ensure data integrity [24]. Furthermore, porcine organs should be unified under a specialized regulatory status as "xenotransplantation products" rather than mere "organs," streamlining the approval process between the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) and the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) to maximize administrative efficiency [25].

In summary, the transition from an experimental phase to standardized clinical practice necessitates a robust legal infrastructure. Through a dedicated Xenotransplantation Special Act, South Korea can lead the global effort to create a transparent, ethical, and blockchain-secured clinical ecosystem that simultaneously protects individual patient rights and collective biosafety.

Strategic Integration of Existing Public Infrastructures for Future-Oriented Xenotransplantation

The clinical success and social acceptance of xenotransplantation depend not only on medical readiness but also on the establishment of a robust regulatory and monitoring infrastructure. South Korea is uniquely positioned to build a cohesive xenotransplantation network by leveraging the high level of expertise within its well-structured, centralized public health institutions.

Establishing a multiministerial regulatory sandbox

To enhance the practical efficacy of a Xenotransplantation Special Act, a collaborative regulatory sandbox involving the MFDS and the KDCA is essential. While the MFDS focuses on the lifecycle management and regulation of genetically engineered porcine grafts and novel immunosuppressants, the KDCA should provide a national-level surveillance system for xenozoonosis. By utilizing the existing infrastructure of the National Institute of Health to implement long-term bio-banking of recipient samples, the government can mitigate public anxiety regarding cross-species infections and secure institutional trust [36].

Ensuring data sovereignty and ethics via public health IT infrastructure

South Korea’s advanced digital healthcare infrastructure, spearheaded by the Korea Health Information Service (K-HII), offers a decisive competitive advantage. By integrating xenotransplantation follow-up protocols into the national MyHealthWay (Medical MyData) platform, the longitudinal monitoring of recipients can be digitized and secured through blockchain technology to ensure data integrity. Furthermore, the National Institute for Bioethics Policy (KoNIBE) should take a proactive role in standardizing informed consent specific to xenograft recipients, preemptively addressing unique ethical and legal challenges such as data portability and the mandate for permanent surveillance [37].

Strategic industrialization through public-private partnerships and regional clusters

The Korea Health Industry Development Institute should serve as a bridge between academic research and global industrialization. The Hybrid Hospital Model proposed in this study can be linked to the national research-driven hospital initiative; furthermore, the active participation of local governments (municipalities) can contribute significantly to regional vitalization through the creation of specialized medical clusters [38]. Central government-led research support programs should facilitate the development of domestic pharmaceutical pipelines (e.g., PG-405, MD-3) and provide diverse tax incentives and funding. Such synergy between specialized CROs and public agencies will transform xenotransplantation from a localized medical procedure into a scalable national strategic industry (K-Xeno).

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES: ACHIEVING SUSTAINABILITY

Xenotransplantation has reached the threshold of clinical implementation [39]. For this clinical reality to achieve long-term permanence, we must move beyond the success of individual surgeries and postoperative immunosuppression to build an ecosystem where economic, ethical, and technological sustainability is secured.

Balancing Commercial Efficiency with Medical Precision

The industrialization of xenotransplantation must prioritize efficiency in donor animal production through minimalism in genetic engineering. Rather than bearing the excessive costs of overengineering genomes, we must achieve economic sustainability by combining physiologically optimal models, such as miniature pigs, with novel immunosuppressants tailored for xenotransplantation. This will lower production costs while improving transplant outcomes, providing the foundation for xenotransplantation to evolve from an expensive, rare treatment into a popularized, universal medical service.

Reciprocal Research Environments via Blockchain and Medical Coins

A Xenotransplantation Medical Coin system based on data sovereignty will be the core engine for sustainable research. By providing fair compensation for high-quality clinical data provided by recipients and medical professionals, we can build a vast xenotransplantation big data repository unprecedented worldwide. This virtuous cycle of data will enhance the precision of artificial intelligence and digital twins, completing a reciprocal ecosystem that further solidifies patient safety [40].

Hybrid Infrastructure and Balanced Regional Development

The Hybrid Hospital Model proposed in this review will exert powerful synergy when linked with regional flagship national universities and local governments as a national strategic industry. By utilizing these facilities as hubs to alleviate the concentration of resources in metropolitan areas and innovate regional medical infrastructure, we can secure the justification for national-level research and development support and realize social sustainability.

CONCLUSION

As we stand on the cusp of clinical xenotransplantation, this paper has critically reviewed the scientific limitations we face and presented a Korean-style governance and digital innovation model to overcome them. Xenotransplantation is no longer a distant possibility; it is an imminent medical reality. We must move past the era of excess—where every possible technology is applied—and enter an era of precision, deliberating on what is most efficient and safe.

In conclusion, when the reliability of public institutions like KODA is integrated with the expertise of private CROs, and further bolstered by blockchain-based transparent data management and the physiological suitability of miniature pigs, South Korea will secure a dominant position as a standard setter, moving beyond a mere technology adopter in the global market. Xenotransplantation is not only the key to solving the universal challenge of organ shortages but also a new sovereign territory that will drive the digital transformation and industrial leap of South Korea’s medical system.
