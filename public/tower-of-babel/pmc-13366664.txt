Using medial column support instead of screw density to predict radiological failure requiring revision in osteoporotic proximal humerus fractures
Authors: Evren Karaali, Osman Çiloğlu, Burak Keklikçioğlu, Hakan Uslu, Bedirhan Sarı, Özhan Pazarcı, Harun Sidar Seçkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProximal humerus fractures (PHFs) are fragility fractures that commonly occur in elderly patients and are frequently associated with osteoporosis. Despite the widespread use of locking plate fixation for displaced fractures, complication and reoperation rates remain high, particularly in complex three- and four-part fracture patterns. Although increasing screw density improves fixation stability, the relative significance between screw number and screw position (particularly medial column support) remains unclear. This study aims to determine whether fixation failure is driven by screw density or by the quality of medial column support and fracture biology in osteoporotic PHFs.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included elderly patients (≥ 65 years) with confirmed osteoporosis who underwent locking plate fixation for three- or four-part PHFs between January 2018 and December 2023 with a minimum radiological follow-up of 24 months and subsequently required revision surgery due to radiological failure. The patients were classified based on screw density (low vs. high), and the distribution of mechanical and biological causes of revision was analyzed. In a second analysis, patients who underwent revision surgery were compared with those who did not to examine the relationship between medial support quality (defined by inferomedial and calcar screw placement) and the likelihood of revision.ResultsA total of 24 patients who required revision surgery were included in the primary analysis (15 with low and nine with high screw density). The distribution of revisions for mechanical and biological reasons did not differ between the groups (p > 0.05). In a secondary analysis involving 166 patients (24 revision, 142 non-revision), limited medial support (one inferomedial/calcar screw) was significantly more common among revision cases than non-revision cases (37.5% vs. 4.9%), whereas adequate medial support (≥ 2 inferomedial/calcar screws) predominated in patients without revision (95.1%) (p < 0.001).ConclusionIn osteoporotic PHFs requiring surgical fixation, revision risk is not determined by the total number of screws but by the quality of medial column support. Adequate inferomedial and calcar screw placement was strongly associated with a lower revision rate, emphasizing that targeted screw positioning rather than increased screw density is the critical determinant of fixation durability in osteoporotic bones.Level of Evidence IIIRetrospective cohort study.

Introduction

Proximal humerus fractures (PHFs) represent approximately 5–6% of all fractures and are among the most common fragility fractures in the elderly population [1]. Their incidence continues to increase in parallel with the rise in life expectancy and the widespread occurrence of osteoporosis. In elderly patients, PHFs typically occur following low-energy trauma, and compromised bone quality significantly complicates surgical management.

One of the most commonly used surgical techniques for the treatment of displaced PHFs in osteoporotic bones is locking plate fixation. Angular stable constructs were developed to address poor screw purchase and fixation instability associated with conventional plating systems. Despite these advances, complication rates remain high, particularly in elderly patients with complex fracture patterns, such as three- and four-part fractures [2–5]. Reported complications include implant failure, reduction loss, pseudarthrosis, and avascular necrosis (AVN) of the humeral head.

Various biomechanical studies have reported that increasing screw density may result in structural stiffness and reduce micromotion, thus potentially improving fixation stability in an osteoporotic bone [6, 7]. However, clinical studies investigating the effects of screw density on surgical outcomes have yielded inconsistent results. Recent literature increasingly highlights that fracture morphology, medial column integrity, and humeral head vascularity may play a more decisive role than the absolute number of screws used [8–10].

Therefore, the current study aimed to determine whether fixation failure in elderly patients with osteoporotic three- and four-part PHFs is primarily affected by screw density or the quality of medial column support and fracture biology. By focusing on a cohort consisting only of patients who underwent revision surgery, we aimed to characterize the relative contributions of mechanical and biological failure mechanisms leading to reoperation. In a secondary analysis including non-revision patients, we further assessed whether medial support quality—rather than the absolute number of screws—was associated with the likelihood of revision, thereby testing the hypothesis that screw location, not screw quantity, is the critical determinant of fixation durability in osteoporotic bones.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort study conducted at a single tertiary referral center. Ethical approval was obtained from the participating center’s institutional ethics committee. Geriatric patients (≥ 65 years) with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)-confirmed osteoporosis who underwent surgical fixation with a locking plate for osteoporotic PHFs between January 2018 and December 2023 were retrospectively reviewed.

Patient selection

Patients aged ≥ 65 years were eligible for inclusion in the study if they had been diagnosed with osteoporosis using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and had a T-score of − 2.5 or lower. The primary study cohort included patients who required revision surgery due to radiological failure following locking plate fixation for PHF. A total of 24 patients (18 male and 6 female) who met these criteria and underwent a minimum of 24 months of radiological follow-up were included in the primary analysis.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 65 years, osteoporotic bone quality confirmed by DEXA, surgical treatment with a locking plate system for PHF, the use of a calcar screw during the index procedure, and the availability of complete radiological follow-up data. Exclusion criteria were pathological fractures, open fractures, previous surgery on the affected shoulder, and incomplete radiological follow-up. All fractures were classified as three- or four-part PHF according to the Neer classification [11].

For the secondary analysis, patients who underwent the same surgical procedure during the study period but did not require revision surgery were identified and included to comparatively evaluate medial column support quality and revision status.

Group definition

Patients were divided into two groups according to screw density: the low screw density (LSD) group and the high screw density (HSD) group. The threshold for these groups (LSD: ≤ 8 vs. HSD: ≥ 9) was selected based on the clinical design of modern locking plates, where 9 screws represent the near-complete utilization of available head screw options, providing a clear contrast in construct density. Screw density thresholds (LSD: ≤ 8 vs. HSD: ≥ 9) were used to determine the almost complete or incomplete use of existing proximal humeral head screw options in modern interlocking plates (e.g., up to nine head screws in commonly used PHILOS-type designs) and provide a clinically interpretable contrast in structure density.

For secondary analysis, the fixation constructs were further categorized as follows based on the quality of medial column support, defined by the number and configuration of the inferomedial and calcar screws:

Limited medial support: only one inferomedial supporting screw (calcar screw only), without additional inferomedial screw(s).Adequate medial support: calcar screw and additional inferomedial screw (total inferomedial support screws ≥ 2).

Although the calcar screw was used routinely, the quality of medial column support varied based on the presence and number of additional inferomedial screws. Beyond the routine use of a calcar screw, medial support quality was radiologically verified to be in direct contact with or within 2 mm of the inferomedial cortex to ensure true mechanical support.

Surgical technique

All surgical procedures were performed using a standard deltopectoral approach, that is, under general anesthesia and while the patient was in the beach chair position. Fracture reduction was performed under fluoroscopic guidance and stabilized with a locking anatomical proximal humerus plate. To support the medial column, a calcar screw was used routinely in all patients [2, 8]. Additionally, heavy non-absorbable sutures were routinely used to perform suture augmentation of the rotator cuff and tuberosities to the plate, ensuring stable reduction of the greater and lesser tuberosities. The number and configuration of humeral head screws were determined intraoperatively based on fracture morphology, reduction quality, and bone quality.

Postoperative protocol

Postoperative management was standardized in all patients. A shoulder sling was typically utilized for 4 to 6 weeks postoperatively for protection. Based on the fracture stability, passive range of motion exercises were started at an early stage (usually within the first 10–14 days) and then, gradually, active assisted mobilization was initiated. Full weight-bearing activities were restricted for a minimum of 12 weeks until radiological union was confirmed.on follow-up radiographs.

Radiological outcome assessment

This study focused only on radiological outcomes and did not evaluate clinical outcomes. Standard anteroposterior and axillary shoulder radiographs obtained at predetermined follow-up intervals were used. Computed tomography (CT) was performed as necessary to confirm suspected complications.

Radiological definitions

AVN of the humeral head was diagnosed using CT findings, including subchondral bone collapse, sclerotic or cystic changes, and loss of normal trabecular architecture [12–14]. Implant failure referred to any mechanical failure of the fixation construct detected on serial radiographs, such as plate or implant breakage, screw penetration into the glenohumeral joint, screw loosening, or screw back-out [3, 15]. Reduction loss was defined as secondary displacement or separation of fracture fragments after initial satisfactory reduction, including varus malalignment [4, 9]. Pseudoarthrosis was defined as failure of fracture union at 12 months postoperatively, accompanied by persistent pain and the absence of bridging trabecular bone on CT [5].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed to compare the radiological causes of reoperation between the LSD and HSD groups. Since all patients underwent reoperation, the analysis focused on the distribution of reoperation-related complications. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the secondary analysis, Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the association between medial column support quality and revision status.

Results

A total of 24 patients with a minimum follow-up of 24 months were included in the study. The mean follow-up duration was 28.1 months, ranging from 24 to 36 months. Among them, 15 patients, including 13 men and two women, were in the LSD group, and nine patients, including five men and four women, were in the HSD group. All fractures were classified as three- or four-part PHFs. No statistically significant differences were observed between the LSD and HSD groups in terms of age, gender distribution, fracture type (three- vs. four-part PHFs), or bone mineral density as assessed by DEXA (p > 0.05 for all comparisons) (Table 1). When radiological causes of revision were grouped as mechanical or biological failure, mechanical failure predominated in both groups. Mechanical complications accounted for 73.3 and 88.9% of revisions, whereas biological failure accounted for 26.7 and 11.1%, in the LSD and HSD groups, respectively; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.617). No statistically significant difference was observed between the LSD and HSD groups in terms of the distribution of radiological causes leading to revision surgery (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Increased screw density did not demonstrate a protective effect against mechanical or biological complications.

In a secondary analysis including both revision and non-revision patients (n = 166), limited medial column support (one inferomedial/calcar screw) was significantly more frequent among revision cases than non-revision cases (37.5% vs. 4.9%), whereas adequate medial column support (≥ 2 inferomedial/calcar screws) predominated in the non-revision group (95.1%) (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001) (Table 3). Due to the limited number of revision events and insufficient data, Firth penalized logistic regression was used for multivariate analysis. After adjusting for age, sex, fracture pattern (3-part vs. 4-part), and screw density, limited medial column support—defined as the presence of only one inferomedial/calcar screw—remained independently associated with revision surgery (p < 0.001). Conversely, screw density was not independently associated with revision after accounting for medial column support quality (Table 4).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that the dominant factor associated with revision after locking plate fixation of osteoporotic PHFs is medial column support rather than overall screw density. Consistent with this finding, increasing screw density did not improve surgical success in a revision-only cohort of elderly patients with osteoporotic three- and four-part PHFs. This study focused specifically on patients requiring revision surgery due to radiological failure, assessing whether screw density affects the type and distribution of mechanical and biological complications that lead to revision surgery. Despite the routine use of calcar screws, HSD has failed to reduce the implant failure rates, reduction loss, pseudoarthrosis, or AVN. These findings reveal that once fixation failure has occurred, increasing the number of screws does not compensate for adverse fracture characteristics or compromised bone and vascular biology, underscoring the primacy of fracture-related factors over implant quantity.

This study demonstrates that fixation success in osteoporotic PHFs is determined by the quality of medial column support rather than the density of screws. Our secondary analysis revealed that limited medial support (one inferomedial/calcar screw) was strongly associated with revision surgery, whereas adequate medial support (≥ 2 inferomedial/calcar screws) was predominantly observed in patients not requiring revision. This finding emphasizes the biomechanical importance of targeted screw placement rather than simply increasing screw density. From a biomechanical perspective, a higher number of screws does not necessarily equate to better stability in osteoporotic bone. Excessive stiffening of the construct through high screw density may lead to unfavorable stress distribution and create ‘stress risers’ at the bone-implant interface. In a low-density bone environment, these stress concentrations can accelerate screw cut-out or secondary varus collapse, whereas strategic placement of fewer screws to provide medial column support appears to offer a more biological and mechanically sound load-sharing mechanism. In osteoporotic cancellous bone, excessive screw placement may not improve the structure’s stability and may even exacerbate stress concentration at the bone-screw interface, increasing the risk of loosening and cut-out. Conversely, the inferomedial and calcar screws directly support the medial column, preventing varus collapse and maintaining fracture reduction. From a clinical perspective, these results question the traditional emphasis placed on the number of screws and instead support a strategy that provides robust medial column support, thus reinforcing the principle that in osteoporotic bones, the determinant of fixation durability is not the number of screws but the screw position. Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that implant-related complications persist despite modern plating systems, emphasizing the dominant role of bone quality and fracture biology [6, 7].

Consistent with previous reports, medial column support plays a key role in maintaining reduction [2, 8, 10]. In this study, this variable was effectively controlled in all patients using calcar screws. The lack of difference between groups indicates that once adequate medial support is achieved, additional noninferomedial screws offer limited incremental benefits. However, medial column support quality varied based on the presence of additional inferomedial screws beyond the calcar screw, which explained the observed differences in revision risk.

All fractures in this cohort were three- or four-part patterns, which are known to be associated with comminution and compromised humeral head vascularity—key contributors to fixation failure and AVN [11, 12]. AVN is a multifactorial complication. Previous studies have reported that AVN is more strongly associated with fracture pattern and vascular disruption than with implant configuration [12–14]. Consistent with the Hertel criteria, these complex fracture patterns are primary predictors of humeral head ischemia, suggesting that AVN is more strongly associated with the initial vascular disruption and fracture morphology than with the specific implant configuration or screw density however increasing screw density may further compromise intraosseous blood supply by overcrowding the humeral head, potentially exacerbating biological failure [15, 16].

From a clinical decision-making perspective, the results of this study reveal that increasing screw density should not be considered a reliable strategy to prevent fixation failure in elderly patients with osteoporotic PHFs. In complex three- and four-part fracture patterns, particularly those that require revision surgery, biological factors such as humeral head vascularity, bone quality, and fracture comminution are more important than the mechanical contribution of additional screws. While adequate medial column support remains essential, excessive screw placement may further compromise the intraosseous blood supply. Therefore, surgeons treating this challenging patient population should focus on achieving stable anatomical reduction and preserving fracture biology rather than solely relying on increasing screw density.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces inherent risks of selection bias. Second, although the primary analysis focused solely on the patient group undergoing revision, the secondary analysis comparing patients who underwent revision with those who did not was observational and did not aim to establish a causal relationship. To address this limitation and avoid biased estimates associated with sparse data and separation, Firth penalized logistic regression was used for multivariable analysis. Third, detailed biomechanical tests were not conducted, and the medial column support quality was inferred from radiographic screw configuration rather than direct load-sharing measurements. Additionally, while all radiological assessments were conducted by two independent orthopedic surgeons to achieve a consensus, the study did not include a formal analysis of inter-observer or intra-observer reliability. This potential for observer bias should be considered when interpreting the radiological failure rates. Despite these limitations, the consistent and strong relationship between medial column support quality and revision risk reinforces the clinical significance of the current findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that increasing screw density alone does not improve fixation outcomes in osteoporotic PHFs requiring revision surgery. Although LSD and HSD constructs exhibited comparable distributions of revision-related complications, fixation failure in this high-risk population was predominantly induced by fracture configuration and compromised bone and vascular biology rather than implant-related factors. More importantly, fixation durability was closely associated with the quality of medial column support. Adequate placement of the inferomedial and calcar screws was significantly more common in patients not requiring revision. Increasing the number of screws does not appear to compensate for inadequate medial support or poor fracture biology. These findings support a shift in the surgical approach toward optimizing screw placement and respecting fracture biology rather than maximizing the number of screws. Accordingly, surgical strategies should prioritize anatomical reduction, preservation of medial column integrity, and biologically sound fixation principles, rather than relying on increased screw density to improve outcomes in osteoporotic bones.
