Integrated serum metabolomics and lipidomics for identifying metabolic changes in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Hua-Hui Zhu, Zhao-Hui Ruan, Zi-Xing Zhang, Xin-Yu Fang, Dong-Qing Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder with complex pathogenesis, and conventional biomarkers often fail to capture disease-associated metabolic changes. A more comprehensive understanding of the metabolic alterations in RA may provide deeper insights into disease mechanisms.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify serum metabolic changes associated with RA using non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomics, and to explore the potential impact of these metabolic alterations on RA pathophysiology.MethodsA case–control study was conducted using baseline data from the Inflammatory Immune Disease Cohort at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (October 2021 to June 2023). A total of 91 RA patients and 125 healthy controls were included. Serum samples were analyzed by non-targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Differential metabolites were identified using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), followed by filtration with Student’s t-test (P < 0.05). Logistic regression was applied to identify potential metabolic alterations for further investigation. Pathway enrichment analysis identified significant metabolic pathways associated with RA.ResultsThe median age of both groups was 50.0 years, with no significant demographic differences (P > 0.05). Metabolomics and lipidomics identified 41 differential metabolites, including 16 small molecules and 25 lipids. Pathway analysis revealed disturbances in the urea cycle, sphingolipid metabolism, arginine and proline metabolism, and glycine and serine metabolism. These findings suggest systemic metabolic alterations in RA and provide new insights into the disease’s metabolic profile.ConclusionsThe study identified significant metabolic alterations in RA, contributing to a better understanding of the disease’s pathophysiology. These findings highlight the importance of metabolic reprogramming in RA and offer directions for further investigation into metabolic pathways involved in disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-026-02447-z.

Background

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovial inflammation and progressive joint destruction, leading to substantial disability and impaired quality of life. The global prevalence of RA ranges from approximately 0.25% to 1% (Di Matteo et al., 2023), with a markedly higher incidence in women, who are two to three times more likely to develop the disease than men (Scott et al., 2022). RA predominantly affects individuals over the age of 40, and its burden continues to increase worldwide. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2017 study, the prevalence of RA increased by 7.4%, accompanied by an 8.2% rise in disease burden between 1990 and 2017 (Finckh et al., 2022). In addition to joint damage and functional impairment, RA imposes a growing socioeconomic burden due to long-term treatment costs and loss of productivity. Early diagnosis and timely therapeutic intervention are therefore critical for improving long-term outcomes (Grigor et al., 2004).

At present, the diagnosis of RA relies largely on clinical manifestations, imaging findings, and serological markers such as rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA). However, conventional biomarkers, such as RF and ACPA, exhibit limited sensitivity and specificity, particularly in early or seronegative RA (Ball et al., 2014; Mankia & Emery, 2016; Riccardi et al., 2022). This highlights the need for a better understanding of the metabolic changes that occur in RA, which may provide more accurate biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Metabolomics, which enables comprehensive profiling of small-molecule metabolites, provides a functional readout of genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic alterations and captures dynamic changes in physiological and pathological states (Holčapek et al., 2018; Nielsen, 2017). Increasing evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming plays a central role in immune cell activation and sustained inflammation in RA (Lei et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022). Previous metabolomic studies have identified disturbances in amino acid, energy, and lipid metabolism in patients with RA (Furukawa et al., 2020; Lei et al., 2023; Li et al., 2018; Singh et al., 2015; Song et al., 2020). However, many existing studies have focused on either small-molecule metabolites or lipid species in isolation, limiting their ability to comprehensively characterize disease-associated metabolic alterations. Moreover, relatively small sample sizes and insufficient validation have constrained the translational potential of reported metabolic biomarkers (Lei et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022).

In this context, an integrated non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomics analysis was performed using serum samples from a well-characterized cohort of patients with RA and matched healthy controls, this study aims to provide a more holistic view of RA-related metabolic changes.

Methods

Study subjects

This study was conducted using baseline data from the Inflammatory Immune Disease Cohort at Anhui Medical University between October 2021 and June 2023. A total of 91 patients with RA who fulfilled the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included the presence of metabolic disorders (diabetes, obesity, or hyperlipidemia), recent use of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs, biologic agents, or corticosteroids within the past two weeks, or refusal to participate. A control group comprising 125 age-, sex-, and BMI-matched healthy individuals was recruited from the same cohort. Healthy controls were screened to exclude any history of joint or metabolic diseases, including osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or other musculoskeletal disorders, and were not receiving regular medication. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (No. 83220399), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as follows: categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using chi-square tests. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test; normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Sample preparation

All participants provided fasting peripheral blood samples in the morning after overnight fasting and medication discontinuation for at least two weeks. Blood (5 mL) was collected into plain vacuum tubes without anticoagulant, stored at 4 °C, and processed within 2 h. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the serum supernatant was collected. Hemolyzed samples were recollected. Serum samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

For metabolomics, 100 μL of serum was mixed with 400 μL cold acetonitrile/water (1:4, v/v) containing internal standards to precipitate proteins. The mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was dried in a vacuum concentrator. Dried extracts were reconstituted in 180 μL methanol/water (4:1, v/v) prior to LC–MS analysis. The internal standards used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

For lipidomics, 40 μL of serum was mixed with 300 μL extraction solvent containing internal standards and vortexed for 30 s. Then, 1 mL methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) was added, and the mixture was shaken at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, 300 μL deionized water was added and vortexed for 30 s. After standing at 4 °C for 10 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was dried in a vacuum concentrator. Prior to LC–MS analysis, the dried extracts were reconstituted and diluted as follows: for positive electrospray ionization (ESI⁺) mode, 30 μL of reconstitution solution (dichloromethane/methanol, 2:1, v/v) was mixed with 60 μL of dilution solution (acetonitrile/isopropanol/water, 65:30:5, v/v/v, containing 5 mmol/L ammonium acetate); for negative electrospray ionization (ESI⁻) mode, 20 μL of reconstitution solution was mixed with 40 μL of dilution solution.

To ensure reproducibility and stability, pooled quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining equal aliquots from all serum samples. QC samples were processed identically to study samples. All study and QC samples were randomized prior to injection, and one QC sample was inserted after every 10 study samples throughout the LC–MS analytical sequence. Blank samples consisted of extraction solvents alone.

Non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomics analysis

Non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomics analyses were performed using an UltiMate 3000 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled to a Q Exactive HF Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For small-molecule metabolomics, serum extracts were separated using Waters UPLC C8 (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) and HPLC T3 (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) columns. The mobile phase in positive electrospray ionization (ESI⁺) mode consisted of solvent A (water, 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid), whereas in negative ESI (ESI⁻) mode, solvent A was water containing 6.5 mmol/L ammonium bicarbonate and solvent B was 95% methanol containing 6.5 mmol/L ammonium bicarbonate.

For lipidomics, a Waters UPLC C8 column was used for chromatographic separation. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (acetonitrile/water, 60:40, v/v, 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate) and solvent B (isopropanol/acetonitrile, 90:10, v/v, 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate).

Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes over an m/z range of 150–1250 in full-scan mode using high-resolution Orbitrap detection. To ensure analytical stability and reproducibility, pooled QC samples were injected periodically throughout the analytical sequence. Detailed chromatographic gradient conditions and mass spectrometry parameters are provided in Supplementary Tables 2–4. All solvents (acetonitrile, isopropanol, and methyl-tert-butyl ether [MTBE]) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) (Xuan et al., 2018).

Data processing and statistical analysis

Raw mass spectrometry data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 for metabolomics and LipidSearch 5.2 together with Trace Finder for lipidomics. Data preprocessing included peak detection, retention time alignment, and compound annotation. Metabolites with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% in QC samples were excluded. Subsequently, signal intensities of the remaining metabolites were corrected using corresponding internal standards or reference standards prior to downstream statistical analysis. Small-molecule metabolites were annotated by matching accurate mass and retention time against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). Lipid species were identified using dedicated lipid databases (Supplementary Table S2 in Ref. 16, file: ac8b01331_si_002.xlsx) and further annotated using the LIPID MAPS database (https://lipidmaps.org/) with a mass tolerance of 15 ppm (Xuan et al., 2018).

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) and multivariate orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA-P 14.1. PCA was used to assess overall data structure, analytical reproducibility, and potential batch effects, while OPLS-DA was used to evaluate group separation. Model validity and potential overfitting of OPLS-DA were assessed by permutation tests. Differential metabolites were selected based on two criteria: VIP > 1 from the OPLS-DA model and P < 0.05 from Student’s t-test. Fold-change was not considered, and no adjustment for multiple testing was performed, as metabolite selection was guided primarily by statistical significance and contribution to group separation in the multivariate model. The identified differential metabolites were subsequently used for pathway enrichment analysis using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), mapping metabolites to the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB) and assessing enrichment significance via hypergeometric tests. Pathways with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

For metabolite selection for predictive modeling, participants were randomly divided into a training set (n = 160) and a test set (n = 56). Candidate metabolites with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) > 0.6 or < 0.4 in univariate logistic regression analysis were entered into a forward stepwise multivariate logistic regression model. The model performance was evaluated using ROC curve analysis in both the training and test sets.

Metabolite annotation confidence was assigned according to MSI principles and is provided in Supplementary Table S8. Specifically, compounds confirmed with authentic standards are labeled as Level 1, compounds with observed MS/MS spectra (lipidomics) or HMDB database matches with Δppm confirmation (small molecules) are labeled as Level 2, and compounds annotated solely based on the internal Compound Discoverer 3.3 database are labeled as Level 3. This classification allows transparent evaluation of compound identification confidence and reproducibility.

Data quality assessment

Data quality and analytical reproducibility of the LC–MS platform were evaluated using pooled QC samples. PCA score plots under both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes demonstrated that QC samples clustered tightly within ± 2 standard deviations(2SD) for both metabolomics and lipidomics datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1A-D), indicating high stability and reproducibility of the measurements. PCA of RA and healthy control samples revealed substantial overlap without clear separation or global clustering patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2 A-D), suggesting the absence of significant batch effects.

Global metabolic profiling based on OPLS-DA

To explore global metabolic alterations associated with rheumatoid arthritis, supervised orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to both metabolomics and lipidomics datasets. In the small-molecule metabolomics analysis, OPLS-DA score plots showed clear separation between RA patients and healthy controls under both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes (ESI⁺ and ESI⁻) (Fig. 1A, B). Permutation testing was performed to assess the robustness of the models and exclude potential overfitting. In all OPLS-DA models, the permuted R2 and Q2 values were consistently lower than those of the original models, with the Q2 regression lines showing negative intercepts (Fig. 1 C, D, G, and H). These results confirm the validity and stability of the models. The key parameters of the metabolomics and lipidomics OPLS-DA models are summarized as follows: for metabolomics in positive ion mode (ESI⁺), R2X = 0.553, R2Y = 0.959, and Q2 = 0.685; for metabolomics in negative ion mode (ESI⁻), R2X = 0.538, R2Y = 0.990, and Q2 = 0.922. For lipidomics, in ESI⁺ mode, R2X = 0.750, R2Y = 0.996, and Q2 = 0.949, while in ESI⁻ mode, R2X = 0.621, R2Y = 0.989, and Q2 = 0.970. These results underscore the robustness of the models and their suitability for further analysis.

Differential metabolites and lipids

A total of 41 differential metabolites were identified between RA patients and healthy controls, including 16 small-molecule metabolites and 25 lipid species.

Among the small-molecule metabolites, four were significantly upregulated and twelve were downregulated in RA patients compared with healthy controls. Notably, antioxidant-related metabolites, such as D-α-Tocopherol, were decreased, whereas amino acid-related metabolites, including Ornithine, were increased in RA. Lipidomic profiling revealed pronounced alterations across multiple lipid classes, particularly triglycerides (TGs), phosphatidylcholines (PCs), phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs), and sphingomyelins (SMs). Of the 25 differential lipid species, 20 were upregulated and 5 were downregulated in RA patients.

Discussion

This integrated metabolomics-lipidomics study provides a comprehensive serum metabolic profile of RA and identifies significant metabolic alterations associated with the disease. Among the 41 differential metabolites detected, 12, including D-α-Tocopherol, ornithine, and several sphingomyelins, have been previously reported in RA, confirming the robustness of our analytical platform and the reproducibility of these metabolic alterations. Notably, the remaining 29 metabolites, such as specific triglycerides (e.g., TG(56:1), TG(58:2)), phosphatidylethanolamines (PE(36:1), PE(38:5)), phosphatidylinositols (PI(38:3), PI(40:4)), and small molecules like triethylamine and 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, have not been reported in RA to our knowledge. The high proportion of novel lipid species underscores the value of integrating untargeted lipidomics with metabolomics to capture a more comprehensive view of RA-associated metabolic dysregulation.

Among the observed alterations, the downregulation of D-α-Tocopherol (vitamin E) suggests an impaired antioxidant defense system (Ferreira et al., 2021; Traber & Atkinson, 2007). This finding is consistent with previous reports of reduced vitamin E levels in RA patients and further supports the role of oxidative stress in RA, where excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) leads to chronic inflammation, joint damage, and synovial cell hyperplasia (Knekt et al., 2000; Traber & Atkinson, 2007). Oxidative stress has been shown to activate inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly through NF-κB and MAPK pathways, promoting the persistence of inflammation in RA (Chang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2018). The reduction in D-α-Tocopherol further indicates a disruption in the body’s ability to counteract oxidative damage, reinforcing the connection between impaired antioxidant systems and disease progression.

The significant increase in Ornithine, a key urea cycle intermediate, reflects enhanced protein catabolism and nitrogen turnover, both of which are hallmark features of chronic inflammation and muscle wasting in RA (Huffman et al., 2017; Li et al., 2020). Elevated ornithine levels have been previously reported in RA muscle tissue and are consistent with alterations in arginine metabolism summarized by Xu et al. (2022). Ornithine is involved in the synthesis of polyamines, which play a role in regulating cell proliferation and immune cell activation (Puleston et al., 2021). Elevated levels of Ornithine may therefore indicate an ongoing inflammatory response and increased immune cell turnover in RA, particularly in synovial fibroblasts and macrophages. These changes suggest that the body is in a state of heightened metabolic activity to cope with chronic inflammation. In addition,research has shown that the metabolism of amino acids, particularly arginine metabolism, plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of RA (Brunner et al., 2020; Radhakutty et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2022). Ornithine is a metabolic product of arginine, and the conversion of arginine to ornithine is critical in regulating immune responses. The elevated ornithine levels in RA may therefore reflect disruptions in the arginine metabolism pathway, which is involved in nitric oxide production, immune cell signaling, and regulation of inflammatory processes (Agnihotri et al., 2021; Andreu et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2022). These findings highlight the importance of amino acid metabolism in the inflammatory mechanisms underlying RA.

Alterations in lipid species, particularly sphingomyelins (SM) and phospholipids, indicate disruptions in membrane integrity and lipid-mediated signaling pathways (Gault et al., 2010). Sphingomyelin dysregulation has been previously documented in RA, and our study further identifies specific SM species (e.g., SM(32:1), SM(d35:0), SM(t35:0)) that are altered. Sphingomyelins are crucial for maintaining the stability of cellular membranes, and their dysregulation may affect immune cell signaling and migration. In RA, sphingomyelin metabolites have been linked to the activation of T cells and the regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Luan et al., 2023). Changes in phospholipid composition further suggest alterations in cell membrane fluidity and receptor function, which may impact immune responses, especially in synovial fibroblasts and endothelial cells involved in joint inflammation. Importantly, our study also reveals novel lipid alterations, including multiple triglycerides (TG(52:1) to TG(60:3)) and phosphatidylinositols (PI(38:3), PI(40:4), PI(40:5)), which have not been previously reported in RA and may represent new targets for further investigation.

Interestingly, despite the large number of metabolites detected, only four metabolic pathways were significantly enriched. This may reflect true disease-specific effects, but it could also result from incomplete database annotations, particularly for less characterized lipid species. Expanding lipid annotations or conducting targeted validation may uncover additional affected pathways. Building on these pathway-level insights, a strength of our study is the identification of a five-metabolite diagnostic panel that demonstrates strong discriminatory power, with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.74 in both training and test datasets. The variation in sensitivity and specificity between the datasets is a common phenomenon in biomarker research: the higher specificity in the training set reflects the panel’s ability to capture "typical" RA metabolic patterns, while the improved sensitivity in the test set indicates its potential to detect a broader spectrum of disease-related metabolic heterogeneity. This trade-off underscores the importance of further refinement of the panel, including threshold optimization and validation across diverse populations, while the stable AUC across both datasets highlights its promise as a valuable tool for investigating RA-associated metabolic changes.

However, several important limitations should be noted. Although all statistical tests were performed using exact p-values, no multiple testing correction was applied in this exploratory metabolomics and lipidomics study. Consequently, some identified differential metabolites may represent false positives, and the interpretation of pathway enrichment should be approached with caution. In addition, the study’s single-center design and relatively limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of our findings. Future studies involving larger, multi-center cohorts with appropriate multiple testing adjustments are warranted to validate these findings and confirm their robustness across diverse populations.

Although a two-week washout was required prior to sample collection, residual effects of prior DMARD exposure may not have been fully eliminated, particularly for agents with longer-lasting biological activity such as methotrexate and leflunomide. Detailed pre-enrollment medication histories were unavailable, limiting the ability to quantify potential confounding from prior treatment. Future studies should consider longer washout periods or stratified analyses based on prior medication use to address these issues.

To minimize additional confounding, all control participants were screened to exclude any history of joint diseases, including osteoarthritis and other inflammatory musculoskeletal disorders, and RA patients with common metabolic comorbidities, such as obesity and diabetes, were also excluded. While these criteria enhance internal validity, they may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader RA population, in which such comorbidities are prevalent.

A further limitation is the absence of baseline RF and ACPA data, precluding direct evaluation of whether the identified five-metabolite panel provides diagnostic information complementary to conventional RA biomarkers. This limitation is particularly relevant for seronegative RA patients, in whom RF and ACPA have limited sensitivity. Consequently, it is not possible to determine whether the metabolite panel offers truly novel diagnostic value beyond conventional serological markers or largely recapitulates existing information. Given that one of the study’s key objectives is to identify biomarkers that may be especially useful in seronegative RA, this represents a critical gap to be addressed in future research.

Although lipidomic profiling provided insights at the class level, many lipid classes, such as triglycerides and phosphatidylcholines, contain multiple isomeric species with identical m/z values that could not be fully resolved using our non-targeted LC–MS workflow. Consequently, confident annotation of individual fatty acid species was not feasible, limiting the granularity of biological interpretation and mechanistic understanding. While annotation confidence was provided according to MSI levels, limitations remain for both small-molecule metabolites and lipid species. Future studies employing authentic standards, MS/MS confirmation, or targeted metabolomics/lipidomics approaches could improve annotation specificity and provide more precise biological insights. Targeted lipidomics may therefore be warranted to investigate individual lipid species in greater detail.

To overcome these limitations, future studies should include concurrent measurement of RF and ACPA to allow direct comparison with the metabolite panel and evaluate its incremental diagnostic value, particularly in seronegative patients. They should also perform targeted quantification of the identified metabolites to confirm their clinical utility, and collect detailed pre-enrollment medication histories with potentially longer washout periods or medication-stratified analyses to minimize residual confounding. Expanding validation to external cohorts and including patients with common metabolic comorbidities would further enhance the generalizability and clinical relevance of these findings, enabling a more rigorous evaluation of the five-metabolite panel and its potential role in early detection, risk stratification, and mechanistic studies in RA.

Conclusions

In conclusion, by integrating non-targeted metabolomics and lipidomics, we have identified significant systemic metabolic alterations in RA, shedding light on the underlying pathophysiology of the disease. These metabolic changes provide valuable insights into the disease mechanisms and could serve as potential biomarkers for early detection. However, further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts and quantitative studies is essential to confirm these findings and explore their broader clinical applicability.
