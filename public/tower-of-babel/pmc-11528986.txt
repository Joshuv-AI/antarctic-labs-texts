Diminished negative emotion regulation through affect labeling and reappraisal: insights from functional near infrared spectroscopy on lateral prefrontal cortex activation
Authors: Yoshimura Shinpei, Shimomura Kouga, Onoda Keiichi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReappraisal, an emotion regulation strategy, includes reinterpretation and affect labeling involving verbalizing emotions. In general, reappraisal is supported by lateral prefrontal cortical regions, which are also known to underlie cognitive regulation. Other research has shown that affect labeling combined with reappraisal of negative emotions increases lateral prefrontal cortex activity more than reappraisal alone does, suggesting that affect labeling facilitates emotional regulation. However, the influence of affect labeling on the efficacy of reappraisal in reducing subjective negative emotions has not been determined.MethodsIn the experiment, 35 participants (mean age = 28.2 years (SD = 9.63); 12 women and 23 men) viewed vignettes that aroused negative emotion. Then, they rated subjective negative emotions as baseline values. Following the baseline rating, the task branched into four conditions, combining affect labeling and emotion regulation factors. In the affect-labeling factor, participants selected emotional labels consistent with their own emotions or not. Regarding the emotion regulation factor, participants engaged in reappraisal to regulate their negative emotions. Throughout the experiment, the intensity of negative emotions was measured three times, mirroring the baseline measurement. Oxyhemoglobin (OxyHb) signal values in prefrontal cortex regions during tasks were measured by functional near-infrared spectroscopy.ResultsDifferences between the subjective negative emotion ratings at baseline and after reappraisal indicated that reappraisal significantly reduced negative emotion with and without affect labeling (t (1173.05) = 29.97, p < 0.001), and the combination of affect labeling and reappraisal was less effective in regulating negative emotions at the subjective level than reappraisal without affect labeling (t (1172.03) = 3.15, p < 0.01). Additionally, there was an increase in OxyHb signal in the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal and right ventral prefrontal cortices while participants performed reappraisal with affect labeling.ConclusionOur findings suggest that affect labeling, when performed prior to cognitive reappraisal, may influence the process of negative emotion regulation in complex ways. The interaction between affect labeling and reappraisal appears to modulate prefrontal cortex activity, potentially reflecting changes in cognitive processing during emotion regulation attempts. These results highlight the need for further investigation into the intricate relationship between different emotion regulation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02103-y.Keywords: Affect labeling, Emotion regulation, Cognitive reappraisal, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), Prefrontal cortex activity

Background

Emotional regulation is an automatic and deliberative process that modulates the onset, intensity, duration, and adjustment of emotional states. Gross [1] describes emotion regulation as individuals’ influence on when, how, and to what degree they experience and express emotions. Gross’s model of emotion regulation presents a timeline of events in emotion generation and regulation, showing how situational factors trigger emotional responses that evolve over time, leading to outward emotional expression. Engaging in emotion regulation strategies can influence this process at various points. One such strategy is cognitive reappraisal, defined as the cognitive restructuring of the emotional impact of a situation [2]Cognitive reappraisal, a core element of cognitive behavioral therapy for affective disorders such as depression and anxiety [3], addresses skewed threat evaluations, often marked by an exaggerated sense of threat or an overestimated possibility of catastrophe [4]. Studies have shown that cognitive reappraisal positively affects psychological adjustment by reducing negative emotions [2, 5, 6]. However, Webb et al. [7] reported that the efficacy of reappraisal can be reduced when emotional experiences are complex and ambiguous, suggesting the presence of factors that may impede emotion regulation and reappraisal.

In addition, research on reappraisal has identified several factors that influence the efficacy of emotion regulation strategy. These include individual differences in working memory capacity [8], belief in the success of reappraisal [9], and attitudes toward one’s own emotional experiences [10]. Recent studies have focused on the role of emotion differentiation in reappraisal and reported that changes in daily emotion differentiation can partially affect the success of emotion regulation, including reappraisal [11]. Less distinctive emotion differentiation can complicate the reappraisal process and exacerbate negative emotions [12, 13].

Verbalizing feelings can facilitate the differentiation of emotions. Affect labeling (AL) [14], though not primarily aimed at regulating emotions, is recognized for diminishing negative emotions through linguistic labeling of emotional stimuli and personal emotional experiences [15]. In studies of affect labeling, stimuli that evoke emotions are often used, and participants label the emotions elicited by the stimuli or the stimuli themselves. Labels may be self-generated or selected from a set predetermined by researchers. The validity of the labels is rarely questioned, and the act of labeling itself can induce implicit effects on emotion regulation. Affect labeling is thought to facilitate the interaction between the experiential and linguistic aspects of emotion. Core affect theory [16] posits that emotions emerge when bodily sensations and signals are classified using an individual’s conceptual knowledge of emotions. Therefore, attaching labels to personal emotional concepts is crucial in emotion differentiation. This differentiation is pivotal for understanding emotions and plays a significant role in adaptively managing negative emotions. Vine and Marroquín [17] demonstrated that enhancing emotion differentiation can bolster reappraisal, increasing its effectiveness. This suggests that improving the differentiation of emotions could aid reappraisal, potentially benefiting individuals with affective disorders. Although no conclusion has been reached regarding the mechanism by which AL is able to regulate emotion, the assumption is that labeling one’s emotions and emotion-provoking stimuli draws attention away from emotional experiences, and the verbalization of emotion may shift emotional experiences into an abstract format, thereby creating psychological distance between oneself and one’s emotions [15]. On the other hand, research has indicated that naming emotions before regulating them might weaken control over negative emotions. Nook et al. [18] reported that participants who named their emotions before reappraising them in response to negative images reported higher levels of negative emotions post reappraisal than did those who reappraised without prior naming. Nook et al. [18]suggested that emotion verbalization might “crystallize” one’s affective state, making it more difficult to modify through emotion regulation. They tend to become more structured, making them less susceptible to change compared to core affect. If affect labeling leads to crystallized emotions, individuals may become less influenced by emotion regulation. Especially when employing reappraisal for emotion regulation, cognitive evaluations corresponding to the emotions selected through affect labeling may become entrenched, potentially limiting the opportunity for different cognitive evaluations of stimuli.

The interplay between the AL and the neural mechanisms of emotion reappraisal raises critical questions, particularly regarding the neural correlates of these processes. Recent neuroimaging studies have clarified how reappraisal activates extensive prefrontal cortex regions. The results from a meta-analysis of neuroimaging data indicate that reappraisal is associated with heightened activity in the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) and concurrently in the anterior cingulate cortex and parietal lobule [19, 20]. These findings imply that the DLPFC and VLPFC are involved in selecting emotion regulation targets and linguistic processes that modify emotion interpretations. These intricate cognitive tasks require executive functions such as shifting attention and maintaining cognitive processing, suggesting a significant consumption of cognitive resources. However, neuroimaging research on simultaneous emotion regulation and the AL is lacking. Understanding brain activity during these concurrent processes could provide deeper insights into the cognitive loads and neural pathways involved in emotion regulation and the AL.

Yoshimura et al. [21]examined how AL affects brain activity involved in reappraisal by using fMRI. They observed increased activity within the left DLPFC, right VLPFC, and bilateral MPFC in reappraisal after AL to negative emotion compared to reappraisal only. However, they did not observe a significant effect of reappraisal (with or without AL) on attenuating negative emotions across conditions. This finding leaves the effect of combining AL and reappraisal on regulating negative emotions and their correlation with brain activity somewhat ambiguous. Research focusing on cognitive load and prefrontal cortex activity, such as the study by Fishburn et al. [22], indicates a link between increasing difficulty in n-back tasks and bilateral DLPFC activity. Additionally, Silvers et al. [23] reported that reappraising highly intense emotional stimuli augments activity in the left DLPFC due to elevated cognitive loads. These studies collectively suggest that the simultaneous implementation of ALs and reappraisal for negative emotions could intensify cognitive load during reappraisal, thereby increasing DLPFC activity.

On the other hand, the AL has been associated with the activation of the prefrontal region, mirroring the neural response observed during reappraisal. In particular, the right VLPFC shows a strong correlation with the AL, exhibiting more pronounced activation during the AL than during reappraisal, as noted in studies by Burklund et al. [24] and Tupak et al. [25]. A meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies investigating the entire process of emotion verbalization [26] revealed consistent involvement of the superior temporal cortex and inferior frontal cortex and suppression of amygdala activity in processes including emotion verbalization. These brain activity findings suggest that emotional experience through linguistic processes includes access to emotion and meaning concepts and leads to the suppression of emotion processing. On the other hand, it has been reported that the inhibitory effect on the amygdala associated with AL is stronger in the inferior frontal cortex, including the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, than in the superior temporal cortex [27]. Therefore, although there are similarities and differences between the entire process of emotion verbalization and AL, it is difficult to conclude from neuroimaging findings that there is a definitive difference between them. However, our prior investigation [21]did not incorporate a condition focused solely on AL. In light of this, the current study modified the task design used in Yoshimura et al. [21] to examine the shifts in brain activity during the combined process of AL and reappraisal of negative emotions using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). This study also contrasts the subjective emotional experiences of implementing AL and reappraisal with those of only reappraisal for negative emotions. We hypothesize that if ALs crystallize emotions and amplify cognitive load during reappraisal, the efficacy of emotion regulation for individuals with negative emotions might be diminished when ALs and reappraisal are combined compared to when reappraisal is used alone. Consistent with the findings of Yoshimura et al. [21], increased activity within the VLPFC is anticipated during the combined AL and reappraisal process. Drawing from Nook et al. [18], we further posit that if AL combined with reappraisal is less effective than reappraisal alone, there could be an increase in DLPFC activity during emotion regulation, reflecting heightened cognitive loads. The study employs a two-factor task utilizing stimuli that evoke negative emotions, with one factor involving the implementation of AL or not and the other involving the implementation of reappraisal or not. This design enables the comparison of brain activity and the experience of negative emotions across the conditions of combined AL and reappraisal, reappraisal alone, and AL alone.

Methods

Participants

Adults and university students (N = 35, mean age = 28.2 years (SD = 9.63); age range: 19–47 years; 12 women and 23 men) participated in the experiment. The participants were recruited through two methods: five participants were students from the author’s affiliated university, while the remaining thirty participants were recruited through a participant recruitment service. We excluded participants with a medical history of psychiatric or neurological disorders. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were right-handed, and were native Japanese speakers. We did not perform an a priori power analysis for this study. The sample size was determined based on Tupak et al.‘s [25] fNIRS study on affect labeling involving 35 participants. To the best of our knowledge, the only previous fNIRS study that investigated affect labeling was conducted by Tupak et al. [25]. Although their study employed a different experimental paradigm than ours, we considered it to be relatively relevant to the theme of our study and therefore used it as a reference for sample size calculation. A systematic review of 85 NIRS studies on emotion processing, including emotion regulation [28], revealed that the average number of participants in 67 studies that included only adults was 25.12 (SD = 16.46). Among these studies, the average number of participants in the 9 studies that focused on emotion regulation was 20.89 (SD = 9.93). It is noteworthy that the sample size of the present study exceeds the average sample size of these previous studies.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. We explained the purpose of the study to the participants, and we obtained informed consent after ensuring their understanding of the experimental procedures, associated risks, and their right to withdraw at any time. No participants requested withdrawal during the experiment. The Ethics Review Board of Otemon Gakuin University approved all the procedures (approval number: 2021-14).

Stimuli

We used vignettes developed by Wingenbach et al. [29] to elicit emotional experiences. These vignettes were translated into Japanese for this study. We selected twelve vignettes, four each eliciting emotions of fear, anger, and sadness. The following are examples of stimuli used in the study.

Anger stimuli: “I was driving and the car on my left, which was a little ahead of me, decided to get into my lane, without signaling. He did not even look before throwing his car to the right. I had to brake abruptly so as not to hit the car.”

Sad stimuli: “I was 8 months pregnant, at home alone, when I felt very strong pain, but it passed quickly. When I got out of bed, I saw a lot of blood and called my husband. We determined that I miscarried.”

Fear stimuli: “I was walking in the street late one night when I saw someone waiting for me. The street was deserted, and when I started walking up the hill, he followed me. He grabbed my arm and pulled me with a lot of strength.”

To ensure the appropriateness of these vignettes, we conducted a preliminary study (n = 154) using the same vignettes used in this study. Participants were presented with each vignette individually for 30 s and asked to imagine its content vividly. The participants subsequently rated the intensity of negative emotions elicited on a 10-point scale ranging from 1 (no negative emotion) to 10 (extremely negative). The results indicated that each vignette elicited moderately negative emotions (fear: mean = 5.93, SD = 0.96; anger: mean = 5.33, SD = 0.98; sadness: mean = 5.88, SD = 0.98), validating their use as emotional stimuli in the present study.

Behavioral task

We used PsychoPy [30] for stimulus presentation, control, and response collection. The task incorporated two within-subject factors, affect labeling (AL and no AL) and emotion regulation (reappraisal and no reappraisal), resulting in four conditions (AL + Reappraisal, no AL + Reappraisal, AL + no Reappraisal, no AL + no Reappraisal). Figure 1 illustrates the sequence of one trial in the AL + Reappraisal condition.

Sequence of Events in the Behavioral Task (Illustration of the AL + Reappraisal Condition)

The stimuli were presented in a pseudorandom order, each lasting 40 s. We employed a method wherein a set of 10 stimulus presentation sequences were pregenerated, each representing a different pseudorandom order of stimuli. Subsequently, for each participant, one of these 10 sequences was randomly selected. Participants were instructed to imagine the scene presented in the vignette. Subsequently, they rated the intensity of negative emotions elicited by the imagery on a 0-100 visual analog scale (VAS) for 5 s. These ratings served as baseline values. Following the negative emotion rating, the task branched into four conditions, combining labeling and emotion regulation factors. In each condition, participants engaged in reappraisal (or no reappraisal) for 45 s. During this period, the intensity of negative emotions was measured with the VAS at 15-second intervals, mirroring the baseline measurement. After completing the reappraisal, a fixation cross was displayed for 15 s.

Each trial consisted of the following components: vignette presentation (40 s), baseline negative emotion rating (5 s), selection of emotion label or nonsense syllables (5 s), emotion regulation and ratings during implementation (45 s), and fixation cross-presentation (15 s), totaling 110 s. Each condition was repeated thrice, leading to an overall task comprising 12 trials. We inserted 30-second breaks every four trials. The total time required for the task was 1,410 s. Below, we describe the details of each condition.

AL + reappraisal condition

In this condition, four emotion words (“anger,” “sadness,” “disgust,” “fear”) were presented for 5 s. Participants were instructed to select the emotion word that most closely matched the emotion elicited by the vignette stimulus by pressing the corresponding key (“1,” “2,” “3,” or “4”). The positions of the words on the screen, from left to right, corresponded to “anger,” “sadness,” “disgust,” and “fear,” aligning with the positions of the number keys. Following their selection, the participants were instructed to engage in cognitive reappraisal to reduce negative emotions. Specifically, they were asked to reinterpret the vignette stimulus in a more positive light or think about it calmly, in line with the approach used by Yoshimura et al. (2022).

No AL + reappraisal condition

Similar to the AL + Reappraisal condition, following baseline measurements, participants were presented with four nonsense hiragana syllables (“yu-ma-wo,” “se-hi-shi-ha,” “ro-te-ku,” “nyu-so-mi”) and asked to randomly select one by pressing a key. After selection, participants engaged in cognitive reappraisal identical to that of the AL + Reappraisal condition, aiming to reduce the intensity of the negative emotions.

AL + no reappraisal condition

This condition paralleled the AL + Reappraisal condition in terms of the presentation and selection of emotion words. However, instead of engaging in emotion regulation through reappraisal, participants were instructed to focus on the emotions elicited by the stimulus.

No AL + no reappraisal condition

In this condition, following the presentation of nonsense syllables, as in the AL + no Reappraisal condition, participants selected a syllable by pressing a key. The participants were then instructed to focus on the emotions elicited by the stimulus without engaging in cognitive reappraisal.

Procedure

After the research objectives were explained and informed consent was obtained, the participants were instructed on the task. Then, the participants practiced the behavioral tasks by completing a single trial of each of the four task conditions. Additional practice was given if needed. Notably, the vignette stimuli used in practice differed from those used in the main task.

Participants’ understanding of reappraisal was assessed using the State Emotion Regulation Inventory (SERI) [31], which measures various emotion regulation strategies, including reappraisal, distraction, acceptance, and brooding (Cronbach’s alphas ranged from 0.82 to 0.91). For this study, only the four reappraisal-related items from the SERI were used (e.g., “I tried to change the way I think about the situation”). Participants rated the extent to which they performed reappraisal during the practice phase according to the instructions provided in the SERI items on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The mean score for these items was 6.23 (SD = 0.65). Participants rated their reappraisal during practice on a 7-point scale for each item. The mean score for these items was 6.23 (SD = 0.65).

fNIRS setting

Following practice, participants were fitted with near-infrared spectroscopy (LABNIRS, Shimadzu) probes to record brain activity during the task. Data acquisition was performed utilizing the system control software provided by the manufacturer. Twenty-one probes (10 sources and 11 detectors) were placed to cover the frontal cortex resulting in 32 measurement channels (between-probe distance of 30 mm). The probes were placed on the forehead using a headband holder specifically designed for LABNIRS (“Flexible Adjustable Surface Holder” forehead fiber holder, Shimadzu). This holder is designed with a with a fixed distance of 30 mm between the sockets for probe placement (https://www.j-platpat.inpit.go.jp/c1801/PU/JP-2004-313741/11/en). The holder was positioned to ensure that the probes were placed over the prefrontal cortex, in accordance with the international 10–20 system. The probes were secured in place with adhesive tape to minimize movement during the experiment. The bottom line was positioned in alignment with Fp1 and Fp2. The target channels at the target region of interest (ROI) were defined by the 2 × 2 matrix square of the NIRS measurement area. The channels corresponding to the left and right DLPFC were defined to cover the vicinity of F3 (ROI1) and F4 (ROI2), respectively, according to the international 10–20 system. The MPFC was designated to cover the intermediate region between Fpz and Fz (ROI3). Additionally, the channels corresponding to the left and right VLPFC were defined to cover the vicinity of F7 (ROI4) and F8 (ROI5), respectively. The initiation of behavioral tasks was synchronized with signal recording and triggered from the PC to the LABNIRS system (The sources emit light at three wavelengths of 780, 805, and 830 nm). The data were exported from the system as raw voltages and changed to the optical density. The raw optical density was analyzed by the LABNIRS system control software, which applies the modified Beer-Lambert Law to convert the raw optical density data into changes in the concentration of OxyHb, deOxyHb, and total Hb. The converted OxyHb signal was then subjected to preprocessing, including motion artifact correction. However, the present study focused only on the OxyHb signal. This choice is based on the greater sensitivity of the oxy-Hb signal to cerebral blood flow changes [32], as well as its strong correlation with blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals [33].As in the postpractice period, the SERI was administered again to evaluate reappraisal during the task after the experiment. The average score was 6.33 (SD = 0.66), suggesting that the participants adequately understood the reappraisal procedure during the task.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral data

We analyzed the difference between baseline and postbaseline negative emotion ratings (ΔNegative emotion) for each condition. The ΔNegative emotion, calculated for each of the three postbaseline ratings, reflects changes in negative emotions, with negative values indicating a reduction and positive values indicating an increase.

A linear mixed-effects model was used with ΔNegative emotion as the dependent variable. The model included fixed-effect factors (labeling, emotion regulation, three measurements of postbaseline negative emotion, their interaction, trial order, stimulus presentation order, types of emotion stimulus) and random-effect factors (participants), with baseline emotion values and participant gender serving as covariates. Participants were included as a random effect to account for repeated measures. We examined the interaction between labeling and emotion regulation and conducted multiple comparisons using Bonferroni’s method. All p values reported in the post hoc tests were Bonferroni-corrected. The effect size of the post hoc test was reported as Hedge’s g. Analysis was performed using jamovi 2.3.23 (The jamovi project, 2023) with the GAMLj package 2.6.6.

fNIRS data

fNIRS data were preprocessed using custom scripts in MATLAB R2020a (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA). To ensure acceptable signal-to-noise ratios, the accuracy of the fNIRS signal was checked by negative high voltage value to each source probe prior to recording, and adjustments were made for each probe until all channels met the minimum criteria (0.05 ~ 4 V) defined in the LABNIRS system control software [34]. Despite these adjustments, the voltage of the upper probes tended to be high, and the time series of the upper right and upper left channels remained particularly noisy. In addition, to ensure signal quality, we performed a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) analysis for each channel using the raw data, prior to any motion artifact detection or filtering. The SNR was calculated using the formula: SNR = 20log10(µ/σ) dB, where µ was defined as the mean difference between the task condition signal values and the pre-task baseline, and σ was the standard deviation of the signal values across the entire measurement period for each participant and channel. We then analyzed the average SNR across all participants for each channel. We applied an exclusion criterion of SNR < 5 dB for the mean SNR across participants. Based on this criterion, channel 1 was excluded from further analysis as its mean SNR was below 5 dB. All remaining channels included in our ROI analyses demonstrated mean SNR values above 5 dB, ensuring robust signal quality for our analyses. As a result, these channels were excluded from the subsequent ROI analysis which described later. A motion artifact was defined as present if a signal exceeded the mean ± 5 times the standard deviation (SD). As a result, only one trial of one participant had a signal value exceeding ± 5 times the standard deviation (SD), so that trial was excluded from subsequent analyses. Because the number of outliers was small, we decided to include all study participants in the analysis. Trials including motion artifacts were excluded from the subsequent averaging process. Additionally, a temporal derivative distribution repair algorithm [35] was applied to the time series data for motion corrections. A temporal derivative distribution repair algorithm was chosen for its demonstrated superior performance in removing motion artifacts while preserving the true hemodynamic response, as compared to other methods such as spline interpolation and wavelet filtering [35]. This method provides a comprehensive and robust approach to addressing various types of motion-related noise in fNIRS data. OxyHb time series data were subjected to detrending and a 0.0028–0.1 Hz Butterworth bandpass filter to remove slow drifts and cardiac artifacts. A Butterworth bandpass filter with cutoff frequencies of 0.0028–0.1 Hz was applied to remove slow drifts and cardiac artifacts. This specific frequency range was chosen to optimize signal quality for our experimental design. The lower cutoff (0.0028 Hz) is well below the inverse of our trial duration (1/70 s ≈ 0.0143 Hz), ensuring capture of the full hemodynamic response across each trial. The upper cutoff (0.1 Hz) allows detection of typical hemodynamic responses while effectively removing respiratory (0.2–0.3 Hz) and cardiac (∼1 Hz) artifacts. A wider-than-usual bandwidth (low-cut) was used because of the length of one trial in this experiment, which consisted of 20s rest, 30s task, and 20s rest periods. This filter setting strikes a balance between preserving task-related signals and mitigating physiological noise, making it suitable for our block-design paradigm. The filtered data were Z-transformed based on channel-specific means and standard deviations. The Z score time series, spanning 5 s before stimulus onset to 95 s (end of emotional regulation), was baseline-corrected using the 10 s preceding each trial. The data were separately averaged per condition.

A linear mixed-effects model was used with the average Z score of each ROI as the dependent variable. The model included fixed-effect factors (labeling, emotion regulation, epochs, their interaction), random-effect factors (participants), and covariates (participant gender, number of additions in calculation of average Z score). We examined the interaction between labeling, emotion regulation, and epochs. All p values reported in the post hoc tests were Bonferroni-corrected. The effect size of the post hoc test was reported as Hedge’s g. Analysis was performed using jamovi 2.3.23 (The jamovi project, 2023) with the GAMLj package 2.6.6.

Discussion

This study used fNIRS and measured brain activity related to regulating negative emotions through AL combined with reappraisal. These results align with those of Yoshimura et al. [21], who reported increased activity in the left DLPFC when the AL was paired with reappraisal, which is consistent with prior research suggesting that the AL enhances emotion regulation. However, our behavioral data revealed that combining AL with reappraisal paradoxically diminished the effectiveness of emotion regulation.

Linear mixed-effects model analysis of the behavioral data revealed a reduced regulatory effect on negative emotions when AL and cognitive reappraisal were utilized together, as opposed to cognitive reappraisal alone. This finding is interesting considering the findings of previous reports, such as those by Torre and Lieberman [15], which suggest that AL typically reduces emotional experiences. Our results suggest that conducting AL may disrupt regulation when individuals actively engage with their emotions to regulate them. A procedural distinction in our study compared to that of Nook et al. [18] is noteworthy. Our participants chose a label from a set of five emotion words closely related to their feelings, whereas Nook et al. [18] had participants generate spontaneous labels. This difference necessitates caution in drawing direct comparisons between our findings and those of Nook et al. [18]. Despite potential mismatches in labels and the emotions experienced by participants, the process of verbalizing emotions likely occurred. This discrepancy and challenge in emotion differentiation and the fixation of emotions through AL may impose an additional cognitive load when regulating negative emotions via reappraisal. Further examination of this issue in conjunction with the fNIRS findings of this study is warranted.

Our fNIRS findings indicated a significant increase in bilateral DLPFC activity during the combined use of AL and reappraisal (AL + Reappraisal condition) compared to AL alone (AL + no Reappraisal condition). The DLPFC, while not directly involved in emotion processing, plays a crucial role in cognitive control [19]. Previous research [22, 23] suggested that DLPFC activity is associated with increased cognitive load during tasks and is heightened during the reappraisal of intense emotional stimuli. Therefore, our results suggest that the cognitive processes involved in AL and reappraisal may interact, as reflected by increased DLPFC activity. While this interaction could be associated with changes in cognitive demands, it’s crucial to consider alternative interpretations of the observed DLPFC activity. However, the DLPFC activity observed to correlate with reappraisal could also be involved in cognitive processes other than reappraisal. In prior studies utilizing negative image stimuli, the ‘no reappraisal’ condition typically involved directing participants to focus on the emotions elicited by the stimuli, serving as a contrast to reappraisal tasks [23, 36]. In the ‘no Reappraisal’ condition, participants were instructed to focus on the emotions elicited by the stimuli, suggesting a preferential allocation of attentional resources toward emotional processing. In contrast, reappraisal tasks involve directing attention toward the active regulation of emotions. Thus, differences observed between the ‘AL + Reappraisal’ and ‘AL + no Reappraisal’ conditions could reflect differences in attentional deployment between emotion processing and regulation rather than the presence or absence of reappraisal alone. Moreover, the DLPFC is known to be involved in attentional control processes [37]. Greater DLPFC activity in the ‘AL + Reappraisal’ versus ‘AL + no Reappraisal’ versus ‘AL + no Reappraisal’ group could therefore reflect differences in the deployment of attentional resources when concurrently performing reappraisal and attentional load tasks. Caution may thus be warranted in attributing between-condition differences in DLPFC activity solely to the process of reappraisal.

Neuroimaging research focusing on the neural underpinnings of AL [14, 24, 25, 27] has consistently reported increased activity in the right VLPFC during AL. However, our study showed that the no AL + no Reappraisal condition had higher signal values in the ROIs corresponding to the DLPFC and VLPFC than did the no AL + Reappraisal condition, which was unexpected and not hypothesized. This may suggest that the increased activity in the DLPFC and VLPFC was a result of focusing on the emotions elicited by the emotional stimuli when considered in conjunction with the behavioral data. In this case, the decrease in the emotion regulation effect observed with the combination of AL and reappraisal may not be due to increased cognitive load but rather to other factors that were not elucidated in this study. Nook et al. [18] discussed the possibility that verbalizing emotions not only crystallizes them but also reduces the motivation to regulate them. However, since this study used a within-subjects experimental paradigm, it is unlikely that a decrease in motivation occurred only in certain conditions. However, further investigations are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms involved. Future studies using fMRI could examine differences in brain networks between the combined AL and reappraisal condition and the no AL + no Reappraisal condition. Additionally, it would be valuable to compare different methods of emotion recognition, such as the quantitative assessment of emotional intensity used by Cameron et al. [38]. By comparing these emotion granularity manipulations with AL and examining their impact on reappraisal, we can gain a better understanding of the role of cognitive load in reappraisal effectiveness.

Compared with the combined use of AL and reappraisal, this study revealed no significant brain activity specifically associated with reappraisal alone. Burklund et al. [24] directly compared AL and reappraisal and reported greater activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex during reappraisal than in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex during AL. This finding suggested that AL and reappraisal engage partially distinct neural circuits, implying qualitative differences in these emotion regulation strategies. However, the absence of significant activity increases in reappraisal alone compared to AL does not necessarily imply that the combined strategy simply activates both reappraisal- and AL-related circuits. It is possible that the combination produces unique brain activity patterns not observed in either strategy alone.

Limitations

The findings in the current study are limited in several respects. The vignette stimuli were relatively weak in emotional intensity, and arousal levels were not matched across vignettes. Therefore, comparisons between the results of these studies and those of previous studies employing images, such as Nook et al. [18], should be made cautiously. It remains unclear whether ALs similarly impact other regulatory strategies, such as acceptance. Moreover, in our study, there was no significant difference in negative emotion ratings between the AL + no Reappraisal condition and the no AL + no Reappraisal condition, although AL is known to elicit incidental emotion regulation. This finding contrasts with previous AL research [14, 24] that often used unpleasant images as stimuli, whereas our study employed vignettes. The differing experimental paradigms may account for this discrepancy. In addition, due to the constraints of fNIRS measurements, we could not separately examine brain activity during affect labeling and reappraisal. Therefore, it was inherently difficult to investigate the correlation between affect labeling and right VLPFC activity.

Future directions

One limitation of this study is that fNIRS measurements cannot be used to distinguish between brain activity associated with AL and reappraisal. To clarify how the AL of emotion affects reappraisal, future research should separate and examine the neural correlates of the AL and reappraisal. Additionally, the target of AL (e.g., one’s own emotions vs. emotional stimuli) should be considered. Furthermore, there are variations in AL methods (e.g., selecting from researcher-defined labels vs. self-generating labels). Therefore, future research should also investigate the influence of AL method variations on intentional emotion regulation. Alternatively, we suggest that future studies design experiments that manipulate emotion differentiation procedures differently from those of ALs and investigate subsequent emotion regulation. Cameron et al., [38] demonstrated that manipulating emotion differentiation before emotion regulation leads to an overall decrease in negative emotions. Comparing this procedure, which influences emotion differentiation, with AL could clarify the distinct impacts of accurate emotion differentiation and AL on emotion regulation processes. Finally, while the present study employed a “no reappraisal” condition as a control for the reappraisal condition, it is important to note that this condition not only involved the absence of reappraisal but also required participants to allocate attention to their own emotions. Consequently, the “no reappraisal” condition in this study does not serve as a complete control condition. In future research, it would be preferable to include a comparison with a condition that either instructs participants to respond naturally to emotional stimuli or simply refrain from engaging in emotion regulation, as commonly used in emotion regulation studies [7]. This approach would allow for a more precise examination of the specific effects of reappraisal by providing a more appropriate control condition, which would help to disentangle the effects of reappraisal from those of attentional allocation to one’s emotions. Another limitation of our study design is the fixed order of affect labeling and reappraisal tasks. In all trials involving both processes, affect labeling always preceded reappraisal. This consistent sequence may have influenced the observed interaction effects between affect labeling and reappraisal. The fixed order makes it difficult to determine whether the effects we observed are due to the combination of these processes or simply a result of their specific sequence. Future studies should consider counterbalancing the order of affect labeling and reappraisal tasks to control for potential order effects and to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how these processes interact.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides insights into the interaction between affect labeling and reappraisal in emotion regulation. The behavioral results suggest that affect labeling prior to reappraisal may modulate the effectiveness of emotion regulation. Concurrently, our fNIRS measurements revealed increased lateral prefrontal cortex activation when these processes were combined. These findings indicate a potential interplay between affect labeling and reappraisal, possibly involving alterations in cognitive processing. While affect labeling has been previously associated with incidental emotion regulation, our results hint at a more complex relationship when combined with intentional regulation strategies like reappraisal. However, the precise nature of this interaction and its underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Future research should aim to further clarify these processes and their implications for emotion regulation strategies.
