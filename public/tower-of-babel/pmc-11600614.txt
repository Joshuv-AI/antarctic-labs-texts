Advanced liver fibrosis, but not MASLD, is associated with accelerated biological aging: a population-based study
Authors: Chengcheng Tong, Yufeng Xue, Wei Wang, Xi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe process of biological aging in patients diagnosed with chronic liver disease remains unclear.AimThe current study aims to investigate if there is an accelerated biological aging process in participants with advanced fibrosis (AF) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).MethodsData from the 2017–2018 NHANES cycle were analyzed. AF was determined based on the values of liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and MASLD was defined according to new consensus nomenclature. Klemera-Doubal method biological age (KDM bioage) and Phenotypic age (Phenoage) were adopted to quantify biological age. Phenoage advancement (Phenoage_advance) and KDM advancement (KDM_advance) were generated as the difference between the calculated biological age and chronological age, and a positive residual was regarded as an indicator of accelerated biological aging.ResultsA total of 3974 participants was enrolled. The weight mean KDM_advance and phenoage_advance in AF group was 4.22 years (95%CI: 2.96–5.49 years) and 2.61 years (95%CI: 1.80–3.41 years), while in MASLD group was 0.37 years (95%CI: -0.28–1.03 years) and 0.04 years (95%CI: -0.64–0.72 years), respectively. Multivariate linear regression analysis showed that participants with AF had older KDM_advance and phenoage_advance compared with those without AF (1.50 years (95%CI: 0.23–2.77 years), P = 0.02; 1.00 years (95%CI: 0.18–1.82 years), P = 0.02; respectively), in models adjusting demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities. No significant association was found between MASLD and KDM_advance and phenoage_advance.ConclusionsAF, not MASLD, was independently associated with accelerated biological aging in adults from a US representative sample.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20808-y.Keywords: Liver fibrosis, MASLD, Biological aging, NHANES

Introduction

Liver fibrosis, which characterized by the diffuse deposition of extracellular matrix and the disruption of normal hepatic lobule, contributed to nearly 1.32 million deaths in 2017 and is expected to become a leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. Metabolic associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is caused by fat accumulation and is characterized by the steatosis changes in histology [3]. MASLD has become the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and 20% of MASLD patients will progress to advanced liver fibrosis [4, 5]. A recent study showed that the prevalence of MASLD in U.S was 31.3%, while for MASLD + increased alcohol intake (MetALD) was 2.2%, for alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) was 0.7% [6]. Hence, identifying biomarkers to systemically evaluate the health status of patients diagnosed with MASLD and liver fibrosis can provide valuable information in elucidating pathological mechanisms and clinical treatment.

Biological aging is regarded as a progressive decline of physiological integrity that occurs with advancing age [7]. Difference in exposure to congenital and acquired risk factors causes inequality accumulation levels of molecular changes which are responsible for the decline of tissues and organs’ function, ultimately reflected as the variation in the degree of biological aging [8]. Therefore, to some extent, biological aging is a modifiable process, and differences in the process of biological aging may be observed in people suffering from various diseases. In patients with multiple sclerosis, evidence from a prospective study demonstrated accelerated biological aging [9]. In patients with depression, accelerated biological aging was also observed and found to account for the relationship between depression and high incidence of all-cause mortality [10]. Similar conclusions also had been reported in osteoarthritis and cardiometabolic disease [11, 12].

Several factors contributed to chronic liver disease had been reported to accelerate biological aging. One study showed that hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection was related to greater biological age acceleration [13]. A recent study found that any amount alcohol consumption resulted to the acceleration of biological age [14]. Visceral fat accumulation was also found to associate with the shorter telomere length [15]. These results promote us to investigate whether patients with liver fibrosis or MASLD display accelerated biological aging. Studies showed that consistent fat accumulation leads to the incidence of MASLD and finally progress to liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, while aging increase susceptibility to MASLD and liver fibrotic response [4, 5, 16]. These results suggested that aging and fat accumulation are the common risk factors of MASLD and liver fibrosis, further revealed the similarity between liver fibrosis and MASLD in the diseases pathogenesis in relation to aging. However, it should be noted the complex etiologies of liver fibrosis and the distinct histological changes between liver fibrosis and MASLD [17]. Moreover, liver fibrosis has longer disease course compared with MASLD [18]. These differences also inspire us to detect the heterogeneity between liver fibrosis and MASLD with regard to aging.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a large-scale nutritional and epidemiological research project conducted by the United States Public Health Service, which offers a great opportunity to study the process of aging at a population level. Using the data, this study aims to examine the association of liver fibrosis and MASLD with biological aging, measured by phenotypic age and biological age. We hypothesize that advanced liver fibrosis and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease are each associated with a larger difference between biological aging (phenotypic age and biological age) and chronological age than the healthy control.

Methods

Study design and participants

The NHANES utilizes a multistage, stratified, and probability-cluster sampling method to collect participants of all ages across the US, and written informed consent was submitted by each participant. In the current study, we analyzed data from the 2017–2018 NHANES cycle and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting. All procedures were performed according to the guidelines and instructions provided by NHANES.

A total of 9,254 individuals were enrolled in the NHANES 2017–2018 cycle. We applied the following criteria to exclude participants: (1) incomplete vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) measurements (n = 3760); (2) missing component data for calculating biological aging (n = 595); (3) age younger than 20 years or older than 85 years (n = 925). Ultimately, we included 3974 participants in this study.

Assessment of advanced liver fibrosis (AF) and MASLD

Participants received VCTE examinations in the NHANES Mobile Examination Center (MEC). The results were considered reliable if participants fasted for at least three hours before the examination and obtained 10 or more measurements with an interquartile range/median liver stiffness measurement (LSM) < 30%. The protocol for the VCTE examination can be found in LUX_J (cdc.gov). AF was defined as LSM values ≥ 9.7 (kPa) [19]. MASLD was defined according to new consensus nomenclature. Briefly, participant with steatotic liver disease (SLD) was defined as controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) ≥ 288 (dB/m). SLD participants who had a cardiometabolic feature were subsequently regarded as MASLD. A history of daily alcohol consumption exceeding 30 g for men or 20 g for women in participants with MASLD was further considered MetALD. SLD participants who had no cardiometabolic features and had a history of daily alcohol consumption exceeding 30 g for men or 20 g for women were classified into ALD, otherwise they were defined as Cryptogenic SLD [20]. Finally, MetALD, ALD, and Cryptogenic SLD were named as other-SLD in the current study for analysis. Cardiometabolic features were presented in Supplementary file 1.

Biological age

Two methods, Klemera-Doubal method biological age (KDM bioage) and Phenotypic age (Phenoage), were adopted to quantify biological age. KDM bioage and Phenoage both are well-validated measures of biological age and are widely implemented in evaluating the effect of various factors on aging process [21, 22]. The “Bioage” R package was used to calculate KDM bioage and Phenoage using a biomarker set (albumin, alkaline phosphatase, urea nitrogen, total cholesterol, creatinine, Hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C), systolic blood pressure and high-sensitive creative protein (CRP) for KDM bioage, while chronological age, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, HbA1C, white blood cell count, lymphocyte percentage, mean cell volume, and red cell distribution width for Phenoage) [23]. We first trained KDM bioage and Phenoage in NHANES 3 using the above biomarkers and subsequently calculated KDM bioage and Phenoage in NHANES 2017–2018 cycle. Phenoage advancement (Phenoage_advance) and KDM advancement (KDM_advance) were generated as the difference between the calculated biological age and chronological age, and a positive residual was regarded as an indicator of accelerated biological aging while a negative residual indicates delayed biological aging.

Covariates

Covariates considered in this study included demographic characteristics (age, sex, race), socioeconomic status (education level, marital status, poverty to income ratio), lifestyle factors (BMI category, smoking status, alcohol use), and comorbidities (diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD)). Age was further categorized as < 60, and ≥ 60 years. Race was categorized as non-Hispanic (NH) Black, NH White, NH Asian, Hispanic (including Mexican American and other Hispanics), and other or multiracial. Education level was categorized as college graduate or above (high), high school graduate or GRE (middle), and less than high school graduate (low). BMI categories include obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2), overweight (25—< 30 kg/m2) and normal (< 25 kg/m2). Poverty to income ratio was categorized as < 1.3, 1.3–3.5, > 3.5. Smoking status was classified into never, former, and current based on the household interview data. For alcohol use, we identified participants as never, mild, moderate and heavy as 0 g/d, < 5 g/d, 5- < 15 g/d, ≥ 15 g/d for women and 0 g/d, < 10 g/d, 10- < 30 g/d, ≥ 30 g/d for men. DM was diagnosed by a self-reported previous diagnosis, a HbA1C ≥ 6.5%, or a fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL. CVD was identified based on the self-reported history of coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, or transient ischemic attacks. CKD was identified as eGFR ≤ 60 ml/min/1.73m2.

Statistical analysis

We incorporated sample weights in all analyses given the complex sampling design of NHANES. Categorical variables were presented as weighted percentages and compared across groups using the design-adjusted Rao-Scott chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as weighted means and compared using linear regression. The corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated using the Taylor series linearization method. Survey-weighted linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the association of KDM_advance and Phenoage_advance with MASLD and AF. The regression analysis was constructed using four models: model 1 (no adjusted), model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race), model 3 (additionally adjusted for BMI, poverty to income ratio, education level, and marital status), and model 4 (adjusted for covariates in model 2 and smoking history, alcohol use, CVD, DM, and CKD). Subgroup analysis was performed by sex (female, male), age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), race (Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic Asian, Non-Hispanic White, Hispanic, Other), education level (high, middle, low), marital status (single, double), and BMI (normal, overweight, obesity), to account for potential effects of specific populations. The significance of the interaction was assessed using P values for the product terms between the KDM_advance and Phenoage_advance and the stratified variables. In sensitivity analysis, we constructed three models to test the robustness of our results. In model A, we adopted a different LSM cutoff value to diagnose participants with AF as ≥ 10 (kPa) [24]. In model B, we identified participants with AF as a fibrosis-index 4 (FIB4) value ≥ 1.3 (model B.1) or NAFLD (non-alcoholic associated fatty liver disease) Fibrosis Score (NFS) > -1.455 (model B.2) [24]. Finally, in model C, we excluded participants with chronic comorbidities that can influence the process of liver fibrosis including CKD, CVD, and DM. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was used. We conducted all analyses using R software version 4.2.2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Participants were classified into non-AF and AF groups and the baseline characteristics were presented in Table 1. The weighted mean age was 47.90 years (95%CI: 46.54–49.26 years), and the weighted percentage of males was 48.73% (95%CI: 44.62–52.85%). A significantly higher weighted mean KDM bioage and Phenoage of participants in AF group was observed when compared with those in non-AF group (57.35 years (95%CI: 54.48–60.21 years) vs. 46.75 years (95%CI: 45.52–47.99 years), p < 0.001; 55.73 years (95%CI: 53.45–58.00 years) vs. 46.85 years (95%CI: 45.51–48.20 years), P < 0.001). Moreover, the weight mean KDM_advance and phenoage_advance in AF group was 4.22 years (95%CI: 2.96–5.49 years) and 2.61 years (95%CI: 1.80–3.41 years), respectively, which indicate that participants with AF was associated with 4.22 “years” older KDM bioage and 2.61 “years” older Phenoage compared with their chronological age. The accelerated biological aging process in participants with AF also supported by a significantly higher weight mean KDM_advance and phenoage_advance when compared with those with non-AF (4.22 years (95%CI: 2.96–5.49 years) vs. -0.88 years (95%CI: -1.33- -0.42 years), P < 0.001; 2.61 years (95%CI: 1.80–3.41 years) vs. -0.78 years (95%CI: -1.22- -0.33 years), P < 0.001; respectively).

Association of AF and MASLD with biological aging

We next analyze the association of AF and MASLD with KDM_advance and phenoage_advance by linear regression. Compared with those in non-AF group, participants in AF group had a 5.10 years (95%CI:3.59–6.61 years) older KDM bioage and 3.38 years (95%CI:2.52–4.25 years) older Phenoage in Model 1 (Tables 3 and 4). The associations of AF with KDM_advance and phenoage_advance were robust after adjusting for covariates in Model 2 and Model 3, respectively. Further, a larger (1.00 (95% CI: 0.18–1.82)) difference between the Phenoage and chronological age and a larger (1.50 (95% CI: 0.23–2.77)) difference between the KDM bioage and chronological age was also observed after adjusting covariates in Model 4. Participants in MASLD group had a 2.42 years (95%CI:1.68–3.16 years) older KDM bioage and 1.72 years (95%CI:1.20–2.24 years) older Phenoage compared with those in non-SLD group in Model 1 (Tables 3 snd 4). However, the associations of MASLD with KDM_advance and phenoage_advance were attenuated in Model 3 and Model 4 without statistical significance (Tables 3 and 4).

Sensitivity analysis

Applying different LSM cutoff value to determine AF (≥ 10 kPa) also showed the significant association of AF with older KDM bioage and phenoage_advance, as revealed by univariate linear analysis (Supplementary Table 1) and multivariate linear analysis (Table 5) with an increase of 1.53 years (95%CI:0.14–2.92 years) and 0.98 years (95%CI:0.16–1.81 years) older KDM bioage and phenoage_advance, respectively (P = 0.03, = 0.02) in multivariate linear analysis (Table 5). The associations of AF with KDM_advance and phenoage_advance were even stronger after adopting FIB4 and NFS to identify AF with an increase of 2.88 years (95%CI:0.48–5.29 years) and 3.47 years (95%CI:2.00–4.95 years) KDM bioage, and 2.56 years (95%CI:0.22–4.91 years) and 3.05 years (95%CI:1.79–4.31 years) Phenoage in multivariate linear analysis (Table 5). Finally, excluding participants with chronic comorbidities that can influence the process of liver fibrosis did not change the results (Supplementary Table 1, Table 5).

Discussion

This study first investigated the association of SLD and AF with biological age, as assessed by KDM bioage and Phenoage, respectively, at a population level by using the nationally representative data of US adults. The main findings were that participants with AF may have a higher biological age and an accelerated biological aging process when compared with those without AF. Sensitivity analysis was performed to minimize the influence of special populations and the potential bias derived from different AF criteria, and the results were robust.

KDM bioage and Phenoage are two classical and widely used measures of biological age, and the accessibility of the biomarker set to generate KDM bioage and Phenoage provides great feasibility for studies carried out at a population level. Using the data from the UK biobank, KL et al. found that KDM bioage and Phenoage are associated risks of lung and colorectal cancer [25]. Wei et al. reported the satisfied predictive accuracy of KDM bioage for all-cause mortality in the Chinese and US populations [26], and the accuracy of Phenoage for predicting morbidity and mortality also had been demonstrated across diverse subpopulations [27]. Hence, there is a great value of KDM bioage and Phenoage for quantifying biological age. The biological aging process was quantified by the value of KDM_advance and phenoage_advance in this study and a positive value was regarded as an indicator of accelerated biological aging. Using this method, Thomas et al. reported that participants who adhered to healthy lifestyle behaviors had delayed biological aging process compared with those who had a less-healthy lifestyle [28]; Xu et al. suggested that blunted rest-activity circadian rhythm is associated with accelerated aging [29]. In current study, after controlling demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors and comorbidities, we found that participants with AF had older KDM_advance and phenoage_advance compared with those without AF (1.50 years (95%CI:0.23–2.77 years), 1.00 years (95%CI:0.18–1.82 years), respectively). The results provide evidence to support that participants with AF may have a higher biological age and an accelerated biological aging process when compared with those without AF. The subgroups analysis according to age, sex, race, education level, BMI, and marital status further supported an accelerated biological aging process occurred in participants with AF again. In addition, although participants in MASLD group showed a significantly higher weight mean KDM_advance and phenoage_advance than in non-SLD group, the statistical difference may induce by the delayed biological aging in non-SLD group participants. Numerous studies showed the detrimental effect of accelerated biological aging in clinical. One study founded that the acceleration of biological aging was associated with 1.22% increased risk of overall cancer in men and 1.26% increased risk of overall cancer in women [30]. In HF patients, each 1 year increase in phenoage_advance was related to 3% increased risk of all-cause mortality [31]. A recent study also identified that the adjusted hazard ratio for cardiometabolic multimorbidity to death was 1.26 (95% CI: 1.17–1.35) per 1 year increase in phenoage_advance [32]. These studies incorporated with our results provided a feasibility to investigate the clinical value of KDM_advance and phenoage_advance as a prognostic biomarker with regard to the high risk of hepatocellular carcinoma and mortality in patients with AF. However, it also should be noted that the values of albumin and mean cell volume, two components adopted to quantify biological age, are easily disrupted in participants with cirrhosis. Human albumin infusion is recommended for the treatment of cirrhosis [33]. Use of albumin infusion in participants with cirrhosis may induce the improvement of the values of biological age. Participants with cirrhosis also appear to be at high risk of bleeding events [34], which can cause significant fluctuation in mean cell volume. Hence, the tests that determine biological age may be affected in participants with cirrhosis, and the potential deviation of the acceleration of biological aging need to be alternation. Overall population prevalence estimation found that most patients with MASLD are manifested with normal liver function without clinical symptom, and only 4% MASLD patients develop AF [35, 36]. A retrospective study for MASLD patients showed that steatosis alone has a improve clinical course with normal liver function, while patients with steatohepatitis exhibited deteriorated liver function [37]. A cohort study showed that there is no increased risk of adverse liver outcomes for MASLD patients without fibrosis changes [38]. Multivariate analysis in our study also showed that there is no significant association of MASLD with KDM_advance and phenoage_advance. These results revealed that the biological aging process of MASLD patients may be determined by the histological characteristics.

Aging and senescence is becoming one of the frontier fields and have drawn more attention over the past decade. Today, it is clear that senescent hepatocytes are accumulated in the fibrotic liver, and recent studies have expanded the senescent cell types to liver sinusoidal endothelial cells and Kupffer cells [39, 40]. These findings at molecular level provide a new perspective on the mechanism of liver fibrosis and promote us to focus on the biological aging process, which is regarded as a global decline of the body’s function. The accelerated biological aging process, which has been demonstrated an association with mortality and mortality, along with chronological age, may both contribute to the clinical phenotype of AF. Moreover, the current study provides feasibility to estimate the health status of AF patients by evaluating the biological aging process. Methods to delay the biological aging process of AF patients may also have the potential to delay the disease process, as it has been reported that higher adherence to the Mediterranean Diet, a diet pattern with the ability to delay the biological aging process [28], is associate with a decreased risk of liver fibrosis [41].

Some limitations need to be considered during interpretation. First, we quantify biological age and biological aging process by calculating KDM bioage and Phenoage and KDM_advance and phenoage_advance. However, no gold standard for accurately reflecting the biological aging process and biological age at current, and KDM bioage and Phenoage both are widely used indicators for measuring biological age with satisfied performance for measuring the declined function of an organ system. Second, given the data in NHANES were collected at a single time-point, the causality between the biological aging process and AF remains unclear, and quantifying of biological aging process in AF participants cannot thoroughly exclude the impact of confounders such as comorbidities. Moreover, given the complex sample survey design of NHANES, the traditional parametric tests are not applicable and a standard analytic procedure is required to rational interpret the data and the results derived from NHANES. Next, no histological evidence to demonstrate the presence of hepatic steatosis and liver fibrosis in this study. Conducting liver biopsies in a national population is infeasible, and the results of VCTE have been demonstrated as a useful non-invasive method to estimate hepatic steatosis and liver fibrosis in a population-based setting with satisfactory sensitivity and specificity [42]. We also performed sensitivity analysis by applying different criteria to diagnose AF and the funding was robust. Finally, we excluded participants with incomplete data, which could lead to a risk of selection bias.

Conclusions

In summary, our study observed that AF, not MASLD, was independently associated with an accelerated biological aging process in adults from a US representative sample. Further studies incorporating liver tissue biopsies are required to confirm these findings.
