PTEN inhibits epithelial mesenchymal transition of thyroid cancer cells by regulating the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway
Authors: Li Wei, Qianhui Liang, Chang Zhou, Rong Liu, Yun Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe global incidence of thyroid cancer (THCA) has significantly risen in recent years. This study aims to investigate the role and mechanisms of PTEN in epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), invasion and migration of THCA cells.MethodsPTEN expression in THCA was analyzed through bioinformatics databases. RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses were performed to quantify PTEN levels in the Nthy-ori 3–1 cell line and three THCA cell types (TPC-1, B-CPAP, FTC-133). TPC-1 cells were transfected with a PTEN overexpression plasmid and treated with the Wnt activator. Cell viability and apoptosis were assessed via CCK-8 and flow cytometry, respectively. The expression levels of E-Cadherin, N-Cadherin, and Vimentin in TPC-1 cells were evaluated using Western blot. The invasive, migratory, and wound-healing abilities of the cells were examined using Transwell and scratch assays. Activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway was assessed through Western blot.ResultsPTEN expression was significantly lower in THCA cells, particularly in TPC-1 cells compared to other cell lines. PTEN overexpression led to decreased viability in TPC-1 cells, increased apoptosis, and a rise in E-Cadherin levels while reducing N-Cadherin and Vimentin levels, thereby inhibiting EMT. Furthermore, PTEN overexpression diminished the invasive, migratory and wound-healing capabilities of TPC-1 cells and suppressed activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Treatment with the Wnt activator partially counteracted the effects of PTEN overexpression on TPC-1 cells.ConclusionPTEN functions to inhibit EMT and the invasive and migratory characteristics of THCA cells by blocking the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (THCA) ranks among the most prevalent malignant neoplasm of the endocrine system, with a global annual incidence of 586,000 cases, placing it 9th in terms of cancer frequency in 2020. The global incidence rate in women is 10.1 per 100,000 individuals, which is three times higher than the rate observed in men [1, 2]. Papillary THCA, the most common subtype of THCA, has experienced a significant rise in its incidence [3, 4]. The current standard of care for THCA involves surgical resection, often supplemented with radiotherapy and chemotherapy [5]. Although most patients with THCA have favorable prognosis, a subset with advanced disease may experience increased morbidity and mortality [6]. THCA progresses insidiously, with a slow and often difficult-to-detect onset, making early detection and intervention crucial [7]. Despite advances in treatment, the mechanisms driving THCA metastasis remain elusive. As a result, further research is essential to uncover the pathways involved in metastasis and to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a process in which cells transition transitions from an epithelial to a mesenchymal phenotype, resulting in enhanced migratory and invasive properties [8]. In the context of cancer progression and metastasis, aberrant EMT activation is believed to contribute significantly to tumor cell migration and invasion, as well as potentially increasing resistance to multiple therapeutic modalities [9]. Despite its importance, the regulatory mechanism of EMT in THCA are still not fully understood, necessitating further research in this area.

Phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome 10 (PTEN) acts as a tumor suppressor and plays a crucial role in negatively regulating the PI3K signaling pathway [10]. Research has shown that PTEN suppresses the proliferation of THCA cells [11]. Silencing of miR-144-3p has been linked to increased PTEN expression, which in turn downregulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, inhibiting cell proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis of THCA cells [12]. Although PTEN has been shown to inhibit EMT, the exact mechanisms by which it impacts EMT and metastasis are not fully understood [13]. PTEN expression is notably reduced in THCA [14, 15], and its role in regulating EMT in THCA has also been documented [16, 17]. However, further research is needed to clarify how PTEN influences EMT in the context of THCA.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a key regulator of EMT in various cancers, with Serpin Family H Member 1 identified as a modulator of EMT and the migratory capabilities of gastric cancer cells through this pathway [18, 19]. A prior study revealed that lncRNA ABCC6P1 promotes the the proliferative, migratory and EMT properties of papillary thyroid carcinoma cells via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [20]. Furthermore, recent research has shown that PTEN can suppress EMT, metastasis and chemoresistance in bladder cancer cells by inhibiting the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway [21]. However, the potential of PTEN to inhibit EMT, invasion, and metastasis in THCA via the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway remains uncertain. Thus, our investigation focuses on the role of PTEN in EMT and invasion and metastasis of THCA cells through the modulation of the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for better understanding the metastatic processes in THCA.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

To analyze PTEN gene expression differences between THCA tumor tissues and normal tissues, we employed the bioinformatics database GEPIA and ENCORI. Using GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), which integrates data from TCGA and GTEX, we searched for “PTEN”, selected “Expression DIY” “Boxplot”, and specified “THCA” as the cancer type. Log2 (TPM + 1) was used to quantify gene expression. After generating the plot, we downloaded the image showing the analysis results. This analysis included 512 THCA tumor samples and 337 normal samples. Additionally, we accessed ENCORI (https://rnasysu.com/encori/), a web platform that also utilizes TCGA data. We selected “Pan-Cancer”, followed by “Gene Differential Expression”, and entered “PTEN (ENSG00000171862)”, and specified “THCA” as the cancer type. We then downloaded the analysis images based on a dataset comprising 510 THCA tumor samples and 58 normal samples.

Cell culture

Normal human thyroid follicular epithelial cells Nthy-ori 3–1 (CL-0817) and human THCA cell lines B-CPAP (CL-0575), FTC-133 (CL-0644) and TPC-1 (CL-0643) were provided by Procell (Wuhan, Hubei, China). Cell line authentication was performed using short tandem repeat profiling. Nthy-ori 3–1 and B-CPAP cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, FTC-133 cells in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM)/F12 medium, and TPC-1 cells in DMEM. All cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin. Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA) supplied the culture media and reagents.

Cell grouping and treatment

TPC-1 cells at the logarithmic growth stage were seeded in 96-well plates (6 × 103 cells/well). LipofectamineTM2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to transfect the cells when they reached 70–80% confluence. As previously described [22], 100 ng/well PTEN overexpression plasmid pcDNA3.1-PTEN (oe-PTEN) and negative control empty plasmid pcDNA3.1-NC (oe-NC) designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Jiangsu, China) were transfected into the TPC-1 cells. The experiment involved the following groups: [1] control group, cells cultured in DMEM medium for 72 h; [2] oe-NC group and the oe-PTEN group: cells were transfected with oe-PTEN or negative control empty plasmid oe-NC. After transfection for 48 h, cells were cultured for 72 h in DMEM medium; [3] oe-PTEN + Vehicle and oe-PTEN + BML-284 groups: cells were transfected with oe-PTEN. After 48-h of oe-PTEN transfection, as previously described [22], TPC-1 cells were treated with 0.5 μM Wnt activator BML-284 (HY-19987, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) or an equivalent amount of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 24 h, followed by culturing for an additional 72 h.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Nthy-ori 3–1, B-CPAP, FTC-133 cells or TPC-1 cells subjected to different treatments were collected for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using the Total RNA kit (R1200, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The RNA concentration and purity were determined using a Nanodrop 2000 ultra-micro spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Subsequently, the isolated RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using a reverse transcription kit (LM-63826, LMAI Bio, Shanghai, China). RT-qPCR was conducted on the Quantstudio™DX system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA) using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan) to measure PTEN mRNA levels. The program included an initial pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 180 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 58 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. PTEN expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the internal control. Cell counting kit (CCK)-8 assay

The cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 kit (YT8193, YITABIO, Beijing, China). Briefly, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well of a 96-well plate at specific time points (0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h). The cells were then incubated for 2 h at 37 ℃. Following incubation, absorbance values at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) to evaluate the viability.

Flow cytometry detection of apoptosis

The apoptosis of TPC-1 cells was evaluated using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (HY-K1073, MCE). After treatment, TPC-1 cells were harvested, and 5 μL of propidium reagent along with 10 μL of Annexin V-FITC reagent were added to each sample. The cells were mixed gently to ensure thorough reagent distribution and then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. After washing with PBS, the apoptosis rate was determined using a FACSCanto II flow cytometer and the software FlowJo V10.7.1 (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Transwell assay

To evaluate the invasive and migratory abilities of TPC-1 cells, the Transwell assay was conducted. Transwell chambers (Corning, CA, USA) were positioned in 24-well plates, where TPC-1 cells (1 × 104) were seeded in the apical chamber of the Transwell plate that was coated with Matrigel (Corning) to assess invasion, Matrigel was omitted, and all other conditions remained the same. Complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the basolateral chamber. After 24 h of culture, the invaded or migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (R20755, Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. The cells were then observed and photographed under a microscope (Leica Microystems, Weztlar, Germany). The number of cells in 5 randomly chosen, non-overlapping fields was counted, and the results were averaged for analysis.

Scratch assay

The wound-healing capacity of TPC-1 cells was assessed using a standardised scratch assay. TPC-1 cells subjected to different treatments were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. The cells were cultured until they reached over 90% confluence. A uniform and flat wound was created by scratching the plate with a 200 μL sterile pipette tip. The cells were then allowed to continue culturing for 24 h. Subsequently, cell migration into the wound area was observed and photographed under a microscope (Leica) at 0 and 24 h. The wound-healing rate (%) was calculated using Image Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA).

Western blot assay

Cells Nthy-ori 3–1, B-CPAP, FTC-133 cells and TPC-1 treated with different conditions were harvested for protein extraction. Total cellular protein was obtained using radio-immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (R0100-100 mL, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA protein quantification kit (PA115-01, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). For analysis, 30 μg of protein per well was loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The proteins were then electrotransferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 2 h, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (all from Abcam, Cambridge, UK) anti-PTEN (ab32199), anti-AKT1 (ab227385), anti-AKT1(phospho T308) (p-AKT1, ab278565), anti-E-cadherin (ab40772), anti-N-cadherin (ab18203), anti-Vimentin (ab92547), anti-Wnt1 (ab15251), anti-β-catenin (ab16051), anti-c-myc (ab32072), and anti-GAPDH (ab181602). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G secondary antibodies (ab6721, Abcam) at room temperature for 30 min. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (R30199, Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology) and quantified using Image Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, USA), with GAPDH serving as the internal control.

Statistical analysis

All data were processed and analyzed using SPSS 21.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and graphical representations were created using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Cell experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To compare data among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance was employed, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. P values were calculated using a two-sided test, and differences were considered statistically significant at a significance level of P < 0.05.

The findings indicated that PTEN was downregulated across all THCA cell lines, with TPC-1 cells showing notably lower levels than B-CPAP and FTC-133 cells (Fig. 1C, D, all P < 0.05). Wnt activator partially reversed the inhibitory effect of PTEN overexpression on malignant behavior of THCA cells

Following PTEN overexpression, TPC-1 cells were treated with the Wnt activator BML-284 to reactivate the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway. The results demonstrated that BML-284 treatment elevated the protein levels of β-Catenin and c-myc in TPC-1 cells, indicating activation of the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway (Fig. 5A, P < 0.01). Moreover, BML-284 treatment enhanced TPC-1 cell viability and decreased apoptosis (Fig. 5B, C, both P < 0.05). It also resulted in downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin, promoting EMT (Fig. 5D, P < 0.05). Simultaneously, TPC-1 cells exhibited partial reversal in migratory, invasive and wound-healing abilities (Fig. 5E, F, both P < 0.05). Overall, these findings reveal that the Wnt activator can partially counteract the suppressive effects of PTEN overexpression on the malignant behavior of THCA cells. Discussion

THCA ranks among the most prevalent cancers in women in the United States, and its incidence continues to increase globally [23]. An estimated 10–20% of THCA patients experience poor survival outcomes, exhibiting characteristics such as recurrence, drug resistance, distant metastasis and extraglandular invasion [24]. Surgery remains the standard treatment for THCA; however, there is a notable lack of specific drug targets for therapy [25]. Consequently, research into the molecular mechanisms of THCA is critical for enhancing our understanding of its pathogenesis. Against this backdrop, our study focuses on assessing the function of PTEN in THCA and investigating its underlying mechanisms. Our experimental results demonstrated that PTEN inhibited EMT and invasive and reduced the invasive and migratory potential of THCA cells by repressing the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

EMT is a fundamental biological process that plays a critical role in promoting the invasive and migratory characteristics of tumors [26]. In EMT, the downregulation of E-cadherin and the upregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin drive the progression of THCA [27]. Studies have shown that microRNAs are key regulators of THCA cell migration and invasion through the modulation of EMT [28], and EMT regulation is closely linked to THCA metastasis [29]. PTEN dysregulation is involved in the progression of several malignancies, including THCA [30]. Shi et al. reported that PTEN is underexpressed in THCA cells, and its overexpression inhibits cell proliferation while promoting apoptosis [11]. Moreover, EMT related transcription factors regulate PTEN expression, which in turn coordinates EMT and cancer metastasis by modulating various signaling pathways [13]. It has been documented that PTEN coexpression can reverse the EMT process in THCA [17]. PTEN negatively regulates the PI3K/AKT pathway by dephosphorylating the PI3K product 3,4,5-phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PIP3) into PIP2, and the activation of AKT further influences downstream signaling molecules [31, 32]. Activation of the PI3K/AKT, which is negatively regulated by PTEN, promotes EMT and metastasis in THCA [16]. Modulating the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway by targeting PTEN effectively controls THCA cell proliferation, invasion, and EMT [12]. In our study, we explored the role of PTEN in THCA and its effects on genes related to cell proliferation, EMT, and apoptosis. Our findings confirmed that PTEN expression was significantly reduced in THCA cells. Furthermore, our cellular experiments demonstrated that overexpression of PTEN inhibited THCA cell proliferation and EMT, promoted apoptosis, and limited invasion and migration.

The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a highly conserved signaling cascade that plays an essential role in EMT and cell migration [33]. In colorectal cancer, TM4SF1 inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been found to trigger EMT and enhance cancer stemness [34]. Liu et al. reported that activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway induces EMT, promotes invasiveness and accelerates metastasis in THCA cells [35]. Moreover, research has shown that PTEN suppression activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby regulating the proliferation and apoptosis of lung cancer cells [36]. Despite this, this link between PTEN and the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway in THCA cells has not been thoroughly explored. Our study, consistent with previous research, confirmed that PTEN overexpression suppressed Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway activation. Additionally, activation of the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway partially reversed the inhibitory effects of PTEN overexpression on the malignant behavior of THCA cells.

PTEN is frequently either partially or completely inactivated in a range of cancers, including THCA, liver cancer, and osteosarcoma [10, 11, 13]. PTEN inhibits the PI3K pathways by suppressing membrane recruitment of AKT and its activation, which in tuner curbs cell proliferation, growth and survival. In numerous cancers, mutations or deletions of PTEN expression lead to overactivation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby promoting tumor progression. This positions for PTEN gene as a valuable target for potential cancer therapies [37, 38]. Restoring the tumor-suppressive function of PTEN through technologies like gene editing or gene transfer represents a promising direction for cancer therapy, though it remains in the experimental research phase. For instance, transfection of PTEN-overexpressing lentivirus into non-small cell lung cancer cells has been shown to suppress cell growth, migration, and invasion, and inhibit tumor growth in nude mice [39]. Our study demonstrated that transfecting PTEN-overexpressing plasmid into THCA cells inhibited the proliferation and EMT of TPC-1 cells and enhanced apoptosis, ultimately hindering tumor cell growth and metastasis. Furthermore, the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, which is downstream of PTEN, is currently a key target in cancer therapies. Patients with PTEN deficiencies may benefit from treatments targeting this pathway, such as PI3K or mTOR inhibitors, although tumor resistance continues to challenge the broader application of these treatments [40–42]. Overall, PTEN holds great promise for cancer therapy, but its clinical applications must be firmly based on scientific research findings and undergo extensive clinical trials. With further insights into PTEN and its related pathways, future cancer therapies could become more targeted and effective.

In summary, our findings indicate that PTEN inhibits EMT and reduces the proliferative, migrating, and invasive capacities of THCA cells by suppressing the activation of the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway. This study offers a new potential target for THCA treatment and provides a theoretical basis for further investigation into the mechanisms underlying EMT and metastasis in THCA. Nevertheless, this research is limited by its reliance on a single THCA cell line, and we did not conduct in vivo validation or explore the detailed mechanisms of PTEN’s regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Moving forward, we aim to investigate additional THCA cells and perform in vivo studies to substantiate and deepen our understanding of PTEN’s regulatory function and the specific mechanisms involved in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.
