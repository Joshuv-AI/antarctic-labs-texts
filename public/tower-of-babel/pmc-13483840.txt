Multifactorial effects of LGALS1 blockade sensitize tumors to immune checkpoint inhibitor
Authors: Jessy John, Mohammad A I Al-Hatamleh, Monika Vashisht, Arpitha H Shivarudrappa, Huaibin Ge, Karen Siddoway, Anthony J Bragoli, Priya Singh, Mohamed Y Zaky, Dan P Zandberg, Qiyiwen Zhang, Mathias Danielsen, Marija Ciba, Tomáš Lášek, Balazs Schafer, Marvin H Caruthers, Yuefeng Gao, Xuedong Liu, Zhangguo Chen, Jing H Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) accounts for 90% of all head and neck cancers. While immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved HNSCC outcomes, response rates remain low (10–20%). Identifying novel targets to sensitize HNSCC to ICI therapy is therefore a critical unmet clinical need.MethodsTo identify potential new targets, we analyzed human HNSCC clinical samples and mouse HNSCC models. We evaluated blood-derived samples from ICI-treated HNSCC patients and tumor-bearing mice (responders vs. non-responders to anti-PD-L1 treatment). We tested the efficacy of combined LGALS1 inhibition and anti-PD-L1 therapy, characterizing immune profiles via flow cytometry. Additionally, we employed mechanistic assays to determine the effects of LGALS1 blockade on myeloid cell differentiation and CD8 T cell activation.ResultsWe showed that LGALS1 is upregulated in HNSCC in a subtype- and stage-dependent manner. Contrary to prior findings focusing on extracellular LGALS1, our study reveals that LGALS1 expression within SCC tumor cells, tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, and activated CD8 T cells is predominantly intracellular. In mouse models, lower intracellular LGALS1 in myeloid cells and higher plasma LGALS1 levels correlated with anti-PD-L1 unresponsiveness; consistently, elevated serum LGALS1 predicted poor survival in ICI-treated HNSCC patients. Pharmacological LGALS1 inhibition sensitized tumors to anti-PD-L1 and markedly prolonged recipient survival. Notably, LGALS1 inhibition did not alter expression levels; instead, its efficacy depended on tumor-intrinsic and tumor-extrinsic mechanisms. The combination of LGALS1 blockade and anti-PD-L1 effectively remodeled the myeloid compartment, significantly decreasing the frequency of total CD11b+ cells and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) while shifting the remaining TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype. These changes, coupled with enhanced CD8 and CD4 T cell infiltration and effector function, promoted a less immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In vitro co-culture assays demonstrated that LGALS1 inhibition shifted TAM polarization toward an M1-like phenotype. Furthermore, LGALS1 blockade directly activated CD8 T cells via a STAT3-dependent mechanism.ConclusionsWe conclude that LGALS1 possesses significant prognostic value for predicting ICI response in HNSCC. LGALS1 may represent a multimodal therapeutic target to sensitize tumors to immunotherapy, as its blockade simultaneously modulates tumor cells, myeloid cells, and CD8 T cells to overcome multi-layered resistance and promote robust anti-tumor immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40164-026-00784-2.Keywords: LGALS1, Myeloid cells, Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, CD8 T cells, OTX-008

Background

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is the predominant subtype of head and neck cancer with common primary sites of oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx and hypopharynx. Risk factors for HNSCC include human papilloma virus (HPV) in the oropharynx, and tobacco and alcohol usage [1]. While the 5-year survival rate of primary HNSCC is about 55–66% [2], there is a great unmet need for improving outcomes in the recurrent/metastatic (R/M) setting where 5-year survival is only 5–15% [3]. HPV+ oropharyngeal SCC has a better prognosis, especially in the curative intent setting; however, systemic therapy is also palliative for HPV+ patients in R/M setting [3]. Thus, it is critically important to develop new strategies to improve outcomes for HNSCC patients. Anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapy is now standard-of-care in R/M HNSCC, first with the approval of nivolumab and pembrolizumab monotherapy in the platinum failure setting, and more recently with pembrolizumab monotherapy for PD-L1 expressing patients or chemotherapy plus pembrolizumab for all patients in the frontline setting [4–7]. However, the response rate with anti-PD-1 monotherapy is only between 16 and 20% [4–8]. Multiple subsequent phase III trials of combination therapy with anti-PD-1 have not generated positive outcomes in the R/M frontline setting [9–11]. Thus, there is a significant unmet need to develop new combination therapies [6].

HNSCC is highly enriched in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in both human samples and mouse models including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [12]. A higher level of TAMs correlated with lymph node metastasis and advanced stage of HNSCCs [13–17]. We found that tumor volume was reduced when macrophages were depleted by treating Smad4−/− SCC tumor-bearing mice with clodronate [18]. Strategies for targeting TAMs (via inhibition or reprogramming) in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in ongoing clinical trials have shown promising results in other cancer types [15, 19–22]. Phase 1/1b Macrophage Reprogramming in Immuno-Oncology-1 (MARIO-1) trial tested Eganelisib, a selective PI3K-γ inhibitor targeting macrophages, and reported anti-tumor activity in combination with anti-PD-1 including patients who had progressed on ICI [23], leading to ongoing phase 2 trials (MARIO-3 and MARIO-8) including HNSCC. Apart from TAMs, myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSC) may play a pivotal role in predicting response to various cancer immunotherapies [24–29]. MDSCs are present in peripheral blood, lymphoid tissues and HNSCC tumors [30–32]. MDSCs can be further divided into polymorphonuclear MDSC (PMN-MDSC) and monocytic MDSC (M-MDSC) [33–35]. Therapies selectively inhibiting or reprogramming MDSCs may provide a functional remedy to combat HNSCCs with a high density of MDSCs.

LGALS1 (also called Galectin-1) is a ubiquitously expressed small protein (14 kDa) with a binding site for β-galactoside. LGALS1 expression is upregulated in tumor tissues, associated with tumor progression, metastasis, and immune evasion [36, 37]. LGALS1 can be expressed on cell surface, probably via binding to surface proteins containing β-galactoside, or intracellularly, and secreted out extracellularly with its release as a common feature of inflammatory cell death [38]. LGALS1 is implicated in modulating cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, inducing T cell apoptosis, and promoting inflammation [36]. However, the effects of LGALS1 on T cells are complex, and the exact mechanisms by which LGALS1 induces T cell apoptosis remain controversial and incompletely understood [36]. A higher level of LGALS1 expression significantly correlated with worse prognosis in HNSCC (TCGA dataset) [39]. Prior studies examined the effects of LGALS1 blockade using anti-Galectin-1 Ab on inhibiting HNSCC growth [39] and concluded that LGALS1 blockade mediated tumor inhibitory effects by reprogramming endothelial cells [39]. OTX-008 is a small molecule inhibitor of LGALS1 and was tested as an anti-cancer agent in oral SCC (OSCC), with one study showing OTX-008 induced tumor vessel normalization and inhibited tumor growth moderately [40] and another one showing OTX-008’s effects on OSCC cell lines in vitro [41]. However, OTX-008 has not been tested for combinatorial treatment with ICI in HNSCC.

In the current study, we showed that LGALS1 is abundantly expressed in mouse SCC tumor cells and highly enriched in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in both human and mouse HNSCC. Combined treatment of LGALS1 inhibitor (OTX-008) and anti-PD-L1 suppressed tumor growth more significantly compared to single agent and substantially prolonged recipient survival. We investigated how the combination of OTX-008 and anti-PD-L1 therapy led to better efficacy, evaluated the prognostic value of LGALS1 level in predicting or correlating responses to the combination treatment in mouse HNSCC models and validated our results using ICI-treated HNSCC patient samples. Our study may lay a scientific foundation for designing more effective therapy in clinical settings for R/M HNSCC and helping patient stratification to maximize the therapeutic effects.

Material and methods

In vivo mouse work and tumor injection

A223 tumor cell line was described previously [42]. A1419 [43] and A1206 [44] SCC cells were derived from spontaneous 4-NQO-driven tongue carcinomas. Tumor cells (1 × 105 A223 or 1 × 106 A1419 or A1206) were injected into wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 (B6) mice (Stock no. 000664, Jackson Laboratories). Both male and female mice (6–8 weeks) were used for the study and results were reported for both sexes. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the vivarium facility of University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (Aurora, CO) or in the UPMC Hillman Cancer Center Animal Facility (Pittsburgh, PA). Animal work was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (AMC) (Aurora, CO) and University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, PA).

Tumor cells were cultured in complete DMEM media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 20 mM HEPES buffer, 1 × antibiotic–antimycotic at 37ºC CO2 incubator (5%) until 90% confluent. For tumor injection, cells were washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), trypsinized (0.01% Trypsin–EDTA, Fisher Scientific) and washed sequentially with complete DMEM media or PBS. Cells were resuspended in 1:1 PBS/Matrigel Basement Membrane Matrix (Corning) to a final volume of 100µL and injected subcutaneously into one flank of each mouse. Tumor length and width were measured with calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width2)/2. Anti-PD-L1 (200 µg/mouse/time, clone 10F.9G2, Leinco Technologies, Catalog#P371) diluted in PBS was given by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection 3 times (2-dayinterval). To assess treatment effects, relative change in tumor volume (RCTV) was calculated as the change in tumor volume (TV) from the start of treatment (TV0) to the TV at day n (the endpoint of control group) (TVn) divided by TV0 (RCTV = [TVn − TV0]/TV0). Based on the RCTV, anti-PD-L1 treated recipients were divided into R (RCTV < 0), SP (0 < RCTV ≤ 1.5) and NR (RCTV > 1.5). Recipient survival was monitored until mice reached experimental endpoints including severe tumor ulceration, tumor size > 2 cm in any dimension or other humane end points. Mice were euthanized in accordance with institutional guidelines.

OTX-008 (MedChemExpress, Cat. No#HY-19756) was administered by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection (5 mg/kg/mouse/time). The compound was initially dissolved in 100% ethanol and subsequently diluted in PBS to a final vehicle composition of 3% ethanol and 97% PBS. Treatments were administered seven times at 2-day intervals. The OTX-008 dosage and schedule were based on established preclinical models demonstrating effective LGALS1 inhibition [45–47]. The Human Equivalent Dose (HED) was calculated using standard FDA-recommended scaling factors (HED = Mouse Dose × 3/37). The mouse dose of 5 mg/kg corresponds to a human dose of approximately 0.4 mg/kg, well within the range of doses evaluated and found to be safe in Phase I clinical trials for OTX-008 (up to 450 mg/m2).

Flow cytometry of tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Tumors were minced with razor blades and dissociated with 50 µg/ml Liberase DL (Sigma-Aldrich) in plain DMEM for 30 min at 37 ºC. Digested tumors were mashed through 70 µm filters, washed with 1 × PBS (2% FBS), and cells were RBC lysed using RBC lysing buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) and filtered with cell strainers to prepare single-cell suspensions. Single-cell suspensions were immediately stained with flow cytometry Ab or used for ex vivo stimulation followed by Ab staining. For ex vivo stimulation, cells were cultured in 12-well plates (2 − 5 × 106 cells/sample/well) for 4 h at 37 ºC in the presence of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (40 nM) and Ionomycin (650 nM) (LC Laboratories), and Brefeldin A (BFA) Solution (1 ×) (BD Biosciences, Catalog#347,688) in DMEM complete media with β-mercaptoethanol (100 µM). Stimulated cells were harvested, washed, and stained as follows. Dead cells were excluded by LIVE/DEAD (1:1000) fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain (Invitrogen) followed by surface Ab staining, fixation/permeabilization (BD Fixation/Permeabilization kit), and intracellular Ab staining. TruStain FcX™ (anti-mouse CD16/32) Ab (BioLegend) and Brilliant Stain Buffer Plus (BD Horizon) were added into each flow panel mixture according to manufacturer’s instructions. Abs used for flow cytometry are listed in Supplemental Table 1. Data were acquired on BD LSRFortessa X-20 cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo™ software V10 (FLOWJO).

Mouse CD45+ scRNA-seq, human scRNA-seq data analysis and TCGA data analysis

Tumors from 3 R and 3 NR mice were harvested on day 18 after tumor inoculation and single-cell suspensions were prepared as described above. CD45+ immune cells were isolated using EasySep™ Mouse CD45 Positive Selection Kit (StemCell Technologies, Catalog#18,945) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD45+ immune cell samples were submitted to the University of Colorado AMC Genomics and Microarray Core for single-cell capture and library preparation. Cells were loaded into a 10 × Genomics Single-cell Chip G for the 3’ capture. Single-cell gene expression libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits (v3.1: Dual Index Libraries) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform for an estimated read depth of 50,000 reads/cell. After sequencing, RNA-seq reads were mapped to mm10 (mouse reference genome) using 10 × Genomics CellRanger (version 4.0.0) count pipeline. The single gene expression analysis was performed using Seurat version 4.0.2 [48]. From each sample, low quality cells (cells with < 500 features detected or > 10% mitochondrial RNA content) and the presumed doublets (outlier cells in the scatter plot between the number of genes detected in each cell (nFeature_RNA) and the total number of molecules per cell (nCount_RNA)) were removed from further analysis. The samples were processed using the following functions: NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData, CellCycleScoring, ScaleData as described previously [49]. Dimensionality reduction was performed by running principal component analysis with RunPCA function and integrated variable features were then used to cluster and visualize all cells by UMAP analysis with RunUMAP, FindNeighbors, and FindClusters functions on the first 30 principal components. FindMarkers function was used to identify DEG in each cluster and individual clusters were then named based on the highly expressed genes in each cluster. UMAPs, bar graphs and violin plots were created with R packages. The expression of canonical and cluster-defining markers for each identified population is provided in Supplemental Fig. 2A, B and was used for cell type annotation.

Two previously published datasets [50, 51] were analyzed for LGALS1 expression in human samples which include 18 HPV− and 11 HPV+ HNSCC patients’ tumor samples (sorted CD45+ immune cells) by using Seurat (version 4.3.0) in R (version 4.2.0). Quality control was performed to filter out low quality cells for each sample separately. Low quality cells are defined as: (1) the number of counts per barcode (nCount) is above or below 5 median absolute deviations (MAD); (2) The number of genes per barcode (nFeature) is above or below 5 MAD; (3) the percentage of counts from mitochondrial genes per barcode is above 10 percent; (4) the percentage of counts from ribosomal genes per barcode is above 60 percent; (5) the percentage of counts from hemoglobin genes per barcode is above 0.1 percent. Then we run through standard workflow of Seurat (v4), including normalization, choosing top 2000 variable features, scaling and applying dimensional reduction by using PCA, and finally applied Harmony to integrate all samples to eliminate batch effect. Top 15 principal components (PC) were used for clustering and UMAP visualization. Clusters were annotated using SingleR with reference data HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData and BluePrintEncodeData.

TCGA datasets from HNSCC (n = 518) or breast cancer patients (n = 1070) in the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) were analyzed for the relationship between LGALS1 expression and overall survival of patients. Kaplan–Meier survival plots were generated using the GEPIA online tool. The RNA expression profile of LGALS1 in HNSCC patients with different stages was examined using UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html) database.

Western blotting

Cells were harvested and lysed with lysis buffer [Tris-base (50 mM, pH 7.5), EDTA (1 mM), NaCl (150 mM), Sodium orthovanadate (2 mM), Sodium Fluoride (4 mM), Triton-X100 (1%), SDS (0.1%), and Sodium deoxycholate (0.5%)], incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min to collect supernatant. Protein concentration was quantified using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Catalog#23,228, Thermo Scientific). The cellular protein (30 μg) was loaded onto a 12% gel and separated by SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The separated proteins on SDS-PAGE were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Catalog#1,620,112, Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked with 6% dried skimmed milk and subsequently probed with specific primary Abs followed by HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary Abs (Supplemental Table 1). Protein bands were visualized using ECL Plus (Cytiva) on the G:Box Chemi-XX6 platform (Syngene, Frederick, MD).

LGALS1 ELISA, collection of patient serum samples and mouse samples

Human serum samples were collected according to institutional clinical protocols. For murine studies, blood was collected in EDTA-coated tubes to facilitate concurrent flow cytometric analysis of circulating leukocytes. Serum samples of HNSCC patients were kindly prepared and provided by UPMC Hillman Cancer Center HNC tissue bank (IRB protocol# HCC 99–069). Pre-treatment blood specimens were collected in a de-identified manner from HNSCC patients (n = 41) treated with anti-PD-1 (Nivolumab or Pembrolizumab) with clinical data including baseline characteristics and overall survival (OS). Whole blood samples from cheek bleed of tumor-bearing mice before and after anti-PD-L1 treatment were collected in BD Microtainer tubes containing dipotassium EDTA (catalog#365,974) and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Mouse plasma was diluted and the LGALS1 concentration was measured using the mouse ELISA kit (Catalog#DY1245, R&D Systems). LGALS1 levels in human serum samples were measured using human LGALS1 ELISA kit (Catalog#DGAL10, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

LGALS1 KO in A223 tumor cells and cell proliferation assay

Stable A223 LGALS1-KO cells were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 approach with LGALS1-KO Plasmid (m2) (Catalog#sc-421415-KO-2, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies). A223 cells were transfected with LGALS1-KO Plasmid containing a GFP reporter by Lipofectamine 3000 (Catalog#L3000008, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The GFP-positive cells were sorted with a flow sorter (Beckman Coulter MoFlo Astrios High Speed Sorter) into 96 well plates (1 cell/well) and cultured to establish single clones of LGALS1-KO cells. LGALS1 deletion was validated by Western blotting.

The proliferation of parental A223 and LGALS1 KO (clone A8 and B71) tumor cells was examined using CellTrace™ Violet Cell Proliferation Kit (Cat.#C34557, Thermo Fischer Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were trypsinized and washed with PBS. Subsequently, 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 5 µM CellTrace Violet in PBS, incubated in the dark at room temperature (RT) for 20 min, neutralized by adding culture medium, and followed by 5 min incubation at RT in the dark. Labeled cells were washed with medium and 4 × 104 cells were seeded onto the culture plates (12 well) in triplicates. Cultured cells were harvested on day 0, day 1, day 2, and day 3, stained with LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Green Dead Cell Stain Kit (Cat. No. L23101, Invitrogen) for live/dead cells, and stored at 4 °C in 1% paraformaldehyde until flow cytometry analysis. All samples collected on specified days were run on BD Fortessa on day 3 of cell culture. Data were analyzed using FlowJoTM software (FLOWJO, Oregon, USA).

Co-culture of BM and tumor cells

Tumor cells were cultured as described above. BM or CD8 T cells were maintained in complete RPMI media containing 10% FBS, 10 mM HEPES buffer (100 × , Catalog 25-060-CI), MEM Nonessential Amino Acid (100 × , Catalog 25-020-CI), 1 mM Sodium pyruvate (100 × , Catalog 25-000-CI) from Mediatech Inc., 2 mM L-glutamine (100 × , Catalog 25,030,081) and Gibco™ Antibiotic–Antimycotic (100 × , Catalog 15,240,062) from Life Science Corporation, and 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Catalog M3148-100ML, Sigma). Cells were cultured at 37ºC CO2 incubator (5%).

BM cells were harvested from WT B6 mice using a 25-gauge needle and syringe as previously described [52]. The collected BM cells were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer and subjected to RBC lysis using RBC lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich). Following RBC lysis, BM cells (1 × 106) were co-cultured with either A223 or A1419C tumor cells (2.5 × 104) in 24-well plates. A223 and A1419C tumor cells were separately pre-seeded in complete DMEM in 24-well plates 24 h prior to BM collection. The tumor culture supernatant was removed, and BM cells in 1 mL of complete RPMI media were added (Day 0), with or without 10 μM of OTX-008 (first dose; Catalog HY-19756, MedChemExpress). BM cells cultured in complete RPMI media alone served as the control group. After 48 h (Day 2), the supernatant of the co-culture was gently removed, and 1 mL of fresh RPMI complete media was added, again with or without 10 μM of OTX-008 (second dose). Co-cultured cells were then harvested and stained for myeloid cell populations as previously described [53, 54] on Day 4. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD LSRFortessa™ (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo™ software version 10 (FlowJo LLC).

Phagocytosis assay

To assess the phagocytic activity of TAMs, 20 µg/mL of Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Zymosan A (S. cerevisiae) BioParticles (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the co-culture of BM and A223 tumor cells 2 h prior to collection on Day 4. Following incubation, cells were processed according to the surface antibody (Ab) staining protocol described above for flow cytometry. For this specific assay, CD206 was included as a surface marker to differentiate macrophage subsets.

CD8 T cell isolation and culture

CD8 T cells were isolated from WT B6 spleen with the EasySep™ Mouse CD8 T Cell Isolation Kit (Catalog#9853; STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD8 T cells were cultured for 72 h (0.5 × 106 cells/mL) in complete RPMI medium supplemented with human interleukin-2 (hIL-2; 33 IU/mL) in 24-well plates. For activation, cells were stimulated using Gibco Dynabeads™ Mouse T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Catalog#11452D; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a bead-to-cell ratio of 1:4. Cells were treated with either 20 μM OTX-008, STAT3-specific ASOs (MBD40 knocking down STAT3 or MBD9 converting STAT3α to STAT3β) or both. The thiomorpholino oligonucleotides (MBD9 and MBD40) were prepared using the chemical procedures as outlined previously in the laboratory of Marvin Caruthers [55]. Three days after stimulation, activation beads were removed, and cells were washed and subsequently stained for viability using the Invitrogen Live/Dead™ (1:1000) Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Catalog#L34966; Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by surface marker and intracellular cytokine staining. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated 4 h prior to collection with a cocktail containing PMA (40 nM), ionomycin (650 nM) and BFA (1 ×) as described above. For apoptosis detection, 1 μL of NucView® Caspase-3 Substrates (Catalog#10,402; Biotium) was added to each well containing 1 mL of cultured CD8 T cells (0.5 × 106 cells/mL), with or without 10 μM OTX-008. Flow cytometry was performed on a BD LSRFortessa™ (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo™ software version 10 (FlowJo LLC).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was calculated with unpaired t test or one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software).

Results

Higher LGALS1 transcript levels in non-responder (NR) tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells

To investigate differential responses to anti-PD-L1, we employed a previously characterized Smad4−/− SCC model (termed A223) [42, 56, 57]. WT B6 mice were injected with A223 cells; once tumors reached ~ 300 mm3, mice were randomized and treated with either PBS or anti-PD-L1. Upon treatment, recipients diverged into responders (R) and non-responders (NR) based on relative change in tumor volume (RCTV) (Supplemental Fig. 1) [58]. To delineate the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), we performed scRNA-seq on CD45+ immune cells purified from R and NR tumors (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised clustering of scRNA-seq data identified 16 distinct clusters based on the expression of representative genes (Fig. 1B, Supplemental Fig. 2). While R samples were enriched in T and B cells, NR tumors were predominantly composed of myeloid clusters (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that Lgals1 was significantly upregulated in NR myeloid cells (Fig. 1C). We prioritized LGALS1 because TCGA data indicated that high LGALS1 expression correlates with poor prognosis specifically in HNSCC (Fig. 1D, left) [39], but not in other malignancies such as breast cancer (Fig. 1D, right). Multivariable survival analyses, adjusting for age, sex, and race, demonstrated that LGALS1 was significantly associated with overall survival (OS; p = 0.010; Supplemental Fig. 4A). Model-based adjusted survival curves consistently showed that lower LGALS1 expression correlated with a higher probability of survival (Supplemental Fig. 4B). Furthermore, time-dependent AUC analyses at 2 and 3 years revealed that including LGALS1 in the baseline model improved predictive accuracy (Supplemental Fig. 4C). These data suggest a unique role for LGALS1 in HNSCC pathogenesis and immunotherapy resistance.

LGALS1 expression pattern in mouse model and human samples

Analysis of our mouse scRNA-seq data identified myeloid cells as the primary immune subset expressing LGALS1 (Fig. 1E), with higher levels in NR tumors (Supplemental Figs. 5 and 6). This was confirmed by real-time PCR in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells from NR versus (vs.) R tumors (Supplemental Fig. 7). While activated T and NK cells also expressed LGALS1, high expression was noted in two non-immune clusters (Other-1 and Other-2) likely representing contaminating tumor cells (Fig. 1E). Consistent with this, Western blotting confirmed abundant LGALS1 expression in mouse SCC cell lines (Fig. 1F).

To validate these findings in humans, we analyzed two previously collected datasets [50, 51] including 18 HPV− and 11 HPV+ HNSCC tumor samples (sorted on CD45+ immune cells). We processed the scRNA-seq data using CellRanger and Seurat as described previously [49, 53, 58] and identified 10 different clusters (Supplemental Fig. 8A). LGALS1 expression was highly enriched in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (Supplemental Fig. 8B, C), specifically FCGR3A+ and CD14+ monocytes, as well as activated CD8 T cells (Fig. 1G). Although LGALS1 levels appeared higher in HPV– versus HPV+ tumors in some clusters, pseudobulk DEG analysis revealed no significant difference between the groups across any cell type (Supplemental Fig. 9). Finally, analysis of clinical databases confirmed that LGALS1 is significantly upregulated in HNSCC (Fig. 1H) in a stage- and subtype-dependent manner (Fig. 1I, Supplemental Fig. 10).

Correlation of LGALS1 level with the outcome of ICI therapy in mouse and human system

To test whether LGALS1 level correlates with the outcome of anti-PD-L1 treatment in the mouse model, we examined the expression level of LGALS1 in R (RCTV < 0), slow progressor (SP) (0 < RCTV ≤ 1.5) and NR (RCTV > 1.5) upon anti-PD-L1 treatment. LGALS1 exhibits a complex expression pattern as it can be secreted or expressed on cell surface or intracellularly. We analyzed intracellular LGALS1 expression in CD11b+ myeloid cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL). We collected PBMCs pre- and post-treatment (referred to as pre- and post-PBMC). TIL collection was restricted to the post-treatment timepoint because of limited tumor tissue in the mouse model. The percentage of CD11b+ cells expressing intracellular LGALS1 inversely correlated with RCTV value in both pre-PBMC (Fig. 2A, r = − 0.5513, p < 0.0001) and post-PBMC samples (Fig. 2B, r = − 0.7501, p < 0.0001). Notably, the percentage of CD11b+ cells expressing intracellular LGALS1 was significantly higher in R than in NR in both pre-PBMC (Fig. 2C, D) and post-PBMC samples (Fig. 2E, F). While intracellular LGALS1 decreased significantly in the post-treatment PBMCs of control and NR groups, levels remained stable in R and SP groups (Supplemental Fig. 11A).

Similar trends were observed in the TME; intracellular LGALS1 levels in CD11b+ tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells inversely correlated with RCTV (Fig. 2G, Supplemental Fig. 11B), with R showing significantly higher MFI compared to NR (Fig. 2H, I; Supplemental Fig. 11C). LGALS1 expression in CD45− cells (presumably tumor cells) did not correlate with anti-PD-L1 treatment outcomes (Supplemental Fig. 11D, E). The MFI of intracellular LGALS1 was significantly higher in R than NR CD8 TILs (Fig. 2J, K), but not in PBMCs (Supplemental Fig. 11F, G). Similar results were observed for CD4 TILs and PBMCs (Supplemental Fig. 11H–J).

We propose a “model of LGALS1 redistribution” to explain the inverse relationship between transcript and intracellular protein levels. In this model (Fig. 2L), myeloid cells from R mice retain LGALS1 intracellularly, whereas NR myeloid cells—despite higher transcript levels—may release the protein, thereby reducing the intracellular pool and increasing extracellular LGALS1 levels. Based on this model, we measured plasma LGALS1 levels pre- and post-treatment. Pre-treatment plasma LGALS1 significantly correlated with RCTV (Supplemental Fig. 11K), though no significant difference was observed between R and NR at baseline (Supplemental Fig. 11L). Consistently, post-treatment plasma LGALS1 strongly correlated with RCTV (r = 0.6524, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2M). Furthermore, post-treatment LGALS1 levels were significantly higher in control and NR groups compared to R and SP groups (Fig. 2N). Within each group, plasma LGALS1 increased post-treatment, with the NR group exhibiting the most significant elevation (Supplemental Fig. 11M). We conclude that favorable anti-PD-L1 responses correlate with higher intracellular LGALS1 in CD11b+ cells and lower plasma LGALS1 levels.

To determine if these findings translate to human patients, we measured pre-treatment serum LGALS1 levels in HNSCC patients treated with anti-PD-1 (Nivolumab or Pembrolizumab). Serum LGALS1 levels inversely correlated with overall survival (OS) in both the Nivolumab and Pembrolizumab cohorts (Fig. 2O, P). This correlation was further strengthened when the two treatment groups were combined (Fig. 2Q). Multivariable survival analyses, adjusting for age and sex, demonstrated that LGALS1 was significantly associated with OS (p = 0.031; Supplemental Fig. 12A). To further illustrate this, model-based adjusted survival curves evaluated at the 25th and 75th percentiles confirmed that lower LGALS1 levels consistently correlated with a higher probability of survival (Supplemental Fig. 12B). Furthermore, time-dependent AUC analyses at 2 and 3 years revealed that incorporating LGALS1 into the baseline model enhanced its predictive accuracy (Supplemental Fig. 12C). These data suggest that pre-treatment serum LGALS1 levels serve as a prognostic biomarker for OS in anti-PD-1-treated HNSCC patients.

Combined LGALS1 and PD-L1 blockade suppresses tumor growth and prolongs survival

To investigate the efficacy of dual LGALS1 and PD-L1 inhibition, we utilized OTX-008, a small molecule inhibitor that binds the allosteric site on the back-face β-sheet of LGALS1 [40, 59, 60]. We inoculated A223 tumors at the flank region of WT B6 mice. When tumor sizes reached ~ 250mm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomized into 4 groups and treated with PBS control, OTX-008 (i.p.), anti-PD-L1 (i.p.), or OTX-008 plus anti-PD-L1. RCTV was measured until the endpoint of experiments and recipient survival was monitored. Our data showed that the combination of OTX-008 and anti-PD-L1 suppressed tumor growth more effectively than monotherapy (Fig. 3A, B). Notably, the combination significantly prolonged survival, with > 60% of recipients remaining alive 200 days post-treatment (Fig. 3C). These results demonstrate that combining OTX-008 with anti-PD-L1 provides superior tumor control and long-term survival in the A223 model.

Relative contribution of tumor-derived and TIME-derived LGALS1 to therapy efficacy

LGALS1 can be expressed by both tumor cells and tumor-infiltrating immune cells (Fig. 1). However, it remains unclear whether blocking tumor- versus TIME-derived LGALS1 contributes to the efficacy of the combination treatment (Fig. 4A). To address this, we generated two independent LGALS1-KO A223 tumor clones (A8 and B71) using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. 4B). We detected no differences in the in vitro proliferation of WT and KO cells (Supplemental Fig. 13). Next, A8 or B71 LGALS1-KO tumor cells were injected into WT B6 mice. Deletion of tumor-derived LGALS1 significantly reduced tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 4C, D) and improved recipient survival compared to WT A223 tumors (Fig. 4E), suggesting an important role of blocking tumor-derived LGALS1.

To isolate the contribution of TIME-derived LGALS1, we injected LGALS1-KO A223 tumors (clone A8) into WT mice. Once tumors reached ~ 200 mm3, mice received anti-PD-L1 alone or the anti-PD-L1/OTX-008 combination. Combined treatment inhibited LGALS1-KO tumor growth more effectively than anti-PD-L1 alone (Fig. 4F, G), indicating that blocking TIME-derived LGALS1 provides additional therapeutic benefit. Thus, the efficacy of the combination treatment relies on the simultaneous inhibition of both tumor- and TIME-derived LGALS1.

A223 tumors are relatively sensitive to anti-PD-L1 treatment [58]. We next examined whether OTX-008 treatment could sensitize anti-PD-L1-resistant tumors to combo treatment. We utilized two SCC lines induced by a tobacco surrogate 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide (4-NQO), A1419 [43] and A1206 [44], which both express high levels of LGALS1 (Fig. 1F) but are non-responsive to anti-PD-L1 monotherapy (Supplemental Fig. 14A, B). We inoculated A1419 or A1206 tumor cells into WT B6 mice and treated tumor-bearing mice with control or anti-PD-L1/OTX-008. In both models, anti-PD-L1/OTX-008 combination therapy significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to controls (Supplemental Fig. 14C-F). Thus, OTX-008 treatment sensitized anti-PD-L1-resistant SCC tumors to the combination treatment of anti-PD-L1/OTX-008.

Combination therapy reduces immunosuppressive myeloid infiltration in the TIME

To investigate how combination treatment affects tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, we analyzed myeloid subsets in the TIME using flow cytometry. The percentage of the CD11b+ population within CD45+ hematopoietic cells was significantly lower in the combo group than in all other groups (Fig. 5A, B). By gating on the CD11b+ population using established gating strategies (Supplemental Fig. 15) [33, 61], we quantified M-MDSCs (CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chigh), PMN-MDSCs (CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clow) and the CD11b+Ly6C−Ly6G− population in different groups (Fig. 5C). We found that the percentage of M-MDSCs was significantly higher in combo or anti-PD-L1 than control or OTX-008 group, whereas the percentage of PMN-MDSCs was not statistically different across all groups (Fig. 5D).

OTX-008 modulates TAM differentiation without altering total LGALS1 expression

Given the complex interplay within the TIME, which complicates the in vivo dissection of specific cell populations, we employed an established co-culture system [53, 54] to evaluate the effect of OTX-008 on myeloid differentiation. In this system, bone-marrow (BM) cells containing myeloid precursors were co-cultured with tumor cells, which induced the differentiation of TAMs, M-MDSC and PMN-MDSC [53, 54]. OTX-008 treatment did not change the total percentage of TAMs in co-cultures with A223 tumors (Supplemental Fig. 16A–E). Instead, OTX-008 shifted TAM polarization, increasing M1-like TAMs (MHCII+CD206− or CD86+CD206−) and reducing M2-like TAMs (CD206+MHCII− or CD206+CD86−) (Supplemental Fig. 16F–I). Similar results were observed with A1419 tumor cells (Supplemental Fig. 17A–I).

We next utilized LGALS1-KO A223 cells to compare the effects of eliminating tumor-derived LGALS1 versus OTX-008 treatment, which blocks both tumor- and myeloid-derived LGALS1. WT or LGALS1-KO A223 tumors were co-cultured with BM progenitor cells from WT B6 mice in the absence or presence of OTX-008. The percentage of TAMs did not differ across all groups, demonstrating that OTX-008 treatment or deletion of LGALS1 in tumor cells did not affect the generation of TAMs from BM progenitors (Supplemental Fig. 18A, B). In contrast, the combination of LGALS1-KO A223 tumors and OTX-008 treatment resulted in the highest M1-like (MHCII+CD206− or CD86+CD206−) and lowest M2-like (CD206+MHCII− or CD206+CD86−) TAM percentages compared to all other groups (Fig. 6A–D).

Combination therapy increases infiltration and enhances effector functions of CD4 and CD8 TILs

To delineate the mechanisms underlying the efficacy of OTX-008 and anti-PD-L1, we examined T cell populations in the TIME (see gating strategy in Supplemental Fig. 21). Both CD4 and CD8 TIL percentages were significantly higher in the combo group compared to control or monotherapy groups (Fig. 8A, B). We next assessed the function of CD4 and CD8 TILs via cytokine production (Fig. 8C–F, Supplemental Fig. 22A-H). The percentage of IFNγ+TNFα+ CD8 TILs was significantly higher in the combo group than in all other groups (Fig. 8C, D). This population was also significantly higher in the anti-PD-L1 group compared to control or OTX-008 (Fig. 8C, D). Similarly, IFNγ+TNFα+ CD4 TILs significantly increased in the combo group versus control or OTX-008, but not anti-PD-L1 (Fig. 8E, F). The percentage of IFNγ-producing CD8 TIL was significantly higher in the combo group than in control or OTX-008, but not anti-PD-L1 group (Supplemental Fig. 22A, B), while the percentage of TNFα-producing CD8 TIL was significantly higher in the combo than in all other groups (Supplemental Fig. 22C, D). Similar trends were observed for CD4 TILs (Supplemental Fig. 22E–H). Consistent with these functional data, the percentage of effector memory (EM) CD8 TILs, but not central memory (CM) or naïve cells, was significantly higher in the combo group (Fig. 8G–I). In contrast, percentages of CD8 TILs expressing PD-1, LAG-3, or both were comparable across all groups (Supplemental Fig. 23A–D). Overall, these data indicate that combination therapy increases TIL infiltration and significantly enhances their effector functions.

OTX-008 enhances CD8 T-cell activation via STAT3 signaling without affecting LGALS1 expression

As scRNA-seq indicated LGALS1 expression in CD8 TILs (Fig. 1), we further characterized its expression patterns in CD8 T-cells. We found that activated CD8 T cells do not express surface LGALS1; instead, expression is exclusively intracellular (Supplemental Fig. 24A, B). OTX-008 treatment had no effect on the expression levels or subcellular localization of LGALS1 (Supplemental Fig. 24A, B). Next, we investigated the direct impact of OTX-008 on CD8 T-cell activation and survival (Fig. 9A). OTX-008 significantly enhanced activation, as evidenced by increased CD69 expression (Fig. 9B, D) and a higher percentage of CD8 T cells producing TNFα, IFNγ, or both (Fig. 9C, D, Supplemental Fig. 24C, D). Additionally, OTX-008 treatment reduced apoptosis in activated CD8 T cells (Fig. 9E, Supplemental Fig. 24E).

This activation correlated with elevated levels of total (t-STAT3) and phospho-STAT3 (p-STAT3) (Fig. 9F, G), as well as the direct transcriptional targets of STAT3, c-Myc and Bcl-XL (Fig. 9H–K). We therefore hypothesized that the STAT3 pathway may mediate this OTX-008-induced T-cell activation. STAT3-specific antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) were efficiently internalized (Supplemental Fig. 25D) and successfully abrogated OTX-008-induced t-STAT3/p-STAT3 upregulation (Supplemental Fig. 25A, B) in CD8 T cells. Discussion

In this study, we revealed a critical role for LGALS1 blockade in sensitizing HNSCC to ICI therapy via multifactorial mechanisms and identified the prognostic significance of LGALS1 levels in predicting ICI responses. We found that (1) LGALS1 is highly expressed in tumor cells, tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells and activated CD8 T cells in both human and murine systems; (2) higher intracellular LGALS1 in CD11b+ cells and lower plasma LGALS1 levels correlate with favorable responses to anti-PD-L1 in the mouse model. Consistently, serum LGALS1 levels exhibit prognostic value in predicting the overall survival of anti-PD-1 treated HNSCC patients; (3) combination OTX-008 and anti-PD-L1 treatment yields superior tumor control and prolonged survival. These outcomes correlate with increased CD4 and CD8 TIL infiltration and enhanced effector function, alongside a reduction in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, particularly M2-like TAMs; (4) the therapeutic efficacy of this combination relies on the simultaneous blockade of both tumor- and TIME-derived LGALS1; (5) OTX-008 does not affect LGALS1 expression; instead, it modulates tumor-associated myeloid cell differentiation and activates CD8 T cells in a STAT3-dependent manner.

LGALS1 (a.k.a. Galectin-1) is a ubiquitously expressed 14 kDa protein with a binding site for β-galactoside. It exerts distinct effects on various immune cell subsets, including CD8 and CD4 T cells, Tregs, and myeloid cells and others [36]. A recent review summarized the findings on the role of LGALS1 in the TIME and current therapeutic strategies for targeting LGALS1 [62]. Additionally, recent studies have indicated LGALS1 as a promising therapeutic target for prostate cancer [63] and renal cell carcinoma [64]. In the current study, we focused on myeloid cells and CD8 T cells, as these populations express relatively higher levels of LGALS1 compared to other immune subsets. While we utilized LGALS1-KO tumor cells to address tumor-intrinsic effects and pharmacological inhibition to target the TIME, future studies involving the development of conditional KO models (e.g., Lyz2-Cre) would be a valuable next step in precisely dissecting these cell-specific immune contributions. These models would provide deeper mechanistic insight into the individual roles of myeloid- versus T cell-derived LGALS1 and represent a promising direction for future research.

Previous studies indicated a role for LGALS1 in regulating TAM function; for example, LGALS1 can reduce the expression of MHC-II, a marker of M1-like TAMs [65]. Furthermore, serum LGALS1 is positively associated with soluble CD163 in multiple myeloma patients, suggesting its contribution to M2-like macrophage activation [66]. Consistent with these reports, we observed that TAMs exhibit significantly higher LGALS1 surface expression compared to total CD11b+ population or tumor cells (Fig. 7A, B), suggesting that TAMs may be primary mediators of LGALS1-driven immunosuppression. This high surface density likely facilitates LGALS1 binding to glycoproteins, potentially modulating TAM differentiation and function within the tumor continuum. While OTX-008 treatment or LGALS1 deletion in tumor cells promoted M1-like differentiation while reducing M2-like TAMs in our co-culture system, the molecular mechanisms underlying TAM polarization remain to be further investigated. In this regard, LGALS1 knockdown in glioma decreased M2 macrophages, associated with the reduced expression of immunosuppressive cytokines, e.g., VEGF-A, CCL2, and TGF-β [67]. We propose that LGALS1 deletion may influence TAM differentiation through distinct, cancer-type-specific mechanisms that warrant future study.

LGALS1 release (from inside to outside of cells) is a hallmark of inflammatory cell death or necroptosis, which can be induced by LPS or other inflammatory stimuli such as TLR ligands [36, 38]. LGALS1 can be released via GSDMD-formed pores on the cell surface since its release is blocked in GSDMD-KO cells [38]. We propose that in NR tumors, the TIME likely favors an inflammatory state mediated by cytokines, such as IL-1α, IL-1β, etc., that induce LGALS1 secretion. This results in the lower intracellular levels and higher plasma concentrations observed in our NR cohort. Conversely, in R tumors, the TIME is reprogrammed with fewer stimuli to induce LGALS1 release, leading to a ‘secretion-retention’ shift where LGALS1 remains sequestered intracellularly. The benefit of high intracellular LGALS1 may not stem from active intracellular signaling, but rather from its sequestration within the cell. This prevents its secretion into the extracellular space, thereby preventing the formation of an immunosuppressive LGALS1 shield. We hypothesize that the factors reprogramming the TIME in R mice are CD8 T cell-dependent, as anti-PD-L1 efficacy was abrogated in CD8-KO mice [58]. Thus, the shift in LGALS1 expression patterns may not drive the anti-PD-L1 response; rather, it serves as a robust surrogate marker for a T-cell-productive TIME associated with therapeutic efficacy.

An early report showed that LGALS1 could increase T cell apoptosis [68]. However, the effects of LGALS1 on T cells are complex, and the precise mechanism by which LGALS1 modulates T cell function remains controversial and is not completely understood [36]. Prior studies have suggested that cell surface glycoproteins, such as CD2, CD3, CD7, CD43, CD45, CD69, and CD95 as well as T cell receptor (TCR), could serve as binding sites for LGALS1 [68–70]. However, we failed to detect cell surface expression of LGALS1 on activated CD8 T cells and only detected intracellular expression of LGALS1, at least in our system; furthermore, OTX-008 treatment did not affect LGALS1 expression in CD8 T cells. These results suggest that LGALS1 modulates T cell function likely by affecting signaling pathways inside of CD8 T cells. Consistent with this notion, we found that OTX-008-induced CD8 T cell activation is STAT3-dependent. OTX-008 is an allosteric inhibitor of LGALS1 [59]. LGALS1 contains a front and back face β-sheet, and OTX-008 can bind to the back face β-sheet of LGALS1 [60]. This direct interaction may block LGALS1’s ability to interact with other intracellular proteins, thereby facilitating STAT3 activation. Future studies are needed to dissect how LGALS1 intersects with STAT3 pathway to suppress CD8 T cell activation. Notably, OTX-008-induced enhanced survival of CD8 T cells is not STAT3-dependent, suggesting additional unidentified signaling pathways involved in this process.

We propose the following potential tumor-intrinsic mechanisms that may influence the outcome of LGALS1 deletion in tumor cells or OTX-008 treatment: (1) LGALS1 deletion or OTX-008 may alter cell–cell communication during tumor growth in vivo. Specifically, the absence of LGALS1 could modulate the glycosylation patterns of surface proteins on tumor cells, surrounding fibroblasts, or within the extracellular matrix. These alterations might influence anchorage-independent growth, migration, or invasiveness, thereby contributing to the reduced growth of LGALS1-KO tumors in vivo. Notably, our in vitro studies demonstrated that neither LGALS1 deletion nor OTX-008 treatment directly affected tumor cell proliferation or survival (Supplemental Figs. 13 and 26), highlighting the importance of the microenvironmental context. (2) LGALS1-KO tumors may exhibit distinct expression patterns or levels of cytokines and chemokines, which can reshape the TIME and indirectly modulate anti-tumor immunity. Furthermore, LGALS1 deletion may affect tumor immunogenicity and permit the immune system to better recognize tumor cells.

Although STAT3-ASO treatment reduced IFNγ production, IFNγ likely serves as a downstream indicator of activation rather than a direct transcriptional target of STAT3 in this context. Blocking STAT3 may impair IFNγ production by depleting c-Myc-driven metabolic resources required for cytokine synthesis. Alternatively, STAT3 might influence the signaling complexes driving IFNγ transcription or the chromatin accessibility of the IFNγ locus. We show that while STAT3 is essential for the OTX-008-induced effector program, it is not required for OTX-008-induced T-cell survival. The upregulation of c-Myc and Bcl-XL likely enhances the bioenergetic fitness and mitochondrial stability necessary for sustained effector function. However, the inability of STAT3-ASO to abrogate the survival benefit suggests that OTX-008 engages parallel, STAT3-independent pathways, such as AKT or STAT5, to ensure T-cell persistence even when the STAT3-dependent activation program is inhibited. The observed upregulation of p-STAT3, Bcl-XL, and c-Myc in T cells following OTX-008 treatment suggests that LGALS1 may act as an alternative checkpoint that limits T-cell activation and proliferation.

While plasma LGALS1 serves as a robust biomarker for anti-PD-L1 resistance, it likely also functions as a primary effector of immune suppression. We propose an integrated model for ICI response that takes into consideration both secreted and intracellular LGALS1 (Supplemental Fig. 27). While intracellular LGALS1 may modulate key signaling pathways like STAT3 in CD8 T cells, the presence of LGALS1 on the surface of TAMs and tumor cells suggests a simultaneous paracrine/autocrine role for the secreted protein. In NR, the combination of extracellular 'shielding' by elevated plasma LGALS1 and dysfunctional intracellular signaling creates a profoundly hostile TIME. This multifaceted signaling, spanning both intracellular and extracellular compartments, underlines the therapeutic advantage of systemic inhibition with OTX-008. Ultimately, our study suggests that the OTX-008/anti-PD-L1 combination works by dismantling this multi-layered LGALS1-mediated resistance, effectively ‘re-arming’ CD8 T-cell compartment within the TIME.

While a prior study investigated LGALS1 blockade via anti-Gal-1 antibodies specifically focusing on tumor endothelium reprogramming [39], our findings reveal a broader role for this protein. We show that LGALS1 is predominantly expressed intracellularly in mouse SCC and is significantly upregulated in advanced-stage and HPV− human HNSCC. Across both systems, LGALS1 is highly enriched within tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells and CD8 TILs. We propose that targeting factors like LGALS1, which influence both tumor-intrinsic properties and the TIME, may enhance anti-tumor immunity more effectively (Supplemental Fig. 27). Notably, as a cell-permeable small molecule, OTX-008 combined with anti-PD-L1 significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged survival, highlighting the importance of targeting the intracellular LGALS1 pool that is largely inaccessible to antibodies. Although a Phase 1 trial (NCT01724320) established the safety of OTX-008, its limited clinical progression underscores the need for next-generation inhibitors with optimized potency or pharmacokinetics. Our findings highlight LGALS1 as a promising target for further clinical evaluation within combination therapy frameworks.

Conclusions

In the current study, we revealed that LGALS1 blockade plays a critical role in sensitizing HNSCC to ICI therapy through multifactorial mechanisms, while establishing the prognostic significance of LGALS1 levels in predicting ICI responses. Unlike prior models focusing on extracellular LGALS1, our study demonstrates that LGALS1 is predominantly expressed intracellularly within tumor cells, tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, and activated CD8 T cells. We show that the efficacy of LGALS1 inhibition depends on both tumor-intrinsic and -extrinsic mechanisms and is likely mediated by changes in the differentiation and function of myeloid and CD8 T cells, rather than changes in LGALS1 expression levels. These data provide novel insights into the therapeutic mechanisms of LGALS1 blockade and underscore its prognostic value in predicting ICI responses in HNSCC. Our study highlights LGALS1 as an ideal candidate for effective combinatorial therapy in HNSCC, as its blockade simultaneously modulates tumor cells, myeloid cells, and CD8 T cells to promote anti-tumor immunity. These findings may be translatable to individual HNSCC patients with a unique TIME (e.g., higher expression of LGALS1) and could benefit these patients by guiding the design of more effective combinatorial therapies.
