Genome-wide identification and characterization of GATA gene family involved in ovule development of Korean pine (Pinus koraiensis)
Authors: Yue Zhang, Caihong Zhao, Ling Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGATA transcription factors, a type of zinc-finger DNA-binding protein, play a crucial role in regulating various biological processes including plant growth, stress response, and hormone signaling by controlling the expression of target genes. Recently, GATA genes have been discovered in numerous plant species. However, they have not yet been reported in Korean pine (Pinus koraiensis).ResultsIn this study, a total of 15 PkorGATA genes were identified in the whole genome of Korean pine. These PkorGATA genes are unevenly distributed across nine chromosomes and are categorized into four subfamilies based on their conserved structural domains. The results of cis-acting element analysis, RNA-seq, and qRT-PCR indicate that PkorGATA genes play a significant role in the developmental processes of Korean pine ovules. Moreover, subcellular localization results revealed that PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10 and PkorGATA11 are localized in the nucleus. The transcriptional activation activity of six PkorGATA proteins in yeast showed that PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, and PkorGATA10 exhibit transcriptional activation capabilities, suggesting that their potential function as transcription factors within the nucleus.ConclusionIn general, this study provides candidate gene resources for functional exploration of GATA genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07875-y.

Background

Transcription factor (TF) regulates various physiological responses in plants by binding to gene promoters or interacting with other proteins [1]. In plants, many important transcription factor families have been found to participate in plant growth and enhance plant stress resistance, such as GATA [2], NAC [3], WRKY [4], MYB [5], bZIP [6] and WOX [7]. The DNA-binding domains of GATA proteins all contain a highly conserved C-X2-C-X17 − 20-C-X2-C type zinc finger motif, which can specifically recognize and bind to the W-G-A-T-A-R sequence, thereby regulating transcriptional activity [2]. The first GATA transcription factor, NTL1, was identified in tobacco. Its amino acid sequence is highly homologous to the nitrogen regulatory gene NTL2 in Neurospora crassa, which mediates the nitrate assimilation pathway [8]. Subsequently, the GATA family has been identified in many different plant types, such as Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) [9], rice (Oryza sativa) [10], Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) [11], Apple (Malus domestica) [12], Phoebe bournei [13], Populus (Populus trichocarpa) [14] and Eucalyptus (Eucalyptus urophylla) [15].

GATA transcription factors play an important role in regulating plant light response, seed and seedling development, chlorophyll synthesis, hormone signal transduction, and environmental response. There are 30 GATA members in the Arabidopsis genome, divided into four subfamilies. AtGATA2 in subfamily A influences seedling development by promoting photomorphogenesis [16]. The RGL2-DOF6 complex in gibberellin (GA) signaling pathway can activate the expression of AtGATA12 and promote the primary dormancy of Arabidopsis seeds [16]. In subfamily B, GNC (GATA, Nitrate-inducible, Carbon-metabolism-involved), GNL/CGA1 (GNC-like/Cytokinin-responsive GATA Factor1), and their homologous gene OsGATA16 in rice act as positive regulators by enhancing chlorophyll biosynthesis and promoting chloroplast development in seedlings [17, 18]. Overexpression of the PdGNC gene in poplar has been shown to enhance carbon assimilation in the leaves and nitrogen absorption in the roots, leading to improved photosynthetic efficiency and promoting vegetative growth of the plant [19, 20]. In addition, studies have shown that Arabidopsis GATA member GNC is a key regulator involved in germination, leaf greening, senescence and flowering time, which can inhibit flowering by inhibiting SOC1 gene expression [21]. Overexpression of PdeGATA3 results in a dwarf phenotype in poplar by promoting the expression of PdeSTM and altering the content of gibberellins [22]. Overexpression of BdGATA13 in Brachypodium distachyon increases root length in GA3 treated plant seedlings [23].

GATA is also involved in the nitrogen metabolism of soybean leaves. It was found that the expression of GmGATA44 and GmGATA58 transcription factors significantly increased under low nitrogen stress [24]. Treatment with exogenous hormones such as salicylic acid (SA), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), and abscisic acid (ABA) can also influence the expression of the PbGATA gene, ultimately leading to the promotion of fruit ripening in pear (Pyrus bretschneideri) [25]. Recent research has shown that GATA transcription factors not only regulate plant development but also play a crucial role in controlling plant responses to abiotic stress. During the seedling stage of rice, OsGATA16 binds to the promoter of OsWRKY45-1, leading to the inhibition of OsWRKY45-1 gene transcription and ultimately enhancing cold tolerance in rice [26]. OsGATA8 enhances rice resistance to salt and drought stress by regulating ion homeostasis and ROS scavenging enzyme activity [27]. BdGATA13 gene enhances plant drought tolerance by modulating the expression of drought-responsive genes like RD29A and NCED3 [23]. SlGATA17 not only regulates the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway to enhance tomato plant drought resistance but also interacts with SlHY5 to suppress ABA biosynthesis, promoting seed germination and reducing seedling salt tolerance [28, 29].

Korean pine, an evergreen tree, is primarily found in Northeast China, the Korean Peninsula, and the Russian Far East [30, 31]. Korean pine is a prominent coniferous species in cold temperate zones, known for its strong cold tolerance [32]. It is a valuable woody economic species in the Northeast region, prized for its high corrosion resistance. Korean pine is primarily utilized as raw material in construction, shipbuilding, furniture, and the wood fiber industry [33]. Furthermore, the seeds of Korean pine are abundant in fat, protein, and carbohydrates, making them suitable for producing edible vegetable oil with nutritional value [30, 31]. This study utilized bioinformatics methods to identify the members of the PkorGATA gene family in Korean pine based on its genome. The study provided a comprehensive analysis of the family members, including their physical and chemical properties, gene structure, chromosome distribution, cis-acting elements, and interspecies differences. Additionally, the study systematically examined the evolutionary relationships within the gene family. The expression patterns of 15 PkorGATA genes were analyzed at various developmental stages of Korean pine ovule using qRT-PCR. Subcellular localization and transcriptional activation activities of PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, and PkorGATA11 proteins were studied, laying a crucial foundation for exploring the biological functions of the PkorGATA gene family.

Methods

Identification of the GATA gene family in Korean pine

The complete genome and annotation information of Korean pine were obtained from our laboratory. The identification results found that the size of the Korean pine genome was 27.28 Gb. The total length of the assembled sequence was 37.42 Gb. Contig N50 was 81.75 Mb. The BUSCO evaluation results showed that the gene set had a complete coverage of 1,150 conserved proteins, with a completeness of 71.25%, of which 971 (84.43%) were in single copy, and 179 (15.57%) were in multiple copy. The final annotation was 31,345 genes. To identify all members of the GATA gene family in Korean pine, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) file of the GATA structural domain (PF00320) was initially obtained from the Pfam protein family database [34]. Sequences lacking GATA conserved structural domains were then filtered out by comparing BLAST results from ClustalW2.1 and the NCBI CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) [35]. Ultimately, a total of 15 PkorGATA genes were identified and renamed based on their chromosomal location.

Physicochemical characterization of PkorGATA proteins

The physicochemical properties of all PkorGATA proteins were analyzed using ExPASy (http://www.expasy.ch/tools/pi_tool.html), including amino acid number, molecular weight (MW) and isoelectric point (pI) [36]. The transmembrane structural domains of PkorGATA proteins were predicted using TMHMM (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM). Additionally, the Plant-Ploc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/) was utilized to predict the stability of the PkorGATA proteins.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of PkorGATAs

Intron-exon structures were determined by comparing the PkorGATA gene family CDS with their corresponding genomic DNA sequences. Gene structures were then mapped using the Gene Structure View (Advanced) feature of the TBtools software [37]. Prediction of protein conserved motifs based on MEME online tool (https://meme-suite.org/) [38]. Finally, the Gene Structure View (Advanced) feature of TBtools was utilized to merge gene structures, protein conserved motifs, and protein structural domains [39].

Phylogenetic analysis of PkorGATAs

GATA protein sequences from Arabidopsis were obtained from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/), while GATA protein sequences from rice and Populus were downloaded from [10] and [40]. The phylogenetic tree was generated using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method in MEGA 11.0 [41]. Multiple sequence comparison of the GATA amino acids from various plants was performed by Muscle function with a bootstrap number of 1000 and the results were visualized on the iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) [42].

Cis-acting elements of PkorGATA gene promoters

The 2000 bp sequence upstream of the PkorGATA genes were extracted for analysis in this study. Cis-acting elements within the promoter sequence were predicted using PlantCARE (http://www.dna.affrc.go.jp/PLACE/) [43]. Functionally identical cis-acting elements were uniformly named and the Simple BioSequence Viewer function of TBtools [39] was utilized to visualize their location and number.

Plant materials and sample collections

The samples for this study were taken from the Hongwei seed orchard, Baishan City, Jilin Province, China. The study was performed on elite clonal grafted mother trees colonized in 1989. The material source of this experiment is Hongwei seed orchard cooperation, through the seed orchard cultivation, provided to us for research. The collection was performed in June of the second year (2021) after natural pollination. The samples for the two weeks before fertilization were obtained on June 8 (ZA), while the samples for the one week before fertilization were obtained on June 15 (ZB). The samples for the week of fertilization were obtained on June 22 (ZC), and the samples for the one week after fertilization were obtained on June 30 (ZD). The samples were stored at −80℃ for further RNA extraction.

Expression pattern analysis of PkorGATA genes

RNA-seq data of PkorGATA genes at various developmental stages were collected from our previous study, including the ovules two weeks before fertilization (ZA), one week before fertilization (ZB), at fertilization week (ZC), and one week after fertilization (ZD) [44]. RNA-seq data were deposited on the NCBI (Bioproject ID: SUB12731425). The total RNA from the sample was extracted using the EZNA Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA), followed by reverse transcription using the Hifair® III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) to obtain cDNA. Previous studies have found that 18 S is used with high frequency and stable expression as an internal reference gene in Korean pine [33, 44, 45]. Subsequently, 18 S was demonstrated to be suitable for gene expression studies as the most stable and suitable internal reference gene in other species, such as Gossypium hirsutum [46], Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer [47] and Solanum melongena [48]. qRT-PCR specific primers for the PkorGATA genes were designed using Primer 5.0 software, with 18 S serving as the internal reference gene. The primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table 1. qRT-PCR analysis was performed on ovules at various developmental stages using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II FAST qPCR (Takara, Dalian, China). Relative expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method to identify significant differences between samples, with the expression level during the ZA stage normalized to 1. The experiment was carried out three times, with three replications for each treatment. RNA-seq data were merged with qRT-PCR results and visualized using Graphpad software.

Subcellular localization of the PkorGATAs

Specific cloning primers were designed based on the transcriptional sequence of PkorGATA genes to amplify the CDS sequences of PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, and PkorGATA11 (Supplementary Table 1), Homologous PCR primers (Supplementary Table 1) were designed using homologous recombination method, pFGC-eGFP vector was linearized using restriction endonuclease BamHI, and the linearized vector was ligated to the gene fragments according to the Hieff Clone® Plus One Step Cloning Kit to obtain PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, PkorGATA11 fusion expression vectors with pFGC proteins. The fusion expression vector was transformed into Agrobacterium strain GV3101, and the resuspension solution was transformed into healthy-growing leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana using a 1 mL syringe, and after 2 d of incubation, GFP fluorescence was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, LSM800) for subcellular localization analysis.

Detection of transcriptional activity of PkorGATAs

The full-length CDS sequence of PkorGATA was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector. Fusion vectors pGBKT7-PkorGATA2, pGBKT7-PkorGATA3, pGBKT7-PkorGATA8, pGBKT7-PkorGATA9, pGBKT7-PkorGATA10 and pGBKT7-PkorGATA11 were then constructed. Subsequently, plasmids were extracted and were further transformed into Y2H Gold yeast cells. The transformed Y2H Gold yeast cells were cultured on SD/-Trp and SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade/X-α-Gal yeast media at 30 °C for 2 to 4 days. The pGBKT7 vector served as a negative control. Gene-specific primers pairs are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All findings have three independent biological replicates and were analyzed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS software [49]. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Identification and characterization of PkorGATA genes

A total of 15 genes were identified in this study and named PkorGATA1 to PkorGATA15 based on their chromosomal locations (Supplementary Table 2). Analysis of the physicochemical properties of PkorGATAs revealed that the coding sequences (CDS) of these genes varied in length from 543 to 1191 bp and amino acid lengths of the proteins were in the range of 180 to 396 aa, and the molecular weight (MW) ranged from 20775.78 to 43856.08 Da. The isoelectric point (pI) distribution ranged from 5.03 to 9.97 and most of them were less than 7, suggesting that PkorGATA family proteins tended to be enriched in acidic amino acids. The predicted transmembrane (TM) structural domains of the PkorGATA proteins showed that all PkorGATA members did not contain transmembrane structural domains. The instability index (II) of the PkorGATA proteins all exceeded 40, suggesting that all of them are unstable proteins (Supplementary Table 2). The detailed information of genes and proteins were listed in Supplementary Tables 3 and Supplementary Table 4.

Gene and protein structures analysis of PkorGATAs

Analysis of conserved motifs in PkorGATA proteins identified seven motifs with varying amino acid lengths (Fig. 1b). All 15 PkorGATA proteins contained motif 1, with each protein in G1 subfamily harboring four identical conserved motifs (Fig. 1b). PkorGATA proteins in G3 and G4 subfamily had two identical conserved motifs, while G2 subfamily proteins had the fewest motifs, with only one conserved motif present (Fig. 1b). Conserved domain analysis revealed that PkorGATA proteins possess a complete ZnF-GATA domain. Within the same subfamily, different genes exhibited conserved and differential regions in their structures, suggesting a high conservation of zinc finger domains across most GATA amino acid sites (Fig. 1c). In addition to the conserved ZnF-GATA domain, the PkorGATA8 protein sequence contains two unique structural domains, CCT and Tify. The primary function of the CCT domain is to facilitate the nuclear localization of proteins, while the tify domain primarily mediates protein-protein interactions (Fig. 1c). This suggests that PkorGATA8 may be involved in gene or protein regulation. The number of exons of PkorGATA gene family members ranges from 2 to 6, among which PkorGATA8 has the highest number of exons (Fig. 1d).

Chromosome distribution of PkorGATAs

Based on the annotation information of PkorGATA genes in Korean pine, we conducted an analysis of the chromosomal distribution of PkorGATAs. The results revealed that 15 PkorGATA genes were unevenly distributed across 9 chromosomes of Korean pine (Fig. 2). Chromosome 01 (Chr01) contained the highest number of PkorGATA genes, followed by Chr02, Chr04, Chr08, and Chr09 with two PkorGATA genes. Chr03, Chr06, Chr07, and Chr11 had the lowest gene count, each containing only one PkorGATA gene.

Phylogenetic analysis of PkorGATAs in Korean pine

In this study, a phylogenetic tree was constructed to analyze the phylogenetic relationships of GATAs among different species and investigate the potential biological functions of PkorGATAs (Fig. 3). The detailed sequences of GATA proteins in different species are shown in Supplementary Table 5. All GATA proteins were categorized into four subfamilies, with subfamily I having the highest number of members, including nine PkorGATA proteins. Subfamilies II and III had five and one PkorGATA proteins, respectively, while subfamily IV has no PkorGATA proteins (Fig. 3). Interestingly, GATA proteins of Korean pine and Populus showed a close relationship, indicating potential similar functions during the evolution of woody plants. We compared GATA genes in subfamily IV of Arabidopsis and Populus to the Korean pine GATA family by the NCBI Blast comparison function, and the results showed that the GATA genes of Populus and Arabidopsis had no genetic relationship with the GATA genes of Korean pine.

Cis-acting element analysis of PkorGATA gene promoters

To explore the functions of PkorGATA genes, we examined cis-acting elements in the 2000 bp sequence upstream of PkorGATA genes using Plant-CARE (Supplementary Table 6). Results revealed that among the 15 PkorGATA genes promoter sequences, the highest number of light-responsive elements, such as GATA-motif, Box 4, MRE, G-box, AE-box, and GT1-motif, were present (Supplementary Table 7). This was followed by plant hormone response elements, including MeJA responsive elements (CGTCA-motif, TGACG-motif), ABA responsive elements (ABRE), salicylic acid responsive elements (TCA), auxin response elements (AuxRR-core, TGA), and gibberellin responsive elements (P-box, the GARE-motif). Additionally, plant growth and development, as well as abiotic stress response elements, were identified (Fig. 4). The analysis of cis-acting elements suggests that PkorGATA genes regulate Korean pine growth, development, as well as various hormone-induced and stress-responsive pathways (Fig. 4).

Expression patterns of PkorGATA genes at different developmental stages

GATA transcription factors are a class closely associated with growth and development. In this study, we analyzed RNA-seq data from different developmental stages (ZA, ZB, ZC, and ZD) to compare the expression levels of PkorGATA gene family members (Supplementary Table 8). Our findings revealed that three members (PkorGATA6, PkorGATA14, and PkorGATA15) belonged to subfamily I, two members (PkorGATA9 and PkorGATA10) to subfamily II, and one member (PkorGATA8) to subfamily III. Interestingly, these genes exhibited high expression levels during the ZA stage as shown in Fig. 5. During the ZD stage, most genes exhibited low expression levels. PkorGATA7, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA2, PkorGATA12, and PkorGATA4 are part of subfamily I and subfamily II, respectively, exhibiting low expression levels during the ZA stage. However, their expression levels significantly increased during the ZC and ZD stages. In addition, PkorGATA4, PkorGATA5, PkorGATA11, and PkorGATA13 exhibited the lowest expression levels during the ZA stage. However, their expression significantly increased during the ZB stage (Fig. 5).

The results of qRT-PCR showed that the expression levels of 15 PkorGATA genes at different developmental stages were highly correlated with RNA-seq results, confirming the reliability of the data (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, PkorGATA2 exhibited a consistent up-regulation at all developmental stages, while PkorGATA4, PkorGATA5, and PkorGATA11 showed significant up-regulation from ZA to ZB. Furthermore, PkorGATA4, PkorGATA5, and PkorGATA11 increased significantly from ZA to ZB, with the highest expression levels in ZB compared to other developmental stages. On the other hand, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA7, PkorGATA10, PkorGATA13, and PkorGATA14 exhibited significant up-regulation from ZA to ZC, with the peak expression level observed during the ZC stage. PkorGATA6 and PkorGATA9 showed a significant decrease in expression from ZA to ZB. PkorGATA8 exhibited the highest expression level in ZA, but lower levels in all other developmental stages. PkorGATA12 showed lower expression in ZA and ZB, yet significantly higher levels in ZC and ZD (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that PkorGATA genes within the same subfamily have distinct functions across various developmental stage.

Subcellular localization of PkorGATAs

To confirm the subcellular location of PkorGATA protein, representative genes PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, and PkorGATA11 from the three subfamilies of PkorGATA were selected for subcellular localization analysis (Fig. 6). Then pFGC-eGFP empty vector and six fusion vectors were expressed transiently in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-regulated transformation. The results showed that the control vector pFGC-eGFP had a strong fluorescence signal in the whole cell. The six fusion proteins mainly detected fluorescence signals in the nucleus, indicating that PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10 and PkorGATA11 were nuclear proteins (Fig. 6).

Transcriptional activity of PkorGATAs

Transcription activity is essential for the function of transcription factors. In this study, the CDS sequences of PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, and PkorGATA11 were recombined into the pGBKT7 vector. Subsequently, both the pGBKT7 empty vector and the six fusion vectors were co-transformed into yeast cells. The results showed that pGBKT7-PkorGATA2, pGBKT7-PkorGATA3, and pGBKT7-PkorGATA10 exhibited blue on SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade/X-α-Gal media (Fig. 7). This suggests that PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, and PkorGATA10 functioned as transcriptional auto-activators, while PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9 and PkorGATA11 had no transcriptional auto activation ability.

Discussions

Identification and characterization of GATA genes in Korean pine

So far, the GATA gene family has been identified in a variety of plants. For example, there are 30 members in Arabidopsis [9], 28 members in rice [10], and 39 members in Populus [40], However, genome-wide analysis of the GATA gene family has not been performed in Korean pine. In this study, for the first time, a total of 15 members of the GATA gene family were identified from the Korean pine genome, which were unevenly distributed across the nine chromosomes of Korean pine (Fig. 2). Notably, chromosome 3 had the second longest length after chromosome 1, yet it only contained one GATA gene. Similarly, despite chromosome 5 of Eucalyptus being the second longest after chromosome 3, it also distributed with only one GATA gene [15]. It suggests that there is a similarity in the distribution on chromosomes of woody plants and that there is no necessary biological correlation between the number of genes and the distribution of PkorGATA on chromosomes. The protein molecular weights (MW), theoretical isoelectric points (pI), instability coefficients, and other physicochemical properties were analyzed using bioinformatics methods. The 15 PkorGATA genes exhibited significant variability in terms of amino acid lengths and molecular weights. Additionally, most of the PkorGATA proteins were found to be enriched in acidic amino acids, whereas in angiosperms, the GATA family tended to be enriched in weakly basic amino acids [11]. This indicates that there are significant differences in genotypes between gymnosperms and angiosperms. The conserved motifs and gene structures of GATA proteins are key factors in the functional variation of genes [24, 50]. Our study revealed that nearly all PkorGATA genes possess ZnF_GATA structural domains, with members of the same subfamily sharing similar conserved motifs (Fig. 1). However, significant variations in gene structure and conserved motifs were observed among different subfamilies, aligning with findings in other plant GATAs [51–53]. This indicates that the structural diversity of PkorGATA proteins results in distinct biological functions.

Phylogenetic relationships of PkorGATAs in Korean pine

To hypothesize the biological function of the PkorGATA genes, this study clustered all GATA proteins in Korean pine with those from various model plants such as Arabidopsis, rice, and Populus, into four subfamilies (Fig. 3). These findings align with previous reports across different species [9, 10, 14, 40]. Similar to Arabidopsis and Populus, PkorGATAs were most widely distributed in subfamily I, with each subfamily containing at least one gene from Arabidopsis, rice, and Populus. In Arabidopsis, AtGATA8 and AtGATA12, both members of the same subfamily, play roles in regulating seed dormancy and seed germination processes, respectively [16, 54]. PtrGATA12 is highly expressed in the stem of Populus trichocarpa, which increases the content of cellulose and lignin, thereby promoting the synthesis of secondary cell wall and enhancing stem development [55]. This suggests that PkorGATA6, PkorGATA7, PkorGATA12, PkorGATA13, PkorGATA14, and PkorGATA15, which belong to subfamily I (Fig. 3), may also play a role in regulating the pathway of seed germination and secondary cell wall biosynthesis in Korean pine. Additionally, AtGATA18 is known to regulate shoot apical meristem and flower development [56]. Therefore, PkorGATA9, which belongs to the same subfamily II as AtGATA18, may also play an important role in organ development. Overexpression of AtGATA25 significantly promoted hypocotyl and petiole elongation in Arabidopsis [57]. It indicates that PkorGATA8 has the closest genetic relationship with AtGATA25 and may positively regulate hypocotyl elongation. PkorGATA is functionally conserved across plants. Interestingly, GATA genes of Populus and Arabidopsis had no genetic relationship with the GATA genes of Korean pine in subfamily IV (Fig. 3). In addition, we found that PtrGATA2 and PtrGATA8 of subfamily IV of Populus had no C-X2-C-X20-C-X2-C conserved structural domains, which might have been changed during evolution. The sequence characterization is consistent with the study in Arabidopsis [14]. It suggests that the gene functions of the GATA genes in subfamily IV were gradually lost during evolution, and therefore the function of PkorGATA genes in subfamily IV may have been completely lost as well.

Analysis of cis-acting element in promoters

Cis-acting elements regulate the activity of target genes by binding to trans-acting factors [58]. For instance, CBF1 responses to low temperature and water deficit stress by binding to the DNA regulatory element DRE [59]. Additionally, CrPIF1 inhibits CrGATA1 promoter activity by binding to the light-responsive element G-box [58]. In this study, we analyzed the cis-acting elements of PkorGATA genes and found that the promoter region of the PkorGATA genes contained mainly light-responsive, phytohormone-responsive, abiotic stress-responsive, and seed development-regulatory elements (Fig. 4). It is consistent with the findings of Eucalyptus [15] and Sorghum [11].

Gene expression characteristics in different developmental stages

The fertilization process of Korean pine ovules is a key step in plant reproduction. Based on the results of RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR analysis of PkorGATA genes during ovule development. This study identified differential transcription levels of 15 PkorGATA genes in various developmental stages, suggesting distinct functions of these genes (Fig. 5). For example, subfamily I members PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA7, and PkorGATA12 have relatively low transcript levels in the ZA and ZB stages and relatively high transcript levels in the ZC and ZD stages. In Arabidopsis, AtGATA8 and AtGATA12 are members of the same subfamily that promotes seed dormancy and seed germination processes, respectively. AtGATA12 maintains primary seed dormancy by inhibiting the time of testa rupture and radicle elongation. In contrast, AtGATA8 is a positive regulator of seed germination in Arabidopsis, which promotes seed germination by facilitating testa rupture. Similarly, PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA7, and PkorGATA12, which belong to subfamily I with AtGATA8 and AtGATA12, may play similar functions during seed germination. Therefore, they showed high expression levels at ZC and ZD stages to promote Korean pine ovule development [16, 54]. AtGATA23 affects lateral root initiation by regulating the expression of ARF7 and ARF19 [60]. Subfamily II members PkorGATA4, PkorGATA5, and AtGATA23 exhibit the highest homology. The findings showed that the expression levels of PkorGATA4 and PkorGATA5 were lowest during the ZA stage, but significantly increased during the ZB stage compared to the ZC and ZD stages. This suggests that these genes play a key role in regulating the transition of the ovule from the female gametophyte to the elongation of the pollen tube.

Analysis of the transcriptional activity

The results of subcellular localization showed that PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, and PkorGATA11 were localized to the nucleus and belonged to the nuclear proteins (Fig. 6). This observation aligns with the typical localization of most transcription factors [52]. PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, PkorGATA10, and PkorGATA11 are identified as transcription factors that potentially regulate the transcription of target genes within the nucleus. Moreover, PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3 and PkorGATA10 exhibit transcriptional activation capacity (Fig. 7). It has been shown that the transcriptional activation activity of GATA proteins depends on transcriptional activation domains other than the zinc finger structural domains. AtGATA22 contains a strong acidic activation domain at the C-terminus with strong transcriptional activation capacity, and tomato SlGATA17 has an atypical activation domain or repressive sequences, and thus its transcriptional activation capacity is weak [13, 50]. Upon comparison, we found that the PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, and PkorGATA10 members exhibit a high degree of similarity to the C-terminal structural domain composition of AtGATA22 in Arabidopsis. Consequently, we hypothesize that the transcriptional activation activity of these three members of the Korean pine GATA gene family may also be associated with the presence of a strong acidic activation domain at the C-terminus.

In contrast, PkorGATA8, PkorGATA9, and PkorGATA11 do not have typical strong acidic structural domains at the C terminus and therefore do not have transcriptional activation activity, and may function through interactions with other proteins. Therefore, in the future, yeast one-hybrid library can be utilized to construct and screen possible downstream regulatory genes to deeply explore the functions of PkorGATA gene members.

Conclusions

In this study, the first genome-wide analysis of the GATA gene family of Korean pine, and a total of 15 PkorGATA genes were identified. These genes were classified into four subfamilies, with each subfamily displaying similar gene structures and conserved protein motifs. Cis-acting element analysis indicated that PkorGATA genes are involved in plant development and stress response processes. RNA-seq and qRT-PCR results revealed the diversity of functions of 15 PkorGATA genes in different stages of Korean pine ovule development. In addition, PkorGATA2, PkorGATA3, and PkorGATA10 were localized in the nucleus and exhibit transcriptional activities. This study identifies key candidate genes for the molecular breeding of Korean pine, offering a significant scientific foundation for exploring the roles of GATA family genes in woody plants.
