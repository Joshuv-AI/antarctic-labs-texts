Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation in Cardiac Amyloidosis: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Adivitch Sripusanapan, Natee Deepan, Bheem Pongsukvechkul, Suphatcha Kittiwongsopon, Min Choon Tan, Pattara Rattanawong, Dan Sorajja, Win‐Kuang Shen, Ronpichai Chokesuwattanaskul, Narut Prasitlumkum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is prevalent in cardiac amyloidosis (CA) and is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Catheter ablation is increasingly used for rhythm control in this population, yet its clinical outcomes remain uncertain. We performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis to evaluate arrhythmia recurrence and clinical outcomes following catheter ablation compared with medical therapy in patients with CA.MethodsWe systematically searched MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus from inception through December 2025 for studies evaluating catheter ablation of atrial arrhythmias in CA. Cohort studies reporting arrhythmia recurrence and/or clinical outcomes were included. The primary outcome was freedom from atrial arrhythmia (AA) following ablation. Secondary outcomes included all‐cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and stroke. Random‐effects models were used to pool event rates and odds ratios (ORs).ResultsThirteen observational studies comprising 797 patients with CA and AF were included. The pooled rate of freedom from AA following catheter ablation was approximately 50% (95% CI 37%–63%; I2 = 71.7%). The all‐cause mortality analysis included 4 observational studies comprising 616 patients. Catheter ablation was associated with lower odds of all‐cause mortality (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.11–0.60; I2 = 18.4%); however, this association should be interpreted with caution given the retrospective design and risk of bias. No statistically significant differences were observed for heart failure hospitalization or stroke.ConclusionsIn patients with CA, catheter ablation was associated with modest arrhythmia‐free survival and lower odds of all‐cause mortality. However, all contributing studies were observational cohorts. Randomized studies are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: ablation, atrial fibrillation, cardiac amyloidosis

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common arrhythmia in patients with cardiac amyloidosis (CA), particularly in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy, and is associated with significant morbidity [1]. Restrictive ventricular physiology and atrial amyloid infiltration promote atrial remodeling, impaired atrial function, and increased susceptibility to AF [2, 3]. Rate control is often poorly tolerated in CA, and rhythm control strategies are frequently pursued, especially earlier in the disease course [4]. Catheter ablation has emerged as a rhythm control option for AF in CA, though published studies report variable efficacy and clinical outcomes [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Given the limited sample sizes and heterogeneity of existing studies, we performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis to evaluate arrhythmia recurrence and clinical outcomes following catheter ablation in patients with CA.

Materials and Methods

Literature Search

This systematic review and meta‐analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [10]. Two investigators (A.S. and N.D.) independently performed a comprehensive literature search of MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus from database inception through December 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords related to cardiac amyloidosis, atrial fibrillation or atrial arrhythmias, and catheter ablation, including “cardiac amyloidosis,” “atrial fibrillation,” “atrial arrhythmia,” “catheter ablation,” “arrhythmia recurrence,” “heart failure,” “stroke,” and “mortality,” along with relevant synonyms. Reference lists of included studies and pertinent review articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Selection Criteria

Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, prospective or retrospective cohort studies, and case–control studies. Studies were included if they met the following criteria:

Enrolled patients with CA undergoing first‐time catheter ablation for AA.

Enrolled patients with CA undergoing first‐time catheter ablation for AA.

Reported outcomes related to AA recurrence or freedom from AA, and/or clinical outcomes including all‐cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, or stroke during follow‐up.

Reported outcomes related to AA recurrence or freedom from AA, and/or clinical outcomes including all‐cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, or stroke during follow‐up.

Provided sufficient data to extract or derive event counts or proportions for at least one outcome of interest.

Provided sufficient data to extract or derive event counts or proportions for at least one outcome of interest.

Studies were excluded if they were case reports, editorials, expert opinions, review articles, conference abstracts without sufficient data, unpublished works, or studies reporting procedural or in‐hospital safety outcomes only without post‐discharge follow‐up. Study screening and selection were performed independently by two investigators (A.S. and N.D.). Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus with a third investigator (N.P.).

Data Extraction

Two investigators (AS and ND) independently performed data extraction using the Covidence software. Extracted information included the first author's name, publication year, study location, study design, total number of patients, age, sex, body mass index, the number of patients with paroxysmal and persistent AF, the number of patients with each amyloidosis type, the number of patients with recurrence or freedom from AA, the ablation technique used in ablation procedures, follow‐up period and complication events including stroke, heart failure hospitalization, and all‐cause mortality.

Definition of Atrial Arrhythmia Recurrence

AA recurrence was defined as any documented episode of AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia occurring beyond a 3‐month blanking period following catheter ablation, lasting ≥ 30 s, and confirmed by 12‐lead electrocardiography, ambulatory monitoring, or interrogation of implanted cardiac devices.

Outcomes of Interest

The primary outcome was freedom from AA following catheter ablation. Secondary outcomes included all‐cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and stroke during follow‐up.

Quality Assessment

Cohort studies were assessed for methodological quality using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates selection, comparability, and outcome domains through a maximum 9‐point scoring system for comparative cohort studies. For single‐arm ablation cohorts, two NOS items (selection of the non‐exposed cohort and comparability) were not applicable, reducing the maximum applicable score to 7 points (Table S2). Discrepancies between reviewers (AS and ND) were resolved through discussion and mutual agreement.

The certainty of evidence for each comparative outcome was additionally assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) framework. The starting certainty was Low for all outcomes, as all included studies were observational. Certainty was downgraded based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision. The full GRADE assessment is presented in the Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Pooled estimates were calculated using random‐effects models, given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity among included studies.

For single‐arm outcomes (AA recurrence, mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and stroke), pooled event proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the “metaprop” function from the meta package with logit transformation. For comparative studies reporting outcomes between catheter ablation and medical therapy, pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were calculated using random‐effects models. For comparative outcomes with three or fewer contributing studies, a Hartung–Knapp adjustment was applied to provide more conservative confidence intervals.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Higgins I2 statistic, with values > 75% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to AF subtype, amyloidosis subtype, ablation strategy, and follow‐up duration. Meta‐regression analyses were performed using the metafor package to explore potential sources of heterogeneity.

Although four studies (Donnellan et al., Kanazawa et al., Yakabe et al., and Tan et al.) provided adjusted or propensity‐matched effect estimates for the ablation vs. medical therapy comparison, these estimates were not pooled owing to heterogeneity in effect measures (hazard ratios vs. odds ratios), adjustment strategies (multivariable Cox regression vs. propensity score matching), and outcome definitions. The primary comparative analysis was therefore based on crude event‐count odds ratios, and this approach is acknowledged as a limitation.

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger's regression test when at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome.

Post hoc amyloid subtype–stratified subgroup analyses for comparative outcomes (all‐cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and stroke) were performed, stratifying studies into ATTR‐specific cohorts (Donnellan et al., Kanazawa et al., and Yakabe et al.) and non‐ATTR‐specific cohorts ([11], which identified amyloidosis using administrative codes without subtype differentiation). A leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was also performed for all‐cause mortality to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate.

Results

A total of 941 potentially relevant records were identified through the systematic database search. After removal of 81 duplicate records, 860 studies underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 829 studies were excluded due to irrelevance, leaving 31 studies for full‐text review.

Following full‐text assessment, 18 studies were excluded for the following reasons: Irrelevant outcomes (n = 9), conference abstracts with insufficient data (n = 1), and irrelevant study populations (n = 8). Ultimately, 13 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final systematic review and meta‐analysis (Figure 1).

Patient Characteristics

Across the 13 included studies, a total of 797 patients with CA and AF were included, of whom 430 patients (54.0%) underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation or cryoballoon ablation, and 367 patients (46.0%) were managed without ablation. Individual study sample sizes ranged from 7 to 236 patients. In studies reporting sex distribution, the population was predominantly male, with male proportions ranging from approximately 74% to 96%.

Across included studies, mean age ranged from approximately 63 to 81 years, with the majority of cohorts reporting a mean age in the early to mid‐70s. Amyloid subtype was reported in 526 patients, among whom 503 patients had transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR), and 23 patients had light‐chain amyloidosis (AL). Amyloid subtype was not specified in the remaining 271 patients.

Among the 430 patients undergoing catheter ablation, 131 patients (29.8%) had paroxysmal AF, 194 patients (44.1%) had persistent AF, and 114 patients (26.1%) had AF with subtype not specified. Several studies additionally included patients with atrial flutter or atrial tachycardia, either alone or in combination with AF.

With respect to ablation modality, 238 patients (55.3%) underwent radiofrequency ablation, while 21 patients (4.9%) underwent either radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation; ablation modality was not reported for 171 patients (39.8%). Reported follow‐up duration ranged from approximately 11.9 to 60 months (Table 1).

Primary Outcome

Across 12 studies comprising 312 patients, the pooled rate of freedom from atrial arrhythmia (AA) recurrence following catheter ablation was 50% (95% CI 37%–63%), with moderate to high heterogeneity (I2 = 71.7%) (Figure 2). The study by [11] was excluded from this analysis due to the absence of reported AA recurrence outcomes.

Freedom from Atrial Arrhythmia.

In subgroup analyses by amyloidosis subtype, among 152 patients with ATTR amyloidosis alone, the pooled freedom from AA rate was 49% (95% CI 41%–57%; I2 = 0%), whereas among 160 patients with combined ATTR and AL amyloidosis, the pooled rate was 50% (95% CI 30%–71%; I2 = 80.7%) (Figure 3). When stratified by duration of follow‐up, the pooled freedom from AA rate was 47% (95% CI 35%–59%; I2 = 63.4%) in 237 patients with ≥ 24 months of follow‐up and 54% (95% CI 22%–82%; I2 = 80.2%) in 75 patients with < 24 months of follow‐up (Figure 4).

Secondary Outcome

The all‐cause mortality analysis included 4 observational studies (Donnellan et al., Kanazawa et al., Yakabe et al., and Tan et al.) comprising 249 patients in the catheter ablation group and 367 patients in the medical therapy group. Catheter ablation was associated with lower odds of all‐cause mortality (OR 0.26; 95% CI 0.11–0.60; I2 = 18.4%) (Figure 5A). Leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis confirmed that no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled mortality estimate (Figure S2). Heart failure hospitalization was assessed in 3 studies (Kanazawa et al., Yakabe et al., and Tan et al.) comprising 225 ablation and 319 medical therapy patients, and no statistically significant difference was observed (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.03–2.63; I2 = 78.6%) (Figure 5B). Stroke was assessed in 3 studies (Donnellan et al., Yakabe et al., and Tan et al.) comprising 195 ablation and 262 medical therapy patients, and no statistically significant difference was observed (OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.18–3.45; I2 = 0%) (Figure 5C). Pool event rates among patients undergoing catheter ablation were 8% (95% CI 0.03‐0.21) for all‐cause mortality, 13% (95% CI 0.08‐0.20) for heart failure hospitalization, and 2% (95% CI 0.01‐0.09) for stroke (Figure 6).

In amyloid subtype–stratified subgroup analyses, catheter ablation remained associated with lower all‐cause mortality in ATTR‐specific cohorts (OR 0.21, 95% CI 0.06–0.75; I2 = 1.5%; Figure S3), with substantially lower statistical heterogeneity compared with the overall analysis. No statistically significant association was observed for heart failure hospitalization (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.00–50.58; I2 = 60.7%; Figure S4) or stroke (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.00–1292.21; I2 = 0%; Figure S5) in ATTR‐specific cohorts.

Meta Regression

Meta‐regression analyses assessed follow‐up duration and amyloidosis subtype as potential moderators of freedom from AA (Table SS1).

Follow‐up duration was also not a significant moderator (QM = 3.55, p = 0.170). Studies with follow‐up durations of less than 24 months (estimate = 0.64, p = 0.10) and those with follow‐up durations exceeding 24 months (estimate = 0.84, p = 0.07) showed no statistically significant differences in effect size compared with the reference category.

Amyloidosis subtype was not a significant moderator (QM = 0.87, p = 0.646). Studies including both ATTR and AL amyloidosis (estimate = 0.07, p = 0.90) and those in which amyloidosis subtype was ATTR only (estimate = 0.00, p = 0.99) showed no significant differences in effect size relative to the reference group.

Study Quality Assessment

Revised Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale scoring demonstrated a range of methodological quality across the 13 included studies, reflecting differences in study design and degree of confounder adjustment. Among comparative studies, scores ranged from 6 to 8 out of 9 applicable points: Five studies achieved 8/9 (Donnellan, Kanazawa, Oraii, Tan 2025, Yakabe), reflecting formal adjustment via propensity‐score matching or multivariable regression; two scored 7/9 (Barbhaiya, Dale); and one scored 6/9 [6]. Among single‐arm studies, Petzl achieved 7 of 7 applicable points; Sweat and Maury each scored 6/7; Black‐Maier scored 5/7; and Miyamoto scored 4/7, reflecting its design as a prevalence study with a small ablation subgroup. The revised NOS scores are presented in the Table S2. Certainty of evidence, assessed using the GRADE framework, was rated as Very Low for all comparative outcomes (all‐cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and stroke), based on serious risk of bias from retrospective non‐randomized design, serious inconsistency for outcomes with high I2, serious indirectness from heterogeneous amyloid subtypes and comparator definitions, and serious imprecision from wide confidence intervals and sparse event counts (Table S3).

Publication Bias

Egger's test showed no evidence of publication bias (t = 1.12, df = 10, p = 0.2895). Funnel plot inspection demonstrated no substantial asymmetry (estimated bias = 1.94, SE 1.73) (Figure S1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest systematic review and meta‐analysis to directly compare catheter ablation vs. medical therapy in patients with AF and CA while simultaneously evaluating arrhythmia‐related outcomes and major clinical endpoints, including mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and stroke. Our principal findings are threefold. First, freedom from AA recurrence following ablation was modest, with approximately half of patients remaining arrhythmia‐free during follow‐up, highlighting the challenges of rhythm control in this complex disease substrate. While this modest effect is slightly lower than the 60%–70% arrhythmia‐free survival typically reported for the general persistent AF population, it reflects real‐world clinical practice where success rates often approximate 50% [12, 13]. Second, catheter ablation was associated with lower odds of all‐cause mortality compared with non‐ablative medical management. Lastly, no statistically significant association was observed for heart failure hospitalization or stroke. All comparative effect estimates are derived from a small number of retrospective observational studies and should be interpreted cautiously regardless of their direction or statistical significance.

Freedom from Atrial Arrhythmias

The pooled freedom from AA recurrence following ablation was approximately 50% (95% CI 37%–63%), with substantial between‐study heterogeneity (I2 = 71.7%). This success rate is lower than that reported on AF ablation among the general population, of which single‐procedure success rates commonly range from 60%–70% and repeated ablations often improve AA survival [13, 14, 15]. These findings suggest that AA in the setting of amyloidosis represents a more treatment‐resistant substrate.

Several disease‐specific factors likely contribute to the reduced efficacy of ablation in this population. Patients with amyloidosis frequently present with advanced atrial remodeling, extensive fibrosis, atrial dilation, and conduction abnormalities, which, indeed, limit lesion durability [4, 16]. Further, in amyloidosis, the pathogenesis often extends beyond pulmonary vein triggers, non–pulmonary vein drivers, and extensive atrial scars, necessitating more extensive substrate modification [17].

The substantial heterogeneity across studies likely reflects differences in amyloidosis subtype, AF subtype, ablation strategy, degree of atrial remodeling and scarring, disease stage, operator experience, procedural endpoints, post‐ablation antiarrhythmic drug use, repeat ablation rate, rhythm monitoring intensity and follow‐up duration. In subgroup analyses, studies with longer follow‐up [6, 9, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23] demonstrated numerically lower arrhythmia‐free survival compared with those with shorter follow‐up, although this difference was not statistically significant. This pattern is consistent with the progressive nature of amyloid cardiomyopathy and the potential for late arrhythmia recurrence despite initial procedural success.

Mortality and Clinical Outcomes

The observed association between catheter ablation and lower odds of all‐cause mortality in our pooled analysis is notable in the context of the historically poor prognosis of CA. Current expert consensus supports rhythm‐control strategies in this population, with amiodarone and AF ablation commonly employed, while other antiarrhythmic agents are generally discouraged [24]. Amyloid infiltration leads to restrictive physiology and marked diastolic dysfunction, such that loss of atrioventricular synchrony and rapid ventricular rates may be poorly tolerated and complicate rate‐control strategies [25]. Accordingly, observational data suggest that AF is an adverse prognostic marker in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy [26], likely as a result of the aforementioned postulation. In addition, comparative data in amyloid cardiomyopathy indicate that rhythm‐control strategies are associated with improved clinical outcomes compared with rate‐control approaches [27]. These findings provide biological plausibility for the mortality benefit observed in association with catheter ablation.

Although the pooled analysis favored catheter ablation for heart failure hospitalization, no statistically significant association was observed, and this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of contributing studies and substantial heterogeneity between studies. No significant association was observed for stroke. In particular, the confidence intervals for the ATTR‐specific heart failure hospitalization and stroke subgroup analyses are extremely wide and non‐informative; these estimates should be considered hypothesis‐generating only. These findings likely reflect limited sample sizes, relatively short follow‐up durations in several included studies, and heterogeneity in outcome definitions. Notably, similar neutral effects on heart failure hospitalization have been reported in contemporary studies of AF ablation in patients with HFpEF, a population characterized by advanced atrial and ventricular myopathy [28]. Nonetheless, the consistency of point estimates across studies supports the need for confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

Limitations

This meta‐analysis has several important limitations that substantially constrain interpretation of its findings. First and most critically, all 13 included studies are observational and non‐randomized cohorts subject to major selection bias and indication bias. Patients selected for catheter ablation were likely younger, at an earlier disease stage, had better‐preserved cardiac function, were more clinically stable, and were more likely to be receiving disease‐modifying therapy than those managed medically. This differential selection means the observed association between catheter ablation and lower all‐cause mortality cannot be interpreted as evidence of a causal treatment effect. Residual confounding by unmeasured variables including frailty, systemic amyloid burden, and disease trajectory cannot be excluded. Second, the primary comparative analysis was based on crude event‐count odds ratios. Four studies provided adjusted or propensity‐matched effect estimates, but these reported different effect measures, adjustment strategies, and outcome definitions, precluding a pooled adjusted analysis. Although all four studies’ adjusted estimates are directionally consistent with the pooled crude result, the crude pooled analysis may overestimate the true association if unmeasured confounders systematically favor the ablation group. Third, disease‐modifying therapy represents a critical potential confounder that could not be adequately addressed. Tafamidis and other transthyretin‐directed therapies were introduced during the period covered by included studies (2016–2025) [29], and their use varied substantially across cohorts from 0% in pre‐tafamidis‐era studies to 84%–100% in more recent ATTR‐specific cohorts. Patients receiving disease‐modifying therapy may have had earlier‐stage disease, better functional status, and a higher likelihood of referral for catheter ablation. A formal sensitivity analysis stratified by disease‐modifying therapy use was not feasible owing to inconsistent and incomplete reporting across included studies; the available information is summarized in Table S4. Fourth, the certainty of evidence for all comparative outcomes is rated as Very Low per GRADE criteria, based on serious risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision. All comparative effect estimates should be interpreted with corresponding caution, and the available evidence is insufficient to support definitive clinical recommendations. Additional limitations include variations in definitions of arrhythmia recurrence, ablation technique, follow‐up duration, and rhythm monitoring intensity; limited event counts for heart failure hospitalization and stroke; and the inability to account for individual patient‐level covariates including amyloid stage and systemic disease burden.

Conclusions

In patients with cardiac amyloidosis, AF catheter ablation was associated with a modest arrhythmia‐free survival rate of approximately 50% and with lower odds of all‐cause mortality compared with medical therapy, based on crude event‐count odds ratios from selected observational cohorts. However, all contributing studies were observational and non‐randomized cohorts, and patients selected for ablation were likely younger and at an earlier disease stage than those managed medically. Catheter ablation may represent a reasonable rhythm‐control option in carefully selected patients with cardiac amyloidosis, but prospective controlled studies are required to establish the comparative efficacy and safety of this approach.
