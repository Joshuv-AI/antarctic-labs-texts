Decoding Non-Traumatic Acute Abdomen in Children: Insights from the Emergency Department of a Tertiary Hospital in Mogadishu, Somalia
Authors: Salad Mohamud Mohamed Hassan, Nasteho Mohamed Sheikh Omar, Marian Muse Osman, Hussein Hassan Mohamed, Hassan Adan Ali Adan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAcute abdomen is characterized by acute and severe abdominal pain caused by intra-abdominal disease and often requires surgical intervention. Accurate diagnosis is difficult because of symptom similarity and diverse causes, such as appendicitis, intussusception, and mesenteric adenitis, which can present with overlapping symptoms in pediatric patients. The aim of this study is to analyze the causes, diagnosis, and outcome of non-traumatic acute abdomen in pediatric patients at a tertiary hospital in Mogadishu.MethodologyA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted from January 1 to December 31, 2023, including 92 children aged 0–18 who presented with non-traumatic acute abdomen and received emergency surgical intervention. All definitive diagnoses were confirmed based on intraoperative findings. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0, incorporating univariate and bivariate analyses with a significance level of p < 0.05.ResultsOut of 6390 pediatric emergency visits, 92 children (1.4%) were diagnosed with acute abdomen. The main reasons were appendicitis (53.3%), intestinal obstruction (16.3%), and perforation (15.2%). Appendicitis predominantly impacted school-aged children (72.7%) and adolescents (66.7%), whereas intussusception was more prevalent in newborns (40.0%) and toddlers (33.3%). Fever was strongly correlated with appendicitis (59.6%, p < 0.001) and perforation (25.0%, p < 0.001). Regarding diagnostic performance, plain radiography was negative in 71.4% of appendicitis cases, whereas ultrasonography demonstrated a significantly higher diagnostic yield of 52.1% (p = 0.015). The median duration of hospitalization was 6 days, with a low mortality rate of 2.2% (p = 0.741).ConclusionThe study demonstrates a significant incidence of appendicitis and other surgical emergencies in this population. We recommend early ultrasonography over plain radiography to improve diagnostic precision and prevent delays in pediatric surgical care in Somalia.

Introduction

Pediatric acute abdomen is a clinical condition characterized by sudden onset or rapidly progressive abdominal pain requiring urgent intervention.1,2 It is a major health problem and requires careful and detailed assessment for correct diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment.3 Unlike adults, children have different ways of experiencing and expressing pain because of their different physiology and psychology.4

The emergency nature of these cases poses a special diagnostic challenge to the health care providers, compounded by the inability of young children to describe accurately the nature of their symptoms or to cooperate during physical examination.5 Due to varying developmental stages, communication barriers, and heightened anxiety during medical procedures, pediatric patients often struggle to convey their pain effectively.4 Therefore, evaluation of abdominal pain in children involves particular difficulties due to the often non-specific clinical pictures and the poor verbal skills in the younger age groups, making a diagnostic delay more probable.6

Globally, surgical conditions account for approximately 6–12% of pediatric hospital admissions in sub-Saharan Africa, with higher rates reported in some urban centers.7,8 The overall prevalence of non-traumatic abdominal conditions (NTA) in pediatric patients ranges from 5.1% to 8.3%. In developing countries, pediatric surgical emergencies are associated with significantly higher morbidity and mortality compared with elective procedures.9 In children with acute abdominal conditions, factors such as late clinical presentation, limited intensive care capacity, and socioeconomic barriers often exacerbate adverse outcomes.9,10

The etiologies of pediatric acute abdomen vary by age and are broadly categorized as requiring medical therapy versus urgent surgical intervention.11 These non-traumatic causes vary from benign, self-limiting conditions to serious, life-threatening emergencies.12–14

Predominant pediatric surgical diagnoses include acute appendicitis, incarcerated or strangulated hernias, intussusception, intestinal obstruction, volvulus, and ovarian torsion.2

While abdominal pain and vomiting are the primary presenting symptoms, clinical signs such as guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness, and abdominal distention remain the cornerstones of emergency triage, particularly where advanced imaging is selectively utilized.2,15,16

Despite its high prevalence and clinical importance, non-traumatic acute abdomen in pediatric patients in Somalia has not been previously explored. This study aims to fill this gap by assessing causes, diagnosis, and outcomes of non-traumatic acute abdomen in pediatric patients admitted to the emergency department at a tertiary teaching hospital in Mogadishu.

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted at the Emergency Department of Mogadishu Somali–Türkiye Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital (MSTH). MSTH is the primary multidisciplinary referral and tertiary teaching hospital in Mogadishu, the capital city of Somalia.

Study Period and Population

The study period spanned from January 1 to December 31, 2023. The study population consisted of 92 consecutive children aged 0–18 years who presented to the emergency department with non-traumatic acute abdomen and subsequently underwent emergency abdominal surgery during this period. The study only included patients who had emergency surgery. As a result, intraoperative findings, the surgical “gold standard” were used to determine the final diagnosis for all 92 cases, enabling a direct link with preoperative clinical and radiological evaluations.

While this focus excludes conservatively managed cases, the sample size of 92 participants represents a consecutive census of all eligible pediatric surgical emergencies at our facility during the study year. This provides a representative cohort for this specific clinical setting without the need for an external power-based sample size calculation. The study excluded children whose caregivers refused surgical intervention despite clinical necessity, along with cases possessing incomplete medical records.

Data Collection

Data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital’s electronic information system. The gathered data encompassed demographics, clinical presentations, patterns of acute abdomen, radiological investigations, laboratory assessments, admission status, duration of hospital stay, and patient outcomes.

Data Analysis

Prior to analysis, all extracted data were screened for completeness to ensure data integrity. Only cases with comprehensive clinical, radiological, and operative records were included; no significant missing data were identified for the primary variables in this cohort. We used SPSS version 26.0 to analyze the data. We used univariate analysis on all variables. For categorical data, we showed frequencies and percentages, and for continuous data, we showed means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on how they were distributed. We used the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for bivariate analysis, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study received ethical approval from the MSTH local ethical review board (Approval No. MSTH/10264). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all enrolled children prior to inclusion in the study.

Results

In our analysis of 6390 pediatric emergency department visits, we identified 92 children (1.4%) with acute abdomen necessitating immediate intervention. The ages of these children varied from newborn to 18 years. For clarity, we grouped them into common developmental stages: infants, toddlers, preschoolers, school-aged children, and adolescents.

Acute abdomen was primarily noted in older children, with school-age children (6–12 years) and adolescents (13–18 years) each representing 35.9% of our research population. Infants (0–12 months) represented 16.3% of cases, while preschoolers (3–5 years) accounted for 8.7%, and toddlers (1–3 years) comprised 3.3%. Regarding sex distribution, boys were more likely to be affected than girls, making up about 72.8% of the cases. Girls made up 27.2% of the cases, which means that there were about 2.68 boys for every girl. The children had various symptoms. Vomiting was the predominant symptom, occurring in 81.9% of cases, followed by fever in 56.5%. Abdominal pain was reported in 100% of cases, with 57.6% suggesting localized pain and 42.4% expressing diffuse pain. Furthermore, abdominal distention was observed in 46.7% of patients, whereas diarrhea occurred in 10.9%.

Imaging was employed to ascertain the etiology of acute abdominal pain. In 45.7% of cases, plain X-rays came back negative, and 32.6% of cases showed intestinal dilation. Additional findings included air beneath the diaphragm (3.3%) and air-fluid levels (18.5%). Ultrasonography was the main way to make a diagnosis, used in 79.3% of cases, while abdominal CT scans were used in 20.7% of cases. While these imaging findings guided clinical management, this study focuses on the descriptive utility and diagnostic yield of these tools within our clinical context rather than a formal assessment of diagnostic accuracy metrics (such as sensitivity or specificity).

Most of the children (84.8%) required admission to the hospital ward, while 15.2% needed more intensive care in the ICU. The typical hospital stay was around 6 days (IQR, 5–10). In the evaluation of the underlying causes of non-traumatic acute abdomen, appendicitis was the most common, accounting for 53.3% of cases among the children studied. Intestinal obstruction was another major cause, making up 16.3% of cases, and perforation was another major cause, making up 15.2%. Intussusception, which is a condition where a part of the intestine folds into another section, was responsible for 9.8% of cases. Volvulus, torsion, and incarcerated hernia were also uncommon, making up less than 10% of cases each (Figure 1). Appendicitis is most prevalent in school-aged children (72.7%) and teenagers (66.7%), whereas intussusception predominantly affects infants (40.0%) and toddlers (33.3%). There exists no significant statistical correlation between diagnosis and age group (p = 0.688), indicating that acute surgical abdomen requires vigilant monitoring across all pediatric age categories. Males had a higher rate of appendicitis (55.2%) and intestinal perforation (16.4%), but the difference between the sexes was not statistically significant (p = 0.683). This suggests that sex did not play a role in determining the specific surgical causes in this study. Fever is significantly correlated with appendicitis (59.6%) and perforation (25.0%) (p < 0.001), indicating the importance of systemic inflammatory markers in diagnosing these particular conditions.

Diarrhea is associated with intussusception (80.0%) (p < 0.001), serving as a significant clinical indicator for this condition. Abdominal distention is predominantly associated with intestinal obstruction (34.9%); however, localized abdominal pain signifies appendicitis (71.7%), in contrast to the diffuse pain noted in intestinal obstruction (35.9%) (p < 0.001), thereby confirming that physical examination findings are a key distinguishing factor in the emergency setting.

Radiographic findings varied by diagnosis: negative x-ray findings are common in appendicitis (71.4%), air-fluid levels in intestinal obstruction (52.9%), and air under the diaphragm in perforation (66.7%) (p < 0.001), indicating a significant association between x-ray pattern and diagnosis. Ultrasonography provided a definitive diagnostic yield for appendicitis (52.1%) and intestinal obstruction (19.2%) (p = 0.015), demonstrating its high diagnostic yield as a first-line modality even in resource-constrained environments.

ICU admissions were highest for intestinal obstruction (50%), whereas ward admissions are typical for appendicitis (59.0%) (p < 0.001), identifying intestinal obstruction as a marker for higher clinical severity and the need for more intensive surveillance. Despite these complexities, observed mortality rates were low overall (2.2%); there were no statistically significant differences in mortality between diagnostic groups (p = 0.741), suggesting consistent outcomes regardless of the underlying surgical etiology. Discussion

This review of 6390 pediatric emergency department visits offers important insights into the etiological assessment of non-traumatic acute abdomen in children. Of these cases, 92 (1.4%) required urgent surgical intervention—a slightly lower rate than the 1.7% previously reported in centers from North Central Nigeria.5 The data indicate that acute abdomen is most prevalent in school-aged children and adolescents, each comprising 35.9% of cases, and this aligns with findings from Buddhist Tzu Chi General Hospital in Taiwan, which similarly reported a higher incidence in these age groups.15 A male predominance was noted (72.8%), especially in appendicitis (p = 0.683), aligning with Ethiopian tertiary hospital data that likewise highlight a higher incidence of acute abdominal conditions in boys.9 The male-to-female ratio in this study, approximately 2.6:1, is comparable to ratios reported in North Central Nigeria (2.5:1)5 and at Moi University, Eldoret, Kenya (2.1:1),10 underscoring a consistent gender disparity in acute abdominal conditions. In our study, the most common presenting symptoms of non-traumatic acute abdomen were abdominal pain (92%) and vomiting (81.9%). This is similar to the research done at Chamarajanagar Institute of Medical Sciences, Karnataka, India, where these symptoms were also found to be important indicators.3 A notable observation in our study was that 57.6% of cases had localized abdominal pain, which was strongly associated with appendicitis (p < 0.001) and can be considered a reliable clinical marker for early surgical consultation in our setting. This is in accordance with findings from Buddhist Tzu Chi General Hospital, Taiwan, which also emphasizes the importance of localization of pain in diagnosis.15 Fever was present in 56.5% of cases, suggesting an inflammatory process such as perforation or appendicitis (p < 0.001), which is consistent with the findings from Buddhist Tzu Chi General Hospital, Taiwan.15

In our study diarrhea was significantly associated with intussusception (80.0%, p < 0.001), although it was less common. This high statistical significance underscores the clinical value of identifying gastrointestinal symptoms early to prevent complications, emphasizing the importance for clinicians to carefully evaluate gastrointestinal symptoms. In our study, ultrasonography was used in 79.3% of cases, which is the most used method of diagnosis and highlights its usefulness in pediatric emergency evaluations. This data is consistent with the findings of J.N.M. Hospital in Aligarh, India, where ultrasonography is recommended as a first-line imaging modality because of its safety and efficacy.17 With a diagnostic yield of 52.1% in our cohort, ultrasonography was found to be an important, radiation-free, and readily available tool for real-time evaluation, particularly in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, the high proportion of negative plain X-rays in our study (45.7%) highlights the limitations of X-ray imaging, which is similar to the findings of the pediatric surgery division at J.N.M.C. and further emphasizes the need for a robust imaging protocol.18 The high yield of negative radiographs suggests that plain X-ray is an unreliable modality to rule out surgical conditions, and we strongly advocate for a prioritization of ultrasonography to improve diagnostic accuracy and avert delays in low-resource settings. In 19 (20.7%) cases where ultrasound failed to provide a conclusive diagnosis, abdominal CT scans were utilized in our study, consistent with practices at Changhua Christian Hospital, Taiwan.19

Appendicitis was the leading cause of acute abdomen in our study, representing 53.3% of cases. This finding is comparable with previous data, including 74% at KASCH in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia,10 and 29% at Sir Ganga Ram Hospital in New Delhi,20 emphasizing its significance as the most prevalent surgical emergency in children. Our study demonstrated age-related trends in pediatric acute abdomen. Intussusception was the major cause in infants (40.0%); however, appendicitis was the most common in school-aged children (72.7%) and adolescents (66.7%). These results are consistent with those published by Kenyatta National Hospital (KNH) in Nairobi, Kenya, where intussusception (25.7%) and appendicitis (30%) were the most common surgical reasons in infants and children over the age of six.21 The overall mortality rate observed in this study was 2.2%, which is comparable to the 2% mortality rate reported at TASH, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia,6 but lower than the 6.45% mortality rate reported at the regional hospital of Togo.22 Finally, the median length of stay was 6 days, compared to 4.8 days in the Ethiopian tertiary hospital.9

Study Limitations

Methodological Limitations: Information bias may be due to retrospective methodology and reliance on emergency paperwork due to lack of clinical data. The study was done in one site of a large tertiary hospital, and the results may not accurately reflect the spectrum of pediatric emergency care available in Somalia.

Selection and Generalizability: Our sample may include patients with better access to transportation or higher acuity cases and therefore may not be fully generalizable to the larger population or primary health care settings.

Analytical Scope: Only surgical cases were included, so it was not possible to calculate formal diagnostic metrics such as sensitivity and specificity. Instead, we used diagnostic yield to assess the therapeutic utility of our regimens.

Systemic Factors: In interpreting the findings, it is important to appreciate the unique context of Somalia, which is characterized by resource constraints, security concerns, and systemic delays in clinical presentation.

Our results were probably influenced by the fragility of the health system in Somalia: shortage of personnel, limited access to imaging, and security concerns increased the reliance on bedside ultrasonography, while suboptimal referral networks and transportation barriers likely led to a sample biased toward patients able to reach the facilities. We should interpret our findings in the context of this specific healthcare setting. Systemic issues such as delayed clinical presentation and health literacy inequalities likely explain the high acuity of the 92 cases requiring urgent intervention. Recognizing these resource constraints helps explain why ultrasonography, with an overall diagnostic yield of 79.3% in our series and a diagnostic yield of 52.1% for appendicitis specifically, is a vital diagnostic cornerstone in Mogadishu and directly contributes to the favorable survival rates (97.8%) observed.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that pediatric acute abdomen in Mogadishu constitutes a critical condition requiring prompt identification and intervention. Appendicitis was the most common cause, especially in school-aged children, while intussusception was more common in infants. Symptoms varied by cause, but abdominal pain, vomiting, and fever were the most common. Imaging, mainly point-of-care ultrasonography (POCUS), with CT used only when ultrasound results were unclear, showed clear clinical use and helped with management. However, the study was retrospective and only included patients who had surgery, so a formal assessment of comparative diagnostic accuracy is not possible. Most children needed to be hospitalized, but the results were excellent, with a 97.8% survival rate. These results show how important it is to consider age when making a diagnosis, to use imaging wisely, and to act quickly. To improve outcomes in the region, policy priorities should focus on standardizing POCUS training, integrating ultrasound into triage pathways, and strengthening referral networks. Future research should concentrate on prospective, multi-center or community-based studies throughout Somalia to systematically assess the diagnostic efficacy of standardized protocols in primary triage.
