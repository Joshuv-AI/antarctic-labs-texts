The Effect of Dicloxacillin Administration on Pharmacokinetics of Narrow Therapeutic Window Drugs: Phenytoin and Warfarin
Authors: Chanan Shaul, Waseem Mujahed, Ibrahim Idries, Maor Wanounou, Meir Bialer, Yoseph Caraco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dicloxacillin is a penicillinase‐resistant beta‐lactam antibiotic and potent activator of the Pregnane X receptor (PXR), known to induce CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4 activity. Clinical data suggest it reduces anticoagulation in warfarin‐treated patients and increases the risk of thromboembolic events. This open‐label, sequential drug–drug interaction study quantitatively evaluated the effect of dicloxacillin on the pharmacokinetics of warfarin and phenytoin, two prototype CYP2C9 substrates with narrow therapeutic indices, and possible impact of CYP2C9 polymorphisms on the extent of this interaction. Twenty‐eight healthy non‐smoker subjects, 15 carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1, and 13 carriers of a single CYP2C9*2 or CYP2C9*3 allele, received single doses of warfarin (20 mg) and 1 week later, phenytoin (300 mg) before and during a 21‐day course of dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily. Plasma concentration of warfarin enantiomers and phenytoin and urine concentration of p‐HPPH were measured using validated HPLC methods. Warfarin pharmacodynamics was monitored via serial INR measurements for up to 120 hours. Dicloxacillin increased oral clearance of (S)‐warfarin and (R)‐warfarin by 53.2% and 63.2%, respectively (P < 0.001). The area under the INR‐time curve decreased by 15.5% (P < 0.001). Phenytoin oral clearance and p‐HPPH formation clearance increased by 42.4% and 40.5%, respectively (P < 0.001). CYP2C9 induction was significantly greater in carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype and INR reduction was more pronounced among VKORC1 AA haplotype carriers. Dicloxacillin substantially induces the metabolism of warfarin and phenytoin. Whenever dicloxacillin is initiated, the dose of CYP2C9 substrates characterized by a narrow therapeutic window should be increased and drug levels and/or pharmacodynamic response should be closely monitored.

CYP2C9 is involved in the oxidative metabolism of approximately 16% of commonly used drugs. 1 , 2 The activity of CYP2C9 can be modulated by the coadministration of enzyme inducers such as rifampicin and carbamazepine, or specific inhibitors, including fluconazole, and amiodarone. 3 , 4 Some CYP2C9 substrates, such as warfarin and phenytoin, are characterized by a narrow therapeutic index (NTI). For such drugs, the coadministration of CYP2C9 inducers or inhibitors may lead to subtherapeutic levels or concentration‐related drug toxicity, respectively. In the case of warfarin, the clinical significance of CYP2C9 induction or inhibition is translated into insufficient or excessive anticoagulation, respectively. Similarly, induction of CYP2C9 activity may lead to subtherapeutic phenytoin plasma concentrations and the emergence of seizures, on the one hand, whereas inhibition of CYP2C9 activity may result in elevated plasma concentrations and neurotoxicity.

Dicloxacillin is a penicillinase‐resistant beta‐lactam, used primarily for the treatment of skin, soft tissue, and bone infections caused by Staphylococcus aureus. 5 As a narrow‐spectrum antibiotic, it is commonly used to treat mild infections in many countries worldwide. Several case reports, small case series, and cross‐sectional population studies have suggested that the administration of dicloxacillin to patients on chronic warfarin therapy may be associated with reduced anticoagulation and a higher warfarin dose requirement. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Furthermore, results obtained in a propensity score cohort study implied that the addition of dicloxacillin to patients treated with warfarin for atrial fibrillation or heart valve replacement is associated with approximately a twofold increase in the risk for ischemic stroke or systemic embolism. 8

The mechanism of dicloxacillin–warfarin interaction has been the subject of research in recent years. Dicloxacillin is considered a potent activator of Pregnane X receptor (PXR), second only to rifampicin, a powerful inducer of several nuclear receptors, including PXR. 14 Using human cell lines, it has been shown that PXR activation is paralleled by enhanced intestinal and hepatic expression and activity of CYP3A4. 9 , 14 , 15 Finally, the intake of dicloxacillin for 10 days has been shown to enhance the activity of CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4 as reflected by the increased formation clearance of tolbutamide, omeprazole, and midazolam metabolites, respectively. 16 The combined evidence from these studies suggests that the reduction in the anticoagulant effect of warfarin following dicloxacillin administration is primarily due to reduced warfarin plasma concentration, mediated by induction of CYP2C9, and possibly CYP3A4. However, despite the abundance of research concerning this interaction, direct information on warfarin pharmacokinetics following dicloxacillin administration is scarce. Thus, the purpose of the current study was to evaluate the impact of dicloxacillin administration on the oral clearance of warfarin enantiomers and the extent of anticoagulation exerted by warfarin. Similarly, we aimed to assess the influence of dicloxacillin intake on the pharmacokinetics of phenytoin, a prototype substrate of CYP2C9, which, like warfarin, is identified as a NTI drug. The effect of dicloxacillin on the extent of warfarin anticoagulation exhibited substantial interindividual variability. 10 , 12 All previous studies, except one, 16 did not account for CYP2C9 genetic polymorphisms as a possible determinant of the extent of the dicloxacillin–warfarin interaction. Thus, a secondary objective of the study was to compare the effect of dicloxacillin administration on warfarin and phenytoin metabolism between carriers of the CYP2C9*1/*1 wild‐type genotype and subjects carrying a single CYP2C9*2 or CYP2C9*3 variant allele.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was conducted in the Clinical Pharmacology Unit affiliated with the Division of Medicine at the Hadassah‐Hebrew University Medical Center.

Subjects

Potential subjects were recruited through an advertisement posted on the university campus. Eligible subjects had to be in good health, as documented by a health statement from their primary physician, not taking any chronic medication, non‐smokers, and between 18 and 50 years old. Subjects with a history of penicillin or cephalosporin allergy were excluded from the study. Prior to enrollment, CYP2C9 genotyping was performed by direct sequencing in order to allow recruitment of 15 subjects carrying CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype and an additional 13 subjects carrying either CYP2C9*1/*2 or CYP2C9*1/*3 genotypes. In our previous study, the mean (±SD) oral clearance of (S)‐warfarin among carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 and carriers of a single CYP2C9*2 or CYP2C9*3 variant allele was 217 ± 82 mL/h. 17 Thus, a study of 26 subjects may detect a difference of > 55 mL/h in the oral clearance (CL/F) of (S)‐warfarin, with 90% power and a two‐sided significance level of 5%.

Study design

The study was conducted as an open‐label, sequential drug–drug interaction study (Figure 1 ). Following an overnight fast, participants received a single dose of 20 mg warfarin and, at least 1 week later, a single dose of 300 mg phenytoin with 200 mL of tap water. Fasting was continued for four additional hours post‐drug intake. Periodic blood samples were obtained immediately before and at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 24, 30, 36, 48, 54, 60, 72, and 96 hours post drug administration. An additional blood sample was obtained 120 hours after warfarin administration. Plasma was immediately separated and kept frozen at −20°C until analysis. On the warfarin study day, additional plasma samples were obtained for the evaluation of international normalized ratio (INR) just before and at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 96, and 120 hours post‐warfarin intake. Following phenytoin administration, subjects were requested to collect their urine at pre‐defined intervals for 96 hours. Immediately after obtaining the last 96 hours post‐phenytoin plasma sample, the subjects were prescribed dicloxacillin, 500 mg q.i.d. for the next 21 days. Single doses of warfarin (20 mg) and phenytoin (300 mg) were again given on days 10 and 17 of dicloxacillin, respectively, and the same blood sampling and urine collection procedure was repeated. CYP2C9 induction by dicloxacillin is fully established by day 10 of dosing, 16 so both warfarin and phenytoin were administered within the plateau of the induction effect. The 7‐day washout interval between warfarin and phenytoin administration ensures that more than four half‐lives of (S)‐warfarin would have elapsed irrespective of CYP2C9 genotype, rendering residual warfarin concentrations negligible and precluding any pharmacokinetic interference between the two probe drugs. To avoid possible interference with the absorption process, subjects were instructed to omit the dicloxacillin morning dose on the warfarin and phenytoin study days.

Study design scheme: sequential open label drug–drug interaction study (N = 28). Single doses of warfarin (20 mg) and phenytoin (300 mg) were given at baseline, ≥ 1 week apart. Blood samples were obtained periodically for pharmacokinetics and (for warfarin) pharmacodynamics (INR) assessment. Immediately after the 96‐h phenytoin sampling, a 21‐day course of dicloxacillin (DCX, 500 mg q.i.d.) was initiated. The probe drugs re‐administered on DCX days 10 and 17 using the same sampling schedules.

Analysis of plasma (S)‐warfarin and (R)‐warfarin

The analysis of (S)‐warfarin and (R)‐warfarin enantiomers plasma concentration was performed using an HPLC method as described previously, with some modifications. 18 The system consisted of an e2695 Separation Module (Waters, Milford, MA) equipped with a 2,489 UV/visible detector (Waters). Separation was achieved using CHIRAL ART Cellulose‐SC, Classical Analytical HPLC Column (4.6 mm Inner Diameter), S‐5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm (YMC America, Devens, MA). The detection limit for both enantiomers was 30 ng/mL, and the intraday and interday coefficients of variation were 1.7% and 3.4% for warfarin enantiomer concentration, respectively.

Analysis of p‐HPPH in urine and plasma phenytoin

The concentration of plasma phenytoin and urinary p‐HPPH were measured by two separate high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) methods developed in our laboratory. 19

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics analysis

The pharmacokinetics of (R)‐warfarin, (S)‐warfarin, and phenytoin were evaluated by a non‐compartmental model using Phoenix WinNonlin software (Certara, Princeton, NJ). Elimination half‐life was derived from the terminal slope of the log drug plasma concentration vs. time curve. The area under the plasma drug concentration‐time curve from time zero until 96 hours post dosing for phenytoin (PAUC0→96h) and 120 hours for warfarin enantiomers (WRAUC0→120h and WSAUC0→120h) was calculated using the linear trapezoidal method and extrapolated to infinity (WRAUC0→∞ and WSAUC0→∞ for warfarin enantiomers; PAUC0→∞ for phenytoin) by adding the ratio of the last measured concentration (C 120/96) to the respective elimination rate constant (K elim). The oral clearance of (R)‐warfarin (RCL/F) and (S)‐warfarin (SCL/F) was derived from the ratio of warfarin dose (half of the racemic dose) to WRAUC0→∞ and WSAUC0→∞ respectively. Similarly, phenytoin oral clearance (PCL/F) was derived from the ratio of phenytoin dose to PAUC0→∞. The formation clearance of p‐HPPH (CLp‐HPPH) was derived from the molar ratio of p‐HPPH excreted in the urine during 96 hours of the study period (Up‐HPPH) divided by PAUC0→96h. Warfarin pharmacodynamics was assessed by calculating the area under the INR‐time curve until 120 hours (AUCINR0→120h) and the observed maximal INR value (INRMAX).

CYP2C9 and VKORC1 genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral leukocytes using the traditional phenol‐chloroform extraction procedure. CYP2C9 genetic analysis was performed by direct sequencing of all 9 exons with surrounding intronic boundaries (The Center for Genomic Technologies, The Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Givat Ram). The analysis of VKORC1 haplotype group combinations was performed by identifying the 16:31093557C>T (6484C>T) polymorphism through direct sequencing of the relevant region on chromosome 16.

Statistical analysis

Warfarin and phenytoin pharmacokinetics and warfarin pharmacodynamics results before and during dicloxacillin intake were compared using paired Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon Signed‐Rank test as appropriate. The change in the same parameters after dicloxacillin administration was compared between carriers of the CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype and carriers of either CYP2C9*1/*2 or CYP2C9*1/*3 using Student's t‐test for independent groups or the Mann–Whitney test, as appropriate. The change in AUCINR0→120h and INRMAX among carriers of different VKORC1 haplotypes was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed, when justified, by the Mann–Whitney test. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (IBM® SPSS® Statistics, Chicago, IL, version 26). Data are presented as mean (±SD), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hadassah University Hospital. Following a detailed explanation of the study characteristics and prior to enrollment, all participants provided written consent to participate in the study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00162474).

RESULTS

Subjects

All subjects completed the different parts of the study. Subjects' demographic details and VKORC1 genotype for the entire cohort and for each CYP2C9 genotype study group are presented in Table S1 . The variant allele group consisted of eight carriers of CYP2C9*1/*2 genotype and five carriers of CYP2C9*1/*3 genotype. No significant differences were noted in age, weight, body mass index (BMI), male to female ratio, and VKORC1 haplotype between carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype and heterozygote carriers of CYP2C9 variant allele (Table S1 ).

Warfarin pharmacokinetics

The pharmacokinetic parameters before and after dicloxacillin administration are presented in Table 1 and Figure 2 . The plasma concentrations of warfarin enantiomers over time before and after dicloxacillin administration are described in Figure 3 a,b [(R)‐Warfarin] and Figure 3 c,d [(S)‐warfarin]. Dicloxacillin administration was associated with significant increases in WRCL/F and WSCL/F by 63.2 ± 23.1% and by 53.2 ± 20.5%, respectively (P < 0.001). The absolute increase in (S)‐Warfarin oral clearance caused by dicloxacillin administration was significantly greater among carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype as compared to carriers of a single CYP2C9*2 or CYP2C9*3 variant allele (P < 0.03). Warfarin pharmacodynamics

The administration of dicloxacillin was associated with a significant 15.5 ± 8.8% (P < 0.001) decrease in AUCINR0→120h and a significant 22.3 ± 10.6% decrease in INRMAX (Table 1 , Figure 4 and Figure S1 ). The extent of warfarin anticoagulant effect did not vary between carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 and those carrying a single CYP2C9*2 or CYP2C9*3 variant allele (Table S2 ). However, VKORC1 genotype was significantly correlated with the decrease caused by dicloxacillin in both AUCINR0→120h (P < 0.04) and INRMAX (P < 0.04) (Figure S2 ). Thus, the decrease in AUCINR0→120h was significantly greater in carriers of the most sensitive VKORC1 AA haplotype as compared to VKORC1 AB heterozygotes (P < 0.04) or carriers of the least sensitive VKORC1 BB haplotype (P < 0.02). Similarly, the decline in INRMAX was significantly greater in carriers of VKORC1 AA haplotype as compared to carriers of VKORC1 AB (P < 0.02) or VKORC1 BB (P < 0.04) haplotypes. The extent of dicloxacillin effect on warfarin anticoagulation stratified by combined CYP2C9‐VKORC1 genotype is presented in Table S3 . The small sample size in some genotypes did not allow a meaningful statistical comparison. However, it was still apparent that the decrease in AUCINR0→120h and INRMAX was most prominent in carriers of the most sensitive VKORC1 AA genotype irrespective of CYP2C9 genotype.

Phenytoin pharmacokinetics

Phenytoin plasma concentrations over time before and after dicloxacillin administration are described in Figure 3 e,f . Phenytoin pharmacokinetic parameters before and after dicloxacillin administration are presented in Table 2 and Figure 5 . Following the administration of dicloxacillin, PCL/F was significantly increased by 42.4 ± 21.7% (P < 0.001). In parallel with the increase in PCL/F after dicloxacillin, the area under phenytoin plasma concentration–time curve (PAUC0→∞) was significantly decreased by 27.9 ± 12.8% (P < 0.001), and phenytoin elimination half‐life was shortened by 22.4 ± 14.3% (P < 0.001). The molar excretion of p‐HPPH over 96 hours was unaffected by dicloxacillin (P > 0.7) (Table 2 ). However, dicloxacillin administration was associated with a significant mean (±SD) rise of 40.5 ± 25.0% in the formation clearance of p‐HPPH (CLp‐HPPH) (P < 0.001). No significant differences were noted between carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 and CYP2C9 heterozygotes in the extent of dicloxacillin effects on PAUC0→∞ (P > 0.1), phenytoin C max (P > 0.7), and urinary excretion of p‐HPPH (P > 0.5) (Table S4 ). However, the increase in p‐HPPH formation clearance after dicloxacillin was significantly greater in the former group (4.34 ± 2.65 mL/min vs. 2.51 ± 1.04 mL/min, respectively; P < 0.03). The absolute increase in p‐HPPH formation was numerically greater among carriers of CYP2C9*1/*2 as compared to carriers of CYP2C9*1/*3, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (2.70 ± 0.88 vs. 2.20 ± 1.30 mL/min, respectively, P > 0.4). In addition, the increase in phenytoin clearance following dicloxacillin was numerically greater in carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype as compared to CYP2C9 heterozygotes (14.9 ± 8.6 mL/min vs. 10.5 ± 4.6 mL/min, respectively, P = 0.058). In contrast, shortening of phenytoin half‐life following dicloxacillin was significantly smaller in carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 as compared to CYP2C9 heterozygotes (2.49 ± 2.42 h vs. 4.54 ± 2.53 h, P < 0.03, respectively) (Table S4 ).

DISCUSSION

The administration of dicloxacillin resulted in a significant increase in the clearance of (R)‐warfarin, (S)‐warfarin, and phenytoin. Furthermore, following the administration of dicloxacillin, the formation clearance of phenytoin main metabolite, p‐HPPH, was significantly enhanced. Previous studies have shown that the addition of dicloxacillin on top of chronic warfarin treatment results in a substantial rise in warfarin dose requirement, a decrease in the extent of its anticoagulation effect, coupled with approximately a twofold increase in the risk of thrombotic events and systemic embolization. 6 , 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The presumed mechanism for these observations was attributed to the ability of dicloxacillin to enhance the activity of several cytochrome P450 isoforms and, in particular, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 that mediate the oxidative metabolism of (S)‐ and (R)‐warfarin, respectively. 16 However, a thorough literature search revealed only one case report describing a 25% decrease in (S)‐warfarin plasma concentration and a 20% decrease in (R)‐warfarin plasma concentration on day 5 after initiation of dicloxacillin in a single patient. 12 To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to describe in detail the changes in warfarin pharmacokinetics and anticoagulant effect caused by co‐administration of dicloxacillin. Furthermore, the current study is the first to describe the effect of dicloxacillin administration on the pharmacokinetics of phenytoin, another drug characterized as a narrow therapeutic window drug. Thus, this study provides important additional knowledge that could not be inferred from the findings of previous studies 16 that will assist clinicians in managing drug–drug interactions in which dicloxacillin is the perpetrator, particularly when the victim drug has a NTI.

Dicloxacillin is a semisynthetic isoxazolyl penicillin prescribed in certain countries as the drug of choice for the treatment of uncomplicated skin and soft tissue infections caused by methicillin‐susceptible Staphylococcus aureus. 5 Dicloxacillin is a potent activator of PXR, a nuclear receptor that regulates the transcription of several cytochrome P450 isoforms. In vitro incubation of dicloxacillin with a human hepatocyte cell line resulted in a significant increase in transcription activity and increased mRNA and protein expression of several PXR‐dependent cytochrome P450 isoforms. 9 , 14 , 15 , 16 The impact on CYP3A4 was most prominent, but CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2C8, and CYP2B6 were also affected. In vivo, exposure of healthy subjects to dicloxacillin was associated with a significant increase in the metabolism of midazolam, omeprazole, and tolbutamide, three classical probe substrates of CYP3A4, CYP2C19, and CYP2C9, respectively. 16 CYP2C9 is responsible for about 80–90% of phenytoin metabolism, with CYP2C19 accounting for an additional 10–20%. 20 CYP2C9 is also the major enzyme mediating the oxidative metabolism of (S)‐warfarin, whereas (R)‐warfarin metabolism is mediated primarily by CYP3A4 and CYP1A2, with minor contribution of CYP2C19. 21 Considering the central role played by CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4 in the metabolism of phenytoin and warfarin, the enhanced clearance of both drugs observed in the current study following dicloxacillin is consistent with enzyme induction as the predominant mechanism.

Findings obtained through in vitro studies suggest that, similar to its effect on hepatic cell lines, dicloxacillin may also enhance the transcription activity and expression of intestinal CYP3A4. 14 CYP3A4 is abundantly found in the intestine, and thus, its induction by dicloxacillin might have resulted in reduced oral bioavailability of (R)‐warfarin. In addition, P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp, also referred to as ABCB1) is an essential efflux transporter responsible for extruding toxins out of the cell. P‐gp is found in the intestine and its main function is to limit the absorption of xenobiotics. Incubation of human hepatocyte or intestine cells with dicloxacillin resulted in a significant increase in ABCB1 mRNA and protein expression. 15 , 22 Furthermore, dicloxacillin administration resulted in decreased area under the plasma concentration‐time curve of the P‐gp substrate dabigatran, suggesting induction of P‐gp. 22 Phenytoin is a P‐gp substrate 23 , 24 and therefore, the reduction in phenytoin plasma concentrations following dicloxacillin could reflect reduced absorption secondary to P‐gp induction in the intestine. However, although the contribution of lower bioavailability of warfarin or phenytoin due to intestinal induction of CYP3A4 and possibly CYP2C9 and P‐gp cannot be ruled out, the fact that the formation clearance of p‐HPPH, (i.e., a marker of liver CYP2C9 activity) was significantly increased, strongly support the possibility that enhanced hepatic metabolism played the predominant role. The significant decrease in the elimination half‐life of both warfarin enantiomers and phenytoin further support the hypothesis of liver enzyme induction.

Warfarin, phenytoin, and dicloxacillin are highly bound to plasma proteins, mainly to albumin. 25 , 26 , 27 An early study conducted in rabbits suggested that the concomitant intravenous administration of phenytoin and dicloxacillin results in an immediate rise in free phenytoin plasma concentration. 28 This was mirrored by a decrease in total phenytoin concentration and 30% increase in phenytoin clearance with no apparent change in its elimination half‐life. The authors suggested that displacement of phenytoin from its albumin binding site by dicloxacillin is the reason for the changes in phenytoin pharmacokinetics. The impact of dicloxacillin on p‐HPPH formation was not assessed in this study and therefore, the possibility of liver enzyme induction and enhanced phenytoin metabolic breakdown as alternative explanations could not be ruled out. In order to cause a displacement to a drug that is extensively bound (fu < 10%) to albumin two conditions should be met: First the displacer must occupy the majority of the albumin binding sites. 29 However, the maximal plasma concentration of dicloxacillin following 500 mg q.i.d. is 0.06 mM which is substantially lower compared to average plasma concentration of albumin in adults (i.e., 0.6 mM). Second, the binding site of the displacer and the displaced drug should be the same. However, phenytoin and warfarin bind to a different binding site than the binding site of dicloxacillin, designated as site 1 and site 2 respectively in the literature. 29 The markedly low plasma concentration of dicloxacillin compared to albumin and the fact that phenytoin and warfarin do not share the same albumin‐binding site suggest that the likelihood of displacement as the predominant mechanism for the observed increase in phenytoin and warfarin clearance is slim.

The decrease in prothrombin time following administration of dicloxacillin 500 mg q.i.d. varied significantly, ranging from 2%, and up to 24%. 10 Similar variability was also noted in another publication. 12 Intersubject variability in the extent of decrease in warfarin anticoagulant effect following dicloxacillin was also noted in the current study. Following dicloxacillin, the decline in AUCINR0→120h and INRMAX ranged from 2.5% to 29.6% and from 7.8% to 45.3%, respectively.

The factors that govern the extent of enzyme induction in response to a potent inducer (e.g., dicloxacillin) are the subject of intense research. The possibility that CYP2C9 genotype may play a role in determining the extent of increase in CYP2C9 activity by dicloxacillin was not investigated in previous studies. In fact, CYP2C9 genotype was performed only in one study. 16 However, this study included only 12 healthy subjects, three carriers of CYP2C9*2 and four carriers of CYP2C9*3 and therefore, a meaningful evaluation of the role of CYP2C9 genotype as a determinant of the extent of dicloxacillin‐induced induction could not be performed. Following dicloxacillin, the absolute increase in the oral clearance of (S)‐warfarin and phenytoin, and the formation clearance of p‐HPPH was greater among carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype. Furthermore, the extent of increase in (S)‐warfarin oral clearance after dicloxacillin was significantly correlated with baseline clearance values prior to dicloxacillin (r = 0.656, P < 0.001). Similar observations were noted in another study that evaluated the extent of increase in tolbutamide clearance following rifampicin administration among 130 healthy subjects with different CYP2C9 genotypes. 30 The authors concluded that rifampicin administration was associated with approximately a twofold increase in tolbutamide clearance irrespective of CYP2C9 genotype. However, similar to the findings in the present study, the absolute increase in tolbutamide clearance was numerically greater in carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 as compared to carriers of either CYP2C9*1/*2 or CYP2C9*1/*3. The difference in the extent of induction between carriers of CYP2C9*1/*1 genotype and carriers of a single variant allele did not translate into greater decline in AUCINR0→120h and INRMAX among the former group. On the other hand, the decrease in warfarin pharmacodynamic effect caused by dicloxacillin was significantly correlated with VKORC1 genotype. This is no surprise, since VKORC1 genetic polymorphisms have been shown to account for approximately 30% of the variability in warfarin response, whereas CYP2C9 genotype can explain < 10%. 31 Of note, the decline in the anticoagulation exerted by warfarin after dicloxacillin was substantially lower than the increase in warfarin enantiomer clearance. This discrepancy is likely due to reduced vitamin K production in the gut caused by antibiotic administration, including penicillin. 32

The current study is the first to demonstrate the pharmacokinetic mechanism that accounts for the previously observed decrease in warfarin anticoagulant effect when dicloxacillin is added. In addition, the findings of the present study extend our knowledge regarding the risk of dicloxacillin interaction with phenytoin, one of the most widely prescribed antiseizure medications in the United States. 24 It can be predicted that the expected decrease in phenytoin plasma concentration following dicloxacillin administration may put the patient at risk of breakthrough seizures. The strength of our study stems from the fact that it was conducted in healthy, nonsmoker subjects, not treated regularly by any medications so that the confounding effect of disease state or drug consumption can be safely ruled out. Furthermore, the cross‐over study design, in which each subject served as his own control, practically excludes host factors that might have confounded the results. However, our study has several limitations. First, only total, rather than unbound, plasma drug concentrations were measured. The pharmacokinetic data and the significantly lower molar plasma concentrations of dicloxacillin, warfarin, and phenytoin compared to albumin concentration collectively make protein binding displacement an implausible mechanism. However, formal calculation of intrinsic clearance independent of changes in the unbound drug fraction was not possible. Second, dicloxacillin plasma concentrations were not measured. Although the standardized dosing regimen was selected to ensure consistent systemic exposure based on established population pharmacokinetic data, interindividual variability in dicloxacillin absorption or clearance may have contributed to the observed variability in the magnitude of enzyme induction. Third, we evaluated the effect of dicloxacillin on the pharmacokinetics of phenytoin and warfarin after a single dose. In clinical practice, both phenytoin and warfarin are taken chronically and therefore, examining the effect of dicloxacillin on phenytoin and warfarin pharmacokinetics after multiple‐dose administration, once steady‐state is reached, seems more clinically relevant. This is particularly important for phenytoin because, at plasma concentrations above 10 mg/L, its metabolism becomes saturable and non‐linear. In the present study, phenytoin plasma concentrations were much lower and therefore, it is unclear whether the induction effect of dicloxacillin will be the same when plasma concentrations are within the preferred therapeutic range of 10–20 mg/L. Fourth, the formation clearance of p‐HPPH was based on molar excretion of “total” p‐HPPH, which consists of both (S)‐p‐HPPH and (R)‐p‐HPPH. However, these enantiomers are generated stereoselectivity by different isoenzymes so that (S)‐p‐HPPH is produced by CYP2C9 and (R)‐p‐HPPH by CYP2C19. 33 The induction effect of dicloxacillin may vary across different isoforms, but since (R)‐p‐HPPH accounts for only 5% of total p‐HPPH, the formation clearance of p‐HPPH is considered a reliable marker of CYP2C9 activity. 34 Finally, the variant allele subgroup comprised eight carriers of CYP2C9*1/*2 and five carriers of CYP2C9*1/*3. Since the basal metabolic activity is much lower in carriers of CYP2C9*1/*3 genotype, the absolute increase in CYP2C9 activity following exposure to a strong inducer may be attenuated as compared to carriers of CYP2C9*1/*2 genotype. 28 Indeed, the absolute increase in (S)‐warfarin oral clearance and p‐HPPH formation clearance was numerically greater in carriers of CYP2C9*1/*2 as compared to carriers of CYP2C9*1/*3, however, the small sample sizes were insufficient to permit a statistically meaningful analysis.

In conclusion, the administration of dicloxacillin is associated with a significant increase in the clearance of warfarin enantiomers and phenytoin due to induction of cytochrome P450 isoforms CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19 mediating the metabolism of warfarin and phenytoin. It is also possible that the decrease in plasma concentration of warfarin and phenytoin is partly accounted for by reduced bioavailability caused by gut CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and P‐gp intestinal induction by dicloxacillin. Since both warfarin and phenytoin are NTI drugs, dicloxacillin co‐administration to patients treated with warfarin or phenytoin may result in subtherapeutic plasma concentration and possibly therapeutic failure. Whenever dicloxacillin is initiated, the dose of CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 substrates characterized as NTI drugs should be increased, and drug levels and pharmacodynamic response should be closely monitored.
