Chronic cough suspected as pneumothorax and identified as esophago-pleural fistula
Authors: Amaze Grace Sira, Loury Priskila, Hendi Wicaksono, Jimmy Indarto Gunawan, Ariane Yudhianti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic cough, defined as a cough lasting 8 weeks or longer in adults, presents a significant diagnostic challenge due to the complexity of underlying causes. Unexplained chronic cough accompanied by shortness of breath that does not improve with chest tube placement administration may be suspected of esophago-pleural fistula.A 54-year-old woman experienced a 2-month chronic cough, shortness of breath, and chest and abdominal pain. Initially treated symptomatically, she was later diagnosed with right-sided pneumothorax. The patient underwent chest tube placement, fluid therapy, antibiotics, gastroprotective agents, and breathing exercises. A chest CT scan suggested an esophago-pleural fistula, although this diagnosis was suspected radiologically and not definitively confirmed by esophagography or endoscopy, and she was referred for thoracic surgical intervention. This case highlights the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic approach in chronic cough with systemic symptoms. Pneumothorax with esophago-pleural fistula is a rare but serious condition requiring early stabilization, empirical antibiotic therapy, and further imaging evaluation.Esophago-pleural fistula should be considered in cases of atypical pneumothorax with gastrointestinal symptoms. Early diagnosis, appropriate intervention, and multidisciplinary collaboration are key to successful treatment.Keywords: Chronic cough, Pneumothorax, Esophago-pleural fistula, Radiology case report

Introduction

The most common cause of chronic cough is postnasal drip, GERD, asthma, infections like chronic bronchitis or medications called ACE inhibitors. Chronic cough can manifest in various conditions, ranging from nonemergency to life-threatening cases. In severe cases, several differential diagnoses should be considered, including pneumonia, severe exacerbations of asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pulmonary embolism, heart failure, and pneumothorax (PTX) [1]. While these conditions are more frequently encountered, rare etiologies such as esophago-pleural fistula (EPF) should also be considered, especially when standard treatments fail or when imaging findings suggest an alternative pathology.

EPF is a rare pathological entity characterized by an abnormal connection between the esophagus and the pleural space [2]. It can result from malignancy, trauma, infection, esophageal perforation, or iatrogenic injury. Due to its rarity and nonspecific presentation, EPF is often misdiagnosed as a more common respiratory or gastrointestinal disorder, leading to delays in appropriate management., while early recognition is crucial to prevent complications such as aspiration pneumonia, empyema, or sepsis [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

Case presentation

A 54-year-old woman presented to a primary health care (PHC) facility with a 2-month history of chronic cough. The patient has a cough with purulent sputum that has lasted for 2 months and there are no complaints of difficulty swallowing. A rapid molecular test ruled out pulmonary tuberculosis, and blood tests were unremarkable. Due to associated chest pain and tightness, exacerbated by coughing, the patient was prescribed diclofenac sodium and referred to Prambanan Regional Public Hospital.

At the pulmonology outpatient clinic, patient still has the same complaints and has not improved. Physical examination revealed diminished vesicular breath sounds, asymmetrical chest expansion, and reduced tactile fremitus on the right side, though vital signs remained stable. Blood tests, including complete blood count, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, random blood sugar, ALT/AST, and urinalysis, were within normal limits. A chest X-ray (Fig. 1) confirmed PTX, prompting hospital admission with differential diagnoses of lung fibrosis, atelectasis, and COPD. Water-sealed drainage (WSD) was recommended, but the patient initially declined. A thoracic CT scan was scheduled, and the attending internist initiated intravenous NaCl 0.9%, ranitidine 50 mg twice daily, oral sucralfate 15 mL 3 times daily, and ketorolac 30 mg injection as needed (Fig. 2).

Chest CT scan of the patient 24 hours after water-sealed drainage (WSD) placement. The left panel shows the right pleural space filled with a hypodense collection containing air density, causing collapse of the right lung. The right panel shows an axial chest CT scan at the T4-T5 level, demonstrating an area suspicious for an esophagopleural fistula, corresponding to the azygo-esophageal recess and marked with an asterisk (*). However, this finding was not confirmed by esophagography, maximum intensity projection (MIP) reconstruction, or endoscopy.

On the second day, the patient consented to WSD placement on the right chest. The procedure was performed successfully without complications, there was no fluid drain in the WSD tube. Follow-up included semi-erect chest X-ray evaluation within 24 hours (Fig. 1) and WSD patency checks every 12 hours. As infection prophylaxis, ceftriaxone 1 g IV twice daily was prescribed. The patient was also instructed to perform breathing exercises by blowing into a 500 mL container of distilled water using a straw. Vital signs were monitored closely.

By the third day, the attending surgeon suspected EPF due to postswallowing cough and difficulty eating. Chest CT scan also revealed connection between esophagus to pleural space. Nasogastric tube feeding was advised, but the patient refused. Despite persistent cough-exacerbated chest pain, the patient remained hemodynamically stable without respiratory distress. Given the suspected EPF, the patient was referred to a tertiary hospital for cardiothoracic surgical evaluation. The final diagnosis at the tertiary hospital was EPF, but the patient refused treatment and did not return for follow-up.

Discussion

EPF is a rare but serious condition characterized by an abnormal connection between the esophagus and the pleural space. It is often associated with malignancy, trauma, infection, or esophageal perforation, but in some cases, the etiology remains unclear [5]. Our case describes a 54-year-old woman initially diagnosed with PTX but later found to have EPF, underscoring the importance of comprehensive evaluation in patients with persistent respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms.

The diagnostic challenge in this case stemmed from the overlapping presentation of PTX and EPF. Chronic cough is a nonspecific symptom, often attributed to more common respiratory conditions such as pneumonia, asthma exacerbations, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [2]. However, in cases where PTX is present without a clear underlying cause, the possibility of EPF should be considered, particularly when symptoms such as cough exacerbation after swallowing are observed. Previous reports have similarly highlighted the difficulty in diagnosing EPF due to its nonspecific presentation and frequent misidentification as other thoracic conditions [3].

EPF has been reported in diverse clinical contexts. A case by Wang et al. described an EPF secondary to severe esophagitis, where the fistula was only identified after persistent pleural drainage raised suspicion [2]. Similarly, Dash et al. reported a spontaneous EPF in a tuberculosis patient, highlighting an infectious etiology [4]. Unlike these cases, our patient had no history of esophageal diseases, malignancy, or infection, making her presentation atypical. This is consistent with reports indicating that EPF can occasionally occur without a clear predisposing factor, further complicating diagnosis [5].

The role of imaging in detecting EPF is crucial. While chest X-ray initially revealed PTX in our patient, it was the chest CT scan that later suggested an esophageal-pleural connection. This finding is in keeping with prior studies that emphasize the importance of advanced imaging modalities such as CT with oral contrast in confirming EPF [3]. In cases where imaging remains inconclusive, additional diagnostic tools such as esophagography or esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) may be required [2].

Management of EPF depends on its severity and underlying cause. Conservative approaches, including fasting, antibiotic therapy, and nutritional support, may be attempted in stable cases [5]. More invasive strategies, such as esophageal stenting or surgical repair, are often required to prevent complications such as aspiration pneumonia or sepsis [3]. Our patient was referred to a tertiary center for further evaluation by a cardiothoracic surgeon, emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach. Previous studies have shown that early intervention, particularly with esophageal stenting, can significantly improve outcomes in EPF cases [2].

Ultimately, this case reinforces the importance of considering EPF in patients with unexplained PTX, particularly when symptoms suggest an esophageal involvement. A high index of suspicion, prompt imaging, and timely referral to a multidisciplinary team are essential for optimizing patient outcomes.

Limitation

This case report has several limitations. The diagnosis of EPF was based solely on contrast-enhanced chest CT and remained presumptive; confirmatory studies such as an esophagogram, a sagittal or oblique-sagittal maximum-intensity-projection (MIP) reconstruction, or upper endoscopy, which could have delineated the fistulous tract more clearly, were not performed because the patient was transferred for surgical evaluation before these studies could be arranged. The exact site of fistulous communication, most consistent with the azygo-esophageal recess on the available images, could therefore not be definitively confirmed, and no histopathological or endoscopic correlation was obtained. In addition, the patient declined definitive surgical treatment and did not return for follow-up at the tertiary hospital, so the clinical course, treatment outcome, and long-term resolution of the fistula could not be assessed. These gaps reflect constraints of the referral setting rather than the absence of clinical suspicion, and they should be considered when interpreting the imaging findings and conclusions presented in this report.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the diagnostic challenges of esophago-pleural EPF, a rare condition that can present with nonspecific respiratory symptoms and be mistaken for primary pulmonary pathology. The patient's chronic cough, exacerbated by swallowing, served as a crucial clinical clue leading to further evaluation. Unlike previously reported cases linked to malignancy, trauma, or infection, this patient had no identifiable predisposing factors, making her case unique.

Given the high morbidity and mortality associated with delayed EPF diagnosis, early recognition, comprehensive imaging, and multidisciplinary collaboration are critical for improving patient outcomes. This case underscores the need for clinicians to consider EPF in cases of unexplained pneumothorax, particularly when atypical symptoms such as postswallowing cough are present.
