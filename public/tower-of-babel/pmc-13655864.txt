Short-term clinical outcomes of nasoduodenal tube versus feeding jejunostomy after minimally invasive esophagectomy: a retrospective cohort study with exploratory microbiome analysis
Authors: Qi-Yue Ge, Chang-Yong Wang, Chao Zheng, Hao-Tian Zheng, Yu-Xuan Wen, Jing Luo, Yong Qiang, Yan-Qing Wang, Zhuang-Zhuang Cong, Yi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEsophagectomy remains an effective treatment for esophageal cancer but is associated with severe postoperative complications, in which nutritional status plays a critical role. The optimal route for enteral feeding remains uncertain. To address this uncertainty, we compared the short-term clinical outcomes of the two approaches and exploratorily analyzed the differences in gut microbiota.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled patients who underwent minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) for esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) between July 2018 and June 2021. Primary outcomes were surgery-related complications (including anastomotic leakage, anastomotic stenosis, pneumonia, respiratory failure, cardiac events, chylothorax, intestinal fistula and bowel obstruction) and catheter-related outcomes (including occlusion, accidental removal, displacement and retention time). Baseline data were extracted from medical records. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to control for potential confounders. Intestinal fluid and fecal samples were collected after enteral nutrition for 16S ribosomal DNA (16S rDNA) sequencing.ResultsAfter PSM, a total of 381 patients (nasoduodenal tube: 282; feeding jejunostomy: 153) was finally included. The feeding jejunostomy group exhibited a higher incidence of pneumonia (21.49% vs. 33.99%, P=0.007), respiratory failure (3.07% vs. 13.07%, P<0.001), anastomotic stenosis (1.32% vs. 5.23%, P=0.03) and bowel obstruction (0% vs. 2.16%, P=0.03). Nasoduodenal tube placement was associated with more accidental removal (13.16% vs. 0.65%, P<0.001) and catheter displacement (13.60% vs. 6.54%, P=0.03). The catheter retention time {37 [interquartile range (IQR): 15–40] days vs. 39 (IQR: 37–42) days, P<0.001} was also shorter in nasoduodenal tube group.ConclusionsIn this study, nasoduodenal tube placement was associated with a higher incidence of catheter-related complications, whereas feeding jejunostomy was linked to more surgery-related complications. Nasoduodenal tube placement also showed potential functional advantages for the gut microbiome. However, future multi-center randomized controlled trials are still needed to minimize potential confounders and validate the differences between the two enteral nutrition routes.Keywords: Minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE), nasoduodenal tube placement, feeding jejunostomy, nutrition, short-term outcome

Key findings

• Nasoduodenal tube placement was associated with a higher incidence of catheter-related complications, whereas feeding jejunostomy was linked to more surgery-related complications. Nasoduodenal tube placement also showed potential functional advantages for the gut microbiome.

What is known and what is new?

• Early enteral nutrition is recommended by guidelines for post‑operative esophageal cancer patients, but no consensus has been reached regarding the optimal approach to enteral nutrition.

• This study compares the two enteral nutrition approaches from multiple perspectives and attempts to explore this issue from the gut microbiota perspective.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The selection between the two enteral nutrition approaches should not depend on surgeons’ preference, but rather be tailored to the short‑term outcome characteristics of each approach.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer was reported as the seventh highest in mortality and the eleventh highest in incidence (1). Surgical intervention remains as the most effective treatment for esophageal cancer. Guidelines recommend esophagectomy for T1b thoracic esophageal cancer and neoadjuvant chemotherapy or neoadjuvant concurrent chemoradiotherapy together with esophagectomy for thoracic esophageal cancer in stage cT1b-cT2N+ or cT3-cT4a any N. Esophagectomy could even be performed for locally advanced cases following neoadjuvant therapy, as indicated in the latest guideline (2,3). Since the first esophagectomy was conducted in 1913 (4), the surgical techniques of esophagectomy have undergone numerous improvements in the past decades. Video-assisted or robot-assisted minimally invasive approaches, such as McKeown or Ivor-Lewis approach, have become the most accepted surgical method, demonstrating improved long-term and short-term outcomes (5,6).

Esophageal cancer is a debilitating disease and is usually accompanied by dysphagia, leading to a rapid decline in nutritional status (7,8). Nutritional therapy plays a vital role in the management of patients with esophageal cancer. Adequate nutritional supplementation significantly enhances the recovery and decreases the incidence of several postoperative complications (9). The widely recognized enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols also emphasize the importance of perioperative nutritional care (10). Therefore, correcting malnutrition and maintaining the nutritional status are critical components of effective perioperative management.

Enteral nutrition and parenteral nutrition are two efficient ways for providing nourishment (11). Although parenteral nutrition can rapidly improve patients’ nitrogen balance and facilitate postoperative rehabilitation, it is assumed to have a higher incidence of hypercapnia and refeeding syndrome (12). Enteral nutrition is one of the most economical and effective strategies for maintaining nutritional status after esophagectomy and is correlated with better rehabilitation outcomes and fewer complications (13,14). Previous studies have demonstrated that postoperative enteral nutrition in patients who have undergone esophagectomy can accelerate the recovery of gastrointestinal function and help maintain the stable state of intestinal flora (15,16). According to the European Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN) guidelines, early enteral nutrition should be implemented in patients following gastrointestinal surgery (17). Nasogastric tube, oral-gastric tube, nasoduodenal tube, feeding jejunostomy and feeding gastrostomy are commonly utilized forms of enteral nutrition (18). The esophagus is substituted by the stomach to reconstruct the digestive tract in patients undergoing esophagectomy, making nasoduodenal tube placement and feeding jejunostomy the preferred methods for providing enteral nutrition. However, no current guideline provides explicit criteria for selecting the appropriate enteral nutrition approach during the perioperative period for esophageal cancer. Recent studies have reported that feeding jejunostomy is preferable for patients requiring long-term enteral nutrition, yet it carries an elevated risk of bowel obstruction (19,20). Meanwhile, existing investigations have explored only a limited set of outcome measures, which fail to fully reflect the disparities between these two feeding modalities. Consequently, no consensus exists on the optimal nutritional access strategy and the selection of feeding route generally relies on surgeon preference.

Accumulating evidence has established the gut microbiome as a critical determinant of postoperative recovery, wherein dysbiosis is associated with an increased risk of infectious complications and delayed wound healing, particularly following gastrointestinal surgery (21). Emerging data further suggest that fecal microbial composition is tightly linked to prognosis in patients undergoing esophagectomy (22). Certain bacterial taxa can produce collagenases following gastrointestinal operations, which may damage intestinal anastomoses and impair short-term outcomes (23). Meanwhile, their regulatory effects on inflammatory status may lead to heterogeneous responses to adjuvant therapy among patients, ultimately affecting long-term prognosis (24). Compared with nasoduodenal tube feeding, feeding jejunostomy is an invasive nutritional access that causes trauma to the jejunal wall. This mechanical disruption of the jejunal wall may substantially alter the gut microbiota.

In order to investigate the optimal enteral nutrition approach during the perioperative period, we conducted this study comparing short-term clinical outcomes between two approaches in patients who underwent minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE). Furthermore, we aimed to explore the potential mechanisms involved from a microbial perspective. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1470/rc).

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) who underwent MIE at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Jinling Hospital, Nanjing, between 1st July 2018 and 30th June 2021. The inclusion criteria were: (I) pathologically confirmed ESCC; (II) underwent video-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (VAMIE) or robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE) at Jinling Hospital; (III) received standard postoperative enteral nutrition support; (IV) aged ≥18 years at the time of surgery; The exclusion criteria were: (I) poor treatment compliance and inability to complete scheduled follow-up; (II) incomplete clinical data or lost to follow-up; (III) all-cause death within 30 days postoperatively due to confirmed non-surgical factors; (IV) history of previous major gastrointestinal surgery.

For the exploratory intestinal microbiome analysis, we prospectively recruited an independent paired cohort. A total of 6 patients were enrolled, with 3 assigned to the nasoduodenal tube group and 3 to the feeding jejunostomy group. Eligibility criteria for the microbiome cohort were as follows: (I) pathologically confirmed ESCC; (II) underwent MIE; (III) patients who had received postoperative enteral nutrition for at least 1 month. Exclusion criteria included: (I) receipt of antibiotic therapy or probiotics within 1 month before catheter removal; (II) occurrence of severe postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III) within 30 days after surgery; (III) inability to provide qualified stool and intestinal fluid samples.

Surgical techniques

All the patients underwent either RAMIE or VAMIE. All participating surgeons had accumulated experience of more than 100 esophagectomy procedures and were proficient in both enteral nutrition access techniques, to ensure procedural consistency and minimize operator-related bias.

Radical resection of esophageal cancer followed a standardized institutional protocol, consisting of MIE, two-field lymph node dissection, and digestive tract reconstruction. The core radical procedures were identical between the two groups, with the only difference being the route of enteral nutrition access established after resection: (I) a nasoduodenal feeding tube was placed into the distal duodenum, with position confirmed before the end of surgery; (II) a feeding jejunostomy was created 30–40 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, and the feeding catheter was fixed to both the jejunal wall and abdominal wall to reduce dislocation risk.

Nutritional treatment

All patients received parenteral nutrition on the day of operation and gradually advanced to full enteral nutrition by postoperative day (POD) 3. Postoperative daily energy intake was set at 25–30 kcal/kg body weight, with full enteral nutrition sustained until discharge. All patients received standardized perioperative management and postoperative enteral nutrition protocols in accordance with institutional ERAS guidelines. Routine disease-related perioperative medication, feeding initiation timelines, and nursing interventions were standardized for both cohorts.

Data collection

Data of esophageal cancer patients were collected from Jinling Hospital. All baseline characteristics and outcome data were extracted from the electronic medical record system by reviewing inpatient progress notes, nursing records, pathological reports, laboratory test results, imaging examinations and outpatient recording documents. Data collection was independently performed by two authors, and all extracted data were subsequently merged and summarized by a third investigator. Baseline characteristics included were list as follows: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), neoadjuvant therapy, pathological stage, tumor location, history of thoracic surgery, smoking history, alcohol consumption, history of cardiac disease, history of diabetes, history of liver disease, history of lung disease and anastomotic location. Primary outcomes were incidence of surgery-related complications (anastomotic stenosis, anastomotic leakage, pneumonia, respiratory failure, cardiac event, chylothorax, bowel obstruction and intestinal fistula) and catheter-related outcomes (occlusion, accidental removal, displacement and catheter retention time). Secondary outcomes were recovery outcomes (time to first oral water intake and time to drainage removal), neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) level (preoperative, POD 1, POD 3 and POD 7) and albumin level (preoperative, POD 1 and POD 4).

Pneumonia was defined according to standard hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) criteria: presence of new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography, plus at least two of the following: body temperature >38 ℃, abnormal white blood cell count, or purulent respiratory secretions. Respiratory failure was defined as the need for reintubation, non-invasive ventilatory support, or prolonged mechanical ventilation (≥48 hours postoperatively). Diagnoses of anastomotic leakage and anastomotic stricture were confirmed by endoscopy. Diagnoses of bowel obstruction and intestinal fistula were established based on clinical manifestations combined with imaging examinations. Diagnosis of feeding tube displacement also required confirmation by imaging.

Sample collection

Samples of esophageal cancer patients were collected from Jinling Hospital. All patients received routine clinical revisit examinations one month after hospital discharge. Intestinal fluid samples were collected at the time of enteral feeding tube removal. A swab was used to collect intestinal fluid samples from the tube insertion site. Fecal samples were obtained from patients on the same day of tube extraction. The samples were immediately placed into preservation solution (containing guanidinium thiocyanate and purified water) and stored at 2–30 ℃. All collected samples underwent 16S ribosomal DNA (16S rDNA) sequencing (technical support provided by Xiamen Treatgut Bio-technology Co., Ltd).

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jinling Hospital (No. 2025DZKY-023-02). All patient medical records were anonymized, and sample collection was performed with informed consent from the patients.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed by Stata software (version 16.0, SE), SPSS software (version 25.0), R software (version 4.4.3) and PICRUSt software (version 2.5.2). Propensity score matching (PSM) was adopted to mitigate confounding factors. Multiple imputation was used to handle a small proportion of missing data. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was utilized to identify statistically significant differences in microbial species composition. Categorical variables were analyzed by Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were analyzed by t-test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. All statistical tests were two-sided, with the significance level set at α=0.05.

Main outcomes

Patients who underwent feeding jejunostomy presented a higher incidence in surgery-related complication, including pneumonia (21.49% vs. 33.99%, P=0.007), respiratory failure (3.07% vs. 13.07%, P<0.001), anastomotic stenosis (1.32% vs. 5.23%, P=0.03) and bowel obstruction (0% vs. 2.61%, P=0.03), compared to those who received nasoduodenal tube placement. Conversely, patients who receive nasoduodenal tube placement were more likely to have catheter-related events, including accidental removal (13.16% vs. 0.65%, P<0.001) and displacement (13.60% vs. 6.54%, P=0.03). Patients who underwent feeding jejunostomy had a longer duration of catheter retention than those placed nasoduodenal tube {37 [interquartile range (IQR): 15–40] vs. 39 (IQR: 37–42) days, P<0.001}. Notably, no significant difference was found in anastomotic leakage (14.91% vs. 11.76%, P=0.38), cardiac events (1.75% vs. 4.58%, P=0.13), chylothorax (1.32% vs. 1.96%, P=0.69) and intestinal fistula (0.44% vs. 0.65%, P>0.99). In addition, no statistically significant intergroup differences were observed in recovery-related outcomes, including time to first oral water intake [4 (IQR: 4–4) vs. 4 (IQR: 4–4) days, P=0.44] and time to drainage removal [7 (IQR: 6–9) vs. 7 (IQR: 6–9) days, P=0.97]. Discussion

The study retrospectively compared the short-time outcomes of two enteral nutrition approaches following esophagectomy. Analyses of the short-time outcomes indicated that feeding jejunostomy group experienced a higher incidence of pneumonia, respiratory failure, anastomotic stenosis and bowel obstruction. On the contrary, the nasoduodenal tube group was associated with a greater frequency of catheter accidental remove, catheter displacement and shorter catheter retention time. These results suggested that patients undergone feeding jejunostomy were more likely to develop surgery-related complications, whereas those with nasoduodenal tube turned to face a higher risk of catheter-related complications.

The higher incidence of surgery-related complications associated with feeding jejunostomy could be explained by several factors. One common complication during enteral nutrition was bowel obstruction. Previous studies reported that the attachment of jejunum to the parietal peritoneum and the presence of abdominal adhesion might lead to mechanical bowel obstruction in patients with feeding jejunostomy (25-27). Furthermore, pneumonia and respiratory failure were closely correspondent to infection and aspiration (28). One possible reason for the differences was the longer duration of exposure to aspiration. The nasogastric catheter was often placed to drain gastric fluid and reduce the reflux. Placement of the nasogastric drainage could be challenging in patients who could not cooperate, as many surgeons preferred to insert the catheter after patients regained consciousness from anesthesia. For patients requiring nasoduodenal tube placement, surgeons typically performed concurrent nasogastric tube insertion for drainage purposes, eliminating the need for separate insertion procedures. This approach minimized the duration of exposure to aspiration risk by reducing the window period during which patients were unprotected. Another explanation could be that jejunostomy-induced intestinal wall trauma disrupts small bowel motility, subsequently retarding gastric emptying of the stomach. This motility impairment might elevate the risk of gastroesophageal reflux and aspiration by prolonging intragastric residue time. Notably, after PSM, the number of patients with a history of thoracic surgery was slightly higher in the feeding jejunostomy group than in the nasoduodenal tube group. Nevertheless, only 8 and 3 patients fell into these two subgroups respectively, which is far fewer than the number of pneumonia events. Accordingly, we believe this imbalance cannot serve as a plausible explanation for the intergroup difference in pneumonia incidence. A vital reason contributing to the higher incidence of catheter-related complications in nasoduodenal group was the method of securing catheters. Catheter in feeding jejunostomy was secured with sutures while nasoduodenal tube was fixed using nasal strip. In post-operative patients, altered levels of consciousness can increase the likelihood of damage to the nasal strip fixation from external forces, leading to a greater incidence of catheter removal and position change (29). Additionally, patients usually paid more attention to the invasive procedure, resulting in a higher focus on protecting their catheters. The statistical analysis of catheter retention time in this study further supported this hypothesis.

To investigate the difference between the two approaches more precisely, we utilized multi-measurements of NLR and albumin levels to reflect the dynamic changes in post-operative status. Although NLR levels on POD 1, POD 3 and POD 7 were higher in patients who underwent feeding jejunostomy, the difference between two groups diminished over time. Similar trend was also observed in measurements of albumin. The trend revealed that, despite the differences in early-stage post-operative complications, patients tended to exhibit comparable short-term nutritional status. Both nasoduodenal tube and feeding jejunostomy were effective nutritional method for recovery.

Furthermore, a preliminary experiment from our project on microbiomics analyzed the microcosmic difference between the two approaches after a sufficient period of enteral nutrition. As illustrated in Figure 3C,3D, the two approaches showed differences in microbial community distribution at the class level, including variations in Alphaproteobacteria, Fusobecteria, Bacteroidia (intestinal fluid, nasoduodenal tube group higher), Gammaproteobacteria (intestinal fluid, feeding jejunostomy group higher) and Deltaproteobacteria (fecal, feeding jejunostomy group higher). Research indicated that Bacteroides, as a cornerstone community of the human gut microbiome, exhibited a positive correlation with the breakdown and utilization of ingested nutrients. It could contribute to maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis and reducing infection risks through multiple immunomodulatory mechanisms (30). Bacteria belonging to the Gammaproteobacteria class, such as Escherichia coli, could exhibit dual characteristics of pathogenicity and commensalism within the human host (31). Fusobacteria have been extensively reported to correlate with the initiation and progression of various malignant tumors (32-34). Notably, Li et al. demonstrated that Fusobacterium nucleatum inhibits T-cell proliferation and cytokine secretion, while promoting esophageal cancer progression through the binding of its virulence factor to the PD-L1 gene promoter (35). Although Alphaproteobacteria exhibited relatively low abundance in the gut microbiota, a study reported that the associations between specific members of this class and the pathogenesis of gastrointestinal disease (36). However, the biological significance of Deltaproteobacteria in humans remains underexplored, with limited studies suggesting their involvement in periodontal disease etiology (37). Microbial species composition analysis revealed significantly lower variability in the lower digestive tract (fecal samples) compared to the upper digestive tract (intestinal fluid samples). To explain this difference, one reason is that the jejunostomy stoma may alter the intestinal microenvironment, though this remains unverified by rigorous investigation. Another is the anatomical disparity in catheter positioning, despite similar physicochemical properties reported in the duodenum and jejunum by prior study (38).

The KEGG pathway enrichment analyses explored the potential different function between two groups. Significant differences were observed in lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis proteins pathway between two groups in fecal samples, with higher levels noted in the nasoduodenal tube group. Its effects on the human body might lie in modulating the intestinal immune environment (39). Pathways like carotenoid biosynthesis and bacteria toxins were also highly enriched in nasoduodenal tube pathways in intestinal fluid samples. The carotenoid pathway produces β-carotene (retinol precursor) and vitamin A, whereas retinol deficiency inhibits gut Bacteroides growth (40). Studies demonstrated that carotenoids participate in human physiological processes, including absorption and postprandial metabolism, while conferring intestinal protection via IgA modulation (41,42). Bacterial toxins exhibit diverse functional roles, yet their implications in postoperative nutrition remain to be fully elucidated (43).

A key strength of this study lied in our investigation of microbial community distribution associated with the two approaches, as well as our elucidation of the observed differences from a microbial perspective. Furthermore, this study focused on distinct nutritional strategies in patients who underwent MIE and provided an in-depth analysis of variations in laboratory indicators and clinical outcomes. Notably, it was conducted with the largest sample size reported to date.

Several limitations still existed in our study. Firstly, as it was a retrospective study, various biases were unavoidable. Then, the research only concentrated on objective outcomes. Subjective indicators such as quality of life should be explored in future research. Finally, further mechanistic studies are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

Nasoduodenal tube placement may be associated with a higher incidence of catheter-related events, whereas feeding jejunostomy may be correlated with an increased risk of surgery‑related complications. Moreover, nasoduodenal tube placement appeared to confer potential functional benefits for the gut microbiome. However, residual confounding between the two groups cannot be fully excluded. These findings still require further validation in prospective studies.
