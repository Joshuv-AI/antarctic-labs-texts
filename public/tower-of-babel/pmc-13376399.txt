Problem-based learning design multi-objective optimization to smart education: a digital transformation digital literacy model of higher education
Authors: Wang Junce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Data-driven approaches of instructional design are more necessary than ever for higher education institutions in the era of digital transformation, especially when faced with noisy and heterogenous educational data. In the digital transformation age, the requirement for data-driven approaches to instructional design that are stable for educational data is growing. Past studies of smart education and problem-based learning (PBL) have been mostly descriptive models or single-dataset studies, with few studies providing evidence for robustness-aware instructional design under different data characteristics. To fill this gap, this study presents a robustness-aware multi-objective optimization method called Cross-Dataset Robust Multi-Objective Optimization (CD-RMO) for exploring PBL design configurations in intelligent learning environments. CD-RMO presents learning gain, development of digital literacy, and implementation cost/risk as three objectives to be optimized simultaneously, and adds robustness to data variability to the objective measurement. The framework is tested on three different heterogeneous datasets related to education: OULAD, EdNet, and KDD Cup 2010, by employing a common feature space and a common PBL design variable. Convergence, diversity, robustness and stability are used as indicators for performance assessment. Under the proposed modeling framework, the results show that CD-RMO yields higher quality Pareto-front, convergence accuracy and robustness consistency compared to the baselines based on experts, classical evolutionary algorithms and a state-of-the-art multi-objective optimizer. In addition, sensitivity and interaction analyses indicate that Scaffolding Strength, PBL Intensity, and Feedback Frequency are significant on robustness-aware optimization performance and other design variables have their effect mostly through interaction effects. Overall, the proposed framework shows that robustness-aware multi-objective optimization is a principled computational methodology to study various trade-offs in ID for intelligent higher education, offering a much more stable and interpretable framework for making decisions in the setting of realistic data uncertainty.

Introduction

The accelerated development of digital technologies has re-engineered the higher education systems radically, which resulted in the introduction of smart education as a strategic paradigm to redesign the learning processes to make them more effective, flexible and personalized. Smart education includes the implementation of digital technologies, data-driven processes, and learner-centered pedagogies to help sustain educational development in the academic environments that are becoming more complex1,2. In this paradigm, digital transformation is turning into a fundamental institutional agenda, which does not just involve the implementation of learning management systems and digital tools but includes more global changes in organizations, pedagogy, and culture that alter the practices of teaching and learning3. Smart education and digital transformation are becoming more and more terms of a cooperation instead of a one-duke exercise as higher education institutions strive to react to the disruptions of the world and as well as adjust to the emerging demands of learners4.

Although it becomes increasingly prominent, a lot of digital transformation work in higher education remains based on descriptive, technology-focused and non-computational models. The available literature tends to be either conceptual in nature, or policy-oriented, or a qualitative analysis of digital initiatives, and can be very helpful in understanding institutional issues and opportunities, but lacks the systematic instructional decision-making guidance5. The methods typically do not provide formal frameworks for modelling trade-offs, optimizing pedagogical design decisions and/or testing alternative pedagogical design options under realistic constraints. This means that digital transformation decisions are frequently based on a subjective assessment and a process of ad hoc decision making, cannot be scaled, replicated, or extended to diverse educational contexts6, and are less likely to be robust, reproducible, or scalable.

Problem-Based Learning (PBL) has also become a well-known approach in the digital learning environment and has been introduced as a solution to achieving the objectives of smart learning. The approach is especially appropriate in the context of a technology-rich education, as PBL focuses on real-world problem solving, teamwork, self-regulated learning, and higher order thinking7. Empirical studies suggest that effective PBL environments can enhance learner engagement and conceptual understanding, with the addition of digital tools like online collaboration platforms and learning analytics dashboards. However, this challenge of successful PBL online is multidimensional in nature. The complexity in problems, the level of collaboration, the frequency of feedback, the way scaffolding is performed, the authenticity of the assessment and the integration of digital tools have high interdependence, and the sub-optimal combinations will affect the learning and the learning experience of the learner. Majority of the available PBL articles address these design aspects separately or based on qualitative/statistical analysis, and thus they offer less information on how to scientifically determine the best PBL configurations in various learning situations8.

Simultaneously, digital literacy is turning out to be a primary learning outcome in smart education and digital transformation projects. Digital literacy encompasses more than just the core technical expertise to the assessment of information, awareness of data, ethical thinking and the capacity to study and work in digitally mediated contexts9. Despite being recognized as a highly important competence of higher education graduates, digital literacy is commonly viewed as an unspoken or secondary consequence of teaching research. Not many studies explicitly address the issue of digital literacy development as a purposeful and systematic design and optimization of learning space, especially in problem-based and digitally enhanced pedagogies.

Methodologically, another weakness of the current studies is related to the inability to evaluate and compare the data across to state-of-the-art optimization techniques. Numerous presented models and frameworks are tested by one dataset or a certain institutional setting that concerns their strength and external validity10. Additionally, the comparative assessments, in case they exist, are often based on classical benchmarks and ignore the recent development of multi-objective evolutionary optimization, which proved to be highly effective in solving multi-objective challenging decision-making tasks11. This disparity constrains the capacity to evaluate the potential of suggested approaches to optimization of education in seeking to determine how they can consistently outperform both conventional and modern computational strategies in a heterogeneous setting of learning.

Combined these constraints indicate a definite study void in the overlap involving smart education, digital transformation and problem-based learning. An optimal, computationally based, optimization-based framework is needed that can be used to design PBL environments in a systematic way that simultaneously looks at learning effectiveness, development of digital literacy and the feasibility of the implementation. Such framework must be able to work uniformly on various datasets and in learning scenarios and it must be tested using both baseline methods and the current state-of-the-art optimization methods.

To address this gap, the current work develops PBL design of smart education as a multi-objective optimization model, and suggests a unified computational system consolidating the mechanisms of digital transformation, the approaches to learning, and finally, the results of the digital literacy12. The given approach is implemented similarly to a variety of heterogeneous educational datasets by building a single feature space, which allows comparing it across data in the same way and fairly. More so, the suggested method is strictly compared to the benchmark of the baseline methods and a state-of-the-art evolutionary algorithm, which offers a current evaluation of the efficiency and strength of the suggested method.

The three folds of the research of this work are the findings. First of all, it proposes a multi-objective optimization model which is clearly reflected in the performance of learning, the development of digital literacy and the cost of the problem-based learning design. Secondly, it suggests that there is some common representation of features, and the same optimization model will apply to many different academic data sets. Third, it introduces a consolidated cross-dataset benchmarking framework that allows for comparison with classical baselines as well as a state-of-the-art optimization algorithm, for evaluating robustness consistency, comparative optimization behavior and method applicability in the context of intelligent higher education environments.

Literature review

Digital transformation in academic institutions has been widely addressed in terms of responding to a swift technological advancement, more intense global competitiveness, and shifting learner demands. Initial research largely defined digital transformation as the use of information and communication technologies in teaching, learning, and administration13. Further on this basis, more recent studies have uncovered new directions in which organizational culture, pedagogical innovation, and data-driven decision-making can also facilitate the emergence of sustainable and meaningful change in institutions of higher learning14. In this wider lens, smart education has become an integrated paradigm bridging the gap between digital infrastructure and intelligent learning spaces and adaptive educational services, to promote quality and resilience of learning in institutions15.

Regardless of these theoretical innovations, large parts of the digital transformation literature in higher education are largely descriptive and framework-based. The literature tends to concentrate on the readiness assessment, the maturity model, and policy guidelines, which can be helpful material on the macro-level of understanding the institutional change processes16,17. Nevertheless, such methods are seldom associated with the computational or operational processes that are able to facilitate systematic decisions in the instructional practice. To the extent that it has been summarized in Table 1, the research on digital transformation has so far focused significantly on conceptual clarity and the strategic alignment, yet has rarely provided any advice on how to optimize the pedagogical design decisions or assess variants of instructional setups under the conditions of reality.

Problem-Based Learning (PBL) is one of the most powerful pedagogical strategies that complies with the notions of smart education and learner-oriented digital transformation. PBL is currently well-known in terms of its ability to promote active learning, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills in higher education settings18. As digital and blended learning environments gain more acceptance, researchers have investigated the combination of PBL and online collaboration tools, learning management systems, and learning analytics to improve learner engagement and autonomy19.

However, the success of PBL in online spaces is quite contingent on intricate and interdependent decisions concerning the instruction design, such as the complexity of the problem, the organization of collaboration, scaffolding techniques, genuine assessment, and feedback20. In the majority of the existing research, it is the design dimensions that are studied separately or are evaluated based on the qualitative and statistical analysis to determine the effects. Although informative, these methods are not very helpful in the optimization of multiple design variables especially in cases where learning outcomes, engagement, and implementation constraints have to be taken into account. PBL research has developed very solid pedagogical principles, but, because of the lack of formal computational models, it has not developed systematic guidelines on how to design PBL in digitally transformed learning contexts, as noted in Table 1.

Digital literacy as a key competency in smart education and digitally transformed higher education systems also have been receiving growing concern. Modern descriptions of digital literacy are far broader in their technical competency and encompass information assessment, communication and content production, information awareness, moral reasoning, and effective learning in technology-mediated environments21. Other frameworks that promote the multidimensionality of digital literacy and its significance in lifelong learning and employability include international frameworks like DigComp22.

Digital literacy is often noted as one of the most important achievements of the digital transformation efforts, but its role in the instructional design research is yet to be explored. Numerous researches consider digital literacy as an indirect or implicit outcome, and it is frequently measured by self-report or isolated skills23. As summarized in Table 1, digital literacy frameworks present useful conceptual and assessment-oriented information but seldom make digital literacy an overt design or optimization goal. This shortcoming becomes especially clear in the studies of PBL and smart learning environment, where the development of digital literacy is not modeled and optimized in relation to learning performance, but assumed instead.

Simultaneously, there has been an increase in the number of researchers examining the use of optimization and computer intelligence approaches to the education system. Initial research in this direction concentrated on the operational issues like scheduling and resource assignment as well as student group formation which was usually done by a single objective approach or by intuition24. Recent research has utilized multi-objective optimization methods to solve conflicting objectives of learning performance, training utility, and resource efficiency and has shown that evolutionary algorithms and learning analytics have the potential to enhance sophisticated educational decision-making25.

In spite of these developments, multi-objective optimization as applied to instructional design and pedagogy in the context of PBL and smart education, is little explored. The literature on optimization-based research is commonly tested on single datasets or artificial conditions, making it questionable as to their strength and breadth26. Furthermore, as it is presented in Table 1, not many studies compare their methods with the current state of the art evolutionary algorithms including reference-vector-based algorithms, which proved to have even better performance in the complex multi-objective problems27. This methodology shortcoming restricts the capacity to determine the capacity of suggested educational optimization frameworks to repeatedly outdo classical and modern computational strategies in different learning settings that are heterogeneous.

Collectively, the reviewed literature in this section indicates that the studies concerning the digital transformation, PBL, digital literacy, and the optimization of education have followed parallel but independent paths. Table 1 synthesizes the results of digital transformation research, which are predominantly descriptive, PBL research lacks formalized optimization techniques, pedagogical design is rarely a formal design target and cross-dataset robustness is seldom a subject of study. All these limitations indicate a research gap, the lack of a single, multi-purpose optimization framework that would systematically incorporate PBL design, digital transformation mechanisms, and digital literacy outcomes, and be tested across heterogeneous educational datasets and compared to the modern state-of-the-art optimization techniques.

Methodology

This part contains the research methodology that is used to construct and assess the provided framework on how to optimize the problem-based learning (PBL) design in smart education. The methodology design is oriented towards the overcoming of the limitations of previous studies, introducing pedagogical and computational soundness. In particular, it will attempt to move beyond description and context-specific analysis to make instructional design into a data-driven optimization problem. A consistent modeling framework is used across several heterogeneous educational datasets to facilitate their comparative robustness evaluation with respect to various data characteristics and learning environments.

The methodological framework combines the principles of design science and multi-objective optimization, which allows exploring the trade-offs between the learning performance, development of digital literacy, and implementation feasibility in a systematic way. In such a manner, the research offers a replicable and comparative assessment of other PBL design options in higher education settings that have been digitally transformed.

Research design

The research design used in this study is design science research (DSR) with a mixture of computational optimization to design and test rigorously a smart education framework to problem-based learning (PBL). Design science is quite applicable to the aim of this study, as it focuses on the goal of intended design, creation, and analysis of artifacts that are supposed to solve complex problems in the real world35. In this regard, the suggested artifact is a decision-based optimization system, which methodically underpins instructional design decisions in digitally transformed institutions of higher learning.

Under the context of this paradigm of design science, the study shifts away the descriptive and experience-based analysis and takes up a computationally based design process. The framework allows exploring the alternative PBL design configurations and the trade-offs involved systematically in lieu of prescribing particular pedagogical solutions. The operationalization of pedagogical principles based on the theory of PBL and smart education is carried out on the basis of a formal optimization procedure, which enables instructional decisions to be objectively assessed transparently, reproducibly, and using data.

One of the key features of the research design is the application of one and the same optimization model in the course of the research. The proposed model where there is a set of decision variables that are fixed, objective functions that are fixed, and algorithmic settings that are fixed is the same in all the experimental conditions. The disparities between learning conditions are resolved solely by the means of data preparation and feature extraction, which are reconciled, through a single feature space. The fact that model structure and data representation are separated will provide methodological consistency and enable observed performance differences to be linked to data properties, as opposed to model-specific adoptions.

In order to determine robustness and generalizability the research design is designed with a multi-dataset evaluation strategy. The proposed framework is applied to various publicly accessible educational data sets that are related to different learning settings and data properties independently. As shown in Fig. 1, all datasets meet the same methodological pipeline, i.e. feature extraction and optimization, despite the same experimental conditions. The design reduces bias that may be present in datasets, and it helps overcome one of the major flaws of the previous literature that uses only one dataset in its validation.

The research design also contains a comparative benchmarking strategy in addition to cross-dataset evaluation. It is proposed that the given framework be compared systematically to the representative baseline methods and existing state-of-the-art optimization methods using the same experimental conditions. This controlled comparison is part and parcel of the research design and makes sure that any improvements that were noticed in the performances can be linked to the proposed methodological contributions as opposed to variations in the data representation, model structure, and evaluation processes. In Sect. 3.7, the baseline and state-of-the-art methods have been described in detail.

All in all, the research design is a mixture of the problem-solving orientation of design science and the analytical rigor of computational optimization. The proposed methodology enables the creation of a reproducible, generalizable and decision-oriented framework of optimizing problem-based learning design, using smart higher education settings, by applying a coherent model, a multi-dataset analysis, and systematic benchmarking.

Datasets

Three publicly available and popular educational datasets are used to test the strength of the suggested optimization framework and its overall generalizability: OULAD, EdNet, and KDD Cup 2010. The choice of these datasets is due to the fact that they represent diverse learning settings, vary widely in size and data format, and that they have been widely applied in previous learning analytics as well as educational data mining studies. Collectively, they will offer a complete testbed in estimating the applicability of the proposed framework to a wide range of smart education situations.

OULAD (Open University Learning Analytics Dataset) includes the records of student contacts, grades, and demographic data of distance and blended education courses of higher education36. The dataset will record the learner interaction in the virtual learning environment (VLE) available, including the access of content, forums, and submission of assignments. OULAD can be easily employed to model learning gain and engagement-related features in the higher education settings because of its structured representation of learning behaviors and outcomes.

EdNet37 is a massive dataset formed on the basis of online tutoring systems and digital learning platforms, with the time-stamped fine-grained logs of the system-learner interactions. In contrast to OULAD, EdNet focuses on the learning processes at the micro-level, such as attempts at problem-solving, correctness of a response, and time-based learning patterns. Such granularity renders EdNet especially useful in the analysis of adaptive learning behaviors and digital literacy proxies in smart learning environments with a great amount of data.

KDD Cup 2010 is an initial data set that was first published as a part of Educational Data Mining Challenge and has since been adopted as a standard in the area38. The data set is concerned with the problem-solving procedures of students in intelligent tutoring systems, and has detailed logs of skill usage, attempt as well as performance results. KDD Cup 2010 is most appropriate to comparative assessment and methodological validation because of its long history of use in benchmarking studies.

These datasets are quite different in terms of education setting, size, and data format; however, they all reflect the essential elements of the learner behavior, performance, and the interaction with the digital learning systems. In this work, dataset specific properties are only carried out when preprocessing and extracting features. Once a single feature space (Sect. 3.3) has been obtained, the same optimization problem, variables of decision, and experimental conditions are implemented on each of the datasets. This approach allows the fair comparison and contributes to the assessment of the cross-dataset robustness without the situation of dataset-specific modelling bias.

Construction of unified features spaces compositely

The educational datasets that were employed in the research have a significant difference in terms of design, granularity, and context of learning. OULAD is interested in course level learning management systems, EdNet is interested in fine-grained interactions between learners and the system and KDD Cup 2010 is a problem-solving process in intelligent tutoring systems. Direct application of one optimization model to such heterogeneous raw data would lead to inconsistency in representations and biased comparisons. In order to resolve this issue, a Unified Feature Space (UFS) is built as a fundamental aspect of the suggested methodology.

UFS has the design of a single transformation block to convert raw educational data to a dataset-agnostic representation that is compatible with downstream optimization. The UFS (Fig. 2) takes as input the raw educational data and represents it as the output of a series of processing steps propagating inside the system. The design guarantees that there is a clear distinction between dataset-related preprocessing and model-level optimization, and thus, the design helps to maintain a methodological similarity in heterogeneous learning settings.

The initial processing phase in the UFS is feature extraction, in which raw logs, assessment records, and interaction traces are processed to obtain more high-level behavioral indicators. Temporal statistics and summary measures are calculated to identify meaningful learning patterns without much noise and sparsity of the raw data. Such extracted features will be then grouped using feature grouping, in which related conceptually indicators are configured into shared pedagogical facets, such as, learner engagement, performance, self-regulation, and digital literacy proxies. This step of grouping is necessary so that each dimension of features can be mapped to a similar educational concept, in all datasets.

Normalization and alignment are the last phase of the UFS that converts all the grouped features to the same scale of numeric values and ensures that the features are ordered in a uniform manner. The rescaling of feature values to the [0,1] interval in this paper is done with the help of minmax normalization that guarantees the numerical stability of optimization. Problem-based learning design variables

Problem-based learning (PBL) design is developed as a decision problem in the proposed framework, where instructional settings are manipulated directly by a set of predetermined decision variables. These variables are the important dimensions of PBL design that are identified to impact the learning processes and outcomes of smart education environment. Notably, the decision variables (and their definition and admissible range) are the same in all datasets that are involved in this research. Such design selection guarantees the methodological consistency and makes the cross-dataset comparison just.

Instead of customizing instructional variables to characteristics of the dataset, the suggested framework considers the design of PBL as a dataset-free pedagogical space. The heterogeneity of datasets is solved only with the help of the unitary feature space that is presented in Sect. 3.3, but the variables of decision are the same. When this happens, the variations found in optimization results may be explained by the inherent learning behaviors and contextual trends and not by variations in the instructional design space.

The chosen variables of the decision represent each other aspect of the PBL design such as learner engagement, collaboration, instructional support, feedback mechanisms, and digital integration. All variables are brought to a normalized scale to make optimization and interpretation easier. All of these variables are the design space used by the multi-objective optimization algorithm.

Each decision variable is a controllable design parameter which can be changed independently in its range. Candidate solutions provide alternative PBL design configurations during optimization, consisting of a particular set of values of these variables. The proposed framework allows the optimization process to search the same pedagogical design space even though it uses a different underlying source of data by holding the set of decision variables fixed across all the datasets.

The way this formulation is written also permits the optimization algorithm to discover Pareto-optimal PBL designs to trade competing goals, including learning gain, development of digital literacy, and feasibility of implementation. The resulting design configurations can hence be understood pedagogically and can be applied directly by smart education practitioners.

Formulation of multi-objective problems

The unified feature representation and the fixed set of problem-based learning (PBL) design variables generates an instructional design task as a constrained multi-objective optimization problem. The goal is to determine pedagogically meaningful PBL configurations which maximize learning performance, digital literacy development and the feasibility of implementation in realistic educational constraints.

One of the main problems in this formulation is that no direct observation of the causal mappings between decisions in the design process and long-term educational consequences can be made. To overcome this drawback, the present study uses a feature-driven proxy modeling approach, which is based on the use of objective functions derived from observable learner behaviors collected from educational data. This allows for a consistent and interpretable optimization framework based on data and without any unsupported assumptions about causal learning outcomes39.

The pedagogical design space for optimization is normalized to ensure feasible pedagogical configurations:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0 \leqslant {x_i} \leqslant 1,\quad i=1, \ldots ,7$$\end{document}

Additionally, it is important to point out that the suggested objective formulations are designed to offer a computational level of abstraction of PBL-oriented instruction instead of a causal and predictive model of learning outcomes. Heuristic formulations of the weighting coefficients, the influence functions and the feature mappings were derived from some known pedagogical assumptions and uniformly normalized contributions of the features in the Unified Feature Space (UFS). To this end, the proposed framework is aimed at assessing the robustness-aware optimization behavior when facing different educational representations, instead of drawing validated causal links between different educational interventions and learning outcomes.

The suggested formulation offers a theoretical basis for determining a set of Pareto-optimal instructional design configurations in a computational way. The objective functions are not to be considered as “true,” but rather as model-based proxies that are based on observable learner behavior, and the resulting solutions are not measures of actual effectiveness of the educational system, but are rather comparative measures of instructional design alternatives within the modeled system.

Optimization procedure and implementation details

The optimization procedure and the details on how to implement the proposed framework are described in an integrated way, to ensure both methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Cross-Dataset Robust Multi-Objective Optimization (CD-RMO) is a population-based evolutionary algorithm that is used to find an approximate solution to the Pareto front of instructional design configurations. Each candidate solution is a vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{x}} \in {[0,1]^7}$$\end{document} that is a design configuration for PBL. The objective functions for the candidate solutions at each generation are the three functions outlined in Sect. 3.5, namely learning gain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_1}({\mathbf{x}})$$\end{document}, digital literacy development \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_2}({\mathbf{x}})$$\end{document}, and implementation cost \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_3}({\mathbf{x}})$$\end{document}.

The Pareto dominance criteria is used to identify non-dominated solutions and the diversity within the solution set is preserved by applying crowding-based distance measures. Offspring solutions are produced by the crossover and mutation operators and allows an effective exploration of the design space. The solutions are iteratively updated by mixing the parent and offspring solutions and choosing the next generation according to Pareto dominance and diversity.

One of the major elements of the framework is the process of converting the raw interactions into the unified feature representation. Raw logs of the OULAD, EdNet and KDD Cup 2010 datasets are processed to create a tidy set of behavioral features, consisting of interaction events, correctness records, times, and submission data. These features are mapped into the unified feature space defined in Sect. 3.3 which includes performance indicators, engagement measures, progression dynamics and digital interaction behaviors. All features are then scaled to the range [0,1] to allow for comparison between the sets and numerical stability in optimization40.

The objective functions are parameterized in a straightforward way from the formulation described in Sect. 3.5. The feature weighting schemes and the effect of instructional design variables on the contribution of features are defined as specified in Eqs. (1)– (7), so that there is uniformity between the conceptual model and its computational implementation. This formulation enables the optimization process to systematically examine the impact that different PBL design settings have on the combined measures of learning and digital behavior measures.

Standard evolutionary settings are used for the implementation of the optimization procedure to obtain a balance between convergence and diversification. The number of individuals in the population will be N = 100 and the number of generations will be G = 200. The probabilities of the simulated binary crossover and the polynomial mutation are 0.9 and 0.1, respectively. These settings were chosen for being stable in their convergence behavior and also to be well suited for exploration of the design space.

To capture the variation and robustness of candidate solutions, a set of perturbations of the feature space (uncertainty about learner behavior and data variations) is used. These realizations are used to calculate robustness-aware performance indicators to evaluate the (robustness) consistency of the solutions.

All components of the proposed framework, such as feature construction, objective function parameterization and optimization procedure, are clearly defined to enable the replication of the results. Note that the framework is a data-driven computational model – where objective functions are defined based on measurable behavioral aspects. Therefore, the solutions obtained should be read as instructional design configuration comparisons of the modeled system, not as predictions of actual outcomes in education.

Model proposed: cross-dataset robust multi-objective optimization (CD-RMO)

In a bid to overcome the shortcomings of traditional evolutionary multi-objective optimization models in the heterogeneous and data sensitive educational setting, this paper presents a new optimization model that can be dubbed Cross-Dataset Robust Multi-Objective Optimization (CD-RMO). The main aim of CD-RMO is not to find high-performing instructional design solutions to a single dataset, but to find robust and comparatively stable Pareto-optimal PBL configurations, the underlying performance of which is consistent in the face of data variability and sampling uncertainty.

Two important observations lead to the motivation of CD-RMO. First, educational data are always noisy, partial and context-specific, which may cause overfitting in case optimization is carried out on a single fixed data realization. Second, intelligent education systems need instructional designs that are not susceptible to changes in the population of learners, the pattern of engagement, and the digital infrastructure. The considerations are explicitly applied in the optimization by CD-RMO by integrating the notion of robustness into the objective evaluation phase.

Figure 3 shows that CD-RMO augments the conventional NSGA-II model35 by the inclusion of a powerful assessment layer between the unified feature space and the evolutionary selection scheme. This layer will assess the possible candidate solutions of the PBL design over many data realizations so that the optimization algorithm can prefer solutions that obtain consistent performance, as opposed to one-off high performance.

CD-RMO instead of evaluating \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{x}}$$\end{document} on a fixed dataset, evaluates it on a collection of plus data realizations of the dataset, obtained by resampling or partitioning the dataset. Strong framework of multi-objectives optimization

The CD-RMO model redefines the initial multi-objective PBL model design problem by substituting the nominal objective functions with their robust counterparts. In particular, the optimization problem is stated as follows:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox{max} ;\left( {f_{1}^{{rob}}({\mathbf{x}}),f_{2}^{{rob}}({\mathbf{x}})} \right),\quad \hbox{min} ;f_{3}^{{rob}}({\mathbf{x}})$$\end{document}

under the feasibility requirements that are outlined in Sect. 3.5. This formulation will make sure that candidate solutions are compared and discriminated according to their performance as well as their resistance to variance in data.

The standard non-dominated sorting and diversity preservation procedures of NSGA-II are used by CD-RMO within the evolutionary process. The dominance relations and crowding distances are however computed by using the robust objective values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{k}^{{rob}}({\mathbf{x}})$$\end{document} instead of nominal objective values. Therefore, the explicit evolutionary selection process is biased in favor of the solutions, which attain a balanced trade-off between the performance and the stability.

Workflow and novelty algorithms

The general flow of CD-RMO is in an iterative evolution cycle as shown in Fig. 4. Candidate PBL design solutions are decoded into instructional ones and tested in the unified feature space at every generation. Strong objective values are obtained by computing the performance of many data realizations. According to these values, the non-dominated sorting, selection, crossover, and mutation operations are used to produce new candidate solutions. The main novelty of CD-RMO is the incorporation of cross-dataset robustness in the fitness assessment step of multi-objective optimization, as opposed to the orchestration of robustness as the post hoc analysis. This design guarantees that the feature of robustness is inherent to the optimization process and it has a direct influence on the optimization outcome in terms of Pareto front. The derived solutions are therefore, high-performing, as well as resistant to changes in educational data, instructional designs. Using stability as an ingredient of the optimization loop, CD-RMO offers a data-conscious yet principled process of identifying stable and interpretable solutions of PBL design. The method is specifically applicable to intelligent learning settings, in which educational choices should be efficient throughout the learner groups and the digital environment.

State-of-the-art and baseline methods of comparisons

In order to strictly evaluate the performance of the proposed CD-RMO framework, we contrast it with a set of recommended and state of the art methods that can be seen as a particular type of instructional design and multi-objective optimization strategies. The chosen techniques of comparison include (i) un-optimized expert design, (ii) classical scalarized evolutionary optimization, (iii) canonical Pareto-based MOEAs and (iv) modern reference-vector-guided MOEAs. The design will allow the observed improvements to be ascribed to the strong multi-objective formulation and evaluation mechanism of CD-RMO instead of accidental algorithm decisions.

Expert-driven PBL

Method description

Such a baseline is a traditional instructional design environment whereby the parameters of PBL are defined by domain experts through heuristics of a pedagogical nature, school policy, and previous teaching experience. In practice, this is the choice of fixed values of the decision variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{x}}$$\end{document} (Sect. 3.4) based on no iterative search or data-driven optimality. The design that results is usually coherent in a pedagogical perspective, but not likely to be optimum with respect to competing quantitative outcomes.

Rationale for inclusion

This groundwork is necessary due to the fact that it provides the study with a realistic background in higher education practice. Computer decision support is not widely used in most institutions as they use expert committees to implement digital transformation initiatives and PBL. Thus, the approach can be used as a significant reference point to measure the added value of smart education design based on optimization.

Weighted-Sum in Genetic Algorithm (WS-GA)

WS-GA transforms the multi-objective problem into a single-objective one by constructing a scalar fitness function by using multiple objectives. In particular, the goals are pooled to form a weighted sum and a conventional genetic algorithm is used to maximize the ensuing scalar goal. This method is easy and it is computationally feasible but with proper weight tuning and in many cases the method fails to obtain more than one solution of the Pareto when trade-offs are not linear.

WS-GA is a popular classical method of educational optimization and multi-objective optimization in the cases when only one optimal configuration is sought. Its inclusion constitutes a direct display of the restraints of scalarization in the current context: (i) the sensitivity to subjective decisions concerning weight, (ii) the lack of reflecting non-convex trade-offs, and (iii) the lack of alternatives of design of instruction. With this background, the multi-objective character of CD-RMO can be justified, as well as the significance of Pareto-based decision support.

NSGA-II

NSGA-II is a canonical multi-objective evolutionary algorithm that approximates the Pareto front by using the fast non-dominated sorting and using the crowding-distance-based diversity preservation. In comparison with the weighted-sum strategies, NSGA-II directly chooses a population of solutions in the direction of a group of non-dominated alternatives without objective aggregation. NSGA-II applies the nominal objective assessment in Sect. 3.5 of this paper, with no powerful mean variance aggregation.

NSGA-II is added because of two reasons. First, it is an effective and well-known benchmark of multi-objective optimization, allowing the comparison with an accepted standard approach in different fields. Second, it offers a controlled reference in isolating the contribution of CD-RMO: as CD-RMO is constructed using the same principles of evolutionary selection only that nominal objectives are replaced with robust objectives, a direct comparison between CD-RMO and NSGA-II reveals the effect of robustness integration under otherwise similar evolutionary dynamics.

RVEA

RVEA refers to the recent multi-objective evolutionary algorithm that utilizes a range of reference vectors that direct the search process and preserve diversity. The given mechanism is one of the most efficient in the complicated trade-off landscapes and is often utilized in modern research as a very potent competitor to the classical MOEAs. The selection pressure of RVEA is organized in reference directions and this assists it in maintaining a well-spread approximation to the Pareto front in various areas of an objective space.

Incorporation of RVEA would mean that, besides being compared with the classical approaches, CD-RMO is also compared with the strategies of modern MOEA which are competitive in the existing literature. This has significance in determining that CD-RMO is viable in comparison to the current day optimization practice. In addition, the conceptual meaning of a comparison is that, whereas RVEA enhances diversity management by reference vectors, CD-RMO enhances the reliability of solutions by robust objective assessment. When both are evaluated in the same conditions, it will be made clear whether optimization based on robustness has benefits other than enhanced diversity control.

In general, the spectrum of comparison set is realistic in human-designed PBL configurations down to classical and modern evolutionary optimizers. The practical relevance of the expert-driven baseline test is validated; the scalarization limit is tested on WS-GA; Pareto based reference is offered by NSGA-II; and a modern SOTA is provided by RVEA. A combination of these will allow performing a strict assessment of CD-RMO with respect to convergence quality, diversity and cross-dataset robustness under experimental conditions. Experimental design

The protocol of the experiment was constructed in a way that provided fairness, reproducibility and methodological rigor in the comparison of the proposed CD-RMO framework with baseline and state-of-the-art approaches. Since we were working with stochastic evolutionary algorithms, we paid special attention to controlling the settings of the algorithms, computation budgets, and execution in each of the experiments due to the emphasis on robustness and cross-dataset consistency.

Every optimization procedure thought in this paper was implemented using a single experimental framework. Where applicable, core algorithmic settings, i.e. the population size, generations, crossover probability, mutation probability and termination criteria were fixed. In the case of parts of the methods (e.g., aggregating the objectives in weighted-sum GA with weight), the method standard settings were normally selected and kept constant across all datasets. All methods did not receive any parameter tuning or post hoc adjustment parameter-specific to a dataset. The design option avoids implicit overfitting and provides an assurance that observed performance differences are due to inherent methodological characteristics as opposed to good parameterization.

To ensure that the strictly fair comparison occurred, all optimization methods were given a fixed computational budget. Every algorithm had the same number of fitness evaluations in each run regardless of dataset size and objective formulation. In the case of CD-RMO where solutions to a problem are evaluated on a number of data realizations in order to compute robust objective values, the extra evaluation cost was perfectly reflected in the same budget constraint. As a result, the robustness was obtained without adding the total computational allowance to the baseline methods.

Three disparate educational datasets were experimented separately namely, OULAD, EdNet and KDD Cup 2010. The corresponding unified feature space construction process and the same definitions of PBL design variables were applied to each dataset. Each dataset was optimized individually and there was no transfer of information, parameters or intermediate results between datasets during the optimization process. The protocol allows one to test dataset-specific behavior in a controlled way and can be used to perform cross-dataset testing of robustness consistency.

In order to reduce the effect of randomization and chance variation, each of the optimization processes was run repeatedly on each dataset with a different random seed. The run-to-run performance statistics were then averaged to give average results. This is achieved through the improvement of the reliability of the experimental results and the meaningfulness of comparative analysis because it is a repetition-based execution protocol. Evaluation metrics

In order to critically evaluate the work of the proposed CD-RMO framework, a mathematically based evaluation protocol is taken. The metrics of evaluation are clearly defined and grouped into two supplementary, namely, the computational performance metrics which describe quality of Pareto front approximation and search behavior, and the statistical robustness metrics which quantifies stability, reliability, and cross-dataset cross-generalizability with data variability.

Performance in metrics computation

Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}={{\mathbf{x}}_1}, \ldots ,{{\mathbf{x}}_n}$$\end{document} refers to the set of Pareto-optimal solutions obtained, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})=({f_1}({\mathbf{x}}),{f_2}({\mathbf{x}}),{f_3}({\mathbf{x}}))$$\end{document} is the objective values.

a. Hypervolume (HV)

The hypervolume indicator is used to evaluate the volume of the objective space covered by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}$$\end{document} issues and spanned by a reference point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{r}}$$\end{document}. It is considered to be the Lebesgue measure:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{HV}}(\mathcal{P},{\mathbf{r}})=\lambda \left( {\bigcup\limits_{{i=1}}^{N} {\left[ {f_{1}^{i},{r_1}} \right] \times \left[ {f_{2}^{i},{r_2}} \right] \times ... \times \left[ {f_{k}^{i},{r_k}} \right]} } \right)$$\end{document}

and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda ( \cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the Lebesgue measure. Greater values of HV denote the high convergence and coverage of the Pareto front.

b. Inverted generational distance

For the Given a reference Pareto set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{P}^*}$$\end{document}, Inverted Generational Distance (IGD) is defined by the following Eq. 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{IGD}}(\mathcal{P},{\mathcal{P}^*})=\frac{1}{{\left| \mathcal{P} \right|}}\sum\limits_{{{\mathbf{y}} \in {\mathcal{P}^*}}} {{{\hbox{min} }_{{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathcal{P}}}} {\left\| {{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}}) - {\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{y}})} \right\|_2}$$\end{document}

Reduced values of IGD mean reduced distance to the reference Pareto front.

c. Spread (Δ)

The spread parameter determines how evenly the distribution of the solution along the Pareto front is and it is given by:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta =\frac{{\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{n} | {d_i} - \bar {d}|}}{{\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{d_i}} +(n - 1)\bar {d}}}$$\end{document}

In which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d_i}$$\end{document} represents the distance between successive solutions in objective space and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar {d}$$\end{document} is an average value. Minor values suggest superior diversity.

d. Spacing metric (SP)

The distance between neighboring solutions is measured by the way far apart these solutions are:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{SP}}=\sqrt {\frac{1}{{n - 1}}\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{n} {{{({d_i} - \bar {d})}^2}} }$$\end{document}

Reduced values of spacing are associated with regularly spaced Pareto fronts.

e. Convergence speed (CS)

The rate of convergence is used as a measure of the number of generations to achieve a steady plateau of the hypervolume:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{CS}}=\hbox{min} t\mid |{\mathrm{HV}}t - {\mathrm{HV}}t - 1|<\epsilon$$\end{document}

in which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon$$\end{document} is a minute convergence parameter.

Statistical and strength metrics

The measures of the statistical stability are used to evaluate the stability of the optimization results in the presence of stochastic effects, data resampling, and cross-dataset execution.

a. Objective Variability Index (OVI)

For objective k, the variability of a solution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{x}}$$\end{document} of individuals S data is defined as:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma _k}({\mathbf{x}})=\sqrt {\frac{1}{S}\sum\limits_{{s=1}}^{S} {{{\left( {f_{k}^{{(s)}}({\mathbf{x}}) - {{\bar {f}}_k}({\mathbf{x}})} \right)}^2}} }$$\end{document}

Mean variability in the Pareto set is then

22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{OVI}}k=\frac{1}{{|\mathcal{P}|}}\sum {{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathcal{P}} {\sigma _k}({\mathbf{x}})$$\end{document}

The lower the values the greater the robustness.

b. Robust Hypervolume (R-HV)

In order to calculate robust hypervolume, the robust objective evaluations are calculated under various perturbations and then used in a similar way as in Eq. (16). In particular, it is defined as:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{R-HV}}(\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{S}\sum\limits_{{s=1}}^{S} H {V^{(s)}}(\mathcal{P}) - \lambda \sqrt {\frac{1}{S}\sum\limits_{{s=1}}^{S} {{{\left( {H{V^{(s)}}(\mathcal{P}) - \overline {{HV}} (\mathcal{P})} \right)}^2}} }$$\end{document}

where the mean hypervolume is given by:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline {{HV}} (\mathcal{P})=\frac{1}{S}\sum\limits_{{k=1}}^{S} H {V^{(k)}}(\mathcal{P})$$\end{document}

Since in the robustness formulation the term is defined as the mean hypervolume, a separate summation index is used to prevent the definition from being ambiguous. Next, the variance component is calculated using the deviations from this mean, thus providing a clear and consistent definition of the robustness term.

c. Cross-Dataset Stability Index (CDSI)

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M_d}$$\end{document} be a performance measure of dataset d. Run to run consistency is used to determine stochastic reliability by calculating the standard deviation of something across multiple independent runs:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{RRC}}=\sqrt {\frac{1}{R}\sum\limits_{{r=1}}^{R} {{{({M_r} - \bar {M})}^2}} }$$\end{document}

Reduced values of RRC mean more stable optimization behavior. The proposed evaluation framework will be transparent, rigorous, and reproducible because it presents clear mathematical definitions of all metrics of evaluation. The two measures, computational and statistical metrics, can be used together to provide a more profound evaluation of the quality of convergence, the maintenance of diversity, the ability to withstand the variability of the data, and the stability of the results across the datasets, which, in turn, will allow providing the results with rich quantitative analysis and various visualizations. Results

The section provides an all-inclusive empirical assessment of the suggested Cross-Dataset Robust Multi-Objective Optimization (CD-RMO) framework. The outcomes are systematized in such a way that they analyze systematically the behavior of algorithms, quality of optimization, and robustness and existing in general across non-homogeneous educational data. The analysis does not focus solely on the final performance indicators; instead, it also examines the behavior of the optimization process, as well as the stability and cross-dataset robustness consistency of the resulting solutions under data variability and stochastic effects.

The analysis commences with the clear assessment of the convergence behavior and dynamics of search to disclose the inherent optimization properties of CD-RMO. The following subsections can analyze the geometric organization of Pareto fronts, their performance relative to those of the base and state-of-the-art algorithms and their resistance to data perturbation. The next two questions to explore the reliability and consistency of the proposed approach in various learning settings are the cross-dataset robustness consistency and run-to-run stability. Lastly, problem-based learning design variables sensitivity analysis also offers interpretability of multi-objective outcome in relation to instructional design decision-making.

Put together, these outcomes offer a multi-layered and graphically-enriched evaluation of CD-RMO, which allows the clear and evidence-based comparison and emphasizes the practical implications of robustness-conscious optimization on smart educational systems.

Strength in response to variation of data

In addition to average performance and quality of convergence, under data variability is a basic demand of optimization frameworks implemented in smart education setting. Educational data is highly noisy, incomplete, and context-dependent and optimization algorithms that are based on nominal objective assessment can become unstable when subjected to either data perturbation or resampling artifacts. The present subsection provides the assessment of the strength of the proposed CD-RMO structure against the background and state-of-the-art approaches based on the robustness-oriented indicators and supplementary visual inspection.

As a quantitative measure of robustness, two complementary measures are used, namely the Objective Variability Index (OVI) and robust hypervolume (R-HV). OVI records the sensitivity of objective values over a range of data realizations, and R-HV is the extension of the traditional measure of hypervolume by performance aggregation, which is based on robustness-aware performance. The smaller OVI values and the larger R-HV values are all evidence of greater strength in the face of data variability.

Fig. 12Procedures and data of contour maps of OVI.

Procedures and data of contour maps of OVI.

The robustness maps in terms of contour of the values of OVI in methods and data realizations of each data set are shown in Fig. 12. The contour presentation does not only show average robustness levels but also indicates the stability of each of the methods as data variability increases. Figure 12 demonstrates that CD-RMO always has the lowest OVI values in all datasets, which implies that it is less sensitive to the effects of data perturbation and resampling. Conversely, WS-GA has strong OVI variation in realizations, which is due to unstable behavior in the presence of data variability. The robustness of NSGA-II and RVEA are moderate on the OULAD data, but on EdNet and KDD Cup 2010, their OVI curves are left-shifted to high values and greater variability. CD-RMO preserves the small and low-variance OVI contours across datasets, which is key to the success of introducing the robustness into the optimization procedure.

Cross-dataset robustness consistency analysis

Besides resiliency to data variability, consistency on heterogeneous educational data is another significant feature of optimization frameworks in intelligent education environment. The learner sets in educational datasets vary significantly in terms of the number of learners, interactions and behavioral patterns. Cross-dataset robustness consistency evaluates how robust the behavior of optimization is across conditions of educational data that are different in important ways, while robustness-oriented measures evaluate how sensitive a measure is to perturbations within a single dataset.

To quantify this property, a Cross-Dataset Stability Index (CDSI) is used to quantify the variation in the optimization performance across OULAD, EdNet and KDD Cup 2010. The lower the CDSI value the relatively more stable the optimization behavior and the less sensitive the optimization behavior to the characteristics of the set of data.

Run-to-run stability and stochastic reliability

The evolutionary optimization algorithms are stochastic in nature; thus, the run-to-run stability and stochastic reliability are important parameters in deciding the practical implement ability of the algorithm in smart learning systems. Even when experiment conditions are the same, the results of various random initializations and stochastic operators can vary significantly. This means that optimization processes with high run-to-run variance may compromise the credibility of the advice given on instructional design.

Run-to-run variability is initially analyzed with indicators of variance based on the end-of-run hypervolume (HV), in the form of variance, standard deviation, coefficient of variation (CV), interquartile range (IQR), and the run-to-run coefficient (RRC). Figure 15 represents the HV distributions at each run-to-run in density-based contour representations of each dataset. WS-GA has a wide and diffuse density contour, meaning that there is great dispersion of HV values in independent runs (Fig. 15). The contours in NSGA-II and RVEA are more concentrated on OULAD, and more distributed on EdNet and KDD Cup 2010 indicating less stability with the increase in dataset complexity. Conversely, CD-RMO is uniformly characterized by compact and densely peaked density contours in all datasets and thus is exhibits a consistent convergence behavior and lowering stochastic sensitivity.

In addition to the measures of dispersion, stochastic reliability is also measured based on frequency-based performance. These are the likelihood of performing nearly optimally (Reliability), the likelihood of being the most performing method in a run (ProbBest), and the likelihood of being of the two most performing methods (Top2Freq). Table 13 reports the highest values of reliability of CD-RMO with all datasets of the models with the highest values of 0.833 on OULAD, 0.733 on EdNet and 0.800 on KDD Cup 2010. In addition to this, CD-RMO performs optimally on the ProbBest and Top2Freq measures, with the highest rate of performance on OULAD and in more than 83% of runs on all datasets. Conversely, WS-GA has almost zero ProbBest values on EdNet and KDD Cup 2010, which are the poorest stochastic reliabilities.

Lastly, the 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the mean HV would be available using a method of bootstrapping which further lends credence to these discoveries. On OULAD, CD-RMO always achieves the smallest confidence intervals, e.g., [0.7395, 0.7461], and on KDD Cup 2010, e.g., [0.6693, 0.6792], which means that its estimates of performance are narrow and consistent. An increased uncertainty indicated by a wider confidence interval in WS-GA and RVEA, and high sensitivity to stochastic variation.

Altogether, both the visual and numerical findings in Fig. 15; Table 13 indicate that the CD-RMO has better run-to-run stability and stochastic reliability than the baseline and state-of-the-art methods. These properties are needed in real world smart education applications where the result of the optimization should be replicable, reliable as well as interpretable under repeated executions.

PBL design variables sensitivity analysis

This section explores how sensitive the proposed CD-RMO framework is to the underlying PBL design variables to complement the performance and robustness analyses that were included in the previous subsections. There are two purposes for this analysis. First, it is useful for improving the interpretability of the Pareto-optimal solutions obtained, clarifying which of the dimensions of instructional design has the greatest influence on the obtained Pareto-optimal solutions. Second, it offers a window to the interaction mechanisms that lead to robustness-aware optimization performance over a broad span of educational datasets that are heterogeneous.

The same set of decision variables, presented in Sect. 3.4, is used throughout OULAD, EdNet and the KDD Cup 2010 for the sensitivity analysis. This therefore allows direct comparison between the seven normalized PBL design variables x1 – x7 presented in Table 2 and the analyses performed in this section, to ensure methodological consistency between the optimization formulation and the interpretation of the results of the sensitivity analysis.

The normalized main-effect sensitivity of the seven PBL design variables is shown in Fig. 16, where the three objectives of optimization – learning gain, digital literacy development and implementation cost/risk – are presented. The sensitivity patterns are similar across the datasets, with sensitivity focusing more on variables related to the pedagogical core of PBL rather than variables related to (primarily) “how” PBL is delivered and orchestrated. This tendency is summarized in aggregated, quantitative main-effect sensitivity scores in Table 14.

Scaffolding Strength (x3) and PBL Intensity (x1) have the highest overall influence score, followed by the other factors. This finding aligns with the principles of problem-based learning (PBL), which emphasize the progressive relationship between the level of instructional support and problem-based learning activities as a key to the learners’ performance and engagement. The effect of Digital Tool Integration (x5) and Collaboration Level (x2) is also significant, indicating that digitally mediated interaction and collaborative learning structures have a significant impact on the optimization trade-offs, especially the one for digital literacy development.

In contrast, the values for Authenticity of Assessment (x6) are relatively low to show the sensitivity, suggesting that assessment realism appears to be a secondary moderating factor once the basic instruction is in place. The same applies for Personalization Degree (x7), which has a moderate effect in all objectives and indicates that adaptive support mechanisms can enhance optimization performance mainly in conjunction with adequate amounts of scaffolding and collaboration.

As a whole, the results presented in Figs. 16, 17 and 18; Tables 14, 15, 16 and 17 reveal that the proposed CD-RMO framework can generate strong Pareto-optimal instructional design configurations and provide pedagogical insights that help understand the relative influence and interaction structure of PBL design variables. Discussion

The present study aims to present a robustness-aware approach towards instructional design optimization in intelligent higher education environment, due to the heterogeneities, noises, and contexts in educational data. The proposed Cross-Dataset Robust Multi-Objective Optimization (CD-RMO) framework introduces the robustness criterion as a direct part of the multi-objective optimization process, thereby avoiding the focus on just nominal performance maximization within each particular dataset. The framework seeks to examine instructional design configurations which are stable, under realistic data variability and stochastic perturbations. The results of the different analyses presented in Sect. 4 all indicate the relevance of this robustness-aware perspective not only from a methodological point of view, but also from a pedagogical point of view.

The comparative evaluation results show that under the proposed modeling framework, CD-RMO offers relatively good Pareto-front quality, convergence consistency and robustness-consistent optimization behavior, compared to the expert-based baselines, classic EA and recent state-of-the-art optimization approaches. Importantly, these behaviours are relatively consistent across the different heterogeneous datasets and evaluation measures, such as robust hypervolume and objective variability. The consistency in this suggests that the observed optimization behavior cannot just be explained by the sampling effects and by favorable data correlations in the datasets alone. Rather, the results show that the formulation of CD-RMO with robustness explicitly built into it is systematically penalizing sensitivity to data perturbations when optimized. The growing disparity in performance in more complex datasets also indicates that the evaluation of intelligent learning systems must be robust as the learning systems in the real world are increasingly large, heterogeneous, and structurally complex, as is typical in educational datasets.

In addition to aggregate optimization performance, the sensitivity and interaction analysis gives insights into the internal structure of robustness-aware instructional design solutions that are interpretable. Specifically, the factors of Scaffolding Strength and PBL Intensity are consistently the most important factors influencing the performance of optimization under the condition of robustness, indicating that instructional support and problem-centeredness learning structure are important factors in determining learning behavior under conditions of uncertainty in learning. Concurrently, there are several variables that show relatively low independent effects and significant interaction effects. The results in this study align with the notion that a good PBL design is not simply defined as a combination of specific PBL parameters but is influenced by the interaction of the various elements of PBL that form a larger pedagogical context.

The sensitivity analysis also confirms the interaction-driven behavior seen in the robustness evaluation (Sobol style). In particular, negative first-order Sobol estimates for some of the variables should not be considered as a sign of numerical instability. Rather, these estimates indicate that under robustness-aware evaluation conditions the influence of some of the instructional variables is mainly mediated through higher-order interactions. This finding is in line with the non-linear and systemic nature of learning contexts, where learning elements often reinforce, modify or limit each other, depending on the overall educational framework.

The comparative robustness evaluation of the study, on heterogeneous educational data, is one of its key contributions. The optimization framework is tested with actual real educational data, however the abstraction performed based on the robustness concept in CD-RMO is supposed to be able to detect the optimization behaviors that are relatively stable when the data set varies or is disturbed by stochastic noise. Instead of depending on the characteristics of the datasets or platform-dependent correlations, the framework recognizes instructional design configurations that show the robustness-consistent optimization behavior on a variety of data realizations. The cross-dataset stability analyses confirm this interpretation and show that ranking behavior is relatively stable across datasets and that there is less ranking variation between less and more structurally complex datasets. The results therefore indicate that robustness-aware optimization can offer a fruitful computational framework to consider instructional design decisions given uncertain data conditions in the educational space.

Combined, the findings imply that robustness can be considered one of the key design principles of intelligent education systems computational approaches. The proposed CD-RMO framework presents a way to connect multi-objective evolutionary optimization and decision oriented computational modeling to jointly tackle the robustness-versus-interpretability-versus-instructional design trade-off in a single optimization process. More generally, this study suggests that pedagogically-based modeling and computational optimization methods are useful in facilitating computationally-informed digital transformation in higher education contexts.

Future work and conclusion

This study presented a robustness-aware computational framework of problem-based learning (PBL) design in intelligent higher education environments, called Cross-Dataset Robust Multi-Objective Optimization (CD-RMO). The CD-RMO models instructional design as a robustness-aware decision-making problem instead of simply a dataset-specific performance maximization problem by combining digital transformation goals, instructional design variables and digital literacy aspects in a single optimization formulation.

Experimental assessment on the heterogeneous educational datasets OULAD, EdNet and KDD Cup 2010 shows that CD-RMO results in the comparatively good Pareto-front quality, convergence consistency and optimization behavior that is robust against the presence of inaccuracies in the datasets when using the proposed modelling framework, as compared to expert-based baselines, classical evolutionary algorithms and recent state of the art approaches. The observed consistency of these behaviors over datasets further emphasizes the need to explicitly consider data consistency and uncertainty in computational methods for smart education optimization.

In addition to optimization performance, the sensitivity and interaction analyses deliver to interpretable suggestions of the structure of PBL instructional design. Specifically, the design variables of Scaffolding Strength, PBL Intensity, and Feedback Frequency are shown to have significant impacts on robustness-aware optimization performance, whereas some of the design variables drive their performance in predominantly interactive ways. The data confirm the idea that good instructional design is the outcome of concerted interactions among elements of the pedagogy, not just the simple change of individual parameters.

The optimization model is tested with actual education data, but the abstraction by robustness that is used in CD-RMO is designed to find optimization behaviors that are relatively stable in case of data variability and stochastic perturbations. The proposed framework thus facilitates identifying instructional design configurations that perform robustly with diverse sets of data, not necessarily across real-world educational contexts, learner populations or institutional environments.

This study also offers some ideas for further research. One, including stakeholders’ preferences in multi-objective evolutionary optimization could help in interactive exploration of context-specific parts of the Pareto front. Secondly, the framework can be extended to include temporal features such as concept drift and changing learner behavior to make the framework more realistic in practice. Finally, CD-RMO is an opportunity to investigate the robustness-aware optimization in an operational smart education environment, along with adaptive learning systems and learning management systems.

The findings of this study suggest that robust optimization based multi-objective optimization can be a principled computational approach to exploring trade-offs in instructional design in higher education driven by an intelligent system. The proposed framework combines the ideas of computational optimization and pedagogically-driven modeling, and was shown to offer methodological understanding into the robustness-aware instructional design when educational data conditions are heterogeneous.
