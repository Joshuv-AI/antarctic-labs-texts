Dynamic Correlation Analysis between Stress–Strain Curve and Polymer Film Structure Using Persistent Homology
Authors: Ryuhei Sato, Shinya Kawakami, Hirotaka Ejima, Takahiro Ujii, Koichi Sato, Takanori Ichiki, Yasushi Shibuta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Coarse-grained molecular dynamics (CG-MD) simulations and subsequent persistent homology (PH) analysis were performed to correlate the structure and stress–strain behavior of polymer films. During uniaxial tensile MD simulations, the first principal component of the persistence diagram obtained by principal component analysis (PCA) was in good agreement with the stress–strain curve. This indicates that PH + PCA can identify critical ring structures relevant to the dynamic changes in MD simulations without requiring any prior knowledge. Inverse analysis of the persistence diagram revealed that smaller rings with ten or fewer CG beads mainly contribute to changes in the first principal component of the persistence diagram. This is due to the properties of the poly(ethylene oxide) chain, which favors the formation of a seven-membered helical structure during the self-entanglement process. The PH + PCA approach successfully reproduced the stress–strain curves for polymers with different nonbonding interactions and bond lengths. Moreover, the changes in the yield stress of each polymer film were qualitatively explained by the ring distribution in the persistence diagram. These results suggest that persistent homology analysis followed by PCA provides a versatile and powerful framework for correlating structural features with physical properties, such as ring distribution and stress–strain behavior in polymer films.

Introduction

Polymer latex films, used as painting materials,1−3 are mass-produced commodities of significant commercial importance. The demand for waterborne paints, in particular, has increased due to their environmental friendliness and safety. These polymer latex films are typically formed by the interdiffusion of polymer chains in emulsions, accompanying the volatilization of water.1,2 The mechanical strength and stability of the resulting films are critical for industrial applications and are known to be influenced by additives and the glass transition temperature of binders.2

While macroscopic experimental analyses, such as small-angle neutron4 (or X-ray5) scattering and fluorescence resonance energy transfer,6 have provided valuable insights into the interdiffusion between polymers and their effects on mechanical properties, there is a certain demand for atomic-scale analyses. Such analyses are important for directly linking polymer structure with mechanical properties, offering a better understanding for these polymer films. Theoretical simulations have played a pivotal role in advancing polymer science at the microscopic level. For instance, Brownian dynamics simulations have been used to explore the effects of surface charge during polymer film formation.7 The molecular dynamics (MD) simulation has been used to evaluate phenomena related to mechanical properties such as stretch-induced crystallization8 and the deformation of amorphous polymers.9−13 However, it is not easy to identify the local structure during dynamic deformation processes such as tensile tests during MD simulations. Consequently, clarifying the correlation between the mechanical properties of polymeric materials and their structures is not straightforward.

Persistent homology analysis has been used for analyzing medium-range order in amorphous structures,14 describing the higher-order structures of polymer materials,15 and estimating the glass transition temperature of polymer materials.16 Therefore, it is expected that persistent homology analysis can identify important structures from polymer films with complex higher-order structures due to the self-entanglement and interdiffusion of polymers. When combined with machine learning, persistent homology analysis has been utilized to predict physical properties such as the thermal conductivity17 and stress–strain curves18 of inorganic materials. Recently, it has also been successfully applied as a method for tracking physical phenomena during MD simulations, such as ion migration19 and nucleation.20 Therefore, by analyzing changes in the persistence diagram during MD simulations, it is also anticipated that phenomena occurring during MD simulations can be correlated with the ring structures. In fact, previous research on glassy polymers12 successfully correlated the stress–strain curve with the number of voids using persistent homology analysis. However, a more detailed analysis is required to establish a direct relationship between mechanical properties and specific ring or void structures. In this context, we propose a method (PH + PCA) that combines persistent homology analysis with principal component analysis to analyze MD trajectories, which can be applied to a wide variety of MD simulations involving dynamic changes such as reactions and phase transitions. The PH + PCA approach extracts significant ring structures corresponding to dynamic changes during MD simulations from persistence diagrams without any prior knowledge. Here, we applied PH + PCA to the trajectories obtained from coarse-grained (CG) MD simulations, aiming to deepen our understanding of the relationship between the polymer structure and the stress–strain behavior during uniaxial tensile simulations of polymer films.

Simulation Methodology

CG̵MD simulations were performed to analyze the stress–strain behavior of the polymer films. We used the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS)21 for these simulations. We used the MARTINI CG force field,22−24 a CG model that accurately reproduces the behavior of poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) in aqueous solutions. In this model, the O–C–C or C–O–C fragments in these polymers are approximated as CG beads. This potential consists of a harmonic potential for the bond length and angle of the covalent bond between CG beads, a dihedral angle term (eqs 1-(3)), and Lennard-Jones parameters (eq 4) that describe the nonbonding interaction between CG beads.1234

We created a linear polymer chain by combining 1000 CG beads. This polymer chain was relaxed using 1.0 ns NVT-MD simulation at 296 K to create a self-entangled polymer chain (Figure 1). Then, we placed 27 such self-entangled polymers in a cubic crystal cell with a side length of 240 Å. The polymers contacted each other and interdiffusion occurs during 0.75 ns NPT-MD simulation at 500 K. To make the polymers stick together, the pressure is initially set to 0.2 atm for 0.25 ns, and then reduced to 0.0 atm for the relaxation. Note that 500 K was used solely for the initial coordinate preparation to achieve an interdiffusion structure within the time frame of the MD simulation. After this process, to eliminate the effects of thermal lattice expansion, this polymer film was cooled from 500 to 296 K using 0.75 ns NPT-MD simulations. Uniaxial tensile (NVT) MD simulations were then performed by deforming the obtained polymer film in the z-axis direction until dz/z reaches to 100%. The time step is set to 5 fs, and the deformation speed of the cell is set so that dz/z becomes 100% at 200000 steps. Nose–Hoover thermostat25,26 was employed for the temperature and pressure control of all simulations in this study. Open Visualization Tool (OVITO)27 was used for the visualization of the structures obtained during the MD simulations.

Schematic image of the workflow used to make the polymer film used in this research. Each polymer is made of 1000 CG beads, and the different colors correspond to the different polymers.

After MD simulations, a persistent homology analysis was performed on the obtained coordinates of CG beads. Homcloud28 was used as the package for the persistent homology analysis. In persistent homology analysis, we analyzed first-order holes defined in homology as ring structures using positions of CG beads. Results and Discussion

Persistent Homology Analysis of the Uniaxial Tensile MD Simulation for Polymer Film

Figure 2 shows snapshots of the polymer film during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation. As shown in Figure 2A, up to γ = 12.5%, each polymer undergoes elastic deformation and stretches. On the other hand, at γ = 50%, voids are formed between the polymers, and the nonbonding interactions between these polymers partially disappear. Figure 2B shows a snapshot of the selected four polymers among 27 polymers in the film. A void among the polymers appears between γ = 12.5% and 17.5%. In addition, at γ = 50% and 100%, where the polymer film has been further deformed, these polymer chains are elongated vertically and becoming longer in the simulation cell. In other words, some of these polymers are stretching by breaking the nonbonding interactions within them.

Snapshots during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation of the polymer film consisting of CG beads for PEO. Each polymer in the calculation cell is displayed in a different color. (A) Overall view and (B) enlarged view showing only four selected polymers of 27 in the system. Note that the initial cell parameter at γ = 0% was 114 × 140 × 111 Å3.

Figure 3 shows the stress–strain curve (S–S curve) during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation. The stress reaches its maximum at around γ = 14%, corresponding to the yield stress for this polymer film. This S–S curve shows that the irreversible deformation due to the breaking of nonbonding interaction between polymers at γ = 50% is quite reasonable. Figure 3 (bottom) shows the PC1 obtained by PCA of the persistent image as a function of γ during MD simulation. Note that the explained variance ratio of PC1 is 0.94, showing that PC1 explained the change in persistent image during MD simulation well. PC1 agrees well with the S–S curve during the MD simulation, and the correlation coefficient is 0.95, showing the strong correlation between them. In other words, it is considered that the change in stress during uniaxial tensile MD simulation can be characterized by the ring structure increase and decrease identified by the persistent image.

(Top) Stress–strain curve and (bottom) PC1 of the persistent image obtained by PCA during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation for the polymer film.

Figure 4A shows weighted persistence diagrams during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation using eq 7. In these figures, the (b, d) meshes that largely contribute to PC1 are emphasized to find the important ring structures during PCA. As shown in Figure 4A, the ring structures are distributed around birth = 4–5 Å. Before entanglement, the polymer is a single chain, and it is impossible to create ring structures with only the bonds between CG beads within the polymer, whose distance is 3.3 Å. Therefore, the ring structures should contain nonbonding interactions between CG beads. This is the bottleneck for ring formation during persistent homology analysis. In other words, most ring structures should appear around birth ≈4.8 Å, which corresponds to the equilibrium distance of the LJ potential for this nonbonded interaction.

Therefore, these diagrams emphasize the ring structures with a large contribution to PC1. The colors in the figure were determined according to the color scale in the upper right, and only meshes with values of 0.001 or more are shown for the sake of clarity. The mesh is divided into 0.02 × 0.02 Å2, and the calculation is performed in the range [2.0, 12.0] × [2.0, 12.0] (Å2). (B) Distribution of w’bd = tan–1(0.05(d–b)3)wbd, the coefficient for PC1, at each (b, d) mesh. As indicated by the color scale, red corresponds to positive and blue corresponds to negative coefficients for PC1. The contour lines in the figure represent the time-averaged persistence diagrams during the MD simulation.

In Figure 4A, the number of two kinds of ring structures mainly changes during the MD simulation: one is the ring structure (ring1) around b, d = (4.5, 5.5), which has already existed before the uniaxial tensile simulation. The other is ring2 around (birth, death) = (4.5, 6.0), whose distribution becomes most prominent at γ = 14%. As shown in the figure, ring1 decreases around γ = 14% and then increases again. In contrast, ring2 increases up to around γ = 14% and then decreases. Therefore, it is expected that the increase and decrease of these two ring structures is related to the stress–strain behavior in this polymer film. Figure 4B shows the distribution of w’bd = tan–1(0.05(d–b)3)wbd, which is multiplied to each (b, d) mesh to calculate PC1. PC1 value increases as the ring structure (ring2) in the red part of Figure 4B increases. On the other hand, the blue area (ring1) corresponds to the area where w’bd is negative. Thus, if the blue area in the persistent diagram increases, PC1 decreases.

Note that large cavities are formed during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation, as shown in Figure 2. Therefore, ring structures corresponding to these large cavities are observed in the raw persistent diagrams without multiplying any coefficients like those in Figure S2. However, the number of these rings is small, and they hardly contribute to PC1. Therefore, we ignored the rings for large cavities (with d > 12 Å) in these PCA analysis.

Inverse Analysis of the Persistence Diagram for Uniaxial Tensile MD Simulation

In order to clarify the structural differences between ring1 and ring2, we performed an inverse analysis and took statistics on these ring structures. Due to the computational cost of the inverse analysis of the persistent diagram, we only analyzed approximately 10,000 particles in the simulation cell as shown in Figure 5A. Although we used a smaller number of CG beads, PC1 is in good agreement with that of all the particles (Figure 5B). In addition, in order to obtain statistics only for the ring structures that contribute largely to PC1, we only analyzed rings whose |w’bd| is larger than 10% of the maximum |w’bd| value in the persistence diagram.

(A) Snapshots of the polymer film used in the inverse analysis of the persistent homology analysis. (B) PC1 as a function of γ (black line) calculated with all the CG beads in the polymer film and (red circles) with CG beads used for the inverse analysis as shown in panel (A).

Figure 6A shows the number distribution of CG beads that constitutes each ring (ring1 and ring2) in the polymer film during the MD simulation. From the distribution for ring1, it is found that the polymer film before the uniaxial tensile is mainly composed of rings with 10 or fewer CG beads. As can be seen from the change in the total number of ring1 + ring2 in the lower left of Figure 6A, the number of rings with 10 or more CG beads is almost constant during the MD simulation, showing the importance of the rings with less than 10 CG beads for the mechanistic property during uniaxial tensile MD simulation. The number of rings with 10 or more CG beads in ring2 is much higher than that in ring1. Therefore, it is considered that the decrease in ring1 contributed to the S–S curve being larger than the increase in ring2. Figure 6B shows the sum of the radial distribution functions of all of the CG beads in these ring structures. Here, since we would like to analyze the bond distribution within the ring structure, we did not calculate average RDF around each CG bead but the sum of these RDFs within the ring structure. Because the average numbers of CG beads in ring1 and ring2 at each γ are different, the number of bonded and nonbonded interactions within the rings could be also different. In fact, the height of the peak around r = 3.3 Å changes in accordance with the change in the average number of CG beads in the ring structure. However, as shown in Figure 6B, there is no significant change in the distribution around r = 4–4.6 Å, which corresponds to nonbonded interactions. Number of bonds between CG beads in the polymer film should be constant regardless of the degree of entanglement of each polymer. Therefore, the increase or decrease in nonbonding interactions between CG beads is more likely to contribute to the S–S curve than bonding interaction. From this perspective, we concluded that the change in the number of rings rather than the unique structure of ring 1 and ring2 contributes to the mechanistic property. In fact, as shown in Figure 6C, when the averaged RDF for all CG beads in polymer film was analyzed, the peak around r = 4–5 Å, which corresponds to the nonbonding interaction, increases and decreases with the stress in the S–S curve.

The contribution of small ring structures with 10 or fewer CG beads is reasonable, in terms of the self-entangled structure of the PEO polymer chain. The bond angle formed by the three CG beads in the PEO polymer is approximately 130° based on the functional form of the MARTINI CG force field. If a circle is formed with CG beads in the polymer, it becomes a seven-membered ring as shown in Figure 7A (top left). Therefore, in the initial stages of the MD simulation for the polymer self-entanglement, seven-membered helical structures were observed (Figure 7A). As the self-entanglement proceeds, the polymer chain could coil around other CG beads (Figure 7B). In this case, up to 8 or 9 CG beads will be required to form a circle, as shown in Figure 7B (left). Therefore, considering the way of self-entanglement of the polymer, it is most likely that ring structures are formed using 10 or fewer CG beads. In fact, the distribution of large ring structures in the persistence diagram during polymer self-entanglement agrees with that of the polymer film (Figure 4A). In addition, as shown in Figure S3, the inverse analysis has confirmed that ring1 and ring2 in the persistence diagrams at least contain CG bead fragments similar to those in Figure 7A,B. Therefore, we conclude that persistent homology analysis identifies the untangling and stretching behaviors of such polymer structures by PCA during the MD simulation.

PCA of Persistent Homology Analysis for Polymers with Different Bond Properties

As described above, persistent homology analysis and subsequent PCA revealed the relationship between the stress–strain behavior and the local structure of the polymer film during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation under a specific condition. To confirm that the method can be extended to polymer films with a wide range of properties, we next applied it to polymer films with varying characteristics. Here, persistent homology analysis and subsequent PCA were performed for polymer films with different bond properties (i.e., nonbonding interactions and bond lengths) under the uniaxial tensile MD simulations. In Figure 8, uniaxial tensile MD simulations were performed by changing the nonbonding interactions between CG beads in the Martini CG force filed. Here, we changed ε in eq 4, the depth of the LJ potential used as the nonbonding interaction between CG beads. Thus, increasing ε strengthens the nonbonded interaction, whereas decreasing ε weakens it.

(A) Stress–strain curves and PC1 during uniaxial tensile MD simulations of polymer films with different nonbonding interactions (ε = 1.25ε0, 0.625ε0). (B) Persistence diagrams for ε = 0.625ε0 and 1.25ε0 subtracting that for ε = ε0 as a reference. Here, the mesh is divided into 0.05 × 0.05 Å2, and the calculation is performed in the range [2.0, 12.0] × [2.0, 12.0] (Å2).

Figure 8A shows the S–S curve and PC1 during the uniaxial tensile MD simulations with different ε (see Figure S4 for the snapshots of polymer films during these MD simulations). When the nonbonding interaction changes, PC1 still agrees well with the S–S curve. The change in the microscopic structure of the polymer film is reflected in the persistence diagram. Figure 9B shows the persistence diagram for ε = 0.625ε0 and 1.25ε0 subtracting that for ε = ε0 as a reference. Since coefficients for PC1 differ for each MD simulation, we employed the raw persistent diagrams without multiplying any coefficient. When ε is decreased, the number of ring structures with a large death–birth increases as shown in Figure 8B. In other words, the distance between CG particles in the ring became large, and polymer chains were entangled loosely, resulting in the decrease in the yield stress. On the other hand, when ε is increased, the number of small death–birth rings increases, indicating that the network of nonbonding interactions becomes denser. The comparison between these persistence diagrams also shows that not only the relatively large rings around (b, d) = (4.5, 5.5) but also the small rings around (b, d) = (4.8, 5.0) increase and decrease with ε. Owing to their number, these smaller rings also contribute to PC1, as shown in Figure 4A. These ring structures are considered to correspond to the small nonbonding interaction networks such as those between seven-membered rings of the helical structure in the lateral view in Figure 7A. Therefore, it is considered that PCA of the persistence diagram not only identifies the entangling, unentangling, and stretching of the polymer chains as the increase or decrease in larger rings but also considers, to some extent, the density of the nonbonding interaction networks.

Same as the results of polymer films with different nonbonding interaction, PC1 agrees well with the S–S curve when the polymer morphology changes. Figure 9A shows the S–S curve and PC1 obtained from uniaxial tensile MD simulations of polymer films with different bond lengths between CG beads (dis0 = 2.0 and 2.7 Å). Please refer to Figure S5 for the snapshots of polymer film during these MD simulations. PC1 is in good agreement with the S–S curve. Figure 9B shows the persistence diagram for dis0 = 2.0 and 2.7 Å subtracting that for PEO (dis0 = 3.3 Å) as a reference. As with the results for nonbonding interactions, the number of small ring structures increases with the yield stress. Therefore, we concluded that the yield stress increased as a result of the dense nonbonded interactions owing to the shorter bond length of CG beads, which increases the density of CG beads. Above study shows that persistent homology analysis is a robust approach to analyze stress–strain behavior of a wide variety of polymer films not only with different nonbonding interactions, but also with different polymer morphologies (bond lengths).

These results are consistent with those of previous research. Ichinomiya et al.12 conducted persistence homology analysis in the same way as this study and analyzed three-dimensional voids (holes on the second-order homology). As a result, it was shown that the number of voids can roughly explain the S–S curve. Since it is thought that the breaking of nonbonding interactions is essential for the formation of voids, this is similar to the conclusions of our study. Makke et al.13 also analyzed the formation of cavities in tensile MD simulations of glassy polymers. They showed that cavities form in regions with a low elasticity. From this, it can be expected that cavities will form in regions with a high density of nonbonding interactions, where CG beads are difficult to move. Therefore, in cases where the density of CG beads increases, such as by changing the bond length in this study, the formation of voids requires breaking more nonbonding interactions, leading to a corresponding increase in the stress required. This interpretation aligns with the results of this study (Figure 9). Thus, the discussion in our paper that the density of nonbonding interactions is proportional to stress is consistent with the cavity generation mechanism in the previous study.13

Although the persistent-homology-analysis study by Ichinomiya et al.12 are similar to this report, the advantage of our approach (PH + PCA) is its generality. In other words, PH + PCA can extract features by using only MD trajectories without any prior knowledge. It is not necessary to have knowledge like that the number of voids is strongly related to stress–strain behavior. In addition, PH+PCA is not limited to polymer materials or stress–strain behavior. Although the actual application is a future work, in principle, we could apply PH + PCA to other phenomena during MD simulations.

Conclusions

In order to correlate the stress–strain behavior with the structure in polymer film, we performed a persistent homology analysis during uniaxial tensile MD simulations using the coarse-grained (CG) model. The first principal component (PC1) of the persistent diagram obtained by principal component analysis (PCA) agrees well with the stress–strain (S–S) curve during uniaxial tensile MD simulations. In other words, it is confirmed that the S–S curve is characterized by the ring structure in the persistence diagram. In the inverse analysis of the persistence diagram, the number of ring structures with 10 or fewer CG beads decreased during the MD simulation. This is consistent with the seven-membered helical structure obtained during the self-entanglement process of a single PEO polymer chain. This is also supported from the functional form of the Martini CG force field. Therefore, it is considered that the persistence diagram captures the untangling and stretching behavior of this seven-membered helical structure during the uniaxial tensile MD simulation. PCA of persistence diagrams is also effective for polymer chains with different nonbonding interactions and bond lengths, reproducing the behavior of the S–S curve well with PC1. In addition, the increase in yield stress was qualitatively proportional to the size of the ring in the persistent diagram, and it was confirmed that it depended on the density of nonbonding interactions. Consequently, we concluded that persistent homology analysis can characterize the stress–strain behavior of polymer film without any prior knowledge and is effective for analyzing the structures that are important for dynamical process.
