Robust associations between physiological CTG interpretation and improved neonatal outcomes after excluding tocolysis
Authors: Sophia Andres, Frank Reister, Kay Stankov
Published: 2026

Sir,

We thank Dr. Boujenah for his valuable comments on our manuscript. 1 He raised methodological concerns which we are very happy to comment on. 2

We agree that tocolysis differs from baseline characteristics, such as maternal age, BMI, or parity. It is a clinical intervention that occurs during labor and may itself be influenced by the implementation of physiological CTG interpretation (PCI).

To evaluate whether the original inclusion of tocolysis influenced our findings, we repeated our statistical analyses and modeling without tocolysis. We also revised the covariate set in accordance with the methodological recommendations raised in your letter. Covariates were selected a priori according to their clinical relevance and potential association with neonatal outcome, rather than because they differed significantly between the two study periods. The revised model included maternal age, maternal pre‐pregnancy BMI, parity, gravidity, gestational age in days, induction of labor, epidural analgesia, and fetal malpresentation. Multicollinearity was acceptable, with all variance inflation factors below 5.

The revised analyses confirmed the direction and statistical significance of our original findings. In the multivariate logistic regression, implementation of physiological CTG interpretation remained associated with lower odds of the composite neonatal outcome (adjusted OR 0.699, 95% CI 0.531–0.914; p = 0.0096). This corresponds to approximately 30% lower adjusted odds in the intervention period.

We also repeated the propensity‐score matching analysis using the same covariates and excluding tocolysis. In the matched cohort, the composite neonatal outcome occurred in 6.66% in the control group and in 4.60% births in the intervention group (cOR 0.672; 95% CI 0.508–0.889; Mantel–Haenszel p = 0.0051). Thus, regression and matching produced closely aligned estimates.

We additionally explored whether tocolysis might mediate part of the association. Implementation was strongly associated with increased use of tocolysis (adjusted OR 3.257, 95% CI 2.810–3.776; p < 0.001). However, the estimated indirect effect through tocolysis was positive (average causal mediation effect 0.0062, 95% CI 0.0031–0.0103; p < 0.001), whereas the estimated direct effect was negative (average direct effect −0.0135, 95% CI −0.0244 to −0.0021; p = 0.018). These opposing effects indicate inconsistent mediation.

This mediation result should be interpreted cautiously. Tocolysis was administered in response to uterine hyperstimulation or CTG abnormalities and was associated with both PCI implementation and neonatal outcomes. However, the observational data do not establish a causal or mediating effect. Increased tocolysis may instead reflect earlier recognition of fetal compromise and more timely intervention. Most importantly, after excluding tocolysis and applying a broader, clinically defined covariate set, both revised analyses continued to show an approximately 30% reduction in the odds of the composite neonatal outcome. This argues against the suggestion that the original association was created by adjusting or matching for tocolysis.

We fully agree that a Hawthorne effect may have contributed to the observed outcome changes. We addressed this limitation extensively in our discussion, although without explicitly using the term “Hawthorne effect.” At the same time, we would like to emphasize that education and increased awareness alone do not necessarily translate into improved neonatal outcomes. Thellesen et al. evaluated a national interprofessional CTG education program in Denmark and did not observe a reduction in fetal hypoxia‐related neonatal outcomes after implementation. 3 Thus, while a Hawthorne effect and increased staff awareness cannot be excluded, they may not fully explain the observed changes in our cohort. It is possible that the specific physiology‐based interpretation framework, together with the resulting changes in intrapartum management, contributed to the observed improvement.

The 3‐year interval between cohorts reflect the implementation period and was chosen to minimize contamination. As already acknowledged in the manuscript, the pre–post design demonstrates association, not causality.

We fully agree that further investigation using multicenter designs are needed. Nevertheless, the consistency of our findings after all requested statistical modifications supports their robustness and suggests that our study is an important first step toward demonstrating that physiological CTG interpretation may improve neonatal outcomes without compromising maternal outcomes.
