Contextual priority attention enables linear time sequence modeling in transformers
Authors: Karim Ben Khaled, Davy Monticolo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quadratic computational complexity of the self-attention mechanism in Transformer models severely constrains their applicability to long sequence inputs. We propose Contextual Priority Attention (CPA), a novel attention mechanism that reduces computational complexity from to theoretically, with experimentally observed linear scaling, while preserving the essential contextual modeling capabilities of traditional attention. Unlike existing efficient attention variants that approximate the full attention matrix, CPA fundamentally reimagines attention as a global-context-driven priority system. Our approach first computes a Global Context Vector (GCV) to capture the overall sequence information, then uses this context to assign priority scores to individual tokens, enabling sparse attention allocation. In encoder-decoder architectures, we apply CPA to the encoder while maintaining standard attention in the decoder, achieving substantial efficiency improvements while preserving full model expressivity for sequence-to-sequence tasks. Through theoretical analysis and extensive experimentation across language understanding, translation, and long document tasks, we demonstrate that CPA achieves comparable performance to standard self-attention while offering significant efficiency gains. On long-sequence benchmarks (8K+ tokens), CPA outperforms traditional Transformers and state-of-the-art efficient attention variants while using substantially fewer computational resources. Our findings suggest that pairwise token interactions may be unnecessary for effective contextual modeling, opening new avenues for efficient large-scale language models.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Transformer-based models1 have revolutionized natural language processing and many other domains of artificial intelligence. Their success stems largely from the self-attention mechanism, which enables each element in a sequence to attend to all other elements, capturing complex dependencies regardless of positional distance. However, this attention mechanism scales quadratically with sequence length, leading to significant computational and memory constraints when processing long inputs.

This quadratic scaling poses a fundamental challenge for applications requiring long context, such as document-level machine translation, long-form question answering, and whole-document analysis. While various approaches have been proposed to address this bottleneck2–5, most attempt to approximate standard self-attention through techniques such as low-rank approximations, localized attention patterns, or kernel methods.

In this paper, we introduce Contextual Priority Attention (CPA), which takes a fundamentally different approach. Rather than approximating the pairwise attention matrix, CPA reimagines attention as a global-to-local relevance filtering process inspired by cognitive theories of human attention6,7. Our approach first computes a Global Context Vector (GCV) that captures the “big picture” of the input sequence. Then, using this global context, we compute a priority score for each input element, measuring its relevance to the current context. Finally, we apply a sparse transformation to these scores, allowing the model to focus exclusively on the most relevant elements (see Fig. 1).

Related work

Efficient attention mechanisms

The quadratic complexity of self-attention has inspired numerous approaches to improve efficiency. These can be broadly categorized into several strategies:

Pattern-based sparsity: Methods like Sparse Transformer2 and Longformer8 limit attention to predefined patterns, such as local windows, dilated windows, or fixed global tokens. BlockBERT9 and BigBird10 use block-sparse attention patterns. While effective, these approaches incorporate structural biases that may not align with the actual dependencies in the data.

Learnable sparsity: Methods such as Reformer3 use locality-sensitive hashing to dynamically identify similar tokens that should attend to each other. Routing Transformer11 employs content-based clustering. These approaches reduce complexity but still fundamentally rely on pairwise token interactions.

Low-rank approximations: Linformer4 projects the key and value matrices to lower dimensions, achieving linear complexity. However, this approach struggles with variable-length inputs and loses the flexibility of the original attention mechanism.

Kernel methods: Performers5 and Linear Transformers12 rewrite the softmax attention using kernel methods, approximating the attention matrix implicitly. While mathematically elegant, these methods may sacrifice some of the expressivity of the original attention mechanism.

Recurrence-based methods: Models like Transformer-XL13 and Compressive Transformer14 incorporate recurrence to handle long sequences. These approaches maintain the standard attention within segments but use recurrent connections between segments.

Cognitive models of attention

Our approach is loosely inspired by cognitive theories of human attention, which suggest that perception involves both bottom-up feature processing and top-down attentional control guided by current goals and context15,16. Particularly relevant is Broadbent’s filter theory7, which proposes that cognitive processing employs selective filters to focus only on relevant stimuli.

In neuroscience, the interaction between global brain states and local neural activity parallels our approach of using global context to guide local attention allocation17. Several computational models of attention in computer vision have implemented related concepts, particularly through top-down modulation of bottom-up saliency18,19.

Sparse neural networks

Our work also relates to research on sparse neural networks, particularly approaches that dynamically determine sparsity patterns. Techniques such as Sparse Mixture of Experts20,21 and conditional computation22,23 activate only relevant parts of the network based on input. These approaches demonstrate that sparse activation can achieve strong performance while improving computational efficiency.

Contextual priority attention

In this section, we describe our proposed Contextual Priority Attention mechanism in detail, first presenting the intuition and then formalizing the approach.

Intuition

Traditional self-attention computes pairwise interactions between all tokens in a sequence, requiring comparisons for a sequence of length n. This exhaustive approach implicitly assumes that every token-to-token relationship is potentially informative and must be explicitly evaluated. However, this assumption may be unnecessarily conservative for many natural language tasks, where the semantic content of a sequence can often be summarized more compactly.

Human attention operates fundamentally differently. Cognitive research suggests that we do not exhaustively compare every perceived element to every other element. Instead, we rapidly construct a holistic understanding of our environment–a “gist” or mental model–and then selectively attend to elements that are most relevant to our current goals and context16,24. This two-stage process of global comprehension followed by selective focus allows humans to efficiently process complex scenes without cognitive overload.

CPA draws direct inspiration from this cognitive model. Rather than asking “how relevant is token A to token B?” for all possible pairs, we decompose attention into two sequential stages:

Global comprehension: First, we compute a Global Context Vector (GCV) that captures a compressed, holistic representation of the entire input sequence. This vector serves as a summary of “what is this sequence about?”

Selective prioritization: Then, for each token, we evaluate a single question: “how relevant is this token to the global context?” This yields a priority score indicating contextual importance.

This reformulation reduces the computational burden from pairwise comparisons to n context-relative evaluations, while preserving the essential selective nature of attention. Figure 2 illustrates this fundamental conceptual difference between standard self-attention and CPA.

Conceptual comparison between standard self-attention (left) and Contextual Priority Attention (right). To illustrate with a concrete example, consider processing the sentence: “The cat, which had been sleeping peacefully on the warm windowsill all afternoon, suddenly jumped down.” Standard self-attention would compute interactions, including many between tokens with minimal semantic relationship (e.g., “peacefully” and “down”). In contrast, CPA first forms a global understanding that this sentence describes a cat’s action, then prioritizes tokens most relevant to this context–likely “cat,” “sleeping,” and “jumped”–while assigning lower priority to modifiers and function words.

This approach offers an additional benefit beyond computational efficiency: it provides an implicit regularization effect. By forcing the model to route all attention decisions through a shared global context, CPA encourages the learning of representations that capture semantically meaningful aspects of the input rather than spurious local correlations. The global context acts as an information bottleneck25, filtering out noise while retaining task-relevant signal.

By computing relevance scores against a shared global context, we can prioritize tokens holistically, focusing computational resources on the most contextually important elements while naturally filtering out irrelevant ones. This design philosophy ”global understanding before local selection” mirrors not only human cognition but also efficient algorithms in other domains, such as hierarchical search and coarse-to-fine processing in computer vision26.

Our implementation is based on PyTorch42 and the HuggingFace Transformers library43. The sparse transformations (sparsemax and top-k Gumbel-Softmax) are implemented using custom CUDA kernels for efficiency. We employ mixed-precision training using NVIDIA Apex for all experiments.

The OOM results for standard Transformer occur during training when maintaining consistent batch sizes across all sequence lengths for fair comparison. With standard data parallelism, each GPU maintains a complete copy of the model, optimizer states, and gradients; thus, the 840GB = 320GB total memory cannot be pooled, and each GPU independently faces the 40GB memory constraint. The quadratic memory footprint includes: (1) the attention matrices (approximately 32GB per layer for batch size of 16 at 8192 tokens with mixed precision), (2) activation storage for backpropagation, (3) optimizer states (Adam requires 2 parameter memory), and (4) gradient accumulation. While it is possible to train longer sequences with reduced batch size or gradient checkpointing, we maintain consistent training configurations across all models for fair efficiency comparison.

CPA demonstrates excellent scalability, with memory usage and computation time scaling linearly with sequence length. For short sequences (512 tokens), the efficiency gains are moderate compared to standard Transformers. However, as sequence length increases, the benefits become increasingly pronounced.

At 8,192 tokens, standard Transformers run out of memory (OOM) on our hardware, while CPA continues to function efficiently. At 32,768 tokens, CPA maintains the lowest memory usage and highest inference speed among all compared models who went Out-Of-Memory.

Discussion

Key findings

Our experimental results demonstrate that Contextual Priority Attention achieves performance competitive with standard self-attention while significantly reducing computational costs. This supports our hypothesis that explicit pairwise token interactions may be unnecessary for effective contextual modeling.

The global-to-local attention paradigm of CPA offers several advantages:

Efficiency: Linear scaling with sequence length enables processing of much longer sequences than standard Transformers can handle. Our results show CPA can efficiently process sequences up to 32K tokens, where standard Transformers run out of memory.

This limitation becomes more pronounced as sequence length increases beyond the sizes tested in our experiments.

Conclusion

We presented Contextual Priority Attention (CPA), a novel attention mechanism that achieves theoretical complexity while empirically demonstrating linear scaling and maintaining competitive performance with standard Transformers. By reimagining attention as a global-context-driven priority system rather than exhaustive pairwise comparisons, CPA offers significant efficiency improvements, particularly for long sequences.

Our experimental results across diverse tasks demonstrate that CPA achieves performance comparable to full attention while scaling more efficiently to long inputs. The emergent attention patterns reveal that CPA learns meaningful prioritization strategies that adapt to input complexity. On the Long Range Arena benchmark specifically designed to test long-range modeling capabilities, CPA outperforms other efficient attention variants by a significant margin.

The success of CPA suggests that the quadratic computational complexity of standard self-attention may be unnecessary for effective contextual modeling. By focusing computational resources on the most contextually relevant tokens, CPA provides an efficient alternative that scales better to the increasingly long contexts required by modern NLP applications.

The ability to process sequences of tens of thousands of tokens efficiently opens up new possibilities for applications such as document-level translation, long-form question answering, and full-document summarization. As models continue to scale and applications demand increasingly longer context windows, approaches like CPA that break the quadratic scaling barrier without significant performance degradation will become increasingly important.

We believe this work represents an important step toward more efficient transformer architectures and hope that the global-context approach inspires further innovations in attention mechanisms. Future work will explore hierarchical extensions of CPA, dynamic sparsity approaches, and applications to multimodal tasks, further extending the capabilities of this promising approach.
