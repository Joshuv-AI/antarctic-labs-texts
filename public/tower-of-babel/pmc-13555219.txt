Postoperative Surveillance in Differentiated Thyroid Cancer: Response-Adapted De-Escalation in the 2025 American Thyroid Association and 2026 Korean Thyroid Association Guidelines
Authors: Sun Wook Cho
Published: 2026

Postoperative surveillance for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) is shifting from routine, uniformly intensive follow-up toward risk-adapted, response-based care. Because most patients with DTC have an excellent prognosis, surveillance should not rely on indefinite testing to maximize recurrence detection. Rather, it should prioritize detection of clinically meaningful recurrence while reducing unnecessary biochemical testing, imaging, and prolonged thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) suppression [1,2].

Although initial clinicopathological risk stratification remains essential, recurrence risk should be recalibrated over time according to surgical extent, radioactive iodine (RAI) use, serum thyroglobulin (Tg) level, anti-thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) status, imaging findings, and treatment response [3,4]. The 2026 Korean Thyroid Association (KTA) and 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines share this overall direction [1,2], but they differ in several clinically important respects: the timing of early postoperative assessment, Tg interpretation after treatment without RAI or after lobectomy, and the extent of long-term surveillance de-escalation (Table 1).

The 6–12-week postoperative checkpoint

The early postoperative period provides the first opportunity to reassess disease status after surgery. In this context, the 6–12-week assessment should be viewed not as a routine laboratory visit but as the first major decision point in postoperative surveillance. Serum Tg, TgAb, and TSH measured during this period establish a biochemical baseline that helps guide subsequent surveillance intensity, the need for additional imaging, and the appropriate TSH target [1,5].

The interpretation of Tg at this stage depends strongly on the clinical context. After total thyroidectomy with RAI, a very low Tg level in the absence of TgAb and structural disease supports classification as an excellent response. After total thyroidectomy without RAI, Tg interpretation is more nuanced because residual thyroid tissue may contribute to measurable Tg [5,6]. After lobectomy, normal residual thyroid tissue makes absolute Tg thresholds particularly difficult to interpret [1,2]. Therefore, Tg should not be interpreted in isolation; it should be evaluated together with surgical extent, RAI status, TSH level, TgAb status, and imaging findings.

This early checkpoint also helps prevent both under- and over-surveillance. Patients with clearly favorable early biochemical and structural findings may be candidates for less intensive follow-up, whereas those with elevated or rising Tg, increasing TgAb, suspicious imaging findings, or high-risk pathological features require closer evaluation. In this respect, early postoperative assessment links initial pathology-based risk stratification with ongoing response-based risk stratification [3,4].

Ongoing risk stratification as the central framework

Ongoing risk stratification (ORS), also referred to as dynamic risk stratification, is now central to postoperative surveillance in DTC [3,4]. The underlying concept is straightforward but clinically important: initial risk stratification provides the starting point, whereas treatment response determines how surveillance should change over time.

Patients are generally classified into four response categories: excellent response, indeterminate response, biochemical incomplete response, and structural incomplete response [1,2]. These categories integrate clinical, biochemical, and imaging data. An excellent response indicates no clinical, biochemical, or structural evidence of disease. An indeterminate response reflects nonspecific biochemical or imaging findings. A biochemical incomplete response indicates abnormal Tg or rising TgAb without identifiable structural disease. A structural incomplete response indicates persistent or newly identified local or distant disease, regardless of Tg or TgAb status.

The practical implication is that surveillance should be adjusted according to response category. A low- or intermediate-risk patient with a sustained excellent response does not require the same intensity of follow-up as a patient with biochemical or structural evidence of persistent disease [3,4]. Conversely, a high-risk patient with an excellent response may still warrant more cautious follow-up than a low-risk patient in the same response category. Thus, ORS does not replace initial risk stratification; rather, it refines it.

Tg, TSH, and ultrasonography: complementary tools, not interchangeable tests

One important implication of response-adapted surveillance is that Tg, TSH, and neck ultrasonography should not be treated as redundant tests. Each provides distinct information.

Serum Tg and TgAb primarily provide biochemical information. Tg is most informative after total thyroidectomy and RAI, but its interpretation becomes progressively more complex when RAI is not performed or when residual thyroid tissue remains after lobectomy [5,6]. TgAb should be assessed concurrently because it can interfere with Tg interpretation and because rising TgAb levels may serve as a biochemical indicator of persistent or recurrent disease. Importantly, Tg trends may be more informative than isolated values, particularly in patients without structural abnormalities [1,2].

TSH has a dual role: it influences Tg interpretation and serves as a therapeutic target. Historically, TSH suppression was often applied according to initial recurrence risk. However, contemporary care is moving away from uniform long-term suppression. In patients without biochemical or structural evidence of disease, particularly those with low- or intermediate-risk disease, prolonged TSH suppression may provide limited benefit while increasing the risk of atrial fibrillation, bone loss, and other adverse outcomes [7,8]. Therefore, the TSH target should be adjusted according to ORS, recurrence risk, age, comorbidities, and patient preference [1,2].

Neck ultrasonography remains the key structural surveillance tool for detecting cervical recurrence. However, repeated routine ultrasound in all patients may lead to overdiagnosis, unnecessary biopsy, patient anxiety, and detection of clinically insignificant findings [1,2]. In patients with a sustained excellent response, particularly those with low-risk disease, ultrasound intervals can be lengthened and imaging can be performed more selectively. In contrast, suspicious physical findings, rising Tg levels, increasing TgAb levels, or high-risk clinicopathological features should prompt more active structural evaluation.

De-escalation without abandonment

Surveillance de-escalation should not be misconstrued as neglect. Rather, it is a rational response to repeated evidence of favorable treatment response. The purpose is to reduce low-yield surveillance while preserving the ability to detect clinically meaningful recurrence.

A practical surveillance model can be conceptualized in phases. First, the 6–12-week postoperative checkpoint establishes the biochemical and structural baseline. Second, during the first 1–2 years, ORS classifies the patient’s response to initial therapy and determines whether follow-up should be intensified, maintained, or de-escalated. Third, in patients with a sustained excellent response, especially those with low-risk disease, the frequency of Tg/TgAb testing, TSH monitoring, and neck ultrasound can be progressively reduced [1,2]. Fourth, surveillance can be re-escalated if Tg rises, TgAb increases, suspicious imaging findings appear, or new symptoms or physical findings develop.

This approach is particularly important in the Republic of Korea, where thyroid cancer is common and many patients are diagnosed at an early stage [9]. In this setting, excessive surveillance can consume medical resources and increase patient burden without improving clinically meaningful outcomes. A more selective strategy may improve survivorship care by aligning surveillance intensity with actual disease risk.

At the same time, de-escalation should be individualized. Patients with aggressive histology, extensive nodal disease, extranodal extension, distant metastasis, incomplete resection, or other high-risk features require more cautious follow-up, even when the early response appears favorable [1,2]. Similarly, a biochemical incomplete response or structural incomplete response should prompt more active evaluation rather than routine observation alone. The goal is not to reduce surveillance for all patients, but to match surveillance intensity to evolving risk.

In conclusion, postoperative surveillance in DTC is increasingly guided by response-adapted risk assessment. The central issue is how surveillance should be individualized according to surgical extent, RAI use, Tg level, TgAb status, TSH target, ultrasound findings, and longitudinal response to therapy.

The 2026 KTA and 2025 ATA guidelines converge on the principle of response-adapted care [1,2], but they differ in clinically relevant ways. The KTA guideline provides a clearer early postoperative checkpoint, applies a cautious approach to Tg interpretation after treatment without RAI or after lobectomy, and favors gradual de-escalation rather than broad discontinuation of long-term biochemical monitoring. These distinctions are important because they translate response-adapted surveillance into a practical strategy suited to routine clinical practice.

The goal of postoperative surveillance is not indefinite testing to maximize detection, but response-adapted de-escalation of biochemical testing, imaging, and TSH suppression while preserving detection of clinically meaningful recurrence [1-4].
