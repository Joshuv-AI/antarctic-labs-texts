Respiratory health outcomes of children with Down Syndrome following dysphagia management: a service evaluation
Authors: Himali de Silva, Neekita Shah, Narad Mathura, Christina Smith
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Children living with Down syndrome are at a high risk of eating and drinking difficulties (dysphagia) and are more susceptible to respiratory infections. In an urban National Health Service community Trust, a retrospective service evaluation was carried out for referrals received during 2012–2017. The study examined the respiratory health outcomes 3 years after dysphagia intervention. Children whose dysphagia was identified early (<12 months) using cervical auscultation with subsequent intervention did not develop lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) post intervention. Children whose dysphagia management was commenced after 12 months of age and those who had no intervention developed LRTI.

Children living with Down syndrome (clwDS) have >50% risk of dysphagia (difficulties with eating, drinking and swallowing).1 2 Over half of the clwDS with dysphagia who have instrumental assessments, aspirate3 which is often silent (90.2%) (no overt signs, eg, coughing or wheezing).2 Respiratory illness is prevalent and the primary cause of hospitalisation (42% of hospital admission) and mortality for clwDS and can result from aspiration.4 5 Speech and language therapists use cervical auscultation (CA) to detect changes to respiratory sounds during swallowing.6 These subtle audible wet pharyngeal sounds (SAWPS) can predict oropharyngeal dysfunction and aspiration.1

A service evaluation of dysphagia management of clwDS was carried out by interrogating longitudinal clinical records collected at an National Health Service community dysphagia service in London between January 2012 and December 2017. Three years of respiratory health history was evaluated for each of the following groups. Children were grouped based on the timing of dysphagia identification and intervention;

Early: clwDS whose swallow function was screened between 4 and 8 months using CA to identify SAWPS, followed by a Video Fluorosocpy Swallow Study (VFSS) by 12 months old to confirm aspiration, with earlier dysphagia management.

Early: clwDS whose swallow function was screened between 4 and 8 months using CA to identify SAWPS, followed by a Video Fluorosocpy Swallow Study (VFSS) by 12 months old to confirm aspiration, with earlier dysphagia management.

Late: clwDS had a VFSS after 12 months with later dysphagia management.

Late: clwDS had a VFSS after 12 months with later dysphagia management.

No-intervention: Children who did not have a VFSS, either as SAWPS was negative or SAWPS was positive but it was deemed not indicative of risk.

No-intervention: Children who did not have a VFSS, either as SAWPS was negative or SAWPS was positive but it was deemed not indicative of risk.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Children who were screened for dysphagia using CA and received early intervention did not show any respiratory illnesses related to LRTIs in the following 3 years. From the Late group where CA was carried out after 12 months, three children contracted at least one episode of LRTI including pneumonia. Five of the remaining seven children developed an episode of upper respiratory tract infection. From the No-intervention group, 12 of the 15 children who showed negative SAWPS showed no respiratory complications whereas the clwDS who had positive SAWPS developed at least one respiratory tract infection.

Using routine clinical data, we interrogated respiratory outcomes of clwDS in relation to the timing of screening for dysphagia using CA and subsequent management. We identified a positive trend between early dysphagia intervention and respiratory illness with clwDS receiving dysphagia management before 12 months of age not developing respiratory illnesses whereas those who had late management developed respiratory illnesses. Most of the children who did not present with SAWPS on CA and were not therefore referred for VFSS did not develop LRTIs. In contrast, clwDS who had SAWPS on CA at 4–8 months was indicative of swallowing difficulties causing silent aspiration (identified using VFSS). This suggests that using CA and VFSS in combination to identify aspiration can reduce respiratory illness. Our study suggests that dysphagia is a modifiable risk factor for respiratory illness.

The study’s primary limitation was the small sample size causing disparities in the intervention group sizes. This service evaluation provides preliminary support for the early utilisation of CA (at 4–8 months old) to screen for SAWPS. Those with positive SAWPS can have a detailed assessment using VFSS for identification and dysphagia management.
