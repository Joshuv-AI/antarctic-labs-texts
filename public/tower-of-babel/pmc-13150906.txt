Optimising the utility of patient-reported outcome measurements during recovery after orthopaedic extremity fractures: protocol for a multicentre prospective cohort study
Authors: AOBERT Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRecovery after extremity fracture is influenced by injury-related impairments, functional capacity, symptom severity and psychosocial well-being, emphasising the need for patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs). Despite increasing adoption of Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) and legacy PROMs in orthopaedic research, substantial knowledge gaps remain regarding their measurement properties, comparability and clinical utility, particularly in fracture populations. Our aim is to develop meaningful guidance to clinicians regarding the use of PROMs in treating orthopaedic fracture patients.MethodsA prospective cohort of 1500 patients across 14 centres presenting with isolated extremity fractures being treated operatively or non-operatively will be recruited prospectively. We will aim to recruit 300 patients for each of five fracture types: isolated hip, tibial shaft, ankle/pilon, proximal humerus and distal radius fracture. All procedures and management will be performed according to the site’s standard of care and treatment protocol. For patients treated non-operatively, the index visit (first study assessment) will be performed at the first orthopaedic treatment encounter (eg, emergency department visit or outpatient fracture clinic). Surgically treated patients will have PROMs collected anytime from the day of the surgery up to discharge from hospital for the index visit. Follow-up visits will be performed at 2–3 weeks, 6–8 weeks, 10–13 weeks, 6 months and 1 year after the index visit. At baseline, we will document injury data, demographic and sociodemographic data, and radiographic classification using the AO classification system. Patient-perceived functioning will be assessed with PROMIS Physical Function (PF), PROMIS Upper Extremity, PROMIS Global Health, Knee and Injury Osteoarthritis Outcome Score-12, Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score-12, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure and Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand. The following domains and PROMs will also be captured: symptoms of anxiety and depression (PROMIS Anxiety; PROMIS Depression), patient activation (Patient Activation Measures (PAM)-10) and a patient’s ability to fulfil social roles (PROMIS Social Roles).The range and normative limits of the PROMs collected will be defined using standard descriptive statistics. We will crosswalk or validate PROMIS measures with legacy instruments for PF using an Item Response Theory (IRT)-based linking model and compare it to non-IRT models (such as equipercentile linking). Lastly, we will assess the PROM-based recovery trajectory after fracture, overall and after adjusting for relevant demographic, clinical or biopsychosocial factors.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval for this study was granted from the local Ethics Committees or Institutional Review Board at each of the participating sites prior to patient enrolment. Introduction

A patient’s recovery following injury, including extremity fractures, is influenced by external determinants and, importantly, by multiple factors that are unique to the individual. Thus, health systems and clinicians are increasingly recognising the importance of a comprehensive person-centred approach to orthopaedic patient care that incorporates their specific level of capability (valued life activities), symptom intensity and aspects of mental health and social health, in addition to the level of impairment related to their injury. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) play a pivotal role in such an approach. The Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) is a suite of PROMs designed to help standardise the collection of subjective health information with greater precision and efficiency.1 While there has been substantial musculoskeletal research conducted using these tools,2 3 significant knowledge gaps remain in key areas, limiting their optimal application.

First, uncertainty remains regarding the psychometric properties of these PROMs in quantifying health status as well as their suitability for evaluating recovery following orthopaedic fracture. Thus, there is a critical need for guidance on measurement properties to help decide what PROMs to use and when to use them. Limitations in knowledge regarding PROMs in musculoskeletal research are not limited to PROMIS. In a systematic review of hip fractures in elderly patients, Haywood et al identified 28 PROMs used in 71 studies, but they failed to recommend which PROMs to use due to the paucity of information regarding important aspects such as evidence of relevance of the PROM for hip fracture patients, test–retest reliability, measurement error, structural validity and score interpretation.4 Another systematic review assessing the use of PROMs to quantify levels of function and other health domains after upper extremity trauma reported the use of 144 upper extremity PROMs, signalling some uncertainty around which measures to select, especially considering the high levels of heterogeneity among cohorts within these studies and the relative lack of studies involving longitudinal evaluations using these tools in extremity fracture populations.5 This presents challenges in comparing and acting on outcomes across clinical settings and research studies.6,8

Second, there is a lack of evidence and guidance on how to compare and link the newer PROMIS measures to legacy PROMs (usually fixed scale surveys developed using classical test theory) commonly used in orthopaedics to date. Without this, it not only becomes difficult to transition to newer and improved measures but also limits the ability to use datasets generated with these legacy measures. Although the psychometric superiority of PROMIS measures has been supported and a growing number of orthopaedic studies have incorporated them,9,12 there is still hesitancy among clinicians to use PROMIS measures due to the large quantity of existing data collected using legacy PROMs. Hence, substantial work is required to establish the crosswalk scores and clinical thresholds between PROMIS and legacy measures in the management of orthopaedic extremity fracture.13

Finally, more information is required around PROMs and their clinical utility for medical decision making at the point of care following orthopaedic injury. For instance, there is a paucity of data on the modifiable factors associated with long-term capability over the course of an individual’s recovery trajectory following extremity fracture. In addition, while there is growing evidence examining patient subgroups within orthopaedic populations experiencing common musculoskeletal conditions, there is a lack of data on patient phenotypes based on subjective health status gathered using PROMs (ie, via measures of capability, symptom intensity, mental and social health) and recovery trajectory. These recovery data can help inform the design of future orthopaedic trauma care pathways including a comprehensive range of treatment strategies.

Based on these knowledge gaps, we designed a prospective cohort study involving longitudinal capture of PROMs among patients presenting with extremity fracture across multiple centres internationally, organised into two projects: ‘Project 1. Linkage of PROMIS Measures to Legacy Measures’ and ‘Project 2. Recovery Trajectory after Fracture using PROMIS’.

Objectives

The first objective (Project 1) is to expand the validity evidence of crosswalk tables that link scores between legacy measures and the newer PROMIS measures. This includes PROMIS Physical Function (PF) linked with physical function domains from the Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS), Knee and Injury Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) and Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM), and similarly the PROMIS Upper Extremity function (UE) linked with the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH).14,23 Similarly, it includes PROMIS Pain Interference and legacy pain domains from the HOOS and KOOS. For crosswalk tables that already exist from prior linking analysis, we will perform crosswalk validation analyses using our cohort data.24 This work will define the range and normative limits concerning PROMIS PF, UE and Pain Interference and perform psychometric evaluation of reliability, validity and responsiveness for each measure in a cohort of fracture patients.

The second objective (Project 2) is to identify factors at baseline (early after injury) associated with medium-term and longer-term (6 and 12 months, respectively) physical function and pain interference PROMs after extremity fractures, accounting for sociodemographic, clinical and psychosocial factors. We will also identify factors at baseline (early after injury) associated with medium-term and longer-term level of symptom intensity (Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) Pain), and general health (PROMIS Global) after these injuries. We will further assess distinct recovery trajectories based on level of physical function, pain interference, pain intensity and global health.

Our overarching goal and the impetus for these studies within the AO (Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen) Trauma Clinical Priority Programme in Fracture Outcome Measurements is to provide clinicians with meaningful guidance on the use of patient-reported outcomes and to promote the development of an accepted PROM framework that can be systemically integrated across health systems in orthopaedic trauma and fracture care.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This is a prospective multicentre cohort study of patients presenting with isolated extremity fractures at 14 participating clinics in six countries.

Participants

We will collect 300 patients per injury group (ie, hip fracture, tibial shaft fracture, ankle/pilon fracture, proximal humerus fracture and distal radius fracture), totalling approximately 1500 patients.

Eligible patients will need to meet the following inclusion criteria: being 18 years and older, having a diagnosis of one isolated injury (ie, hip fracture, tibial shaft fracture (with and without associated fibular fracture), ankle/pilon fracture, proximal humerus fracture or distal radius fracture). Further, patients must be English, German, Spanish or Dutch speaking, and able to provide informed consent (ie, have the ability to understand the content of the patient informed consent form (ICF)). They must have the willingness and ability to participate in the clinical investigation.

Exclusion criteria are when participant contact for enrolment falls beyond 21 days from day of injury, those having multiple fractures, pathological fractures due to cancer, a recent history of substance abuse (ie, recreational drugs, alcohol) that would preclude reliable assessment and those being pregnant or planning to become pregnant within the study period, prisoners or participating in any other medical device or medicinal product study within the previous month that could influence the results of the present study, or a patient unable to likely achieve anticipated follow-up of 1 year.

Recruitment

Study recruitment started in 2021 with First Patient First Visit in April 2021, with Last Patient Last Visit due in June 2026 and data analysis planned for 2027. Eligible patients will be identified at each participating site by an investigator or a study coordinator who will approach and assess the eligibility of each potential study participant. If the patient wishes to participate, a member of the research team will go through the informed consent process, explaining the purpose of the study, procedures, risks, benefits, alternatives to participation and aspects of data sharing and protection. Participants will be requested to complete a written ICF, designated a unique identifier number and enrolled onto the data capture platform (Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) Cloud).25 26 A copy of the signed ICF will be placed into the patient’s medical record, and the investigator site file and one copy will be provided to the patient.

Index assessment

All patients who are prescreened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be entered onto a patient prescreen and enrolment log which will be maintained at each study site. Details about the fracture pattern, injury mechanism and treatment will be documented. Participants will be requested to provide sociodemographic data (age, sex, education level, marital status, employment status, social status and preinjury living situation), medical comorbidities (specified by the Charlson Comorbidity Index), any diagnoses of depression or anxiety, or chronic pain disorder, use of preinjury walking aids and current prescription and use of pain and/or antidepressant medications at their baseline assessment. For upper extremity injuries, dominant handedness will also be documented. Clinical data including fracture classification, open versus closed fracture status, associated dislocation/tendon injury, operative versus non-operative management and type of surgery if applicable will be collected through chart review.

Interventions and follow-up

All patients will be treated per usual standard of care and pathways of non-operative or operative management at each site. For patients undergoing operative management, there will be no standardised surgical treatment specifications and thus the choice of surgical approach, instrumentation and adjuncts such as bone graft or bone graft substitutes will be determined by the lead surgeon’s preference and local institutional protocol.

For surgically managed patients, the index assessment will be performed between the day of the surgery and hospital discharge. For patients managed non-operatively, the index assessment will be conducted at the first treatment encounter (ie, in hospital if admitted or first orthopaedic clinic visit). The subsequent study assessments will be performed during follow-up visits (or contacted via email or telephone calls) at 2–3 weeks (second visit), 6–8 weeks (third visit), 10–13 weeks (fourth visit), 6 months (fifth visit) and 1 year (sixth visit) after the index visit. Participants who miss multiple follow-up assessments will be evaluated by the study team at the end of the study during data review meetings for withdrawal from the study due to lack of follow-up. Unless the patient’s participation is prematurely terminated, we will aim for all enrolled patients to be followed up within the study.

Due to the nature and design of the study, there are no a priori defined termination criteria. The study may be prematurely terminated if: (1) poor quality of data collected prevents the study from fulfilling its purpose; (2) the study is no longer relevant; or (3) loss of funding, staff or any other support required to carry out the study. If the study were to be prematurely terminated, all patients enrolled would be followed through their last study visit as defined in the clinical investigation plan. The PROMIS measures are designed for patient self-completion and have demonstrated validity in orthopaedic patient populations.2 3 20 27 28 The PROMIS PF is a generic measure that applies across all ability levels and conditions. It includes content ranging from self-care to strenuous activities.15 The PROMIS UE was created out of the PF item bank to assess arm-specific and hand-specific limitations (eg, difficulty writing, lifting heavy objects) in patients with known or suspected upper extremity injury/disease.11 The PROMIS V.1.2 Global Health Scale includes 10 questions that assess overall physical and mental health. PROMIS Pain Interference assesses the impact of pain on common activities of daily life including social, cognitive, emotional, physical and recreational aspects.29 30 PROMIS Depression assesses negative mood (sadness, guilt), views of self (worthlessness, self-criticism) and diminished positive affect and engagement (loss of interest) within the previous 7 days.31 PROMIS Anxiety assesses aspects of fear (eg, fearfulness, feelings of panic), anxious misery (eg, worry, dread), hyperarousal (eg, tension, nervousness, restlessness) and some somatic symptoms related to arousal (eg, dizziness).31 PROMIS Ability to Participate in Social Roles and Activities assesses the perceived ability to perform one’s usual social roles and activities. All PROMIS measures generate a T-score with a general population mean of 50 and a SD of 10. Higher scores indicate more of the concept being measured. In this study, guided by methodology experts for PROMIS, custom short forms (PROMIS PF SF Custom AO14 and PROMIS UE SF Custom AO8) were developed to contain only clinically relevant items (relevant to orthopaedic trauma recovery) with a focus on mobility, physical function and upper extremity capacity.

The HOOS and the KOOS are both measures developed as extensions of the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index to assess short- and long-term symptoms and function in subjects with knee/hip injuries and osteoarthritis.32 33 They are intended to be completed by self-report. The full HOOS includes 40 questions in five areas (pain, symptoms and stiffness, ADL, function in sports and recreational activities, and quality of life) and the score ranges from 0 to 100 points (100 is best score possible).32 The full KOOS includes 42 questions in five areas (pain, symptoms, function in daily activities, function in sport and recreational activities, and quality of life) with scores ranging from 0 (extreme knee problems) to 100 (no knee problems).33 For this study, we will use the 12-item short forms of each measure.17 The FAAM is a measure of physical function for the lower leg, ankle and foot and has been shown to be a reliable and responsive tool in a broad range of orthopaedic patients.34 In the current study, only the 21 items related to ADL will be used, with each item score using a 5-point Likert scale where 0 means unable to do and 4 representing no difficulties at all. The score range of the ADL scale of the FAAM is 0 to 84. The DASH and QuickDASH were developed to assess outcomes in UE disorders and have been validated and widely used in the orthopaedic patient population.18 35 The QuickDASH will be used in this study; it is an 11-item PROM, each response is presented as a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores range from 0 (no disability) to 100 (most severe disability).

The Pain Self-efficacy Questionnaire-2 is a self-report measure that assesses adaptive coping strategies and the confidence that one can achieve one’s goals in spite of pain. Items are scored on a 7-point Likert scale and summed to form a total score ranging 0–12, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy.36

The PAM−10 assesses activation and the personal and psychological competencies (eg, knowledge, skills, confidence) required to manage one’s health and health services.37 The items can be categorised into four categories: (1) baseline roles and responsibilities of individuals with their health; (2) confidence with health and healthcare services; (3) the ability to maintain health/prevent illness; and (4) the propensity of adaptive health behaviours and levels of resilience. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating higher patient activation.

The NRS-Pain is an ordinal pain intensity scale ranging from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating extreme pain.

The RUSH and RUST scales are used to define radiographic healing. The RUSH quantifies four measures of healing with scores from 1 to 338: cortical bridging (1=no bridging and 3=complete bridging), fracture line visibility (1=fracture line fully visible and 3=no visibility of the fracture line), trabecular consolidation (1=no consolidation and 3=complete consolidation) and trabecular fracture disappearance (1=fully visible fracture line and 3=no evidence of fracture line). The total score ranges from 10 to 30. The RUST was designed to assess healing progress in tibial fractures by measuring cortical bridging via callus formation and the lack of fracture line.39 The total score ranges from 4 to 12, with four indicating a fracture without healing and 12 corresponding to a fracture that is considered as healed.

AEs including but not limited to infection, non-union/malunion, failure of fixation, hardware irritation and neurovascular injury will be recorded. In particular, we will collect data on iatrogenic fracture, vessel damage and nerve damage as well as death and intraoperative resuscitation as intraoperative AEs. As postoperative AEs, we will collect fracture-related infection as defined in,40 wound dehiscence, non-union, refracture/peri-implant fracture, compartment syndrome, secondary loss of reduction, secondary displacement of parts of the fixation material, breakage/subsidence/migration of component, dislocation of components, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, avascular necrosis, any other complication leading to a reoperation, ongoing opioid medication 1 year after treatment and perioperative death during hospital admission.

Statistical analyses

An initial sample size estimation of 700 patients per injury group was estimated based on needs for the Linking Analysis substudy to crosswalk the scores of the HOOS-12, KOOS-12 and FAAM measures to PROMIS measures, in line with previous studies.41,43 However, as crosswalks between the HOOS, KOOS and DASH have since been published44 since the origination of this study, the focus on cross-validation analyses of the existing links would require fewer score observations, allowing for a decreased sample size of 300 patients per injury group.

For legacy measures with existing crosswalks to PROMIS, such as the QuickDASH, we will conduct a crosswalk validation analysis. To evaluate the accuracy of existing links, we will compare the actual PROMIS scores to those estimated by linking and use several statistical criteria, following similar recent studies.24 45 First, we calculate the bias (ie, the mean difference) between linked and actual scores. Second, we index-linked score variability using the SD of score differences and the root mean square difference between linked and actual scores. Third, we calculate the association between the scores using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for agreement. Reasonable recovery will be considered an ICC of 0.65 or above and a bias of 2 T-score points or less. We will also apply Bland-Altman plots to gauge the agreement of observed and predicted scores across the range of function.

For legacy measures without existing crosswalks, linking of PROMIS and legacy measures will be conducted by IRT-based analyses modelled after the PROsetta Stone project, as reported in previous methodological studies.46 Prior to linking, standard psychometric analyses will be conducted to ensure that the baseline statistical assumptions for linking are met.

Single group linking will be performed between the PROMIS PF and FAAM. This involves calibrating the legacy items onto the PROMIS metric either by fixing the PROMIS items to their previously calibrated values (fixed anchor calibration), or by recalibrating all items and then calculating linking coefficients based on the PROMIS items (linked cocalibration).13 Analyses will be conducted using a unidimensional graded response IRT model. In the event of unsatisfactory relationships emerging by the use of unidimensional IRT methods, alternative methods will be considered, including equipercentile linking,47 or multidimensional approaches such as calibrated projection.48

For Project 2, a power analysis for the latent basis model—with up to seven time points and testing one covariate at a time, the latent basis model would have 27 degrees of freedom. In order to have 80% power to ensure that the 95% CI around the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) excludes 0.08 when we have a hypothesised RMSEA less than 0.05 would require a sample size of 356 participants. With a risk of attrition at 25%, a sample size of approximately 460 would be suggested. In Project 2, recovery curves for PROMs measuring the key orthopaedic domains of physical functioning or pain (ie, PROMIS PF, PROMIS UE, PROMIS Pain Interference, HOOS, KOOS, QuickDASH and FAAM) will be considered. Sociodemographic and clinical factors, PROMIS Global Health, Depression and anxiety, pain self-efficacy and patient activation will be considered as explanatory variables that may affect the recovery trajectory. Their effect will be examined using conditional effects on latent basis models. Multicollinearity will be assessed through variable inflation factor, evaluation of impact on R2 and sensitivity analysis. Based on the nature of the final dataset, a latent growth curve model with a non-linear trend for time since index visit (ie, a latent basis model) with covariates predicting both the average functioning (latent intercept) and the rate of change (latent slope).

Depending on the quality of the data (eg, completeness of data in each subgroup), specific statistical methods to handle missing data such as mean and multiple imputation will be implemented.

Data collection and management

Data collection

Data handling and protection will be conducted according to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14 155 guidelines and The International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use-good clinical practice (GCP) and applicable regulations. An electronic case report form (eCRF) in REDCap Cloud26 will be designed to accommodate the specific features of the study and its access will be password protected. The eCRF is to be completed in a timely manner after a patient’s visit (ie, 14 days after occurrence of a documentable event). During the site initiation visit and prior to recruiting the first patient, the research team at each site will undergo a defined training programme that will include explanations on inclusion and exclusion criteria, study procedures, how to use the eCRF and general aspects of ISO 14155 and GCPs. Monitoring visits will be performed as frequently as required to guarantee the completeness and accuracy of the information in the eCRF. At the end of the study, a site close-out visit will be performed and all final clarifications completed. Source data and any other essential documents will be archived according to the legal requirements at the study site. Clinical study data (ie, eCRF) and essential documents will be archived by the sponsor according to legal requirements.

Ethical considerations and dissemination

This is an observational study in which no paediatric or mentally incompetent (temporarily or permanently) patients will be included. Discussion

PROMs have become important research and clinical tools in assessing levels of capability and symptom intensity from a patient’s perspective alongside several factors contributing to these important health domains across a variety of patient populations. The relatively newer PROMIS measures provide a precise, efficient and user-friendly way of capturing health status. Although originally developed for people with chronic conditions, they have since undergone extensive psychometric evaluation within a range of orthopaedic conditions and other acute conditions. Few studies, however, have evaluated these measures among large populations of extremity fracture patients in longitudinal investigations across different settings globally.

The overarching goal of this project is to evaluate PROMIS measures to better inform further research and lay the groundwork for integration of PROMs in standard orthopaedic care. We aim to achieve this by using a multimethod approach by first enabling the utility and validity of PROMIS measures in this clinical area using well-established PROM crosswalk and linking methodology, second, identifying which factors are most dominantly associated with levels of capability and symptom intensity, accounting for injury severity (AO fracture classification), levels of capability, mental and social health, and third, examining how these factors change over the recovery trajectory over time and relate to the general population. Few studies have focused on the use of these measures in large-scale fracture populations, fewer have been applied longitudinally and fewer still have been assessed within patients across different backgrounds and countries in this context.49

Systematic evaluation using PROMs, as soon as possible following injury, can uncover the factors that dominantly influence medium to longer term health-related outcomes following extremity fractures. The outputs of this work can drive care delivery opportunities at the individual patient level by advancing clinical decision support and guiding the clinical team to address modifiable risk factors using patient-centric treatment strategies, for example, engaging in additional mental health support alongside usual fracture management. Further, we anticipate our findings will also inform the design of whole person, value-based models of care delivery for populations impacted by these common conditions. To date, a variety of psychosocial variables (eg, depression, anxiety, kinesiophobia, pain catastrophising, self-efficacy, social support) have been shown to explain a substantial proportion of the variation associated with different levels of capability and symptom intensity. However, these findings have commonly involved studies in limited cohorts with non-trauma populations or both trauma and non-trauma patients combined in heterogeneous cohorts.

The relationship between the extent of change in numerical scores and their clinical significance is also lacking. There are several ways to develop interpretive guidance, some of which have been used to a limited extent in orthopaedic populations,50,52 while many have only been demonstrated in other clinical conditions.

We do not know the acceptable or expected range of PROM scores during fracture recovery. Although there are established norms for PROMIS measures in the general US population, this provides little insight into changes during the early stages of recovery where scores are likely to be substantially lower. Further, there is strong evidence that one ‘norm’ mean and SD fails to adequately capture expected score differences across the lifespan even in an uninjured population,53 and thus it is highly likely that adjusted reference values will be needed following an orthopaedic trauma. Defining this in fracture patients in the English, Spanish, Dutch and German-speaking populations could provide guidance on improving future outcomes by implementing changes during the early part of the recovery process.
