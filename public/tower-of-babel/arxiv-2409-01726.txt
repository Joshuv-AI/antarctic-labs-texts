Multi-view Optimal Transport for Multi-view Crowd Localization and Tracking
Kaiyi Zhang, Qi Zhang, Jixuan Chen, Antoni B. Chan, Hui Huang

Abstract
Multi-view crowd localization and tracking aim to predict the ground-plane positions of all individuals in the scene and estimate their trajectories over time. Existing multi-view localization methods typically rely on Gaussian-smoothed density map supervision, whose inherent ambiguity in crowded regions may blur local peaks and degrade localization accuracy. In this work, we introduce a multi-view optimal transport framework that directly supervises point-level predictions on the ground plane, thereby avoiding the limitations of density map regression. Specifically, we propose a Mahalanobis distance-based multi-view optimal transport loss (M-MVOT), whose transport cost incorporates view ray direction and object-to-camera distance within a Mahalanobis formulation, with the direction guiding the ellipse orientation and the distance modulating the cost magnitude. For multi-view tracking, we first extend the proposed localization framework in a tracking-by-detection manner (MVOT-TBD). To further improve performance, we introduce a tracking-specific optimal transport loss (MVOT-Track) that explicitly supervises inter-frame association. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed optimal transport-based framework consistently outperforms density map-based localization and tracking on multiple multi-view crowd datasets. Code is available at https://github.com/zqyq/MVOT-Track .

To mitigate the problems of Gaussian density map supervision in the single-image crowd localization task, point-supervision methods
Ma et al. (2021)
;
Wan et al. (2021)
based on optimal transport (OT) have been proposed and achieved significant gains in localization performance compared to Gaussian density map methods trained with MSE loss.
OT loss directly uses point annotations as supervision and generates more compact density maps
Wan et al. (2021)
.
However, point supervision for multi-view crowd localization methods has not been explored yet.

In this paper, we explore using point supervision for multi-view crowd localization and propose a novel Mahalanobis distance-based multi-view optimal transport (M-MVOT) loss. In the M-MVOT loss, the transport cost is defined using the Mahalanobis distance, which adjusts the cost based on the view ray direction between the ground-truth point and the camera, and the distance to the camera. Specifically, the Mahalanobis distance defines elliptical iso-contours of the cost function for a ground-truth annotation, where the ellipse’s long- and short-axes are guided by the view ray direction of the camera, and the axes extents are guided by the distance of the point to the camera.

We first define optimal transport loss for each single camera view.
In a multi-view crowd localization framework, the projection step of each view causes streaking artifacts of the features along the view ray on the ground plane, which could smear the predicted density map, making localization less accurate. In order to counteract this effect, we penalize predictions that deviate from the ground-truth position more along the view ray. This can be implemented by assigning larger weights in the Mahalanobis distance along the view ray direction. In addition, the object-to-camera distance in each view is used to further adjust the cost, where the locations far away from the camera are more heavily penalized in order to overcome the more severe projection errors there, compared to locations near the camera. We denote this design as M-OT. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, we compare different designs of optimal transport losses, including E-OT, MV-OT, ED-OT, and M-OT. E-OT uses Euclidean distance and considers each point equally, regardless of camera locations. MV-OT uses the Mahalanobis distance based on the view ray direction. ED-OT considers the camera distance in E-OT, and M-OT represents our final design that accounts for both the view ray direction and the camera distance.

Furthermore, we extend the M-MVOT-based localization model to multi-view crowd tracking through two methods, MVOT-TBD and MVOT-Track.

First, we develop MVOT-TBD, a tracking-by-detection extension of M-MVOT. Following EarlyBird
Teepe et al. (2024a)
, MVOT-TBD combines a ReID module with a two-stage association algorithm. The ReID module predicts appearance embeddings from the decoded ground-plane features, while the two-stage association algorithm links individuals across frames using their spatial positions and appearance embeddings.

Although MVOT-TBD is effective, it processes frames independently and does not explicitly optimize temporal association. We therefore introduce MVOT-Track, a tracking-specific OT loss that directly supervises inter-frame associations. The network takes two consecutive multi-view frames as input, adopts the late-to-early temporal fusion strategy
He et al. (2023)
, and predicts the current-frame localization together with backward motion offsets. These offsets are used to warp the current-frame predictions to the previous frame. MVOT-Track then minimizes the OT loss between the warped predictions and the ground-truth positions at the previous frame, while directly supervising both the transport plan
\textbf{P}^{tra}
and its marginals. Unlike M-MVOT, whose transport plan matches predicted density with ground-truth points within a single frame, the transport plan in MVOT-Track represents temporal person correspondences across adjacent frames. Its marginal constraints further distinguish individuals who remain in, exit, or newly enter the scene.

•

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first exploratory study on using point supervision optimal transport (OT) for multi-view crowd localization and tracking.

•

We propose a novel optimal transport loss M-MVOT for multi-view crowd localization, which adapts the Mahalanobis cost with view ray directions and object-to-camera distances and extends it to multi-view via the closest camera.

Related Work

2.1
Multi-view crowd localization

Traditional methods.

Early traditional multi-view crowd localization methods first detect each person in the images and then associate the camera view results via correspondence matching with hand-crafted features
Viola and Jones (2004)
;
Sabzmeydani and Mori (2007)
;
Fleuret et al. (2007)
;
Wu and Nevatia (2007)
.
W. Ge et al.
Ge and Collins (2010)
presented a Bayesian detection and counting approach by sampling from a posterior distribution over crowd configurations.
Traditional methods often rely on hand-crafted features and background subtraction preprocessing, which limit their performance and application scenarios under severe occlusions.

Deep learning methods.

With the development of deep learning frameworks and more multi-view crowd datasets, deep learning-based multi-view crowd localization methods
Baqué et al. (2017)
;
Chavdarova and Fleuret (2017)
;
Song et al. (2021a)
have seen great progress.
The early deep-learning methods did not consider the feature alignment between camera views
Chavdarova and Fleuret (2017)
.
MVMS
Zhang and Chan (2019)
proposed an end-to-end multi-view fusion framework with a feature projection layer for the wide-area crowd-counting task.
MVDet
Hou et al. (2020)
also used feature perspective transformations to fuse multi-view information for the multi-view crowd localization.
These are the first methods utilizing end-to-end frameworks for multi-view crowd localization tasks, which include single-view feature extraction, projection, and multi-view fusion and decoding.
In more recent work, SHOT
Song et al. (2021a)
utilized multi-height projection with an attention-based soft selection module and replaced the perspective transformation with a homography transformation layer in the model for better localization performance.
MVDeTr
Hou and Zheng (2021)
proposed the multi-view deformable transformer network MVDeTr for multi-view fusion by extending the deformable transformer framework in
Zhu et al. (2021)
.
CVCS
Zhang et al. (2021)
has studied multi-view crowd counting methods under the cross-view cross-scene setting, and proposed a large synthetic multi-view crowd dataset CVCS, which could also be used for multi-view crowd localization tasks.
3DROM
Qiu et al. (2022)
proposed a data augmentation method to relieve the model overfitting issue by generating random 3D cylinder occlusion masks in the 3D space.
Instead of projecting camera view representations onto a single
Hou et al. (2020)
or multiple
Song et al. (2021a)
ground planes, TrackTacular
Teepe et al. (2024b)
directly projects them into 3D space to obtain richer representations in the world coordinate system. Prior works generally extract multi-scale features in image space before projecting them onto the ground plane, whereas MSMVD
Yamane et al. (2025b)
explored multi-scale feature fusion directly in the ground plane space, which is neglected in earlier approaches. Since accurate camera calibration is challenging to acquire in practical settings, CaMuViD
Daryani et al. (2025)
introduced a calibration-free multi-view localization framework, utilizing a shared 3D space to enable cross-view association in camera view space independent of camera parameters.

Overall, the existing multi-view crowd localization methods
Hou et al. (2020)
;
Hou and Zheng (2021)
;
Song et al. (2021a)
;
Qiu et al. (2022)
are trained with fixed-size Gaussian density maps as supervision.
However, using Gaussian density maps causes issues for localization in dense crowd regions, since when the Gaussian kernels significantly overlap, the local peaks will be smoothed out and the locations will be unrecoverable. Note that reducing the Gaussian width could potentially solve this problem, but it leads to a more difficult learning problem.
Considering that point-based supervision, such as optimal transport loss, achieved better performance
in the single-image crowd localization task, in this paper, we explore the multi-view optimal transport loss for the multi-view crowd localization task.

2.2
Single-image crowd localization

Single-image crowd localization aims to estimate head locations using point annotations.
Most existing methods are built upon Gaussian density map supervision

Zhang et al. (2015)
;
Sam et al. (2017)
;
Onoro-Rubio and López-Sastre (2016)
;
Li et al. (2018)
;
Sindagi and Patel (2017)
;
Shi et al. (2019)
;
Yan et al. (2019)
,
whose predictions can be post-processed for localization.
However, the intrinsic smoothness of Gaussian density maps often blurs local peaks in crowded regions, limiting localization accuracy.

Thus, point supervision methods
Ma et al. (2019)
;
Abousamra et al. (2021)
;
Song et al. (2021b)
;
Liu et al. (2021)
;
Yan et al. (2022)
;
Liu et al. (2023)
, blob segmentation
Laradji et al. (2018)
and inverse distance maps
Liang et al. (2022)
have been proposed, many of which show better localization performance.
Among them, optimal transport (OT)-based formulations
Wang et al. (2020)
;
Ma et al. (2021)
;
Wan et al. (2021)
have shown strong performance by directly minimizing the transport distance between predicted density maps and ground-truth point distributions.
In particular, unbalanced OT (UOT) has been introduced to better handle count mismatches and perspective effects
Ma et al. (2021)
;
Wan et al. (2021)
.
Recent work such as OT-M
Lin and Chan (2023)
also applies OT minimization to refine localization results from predicted density maps, formulating it as an iterative procedure that identifies the set of locations with minimal OT loss relative to the density map.

Despite OT’s success in single-view crowd localization, point supervision with optimal transport for multi-view crowd localization is an unexplored area. The multi-view setting introduces new difficulties, such as variations in the camera view, projection errors due to poor camera calibration, and artifacts introduced when projecting features from the camera view to the 2D ground plane, which could hinder model training and localization ability.

To address these issues, our proposed loss considers the camera view directions and distances when computing the transport cost, which is implemented via a Mahalanobis distance function.

2.3
Multi-view crowd tracking

Existing multi-view crowd tracking methods can be divided into camera-view-first approaches and ground-plane tracking approaches.

Camera-view-first tracking.

A common paradigm first performs detection and tracking independently in each camera view, followed by cross-camera association. Representative works extend single-camera tracking frameworks with cross-view constraints or ID embedding modeling.
For example, MCTR
Niculescu-Mizil et al. (2025)
adopts a transformer-based multi-camera tracking architecture built upon DETR
Carion et al. (2020)
with tracking embeddings, while other methods incorporate spatial-temporal constraints or human pose cues to improve cross-view consistency
Cherdchusakulchai et al. (2024)
;
Jeongho Kim and Baek (2023)
;
Kim et al. (2024)
.
Trajectory prediction has also been introduced to assist association across frames
Jeon et al. (2023)
, and additional modalities such as infrared have been explored to improve robustness under low-light conditions
Fan et al. (2025)
.
Although effective, these above methods rely on accurate per-view tracking and subsequent cross-camera matching, making them sensitive to occlusion and view inconsistency in crowded scenes.

Ground-plane tracking.

To alleviate cross-view association ambiguity, another line of work performs tracking directly on the ground plane.
MVFlow
Engilberge et al. (2023)
models motion offsets on the ground plane to infer people’s presence, improving robustness to occlusions. However, it assumes that each person moves at most one grid cell per time step, which breaks down in realistic low-frame-rate scenarios where larger displacements occur.

EarlyBird
Teepe et al. (2024a)
projects multi-view image features into the world space for pedestrian localization and introduces a ReID branch for trajectory association. Its extension, TrackTacular
Teepe et al. (2024b)
, further lifts the ground-plane representation into 3D and predicts motion offsets to facilitate association.

MVTrajecter
Yamane et al. (2025a)
extends pairwise similarity computation in tracking to multiple temporal intervals by computing and fusing motion and appearance costs across different time steps. While this enhances robustness, it leads to increased computational overhead due to the need for evaluating multiple temporal pairs.

Compared with existing ground-plane multi-view crowd tracking methods, MVOT-Track formulates inter-frame identity association as an OT problem and explicitly supervises the temporal transport plan and its marginals.

Multi-view Optimal Transport

Figure 3:

In this section, we first review optimal transport based on Euclidean distance cost (denoted as E-OT) for single-image crowd localization. Then, we introduce the proposed view ray Mahalanobis distance optimal transport (M-OT), where the cost can be guided either by the view ray direction of each GT location (MV-OT), or by the object-to-camera distance of each location (ED-OT), or both (M-OT). Finally, the proposed M-OT is applied under multi-views, where each prediction point’s optimal transport cost is calculated under the closest camera view via M-OT, denoted as M-MVOT.

3.1
Euclidean distance optimal transport

We follow the loss formulation in
Wan et al. (2021)
, which is based on an unbalanced optimal transport formulation with Euclidean distance cost (E-OT). Denote
{\cal X}=\{(a_{i},\textbf{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
as the predicted density map, where
a_{i}
is pixel
\textbf{x}_{i}

\textbf{a}=[a_{i}]_{i}
is the density of all pixels, and
n
is the number of pixels. We denote
{\cal Y}=\{(b_{j},\textbf{y}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
as the ground-truth dot map, where
b_{j}\in\{0,1\}
indicates there is a person at location
\textbf{y}_{j}
,
\textbf{b}=[b_{j}]_{j}=\textbf{1}_{m}
, and
m
is the ground-truth number of people.
The entropic-regularized unbalanced optimal transport loss is as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\bf{C}}^{\tau}=

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{P}^{loc}\in{\cal R}_{+}^{n\times m}}\langle\textbf{C},\mathbf{P}^{loc}\rangle-\epsilon H(\mathbf{P}^{loc})

(1)

\displaystyle+\tau\|\mathbf{P}^{loc}\bf{1}_{m}-\textbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+\tau\|(\mathbf{P}^{loc})^{T}\bf{1}_{n}-\textbf{b}\|_{1},

where
\mathbf{P}^{loc}\in{\cal R}_{+}^{n\times m}
is the transport plan matrix, which assigns each density
\textbf{x}_{i}
of
\cal X
to location
\textbf{y}_{i}
of
\cal Y
for measuring the cost.

\bf{C}\in{\cal R}_{+}^{n\times m}
is the transport cost matrix, where
\textit{C}_{ij}
measures the cost of moving the predicted density at
\textbf{x}_{i}
to ground-truth dot location
\textbf{y}_{j}
. The cost function is defined as the exponential Euclidean distance between the predicted density of
\textbf{x}_{i}
and the ground-truth dot
\textbf{y}_{j}
:

\displaystyle C_{ij}=c(\textbf{x}_{i},\textbf{y}_{j})=\exp(\|\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{y}_{j}\|).

(2)

The second term
H(\mathbf{P}^{loc})=-\sum_{ij}P^{loc}_{ij}\log P^{loc}_{ij}
is the entropic regularization term.

Wan et al. (2021)
sets

D_{1}(\mathbf{P}^{loc}\bf{1}_{m}|\textbf{a})=\|\mathbf{P}^{\mathit{loc}}\bf{1}_{m}-\textbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}

3.2
Mahalanobis distance optimal transport

The Euclidean distance in the transport cost matrix
C
in (
2
) considers all directions of deviation from the ground-truth point equally. For multi-view crowd localization, we aim to apply the OT loss on the predicted density map on the ground plane.
However, the projection step, which projects features from the camera view to the ground plane, introduces streaking artifacts in the feature map due to missing information,
i.e.
, the unknown height of the corresponding object in the 3D world, which may lead to streaking artifacts in the density map that hinder its localization ability.
To counteract this effect, we use Mahalanobis distance to replace the Euclidean distance in (
2
), where the contours of the Mahalanobis distance are guided by the view ray direction at the location, denoted as MV-OT.
Besides, the object-to-camera distance could also adjust the calculation of the cost matrix for different regions, denoted as ED-OT.
Finally, both the view ray directions and object-to-camera distances can be considered in the Mahalanobis distance optimal transport loss,
denoted as M-OT.

View ray-directed transport cost (MV-OT).

Using the Mahalanobis distance defines elliptical iso-contours in the transport cost, where certain directions will incur larger costs than others.
Here, we set the ellipse shape to counteract the errors and streaking artifacts introduced by the projection step.
Specifically, the short axis of the ellipse is set to be aligned with the view ray between the ground-truth point and the camera, and the long axis is set to be perpendicular to the view ray (MV-OT). In this way, the predictions that deviate from the ground-truth along the view ray from the ground-truth points are assigned with larger penalties (see “MV-OT” in Fig.
1
, where more penalties are given along the view ray), in order to penalize prediction errors caused by projection distortion.
Concretely, we first calculate the covariance matrix for each GT point:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{-1},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}^{2}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\beta&-\sin\beta\\
\sin\beta&\cos\beta\end{bmatrix},

where
\bf{R}
represents the rotation matrix from the original coordinate to the view ray coordinate, and
\beta
is the rotation angle.
\bf{\Sigma}
is the variance matrix with diagonal entries
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
corresponding to the variances along the view ray direction and perpendicular to the view ray direction, respectively. Here we set
\sigma_{1}^{2}<\sigma_{2}^{2}
, and thus
1/\sigma_{1}^{2}>1/\sigma_{2}^{2}
, indicating deviating predictions along the view ray direction incur the larger cost and thus are more heavily penalized.
Then, the Mahalanobis distance cost in MV-OT can be written as:

\displaystyle C_{ij}=\exp(\sqrt{(\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{y}_{j})^{\text{T}}\textbf{S}^{-1}(\textbf{x}_{i}-\textbf{y}_{j})}).

(4)

Note that when
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}=1
, the Mahalanobis distance degenerates into the Euclidean distance.
We choose
\sigma_{1}^{2}=1
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}=1.2
in the experiments for MV-OT.

Distance-adjusted transport cost (ED-OT).

The object-to-camera distance also influences the localization accuracy for the points, where faraway points tend to have larger prediction errors. Thus, we use the object-to-camera distance to adjust the cost calculation and give more penalties to faraway regions. We rewrite
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
as follows.

\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}=1/\exp(\alpha*\MinMaxNorm(d_{cam})),

(5)

where
d_{cam}
denotes the distance from the ground-truth point to the camera, which can be obtained from the camera calibrations and point coordinates, and
\MinMaxNorm
normalizes the object-to-camera distance into
[0,1]
through (
d_{cam}
-
\min(d_{cam})
) / (
\max(d_{cam})
-
\min(d_{cam})
). Here
\alpha
is an adaptive factor to adjust the influence of distance to the cost function in (
5
) and (
6
). When
d_{cam}
is larger,
\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
decrease, and thus more penalties are assigned. Since
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}
, the Euclidean distance is actually used in the cost, but the object-to-camera distance adjusts the Euclidean distance for different points, which we denote as ED-OT.

View ray-directed and distance-adjusted transport cost (M-OT).

To consider both the influence of the view ray direction and object-to-camera distance, we use the object-to-camera distance to adjust the long-axis and short-axis extents of the ellipse iso-contours in the Mahalanobis distance. Specifically, for the GT points that are far away from the camera (
d_{cam}
is larger), a larger variance is assigned perpendicular to the view ray (
\sigma_{2}^{2}
), which has the effect of penalizing deviations perpendicular to the view ray less, and penalizing deviations along the view ray (
\sigma_{1}^{2}
) more:

\sigma_{1}^{2}=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma_{2}^{2}=\exp(\alpha*\MinMaxNorm(d_{cam})).

(6)

By combining (
3
) and (
6
), we obtain the view ray-directed and distance-adjusted Mahalanobis distance cost function for OT, denoted as M-OT.
Note that when
\alpha=0
, then
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}=1
, and the proposed Mahalanobis distance cost of M-OT degenerates into E-OT in (
2
).

3.3
Mahalanobis distance multi-view optimal transport (M-MVOT)

We introduce the Mahalanobis distance optimal transport (M-OT) for single views in Sec.
3.2
.
We further introduce the Mahalanobis distance multi-view optimal transport (M-MVOT) by combining the proposed Mahalanobis distance optimal transport via a distance-based selection strategy.
Specifically, the cost of a point is calculated using the closest camera view’s M-OT defined as follows.

C_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}(d^{k}_{\text{cam}})\exp\Big(\sqrt{(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j})}\Big),

(7)

where the binary indicator
\mathbbm{1}(d^{k}_{\text{cam}})
selects the closest camera:

\mathbbm{1}(d^{k}_{\text{cam}})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }d^{k}_{\text{cam}}=\min_{p\in\{1,\dots,K\}}d^{p}_{\text{cam}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

Here,
K
is the number of cameras, and
d^{k}_{\text{cam}}
denotes the distance from the ground-truth point to camera
k
.

Following the strategy, we extend E-OT, MV-OT, ED-OT, and M-OT to multi-camera view settings for multi-view crowd localization tasks, denoted as E-MVOT, MV-MVOT, ED-MVOT, and M-MVOT, respectively, where the closest camera is used to compute the cost function for a given ground-truth point, as in Fig.

4
MVOT-based Multi-view Crowd Tracking

In this section, we extend the M-MVOT-based localization framework to multi-view crowd tracking through two methods. MVOT-TBD is a tracking-by-detection baseline that combines the M-MVOT-supervised localization model with a ReID module and a two-stage association algorithm following EarlyBird
Teepe et al. (2024a)
. MVOT-Track further formulates inter-frame identity association as an optimal transport problem and explicitly supervises temporal correspondences across adjacent frames.

4.1
MVOT-TBD

As shown in Fig.
4
, MVOT-TBD extends the M-MVOT-based localization framework with a person ReID head and a two-stage association algorithm to link localized pedestrians across consecutive frames.

The ReID head aims to extract discriminative identity features for each tracked individual.
To obtain reliable ReID features that can be sampled at ground-plane locations during inference,
we jointly train the ReID head with the entire model.
Specifically, we adopt two supervision strategies: a classification loss and a supervised contrastive loss.
For each detected instance
i
in a training batch of size
N
, we extract a ReID feature vector

\mathbf{f}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at the corresponding ground-plane detection location,
where
d
denotes the feature dimension.
The feature
\mathbf{f}_{i}
is then fed into a linear classification layer to produce identity logits

\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{id}}}
, where
N_{\text{id}}
denotes the total number of unique identities,
with each individual treated as an independent class in the classification space.
We denote by
\mathbf{s}_{i,j}
the logit corresponding to the
j
-th identity, and

y_{i}\in\{1,\dots,N_{\text{id}}\}
the ground-truth identity label of instance
i
.
The classification loss is formulated as the standard cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\frac{\exp(\mathbf{s}_{i,y_{i}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{id}}}\exp(\mathbf{s}_{i,j})}.

(9)

In addition, we apply a supervised contrastive loss
Khosla et al. (2020)
on the decoder features, encouraging features of the same identity that are sampled from both the ground plane and the camera views to cluster closely, while separating those of different identities:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}=\sum\limits_{i\in I}\frac{-1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\text{pos}}(i)|}\sum\limits_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{pos}}(i)}\log\frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{p}/\tau\right)}{\sum\limits_{a\in A(i)}\exp\left(\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{a}/\tau\right)}\,,

(10)

where
\mathcal{P}_{\text{pos}}(i)
denotes the set of positive samples sharing the same identity with anchor
i
(including both ground-plane and camera-view samples),
A(i)
represents the set of all samples in the batch excluding the anchor
i
,
\tau
is a temperature parameter, and
\mathbf{f}_{i}
is the feature embedding of sample
i
.
The overall ReID objective is a sum of the classification and contrastive losses as in
Teepe et al. (2024a)
.

We use a standard Kalman filter
Kalman (1960)
here to estimate the position and velocity of an object from noisy observations,
where the bounding box coordinates are directly taken as observations of the object state. For each target, the state is modeled as

\mathbf{x}=[x,y,a,h,\dot{x},\dot{y},\dot{a},\dot{h}]^{T},

(11)

where
(x,y)
denotes the center position of the bounding box,
a
is the aspect ratio,
h
is the height, and
(\dot{x},\dot{y},\dot{a},\dot{h})
represent the corresponding velocities. Although predictions are performed on ground-plane points, we assign a fixed bounding box size to each detection for state representation and tracking.

For frame association, we implement a two-stage association strategy using the Hungarian algorithm
Kuhn (1955)
. In the first stage, matching costs between frames are computed by combining appearance and motion cues. Appearance distance is defined as

\displaystyle D_{r}(\mathbf{f}_{i},\mathbf{f}_{j})=1-\frac{\mathbf{f}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{f}_{i}\|\|\mathbf{f}_{j}\|},

(12)

where
\mathbf{f}_{i}
and
\mathbf{f}_{j}
are ReID features of a tracklet and a detection. Motion distance is

\displaystyle D_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})=(\mathbf{d}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{i}^{-1}(\mathbf{d}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{i}),

(13)

where
(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{S}_{i})
are the projected mean and covariance of the
i
-th tracklet estimated by Kalman filter, and
\mathbf{d}_{j}
denotes the
j
-th detection. These two are fused as
D=\lambda D_{r}+(1-\lambda)D_{m}
, balancing appearance and motion, with gating applied before Hungarian matching.
In the second stage, unmatched tracks and detections are associated spatially using the Euclidean distance between target centers with a relaxed spatial threshold, which helps handle appearance variations and improves robustness in challenging cases.

4.2
MVOT-Track

MVOT-TBD performs localization followed by trajectory association and therefore does not directly optimize inter-frame correspondences. To address this limitation, we introduce MVOT-Track, a tracking-specific optimal transport formulation that models identity association between adjacent frames. Unlike M-MVOT, whose transport plan matches predicted density with ground-truth points within a single frame, MVOT-Track constructs a temporal transport plan between motion-warped predictions at frame
t
and ground-truth positions at frame
t-1
, and directly supervises the plan and its marginals to capture target-presence changes across frames.

Model architecture.

To enable the use of this loss, we extend the existing backbone MVDeTr
Hou and Zheng (2021)
to the temporal domain, as illustrated in Fig.
5
. MVDeTr is originally designed for multi-view localization, where a ground-plane decoder composed of several multi-view deformable attention layers is employed.
In contrast to the localization setting, which processes a single frame at a time, our approach takes two consecutive frames as input to capture temporal information, denoted as
I_{t-1}
and
I_{t}
, corresponding to the previous and current frames, respectively. These frames are first encoded by an image encoder and then projected onto the ground plane, following the same procedure as in multi-view localization. Subsequently, the ground-plane features from the two frames are jointly processed by multi-view deformable attention layers to perform temporal fusion.

The predicted result at frame

Multi-view deformable attention.

We follow the formulation of the multi-view deformable attention defined in MVDeTr
Hou and Zheng (2021)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathrm{MVDeformAttn}(f_{\hat{c}},p,\hat{c})\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt=\sum_{m=1}^{M}W_{m}\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{mkc^{\prime}}W^{\prime}_{m}f^{c^{\prime}}(p+\Delta p_{mkc^{\prime}}),\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt\text{subject to: }\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{C}a_{mkc^{\prime}}=1,\end{split}

(14)

where
f_{\hat{c}}
denotes the fused ground-plane feature at query position
p
for the current view
\hat{c}
, and
f^{c^{\prime}}
is the projected ground-plane feature from source camera
c^{\prime}
.
For each attention head
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
,
K
reference points
\Delta p_{mkc^{\prime}}
are sampled from each camera view
c^{\prime}
and projected via
W^{\prime}_{m}
.
The attention weights
a_{mkc^{\prime}}
indicate the relative importance of each reference point and satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{C}a_{mkc^{\prime}}=1
, ensuring a soft selection across reference points and views.
The weighted features are summed within each head, and outputs of all
M
heads are aggregated via learned weight
W_{m}
.
Elliptical distortions along camera view rays involved by ground-plane projection can be alleviated by dynamically offsetting the reference points
\Delta p_{mkc^{\prime}}
.

Temporal fusion of multi-view features.

Inspired by the Late-to-Early fusion strategy
He et al. (2023)
;
Teepe et al. (2024b)
,
we perform temporal fusion on multi-view ground-plane features.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}=\{f_{t-1}^{c^{\prime}}\}_{{c^{\prime}}=1}^{C}
and

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{f_{t}^{{c^{\prime}}}\}_{{c^{\prime}}=1}^{C}
denote the sets of projected
ground-plane feature maps from all camera views at frames
t-1
and
t
, respectively.
We first apply
L
stacked multi-view deformable attention layers to
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
to aggregate multi-view information:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{decoded}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{MVDeformAttn}^{(L)}\Big(\dots

(15)

\displaystyle\mathrm{MVDeformAttn}^{(1)}(\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\dots\Big),

where each MVDeformAttn layer operates jointly over all camera features.
In our implementation, we set
L=3
.
The decoded features
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{decoded}
are then fused with

\mathcal{F}_{t}
via channel-wise concatenation, denoted by
\oplus
,
and passed through the same MVDeformAttn layers again to obtain the final
decoded representation:

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{F}_{t}^{decoded}&=\mathrm{MVDeformAttn}^{(L)}\Big(\dots\\
&\mathrm{MVDeformAttn}^{(1)}(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{decoded}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{t})\\
\dots\Big),\end{aligned}

(16)

where
\oplus
represents concatenation along the channel dimension.
The resulting
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{decoded}
encodes both multi-view and
temporal information from adjacent frames. It is subsequently fed into
two task-specific heads: one for predicting the ground-plane density map
for crowd localization, and the other for estimating per-person motion
offsets from frame
t
to frame
t\!-\!1
, enabling crowd association
for tracking.

Loss definition.
To optimize the above architecture, we first construct a pseudo localization prediction at frame
t\!-\!1
by warping the current frame
t
localization predictions using the predicted motion offsets, which means
d_{t-1}^{pseudo}=d_{t}+\delta_{pred}
.
\delta
represents the offsets. We then compute the optimal transport loss between the constructed pseudo localization
d_{t-1}^{pseudo}
and the ground-truth
d_{t-1}^{gt}
. It is crucial to recognize that the optimal transport between the pseudo localization at frame
t\!-\!1
and the ground-truth localization at frame
t\!-\!1
is inherently unbalanced. This is because some individuals in frame
t\!-\!1
may have exited the scene and thus have no corresponding matches in frame
t
, while frame
t
may include newly arrived individuals who have no counterparts in the previous frame (Fig.
6
).

This phenomenon is specific to tracking and does not arise in localization tasks, which neither model per-person identity nor enforce temporal consistency. To address this, we formulate the people temporal association problem as an optimal transport task and introduce three dedicated regularization terms. The formulation of MVOT-Track is as follows:

\begin{aligned} {\cal L}_{\bf{C}}^{\tau}(d_{t-1}^{pseudo},d_{t-1}^{gt})=&\min_{\textbf{P}^{tra}\in{\cal R}_{+}^{n\times m}}\langle\textbf{C},\textbf{P}^{tra}\rangle-\epsilon H(\textbf{P}^{tra})\\
&+\gamma\|\textbf{P}^{tra}\mathbf{1}_{m}-\textbf{P}_{GT}^{tra}\mathbf{1}_{m}\|_{1}\\
&+\gamma\|(\textbf{P}^{tra})^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}-(\textbf{P}_{GT}^{tra})^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}\|_{1}\\
&+\psi\|\textbf{P}^{tra}-\textbf{P}_{GT}^{tra}\|_{1}\end{aligned}

(17)

C
is defined as the Euclidean distance between
d_{t-1}^{\text{pseudo}}
and
d_{t-1}^{\text{gt}}
. Here,
\textbf{P}^{tra}

is the temporal transport plan, which represents the correspondence between adjacent frames.
Note that we do not compute the correspondence directly between the original localization results of adjacent frames. Instead, we first warp the current frame’s prediction to the previous frame using the predicted offsets, and then compute the correspondence. This design encourages the offsets prediction to be more accurate by explicitly aligning the warped predictions with the ground truth.

We introduce three additional regularization terms on
\textbf{P}^{tra}
. The first two terms supervise the marginals
\textbf{P}^{tra}\mathbf{1}_{m}
and
(\textbf{P}^{tra})^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}
, which are
n
- and
m
-dimensional vectors respectively. Here,
\textbf{P}^{tra}\mathbf{1}_{m}
represents the retention status of individuals from frame
t\!-\!1
: each value equals to 1 indicates the person remains at frame
t
, while values equal to 0 imply the person has exited the scene. Similarly,
(\textbf{P}^{tra})^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}
indicates the origin of individuals in frame
t
: zero value suggest a new arrival not present in frame
t\!-\!1
. By supervising these marginals, the network is encouraged to distinguish between persistent individuals and those entering or exiting the scene. The third term directly supervises the transport plan
\textbf{P}^{tra}
itself, encouraging accurate pairwise associations between individuals across frames based on the ground-truth correspondence matrix
\textbf{P}_{GT}^{tra}
. This matrix is constructed from identity-level annotations and reflects the true temporal correspondences.
\gamma
, and
\psi
are weighting hyperparameters. These three terms work together to encourage the model to learn temporally consistent assignments.

The overall loss related to tracking can be formulated as:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{tracking}={\cal L}_{\bf{C}}^{\tau}(d_{t-1}^{pseudo},d_{t-1}^{gt})+{\cal L}_{offset}^{L_{1}},

(18)

where
{\cal L}_{offset}^{L_{1}}
denotes the
L_{1}
distance between the predicted and ground-truth offsets, which is commonly adopted in previous works
Teepe et al. (2024b)
;
Zhang et al. (2025)
. We combine it with the proposed MVOT-Track loss
{\cal L}_{\bf{C}}^{\tau}(d_{t-1}^{pseudo},d_{t-1}^{gt})
to jointly supervise the crowd correspondences across adjacent frames for tracking.

Experiments and Results

5.1
Methods overview

For better clarity, we summarize all variants of our framework in Table
1
.
The localization variants (E-OT, MV-OT, ED-OT, M-OT, E-MVOT, MV-MVOT, ED-MVOT, and M-MVOT)
mainly differ in the variance defined in the covariance matrix when computing the transport cost matrix,
as well as whether distance-based camera selection is applied for multi-camera fusion.
Here,
\sigma_{1}^{2}
denotes the variance along the camera view ray, and
\sigma_{2}^{2}
denotes the variance perpendicular to the view ray.
In addition, we extend the framework to tracking with two variants, MVOT-TBD and MVOT-Track.
These tracking variants focus on temporal association modeling rather than variance design or camera selection.

5.2
Experiment settings

Datasets.

We evaluate the proposed methods on the multi-view localization task on 3 public datasets in the experiments, including CVCS
Zhang et al. (2021)
, Wildtrack
Chavdarova et al. (2018)

Hou et al. (2020)

Wildtrack and MultiviewX are also used for evaluating the multi-view crowd tracking performance.

CVCS
is a synthetic multi-view crowd dataset, containing 31 scenes, where 23 are for training and the remaining 8 for testing. Each scene contains 100 multi-view frames with about 60-120 camera views. The size of the scenes in CVCS varies from 10m
\times
20m to 90m
\times
80m. Each scene contains 90-180 people.
Wildtrack
is a real-world single-scene multi-view dataset, whose scene size is 12m
\times
36m. There are 20 people per frame on average in the dataset. The dataset consists of 7 camera views and 400 synchronized multi-view frames, in which the first 360 frames are used for training and the 40 frames are for testing.

MultiviewX
is a synthetic single-scene multi-view dataset, whose scene size is 16m
\times
25m and consists of 6 camera views.
The dataset includes 400 multi-view frames, and the first 360 are used for training and the last 40 are for testing. MultiviewX has around 40 synthetic people per frame.
The resolution of the camera view images in the 3 datasets is 1920
\times
1080.
Note that CVCS is a
cross-scene
dataset, where the same scene and camera layout do not appear in both the training set and test set. In contrast, both Wildtrack and MultiviewX are
single-scene

Evaluation metrics.

We use 5 metrics
Bernardin and Stiefelhagen (2008)
to evaluate and compare the multi-view crowd localization methods:
Multiple Object Detection Accuracy (MODA), Multiple Object Detection Precision (MODP), Precision (P), Recall (R), and F1_score (F1).
Specifically,

MODA=1-(FP+FN)/(TP+FN)
measures the detection accuracy.

MODP=(\sum(1-d[d<t]/t))/TP
measures the precision of detection, where
d
is the distance from a detected person point to its ground truth and
t
is the distance threshold.
Meanwhile,

P=TP/(FP+TP)
,
R=TP/(TP+FN)
, and
F1=2P*R/(P+R)
.
Here,
TP
,
FP
, and
FN
are the number of true positives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.
Overall,
we use
MODA
and
F1_score
as the main metrics for multi-view crowd localization
, since they comprehensively measure the performance of detecting the crowd both completely and precisely.

We use another 5 metrics
Bernardin and Stiefelhagen (2008)
to evaluate and compare the performance of multi-view crowd tracking methods: Identification F1 score (IDF1), Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA), Multiple Object Tracking Precision (MOTP), Mostly Tracked Targets (MT), and Mostly Lost Targets (ML).
Specifically,
IDF1
is the ratio of correctly identified detections over the average number of ground-truth and computed detections, which reflects the identity preservation capability.

MOTA
measures the overall tracking accuracy by considering three types of errors: false positives, missed targets, and identity switches. It is defined as:

\text{MOTA}=1-\frac{FP+FN+IDS}{GT},

(19)

where
FP
,
FN
, and
IDS
are the numbers of false positives, false negatives, and identity switches, and
GT
is the number of ground truth objects.

MOTP
evaluates the average localization precision of correctly matched object trajectories across all frames and is defined as:

\text{MOTP}=\frac{\sum_{i,t}d_{i}^{t}}{\sum_{t}c^{t}},

(20)

where
d_{i}^{t}
is the distance between the ground truth and predicted location of object
i
at time
t
, and
c^{t}
is the number of matches at time
t
.

MT
is the percentage of ground-truth trajectories that are successfully tracked for more than 80% of their lifespan, while
ML
denotes the percentage of trajectories that are tracked for less than 20% of their lifespan.
Overall,
we use
IDF1
and
MOTA
as the main quantitative metrics for multi-view crowd tracking
, since they provide a comprehensive evaluation of both identity consistency and tracking accuracy.

and

adjusts the object-to-camera distance in the M-MVOT loss in (

.

Comparison with learnable covariance matrix (LCM).

We compare the proposed M-MVOT with a data-driven learnable covariance matrix (LCM) in Table
8
. For LCM, the covariance matrices are estimated from the ground-plane features using two convolutional layers. As shown in Table
8
, LCM does not achieve better performance than M-MVOT. The main reason is that M-MVOT explicitly incorporates the domain knowledge of camera geometry into the covariance matrix by considering the camera view direction and the object-to-camera distance. In contrast, LCM is optimized from the ground-plane features without explicit geometric constraints. Although LCM has the flexibility to adapt to the data distribution, it is difficult for the network to automatically discover and encode such view-dependent geometric priors during training. By explicitly modeling these geometry-aware factors, M-MVOT provides a more effective and interpretable covariance computation, making it more suitable for the multi-view localization task. Exploring how to incorporate geometric priors into learnable covariance matrices is an interesting direction for future work.

Furthermore, MVOT-Track achieves the highest IDF1 and MOTA on the MultiviewX dataset among all compared methods, outperforming MVTrajecter. This may be attributed to the fact that, although MVTrajecter leverages both motion and appearance cues, it assigns a very low weight to motion (0.02) and thus relies heavily on appearance (0.98). As MultiviewX is a synthetic dataset with limited appearance variations, such as illumination changes and sensor noise, it is more challenging to learn identity-consistent appearance embeddings than in real-world scenarios.
On Wildtrack, MVTrajecter reports the best performance in terms of IDF1 and MOTA, while MVOT-Track achieves competitive results. A possible explanation for this gap is that MVTrajecter incorporates longer temporal appearance context (up to 7 frames), which may better exploit temporal consistency. However, since the official implementation is not publicly available, we provide our own reproduction, which yields noticeably lower performance. This discrepancy suggests that the reported results may be sensitive to implementation details.
Overall, the performance gains of MVOT-Track on both datasets can be attributed to the explicit supervision of cross-frame associations, which results in fewer identity switches, better preservation of long-term trajectories, and reduced track fragmentation.

Ablation study on the number of frames.

We conduct an ablation study to investigate the effect of the number of temporal frames on both tracking performance and computational cost. In the default setting, we use two frames, namely the current frame
t
and the previous frame
t-1
. As shown in Table
17
, this setting achieves the best IDF1, MOTA, and MT while maintaining moderate memory consumption, FLOPs, and runtime. Using only the current frame requires less computation but leads to lower tracking performance, demonstrating the benefit of incorporating temporal information. Increasing the number of frames to three or four substantially increases the computational cost without further improving tracking performance. These results indicate that two-frame temporal fusion provides the best trade-off between tracking effectiveness and computational efficiency.

The different trends of the three- and four-view subsets further indicate that performance depends on both the number and configuration of cameras.

Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce M-MVOT, a novel optimal transport loss for point-supervised multi-view crowd localization. It incorporates camera view directions and object-to-camera distances into the Mahalanobis transport cost and applies the cost from the closest camera to reduce ground-plane projection distortions. We further extend the framework to tracking through MVOT-TBD, a complete tracking-by-detection pipeline, and MVOT-Track, a tracking-specific OT formulation that directly supervises the temporal transport plan and its marginals across consecutive frames. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of M-MVOT for localization and the competitive tracking performance of MVOT-TBD and MVOT-Track.

Declarations

Ethical consideration
. Multi-view crowd localization and tracking could be used for crowd analysis, autonomous driving, and public traffic management. To enhance privacy protection, images can be encoded as features, replaced with synthetic data, or anonymized by masking human faces before being processed by the localization models.

Data availability
. We use the publicly available CVCS, Wildtrack, and MultiviewX datasets for multi-view crowd localization and tracking. All datasets are used in accordance with their original licenses and terms of use.
