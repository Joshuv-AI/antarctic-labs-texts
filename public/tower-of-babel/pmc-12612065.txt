Refining cancer prediction with DNA sequencing and combined machine learning approaches
Authors: Seyed Matin Malakouti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A high-accuracy DNA-based cancer risk predictor was developed by blending Logistic Regression with Gaussian Naive Bayes, and its hyperparameters were optimized via grid search. Five cancer types (BRCA1, KIRC, COAD, LUAD, PRAD) were classified in a cohort of 390 patients. Accuracies of 100% were attained for BRCA1, KIRC, and COAD, while 98% was achieved for LUAD and PRAD—representing improvements of 1–2% over recent deep-learning and multi-omic benchmarks. A micro- and macro-average ROC AUC of 0.99 was obtained. The blended ensemble was shown to outperform each individual algorithm and surpass existing state-of-the-art methods, providing a lightweight, interpretable, and highly effective tool for early cancer prediction.

Introduction

Studying specific genetic mutations in oncology plays a pivotal role in advancing personalized medicine and improving patient outcomes. An exemplary case is the breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1), where mutations can significantly elevate the risk of various cancers such as breast and ovarian. Research endeavors focusing on BRCA1 aim to comprehend the RING and BRCT domains, which are frequently sites of mutations, and to develop personalized treatment strategies for patients carrying these mutations63.

The study encompasses five cancer types: Breast Cancer gene 1, Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma, Colorectal Adenocarcinoma, Lung Adenocarcinoma, and Prostate Adenocarcinoma. Each type is characterized by specific genetic mutations and distinctive features.

Intensive research is underway in Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC-2). Investigations are delving into the molecular characteristics of KIRC, with a particular emphasis on immunological responses and metabolic pathways. High-throughput sequencing techniques are employed to pinpoint novel candidate genes that could serve as focal points for future cancer treatments. Moreover, predictive models are being crafted to enhance the accuracy of survival predictions for KIRC patients64,65.

Research in Colorectal Adenocarcinoma (COAD-3) centers on the genetic foundations of colorectal cancer, frequently associated with mutations in the APC gene. The objective is to pinpoint new genetic markers and formulate targeted therapies to combat this disease.

Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD-4) research concentrates on the EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor) pathway, a critical driver of lung adenocarcinoma initiation and progression. Overexpression of the EGFR protein due to mutations can spur cancer cell proliferation and survival, thereby prompting the development of EGFR inhibitors as potential therapeutic interventions67.

Prostate Adenocarcinoma (PRAD-5) research aims to decipher the genetic underpinnings of prostate cancer, with a specific focus on the androgen receptor (AR) pathway. Mutations in the AR gene can trigger overexpression of the AR protein, fostering prostate cancer cell proliferation and survival. Consequently, AR inhibitors are under development as potential therapeutic avenues67.

These studies epitomize a collective effort to leverage genetic research’s power to guide the development of targeted therapies, ultimately leading to more efficacious and personalized cancer treatments for patients.

This paper encompasses a classification of patient DNA from five cancer types (Breast Cancer gene 1, Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma, Colorectal Adenocarcinoma, Lung Adenocarcinoma, and Prostate Adenocarcinoma) using Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting, Gaussian NB, and a Blending approach incorporating Logistic Regression and Gaussian NB. It’s noteworthy that these genetic mutations can elevate the risk of developing these cancer types. Therefore, comprehending these genetic mutations and their ramifications can aid in early detection and prevention efforts.

The article introduces a novel approach utilizing a blend of Logistic Regression and Gaussian Naive Bayes, augmented by the grid search technique for hyperparameter fine-tuning.

Part 2 of this paper entails a comprehensive literature review, setting the foundation by providing insights into existing research. Part 3 elucidates the methodology, outlining various aspects such as dataset characteristics, machine learning algorithms employed, hyperparameter optimization methodologies, and evaluation criteria.

Section 4 delves into the outcomes of employing machine learning algorithms, offering a detailed analysis of results and findings. Finally, the article concludes by synthesizing key takeaways and implications gleaned from the study.

k-fold-cross-validation

The objective of this methodology is to generate numerous subcategories by evenly partitioning the entire dataset into k distinct subsets (refer to Fig. 2). The leftover data is then utilized for validating the training process, while the k-1 subsets (in this study, k was set to 10)45 are employed for training the network. The validation subsets are rotated iteratively as this process is repeated k times. Prior to employing this approach, the dataset needed to be segregated into two subcategories: training and test data.

Fig. 2A k-fold-cross-validation.

A k-fold-cross-validation.

The research employed a 10-fold cross-validation technique, which involved dividing the dataset into ten distinct subsets. During the training phase, nine subsets (comprising 194 patients collectively) were used iteratively for training, leaving one subset (consisting of 98 patients) for validation. This cyclic process was repeated ten times, with each subset taking a turn as the validation set.

Concerning the aggregation of K-trained models for prediction, after training each model using a distinct subset, predictions were generated on the corresponding validation set. This procedure was iterated across all ten subsets, resulting in ten sets of predictions. These predictions were subsequently combined or averaged to derive the final prediction for each instance within the dataset.

Model selection and evaluation were carried out using stratified 10-fold cross-validation on the training set, ensuring that each fold preserved the proportion of samples from all five cancer classes. Within each fold, grid search was performed to optimize hyperparameters, and no data leakage between training and validation splits was permitted. In addition to cross-validation, an independent hold-out test set—comprisin 20% of the full cohort and set aside before any model fitting or parameter tuning—was used for final assessment of generalization performance. Metrics reported in the Results section (accuracy, ROC AUC, etc.) therefore reflect both the averaged cross-validation performance and the model’s accuracy on truly unseen data.

Modeling logistic regression for cancer prediction can provide various problems and limitations:

Data Quality: For effective prediction, logistic regression requires a high-quality dataset. The model’s predicted performance may suffer significantly if the dataset contains mistakes and missing or irregular values.

Feature Choice: Including unimportant factors may result in overfitting, while omitting critical ones may result in underfitting.

Multicollinearity: Independent variables in logistic regression should not be substantially connected. It can make it challenging to determine the influence of independent factors on the dependent variable.

Outlier Sensitivity: Outliers can considerably impact the predicted regression coefficients in logistic regression.

Overfitting happens when the model is overly complicated, such as having too many parameters in comparison to the quantity of data. Overfitted models have poor predicting ability because they exaggerate tiny variations in the data.

Linear Decision Boundary: In most cases, real-world data is not linearly separable, which might restrict the usefulness of a logistic regression model.

When used to predict cancer, the Gaussian Naive Bayes (NB) model has certain limits and challenges:

The assumption that all characteristics are independent of one other is a significant restriction of Gaussian NB. This might be a severe issue in real-world applications such as cancer prediction when various symptoms or causes are often interconnected.

As with any model, inconsistent, missing, or erroneous data can substantially influence the model’s performance. Furthermore, an unbalanced dataset (for example, more non-cancer cases than cancer instances) might skew the model’s predictions.

Continuous Data: Gaussian NB presumes that the constant values associated with each class have a Gaussian distribution. If the data distribution is non-normal, there may be better choices than this model.

Feature Selection: The features’ relevance significantly impacts the effectiveness of a Gaussian NB classifier. Irrelevant or partially relevant features might degrade model performance.

Gradient Boosting is a sophisticated machine learning technique well-known for its success in structured data issues and its ability to handle many predictor variables. When used to predict cancer, however, it confronts various obstacles and limitations:

Gradient Boosting Models (GBMs) feature various hyperparameters, such as learning rate, number of trees, depth of trees, and so on, that must be carefully tuned. Incorrect settings might result in overfitting (recording too much noise) or underfitting (catching insufficient patterns).

Overfitting: If a training dataset is not adequately regularized, GBMs can easily overfit it. Therefore, they may perform poorly on previously unknown data.

Cost of computation: GBMs often need more excellent processing resources. It constructs trees one at a time; thus, learning may take longer. When dealing with enormous datasets, this becomes an issue.

Outliers, missing values, and unbalanced datasets can all impact the performance of gradient-boosting models.

Gradient Boosting is a type of ensemble learning that combines numerous weak predicting models to create a more potent predictive model. However, the model’s intricacy comes at the expense of its interpretability. Understanding why a forecast was made can be challenging and complicated when obtaining business insights.

Sensitivity to Noisy Data: GBMs may be sensitive to noise in input data, which can harm the model’s performance.

Static ensemble

Blending represents a technique within ensemble machine learning frameworks that leverages a machine learning (ML) approach to ascertain the optimal manner of integrating predictions derived from multiple constituent ensemble member methods. This approach is also commonly referred to as stacked generalization or stacking, with these terms often being used interchangeably.

The architecture of a blending model (refer to Fig. 3) comprises two or more base methods, often denoted as level-zero models, alongside a meta-method responsible for amalgamating the predictions generated by the base models, termed as a level-one model. The meta-model undergoes training using the predictions generated by the base methods on out-of-sample data.

The blending process encompasses the following steps:

Division of the training data into two distinct sections: the training and validation sets.Independent training of the base models on the training set.Utilization of the base models to predict outcomes for both the validation set and new, unseen data instances.Fusion of these predictions through a predefined blending function or aggregation technique (such as weighted averaging or majority voting) to produce the final output.

Adjusting the algorithms’ hyperparameters

Contrary to Grid search, Random Search selects a predetermined number of parameter combinations from the given distribution, which increases system effectiveness by lowering the likelihood of spending a significant amount of time on a small, underperforming area. Search The computational complexity of Random Search is O(n)46 because the total number of evaluations in Random Search is fixed at n before the optimization process begins. When given adequate resources, Random Search can also find the global or nearly global optimal47. Random Search is more effective than Grid Search for vast search areas, but since it ignores the previously effective regions, there are still many pointless function evaluations48. As a result, both Random Search and Grid Search spend much time investigating portions of the search space, which could be better since each review in its iterations is independent of the prior evaluations. Other optimization techniques, such as Bayesian optimization, which bases the subsequent assessment on data from previous evaluations, may resolve this problem49. In this manuscript, a grid search algorithm has been used.

Evaluation metrics

To emphasize the performance evaluation of machine learning (ML) methods in cancer classification, metrics such as F1-Score and Recall were computed and presented within the Classification Report. Furthermore, visualization techniques such as the ROC curve, Confusion Matrix, Precision-Recall Curve, and Class Prediction Error were employed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the results.

The significance of various evaluation metrics and visualization methods in cancer classification is as follows:

Importance of AUC in cancer classification: The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is a crucial metric in evaluating the performance of classifiers. A perfect classifier achieves an AUC value of 1, while a random classifier attains an AUC value of 0.550,51. AUC provides a nuanced assessment of the algorithm’s effectiveness beyond accuracy alone, making it particularly valuable in cancer classification52,53.

Importance of Confusion Matrix in cancer classification: The Confusion Matrix is a vital tool, especially in scenarios where the costs associated with false positives and false negatives are substantial, such as in cancer detection. High rates of false negatives, where cancer cases are misdiagnosed as healthy, can lead to treatment delays and adverse patient outcomes. Conversely, elevated false positives, indicating healthy instances misdiagnosed as cancerous, may result in unnecessary treatments and patient distress. The Confusion Matrix offers insightful information regarding the model’s performance by delineating true positives (TP), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN)54.

Importance of Precision-Recall Curve in cancer classification: The Precision-Recall Curve provides valuable insights into the model’s ability to correctly classify cancer cases. It illustrates the trade-off between true positives, false positives, and false negatives, aiding in identifying areas for model improvement and error analysis55.

Importance of Classification Report in cancer classification: A Classification Report is an indispensable tool for evaluating the overall performance of a classification model in cancer prediction. It provides a detailed breakdown of key metrics such as true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, offering a comprehensive assessment of the model’s efficacy in binary or multiclass classification scenarios.

Logistic regression is a commonly used method for binary classification tasks, including cancer prediction. However, it presents several challenges and limitations:

Linearity Assumption: Logistic regression assumes a linear relationship between input characteristics and the log odds of the output. This assumption may not fully capture the complex relationships between certain characteristics and cancer risk, impacting the model’s predictive ability56.

Feature Selection Difficulty: Logistic regression does not inherently handle feature selection, requiring manual selection of features. This may lead to overlooking important features or including irrelevant ones, affecting model performance57.

Overfitting Risk: A high number of features in logistic regression models can lead to overfitting, where the model performs well on training data but poorly on unseen data. Overfitting can lead to inaccurate predictions in cancer prediction scenarios58.

Challenges with Regularization: Non-differentiable regularization terms can pose challenges in optimizing logistic regression models, particularly in cancer prediction tasks58.

Sensitivity to Outliers: Logistic regression models are sensitive to data outliers, which can significantly influence model predictions and lead to unreliable results56.

Conclusion

This study aimed to enhance the classification accuracy of Breast Cancer gene 1, Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma, Colorectal Adenocarcinoma, Lung Adenocarcinoma, and Prostate Adenocarcinoma cancers by analyzing DNA information from 390 patients. It employed Logistic Regression, Gradient Boosting, Gaussian Naïve Bayes, and a Blending algorithm combining Logistic Regression and Gaussian Naïve Bayes.

Improved Cancer Classification Performance: The Blending method, incorporating Logistic Regression and Gaussian Naïve Bayes, demonstrated superior accuracy in cancer classification, achieving up to 98% accuracy across the five cancer types. Particularly noteworthy was its outperformance of individual algorithms in accurately classifying Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD-4) and Prostate Adenocarcinoma (PRAD-5) with 98% accuracy.

The innovative blending approach exhibited substantial enhancements over individual algorithms utilized in prior research endeavors.

Considering the dataset’s limitation of 390 patients, future investigations should prioritize expanding the data size to bolster model robustness and enhance generalizability to diverse patient cohorts.

Logistic Regression and Gaussian Naïve Bayes algorithms are predicated on specific assumptions, such as linear associations and Gaussian distributions. Subsequent research endeavors could explore alternative machine learning models and feature engineering techniques to more effectively capture intricate, nonlinear interactions.

This conclusion underscores the importance and implications of advancing cancer detection methodologies by quantifying improvements and emphasizing key discoveries. The findings from this study offer valuable insights for future research trajectories and the development of more precise and dependable cancer diagnostic tools.
