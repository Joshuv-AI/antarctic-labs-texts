The association between naloxone distribution, buprenorphine treatment and retention and incident high‐risk opioid prescribing with opioid overdose death in Kentucky, Massachusetts, New York and Ohio, United States: An exploratory community‐level cohort study of data from the HEALing Communities Study
Authors: Alexander Y. Walley, Debbie M. Cheng, Nathan Vandergrift, Marc Larochelle, JaNae Holloway, Jennifer L. Brown, Redonna Chandler, Laura C. Fanucchi, Daniel J. Feaster, Patricia R. Freeman, Timothy Hunt, Michelle R. Lofwall, Hannah K. Knudsen, Edward V. Nunes, Emmanuel Oga, Douglas R. Oyler, Jeffrey H. Samet, Jennifer Villani, Sharon L. Walsh, T. John Winhusen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimWe evaluated whether community‐level naloxone distribution, medication for opioid use disorder treatment and retention and incident high‐risk opioid prescribing rates were associated with opioid overdose death rates.DesignObservational cohort conducted using 2019 to 2023 community‐level data as an exploratory analysis of the HEALing (Helping to End Addiction Long‐term®) Communities Study (HCS). Exposures included: (1) community‐level naloxone distribution, past 12‐months, categorized as ≤1000 units per 100 000 population vs. 1001–3000 units per 100 000 population vs. >3000 units per 100 000 population; (2) individuals treated with buprenorphine per 100 000 adult population in the current quarter; (3) individuals retained on buprenorphine for ≥ 180 days per 100 000 adult population in the current quarter; and (4) incident high‐risk opioid prescribing per 100 000 adult population in the current quarter.Setting and participantsPopulation‐based study of 67 communities with 8.2 million adults in Kentucky, Massachusetts, New York and Ohio, USA, with required annual opioid overdose death rates of > 25 per 100 000 adult population and at least 30% rural. Across the 67 communities participating in the HCS, the adult population was 31% 18–34 years, 31% 35–54 years, 38% 55 years and over, 52% female, 73% non‐Hispanic White, 15% non‐Hispanic Black and 7.4% Hispanic.MeasurementsQuarterly community‐level opioid overdose death rates from 2020 through 2023.FindingsThe 2019 annual rates were 40.4 opioid overdose deaths, 1287 naloxone rescue units distributed, 977.7 people received buprenorphine treatment, 546.3 people retained for more than 180 days on buprenorphine and 1266.7 high‐risk opioid prescribing incidents per 100 000 population. In models adjusted for state, community age, sex, race/ethnicity, rurality, HCS intervention group assignment, 2019 rates of opioid overdose death, naloxone distribution, buprenorphine and high‐risk opioid prescribing, and the ratio of opioid overdose deaths involving fentanyl, an increase in 100 people treated with buprenorphine per 100 000 population was associated with a decrease of 0.92 [95% confidence interval (CI) = −1.30 to −0.55] in the quarterly opioid overdose death rate, while an increase of 100 people retained on buprenorphine for more than 180 days per 100 000 population was associated with a decrease of 1.3 (95% CI = −1.8 to −0. 76). There were no statistically significant associations between naloxone distribution or incident high‐risk opioid prescribing with change in quarterly opioid overdose death rates.ConclusionsIn this exploratory analysis, increases in both buprenorphine treatment and retention were statistically significantly associated with decreases in opioid overdose death rates, after adjusting for baseline rates of buprenorphine treatment and retention.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, an estimated 61 million people used unregulated opioids in 2023 and 137 000 people died from overdose in 2021 [1, 2]. Overdose is the leading cause of preventable injury death in the United States (US), killing over 100 000 Americans per year in 2021 through 2023 [1, 2].

Evidence‐based practices (EBPs) to address opioid overdose include: (1) overdose education and naloxone distribution (OEND); (2) medication for opioid use disorder (MOUD); and (3) prescription opioid safety. The existing evidence for reducing fatal opioid overdose by each of these EBPs varies by individual‐ vs. community‐level studies. For OEND, there are no placebos or treatment‐controlled randomized trials as it would be unethical to restrict access to the antidote to an opioid overdose. Yet several systematic reviews based on quasi‐experimental studies demonstrate that opioid‐involved overdose death rates and emergency department visits decrease in communities and among individuals accessing OEND compared to those that do not [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. For methadone and buprenorphine, a 2020 meta‐analysis involving 3852 randomized controlled trial (RCT) participants and 749 634 cohort participants found that the relative risk of drug‐related death while retained on buprenorphine or methadone treatment was 0.41 (95% CI = 0.33–0.52) compared to those not treated with methadone or buprenorphine [10]. This association was similar for methadone and buprenorphine, across age, sex, geography and co‐morbidities. Although fewer data are available, the mortality benefit of methadone and buprenorphine appears to persist in the era of widespread fentanyl adulteration in the opioid supply [11]. Similar mortality reductions have not been shown with naltrexone [12]. For prescription opioid safety practices, one national interrupted time series study, which examined the association of state‐level safer opioid prescribing policies in 2014 with overdose deaths through 2016, found that mandated prescriber education and maximum opioid dose threshold limitations were associated with lower state overdose death rates [13]. Although these associations of OEND, MOUD and safer opioid prescribing with decreasing opioid overdose deaths (OODs) have been evaluated in quasi‐experimental studies, longitudinal cohorts and meta‐analyses, few of these studies have been conducted at a time when fentanyl was the dominant cause of overdose death, and none have evaluated each of these concurrently within the same population.

To reduce community level opioid overdose, the National Institutes of Health and Substance Abuse Mental Health Services Administration sponsored the Helping to End Addiction Long‐term (HEALing) Communities Study (HCS), a large, multi‐state, randomized wait‐list control implementation science trial designed to reduce OODs at the community level by increasing access to EBPs (i.e. OEND, MOUD and prescription opioid safety) and reducing stigma through communication campaigns [14, 15, 16]. The HCS focused on community‐level implementation of OEND, MOUD and prescription opioid safety as three EBPs to reduce OODs [12]. The HCS intervention was implemented across 67 communities in two waves (34 communities during January 2020–June 2022, and 33 communities during July 2022–December 2023). Main analyses demonstrated that the HCS intervention statistically significantly increased naloxone distribution by 79% at the community‐level [3378 vs. 1884 naloxone units distributed per 100 000 residents (adjusted relative rate [aRR] = 1.79; 95% CI = 1.28–2.51)] [17], but did not significantly increase MOUD treatment [578 vs. 596 people treated per 1000 Medicaid enrollees (aRR = 0.97; 95% CI = 0.93–1.01)], retention (continuous receipt for 180 days) [614 vs. 620 people retained per 1000 Medicaid enrolees (aRR = 0.99; 95% CI = 0.95–1.04)] or linkage within 31 days following an opioid‐related emergency department or hospital encounter [280 vs. 252 people linked per 1000 encounters (aRR = 1.11; 95% CI = 0.96–1.28)] [18], nor decrease incident high‐risk opioid prescribing [1094 vs. 1122 incident encounters per 100 000 population (aRR = 0.98; 95% CI = 0.93–1.02)] [19]. A statistically significant effect on OODs was not found [47.2 vs. 51.7 per 100 000 population (aRR = 0.91; 95% CI = 0.76–1.09)] [14].

The underlying conceptualization of the HCS design was that if communities increased OEND and MOUD access and retention, and reduced high‐risk opioid prescribing, then OODs would decrease. The data collected from the 67 HCS communities from 2019 through 2023 provide the opportunity to analyze the association between each of the EBPs and OODs, independent of the impact of the HCS intervention. Unlike the analyses of the RCT, which focused on the exposure to the intervention among the intervention communities compared to the control communities during a 1‐year period [14], here, we focused on the changing community rates of the EBPs and their associations with overdose deaths across the 5 years of data collected. In this current analysis, we determined whether community‐level naloxone distribution, buprenorphine treatment and retention as a proxy measure for MOUD and incident high‐risk opioid prescribing rates were associated with OOD rates.

METHODS

This is an exploratory analysis of community‐level data collected as part of the HCS that was conducted from 2019 to 2023. There were four research sites in the HCS: Kentucky (KY), Massachusetts (MA), New York (NY) and Ohio (OH). Each site recruited communities from their state to participate in the HCS for a total of N = 67 communities coming from KY (n = 16), MA (n = 16), NY (n = 16) and OH (n = 19). Among the 67 communities, 29 were rural and 38 were urban. To qualify to participate in the study, communities had to be both highly impacted by opioid‐related overdose deaths with an overdose death rate of at least 25 per 100 000 people, based on 2016 data and willing to partner to expand access to MOUD and OEND. The communities together from within each participating state had to record at least 150 opioid‐related overdose deaths (i.e. >600 total with rural communities accounting for 15% of deaths) in 2016 and at least 30% of the communities had to be classified as rural. [15].

Primary outcome and data sources

The primary outcome for this analysis and for the HCS RCT was the quarterly rate of OODs per 100 000 population among community residents 18 years or older, as determined from death certificates with drug overdose as an underlying cause of death [International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes X40–X44, X60–X64, X85 and Y10–Y14) because of opioids (contributing cause of death, ICD‐10 codes, T40.0–T40.4 and T40.6) measured in each observation quarter calendar year for each community from 2020 to 2023. These certificates are the official and finalized state‐wide records of death. For Massachusetts, Ohio and New York, the completeness of overdose death records that indicated a specific drug is consistently greater than 95% [2]. In Kentucky, where completeness was 93% or greater, additional death investigation records determined opioid involvement when specific drugs were absent from death certificates [20]. Deaths were attributed to individual communities based on the decedent's address on the death certificate. The outcome, exposures and time‐varying covariates were measured at the community‐quarter level, where 3‐month quarters were coded starting at zero for the quarter January through March 2020 then sequentially ending at 15 for the quarter October through December 2023 (see Supplement Table S1 for further details).

Exposures and data sources

There were four exposures of interest. Total naloxone distribution was measured by the sum of the number of naloxone units distributed through community programs with support by state and federal funding (including HCS funding) and the number of naloxone units dispensed by retail pharmacies located within the communities, as previously described [20]. Briefly, data were from the IQVIA Xponent database for retail pharmacy dispensing and from administrative records from state agencies and HCS study records for community program distribution. One unit of naloxone equaled one carton or kit, which included two doses. The OEND exposure variable was defined as the rate of naloxone units distributed in the community via pharmacies and community agencies per 100 000 community residents in the 12 months preceding the quarter in which the outcome was measured. The naloxone measures were lagged across the 12 months preceding the current quarter to reflect that naloxone is typically carried by people likely to respond to an overdose and may be administered at a later time, consistent with the public health goal of increasing circulating supply of naloxone in communities. Prior community level studies have suggested potential non‐linear and threshold relationships between naloxone distribution and fatal overdose rates [6, 9]. In our data, exploratory analyses of naloxone distribution rates across community‐quarters from 2019 through 2023 also demonstrated departures from linearity. Although continuous modeling approaches (e.g. transformations or flexible functional forms) can be used to characterize such relationships, we categorized naloxone distribution rates into tertiles to facilitate interpretation of potential threshold effects. The tertile cut points, calculated by pooling data across all time points, were defined as: level 1: ≤1000 units per 100 000 population in the 12 months before the observation quarter; level 2: 1001 to 3000 units per 100 000 population in the 12 months before the observation quarter; level 3: >3000 units per 100 000 population in the 12 months before the observation quarter. The OEND variable was modeled as a time‐varying, three‐category, class variable.

The MOUD exposure variables focused on buprenorphine treatment and retention rates because the HCS outpatient exposure to prescribed buprenorphine data were complete in each state's prescription drug monitoring program (PDMP) [20]. Dispensed buprenorphine was identified using national drug codes for transmucosal buprenorphine, buprenorphine/naloxone and injectable buprenorphine formulations US Food and Drug Administration‐approved for opioid use disorder (OUD). There were two buprenorphine main independent variables. The first was the rate of individuals who received buprenorphine for the treatment of OUD per 100 000 residents 18 years or older at the community level in the quarter the outcome was measured. The second was the rate of individuals retained in buprenorphine treatment per 100 000 residents 18 years or older at the community level in the quarter the outcome was measured. Retained was defined as documented receipt of buprenorphine continuously for at least 180 days in the PDMP, allowing for gaps in prescription coverage of no more than 7 days. This definition was based on the National Quality Forum OUD retention metrics [21].

The safer opioid prescribing exposure variable was the rate of incident high‐risk opioid prescribing per 100 000 residents 18 years or older at the community level in the quarter the outcome was measured. Incident high‐risk opioid prescribing included a new opioid episode lasting at least 31 days, initiating opioid treatment with extended‐release or long‐acting opioid, incident high dosage (average ≥90 mg morphine equivalent per day) or incident overlapping opioid and benzodiazepine for at least 31 days [20]. Similar to the MOUD‐buprenorphine exposures, the safer opioid prescribing exposure variable was collected from each state's PDMP, which also requires that all opioid prescriptions for pain and benzodiazepines are entered into their respective PDMPs (see Supplement Table S2 for further details).

Exposures for pre‐specified sensitivity analyses

We pre‐specified three additional exposure variables: (1) a continuous OEND exposure; (2) MOUD treatment; and (3) retention exposures that were defined by methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone sourced from each states' Medicaid database rather than buprenorphine from the PDMP. The Medicaid MOUD data were available from the first quarter of 2019 through the second quarter of 2022 for Ohio, the fourth quarter of 2022 for Massachusetts, the third quarter of 2023 for New York and the fourth quarter of 2023 for Kentucky (see Supplement Table S3 for further details).

Covariates

The following covariates were collected at the community level: (1) state (Kentucky, Massachusetts, New York, Ohio); (2) rural/urban status; (3) age distribution (ages 18–34 years, 35–54 years, 55 years and greater); (4) sex distribution (male, female); (5) race/ethnicity distribution (Hispanic, Black non‐Hispanic, White non‐Hispanic and other non‐Hispanic); (6) 2019 (baseline) community OOD rate per 100 000 adult population; and (7) time coded starting at zero in Q1 2020 then sequentially ending at 15 for Q4 2023 to control for secular trends. For models that included only the OEND exposure variable, time was coded starting at zero in Q1 2021 then sequentially ending at 11 for Q4 2023 because of the 1‐year lag in the variable. Population age, sex and race/ethnicity demographics were all collected from the 2017 to 2021 American Community Survey 5‐Year data. Estimates for communities representing units smaller than counties (n = 19) [22] and from the 2020 Bridged‐Race Population Estimates for communities representing counties (n = 48) in the same manner [23]. The 2019 community overdose death rates were collected from the respective state death certificate databases in the same manner as the outcome. We also generated baseline 2019 calendar community rates for each of the independent variables that indicated the community rates for each exposure at the beginning of the analytic window. To adjust the analyses for the penetrance of fentanyl into the unregulated drug supply, we used toxicology information from the death certificates to calculate a proportion of opioid‐related deaths where synthetic opioids, excluding methadone, were present for each community in each quarter as a time‐varying covariate. Fentanyl is the main synthetic opioid present in decedent toxicology in all four states, and therefore, we refer to this variable as fentanyl. Randomization assignment (HCS intervention vs. control) was also included as a covariate. There were no major changes in the composition of these communities and the mechanisms of reporting the outcomes of interest did not change across the study period.

Statistical analysis

We performed a series of analyses using repeated measures linear mixed‐effects models to account for the longitudinal structure of the data. All four of the main exposures were modeled as time‐varying independent variables. We had a sample size of 67 communities, each contributing longitudinal data recorded over 16 quarters, which provided 1072 observations. The study aimed to evaluate the main effects of the four exposure variables, and the interactions were not tested. The estimated association between the four independent variables (i.e. naloxone distribution, buprenorphine treatment, buprenorphine retention and incident high‐risk opioid prescribing) and the outcome rate of opioid overdose deaths were all assessed at the community level (see Supplement Tables S4 and S5 for further details). The only missing data were the OEND variable for one community in 2022 and two communities in 2023. To handle these missing data, we used linear mixed models where there is a full information maximum likelihood (FIML) approach to missing data. We adjusted models for the following time‐invariant, potential confounding variables: state, community age, sex and race/ethnicity distributions, rurality, intervention group or control assignment in the HCS trial and the 2019 OOD rate. For descriptive purposes, we calculated 2019 annual rates per 100 000 adult population for each of the four exposure variables, the outcome and the time‐varying covariate (% fentanyl OOD) stratified by each of the time‐invariant potentially confounding variables (Table 1). All models were fitted with random effects for the intercept and temporal slope with an unstructured covariance matrix using restricted maximum likelihood estimation.

The primary analysis was based on two models including the main exposure variables: (1) buprenorphine treatment, total naloxone distribution and high‐risk opioid prescribing; and (2) buprenorphine retention, total naloxone distribution and high‐risk opioid prescribing. Two separate primary models were required because the MOUD‐focused independent variables, buprenorphine treatment and buprenorphine retention were highly correlated. In addition to these exposure variables, the primary analysis adjusted for the seven covariates listed above, the ratio between OODs involving fentanyl to total OODs and the 2019 values for each independent variable in the model. These adjustments were intended to address confounding arising from differing baseline levels of each exposure variable [24]. Preliminary analyses first evaluated each exposure variable separately. Ancillary analyses were also conducted using reduced sets of covariates to assess potential confounding by different factors. Specifically, the reduced models in ancillary analyses included: (1) only the seven covariates listed above; and (2) the seven covariates listed above plus the fentanyl ratio (see Table S7 for full details).

Model fit was assessed using residual plots and multi‐collinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Covariates with VIFs greater than 10 were sequentially removed. β estimates for the independent variables remained stable following these exclusions, with the exception of the 2019 baseline rates for the MOUD‐related variables (i.e. buprenorphine treatment and buprenorphine retention).

Ancillary analyses used community‐mean centered values (i.e. we subtracted each community's mean value across time from their observed values at each time point, for all time‐varying continuous variables). This approach aimed to confirm results and reduce multi‐collinearity. The resulting regression coefficients for centered variables reflected within‐community association for each independent variable and OOD rate. Results from the primary models using centered independent variables were expected to be consistent with those from models using uncentered variables.

We conducted three additional, pre‐specified sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of our model results. The first replaced the categorized OEND variable with a continuous OEND variable in the fully adjusted model. The second and third substituted the PDMP‐derived buprenorphine and retention rates with the Medicaid‐sourced rate of MOUD treatment and retention in fully adjusted models. All analyses used two‐tailed tests with a significance level of 0.05 and 95% CIs. No adjustment was made for multiple testing. All analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.4 [25].

RESULTS

In 2019, there were 3317 OODs among 8.2 million adults residing in the 67 communities participating in the HCS for an annual rate of 40.4 per 100 000 adult population. The community adult population was 31% 18 to 34 years, 31% 35 to 54 years, 38% 55 years and over, 52% female, 73% non‐Hispanic White, 15% non‐Hispanic Black and 7.4% Hispanic. The naloxone distribution rate was 1286.6 naloxone rescue units per 100 000 total population. Per 100 000 adult population, 977.7 people received buprenorphine treatment, 546.3 people received more than 180 days of buprenorphine and there were 1266.7 high‐risk opioid prescribing incidents. The percentage of OODs that involved fentanyl was 88.7% (Table 1). The average annual OOD rate for the 67 communities in the years 2020 to 2023 increased to 50.4 per 100 000 population (see Supplement Table S6 for further details).

In the two fully adjusted models, one with buprenorphine treatment rate and the other with buprenorphine retention rate, which both included fentanyl decedent toxicology, baseline EBP exposures and the naloxone distribution and incident high risk prescribing exposures, increases in both buprenorphine treatment rate and buprenorphine retention rates were statistically significant in their association with decreases in the community OOD rates (Figure 1). An increase of 100 people treated with buprenorphine per 100 000 adult population in a community was associated with a decrease of 0.92 (95% CI = −1.3 to −0.55) in the quarterly OOD rate. An increase of 100 people retained on buprenorphine for more than 180 days per 100 000 adult population in a community was associated with a decrease of 1.3 (95% CI = −1.8 to −0.076) in the quarterly OOD rate. The associations between the naloxone distribution rate categories, incident high‐risk opioid prescribing rate and change in quarterly OOD rates were not statistically significant.

Adjustment by baseline buprenorphine treatment and retention exposure affected the magnitude and direction of some relationships (Table 2). In the sets of models that were not adjusted for baseline exposure, buprenorphine treatment and retention rates were positively associated with increases in OOD rates. After including the respective baseline buprenorphine treatment or retention exposure, the associations became negative, consistent with our hypotheses. Confirmatory analyses using mean‐centered variables similarly showed significant negative within‐community associations for both between buprenorphine treatment and retention with OOD rate (Tables S9 and S10).

DISCUSSION

In this exploratory study of the 67 HCS communities from 2019 to 2023, increases in buprenorphine treatment rates and retention were significantly associated with decreases in OOD rates. Our results suggest that doubling the rate during the study from 1000 to 2000 per 100 000 population treated on buprenorphine would be associated with 9.2 fewer deaths per 100 000 population per quarter, while doubling the rate during the study from 500 to 1000 per 100 000 population retained on buprenorphine would be associated with 6.4 fewer deaths per 100 000 population per quarter. Given that the average annual community OOD rate was 40.4 per 100 000 population in 2019 and increased during 2020 to 2023 to 50.4 per 100 000 population, these magnitudes of associations were substantial. Our findings of community‐level associations of buprenorphine treatment and retention with fatal overdose rates are consistent with meta‐analyses of individual cohorts and RCTs that have found that people with OUD treated with and retained on buprenorphine or methadone reduce their risk of fatal overdose in half [10]. In the United States, in 2021, an estimated 542 people per 100 000 adult population were treated with buprenorphine for OUD [26], which is approximately half the rate that existed in the HCS communities, although these were communities specifically selected for their high burden of OODs. Although it is encouraging that higher community levels of buprenorphine treatment and retention were associated with fewer OODs, finding effective ways to increase MOUD treatment and retention remains a significant challenge. Expanding MOUD treatment and retention warrants further implementation research, especially because interventions like the HCS intervention did not succeed in increasing MOUD [18] and buprenorphine prescription rates nationwide have been flat throughout the 2020s [26].

Neither naloxone distribution nor incident high‐risk prescribing was significantly associated with OOD rates. For incident high‐risk prescribing, unregulated fentanyl was the primary driver of OODs in all four states by 2020, and therefore, the mechanistic connection with fatal overdose may not have been as strong as in the past on OOD. For naloxone distribution, the effect estimate was in the hypothesized direction, but not statistically significant. Previous analyses of naloxone distribution and OOD have found community‐level associations [5, 6, 9]. It is possible that naloxone distribution in the HCS communities had achieved a threshold beyond which further naloxone distribution, in the manner that it was distributed in the HCS communities, was not going to make substantial difference in OODs. The 2019 baseline community naloxone distribution rate was 1286 units per 100 000 people, which is several‐fold greater than the top distribution categories analyzed in the 2014 Massachusetts [6] and 2016 Scottish [5] studies. In a 2025 eastern Missouri study, which included data through 2022 when fentanyl was present, associations with reductions in opioid overdose were substantial in St. Louis when naloxone distribution was initially ramping up [9]. The magnitude of the association diminished when naloxone kits per 100 000 people substantially surpassed rates assessed in the Massachusetts and Scottish studies. Another limitation on naloxone's potential impact is that someone must be present and ready to administer it at the time of the overdose, which with fentanyl, occurs within minutes of use. In each of the four states in 2023, only 10% or fewer overdose deaths occurred among people who were witnessed at the time of their drug use [27]. Although there were potential bystanders present nearby, ranging from 30% in Ohio to 50% in Kentucky [27], these potential bystanders were usually spatially separated from the person overdosing (i.e. in another room in the house), not aware that an overdose was happening, and therefore, not able to respond.

Notably, although the HCS intervention did not improve MOUD or high‐risk opioid prescribing rates [18, 19], it did increase naloxone distribution at the community‐level by 79% [17] and the overdose death rates observed in the intervention communities were 9% less than the control communities, which was not a statistically significant difference [14]. Therefore, although increasing community naloxone distribution is a key strategy to preventing fatal overdoses, to reduce overdoses more than 10%, we need to substantially increase the number of people receiving and remaining on buprenorphine and other MOUD. This need for MOUD expansion was predicted in an HCS modeling analysis completed at the beginning of the study [28]. Additional strategies, such as overdose prevention sites [29] and virtual overdose detection technologies [30], which prevent overdoses occurring in the setting of unmonitored drug use, are likely needed for people with OUDs and people using other drugs contaminated with fentanyl. Furthermore, there are demographic groups, such as Black, Native American and youth populations where there are addressable inequities in both naloxone and MOUD access that drive disparities in overdose deaths that would benefit from tailored efforts [31, 32, 33].

For incident high‐risk opioid prescribing, the apparent lack of association is most likely because of the shift from prescription opioids to heroin, and more recently fentanyl, driving OODs before 2019 [31]. In Massachusetts, fewer than 20% of OODs have involved prescription opioids since 2015 [34]. Prescription drug monitoring requirements and opioid prescribing guidance has reduced the contributions of prescription opioids to overdose deaths. Although discontinuation of chronic opioids, initially thought to be beneficial, has been associated with increases in overdose and suicide, especially when done abruptly [35, 36, 37].

Our analyses demonstrated that it was important to adjust for baseline OOD rates and baseline MOUD when analyzing community level associations between MOUD and OODs [24]. Where we did not adjust for baseline buprenorphine treatment or retention rates, the models demonstrated a statistically significant association in the opposite direction from the association we hypothesized. HCS community inclusion criteria required that enrolled communities had a baseline OOD rate of at least 25 per 100 000 population per year [15]. Beyond this minimum, there was substantial variation among communities in overdose death rates, as well as variation in baseline levels of MOUD (Supplementary Figures). Those communities with higher overdose rates already had more people treated with MOUD. Without adjusting for baseline MOUD, communities with more MOUD exposure during the intervention period would appear to have higher OOD rates.

Limitations

These analyses have potential limitations. First, for naloxone distribution, we created a three tertile‐category primary exposure variable to reflect observed non‐linear patterns in the data and prior community‐level studies suggesting potential threshold effects [5, 6, 9]. Although this approach improves interpretability, categorization of a continuous exposure may result in loss of information and reduced ability to detect more complex, non‐linear relationships. A pre‐specified sensitivity analysis that modeled naloxone distribution as a continuous variable was not associated with the community OOD rate. However, this approach may not have fully captured non‐linear relationships that may exist between naloxone distribution and overdose outcomes. Future studies are warranted to explore in more detail naloxone distribution population rate threshold effects and how they operate. Second, to represent MOUD as an EBP, we chose to use buprenorphine treatment and retention rates sourced from the state PDMPs, because these data were available and complete. However, this measure excludes patients treated with methadone and naltrexone, which means that many of the people receiving MOUD and its overdose prevention benefits were excluded, therefore, potentially diluting the association of these patients. The Medicaid‐sourced MOUD measure was inclusive of methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone, but restricted to people insured by Medicaid, which also dilutes the full effect of MOUD. Third, mortality rates in small communities are subject to much larger changes for each additional death than in larger communities. Fourth, the study data were from US communities heavily impacted by the opioid overdose epidemic that participated in a study designed to expand OEND, MOUD access and safer prescribing, therefore, the extent to which the present results will generalize to other communities is unknown.

CONCLUSION

In this exploratory analysis of the HCS, both increased buprenorphine treatment and buprenorphine retention were associated with decreases in OOD rates, after adjusting for community characteristics including the baseline rates of buprenorphine treatment and retention. These findings are consistent with meta‐analyses of individual cohorts and RCTs that have found that people with OUD treated with buprenorphine or methadone reduce their risk of fatal overdose by half [10]. We did not detect statistically significant associations for community naloxone distribution or incident high‐risk opioid prescribing alone with OOD rates. In communities with high OOD rates, substantially increasing MOUD treatment and retention may reduce overdose deaths. Given the challenges faced by previous community‐wide implementation efforts, such as the HCS, and the results of this analysis highlighting the mortality reduction potential of MOUD, further implementation research is warranted to increase the number of people initiated and retained on MOUD.
