Meropenem‐Induced Possible Mixed Drug‐Induced Liver Injury With Cholestatic Evolution: A Case Report Evaluated Using the Updated RUCAM (2016)
Authors: Nidal Mutawodeh, Sarah Saife, Akram Shalaby, Kamaleldein Einar, Motasem Kamal, Malik Hijaz, Tariq Hamadneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDrug‐induced liver injury (DILI) remains a diagnosis of exclusion and requires systematic causality assessment. Meropenem is generally considered hepatically safe; however, rare cases of idiosyncratic DILI have been reported. We describe a 33‐year‐old female who developed progressive elevation of liver tests following exposure to meropenem. Based on R‐value calculation, the liver injury pattern was mixed at presentation and peak, with transient hepatocellular features followed by evolution toward a cholestatic pattern during recovery. Extensive imaging, endoscopic evaluation, and serologic testing excluded biliary obstruction, viral hepatitis, and autoimmune disease. Causality assessment using the updated Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM, 2016) yielded a score of 4, indicating possible DILI. This case expands the demographic spectrum of reported meropenem‐associated DILI and underscores the importance of early R‐value calculation, formal RUCAM‐based causality assessment, and prompt withdrawal of the suspected agent.Keywords: case report, drug‐induced liver injury, meropenem, mixed liver injury, RUCAM (2016), updated RUCAM

Introduction

Drug‐induced liver injury (DILI) is a leading cause of acute liver dysfunction and represents a major diagnostic challenge in clinical practice, as it lacks specific biomarkers and is largely a diagnosis of exclusion. Its clinical presentation is highly variable, ranging from asymptomatic elevation of liver tests to acute liver failure. Accurate identification of DILI requires careful temporal correlation with drug exposure, systematic exclusion of alternative etiologies, and structured causality assessment using validated tools [1].

Meropenem is a broad‐spectrum carbapenem antibiotic widely used for the treatment of severe and complicated infections, particularly in hospitalized and critically ill patients. It is generally considered to have a favorable hepatic safety profile, with transient and mild elevations in liver enzymes reported in a small proportion of patients. Clinically significant hepatotoxicity attributed to meropenem is rare, and only a limited number of case reports describing meropenem‐associated DILI have been published to date [2].

The biochemical patterns of DILI are classically categorized as hepatocellular, cholestatic, or mixed based on the R‐value, which reflects the relative elevation of alanine aminotransferase and alkaline phosphatase. Notably, several previously reported cases of meropenem‐associated liver injury have been labeled as “cholestatic,” although closer examination of laboratory values suggests that mixed injury patterns are frequent. Accurate classification is essential, as it influences diagnostic reasoning, causality assessment, and comparison across reports [3].

Given the diagnostic complexity of DILI, structured causality assessment is strongly recommended. The updated Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM, 2016) remains the most widely validated and internationally accepted tool for this purpose, allowing standardized evaluation of temporal relationships, exclusion of competing causes, and response to drug withdrawal [4].

Here, we report a case of meropenem‐associated drug‐induced liver injury in a young patient, characterized by a mixed biochemical pattern with transient hepatocellular features followed by evolution toward a cholestatic pattern during recovery, assessed using the updated RUCAM (2016). This case adds to the limited literature on meropenem‐induced DILI and is unique in incorporating histopathologic evaluation, enabling distinction between pre‐existing chronic liver disease and superimposed acute drug‐induced injury.

Case Presentation

A 33‐year‐old female presented to the emergency department with a 10‐day history of epigastric pain. The pain was dull in character, intermittent but persistent, rated as 8/10 in severity, non‐radiating, and associated with nausea, multiple episodes of vomiting, and documented fever reaching up to 40°C (oral). She denied changes in stool color, urine color, bowel habits, or weight.

Her past medical history was unremarkable. She had undergone cholecystectomy 12 years earlier for biliary pancreatitis. She denied alcohol consumption, smoking, use of herbal products, or intake of over‐the‐counter medications. There was no family history of chronic liver or hepatobiliary disease. Review of systems was negative for fatigue, pruritus, skin discoloration, arthralgia, or symptoms suggestive of autoimmune disease. There were no clinical signs of hepatic encephalopathy, including altered mental status or asterixis. Body mass index (BMI) and metabolic profile data were not available.

Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated elevated liver tests, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT), and both total and direct bilirubin, while the white blood cell count was within normal limits. The calculated R‐values were 2.15 at presentation, 4.24 at peak, 5.78 at discharge, and 1.20 during outpatient follow‐up, demonstrating a dynamic pattern with mixed injury initially, transient hepatocellular features, and eventual cholestatic evolution during recovery.

Right upper quadrant ultrasonography revealed mild intrahepatic biliary ductal dilatation, predominantly involving the left hepatic ducts, with absence of the gallbladder consistent with prior cholecystectomy. Based on the presence of fever, abdominal pain, cholestatic biochemical abnormalities, and biliary ductal dilatation, acute cholangitis was considered among the differential diagnoses, and the patient was admitted for inpatient management. Empiric intravenous antibiotics were initiated, including meropenem, along with supportive care.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed and demonstrated normal‐appearing duodenal mucosa and ampulla, with a mildly dilated common bile duct (CBD) and no filling defects. Endoscopic sphincterotomy and balloon sweeping were performed, during which purulent discharge was observed, leading to the impression of a possibly passed stone. The patient's symptoms partially improved over the following 2 days but subsequently worsened, with progressive jaundice becoming clinically evident.

During hospitalization, the patient received several medications, including pantoprazole, paracetamol, meropenem, ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, vitamin K, and potassium chloride. Meropenem was administered for a total duration of 9 days at a dose of 1 g every 8 h (total cumulative dose of 27 g), initiated for suspected acute cholangitis.

Due to ongoing clinical deterioration, a second ERCP was performed, which demonstrated an edematous ampulla at the site of prior sphincterotomy, a mildly dilated CBD without filling defects, and a normal intrahepatic biliary tree. Balloon sweeping was repeated, and a 10‐French plastic stent was placed in the CBD extending into the right hepatic duct.

Despite these interventions, liver tests continued to worsen, prompting gastroenterology consultation and multidisciplinary evaluation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the liver with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed a congested liver with heterogeneous early arterial enhancement and periportal edema. The portal and hepatic veins were patent without filling defects. The CBD measured approximately 0.5 cm, which was within normal limits for this patient.

An extensive serologic evaluation was performed. Hepatitis A, B, and C serologies were negative. Autoimmune markers, including anti‐mitochondrial antibodies (AMA) and anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), were negative. Tumor markers, including carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA 19–9), alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), were within normal limits.

Given the rapid progression of liver test abnormalities and absence of an identifiable obstructive or infectious etiology, seronegative autoimmune hepatitis was considered in the differential diagnosis, and a percutaneous liver biopsy was performed. Histopathologic examination demonstrated lobular inflammation (score 2), focal ballooning degeneration (score 1), portal lymphocyte‐predominant inflammation with ductular reaction, and moderate centrilobular cholestasis (Figure 1). Approximately 40% macrovesicular steatosis was present. Masson trichrome staining revealed bridging fibrosis consistent with stage 3 fibrosis (Figure 2). Iron staining was negative. The NAFLD activity score (NAS) was 5.

Following multidisciplinary review, drug‐induced liver injury was considered the most plausible explanation for the patient's clinical course. Causality assessment using the updated Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM, 2016) yielded a score of 4 (Table 2), consistent with a possible drug‐induced liver injury. Meropenem was identified as the most likely offending agent based on temporal association and exclusion of alternative causes. Meropenem was discontinued, and hydrocortisone was initiated for a less likely diagnosis of occult autoimmune hepatitis.

She was discharged in stable condition and followed in the outpatient clinic, where continued biochemical and clinical improvement was observed.

Discussion

Drug‐induced liver injury (DILI) remains a challenging diagnosis due to its heterogeneous clinical presentation and the absence of specific diagnostic biomarkers. Accurate diagnosis relies on careful temporal correlation with drug exposure, systematic exclusion of alternative etiologies, and structured causality assessment using validated tools.

The clinical picture of DILI varies according to the drug. Some studies use the term ‘signature’ to refer to the constellation of the clinical symptoms, pathological pattern, and a latency period. The most common signatures are acute hepatitis, acute cholestasis, or a mixed presentation. Other signatures include nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, fibrosis, microvesicular steatosis, and veno‐occlusive disease [5].

This case demonstrates a dynamic biochemical pattern, with mixed injury at presentation and peak, transient hepatocellular features, and subsequent cholestatic evolution during recovery. Formal causality assessment using the updated RUCAM (2016) yielded a possible association [4]. Importantly, this report uniquely incorporates liver biopsy, enabling distinction between background chronic liver disease and acute DILI. Prospective application of structured causality tools may strengthen future reports [6].

Based on R‐value calculation, the pattern of liver injury in this patient was mixed at presentation and at peak liver test elevation with transient hepatocellular features and subsequent cholestatic evolution during recovery. This dynamic pattern is consistent with current guideline‐based classification of DILI and underscores the importance of calculating the R‐value at multiple time points rather than relying on static labels. Although several previously published reports describe meropenem‐associated liver injury as “cholestatic,” closer examination of reported laboratory values suggests that mixed phenotypes are frequent. Accurate classification is essential, as it informs diagnostic reasoning, facilitates comparison across cases, and avoids misleading conclusions [7].

Causality assessment using the updated Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM, 2016) yielded a total score of 4, corresponding to a possible association [4] This relatively low causality grading reflects the retrospective nature of the assessment and the presence of important confounders at presentation, including suspected biliary pathology and acute illness. Alternative explanations such as a transiently passed biliary stone, ischemic cholangiopathy, or infection‐related cholestasis cannot be entirely excluded. Nevertheless, the consistent temporal relationship between meropenem exposure and worsening liver tests, followed by gradual biochemical improvement after drug withdrawal, supports a contributory role for meropenem. This observation mirrors prior case reports in which discontinuation of meropenem was followed by biochemical recovery, often within weeks.

Histopathologic evaluation provided additional insight into the complexity of this case. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis with bridging fibrosis (stage 3), findings that are unlikely to result from an acute drug‐induced process alone. These features most likely reflect pre‐existing chronic liver disease predating the acute presentation, while the observed cholestatic and inflammatory changes were attributed to a superimposed acute injury. Importantly, inclusion of histopathology allowed differentiation between background liver disease and acute DILI, a distinction that has not been available in previously reported cases of meropenem‐associated liver injury.

When compared with published cases, several distinguishing features emerge. Most reported patients were elderly and had significant comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, or chronic infection. In contrast, our patient was relatively young and without known chronic medical conditions, thereby expanding the demographic spectrum of meropenem‐associated DILI. Additionally, this is, to our knowledge, the only reported case incorporating liver biopsy findings, providing objective histologic correlation. Despite differences in patient characteristics, a common feature across reports including the present case is biochemical improvement following discontinuation of meropenem, supporting a reproducible clinical pattern (Table 3).

The mechanism underlying meropenem‐associated DILI remains incompletely understood. The injury is presumed to be idiosyncratic, potentially mediated by immune mechanisms rather than dose‐dependent toxicity [13]. The absence of eosinophilia, rash, or systemic hypersensitivity features in this case does not exclude an immune‐mediated process, as such manifestations are inconsistently present in idiosyncratic DILI [14]. Another less probable theory is beta‐lactam cross reactivity evidenced by the shared beta‐lactam ring between the drugs of this class of antibiotics. β‐lactam cross‐reactivity is typically mediated by shared R1 and R2 side chains, mainly between penicillins and cephalosporins [10, 15]. However, this theory is unlikely in our case, as the patient received only meropenem without prior or concurrent β‐lactam exposure.

This case has several limitations. The causality grading was limited to possible by RUCAM criteria, and rechallenge was neither ethical nor performed. In addition, the presence of pre‐existing liver disease and initial biliary suspicion introduce unavoidable confounders. However, these limitations also reflect real‐world diagnostic complexity and emphasize the importance of structured, transparent assessment rather than categorical attribution.

In summary, this case reinforces several clinically relevant points: meropenem, although generally considered hepatically safe, can rarely be associated with clinically significant DILI; mixed biochemical patterns are common and may evolve over time; and structured causality assessment using the updated RUCAM (2016) is essential for accurate diagnosis and reporting. Prospective application of causality tools and careful phenotypic classification will strengthen future reports and improve understanding of this rare adverse effect.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates a rare presentation of meropenem‐associated drug‐induced liver injury with a mixed biochemical phenotype with transient hepatocellular features followed by evolution toward a cholestatic pattern during recovery. Formal causality assessment using the updated RUCAM (2016) yielded a possible association. Importantly, this report uniquely incorporates liver biopsy, enabling distinction between background chronic liver disease and acute DILI. Prospective application of structured causality tools may strengthen future reports.
