Ultrasound-assisted processing improves germination and mashing efficiency and the functional quality of pumpkin seed-based fermented beverages

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The growing emphasis on sustainability and circular economy principles has intensified efforts to reduce waste generation and promote the valorization of agro-industrial residues, encouraging both industry and scientific research to explore strategies for their recovery and transformation into value-added products [1]. Among these residues, pumpkin seeds (Cucurbita moschata) stand out as an abundant by-product of the Brazilian food processing industry [2]. Often discarded during processing, these seeds contain considerable levels of proteins, lipids, and bioactive compounds, making them a promising raw material for the development of functional and clean-label ingredients [3], [4].

However, the direct use of pumpkin seed flour may be limited by the presence of antinutritional factors, which reduce mineral bioavailability and impair the digestibility of proteins and carbohydrates [5]. Germination has been widely reported as an effective strategy to improve the nutritional and functional quality of seeds, as it promotes the activation of endogenous enzymes, the degradation of antinutritional compounds, and the increased availability of amino acids, minerals, and bioactive compounds [6]. Despite these advantages, the initial hydration stage represents a major limitation of the germination process, as it generally requires prolonged periods that may reach up to 48 h and may also increase the risk of microbial contamination [7].

In this context, Pacheco et al. [2] evaluated ultrasound (US) as a promising emerging technology to optimize the hydration step and, consequently, the germination of pumpkin seeds. Ultrasound enhances food processing primarily through acoustic cavitation, involving the formation, growth, and collapse of microscopic bubbles in the liquid medium [8]. The physical effects generated by cavitation, including microjets and microstreaming, can promote partial disruption of the external seed layers [9], increasing water permeability and accelerating hydration kinetics [2], [10]. These effects reduced germination time and positively affected the nutritional and functional properties of the resulting flour [2], [11].

Thus, the use of germinated pumpkin seed flour obtained through ultrasound-assisted hydration represents a promising strategy for the development of value-added food products, particularly functional beverages. However, current studies have primarily focused on the effects of ultrasound during hydration and germination, whereas the subsequent processing stages remain poorly explored. In particular, limited information is available regarding the integration of ultrasound-assisted hydration, germination, and ultrasound-assisted mashing, as well as their combined effects on compound extraction, fermentation performance, and the functional quality of plant-based fermented beverages.

In this context, mashing represents an important processing step because it promotes the enzymatic hydrolysis of reserve carbohydrates, increasing the availability of fermentable sugars and soluble compounds that support microbial growth during fermentation [12]. Fermentation further contributes to the release and biotransformation of bioactive compounds, improving the nutritional and functional properties of plant-based beverages [13]. Although ultrasound has demonstrated considerable potential for improving hydration and germination, the application of ultrasound-assisted mashing in germinated plant materials for fermented beverage production remains largely unexplored.

Furthermore, mashing of germinated pumpkin seed flour may be constrained by the structural complexity of the particulate seed matrix and the limited accessibility of enzymes to their substrates, which can restrict mass transfer and the conversion of reserve carbohydrates into fermentable sugars [14], [15]. Ultrasound application during mashing may mitigate these constraints through acoustic cavitation, promoting particle disaggregation, reducing mass-transfer resistance, enhancing substrate accessibility, and facilitating enzyme–substrate interactions [16], [17]. These effects are expected to favor the release of reducing sugars, soluble proteins, and other soluble compounds during mashing, thereby increasing the availability of fermentable substrates for subsequent fermentation [18].

Accordingly, ultrasound-assisted mashing was hypothesized to improve the accessibility of endogenous hydrolytic enzymes to their substrates within the particulate pumpkin seed matrix, thereby facilitating the release of soluble compounds and increasing the availability of fermentable substrates. Enhanced substrate availability may consequently improve fermentation performance and the functional properties of the resulting beverage. This mechanistic rationale provides a specific scientific basis for investigating ultrasound-assisted mashing beyond its general role as a process-intensification technology.

Based on the previously reported benefits of ultrasound-assisted hydration on the germination process and the bioactive potential of pumpkin seed flour [2], [11], the ultrasound operating conditions (38 W/L, 25 kHz, two 10-min sonication cycles at 25 °C) and germination parameters (72 h at 25 °C) were established according to previous studies [2], [11]. Nevertheless, the combined application of ultrasound-assisted hydration, germination, and ultrasound-assisted mashing during beverage production has not been previously investigated. Therefore, the present study evaluated the effects of integrating these biotechnological processes in fermented beverages produced from pumpkin seed flour by comparing conventional mashing with ultrasound-assisted mashing under different sonication time conditions (0 %, 50 %, and 100 % application during the isothermal resting stages). The effects of these processing strategies on mashing efficiency, fermentation kinetics, physicochemical characteristics, bioactive potential, and storage stability were investigated. Specifically, the study aimed to determine whether ultrasound-assisted mashing could overcome substrate accessibility and mass-transfer limitations in germinated pumpkin seed flour, thereby improving the technological and functional performance of the resulting fermented beverages.

Material and Methods

Raw material

The pumpkin seeds (Cucurbita moschata D., cv. Caravela) were supplied by the company Doces Mirahy (Miraí, Minas Gerais, Brazil) as an agro-industrial by-product generated during the processing of pumpkin-based sweets. The seeds used in the present study originated from a single production batch. Upon receipt, residues and impurities were removed, followed by drying at 45 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the seeds, with a composition of 22.8 % protein, 35.9 % lipids, 3.65 % ash, and 4.0 % moisture, were vacuum-packed and stored at 7 °C until further processing.

Ultrasound-assisted hydration, germination and obtaining the flours

Pumpkin seeds (40 g, wet basis) were sanitized with a sodium hypochlorite solution (200 mg/L) for 15 min [19]. Subsequently, intermittent hydration was performed with and without ultrasound application, as described by Pacheco et al. [2], [11]. In these previous studies, different ultrasound-assisted hydration conditions were systematically evaluated by combining one to four 10-min sonication cycles, each separated by 50 min of conventional hydration, at temperatures of 25 and 45 °C. Following the ultrasound treatment, hydration was continued until the seeds reached a moisture content of 45 g/100 g, which is required to initiate the germination process. The effects of these processing conditions on the proximate composition, biological properties, and techno-functional characteristics of germinated pumpkin seed flour were subsequently investigated. Among the evaluated conditions, two 10-min sonication cycles performed at 25 °C produced the most favorable overall results, promoting higher levels of phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity, GABA, reducing sugars, soluble proteins, and improved techno-functional properties of the flour [2], [11]. Therefore, this condition was selected for the present study.

Accordingly, intermittent ultrasound-assisted hydration was carried out using two 10-min sonication cycles, separated by 50 min of conventional hydration in an ultrasonic bath until the seeds reached a moisture content of 45 g/100 g. Ultrasound treatment was performed using an ultrasonic bath (Unique, model USC 2800 A, Brazil), operating at a fixed frequency of 25 kHz and a nominal power of 450 W. The actual ultrasonic power delivered to the liquid medium corresponded to a volumetric power density of 38 W/L, as determined by the calorimetric method described by Pacheco et al. [20]. During sonication, the temperature was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C using a thermostatic bath (SSDu 10 L, Solidsteel, Piracicaba, Brazil). Conventional hydration process was performed under the same conditions, except for the absence of ultrasound application.

After hydration, the seeds were incubated in a BOD-type chamber (Novaética, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil) at 25 °C for 72 h, in the dark, with periodic water spraying to maintain relative humidity between 80 and 85 % during germination. Subsequently, the germinated seeds were dried in an oven (Q819V2, QUIMIS, Diadema, Brazil) at 50 °C for 12 h. The radicles were removed, and the seeds were ground using an analytical mill (IKA® A11 basic, Germany) at 25,000 rpm for 2 min at 25 °C. The resulting flour was passed through a No. 20 mesh sieve to obtain a standardized particle fraction. The flour was then vacuum-packaged and stored at 7 °C until beverage preparation.

For comparison purposes, a control flour was also produced from non-germinated seeds under the same drying, milling, and sieving conditions. Thus, three flour samples were obtained: non-germinated control flour, germinated flour without ultrasound pretreatment, and germinated flour obtained after ultrasound-assisted hydration (US 20 min + germinated). Ultrasound-assisted mashing process

For beverage preparation, the flours were dispersed at a ratio of 1:8 (40 g of flour to 320 g of water, w/w). The mashing process was conducted using three different ultrasound-assisted mashing (USM) conditions: 0 %, 50 %, and 100 % USM. These percentages refer specifically to the proportion of each isothermal resting stage during which ultrasound was applied, rather than to the total duration of the mashing process. The 0 % USM treatment was used as the conventional mashing, whereas the 50 and 100 % USM treatments were designed to evaluate partial and continuous ultrasound application during the isothermal resting stages, respectively. Conventional mashing was performed in a thermostatic bath (SL-150, Solab, Piracicaba, Brazil) for a total process time of 130 min, following a controlled heating program with six isothermal resting stages at 35 °C for 20 min, 45 °C for 10 min, 52 °C for 10 min, 63 °C for 20 min, 72 °C for 20 min, and 78 °C for 10 min, as described by Andressa et al. [21]. These isothermal stages corresponded to a total of 90 min of isothermal processing, while the temperature increases between consecutive stages represented the come-up periods, which accounted for approximately 40 min of the total process. Ultrasound was not applied during any of the come-up periods in any treatment. Thus, ultrasound exposure was considered only during the isothermal resting stages, after the respective target temperature had been reached. In the 50 % USM treatment, ultrasound was initiated at the beginning of each isothermal resting stage and discontinued after 50 % of the duration of that stage, with the remaining portion conducted without ultrasound. Thus, the 50 % USM treatment represents 50 % ultrasound application within each individual isothermal resting stage, resulting in approximately 45 min of cumulative ultrasound exposure across the six resting stages. In the 100 % USM treatment, ultrasound was applied continuously throughout each isothermal resting stage, corresponding to 100 % ultrasound application during the 90 min of cumulative isothermal resting time. The intermediate 50 % condition was included to investigate whether partial sonication could retain the technological benefits of ultrasound while reducing ultrasonic exposure and energy input compared with continuous application.

The ultrasonic bath used for mashing was the same equipment described in Section 2.2, operating at a fixed frequency of 25 kHz and nominal power of 450 W (volumetric power density of 38 W/L). Fermentation

After mashing, the beverages were subjected to fermentation using Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG ATCC 53103® (Culturelle®, USA). Fermentation was conducted at 42 °C until the pH reached 4.6, which was defined as the endpoint of fermentation. pH was monitored at 30 min intervals to evaluate fermentation kinetics. The pH data were modeled using the modified Gompertz equation (Eq. (1)), as adapted by De Brabandere & De Baerdemaeker [22], to estimate the lag phase duration (λ, h) and the maximum rate of pH decrease (μ, h−1). The time required to reach pH 5.0 was also recorded as an additional reference point for comparing the acidification profiles among treatments.pH=pH0+pH∞-pH0exp-expμepH0-pH∞λ-t(Eq.1)where pH0 is the initial pH, pH∞ is the final pH, μ is the maximum rate of pH reduction (h−1), λ is the lag phase duration (h), and t is the time (h).

After fermentation, the products were cooled to 5 °C for 24 h. Analyses were carried out after 1 and 15 days of storage at 5 °C.

Evaluation of the mashing process

Soluble solids, reducing sugars and soluble protein

To evaluate the mashing process, the contents of soluble solids, reducing sugars, and soluble proteins were determined before and after mashing under the time and temperature conditions described in Section 2.3.1. Soluble solids content was measured using a digital refractometer (EEQ 9030, Lorden, Porto Alegre, Brazil). Reducing sugars were quantified by the dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method described by Saqib & Whitney [23], using a glucose standard curve. The results were expressed as mg glucose equivalents per 100 mL (mg/100 mL). For soluble protein determination, 2 mL of each sample were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min, and 1 mL of the supernatant was collected for analysis according to the method of Lowry et al. [24]. Soluble protein content was expressed as g of protein per 100 mL of beverage (g/100 mL), based on a standard curve prepared with bovine serum albumin.

Characterization of the fermented beverage

pH, acidity, viability of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and color parameters

pH and titratable acidity (expressed as % lactic acid) were determined according to AOAC [25]. The viability of lactic acid bacteria was evaluated following the methodology described by IDF [26].

The instrumental color of the beverages was measured using a colorimeter (Konica Minolta CR-10, Minolta, Chiyoda, Japan). The results were expressed as lightness (L*), and chromaticity coordinates (a*, red/green; b*, yellow/blue). Total phenolic compounds and in vitro antioxidant activity

To determine total phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity, 2 mL of the beverages, obtained before and after fermentation, were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was used for analysis. Total phenolic content was quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) method, according to Saavedra et al. [27], and the results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per 100 mL of beverage (mg GAE/100 mL).

In vitro antioxidant activity was evaluated using the DPPH and ABTS assays. For this purpose, 1 mL of each sample was mixed with 20 mL of 80 % methanol and sonicated (Planatec, São Paulo, Brazil; 40 kHz, 100 W) for 5 min at 25 °C to extract antioxidant compounds. The extracts were centrifuged (Sorvall Legend Micro 17R, Thermo Scientific, USA) at 5000 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. The DPPH assay was performed according to Bubelová et al. [28]. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm, and antioxidant activity was calculated using the corresponding Trolox calibration curve (Abs517 = -0.0026 × [Trolox] + 0.7121; R2 = 0.9857). The results were expressed as mg Trolox equivalents per 100 mL of beverage (mg TE/100 mL).

The ABTS assay was carried out according to Re et al. [29], with minor modifications. The ABTS+ stock solution was prepared by mixing 7 mM ABTS with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate in a 1:1 ratio and allowing the mixture to stand in the dark at room temperature for 16 h before use. The solution was then diluted with ethanol to an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.05. The reaction mixture consisted of 50 μL of sample and 3.5 mL of ABTS+ solution, followed by incubation in the dark for 60 min. Absorbance was measured at 734 nm, and antioxidant activity was calculated using the corresponding Trolox calibration curve (Abs734 = -0.0036 × [Trolox] + 0.5289; R2 = 0.9971). The results were expressed as mg TE/100 mL of beverage.

In vitro α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities

The in vitro α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour were determined using enzymatic inhibition assays, based on extracts obtained before and after fermentation.

For α-glucosidase, the in vitro inhibitory activity was evaluated according to Obaroakpo et al. [30]. Briefly, 50 μL of sample was mixed with 25 μL of α-glucosidase solution (10 U·mL−1 in 0.1 mol·L−1 buffer, pH 6.8) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG, 10 mmol·L−1, pH 6.8) were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 1000 μL of 1 mol·L−1 Na2CO3. Enzymatic activity was determined by measuring the release of p-nitrophenol at 405 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity was expressed as percentage inhibition according to Equation 5 (Eq. 5).α-glucosidase inhibition(%)=Abscontrol-AbssampleAbscontrolx100(Eq.5)

For ACE inhibition, in vitro angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibition activity was evaluated based on the release of hippuric acid (HA) from hippuryl-L-histidyl-L-leucine (HHL), catalyzed by ACE, according to the modified method of Chaudhary et al. [31]. Briefly, 50 μL of sample and 50 μL of ACE solution (25 mU·mL−1 in sodium borate buffer) were pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 150 μL of HHL substrate (8.3 mM in sodium borate buffer) were added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 250 μL of 1 M HCl. The released hippuric acid was extracted with 0.5 mL of ethyl acetate, followed by centrifugation (800 × g, 15 min, 4 °C). Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and each experimental unit was analyzed in triplicate. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Prior to analysis of variance, data normality and homogeneity of variances were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to compare differences among treatments at a 95 % confidence level. Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica software (version 9.2, StatSoft Inc., USA). Principal component analysis (PCA) and Pearson’s correlation analysis were performed to evaluate the multivariate relationships among physicochemical properties, fermentation kinetic parameters, and bioactive compounds of the samples. The interpretation of the PCA was based on both the magnitude and sign of the loading values. Variables with the highest absolute loading values were considered the main contributors to each principal component, whereas the sign of the loading values was used to interpret the direction of the relationships among variables and the distribution of samples in the score plot. All analyses were carried out using the same software.

Prior to mashing, germination, either alone or combined with ultrasound pretreatment, increased the initial levels of TSS (up to 2.5-fold), reducing sugars (up to 2.8-fold), and soluble proteins (up to 3.9-fold) compared to the non-germinated control (p < 0.05). These increases are consistent with the activation of endogenous hydrolytic enzymes during germination, which promotes the mobilization and solubilization of seed reserve compounds [21]. Similar behavior has been reported for beverages produced from germinated red bean sprouts, in which soluble protein content increased with germination time [32]. During germination, amylolytic, proteolytic, and cell wall-degrading enzymes are activated as part of the metabolic processes associated with seed development, contributing to the mobilization of reserve compounds [33]. The greater responses observed in germinated samples subjected to ultrasound pretreatment suggest that the two processes may act complementarily. Germination promotes biochemical mobilization of seed reserves [34], whereas ultrasound treatment has been reported to facilitate water penetration and structural disruption of plant tissues through acoustic cavitation, potentially increasing the accessibility of intracellular compounds during subsequent processing [35].

After mashing, the combined treatments increased the release of soluble compounds. Under conventional mashing, increases of up to 2.2-fold in TSS, 9.4-fold in reducing sugars, and 4.2-fold in soluble proteins were observed for samples pretreated with ultrasound (20 min) and germinated compared with the non-germinated control. Ultrasound-assisted mashing further intensified these responses. The 50 % ultrasound-assisted condition resulted in pronounced increases in reducing sugars, reaching up to 16.4-fold in US-pretreated and germinated samples compared to the non-germinated control, and up to 18-fold compared with conventionally mashed non-germinated samples. The greatest effects were observed under 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, in which ultrasound-pretreated and germinated samples showed increases of up to 2.5-fold in TSS, 25.4-fold in reducing sugars, and 5.8-fold in soluble proteins compared with conventionally mashed non-germinated samples.

The marked increase in reducing sugars after ultrasound-assisted mashing is consistent with enhanced conversion and solubilization of reserve carbohydrates during the mashing process. Germination activates endogenous hydrolytic enzymes involved in carbohydrate mobilization [21], [33], while the physical effects associated with acoustic cavitation have been reported to promote tissue disruption, improve liquid penetration, and facilitate mass transfer in plant matrices [9], [10]. Accordingly, the higher reducing sugar concentrations observed after ultrasound-assisted mashing may be associated with greater accessibility of carbohydrate substrates to endogenous enzymes and enhanced release of soluble carbohydrate-derived compounds.

A similar trend was observed for soluble proteins. The higher soluble protein concentrations obtained after combined germination and ultrasound-assisted mashing are consistent with enhanced protein solubilization during processing. Germination promotes the mobilization of storage proteins through endogenous proteolytic activity [33], while ultrasound-induced cavitation may contribute to tissue disruption and reduce mass-transfer resistance, facilitating the release of intracellular and matrix-associated soluble components [36], [37]. Therefore, the greater protein solubilization observed with ultrasound-assisted mashing may be associated with improved accessibility of the aqueous phase to protein-containing structures and enhanced mass transfer during mashing.

The controlled heating profile applied during mashing may further contribute to the enzymatic conversion of seed reserves by exposing the substrate to different temperature ranges associated with the activity of endogenous hydrolytic enzymes. β-amylases generally exhibit substantial activity at approximately 60–65 °C, whereas α-amylases may show higher activity at approximately 70–75 °C, depending on the enzyme source and processing conditions [38]. Proteolytic enzymes also exhibit temperature-dependent activity, with exopeptidases reported to be active at approximately 40–50 °C and endopeptidases at approximately 50–60 °C, depending on pH and exposure time [38]. Thus, the sequential temperature profile used during mashing provides conditions favorable for the action of different endogenous enzymes [39], while ultrasound-assisted processing may facilitate the interaction between these enzymes and their substrates throughout the isothermal stages.

The complementary contributions of germination, controlled heating, and ultrasound provide a plausible explanation for the greater release of soluble compounds observed under ultrasound-assisted mashing. Similar effects have been reported in other plant-based systems, Bora et al. [40], for example, demonstrated that the combined use of ultrasound with pectinase and cellulase increased soluble solids and product yield during banana juice processing, whereas Zhang et al. [41] reported that ultrasound-induced disruption of plant tissues can facilitate the release of intracellular compounds. In the present study, the pronounced increases in TSS, reducing sugars, and soluble proteins support the role of ultrasound-assisted mashing in promoting the release and solubilization of compounds from germinated pumpkin seed flour. The increased availability of reducing sugars is particularly relevant for subsequent fermentation, as these compounds can serve as fermentable substrates for microbial growth [21], while soluble proteins contribute to the nutritional composition of the beverage [42]. Overall, these results demonstrate that ultrasound-assisted mashing, particularly under continuous sonication during the isothermal stages, can enhance the technological suitability of germinated pumpkin seed flour for the production of fermented beverages.

Regarding the parameter λ, which represents the microbial adaptation phase, the effect of mashing varied according to the sample evaluated. For both non-germinated and germinated samples, ultrasound-assisted mashing (50 % and 100 %) significant reduced λ (up to 2.4-fold) compared with their respective conventionally mashed counterparts (p < 0.05). This shorter adaptation phase is consistent with the greater availability of soluble nutrients observed after ultrasound-assisted mashing (Section 3.1). In particular, the increases in reducing sugars and soluble proteins provide more readily available carbon and nitrogen sources that may facilitate microbial adaptation to the fermentation medium. Consequently, the earlier availability of these nutrients may have contributed to the shorter lag phase observed in the ultrasound-assisted treatments.

Regarding the maximum rate of pH decline (parameter μ), the US 20 min + germinated sample showed higher fermentation rates than the control and germinated samples under most mashing conditions evaluated. Specifically, μ increased by 1.3-, 1.7-, and 2.2-fold for the US 20 min + germinated sample under conventional mashing, 50 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, and 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, respectively (p < 0.05), relative to the conventionally mashed control sample. These differences in acidification kinetics were also reflected in the time required to reach pH 5.0 and the fermentation endpoint (pH 4.6) (Table 3). The time to reach pH 5.0 was lower in the germinated and ultrasound-treated beverages, particularly under ultrasound-assisted mashing. For the US 20 min + germinated sample, the time to reach pH 5.0 decreased from 5.8 h under conventional mashing to 4.6 and 4.0 h under 50 % and 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, respectively (p < 0.05). This additional reference point was used to characterize the later portion of the acidification curves, where the rate of pH decline began to decrease after the main acidification phase (Fig. 3). The differences were also evident when considering the fermentation endpoint (pH 4.6). According to the fermentation profiles (Fig. 3), the conventionally mashed non-germinated beverage reached the fermentation endpoint after approximately 9 h, whereas the US 20 min + germinated beverage subjected to 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing reached the same endpoint after approximately 5 h, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 44 % in fermentation time.

These results indicate that ultrasound applied during both the hydration and mashing was associated with more efficient subsequent fermentation process. This behavior is consistent with the greater availability of reducing sugars, soluble solids, and soluble proteins generated during germination [44], [45] and further increased during ultrasound-assisted mashing (Section 3.1). The greater availability of fermentable carbohydrates can provide substrates for glycolysis and lactic acid production, whereas soluble nitrogen compounds can contribute to bacterial growth and cellular metabolism [46]. These changes in substrate availability may therefore favor more rapid substrate utilization and organic acid production, resulting in faster acidification kinetics [47], [48].

Furthermore, the effects observed during fermentation occurred without ultrasound application during fermentation itself. Thus, the faster acidification observed in ultrasound-treated samples is more appropriately interpreted as a consequence of the changes in substrate availability produced during the preceding processing steps. The greater concentrations of soluble sugars and proteins observed after ultrasound-assisted mashing (Section 3.1) provide experimental support for this interpretation. Similar behavior has been reported in ultrasound-assisted processing of plant-based matrices, in which increased substrate availability was associated with faster microbial growth and acidification [41].

Overall, the results indicate that ultrasound-assisted mashing can improve subsequent fermentation performance by modifying the availability of soluble nutrients before fermentation. The reduction in λ and increase in μ demonstrate that the combined processing strategy provided a more favorable substrate composition for lactic acid bacteria, resulting in faster adaptation and acidification. The shorter time required to reach the fermentation endpoint further confirms the improved fermentation performance of the ultrasound-assisted treatments. These findings support the relevance of ultrasound-assisted mashing as a process-intensification step preceding fermentation, particularly when combined with ultrasound-assisted hydration and germination.

Although significant, this increase was relatively small (approximately 0.4–0.6 log CFU/mL), indicating that Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus remained viable under refrigeration conditions without substantial microbial proliferation. This behavior suggests that refrigeration effectively reduced bacterial metabolic activity while preserving cell viability. The greater availability of reducing sugars and soluble nitrogen compounds generated during germination and enhanced by ultrasound-assisted mashing likely provided sufficient nutrients to maintain bacterial survival during storage without promoting excessive acid production. In addition, soluble peptides generated by endogenous proteolysis may have served as readily assimilable nitrogen sources, contributing to the maintenance of cellular metabolism under low-temperature conditions.

The absence of significant differences in LAB counts among the different mashing treatments indicates that ultrasound-assisted processing did not negatively affect probiotic viability during storage. Rather than directly influencing bacterial cells, the beneficial effects of ultrasound appear to result from modifications in the substrate composition before fermentation, increasing the availability of nutrients that supported bacterial maintenance throughout storage. Similar behavior was reported by Zagmignan et al. [49], who evaluated the viability of L. rhamnosus ATCC 9595 in fermented cupuaçu juice and observed that the strain maintained high viable counts for up to 28 days at 4 °C without supplementation, relying on nutrients naturally present in the food matrix.

These findings demonstrate that ultrasound-assisted mashing improved substrate availability during processing without compromising either the physicochemical stability of the beverages or the viability of the probiotic culture during refrigerated storage. The combination of stable pH and acidity with consistently high LAB populations (>9 log CFU/mL after 15 days) highlights the technological potential of integrating ultrasound-assisted processing with germination for the production of refrigerated fermented plant-based beverages.

Overall, germination significantly influenced the color parameters. Regardless of the mashing condition, germinated samples showed higher L* values compared to non-germinated ones (up to ∼ 20 %), indicating increased luminosity (p < 0.05). A similar trend was observed for a* and b*, with higher values observed in germinated samples (p < 0.05), corresponding to increased red and yellow intensities, respectively. These differences are likely associated with compositional changes promoted by germination, including the increased release of reducing sugars and other soluble compounds, which may influence light absorption and scattering within the beverage matrix. In addition, slight enzymatic browning reactions occurring during germination and processing may also have contributed to the observed increase in yellow coloration [41].

In contrast, when evaluating the isolated effect of ultrasound-assisted mashing on non-germinated samples, an increase in L* (up to ∼ 10 %) and reductions in a* and b* were observed for samples mashed under ultrasound compared to the conventionally mashed non-germinated sample (p < 0.05). Consequently, higher WI (up to ∼ 11.6 %) and lower YI (up to ∼ 28 %) values were obtained (p < 0.05), indicating clearer and less yellow beverages. The higher luminosity observed after ultrasound-assisted mashing may also be associated with a more uniform distribution of suspended components within the beverage, enhancing light scattering and resulting in a brighter appearance. This effect may be advantageous for plant-based products intended to mimic dairy beverages, which are typically characterized by high luminosity and low color intensity.

Regarding ΔE, all samples showed values higher than 3 compared to the reference (non-germinated, conventionally mashed sample). According to Xiong et al. [50], ΔE values greater than 3 indicate perceptible color differences among samples. These results indicate that both pretreatments (germination and sonication) and ultrasound-assisted mashing influenced the color parameters at levels likely perceptible to consumers.

After 15 days of storage, although changes in color parameters were observed, with reductions in L*, b*, and YI, these reductions were small (up to 3.3 %, 11 %, and 10 %, respectively). In addition, no significant changes were detected for a* and WI. Importantly, the relative differences among samples were maintained, indicating good color stability during storage. Furthermore, the non-germinated, conventionally mashed sample (used as the reference at day 0) showed ΔE < 3 after 15 days of storage, further confirming the overall color stability of the beverages.

Effects of ultrasound-assisted mashing on the bioactive potential of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour during storage

Phenolic compounds and in vitro antioxidant activity in beverages

Fig. 4 shows the phenolic compound content and antioxidant activity of the beverages before fermentation and after 1 and 15 days of storage at 5 °C. After conventional mashing and prior to fermentation, germination increased phenolic content by approximately 1.4-fold and antioxidant activity by 3.0-fold (ABTS) and 3.1-fold (DPPH), compared with the non-germinated sample (p < 0.05). When combined with ultrasound (US 20 min + germinated sample), even greater increases were observed, with values reaching 1.7-fold for phenolic compounds, 4.5-fold for ABTS, and 7.2-fold for DPPH (p < 0.05).Fig. 4Total phenolic content (TPC) (A) and in vitro antioxidant activity, evaluated by ABTS (B) and DPPH (C) assays, of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

Total phenolic content (TPC) (A) and in vitro antioxidant activity, evaluated by ABTS (B) and DPPH (C) assays, of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

When mashing was performed under 50 % and 100 % sonication, the effects observed before fermentation were intensified (p < 0.05). Specifically, the US-treated germinated sample combined with 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing promoted 2.0-fold increases in phenolic content and 5.6-fold increases in antioxidant activity (ABTS), compared with the conventionally mashed non-germinated sample (p < 0.05). For antioxidant activity evaluated by DPPH, the greatest increases were observed for this sample (US-treated germinated) under both 50 % and 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing conditions, which did not differ from each other, reaching increases of up to 12.9-fold.

In previous studies, Pacheco et al. [2], [11] reported the presence of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity in pumpkin seed flour, with mean values of 34.58 mg GAE/100 g, 45.0 μmol Trolox/100 g DM (ABTS), and 32.9 μmol Trolox/100 g DM (DPPH). After ultrasound-assisted hydration followed by germination, a significant increase in these compounds was observed, with germinated flours reaching 2.4-fold (83.24 mg GAE/100 g), 2.7-fold (122.4 μmol Trolox/100 g DM, ABTS), and 1.7-fold (57.3 μmol Trolox/100 g DM, DPPH) higher values, respectively [2], [11]. However, during the production of the water-soluble extract, only the soluble fraction of these compounds is effectively transferred to the beverage, while a considerable portion of the insoluble fraction remains in the residue. Therefore, technological strategies that enhance the solubilization of these bioactive compounds are particularly relevant for the development of plant-based functional beverages. In this regard, mashing plays an important role by promoting the release of phenolic compounds, thereby increasing the nutritional and functional value of the product.

The increase in phenolic compound content after mashing can be associated with the combined effects of germination and ultrasound-assisted processing. Germination promotes the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds through activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway while also weakening structural barriers through the action of endogenous hydrolytic enzymes. During ultrasound-assisted mashing, acoustic cavitation may further disrupts plant tissues, facilitating solvent penetration, enhancing mass transfer, and promoting the release of soluble phenolic compounds previously entrapped within the cellular matrix. These effects may increase the extractability of phenolics and facilitate their transfer from the flour into the aqueous beverage fraction. Furthermore, the repeated formation, growth, and collapse of cavitation bubbles generate localized shear forces, microjets, and microstreaming, which may reduce diffusional limitations and improve the accessibility of intracellular bioactive compounds. Similar effects have been described by Yang et al. [51] and Li et al. [52], who reported that ultrasound can enhance the extraction efficiency and bioaccessibility of phenolic compounds through cavitation-induced disruption of plant tissues and improved mass transfer. Likewise, Iqbal et al. [53] demonstrated that ultrasound processing may reduce oxidative losses by partially inactivating oxidative enzymes, potentially contributing to greater preservation of phenolic compounds during processing. In addition, according to Lamuela-Raventós [54], increases in phenolic content in food products tend to correlate positively with antioxidant activity. This relationship was also observed in the present study, where the progressive increase in phenolic compounds was accompanied by higher ABTS and DPPH values (p < 0.05). Furthermore, ultrasound-assisted processing may reduce diffusional limitations during mashing, potentially favoring the recovery of antioxidant compounds released during germination. Overall, the combined processing strategy increased the concentration of phenolic compounds and was accompanied by greater antioxidant capacity in the resulting beverages.

Ultrasound has been widely explored in the literature, and several studies have demonstrated improvements in the antioxidant properties of pumpkin seeds. For example, the application of ultrasound in the extraction of bioactive compounds from roasted and unroasted seeds resulted in increases of up to 50 % in phenolic content and 10 % in antioxidant activity assessed by DPPH [55]. In addition, recent studies on protein concentrates obtained from pumpkin seeds indicate that sonication may enhance protease activity during the production of bioactive peptides with potential antioxidant properties [20], [45], [56], [57], as well as improve other biological properties of these protein concentrates [58], [59]. These findings support the potential of ultrasound-assisted processing for the valorization of food by-products and their conversion into value-added value functional ingredients and beverages, contributing to sustainability and enhancing the nutritional value of final products.

Fermentation is recognized for enhancing the release and bioconversion of bioactive compounds, especially phenolics, as well as increasing antioxidant activity through the enzymatic activity of microorganisms and the degradation of matrix macromolecules [60]. This process can contribute to the formation of lower molecular weight metabolites and the liberation of phenolic compounds associated with the food matrix, potentially increasing the concentration and accessibility of soluble antioxidant compounds [60], [61]. After 1 day of fermentation, significant increases were observed in all parameters, with a greater impact on germinated samples combined with ultrasound. Under conventional mashing, germination promoted increases of 1.5-fold in phenolic compounds, 3.2-fold in ABTS, and 3.9-fold in DPPH compared with the non-germinated sample. When combined with ultrasound (US 20 min + germinated), further increases were observed, with approximately 2.0-fold higher phenolic content and 4.5-fold higher antioxidant activity (ABTS and DPPH) relative to the non-germinated sample. Under ultrasound-assisted mashing conditions (50 % and 100 %), the effects were even more pronounced. The highest increase in phenolic compound content (up to 2.3-fold) was observed in the germinated sample subjected to US treatment and 50 % ultrasound-assisted mashing. For antioxidant activity, the maximum increases were observed under 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, reaching up to 5.1-fold (ABTS) and 4.9-fold (DPPH) for the same sample (US 20 min + germinated). The positive association between phenolic compound content and antioxidant activity observed throughout processing indicates that phenolic compounds likely contributed substantially to the antioxidant potential of the beverages.

Although a reduction in bioactive compounds and antioxidant activity is expected during storage due to natural oxidative degradation processes, including oxidation reactions, polymerization, and interactions with proteins and other matrix components, which may reduce the availability of free phenolic compounds [62], only slight reductions were observed in the present study. After 15 days of storage, phenolic compounds decreased by only 3–9 %, with the US-treated germinated sample showing the lowest losses. A similar trend was observed for antioxidant activity, with reductions ranging from 1.3 to 6.7 % for ABTS and from 1.3 to 7.3 % for DPPH, while germinated samples, particularly those subjected to ultrasound, exhibited the greatest preservation of antioxidant capacity. The relatively high retention of phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity during refrigerated storage indicates good stability of the functional properties of the beverages over the evaluated period. Similar observations have been reported for beverages processed using non-thermal technologies, in which improved retention of antioxidant compounds has been associated with reduced oxidative losses during storage [63], [64], [65]. Overall, the combination of germination and ultrasound-assisted processing enhanced the release of bioactive compounds during beverage production and was associated with good preservation of their antioxidant potential throughout refrigerated storage.

In vitro α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities in beverages

Fig. 5 shows the effect of germination, with or without ultrasound pretreatment, and different mashing conditions on α-glucosidase inhibitory activity (Fig. 5A) and ACE inhibitory activity (Fig. 5B). Before fermentation, under conventional mashing, germination increased α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities by 1.5- and 1.4-fold, respectively, compared with the non-germinated sample (p < 0.05), with more pronounced increases observed for the US-treated germinated sample, reaching 1.89- and 1.87-fold, respectively. Under 50 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, the increases were higher, particularly for the US-treated germinated condition, which showed increases of 2.2- and 2.1-fold in α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities, respectively, compared with the non-germinated control. Under 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing, the germinated sample showed increases of 2.8-fold in α-glucosidase inhibitory activity and 2.1-fold in ACE inhibitory activity, while the US-treated germinated condition achieved the highest values, reaching 3.3- and 2.6-fold, respectively. After 1 day of fermentation, bioactivity increased in all samples, with the US-treated germinated sample under 100 % ultrasound-assisted mashing showing the highest values (2.6- and 1.9-fold for α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities, respectively) compared with the non-germinated control. After 15 days of storage, the values remained relatively stable, with maximum reductions of 1.5 % for α-glucosidase inhibitory activity and 12 % for ACE inhibitory activity, indicating that these inhibitory activities were maintained throughout storage.Fig. 5In vitro α-glucosidase (A) and ACE (B) inhibitory activities of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

In vitro α-glucosidase (A) and ACE (B) inhibitory activities of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

Inhibition of α-glucosidase is widely recognized as a key indicator of antidiabetic potential in fermented products [66]. This activity has been associated with several classes of bioactive compounds, including phenolic compounds and peptides, whose availability can be influenced by germination and fermentation processes [67], [68]. During fermentation, microbial and endogenous enzymatic activities can contribute to the release and biotransformation of compounds with potential biological activity [68]. Likewise, processing conditions such as mashing and ultrasound application may influence the accessibility and solubilization of these compounds, potentially contributing to the higher inhibitory activities observed in the present study.

Recent studies have demonstrated that ultrasound-assisted processing can modify protein accessibility and facilitate interactions between proteins and hydrolytic enzymes, which may favor the release of soluble bioactive compounds and peptides [20], [69]. Bioactive peptides have also been associated with ACE inhibitory activity, particularly following protein hydrolysis and fermentation [67], [69], [70]. According to Cavalheiro et al. [70], the combined action of fermentative microorganisms and endogenous enzymes can modify the composition and availability of bioactive compounds in food matrices, potentially favoring the release of compounds with ACE-inhibitory activity. In the present study, the higher α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities observed in germinated and ultrasound-treated beverages are consistent with the greater release of soluble compounds observed after ultrasound-assisted mashing (Section 3.1) and the subsequent contribution of fermentation. Therefore, the greater inhibitory activities observed under the combined processing conditions indicate that the integration of germination, ultrasound-assisted mashing, and fermentation favored the development of beverages with enhanced bioactive potential.

Principal component analysis and general discussion

PCA was applied to evaluate the relationships among mashing parameters, fermentation kinetics, physicochemical properties, and bioactive compounds after the different treatments used in the production of the fermented beverage (Fig. 6). The first two principal components explained 87.19 % of the total data variability (PC1 = 70.71 % and PC2 = 16.48 %), indicating that the PCA adequately summarized the relationships among the evaluated variables. PC1 was mainly associated with variables related to mashing efficiency and bioactive potential, whereas PC2 primarily reflected differences associated with color attributes and fermentation kinetics. In general, conventionally mashed samples were associated with the parameter λ, indicating that ultrasound-assisted mashing enhanced microbial adaptation during the fermentation process. Furthermore, non-germinated samples were associated with lower rates of pH reduction (parameter μ), suggesting that ultrasound and germination pre-treatments played a key role in accelerating pH decrease, resulting in faster fermentation (with reductions of up to 4 h in fermentation time, as discussed in Section 3.2).Fig. 6Principal component analysis (PCA) of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

Regarding physicochemical properties and potential bioactive compounds, a synergistic effect was observed between germination, sonication, and ultrasound-assisted mashing, enhancing total soluble solids, reducing sugars, soluble proteins, phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity (ABTS and DPPH), as well as in vitro ACE and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities. The high positive loadings of these variables on PC1 indicate that they were the main contributors to sample discrimination along this axis. Accordingly, samples subjected to germination and ultrasound-assisted processing, particularly the US20 + G-100 %USM treatment, were positioned on the positive side of PC1, reflecting the combined contribution of these technologies to improving mashing efficiency and the functional quality of the beverages. These strategies enhanced the release and availability of soluble compounds throughout germination, mashing, and fermentation, contributing to observed increases in bioactive responses. Germination alone improved the availability of soluble and bioactive compounds, while the subsequent integration of ultrasound-assisted hydration and mashing further intensified these responses, resulting in improved fermentation performance and functional properties. These results demonstrate that the combination of germination, ultrasound, and mashing represents a promising technological strategy to enhance the functional, nutritional, and bioactive characteristics of fermented beverages based on pumpkin seed flour, a product still scarcely explored in the scientific literature under this integrated approach.

Regarding color parameters, germination was associated with YI, indicating that these samples exhibited a more pronounced yellow hue. This behavior is consistent with the PCA loading plot, in which YI presented a positive loading on PC2, whereas WI exhibited a negative loading, explaining the vertical separation of samples according to their color characteristics. In contrast, samples subjected to ultrasound-assisted mashing, especially non-germinated ones, were associated with WI, indicating higher whiteness indices, which is desirable for the development of plant-based beverages analogous to milk. When comparing the two ultrasound-assisted mashing conditions (50 % and 100 %), the 100 % condition provided the most favorable results for protein solubility, sugar consumption, phenolic compounds, antioxidant activity (ABTS and DPPH), and in vitro α-glucosidase and ACE inhibitory activities, particularly in germinated samples pre-treated with ultrasound. However, economic aspects related to continuous ultrasound application should be considered when determining whether the 50 % or 100 % sonication time condition represents the most advantageous processing strategy.

Pearson’s correlation heatmap (Fig. 7) confirmed the relationships observed in the PCA, showing strong positive correlations (r > 0.80) between total phenolic content, antioxidant activity (ABTS and DPPH), and both in vitro ACE and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities These associations indicate that the phenolic content was closely related to the bioactive properties observed in the beverages. In addition, parameters such as total soluble solids, reducing sugars, and soluble proteins were also highly correlated with these attributes, suggesting that the solubilization and transformation of matrix components were associated with the release of functional compounds contributing to the bioactive properties of the beverages. It is important to note that the fermentation kinetics parameter μ (maximum acidification rate) showed strong negative correlations with most physicochemical and bioactive parameters. However, this reflects the mathematical representation of μ rather than an inverse biological relationship, since more negative μ values indicate faster pH reduction and, consequently, more intense fermentation. Likewise, λ (lag phase duration) showed weak to moderate correlations, which may be attributed to the fact that lower λ values correspond to shorter microbial adaptation times and a faster onset of fermentation, indicating that faster fermentation is associated with higher availability of bioactive compounds. Additionally, YI was positively correlated with most bioactive responses, reinforcing the relationship between color development and the formation or release of compounds with bioactive potential.Fig. 7Pearson’s correlation matrix showing the relationships among fermentation kinetic parameters, physicochemical properties, and bioactive compounds of fermented beverages produced from germinated pumpkin seed flour under conventional or ultrasound-assisted mashing.

These findings provide a basis for evaluating the technological feasibility of the proposed processing strategies. From a technological perspective, germination is a low-cost process based on controlled humidity, temperature, and time, although it requires longer processing periods and involves risks of contamination [71]. Ultrasound, in contrast, requires initial investment and energy input but reduces processing time and improves extraction efficiency, potentially optimizing industrial operations [72], [73]. Conventional mashing presents higher energy demand due to heating requirements [74]. In this context, ultrasound-assisted processing emerges as a strategy to improve yields of soluble proteins, sugars, and bioactive compounds while reducing processing time.

From an industrial perspective, the implementation of ultrasound-assisted mashing would require the incorporation of ultrasonic equipment into conventional processing systems. Although this represents an additional capital investment, the enhanced extraction efficiency observed in the present study may partially offset these costs through improved utilization of raw materials, higher product yields, and increased recovery of value-added compounds, as also highlighted in recent studies on ultrasound-assisted food processing [75]. The choice between the 50 % and 100 % sonication time conditions should therefore consider both product quality and process economics. While the 100 % condition generally provided the highest extraction efficiency and bioactive potential, the 50 % condition produced comparable improvements for several quality attributes with a lower ultrasound application time, suggesting that it may represent an attractive compromise between technological performance and energy consumption. Nevertheless, because energy use and production costs were not directly quantified in this study, no definitive economic conclusions can be drawn. Future studies should include pilot-scale validation and techno-economic assessments incorporating energy consumption, equipment costs, processing capacity, productivity, and product quality under continuous industrial operating conditions. Such analyses will be essential to determine the commercial feasibility of ultrasound-assisted mashing for the production of fermented plant-based beverages.

Despite the promising technological and functional improvements demonstrated in this study, some aspects should be further investigated before industrial implementation. The present work included the evaluation of refrigerated storage for 15 days, which provided sufficient time to assess the stability of probiotic viability, physicochemical properties, color parameters, and functional characteristics under refrigerated conditions. Nevertheless, longer storage periods should be investigated to determine the commercial shelf life of the beverages, including the evolution of physical stability (e.g., sedimentation and phase separation), possible browning, and the long-term retention of phenolic compounds, antioxidant capacity, and other bioactive constituents.

In addition, although the combination of ultrasound-assisted processing and germination significantly improved the nutritional, technological, and functional quality of the beverages, sensory evaluation was beyond the scope of the present study. Consumer acceptance, including aroma, flavor, texture, and overall acceptability, should therefore be investigated using the processing conditions identified here as the most promising. Further studies could also combine these evaluations with structural, biochemical, and rheological analyses to provide deeper insight into the physicochemical changes associated with ultrasound-assisted mashing and to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed improvements. Such integrated approaches will be important to determine whether the nutritional and functional advantages observed in the present study are accompanied by desirable sensory attributes and to support the optimization of the process under industrially relevant conditions.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that the combination of ultrasound-assisted hydration, germination and ultrasound-assisted mashing is an effective strategy to improve the technological, nutritional, and functional quality of fermented beverages produced from pumpkin seed flour. The integrated application of these processes enhanced mashing efficiency by increasing the release of soluble solids, reducing sugars, soluble proteins, and phenolic compounds, and was associated with improved fermentation performance and greater antioxidant activity, as well as in vitro ACE and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities. Multivariate analyses further confirmed the close relationships between phenolic compounds and biological activities of the beverages, highlighting the contribution of these compounds to the functional quality of the final product.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically integrate ultrasound-assisted hydration, germination, and ultrasound-assisted mashing for the production of fermented beverages based on pumpkin seed flour. The synergistic effects observed demonstrate the potential of combining emerging processing technologies with germination to enhance the valorization of plant-based raw materials and agro-industrial by-products, contributing to the development of innovative functional beverages aligned with sustainability and clean-label trends.

From an industrial perspective, the proposed strategy shows promising potential for improving extraction efficiency and the recovery of value-added compounds while enhancing fermentation performance. However, the selection of the most appropriate sonication time condition should consider both technological performance and processing costs, and its commercial implementation will require pilot-scale validation and comprehensive techno-economic assessments. Despite these promising findings, some limitations should be acknowledged. The present study did not evaluate sensory acceptance, aroma and flavor characteristics, long-term shelf-life stability, or the bioaccessibility of the released bioactive compounds. Future studies should therefore investigate these aspects using the optimized processing conditions identified here, while also evaluating continuous-process scale-up, life-cycle assessment, and techno-economic analyses to support the industrial application of ultrasound-assisted processing for the production of fermented plant-based beverages.
