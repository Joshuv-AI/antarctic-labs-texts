Chromosome-level genome assembly of the Suminoe oyster Crassostrea ariakensis in south China
Authors: Ao Li, Jinlong Zhao, He Dai, Mingjie Zhao, Mengshi Zhang, Wei Wang, Guofan Zhang, Li Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The Suminoe oyster Crassostrea ariakensis (Fujita, 1913) is one of the most important ecological and fishery bivalve mollusks with a worldwide distribution. Here, we reported an improved high-quality chromosomal-level genome assembly of C. ariakensis inhabiting the South China Sea, using Nanopore technology, Illumina sequencing, and high-throughput chromosomal conformation capture analysis. The assembled genome size is 631.73 Mb, with contig N50 length of 5.36 Mb and scaffold N50 length of 61.15 Mb, and is assigned to 10 chromosomes. A total of 29,357 protein-coding genes are predicted, 96.68% of which are functionally annotated. The genome contains 347.11 Mb (54.94%) of repetitive elements and 1130 noncoding RNAs. This improved genome assembly of south C. ariakensis is an important resource for understanding oyster diversity and evolution, and provides insights into genetic improvement, protection and management of oyster resource.

Background & Summary

Mollusca is the second most speciose phylum of animals after Arthropoda. Oysters are representative bivalve molluscs that are widely distributed in oceans. They are keystone species for coastal and estuarine ecosystems, providing critical ecosystem services. Oysters are also important fishery and aquaculture species and have highly abundant yield with an annual production of 6.67 million metric tons (https://openknowledge.fao.org/handle/20.500.14283/cc9523en). There were 78–99 reported species or lineages of living oysters1 (http://www.marinespecies.org). However, the exact number of oyster species is not known due to taxonomic confusions and inadequacies. Specifically, shell morphology of oysters is highly plastic and sensitive to environmental conditions, which has hindered the understanding of oyster taxonomy and evolution. Traditional classification methods, including morphological, anatomical and a few neutral molecular markers, have limitations in systematically and precisely defining taxonomic status of oyster species. Development of high-through, low-cost, and long-reads whole-genome sequencing platforms have greatly promoted the knowledge of oysters’ genetic diversity at both species and population levels.

Whole genome sequencing of oysters focused on genus Crassostrea that most of them are major culturing or reef species, including Pacific oyster (Crassostrea gigas)2–4, Portuguese oyster (Crassostrea angulata)4,5, Eastern oyster (Crassotrea virginica)6, Hongkong oyster (Crassostrea hongkongensis)7,8, and Suminoe oyster (Crassostrea ariakensis)7,9,10. Based on these de novo genome assemblies, comparative genomic analysis systematically revealed their phylogenetic relationships that the coastal species of C. gigias and C. angulata, and the estuarine species of C. ariakensis and C. hongkongensis were clustered together respectively2,4,7, which is consistent with previous findings using molecular markers from nuclear or mitochondrial regions11–13. Furthermore, two congeneric species, C. gigas and C. angulata, exhibit allopatric distribution patterns that inhabit the north and south coasts of China separated by the Yangtze River14,15. Considering their phylogeography and adaptive differentiation (genetic distance and divergence time) between them, Wang et al. (2010) suggested that they should be classified as two subspecies14, which was commonly used in later researches1,2,4,16–21. Consistently, C. ariakensis (Fujita, 1913) broadly distributes along the coasts of the East and Southeast Asia, and is the most well-known estuarine oyster species in China ranging from the northern border with Korea and the southern border with Vietnamese22–24. Previous studies found that the north (the Bohai Sea and the north Yellow Sea) and south (the South China Sea) geographic populations evolved distinct genomic structure and high extent of genetic divergence9,22,25,26, as well as adaptive differentiations in fitness-related traits27–29. Recent studies estimated divergence time between north and south geographic populations that they diverged about 2.6–3.2 Mya based on the mitochondrial COX1 and whole genome sequences5,7, which is similar with that between two subspecies of C. gigas and C. angulata (2.7–4.8 Mya) based on single marker, all mitochondrial or whole-genome coding sequences4,5,11. In addition, hybridization experiments revealed that the north and south geographic populations can reciprocally cross with some degree of reproductive isolation27,30–32. These findings impel us to re-classify the taxonomic status of C. ariakensis dwelling in north and south China, rather than at population or species levels22,31.

Although Zhang et al. (2023) reported a chromosomal-level genome assembly of south C. ariakensis (Car_S) by employing Pacbio sequencing and Hi-C technology, its collinearity with closely related Crassostrea oyster species, especially for north C. ariakensis, were low7. Here, we aim to construct a high-quality chromosome-level assembly of C. ariakensis inhabiting the South China Sea (CarS), using integrated strategy with Illumina platform, long reads generated by Oxford Nanopore technology (ONT) sequencing, and high-throughput chromosomal conformation capture (Hi-C) analysis (Fig. 1). The genome size of CarS is 631.73 Mb assembled from 576 contigs, with a contig N50 length of 5.36 Mb and scaffold N50 length of 61.15 Mb. Moreover, 99.83% (630.68 Mb) of the total contig length were anchored on 10 chromosomes. A total of 347.11 Mb (54.94%) of repetitive elements and 1130 noncoding RNAs were identified. The CarS genome encoded 29,357 protein-coding genes as indicated by de novo prediction, mRNA transcripts and homolog-based strategies, 96.68% of which (28,383) were annotated in the publicly available NCBI RefSeq non-redundant protein, KEGG, TrEMBL, GO, and KOG databases. Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

A wild adult estuarine oyster C. ariakensis was obtained from Taishan, South China Sea, southern China. Four tissue samples (gill, mantle, adductor muscle and labial palp) were collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Genomic DNA was extracted from approximately 30 mg of adductor muscle tissue using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA concentration and quality were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis. The genomic DNA was concentrated and purified by magnetic beads to obtain large fragments of DNA, which was used to construct libraries for ONT long-read sequencing. gDNA (2 μg) was repaired using NEB Next FFPE DNA Repair Mix kit (M6630, USA) and subsequently processed using the ONT Template prep kit (SQK-LSK109, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The library was premixed with loading beads, pipetted into nine R9 flow cells and sequenced on the ONT PromethION platform with ONT sequencing reagent kit (EXP-FLP001.PRO.6, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. As a result, we obtained a total of 91.16 Gb of ONT clean data (144.30 × ). There were 3,867,385 reads with an N50 of 32.62 Kb and an average read length of 23.57 Kb. The Illumina short-insert (350 bp) library was constructed and paired-end sequencing was conducted on the Illumina Novaseq. 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) according to the protocol, which obtained 68.01 Gb clean data. For the Hi-C sequencing, the same fresh adductor muscle was used for crosslink with 1.0% formaldehyde and terminated with 0.2 M glycine. gDNA was digested with HindIII restriction enzyme and then labeled with Biotin-14-dATP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), followed by ligation using T4 DNA Ligase. The ligated DNA was sheared into 300–700 bp fragments and then selectively captured using streptavidin magnetic beads. The Hi-C library was constructed using Illumina’s paired-end kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions, which was subsequently sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq. 6000 platform33,34. A total of 58.34 Gb of clean data was yielded.

Genome assessment and assembly

Genomic feature of CarS was surveyed using 68.01 Gb Illumina paired-end sequencing data with the k-mer method. Jellyfish v2.992735 was used to perform k-mer count histogram (k = 19). The genome size was estimated using the formula: G = N k-mer/Daverage k-mer, where G is genome size, N k-mer is the total number of k-mers, Daverage k-mer is the average depth of k-mers. A total of 56,933,937,789 k-mers were obtained with a major k-mers peak at a depth of 91 (Fig. 1). Chromosomal-level assemble with Hi-C

In total, 194.81 million read pairs were obtained from the Hi-C sequencing. HiC-Pro v. 2.8.042 was used for quality control, removal of duplications, and sorting. A robust 47.01% (91.57 million read pairs) of the clean data were uniquely aligned to the draft genome assembly using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) v0.7.1043. These valid interaction read pairs were used for in-depth analysis. Contigs were clustered, ordered, and oriented using LACHESIS44 with the following parameters: cluster_min_re_sites = 34, cluster_max_link_density = 2, cluster_noninformative_ratio = 42, order_min_n_res_in_trunk = 42, order_min_n_res_in_shreds = 100. The resulting assembly was 630.68 Mb, with their lengths ranging from 40.81 Mb to 80.26 Mb (Table 3). After Hi-C scaffolding, 99.83% of which or 329 scaffolds with a scaffold N50 of 61.15 Mb were assembled into 10 chromosomes, which is similar with published genome assemblies of south (Car_S)7 and north (CarN)9 C. ariakensis. Repeat sequences, noncoding RNAs and pseudogene annotation

We used combined strategy of homology alignment and ab initio prediction to annotate whole-genome repetitive elements of south C. ariakensis. Both LTR_FINDER v1.146 and RepeatScout v1.0.747 with default parameters were used to build a de novo repetitive element database and then classified by PASTEClassifier v1.048 with default parameters, which was further integrated with Repbase v19.06 database49. RepeatMasker v. 4.0.750 was used to annotate the repeat elements based on the above integrated database with the following parameters: -nolow -no_is -norna -engine wublast. A total of 347.11 Mb of repetitive sequences were identified, representing 54.94% of the south C. ariakensis genome assembly (Table 4). This proportion was consistent with published assemblies of north (54.14%) and south (52.1%) C. ariakensis, and C. hongkongensis (50.9%) that dwelling in estuarine habitats. However, the ratios of repeat elements in C. gigas (48.87%) and C. angulata (48.02%) that dwelling in natural sea areas were slightly lower than these two estuarine species. Pseudogenes evolved similar sequences to functional genes but lost biological function due to mutations. We used the GenBlastA v1.0.455 to identify homologous pseudogenes after excluding functional genes with parameters of ‘-e 1e-5’, and performed GeneWise v2.4.156 to search for immature stop codon and frameshift mutations. A total of 2,169 pseudogenes were predicted, spanning a length of 9.33 Mb, with an average length of 4.30 Kb (Table 5).

Protein-coding gene annotation

An integrated prediction strategy with three methods of de novo prediction, homology-based prediction, and mRNA-based prediction were used to annotate the protein coding genes in the south C. ariakensis genome. For de novo prediction, four ab initio gene prediction programs, including Genscan v1.157, GeneID v1.458, SNAP v2006-07-2859 and Augustus v2.460 (pre-trained with RNA-seq data of south C. ariakensis), were used to predict genes in the repeat-masked assembly (hard-masking). For homology-based prediction, we first downloaded protein sequences of 10 well-annotated species from NCBI database (Homo sapiens, Danio rerio, Aplysia californica, Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, C. gigas, C. virginica, Biomphalaria glabrata, Lingula anatina, Octopus bimaculoides and Mizuhopecten yessoensis) that were annotated by the NCBI Eukaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline. These protein sequences were then aligned to the repeat-masked genome assembly using TBLASTN v2.2.263161 with an E-value ≤ 1E-05. The GeMoMa v1.762 was used for gene model prediction based on alignment sequences. For mRNA-based prediction, four tissues (adductor muscle, gill, mantle, and labial palp) were used for RNA-seq. The library was constructed using SMRTbell® Template Prep Kit 2.0 (PacBio, USA). cDNA was sequenced by PacBio Sequel II platform with SMRT cell 8 M (Biomarker, China). A total of 8.35 Gb of clean data was generated, which was aligned to the genome assembly using Hisat v2.0.463 (with parameters:–max-intronlen 20000,–min-intronlen 20) and then assembled using Stringtie v1.2.364. The GeneMarkS-T v5.165 and TransDecoder v5.5.0 (http://transdecoder.github.io) were used to predict genes based on the assembled transcripts. Full transcriptome-based genome annotation of unigene sequences was predicted using PASA v2.0.266 (with parameters: -align_tools gmap, -maxIntronLen 20000). Finally, all gene models predicted by the three methods were integrated by EVidenceModeler (EVM) v1.1.167 and modified by PASA to generate a weighted and nonredundant gene set. A total of 29,357 protein-coding genes were predicted (Table 6) with an average gene length of 8.48 Kb and an average exon number of 7.61 within a gene (Table 7). To functionally annotate the gene models, we searched public biological functional databases, including Non-Redundant (NR), TrEMBL, EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG), Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases, for their sequences using BLAST v2.2.3168 with parameters of ‘-e1e-5’. Technical Validation

Assessment of genome assembly and completeness

Three criteria were used to evaluate the genome assembly: the mapping of Illumina reads, BUSCO assessment, and core gene integrity. The Illumina short-reads were re-mapped onto the assembly by BWA43, and 97.85% of them were mapped to the assembled genome including 92.99% of paired-end reads (Table 9). The completeness and quality of the assembly were evaluated against the metazoa_odb10 database using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) v2.072 and Core Eukaryotic Genes Mapping Approach (CEGMA) v2.573. A total of 895 (91.51%) out of the complete 978 BUSCO orthologous groups and 419 (91.48%) out of 458 conserved eukaryotic core genes from the CEGMA database were identified in the assembled genome (Table 10). The Hi-C heatmap exhibits a well-organized interaction pattern within each of 10 chromosomes (Fig. 2).
