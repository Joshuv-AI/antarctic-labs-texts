Nudging implementation of low tidal volume ventilation: a stepped wedge, cluster randomized trial
Authors: Meeta Prasad Kerlin, Michael O Harhay, Fan Li, Dylan S Small, Yingying Lu, Wei Wang, Barry D Fuchs, Mark E Mikkelsen, Teresa Tran, Aerielle Belk, Jasmine A Silvestri, Tamar Klaiman, Stefania Scott, Elizabeth Levy, Michael W Sjoding, Rachel Kohn, Karsten J Roberts, Rinad S Beidas, Scott D Halpern

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground“Nudges” embedded in the electronic health record (EHR) facilitate desired decisions while preserving autonomy and may provide a scalable strategy to overcome the common implementation barrier of lack of knowledge about a best practice. We sought to test whether EHR-based nudges targeting two intensive care unit (ICU) clinician groups would safely increase evidence-based use of low tidal volume ventilation.MethodsWe performed a stepped-wedge, cluster randomized, hybrid type 3 effectiveness-implementation trial in 12 ICUs from February 2021 to May 2023 to test three nudges targeting clinicians responsible for order entry and respiratory therapists responsible for operationalizing orders and documentation. A default ventilation order auto-populated a low tidal volume setting; an accountable justification order required a free-text justification to order high tidal volume; and an accountable justification flowsheet required a free-text justification to document delivery of high tidal volume. ICUs were randomly assigned to launch one of the two order nudges on a pre-specified date, followed by the flowsheet nudge six months thereafter. The primary outcome was fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation, defined as percentage of time during the first 72 h of ventilation with low tidal volumes. For additional contextual inquiry, we conducted qualitative interviews with ICU clinicians regarding their perspectives on low tidal volume ventilation and study nudges.ResultsThe primary analysis included 4412 patients. Unadjusted median fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation was 45.7%. Using multivariable mixed effects regression, marginal estimates of fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation ranged from 47.1% to 57.8% across study groups, with no significant differences after Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. ICUs experienced variable changes with nudges in fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation. Clinician interviews revealed potential explanations for this variability, including the possibility of differential effects by experience level of clinicians and culture of interprofessional collaboration, and influence of the COVID-19 pandemic on familiarity with and use of low tidal volume ventilation.ConclusionsEHR-based default and accountable justification nudges did not increase utilization of low tidal volume ventilation in a broad population of mechanically ventilated patients; however, nudge effectiveness varied by ICU.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov, NCT04663802. Introduction

Grounded in behavioral economic theory, “nudges” are designed to promote desired actions while preserving decision-making autonomy. By changing how choices in clinical decisions are framed for clinicians, nudges can promote implementation by overcoming common impediments such as knowledge barriers by providing an easy path to an unfamiliar treatment [1, 2]. Specifically, default options, in which the desired choice is implemented unless a clinician opts out, may increase use of a treatment that is desirable for most patients in a defined group. Alternatively, suggesting a choice and asking clinicians to indicate if it has been made may promote clinician discretion while also potentially increasing use.

Although several interventions in critical care are supported by high-quality evidence, uptake has been slow and uneven in this high-risk population [3]. For example, “low tidal volume ventilation” (where the amount of air in an artificial breath from a mechanical ventilator is limited to avoid injuring the lungs) has been studied in randomized controlled trials and proven to be at least safe among heterogeneous populations of mechanically ventilated patients [4] and potentially beneficial to specific subgroups [5]. It is clearly life-saving in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [6, 7], a common form of acute respiratory failure associated with high morbidity and mortality, and is recommended by international clinical guidelines for ARDS management [8, 9]. Yet, more than a quarter-century has passed since the landmark clinical trial demonstrating an absolute mortality reduction of 16% with low tidal volume ventilation, and recent evidence suggests that more than one-third of ARDS patients still do not receive it [10, 11]. Two barriers to use identified across several studies are lack of knowledge about what low tidal volume ventilation entails [12–16], and clinician reluctance to prescribe low tidal volumes without definitive diagnosis of ARDS due to theoretical, but unproven, risks of low tidal volume ventilation [13, 14]. However, ARDS diagnosis is often challenging and estimating low tidal volumes requires cumbersome calculations prone to errors [17, 18].

Given the clear benefit of low tidal volume ventilation in ARDS and evidence of safety and possible benefit without ARDS, a preferred approach to implementation may be to encourage low tidal volumes among allmechanically ventilated patients. In fact, a recent study of clinical decision support (CDS) to promote use of low tidal volume ventilation suggested that application of a CDS tool to all mechanically ventilated patients would facilitate use of that tool [19]. We therefore reasoned that nudges embedded in electronic health record (EHR) orders and documentation for mechanical ventilation, targeting clinicians responsible for entering orders and for adjusting ventilator settings, may provide simple and scalable means to safely increase use of low tidal volume ventilation. Specifically, we hypothesized that (1) order panel nudges targeting prescribing clinicians would increase utilization compared to usual care, (2) additionally nudging respiratory therapists would augment the effects of the order panel nudges, and (3) the effects of nudges would be greater among patients with versus those without ARDS.

Methods

Overview of study design

We previously published a study protocol in Implementation Science [20], and further details of our approach are found in the Online Supplement (Appendix A). Briefly, the Implementing Nudges to Promote Utilization of low Tidal volume ventilation (INPUT) study was a stepped wedge, cluster randomized, hybrid type 3 effectiveness-implementation trial across 12 ICUs within 5 hospitals within one academic health system, from February 21, 2021, to May 15, 2023, to evaluate three nudges embedded in the health system’s EHR. The stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized design minimized contamination that would have occurred if clinicians were assigned different interventions for different patients and increased power relative to a parallel-cluster design [21]. All clusters (ICUs) started in usual care for at least 3 months, launched one of two nudges incorporated into orders for invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) at a pre-specified time, and then launched a second nudge incorporated into IMV documentation six months after the first nudge (Figure E1). The institutional review boards of the University of Pennsylvania and Chester County Hospital approved this trial with a waiver of informed consent. The trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04663802).

Study settings and population

Participating hospitals included a quaternary care urban academic center; two academic urban referral hospitals; and two suburban community-based hospitals. ICUs were organizationally separate and varied in case mix and staffing (Table E1).

All patients who underwent IMV in a study ICU were eligible for inclusion. We excluded patients who were (1) on IMV for less than 12 h, as they were unlikely to benefit from low tidal volume ventilation; (2) on a spontaneous mode of ventilation with minimal support, as patient effort determined tidal volume, rather than IMV settings; (3) restricted to comfort measures within 72 h after trial enrollment, as low tidal volume ventilation would not be prioritized; or (4) lacked a recorded height or had a height less than 4 feet in the EHR, as required to calculate what constitutes “low” tidal volumes. For patients who had multiple episodes of mechanical ventilation, we included only the first episode of mechanical ventilation due to non-independence of observations within the same patient.

Nudges were deployed based on patient location in the EHR; thus, patients with IMV orders activated prior to ICU admission would not be exposed to order panel nudges. Therefore, we pre-specified three study populations. The intention-to-treat (ITT) population included all eligible patients admitted to study ICUs. The modified intention-to-treat (MITT) population included all patients with any new order for IMV after ICU admission and therefore intended to be exposed to nudges. The per-protocol population included patients who were actually exposed to a nudge order panel during the intervention period or a new IMV order during the usual care period. The MITT population was the primary analytic population. We additionally analyzed the primary outcome in the ITT and per-protocol populations, under hypotheses that the effects of the nudges would be attenuated in the ITT population and augmented in the per-protocol population.

Experimental nudges and implementation strategies

Prior to the study and during the usual care period, all ICUs had access to a dashboard with prompts for several clinical decisions such as low tidal volume ventilation for patients with ARDS, as identified by an EHR algorithm [22]. Dashboard use was at the discretion of the ICU team.

Default order nudge

A default nudge provides the recommended choice up front but allows a user to opt-out, thereby helping to overcome knowledge barriers [1, 2]. The default order panel (nudge A) auto-populated the IMV order with volume assist-controlled ventilation with a set tidal volume of 6 ml per kilogram of predicted body weight (ml/kg PBW) [20], consistent with the low tidal volume intervention demonstrating mortality benefit for ARDS [7].

Accountable justification nudges

Accountable justification requires that clinicians provide a brief justification when deviating from a desirable choice, thereby nudging through several behavioral principles: it signals an injunctive norm (what “should” be done), makes that choice easier, and incorporates social accountability when choices may be audited [23, 24]. The accountable justification order panel (nudge B) added a free-text requirement to the usual care order panel if the ordered tidal volume was higher than 6.5 ml/kg PBW (to allow for rounding up of a target tidal volume of 6 ml/kg PBW) [20]. The accountable justification flowsheet (nudge C) required respiratory therapists to provide a free-text justification if the set tidal volume recorded in the EHR flowsheets, which reflects the actual setting on the ventilator, was above 6.5 ml/kg PBW [20].

In addition to the experimental nudges, trial planning and procedures required several additional implementation strategies that were broadly applied across study sites, such as engagement with ICU leadership, collaboration with the informatics team for EHR builds, and educational initiatives with bedside clinicians about the nudge. To catalog these strategies, three study investigators (MPK, JAS, RB) collaboratively developed a matrix following Proctor et al.’s recommendations for specifying and reporting implementation strategies, [25] using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [26] as an organizing tool (Online Supplement, Appendix B).

Randomization

There were two sources of randomization. To balance factors that could influence the effectiveness of nudges, we created pairs of ICUs matched on patient volume and pre-trial low tidal volume ventilation use. Within each pair, ICUs were randomly assigned to receive nudge A or B on the pre-specified transition date, thereby creating the first source of randomization. Second, each pair transitioned to the intervention phase in a randomly selected sequence every three months. After 6 months, nudge C was added in all ICUs for the remaining duration of the trial. Thus, there were five study groups: usual care, nudge A alone, nudge B alone, nudges A + C, and nudges B + C. Randomization within matched pairs supported comparison of A versus B and A + C versus B + C. Random assignment of the pre-specified transition date supported comparison of any group versus control.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation, defined as the percentage of time during IMV in the study ICU for up to 72 h that a patient received tidal volumes less than or equal to 6.5 ml/kg PBW (further details in Online Supplement). Secondary implementation outcomes included: (1) total duration of time in the first 72 h spent on tidal volumes of greater than 8 ml/kg PBW, in order to test whether the nudges reduced exposure to potentially injurious tidal volumes; and (2) initial administered tidal volume, given evidence that tidal volume settings tend to remain unchanged over time [27].

Effectiveness outcomes included in-hospital mortality, discharge disposition, and ventilator-, ICU-, and hospital-free days in the 30 days after start of IMV in the study ICU. Patients discharged to hospice were considered expired at the time of discharge. To evaluate the concern that increased use of low tidal volume ventilation could increase sedation requirements, we also studied three measures of sedation: deep sedation (defined as Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale [RASS] score of −3 to −5) for the entirety of the first 72 h of IMV after enrollment, percentage of time in deep sedation during the first 72 h, and average RASS score for the first 72 h. Finally, we monitored for the occurrence of three potentially serious adverse events that could occur with inappropriate use of low tidal volume ventilation: life-threatening acidemia (defined as a pH of less than 7.10 in the first blood gas after enrollment), cardiac arrest within 24 h after enrollment, and death within 24 h after enrollment.

Statistical analysis

The detailed statistical analysis plan is provided in the Online Supplement (Appendix C). The unit of analysis was the episode of IMV. The study group was assigned according to the status of the admitting ICU at the time of enrollment. If a hospital admission included multiple episodes, we included only the first eligible episode because the ventilator setting of a prior episode could influence choice of ventilator settings in a subsequent episode.

For the primary analysis, we fit a linear mixed effects model with a random effect for ICU to account for clustering and fixed effects for the period of time during which the patient was admitted [28, 29]. In addition, we adjusted for cluster-period size (i.e., the number of patients admitted to the ICU during that period) and pre-trial low tidal volume use for each ICU, to account for the matched pair approach. Cluster-period size was determined by hospital admitting practices and ICU admission criteria and would not plausibly influenced by the interventions, and we felt this approach would provide better precision compared to simply accounting for pairs, given the small number. We also included pre-specified covariates to account for potential imbalances of patient characteristics across study ICUs: age at the time of enrollment, sex, height, COVID-19 status, Laboratory and Acute Physiology Score, version 2 (LAPS2) [30] at the time of hospital admission and at the time of enrollment, duration of hospitalization prior to enrollment, duration of IMV prior to enrollment, and source of ICU admission. All covariates were measured before or at the time of enrollment. We fit separate models using the same random and fixed effects and covariates on the ITT and per protocol populations. We estimated standard errors using the bias-corrected robust variance estimator to address finite-sample bias [31] and account for potential model misspecification [32].

We pre-specified five comparisons of primary interest: (1) default order panel versus accountable justification order panel; (2) default order panel versus usual care; (3) accountable justification order panel versus usual care; (4) default order panel alone versus default order panel combined with accountable justification flowsheet; and (5) accountable justification order panel versus accountable justification order panel combined with accountable justification flowsheet. To account for multiple comparisons, we took a two-step approach. To preserve an overall familywise error rate of 0.05, we specified a type I error rate threshold for the comparison of default order panel versus accountable justification order panel at 0.025 and used the Holm method to control the familywise error rate for the other four comparisons at 0.025 [33]. Secondary comparisons of interest were (1) usual care versus default order panel combined with accountable justification flowsheet and (2) usual care versus accountable justification order panel versus accountable justification order panel combined with accountable justification flowsheet. We did not account for multiple comparisons in any analyses other than the primary contrasts of interest in the MITT population [34].

For the primary outcome, we conducted two subgroup analyses: patients with and without ARDS identified using an EHR-based classifier [35], and patients with and without a positive COVID-19 test during the hospitalization. In addition, we conducted pre-specified sensitivity analyses to test the following alternatives to the assumptions of primary analyses: (1) that the between-period intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) may differ from the within-period ICC, (2) that the treatment effect may differ across ICUs, and (3) that exclusion of subsequent IMV episodes may lead to erroneous effect estimates due to selection bias. Finally, we performed one post hoc sensitivity analysis including a fixed effect for hospital, to account for hospital-level effects.

Analyses of all secondary outcomes were similar to the analyses described above. For binary outcomes, we fit generalized linear mixed models with a logit link. For ventilator-, ICU- and hospital-free days outcomes, we fit generalized linear mixed models with a negative binomial link. For clinical outcomes, we added the patient’s Elixhauser score [36, 37] for further risk adjustment. All statistical analyses were completed using R. For all subgroup and secondary analyses, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons, and results should be interpreted as suggestive of effects, without implication of strict conclusions about statistical significance.

Power and sample size calculations

Based on pre-trial data and updated estimates at the time of the first interim analysis, we anticipated enrollment of 5100 patients in the MITT population and 6900 in the ITT population. We used statistical simulation to calculate the minimal detectable differences with 80% power in pairwise comparisons of any intervention groups (nudge A or B alone or in combination with C) with the usual care group. We conservatively assumed an ICC within ICUs of 0.1, a baseline mean value of the primary outcome of 45% with standard deviation of 45%, and a significance threshold of p-value equal to 0.0125 (to account for multiple comparisons). The simulation included a variable for study period to account for the stepped wedge design, assuming a homogenous period effect across all study groups. We estimated that we would have at least 80% power to detect an increase of 17% or greater in the mean value of the primary outcome (from 45% to 62%) in any of the pairwise comparisons within the MITT population.

Assessment of contextual factors

To understand contextual influences on nudge effectiveness, we embedded a mixed methods study. In the three months after adoption of each nudge, we conducted voluntary semi-structured interviews of clinicians about their perceptions of the nudges, their knowledge of and beliefs about low tidal volume ventilation, and the ICU’s culture and practices. Clinicians were identified through EHR reports and contacted by email and phone. Participants were compensated with a $50 gift card. Interviews were audio-recorded, professionally transcribed, coded by two trained team members, and analyzed using standard thematic analysis.

To assess ICU-level variability in use of low tidal volume ventilation and effect of nudges, we generated unadjusted estimates of the following within each ICU for each intervention period: (1) percentage of initial orders for a tidal volume less than 6.5 ml/kg, reflecting the clinician decision; (2) percentage of initial tidal volume documented in flowsheets, reflecting the actual initial tidal volume delivered; and (3) the average of enrolled patients in the ICU of the percentage of time during the first 72 h that a patient had tidal volumes less than 6.5 ml/kg PBW. We triangulated qualitative themes and quantitative data with a goal of understanding ICU-level variation in low tidal volume use and nudge effectiveness.

Primary outcome

In the MITT population, the median percentage of time with tidal volume less than or equal to 6.5 ml/kg PBW during the first 72 h of IMV after enrollment was 45.7% (IQR 0% to 100%) and ranged from 27.3% to 82.8% across intervention groups (Table E2). The distributions overall and in each ICU were u-shaped (Figure E2). After fitting the mixed effects model, adjusted fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation in the MITT population was 48.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 42.1% to 54.9%) with usual care, 47.1% (95% CI 40.8% to 53.4%) with the default order panel alone, 57.8% (95% CI 51.2% to 64.4%) with the accountable justification order panel alone, 49.3% (95% CI 42.7% to 55.8%) for the default order panel combined with the accountable justification flowsheet, and 57.8% (95% CI 51.0% to 64.6%) for the accountable justification order panel combined with the accountable justification flowsheet (Fig. 2). There were no statistically significant differences between intervention groups (Tables 2, E3). The addition of an accountable justification nudge in the flowsheet targeting respiratory therapists did not augment either order panel nudge (adjusted mean difference 2.1 [95% CI −2.1 to 6.4] when added to the default order panel and 0.0 [95% COI −8.1 to 8.1] when added to the accountable justification order panel). These effect sizes were similar or slightly smaller in the ITT group, similar or slightly larger in the per-protocol group (Figure E3, Table E4), and similar in sensitivity analyses (Table E5).

Among 369 patients with ARDS, the accountable justification order panel yielded higher fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation than the default order panel, with adjusted mean difference of 19.6% (95% CI 2.19% to 37.08%, p-value 0.027). Among 4,043 patients without ARDS, adjusted mean difference was −9.52% [95% CI −21.05% to 2.01%, p-value 0.106]). No other pairwise comparison suggested effect modification by ARDS status (Table E6). Among 4173 patients without COVID-19, the accountable justification order panel suggested higher fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation than usual care (adjusted mean difference 10.03% [95% CI 1.62% to 18.45%, p-value 0.019]) but not among patients with COVID-19, nor was there any evidence of effect modification in any other comparisons (Table E7).

Secondary implementation outcomes

In the MITT group, patients exposed to the accountable justification order panel alone were more likely to have initial tidal volume less than 6.5 ml/kg PBW (54.09%) compared to usual care (43.33%) or the default order panel (45.60%; adjusted ORs 1.81 and 1.79, respectively, both p < 0.01; Tables E8 and E9). None of the study nudges significantly reduced the duration of exposure to potentially injurious tidal volumes greater than 8 ml/kg PBW.

Clinical outcomes

In the MITT group, 1289 (29.2%) died in the hospital or were discharged to hospice within 30 days of study enrollment (Table E10); mortality varied by ICU and by step (Table E11). There were no significant differences in adjusted odds ratios for mortality among any study groups (Table E12). Similarly, there were no significant differences in ventilator-, ICU-, and hospital-free days between study groups (Fig. 3 and Table 3). In analyses of sedation outcomes, patients in the default order panel group were more deeply sedated than those in the accountable justification group, with significantly higher odds ratio for deep sedation for the entirety of the first 72 h of IMV, deeper average sedation intensity, and higher proportion of time in deep sedation (Table E13). Sedation depth by all measures did not differ in other key comparisons. Discharge disposition did not differ by intervention group (Figure E4). There were low and non-differential rates of potential adverse events across all intervention groups (Table E14).

Contextual factors

Management of IMV using the three specified measures (initial tidal volume ordered, initial tidal volume documented, and low tidal volume use in the first 72 h) varied by ICU (Figure E5). In three ICUs (3, 6, and 10), all three measures were increased by nudged by an absolute 10% or more. Two ICUs (5 and 9) had absolute increases of 10% or more in initial orders for low tidal volume ventilation without commensurate increases in rates of actual delivery of low tidal volume initially or over the first 72 h. Two ICUs (4 and 7) had high baseline adherence to low tidal volume with little change with nudges, and three ICUs (1, 2, and 11) had low baseline adherence with little change.

Qualitative interviews with 59 ordering clinicians and 25 respiratory therapists did not reveal a singular explanation for why nudges increased low tidal volume ventilation utilization in ICUs 3, 6, and 10, though four themes emerged (Table E15). First, interviewees from several ICUs felt that nudges would be more likely to aid ordering clinicians with less experience or supervision by attending physicians in their decision-making. In ICUs 6 and 10, ordering clinicians reported that attending physicians were not always immediately available to contribute to clinical decisions, particularly during night shifts. Second, clinicians from these two ICUs also reported that they frequently entered orders in the EHR with “placeholder” values with a plan to change them after collaboration with other team members; in this scenario, the nudges could have facilitated earlier ordering of low tidal volumes instead of placeholder settings of higher tidal volumes. Third, clinicians from ICU 10 expressed with more certainty than other clinicians that the order panel nudge caused them to reconsider their tidal volume orders and prompted collaboration with respiratory therapists. Finally, clinicians from ICUs 3, 6, and 10, along with two other ICUs, reported that exposure to patients with COVID-19-associated ARDS had increased awareness of the importance of low tidal volume ventilation and changed their practices accordingly. Exposure to caring for COVID-19 patients varied by ICU and higher rates of exposure corresponded to higher adherence to low tidal volume ventilation in the usual care period (Figure E6).

Discussion

In this stepped wedge, cluster randomized, hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial, default and accountable justification nudges to ordering clinicians did not increase use of low tidal volume ventilation compared to usual care or to each other. Furthermore, adding the accountable justification strategy to flowsheets targeting respiratory therapists did not augment the effects of either nudge to ordering clinicians.

Though not statistically significant, patients in the accountable justification order panel group, on average, had 8%–10% higher fidelity to low tidal volume ventilation compared to the default order panel or usual care in primary and sensitivity analyses. Additionally, patients exposed to the accountable justification order panel had higher odds of initial tidal volumes less than 6.5 ml/kg PBW in several comparisons. Among ARDS patients, the effect of the accountable justification order nudge was even larger at 19.6%. Taken together, these findings suggest that the accountable justification nudge targeting order entry may be a useful strategy to promote evidence-based low tidal volume delivery among ARDS patients and is at least safe if not also beneficial when used among all mechanically ventilated patients.

The primary findings are counter to our hypotheses that one or both order panel nudges would increase use of low tidal volume ventilation and our secondary hypothesis that nudging respiratory therapists would enhance the effects. Furthermore, they are not wholly consistent with prior research in behavioral economics in implementation, in which nudges have been found to have modest effects in changing clinical decisions and that default options tend to exert stronger effects than accountable justification [23]. These results, along with other recent studies of low tidal volume ventilation implementation [19, 38], highlight the importance of contextual factors in delivery of evidence-based practices and suggest limits to the effects of clinician-targeted nudges in the complex, interprofessional context of the ICU. In a mixed methods study of barriers and facilitators of clinical decision support for low tidal volume ventilation, the nature of collaboration between the physician and respiratory was highlighted as a key factor [19]; simple nudges may not sufficiently address the complexity of this interaction. Also consistent with this previous study, our contextual inquiry suggests that the effect of the nudges varied by ICU and further sheds some light this variability. For example, nudges may be more effective in influencing decisions among inexperienced clinicians, who have less knowledge about low tidal volume ventilation and may be more likely to accept auto-populated orders without question. There was also evidence of a ceiling effect, in that nudges were also generally less effective in settings with high rates of low tidal volume ventilation use at baseline. Yet, in some ICUs with low baseline rates, nudges also did not substantively increase ordering of low tidal volume ventilation, suggesting the presence of other factors impeding prescribing of low tidal volume ventilation. Finally, we note that increases in ordering alone did not always translate into increases in actual delivery of low tidal volume ventilation. The addition of the flowsheet nudge was intended to address this possibility, but it was not universally successful at doing so. Future studies should seek to better understand the steps between EHR order entry and the actual setting of tidal volume on the ventilator to inform a more comprehensive implementation program to promote this and other interdisciplinary critical care practices.

The results must also be interpreted with the study limitations in mind. First, there may have been chance imbalance in ICU-level determinants of IMV management or baseline rates of low tidal volume ventilation, despite our attempt at randomization and accounting for clustering. For example, two medical ICUs in the accountable justification group with high volumes of ARDS patients had high baseline rates of adherence to low tidal volume ventilation. Second, the study design may not have completed accounted for secular trends in delivery of low tidal volume ventilation. The trial launched shortly following the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, which had effects on the management of patients with acute respiratory failure [39] and use of low tidal volume ventilation [35]. Indeed, in our study population, ICU-level adherence to low tidal volume ventilation during the usual care period corresponded to COVID-19 caseload. This could have impacted this study in multiple ways. For example, the baseline rates of low tidal volume ventilation use may have changed from the historical estimates used to design the study, resulting in misleading power calculations. In addition, higher baseline rates may have created a ceiling effect, limiting the room for improvement. Furthermore, there is evidence from another recent pragmatic trial of clinical decision support for low tidal volume ventilation that adherence to low tidal volume was improving over time even before the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic [38]. Third, the primary outcome variable was not normally distributed, and there is no universally accepted statistical approach to handle the U-shaped distribution we observed. We chose the linear mixed model as it is typically robust to deviation from the normality assumption and the mean difference estimates on the primary outcomes are straightforward to interpret; however, there are potential limitations of this choice given the underlying data structure. In future work, we will explore alternate modeling approaches for bounded outcomes to assess the robustness of our findings. Fourth, our design did not seek to balance hospital-level factors, and the primary analyses did not account for hospital-level clustering or heterogeneity, nor for the potential of interference from shared clinicians, protocols, and practices, because there are no well-established methods to do so. However, our sensitivity analysis including hospital as a fixed effect yielded similar results, providing some reassurance. Finally, the size of the primary analytic population was smaller than anticipated, perhaps due to changes in admission patterns, clinical documentation, and order entry with the pandemic. Therefore, the study may have been underpowered to detect differences.

Conclusions

In summary, we found no significant improvement or harm with broad implementation of EHR-based default and accountable justification nudges to increase utilization of low tidal volume ventilation, contrary to previously studies demonstrating effectiveness of nudges to influence clinical decisions. There was evidence of variability in the effectiveness of nudges by patient subgroups and across ICUs. These findings suggest that complex processes are required to manage mechanical ventilation, and also that there may be lingering and confounding effects of the pandemic. Future work should seek to further elucidate the contextual factors influencing differences in nudge effectiveness to inform future implementation efforts.
