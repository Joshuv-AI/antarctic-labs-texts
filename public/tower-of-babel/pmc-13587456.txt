Comment on: effects of emergency department length of stay on inpatient utilization and mortality
Authors: Ching-Yi Chen, Shih-Fen Tseng, Yung-Shuo Kao, Raymond Nien-Chen Kuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmergency department (ED) crowding is a persistent global challenge with well-documented consequences for patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. A recent study by Ma et al. reported that prolonged ED length of stay was associated with lower inpatient hospitalization costs, a finding that merits careful methodological scrutiny.We outline three considerations that may inform the interpretation of these findings. First, the aggregate cost of an admitted population is sensitive to its case-mix; hospitalization costs vary substantially across and within clinical services, and the relative proportion of admissions across departments, which the study does not report across length-of-stay strata, could materially affect the cost comparison; disentangling a true departmental effect from differences in case severity and procedure intensity would require risk-adjusted, standardized cost data. The cost outcome is further limited to inpatient charges, with ED costs unmeasured, yet the Discussion attributes lower costs among longer-stay patients to resource use shifting into the ED; because ED costs were not captured, this resource-shifting mechanism is asserted rather than demonstrated, and the pattern is equally consistent with a truncated or front-loaded inpatient work-up, differential case-mix, or survivorship effects given the concurrently higher mortality in the same strata. Second, comorbidity is operationalized as a binary variable, and the counterintuitive finding of higher adjusted mortality among patients recorded as comorbidity-free may, at least in part, reflect an apparent inversion of the comorbidity labels in the original tables, the limits of administrative coding, acute severity not captured by the Elixhauser index, or residual confounding. Third, the association between prolonged stays and lower costs may partly reflect institutional bed-allocation policy rather than patient-level factors alone, because occupancy measures and allocation rules are distinct.Addressing these considerations would strengthen the causal interpretability of the findings and advance the evidence base for ED operational policy.Keywords: Hospital length of stay, ED overcrowding, Hospitalization costs

Overcrowding in emergency departments (EDs) has become a pressing global public health concern. We read with interest the recent study reporting that prolonged ED length of stay was associated with lower hospitalization costs [1]. The finding is thought-provoking; however, several methodological considerations may inform how it is interpreted, and we describe three here.

First, the aggregate cost of an admitted population is sensitive to its case-mix. Hospitalization costs vary substantially across and within clinical services, so the relative proportion of admissions across departments, which the study does not report across length-of-stay strata, could materially affect the cost comparison; disentangling a true departmental effect from differences in case severity and procedure intensity would require risk-adjusted, standardized cost data. Importantly, the cost outcome is limited to charges incurred during the inpatient stay—the original article’s cost analysis, as stated under “Healthcare utilization,” focused solely on events occurring during the inpatient period—and ED costs are therefore not measured. The Discussion nonetheless attributes the lower costs of longer-stay patients to resource use shifting into the ED. Because ED costs were not captured, this resource-shifting mechanism is asserted rather than demonstrated, and the pattern is also consistent with a truncated or front-loaded inpatient work-up, differential case-mix, or survivorship effects given the concurrently higher mortality in the same strata. Reporting total episode-level cost (ED plus inpatient) would allow this mechanism to be tested directly.

Second, the prevalence of comorbidity appears internally inconsistent in the original article. The text reports that 65.5% of patients had at least one comorbidity, whereas the original Table 1 “Characteristics of the samples studied” lists comorbidity as present (Y) in 34.5% and absent (N) in 65.5%—the opposite distribution. The most parsimonious explanation is an inversion of the Y/N labels in their Table 1 “Characteristics of the samples studied” and 3 “Adjusted mortality risk by EDLOS time groups”: under that reading, comorbidity prevalence would be 65.5%, consistent with the text, and the higher adjusted mortality reported for the N (comorbidity-absent) group (adjusted odds ratio 1.822) would instead attach to patients with comorbidity, consistent with clinical expectation. A single labeling correction would thus resolve both the prevalence discrepancy and the otherwise counterintuitive mortality finding. Should the labels be correct, several factors could contribute. (a) Comorbidity is operationalized as a binary variable, which may obscure clinically meaningful gradients in disease burden; approximately one in ten ED patients meet an Elixhauser-based definition of multimorbidity, and multimorbidity is associated with adverse outcomes, more strongly in younger patients [2], so collapsing this spectrum into a dichotomy could attenuate the true association. (b) The Elixhauser index relies on administrative coding, which may undercode chronic conditions in younger or less-frequently hospitalized patients. (c) Patients without documented chronic conditions may present with acute, rapidly deteriorating illnesses, such as sepsis or acute myocardial infarction, that carries high short-term mortality yet is not captured by Elixhauser categories. (d) Unmeasured variables, including socioeconomic status, functional status, or acute-severity scores (e.g., APACHE II or SOFA) absent from administrative data, may confound the association; relatedly, the original study did not exclude patients who received life-saving interventions. For context, fewer than 30% of acute elderly admissions in England lacked comorbid conditions in 2006, a proportion that has since declined [3]. We respectfully suggest that the authors verify the comorbidity coding and reconcile the text with their Table 1 “Characteristics of the samples studied” and 3 “Adjusted mortality risk by EDLOS time groups”.

Third, the association may partly reflect institutional bed-allocation policy. The original article attributes variation in ED length of stay to capacity strain and references the ED bed occupancy rate (EDBOR) as a system-level proxy. EDBOR, however, quantifies the degree of strain, whereas bed-allocation policy governs how that strain is managed; two settings with identical EDBOR can produce different length-of-stay distributions depending on whether admission follows first-come-first-served, intra-departmental bed-sharing, or cross-departmental bed-lending rules. A discrete-event simulation of 29,718 ED visits at a regional teaching hospital in Taiwan illustrates this point: holding capacity fixed, strategic bed allocation combined with controlled bed-sharing reduced prolonged length of stay by approximately 30% without increasing nursing workload [4]. Notably, although the original article discusses EDBOR conceptually, EDBOR is not an analytic variable in its models, which are specified on length-of-stay categories; the absence of study-period allocation-policy information therefore limits causal interpretation. The observed association between prolonged stays and lower hospitalization costs may, at least in part, reflect this policy context rather than patient-level factors alone.

These observations do not diminish the study’s contribution to an important and understudied question. They do suggest, however, that several specific additions would strengthen their conclusions: reporting departmental case-mix and total episode-level cost, verifying the comorbidity coding against their Table 1 “Characteristics of the samples studied” and 3 “Adjusted mortality risk by EDLOS time groups”, and documenting bed-allocation policy during the study period. Pursuing these steps could clarify whether the inverse relationship between ED length of stay and hospitalization cost reflects resource use, measurement, or the operational context in which patients are admitted.
