Association between night sleep duration and depression among Chinese elderly: evidence from the China health and retirement longitudinal study 2020
Authors: Li Shen, Guangyao Jia, Yumin Zhang, Zhiqiang Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between night sleep duration and depression has been documented in previous studies, but remains controversial. This study aims to explore the relationship between depression and nighttime sleep time in Chinese elderly people and its influencing factors.MethodsThe data originated from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study 2020 (CHARLS 2020), including 8,365 residents aged at least 60. The logistic regression analysis model was used to analyze the depression-related factors, and the restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to examine the dose-response relationship between night sleep duration and depression.ResultsIn 2020, the prevalence of depression among the Chinese elderly was 41.02%. Females, living in rural areas, having no spouse/partner, receiving medical treatment within 1 month, experiencing physical pain, perceiving poor health status, and being dissatisfied with the relationship with children were risk factors for depression in the elderly. The relationship between night sleep duration and depression differs by gender. In males, shorter sleep (less than 6 h) raises the risk of depression; in females, it shows a U-shaped relationship. The RCS indicated a non-linear dose-response (male χ2 = 30.56, P < 0.001; female χ2 = 32.26, P < 0.001). After stratification a non-linear relationship was still present (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe impact of night sleep duration on depression is different between male and female. For males, less than 6 h of sleep is a risk factor, and more than 6 h has no significance, while for females, a U-shape exists, with too little (less than 6 h) or too much (9 h or more) are both risk factors for depression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06547-8.

Background

Depression is a significant public health concern affecting more than 264 million people, and it is projected to become one of the three leading causes of the global disease burden by 2030 [1]. China has emerged as one of the fastest aging nations in the world [2]. Depression is the most prevalent mental health issue affecting the well-being of older individuals. It not only has serious effects on the physical health of the elderly, but also reduces their social function and quality of life [3]. An aging population is linked to a higher prevalence of depression or depressive symptoms [4]. The incidence of depression in China is increasing annually, with more than 95 million people suffering from it, further intensifying the burden on individuals and society, which is primarily related to the expansion of the aging population [5].

Sleep problems are both a risk factor and a symptom of depression [6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that high-quality sleep can enhance cardiovascular health, mental well-being, cognition, memory consolidation, immunity, reproductive health, and hormonal regulation [8]. Correspondingly, poor sleep is associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, depression, anxiety, inflammatory responses, strokes, and all-cause mortality [9–12]. Sleep problems have long been considered a byproduct of depression, but it is now widely recognized that sleep and mood have bidirectional associations [13]. Sleep duration is one of the most distinct characteristics of sleep and is related to various aspects of an individual’s health and well-being [11, 14, 15].

Previous studies have indicated that there are bi-directional relationships between sleep quality and depression [16–18]. However, there are still some disputes regarding the relationship between sleep duration and depression. Many studies have shown that both short and long sleep duration are independently and significantly correlated with depression [7, 19]. Short sleep is associated with more depressive symptoms in a multi-ethnic cohort of older adults [20]. But Sun Y’s research suggests that long sleep duration (exceeding 9 h) does not pose a significant risk of depression [21]. Further research has revealed a significant U-shaped relationship between sleep time and depression [22, 23].

This study used the data of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to explore the relationship between depression and nighttime sleep duration among elderly men and women in China. Given the increasing prevalence of depression and sleep disorders among the elderly population in China, understanding this relationship is essential for developing targeted interventions to improve mental health and quality of life. The findings of this study may provide valuable insights for public health policies aimed at addressing the mental health needs of the aging population.

Data and methods

Data source

Data were collected from the CHARLS 2020 project, the first nationally representative survey of the middle-aged and elderly population in China. The survey design was carried out with reference to a series of international aging surveys including the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS). The baseline survey was launched in 2011, covering 150 county-level units, 450 village-level units, and about 17,000 people in 10,000 households, and these samples were tracked every 2–3 years thereafter. The survey mainly involved the basic information, social support and health status of the middle-aged and elderly people. It is the authoritative micro survey data on the health status of middle and old people in our country [24]. The detailed information about CHARLS had been published in previous literature. The CHARLS datasets can be downloaded at the CHARLS home page at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en. In order to protect the privacy of the participants, the CHARLS datasets have been anonymized. The CHARLS survey project was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University, and all participants were required to sign informed consent.

Method

Troubled with body pain was self-rated by the participants and was classified as none, a little, somewhat, quite a bit and a lot. Sleep duration was the average amount of time participants reported actually falling asleep each night over the past month. Depression was measured using the 10-question center for epidemiologic studies-depression scale (CES-D), Cronbach’s α was 0.734, Guttman Split-Half Coefficient was 0.695, the scale was presented in the supplementary material. The correlation coefficients of all items were greater than 0.5 except for one item, which was -0.264 with the total score of CES-D (Table S1). This scale has been widely used to measure the mental health of elderly people in China with high reliability [25]. The depression score is calculated according to the standard, and the scores of the four levels are 0, 1, 2 and 3 points successively, among which the fifth item “I am full of hope for the future” and the eighth item “I am very happy” are scored in reverse. The total score of 10 items is 0–30 points, and ≥ 10 points is considered to have depressive symptoms.

Statistical treatment

Categorical and continuous variables were presented respectively as frequencies (%) and mean ± standard deviations (SD). The chi-square test was employed for the comparison between groups. The logistic regression analysis model was utilized to analyze the factors related to depression, and the likelihood ratio test for the maximum partial likelihood estimation method was applied to screen the independent variables. The dose-response relationship between sleep duration and depression was explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) by the “rms” package [26]. R4.3.2 (Tsinghua University) was employed for the aforementioned statistical analysis. A value of P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Result

Basic characteristics

In the CHARLS 2020 project, 10,654 elderly patients aged ≥ 60 years were screened, and 8,365 samples were finally included after excluding 2,289 with missing or insufficient CES-D information. Among them, there were 4,199 males (50.20%) and 4,166 females (49.80%). Their average age was (67.96 ± 6.01) years, with males being (68.02 ± 5.99) years and females being (67.90 ± 6.04) years respectively. 3,431 patients presented with depression, and the prevalence rate was 41.02% (32.53% for males, 49.57% for females). A total of 4,942 patients suffered from pain, accounting for 59.08% (50.56% for males, 67.67% for females). 6,659 (79.61%) had a spouses/partner. Among them, 1,798 (21.49%) had received medical treatment within 1 month, and 1,902 (22.74%) had been hospitalized within 1 year. There are 7,301 (87.28%) people with pension insurance and 7,966 (95.23%) people with medical insurance. In the past six months, 6,591 (78.79%) people had not lived alone, 739 (8.83%) people had lived alone for 90 days or less, and 1,035 (12.37%) people had lived alone for more than 91 days.

Dose-response relationship between sleep duration and depression

Applied a restrictive cubic spline model to analyze the connection between sleep duration and depression status, after making adjustments for residential address location, age, the number of days living alone in the first half of the year, spouse/partner, medical treatment within 1 month, self-rated health status, pain degree, life satisfaction, and children satisfaction, a non-linear dose-response relationship was identified between sleep duration and depression status (χ2 = 62.49, P < 0.001). Within the sleep time range of 0–6 h, as the sleep duration increased, the risk of depression decreased. After 6 h, with the increase in sleep duration, the risk of depression rose. After stratifying by gender, despite the presence of a non-linear dose-response relationship between sleep duration and depression status (male: χ2 = 29.84, P < 0.001; female: χ2 = 31.20, P < 0.001), there were certain differences between males and females. For males, within 0–6 h, the risk of depression decreased along with the sleep duration, and the risk of depression did not change after 6 h; while for women, within 0–6 h, the risk of depression gradually decreased with the increase in sleep duration (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Dose-response relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptoms in the elderly

Discussion

Based on data from the China Longitudinal Survey of Health and Retirement (CHARLS) in 2020, we compared the characteristics of depression among the elderly. Subsequently, we employed logistic regression models to identify and evaluate key factors associated with depression. Furthermore, we implemented RCS analysis to investigate the non-linear relationship between sleep duration and depression prevalence.

Depression continues to be a significant public health concern that impacts the lives of numerous people worldwide. Previous studies indicated that both the decreasing and increasing of sleep hours towards extreme sleep duration were linked to poor health outcomes [27]. The same holds true for the relationship between sleep and depression. This study discovered that a U-shaped relationship existed between sleep duration and depression; nevertheless, the outcomes differed between males and females. This result was obtained both in logistic and RCS, and the U-shaped relationship still remained in the division of gender, which is in line with previous findings [19, 28–30].There were slight differences between males and females. Males slept for 0–6 h, and as the sleep time increased, the depression risk decreased. The risk of depression for sleep duration < 5 h and 5 ~ 6 h is 2.854 times and 1.787 times that for sleep duration 7 ~ 8 h, respectively. After 6 h, the depression risk did not change significantly; while females slept for 0–6 h, as the sleep time increased, the depression risk decreased. The risk of depression for sleep duration < 5 h and 5 ~ 6 h is 2.334 times and 1.640 times that for sleep duration 7 ~ 8 h, respectively. After 9 h, with the increase of sleep time, the depression risk gradually increased (OR = 1.478). This phenomenon demonstrates that elderly males were more sensitive to short sleep durations, and similar findings were discovered by Yankun Sun, et al. [21].

This gender difference may be related to multiple factors. From a biological perspective, hormonal differences and melatonin secretion may explain the relationship between gender-specific depression risk and sleep duration [31], for example, the changes in hormone levels in postmenopausal women may affect sleep structure and mood regulation, complicating the relationship between sleep duration and the risk of depression [32]. From a psychosocial perspective, women may assume more care responsibilities, leading to a decline in sleep quality [33]. Excessive sleep duration may reflect underlying psychological stress or social isolation. In addition, women’s perception of sleep quality and emotional responses may differ from men’s, which may also affect the risk of depression.

It is highlighted that pain is quite common in older individuals, and is linked to depression, especially among those with poor health [34]. Some studies have indicated a positive correlation between chronic pain and depression [35]. The results of this study also revealed that pain is an independent risk factor for depression in the elderly, and the more severe the pain, the higher the risk of depression. As a kind of complex subjective feeling, pain will inevitably lead to an individual’s emotional response. A large number of negative emotions go hand in hand with pain, and depression is one of them [36]. There are also many debates regarding the relationship between pain and depression. Some researchers believe that pain may be the cause of an individual’s depression, and pain and depression are not a one-way relationship. Many studies have found that depression may also induce pain, indicating that pain and depression may be causally interrelated and mutually promoting [37–39]. Chronic pain and depression may affect each other through repeated vicious cycles: pain increases unpleasant feelings, prompting individuals to recall unpleasant events; in turn, these unpleasant things intensify the unpleasant emotion, which further worsens the pain.

This study indicated that having seen a doctor within 1 month and self-rated physical health condition are risk factors for depression among the elderly, and the poorer the physical condition, the higher the risk of depression, which might influence the mood of these individuals during the time of the investigation. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that, being female, residing in rural areas, dissatisfaction with life, dissatisfaction with children, and living without a partner seemed to notably increase the probability of depression among older adults. These results are consistent with previous epidemiological investigations [40]. These findings suggest that intervention measures for depression in the elderly should comprehensively consider multiple factors such as an individual’s physical health status, gender, living environment, life satisfaction, and family relationships, in order to more accurately identify high-risk groups and provide personalized support, thereby effectively reducing the incidence of depression in the elderly and improving their quality of life.

There are still some potential mechanisms that must be taken into consideration. Socio-economic factors such as place of residence and living habits have a significant impact on sleep quality and depressive mood. There are also differences in depressive symptoms between urban and rural residents, which is consistent with some longitudinal studies in the same cohort [41]. Take rural areas as an example, with a large number of young people moving to cities for work and life, many elderly people remain in rural areas and live alone for a relatively long time, in this study, 13.2% of rural elderly lived alone for more than 91 days, and 56.3% had no social activities. Due to the lack of companionship from their children and sufficient social activities [42], these elderly people are more prone to feelings of loneliness and depression, which in turn affects sleep quality and forms a vicious cycle [43]. The distribution and influence degree of these factors vary among different populations, further complicating the research results. Depressive symptoms in the elderly are caused by multiple factors. We suggest that families should give them more care and love. Children should often meet and keep in touch with the elderly at home, actively participate in social activities and medical insurance, strengthen pain management, improve sleep quality and enrich their later years.

There are several limitations within the current study. Firstly, when evaluating the self-reported sleep duration, we cannot eliminate the possibility of recall bias or response bias. Secondly, questionnaire contains only one question about sleep duration, without distinguishing between time periods such as weekends or weekdays, nor does it include questions about sleep quality, which is evident in the literature [44]. Thirdly, the survey conducted by CHARLS 2020 did not include an assessment regarding sleep quality. Finally, the cross-sectional design means we cannot determine the causal basis for the observed association between sleep duration and depression. In the future, the relationship between sleep duration and depression needs to be verified by well-designed prospective studies. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, the findings indicate a U-shaped association between sleep duration and depressive symptoms among the Chinese elderly, yet there are slight differences between males and females. Males who slept for 0–6 h, as the sleep time increases, the depression risk decreases, and after 6 h, the depression risk does not change significantly; females who slept for 0–6 h, as the sleep time increases, the depression risk decreases, and after 9 h, as the sleep time increases, the depression risk gradually rises.
