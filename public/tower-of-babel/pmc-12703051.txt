Stimulation of High‐Conductivity Points in Human Skin Reduces Biotin Levels to Modulate Intracellular Metabolism and Local Energy Production
Authors: Kazuki Takagaki, Kristina Todorova, Kentaro Kajiya, Andrea Clocchiatti, Stefano Sol, Fabiana Boncimino, Anna Mandinova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAcupuncture is a widely used therapeutic practice that targets specific points on or beneath the skin, known as acupoints. While acupoints are thought to exhibit low electrical resistance, their underlying biophysical and molecular characteristics remain incompletely understood. This study investigates the presence and properties of high‐conductivity regions in surgically isolated human skin.MethodsHigh‐conductivity points (HCPs) were identified on human skin explants using a clinical conductivity‐based acupoint detector. Histological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses were performed on HCP and control regions, with and without needle stimulation. To probe mechanistic pathways, human keratinocytes and fibroblasts were cultured under biotin‐deficient conditions, mimicking stimulation‐induced metabolic changes.ResultsNo significant structural differences were observed between HCPs and control skin. However, RNA sequencing revealed that needled HCPs activated gene programs resembling those of anatomically defined murine acupoints. Metabolomic profiling showed a stimulation‐specific decrease in biotin levels at HCPs. In vitro, biotin deficiency altered acetyl‐CoA carboxylase regulation and increased ATP production via mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis.ConclusionThese findings suggest that biotin‐dependent metabolic reprogramming occurs at electrically distinct skin regions in response to physical stimulation. While the relationship between HCPs and classical acupoints remains to be fully established, this study provides novel insights into the local biochemical responses associated with acupuncture.Keywords: acupuncture, biotin, carboxylase, metabolism, skin conductivity

Introduction

Acupuncture, a well‐established alternative medical treatment in Asia, particularly in China [1], involves physical stimulation through fine needle insertion, electricity, or heat applied to specific points on or under the skin, known as acupoints. These acupoints, identified by specific anatomical landmarks, are believed to correspond to distinct visceral disorders and organ functions [2]. Acupuncture is used worldwide for many symptoms [3, 4, 5]. Its effectiveness has been reported in numerous case studies, and it offers the advantage of safety, simplicity, and minimal side effects, making it a potential alternative to certain pharmacological therapies currently in use [6, 7, 8, 9]. Recognizing its importance and the need for research, the field of Traditional Chinese Medicine (including acupuncture) was established in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 11th revision by the World Health Organization (WHO) [10].

Several basic molecular and cellular mechanisms of acupuncture have been reported in recent years. Although several local pathways, such as adenosine signaling [11, 12, 13, 14], connective tissue mechanotransduction [15, 16, 17], and mast cell activation [18, 19, 20, 21], have been implicated in the effects of acupuncture, primarily in animal models, the specific role of human acupoints remains incompletely understood. Furthermore, understanding these effects is complicated by the complex clinical effects of acupuncture, particularly on human skin, involving central nervous system mechanisms [5, 22, 23, 24], psychological factors [25], and placebo effects [3, 26]. Regarding the structural features of acupoints, although differences in vascular density [27, 28] and in the number of nerve fibers [28, 29, 30] have been reported, a consensus on the structural characteristics of the skin tissue at acupoints is yet to be reached. While some studies have focused on intramuscular or intermuscular connective tissue at acupoints [31] and assessed local environmental changes around them [14, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36], there have been few comprehensive investigations into the structural and molecular characteristics of acupoint skin tissue. As a result, despite being the first site of reaction, the biological basis of acupuncture's effects on humans at the level of the skin remains poorly understood.

Electrical conductivity has been proposed as a biophysical property associated with acupoints, though its interpretation remains controversial [37, 38]. Several studies have reported lower skin resistance at acupoints than at adjacent control points [39, 40, 41]. Acupoint charts have been well‐established, and devices capable of measuring skin conductivity are widely used in clinical practice to locate acupoints in combination with anatomical knowledge.

In this study, we employed an acupuncture location device to detect regions of elevated skin conductivity on surgically removed human skin tissue. These regions, which we refer to as “high‐conductivity points (HCPs),” were subjected to RNA‐sequencing and metabolomics analyses to characterize their molecular responses to needle stimulation. This approach enabled us to investigate local tissue responses in the absence of systemic or psychological confounders. Although these regions are not definitively equivalent to classical acupoints as defined in vivo, we sought to assess whether they exhibit molecular characteristics that align with acupoint‐related features observed in model systems [42]. Our findings indicate that changes in biotin, an essential vitamin for skin physiology [43, 44], may contribute to the functional response of HCPs to acupuncture‐like stimuli.

Methodology

Human skin tissue samples for HCPs detection and primary cell culture isolation were obtained during surgical procedures at Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, MA, with IRB approval for discarded human tissue (2020P001819/MGH). Human epidermal KCs and DFs were isolated from punch biopsy‐human skin samples by overnight incubation at 4°C with 5 U/mL dispase (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) to separate the dermis from the epidermis. The epidermis and dermis were minced into fine pieces and treated with 0.25% trypsin (Corning, Corning, NY) for 15 min, followed by collagenase/hyaluronidase (Stemcell Technologies) for 4 h at 37°C with agitation. The resulting suspension was passed through a 70‐μm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the cell pellets obtained after centrifugation were resuspended in KC serum‐free medium (Invitrogen) with supplements according to the manufacturer's instructions. For DF, the pellets were resuspended in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and an antibiotic‐antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primary cultures were maintained at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Biotin‐deficient (BD) medium was prepared by incubating complete medium for KCs and DFs with avidin agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4°C, ensuring that the biotin concentration was less than 32 pg/mL (0.131 pM, minimum detectable dose), as confirmed by ELISA (MyBioSource). The control medium was prepared by adding biotin solution diluted in PBS to BD medium at 100 nM final concentration, as previously described [45].

HCPs Detection, Needling, Heating, and Sample Processing

HCPs in human skin samples were determined using the Royal Eight device (Ryodoraku Research Institute, Tokyo, Japan) and considered as HCPs in the study. The input power of the device was set to 12 V, and the indifferent electrode was applied to a specific area of the skin. By tracing the skin surface with the exploring electrode, a current of ≤ 20 μA was applied between the electrodes in most of the skin, allowing us to identify skin regions with approximately 100 μA current. These values were consistent regardless of the position of the indifferent electrode. All HCPs used in the experiment were confirmed to be non‐penetrating and non‐wounding through stereomicroscopy.

J‐type acupuncture needles (0.14 mm in diameter and 30 mm in length, Seirin, Shizuoka, Japan) were inserted to a depth of 15 mm and removed 30 min later to provide needle stimulation. For heat stimulation, the skin samples were placed upside down on a hot plate maintained at a constant temperature of 50°C for 20 min. Following HCPs detection, needling, and heating, the biopsy skins used for ELISA, RNA isolation, RNA‐seq, and metabolomics analyses were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% normal human serum using cell culture inserts for 6 h at 37°C in 5% CO2.

To differentiate control‐normal points from control‐HCPs, tattoo ink was injected into the lower dermis before biopsy. Tissue samples were immediately frozen in OCT mounting compound without fixation and used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining or immunofluorescence staining.

Histology and Immunofluorescence Staining of Human Skin Samples

Frozen tissue blocks were cut into 7 μm thickness and fixed in 4% formaldehyde (Boston BioProducts, Ashland, MA). For H&E staining, the sections were stained with hematoxylin 2 solution (Epredia, Kalamazoo, MI) and eosin‐Y alcoholic solution (Epredia). For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton‐X 100 in PBS and blocked with 5% normal donkey serum in PBS. Then, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Data S3) in 1% normal donkey serum, 0.1% Tween 20 in PBS, and a secondary antibody labeled with Alexa Fluor 488, 568, or 647. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), and the sections were scanned with a NanoZoomer S60 Digital slide scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan). Epidermal thickness and the percentage of positive fluorescence areas were measured using Fiji ImageJ software. The measurements were expressed as the average value of a 1‐mm width centered on the ink injection site and 250 μm depth from the basal membrane. For 3D analysis, the epidermis was peeled off from punch biopsy‐skin samples (control points and HCPs) as described in Section 2.1. The dermal samples were fixed in 4% formaldehyde overnight at 4°C. Immunofluorescence staining and tissue clearing were performed following the manufacturer's protocol (RapiClear 1.52 Solution, SunJin Lab, Hsinchu, Taiwan). Briefly, the tissues were permeabilized in 2% Triton‐X 100 in PBS overnight at room temperature and then blocked with 10% normal donkey serum and 1% Triton‐X 100 in PBS (blocking buffer) for 2 days at 4°C. Using the same antibodies used in section staining, the tissue specimens were incubated with the primary antibody in 10‐fold diluted blocking buffer (Ab dilution buffer) for 2–3 days at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibody in Ab dilution buffer overnight at 4°C. After incubating the specimens in RapiClear 1.52 Solution overnight, 250 μm depth regions from the basement membrane were imaged using a Nikon A1SiR confocal microscope (Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). The percentage of positive fluorescence areas was measured using Fiji ImageJ software. 3D images were acquired using ImarisViewer 9.9.1 (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK).

RNA‐Sequencing Analysis

Punch biopsy‐skin samples were minced into small pieces and homogenized in TRIzol (Invitrogen) using a Tissuelyser II (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) for 10 min at 30 oscillations per sec. After chloroform addition, total RNA was purified from the aqueous phase using RNeasy Mini Kits (QIAGEN), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Preparation of RNA library, transcriptome sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis were performed by Novogene (Beijing, China). Genes with an adjusted p‐value < 0.05 found by DESeq. 2R package (v2_1.6.3) were assigned as differentially expressed. ClusterProfiler R package (v2.4.3) was used to test the statistical enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in KEGG pathways.

RNA Isolation and Quantitative RT‐PCR

Control‐ and needle‐HCPs skin, taken out by punch biopsy, were homogenized in TRIzol using a Tissuelyser II for 5 min at 30 oscillations per sec. Total RNA was purified with Direct‐zol RNA Miniprep Plus Kits (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA), following the manufacturer's instructions, and used for cDNA synthesis with a Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cambridge, MA). Quantitative RT‐PCR was performed with Power SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) and gene‐specific primers (Data S4). Relative expression levels to the housekeeping gene, 36B4, were determined using the C t method.

Metabolomics Analysis

Sample preparation from punch biopsy‐skin samples was conducted according to an established protocol [46]. Quantitative polar metabolomic profiling was performed at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center mass spectrometry core facility as previously described [46]. We followed the method MetaboAnalyst uses for data processing. Metabolites with p‐value < 0.05 found by both Limma [47] and MetaboAnalyst [48] R package were assigned as significant.

Biotin and ATP Measurements

Punch biopsy‐skin samples were minced into small pieces and homogenized in 500 μL lysis buffer using a Tissuelyser II for 10 min at 30 oscillations per sec. Supernatants were used for Vitamin B7 ELISAs (MyBioSource), according to the manufacturer's protocol, and for BCA protein assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Supernatants and lysates together were used for ATP bioluminescence detection using a Promega kit. The amount of biotin and ATP in tissues was normalized to the amount of total protein.

Western Blot Analysis

KCs and DFs were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) assay buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (all reagents purchased from Boston BioProducts). Needled‐normal point and needled‐HCPs skin samples taken out by punch biopsy 30 min after needling were minced into small pieces and homogenized in 100 μL RIPA buffer using a Tissuelyser II for 5 min at 30 oscillations per sec. Lysates were resolved in precast NuPAGE Bis‐Tris 4%‐12% gels or Tris‐Acetate 3%–8% gels (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer's instructions, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for detection with antibodies (Data S3). Quantitative measurements were done with the gel plugin Fiji ImageJ software. ﻿All protein expression was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection method (Western Lightning Pro, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA).

Analysis of Mitochondrial Function

Metabolic flux analyses were performed on a Seahorse XFe24 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), using Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kits (Agilent Technologies) to assess mitochondrial function and glycolytic activity in KCs and DFs, under control‐ or BD‐conditions, according to the manufacturer's instructions with modification. Control‐ and BD‐assay media were prepared by adding 100 nM biotin solution in PBS or PBS alone to Seahorse XF Base Medium (supplemented with 2 mM L‐glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 10 mM glucose, pH adjusted to 7.4). KCs and DFs, grown in each control medium overnight, were washed with control‐ or BD‐assay medium and equilibrated in a 37°C non‐CO2 incubator for 45 min. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured under the conditions of three distinct chemical (OXPHOS inhibitor) injections as follows: (1) oligomycin (oligo, 1 μM); (2) carbonyl cyanide‐4‐(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 1 μM); and (3) rotenone plus antimycin A (rot+AA, 0.5 μM). OCR and ECAR were normalized to the relative cell number determined by alamarBlue HS Cell Viability Reagent (Invitrogen) and expressed as % “control‐time point 1.” Parameters of mitochondrial functions were calculated as described on the manufacturer's website. The values of glycolysis and glycolytic capacity were expressed as the average of three measurements immediately before and after oligomycin injection. The glycolytic reserve value referred to the difference between glycolysis and glycolytic capacity. The values of baseline and stressed phenotypes were expressed as the average of the first three measurements, and three measurements just after FCCP injection each.

This adjusted p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For metabolomics analysis, Limma [47] and MetaboAnalyst [48] R package were used to determine the significant metabolites common to both analyses. Paired t‐tests were performed for Figures 1 and 3G,I to compare control‐normal points and control‐HCPs from the same donor, with one‐sample t‐tests performed for the others. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High Conductivity Regions Can Be Detected on Surgically Removed Human Skin Without Structural Differences

To identify acupoints in surgically removed human abdominal skin, we utilized the Royal Eight device, which can measure skin conductivity. Previous studies have reported that acupoints exhibit reduced electrical resistance compared to adjacent control points, thus making skin conductivity a reliable marker [39, 40, 41]. In our ex vivo model, we detected HCPs exhibiting approximately fivefold higher conductivity than surrounding skin regions (Figure 1A). These were designated as HCPs for the purpose of this study, although we do not assert that they correspond to classical in vivo acupoints. Since epidermal thickness and the presence of sweat glands have been reported as possible regulators of conductivity [40, 50], we performed H&E staining to examine structural differences between control points and HCPs (Figure 1B,D). To accurately identify the HCPs in histological sections, we injected tattoo ink into the lower dermis layer to visualize their exact positions, as the entire biopsies were not considered as the point of reaction due to the small size of the electrode. Our analysis revealed no significant structural differences between normal points and HCPs, including epidermal thickness (Figure 1C) or sweat gland structure (Figure 1D).

High Conductivity Regions Exhibit a Response to Needling, Similar to Anatomically Located Acupoints

To determine if skin located at HCPs demonstrates specific transcriptional responses to needling, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing on full‐thickness skin tissue from normal points and HCPs, both with and without needling. This resulted in four experimental groups (control‐normal points, control‐HCPs, needled‐normal points, and needled‐HCPs), with four biological replicates per group. Differential gene expression analysis comparing these groups revealed significant differences in the transcriptional profiles of the skin, with the most pronounced changes observed in HCPs subjected to needling (Figure 2A). We performed differential expression analysis using the DESeq. 2 R package, applying an adjusted p‐value threshold of < 0.05 for significance. We observed a total of 627 genes that were significantly altered between needled‐HCPs and control‐HCPs. In comparison, 95 DEGs were identified when comparing needled‐HCPs with needled‐normal points (Figure 2B). These findings indicate that although there were no significant differences in transcript levels between normal point and HCPs' skin under steady conditions, their transcriptional responsiveness to needling significantly differed. Focusing on 304 DEGs upregulated in needled‐HCPs relative to control‐HCPs (Figure 2C), KEGG pathway analysis revealed activation of MAPK and p53 signaling pathways—consistent with prior reports in anatomically defined mouse acupoints [42] (Figure 2D). These findings were validated via qRT‐PCR for key MAPK‐related genes (Figure 2E). Together, these data suggest that HCPs defined by conductivity in ex vivo tissue exhibit a robust transcriptional response to needling that resembles features of previously characterized acupoints, although the equivalence between these models remains hypothetical.

Needling Reduces Biotin Levels Only in High‐Conductivity Regions of the Skin

To understand the metabolic impact of needling on HCPs, we performed untargeted metabolomics (LC‐MS/MS) on all four experimental groups. A total of 296 metabolite profiles were determined, and after data processing, significant metabolites were identified using Limma [47] and MetaboAnalyst [48]. While strict FDR thresholds revealed no significant hits, relaxing the filter to nominal p < 0.05 revealed 25–54 potentially significant metabolites across methods (Figure 3A, Data S1). Among the overlapping hits, biotin (vitamin B7) emerged as a candidate of interest due to its established roles in skin physiology [43, 44]. At baseline, HCPs exhibited higher biotin levels compared to control points. However, needling selectively decreased biotin in HCPs, while levels in normal points remained unchanged (Figure 3B). These trends were confirmed by biotin‐ELISA (Figure 3C,D), where needled‐HCPs showed a 0.42‐fold decrease in biotin compared to needled‐normal points.

In addition to needling, moxibustion—a technique that applies heat from burning dried mugwort (moxa)—has been utilized to stimulate acupoints (the same points used during acupuncture treatments) for treating various diseases [51]. To determine if this decrease was needle‐specific, we applied heat stimulation (mimicking moxibustion) to additional samples. Moxibustion can be applied directly or indirectly, with indirect moxibustion being more common and involving the use of insulating materials between the moxa and the skin [51]. Since indirect moxibustion is practiced for 5–30 min at the maximum skin surface temperature of 40°C–65°C [52, 53, 54, 55, 56], in most cases, we placed the skin tissue upside down on the 50°C plate for 20 min to simulate heat stimulation. Similar to needling, heated‐HCPs exhibited significantly reduced biotin compared to heated‐normal points (Figure 3E), suggesting that biotin depletion is a stimulation‐dependent phenomenon at HCPs.

Given that biotin in human skin is derived systemically via diet or microbiota and delivered via circulation [57], we examined whether changes in the amount of biotin in HCPs are due to differences in vascular structure. We quantified the percentage of the skin area positive for CD31, a pan‐endothelial marker [58], in 2D and 3D, using immunofluorescence. No significant differences were observed between control and HCPs (Figure 3F–I), indicating that biotin depletion is unlikely due to reduced vascular access. These data suggest that biotin levels are dynamically regulated at HCPs in response to physical stimulation, although the underlying mechanisms (e.g., uptake, degradation, or release) remain to be elucidated.

Biotin Deficiency Suppresses the Activation of Acetyl‐CoA Carboxylases, In Vitro

To explore the functional implications of changes in biotin levels in HCPs during needling at a cellular level, we developed an in vitro model using primary cultures of human KCs and DFs cultivated in BD media (Figure 4A). While chronic deficiency may not fully recapitulate the acute effects seen ex vivo, this model allowed us to assess the cellular consequences of biotin loss. Given that biotin serves as a coenzyme for several carboxylases (acetyl‐CoA carboxylase: ACC; pyruvate carboxylase: PC; propionyl‐CoA carboxylase: PCC; and 3‐methylcrotonyl‐CoA carboxylase: MCC) [57], we hypothesized that biotin depletion may affect post‐translational regulation of these enzymes. Western blot analysis revealed that biotinylation of ACC was significantly decreased in BD media compared to controls in both KCs and DFs, whereas biotinylation levels of PC, PCC, and MCC were not markedly affected (Figure 4B,C). ACC activity is regulated not only by biotinylation but also by phosphorylation [59]. Interestingly, we observed a significant increase in phosphorylated‐ACC (p‐ACC) in BD media compared to control media, in both human KCs and DFs, contrary to the changes observed in biotinylation, as revealed by western blot analyses (Figure 4D,E). These data indicate that biotin deficiency reduces ACC activity via dual mechanisms: diminished biotinylation and increased phosphorylation. Although we did not observe changes in other carboxylases under these conditions, we cannot exclude potential cell‐type–specific effects in vivo.

(B and C) Western blot analysis showed decreased biotinylated‐ACC in BD conditions in both KCs and DFs; levels of biotinylated PC and PCC/MCC were unchanged. (D and E) BD conditions increased ACC phosphorylation (p‐ACC/total ACC) in both cell types. n = 6 per group. Paired t‐test. SD = standard deviation.

Biotin Deficiency Increases ATP Production in Skin Cells

We next investigated how suppression of ACC, a regulator of fatty acid metabolism, might influence cellular bioenergetics. ACC plays a crucial role in converting acetyl‐CoA to malonyl‐CoA, an inhibitor of fatty acid oxidation [59]. Cellular energy requirements for maintaining homeostasis primarily rely on mitochondrial function, specifically oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), the primary source of ATP production. Using the Seahorse XF Mito Stress Test, we assessed real‐time OCRs and ECARs of KCs and DFs, following the sequential addition of oligomycin (an OXPHOS inhibitor), FCCP (an ionophore), and a combination of rotenone and antimycin A (OXPHOS inhibitors) (Figure 5A,C). Cells in BD media exhibited increased baseline and maximal OCR, indicating enhanced mitochondrial respiration (Figure 5B). ATP production and spare respiratory capacity were also significantly elevated or trended higher. In parallel, ECAR analysis showed increased glycolytic activity, including glycolytic capacity and reserve, suggesting that BD conditions enhance glycolytic ATP production as well (Figure 5D). These results indicate that biotin deficiency promotes glycolytic energy production independently of mitochondrial respiration and augments glycolytic activity in response to increased energy requirements. The energetic shift was visualized in cell energy maps derived from OCR and ECAR data, which demonstrated a more energetic phenotype in BD‐cultured cells compared to controls (Figure 5E). Metabolic potential, defined as the difference between baseline and stressed respiration or glycolysis, was also increased (Figure 5F).

Together, these findings suggest that biotin deficiency augments ATP production via both oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis in vitro. While this model represents chronic deprivation, the observed metabolic response supports a potential mechanism whereby biotin depletion at HCPs may lead to increased local ATP production in response to physical stimuli.

The Inactivation of Acetyl‐CoA Carboxylase at HCPs Contributes to Local Metabolic Reprogramming

To assess whether changes in ACC activity observed in vitro also occur in stimulated human skin, we performed western blot analysis on needled‐HCPs versus needled‐normal points. Biotinylated‐ACC levels were significantly reduced, and p‐ACC levels trended higher in needled‐HCPs, recapitulating the in vitro findings (Figure 6A–D). Similarly to our in vitro data, the changes in PC and PCC/MCC were not significant (Figure 6A,B). ACC1 and ACC2 are isoforms that regulate fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, respectively, via conversion of acetyl‐CoA to malonyl‐CoA [59, 60]. Reduced biotinylated‐ACC activity would be expected to suppress lipogenesis and enhance β‐oxidation, increasing flux through the TCA cycle and augmenting ATP production (Figure 6E). Similarly, glycolytic activity may also be elevated, as shown in our in vitro data. Based on these findings, we propose that physical stimulation at HCPs triggers a metabolic shift through reduced ACC activity and increased ATP production, though this model remains hypothetical. Acupuncture often involves stimuli (e.g., rotation, heat, or electrical pulses) that could elicit local energy demand [2], potentially triggering ATP release [61, 62, 63]. Extracellular ATP is rapidly degraded to adenosine, a key mediator of acupuncture's anti‐nociceptive effects [64].

Figure 6F presents a proposed model: (1) stimulation at HCPs reduces biotin levels; (2) ACC inactivation leads to increased ATP production; (3) ATP is released extracellularly and contributes to adenosine‐mediated signaling. We emphasize that this sequence is speculative and not directly tested in our ex vivo system.

Discussion

In this study, we provide evidence suggesting that changes in biotin levels play a role in the local metabolic response to acupuncture‐like stimulation. We identified regions of high conductivity, here referred to as HCPs, in surgically removed human abdominal skin. These regions exhibited transcriptional changes following needling that resembled previously described responses in anatomically defined murine acupoints [42], including activation of stress‐responsive pathways and metabolic alterations. Notably, we observed a decrease in biotin levels and an increase in ATP production capacity in skin cells following stimulation of HCPs. Although we cannot equate these ex vivo regions directly to classical in vivo acupoints, our findings support the notion that skin regions with distinct electrical properties may exhibit unique biochemical responsiveness.

Electrical conductivity has long been associated with acupoint detection, though its functional significance remains debated [37, 38]. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating high‐conductivity skin regions in isolated human tissue. While the biological meaning of conductivity differences remains unclear, our study provides an entry point into their molecular characterization. Structural differences between HCPs and adjacent regions were not observed (Figures 1B–D and 3D–F), suggesting that electrical heterogeneity may be due to biochemical or biophysical factors rather than gross anatomical features. This interpretation aligns with earlier reports linking conductivity to sweat gland activity, neural inputs, and connective tissue architecture [40, 65, 66]. Variability in conductivity between donors also supports the idea that these properties may be modulated by physiological or pathological states [67, 68, 69, 70, 71].

Importantly, we acknowledge that tracing the precise anatomical correlation between our HCPs and defined acupoints was not feasible in this ex vivo setting. Confirmation would require matched pre‐ and post‐excision mapping, which is technically challenging and remains a priority for future studies. Nevertheless, the similarity in transcriptional response between stimulated HCPs and anatomically identified murine acupoints [42] suggests a degree of functional overlap. It should be noted that differences in acupuncture effect among various acupoints have been reported [72], indicating varied responsiveness to needle stimulation. We have reframed our interpretation accordingly and emphasize that our model defines HCPs based on a physical property (conductivity), not on anatomical or therapeutic validation.

Our metabolomics analysis revealed a stimulation‐dependent decrease in biotin levels at HCPs. This was observed with both needling and heat treatment, implying a generalized response to physical stimuli. Although the mechanism of biotin depletion remains unresolved, RNA‐seq data showed modest increases in transporters such as SMVT and MCT1 (Data S2), suggesting possible changes in uptake or utilization. Further mechanistic studies will be necessary to determine whether local biotin loss results from increased consumption, redistribution, or breakdown.

Interestingly, biotin levels at baseline were higher in HCPs than in control points. This observation raises the possibility that baseline biotin enrichment may help define or contribute to the distinctive properties of HCPs. This finding warrants further investigation, as it could inform future efforts to rationalize the empirical mapping of acupoints. One speculative hypothesis is that local microbiota may contribute to biotin enrichment [73, 74], given that both skin hydration [75] and microbiota composition have been linked to conductivity [76, 77]. To our knowledge, the microbiome of human acupoints has not been characterized and represents a novel area for exploration.

Biotin, a water‐soluble vitamin, acts as a coenzyme for carboxylases, involved in skin physiology [44]. In our in vitro model, chronic biotin deprivation led to reduced ACC biotinylation and increased phosphorylation, consistent with reduced enzymatic activity. These findings align with earlier work showing rapid loss of biotinylated ACC in BD liver tissue [78]. Also, as dietary biotin intake significantly increases only biotinylated ACC2, but no other carboxylases in rat liver [79], ACC2 could be more sensitive to changes in biotin levels than other carboxylases. Our ex vivo experiments demonstrated similar patterns at HCPs following stimulation. We interpret these results to suggest that physical stimulation at HCPs reduces local biotin availability, leading to suppression of ACC activity and a metabolic shift toward increased fatty acid oxidation and ATP production.

ACC1 and ACC2, which require biotin for their catalytic roles, promote fatty acid synthesis and inhibit beta‐oxidation by carboxylating acetyl‐CoA to malonyl‐CoA, respectively [59, 80]. This was supported by our real‐time metabolic profiling in vitro, where BD skin cells displayed elevated mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic output. While our in vitro system reflects a sustained deficiency rather than an acute event, it provides proof‐of‐concept for how biotin loss might modulate skin metabolism. The proposed link between ATP production, release, and acupuncture signaling remains speculative in our model. Prior studies have shown that ATP is released from skin and connective tissue in response to physical stimulation [61] and that it is rapidly degraded to adenosine [64], which mediates analgesic effects via A1 receptors [11]. While mast cells and skeletal muscle cells have been identified as major sources of extracellular ATP in acupuncture [20, 21, 81, 82], our results suggest that KCs and fibroblasts may also contribute to this release. We propose a hypothetical model wherein stimulation at HCPs induces local ATP synthesis and release, potentially contributing to acupuncture's downstream effects. Further experiments are needed to test this sequence directly.

Our omics data do not identify which specific cell types within skin are driving these responses. Skin is composed of many cell types, and these cell populations can change depending on various conditions, such as aging [83]. Both KCs and fibroblasts demonstrated metabolic shifts in vitro, but immune, vascular, or neural cell types may also play important roles. As prior studies have noted differences in mast cell density at acupoints [19, 20], future studies employing single‐cell or spatial transcriptomics would be valuable in refining cell‐specific mechanisms.

Conclusions

In summary, we present novel findings that isolated human skin contains regions of elevated conductivity—HCPs—which display unique metabolic and transcriptomic responsiveness to physical stimulation. We propose that stimulation‐dependent biotin depletion at HCPs contributes to metabolic reprogramming and may underlie part of acupuncture's mechanism of action. While we do not claim that these HCPs are equivalent to in vivo acupoints, our study highlights a potential biochemical dimension to acupoint responsiveness and provides a foundation for future mechanistic investigation.
