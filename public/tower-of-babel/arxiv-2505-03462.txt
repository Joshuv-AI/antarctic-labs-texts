Non-relativistic quantum strings from gauged WZW models
José M Figueroa-O’Farrill, Girish S Vishwa

Abstract
. We construct non-relativistic quantum strings from gauged Wess–Zumino–Witten (WZW) models. We depart from the fact that Lie groups with a bi-invariant galilean structure can be seen as the quotient by a null central subgroup of a generalised Nappi–Witten group. We implement the quotient by a chiral null gauging. We use a particular free field realisation of the Nappi–Witten current algebra to compute the Virasoro BRST cohomology of the gauged WZW model, resulting in a closed string theory reminiscent of the bosonic Gomis–Ooguri string, but whose spectrum differs slightly between the holomorphic and antiholomorphic sectors.

The quarter century since the pioneering paper of Gomis and Ooguri

given by a metric (regardless the signature). Progress in this area
has been recently reviewed in

]
. As with much else
in the non-lorentzian approach to fundamental physics, one can obtain
models of non-lorentzian string theories via so-called
non-relativistic

,

,
7
,
8
]
or so-called ultra-local

[

,
10
,

,
12
,
13
,
14
]
limits of the Nambu–Goto or
Polyakov strings, but there are also constructions of non-relativistic
strings via null reduction of the Nambu–Goto
[
15
]

or Polyakov
[
16
,
17
]
actions, and also
intrinsic constructions on stringy Newton–Cartan geometries

[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. A paper
comparing some of these approaches is
[
21
]
. There
are other constructions of non-relativistic strings in the literature:
for example in
[
22
]
they are constructed via
Wess–Zumino terms on the Galilei group and
in
[
23
,
24
]
they are constructed
via sigma models on homogeneous spaces of stringy extended Galilei
and Newton–Hooke groups.

The aim of this paper is to exhibit yet a different construction of
non-relativistic quantum strings, inspired on the construction of
galilean spacetimes by null reduction of a lorentzian manifold

[
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Because strings
propagating on Lie groups are well understood as conformal field
theories of Wess–Zumino–Witten (WZW) type, we will take the
lorentzian manifold to be a Lie group with a bi-invariant lorentzian
metric having a null subgroup that we can gauge. Gauged WZW models
are two-dimensional conformal field theories amenable to a
quantum-mechanical treatment based on vertex operator algebras and
this allows for a wholly quantum mechanical treatment of the resulting
non-relativistic string theory.

Lie groups admitting a bi-invariant lorentzian metric were classified
(up to coverings) by Medina in
[
28
]
. This is roughly
equivalent to the classification of Lie algebras admitting an
ad-invariant lorentzian inner product, which we will refer to as

lorentzian Lie algebras
from now on. Every lorentzian Lie
algebra is either indecomposable or an orthogonal direct sum of an
indecomposable lorentzian Lie algebra with a Lie algebra admitting an
ad-invariant positive-definite inner product. We call these latter
Lie algebras
euclidean
from now on. The list of indecomposable
lorentzian Lie algebras is small: it is either
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
, whose
Killing form has lorentzian signature, or else it is a double
extension
[
29
]
(to be described below) of an
even-dimensional abelian euclidean Lie algebra by a one-dimensional
Lie algebra spanned by a (maximal rank) skew-symmetric derivation.
The paradigmatic example is the Nappi–Witten Lie algebra

[
30
]
(cf. Definition
5
) which is the double
extension of a two-dimensional abelian Lie algebra with a euclidean
inner product by the one-dimensional Lie algebra of skew-symmetric
derivations, and which will play a crucial role in this paper.

This paper is organised as follows. In
Section
2
we review the characterisation of Lie
algebras with ad-invariant galilean, carrollian and bargmannian
structures, following
[
31
]
, and review the
definition of the WZW model. It is there that we define the notion of

with WZW models on generalised Nappi–Witten Lie groups. In
Section
3
we review the gauging of WZW

free-field realisation of this Nappi–Witten current algebra. These
choices allow us to compute the null gauging BRST cohomology to arrive
at a conformal field theory, which when extended with matter fields in
order to reach the critical central charge, we interpret as a
non-relativistic string theory. We compute the Virasoro BRST
cohomology, which displays a slight heterosis between the holomorphic
and antiholomorphic sectors, but which nevertheless is reminiscent of
the closed Gomis–Ooguri bosonic string. Section
5

Appendix
C

cohomology proofs whose end results were only stated in
Section
4

Appendix
D

2.
Preliminaries

We start by recalling the basic notions of galilean, carrollian and
bargmannian Lie algebras (following
[
31
]
)
and how they are related, as well as the basic definition of a
WZW model.

2.1.
Galilean, carrollian and bargmannian structures on Lie groups

There is a rich geometric story to these structures (see, for example,
the recent review
[
32
]
). We are interested in
Lie groups admitting bi-invariant such geometric structures. Modulo
the usual ambiguity inherent in the Lie correspondence, we prefer to
work with their Lie algebras. Lie algebras with ad-invariant galilean
carrollian and bargmannian structures are intimately linked and, as
shown in
[
31
]
, there is a bijection (a
sort of duality) between galilean and carrollian Lie algebras,
mediated by bargmannian Lie algebras, which we now recall.

We start by recalling the notion of a metric Lie algebra, first
systematically introduced by Medina and Revoy
[
29
]
.

Definition 1
.

A
metric Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is a real Lie algebra admitting a
symmetric bilinear inner product
\langle-,-\rangle_{0}
that is
invariant under the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
on itself. That is, for
all
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
,

\langle\operatorname{ad}_{X}Y,Z\rangle_{0}+\langle Y,\operatorname{ad}_{X}Z\rangle_{0}=0;

equivalently,

\left<[Y,X],Z\right>=\left<Y,[X,Z]\right>.

Cartan’s semisimplicity criterion says that every semisimple Lie
algebra is metric relative to its Killing form

\left<X,Y\right>:=\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{ad}_{X}\operatorname{ad}_{Y}
. At the other extreme, given
any real Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
we may give the vector space

\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{*}
the structure of metric Lie algebra as follows:
\mathfrak{h}

acts on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
via the coadjoint representation, whereas
\mathfrak{h}^{*}

becomes an abelian ideal. The inner product is simply the dual
pairing between
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, which has split signature in this
case. This latter example is the simplest example of a double
extension, which we now recall.

Let
(\mathfrak{g}_{0},\left<-,-\right>_{0})
be a metric Lie algebra and let

\mathfrak{so}(\mathfrak{g}_{0})
denote the Lie algebra of skew-symmetric endomorphisms of

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a Lie algebra acting on
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
via skew-symmetric
derivations, with
\partial\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{so}(\mathfrak{g}_{0})
denoting the
corresponding Lie algebra homomorphism. This defines a skew-symmetric
bilinear map
\omega\colon\mathfrak{g}_{0}\times\mathfrak{g}_{0}\to\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by transposition; namely,

(2.1)

\omega(X,Y)(h)=\left<\partial(h)X,Y\right>_{0}.

The vector space
\mathfrak{g}:=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{*}
now becomes a Lie
algebra under the bracket

(2.2)

[(X,h,\alpha),(Y,k,\beta)]=\left([X,Y]_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}+\partial(h)Y-\partial(k)X,[h,k]_{\mathfrak{h}},\operatorname{ad}_{h}^{*}\beta-\operatorname{ad}_{h}^{*}\alpha+\omega(X,Y)\right)

for all
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
,
h,k\in\mathfrak{h}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.
Moreover,
\mathfrak{g}
is metric relative to the inner product

(2.3)

\left<(X,h,\alpha),(Y,k,\beta)\right>=\left<X,Y\right>_{0}+\alpha(k)+\beta(h)+\left<h,k\right>_{\mathfrak{h}},

where
\left<-,-\right>_{\mathfrak{h}}
is any invariant symmetric bilinear
form (not necessarily an inner product) on
\mathfrak{h}
. The metric Lie
algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\left<-,-\right>
is said to be a
double
extension
of the metric Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g}_{0},\left<-,-\right>_{0}
by

\mathfrak{h}
. If
\left<-,-\right>_{0}
has signature
(p,q)
and
\mathfrak{h}
has
dimension
r
, then
\left<-,-\right>
has signature
(p+r,q+r)
.

Given two metric Lie algebras, we can construct a third metric Lie
algebra by taking their orthogonal direct sum. A metric Lie algebra
which is not isomorphic to an orthogonal direct sum of metric Lie
algebras is said to be
indecomposable
. The fundamental theorem
of metric Lie algebras
[
29
, Théorème II]
says that the
class of metric Lie algebras is the smallest class which contains the
simple and one-dimensional Lie algebras and which is closed under the
operations of orthogonal direct sum and double extension.

It follows from this theorem and the above observation about the
signature of the inner product of a double extension, that a euclidean
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is the orthogonal direct sum

(2.4)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{s}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathfrak{s}_{n}\oplus\mathfrak{a},

where the
\mathfrak{s}_{i}
are compact simple Lie algebras, with inner product
given by a negative multiple of the Killing form, and
\mathfrak{a}
is a
euclidean abelian Lie algebra.

A precursor theorem of Medina’s
[
28
, Thm. 4.1]
states that
any lorentzian Lie algebra is either indecomposable or else the
orthogonal direct sum of an indecomposable lorentzian Lie algebra with
a euclidean Lie algebra. Furthermore an indecomposable lorentzian Lie
algebra is either
\mathfrak{sl}(2,R)
with a positive multiple of the Killing
form or a double extension of an even-dimensional euclidean abelian
Lie algebra by a one-dimensional Lie algebra acting via skew-symmetric
derivations. As we will recall below, these are necessarily
generalised Nappi–Witten Lie algebras.

With these prefatory remarks we can now define the notions of
bargmannian, galilean and carrollian Lie algebras, as used in this
paper.

Definition 2
.

A lorentzian metric Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be

bargmannian
if it admits a (nonzero) central null element
Z\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Since
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
has trivial centre, it follows that any
indecomposable bargmannian Lie algebra is a double extension of an
even-dimensional euclidean abelian Lie algebra by a one-dimensional
Lie algebra. A general bargmannian Lie algebra is then the orthogonal
direct sum of an indecomposable bargmannian Lie algebra with a
euclidean Lie algebra (see
[
31
, Theorem 5]

for a more precise statement).

Definition 3
.

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be
carrollian
if it admits a
(nonzero) central element
Z\in\mathfrak{g}
and a positive-semidefinite
ad-invariant symmetric bilinear form
\left<-,-\right>
whose
radical is one-dimensional and spanned by
Z
.

As shown in
[
31
, Prop. 2]
, a carrollian
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{car}}
is isomorphic to a central extension of a
positive-definite metric Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g}_{0},\left<-,-\right>_{0})
; that
is, we have a (typically non-split) short exact sequence

(2.5)

A Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be
galilean
if it admits a
(nonzero) ad-invariant
\tau\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and an ad-invariant
\gamma\in\odot^{2}\mathfrak{g}
which, as a symmetric bilinear form on

As shown in

where
(\mathfrak{g}_{0},\left<-,-\right>_{0})
is a euclidean Lie algebra and

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{barg}}
is a bargmannian Lie algebra obtained by
double-extending
(\mathfrak{g}_{0},\left<-,-\right>_{0})
by the one-dimensional
Lie algebra spanned by a skew-symmetric derivation
D_{0}
. The
underlying vector space of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{barg}}
is
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathbb{R}D\oplus\mathbb{R}Z
with Lie bracket given by

(2.8)

\begin{split}[X,Y]&=[X,Y]_{0}+\left<D_{0}X,Y\right>_{0}Z\\
[D,X]&=D_{0}X\\
[Z,-]&=0,\end{split}

for all

, with
[-,-]_{0}
the Lie bracket on

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
. Via an automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{barg}}
, we may bring the

\left<-,-\right>
to the form

(2.9)

\left<X,Y\right>=\left<X,Y\right>_{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left<D,Z\right>=1,

for all
X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and with all other inner products zero. The
carrollian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{car}}
is the ideal
Z^{\perp}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{barg}}
and the galilean Lie algebra is the quotient

\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{gal}}=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{barg}}/\mathbb{R}Z
and they inherit their
respective structures from those of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathrm{barg}}
.

Indecomposable bargmannian Lie algebras are generalised Nappi–Witten
Lie algebras
\mathfrak{nw}_{2\ell+2}
, defined explicitly as follows.
Consider a symplectic vector space
V
spanned by

P_{1},\dots,P_{2\ell}
with symplectic structure
\omega\in\wedge^{2}V^{*}
with
\omega(P_{i},P_{j}):=\omega_{ij}
. Then the underlying vector
space of
\mathfrak{nw}_{2\ell+2}
is
V\oplus\mathbb{R}D\oplus\mathbb{R}Z
, with
nonzero Lie brackets given by

(2.10)

[P_{i},P_{j}]=\omega_{ij}Z\qquad\text{and}\qquad[D,P_{i}]=\omega_{ij}P_{j},

and with the ad-invariant lorentzian inner product with nonzero entries

(2.11)

\left<P_{i},P_{j}\right>=\delta_{ij}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left<D,Z\right>=1.

Definition 5
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{nw}_{2\ell+2}
is a
generalised Nappi–Witten algebra
.

The justification for the name is that the special case
\ell=1
was
used by Nappi and Witten to build the first WZW model based on a
non-abelian non-semisimple Lie group
[
30
]
.

2.2.
WZW Models

A WZW model on a Lie group
G
admitting a bi-invariant metric is a
two-dimensional field theory described by the action

(2.12)

S[g]=\int_{\Sigma}\langle g^{-1}\partial g,g^{-1}\bar{\partial}g\rangle+\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\tilde{g}^{*}\omega,

where
\Sigma
is an orientable Riemann surface,
g\colon\Sigma\rightarrow G
is a smooth map,
\tilde{\Sigma}
is some smooth
manifold with boundary
\partial\tilde{\Sigma}=\Sigma
and

\tilde{g}\colon\tilde{\Sigma}\to G
extends
g
. In addition,

g^{-1}\partial g
and
g^{-1}\bar{\partial}g
are the
(1,0)
- and

(0,1)
- components, respectively, of the pull-back
g^{*}\vartheta
of
the left-invariant Maurer–Cartan one-form on
G
and
\omega
is the
Cartan
3
-form (see below) on
G
. Finally,
\langle-,-\rangle
stands both for the wedge product of one-forms and the
ad-invariant inner product on
\mathfrak{g}
. The extension of
g\colon\Sigma\to G
to
\tilde{g}\colon\tilde{\Sigma}\to G
is
topologically unobstructed for any Lie group
G
, because of the fact
that the second homotopy group of any Lie group is trivial.

The first term in (
2.12
) is known as
the
principal chiral model (PCM)
whereas the second term is known
as the
Wess–Zumino (WZ)
term, which is the integral over

\tilde{\Sigma}
of the pull-back of the Cartan 3-form
\omega

on
G
. In terms of the (left-invariant) Maurer–Cartan one-form

\vartheta\in\Omega^{1}(G;\mathfrak{g})
,

(2.13)

\omega=\tfrac{1}{6}\langle\vartheta,[\vartheta,\vartheta]\rangle=-\tfrac{1}{3}\langle\vartheta,d\vartheta\rangle,

where we have used the structure equation

(2.14)

d\vartheta+\tfrac{1}{2}[\vartheta,\vartheta]=0.

Hence, we may write (
2.12
) in its more
familiar form (using
g^{*}\vartheta=g^{-1}dg
for any matrix Lie
group G)

(2.15)

S[g]=\int_{\Sigma}\left<g^{-1}\partial g,g^{-1}\bar{\partial}g\right>+\tfrac{1}{6}\int_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\left<\tilde{g}^{-1}d\tilde{g},[\tilde{g}^{-1}d\tilde{g},\tilde{g}^{-1}d\tilde{g}]\right>.

At face value, the WZ term seems to depend on
\tilde{g}\colon\tilde{\Sigma}\to G
, but its variation results in local field
equations for the field
g\colon\Sigma\to G
. The quantum theory does
not depend on
\tilde{\Sigma}
either, provided one normalises the
inner product in such a way that exponential of the action in the path
integral changes by an integer multiple of
2\pi

[
33
]
.

The WZW action
S[g]
is invariant under the following transformations:

•

conformal transformations

•

the isometry group of
G
with its bi-invariant metric. This is
typically
G\times G
, but since the left and right multiplication
by the centre coincide, when
G
has nontrivial centre, we must
quotient by the centre, resulting in
(G\times G)/Z
, with
Z\subset G
the centre.

•

the infinite-dimensional group
G(z)\times G(\bar{z})
, acts by

g(z,\bar{z})\mapsto\Omega(z)g(z,\bar{z})\bar{\Omega}(\bar{z})^{-1}
,
where again constant
\Omega
and
\bar{\Omega}
in the centre act
in the same way, so we should quotient by
Z
, resulting in

\left(G(z)\times G(\bar{z})\right)/Z
.

The final "enhanced" symmetry only exists for our choice of
normalisation of the WZW action. The currents associated to the

G(z)\times G(\bar{z})
symmetry satisfy an affine version of the
Lie algebra of G, which is then encapsulated by the operator product
expansions (OPEs) of these currents in the corresponding quantum
CFT. This lays the bedrock of how WZW models are generally studied.

3.
Gauged WZW models

The study of WZW models with some subgroup of its isometry group being
gauged has been of interest for various reasons, but most notably due
to how this procedure gives rise to coset CFTs as in the GKO
construction
[
34
]
.

However, one cannot gauge any subgroup of the isometry group of the
WZW model. Additionally, the type of gauging we perform in this paper
does not lead to a coset construction. We address these points in this
section, along with an explicit discussion of the gauging procedure
for our purposes.

Throughout this section,
G
refers to a generic Lie group and
{B}
refers to
an indecomposable bargmannian Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{nw}_{2\ell+2}
.

3.1.
What can we gauge?

There are certain (cohomological) restrictions

[
35
,
36
,
37
]
as to what
subgroups of the isometry group
G\times G
we can gauge, summarised in the
following proposition.

Proposition 6
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with a bi-invariant metric and
\mathfrak{g}
its Lie
algebra. Let
\left<-,-\right>
denote the ad-invariant inner
product on
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
H\subset G\times G
be a Lie subgroup with
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
. The embedding
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}
defines
two Lie algebra homomorphisms
l,r\colon\mathfrak{h}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
by
composing with the cartesian projections. Then
H
can be gauged if
and only if

l^{*}\langle-,-\rangle=r^{*}\langle-,-\rangle;

equivalently,

\langle l(X),l(Y)\rangle=\langle r(X),r(Y)\rangle\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{h}.

Such subgroups are said to be
anomaly-free
.

The two most obvious anomaly-free subgroups are:

(1)

l=r\neq 0
, which corresponds to
diagonal gauging
and
give rise to the coset models mentioned earlier;

(2)

and
l=0

or

r=0
, which corresponds to
null chiral gauging
.

For the WZW model on
{B}
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{nw}_{2\ell+2}
, where the subgroup
H
is one-dimensional and
generated by a null element
Z\in\mathfrak{g}
, any embedding

\mathfrak{h}\in Z\mapsto(\alpha Z,\beta Z)\in\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
, for

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, satisfies the condition in
Proposition
6
. Indeed,

\left<l(Z),l(Z)\right>=\left<\alpha Z,\alpha Z\right>=\alpha^{2}\left<Z,Z\right>=0
and, similarly,

\left<r(Z),r(Z)\right>=\beta^{2}\left<Z,Z\right>=0
. But,
moreover, since
Z
is also central, it turns out that for any
\alpha\neq\beta
the gaugings are equivalent. Without loss of generality
we will take
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
in our treatment.

3.2.
How do we gauge?

Consider a subgroup
H\subset G\times G
that obeys
Proposition
6
.
We treat the null chiral gauging of each of the two terms in
(
2.12
) separately. The PCM can be
gauged straightforwardly by minimal coupling; this involves modifying
the exterior derivative as
d\to d+\mathbf{A}
, where

\mathbf{A}=A+\bar{A}
is a gauge field that can be interpreted as a
one-form on
G
with values in
\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

The WZ term is not as straightforward to gauge. There are certain
obstructions to gauging
[
35
]
that are encapsulated by
equivariant cohomology. The key result from

[
36
,
38
]
(also see

[
39
]
) is that the WZ term can be gauged if
and only if one can extend
\omega\in\Omega^{3}(G)
to an equivariant
closed form
{\varOmega}
. It turns out that using an algebraic model
of equivariant cohomology, known as the Cartan model, makes it
feasible to perform this procedure explicitly. We demonstrate this
procedure here, but we refer the reader to

[
39
]
for more details.

Let
\{X_{a}\}_{a=1}^{\dim H}
be a basis for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}

4.
The BRST treatment

This section presents the step-by-step implementation of the null
chiral gauging constraint via the BRST formalism for 2d CFTs. To do
this, we need to bring our theory to the desired form by quantising
the gauged action (

making use of standard manipulations such as those in

[
40

we choose the holomorphic gauge
\bar{A}=0
. This gives rise to the Faddeev–Popov determinant

\mathcal{Z}=\int\mathcal{D}g(\det\bar{\partial})\exp{\left(-S[g]\right)},

where

(4.3)

\det\bar{\partial}=\int\mathcal{D}B\mathcal{D}C\exp{\left(-\int_{\Sigma}\langle B,\bar{\partial}C\rangle\right)}.

We are therefore left with

(4.4)

\mathcal{Z}=\int\mathcal{D}g\mathcal{D}B\mathcal{D}C\exp{\left(-S[g]-\int_{\Sigma}\langle B,\bar{\partial}C\rangle\right)}.

The expression in (

4.5.
Summary

Overall, the resulting closed string theory can be interpreted as a
special case of the bosonic Gomis–Ooguri string. Recall that the
Gomis–Ooguri string spectrum was originally shown to be the Fock
module of 24 free bosons in euclidean space
[
1
]
, up to
a choice of picture coming from a choice of
\beta\gamma
vacuum. This
is shown explicitly in Appendix
C.2
. We
also show that for the purposes of computing the spectrum (i.e., BRST
cohomology), the choice of the
c=24
matter sector that appears does
not matter. Starting with the low-energy limit of the bosonic string
and taking the appropriate limits forces the appearance of the 24 free
bosons. However, if we instead chose to start with the worldsheet
theory, the 24 free bosons simply go along for the ride and do not
alter the BRST cohomology calculations. From this perspective, any

c=24
CFT could have been chosen, but whether or not they admit the
sought after physical interpretation is a separate issue.

In that sense, coming back to the model we have constructed, it
resembles the Gomis–Ooguri string but with an altered
c=24
matter
sector: 24 free bosons in the holomorphic sector,
and 22 free bosons and a
\widetilde{\beta}\widetilde{\gamma}
-system in the
anti-holomorphic sector (note that the holomorphic sector seems to
admit the same galilean symmetry that the Gomis–Ooguri string
does). The most peculiar feature of this model is the differing
holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors, but it is consistent with
the fact that this rendition of a Gomis–Ooguri-like string was
obtained from the null chiral gauging of a WZW model; the chirality of
the gauging procedure manifests itself in the full string theory, as
one would expect.

On that note, we address the free field realisation (
4.21
) not being conformal due to the presence of the

\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{\partial}\bar{\widetilde{\beta}}
term. The key reason why the
c=24

matter sectors in Propositions
9
and

10
are in no way reduced or changed due
to the cohomology calculations is precisely because the BRST
cohomology of both sectors is isomorphic to that of
d_{0}
. Hence, any
part of the embedding (
4.21
) that enters the

c=24
part of the full string theory goes along for the ride and
remains unaltered. In our case, this includes the

\bar{\widetilde{\beta}}\bar{\widetilde{\gamma}}
-system in the anti-holomorphic sector. This is
exactly why the extra term in
T^{\mathrm{sug}}
does not alter the Virasoro BRST
cohomology.

Finally, we also note that for the choice of free field realisation of the
Nappi–Witten CFT given in (
4.21
), the
computation of the null gauging cohomology followed by the computation
of Virasoro BRST cohomology is equivalent to the computation of the
BRST cohomology (or rather, semi-infinite cohomology) of

\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=0}:=\operatorname{Vir}\ltimes\widehat{\mathfrak{u}(1)}
, where
\widehat{\mathfrak{u}(1)}
is affine
\mathfrak{u}(1)
.
Furthermore, the cohomology computations
are in keeping with
[
45
, Theorem 4.1]
,
which means that the computation of the semi-infinite cohomology of

\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=0}
reduces to that of
\operatorname{Vir}

modules (i.e., free field realisations) being carefully chosen. Both
statements are by no means true in general; they hold for our choice
of free field realisation as
(\widetilde{\beta},\widetilde{\gamma},B,C)
arrange
themselves into a “quartet” (see Appendix
B
). Nonetheless, it is an interesting observation that
non-relativistic string models can be realised as
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=0}

field theories.

5.
Discussion

In this paper, we presented an approach by which one can construct
strings propagating in galilean backgrounds using null, chirally
gauged WZW models on generalised Nappi–Witten Lie groups. In
particular, this approach does not rely on taking limits on the target
space or the worldsheet. We presented a large class of examples of WZW
models using which one could take the approach outlined in this
paper. For the special case of the Nappi–Witten model, the null
reduction of its target space is the three-dimensional galilean-AdS
spacetime, as will be shown in Appendix
D
.

The null chiral gauging of the Nappi–Witten model was then performed
using CFT techniques at the level of the worldsheet, as is standard
practice when studying WZW models. A convenient choice of free field
realisation was used to simplify the computations drastically. The
resulting gauged WZW model was then used to a construct a “full” string
theory by adding to it some matter CFTs with the appropriate central
charges needed to gauge the worldsheet Virasoro symmetry. The final
string theory that we constructed can be interpreted as a closed
“Gomis–Ooguri-like” string that displays a slight heterosis between
the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors.

Given the choice of free field realisation (
4.21
) for the Nappi–Witten CFT, our full string theory can be
interpreted as a
\operatorname{Vir}\ltimes\widehat{\mathfrak{u}(1)}\cong\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=0}

field theory. Hence, our work demonstrates a possible string-theoretic
interpretation of
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=0}
field theories, which
complements the tensionless string interpretation of

\mathfrak{bms}\cong\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=-1}
field theories. For
the sake of completeness, it is also worth mentioning the emergence of

\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda=-2}
appearing as a symmetry algebra in the hybrid
formalism of the minimal tension (
k=1
) string in
\operatorname{AdS}_{3}\times S^{3}

[
46
,
47
]
. The fact that the one-parameter
family of Lie algebras
\widehat{\mathfrak{g}}_{\lambda}
appear as symmetry algebras in
different non-relativistic and tensionless string settings for
different values of the parameter
\lambda
could be hinting at a
deeper, yet undiscovered connection between these theories, though as
it stands, their relationships do seem circumstantial.

5.1.
Comments on geometry

The geometric motivation for this work involved making a choice of
lorentzian Lie group with a null isometry. We then performed the
necessary manipulations and simplifications on the resulting conformal
field theory which we obtained from quantisation. However, it is worth
addressing that some of these simplifying steps led us
further away from the geometrical picture with which we started. For
example, as we mentioned in Section
4
, we chose to work with a different real form of
the Nappi–Witten Lie algebra, whose ad-invariant inner product has
split signature
(2,2)
instead of
(3,1)
. Hence, we expect any
string models we have constructed to admit pseudo-galilean symmetry
rather than a strictly galilean one. In fact, this seems to be
encapsulated by the differing holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors
of our full string theory. The presence of a non-trivial

(\widetilde{\beta},\widetilde{\gamma})
spectrum in the anti-holomorphic sector should
contribute a lorentzian signature to the metric of any background
spacetime in which this string theory can be interpreted as
propagating. An intuitive way to infer this is from the embedding of
two lorentzian bosons
\partial\phi^{\pm}
into
(\widetilde{\beta},\widetilde{\gamma})
given by equations
(
5.2
) and (
5.3
).
The lorentzian metric on
(\partial\phi^{+},\partial\phi^{-})
is realised as the
dual pairing on
(\widetilde{\beta},\widetilde{\gamma})
, encapsulated in their OPEs.

It is worth making a few comments on this simple step of
complexification that we so routinely perform for the sake of
mathematical elegance and convenience. Indeed, it is common practice
to complexify the Nappi–Witten algebra in a variety of different
settings
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
and bring it to the form given in
(
4.12
). Even when studying the Lie
groups associated to the generalised Nappi–Witten algebra
\mathfrak{nw}_{2\ell+2}
in (
4.12
), this
confusion is prevalent. There is little distinction made between these
split signature
(\ell+1,\ell+1)
Lie algebras and the centrally extended
euclidean algebra with Lorentzian signature
(1,2\ell+1)
, and it has
become common practice to go between them. At first glance, this
simply seems like going between two real forms of the same complex Lie
algebra. However, a lack of consistency in this practice is observed,
particularly since the desirable Lorentzian signature of one real form
of the generalised Nappi–Witten Lie algebra is used in conjunction with the diagonalisability of

\operatorname{ad}J
and triangular decomposition
(cf. (
4.12
)) of the other real form

[
48
,
49
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
. Furthermore, it is the representation theory
of the complex Nappi–Witten Lie algebra that has been studied in
detail
[
41
,
42
,
56
]
, while the study of real representations of
real Lie algebras is much rarer in general. We hope that our work
serves as a reminder of the importance of this distinction in
physics. That being said, our work merely establishes a proof of
concept for the method of building non-relativistic strings as gauged
WZW models.

On the other hand, it would be worth noting the relevance of string
models on
(p>1,q)
backgrounds. Our work has inadvertently
demonstrated the emergence of string models that resemble the
Gomis–Ooguri string from gauged WZW models of strings propagating on

(2,2)
backgrounds. Other signature
(p,q)
models have also appeared
in the literature earlier, such as the WZW model for the galilean
conformal algebra
[

]
with signature
(3,3)
and
WZW models for the unique indecomposable, nilpotent 5- and
6-dimensional metric Lie algebras
[
58
]
.

or suggested by our results:

•

galilean NSR strings.

The interpretation of other non-Lorentzian string theories as gauged WZW models is very much dependent on the choice of string theory. While it may be true that other string theories with galilean target spaces admit formulations as gauged WZW models on bargammanian geometries, this is not the case for string theories with carrollian target spaces. This is because a carrollian structure is a restriction (to a null hypersurface) of a bargmannian structure, while a galilean structure is a null reduction (i.e., quotient) of a bargmannian one. Unlike the latter, the former procedure cannot be realised as a gauging procedure of a WZW model.
On the other hand, the techniques we used here to study the string worldsheet quantum mechanically can most certainly be applied to other non-Lorentzian strings. We have papers currently in preparation on the spectrum of the bosonic ambitwistor string (Carrollian worldsheet) and the Carrollian string (Carrollian worldsheet and target space).

Appendix B
The Kugo–Ojima quartet mechanism

B.1.
Elementary version

The simplest way to describe the KO mechanism is as follows. Consider
two sets of creation and annihilation operators,
(a^{\dagger},a)
and

(b^{\dagger},b)
, the former bosonic and the latter fermionic, obeying
the following non-trivial (anti-)commutation relations:

(B.1)

[a,a^{\dagger}]=1\iff aa^{\dagger}=1+a^{\dagger}a\quad[b,b^{\dagger}]=1\iff bb^{\dagger}=1-b^{\dagger}b

These act on
V
, the vector space of states generated by the creation
operators
5
5

5

To clarify, the dagger here does not denote
Hermitian conjugation. It is simply notation to denote a creation
operator.

a^{\dagger}
and
b^{\dagger}
acting on a vacuum state

\ket{0}
which obeys

(B.2)

a\ket{0}=b\ket{0}=0.

Let
Q=a^{\dagger}b
. It follows that
Q^{2}=0
and we would like
compute its cohomology. We may try to exhibit a smaller
quasi-isomorphic complex by somehow ruling out states that are
manifestly
Q
-exact. One way to find manifestly
Q
-exact states is
to construct a diagonalisable endomorphism
\Phi\in\operatorname{End}V
such that

(B.3)

\Phi=Q\varphi+\varphi Q,

for some
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}V
. By construction,
\Phi
commutes with
Q

(i.e.,
[Q,\Phi]=0
), so
Q
preserves
\Phi
-eigenspaces. This means
that the complex breaks up into a direct sum of subcomplexes
corresponding to the different eigenvalues of
\Phi
. We claim that
the cohomology resides in
\ker\Phi
. Indeed, consider any

Q
-cocycle
\ket{\psi}
which is also an eigenstate of
\Phi

\lambda\neq 0

(B.4)

\Phi\ket{\psi}=\lambda\ket{\psi}=(Q\varphi+\varphi Q)\ket{\psi}=Q\varphi\ket{\psi},

so that

(B.5)

\ket{\psi}=Q\left(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\varphi\ket{\psi}\right)

is actually a
Q
-coboundary. This means that the cohomology of

can be calculated from the subcomplex
\ker\Phi
.

Indeed, if a suitable choice of
\Phi\in\operatorname{End}V
is available, this

Q
-cohomology
drastically. With this in mind, we define
K\vcentcolon=b^{\dagger}a
,

\begin{split}\mathbf{N}\vcentcolon=&QK+KQ\\

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{N}(a^{\dagger}\ket{0})=a^{\dagger}(1+a^{\dagger}a)\ket{0}=a^{\dagger}\ket{0}\\
\mathbf{N}(b^{\dagger}\ket{0})=b^{\dagger}(1-b^{\dagger}b)\ket{0}=b^{\dagger}\ket{0}\\
\mathbf{N}(b^{\dagger})^{l}(a^{\dagger})^{k}\ket{0}=(k+l)(b^{\dagger})^{l}(a^{\dagger})^{k}\ket{0}.\end{gathered}

Let

\omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mu_{1}&&&&\\

&&0&-\mu_{2}&&\\
&&\mu_{2}&0&&\\

We may parametrise the group by
(x^{1},\dots x^{2\ell},u,v)
where a
generic group element takes the form

(D.2)

The pull-back to the parameter space of the left-invariant Maurer–Cartan one-form is

(D.3)

g^{-1}dg=duD+dvZ+\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(-uD)}\exp(-x^{i}P_{i})d\exp(x^{i}P_{i}).

The derivative of the exponential map is given by (see, e.g.,
[
61

(D.4)

\exp(-x^{i}P_{i})d\exp(x^{i}P_{i})={D}(\operatorname{ad}_{-x^{i}P_{i}})(dx^{i}P_{i}),

where

(D.5)

{D}(z)=\frac{1-\exp(-z)}{z}=1-\tfrac{1}{2}z+\mathcal{O}(z^{2}).

Since
\operatorname{ad}^{2}_{P_{i}}=0

(D.6)

\exp(-x^{i}P_{i})d\exp(x^{i}P_{i})=dx^{i}P_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}[-x^{i}P_{i},dx^{j}P_{j}]=dx^{i}P_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{i}dx^{j}\omega_{ij}Z.

Substituting (
D.6

g^{-1}dg=\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(-uD)}dx^{i}P_{i}+duD+\left(dv+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{i}dx^{j}\omega_{ij}\right)Z.

Next, we use that
\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(-uD)}=\exp(-u\operatorname{ad}_{D})
. Note that because

\operatorname{ad}_{D}P_{i}=\omega_{ij}P_{j},

\operatorname{ad}_{D}
is block diagonal. Let us concentrate on a
single block, say the block spanned by
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
. Then

(D.9)

\operatorname{ad}_{D}P_{i}=\mu_{1}\epsilon_{ij}P_{j}\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\operatorname{ad}_{D}^{2}P_{i}=-\mu_{1}^{2}P_{i}.

Exponentiating,

(D.10)

\exp(-u\operatorname{ad}_{D})(dx^{1}P_{1}+dx^{2}P_{2})=\left(\cos(u\mu_{1})dx^{1}+\sin(u\mu_{1})dx^{2}\right)P_{1}+\left(\cos(u\mu_{1})dx^{2}-\sin(u\mu_{1})dx^{1}\right)P_{2}.

Putting all the blocks together we arrive at

(D.11)

g^{-1}dg=duD+\left(dv+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{i}dx^{j}\omega_{ij}\right)Z\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\left(\cos(u\mu_{i})dx^{2i-1}+\sin(u\mu_{i})dx^{2i}\right)P_{2i-1}+\left(\cos(u\mu_{i})dx^{2i}-\sin(u\mu_{i})dx^{2i-1}\right)P_{2i}\right).

Finally, using (

When
\ell=1

\mu_{1}=1

(D.13)

\eta=2dudv+(x^{1}dx^{2}-x^{2}dx^{1})du+(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}.

We now consider the reduction of this four-dimensional lorentzian Lie

simply translates the coordinate

alone.

G

\alpha,\beta\in\Omega^{1}(M)

\gamma(\alpha,\beta)\in C^{\infty}(M)
be defined by

(D.14)

\pi^{*}\gamma(\alpha,\beta)=\eta((\pi^{*}\alpha)^{\sharp},(\pi^{*}\beta)^{\sharp}).

Explicitly, we write

(D.15)

\eta=du\otimes dv+dv\otimes du+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{1}(dx^{2}\otimes du+du\otimes dx^{2})-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}(dx^{1}\otimes du+du\otimes dx^{1})+dx^{1}\otimes dx^{1}+dx^{2}\otimes dx^{2}

and compute, in addition to
\partial_{v}^{\flat}=du

(D.16)

\partial_{u}^{\flat}=dv+\tfrac{1}{2}(x^{1}dx^{2}-x^{2}dx^{1}),\quad\partial_{x^{1}}^{\flat}=dx^{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}du\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{x^{2}}^{\flat}=dx^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{1}du.

This allows us to invert
\flat

(D.17)

(du)^{\sharp}=\partial_{v},\quad(dx^{1})^{\sharp}=\partial_{x^{1}}+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}\partial_{v}\quad\text{and}\quad(dx^{2})^{\sharp}=\partial_{x^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{1}\partial_{v}

in addition to a more complicated expression for
(dv)^{\sharp}

(D.18)

\gamma=\partial_{x^{1}}\otimes\partial_{x^{1}}+\partial_{x^{2}}\otimes\partial_{x^{2}}.

D.2.
Null reduction of split generalised Nappi–Witten group

In the bulk of the paper we used a different real form of the
complexified Nappi–Witten Lie algebra in which the ad-invariant inner
product has split signature. A basis is now given by
P_{i}^{+},P_{i}^{-},D,Z
, where
i=1,\dots,\ell
. The nonzero Lie brackets are now

(D.21)

[D,P_{i}^{\pm}]=\pm\mu_{i}P_{i}^{\pm}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[P_{i}^{+},P_{j}^{-}]=\mu_{i}\delta_{ij}Z,

where there is no summation in the second expression. The
ad-invariant inner product is given by

(D.22)

\left<P_{i}^{+},P_{j}^{-}\right>=\delta_{ij}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left<D,Z\right>=1.

We parametrise the group with null coordinates
(x^{i},y^{i},u,v)
,
with
i=1,\dots,\ell
, where the generic group element is

(D.23)

g:=g(x^{i},y^{i},u,v)=\exp(x^{i}P_{i}^{+}+y^{i}P_{i}^{-})\exp(uD)\exp(vZ).

The pull-back of the left-invariant Maurer–Cartan one-form is given
by

(D.24)

g^{-1}dg=dvZ+duD+\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(-uD)}\exp(-x^{i}P^{+}_{i}-y^{i}P^{-}_{i})d\exp(x^{i}P^{+}_{i}+y^{i}P^{-}_{i}).

As before, we may calculate

(D.25)

\exp(-x^{i}P^{+}_{i}-y^{i}P^{-}_{i})d\exp(x^{i}P^{+}_{i}+y^{i}P^{-}_{i})=dx^{i}P^{+}_{i}+dy^{i}P^{-}_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}(x^{i}dy^{j}-y^{i}dx^{j})\mu_{i}Z

and

(D.26)

\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(-uD)}\left(dx^{i}P^{+}_{i}+dy^{i}P^{-}_{i}\right)=e^{-u\mu_{i}}dx^{i}P^{+}_{i}+e^{u\mu_{i}}dy^{i}P^{-}_{i},

so that

(D.27)

g^{-1}dg=\left(dv+\tfrac{1}{2}(x^{i}dy^{j}-y^{i}dx^{j})\delta_{ij}\right)Z+duD+e^{-u\mu_{i}}dx^{i}P^{+}_{i}+e^{u\mu_{i}}dy^{i}P^{-}_{i}.

The bi-invariant metric on the split generalised Nappi–Witten group
is then

(D.28)

\eta=\left<g^{-1}dg,g^{-1}dg\right>=2dudv+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu_{i}(x^{i}dy^{i}-y^{i}dx^{i})du+2\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}dx^{i}dy^{i}.

For
\ell=1
, we may set
\mu_{1}=1
by a reciprocal rescaling of

u
and
v
and arrive at

(D.29)

\eta=2dudv+(xdy-ydx)du+2dxdy

relative to null coordinates
(u,v,x,y)
.

The four-dimensional split Nappi–Witten Lie algebra can be understood
either as a double extension of the trivial Lie algebra by the nonabelian
two-dimensional Lie algebra or, equivalently, as an iterated double
extension of the trivial Lie algebra by one-dimensional Lie algebras.

In the latter description, we consider an abelian two-dimensional
lorentzian Lie algebra. Relative to a Witt (a.k.a. ligthcone) frame
we may view the Lie
algebra as
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, where
\mathfrak{h}
is a one-dimensional Lie
algebra and the dual pairing between
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
provides the
(trivially ad-invariant) lorentzian inner product. Let
\mathfrak{h}=\mathbb{R}P_{+}

and
\mathfrak{h}^{*}=\mathbb{R}P_{-}
with
\left<P_{+},P_{-}\right>=1
. Let
D
be the
skew-symmetric derivation defined by
DP_{\pm}=\pm P_{\pm}
. We then
double extend
\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{h}^{*}
by the one-dimensional Lie algebra

\mathbb{R}D
acting via
[D,P_{\pm}]=\pm P_{\pm}
and letting
Z
span the
dual of
\mathbb{R}D
, we have that
[P_{+},P_{-}]=Z
and
\left<D,Z\right>=1
.

In the former description, we let
\mathfrak{a}
be the two-dimensional
non-abelian Lie algebra with basis
X,Y
and bracket
[X,Y]=Y
. Let

\mathfrak{a}^{*}
denote the dual with canonical dual basis
\alpha,\beta
. The
double extension of the trivial Lie algebra by
\mathfrak{a}
is the semidirect
product
\mathfrak{a}\ltimes\mathfrak{a}^{*}
with
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
an abelian ideal. The action of

\mathfrak{a}
on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
is via the coadjoint representation:

[X,\alpha]=\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{X}\alpha=0,\quad[Y,\alpha]=\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{Y}\alpha=0,\quad[X,\beta]=\operatorname{ad}^{*}_{X}\beta=-\beta\quad\text{and}\quad[Y,\beta]=\operatorname{ad}_{Y}^{*}\beta=\alpha.

Then letting
X=D
,
Y=P_{+}
,
\alpha=Z
and
\beta=P_{-}

provides the desired isomorphism with the split Nappi–Witten Lie
algebra.
