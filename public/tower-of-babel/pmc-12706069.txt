Optimizing thermoelectric energy harvesting using deep reinforcement learning for dynamic energy management and system efficiency
Authors: Chirayu Nilesh Chaudhari, N J Rtamanyu, Naga Sai Shreya Kunda, K C Pranave, Mayank Pandey, S Sruthi, Manju Khanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBy addressing the drawbacks of static optimization techniques, this research seeks to improve the dynamic energy management of thermoelectric generators (TEGs). Finding the best deep reinforcement learning (DRL) algorithm to maximize energy distribution, prolong battery life, and boost system efficiency in the face of the variable conditions present in waste heat recovery systems is the goal. The TEG system was modeled using the Markov decision process and implemented in a computer-simulated environment. Three advanced DRL algorithms were used: soft actor-critic (SAC), proximal policy optimization (PPO), and deep Q-networks (DQN); which were trained to act as intelligent controllers. The performance of each algorithm was systematically evaluated and compared using key metrics, including average cumulative reward, battery health, system efficiency, and a novel metric termed the energy fulfillment rate, which measures the ability to meet demand while storing surplus energy. The comparative analysis revealed a critical trade-off between maximizing performance and ensuring hardware longevity. The SAC algorithm demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving the highest average reward (− 7.03) and energy fulfillment rate (22.84%). However, the A2C, DDPG, and PPO algorithms all achieved a perfect average battery health of 100.00%, highlighting their superior capability for preserving system longevity, albeit with slightly lower rewards. The DQN algorithm consistently showed the least effective performance across all metrics, particularly in maintaining battery health (60.73%). The SAC algorithm is the most suitable of the methods tested for dynamically managing TEG systems. Its underlying principle of maximization of entropy enables a better exploration of control strategies, leading to a better balance between immediate energy dispatch and long-term storage goals. Introduction

Waste heat represents a viable and abundant source of energy, offering significant potential for clean energy generation. Substantial heat is produced across industrial processes, transportation systems, and residential operations. This heat is lost energy when it is discharged into the environment without being used, which lowers system efficiency and exacerbates climate change. Given the growing global energy demands and the pressing need to switch to sustainable energy sources, addressing this issue is essential.

One promising method for catching waste heat and turning it into electrical energy is thermoelectric energy harvesting1, which can improve energy efficiency and reduce hazardous emissions. This procedure depends on the Seebeck effect2, which explains how a temperature differential across a material can cause electrical current to be generated3. Heat-to-electricity conversion from various sources is made possible by thermoelectric generators (TEGs)4, which are the fundamental technology in this field. TEGs are incredibly dependable and adaptable due to their small size, lack of moving parts, and ability to function in harsh environments.

Conversion efficiency has significantly increased as a result of recent developments in thermoelectric materials, especially innovative semiconductor formulations. Due to these advancements, TEGs can now be used in large-scale industrial systems as well as micro-scale electronic devices. Nonetheless, a number of restrictions still exist. Even with recent advancements, TEG efficiency is still low when compared to other technologies. Harvesting from low-grade heat sources results in a decline in performance. High-performance materials are also more expensive, and system-level optimization is still difficult, which prevents their widespread use.

The current study aims to improve thermoelectric system efficiency by integrating deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques, such as proximal policy optimization (PPO), soft actor-critic (SAC), and deep Q-network (DQN)5, to overcome these issues. Continuous performance improvement is made possible by AI-driven models that allow for dynamic energy distribution and real-time battery health monitoring.

Literature survey

Zhou et al.6 emphasise the significance of multi-energy systems (MES) in achieving a carbon–neutral future. MES frameworks employ stochastic optimisation models, including Markov decision processes, to address uncertainties associated with renewable energy sources and fluctuating demand. Despite the limitations of risk-neutral model predictive control (MPC) and particle swarm optimisation (PSO), the implementation of soft actor-critic (SAC) and other deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques has demonstrated improved energy dispatch efficiency while accounting for user preferences. However, these approaches remain computationally intensive and data-dependent, necessitating the development of scalable and efficient alternatives.

Prauzek et al.7 introduced a double Q-learning (DQL)-based self-learning controller for thermoelectric generator (TEG)-powered IoT devices. The system, comprising a microcontroller, DC/DC converter, LoRaWAN module, and TEG subsystem, achieved a 96% operational cycle success rate under simulation. Future research aims to validate DQL performance in real-world environments and explore its applicability across broader domains.

Zafar et al.8 optimised power output in centralised TEG plants under non-uniform temperature distributions using a feedforward neural network (FNN) trained with a PID controller and functional data analysis (FDA). The proposed method effectively identified global maximum power points (GMPPs) and is expected to be extended to integrated PV-TEG systems connected to DC microgrids.

Mirza et al.9 developed a feed-forward neural network trained with the squirrel search optimisation (SQS) algorithm to enhance PV-TEG system performance. The model demonstrated an 8% improvement in speed over existing methodologies and addressed challenges such as slow convergence and power loss. Future work includes integration with microgrid systems and practical validation.

Prauzek et al.10 applied Q-learning for energy management in IoT nodes equipped with TEGs and supercapacitors, achieving a 96.1% cycle completion rate. Potential applications include environmental monitoring with tunable learning parameters.

Li et al.11 inspected machine learning applications in thermal energy systems, focusing on layout optimisation, thermal transport simulation, and materials discovery. Feng et al.12 provided an overview of TEG advancements, highlighting progress in materials, thermal management, and structural design.

Jouhara et al.13 discussed improvements in thermoelectric figure of merit (ZT) and cost reduction strategies. Key challenges include material limitations and high production costs. Liu et al.14 demonstrated TEGs for low-temperature gradient energy harvesting in IoT-based health monitoring, emphasising efficiency enhancement and miniaturisation for industrial deployment.

Joung et al.15 analyzed TEG designs, noting that black-painted double-layered blocks significantly improved power output. Future research will refine these designs for rooftop applications. Zoui et al.16 identified square pin fins as optimal configurations for waste heat recovery, yielding a 23% increase in power output. Further optimisation will consider advanced materials.

Toan et al.17 reported that increasing the number of thermocouples in portable TEG devices improved efficiency. Future studies will extend this approach to broader applications. Yang et al.18 developed a flexible wearable TEG powered by body heat, with ongoing challenges in biocompatibility and power conversion efficiency.

Kucova et al.19 examined TEGs in the context of IoT energy solutions, advocating for machine learning integration and strategic material selection. Sun et al.20 addressed the barriers to harvesting human body heat via TEGs, underscoring the need for skin-conformal, high-efficiency materials in wearable devices.

In conclusion, while substantial progress has been made in understanding TEG operation, integration, and deployment across diverse systems, several gaps remain. MES integration using Markov decision processes and DRL methods has shown promise, yet challenges persist in terms of computational cost and scalability. Self-learning controllers such as DQL offer potential but require validation in real-world contexts. Material and design innovations—such as square pin fins and flexible wearable configurations—have improved performance, though further enhancements in efficiency and durability are necessary, particularly for low thermal differential and body heat applications.

Research gap and motivation

Thermoelectric generator (TEG) and multi-energy system (MES) technologies have advanced significantly, but there are still a number of important topics that are not well covered in the literature. The stochastic nature of approaches like Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) and stochastic optimization, which frequently have problems with scalability and data efficiency, is one major obstacle. The creation of innovative dispatch algorithms that are both computationally light and operationally effective is required to overcome these constraints.

In simulation environments, recent research on Deep Q-Learning (DQL) algorithms applied to Internet of Things (IoT) devices driven by TEGs has shown encouraging results. There is a disconnect between theoretical success and actual implementation, though, as their performance in real-world situations is still mainly unproven.

Despite their potential for energy harvesting in mobile applications, wearable TEGs continue to encounter obstacles such as low power conversion efficiency (PCE) and biocompatibility. These drawbacks highlight the necessity of ongoing advancements in wearable TEG design and material research to improve user safety and performance. Furthermore, there are still problems with appropriate control, system stability, and overall energy efficiency in hybrid systems that combine photovoltaic (PV) and TEG technologies for DC microgrid applications. To allow for the dependable and scalable integration of such hybrid systems, these factors need further research.

In the context of waste heat recovery, rooftop installations, and portable devices, current TEG designs require further optimisation. Specific design elements such as fin arrangements—particularly square pin fins—and thermocouple layout structures demand reconsideration to improve thermal performance and energy output.

Collectively, these challenges and gaps reveal promising directions for future research and development. Addressing them will be instrumental in advancing the practical viability and efficiency of MES and TEG technologies across diverse applications.

Main contribution

The present study contributes to the advancement of the design and development of thermoelectric generators (TEGs) by integrating deep reinforcement learning algorithms (DRLs), specifically soft actor-critical (SAC), proximal policy optimization (PPO), and deep Q-network (DQN) to achieve energy optimization, battery health preservation, and robust system responsiveness under dynamic operating conditions. Comparative analysis under consistent energy supply conditions reveals that the SAC algorithm outperforms PPO and DQN in terms of energy conversion efficiency, system stability, and balanced energy distribution. While PPO demonstrates superior battery longevity, DQN exhibits comparatively poor performance across key metrics. Experimental results underscore the potential of DRL-based control strategies to enhance waste heat recovery and extend battery lifespan, thereby improving the overall energy efficiency of renewable energy-based systems (REBs). These outcomes align with broader global energy sustainability objectives and highlight the promise of intelligent control frameworks in next-generation energy harvesting systems.

Dataset description

The dataset used in training a Deep Reinforcement Learning agent in this study is intended to mimic the operational behavior of a thermoelectric energy harvesting system. It consists of a wide range of characteristics that define the state of the system and allow the agent to make logical and well-informed judgments.

Important characteristics include the thermoelectric generator’s voltage output (VTEG), the current obtained using Ohm’s Law (ITEG), and the temperature gradient (Tgrad), all of which have a direct impact on the generator’s efficiency. The dataset additionally tracks the battery health status (Bhealth), the energy stored in the buffer (Bbuffer), and the battery charging level (Blevel) to guarantee a dependable and sustainable energy source.

The time of day (Tday) captures temporal dynamics, and energy supply (Esupply) and energy demand (Edemand) indicate system-level interactions. Along with the associated reward signals that direct the learning process, the dataset also documents the agent’s discrete activities, such as charging the battery, storing energy in the buffer, draining the battery, or being idle.

A strong framework for optimizing energy extraction, storage, and distribution under various operational restrictions and climatic conditions is offered by this time-series dataset. It is essential for providing clever control schemes for thermoelectric systems, especially in applications that call for flexible and effective energy management.

Proposed methodology

The proposed method aims to improve the operational performance of thermoelectric generator (TEG) by using deep reinforcement learning (DRL) techniques. DRL provides a strong framework for real-time power output and energy efficiency optimization by dynamically adjusting to shifting system and environmental variables.

By continuously learning from environmental feedback, DRL, in contrast to traditional optimization techniques, facilitates a more intelligent and adaptive decision making process. The energy management policy can change over time thanks to this iterative learning mechanism, which enhances responsiveness, stability, and efficiency.

To facilitate this approach, the TEG system is modelled using Markov decision process (MDP), wherein the reward functions, state transitions, and action space are carefully designed to support optimal energy distribution. This formulation empowers the DRL agent to make context-aware decisions that maximize energy harvesting and storage while preserving system health and reliability.

Environment and agent interaction

Through interaction with the environment, the agent gains knowledge and improves its performance. While the Environment represents the thermoelectric energy harvesting system and gives the agent feedback and status information, a DRL agent takes decisions based on the system’s condition and learns from reward feedback. Dynamic state transitions are used to model the environment, with each time step representing a distinct energy management choice. Based on its prior actions, the agent obtains an updated state that enables it to modify its policy as necessary. To further represent real-world settings, the environment also incorporates outside elements like shifting temperature gradients and varying energy demands.

Proximal policy optimisation

It is a reinforcement learning algorithm derived from policy gradient methods, developed to address instability and inefficiency issues inherent in earlier approaches such as advantage actor-critic (A2C) and trust region policy optimisation (TRPO). To guarantee more consistent and reliable training, PPO uses a clipped surrogate objective function that prevents overly large, destabilizing updates to its policy.

PPO is suitable for environments with low-dimensional and continuous action spaces, offering computational efficiency and robust convergence. PPO’s clipped objective function, which prevents significant policy changes during training and hence fosters stability, is its primary innovation.

Mnih et al.23 proposed the DQN algorithm, which handles high-dimensional state spaces by combining deep neural networks with the traditional Q-learning framework. Two major innovations—the use of a distinct Target Network and Experience Replay—are largely responsible for its stability and efficacy.

Experience Replay saves previous transitions in a sizable replay buffer rather than training on consecutive samples. In order to increase data efficiency and break detrimental temporal correlations, the network is then trained on mini-batches that are sampled randomly from this buffer. DQN uses a Target Network, which is an updated copy of the main Q-network on a periodic basis, in order to provide a steady target for the Q-value updates in order to avoid the instability of a “moving target” problem.

Training hyper-parameters

A deep reinforcement learning (DRL) agent’s hyperparameters, which are external configurations established before training, have a significant impact on how well it performs. A crucial first step in attaining stability and peak performance is choosing these parameters. Performance versus longevity: a key trade-off

The SAC algorithm is the definitive top performer in terms of pure operational efficiency. Achieving the highest average reward (− 7.03) and energy fulfilment rate (22.84%), its success can be attributed to its maximum entropy framework. This principle encourages broad exploration of the action space, allowing the agent to discover a highly effective and nuanced policy that excels at balancing immediate energy dispatch with long-term storage needs.

However, a critical insight emerges from the battery health metric. The A2C, DDPG, and PPO models all achieved a perfect 100.00% average battery health, suggesting their final policies are inherently more conservative. These algorithms converge to strategies that entirely avoid actions that could cause even minor battery degradation. While SAC’s 99.99% is nearly perfect, this subtle difference is significant. It presents system designers with a clear choice: SAC for maximum energy throughput, or A2C, DDPG, and PPO for guaranteed hardware preservation. For many industrial applications where reliability and component lifespan are paramount, a slightly lower reward may be an acceptable price for zero hardware stress.

The indispensable role of hyperparameter tuning

The comparison between fully optimized models and their single parameter-tuned benchmarks (detailed in Appendix A) underscores that hyperparameter optimization is not only beneficial but fundamental to success. The most striking example is the DDPG agent, whose benchmark version degraded battery health to 50.00%, a catastrophic failure in any real world scenario. The optimized version, in contrast, achieved perfect battery health. This dramatic improvement demonstrates that an untuned algorithm, regardless of its sophistication, can adopt a destructive and unusable policy. DRL is not a “plug-and-play” solution; its potential is only unlocked through a rigorous tuning process that aligns the agent’s learning parameters with the specific dynamics of the environment.

DQN consistently performed poorly, primarily due to the architectural mismatch of applying a discrete-action algorithm to a continuous-control problem. This reinforces the importance of selecting an appropriate algorithm from the outset.

Establishing a benchmark for comparison

To properly evaluate the impact of the comprehensive hyperparameter search, a second set of five benchmark models was established. These benchmarks use the exact same DRL architectures: A2C, DDPG, DQN, PPO, and SAC, but were created with a deliberately simplified tuning process. Rather than performing an exhaustive search across all hyperparameters, each benchmark model was developed using a basic default network architecture where only a single crucial parameter was adjusted. This approach was designed to create a baseline that represents what might be achieved with minimal tuning effort. By comparing fully optimized agents against these benchmarks, we can effectively isolate the performance gains attributable to the rigorous tuning process itself, separating them from the inherent strengths of each algorithm’s underlying design.

Practical considerations for embedded deployment

While, SAC proved to be the most performant algorithm in simulation, moving this controller from a simulated environment to a real-world embedded system introduces practical challenges. The most significant hurdles are latency, computational overhead, and the need for hardware adaptation.

For a TEG management system, decisions do not need to be made in microseconds. The thermal and electrical dynamics of the system are relatively slow, meaning that a decision latency of a few milliseconds, or even up to a second, is perfectly acceptable. The trained SAC policy is essentially a neural network that performs a single forward pass to determine an action. On a modern microcontroller unit (MCU) with basic hardware acceleration for floating point operations, this forward pass is computationally inexpensive and would easily meet these latency requirements. The heavy lifting, the entire training process, is done offline on a powerful machine. The embedded device only needs to run the final lightweight inference model.

The main hardware constraint, therefore, is not raw processing power but rather memory (RAM and Flash). The SAC model, with its actor and critic networks, can be larger than a simpler controller. However, techniques like quantization (reducing the precision of the network’s weights from 32-bit floats to 8-bit integers) and pruning (removing unimportant neural connections) can drastically shrink the model’s memory footprint with minimal loss in performance. Adapting the hardware would likely involve selecting an MCU from a family like STM32 or ESP32, which offer sufficient RAM (tens or hundreds of kilobytes) and Flash storage to house the optimized model and the necessary code to interface with sensors (for temperature, voltage, and current) and actuators (for power switching). The challenge is less about finding exotic hardware and more about careful software optimization to fit a capable model onto cost-effective, readily available components.

Conclusion

This study aimed to identify the optimal Deep Reinforcement Learning strategy for the dynamic control of a Thermoelectric Generator system. Our major finding is that a critical trade-off exists between peak performance and system longevity. We conclude that the SAC algorithm is the superior choice for maximizing energy efficiency and throughput. Its policy yields the highest rewards and best utilization of harvested energy. However, for applications where hardware preservation is the primary objective, we recommend A2C, DDPG, or PPO, as their optimized policies operated with zero negative impact on battery health.

A clear decision framework for deploying DRL agents in real-world energy systems adds value to the work carried. We have also demonstrated conclusively that comprehensive hyperparameter tuning is a critical, non-negotiable step to prevent catastrophic failures and unlock an algorithm’s true potential. The primary limitation of this research is its reliance on a simulated environment. Therefore, future work should focus on validating these optimized DRL controllers on physical TEG hardware to verify their performance and reliability under real-world conditions.
