Blood pressure variability and its utility in acute disease and critical illness: a systematic review
Authors: Xiaoyang Zhu, Haitao Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood pressure variability (BPV) reflects the function of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the relationship between BPV and outcomes in acute disease and critical illness.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed and Google Scholar. Data were extracted from included studies and analysed using Stata 15. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Methods Guide were used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies. Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis were conducted to explore heterogeneity across studies.ResultsThe systematic review yielded 38 articles, 13 of which (comprising 21,475 participants) were included in the meta‐analysis. Meta-analysis revealed that per a 1-unit increase in BPV, the total OR (95% CI) value for non-mortality outcomes was 1.06 (1.04, 1.07), OR (95% CI) of standard deviation of systolic blood pressure variability (SBPV SD), coefficient of variation of SBPV (SBPV CV), successive variation of SBPV (SBPV SV), and SD of diastolic blood pressure variability (DBPV SD) were 1.04 (1.04, 1.09), 1.06 (1.02, 1.09), 1.04 (1.02, 1.07), and 1.17 (1.06, 1.28), respectively. SBPV SD was correlated with non-mortality outcomes (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.04–1.09), with ORs 1.09 (95% CI 1.05–1.13) in the serious infection cohorts and 1.05 (95% CI 1.03–1.08) in the stroke cohort.ConclusionsThe evidence suggests a modest correlation between BPV and non-mortality outcomes in acute disease and critical illness. At the same time, BPV requires further validation as a potential predictive tool in emergency and intensive care settings. Certain BPV metrics, such as SBPV SD, appear more applicable to specific conditions, including stroke and severe infection. Further research is needed to determine whether modulating BPV translates into clinical benefits.Trial registrationThis study was registered on PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) with the registration number CRD42024593808 in Autumn 2024.

Introduction

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) maintains normal physiological functions in the human body, playing a key role in the occurrence and progression of some chronic diseases [1–3]. The ANS function may exacerbate organ damage while enhancing the body’s response to disease; this double-edged sword effect also exists in acute disease and critical illness [4]. Some interventions that modulate ANS can help improve prognosis [5–7]. Still, ANS function is often ignored in treating acute disease and critical illness, mainly due to the difficulty in monitoring ANS at bedside [8]. Fortunately, blood pressure variability (BPV) and heart rate variability are the most common and well-documented assessment tools [9]. Heart rate variability relies on photoplethysmography or dynamic electrocardiogram. In contrast, BPV can be obtained by a non-invasive blood pressure monitor with or without related modules; this simple tool might support clinical work in the emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU) settings.

BPV is defined as the variation characteristics in blood pressure during a specific period; thus, the different types of BPV are derived from different monitoring periods and approaches to measuring: very-short-term BPV [within beat BPV and beat-to-beat BPV]; short-term BPV (within 24 h); mid-term BPV (normally several days), and long-term BPV [a few weeks to several years, such as visit-to-visit blood pressure variability and seasonal BPV]. Moreover, diverse formulae generate various BPV metrics: standard deviation (SD), successive variation (SV), coefficient of variation (CV), average real variability (ARV), variation independent of mean (VIM), etc. [10] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Blood pressure variability (BPV) and its clinical utility. In emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU) settings, BPV may become a non-invasive and convenient tool for assessing autonomic nervous system (ANS) function, guiding fluid resuscitation, evaluating mortality risk, predicting the prognosis of neurological injury or disease, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), coronavirus disease 2019, and sepsis

Many studies have already demonstrated the association between BPV and prognosis of various chronic diseases including cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and renal diseases [11–14], however, few studies have focused on prognostic significance of BPV in acute disease and critical illness, thus our systematic review aims to integrate the relation between BPV and outcome in acute disease and critical illness, and the evidence for the application of BPV for these cohorts. Methods

Searching strategy and eligibility criteria

The search was conducted between Autumn and December 2024, limiting output to the English language, human research, and publications from 2014 to 2024. Two reviewers used the databases of PubMed and Google Scholar based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and the Population, Interventions, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework. Participants with acute disease and critical illness were included in this review; they received appropriate treatment and undergoing dynamic blood pressure monitoring; included studies documented the association of BPV with clinical outcomes or severity of the illness (e.g., mortality, neurological prognosis, ICU admission); Studies were included if they were randomized controlled trial, case–control study, cohort study, or observational study and published in English. A combination of MeSH Terms and/or keywords such as “blood pressure variability”, “acute disease”, “critical illness”, “intensive care unit”, “emergency”, “acute”, “sepsis”, combined with Boolean operators “AND” “OR” was used. No restrictions were placed on the country. Studies were excluded if:

Study selection

Reviewers HT S and XY Z independently conducted a literature search and screening with EndNote X9, assessed the eligibility of these studies based on title and abstract according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, performed full-text review and bias risk assessment for eligible studies, and addressed discrepancies through consensus discussion.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently completed by a single reviewer (XY Z), with independent verification by HT S. Any disagreements were settled by consensus. The aim, study design, population, year of publication, diagnostic criteria, research type, follow-up, variables associated with outcomes, location of research, type of BPV, outcome measures, effect sizes, and statistical approach of the eligible studies were abstracted.

Data analysis

The authors conducted a meta-analysis assessing the correlation of BPV with mortality and non-mortality outcomes. Meta-analysis was performed using Stata 15 (graphically presented as forest plots). Non-mortality outcomes include hematoma enlargement, discharge location, physiological condition, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), ICU admission, cardiovascular outcomes, neurological prognosis and acute kidney injury (AKI). The effect size, odds ratio (OR) or hazard ratio (HR), were entered into Stata as natural logarithm, separate analyses of HR and OR were performed. Analysis was stratified according to BPV indicators and disease category. The meta-analysis and sensitivity analysis using a random-effects model, with a minimum of 3 studies per subgroup to ensure reliable results; otherwise, a narrative qualitative summary will be provided. Subgroup analysis and I2 statistics were used to evaluate heterogeneity: I2 values of 0–40% may indicate no significant heterogeneity; 30%−60% may represent moderate heterogeneity; 50%−75% may suggest substantial heterogeneity; and 75%−100% denote considerable heterogeneity. Funnel plots and Egger's test were performed to test for potential publication bias, and the trim-and-fill method was used to correct the estimate of the effects if publication bias was present.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias of case–control study and cohort study was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), and cross-sectional study was assessed using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) Methods Guide [15]. Studies that fulfilled ≥ 5 items in the NOS and ≥ 6 items in the Methods Guide were considered medium or good quality. Both authors assessed all eligible studies independently; afterwards, the evaluation conclusions were compared, and every controversial item was re-reviewed (Fig. 1).

Conduct an evidence table

The characteristics of eligible studies were summarized in an evidence table that included participants, study design, BPV indices and main findings, both reviewers discussed the content of the evidence table.

Result

Studies included and characteristics of participants

A total of 8209 articles were identified through the search strategy. After duplicate removal (n = 2730), 5286 articles were excluded by title and abstract. Of these, 193 articles were assessed by full‐text review, and 38 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2). When subgroup analyses were conducted by BPV types or disease category, a minimum of three studies per subgroup was required, and only studies reporting effect sizes were included in the meta-analysis. Consequently, a total of 13 studies were eligible for meta-analysis.Fig. 2Flow diagram. The diagram shows the study selection process

Overall, 38 studies were included (80,510 participants, 49,854 men and 30,463 women, among which one article did not specify gender ratio). In these studies, 18 articles [16–33] concerned acute ischemic stroke or cerebral hemorrhage, 5 concerned sepsis [34–38], 4 concerned cardiovascular disease [39–42], 4 concerned cerebral injury [43–46], 4 concerned coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [47–50], 1 concerned acute spinal cord injury (SCI) [51], 2 concerned various diseases in the ICU [52, 53]. Among them, 17 articles demonstrated directly that the clinical data were sourced from ED or various ICU, total 47,296 cases (58.75%); the data of remaining 21 articles were sourced from acute spine unit, trauma center, field hospital or unspecified department, including acute stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI), COVID-19, SCI, and acute myocardial infarction (AMI), total 33,214 cases (41.25%). Overall, several large cohorts came from studies of Hou et al. [52], Kellert et al. [23], Xu et al. [38], and Peng et al. [53], including 54,930 patients with acute stroke or critical illness. The feature of BPV in acute disease and critical illness

Most of the eligible studies demonstrated the numerical value or spectral characteristics of BPV. On the whole, patients who were dead or worse showed higher BPV regardless of disease category, but there is no consensus on the threshold of BPV. A position paper [10], which gathered wisdom from experts, listed a series of studies attempting to analyze the BPV threshold; however, our review was unable to analyze the BPV threshold due to excessive heterogeneity in the included studies. As for spectral analysis of BPV, patients with a poorer prognosis or more severe condition generally have lower low-frequency (LF) and higher high-frequency (HF) components, and a reduced ratio of LF to HF, which means decreased sympathetic nervous activity and increased parasympathetic nerve activity [28, 49]. Only one article [37] performed principal component analysis on the spectrum of BPV, indicating that the first principal component was a risk factor for 28-day mortality, but there was no numerical description of BPV in that article.

Association between BPV and death risk in patients with acute disease and critical illness

12 articles [23, 28, 30, 36–38, 40, 41, 45, 47, 50, 52] (including 48,648 participants) demonstrated that BPV was a risk factor of short-term or long-term mortality in patients with acute disease and critical illness besides the study by Tziomalos et al. [31]; there were 8, 4, 1, 4, and 1 articles concerned in-hospital, 28/30-day, 90-day, 1-year, and 3-year mortality rates among eligible studies, respectively. Since studies [31] and [45] failed to report the effect sizes, [38] and [41] assessed mortality risk using the highest quartile of BPV, and [28] applied spectral analysis for BPV. Consequently, a meta-analysis stratified by all BPV types of the remaining studies was unfeasible, necessitating narrative synthesis.

A cohort study [30] reported the strongest association result between BPV and death risk: the SV of systolic blood pressure variability (SBPV SV) was correlated with in-hospital mortality in spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) patients treated with providing ≥ 5 interventions after initiating mechanical ventilation (pMVI) (OR 26; 95% CI 1.2, > 100). Only one article demonstrated that the area under curve (AUC) of SBPV SD and SD of diastolic blood pressure variability (DBPV SD) for predicting 28-day mortality were 0.746 and 0.890, respectively. Although these were not as high as the AUC of lactate (0.915) [36], they were still significant. Interestingly, [41] and [52] focused on the impact of the number interval of BPV on the risk of death. These restricted cubic spline results [38, 41, 52] revealed that a U-shaped or N-shaped association between BPV and death risk could be due to the BPV threshold; BPV that swings either too high or too low tends to be adverse to outcome.

The relation between BPV and non-mortality outcomes

26 articles (48,875 participants) referring to the association of BPV with indicators of non-mortality outcomes. BPV serves a wide range of purposes in ED or ICU settings, influencing various prognosis-related indicators in patients with different diseases. Among included studies, there are 15 studies [16–25, 27, 28, 31–33] analyzed the association of BPV with neurological functional prognosis in patients with ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, all but one [31] of these studies showed that BPV was a risk factor of neurological prognosis; SBPV SD and SBPV CV are the prognostic metrics that have the most abundant evidence; Divani et al. [21] found that higher SBPV correlates with neurological outcomes rather than hematoma expansion in ICH patients. Moreover, BPV can also assess risks of cerebral perfusion pressure and hematoma expansion in TBI [43, 44]. In research on acute coronary syndrome (ACS), Hassan et al. [39] and Mantzouranis et al. [42] confirmed that BPV is associated with cardiovascular prognosis. In populations experiencing ICH, critical illness, or ACS, increased BPV demonstrates predictive value for renal dysfunction [29, 42, 53]. In the ED or ICU with high-turnover stays, BPV also correlates with the transfer destination, aiding in the clinical assessment of TBI and COVID-19 cases [46, 48]. Also, severe infection commonly seen in the ED and ICU, BPV relates to infection-related indicators, progress, and complications in sepsis [35, 36] and COVID-19 [47, 48, 50]; SBPV SD be shown to be prognostically relevant in each article examining SBPV SD in these cohorts, but CV, ARV and VIM of SBPV do not always correlate with outcome.

Since [18, 31], and [35] failed to provide effect sizes; [9, 28, 39, 43], and [53] utilized unusual BPV metrics; [20] and [48] applied a multiplicative operation to original BPV values; [19, 22, 24] and [32] analysed the prognostic risks of highest BPV quantile. Consequently, these studies were excluded from the meta-analysis; the remaining studies were eligible for quantitative synthesis stratified by BPV types. Figure 3, [16, 17, 21, 23, 25, 29, 33, 36, 42, 44, 46, 47], and [50] summarizes the relation between BPV and non-mortality outcomes stratified by BPV types. Per one unit increase in BPV, the total OR (95% CI) for non-mortality outcomes was 1.06 (1.04, 1.07), OR (95% CI) of SBPV SD, SBPV CV, SBPV SV, and DBPV SD were 1.04 (1.04, 1.09), 1.06 (1.02, 1.09), 1.04 (1.02, 1.07), and 1.17 (1.06, 1.28), respectively. The association between SBPV SD and non-mortality outcomes, subgroup analysis by disease category

Among included studies reporting diverse BPV metrics, some studies failed to report effect sizes. Additionally, there were only one or two studies in some subgroups when summary estimates were stratified by disease category; thus, only the 8 articles documenting SBPV SD were suitable for quantitative synthesis with subgroup analysis by disease category. To ensure sufficient statistical power for subgroup analysis, the authors combined sepsis and COVID-19 into a single disease category (severe infection). Practical application of BPV in ED or ICU

There are 4 articles [26, 30, 34, 51] concerning the application of BPV in patients with acute disease and critical illness, thus a quantitative synthesis could not be reliably performed. A study of a spontaneous ICH cohort showed that, among five related measures of pMVI, appropriate tidal volume, continuous sedative infusion, and quantitative capnography were related to lower SBPV; Chest X-ray and SBPV SV were correlated with in-hospital mortality. Therefore, doctors should perform pMVI more frequently to reduce BPV and improve survival rate [30]. Another study of an ICH cohort indicated that, compared to using labetalol alone, hydralazine alone, or both, ICH patients who receive a nicardipine-based antihypertensive regimen have lower SBPV SD [26], which has been associated with better outcomes.

Apart from MAP, the application of BPV can also contribute to sepsis management. Because even if some patients with septic shock achieve MAP > 65 mmHg after treatment, there are still issues with ANS function and response to treatment, and patients with a good response generally have a higher LF of BPV spectrum, observing BPV to evaluate ANS activity can help assess the actual effectiveness of fluid resuscitation and vasoactive drugs [34].

Lucci et al. [51] utilized BPV spectrum to analyse cardiovascular ANS function that deteriorates over time in SCI patients, showing that the LF of systolic arterial pressure (SAP) could be used to quantization of ANS dysfunction; subsequent cardiovascular ANS symptom and RR-interval variability more likely to be observed while autonomic completeness of injury is defined as high-level SCI with LF of SAP < 2mmHg2. In other words, the LF of SAP may be used for severity stratification of SCI.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we assessed the association of BPV with mortality and non-mortality outcomes in acute disease and critical illness. According to 12 articles [23, 28, 30, 31, 36–38, 40, 41, 45, 47, 50, 52] concerning death risk, most researchers reported that BPV was associated with mortality; however, meta-analysis was unsuitable due to the diversity of BPV metrics. We also found that BPV showed a loose association with non-mortality outcomes in cardiovascular disease, stroke, sepsis, cerebral injury, COVID-19, and critical illness through meta-analysis. Moreover, previous evidence showed that the significance of BPV is independent of MAP and mean systolic blood pressure [34, 54, 55]. These findings suggest whether clinicians should consider BPV in the treatment of acute disease and critical illness.

Mechanisms linking BPV to prognosis

The mechanism of the association between BPV and outcome in patients with acute disease and critical illness is not entirely clear, as many mechanisms are involved, given that ANS function is related to a variety of physiological activities in the human body.

In patients with cardiovascular disease, impaired arterial baroreceptor sensitivity, aortic sclerosis, and cardiac remodeling cause changes in BPV [56–58]. Likewise, acute myocardial infarction may lead to ischemic damage of cardiac ANS directly, which constitutes the basis for dysrhythmia [59–61].

Cerebral perfusion pressure is more greatly affected by BPV when a stroke occurs, particularly large vessel occlusive stroke, which could be why short-term or mid-term BPV impacts neurological outcomes [62]. Similarly, cerebral perfusion pressure below or above optimal levels is harmful to prognosis in TBI [43, 63]. Furthermore, an autonomic storm will occur when the inhibitory impulses of higher cortical centers are interrupted among patients admitted in neurocritical care unit [64]. These findings indicate that ANS and BPV could possibly be targeted in interventions for neurological injury or disease.

Sepsis and severe COVID-19 infection trigger overactivity of ANS, which causes desensitization of adrenergic receptors and damage to ANS, and then the response to fluid resuscitation and vasoactive drugs will be reduced and difficult to reverse [65–67].

Factors causing the heterogeneity

Our review found heterogeneity in these eligible studies, but the vast majority of researchers support that BPV is associated with prognosis in various acute diseases and critical illnesses. Here, we need to discuss the sources of heterogeneity.

Participants

This review focuses on short-term and mid-term BPV of acute disease and critical illness; however, the research subjects of some eligible articles are multiple rather than a single disease. For example, Hou et al. [52] and Peng et al. [53] utilized the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database, which included a wide variety of critical illnesses. Therefore, the excessive number of disease types makes it difficult to perform subgroup analyses, which complicates the interpretation of the research findings.

Difference of BPV indicators

The use of various BPV metrics across these eligible studies may be one reason for the differences in research results. Overall, the two most commonly used indicators are SD and CV. A meta‐analysis by Mena et al. [68] pointed out that the best indicator of 24-h BPV was ARV, but only studies referring to ARV were included in that meta‐analysis. Therefore, more research is needed to investigate which BPV indicator most accurately reflects ANS.

Interference from covariates

Some covariates included in the study may affect the value of BPV, such as vasoactive drugs, antihypertensive drugs, and hypertension. Thus, risk adjustment and stratification will become desirable.

Clinical significance

When doctors deal with health issues in patients with acute disease and critical illness, the first step is to improve physiologic condition; however, patients who arrive at ED or ICU, such as those with cardiovascular diseases, strokes, brain injury, and sepsis, often suffer from ANS dysfunction [64, 69]. Even though there is a wealth of evidence indicating that BPV is related to poor outcomes in such patients, the assessment and management of ANS function are frequently neglected in the ED or ICU environment, as it is difficult to measure ANS function directly in the human body.

Our review supports the following points: a number of published studies have demonstrated that BPV correlates with mortality in patients with acute disease and critical illness; the association between SBPV SD and non-mortality outcomes is robust, especially in the severe infection cohort, with no significant heterogeneity in this association; the relation between SBPV CV or DBPV SD and non-mortality outcomes also showed negligible heterogeneity. Although it is widely acknowledged that BPV is not a novel tool, it is a simple and readily available option. We propose that observing and managing BPV is conducive to modulate ANS function in ED and ICU settings, starting with its application to support clinical work, which may lead to a better physiological status of patients.

Limitation

Our study has limitations. We focused on BPV in patients with acute disease and critical illness, without restricting it to a specific disease. Therefore, multiple diseases across the included studies could result in assessment bias. Meanwhile, the difference in BPV metrics impeded the reliable implementation of meta-analysis, biasing the results. These issues were compounded by the few included studies, which precluded meta-regression and limited us to a quantitative description of the association between BPV and mortality. Consequently, our methodology was not able to perform a quantitative estimate on mortality from BPV and adequate subgroup analyses. Likewise, heterogeneity and publication bias were detected in this study, potentially due to the absence of effect sizes in the limited number of included studies. Also, a one unit increase in BPV is difficult to observe; adjusted OR per several units or per SD increase was utilized in some studies, thus these effect sizes were unsuitable for quantitative estimates, influencing evaluation results. Furthermore, most included studies were retrospective, offering limited ability to show causal relation, requiring further prospective studies to substantiate these findings. We included studies addressing this topic in English only, and excluded conferences, doctoral theses, and some articles without full text; as such, we may not have obtained all relevant articles on this topic, and the information acquisition was limited.

Conclusion

Evidence indicates a modest correlation between BPV and outcomes in acute disease and critical illness. Therefore, BPV might be explored to predict prognosis risk and as a basis for clinical decision-making. We propose that BPV be evaluated as a translatable ANS assessment tool in the ED or ICU to determine if its use might help improve patient prognosis.
