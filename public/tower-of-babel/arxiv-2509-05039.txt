Numerical approximations to statistical conservation laws for scalar hyperbolic equations Thanks: * Corresponding author
Qian Huang*, Christian Rohde

Abstract
. Motivated by the statistical description of turbulence, we study statistical conservation laws in the form of kinetic-type PDEs for joint probability density functions (PDFs) and cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) associated with solutions of scalar balance laws. Starting from viscous balance laws, the resulting PDF/CDF equations involve unclosed conditional averages arising in the viscous terms. We show that these terms exhibit a dissipative anomaly: they remain non-negligible in the vanishing viscosity limit and are essential to preserve the nonnegativity of evolving PDFs. To approximate these PDF/CDF equations in a unified framework, we propose a novel sampling-based estimator for the unclosed terms, constructed from numerical or exact realizations of the underlying balance-law solutions. In certain cases, a priori error bounds can be derived, demonstrating that the deviation between the true and approximate CDFs is controlled by the estimation error of the unclosed terms. Numerical experiments with analytically solvable test problems confirm that the sampling-based approximation converges satisfactorily with the number of samples.

1.
Introduction

The study of statistical conservation laws is motivated by the inherently statistical nature of turbulence, where uncertain measurements lead to markedly different predictions
[
26
]
. The fluid velocity and pressure
(u_{f},p_{f}):(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
are governed by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations (NSE), and uncertainties are naturally modeled as random perturbations of the initial and/or boundary data. Statistical conservation laws refer to kinetic equations (conservation laws) for the joint probability density functions (PDFs)
f^{(K)}=f^{(K)}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K})
of the velocity field
u_{f}
at multiple spatial locations
\{x_{i}\}_{1}^{K}
(
K\in\mathbb{N}
) and time instants
[
28
,
16
]
. For
Q^{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
Borel sets (
i=1,\dots,K
), we have

(1.1)

\text{Prob}(\{u_{f}(t,x_{i})\in Q^{i}\text{ for all }i\})=\int_{\prod Q^{i}}f^{(K)}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K})\,dv_{1}\cdots dv_{K}.

These joint PDFs encode the complete statistical information of the turbulent velocity field. For example, the mean velocity
\langle u_{f}\rangle(t,x)
and the two-point correlation
S_{r}(\ell;x)=\langle|(u_{f}(x+\ell n)-u_{f}(x))\cdot n|^{r}\rangle
(with
n\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a unit vector and
\ell>0
) are moments of
f:=f^{(1)}(t,x,v)
and
f^{(2)}(t,x,v_{1},x+\ell n,v_{2})
, respectively. By
\langle\cdot\rangle
we denote the mean with respect to the given probability measure. The Reynolds-averaged NSE governing
\langle u_{f}\rangle
are central in engineering practice
[
32
]
. For near-wall shear flows,
\langle u_{f}\rangle
obeys a logarithmic law
[
32
]
. As for the structure function
S_{r}(\ell;x)
, it follows Kolmogorov’s scaling law
S_{r}(\ell;x)\sim\ell^{\zeta_{r}}
with
\zeta_{r}=r/3
in the inertial range for
d=3

[
22
]
. This scaling holds for
r=2,3
, but shows significant deviations for larger
r

[
21
]
.

Due to the limited spatio-temporal validity of these phenomenological theories, statistical conservation laws provide a promising unified framework for capturing velocity statistics across all scales
[
16
]
. Generally they read as
\partial_{t}f^{(K)}=L_{K}f^{(K)}
, with
L_{K}
a problem-specific operator. As with many statistical formulations of NSE, however, these equations are
not
closed:
\partial_{t}f^{(K)}=C_{K}f^{(K)}+U_{K}
, where
C_{K}
is known and
U_{K}
the unclosed part, typically arising from pressure and viscous terms in NSE. This term can be expressed as
L_{K,K+1}(f^{(K)},f^{(K+1)})
, forming the infinite Lundgren-Monin-Novikov (LMN) hierarchy
[
24
,
25
,
27
]
, or equivalently through unknown conditional averages such as
\langle\Delta_{x_{i}}u_{f}|u_{f}=v_{i}\rangle

[
16
]
. Various analytical closures for
U_{K}
have been proposed, including statistical independence closures
[
35
]
, ‘Markov-process-in-scale’ ansatz for velocity increments
[
17
,
15
]
, and DNS-data-driven closures
[
6
]
.

Statistical conservations laws also arise in other incompressible flow problems. The ‘PDF method’ for turbulent combustion employs velocity-composition joint PDFs to model turbulence-reaction coupling in the incompressible NSEs with varying densities
[
31
]
. Passive scalar transport in random (incompressible) velocity fields provides another classical example, with long-time scalar PDF behavior analyzed in
[
33
]
.

By contrast, statistical conservation laws for compressible flows and systems of hyperbolic balance laws remain largely unexplored. When the divergence-free condition is absent, even the spatial transport term of the kinetic equations acquires nonlocal contributions (see Appendix
A
for preliminary PDF equations for 1D systems). These PDF equations may connect to the theory of statistical solutions of conservation laws
[
13
,
14
,
23
,
18
]
. A key difficulty is dimensionality: the
K
-point PDF in (
1.1
) depends on
1+2dK
variables. Therefore, most existing work focuses on the scalar setting:

(1.2)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u+\nabla_{x}\cdot g(u)=Q(u,\nabla_{x}u,\Delta_{x}u),\\
&u(0,x,\xi(\omega))=u_{0}(x,\xi(\omega)),\end{split}\quad t>0,x\in D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\omega\in\Omega,

where
u=u(t,x,\xi(\omega))\in\mathbb{R}
,
g=g(u)
is a smooth flux in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\xi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\xi}}
is an absolutely-continuous random variable with a prescribed density function
p(\xi)
, and
Q
represents possible source terms. For scalar conservation laws (
Q=0
), the ‘method of distributions’ derives governing equations for single-point cumulative distribution functions (CDFs), which involve only spatial transport
[
34
,
7
]
. If shocks occur in the solution
u
, a kinetic defect source term must be added
[
29
]
, which is generally unknown a prior but may be inferred from data
[
7
]
. When
Q=Q(u)
, the PDF equation is closed for smooth
u

[
36
]
, equivalent to the mentioned CDF formulation (see also Section
2
). For
Q=Q(u,\nabla_{x}u)
, closed equations for the joint
u
–
\nabla_{x}u
PDF can be derived in one dimension for smooth solutions
[
36
]
, while discontinuities will again introduce defect terms to these kinetic equations.

Another important line of research concerns the viscous Burgers equation with random forcing, corresponding to
g(u)=u^{2}/2
and
Q=\epsilon\Delta_{x}u
in (
1.2
), often viewed as a scalar analogue of NSE without pressure
[
2
,
4
]
. Governing equations for single- and two-point PDFs have been analyzed, some with analytical closure proposals
[
30
,
19
,
11
]
. Notably, these studies clarified the statistics of velocity gradients and resolved a long-standing debate about their tail behavior
[
11
]
.

The objective of this paper is to investigate numerical approximations to statistical conservation laws for hyperbolic balance laws, focusing on the scalar case (
1.2
). Unlike earlier studies
[
36
,
9
,
7
]
, we consider viscous balance laws with
Q=\epsilon\Delta_{x}u
in (
1.2
) to mimic the interplay between hyperbolic transport and viscosity operators. The viscous term ensures global existence of classical solutions of
u
, but leads once again to unclosed statistical conservation laws. In Section
2
we systematically derive these laws. A previously-undocumented
dissipative anomaly
is further revealed: for nonlinear fluxes
g(u)
in (
1.2
), the viscosity-related unclosed term
U_{K}
remains essential even in the vanishing viscosity limit, as it guarantees the positivity of PDFs. Since analytical closures of
U_{K}
often introduce very different model structures, our approach does not attempt to derive them. Instead, we approximate
U_{K}
directly from sampled solutions of
u
, a simple yet, to our knowledge, unexplored pipeline. This sampling-based method is flexible and extends naturally to statistical conservation laws of other systems, including NSE. To validate it, we construct analytical test cases for (
1.2
). Numerical results confirm the a priori error analysis and show that PDF/CDF errors diminish with increasing sample size.

Finally, we remark that the present setting (
1.2
) coincides with random hyperbolic conservation laws. Many numerical strategies exist for such problems, including Monte Carlo, stochastic collocation, and generalized polynomial chaos expansions (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
3
,
8
]
). Recently, methods based on Young-measure-valued solutions and entropy minimization have been developed
[
10
]
. Most of these approaches, however, target only pointwise statistics (moments or single-point PDFs). In contrast, the statistical conservation law framework naturally extends to multi-point joint distributions, offering a structure-based route for capturing correlations as an essential feature in turbulence. Even for single-point PDFs, our numerical experiments indicate that leveraging the PDE structure (though unclosed) ‘enhances sample efficiency’ and yields more accurate results than reconstructing the distribution directly from samples, as in Monte Carlo-type methods. Thus, statistical conservation laws provide a unifying and potentially more predictive perspective.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
derives the statistical conservation laws for (
1.2
), with extensions to systems in Appendix A. Section
3
analyzes positivity-preserving properties of the PDF equations. Section
4
introduces the sampling-based approximation algorithm. Analytical and numerical test cases are discussed in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively. Conclusions are drawn in Section
7
.

2.
Statistical conservation laws for scalar problems (
1.2
)

In this section we derive the statistical conservation laws for the scalar balance law (
1.2
) with viscous term
Q=\epsilon\Delta_{x}u
(
\epsilon>0
constant) and random initial data. Both PDF- and CDF-based formulations are presented. Two different formal derivations are outlined.

Assume that the
K
-point joint PDF of
u
exists and is defined by

(2.1)

f^{(K)}=f^{(K)}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K}):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\delta\Big(u(t,x_{i},\xi(\omega))-v_{i}\Big)\right],

with
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta(\cdot)
denoting the Dirac distribution. The expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}
is taken with respect to the random variable
\xi(\omega)
associated with the initial condition in (
1.2
). The PDF is nonnegative
f^{(K)}\geq 0
and depends on
1+K(d+1)
internal variables. It satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}f^{(K)}\prod dv_{i}=1
(normalization),
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{(K)}(\cdot,x_{K},v_{K})\,dv_{K}=f^{(K-1)}
(marginalization), and exhibits the ‘coincidence’ behaviors like
[
24
]

\lim_{x_{\beta}\to x_{\alpha}}f^{(K)}(\cdot,x_{\alpha},v_{\alpha},x_{\beta},v_{\beta})=f^{(K-1)}(\cdot,x_{\alpha},v_{\alpha})\delta(v_{\alpha}-v_{\beta}),\quad\forall\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,K.

The governing equation for
f^{(K)}
is

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f^{(K)}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\nabla_{x_{i}}\cdot\partial_{v_{i}}\left(g^{\prime}(v_{i})\int_{-\infty}^{v_{i}}f^{(K)}(\cdot,\tilde{v}_{i})\,d\tilde{v}_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\partial_{v_{i}}\left(m_{i}f^{(K)}\right)=0,\\
&m_{i}=m_{i}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K}):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\epsilon\Delta_{x}u(t,x_{i},\xi)|u(t,x_{j},\xi)=v_{j}\text{ for all }j\right],\end{aligned}\right.

which has to be completed by proper initial/boundary data. It is linear and integro-differential in
f^{(K)}
, featuring a spatially nonlocal convective term, an unclosed drift term in
v_{i}
-space through
m_{i}
, but no explicit source terms. Since
m_{i}
depends on the joint distribution of
(u_{1},\dots,u_{K},\Delta_{x_{i}}u)
by the definition of the conditional expectation, it cannot be expressed solely in terms of
f^{(K)}
, leaving (
2.2
) unclosed.

Now define the
K
-point joint CDF
F^{(K)}=F^{(K)}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K})
of
u
as

F^{(K)}=\text{Prob}(\{u(\cdot,x_{i})\leq v_{i}\text{ for all }i\})=\int_{\prod(-\infty,v_{i}]}f^{(K)}(t,x_{1},\tilde{v}_{1},\dots,x_{K},\tilde{v}_{K})\prod d\tilde{v}_{i}.

Clearly,
f^{(K)}=\partial_{v_{1}\cdots v_{K}}^{K}F^{(K)}
, and
F^{(K)}
is non-decreasing in each
v_{i}
. Integrating (
2.2
) over
v_{1},\dots,v_{K}
yields the governing equation for
F^{(K)}
:

(2.3)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}F^{(K)}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}g^{\prime}(v_{i})\cdot\nabla_{x_{i}}F^{(K)}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\prod_{1\leq j\leq K,\,j\neq i}(-\infty,v_{j}]}\left(m_{i}f^{(K)}\right)(\cdot,\tilde{v}_{i-1},v_{i},\tilde{v}_{i+1})\prod\nolimits_{1\leq j\leq K,\,j\neq i}d\tilde{v}_{j}=0,\end{split}

where
f^{(K)}=\partial^{K}_{\tilde{v}_{1}\cdots\tilde{v}_{i-1}v_{i}\tilde{v}_{i+1}\cdots\tilde{v}_{K}}F^{(K)}
, and
m_{i}
is as in (
2.2
). Compared to (
2.2
), the spatial term in (
2.3
) is local, but the unclosed terms are more involved for
K\geq 2
.

For the single-point case
K=1
, write
f=f(t,x,v)=f^{(1)}
and
F=F(t,x,v)=F^{(1)}
. Then (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) reduce to

(2.4)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f+\nabla_{x}\cdot\partial_{v}\left(g^{\prime}(v)\int_{-\infty}^{v}f(t,x,\tilde{v})\,d\tilde{v}\right)+\partial_{v}\left(m(t,x,v)f\right)=0,\\
&m(t,x,v):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\epsilon\Delta_{x}u|u=v\right].\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F+g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}F+m(t,x,v)\partial_{v}F=0,\\
&F(0,x,v)=G(x,v):=\text{Prob}(\{u_{0}(x,\xi)\leq v\})=\int_{\{z|u_{0}(x,z)\leq v\}}p_{\xi}(z)\,dz.\end{aligned}\right.

Eq. (
2.5
) is a linear transport equation, which greatly facilitates the subsequent analysis. In the sequel we always assume that the solution to (
2.5
) exists uniquely.

Remark 2.1
.

In analogy with the LMN hierarchy for the incompressible NSE, the statistical conservation laws for scalar problems, (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), can also be formulated as an infinite hierarchy of evolution equations for
f^{(1)},f^{(2)},\dots
. These results are presented in a separate work
[
20
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

While our main focus is on the random balance laws (
1.2
), the framework of statistical conservation laws also applies to stochastic PDEs of the form

\partial_{t}F+g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}F+m(t,x,v)\partial_{v}F=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(x)^{2}\partial_{v}^{2}F.

See a rigorous derivation of (
2.6
) in
[

]
. We will return to (

.

Remark 2.3
.

For completeness, we record the statistical conservation laws for (

which have the same structures as (

Next, if

\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}(t,x,v,w):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\delta(u(t,x,\xi)-v)\cdot\delta(\nabla_{x}u(t,x,\xi)-w)\right]

\mathcal{N}(u,\nabla_{x}u):=\nabla_{x}\cdot g(u)-Q(u,\nabla_{x}u)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\mathfrak{p}=\partial_{v}\left(\mathcal{N}(v,w)\mathfrak{p}\right)+\nabla_{w}\cdot\left(\immediate\immediate\immediate{{\partial\mathcal{N}}{\partial u}}(v,w)w\mathfrak{p}\right)+\nabla_{w}\cdot\left({\frac{\partial\mathcal{N}}{\partial\nabla_{x}u}}(v,w)\mathcal{A}(t,x,v,w)\right),\\

with

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[(\nabla_{x}\nabla_{x}u)\cdot\delta(u-v)\cdot\delta(\nabla_{x}u-w)\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\partial_{w}\mathcal{A}=-\partial_{x}\mathfrak{p}-w\partial_{v}\mathfrak{p}

\mathcal{A}=-\int_{-\infty}^{w}(\partial_{x}\mathfrak{p}+\tilde{w}\partial_{v}\mathfrak{p})(\cdot,v,\tilde{w})\,d\tilde{w}

2.1.
Formal derivation of (
2.2
) and (
2.4
)

We first record two relations that will be repeatedly used (here
u_{i}:=u(t,x_{i},\xi)
):

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}f^{(K)}=-\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\partial_{v_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\partial_{\eta}u_{k}\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\delta(u_{i}-v_{i})\right],\qquad\qquad\qquad\eta=t,x,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[u_{k}\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\delta(u_{i}-v_{i})\right]=v_{k}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\prod\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\delta(u_{i}-v_{i})\right]=v_{k}f^{(K)},\quad k=1,\dots,K.

For clarity we verify these relations for
K=1
; the extension to
K>1
is immediate.

Validation of (
2.7
).
For smooth compactly supported
\varphi(v)
(here
u=u(t,x,\xi)
),

\int\varphi(v)\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\delta(u-v)]\,dv=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\int\varphi(v)\delta(u-v)\,dv\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\varphi(u)\right].

Differentiating in
\eta
and using
\varphi^{\prime}(u)\partial_{\eta}u=\int\varphi^{\prime}(v)\delta(u-v)dv\cdot\partial_{\eta}u
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\varphi(v)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\delta(u-v)]\,dv=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\varphi^{\prime}(u)\partial_{\eta}u\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\int\varphi^{\prime}(v)\delta(u-v)\,dv\cdot\partial_{\eta}u\right]

\displaystyle=\int\varphi^{\prime}(v)\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\partial_{\eta}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]\,dv=-\int\varphi(v)\partial_{v}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\partial_{\eta}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]\,dv.

Since
\varphi
is arbitrary, (
2.7
) follows (with
f=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\delta(u-v)]
by (
2.1
)).

Validation of (
2.8
).
The desired identity is derived as follows.

\displaystyle\int\varphi(v)\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[u\delta(u-v)\right]\,dv

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\int\varphi(v)u\delta(u-v)\,dv\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\varphi(u)u\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\int\varphi(v)v\delta(u-v)\,dv\right]=\int\varphi(v)v\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\delta(u-v)\right]\,dv.

We now derive (
2.4
); the extension to the multi-point case (
2.2
) is straightforward. It is seen from (
2.7
) and (
1.2
) that

(2.9)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}f&=-\partial_{v}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\partial_{t}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]=\partial_{v}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{x}\cdot g(u)\delta(u-v)\right]-\partial_{v}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\epsilon\Delta_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]\\
&=\partial_{v}\left(g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]\right)-\partial_{v}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\epsilon\Delta_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]\end{split}

and

\nabla_{x}f=-\partial_{v}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right].

The last expression may lead to

(2.10)

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]=-\int_{-\infty}^{v}\nabla_{x}f(\cdot,\tilde{v})\,d\tilde{v}.

Substituting (
2.10
) into (
2.9
) gives the
f
-equation (
2.4
), provided one uses the standard relation (see
[
32
,
16
,
37
]
)

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\epsilon\Delta_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\epsilon\Delta_{x}u|u=v\right]f(\cdot,v).

Remark 2.4
.

The above derivation is rigorous for viscous scalar balance laws with
\epsilon>0
. In the inviscid case

, (

) becomes
\partial_{t}f=\partial_{v}\left(g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\nabla_{x}u\cdot\delta(u-v)\right]\right)
, which, after applying (

), leads formally to conservation laws of the same form as (

) and (

) with the viscous term absent. These equations are valid as long as the solution

2.2.
An intuitive derivation of (
2.5
)

We now present an intuitive derivation of (
2.5
). Recall that
p(\xi)
is the PDF of
\xi
. Start from the definition of the single-point CDF:

F(t,x,v)=\text{Prob}(\{u(t,x,\xi)\leq v\})=\int_{\{\xi:\ u(t,x,\xi)\leq v\}}p(\xi)\,d\xi.

Consider the map
\Xi_{t,x}:\xi\mapsto u(t,x,\xi)=v
parameterized by
(t,x)
, and let
\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
denote the set of preimages of
v
. Then
F(v)-F(v-)=\int_{\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}p(\xi)\,d\xi
. Since
F
is non-decreasing, it is continuous in
v
for almost every
v
. Consequently, for a.e.
v
, either
m(\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v))=0
(here
m
is the Lebesgue measure), or
p(\xi)=0
for a.e.
\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
. Without loss of generality, we assume
m(\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v))=0
and
p(\xi)>0
on
\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
. It then follows that

f(v)=\partial_{v}F=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}p(\xi)|\partial_{\xi}u|^{-1}.

If
u(\xi)\in C^{1}
, then for each
\xi=\xi(t,x,v)\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
, the implicit function theorem gives
(\partial_{\xi}u)(\partial_{t}\xi)=-\partial_{t}u
,
(\partial_{\xi}u)(\nabla_{x}\xi)=-\nabla_{x}u
, and
(\partial_{\xi}u)(\partial_{u}\xi)=1
. Hence, for a.e.
v
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}p(\xi)(\partial_{t}\xi)\cdot\text{sgn}(\partial_{\xi}u)=-\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}(\partial_{t}u)p(\xi)|\partial_{\xi}u|^{-1},

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}F

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}p(\xi)(\nabla_{x}\xi)\cdot\text{sgn}(\partial_{\xi}u)=-\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}(\nabla_{x}u)p(\xi)|\partial_{\xi}u|^{-1}.

Applying the governing equation (
1.2
) for
u
, we obtain

(2.11)

-(\partial_{t}F+g^{\prime}(u)\cdot\nabla_{x}F)=\epsilon\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}(\Delta_{x}u)p(\xi)|\partial_{\xi}u|^{-1}.

To handle the right-hand side, consider the joint PDF of
\Delta_{x}u
and
u
(for
a,v\in\mathbb{R}
):
\mathfrak{p}(a,v):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\delta(\Delta_{x}u(t,x,\xi)-a)\cdot\delta(u(t,x,\xi)-v)\right]=\int\delta(\Delta_{x}u(t,x,\xi)-a)\cdot\delta(u(t,x,\xi)-v)p(\xi)\,d\xi
.
By the definition of conditional expectation and formal properties of the Dirac delta distribution, we have

\displaystyle\epsilon f(\cdot,v)\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Delta_{x}u|u=v]

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int a\mathfrak{p}(a,v)\,da=\epsilon\int\Delta_{x}u(t,x,\xi)\cdot\delta(u(t,x,\xi)-v)p(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}\Delta_{x}u(t,x,\xi)p(\xi)|\partial_{\xi}u|^{-1}.

Substituting this into the right-hand side of (
2.11
) immediately recovers (
2.5
).

3.
Positivity of PDFs

This section addresses an important observation regarding the unclosed term
m=m(t,x,v)=\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\Delta_{x}u|u=v\right]
in the statistical conservation laws (
2.4
) and (
2.5
). Although
m
contains the factor
\epsilon
, we show that, for nonlinear fluxes
g(v)
,

m(t,x,v)\nrightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad\epsilon\to 0.

This non-vanishing behavior can be viewed as a scalar analogue of the so-called dissipative anomaly in turbulence, while the underlying mechanism here is closely related to the shocks formed in the inviscid conservation laws. The same phenomenon occurs for the multipoint terms
m_{i}
in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
): in general, at least one
m_{i}
does not vanish in the inviscid limit
\epsilon\to 0
. The reason is that these terms are essential for preserving the positivity of PDFs, or equivalently, the monotonicity of CDFs
F^{(K)}(\cdot,v_{i})
with respect to
v_{i}
(
\forall i=1,\dots,K
). We illustrate this ‘by contradiction’: the positivity of
f(v)
is not preserved
globally
and
unconditionally
by the following equation with the unclosed term dropped:

(3.1)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}F_{S0}+g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}F_{S0}&=0,\\
F_{S0}(0,x,v)&=G(x,v),\end{split}

where
G(x,v)
has sufficient regularity and is non-decreasing in
v
for all
(x,v)
. Then we have

Theorem 3.1
.

The solution
F_{S0}(t,x,v)
to (
3.1
) is non-decreasing in
v
for all
(t,x,v)
if and only if
g^{\prime\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}G\leq 0
for all
(x,v)
.

Proof.

By the method of characteristics, the solution is
F_{S0}(t,x,v)=G(x-g^{\prime}(v)t,v)
, which gives

\partial_{v}F_{S0}(t,x,v)=-tg^{\prime\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}G(x-g^{\prime}(v)t,v)+\partial_{v}G(x-g^{\prime}(v)t,v).

Since
\partial_{v}G\geq 0
, the monotonicity
\partial_{v}F_{S0}\geq 0
holds if
g^{\prime\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}G\leq 0
for all
(x,v)
. Conversely, if
g^{\prime\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}G(x_{0},v_{0})>0
for some
(x_{0},v_{0})
, then for sufficiently large
t>\partial_{v}G(x_{0},v_{0})/(g^{\prime\prime}(v_{0})\cdot\nabla_{x}G(x_{0},v_{0}))
, one can find
x=x_{0}+g^{\prime}(v_{0})t
such that
\partial_{v}F_{S0}(t,x,v_{0})<0
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

For
d=1
and a convex flux
g(v)
with
g^{\prime\prime}(v)\neq 0
for all
v
,
F_{S0}(t,x,v)
is non-decreasing in
v
for all
(t,x,v)
if and only if

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{x}G\leq 0\text{ for all }(x,v),\quad\text{if }g^{\prime\prime}(v)>0,\\
&\partial_{x}G\geq 0\text{ for all }(x,v),\quad\text{if }g^{\prime\prime}(v)<0.\end{aligned}\right.

Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
imply that, for nonlinear
g(v)
, there always exist physically admissible initial CDFs
G(x,v)
such that the solution to (
3.1
) loses monotonicity in
v
, rendering it non-physical. Hence, (
3.1
) does not constitute the correct statistical conservation law in the inviscid limit. We give a 1D example to illustrate this point.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the 1D scalar Burgers equation
\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(\frac{u^{2}}{2})=0
on
D=[0,2\pi]
with periodic boundary conditions and initial data
u_{0}(x,\xi)=\sin x+\xi
, where
\xi
is a zero-mean Gaussian variable with standard deviation
\sigma
. The initial CDF is

(3.2)

G(x,v)=\Phi\left(\frac{v-\sin x}{\sigma}\right),

where
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{x}\exp(-\frac{s^{2}}{2})\,ds=\frac{1}{2}\text{erfc}(-\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}})
is the standard normal CDF. Here,
\partial_{x}G
can be positive, generating negative
\partial_{v}F_{S0}
according to Corollary
3.2
. Indeed,

(3.3a)

\displaystyle F_{S0}(t,x,v)=G(x-vt,v)=\Phi\left(\frac{v-\sin(x-vt)}{\sigma}\right),

(3.3b)

\displaystyle f_{S0}(t,x,v):=\partial_{v}F_{S0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{(v-\sin(x-vt))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\left(1+t\cos(x-vt)\right).

Given any
x
,
f_{S0}
starts to take negative values at
t=1
, coinciding with shock formation in the underlying Burgers equation.

We continue the discussion from Remark
2.4
to show that, in this example,
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[(\partial_{x}u)\delta(u-v)\right]
becomes discontinuous in
v
for
t>1
, thereby invalidating (
2.10
).
For
t>1
, the (unique entropic weak) solution
u(t,x,0)
develops a shock at
x=\pi
, with
u(t,\pi^{-},0)=v_{s}>0
,
u(t,\pi^{+},0)=-v_{s}
, and the product
|\partial_{x}u(t,\pi^{-},0)\,\partial_{x}u(t,\pi^{+},0)|
is bounded away from zero. For other values of
\xi
, the shock in
u(t,x,\xi)
is shifted to the position
\pi+\xi t\pmod{2\pi}
according to (
5.5
). Moreover, we will show in Theorem
5.1
that the (classical) solution
u(t,\pi,\xi)
is strictly increasing in
\xi
for any
\epsilon>0
. Hence, in the inviscid limit
\epsilon\to 0
, the monotonicity persists for the entropic weak solution, implying
u(t,\pi,0^{-})=-v_{s}
and
u(t,\pi,0^{+})=v_{s}
. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\!\left[(\partial_{x}u)\delta(u-v)\right](t,\pi,v)=\int\partial_{x}u(t,\pi,z)\,\delta(u(t,\pi,z)-v)\,p(z)\,dz

vanishes for
-v_{s}<v<v_{s}
but remains bounded away from zero as
v\nearrow-v_{s}
or
v\searrow v_{s}
. Thus, this quantity is discontinuous at
(t>1,x=\pi,v=\pm v_{s})
in the inviscid limit, which renders (
2.10
) invalid. Intuitively, a ‘correction term’ must be incorporated into (
2.10
) in this limit. This correction is characterized by the nonzero contribution of the
m
-containing term in the statistical conservation laws.

This example will be further analyzed in Section
5.1
and verified numerically in Section
6.1
(referred to as Case
I
).

Remark 3.4
.

For stochastic scalar balance laws, the ‘zero-
\epsilon
’ limit of (
2.6
),
\partial_{t}F+g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}F=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\partial_{v}^{2}F
with
F(0,t,v)=G(x,v)
, does not globally and unconditionally preserve the positivity of
f(v)
. Indeed, differentiating with respect to
v
gives the governing equation for
f=\partial_{v}F
:

\partial_{t}f+g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}f+g^{\prime\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}F=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\partial_{v}^{2}f,\quad f(0,x,v)=\partial_{v}G(x,v).

Take an initial condition
\partial_{v}G(x,v)
such that
\partial_{v}G(x_{0},v_{0})=0
at a local minimum, and assume
g^{\prime\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}G>\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\partial_{v}^{2}(\partial_{v}G)
at
(x_{0},v_{0})
. Clearly, such a
G
can be constructed. Then, for
t>0
, the solution
f(t,x,v)
becomes negative in a neighborhood of
(x_{0},v_{0})
. We note that, for
g(v)=v^{2}/2
, an exact statistical conservation law in the inviscid limit was obtained in
[
11
]
.

The above discussion highlights that the unclosed term
m(t,x,v)
in the statistical conservation laws plays a crucial role: it automatically ensures the positivity of
f(\cdot,v)
for all
(t,x,v)
. In practice, however,
m(t,x,v)
is unknown and can only be approximated via closure models or numerical sampling. It would therefore be desirable to develop a ‘monotonicity-preserving’ criterion for the reconstructed
m(t,x,v)
in (
2.5
). Generally, the available condition is highly implicit, derived through the method of characteristics, as presented below. This analysis provides insights into several special cases, which we summarize as follows.

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider the linear transport equation (
2.5
) with unknown
m(t,x,v)
and a physically admissible initial CDF
G(x,v)
. Suppose that
m(t,x,v)
is sufficiently regular to ensure well-posedness of the characteristic system associated with (
2.5
). Then:

(i). There exist infinitely many choices of
m(t,x,v)
such that
F(\cdot,v)
remains a CDF for all
(t,x)
.

(ii). In the spatially homogeneous case (that (
2.5
) is independent of
x
), if
m(t,v)
satisfies
|m(t,v)|\leq C(t)(1+|v|)
and
C(t)\in L^{1}([0,\infty))
, then
F(\cdot,v)
remains a CDF for all
t>0
.

(iii). If
m=m(t)
(no dependence on
x
or
v
) and
g^{\prime\prime}>0
(convex laws), then there exist initial CDFs
G(x,v)
such that
F(\cdot,v)
becomes decreasing in
v
. This indicates that
m
must depend on
x
and/or
v
in general.

The first statement is immediate: any
\epsilon>0
in (
1.2
), together with a compatible initial datum consistent with
G(x,v)
, produces one valid
m(t,x,v)
. To analyze Statements (ii) and (iii), we examine the characteristic system of (
2.5
) for
\psi(\tau;t,x,v):=(X(\tau;t,x,v),V(\tau;t,x,v))
, which satisfies

(3.4)

\frac{d}{d\tau}\psi=\left(g^{\prime}(V(\tau)),\,m(\tau,\psi(\tau))\right),\quad\psi|_{\tau=t}=(x,\,v).

Thus,

(3.5)

F(t,x,v)=G(\psi(0;t,x,v)).

The requirement
f(\cdot,v)\geq 0
can be expressed as (omitting
(t,x,v)
-dependence):

(3.6)

\partial_{v}F=\nabla_{(x,v)}G(\psi(0))\cdot\partial_{v}\psi(0)\geq 0,

for all
(t,x,v)
. Here,
\partial_{v}\psi(\tau)
evolves according to

\frac{d}{d\tau}\partial_{v}\psi=\left(g^{\prime\prime}(V(\tau))\partial_{v}V,\,\nabla_{(x,v)}m(\tau,\psi(\tau))\cdot\partial_{v}\psi(\tau)\right),\quad(\partial_{v}\psi)|_{\tau=t}=(\bm{0},\,1),

with
\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This yields

(3.7)

\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{v}X(0)\\
\partial_{v}V(0)\end{bmatrix}=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}A(\tau)d\tau}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\text{ with }A(\tau)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}_{d\times d}&g^{\prime\prime}(V(\tau))\\
\nabla_{x}m(\tau,\psi(\tau))^{T}&\partial_{v}m(\tau,\psi(\tau))\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, condition (
3.6
), together with (
3.4
) and (
3.7
), provides a highly implicit realizability criterion for
m(t,x,v)
ensuring the positivity of
f(\cdot,v)
.

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

(ii). For the spatially homogeneous case with
F=F(t,v)
, condition (
3.6
) reduces to

\partial_{v}F(t,v)=G^{\prime}(V(0))\partial_{v}V(0)\geq 0.

From (
3.7
), we have

\partial_{v}V(0;t,v)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{v}m(\tau,V(\tau;t,v))\,d\tau\right)>0,

which shows that the nonnegativity of
f(t,v)
is preserved for all
(t,v)
.
Since
F(t,v)=G(V(0;t,v))
, it remains to verify that
V(0;t,v)\to\pm\infty
as
v\to\pm\infty
. Introduce
\tilde{V}(\tau):=V(t-\tau)
, so that
\tilde{V}(0)=v
,
\tilde{V}(t)=V(0)
, and

\frac{d}{d\tau}\tilde{V}(\tau)=-m(t-\tau,\tilde{V}(\tau)).

Assume
v\gg 1
. Denoting by
M
an upper bound of
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}C(s)\,ds
, we obtain
\frac{d}{d\tau}\tilde{V}\geq-C(t-\tau)(1+\tilde{V})
, which implies

\tilde{V}(t)\geq(v+1)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}C(t-s)\,ds\right)-1\geq(v+1)e^{-M}-1.

Thus
v\to\infty
leads to
V(0)=\tilde{V}(t)\to\infty
for any
t>0
.
Similarly, for
v\ll-1
we obtain
d\tilde{V}/d\tau\leq C(t-\tau)(1-\tilde{V})
and hence
\tilde{V}(t)\leq(v-1)e^{-M}+1
, which shows
V(0)=\tilde{V}(t)\to-\infty
as
v\to-\infty
.

(iii). Consider the one-dimensional case (
x\in\mathbb{R}
). When
m=m(t)
, (
3.7
) yields
\partial_{v}X(0)=-\int_{0}^{t}g^{\prime\prime}(V(\tau))\,d\tau
, and
\partial_{v}V(0)=1
. Hence condition (
3.6
) becomes

(3.8)

\partial_{x}G(\psi(0))\int_{0}^{t}g^{\prime\prime}(V(\tau))\,d\tau\leq\partial_{v}G(\psi(0)).

If
g^{\prime\prime}(v)>0
for all
v
, condition (
3.8
) can be violated by choosing
\partial_{v}G(\psi(0))=0
and
\partial_{x}G(\psi(0))>0
. Such
G(x,v)
are clearly attainable as CDFs in
v
for each
x
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Regarding the special case
m=m(t)
, consider the CDF equation (
2.5
) with
d=1
(one-dimensional),
m=m(t)
, and a convex flux
g(v)
with
g^{\prime\prime}(v)>0
for all
v
. It is seen from (
3.8
) that the solution
F(t,x,v)
preserves the monotonicity in
v
for all
(t,x,v)
if the initial condition
G(x,v)
is non-increasing in
x
for any
(x,v)
. This condition is also necessary if
\inf_{v}g^{\prime\prime}>0
or
m\in L^{1}([0,\infty))
.

4.
Numerical approximation of the unclosed term

We now proceed to approximate the unclosed term
m(t,x,v)=\epsilon\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Delta_{x}u|u]
in (
2.5
) by a data-driven estimator
\hat{m}(t,x,v)
constructed from numerical samples. This straightforward procedure provides a unified framework for handling different types of statistical conservation laws (namely, to evaluate the PDFs/CDFs).

For (
2.5
), the procedures are collected as in Algorithm
1
. The key step is to construct the sampling-based estimator
\hat{m}_{N}(t,x,v)
in (
4.2
) for
m(t,x,v)
. Here,
K_{h_{v}}
is a kernel with bandwidth
h_{v}>0
. A simple choice is the uniform kernel

(4.1)

K_{h_{v}}(x)=\frac{1}{h_{v}}\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq\frac{h_{v}}{2}}.

This means averaging
(\Delta_{x}u)_{i}
over those samples
u_{i}
that fall within distance
\tfrac{h_{v}}{2}
of
v
.

Remark 4.1
.

In many cases, the estimator
\hat{m}_{N}
can only be constructed on a spatio-temporal grid
\{(t^{n},x_{\mathbf{j}})\}
because the samples
u_{i}(t,x)
and
(\Delta_{x}u)_{i}(t,x)
are computed numerically. One has to interpolate
\hat{m}_{N}
to off-grid points
(t,x,v)
when solving (
4.4
). Nevertheless, in this work, we avoid this issue by focusing on analytically solvable special cases (see Section
5
), so that
\hat{m}
can be evaluated by (
4.2
) for all
(t,x,v)
.

Algorithm 1

Sampling-based approximation to the statistical conservation law (
2.5
)

1:

Parameters:
Viscosity
\epsilon>0
in (
1.2
), number of samples
N
, bandwidth
h_{v}>0
for the estimator, and time step
\Delta t
.

2:

Input:
Initial condition
u_{0}(x,\xi)
, and PDF
p(\xi)
of the random variable
\xi
in (
1.2
), which thus determine the initial CDF
G(x,v)
of (
2.5
).

3:

Sampling:
Draw
N
i.i.d. samples
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from
p(\xi)
.

4:

for

i=1,\dots,N

do

5:

Solve the scalar balance law (
1.2
) with initial data
u_{0}(x,\xi_{i})
to obtain

u_{i}(t,x):=u(t,x,\xi_{i}),\quad(\Delta_{x}u)_{i}(t,x):=\Delta_{x}u(t,x,\xi_{i}).

6:

end

for

7:

Estimator construction:
For each
(t,x,v)
, compute the Nadaraya-Watson-type estimator for the conditional expectation:

(4.2)

\hat{m}_{N}(t,x,v)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\epsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\Delta_{x}u)_{i}K_{h_{v}}(u_{i}-v)}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h_{v}}(u_{i}-v)},&\text{if denominator $\neq 0$,}\\[11.99998pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

8:

Approximation of CDF:
Solve the approximating transport equation

(4.3)

\hat{F}_{t}+g^{\prime}(v)\cdot\nabla_{x}\hat{F}+\hat{m}_{N}(t,x,v)\hat{F}_{v}=0,\quad\hat{F}(0,x,v)=G(x,v),

via backward characteristic tracing for
\hat{\psi}(\tau;t,x,v)=(\hat{X}(\tau;t,x,v),\hat{V}(\tau;t,x,v))
. For each
(t,x,v)
,
\hat{\psi}
satisfies

(4.4)

\frac{d}{d\tau}\hat{\psi}=\left(g^{\prime}(\hat{V}(\tau)),\,\hat{m}_{N}(\tau,\hat{\psi}(\tau)\right),\quad\hat{\psi}|_{\tau=t}=(x,\,v).

Use the first-order Euler method to compute
\hat{\psi}(0)
with the time step
\Delta t
.

9:

Output:
Approximate CDF

(4.5)

\hat{F}(t,x,v)=G(\hat{\psi}(0)).

It is seen that, if
h_{v}
is taken as a fixed value (i.e., independent of
N
), then by the law of large numbers, as
N\to\infty
, the estimator
\hat{m}_{N}(t,x,v)/\epsilon
in (
4.2
) converges to

\frac{\int_{v-h_{v}/2}^{v+h_{v}/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}a\mathfrak{p}_{\Delta_{x}u,u}(t,x,a,\tilde{v})\,da\,d\tilde{v}}{\int_{v-h_{v}/2}^{v+h_{v}/2}f(t,x,\tilde{v})\,d\tilde{v}}=\int_{v-h_{v}/2}^{v+h_{v}/2}\omega(t,x,\tilde{v})\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Delta_{x}u|u](t,x,\tilde{v})\,d\tilde{v},

where
\mathfrak{p}_{\Delta_{x}u,u}(t,x,a,v)
denotes the joint PDF of
\Delta_{x}u(t,x)=a
and
u(t,x)=v
, and
\omega(t,x,\tilde{v})=f(t,x,\tilde{v})/\int_{v-h_{v}/2}^{v+h_{v}/2}f(t,x,\tilde{v})\,d\tilde{v}
. Clearly, this is the weighted average of
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Delta_{x}u|u=\tilde{v}]
over the interval
\tilde{v}\in[v-h_{v}/2,v+h_{v}/2]
and in general it does not coincide with the desired quantity
m(v)/\epsilon=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Delta_{x}u|u=v]
whenever
h_{v}>0
(it is therefore referred to as a ‘
biased
estimator’).

Therefore, in the limit
N\to\infty
, it is necessary that
h_{v}\to 0
while simultaneously
Nh_{v}\to\infty
(ensuring that the mean number of samples in the interval goes to infinity). It has been established that, for kernels such as (
4.1
), if the ‘optimal’ bandwidth is chosen as
h_{v}=CN^{-1/5}
, the mean integrated squared error (MISE) satisfies

(4.6)

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\hat{m}_{N}-m\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{v}([v_{min},v_{max}])}\right]=C^{\prime}N^{-4/5}

in the large
N
limit
[
5
]
. It should be emphasized that the coefficients
C,\,C^{\prime}
are usually
not
known in prior as they are related to properties of
m(v)
.

We close this section with an analysis of the discrepancy between the approximated system (
4.3
) and the original CDF equation (
2.5
). In certain settings, the difference
\|F-\hat{F}\|
caused by
\|m-\hat{m}\|
can be quantified with an error estimate.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
g^{\prime\prime}
is bounded with
|g^{\prime\prime}|\leq C
. Consider both (
2.5
) and (
4.3
) on
(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times D_{x,v}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. Further assume that
\nabla_{x,v}(m,G)\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times D_{x,v})
. Then we have

\|(F-\hat{F})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\leq e^{(C+\|\nabla_{x,v}m\|_{L^{\infty}})t}\|\nabla_{x,v}G\|_{L^{\infty}}\|m-\hat{m}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}t,\quad\forall\,t\leq T.

Proof.

For the original CDF equation (
2.5
), the method of characteristics (
3.4
) yields the representation (
3.5
):
F(t,x,v)=G(X(0),V(0))
. Similarly, for the perturbed system (
4.3
), the solution
\hat{F}
is given by (
4.4
) and (
4.5
). Subtracting (
3.4
) and (
4.4
) and integrating over
\tau
, we obtain

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle\psi-\hat{\psi}|(s)\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left(|g^{\prime}(V(\tau))-g^{\prime}(\hat{V}(\tau))|+|m(\tau,\psi(\tau))-\hat{m}_{N}(\tau,\hat{\psi}(\tau))|\right)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left(C|V-\hat{V}|(\tau)+|m(\tau,\psi(\tau))-m(\tau,\hat{\psi}(\tau))|+|m(\tau,\hat{\psi}(\tau))-\hat{m}_{N}(\tau,\hat{\psi}(\tau))|\right)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left((C+\|\nabla_{x,v}m\|_{L^{\infty}})|\psi-\hat{\psi}|(\tau)+\|m-\hat{m}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\,d\tau.

By Gronwall’s inequality, it follows that

|\psi-\hat{\psi}|(s)\leq e^{(C+\|\nabla_{x,v}m\|_{L^{\infty}})(t-s)}\|m-\hat{m}_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}}\cdot(t-s),\quad s\in[0,t].

Therefore, the desired error estimate is a direct consequence of

\|(F-\hat{F})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\leq\|\nabla_{x,v}G\|_{L^{\infty}}|\psi-\hat{\psi}|(0),

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
4.2
shows that, under suitable assumptions, the error between the exact solution
F
and the perturbed solution
\hat{F}
is controlled by the discrepancy between
m(t,x,v)
and
\hat{m}_{N}(t,x,v)
. Moreover, the bound indicates that the error can grow substantially in
t
. The conditions of the theorem (namely, boundedness of
g^{\prime\prime}
and of
\nabla_{x,v}(m,G)
) are satisfied by the test cases presented in the subsequent sections.

5.
Parametric initial data

This section examines special cases of the scalar problem (
1.2
) where explicit analytical results can be obtained. These results will also serve as a foundation for the numerical tests presented in the next section. We focus on two classes of initial conditions for which the mapping
\Xi_{t,x}:\xi\mapsto u(t,x,\xi)=v
is injective for every
(t,x)
. In such cases, we can define the inverse map
\xi=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
, and the system is fully characterized by

(5.1)

\displaystyle F(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(u(t,x,\xi)\leq v)=\int_{-\infty}^{\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}p(\xi^{\prime})\,d\xi^{\prime},

(5.2)

\displaystyle f(t,x,v)

\displaystyle=\partial_{v}F(t,x,v)=p\circ\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)\cdot\left[\left.\partial_{\xi}u\right|_{\xi=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}\right]^{-1},

while the unclosed term in (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) reduces to

(5.3)

m(t,x,v)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Delta_{x}u(t,x,\xi)|u=v]=\left.\Delta_{x}u\right|_{\xi=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)},

since the conditioning uniquely determines
\xi
. Consequently, in this case
m
does not depend on the distribution
p(\xi)
of the random variable
\xi
.

5.1.
First type of random initial data

Let
U_{T}=(0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the scalar problem (
1.2
) on
(t,x,\xi)\in U_{T}\times\mathbb{R}
with initial data
u_{0}(x,\xi)
. Assume that
\partial_{\xi}u(\cdot,\xi)\in C^{1,2}(U_{T})\cap C(\bar{U}_{T})
for every
\xi
.
If
\partial_{\xi}u_{0}\geq 0
(resp.
\partial_{\xi}u_{0}\leq 0
) for all
(x,\xi)
and
\partial_{\xi}u_{0}\not\equiv 0
, then the solution
u(t,x,\xi)
is strictly increasing (resp. decreasing) in
\xi
for all
(t,x,\xi)\in U_{T}\times\mathbb{R}
.

In particular, if
\lambda=\inf_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{\xi}u_{0}(\cdot,\xi)>0
for all
\xi
, then there exists a constant
K=K(\xi,T)
such that
\partial_{\xi}u\geq\lambda e^{Kt}
on
U_{T}
for all
\xi
.

Proof.

We first consider the case
\partial_{\xi}u_{0}\geq 0
; the case
\partial_{\xi}u_{0}\leq 0
follows by replacing
\xi
with
-\xi
. Define
w=w(t,x;\xi):=e^{Mt}\partial_{\xi}u(t,x,\xi)
. Then
w
satisfies

Lw:=\partial_{t}w+g^{\prime}(u)\cdot\nabla_{x}w+(g^{\prime\prime}(u)\cdot\nabla_{x}u+M)w-\epsilon\Delta_{x}w=0

with initial condition
w(0,\cdot;\xi)\geq 0
,
w(0,\cdot;\xi)\not\equiv 0
. Choose
M\geq-\inf_{\bar{U}_{T}}g^{\prime\prime}(u)\cdot\nabla_{x}u
, which is always possible, so that
c(t,x;\xi)=g^{\prime\prime}(u)\cdot\nabla_{x}u+M\geq 0
. The strong maximum principle for parabolic operators
[
12
]
then implies that
w(t,x;\xi)>0
on
U_{T}
for all
\xi
. Hence
\partial_{\xi}u>0
throughout.

For the bound, suppose
w(0,x;\xi)\geq\lambda>0
. Let
K^{\prime}=K^{\prime}(\xi,T)\geq\|c(\cdot;\xi)\|_{C^{0}(\bar{U}_{T})}
. Then,

L(w-\lambda e^{-K^{\prime}t})=\lambda(K^{\prime}-c)e^{-K^{\prime}t}\geq 0.

Since
\left.(w-\lambda e^{-K^{\prime}t})\right|_{t=0}\geq 0
, the weak maximum principle
[
12
]
ensures
w-\lambda e^{-K^{\prime}t}\geq 0
on
U_{T}
. Recalling
w=e^{Mt}\partial_{\xi}u
, we thus obtain
\partial_{\xi}u(t,x,\xi)\geq\lambda e^{-(K^{\prime}+M)t}
.
∎

We now revisit Example
3.3
. Consider the 1D Burgers equation with
d=1
, flux
g(u)=u^{2}/2
, and an initial condition of separable form (referred to as Case
I
):

(5.4)

\left\{\ \begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u=\epsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u,\quad(t,x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}/[0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R},\\
&u(0,x,\xi)=s(x)+\xi,\end{aligned}\right.

with
s(x)
being
2\pi
-periodic, e.g.
s(x)=\sin x
. Then Theorem
5.1
applies obviously. Without loss of generality, assume
\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0
. It is straightforward to verify that

(5.5)

u(t,x,\xi)=u(t,x-\xi t,0)+\xi,

which does not hold for general fluxes
g
. Differentiating with respect to
\xi
yields

(5.6)

\partial_{\xi}u=1-t\left.\partial_{x}u(t,x^{\prime},0)\right|_{x^{\prime}=x-\xi t},

where
\xi=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
is uniquely determined from
u(t,x-\xi t,0)=v-\xi
. Consequently, (
5.3
) reduces to

(5.7)

m(t,x,v)=\left.\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x,\xi)\right|_{\xi=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)}=\left.\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x^{\prime},0)\right|_{x^{\prime}=x-\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)t}.

Proposition 5.2
.

For the model problem (
5.4
),
m(\cdot,v)
in (
5.7
) is periodic in
v
with period
2\pi/t
and satisfies

m(t,x,v)=m(t,0,v-\frac{x}{t}),\quad t>0.

Proof.

Since
u
is
2\pi
-periodic in
x
, (
5.5
) implies
u(t,x,\xi+2\pi/t)=u(t,x,\xi)+2\pi/t
, hence
\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v+2\pi/t)=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)+2\pi/t
. Substituting into (
5.7
) and using the
x
-periodicity of
\partial_{x}^{2}u
, we obtain

m(t,x,v+2\pi/t)=\left.\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x^{\prime},0)\right|_{x^{\prime}=x-\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)t-2\pi}=m(t,x,v).

For the second equality, we show that

U_{t,0}^{-1}\left(v-\frac{x}{t}\right)=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)-\frac{x}{t}.

Let
\xi=U_{t,0}^{-1}\left(v-x/t\right)
. It is seen that
v-x/t=u(t,0,\xi)=u(t,-\xi t,0)+\xi
, which implies
v=u(t,x-(\xi+x/t)t,0)+\xi+x/t=u(t,x,\xi+x/t)
, and thus
\xi+x/t=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle m(t,x,v)=\left.\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x^{\prime},0)\right|_{x^{\prime}=x-\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)t}=\left.\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x^{\prime},0)\right|_{x^{\prime}=0-U_{t,0}^{-1}(v-\frac{x}{t})t}=m(t,0,v-\frac{x}{t}).

This completes the proof.
∎

5.2.
Second type of random initial data

Now restrict ourselves to the 1D Burgers equation. Consider

(5.8)

\left\{\ \begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u=\epsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u,\quad(t,x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}/[0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R},\\
&u(0,x,\xi)=\xi s(x)\end{aligned}\right.

with a periodic profile
s(x)=s(x+2\pi)
. We establish the following property.

Proposition 5.3
.

For system (
5.8
), if
s(x)=-s(2\pi-x)
for all
x
and
s(x)
does not change sign on
(0,\pi)
, then the solution
u(t,x,\xi)
is monotone in
\xi
for all
t,\xi
and
x\in(0,\pi)\cup(\pi,2\pi)
.

Proof.

First note that
u(t,0,\xi)=u(t,\pi,\xi)=0
for all
(t,\xi)
, since both the initial data and the governing Burgers equation preserve the symmetry
u(x,\cdot)=-u(-x,\cdot)=-u(2\pi-x,\cdot)
. Assume without loss of generality that
s>0
on
(0,\pi)
. Then, by arguments analogous to those in the proof of Theorem
5.1
, we consider
w=w(t,x;\xi):=e^{Mt}\partial_{\xi}u
, which satisfies a parabolic equation on
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times[0,\pi]
with boundary conditions
w\equiv 0
at
x=0,\pi
(since
u\equiv 0
at these points). By the strong maximum principle, we immediately deduce
w>0
on
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times(0,\pi)
, and hence
w<0
on
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times(\pi,2\pi)
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

In analogy with Theorem
5.1
, one can further derive an exponentially decaying lower bound for
\partial_{\xi}u
on
(0,\pi)
. Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the principal eigenvalue of the operator
A:=-\epsilon\partial^{2}_{x}+u\partial_{x}+(\partial_{x}u+M)
and let
\varphi
be the corresponding positive eigenfunction on
(0,\pi)
. Recall that
w=e^{Mt}\partial_{\xi}u
and
L=\partial_{t}+A
. Then we have
L(w-\epsilon e^{-Kt}\varphi)=\epsilon(K-\lambda_{1})\varphi e^{-Kt}\geq 0
by choosing
K\geq\lambda_{1}
. Moreover, since
w(0,\cdot)-\epsilon\varphi\geq 0
for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, due to
\partial_{\nu}w(0,\cdot)<0
(negative outward normal derivative on the boundary), the weak maximum principle yields
\partial_{\xi}u\geq\epsilon e^{-(K+M)t}\varphi
.

Therefore, under the assumptions of Proposition
5.3
, the mapping
\Xi_{t,x}:\xi\mapsto v=u(t,x,\cdot)
is invertible for all
x\in(0,2\pi)\setminus\{\pi\}
, with the CDF and PDF given by (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), respectively. By Oleinik’s inequality
\partial_{x}u<1/t
, we obtain
u(t,x,\xi)<x/t
, which implies
f(t,x,v)=0
for all
v\geq x/t
,
t>0
and
x\in(0,\pi)
. Furthermore, it holds that
f(t,0,v)=f(t,\pi,v)=\delta(v)
for all
(t,v)
. For the ‘unclosed’ term in (
5.7
), it is straightforward to verify that
m(0,x,v)=-v
for
x\neq k\pi
.

Remark 5.5
.

The 1D Burgers equation in (
5.4
) and (
5.8
) admits a closed-form solution via the Cole-Hopf transformation:

(5.9)

\displaystyle u(t,x,\xi)

\displaystyle=-2\epsilon\frac{\partial_{x}\phi}{\phi},

(5.10)

\displaystyle\phi=\phi(t,x;\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}A_{0}^{[\epsilon,\xi]}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\epsilon k^{2}t}}{\pi}\left(A_{k}^{[\epsilon,\xi]}\cos kx+B_{k}^{[\epsilon,\xi]}\sin kx\right),

with

\begin{bmatrix}A_{k}^{[\epsilon,\xi]}\\
B_{k}^{[\epsilon,\xi]}\end{bmatrix}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\phi_{0}(x;\epsilon,\xi)\begin{bmatrix}\cos kx\\
\sin kx\end{bmatrix}dx,\quad\phi_{0}(x;\epsilon,\xi)=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\int_{0}^{x}u(0,x^{\prime},\xi)\,dx^{\prime}\right].

Differentiation of (
5.9
) gives
\partial_{x}^{2}u=-2\epsilon\frac{\partial_{x}^{3}\phi}{\phi}+\frac{3}{2\epsilon}u\partial_{x}u-\frac{u^{3}}{4\epsilon^{2}}
, where
\partial_{x}u
and higher derivatives of
\phi
can be obtained analogously. These formulas allow semi-analytical computation of the statistical quantities
f(t,x,v)
,
F(t,x,v)
, and
m(t,x,v)
in (
5.4
). For given
(t,x,v)
, the corresponding
\xi=\Xi_{t,x}^{-1}(v)
is located by solving
u(t,x,\xi)=v
(e.g. via the bisection method), after which (
5.1
), (
5.2
), and (
5.7
) apply.

7.
Conclusions

We have developed a statistical framework for viscous balance laws with random initial data. In the scalar setting, we derived statistical conservation laws governing the evolution of (multi-point) probability density functions (PDFs)
f=f(t,x,v)
and cumulative distribution functions (CDFs)
F=F(t,x,v)
of the solution. These laws take the form of linear but
unclosed
kinetic-type transport equations in the extended
(t,x,v)
-space (see (
2.4
) and (
2.5
)). A drift coefficient
m(t,x,v)
, encoding conditional expectations of the viscous contribution in the original balance law, emerges as the key closure term. Notably, the statistical conservation law exhibits a dissipative anomaly: even as viscosity vanishes, the
m
-term persists because it is essential for maintaining the nonnegativity of PDFs.

For numerical computation, we introduced a sampling-based estimator
\hat{m}_{N}(t,x,v)
constructed from realizations of the initial data. This yields a ‘closed’ equation that allows direct approximation of PDFs/CDFs. For single-point CDFs, we established an a-priori error bound showing that, over finite time, the approximation error is controlled by the estimation error
\|m-\hat{m}_{N}\|
. To validate the method, we designed two analytically solvable examples where the solution
u(t,x,\xi)
remains monotone in the random parameter
\xi
. Numerical tests confirm that the error
\|(F-F_{\mathrm{anal}})(t,x,\cdot)\|
decreases as the number of samples grows, and is consistently smaller than the error of CDFs reconstructed directly from samples. These results demonstrate that the statistical conservation law framework provides a robust and efficient approach for uncertainty propagation and correlation analysis in viscous balance laws.

Future works may extend the framework to multi-point correlations in both scalar and Navier-Stokes systems, which are fundamental to statistical turbulence theory.

Appendix A
Statistical conservation laws for 1D hyperbolic systems

The 1D hyperbolic conservation system for
u=u(t,x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
reads as:

(A.1)

\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}g(u)=\partial_{x}(\bm{\epsilon}\partial_{x}u),\\
&u(0,x,\xi(\omega))=u_{0}(x,\xi(\omega)),\end{split}\quad t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\omega\in\Omega,

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a smooth flux and
\bm{\epsilon}
is an
n\times n
diffusion matrix. Following the procedure outlined in Section
2
, we formally obtain the evolution equations for the
K
-point PDF of
u
,
f^{(K)}=f^{(K)}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\cdots,x_{K},v_{K})
, given by

(A.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f^{(K)}+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\nabla_{v_{i}}\cdot\left(Dg(v_{i})\mathcal{B}_{i}\right)+\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\nabla_{v_{i}}\cdot\left(m_{i}f^{(K)}\right)=0,\\
&\nabla_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathcal{B}_{i}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K})=-\partial_{x_{i}}f^{(K)},\quad i=1,\dots,K,\\
&m_{i}=m_{i}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K}):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}(\bm{\epsilon}\partial_{x_{i}}u)|u(t,x_{j},\xi)=v_{j},\text{ for all }j\right],\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}(t,x_{1},v_{1},\dots,x_{K},v_{K})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\Big[(\partial_{x_{i}}u)\prod_{j=1}^{K}\delta(u(t,x_{j},\xi)-v_{j})\Big]
, and
Dg
denotes the Jacobian of
g
.

For
K=1
, the above system reduces to the single-point PDF
f=f(t,x,v)
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, governed by

(A.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}f+\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(Dg(v)\mathcal{B}(t,x,v)\right)+\nabla_{v}\cdot(mf)=0,\\
&\nabla_{v}\cdot\mathcal{B}(t,x,v)=-\partial_{x}f,\\
&\mathcal{B}(t,x,v):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[(\partial_{x}u)\delta(u(t,x,\xi)-v)],\\
&m=m(t,x,v):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\partial_{x}(\bm{\epsilon}\partial_{x}u)|u(t,x,\xi)=v\right].\end{aligned}\right.

Moreover, additional relations hold between
f
and
\mathcal{B}
. Writing
u=(u^{j})_{1}^{n}
,
v=(v^{j})_{1}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{B}=(B^{j})_{1}^{n}
, we define the marginal PDFs
f^{j}=f^{j}(t,x,v^{j})=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\delta(u^{j}-v^{j})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}f\prod_{i\neq j}dv^{i}
. Then by (
2.7
) we obtain
\partial_{x}f^{j}=-\partial_{v^{j}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\partial_{x}u^{j}\delta(u^{j}-v^{j})]=-\partial_{v^{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}B^{j}\prod_{i\neq j}dv^{i}
, which gives

(A.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\left(B^{j}+\int^{v_{j}}\partial_{x}f(\cdot,v^{1},\dots,\tilde{v}^{j},\dots,v^{n})d\tilde{v}^{j}\right)\prod_{i\neq j}dv^{i}=0

for each
j=1,\dots,n
. These relations indicate the challenges of extending statistical conservation laws to hyperbolic systems.
