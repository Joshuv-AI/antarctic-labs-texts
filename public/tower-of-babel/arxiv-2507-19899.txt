A Gold Standard Dataset and Evaluation Framework for Depression Detection and Explanation in Social Media using LLMs
Prajval Bolegave, Pushpak Bhattacharya

Abstract
Early detection of depression from online social media posts holds promise for providing timely mental health interventions. In this work, we present a high-quality, expert-annotated dataset of 1,017 social media posts labeled with depressive spans and mapped to 12 depression symptom categories. Unlike prior datasets that primarily offer coarse post-level labels Cohan et al. 2018 , our dataset enables fine-grained evaluation of both model predictions and generated explanations. We develop an evaluation framework that leverages this clinically grounded dataset to assess the faithfulness and quality of natural language explanations generated by large language models (LLMs). Through carefully designed prompting strategies, including zero-shot and few-shot approaches with domain-adapted examples, we evaluate state-of-the-art proprietary LLMs including GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Our comprehensive empirical analysis reveals significant differences in how these models perform on clinical explanation tasks, with zero-shot and few-shot prompting. Our findings underscore the value of human expertise in guiding LLM behavior and offer a step toward safer, more transparent AI systems for psychological well-being.

Introduction

Mental health disorders, particularly depression, have become increasingly prevalent and are often expressed through social media platforms
Cohan et al. 2018
;
Guntuku et al. 2017
. The ubiquity of user-generated content has prompted researchers to explore language-based signals for early identification of psychological distress
Calvo et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2023
. However, most existing machine learning models for depression detection operate on weakly-labeled data or binary classification tasks, limiting their clinical utility and interpretability
Chancellor and De Choudhury 2020
.

Further, the explainability of these models remains a critical bottleneck, especially when applied to sensitive domains like mental health. While large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 and Claude offer impressive fluency, their generated explanations often lack alignment with clinically grounded reasoning
Yang et al. 2023
;
Rajani et al. 2019
. This gap between linguistic coherence and domain-specific faithfulness raises important concerns for deploying AI in healthcare contexts
Doshi-Velez and Kim 2017
.

To address these challenges, we introduce a method for constructing a high-quality, expert-annotated dataset for depression detection and explanation. Each post is manually labeled by licensed mental health professionals with two layers of annotation: (i) spans of text indicative of depressive symptoms, and (ii) corresponding mappings to one or more of twelve standard symptoms derived from the DSM-5 criteria (e.g., anhedonia, hopelessness, suicidal ideation)
Association 2013
. This dual-layer annotation enables more granular supervision for both training and evaluation.

2
Related Work

2.1
Mental Health Datasets and Annotation Practices

Annotated datasets have played a critical role in advancing NLP for mental health analysis. The SMHD corpus provided large-scale Reddit data labeled for multiple mental health conditions, enabling early detection through linguistic signals
Cohan et al. 2018
. EmoMent focused on emotion-annotated posts from South Asian populations, highlighting cultural nuances in mental health expression
Atapattu et al. 2022
. More recently, the IMHI dataset introduced by the MentaLLaMA framework offered expert-written explanations for multiple mental health tasks, supporting interpretable model development
Yang et al. 2023
. However, these resources predominantly feature document-level annotations and lack token-level span highlights or symptom-specific mappings grounded in clinical criteria. In contrast, our dataset provides expert annotations at both the span and symptom level based on DSM-5 categories, enabling more granular and clinically faithful evaluation.

2.2
Faithfulness and Explainability in LLMs

Explainability has become a key concern in applying LLMs to sensitive domains such as mental health. The Mental-LLM benchmark evaluated models like GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on diagnostic tasks and found limitations in zero-shot and few-shot settings, particularly in generating faithful rationales
Xu et al. 2023
. Similarly, MentalGLM improved explanation generation through supervised fine-tuning on mental health-specific data
Zhai et al. 2024
. Yet, few studies have proposed structured frameworks to quantify the alignment between model explanations and clinical reasoning. Our work addresses this gap through a span- and symptom-level evaluation framework that enables systematic measurement of explanation
faithfulness
and diagnostic relevance.

2.3
Prompting Strategies for Mental Health Tasks

Prompt-based learning, especially in the form of in-context learning (ICL), has proven effective for adapting LLMs to specialized domains. Previous work has shown the utility of prompting for classification and emotion detection in mental health contexts
Shi et al. 2024
. Inspired by these findings, we design zero-shot and few-shot prompts tailored to symptom identification and rationale generation, leveraging state-of-the-art proprietary LLMs such as GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Unlike prior works that use weak supervision or synthetic data, our prompting approach is grounded in a gold-standard dataset annotated by clinical experts.

2.4
Comparative Evaluation of LLMs

Comparative analyses have shed light on the trade-offs between proprietary and open-source LLMs in mental health applications. Studies like Mental-LLM highlight how instruction tuning can boost model alignment with mental health tasks
Xu et al. 2023
, while other efforts have explored cost-efficient adaptation strategies such as LoRA
Shi et al. 2024
. In our work, we benchmark leading proprietary models using consistent prompting strategies on our expert-annotated dataset. This allows us to evaluate differences in explanation quality, faithfulness, and symptom coverage, offering insights into how well current LLMs align with clinical reasoning under realistic zero and few-shot conditions.

3
Methodology

Our methodology is designed to evaluate the capability of large language models (LLMs) to generate faithful, clinically meaningful natural language explanations (NLEs) for depressive content in social media posts. We leverage our expert-annotated dataset to probe models under prompting-based setups and assess their outputs using a structured evaluation framework.

3.1
Model Setup and Prompting Strategies

We experiment with state-of-the-art proprietary LLMs, including GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet, accessed via APIs. To generate explanations, we employ two prompting strategies:

Zero-Shot Prompting.

In this setup, models are given only a task description and the input post. The prompt instructs the model to identify depressive cues and explain its reasoning without any examples. (Appendix
A.1.1
)

Few-Shot Prompting.

Here, we provide models with a small set of in-context examples curated from our expert-annotated dataset. Each example includes a post and a corresponding expert-written explanation. These demonstrations guide the model to better align with the desired explanation format and content. (Appendix
A.1.2
)

Prompts are manually crafted and iteratively refined to ensure consistency and domain alignment across models. For each input post, we collect model-generated explanations under both prompting setups.

3.2
Evaluation Framework

To assess explanation quality, we propose a multi-faceted evaluation framework grounded in clinical reasoning and expert feedback. Our evaluation comprises three components:

1. Span-Level Faithfulness Evaluation.

We measure how well the model’s explanation identifies the same text spans as the expert annotators. This is done via span-level Recall checking if the expert annotated depressive spans are part of explanation or not. We prioritize recall to assess whether models capture the complete range of clinically significant indicators identified by experts. This evaluates the faithfulness of the explanation to the evidence in the input post.

2. Symptom-Level Alignment Evaluation.

We extract symptom mentions from the explanation and compare them with the annotated symptom labels using F1-score. This quantifies whether the explanation aligns with clinically grounded categories, reflecting its diagnostic utility and balancing both precision and recall in symptom identification.

3. LLM-Based Coherence Scoring.

We use an LLM to rate each explanation based on clarity, logical structure, and emotional interpretation. The prompt is as follows:

Given the explanation below for a social media post related to depression, rate it on a scale of 1 to 5 based on the following:

•

Does it accurately interpret the emotional content of the post?

•

Is the explanation logically coherent and well-structured?

•

Does it clearly and correctly tie phrases in the post to depressive symptoms?

•

Is the explanation concise, avoiding unnecessary repetition or irrelevant elaboration?

Return a single rating from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent).

Post: "..."

Explanation: "..."

We normalize the score to a 0–1 scale for aggregation.

Combined Quality Metric.

To enable holistic comparison of explanation quality across models, we define a unified
Explanation Quality Score
as a weighted sum of three evaluation dimensions:

\begin{split}\mathbf{Quality}&=\mathbf{w_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{\text{Span-Recall}}+\mathbf{w_{2}}\cdot\mathbf{\text{Symptom-F1}}\\
&+\mathbf{w_{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{\text{Coherence Score}}{5}\right)\end{split}

(1)

Here,
w_{1}
,
w_{2}
, and
w_{3}
are weights summing to 1, with values set to 0.3, 0.4, and 0.3, respectively. These values were determined in consultation with clinical experts, who prioritized accurate symptom attribution (captured by
Symptom-F1
) as the most clinically actionable metric. Span-level fidelity and overall coherence were considered equally important but slightly less critical.

This weighting scheme reflects a 70% emphasis on clinical alignment (span recall and symptom attribution) and 30% on the structural and communicative clarity of the explanation. All three component metrics are reported independently to ensure interpretability and enable task-specific analysis.

3.3
Implementation Details

All model outputs and evaluations are conducted on a per-post basis. For span extraction, we implement a text-matching procedure that checks whether expert-annotated depressive spans from the original post are explicitly referenced in the model’s explanation. This is done using exact and fuzzy matching to account for minor paraphrasing while maintaining semantic alignment.

Symptom identification is performed using keyword matching approach, which maps the explanation text to our predefined set of 12 DSM-5 depression symptoms. This ensures robust detection even when the model uses varied clinical language or synonyms.

To ensure consistency in qualitative judgments, the LLM judge (GPT-4.1) is prompted with a fixed, calibrated instruction across all evaluations, reducing prompt-induced variance.

4.3
Annotation Guidelines

To ensure reliable and clinically valid annotations, we developed a comprehensive set of guidelines:

•

Span Selection Criteria
: Annotators were instructed to highlight text spans that directly express or strongly imply a DSM-5 depression symptom. Spans should be:

–

Self-contained enough to convey the depressive indicator.

–

Inclusive of contextual markers necessary for interpretation.

–

Minimal in length while capturing the complete depressive expression.

•

Symptom Classification Guide
: For each symptom category, annotators received detailed descriptions with example phrases and contextual indicators. For instance:

–

For "Depressed Mood," annotators looked for expressions of persistent sadness, emptiness, or emotional pain.

–

For "Anhedonia," they identified statements about diminished interest or pleasure in activities once enjoyed

–

For "Suicidal Thoughts or Self-Harm," they noted both explicit mentions and indirect references to death wishes, self-harm, or suicide.

4.5
Applications and Impact

This dataset can have various applications such as:

•

Fine-tuning open-source LLMs
for symptom-aware depression detection and explanation.

•

Faithfulness evaluation
of LLM-generated explanations by comparing generated spans against expert highlights.

•

Multi-level interpretability research
, enabling both token-level and symptom-level assessments.

•

Clinical validation
of AI-based mental health screening tools.

•

Developing responsible disclosure protocols
for depression detection systems.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dataset to combine span-level depressive indicators with DSM-5-aligned symptom labels. While the dataset comprises 1,017 posts, its strength lies in the depth and precision of annotation rather than sheer volume. Each post is annotated by licensed mental health professionals a process that is inherently time-consuming, cognitively demanding, and requires clinical expertise. This level of expert involvement makes large-scale annotation costly and less feasible, but ensures high-quality, clinically meaningful data. Consequently, even with a modest size, the dataset offers rich supervision and is well-suited for evaluating model faithfulness and interpretability in sensitive applications like mental health detection.

Experiments

We design our experiments to evaluate the ability of large language models (LLMs) to generate faithful, clinically relevant explanations for depressive social media posts. All experiments are conducted using prompting-only setups across multiple proprietary LLMs, leveraging the expert-annotated dataset introduced in Section

.

5.1
Models Evaluated

We assess the performance of the following state-of-the-art proprietary LLMs via API:

•

GPT-4.1 (OpenAI)

•

•

Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google DeepMind)

Each model is evaluated under two prompting conditions:
zero-shot

5.2
Prompting Strategies

To identify the most clinically faithful prompting approaches, we experimented with four variations in the
Zero-Shot
setting and three in the
Few-Shot
setting. These prompts varied in terms of clarity, specificity, and the degree to which they emphasized alignment with standard clinical symptoms of depression.

Each prompt’s outputs were reviewed in collaboration with clinical experts to assess their relevance, coherence, and alignment with expert reasoning. Based on this qualitative feedback, the most effective prompt from each category was selected for final evaluation.

For the few-shot setting, we used a fixed set of synthetically constructed examples, each corresponding to one of the 12 standard depressive symptoms (Section
4
). These examples were designed to ensure broad and balanced symptom coverage without overlapping with the evaluation set. Detailed prompts and examples are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

5.3
Dataset Usage

All 1,017 expert-annotated posts are used for evaluation. Importantly, the few-shot prompting examples are not drawn from this dataset; instead, they are created independently to avoid overlap and data leakage. This setup ensures a clear separation between example inputs and evaluation instances.

5.4
Implementation Details

All model outputs are generated via the OpenRouter API. To promote faithfulness and reduce variance, inference is conducted with a temperature setting of 0.7. Output truncation is applied as needed to fit within each model’s context window (e.g., 1K tokens for GPT-4.1, 1.5K tokens for Gemni 2.5 pro, 1K for Claude 3.7 Sonnet).

6.1.1
Zero-Shot Performance

In zero-shot settings, GPT-4.1 demonstrated superior overall performance (combined rating: 0.6997), primarily due to its exceptional span evaluation score (0.618). Claude 3.7 achieved a lower span score (0.464) but outperformed in symptom identification (0.546 vs. 0.588). Gemini 2.5 Pro was excluded from zero-shot analysis as its outputs consistently failed to conform to our expected format despite multiple prompt variations.

6.1.2
Few-Shot vs. Zero-Shot Comparison

Contrary to expectations, few-shot prompting did not lead to consistent performance improvements across models. While GPT-4.1 exhibited a modest increase in overall combined rating—from 0.6997 to 0.7074 Claude 3.7 saw a slight decrease, dropping from 0.6705 to 0.6701. The span detection metrics were particularly revealing: GPT-4.1’s span score declined notably from 0.618 to 0.578, and Claude’s span performance remained relatively low across both settings.

These results suggest that while few-shot prompting enhances coherence and symptom-level identification, it may inadvertently hinder span detection. One plausible explanation is that the in-context examples anchor the model’s attention to stylistic patterns or conceptual structures, rather than encouraging comprehensive span extraction. Notably, the explanations generated under zero-shot settings were often longer and more exploratory in nature, potentially enabling broader coverage of depressive expressions. This hypothesis is supported by the qualitative differences observed in (Appendix
B
), where zero-shot responses tend to include a wider array of symptom-related phrases.

6.1.3
Symptom Identification and Coherence

Symptom identification remained relatively stable across prompting settings. Claude 3.7 achieved the highest symptom evaluation in the zero-shot setting (0.588), while Gemini 2.5 Pro outperformed others in the few-shot setting (0.626). Coherence scores were consistently high across all models, ranging from 4.932 to 4.987 on a 5-point scale, suggesting that the generated explanations were generally fluent and well-structured regardless of prompting strategy.

6.2
Model-Specific Analysis

6.2.1
GPT-4.1

GPT-4.1 demonstrated the highest overall performance in the zero-shot setting, achieving the top span evaluation score (0.618) and the highest combined rating (0.6997). This indicates a strong inherent ability to detect depressive cues without the need for in-context examples. Interestingly, its performance improved slightly in the few-shot setting on coherence and symptom dimensions but saw a drop in span alignment, suggesting that exemplars may have inadvertently narrowed its focus. This pattern reflects GPT-4.1’s sensitivity to prompt structure and highlights the trade-off between guided and unguided generation in clinical explanation tasks.

6.2.2
Claude 3.7

Claude 3.7 demonstrated the highest zero-shot symptom identification score (0.588) but consistently underperformed in span evaluation across both settings. This indicates that while the model is adept at capturing the overall presence of depressive symptoms, it struggles to accurately pinpoint specific span-level indicators within the text. The drop in performance with few-shot prompting suggests that structured examples may have further limited its flexibility in identifying diverse depressive expressions.

6.2.3
Gemini 2.5 Pro

Gemini 2.5 Pro was evaluated exclusively in the few-shot setting, as its zero-shot outputs exhibited significant formatting inconsistencies and deviated from the expected structure required for reliable annotation alignment. Specifically, the model often failed to produce clearly delineated symptom explanations or misinterpreted the prompt intent entirely, making span and symptom extraction unreliable in the zero-shot case.

In the few-shot setting, however, Gemini achieved the highest combined rating (0.7171) across all evaluated models, indicating strong overall alignment with expert annotations. It showed balanced performance in both span detection (0.569) and symptom identification (0.626). These results suggest that Gemini benefits substantially from structured prompting with domain-adapted examples, which likely help it organize its outputs more coherently and attend to clinically meaningful cues. When provided with clear guidance, the model is capable of producing detailed, contextually appropriate explanations that support both reasoning transparency and annotation fidelity.

6.3
Implications for Clinical Applications

The observed variation in model performance across evaluation dimensions underscores the complexity of automating mental health assessment. Although all models produced generally coherent and readable explanations, their alignment with expert annotations particularly in span detection and symptom classification varied significantly.

GPT-4.1’s relatively strong zero-shot performance highlights its potential utility in real-time or low-resource clinical settings, where prompt engineering and extensive tuning may not be feasible. However, the inconsistent gains from few-shot prompting suggest that curated examples do not uniformly enhance clinical fidelity and must be rigorously validated through collaboration with domain experts.

The trade-off between span-level precision and accurate symptom attribution indicates that no single model currently offers comprehensive reliability across tasks. A hybrid system—using GPT-4.1 for identifying depressive spans and Claude 3.7 or Gemini 2.5 Pro for symptom interpretation may provide a more balanced and clinically useful solution. This approach parallels how clinicians synthesize surface-level cues with deeper diagnostic reasoning. For a qualitative comparison of model explanations, see (Appendix
B.1.3
).

7
Conclusion & Future Work

In this work, we introduce a clinically grounded, expert-annotated dataset for explainable depression symptom detection from social media posts. Each post is labeled with fine-grained depressive spans and corresponding symptom categories based on DSM-5 criteria, establishing a gold-standard resource for evaluating model interpretability and clinical relevance.

To complement the dataset, we propose a multi-dimensional evaluation framework that scores explanations across three axes: fidelity to annotated spans, correctness of symptom attribution, and structural coherence, the latter assessed by a consistent LLM-based rubric. This enables a nuanced understanding of how well generative models align with clinical reasoning, beyond simple classification accuracy.

Through comparative experiments using zero-shot and few-shot prompting, we analyze the behavior of state-of-the-art language models under different instruction regimes. Our findings underscore both the promise and limitations of LLMs in generating structured, symptom-aware explanations, with implications for their use in low-resource or high-stakes clinical contexts.

Looking forward, we also aim to refine prompting techniques to encourage conciseness and reduce hallucinations, as well as explore hybrid prompting–fine-tuning pipelines. Ultimately, we hope this resource will serve as a foundation for training and evaluating more interpretable, clinically aligned, and deployable mental health AI systems.

Limitations

While our work introduces a high-quality, expert-annotated dataset and a comprehensive evaluation framework, several limitations remain:

•

Dataset size:
Our dataset consists of 1,017 annotated posts, which, although curated carefully, may not fully capture the linguistic and cultural diversity present in real-world mental health discourse. This may limit the generalizability of model evaluations.

•

Subjectivity in annotation:
Despite relying on clinical experts, the span-level and symptom annotations are inherently subjective. Language expressing psychological distress is often ambiguous and open to interpretation, which can lead to variability.

•

LLM-based evaluation bias:
We use large language models to assess the structural coherence and faithfulness of explanations. While scalable, these models may introduce biases or fail to capture clinically subtle or incorrect reasoning that human raters might detect.

•

Limited scope of model evaluation:
Our experiments are restricted to zero-shot and few-shot prompting with proprietary LLMs. We do not evaluate fine-tuned open-source models, which may perform differently and offer stronger clinical alignment in future studies.

Ethical Considerations

Given the sensitive nature of mental health data and the implications of automated analysis, we took several measures to ensure ethical integrity throughout this work.

•

Data anonymization and privacy:
All social media posts used in this study were publicly available with any personally identifiable information (PII) removed prior to annotation. We ensured compliance with platform terms of service and respected user privacy.

•

Expert annotation and clinical caution:
Annotations were conducted by mental health professionals to promote clinical reliability. However, our dataset is intended strictly for research purposes and should not be used for clinical diagnosis or intervention without human oversight.

•

Model misuse and risk:
Large language models trained or evaluated on this dataset could be misused to make automated decisions about individuals’ mental health. We emphasize that such models should not be deployed in real-world settings without rigorous validation, ethical review, and expert involvement.

•

Bias and representation:
Depression manifests differently across cultures, languages, and demographic groups. Our dataset, though carefully curated, may not reflect the full range of experiences, and downstream models may inherit these biases if not used critically.

Comparative Remarks

Across the different prompting strategies and models, we observe both commonalities and key differences in the structure, depth, and clinical alignment of generated explanations.

1. Symptom Coverage and Specificity:

All models identified core depressive symptoms such as appetite changes, fatigue, hopelessness, and suicidal ideation. However, few-shot prompting generally led to more comprehensive and symptomatically accurate explanations. Models under few-shot settings, particularly GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, often surfaced more nuanced symptoms like difficulty concentrating and psychomotor retardation, which were sometimes missed in zero-shot outputs.

Claude 3.7 Sonnet produced notably structured and readable explanations even under zero-shot prompting, organizing symptoms into clearly labeled sections. GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.5 Pro, under few-shot prompting, provided similarly well-organized outputs but with deeper clinical reasoning and contextual interpretation.

Few-shot GPT-4.1 and Gemini 2.5 Pro exhibited the highest alignment with expert-annotated spans and symptom categories. They not only matched key phrases but also interpreted their clinical implications with high fidelity. In contrast, zero-shot models sometimes generalized or omitted less overt symptoms.

4. Interpretability and Justification Style:

Few-shot prompted explanations were more reflective and interpretive, often providing psychological context (e.g., linking functional decline to anhedonia or existential despair to passive suicidal ideation). Few-shot prompting significantly enhances the quality, depth, and clinical validity of model explanations.
