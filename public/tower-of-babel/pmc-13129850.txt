Sustainable Valorization of Banana Peel Agro-Waste in the Form of Citric Acid by Aspergillus niger ATCC 10535 Using Response Surface Methodology
Authors: Solomon Kahsay Abraha, Asmelash Gebrekidan Mekonen, Gebresilasie Gebremedhin Gebresilasie, Kiros Fiseha Kahsay, Tinsu Gebreyohans Desta, Tesfay Negassi Gebresilasie, Gidet Godif Meressa, Kedir Yesuf Abdu, Solomon Getachew Abate, Yassin Adem Endris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Banana peel Agro-waste is abundantly generated in Ethiopia but is commonly discarded without adequate disposal, causing notable environmental and aesthetic challenges. As part of a sustainable valorization strategy, this study employed banana peels as a low-cost, renewable substrate for citric acid production. The work focused on harnessing Aspergillus niger ATCC 10535 in solid-state fermentation, with process conditions optimized through Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to maximize yield and process efficiency. Citric acid production trials were conducted in a small-scale solid-state fermenter using banana peel agro-waste as the primary substrate. Process parameters were systematically varied, including substrate loading (18, 36, and 54 g), incubation time (3, 6, and 9 days), and fermentation temperature (25 °C, 30 °C, and 35 °C), to evaluate their individual and interactive effects on citric acid yield. Experimental design and statistical optimization were carried out using RSM, applying a Box–Behnken design to model the relationships between process variables and product yield and to determine the optimal operating conditions for maximum citric acid production. The minimum citric acid yield (28.39 g/L) was obtained at 36 g substrate loading, 3 days of incubation, and 25 °C, while the maximum yield (45.55 g/L) was achieved at 36 g, 6 days, and 30 °C. Statistical analysis confirmed the model’s significance (p < 0.01). Temperature and incubation time exhibited positive linear effects on yield, whereas increased substrate loading beyond the optimal range resulted in decreased production. The optimized process parameters determined by the Box–Behnken model were 35.014 g substrate loading, 5.811 days of incubation, and 31.054 °C fermentation temperature, yielding 45.563 g/L citric acid. The findings are demonstrated that banana peel agro-waste can be sustainably valorized into citric acid through RSM-optimized, low-level sucrose-supplemented solid-state fermentation by A. niger ATCC 10535, providing a cost-effective and environmentally friendly approach for waste management and value-added product generation.

Introduction

Citric acid (CA), with molecular weights of 192 g/mol (anhydrous) and 210 g/mol (monohydrate), occurs naturally in various fruits such as citron, lime, pineapple, pear, and peach. , It is the most widely produced organic acid via fermentation today. The history of CA dates back to 1784, when W. Scheele first isolated it from lemon juice as calcium citrate and subsequently converted it into liquid CA using sulfuric acid. , Commercial production began in England around 1826, using imported Italian lemons containing 7–9% CA. Lemon juice remained the main industrial source until 1919, when pioneering industrial-scale fermentation using Aspergillus niger (A. niger) was established in Belgium. Today, extraction from citrus juice is limited to small-scale operations in Mexico and parts of Africa. ,,,

Early CA fermentations were conducted as surface cultures. The transition to submerged fermentation (SF) marked a major advancement. One of the first precise commercial SF processes was described by Perquin (1938), comparable in quality to Currie’s surface fermentation work. In Japan during the 1960s, a new process emerged using n-alkanes as carbon sources, with yeast from the genus Candida producing both citric and iso-citric acids. ,,, Although some developing countries still extract CA from lemon, lime, and pineapple waste, this accounts for only a small share of global production. Several chemical synthesis routes have been proposed such as the reaction of glycerol-derived 1,3-dichloroacetone with cyanide but none have achieved cost competitiveness with fermentation. , By 2007, global CA production surpassed 1.6 million tons, reflecting its diverse industrial applications. The food and beverage industry consumes about 70% of CA as a pH adjuster, flavor enhancer, and preservative. ,,

Bananas (Musa spp.) are among the most important crops worldwide in terms of both production and economic value, with global production expected to reach 48.9 million tons. ,− CA is a natural plant metabolite, water-soluble, and responsible for the tart flavor of many fruits including lemons, oranges, pineapples, and gooseberries. ,, It is a solid at room temperature, melts at 153 °C, and decomposes upon further heating. While bacteria can produce CA, yields are generally low because CA is tightly linked to energy metabolism, becoming abundant only under specific metabolic imbalances. In yeast, production efficiency is reduced due to concurrent iso-citric acid formation. ,−

A. niger remains the preferred industrial microorganism for CA synthesis due to its ease of cultivation, ability to ferment a wide range of inexpensive raw materials, and consistently high yields. , Using agroindustrial wastes as carbon sources not only reduces production costs but also addresses waste management challenges. Studies have investigated grape pomace, apple pomace, banana peels, sugar beet pulp, okara, jackfruit carpels, and kiwi fruit peels as potential substrates. , Banana peels, in particular, are abundant in Ethiopia due to extensive banana cultivation. However, they are often discarded without proper disposal, contributing to environmental issues such as unpleasant odor, pest attraction, and pollution. Rich in carbohydrates and minerals, banana peels present a low-cost, nutrient-rich substrate that is suitable for microbial fermentation.

Solid-state fermentation (SSF) offers a promising approach for converting banana peels into value-added products, including CA. SSF enables A. niger to efficiently degrade complex biomass under low-moisture conditions, offering higher product concentrations, lower energy demands, and simpler downstream processing compared to submerged systems. Additionally, this approach aligns with circular bioeconomy principles, transforming agricultural waste into valuable biochemicals, while mitigating environmental harm. For this laboratory-scale, waste-valorization study, we prioritized SSF because it minimizes water and energy inputs and allows direct use of the solid peel as both substrate and support.

While CA production from banana peels has been explored in other contexts, limited research exists on their use in Ethiopiaparticularly with systematic process optimization for maximum yield. The current study bridges this gap by employing A. niger ATCC 10535 for CA production via SSF, with process conditions optimized through Response Surface Methodology (RSM). RSM is a powerful statistical tool for modeling and optimizing multivariable processes, enabling the evaluation of both individual and interactive effects of key parameters.

This research not only investigates the influence of substrate loading, incubation time, and fermentation temperature on CA yield but also models the process for maximum efficiency by using a Box–Behnken design. Materials and Methods

Substrate Collection and Preparation

Fresh banana peels were collected from Addis Ababa, Ethiopia fruit vendors, totaling 100 g. They were air-dried for 72 h and then oven-dried at 60 °C to a constant weight. The dried samples were ground and stored in a polythene container for analysis of their nutritional, mineral, and antinutritional content.

Determination of Nutritional Content

The sample was analyzed for proximate composition, including moisture, ash, volatile matter, organic matter, crude protein, lipids, carbohydrates, and crude fiber. Moisture content was determined by oven drying at 105 °C until a stable weight. Lipids were extracted in a Soxhlet apparatus for 6 h. Protein content was evaluated using the Micro Kjeldahl method, and Carbohydrates were calculated by the difference.

Determination of Moisture Content

Moisture levels of over 8% favor the presence of insects, and over 14% there is the risk of contamination by fungi and bacteria. The method is based on drying a sample in an oven and determining moisture content by the weight difference between dry and wet material. 10 g of air-dried (for 72 h) and cleaned fresh banana peel was taken in a crucible and dried in an oven at a temperature of 105 °C for 24 h, and the weight was taken after every 12 h. The procedure was repeated until a constant weight was obtained. After every 12 h, the sample was removed from the oven and cooled in a desiccator for 30 min. It was then removed and reweighed. The percentage loss of weight gave the percentage of moisture in the sample. Ash Content

A dry, porcelain dish containing 10 g of a dry sample (free of moisture) of banana peel was placed in a muffle furnace set at 550 °C for 2 h, and the sample was cooked in a desiccator and weighed. Crude Fiber Determination

Three g of the sample was weighed into a dry 100 mL conical flask. 200 mL of 0.25 N H2SO4 acid was added to the sample and then brought to boiling point. It was boiled gently for 30 min. The flask was rotated every few minutes to mix the contents and remove particles from the sides. A funnel with filter paper was prepared. At the end of the 30 min boiling period, the acid mixture was allowed to stand for a minute and poured immediately. Determination of Crude Lipid Contents

A clean, dried 500 cm3 round-bottom flask containing a few antibumping granules was weighed (W1) with 300 cm3 petroleum ether (40–60 °C) for extraction and then poured into the flask fitted with a Soxhlet extraction unit. The extractor thimble weighing 20 g was fixed into the Soxhlet unit. The round-bottom flask and a condenser were connected to the Soxhlet extractor, and cold-water circulation was connected. The heating mantle was switched on, and the heating rate was adjusted until the solvent was refluxing at a steady rate. Extraction was carried out for 6 h. The solvent was recovered, and the oil was dried in an oven set at 70 °C for 1 h. The round-bottom flask and oil were then weighed (W 2). Protein Content Determination

Digestion

Because of its cost, this is the most important dietary nutrient in a commercial operation; proper evaluation means that the quality of protein intake of the feed provided can be controlled. The analysis is by Kjeldahl’s method, which evaluates the total nitrogen content of the sample after it has been digested in H2SO4. In this study, to milligram precision, 1 g of dry sample was weighed out into a 300 cm3 flask; then 10 g of K2SO4, 0.7 g of HgO, and 20 mL of concentrated H2SO4 were added. The sample was digested until a clear green color was obtained. The digest was cooled and diluted with 100 cm3 distilled water.

Distillation

Determination of Carbohydrate

The total carbohydrate was determined by the difference. The sum of the percentage moisture, ash, crude lipid, crude protein, and crude fiber was subtracted from 100 via eq . 7 %totalcarbohydrate=100−%moisture+%ash+%lipid+%protein+%fiber

Determination of Volatile Matter

The dried sample after moisture removal was taken in a crucible and placed in an electrically heated oven at a temperature of 920 °C for 10 min, and then it was cooled in a desiccator for 30 min. It was then removed and reweighed. The percentage of weight loss gave the volatile matter content. The percentage of volatile matter is computed using eq . 8 volatilesolids(%)=W1−W2W1×100 Where: W 1 is the initial mass (dried) of the sample as delivered, and W 2 is the mass of the sample after drying.

Determinations of fixed and volatile solids do not distinguish precisely between inorganic and organic matter because the loss on ignition is not confined to organic matter. It includes losses due to decomposition or volatilization of some mineral salts.

Determination of Mineral Content

Banana peel samples (2 g) were dried at 105 °C for 3 h and then charred until smoking ceased and ashed at 550 °C to obtain a whitish residue. This ash was treated with concentrated HCl, transferred to a volumetric flask, and diluted to 100 mL for atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS) analysis. A SHIMADZU model AA-6300 spectrophotometer was used with wavelengths set at 422.7 nm for Ca, 285.213 nm for Mg, 248.3 nm for Fe, and 589.0 nm for Na. Stock solutions (1000 ppm) were prepared, and at least four working standards were generated for calibration. To minimize phosphorus interference, LaCl3 was added to achieve a final concentration of 1% La. For HCN determination, 10 g of the sample was soaked in 200 cm3 water and 10 cm3 of H3PO4 acid for 12 h. After distillation to obtain 150 cm3 of distillate, 20 cm3 was diluted with 40 cm3 water, then treated with 8 cm3 of NH4OH and 0.002 L of 5% KI. Titration with 0.02 mol dm–3 AgNO3 revealed permanent turbidity, where 1 cm3 of AgNO3 equated to 1.08 mg of HCN.

Chemicals and Analytical Reagents

The chemicals and analytic reagents’ grades used were 0.1 M NaOH, 0.1 N H2SO4, phenolphthalein as indicator, distilled water, ethanol absolute, activated carbon, deionized water, potato dextrose agar (OXOID, England), 1 M HCl, sucrose­(D­(+)–saccharide sugar), 0.1 N HCl, 4% Na2SO4, 40% NaOH, petroleum ether (boiling point 40–60 °C), HgO, K2SO4, B­(OH)3 indicator solution, 0.255N H2SO4, 0.313N NaOH, Na2CO3, antifoam (octyl alcohol/silicone), ethyl alcohol at 95%(v/v), petroleum ether, 1%(v/v) HCl, tween 80 solutions, MgSO4, CaCl2, KH2PO4, and (NH4)2SO4.

Microorganism and Inoculum Preparation

A. niger ATCC 10535 (American Type Culture Collection), obtained from the Microbiology (Traditional and Modern Medicine) division of the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHL), Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, was used throughout the study as the fermenting organism. It was kept on potato dextrose agar (pH 5.6) slants. The potato dextrose agar medium was prepared by dissolving 4 g of PDA in approximately 80 mL of distilled water bringing it up to 100 mL, and then cooking for 15 min while constantly stirring until a clear solution formed. The pH of the medium was maintained at 5.6 by 0.1 N of HCl and NaOH. 5.0 mL of this medium was decanted into individual test tubes. The test tubes were cotton-plugged and sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C for 15 min. After sterilization, the test tubes were maintained in a slanting position at about 30° to increase the surface area. The potato dextrose agar slants were then autoclaved by transferring a small amount of A. niger conidia from the culture provided and incubated at 28 °C for 5 days for maximum sporulation. After 5 days, the cultures were stored at 4 °C in a lab cool place.

Determination of Fermentation Factors

Design Expert was used to optimize the factors influencing CA production. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the results.

Preliminary Range-Finding (Single-Factor) Experiments: Rationale and Summary

Prior to the Box–Behnken design, single-factor range-finding experiments were performed to identify practical factor bounds and the variables exerting the greatest influence on CA yield under our SSF flask conditions. The factors and ranges tested were as follows: pH (2.5–5.5), initial substrate moisture (50–70% w/w), inoculum loading (0.5–2.0 mL of 1 × 107 spores/mL), substrate loading (18–54 g), incubation time (3–09 days), and temperature (25–35 °C). Substrate loading, incubation time, and temperature produced the largest and most reproducible effects on CA yield; pH, moisture, and inoculum produced smaller effects within the tested ranges and were therefore held constant during the RSM stage.

These single-factor runs were range-finding only, which used to set low/center/high levels for the Box-Behnken design and were not intended as a comprehensive factorial screening. Observations indicated that CA yield was maximized and relatively stable near pH 3.0 to 3.5; variation within this band had less influence than the three selected factors. Accordingly, pH was fixed at 3.5 to concentrate the RSM on substrate loading, incubation time, and temperature, a defensible approach commonly applied in citrate optimization to reduce design dimensionality while exploring the most influential variables.

Effect of Temperature

The impact of temperature on CA production was evaluated by conducting fermentation at various temperatures ranging from 25 to 35 °C, alongside variations in incubation times and substrate loading. These specific temperature ranges were selected because most filamentous fungi are mesophilic and thrive optimally within this interval ,

Effect of Time

Another factor examined was the incubation time across different temperatures and solid substrates. The effect of fermentation duration was analyzed throughout 3 to 9 days, with these timeframes chosen based on common fermentation processes that typically occur within this span. ,

Effect of Solid Substrate

CA accumulation is particularly influenced by the type of carbon source used. The presence of easily metabolizable carbohydrates is vital for optimal production. In this study, banana peels served as the carbon source. To assess the effect of substrate loading, various levels were tested, ranging from 18 to 54 g. The three substrate loading levels were selected, as effective carbon sources generally fall within the range of 50 to 240 g/L. It is important to note that experiments were initiated at a threshold of 50 g/L since CA biosynthesis does not occur below this concentration. ,

Fixed Parameters During RSM

The following parameters were held constant throughout the RSM experiments: initial pH = 3.5 (adjusted with HCl/NaOH), inoculum = 1 mL of a 1 × 107 spores m/L suspension, and initial substrate moisture 60% (w/w). These values were chosen based on preliminary screening experiments and common practice for citric acid production with A. niger in SSF. Holding these variables constant reduced the RSM dimensionality and focused the model on the three factors (substrate loading, time, and temperature) that exhibited the largest effect sizes in screening.

Preparation of Fermentation Media and SSF Setup

Dried banana peel (oven-dried to constant weight; measured moisture of the dried peel prior to experiments = 9.25%) was used as the solid substrate. The basal medium and sterile water were added to reach an initial substrate moisture of 60% (w/w) in the solid matrix, which was verified by oven drying representative wet substrate samples prior to inoculation. Sucrose was added at 10% (w/w) of substrate (i.e., 100 g per 1000 g substrate) to provide a small fraction of readily utilizable sugar for early fungal establishment; this corresponds to 1.8 g sucrose for 18 g substrate, 3.6 g for 36 g substrate, and 5.4 g for 54 g substrate. In low-moisture SSF, a small readily available sugar fraction can accelerate spore germination and early hyphal growth, improve colonization of the solid substrate and reduce lag time. , Sucrose is enzymatically hydrolyzed by A. niger (invertase) to glucose and fructose, which are rapidly taken up for initial biomass formation; however, the overall mass balance demonstrates that the peel supplies the majority of fermentable carbon and that sucrose serves only as a primer rather than the bulk substrate. By contrast, the dried banana peel proximate analysis indicates 41.52% carbohydrate (dry basis) (Table ), so the peel itself contributed the major portion of fermentable carbon in each treatment. After mixing, the substrate was dispensed into 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks (working volume 150 mL), autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min, cooled, and inoculated with 1.0 mL of spore suspension (1 × 107 spores m/L). Flasks were incubated under the conditions specified in each experimental run.

Product Recovery

Different mechanisms can be employed to separate CA from the fermented broth. In this study, a precipitation recovery method was used after 3, 6, and 9 days of incubation. The filtrate from the fermentation of banana peels with ethanol was treated with CaCl2 for recovery. NaOH was added beforehand to prevent the undesirable formation of HCl. Trisodium citrate formed when the pH reached between 8 and 9. The reaction is listed in eq . 9 2C6H5O7Na3·4H2O+3CaCl2→(C6H5O7)2Ca34H2O+6NaCl

For partial recovery of CA, CaCl2solution (28.5 g/70 mL) was gradually added to the agitated filtrate at 70 °C. When the pH reached 7.0, a white tricalcium precipitate formed. This precipitate was centrifuged at 3,000 revs/min for 10 min, filtered with Whatman No.1 paper, and washed with sterilized distilled water to convert the precipitate to soluble CA and insoluble CaSO4, 98% H2SO4 was added based on stoichiometry. The amount of H2SO4 used was less than the theoretical requirement to ensure a complete reaction, as excess acid is hard to separate from citric acid. After the addition of H2SO4, the mixture was stirred at 90 °C for 90 min to allow CaSO4 to precipitate as shown in eq . 10 (C6H5O7)2Ca34H2O+3H2SO4→2C6H8O7+3CaSO42H2O+2H2O

The suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rev/min for 10 min, and the CaSO4 was filtered using filter paper. Thereafter, the filtrate was treated with activated carbon, and under vacuum filtration to remove colorants. Finally, the supernatant, presumed to be CA, was filtered through micromembrane filter paper. Generally, the overall production steps are given in Figure .

Citric Acid Determination

The concentration of CA in the supernatant was estimated by titration in association with official analytical chemists using 0.1 M NaOH and phenolphthalein as indicators. Hence, a known volume of the analyte (presumed to be a citric acid solution) was placed in a titration flask, and the buret was filled with a standard solution (0.1 M NaOH) until it reached the zero-mark using a funnel. Before the titration was started, 3 drops of phenolphthalein were poured into the titration flask with citric acid. A 0.1 M NaOH (standard solution) was slowly added from the buret, drop by drop, with a continual magnetic stirrer to keep it thoroughly mixed. The titration continues to drop by drop until the phenolphthalein achieves the intermediate color (weak pink).

The titration ceases when the system is neither acidic nor basic, referred to as the endpoint. The endpoint corresponds to a perfect stoichiometric relationship between the acid and the base. Once the endpoint was reached, the volume of NaOH used­(titer) on the buret was read. The titration was completed, and the final calculation was done using CA, 1 mL 0.1 M NaOH is equivalent to 0.064 g CA. The amount of CA was expressed in terms of the g/L citric acid factor (0.064) formula. eq , is formulated to calculate the CA. 11 citricacid(g/L)=titre(volumeofNaOH)×0.064×100×10X(mlanalyte)×100ml

Experimental Setup

Fermentation Conditions Preparation and Settings

The experiment was conducted with a total of 17 experiments carried out based on the response surface methodology of a three-variable BBD.

Fermentation Temperature

Temperature plays a very important role in microbial growth and metabolism. The temperature effect on CA production was studied by carrying out the fermentation by varying the temperatures ranging from 25–35 °C for different incubation times and substrate loading. The three temperature levels were selected because most filamentous fungi are mesophilic and require growth optimal temperature between 25 and 35 °C.

Fermentation Time

The organism and fermentation conditions both affect the ideal incubation time for maximizing the CA synthesis. The incubation period at various temperatures and substrate loading were the other factors examined in this study’s analysis of CA synthesis. The incubation period was examined at various intervals between 3 and 9 days to observe the impact of fermentation. Because the fermentation process takes 3–9 days to complete, the three-time levels were chosen. ,,

Substrate Loading

In this study, banana peel was used as a carbon source. To observe the influence of the carbon source, substrate loading was carried out at different substrate levels ranging from 18–54 g. The three levels of substrate loading were selected because the carbon source should range between 50–240 g/L. Considering amounts below 50 g/L carbon source, CA biosynthesis is not realized, and therefore, experiments always started from the threshold of 50 g/L and upward. ,

Preparation of Fermentation Media

Solid-substrate fermentation was carried out to produce CA in this study. The basal medium was prepared by introducing banana peels at different levels in separate 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks with a working volume of 150 mL. The composition of fermentation media for CA production was 0.3 g NH4SO4, 0.23 g KH2PO4, 0.077 g MgSO4.7H2O, 3% (v/w) ethanol, and banana peel 18, 36, and 54 g (quantity of ingredients per 150 mL). Sucrose was added to the medium (100 g/1000 g) to boost the carbon substrate. The initial pH of the substrates was adjusted to about 3.5 with NaOH and HCl. The contents were thoroughly mixed, cotton-plugged, and autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min.

Spores’ suspension of A. niger ATCC 10535 was prepared by washing a 5-day-old culture potato dextrose agar slant with sterilized 0.8% Tween 80 solution by shaking vigorously for a minute. Spores were counted by a hemocytometer to adjust the count to 1 × 107 spores/ml. After cooling, the sterilized substrate including the media was inoculated with 1 mL (1 × 107 spores/mL) of inoculum and then incubated at varying conditions in a rotary shaking incubator, and the shaking speed was kept at 200 rpm. The solid substrate loading, time, and temperature were varied according to the experimental design. At the end of fermentation, the medium was diluted with sterilized distilled water (1:4 w/v) and shaken for 60 min at 121 rpm to mix all the contents. The content was then filtered through filter paper, and the filtrate was used for analysis. Similar techniques were used by. ,

Characterization of the Product

Assessment of Purity of the Product

A melting-point apparatus was used to assess the purity of the product obtained. The temperature at which the product crystals melted was compared to the melting temperature of pure, anhydrous CA crystals. In addition, CA was estimated gravimetrically following the recommended pyridine-acetic anhydride method by Merrier and Boulet. In brief, 4 g of the sample was accurately weighed and dissolved in 40 mL of distilled water, and then for each 1 mL of sample, 1.3 mL of pyridine was added and mixed well. The solution was added to 5.7 mL of acetic anhydride and incubated at 32 °C for 30 min in a water bath. The optical density values of the sample were recorded at 405 nm by using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. The CA yield of the sample was estimated from the reference run in parallel after the CA standard was prepared from citric acid monohydrate by dehydrating it at 90 °C for 72 h to a constant weight.

Determination of the Sulfated Ash

The Silica crucible was dried over a burner for 10 min and cooled in a desiccator containing silica to room temperature. Then, 5 g of the sample was weighed accurately in a tared crucible. It was ignited in a muffle furnace at 650 °C for 2 h, gently at first, until the material was thoroughly charred. The residue was cooled, moistened with 1 mL of H2SO4, and ignited until the carbon source was completely oxidized. The crucible was cooled in a desiccator and weighed. The sulfated ash in percent was determined by using eq . 12 sulfatedash,percentbymass=M1M2×100 Where: M 1 is the mass of the residue, in g; and M 2 is the mass of the material taken for the test, in g.

Preparation of Samples for Determination of Heavy Metals

Digestion of Samples

The sample was prepared for each metal according to a wet-ashing technique. 1 g of the sample was weighed out into an acid-washed glass round-bottom flask. Samples were digested by the addition of 20 mL of aquaria (a mixture of HCl and HNO3, ratio of 3:1). The condenser was fitted to the round-bottom flask that refluxed for 2 h at 90 °C. The round-bottom flask wall was washed with distilled water and the sample was filtered out through Whatman filter No. 1 to separate the insoluble solids from the supernatant liquid. The volume was adjusted to 100 mL with distilled water. All samples and blanks were stored in plastic containers. The standard solution of each sample, As, Pb, and Fe was prepared according to the Sc 2000 standard manufacturer for atomic absorption spectroscopy.

Determination of Metal Concentration of Each Sample

After digestion, the sample was connected to atomic absorption spectroscopy to read the concentration of each metal ion present in the sample. Then, As, Fe, and Pb were analyzed with atomic absorption spectroscopy (Buck Scientific model 210 VGP and ZEEnit 700, German) equipped with a deuterium background corrector and standard air-acetylene flame system using the external calibration curve after the parameters (burner and lamp alignment, silt width, and wavelength adjustment) were optimized for the minimum signal intensity of the instrument. Four replicate determinations were carried out on each sample. Hollow cathode lamps operated at the manufacturer-recommended conditions were used at their respective primary source line. The concentration of the metal ions present in the sample was determined by reading their absorbance using atomic absorption spectroscopy and comparing it to the respective standard curve as studied by.

Fourier Transformation Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to investigate the vibrational framework of the generated citric acid. The FTIR spectrum was analyzed to identify the functional groups, with a resolution of 4 cm–1 and a scanning range from 400 to 4000 cm–1, using the traditional KBr method. Effect of Solid Substrate on Citric Acid

The production of CA by A. niger is notably affected by the type and concentration of carbon sources. Figure a shows that as substrate loading increases from 18 to 36 g, CA production increases. However, yields decline when substrate loading exceeds 36 g or falls below this range. Impact of Temperature on the Yield of CA

This study found that the optimal temperature for the highest yield (45.5 g/L) is 30 °C, as shown in Figure b. Lower temperatures reduce enzyme activity, while higher temperatures lead to decreased CA biosynthesis due to enzyme denaturation, moisture loss, and growth cessation as reported in.

Effect of Incubation Time on Yield of Product

The production of CA from banana peels was studied over different incubation periods. CA production peaked at 6 days and then declined, with the highest concentration recorded at that time, as denoted in Figure c. Similar studies also found 6 days to be the optimal fermentation period. The decrease in CA after 6 days may be due to falling sugar levels, changes in fungal growth, reduced nitrogen availability, and inhibitors produced by the fungi.

Although the linear effect of the incubation time (B) was not statistically significant, the quadratic term (B 2) showed a highly significant contribution to the model. This indicates that the incubation time influences citric acid production in a nonlinear manner, suggesting the existence of an optimal fermentation duration rather than a strictly linear relationship, which would not be captured by a purely linear screening. Removing B from the RSM because its linear term is nonsignificant would have eliminated the model’s ability to represent this curvature and would therefore have biased the search for an optimum. The present Box–Behnken design allowed robust estimation of quadratic terms and interactions and thereby produced a reliable optimum that was independently validated experimentally.

Interaction Effects (RSM Surfaces) on Citric Acid Yield

At higher solid precursor (raw material) and lower solid precursor, CA yield was not favored relative to the intermediate one. The rise in CA concentration upon a rise in solid loading was observed. However, when the solid precursor was kept at an excessive amount, the yield of CA was decreased. This is because the condition favors polyalcohol forming according to. In other words, incubation time and substrate loading must be maintained in their optimum conditions for CA yield to be maximized; otherwise, its production will be affected.

Analytical Limitation and Future Confirmation

We emphasize that titration and FTIR, while useful and consistent with a high-purity citric acid product, do not chromatographically separate structurally related minor organic acids (e.g., iso-citric or oxalic acid). Chromatographic confirmation (ion-exclusion HPLC or LC-MS with appropriate standards) is therefore required to quantify or exclude minor byproducts. Instrument access for HPLC/LC-MS was not available at the time of these experiments; chromatographic confirmation is planned as a follow-up work.

Byproducts (Oxalic and Iso-Citric Acids): Formation, Mitigation, and Limitations

Formation of oxalic and iso-citric acids is a known competing pathway in A. niger fermentations and can lead to reduced net citric acid yields. Mechanistically, oxalate formation is favored under certain carbon/redox imbalances, trace-metal imbalances (e.g., Mn2+, Fe2+), and suboptimal process conditions. Iso-citric acid accumulation is often associated with altered TCA-cycle enzyme activity (aconitase/isocitrate dehydrogenase).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the sustainable valorization of banana peel Agro-waste into citric acid via response surface methodology-optimized solid-state fermentation with A. niger ATCC 10535 under low (10% w/w) sucrose supplementation. As an abundant yet underutilized biomass in Ethiopia, banana peel has proved to be a low-cost, renewable, and nutrient-rich substrate capable of supporting efficient microbial biosynthesis.

Using a Box–Behnken design, substrate loading, fermentation duration, and temperature were optimized to achieve a maximum citric acid yield of 45.563 g/L under 31.014 g of substrate, 5.811 days of incubation, and 31.054 °C. The model exhibited excellent predictive power (R 2 = 0.9948; Pred R 2 = 0.9353), with experimental validation differing by only 0.24% from predicted values. Operational analysis confirmed that moderate substrate loading (36 g), fermentation temperature near 30 °C, and 6 days of incubation consistently maximized yield, underscoring the importance of balancing process variables.

Characterization confirmed that the produced citric acid matched IS131860:2010 and Citrique Belge commercial standards in purity (99.4%), appearance, odor, and physicochemical properties, with heavy metals far below permissible limits. FTIR spectra aligned with analytical-grade (99.9%) citric acid, confirming the structural identity and product purity.

By integration of waste valorization with microbial process optimization, this work offers a scalable, environmentally responsible route for citric acid production, directly supporting circular bioeconomy initiatives. Beyond Ethiopia, the approach can be adapted to other Agro-waste streams worldwide, providing industries with a sustainable alternative to conventional citric acid production while reducing environmental burdens. Future work should focus on pilot-scale validation, techno-economic assessment, and integration with green downstream recovery methods to accelerate industrial adoption.
