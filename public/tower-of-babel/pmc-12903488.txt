Deficiencies in germline DNA repair are associated with early-onset gastrointestinal cancers and inform precision prevention strategies
Authors: Wenhui Yang, Yanxiang Zhang, Minghui Ge, Luyao Liu, Yongqian Shu, Dong Shen, Qin Zhang, Dongsheng Chen, Xiaochun Ping, Yiqian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastrointestinal (GI) cancers impose a significant global health burden and germline gene alterations play a significant role in the malignant transformation of epithelial cells. However, the prevalence and clinical relevance of pathogenic germline variations (PGVs) in Asian populations remain incompletely characterized.MethodsThis multicenter study analyzed six GI cancer types across 9470 Chinese patients through germline sequencing of 112 cancer susceptibility genes. PGVs were identified and characterized, and associated clinical, molecular, and somatic features were evaluated. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) and somatic mutation profiles were compared between PGV carriers and non-carriers to explore the biological implications of germline alterations.ResultsPGVs were identified in 5.4% (504/9,470) of patients, with pancreatic cancer (PC) exhibiting the highest prevalence (6.7%). Defects in the DNA damage response (DDR) pathway dominated, accounting for 84.2% of PGV carriers and demonstrating cancer-type specificity: homologous recombination repair (HRR) variants were enriched in cholangiocarcinoma (CCA; 72.7%) and PC (64.9%), whereas mismatch repair (MMR) defects were predominant in colorectal cancer (CRC; 23.0%). PGV carriers had a younger median age at diagnosis (59 vs. 61 years; p = 0.003) and a higher proportion of advanced-stage disease (82.2% vs. 79.7% for stages III–IV). Dual-PGV carriers manifested an even earlier age of onset (median 53 vs. 61 years; p < 0.001), with CRC dual-PGV carriers presenting the earliest onset (median 48 years; range 33–57). Non-MMR PGV carriers with CCA had a uniquely increased TMB (p = 0.005), redefining immunotherapy stratification. Somatic mutation profiles diverged between PGV carriers and non-carriers, with reduced TP53 mutations in patients with PC, GC, and CRC (p < 0.01) and enrichment of PIK3CA in CRC (p = 0.002).ConclusionsThese findings emphasize that DDR defects are key drivers of GI cancer biology, highlighting dual PGVs as biomarkers for aggressive, early-onset disease and underscoring the need for genetic screening in young patients. The interplay between germline susceptibility and somatic evolution highlights potential avenues for precision interventions—such as PARP inhibitors for HRR-deficient tumors and immunotherapy for MMR-deficient subtypes—offering biologically meaningful hypotheses that, while not yet validated in clinical trials, provide valuable direction for future precision oncology research. The study advocates population-specific genetic screening and biomarker-driven approaches to optimize clinical management in underrepresented Asian populations.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07595-9.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) tract malignancies represent a substantial global health burden, constituting 26% of worldwide cancer incidence and 35% of cancer-associated mortality [1]. In China, this disease spectrum demonstrates an even more pronounced impact, accounting for 33.5% of national cancer incidence and 44.1% of cancer-related deaths [2]. The principal GI malignancies encompass colorectal cancer (CRC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), gastric cancer (GC), pancreatic cancer (PC), esophageal cancer (EC), and cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), each presenting distinct epidemiological patterns and clinical challenges across global populations [3 ,4].

Advances in primary and adjuvant treatments have improved survival times for GI malignancies. However, despite the emergence of numerous treatment strategies, most GI tumors are diagnosed at an advanced stage with metastases, resulting in difficulties with curative surgical control and subsequent tumor-related deaths [4–6]. Cancer development is a gradual process defined by the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations. Increased understanding of tumor genomics and molecular profiling has enhanced our knowledge of cancer pathogenesis [7].

Germline gene alterations play a significant role during malignant transformation of epithelial cells. Germline genetic testing through next-generation sequencing can identify pathogenic variants in cancer-susceptibility genes associated with substantially elevated cancer risks, such as Lynch syndrome and BRCA-associated cancers, enabling risk stratification and targeted interventions [8–10]. Moreover, PGVs carrier in cancer patients have therapeutic implications. For example, a recent large-cohort study across more than 50 malignancies indicated that 9% patients had therapeutically actionable PGVs carrier according to the OncoKB classification [11], including homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes (BRCA1/2, CHEK2, ATM, PALB2, RAD51C/D, BRIP1) and mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM), which predict responses to PARP inhibitors or immunotherapy, respectively [7, 12–14]. However, the prevalence and clinical significance of PGVs carrier in GI cancers remain poorly characterized, with reported rates ranging from 2% to 16% depending on the number of cancer-susceptibility genes evaluated, patient selection criteria, and ethnic backgrounds [15–18].

Existing studies on PGVs carrier in GI cancers are often limited by small sample sizes within pan-cancers cohorts, restrictive inclusion criteria (e.g., young age or strong family history), and a focus on Western populations [17, 19, 20]. For example, the TCGA cohort reported an overall PGVs carrier frequency of 8.9% in GI cancers [20], but its applicability to Asian populations is uncertain due to demographic and genetic heterogeneity. Recent large-scale Chinese cohort studies have begun to address this gap—for instance, Yin et al. profiled over 1100 Chinese pancreatic cancer patients and revealed distinct germline variant spectra compared with Western cohorts, particularly involving BRCA1/2 and ATM genes [21]. Furthermore, prior research has not comprehensively addressed the molecular heterogeneity of PGVs carrier across GI cancer subtypes or their interplay with somatic mutational landscapes. This gap hinders the development of population-specific screening protocols and precision therapies.

To address these limitations, we conducted a multicenter, large-scale, retrospective study of 9470 Chinese patients with GI cancers, systematically analyzing 112 cancer-susceptibility genes to characterize PGVs carrier prevalence, pathway-specific defects, and clinical correlations. Our objectives were to: (1) delineate the molecular heterogeneity of PGVs carrier across GI cancer types; (2) compare PGVs carrier frequencies with Western cohorts to elucidate ethnic disparities; (3) investigate age stratification based on different pathway defects; and (4) explore somatic mutation profiles influenced by germline status. By integrating germline and somatic data, this study provides important insights into the genetic architecture of GI cancers and offers potential implications for precision prevention and therapeutic strategies in underrepres ented populations.

Materials and methods

Patients and sample collection

Between 2019 and 2025, clinical samples across six gastrointestinal cancer types, namely, colorectal cancer (CRC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), gastric cancer (GC), pancreatic cancer (PC), esophageal cancer (EC), and cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), were collected from three hospital centers (Shanxi Province Cancer Hospital, The First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University, and The Affiliated Jiangyin Hospital of Nantong University). Given the retrospective, multi-center design, the study size was determined by the total number of eligible patients during this period, without a priori sample size calculations. Matching tumor tissue and peripheral blood sample pairs from each patient were collected and preserved for subsequent testing. All patients provided informed consent to participate in this study.

Panel sequencing, variant calling and evaluation of pathogenic variants

A gDNA Tissue Extraction Kit (Concert) was used to prepare genomic DNA (gDNA) from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor blocks/slides or frozen fresh tumor tissues. A magnetic universal gDNA kit (TIANGEN) was used to prepare paired leukocyte gDNA. A Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit was used to quantify the extracted gDNA using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Qubit Fluorometer, and an Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent) was used to evaluate gDNA quality. Enzyme treatment was then used to shear 200 ng of extracted eligible gDNA into 200–300 bp fragments. The KAPA Hyper DNA Library Preparation Kit from Roche Diagnostics was used to perform end-repair sheared DNA, and the VAHTSTM Universal DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme) was utilized to perform A-tailing and to synthesize indexed paired-end adaptors for the SimcereDx Illumina platform. The size selection function was then used to remove unligated adaptors using Agencourt AMPure XP beads from Beckman Coulter. The ligation products were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to form a hybridization prelibrary. The prepared final qualified DNA libraries were sequenced by 150-bp paired-end according to the manufacturer’s instructions on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform(illumine, RRID:SCR_016387) by the College of American Pathologist (CAP)-accredited central laboratory at Jiangsu Simcere Diagnostics Co., LTD (Nanjing, China). The sequencing depth of the target regions is more than 500x per tumor tissue sample and 100x per control.

The sequencing raw data were converted to FASTQ files, and fastp software (V0.2.20.0) was used for quality control to trim the adapter and remove low-quality bases [22]. The Burrows-Wheller Aligner (BWA-MEM v0.0.7.17, RRID:SCR_010910) algorithm (http://bio-bwa.sourceforge.net/index.shtml) was used to align the obtained clean paired-end reads to the hg19 human genome reference (UCSC hg19/GRCh37). Dedup with Error Correct was used to remove PCR duplicate reads.

Determination of the somatic variations

Somatic variants were identified and annotated using VarDict (v0.1.5.7; RRID:SCR_023658), and InterVar was used to call and annotate single nucleotide variation (SNV) and insertion/deletion (Indel) mutations, respectively. We then filtered the variants in public databases for common single nucleotide polymorphisms, including the 1000 Genome Project (August 2015, SCR_008801) and Exome Aggregation Consortium Browser (ExAC, RRID:SCR_004068). Fusions and copy number variations, including amplification and deletion, were analyzed using Factera (v1.4.4) and CNVkit (dx1.1, RRID:SCR_021917), respectively. The minimum allelic mutation frequency threshold of SNVs, Indels, and fusions was set at 2%. The Variant Calling Format was subsequently annotated by ANNOVAR (RRID:SCR_012821). The analysis was performed using an NGS panel that covers the exon and partial intron regions of 551 cancer-related genes. The tumor mutation burden (TMB) was calculated as the total number of nonsynonymous somatic mutations in the coding regions per megabase (mut/Mb), excluding variants listed as known somatic alterations in COSMIC (RRID:SCR_002260). TMB values were normalized according to the effective panel size to ensure comparability across cases. Tumor purity was rigorously evaluated through both pathological assessment (H&E staining, ≥20% tumor content) and bioinformatic inference based on copy number variation (CNV) data. Purity-adjusted TMB estimation was implemented to minimize potential underestimation due to stromal contamination. A cutoff of 10 mut/Mb was applied to define TMB-high (TMB-H) versus TMB-low (TMB-L) tumors.

Evaluation of pathogenic variants

We analyzed 112 tumor-related predisposition genes from the germline data. These genes were selected on the basis of evidence from the WHO Classification of Tumors, National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) pancancer hereditary cancer guidelines, and recent literature reports identifying germline pathogenic variants in solid tumors covered by the 551-gene panel to ensure the inclusion of clinically and biologically relevant predisposition genes. Germline mutations were detected by VarDict (v0.1.5.7) and annotated by several public databases, including gnomad (v3.1.2), CLINVAR (202308), dbNSFP (v42a), COSMIC (v98), and the SimcereDx database. The germline mutations were filtered on the basis of the annotation, and systematic false-positive mutations were filtered following the background baseline. After filtration, the pathogenicity judgment for each mutation was determined on the basis of the results of InterVar and databases such as CLINVAR. Germline variants were classified into benign, likely benign, variants of unknown significance, pathogenic, and likely pathogenic categories according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.5.1). Group differences were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Effect sizes were quantified with Cohen’s d for continuous variables, whereas for categorical variables, generalized linear models were applied to estimate odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Missing staging data, arising mainly from incomplete historical records, were treated as missing at random, and patients without staging information were excluded from stage-related analyses.

Relationship between PGVs and clinical characteristics of gi cancers

Pathogenicity evaluation revealed 515 PGVs among 504 patients (Supplementary Table S1). Chi-square analysis of the distribution of the PGV-carrier patients across the three hospitals also showed no significant differences. We further evaluated the distribution of 112 genes harboring PGV mutations among centers. Only WRN showed a significant interhospital difference (p < 0.05), with six carriers identified at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University and one carrier at each of the other two hospitals, suggesting limited statistical power. No significant differences were observed for the remaining genes across centers (Supplementary Table S8). Among these 504 PGV carriers, 11 patients were dual-PGV carriers (Supplementary Table S9). An overview of the clinical characteristics of the PGV carriers is shown in Fig. 2. PC had the highest PGV prevalence (6.7%, 74/1,105) among the GI cancers, followed by CRC (6.2%, 234/3,792), CCA (5.9%, 11/185), GC (4.8%, 94/1,961), and EC (4.5%, 17/380). The lowest PGV prevalence was observed in HCC (3.6%, 74/2,047) (Supplementary Table S2–S7). Analysis of clinical stage revealed distinct distributions between PGV carriers (n = 180 with known stage) and noncarriers (n = 3,348 with known stage). Among the PGV carriers, 82.2% (148/180) presented with advanced-stage disease (III–IV), compared with 79.7% (2,668/3,348) in the non-PGV carriers. Spectrum and characteristics of PGVs in GI cancers

A total of 515 PGVs carriers in 112 unique genes were identified (Supplemental Table S1). The most frequently mutated gene was BRCA2 (13% of PGVs), followed by ATM (10%), PALB2 (8%), MUTYH (7%), BRCA1 (6%), and FANCA (6%), RAD51C/D (5.4%), MLH1 (5%), RAD50 (5%), and MSH2 (4%) (Fig. 3A). Most identified variants were predicted to be truncating (n = 347, 67.3%), including frameshift variants (n = 176, 34.1%), stop-gain variants (n = 166, 32.2%), and start-loss variants (n = 5, 1.0%). Missense variants (n = 81, 15.7%) and splice site variants (n = 81, 15.7%) were observed in equal proportion. Mutations in DNA damage response (DDR)-related gene (n = 435) accounted for 84.2% of all PGVs carriers (n = 515), highlighting defects in genomic stability pathways. Remarkably, alterations in homologous recombination repair (HRR; n = 243) and mismatch repair (MMR; n = 68) pathway dominated, accounting for 71.7% of DDR-related PGVs. The mutated genes were then classified into pathways: HRR, MMR, other DDR pathway (DDR other), and other cancer related pathways (non-DDR). HRR pathway variations were primarily involved genes such as BRCA1/2, ATM, PALB2, FANCA, RAD51D/C, and BRIP1; MMR pathway defects were primarily driven by MLH1 and MSH2 aberrations (Fig. 3C).

The distribution of pathway defects varied significantly across GI cancer types (Fig. 3D). The prevalence of HRR pathway alterations was highest in CCA (72.7%) and PC (64.9%), followed by GC (50.5%) and HCC (48.7%). In contrast, HRR frequencies were lower in EC (23.53%) and CRC (40.17%). MMR pathway defects were most prevalent in CRC (23.01%) and CCA (27.27%), while minimally contributed to PC (1.3%) and GC (3.16%). MMR aberrations were also rare in EC (5.88%) and HCC (6.58%). ‘DDR other’ pathways contributed 23.88% of mutations and were most prominent in EC (58.82%), suggesting alternative DNA damage repair mechanisms in this subtype. The non-DDR pathway, named ‘Other’ category (15.73%) showed relatively uniform distribution, except for elevated rates in HCC (21.05%) and GC (18.95%). These findings highlight PGVs carriers in HRR genes as central vulnerability in CCA and PC, while PGVs carriers in MMR genes are key drivers in CRC. The observed heterogeneity underscores the interplay between tumor type and specific pathway dysregulations, with implications for precision therapies such as PARP inhibitors for HRR-deficient cancers, immunotherapy for MMR-deficient malignancies, chemotherapy-based regimens for DDR-other patients, and targeted therapies for ‘Other’ PGV carriers (e.g. CDKN2A, NF1, SMAD4).

Patients with GI cancers who are PGV carriers are associated with an earlier age of onset of disease

This study assessed the age distribution of PGV carriers across GI cancer types in a cohort of 9470 individuals. The overall median age at diagnosis was 61 years (IQR: 15, 53–68) (Supplementary Figure S1). Patients with PGVs exhibited a younger median age at time of diagnosis (59 years, IQR: 17, 50–67), while dual-PGV carriers had the youngest age at time of diagnosis (53 years, IQR: 10.75, 49–60), which is consistent with the synergistic acceleration of tumorigenesis. Strikingly, dual-PGV carriers demonstrated cancer type-specific age patterns (Table 2). CRC patients harboring dual PGVs presented with an exceptionally young age of onset (median age: 48 years; range 33–57 years), including a 33-year-old male with MLH1/ATM mutations. In pancreatic cancer (PC) patients, three dual-PGV carriers were identified, aged 50, 70, and 77 years, suggesting a tendency toward later onset among PC patients harboring dual PGVs. Notably, only 11 patients (0.12%) carried dual PGVs; therefore, the statistical power for comparisons by age or cancer type was extremely limited. Age-stratified analysis revealed dynamic shifts in the predominance of specific pathways across GI cancers (Fig. 4). In the < 30-year cohort, MMR and non-DDR variants constituted 86% of all pathways in CRC patients, whereas GC patients showed an exclusive HRR dominance (100%). Among patients aged 30–49 years, MMR defects represented 41% of CRC PGVs, in contrast to the predominance of the HRR in CCA (100%) and PC (86%). Patients ≥60 years exhibited an HRR prevalence in PC (64%) and CCA (71%), whereas DDR-other pathways peaked in EC (62%). Critical transitions emerged: the proportion of MMR defects in CRC decreased from 43% ( < 30 years) to 8% (≥60 years). The 50–59-year-old cohort demonstrated balanced contributions across pathways: HRR (37%), MMR (27%), DDR-other (26%), and non-DDR (10%). These patterns demonstrate that early-onset cancers ( < 50 years) are characterized primarily by MMR defects in CRC, whereas HRR and DDR-other defects are more strongly associated with other GI malignancies. To further evaluate the relationship between age and PGV occurrence, logistic regression analysis was performed using the ≤30-year group as a reference. The analysis demonstrated a gradual decline in PGV prevalence with increasing age (OR = 1.15 for 30–39 years, 0.73 for 40–49 years, 0.63 for 50–59 years, and 0.60 for ≥60 years; all p > 0.1), indicating an earlier-onset enrichment pattern consistent with inherited susceptibility. Although the trend did not reach statistical significance, it provides quantitative support for the observed early-onset predominance. Comparisons of the somatic mutation burden between PGV and non-PGV carriers

We hypothesized that the presence of PGVs may lower the threshold for the accumulation of the somatic mutations required for disease onset. To test this hypothesis, TMB values were compared between PGV carriers and noncarriers across GI cancers. The results indicated that the effect of PGVs on the TMB was context dependent (Fig. 5A–G), and a statistically significant difference in the TMB persisted even after the exclusion of MMR-deficient samples (Supplementary Figure S2). Although the overall TMB differed significantly between PGV carriers and noncarriers (p < 0.001), stratification revealed that the increase in the TMB was driven primarily by MMR-deficient tumors (Fig. 5A’–G’). Specifically, MMR-deficient PGVs caused significant TMB increases in CRC (p < 0.001) and CCA (p < 0.001) patients, consistent with hypermutation phenotypes. Unexpectedly, non-MMR PGVs (MMR-proficient PGVs) also increased TMB exclusively in CCA patients (p = 0.0054), in contrast with no significant effects in other GI cancers. This finding identifies CCA as a unique entity in which carriers of both MMR PGVs and non-MMR PGVs (e.g., HRR defects) exhibit amplification of somatic mutagenesis, suggesting potential synergistic DNA repair collapse in CCA. Somatic hallmark gene mutations affected by PGVs

To explore potential differences in somatic hallmark gene mutations between PGV carriers and noncarriers, the top ten most frequently somatically mutated genes for each cancer type were analyzed. The results indicated that TP53 mutations were markedly less frequent in PGV carriers (45.9% vs. 71.3%, p < 0.001) in PC, 44.7% vs. 63.3% in GC (p < 0.001), and 65% vs. 73.5% in CRC (p = 0.005); however, SMAD4 mutations were enriched in PC (31.1% vs. 19.8%, p = 0.03), TGFBR2 mutations were increased in GC (13.8% vs. 6.6%, p = 0.014), and PIK3CA (27.8% vs. 19.1%, p = 0.002) and ACVR2A (25.6% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.001) were significantly enriched in CRC. In CCA, MUC16 (45.5% vs. 11.5%, p = 0.006) and DOT1L (27.3% vs. 1.1%, p < 0.001) mutations were disproportionately prevalent in PGV carriers (Fig. 6A). There were no significant differences in TP53 or KRAS mutation between the PGV and non-PGV groups (p > 0.05) in HCC, CCA, or EC. Notably, the prevalence of KRAS mutations was largely comparable across different cancer types (e.g., PC: p = 0.722 vs. CRC: p = 0.448), whereas that of MUC16 was significantly variable (e.g., CCA: p = 0.006 vs. HCC: p = 0.163). To further investigate germline–somatic interactions, we conducted pathway-level comutation analyses between PGV genes and major somatic drivers (Fig. 6A). The results revealed that germline MMR-PGVs (particularly MSH2 and MLH1) exhibited strong co-occurrence with somatic mutations in KRAS, MUC16, FAT1/3/4, and APC, indicating convergent disruption of DNA repair and proliferative signaling pathways. Interestingly, TP53 exhibited mutual exclusivity with several PGV genes, which was consistent with its significantly lower mutation frequency in PGV carriers. This pattern may reflect distinct evolutionary routes of MMR-deficient tumors, in which hypermutation and MSI phenotypes reduce the selective pressure for canonical TP53 pathway inactivation. Discussion

This large-scale multicenter analysis of 9470 samples provides unprecedented insights into the molecular heterogeneity of PGVs carriers across GI cancers in a Chinese cohort, revealing critical differences in mutation prevalence and pathway dysregulation. Our findings underscore the interplay between germline susceptibility and somatic evolution, with actionable implications for precision oncology.

The overall PGV frequency in our cohort (5.4%, 516/9,470) was notably lower than the 8.9% (147/1,796) reported in the TCGA-GI cohort [20]. Striking disparities were observed in specific cancers: for instance, the TCGA cohort had higher PGV frequencies in PC (14.1% vs. 6.7% in our cohort) and HCC (9.1% vs. 3.6% in our cohort), whereas our cohort had higher rates in CCA (5.9% vs. 2.2% in the TCGA cohort) and CRC (6.2% vs. 5.5% in the TCGA cohort). Notably, we observed unexpectedly high penetrance of HRR (72.7%) and MMR (27.3%) defects in Chinese patients with CCA, whereas discordant findings were reported in Western cohorts, in which BRCA1/2 and MMR genes demonstrated weaker associations with CCA pathogenesis [21]. Additionally, gene–environment interactions specific to Chinese populations may contribute to this divergence, and unmeasured environmental exposures prevalent in China (e.g., liver fluke infection, aflatoxin-contaminated foods, or occupational hepatotoxins) could potentiate the oncogenic effects of DDR defects in CCA. Although our study did not systematically collect exposure histories, this hypothesis warrants validation in future case–control studies integrating germline testing with environmental risk assessment. Differences in inclusion criteria, such as unselected and population-level sampling, may have contributed to the lower overall PGV rates in our study. For example, larger sample sizes in our cohort (e.g., PC: 1105 vs. 185 in the TCGA cohort; HCC: 2047 vs. 375 in the TCGA cohort) increase the generalizability for Asian populations. These findings align with recent studies highlighting the limitations of extrapolating Western genetic data to Asian cohorts [23–25], underscoring the need for ethnicity-tailored genetic screening protocols.

Notably, PGV carriers in our cohort exhibited a marginally higher proportion of advanced-stage disease (III–IV: 82.2% vs. 79.7% in non-PGV carriers), which aligns with prior reports linking germline defects to aggressive tumor biology [26]. This trend raises critical hypotheses, although the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). First, delayed diagnosis may contribute, as hereditary cancer syndromes (e.g., Lynch syndrome) often lack early symptoms, leading to later clinical detection [27]. Second, PGVs in DNA repair pathways (e.g., BRCA1/2 and MLH1) may accelerate tumor progression by promoting genomic instability and clonal evolution. For instance, HRR-deficient tumors exhibit elevated somatic mutation rates and chromosomal rearrangements, resulting in rapid adaptation to therapeutic pressure [28]. Third, the enrichment of late-stage disease in PGV carriers could reflect a biological predisposition to metastasis, as seen in CDH1 mutation-associated gastric cancer, in which germline defects disrupt epithelial integrity and promote invasive growth [29]. These observations parallel findings from the TCGA cohort, in which BRCA2 carriers with pancreatic cancer frequently presented with metastatic disease at diagnosis [20].

DNA damage response (DDR) pathway alterations dominated the PGV landscape (84.2%), with homologous recombination repair (HRR) and mismatch repair (MMR) defects accounting for 71.7% of the DDR-related mutations. HRR aberrations were enriched in CCA (72.7%) and PC (64.9%), mirroring patterns observed in ovarian and breast cancers [30–32], whereas MMR defects peaked in CRC (23.0%), which is consistent with Lynch syndrome prevalence [33, 34]. These results corroborate the therapeutic potential of PARP inhibitors in HRR-deficient GI cancers, as demonstrated in phase II trials for BRCA-mutant pancreatic cancer [13]. Conversely, the hypermutator phenotype in MMR-deficient CRC and CCA (TMB: p < 0.001) reinforces the utility of immune checkpoint inhibitors, as evidenced by the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA4 in MSI-H/dMMR GI tumors [14, 35, 36]. However, the lack of TMB increase in MMR-proficient tumors (e.g., GC and HCC) suggests that biomarker-driven immunotherapy strategies must be context specific, echoing findings from the KEYNOTE-158 and CheckMate-649 trials [37, 38]. Beyond differences in prevalence, mechanistic evidence indicates that inherited defects in DDR pathways (such as HRR and MMR) can shorten the latency of tumor initiation by permitting the early accumulation of DNA lesions and genomic instability [39]. In Chinese populations, this process may be further exacerbated by unique exposures, such as aflatoxin-contaminated staple foods, chronic hepatitis B infection, and endemic liver fluke or hepatotoxin exposure, that increase the baseline DNA damage burden [40]. A recent large Asian cohort study demonstrated that germline HRR variants in biliary tract cancer were associated with increased tumor mutational burden only in exposure-rich regions, supporting a gene–environment synergistic model [41]. Although our study did not collect detailed exposure histories, these mechanistic and epidemiologic insights underscore the importance of future integrative investigations combining germline testing with precise environmental risk assessment in Chinese patients with GI cancers.

PGV carriers exhibited earlier cancer onset (median age: 59 vs. 61 years), with dual-PGV carriers demonstrating the youngest age of onset (median age: 53 years). This finding is consistent with the synergistic acceleration of tumorigenesis when multiple DNA repair pathways are compromised, as exemplified by a 33-year-old male CRC patient carrying MLH1/ATM mutations. The age-stratified distribution of DDR pathway defects revealed distinct patterns: 1) early-onset cancers ( < 50 years) were predominantly driven by MMR defects in CRC (accounting for 41% of PGV carriers in the 30–49-year cohort), and 2) late-onset cancers (≥60 years) showed an HRR predominance in PC (64%) and CCA (71%). These findings align with prior reports that germline MLH1/MSH2 mutations predispose carriers to early-onset CRC [18], whereas HRR variants are associated with later-onset GI cancers [42]. Notably, dual-HRR-PGV carriers represented an exception to this pattern, as observed in our cohort in which a PC patient harboring dual-HRR PGVs presented with early onset of disease (50 years). Intriguingly, the somatic mutation landscape differed between carriers and noncarriers: reduced TP53 mutations in PC/GC/CRC (p < 0.01) and enrichment of PIK3CA in CRC (p = 0.002) suggest germline-somatic epistasis, in which PGV carriers may alter selective pressure during tumor evolution. Similar interactions have been observed in HRR-mutant cancers, in which HRR-driven replication stress is selected against TP53 loss in ovarian cancer [30] and EGFR variations in lung cancer [43].

This study had several limitations. The retrospective design and restricted gene panel (112 genes) may lead to an underestimation of the prevalence of PGVs, particularly for low-penetrance variants or noncoding regions. Additionally, the lack of functional validation for truncating variants (67.3% of PGVs) warrants caution in clinical interpretation. Furthermore, cross-cohort and regional comparisons may be influenced by differences in inclusion criteria, unmeasured confounders, or underlying population structure. Although additional chi-square analyses were conducted to assess potential regional bias, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out, and these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. To elucidate the mechanistic links between PGVs and somatic evolution, future studies should integrate multiomics approaches, such as whole-genome sequencing and transcriptomic profiling. Prospective trials are needed to validate the efficacy of PARP inhibitors in patients with HRR-deficient CCA/PC and immunotherapy in patients with MMR-aberrant CRC/CCA among Asian populations, which are life-saving treatments that have been implicated in previous reports [11, 44].

In conclusion, by delineating the molecular architecture of PGVs carriers in GI cancers, this study advances our understanding of ethnic-specific genetic risk, DDR pathway vulnerabilities, and germline-somatic interplay. The observed trend toward advanced-stage disease in PGV carriers underscores the urgency of implementing population-wide genetic screening programs to enable early detection and intervention. These findings advocate for population-tailored genetic screening protocols and biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies, ultimately bridging the gap between precision oncology research and clinical practice in underrepresented populations.
