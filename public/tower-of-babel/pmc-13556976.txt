pgRNA-m6A levels associate with HBV replication and liver function
Authors: Chaoqi Yang, Shanshan Qin, Rui-Huan Gao, Anling Li, Shihui Song, Yidan Zhou, Song-Mei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTm6A modification has been shown to play a role in regulating HBV RNA expression. However, it remains unclear whether the m6A levels of HBV pregenomic RNA (pgRNA) are related to HBV replication and liver function. Serum HBV pgRNA-m6A levels were determined by T3 DNA ligase assay. HBV DNA copy numbers and gene expression were detected by TaqMan assay and RT-qPCR, respectively. Liver function was evaluated using clinical laboratory indicators. The TET-off stable HBV-producing cell line HepAD38 and targeted pgRNA demethylation by SunTag system (TRADES) were used for the site-specific m6A editing. Flow cytometry was used to measure the cytokine levels in the supernatant of cell culture. Serum HBV pgRNA-m6A levels were positively correlated with HBV DNA copy number, pgRNA expression and liver function indicators (all p < 0.05). Both pgRNA-m6A levels and pgRNA expression were significantly increased in patients with high virus load (HBV DNA > 1.0 × 107 IU/mL). Site-specific demethylation of pgRNA1907-m6A significantly reduced HBV DNA, hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) and HBV pgRNA, while induced levels of interferon alfa 2 (IFN-α2) and apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic subunit 3A (APOBEC3A). pgRNA-m6A modification of HBV associates with viral replication and liver function indicators. Site-specific m6A demethylation of pgRNA provides novel insight into anti-HBV treatment.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection leads to health problems worldwide. Currently, there are 296 million people with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) in the world, especially in low- and middle-income countries [1]. The main complications of CHB are liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and CHB is responsible for 50∼80% of HCC cases [2]. The WHO reported that viral hepatitis B caused 0.82 million deaths in 2019, which was comparable to the number of deaths caused by tuberculosis and higher than those caused by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [3]. If left untreated, the cumulative 5-y incidence of cirrhosis in patients with CHB could be approximately 10–20%, and 2–5% of these patients will develop liver cancer [4].

To date, how the intrahepatic HBV reservoir regulates the progression of liver diseases remains unclear. HBV virions enter hepatocytes via the receptor sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP). Following entry, the virions undergo envelope shedding and uncoating, delivering relaxed circular DNA (rcDNA) to the nucleus. Within the nucleus, rcDNA is converted by host enzymes into covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA). Transcribed by host RNA polymerase II, cccDNA generates pregenomic RNA (pgRNA, 3.5 kb), preS/S mRNAs 2.4kb/2.1kb, and HBx mRNA (0.7 kb). The viral polymerase then mediates reverse transcription of pgRNA into progeny rcDNA. This newly synthesized rcDNA is subsequently assembled into mature, infectious HBV Dane particles [5,6].

In recent years, evidence has revealed that cccDNA molecules play a key role in persistent HBV infection, liver immune tolerance to HBV antigens, virologic rebound and drug resistance emergence after antiviral therapy; as such, intrahepatic HBV cccDNA and serum pgRNA could serve as biomarkers of HBV persistence [7–10].

RNA methylation is an important epitranscriptomic modification that regulates diverse biological processes, including RNA metabolism and function. N6-methyladenosine (m6A), one of the most abundant post-transcriptional mRNA modifications in eukaryotes, occurs predominantly near stop codons and in 3′UTRs [11]. High-throughput sequencing has identified more than 176,642 m6A modification sites in 293T cells [12]. Previous studies have reported that internal m6A modification of viral RNA regulates viral replication in the nucleus, as observed in influenza virus, simian virus 40, and Rous sarcoma virus [13–15]. Internal m6A modifications are present on a subset of both viral and cellular mRNAs synthesized in the nucleus. However, the roles of m6A in several viral infections have only recently begun to be revealed. During infections with SV40, HIV, Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus and enterovirus 71, the m6A modifications on viral RNAs affect viral replication and gene expression [16–19]. Our recent study reported that m6A may also be involved in severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) infection and replication [20]. These data reveal the function of the internal m6A modification complex in modulating mRNA transport and interaction with viral proteins.

In 2018, Imam et al. [21] found a conserved m6A consensus motif located at position 1905–1909 (GGACA) in the epsilon stem loop structure of HBV RNA, and further m6A mapping by MeRIP-seq revealed that A1907 is the site of m6A methylation. This site modification regulates the life cycle of HBV by modulating HBV gene expression and reverse transcription. It plays a positive role in pgRNA reverse transcription and a negative role in the stability of HBV mRNAs, thereby exerting dual regulatory effects on pgRNA. Moreover, m6A modification within the 5′ epsilon structural element also influences pgRNA packaging [22]. ISG20 together with YTHDF2 recognizes m6A-modified HBV transcripts and promotes their degradation, whereas FMRP and YTHDC1 recognize such transcripts and facilitate their nuclear export [23,24]. Thus, m6A methylation of HBV RNA serves a critical functional role and constitutes a key determinant of HBV replication efficiency, underscoring the importance of developing appropriate detection methods. Liu et al. [25] showed that T3 DNA ligase has strong selectivity in discriminating m6A from A in RNA and developed a sensitive quantitative assay to accurately identify m6A modification at single nucleotide resolution. Using this approach, m6A modification at the 2577th site of MALAT1 lncRNA was detected at levels as low as 4 fmol, and the recognition selectivity was increased by 54.1-fold. This method for m6A detection at single-base resolution has greatly enhanced our understanding of the biological function of m6A in RNA. Using this approach, Chen et al. delineated the mechanism of circRNA m6A methylation in maintaining the physiological state of nucleus pulposus cells [26]. Moreover, we previously developed a site-specific m6A editing system called ‘targeted RNA demethylation by SunTag system (TRADES)’ [27]. In brief, our TRADES system can demethylate a specific m6A site via the targeting element dCas13b, which is fused to 10 copies of the 19-amino-acid GCN4 peptide and can recruit multiple scFv-fusion RNA demethylases.

Here, we utilized T3 DNA ligase assay to determine serum pgRNA-m6A levels in HBV-positive patients. Our results showed a correlation between serum pgRNA-m6A levels and pgRNA expression, HBV DNA copy number, liver function indicator levels. This study reported the presence of pgRNA-m6A in human serum, highlighting its clinical significance and contributing to the understanding of active viral replication. By employing the TRADES m6A editing system to manipulate pgRNA-m6A methylation in a site-specific manner, we demonstrated that removing the m6A modification from pgRNA could hinder HBV replication and infection. This finding offers a novel potential target for the development of anti-HBV therapies.

Methods

Patients and samples

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 50 patients with HBV infection on the following morning after hospital admission between 2020 and 2021. The inclusion criteria were both serum HBsAg-positive and HBV DNA-positive. The exclusion criteria were the use of nucleos(t)ide analogs and peginterferon treatment before blood sample collection, and coinfection with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) or Hepatitis C Virus (HCV). Liver function was assessed by serum liver enzymes, proteins, bilirubin, and alpha fetoprotein (AFP). More details are described in the Supplementary Materials. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval Number: 2017058).

Clinical biochemical tests

Clinical laboratory indicators typically used for assessing the severity of liver function include levels of liver enzymes (i.e., alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase [GGT], and alkaline phosphatase [ALP]), proteins (i.e., total protein [TP], albumin, and globulin), and bilirubin (i.e., total bilirubin [TBIL] anddirect bilirubin [DBIL]). The serum levels of AFP, the most common biomarker for HCC risk evaluation, were also determined.

Materials and reagents

The pgRNA1907-A, pgRNA1907-m6A, and pgRNA1861-A segments, as well as RNA probes and primers, were synthesized; their sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Plasmids of pAAV-HBV1.3-B1 (for HBV genotype B) and pAAV-HBV1.3-C1 (for HBV genotype C) were gifts from professor Yuchen Xia (State Key Laboratory of Virology, Wuhan University). T3 DNA ligase and Stick Together TM DNA Ligase Buffer (New England Biolabs), ReverTra Ace TM qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo), and iTaq TM Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) were commercially available.

Extraction of HBV DNA and RNA

Magnetic beads were used to extract serum total DNA and RNA. HBV particles in the supernatant of the cell culture medium were concentrated by PEG precipitation, and then, HBV DNA and RNA were extracted using a TIANamp Virus DNA/RNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China).

Determination of m6A levels in pgRNA

Ligation reaction mixture A consisted of 1 μL of 20 nM probe L, 1 μL of 20 nM probe R, 3 μL of T3 DNA ligase ligation buffer and 1 μL of RNA target. Ligation reaction mixture B consisted of 1 μL of 320 U T3 DNA ligase, 2 μL of ligation buffer and 1 μL of nuclease-free water. After mixture A was incubated at 85°C for 3 min and 35°C for 10 min, mixture B was added, and the reaction mixture was then placed on ice immediately. A volume of 10 μL PCR mixture contained 2 μL of the above ligation product, 10 μM of forward and reverse Ligation primer, SYBR Green Supermix and nuclease-free water; PCR was carried out using a hot start at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s.

Cell culture and site-specific m6A demethylation

The Tet-off inducible HBV-producing stable cell line HepAD38 (a gift from Prof. Yuchen Xia at State Key Laboratory of Virology, Wuhan University) was maintained in high-glucose DMEM (HyClone ™, Logan, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin and 1% streptomycin (Biosharp TM, Beijing, China), and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37°C. The cells were seeded in 6-cm dishes with tetracycline-supplemented medium. After 24 h of incubation, tetracycline was removed to induce HBV replication. The cells in the logarithmic phase of growth were collected and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 0.5 ~ 1.0 × 106 cells per well in 2 mL tetracycline-free complete medium. After incubation at 37°C for 24 h, the cells were transfected with plasmids, including pFlagCMV2-GCN4-GGS6-dCas13b plasmid, scFv-GGS6-FTO plasmid, sgRNA plasmid (sg-HBV) and pc0043 vector plasmid (sg-Control), in the TRADES system using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen) to facilitate the site-specific m6A demethylation of pgRNA1907-A (24). The co-transfection ratio of GCN4-GGS6-dCas13b, SCFV-GGS6-FTO and the sgRNA-expressing plasmid was 1:1:2. The culture medium was changed 24 h after transfection, and the virus-containing supernatant was collected 48 h after transfection. Besides, the pAAV-HBV1.3-B1 and pAAV-HBV1.3-C1 plasmids were transfected into Huh7 cells to test whether this method could detect different HBV genotypes.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Reverse transcription and cDNA synthesis were performed using the HiScript ® III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech). Real‑time quantitative PCR was carried out with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech). GAPDH served as an internal control, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

SELECT quantitative real-time PCR

The SELECT assay was used for detection of site-specific m6A modification in the cultured cells as previously [28]. Briefly, total RNA (1 µg) was mixed with 40 nM up and down primers, and 5 μM dNTPs in 17 μL of 1× CutSmart buffer. Primer annealing was performed by sequential incubation at 90°C, 80°C, 70°C, 60°C, and 50°C for 1 min each, followed by 40°C for 6 min. Then, 3 μL of enzyme mixture containing 0.01 U Bst 2.0 DNA polymerase, 0.5 U SplintR ligase, and 10 nmol ATP was added to a final volume of 20 μL. The reaction was incubated at 40°C for 20 min, inactivated at 80°C for 20 min, and held at 4°C. For the quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR), 2–4 μL of the elongation and ligation samples were used. qPCR was subsequently performed with the qPCR primers for SELECT using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech).

Quantification of HBV markers

HBV DNA levels were detected using the TaqMan assay. RT-qPCR was used to determine the expression of pgRNA. HBeAg levels in serum and culture medium were measured by an automatic chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer. Patients were divided into three groups according to viral load (low-load: HBV DNA < 1.0 × 105 IU/mL, medium-load: 1.0 × 105 ~ 1.0 × 107 IU/mL, high-load: HBV DNA > 1.0 × 107 IU/mL).

Cytokine detection

The flow cytometry-based fluorescent detection kit (Cellgene Biotech, Hangzhou) was used to measure the levels of cytokines IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α in the supernatant of cell culture according to the instructions. Fluorescence detection was performed using flow cytometry, and the results were analyzed by BDFACS Canto Software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 25.0 software. For normally distributed data, the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) or standard error of the mean (Mean ± SEM). Student t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. If the variance was not homogeneous, then the Tamhane’s T2 post hoc tests were conducted. For data with a skewed distribution, the results are presented as medians and quartiles. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Dunn-Bonferroni post hoc test. The chi-square test was used to analyze categorical variables. The correlations across pgRNA expression, pgRNA-m6A level, and HBV DNA copy numbers were assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to assess the association between pgRNA-m6A and serum ALT, AST. All p values were two-sided, and the statistical significance level was 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

Results

Clinical features of participants

The participants included 33 men and 17 women, and the average age was 44 ± 14 y. Of the participants, 25.9% were positive for HBeAg, 33.3% were positive for anti-HBe, and 100.0% were positive for anti-HBc. Serum levels of ALT, AST, GGT, ALP, TBIL, DBIL, TP, albumin, and globulin were abnormal, and the rates ranged from 23.8% to 45.2% (Supplementary Table S2), indicating that the patients’ liver functions were damaged to a certain extent. Approximately 29.6% of the patients had elevated serum AFP levels.

Clinical application of T3 DNA ligase-based assay

Theoretically, if the 1907th A is methylated, the m6A site could hinder the ligation of the DNA probes L1 and R1, which adjacently hybridize with RNA near the 1907th A site and can be ligated by catalysis with T3 DNA ligase (24). Real-time fluorescence amplification curves were generated by pgRNA1907-A fragments in the range of 8 pM to 80 nM (Figure 1(A)). The CT values, the number of cycling thresholds, were plotted against the logarithm transformation of the concentration of pgRNA1907-A, and a good linear relationship was shown. The linear regression equation was CT = −3.467 log10 CpgRNA1907-A +28.559 with a correlation coefficient R2 of 0.999 (Figure 1(B)). Moreover, the CT value generated by pgRNA1907-m6A was approximately 2–3 cycles lower than that generated by pgRNA1907-A at the same RNA concentration, showing significant difference in signal (Figure 1(C)). It is worth noting that the real-time fluorescence signal derived from 8 pM pgRNA1907-m6A was almost identical to that derived from no template control (NTC). In other words, when the total concentration of the target RNA was less than 8 pM, RNA containing m6A produced a negligible signal, which will not contribute to the real-time fluorescence signal. Next, we selected an adenosine-only (A) site, the 1861st site, in the same RNA target molecule as a reference site to determine the total concentration of the RNA target. A T3 DNA ligase assay was also used to generate the amplification curves and construct calibration curves of the pgRNA1861-A fragments hybridized with the DNA probes L2 and R2. There was an excellent linear relationship between the CT value and log10 CpgRNA1907-A within the range of 8 pM to 80 nM (Figure 1(D)). The correlation equation was CT = −3.200 log10CpgRNA1861-A +29.589, and the correlation coefficient R2 was 0.997 (Figure 1(E)). By integrative analysis of the calibration curves of pgRNA1907-A and pgRNA1861-A, we could accurately quantify the pgRNA-m6A levels in real-world biological samples.

Supplementary Table S3 demonstrates that the T3 DNA ligase-based assay achieved a sensitivity of 0.6414 pM and a linear range from 0.6414 to 16.166 pM for pgRNA-m6A quantification. According to the calibration curves (Figure 1(B,E)), we measured the concentration of pgRNA-m6A in an unknown sample as well as its serial dilutions. The observational concentrations of pgRNA-m6A were linearly related to the measured concentrations, and the correlation coefficient was 0.992 (Figure 1(F)), indicating that this assay is feasible. Therefore, we set the cutoff threshold of positive-m6A at 0.6414 pM.

Measurement of HBV pgRNA-m6A levels in serum samples

With the developed T3 DNA ligase assay, we further investigated the concentrations of pgRNA-m6A in 50 patients with HBV infection. The concentration of pgRNA1907-m6A was calculated as the total RNA (pgRNA1861-A) concentration minus the concentration of pgRNA1907-A. For precise quantification, if the observational concentration was higher than 8 pM, the sample was diluted by 10- or 20-fold to ensure that the testing results lie within the linear range of the T3 DNA ligase assay.

The results demonstrated that the pgRNA-m6A concentrations in all clinical samples were above 1.4 pM and were significantly higher in the high-load HBV group compared to the low-load HBV group (p < 0.001, Figure 2(A)). Pearson correlation analysis showed that pgRNA-m6A concentrations were positively correlated with HBV DNA copy numbers (r = 0.3465, p = 0.0137, Figure 2(B)) and pgRNA expression (r = 0.5636, p < 0.0001, Figure 2(C)). These results suggested that pgRNA-m6A modifications could modulate pgRNA expression and HBV replication. Moreover, we found that the level of pgRNA in the high-load group was significantly higher than that in the low-load group and the medium-load group (Figure 2, p < 0.05), and a positive correlation was also observed between pgRNA expression and HBV DNA copy number (r = 0.5987, p < 0.0001), reflecting the role of pgRNA as a replication intermediate in the HBV replication cycle (Figure 2(E)).

m6A modification of pgRNA is associated with liver function indicators

To investigate whether liver damage was related to m6A modification of HBV pgRNA, we explored the correlations between liver functional indicators and pgRNA-m6A levels. As shown in Figure 2(F-I), Pearson correlation analysis showed that the concentration of pgRNA-m6A was positively associated with the serum levels of ALT (r = 0.4186, p = 0.0058), AST (r = 0.3276, p = 0.0342), TBIL (r = 0.3140, p = 0.0485), and DBIL (r = 0.3416, p = 0.0310). However, no correlation was found between the concentration of pgRNA-m6A and the serum levels of GGT, ALP, TP, albumin, globulin, and AFP (Supplementary Figure S1). Multivariable linear regression analysis showed that, after adjustment for HBV DNA, HBeAg, age, and sex, pgRNA-m6A remained associated with serum ALT and AST levels. These findings indicate that serum pgRNA-m6A levels are associated with liver function indicators in patients with chronic HBV infection.

Clinically, a 2.5-fold elevation in ALT (≥125 U/L) or AST (≥100 U/L) levels indicates that patients should receive supportive therapy [29]. The increase in the pgRNA-m6A levels in HBeAg-positive patients showed the same results (p = 0.041). In addition, pgRNA-m6A levels were significantly increased in patients with elevated serum AFP levels (AFP ≥ 8.78 ng/mL, p = 0.013). pgRNA-m6A modification regulates immune response and HBV replication

To further validate the clinical observations linking m6A modification to HBV replication and liver function, we utilized the TRADES system for the site-specific demethylation of the m6A-1907 site on pgRNA. This manipulation was performed in the HepAD38 cell line, a tet-off inducible HBV-producing stable cell line, to mimic a ‘low m6A’ state. This approach allowed us to investigate the functional impact of reduced m6A levels on viral replication and liver function (Figure 3(A)). The T3 DNA ligase assay showed that the pgRNA-m6A level was reduced in the sg-HBV group compared to the sg-control group (Figure 3(B), p = 0.0023, Figure 3, p = 0.0015). Compared to the sg-control group, the expression level of pgRNA decreased in the sg-HBV group (Figure 3, p = 0.0206). Although the expression of total HBV RNA showed a similar decreasing trend, the difference was not statistically significant (Figure 3(D)). Meanwhile, HBV DNA copy number (Figure 3, p = 0.0375) and HBeAg levels (Figure 3(E), p = 0.0320) in the sg-HBV group also markedly decreased, but not HBsAg levels (Figure 3(E)). Host antiviral immune is vital against for HBV infection and replication. m6A modification of HBV RNAs attenuates host innate immunity via RIG-I signaling [30]. Therefore, after site-specific demethylation of pgRNA-m6A in HepAD38 cells, we tested the mRNA expression of several antiviral immune-related genes, including cytidine deaminase gene family (APOBEC3A, APOBEC3B, APOBEC3G), interferon regulatory factor family (IRF3, IRF5, IRF7), mitochondrial anti-viral signaling protein (MAVS), TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), retinoic acid-inducible gene 1 (RIG-I) as well as interferon-alpha 2 (IFN-α2) and lymphotoxin-beta receptor (LTβR). The results showed that targeted demethylation of m6A on pgRNA significantly promoted the mRNA expression of Apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic subunit 3A (APOBEC3A) (Figure 3(F), p = 0.0067) and IFN-α2 (Figure 3(E), p = 0.0053), while the other genes did not significantly change (Supplementary Figure S2). Subsequently, the levels of cytokines IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, andTNF-α in the culture medium of HepAD38 cells were measured. Except for the level of IL-6 significantly decreased (Figure 3(G), p = 0.0022), the other four were not detected due to low abundance. These findings suggested that m6A modification on pgRNA may affect host immune through IFN-α2/APOBEC3A pathway to prevent HBV reactivation and replication, agreement with previous report that APOBEC3A has been shown to participate in cytidine deamination and the formation of no purine/no pyrimidine sites, resulting in degradation of HBV cccDNA [31].

Collectively, our clinical data and experimental findings demonstrated that the site-directed removal of m6A modification from HBV pgRNA1907-A could modulate HBV replication (Figure 4). Discussion

In the current study, we first revealed that HBV pgRNA-m6A modification could regulate HBV replication and is closely associated with liver function indicators using T3 DNA ligase assay and site-specific pgRNA-m6A demethylation, after excluding the influence of HBV relaxed-circular DNA as well as nucleos(t)ide analog and peginterferon treatment.

Previous studies have reported that abnormal m6A modification causes aberrant expression of important viral genes, especially in virus-related human diseases, such as HIV, HBV, HCV, and EBV [32]. Imam et al. [23] reported that m6A-modified HBV transcripts are selectively recognized and processed for degradation by Interferon-stimulated gene 20 (ISG20), and HBV RNAs become less sensitive to ISG20-mediated degradation when the m6A reader protein YTHDF2 is silenced in HBV-yielding cells. Cellular m6A machinery regulates host innate immunity against HBV infections by inducing the m6A modification of viral transcripts [30]. Although m6A-label-seq enables the m6A detection at single-nucleotide resolution [33]; m6A-REF-seq [34], MAZTER-seq [35], m6A-seal [36] and DART-seq [37] have also provided particular advantages and unique information about m6A distribution, these methods are time-consuming and expensive, limiting their applications in clinical practice. We therefore used a sensitive T3 DNA ligase assay to determine serum levels of HBV pgRNA-m6A, which may be promising for clinical practice. First, this method is sensitive, simple and accessible: very low levels of pgRNA-m6A can be detected using T3 DNA ligase and regular quantitative PCR. Second, a standard curve was established for the absolute quantification of pgRNA-m6A levels, so the results could be more accurate. Of note, m6A modification at the 5′ epsilon stem-loop is required for efficient reverse transcription of pgRNA, whereas m6A at the 3′ epsilon stem-loop promotes the destabilization of HBV transcripts [21]. However, this T3 DNA ligase‑based assay was unable to identify the 1907-m6A modification at the 5′ or 3′ epsilon stem-loop of pgRNA due to the similar epsilon stem loop structure. Our clinical data primarily suggested that the overall m6A levels of HBV pgRNA associated with HBV replication and host liver function. Further investigation is warranted to clarify whether the m6A modifications at the 5′ or 3′ epsilon stem-loops affect HBV replication and host liver damage.

Currently, the clinical diagnosis of HBV infection mainly depends on the detection of serum HBV DNA and traditional serological biomarkers, such as HBsAg, HBeAg, anti-HBe, HB surface antibody (anti-HBs), and HB core antibody (anti-HBc). In particular, HBV DNA levels can reflect HBV replication in vivo. These test results could help guide clinical decision-making for antiviral therapy [38–40]. However, some studies have shown that even if serum HBV DNA level is lower than the detection limit, a large number of patients will still experience virological rebound after the termination of antiviral therapy [10]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find a new serological marker that can be used to predict the efficacy of antiviral therapy and the safe withdrawal of drugs. Here, we found a positive correlation between pgRNA-m6A levels and HBV DNA copy numbers, suggesting that the m6A modification of pgRNA increases HBV replication. In addition, the expression of pgRNA showed a similar trend, which was consistent with previous reports [41,42]. Since the level of pgRNA also reflects the presence and transcriptional activity of HBV cccDNA, the dynamic changes in serum pgRNA levels might serve as a new alternative biomarker for the clinical evaluation of antiviral efficacy, providing guidance for clinical decisions about appropriate antiviral drug therapy and withdrawal time [43,44]. Using the m6A editing system TRADES to demethylate pgRNA-m6A, we demonstrated that FTO could specifically remove pgRNA-m6A, which could suppress HBV replication in an m6A-dependent manner. In terms of specificity, the potential off-target effects on other HBV transcripts by the TRADES system cannot be completely excluded. m6A methylations present in all HBV transcripts since different HBV transcripts share identical 3′-terminal nucleotide sequences [21]. To address this issue, new methods need to be developed to enhance pgRNA specificity.

Our clinical data further indicated that higher serum pgRNA-m6A levels were associated with increased serum ALT and AST levels, reflecting by an increase of serum ALT and AST. Moreover, the concentration of pgRNA-m6A was higher when the level of ALT or AST increased by ≥2.5-fold of their upper reference limits, further providing evidence of correlation between m6A modification and liver function indicators. In addition, pgRNA-m6A level was higher in AFP increased group (≥8.78 ng/mL), supporting previous report that pgRNA could increase the stemness and promote the development of HBV-related HCC through reciprocal regulation with the m6A reader protein IGF2BP3 [45]. Although this study confirmed positive correlations between pgRNA-m6A levels, these findings do not establish a direct causal relationship. Further investigations include a long-term follow-up and cell-based or animal model experiments to elucidate the causal relationships.

This study has several limitations: (1) a small sample size collected from a single center may have limited the clinical interpretation; (2) although the R1 and L1 probes are specific to pgRNA containing 1907-m6A site of HBV genotype D, this T3 DNA ligase assay is also applicable for the detection of HBV genotypes B and C (Supplementary Figure S3). Further investigations are required to determine whether pgRNA-m6A levels vary among patients with different HBV genotypes; (3) several confounding factors have not been taken into consideration, including disease phase, fibrosis/cirrhosis status, HBV genotype, alcohol use, and metabolic liver disease; (4) more analytical validations, including assessments of intra- and inter-assay reproducibility, and lower limit of detection would further strengthen the clinical translation of this method; and (5) lack of inactive-editor and site-mutant controls, and validation in different cells such as HBV-infected HepG2/Huh7-NTCP cells.
