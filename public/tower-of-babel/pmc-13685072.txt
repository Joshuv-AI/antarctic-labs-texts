Occupational hazards and workplace safety practices among nurses in Nablus, Palestine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ahmad Eshtaya, Rajaa Shaheen
Published: 2026

Background

Hospital nurses provide continuous direct care and routinely encounter work demands related to patient handling, prolonged standing, rotating shifts, body fluids, sharps, chemical agents, and emotionally demanding clinical situations [1–9]. These exposures matter for both workforce well-being and care delivery because health-worker safety is closely linked to staffing stability, sustained clinical attention, and organizational resilience [10, 11].

The burden of work-related physical symptoms in nursing is substantial. A 2023 meta-analysis estimated an annual prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders of 77.2% among nurses [12]. Sleep-related problems are also common in shift nursing; a systematic review and meta-analysis reported poor sleep quality across included shift-work nurse studies, and a recent meta-analysis estimated a pooled prevalence of shift work disorder of 45.5% among nurses [13, 14]. These findings underline the occupational significance of musculoskeletal strain, fatigue, and sleep disruption, although prevalence estimates vary with setting and measurement instrument.

Occupational hazards in nursing also include biological exposure to blood and body fluids, needlestick and sharps injuries, contact with hazardous substances, and psychosocial strain [6–9, 15, 16]. Psychological and social consequences may include stress, negative self-perception, interpersonal difficulties, and burnout-related experiences; the World Health Organization recognizes burnout as an occupational phenomenon associated with chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed [4, 5, 17].

Conceptually, the hospital work environment generates occupational exposures and work demands that may be physical or ergonomic, biological or chemical, and psychological or social. These exposures may contribute to physical, psychological, and social work-related symptoms. Preventive and organizational responses - including hand hygiene, task-specific personal protective equipment (PPE), safety training, incident reporting, health surveillance, ergonomic measures, and safe-patient-handling systems - may reduce exposure or mitigate consequences [3, 10, 11, 18–22]. In the present study, the WRSS assessed work-related symptom domains, whereas the separate 18-item study-specific set captured selected exposure conditions, preventive behaviors, PPE practices, cognitive demands, and organizational practices. The latter was therefore not treated as a single theoretical safety construct in the revised analysis.

A specific knowledge gap remains in Palestine. Existing Palestinian evidence has focused more narrowly on infection-control knowledge and practice, while multidimensional evidence simultaneously describing physical, psychological, and social work-related symptoms together with selected workplace safety-related items among hospital nurses remains limited [23]. Comparative evidence across clinical departments in Nablus is also scarce. This gap is important because occupational-health planning may require different responses for musculoskeletal strain, sleep-related demands, PPE availability, incident reporting, and department-specific exposures.

Accordingly, this study was necessary to provide a locally grounded description of multidimensional work-related symptoms and selected workplace safety-related practices without assuming that all safety-related items reflect a single construct. The study aimed to investigate multidimensional occupational hazards and reported workplace safety practices among hospital nurses in Nablus, Palestine, and to explore differences in WRSS domain scores according to demographic and professional characteristics.

The study addressed three questions: (1) Which physical, psychological, and social work-related symptoms were most frequently reported? (2) What response patterns were observed across the study-specific workplace exposure, prevention, PPE, cognitive-demand, and organizational safety items? (3) Did WRSS domain scores differ across selected demographic and professional groups?

Methods

Study design and setting

An analytical cross-sectional design was employed to describe work-related symptom patterns and selected workplace safety-related responses at one point in time. The design permits description of group differences but does not establish causality. The study was reported with reference to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cross-sectional studies [24].

The study was conducted in three hospitals in Nablus, Palestine: An-Najah National University Hospital, Rafidia Hospital, and Al-Watani Hospital. The participating hospitals are major healthcare institutions in Nablus and include departments in which nurses provide direct inpatient care.

Participants and sampling

The study population comprised registered nurses working in medical, surgical, pediatric, and gynecology/obstetrics departments in the participating hospitals. A non-probability convenience sampling approach was used to recruit eligible nurses from these departments. A total of 160 questionnaires were distributed and 140 were available for the final analysis, corresponding to an analytical completion proportion of 87.5%. The archived study documentation does not permit the 20 unavailable questionnaires to be separated reliably into exact counts of non-participation and incomplete returns; therefore, no unsupported split is reported. The total number of eligible nurses across the three hospitals was not retained in the archived records, so a population-based response rate could not be calculated.

Sensitivity analysis for sample adequacy

The original study documentation stated that G*Power had been used before data collection, but the original output and prespecified parameters were not retained; consequently, the original a priori calculation could not be reconstructed reliably and no unsupported target sample size is claimed. To characterize the detectable magnitude of effects with the achieved sample, a retrospective sensitivity analysis was performed. With a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, the observed gender-group sizes (64 male and 76 female nurses) could detect an independent-groups standardized mean difference of approximately Cohen’s d = 0.48. For a one-way ANOVA with total N = 140 and four groups under approximate equal-allocation assumptions, the detectable effect was approximately Cohen’s f = 0.28; for three groups, approximately f = 0.27. These values are sensitivity estimates only and do not replace an a priori sample-size calculation, particularly where subgroup sizes were highly unequal.

Eligibility criteria

Registered nurses working in medical, surgical, pediatric, and gynecology/obstetrics departments in the participating Nablus hospitals were eligible. Nurses who declined participation, did not provide informed consent, or returned questionnaires that could not be included in the final analytical dataset were excluded. No additional eligibility restrictions by employment fraction, minimum tenure, or shift pattern were documented in the archived study records; these workforce characteristics were not available for the present analysis.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured self-administered English-language questionnaire prepared for the present study. The questionnaire consisted of three parts. Part I assessed demographic and professional characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, years of nursing experience, department, and highest educational qualification.

Part II used the Work-Related Symptom Scale (WRSS), a component of the previously published Work-Related Illness Risk Inventory (WRIRI), to assess physical, psychological, and social work-related symptom domains [25]. Items were scored from 1 (highly absent) to 5 (highly present), with higher domain means indicating greater perceived presence of symptoms.

Part III comprised 18 study-specific items assembled to capture selected workplace exposure conditions, preventive and PPE-related behaviors, cognitive/work demands, and organizational safety practices. Examples included standing for long hours, direct contact with patients, carrying heavy weight, hand washing, glove and mask use, protective eyewear, safety training, incident reporting, and medical examinations. The common response anchors were 1 (always), 2 (mostly), 3 (sometimes), 4 (rarely), and 5 (not needed). Because these heterogeneous items do not represent a single clearly defined construct and no factor-analytic validation was available, the revised manuscript interprets them primarily at item level and does not treat their aggregate as a validated unidimensional safety scale. The item groupings used in Table 1 are descriptive only and are not psychometrically validated subscales. An English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

The questionnaire was administered in English. No Arabic translation/back-translation procedure was documented. The original study records state that the questionnaire was written in simple language and that explanations were available to respondents; a pilot study was also used to assess clarity and feasibility. Potential comprehension bias from English-only administration is acknowledged.

Validity and reliability

Content validity was assessed through pre-distribution expert review. The archived records do not retain a sufficiently reliable account of the exact number and disciplines of reviewers or a formal content-validity-index procedure; therefore, these details are not reconstructed. A pilot study with 15 nurses who were not included in the main sample assessed item clarity and questionnaire feasibility. A formal item-by-item revision log was not retained.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The overall occupational-hazards scale had an alpha of 0.945; domain values were 0.901 for physical symptoms, 0.910 for psychological symptoms, and 0.924 for social symptoms. The 18-item study-specific set had an alpha of 0.888. These coefficients are reported as internal-consistency estimates only. Because the 18 items were conceptually heterogeneous and no exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis was performed, the alpha value was not interpreted as evidence of unidimensionality or construct validity.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected from 1 October to 15 October 2024. Questionnaires were self-administered during working hours. Participation was voluntary, and researchers were available to clarify item wording without directing responses. Confidentiality and anonymity were emphasized. The archived records do not preserve sufficiently reliable details on supervisor presence, use of sealed envelopes, or average completion time, and these procedures are therefore not inferred.

Bias

Several potential sources of bias were considered. Convenience sampling from selected departments in three hospitals may have introduced selection bias and limits generalizability. Because the total eligible-nurse denominator was unavailable and the 20 questionnaires not included in analysis could not be classified precisely, non-response bias could not be fully assessed. Self-administered responses may be affected by recall and social-desirability bias, particularly for safety-related behaviors and psychological or social symptoms. Use of one questionnaire for multiple domains may also introduce common-method bias. Voluntary participation, anonymity/confidentiality, simple-language wording, pilot testing, and availability of item clarification were used to support response quality, but no formal social-desirability instrument was administered.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 19.0, as documented in the original study records. Frequencies and percentages summarized categorical variables and item-level responses. WRSS physical, psychological, and social values were interpreted as domain-level mean scores; the overall WRSS value represented the mean across the administered WRSS items. The 18 heterogeneous study-specific workplace items were analyzed primarily at item level, and the previously reported aggregate-score subgroup analysis was removed from the revised manuscript.

Independent-samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were retained only as exploratory reproductions of the original subgroup analyses for WRSS scores. The archived materials did not retain formal normality diagnostics, homogeneity-of-variance output, or nonparametric sensitivity analyses; therefore, the parametric results are not presented as confirmatory. No multiplicity correction was documented in the original analysis, and the potential for type I error inflation across multiple subgroup tests is explicitly acknowledged. No post-hoc comparisons were undertaken for the retained WRSS analyses because none of the retained one-way ANOVA omnibus tests was statistically significant. Missing-value imputation was not documented; questionnaires not available for complete analysis were excluded according to the archived study procedure. Statistical significance in the original exploratory tests was set at p < 0.05, and exact p-values are interpreted cautiously.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University, and administrative permission was obtained from the participating hospitals. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained before participation, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw without consequence. Confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study.

Use of large language model tools

During manuscript preparation, a large language model tool was used for language editing, formatting support, and clarity refinement only. The authors critically reviewed, edited, and verified the final manuscript, including all data, interpretations, citations, and conclusions, and take full responsibility for the content.

Study-specific workplace exposure, prevention, PPE, and organizational items

Because the 18 study-specific items represent heterogeneous concepts, results are presented at item level and organized below into descriptive categories only. The response anchors are reproduced as administered. Among preventive/PPE-related items, the largest proportion selecting “always” was observed for hand washing (60.7%), followed by mask use (55.0%) and general safety measures (55.0%). Among exposure/work-demand items, 56.4% selected “always” for direct contact with patients and 46.4% for standing for long hours. For organizational items, 50.7% selected “always” for documented incident reporting and 43.6% for both safety training and medical examinations. These responses should be interpreted item by item rather than as a single safety-practice score.

Discussion

This study described multidimensional work-related symptoms and selected workplace exposure, prevention, PPE, cognitive-demand, and organizational safety responses among nurses in three hospitals in Nablus. The principal pattern was a greater reported burden of physical symptoms - particularly sleep disturbance, leg pain, and back pain - than psychological or social symptoms. The revised interpretation also distinguishes the heterogeneous 18-item workplace set from a validated unidimensional safety scale and therefore emphasizes item-level findings.

The prominence of musculoskeletal symptoms is consistent with international evidence. A 2023 meta-analysis estimated an annual prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders of 77.2% among nurses [12], while earlier comprehensive evidence has repeatedly identified the lower back, shoulders, and neck as important sites of nursing-related musculoskeletal burden [26]. The current WRSS findings cannot be treated as directly equivalent to prevalence estimates obtained with the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire or other dedicated musculoskeletal instruments, but they point in the same broad direction: physically demanding nursing work warrants sustained ergonomic attention.

Sleep disturbance was the most frequently reported physical symptom. This finding is occupationally plausible because nursing commonly involves rotating schedules, night work, and recovery challenges. Systematic evidence has reported poor sleep quality among shift-work nurses across included studies, and a recent meta-analysis estimated shift work disorder in 45.5% of nurses [13, 14, 27]. Direct numerical comparison with the present study is limited because the WRSS item “sleep disturbances” is not equivalent to the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index or formal shift-work-disorder criteria. Nonetheless, the convergence of evidence supports careful attention to shift design, recovery time, fatigue risk, and access to occupational-health support.

The practical implications of leg and back pain are most relevant at hospital and nursing-management levels. Participating hospitals may consider routine ergonomic risk screening for high-load tasks, safe-patient-handling protocols, competency refreshers for transfers and repositioning, and access to lifting or transfer aids where feasible [19–22]. Staffing and task allocation should also be reviewed when repeated manual handling is concentrated within particular units. These actions should be guided by local risk assessment rather than inferred as proven effects of the present cross-sectional study.

Psychological and social symptoms were generally reported at lower perceived levels than physical symptoms, but this should not be interpreted as definitive absence of psychosocial burden. Self-report, cultural hesitation to disclose distress, normalization of chronic workplace stress, and social-desirability pressures may all influence responses. The World Health Organization’s framing of burnout as an occupational phenomenon reinforces the need for confidential support pathways and appropriate validated psychosocial instruments in future research [17]. No formal social-desirability measure or validated burnout instrument was used in the current study.

The 18-item study-specific set requires equally cautious interpretation. Preventive/PPE-related responses showed high proportions selecting “always” for hand washing, masks, and general safety measures, which is broadly consistent with the central role of standard precautions and infection-control education [18, 23]. However, exposure and work-demand items such as standing for long hours, direct contact with patients, and carrying heavy weight are not safety measures and should not be interpreted as adherence behaviors. Organizational items such as training, incident reporting, and medical examinations reflect yet another construct. Separating these descriptive domains in the revision improves conceptual clarity but does not convert them into validated subscales.

Protective eyewear and hearing-protection responses were less frequently marked “always” than hand hygiene or mask use. This finding does not necessarily indicate poor practice because PPE should be selected according to task-specific exposure risk. Hospital policies should therefore specify when splash protection, hearing protection, gowns, or other PPE are indicated and ensure availability where risk assessment supports their use [3, 11, 18]. Similarly, exposure to direct patient contact or sharps should be interpreted alongside preventive systems rather than as safety practice itself.

The exploratory subgroup analysis showed a higher physical WRSS mean among female than male nurses, with a mean difference of 0.44. Possible explanations include differences in work assignment, symptom reporting, musculoskeletal vulnerability, and task distribution, but the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish among these mechanisms. Because multiple subgroup comparisons were conducted and no multiplicity correction was documented, this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. No retained one-way ANOVA was statistically significant, so post-hoc pairwise claims were not made.

For hospital managers in the study setting, the most defensible translation is targeted rather than generic. Potential priorities include department-level occupational risk assessment; ergonomic screening of transfers, prolonged standing, and repetitive manual tasks; safe-patient-handling support; fatigue-sensitive scheduling review; task-specific PPE availability; non-punitive incident reporting; periodic occupational-health surveillance; and confidential psychosocial referral pathways [10, 11, 19–22, 28]. Future work should also measure shift patterns, staffing levels, and access to lifting or transfer aids because these variables were not recorded here and may contextualize musculoskeletal and sleep-related findings.

Methodologically, the revision intentionally reduces claims that were not supported by the available measurement evidence. The study-specific 18-item set is reported primarily at item level, Cronbach’s alpha is not treated as proof of construct validity, and the previous aggregate-score subgroup analysis has been removed. The remaining parametric subgroup analyses reproduce the original WRSS analysis but are explicitly exploratory because formal diagnostic output for distributional and variance assumptions was not retained.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, self-reported data are susceptible to recall and social-desirability bias, particularly for safety-related behaviors and psychological or social symptoms; use of one questionnaire for multiple domains may also introduce common-method bias. Third, convenience sampling from selected departments in three Nablus hospitals limits representativeness and generalizability beyond the participating settings. The total eligible-nurse denominator was not retained, and the archived records did not permit the 20 questionnaires unavailable for analysis to be separated into exact counts of non-participation and incomplete returns, limiting assessment of non-response bias. Fourth, the questionnaire was administered in English without a documented translation/back-translation process, creating potential comprehension bias despite simple-language wording, pilot testing, and availability of clarification. Fifth, the 18 study-specific workplace items mixed exposure conditions, preventive/PPE behaviors, cognitive demands, and organizational practices. No EFA or CFA was performed, formal construct validity was not established, and Cronbach’s alpha alone cannot demonstrate unidimensionality; accordingly, the revised analysis prioritizes item-level interpretation. Sixth, formal archived diagnostics for normality and homogeneity of variance were unavailable, no multiplicity correction was documented, and the retained subgroup tests should therefore be considered exploratory. Seventh, objective ergonomic assessments, incident records, biological monitoring, shift patterns, staffing levels, and access to lifting or transfer aids were not recorded. Finally, direct benchmarking against studies using the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, validated burnout measures, or formal shift-work-disorder criteria is limited because the present study used different measures.

Conclusions

Among nurses in the participating Nablus hospitals, the most prominent self-reported work-related symptoms were sleep disturbance, leg pain, and back pain. Psychological and social symptoms were reported at lower perceived levels than physical symptoms, but underreporting and social-desirability influences cannot be excluded. The study-specific workplace items showed heterogeneous patterns across exposure conditions, preventive/PPE behaviors, cognitive demands, and organizational practices and should be interpreted item by item rather than as a single validated safety construct. The exploratory gender difference in physical WRSS score is hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the findings may inform consideration of targeted ergonomic assessment, safe-patient-handling support, fatigue-sensitive work planning, task-specific PPE policies, non-punitive incident reporting, occupational-health surveillance, and department-level risk assessment in the participating hospitals. Longitudinal and psychometrically stronger studies are needed before causal or population-wide policy conclusions are drawn.
