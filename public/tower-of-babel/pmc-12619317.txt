Efficacy and safety of Chinese herbal compounds for lumbar disc herniation: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials
Authors: JianBin Guan, Guang Yang, KaiTan Yang, Haohao Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a leading cause of back pain, and its management remains challenging due to limited therapeutic options. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), particularly Chinese herbal compounds (CHC), has been used to treat LDH based on empirical clinical experience. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CHC in treating LDH, comparing it with conventional Western medicine treatments.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, and Chinese databases (CNKI, WanFang, VIP, and SinoMed), up to May 2023. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing TCM with Western medicine for LDH were included. Data extraction and bias assessment were performed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool. Statistical analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.0 software. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (number CRD42023434493).ResultsTwenty-seven RCTs with 3133 patients were included. Meta-analysis showed that CHC had superior outcomes compared to Western medicine in several key measures: effective rate (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.08–1.16, P < 0.00001), final follow-up (RR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.08–1.18, P < 0.00001), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score (SMD = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.17–1.55, P = 0.01), visual analog scale (VAS) score (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.29–1.03, P = 0.0004), and safety (RR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01–0.25, P = 0.0003). Subgroup analyses indicated that CHC was particularly beneficial for patients with longstanding disease (LDH > 18 months) and those intolerant to NSAIDs.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis suggests that CHC is more effective than Western medicine in treating LDH, with benefits in pain relief and functional improvement. However, the evidence quality was downgraded due to significant risks of bias, high heterogeneity in outcomes, and the limited number of studies reporting safety data. Future studies should address these limitations through more rigorous study designs, longer follow-up periods, and standardized protocols. If future trials confirm these findings, CHC may be a valuable complementary treatment for LDH, particularly in patients with NSAID intolerance or chronic conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02963-4.Keywords: Lumbar disc herniation, Chinese herbal compounds, Traditional Chinese medicine, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Functional improvement

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a prevalent and debilitating condition that represents a significant cause of disability worldwide [1, 2]. It is characterized by the displacement of the intervertebral disc in the lumbar spine, which often leads to nerve compression, resulting in symptoms such as low back pain, leg pain, and neurological deficits [1]. Clinically, the condition is associated with chronic pain, disability, and a decreased quality of life, particularly among working-age adults [3]. According to global estimates, approximately 60–80% of people will experience low back pain at some point in their lives, with LDH being a leading cause of these symptoms [4]. In China, approximately 20% of adults suffer from chronic low back pain, a significant portion of which is attributable to LDH [5]. As the aging population increases and sedentary lifestyles become more prevalent, the incidence of LDH is expected to rise, placing substantial pressure on the healthcare system and underscoring the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies [6].

From the modern medical perspective, treatment for LDH typically involves two main approaches: surgical and conservative management [7]. Surgical intervention is generally reserved for patients with severe symptoms, such as persistent sciatica lasting for more than 4 to 6 weeks, or those with neurological deficits that interfere with daily functioning [8]. Non-surgical treatments, including physical therapy, pharmacological interventions, and lifestyle modifications, form the cornerstone of care for most patients. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids are commonly used to manage pain and inflammation. However, long-term use of NSAIDs is associated with adverse effects, including gastrointestinal disturbances, renal impairment, and cardiovascular risks [9]. In response to these limitations, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) offers an alternative approach, rooted in thousands of years of empirical clinical experience and holistic principles [10, 11]. In treating LDH, TCM emphasizes the regulation of Qi (vital energy), the balance of Yin and Yang, and the improvement of blood flow [10]. Chinese herbal compounds (CHC) play a pivotal role in the conservative treatment of LDH and are believed to possess anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and nerve-repairing properties [12, 13]. Recent studies have indicated that CHC may modulate inflammation, promote circulation, and enhance the healing of damaged tissues [14, 15]. These effects are thought to involve the regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the promotion of neurotrophic factors that support nerve regeneration [16]. Despite the increasing interest and historical use of CHC, its integration into mainstream clinical practice remains limited, primarily due to the lack of high-quality, evidence-based studies directly comparing it to Western medicine. Recent research exploring the efficacy and mechanisms of CHC in the treatment of LDH has surged. Some clinical trials have suggested superior outcomes with CHC compared to conventional treatments [17, 18], while others have failed to demonstrate significant differences. This discrepancy underscores the need for a rigorous evaluation of the existing literature through systematic reviews and meta-analyses to clarify the role of CHC in LDH treatment.

In summary, while Western medicine offers effective management for many cases of LDH, the long-term use of pharmacological agents is often accompanied by significant risks. TCM, particularly Chinese herbal compounds, presents a promising alternative, yet the scientific evidence remains inconclusive. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to synthesize data from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing CHC to conventional Western medicine for the management of LDH, with a focus on clinical outcomes such as pain relief, functional improvement, and safety. By providing a critical assessment of the available evidence, this review will help clarify the role of CHC in the treatment of LDH and inform clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

To ensure comprehensive literature retrieval, we systematically searched several electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), China National Knowledge Internet (CNKI), WanFang Database, Chongqing VIP Database, and SinoMed Database, covering publications from the inception of each database until May 1, 2023. The search terms included “Chinese herbal compound,” “granule,” “decoction,” “powder,” “pill,” “tablet,” and “lumbar disc herniation.” These keywords were combined using Boolean operators (e.g., “AND,” “OR,” “NOT”) to ensure all relevant literature was captured. Detailed search strategies are provided in Appendix 1. For example, the search strategies for PubMed and Embase were as follows: for PubMed, the database search was conducted via NCBI, with the last search date on May 1, 2023. The search terms included: ((“Intervertebral Disc Displacement”[MeSH Terms] OR “lumbar disc herniation”[Title/Abstract] OR “lumbar intervertebral disc herniation”[Title/Abstract] OR “LDH”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Chinese herbal compound”[Title/Abstract] OR “herbal formula”[Title/Abstract] OR “traditional Chinese medicine”[Title/Abstract] OR “TCM”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Randomized Controlled Trial”[Publication Type] OR “randomized”[Title/Abstract] OR “randomised”[Title/Abstract] OR “random allocation”[MeSH Terms])). For Embase, the search was also performed on May 1, 2023, using the following search terms: (‘intervertebral disc displacement’/exp OR ‘lumbar disc herniation’:ti,ab OR ‘LDH’:ti,ab) AND (‘chinese herbal medicine’/exp OR ‘chinese herbal compound’:ti,ab OR ‘traditional chinese medicine’:ti,ab OR ‘TCM’:ti,ab OR ‘herbal formula’:ti,ab) AND (‘randomized controlled trial’/exp OR random*:ti,ab).

Additionally, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov, the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP), and the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) for ongoing or unpublished trials to minimize publication bias. Furthermore, gray literature sources such as Google Scholar, the Chinese Dissertation Database, and the Degree Thesis Database were also searched to identify potentially relevant studies. However, in accordance with our pre-specified exclusion criteria, only peer-reviewed full-text articles were ultimately included in the meta-analysis. Non-peer-reviewed materials such as conference abstracts, study protocols, and dissertations were excluded during the screening process.

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines and shown in Appendix 2. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (number CRD42023434493).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) study type: RCTs; (2) participants: patients with a definite diagnosis of LDH based on CT or MRI, without surgical indications; (3) interventions: the experimental group was treated with CHC, while the control group received Western medicine treatment, either as monotherapy or in combination with other Western drugs; (4) co-interventions: no additional therapies such as acupuncture, moxibustion, Tuina, or osteopathy were used in either group; (5) outcomes: studies were required to report at least one relevant clinical outcome related to pain relief or functional improvement (e.g., VAS, ODI); (6) only studies published in English or Chinese were included.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) animal or in vitro studies; (2) self-controlled trials; (3) studies involving additional non-drug interventions applied to either group (e.g., acupuncture, physical therapy); (4) non-peer-reviewed publications such as conference abstracts, reviews, or protocols. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts according to the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Any disagreement was discussed and resolved by a third independent author. No age restrictions were imposed, but given that LDH primarily affects adults, studies involving pediatric or adolescent populations were not identified.

Data extraction

Two independent investigators reviewed the titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies to assess eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then obtained for further evaluation. Data extraction was performed independently by two authors using a pre-designed standardized form. To ensure consistency and accuracy in the data extraction process, two independent reviewers performed the extraction using a standardized form. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary. To further enhance inter-rater reliability, the two primary reviewers were trained on the extraction protocol and conducted a pilot extraction on a subset of studies before proceeding with full data extraction. This process was designed to ensure that all relevant information was captured accurately and consistently across all included studies. Furthermore, regular meetings between the reviewers were held to discuss any uncertainties or ambiguities, and final consensus was reached with the involvement of the third reviewer when required. To ensure completeness of the data, if essential information was missing or unclear, efforts were made to contact the original study authors. When data could not be obtained, appropriate estimation or imputation methods were considered where applicable.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included RCTs was independently evaluated by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool recommended by the Cochrane Back Review Group [19]. This tool covers seven domains: A: random sequence generation; B: allocation concealment; C: blinding of participants and personnel; D: blinding of outcome assessment; E: incomplete outcome data; F: selective reporting; G: other bias. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk” based on the information reported in the articles. Discrepancies between reviewers were addressed through discussion, and where consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to arbitrate the decision. This approach ensured that any potential biases in the included studies were thoroughly assessed. To verify the reliability of the risk of bias assessment, both reviewers independently evaluated a random sample of 5 studies at the beginning of the process, followed by cross-checking their assessments for agreement. The final risk of bias evaluation for all studies was based on this iterative process, ensuring consistency and reliability in the assessment of study quality.

Data synthesis and analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan 5.0 software (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). For dichotomous data, we calculated risk ratios (RR), and for continuous data, we calculated standardized mean differences (SMD), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all effect estimates. We assessed statistical heterogeneity using the I2 statistic and chi-squared test. If the I2 value was greater than 50% or the P value was less than 0.05, significant heterogeneity was assumed. In cases of significant heterogeneity, we applied the random-effects model for analysis; when heterogeneity was low (I2 ≤ 50%), the fixed-effect model was used.

Assessment of evidence quality

We assessed the certainty of the evidence for each primary outcome using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) approach, which evaluates the quality of the evidence across five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Risk of bias evaluates factors like randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding, where a high risk could lead to an overestimation or underestimation of treatment effects, leading to downgrading of studies with unclear or high risk. Inconsistency refers to variability in results across studies, where significant heterogeneity due to variations in treatment protocols, populations, or outcome measures can lower the evidence quality. Indirectness assesses whether the study results apply to the research question, with downgrading occurring if the populations or interventions differ substantially. Imprecision evaluates the certainty of the effect estimate, considering sample size and the number of studies, where studies with smaller sample sizes or wide confidence intervals are downgraded for imprecision. Publication bias addresses the potential for selective reporting, with the risk assessed through direct review and indirect methods like funnel plots. Based on these domains, the certainty of the evidence was classified into four levels: high (very confident in the estimate, unlikely to change with further research), moderate (moderately confident, further research could impact the confidence and estimate), low (limited confidence, further research is likely to change the estimate), and very low (very little confidence, further research is likely to change the estimate significantly). The assessment was conducted independently by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. A summary of findings table was constructed to present the overall certainty ratings.

Subgroup analysis

We also conducted planned subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The following subgroup analyses were performed: (1) type of Western medicine: CHC vs. NSAIDs, CHC vs. non-NSAIDs, CHC vs. combined Western medicine. (2) Course of disease: subgroups were defined based on disease duration: <6 months, 6–18 months, 18–36 months, and >36 months. (3) Treatment duration: subgroups based on treatment duration: 7 days, 14 days, 21 days, and 30 days. (4) Age groups: subgroups based on mean age: 30–40 years, 40–50 years, and >50 years. These subgroup analyses were conducted whenever more than one study with an estimable effect was available in each subgroup.

Publication bias

To evaluate publication bias, we generated funnel plots for each outcome. Asymmetry in the funnel plots indicated potential publication bias, suggesting that small studies with negative results may not have been published. If at least 10 studies were included in the analysis, we also performed Egger’s test to statistically assess funnel plot asymmetry.

Results

Selection of studies

A total of 2324 studies were initially identified through database searches and 1607 studies were initially identified through other gray literature sources, of which 3636 studies were excluded after reviewing their titles and abstracts. After full-text screening of the remaining 295 studies, 268 studies were excluded based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 27 studies with 3133 patients were included in the meta-analysis [20–46]. These studies evaluated the efficacy of CHC in treating LDH, either as monotherapy or in combination with other treatments. Description of the included studies

The 27 studies included in this review were all conducted and published in Chinese from 2005 to 2017. Together, these studies included 3133 patients with LDH (consisting of 1627 patients in the CHC group and 1506 patients in the Western medicine groups). Among the research included, one reported using three groups [45], while the others reported using two-group parallel control methods. All studies were designed as RCT with kinds of 25 CHC in the treatment group, as well as with 19 Western medicines in the control group. The ingredient of every formula was enumerated in Table 1. Moreover, according to the included articles, 19 Western medicines were generally divided into 3 types. (1) NSAIDs: aceclofenac, aspirin, celebrex, diclofenac, fenbid, ibuprofen, indomethacin, loxoprofen, meloxicam, piroxicam. (2) Neurotrophy medicine: mecobalamin, vitamin B1, vitamin B2, vitamin D. (3) Other medicines: colchicine, mannitol, calcium, blocking treatment. Sixteen studies investigated the comparison between CHC and NSAIDs [20, 21, 24, 26, 30, 31, 33, 34, 36, 38–42, 45, 46]. Six studies investigated the comparison between CHC and non-NSAIDs [23, 28, 29, 32, 37, 44]. Five studies showed the comparison between CHC and combined Western medicine [22, 25, 27, 35, 43]. Four articles provided the follow-up data after taking medicine [24, 33, 39, 45]. Course of disease, 12 studies supplied sufficient data, and based on our planned scheme, 4 time intervals were <6 months [21, 22, 35], 6–18 months [28, 36, 39], 18–36 months [36, 44, 45], and >36 months [26, 29, 41]. The range of course of treatment was 7–30 days: 7 days [27, 43], 14 days [22, 24, 26, 28, 34, 46], 21 days [20, 21, 25, 29, 31, 39, 41, 42, 44], 30 days [23, 30, 32, 33, 35–37, 40, 41, 45].

Twenty-six articles provide us with mean age and are then divided into 3 intervals: 30–40 years [18, 20, 21, 25, 26, 30, 33, 37, 38], 40–50 years [19, 22, 23, 27, 31, 32, 35, 36, 41, 43], >50 years [26, 36, 41, 42, 44–46]. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the included studies.

Methodological study quality assessment

The details of the risk of bias assessed by the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for each of the included articles were summarized in Fig. 2. Of the 27 included studies, the randomization procedure was reported in adequate detail in 14 studies, and 6 studies had a high risk of bias in sequence generation. Four trials clearly reported the allocation concealment. Eight studies clearly described the blinding of participants and personnel, and other descriptions of the studies were unclear. Nine studies reported the blinding of outcome assessment. Fifteen studies described the selective reporting clearly. As a whole, 8 out of 27 studies (29.62%) were judged as at high risk of bias because one or the main aspects of the bias assessments were labeled high.

Risk of bias summary

Effective rate

Pooled analysis of the 27 studies [18–46] indicated that CHC significantly improved the effective rate compared with Western medicine. The overall effect of the effective rate was RR = 1.12 (95% CI: 1.08–1.16, P < 0.00001), with low heterogeneity (P = 0.003, I2 = 47%), leading us to use a fixed model for the analysis (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of effective rate meta-analysis

Final follow-up

Pooled analysis of 4 studies [24, 33, 39, 45] showed that the CHC group exhibited superior improvement in final follow-up compared with the Western medicine group. The overall effect was RR = 1.13 (95% CI: 1.08–1.18, P < 0.00001) with low heterogeneity (P = 0.16, I2 = 39%), and a fixed model was used for this analysis (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of final follow-up meta-analysis

JOA score

Six studies [20–22, 41, 42, 44] evaluated the JOA score. The pooled results showed that the CHC group had a significantly better JOA score compared to the Western medicine group (SMD = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.17–1.55, P = 0.01). However, high heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 94%), indicating variability in treatment protocols and study designs (Fig. 5).

Forest plot of JOA score meta-analysis

VAS score

Analysis of 8 studies [27, 31, 32, 36, 41, 42, 44, 45] indicated that CHC significantly improved the VAS score compared to Western medicine (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.29–1.03, P = 0.0004). The heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 87%), highlighting the variability in the studies included in the analysis (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of VAS score meta-analysis

The substantial heterogeneity observed in the outcomes for both VAS and JOA scores (I2 > 85%) may be attributed to several factors. First, CHC formulations varied significantly across studies in terms of herbal ingredients, dosage forms, and preparation methods. Such inconsistencies likely introduced variability in treatment effects. Second, the treatment duration differed across trials, ranging from short-term (7 days) to extended use (up to 30 days), which may have influenced the degree of symptom relief observed. Third, methodological differences, including risk of bias due to inadequate blinding, allocation concealment, and inconsistent randomization, could have exaggerated the estimated effects. Fourth, heterogeneity in patient characteristics such as age, chronicity of LDH, and baseline symptom severity may have affected outcomes. Finally, variability in outcome measurement tools and time points further contributed to inconsistent findings. These factors should be considered when interpreting the pooled results, and future trials should aim for greater standardization to reduce heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis

To obtain an in-depth understanding of CHC, we conducted the subgroup analysis according to our previous scheme including CHC versus NSAIDs, CHC versus non-NSAIDs, CHC versus combined Western medicine, and course of disease including <6 months, 6–18 months, 18–36 months, and >36 months. Course of treatment embracing 4 time points: 7 days, 14 days, 21 days, 30 days. Mean age with 3 intervals: 30–40 years, 40–50 years, >50 years. Although the fixed model was fit for P > 0.05, I2 < 50%, we draw relatively more stable results by using a random model in all subgroup analysis in this study (Table 2). First, the pooled result of CHC versus NSAIDs, 17 studies with 2183 patients, showed that the CHC is superior to the NSAIDs with medium heterogeneity [20, 21, 24, 26, 30, 31, 33, 34, 36, 38–42, 44–46]. But, compared with the non-NSAIDs, the CHC group exhibits no better results than the non-NSAIDs group, 4 studies with 319 patients were included, and there was low heterogeneity 34% existed among studies [23, 28, 32, 37]. And then, we tried to use a fixed model; the results exhibit that the CHC group is superior to non-NSAIDs 1.11 (95% CI: 1.02–1.21, P = 0.02, I2 = 34%). We speculated that the unstable result was caused by the insufficient data from only 4 studies. So, we still used the relatively stable random model. Lastly, 8 studies with 830 patients were pooled; we also found that the result favored the CHC group compared with the combined Western medicine group [22, 25, 27, 28, 32, 35, 37, 43] (Supplement 1). Furthermore, the duration of the LDH before receiving treatment was imperative for clinicians to make a reasonable decision. So, we further conducted subgroups including 4 time phases involving <6 months, 6–18 months, 18–36 months, and >36 months. The pooled results indicated that both 18–36 months and >36 months phases showed favorable outcomes for the CHC group compared the Western group. However, there was no statistically significant difference between <6 months and 6–18 months phases. Additionally, the course of treatment was also an important index to make a rational judgment for economy and assessment (Supplement 2). The pooled results showed that except for 7 days 1.11 (95% CI: 0.96–1.28, P = 0.16, I2 = 35%) [27, 43], the rest of the 3 time points embracing 14 days 1.12 (95% CI: 1.03–1.22, P = 0.009, I2 = 32%) [22, 24, 26, 28, 34, 46], 21 days 1.15 (95% CI: 1.06–1.25, P = 0.0006, I2 = 52%) [20, 21, 25, 29, 31, 38, 39, 42, 44], and 30 days 1.10 (95% CI: 1.05–1.16, P = 0.0001, I2 = 50%) [23, 30, 32, 33, 35–37, 40, 41, 45] drew better results in the CHC group than in the Western medicine group. This may suggest that 7 days may be the shortest point for taking medicine. However, because of only 2 studies, more eligible research needs confirming this result (Supplement 3). Finally, we conducted the mean age subgroup with 3 intervals to find out the optimum age bracket: 30–40 years 1.13 (95% CI: 1.04–1.23, P = 0.004, I2 = 54%) [20, 23, 27, 28, 32, 34, 35, 39, 40], 40–50 years 1.12 (95% CI: 1.05–1.19, P = 0.0004, I2 = 54%) [21, 24, 25, 29, 33, 34, 37, 38, 43, 45], and >50 years 1.10 (95% CI: 1.04–1.18, P = 0.002, I2 = 39%) [24, 34, 39, 40, 42–44]; interestingly, all ranges of age exhibited more favorable results in the CHC group than in the Western medicine group (Supplement 4).

Subgroup analysis of included studies

Safety

The pooled analysis indicated that the CHC group had a lower incidence of adverse events compared to the Western medicine group, with no significant heterogeneity (P = 0.69, I2 = 0%). However, only three studies [21, 39, 41] reported safety outcomes, which limits the robustness of conclusions regarding the safety profile of CHC. In these studies, adverse events were observed exclusively in the Western medicine groups and included anaphylaxis (5), edema (5), hypothermia (4), glossitis (3), gastrointestinal bleeding (2) [21], dyspepsia (5), hepatic dysfunction (1), and anaphylaxis (1) [39], as well as dyspepsia (2) [41] (Fig. 7). No adverse events were reported in the CHC groups. The pooled analysis revealed a significantly lower incidence of adverse events in the CHC group (RR = 0.05, 95% CI: 0.01–0.25, P = 0.0003). However, due to the limited number of studies reporting adverse events and potential underreporting, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further research with comprehensive safety monitoring and standardized reporting of adverse events is needed to more accurately assess the safety of CHC.

Forest plot of adverse events

Publication bias

Publication bias was reported via the funnel plot (Fig. 8), where the asymmetry of the funnel plots may have arisen through heterogeneity. The funnel plot was symmetric: almost all articles were on the top of the triangle. Egger’s test was performed to assess potential publication bias. The regression analysis of effect sizes against standard errors yielded a slope of 7.00 (95% CI: −5.72, 19.72). The P-value for the test was 0.42, indicating no significant evidence of publication bias, as the P-value is greater than the commonly used threshold of 0.05.

Funnel plot of publication bias

GRADE assessment of evidence quality

The certainty of evidence for each primary outcome was assessed using the GRADE framework, which considers five key domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Based on this evaluation, the evidence was downgraded for most outcomes due to serious risk of bias in the included studies and heterogeneity in the results. The majority of the studies had issues with randomization, allocation concealment, and lack of blinding, which led to a serious risk of bias. Only 4 of the 27 studies reported appropriate allocation concealment, and most did not blind participants or assessors. This introduces significant risks to the reliability of the findings. Additionally, there was high statistical heterogeneity, particularly for the JOA score (I2 = 94%) and VAS score (I2 = 87%), indicating large variation in the treatment effects across studies. This inconsistency in results further weakened the confidence in the pooled estimates. Evidence for most outcomes was downgraded to low or very low quality. The effective rate and final follow-up were rated low due to serious risk of bias, imprecision, and potential selective reporting. The JOA score was rated very low due to serious risk of bias, very serious inconsistency, and imprecision. The VAS score was rated low due to serious risk of bias and serious inconsistency. The evidence for adverse events was rated very low due to the serious risk of bias, small sample sizes, and potential underreporting. Overall, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with better methodological designs, larger sample sizes, standardized protocols, and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the efficacy and safety of CHC for managing LDH. Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of CHC compared to Western medicine for the treatment of LDH. Our findings demonstrate that CHC is superior to Western medicine in improving clinical outcomes such as the effective rate, final follow-up, JOA score, and VAS score. Specifically, CHC was associated with a 12% higher effective rate (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.08–1.16, P < 0.00001) and a significant improvement in both the JOA (SMD = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.17–1.55, P = 0.01) and VAS scores (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.29–1.03, P = 0.0004), suggesting better pain relief and functional recovery. Further subgroup analyses provided deeper insights into the comparative effectiveness of CHC in specific populations. In patients unresponsive to NSAIDs, CHC showed superior efficacy compared to both NSAIDs and combined Western treatments (RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.10–1.38, P < 0.0001). In contrast, there was no significant difference when CHC was compared to non-NSAIDs, which include medications such as colchicine tablets, mannitol injection, and vitamin B1, all known for their anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects (P = 0.45, I2 = 35%). This suggests that CHC may be particularly advantageous in patients with poor responses to NSAIDs or those seeking an alternative to pharmacological treatment with higher side effect profiles. For patients with a disease duration longer than 18 months, CHC also showed significantly better results (RR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.08–1.20, P = 0.0001), indicating that CHC may be more effective in chronic LDH cases. Our subgroup analysis on treatment duration revealed that, while CHC and Western medicine had similar effects during the first 7 days (SMD = 0.15, 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.35, P = 0.14), CHC demonstrated progressively better outcomes at 14 days (SMD = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.20–0.68, P = 0.0003), 21 days (SMD = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.36–0.85, P < 0.00001), and 30 days (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.45–0.99, P < 0.0001). This suggests that the benefits of CHC may become more apparent over time, particularly for longer-term management of LDH. Additionally, our analysis across age groups (>30 years) showed that CHC performed consistently better than Western medicine in terms of effectiveness, further supporting its broader applicability in clinical practice.

From a clinical perspective, our findings align with the TCM principle that CHC works by promoting blood circulation, invigorating Qi, and dispelling stagnation, which may be particularly effective for patients with longstanding conditions where the therapeutic challenge is more complex [47, 48]. Mechanistically, the efficacy of CHC can be understood through both traditional and biomedical frameworks. In TCM, CHC operates by restoring the balance of Qi and blood, resolving blood stasis, and improving meridian flow, which is believed to be crucial in alleviating pain and inflammation in conditions like LDH [48, 49]. The traditional use of herbs like Angelica sinensis (Dang Gui), known for its blood-invigorating properties [50], and Salvia miltiorrhiza (Dan Shen), which enhances microcirculation and reduces inflammation [51], directly correlates with the observed improvement in both pain and functional outcomes, as seen in our study results. The positive outcomes in VAS and JOA scores support the idea that CHC’s multi-target effects may address the root causes of pain and dysfunction, rather than merely masking the symptoms. From a modern biomedical perspective, several active compounds in CHC have demonstrated significant pharmacological effects relevant to the treatment of LDH. For instance, ligustrazine from Chuanxiong (Chuan Xiong) has been shown to reduce inflammation by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokines [52], which is consistent with our findings of superior anti-inflammatory effects in the CHC group when compared to Western medicine. The superior VAS score in the CHC group could be attributed to the ability of tanshinone IIA (from Dan Shen) to modulate vascular endothelial function and reduce oxidative stress [53], both of which are crucial in the management of nerve root inflammation and edema. Additionally, harpagoside, from Harpagophytum (devil’s claw), has demonstrated analgesic properties that could explain the improved pain relief seen in CHC treatment [54], which was evident in the subgroup analysis showing its superiority over NSAIDs in patients who did not respond to traditional pain medications. The multi-target, system-level modulation characteristic of CHC provides a more holistic approach to treating LDH, in contrast to the single-target mechanism of NSAIDs, which primarily focus on inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes [55]. This comprehensive approach is likely why CHC outperforms NSAIDs and combined Western treatments, particularly for patients unresponsive to NSAIDs (RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.10–1.38, P < 0.0001) or those seeking alternatives with fewer side effects. Our results support this theory, as CHC was found to offer significant advantages in multiple aspects of LDH treatment, including pain relief and functional improvement, without the adverse effects commonly seen with NSAIDs, as highlighted in our subgroup analysis. In clinical practice, CHC offers a viable treatment option, particularly for patients who cannot tolerate conventional treatments or who require a more comprehensive, integrated approach. The superior results in the subgroup with chronic LDH cases further suggest that CHC may be particularly beneficial for long-term disease management, as evidenced by its better performance in patients with disease duration >18 months. Future research should focus on further elucidating the pharmacokinetics and bioavailability of CHC formulations, as well as conducting large-scale, long-term trials to fully understand its role in the management of LDH.

Limitations

While the results of this review are encouraging, they must be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. A key issue is the substantial heterogeneity observed in outcomes such as VAS and JOA scores (I2 > 85%), likely stemming from variations in herbal formulations, treatment durations, and comparator regimens. This heterogeneity complicates the interpretation of the results and underscores the urgent need for standardized CHC formulations and treatment protocols in future studies. Without consistency, it is unclear whether the observed benefits are attributable to the specific CHC formulation or the broader category of Chinese herbal compounds. Future studies should prioritize standardization to reduce variability and improve the reliability of results. According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence across most outcomes was rated as low to very low, primarily due to methodological shortcomings. These include high or unclear risk of bias in random sequence generation (13/27 trials), allocation concealment (only 4/27 adequately reported), and lack of blinding in over 60% of the studies. Such issues raise concerns about the internal validity of the findings, as biases in these areas can significantly distort the results. The lack of blinding introduces the risk of performance bias, especially for subjective outcomes like pain relief and functional recovery, which are prone to overestimation. Moreover, only 4 trials provided long-term follow-up data, and 3 trials reported on adverse events, which limits our ability to assess the sustained efficacy and safety of CHC. The short follow-up periods and lack of comprehensive safety data hinder a full evaluation of CHC’s long-term benefits and risks, which are critical for understanding its role in clinical practice. Additionally, all included studies were conducted in mainland China, which raises the possibility of regional biases and limits the generalizability of the results. The findings may not apply to populations outside of China, highlighting the need for more diverse, international studies to better understand the global applicability of CHC. Inconsistent randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding further complicate the interpretation of the results. Studies with unclear randomization or blinding procedures are more likely to overestimate the effects of CHC, particularly in studies that did not adequately explain or implement these procedures. This is a critical limitation that reduces the overall reliability and accuracy of the findings, especially in subjective measures where bias is more likely. Given these issues, the observed effects of CHC should be treated with caution. While some subgroup analyses suggest that CHC may outperform Western medicine, particularly in patients unresponsive to NSAIDs or with chronic disease, the methodological flaws indicate that the true effect may not be as pronounced as reported.

Recommendations for future research

To ensure the reliability of future conclusions, I recommend that future studies minimize biases by employing rigorous randomization, adequate blinding, and robust data management practices. This is especially important for studies involving subjective outcomes, where biases are most likely to occur. Additionally, multicenter RCTs with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, comprehensive safety reporting, and international representation are necessary to improve the scientific rigor of the evidence. These studies should include a diverse range of populations to enhance the generalizability of the findings and provide a clearer understanding of CHC’s effectiveness across various settings.

Conclusions

Based on the current meta-analysis of 27 randomized controlled trials involving 3133 patients, CHC demonstrated superior short-term efficacy compared to conventional Western medicine in the treatment of LDH, particularly in terms of clinical effective rate, pain relief, and functional improvement. The subgroup analyses further suggested that CHC may provide additional benefits for patients with disease duration longer than 18 months and for those receiving treatment for more than 7 days. Notably, CHC showed potential advantages across all age subgroups above 30 years. However, due to methodological limitations of the included studies and limited safety data, these findings should be interpreted with caution. More rigorously designed, large-scale, multicenter RCTs with standardized outcome reporting and long-term follow-up are warranted to confirm the efficacy and safety of CHC in the management of LDH.
