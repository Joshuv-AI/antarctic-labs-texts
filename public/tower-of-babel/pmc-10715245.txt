Microbiome Alteration in Lung Tissues of Tuberculosis Patients Revealed by Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing and Immune-Related Transcriptional Profile Identified by Transcriptome Sequencing
Authors: Hong Liu, Saiguang Ji, Yuan Fang, Xiaoli Yi, Fengsheng Wu, Fuchen Xing, Chenyan Wang, Hai Zhou, Jian Xu, Wei Sun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored alterations in the respiratory microbiome and transcriptome after Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection in tuberculosis (TB) patients. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was adopted to reveal the microbiome in lung tissues from 110 TB and 25 nontuberculous (NonTB) patients. Transcriptome sequencing was performed in TB tissues (n = 3), tissues adjacent to TB (ParaTB, n = 3), and NonTB tissues (n = 3) to analyze differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and functional pathways. The microbial β diversity (p = 0.01325) in TB patients differed from that in the NonTB group, with 17 microbial species distinctively distributed. Eighty-three co-up-regulated DEGs were identified in the TB versus NonTB and the TB versus ParaTB comparison groups, and six were associated with immune response to Mtb. These DEGs were significantly enriched in the signaling pathways such as immune response, NF-κB, and B cell receptor. Data in the lung tissue microbiome and transcriptome in TB patients offer a sufficient understanding of the pathogenesis of TB.Keywords: tuberculosis, microbiome, mNGS, transcriptional profile, transcriptome sequencing

Tuberculosis (TB) is an ancient and severe infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection. Despite concerted efforts to develop vaccines with expanding pipelines, new diagnoses, and drugs in recent decades, TB remained one of the leading cause of death from a single etiological agent before the COVID-19 pandemic.1 Mtb adapts to human hosts and settles in the lungs and respiratory tract.2 However, numerous microorganisms live in the lungs and airways of healthy individuals, and the microbial flora acts as a biological barrier that prevents the invasion of pathogens and contributes to body immune regulation. On the one hand, Mtb infection alters the composition of the microbial flora in the lung and airways.3 On the other hand, dysbiosis of human microbiota plays a vital role in the pathophysiology of TB and response to therapy, clinical outcomes, and post-treatment outcomes.4 Given this, we engaged in microbial analysis to reveal the microbiome characteristics in TB patients and elucidate the impact of Mtb infection on the microbial flora.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is emerging as a novel tool for microbial community research. It enables the discovery and characterization of unculturable microbes with the prediction of their function.5 A study performing mNGS for 92 acute respiratory failure patients revealed the pathogens and the airway microbiome, thereby identifying patients with lower respiratory tract infections.6 In the patients with acute exacerbation phenotype chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, mNGS was employed to explore the lung and gut microbiota, and it is expected that the clarified intestinal microenvironment of patients will benefit the drug development.7

Advances in transcriptome sequencing technology (RNA-seq) are broadening our vision of host responses to Mtb infection. Cai et al. performed single-cell transcriptomics of blood and proved a depletion of natural killer cell subset in TB patients, which could be relieved after anti-TB treatment.8 They confirmed that the changes in this subset could distinguish TB patients from those with latent TB infection and healthy controls. In addition, a global RNA-seq on peripheral blood neutrophils from active TB, latent TB infection, and healthy controls indicated great perturbations of gene expression, activation, and inhibition of signal pathways in active TB individuals.9 At present, transcriptome sequencing is mainly adopted in peripheral blood samples, but lung tissue is rarely involved.

In this study, we conducted mNGS to depict the microbiome landscape in patients with TB, aiming to present sufficient clinical data for microbiome science and precision medicine. We also performed transcriptome sequencing in the lung tissues of patients with TB to characterize the changes in the transcriptional profiles and immune response after Mtb infection.

Results

Based on the microbial spectrum reported by mNGS, the microbial distribution and the species diversity in TB and NonTB groups were compared, and individual species with statistical differences were explored and discussed. Microbial Diversity in TB and NonTB Patients

For 135 lung tissue samples, mNGS data information, including raw reads, clean reads, clean reads/raw reads, unmapped reads (microbial reads), % of unmapped reads (% of microbial reads), and % of human reads, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. To assess the species richness of this study area with 135 lung tissue samples, we plotted the species accumulation curve; initially, the number of species observed increased with the number of samples, but it tended to be gentle when the number of samples exceeded 70 (Figure 1A), suggesting that the current sample size was sufficient to reflect the species richness. The microbiome analysis was performed at the species level. The difference in microbiota diversity between TB and NonTB groups was evaluated by the α (within community diversity) and β (diversity between a group of samples among communities), which comprise the overall diversity in environmental communities. No significant difference was noted between the two groups in the α diversity assessed by the Chao1 index (P = 0.221), Shannon index (P = 0.123), Simpson index (P = 0.070), and inverse Simpson index (P = 0.314) (Figure 1B). However, β diversity in the TB group was significantly lower than in the NonTB group (P = 0.0003) (Figure 1C). Principal component analysis (PCA) illustrated that the samples in these two groups were scattered and extensively overlapped with each other at the species level (Figure 1D), showing no difference in the overall structure of the lung microbial composition.

Microbial Distribution in TB Differs from That in NonTB Patients

The distribution of the top 18 species in lung tissues of TB and NonTB patients is presented in Figure 2A, suggesting that Mtb occupied the highest proportion in the TB group’s lung tissues, followed by Megasphaera stantonii, Vibrio vulnificus, and Furfurilactobacillus rossiae. Dissimilarly, Staphylococcus aureus was dominant in the lung tissue of the NonTB patients, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, M. stantonii, and Ralstonia pickettii were all the dominant compositions in the NonTB patients (Figure 2A). When displaying the top 18 species individually in the 135 samples, we noted that Mtb was the predominant population in the individual samples from TB patients. Still, Staphylococcus epidermidis, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa were the main compositions in the NonTB group (Figure 2B).

We compared their relative abundances in TB and NonTB groups to explore the distribution difference in the individual species. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis revealed 17 microbial species with significant differences (P < 0.05 and LDA > 2) between the TB and NonTB groups, with Pseudomonas pharmacofabricae listed first with the highest LDA score of 6.36 (P = 0.018) (Figure 2C). The LDA score of Enterobius vermicularis was 3.74 (P = 0.031). Then, the top 20 influential taxa were identified by random forest analysis and ranked by the mean decrease accuracy of each taxon (Figure 2D).

To preliminarily assess the dispersion degree of samples in the three groups, we performed PCA by extracting the top 5000 highly variable genes after normalizing the raw expression levels of each gene in all samples. The result of PCA demonstrated no clear separation between the samples from TB and NonTB groups or from the TB and ParaTB groups (Figure 3D).

DEGs Were Found in TB Patients

We tried to find the DEGs in pairwise comparisons of TB with NonTB and TB with ParaTB groups and the distribution of the DEGs presented in the volcano maps illustrating that 799 DEGs (including 541 up-regulated and 258 down-regulated genes) were identified in the TB vs NonTB comparison group, as well as 306 DEGs (including 220 up-regulated and 86 down-regulated genes) in the TB versus ParaTB comparison group (Figure 4A). Hierarchical clustering analysis was processed on DEGs between TB vs NonTB and TB vs ParaTB. The obtained heatmap demonstrated that the gene expression profile in TB group was distinct from the other two groups (Figure 4B).

(B) DEGs between TB vs NonTB and TB vs ParaTB were displayed in a heatmap by hierarchical clustering. (C) The top 18 co-up-regulated DEGs in the TB group when compared with the NonTB and ParaTB groups. (D) Protein–protein interaction network of CXCL13, CR2, CD19, CCL19, CD79A, and TLR10.

Eighty-three DEGs up-regulated simultaneously (co-up-regulated) in the TB vs NonTB and TB vs ParaTB comparison groups and three co-down-regulated DEGs were noted (Supplementary Table 2). Most co-up-regulated DEGs were found to be related to the immune response; in detail, we displayed the top 18 immune-related co-up-regulated DEGs (ranked by the value of log2FC) in the TB vs NonTB and TB vs ParaTB comparison groups: FDCSP, CXCL13, CILP2, CR2, TNFRSF13C, CD19, IGKV2D-29, PAX5, MS4A1, CCL19, IGKV1–33, and FCRLA, CD79A, IGLL5, TNFRSF13B, POU2AF1, TLR10, and MZB1 (Figure 4C). We noted that follicular dendritic cell-secreted protein (FDCSP, log2FC = 9.19) and C–X–C motif chemokine 13 (CXCL13, log2FC = 7.4) were the top two immune-related co-up-regulated genes in the comparisons between TB vs NonTB, followed by cartilage intermediate layer protein 2 (CILP2, log2FC = 7.02) (Figure 4C). Through literature research, six immune-related co-up-regulated DEGs were involved in the immune response to Mtb, including CXCL13, CR2, CD19, CCL19, CD79A, and TLR10. A protein–protein interaction network was established, indicating that CXCL13 was more likely to interact with CCL19, also for CD79A and CD19, and CD19 and CR2 (Figure 4D).

Different Functional Pathways Were Enriched in TB Patients

We conducted a functional pathway enrichment analysis to explore further the identified DEGs’ functions. The Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification was performed to provide orthologous classification information on gene products. It demonstrated that the “post-translational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones” (O) and the “general function prediction only” (R) were the most frequent classes in the TB group when compared with the NonTB group, followed by the signal transduction mechanisms class (T) (Figure 5A). For the comparison between TB and ParaTB groups, the item involving “post-translational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones” was predominant. The results indicated more differences in COG classification between the TB vs NonTB groups than the TB vs ParaTB pair, suggesting that the function of DEGs in TB was altered essentially in TB patients.

Analyses on functional pathway enrichment in TB, NonTB, and ParaTB tissues. (A) COG classifications on DEGs demonstrated main pathways in TB vs NonTB comparison group and in the TB vs ParaTB. (B) GO analysis results of DEGs enriched in functional pathways in pairwise comparisons of TB with NonTB and TB with ParaTB groups. (C) KEGG analysis results of enriched functional pathways in pairwise comparisons of TB with NonTB and TB with ParaTB groups.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was conducted to annotate the gene product functions composed of biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular component. When compared with the NonTB group, the DEGs in TB group were enriched in response to stimulus (biological process), an integral component of membrane (cellular component), and receptor binding (molecular function) categories (Figure 5B). Similarly, the top enriched terms mainly focused on the immune response (biological process), extracellular space and extracellular region (cellular component), and receptor binding (molecular function) in the comparison between TB and ParaTB groups. In summary, the enriched GO terms primarily involved in response to stimulus, component of membrane, and receptor binding in TB patients were more likely to be related to the response to infection.

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) annotations of DEGs showed a significantly enriched NF-κB signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, and Fc epsilon RI signaling pathway in the TB group compared with the NonTB group and pathways in response to infection by multiple pathogens, including Mtb (Figure 5C). Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and Toll-like signaling pathways were enriched in TB vs ParaTB comparison sets, which were discrepant from the other comparison pair. In addition, the number of DEGs (GeneNum) enriched in the KEGG pathway in the TB vs NonTB comparison group was far more than in the TB vs ParaTB comparison group. These findings suggested that the DEGs in TB generally played a role in signaling pathways related to the immune response to pathogen infection.

Discussion

In this study, we applied mNGS and transcriptome sequencing to display the microbiome and transcriptome profiles in the lung tissues of TB patients. These results highlighted that Mtb infection primarily affected TB patients’ lung tissue microbial community structure and host transcriptional landscape. The present study broke the routine of the previous research focusing on an individual pathogen in TB, proving the significant impact of microbial community dysbiosis on disease development and gene expression change in hosts.

The dynamic balance of microecological composition and abundance is critical for the human body to keep fit. The microbe species and amount differ from diverse types of samples.10 Our data found that the microbial β diversity in the TB group was significantly lower than in the NonTB group (Figure 1C). This phenomenon may be attributed to the absolute predominance of Mtb in TB patients. A previous study reported that TB patients displayed lower α diversity in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) microbiome than healthy control participants.10 Therefore, the lung tissue microbiome features revealed in our study may be distinct from those from BALF. Our study is the first to explore the lung tissue microbiome in TB cases, which reflects the situation more truthfully, filling the data gap in the microbiome and transcriptome in lung tissues. In the microbial species with significantly different relative abundances in lung tissues between TB and NonTB patients, P. pharmacofabricae and E. vermicularis were the top two species with high LDA scores (Figure 2C). P. pharmacofabricae has been identified in laundry wastewater and can degrade a wide range of organic compounds.11 However, the correlation between P. pharmacofabricae and TB has not been reported. Co-infection of Mtb and intestinal parasites is a typical joint and public health problem, including the E. vermicularis, causing immune activation in TB patients.12 In addition, the relative abundance of Delftia tsuruhatensis was also different in TB patients when compared to that in NonTB patients. D. tsuruhatensis is an emerging pathogen in humans causing pneumonia in immunocompromised patients,13 while its impact on TB pathology has not been found yet. In the future, more efficient prediction models should be established to find biomarkers for distinguishing TB from NonTB patients. Understanding the characteristics of microbial species distribution and diversity from lung tissues may contribute to a broader viewpoint on the pathogenesis of pulmonary TB.

Transcriptome sequencing analyses in TB have mainly focused on specific immune-related cell groups in peripheral blood,14 like mononuclear cells,8 neutrophils,9 and macrophages.15 Lung tissue transcriptome sequencing in the current study found 83 co-up-regulated DEGs in the comparison groups between TB and NonTB and between TB and ParaTB groups. FDCSP is related to immune cell activation and maturation and committing to regulating immune processes,16 but its role in response to Mtb infection remains unclear. Among the 18 top immune-related co-up-regulated DEGs, six were associated with immune response in tuberculosis, including CXCL13, CR2, CD19, CCL19, CD79A, and TLR10 (Figure 4D). The recent literature revealed that the C–X–C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CXCR5)–CXCL13 axis is involved in Mtb control, and the increased plasma level of CXCL13 participates in the early protective immunity against TB.17 CR2 has been reported to reduce local inflammatory response by regulating the proinflammatory cytokines secreted by macrophages, promoting the enhancement of immune function, and facilitating the killing of Mtb.18 In addition, we observed significant changes in B-lymphocyte antigen CD19 expression in TB tissues. CD19 has been confirmed to inhibit Th17 cell activation, which may impact the clinical outcome of TB.19 CCL19 has been proven to be highly expressed in TB lung tissue and able to distinguish the TB group from other respiratory diseases.20 The CD79A gene encodes the Ig-α protein of the B cell antigenic complex and is essential for TB immunopathogenesis.21 Recent works have described novel mutations in the TLR10 gene associated with susceptibility to TB in some populations.22,23 TLR10 is a modulatory pattern-recognition receptor, mainly inhibiting TLR2-derived immune responses in TB.24 These findings suggest that CXCL13, CR2, CD19, CCL19, CD79A, and TLR10 are closely associated with the immune response to Mtb infection and are promising transcriptional markers of the course of TB.

The results of GO analysis suggested that these DEGs in the TB group were enriched in response to a stimulus or immune response, component of membrane, and receptor binding. They are all vital processes and elements in the early stages of Mtb exposure. In the first phase of encountering Mtb, innate immune cells are prone to activation, in which Mtb is detected by pattern recognition receptors.25 Toll-like receptors are one of the pattern recognition receptors contributing to the recognition of Mtb.26 Our KEGG analysis data showed that NF-κB, B cell receptor, Fc epsilon RI, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways were enriched in the TB group compared with NonTB or ParaTB groups. Thereinto, the NF-κB signaling pathway is up-regulated considerably in active TB patients and regulates immune response,9 B cells modulate IL-10 and inflammation during Mtb infection,27 Fc receptors on immune cells can influence TB’s local and systemic adaptive immune responses,28 and Toll-like receptors are the essential pattern recognition receptors detecting Mtb in the first step of initiation.25 These signaling pathways are substantially involved in the early immune reaction of encountering Mtb. In addition, the immune-related co-up-regulated DEGs are found to be closely bound up with these signaling pathways. For example, the CD79A gene encodes the Ig-α protein of the B cell antigenic complex,21 and TLR10 is a critical member of Toll-like receptors and is highly expressed in human B cells. Briefly, the DEGs participate in the immune response against Mtb by throwing themselves into these signaling pathways.

In other studies, BALF and sputum samples are commonly used in microbiome and transcriptome analyses. The present study reveals microbiome characteristics and transcriptome profile characteristics in lung tissues of TB patients, which reflect the TB situation more truthfully. These data fill the data gap of microbiome features and gene expression profiles in lung tissue, offering clues for studies of auxiliary diagnostic markers for TB patients. An overall perspective on the interactions among Mtb, the other microorganisms, and the host response deserves much attention for clinicians to enhance their cognition of TB occurrence and development.

Methods

Participants for Microbial Analyses

From January 2021 to 2023, in the cardiothoracic surgery department at The Second Hospital of Nanjing, patients with pulmonary diseases suspected of infection were enrolled, especially for tuberculosis. The eligibility criteria for TB were as follows: (1) Mtb-positive in the bacteriological examination, including sputum smear or sputum culture, mNGS, acid-fast staining, T-spot, adenosine deaminase, PCR, and interferon-gamma release assay; (2) clinical manifestations such as constitutional symptoms and imaging manifestations; (3) improvement following initiation of anti-TB therapy.

The exclusion criteria include (1) patients with severe kidney diseases, heart diseases, or other diseases in a serious condition, (2) patients complicated by HIV infection, other infectious diseases, tumors, or other chronic diseases like diabetes mellitus, and (3) patients who are taking immune modulators or have received hormonal therapy recently. A total of 110 patients with TB and 25 patients with NonTB pulmonary diseases were recruited in this study, and 135 lung tissues were collected from each. Patients diagnosed with pulmonary shadow, pleural effusion, chest wall masses, and pulmonary nodules were defined as NonTB pulmonary diseases, in whom TB was excluded.

Sample Collection and Nucleic Acid Extraction

During the surgery or lung biopsy, lung tissues were collected for acid-fast staining or other examinations. All the samples were sent for the mNGS test, and the whole process was performed by Genoxor Medical Science and Technology Inc. (Shanghai, China). After pretreatment, nucleic acid was extracted from the samples using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) or Biomarker Micro Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Biomarker Technologies, Beijing, China) following the instructions of the manufacturer. DNA extraction yield was quantified using a Quant-iT dsDNA HS Assay Kit and Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Construction of Sequencing Library and mNGS Sequencing

Fragmentation of the DNA molecules was achieved by enzymatic shearing. DNA libraries were then constructed using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA), undergoing end-repair, adapter ligation, and 5–7 PCR recycles for amplification to produce a metagenomic library with 1 μg. The quality of the libraries was analyzed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technology, USA). Metagenome shotgun sequencing was performed in a single-end 75 bp mode on the NextSeqTM 550DX sequencer using the NextSeq 500/550 High Output Kit (92 cycles). No-template-control (NTC) samples were sequenced simultaneously to monitor contamination during the experiments.

Data Processing and Bioinformatics Analysis

Raw data were first subjected to a quality control process for trimming adapter sequences and removing low-quality tails and reads. The remaining high-quality data were termed clean reads. Next, the clean reads were filtered by aligning to the human reference genome GRCh37 with the short-read alignment tool Bowtie v2.2.629 to remove the reads mapping to the human genome. The remaining nonhost sequence data are deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database, accession number PRJNA978021. The identical duplicated reads were considered technical artifacts by PCR and deduplicated through an in-house Perl script.30 The number of reads in the Kraken classification report was further estimated by the Bayesian algorithm implemented by Bracken v2.231 for producing species-level abundance estimates. An NCBI microbial genome database containing 51,543 genomes of about 27,000 species (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/) was employed to identify the sequences. According to the information from NTC samples, the microbial taxa-bearing putative contamination introduced during the experiments was removed from the final profiles. The estimates of the percentage relative abundance of each species were computed for bacteria, viruses (excluding phages), and fungi, respectively, via a normalization method called reads per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (RPKM) as described previously.32 RPKM was calculated with the formula gene reads/[the total mapped reads (millions) × genome length (kb)].

Visualization of Metagenomic Sequencing Data

Statistical analysis and visualization of metagenomic sequencing (SAV-mNGS), developed and maintained by Genoxor Medical Science and Technology Inc. (Shanghai, China), are R Shiny applications for researchers and clinicians without bioinformatics expertise to analyze and visualize clinical metagenomic sequencing data for infectious diseases. In this study, SAV-mNGS was applied for (1) global visualization of sampling effort and distribution of dominant taxa among groups or individual samples at the species, (2) statistical analysis and visualization of α and β diversity, PCA of β diversity on Bray–Curtis or UniFrac distance, and visualization, and (3) LEfSe analysis to determine the difference in the relative abundance of the microbial species between groups.

Study Design and Samples for Transcriptome Sequencing

The pulmonary TB tissues (TB, n = 3) and tissues adjacent to TB (ParaTB, n = 3) were obtained from diagnosed TB sufferers during surgery, and NonTB tissues (n = 3) were collected from NonTB pulmonary diseases. Tissues in these three groups were subjected to transcriptome sequencing performed by a third company, Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Hospital of Nanjing (no. 2023-LY-kt088), and written informed consent was obtained from all of the patients.

Transcriptome Sequencing Processing

The process of transcriptome sequencing includes total RNA extraction and quality control, mRNA enrichment, reverse transcription, end-repair and A-tailing, adapter ligation and purification, PCR amplification, library quality assessment, and sequencing. RNA was extracted and purified using TRlzol Reagent (Life Technologies, USA) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. A NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to measure the RNA concentration and purity. RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). A total of 1 μg of RNA per sample was used as input material for the library preparations. The NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) was used to construct RNA-seq libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The library quality was determined on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Sequencing was then performed using an Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencer with 150 bp paired-end reads generated. Transcriptome sequencing data for each sample are deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database under accession number PRJNA980437.

Bioinformatics Analyses for Transcriptome Sequencing Data

Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China) completed the analysis flow of the sequencing data. Raw data were first processed through in-house Perl scripts, which removed low-quality reads and reads containing adapter and ploy-N to obtain the clean reads. All of the downstream analyses were based on clean data with high quality. At the same time, the clean data Q20, Q30, GC-content, and sequence duplication levels were calculated. These clean data were then aligned to the UCSC human reference genome (hg19) using Hisat2 tools,33 and only reads with a perfect match or one mismatch were further analyzed and annotated. Gene expression (mRNA abundance) was normalized using FPKM values, and differential expression analysis of pairwise comparisons was performed using the DESeq2 algorithm. In this study, the screening criteria for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were P < 0.05 and the absolute value of log2FoldChange (FC) ≥ 2. False discovery rate (FDR) refers to the adjusted P-value used to measure the significance of the difference. The R package “factoextrap” (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=factoextra) was used for PCA. After the DEG analysis, volcano plots and heatmaps were drawn using the “ggplot2” (https://CRAN.R-project.org/web/packages/ggplot2) and the “pheatmap” (http://CRAN.Rproject.org/package=pheatmap) R packages, respectively. GO enrichment analysis of DEGs was implemented by the GOseq R packages through Wallenius noncentral hyper-geometric distribution.34 We used KOBAS software to test the statistical enrichment of differential expression genes in KEGG pathways.35 Protein–protein interaction network was constructed using the STRING database (https://www.string-db.org/).

Statistical Analysis

Participant demographics and laboratory results are reported with mean and standard deviation (M ± SD). Chi-square testing was performed to compare binary data. GraphPad 8.0.2 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc., San Diego, CA) performed all statistical analyses and visualization. A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.
