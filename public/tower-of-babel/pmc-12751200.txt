Prevalence of hepatitis B virus infection among individuals with active tuberculosis attending selected Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) clinics in Oyo State, Nigeria
Authors: Adeola D Ayanyinka, Itunuoluwa C Oyelayo, Habeeb O Abiona, Fiyinfoluwa Demilade Ojeniyi, Oluyinka O Opaleye, Olusola Ojurongbe, Olugbenga A Olowe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) remains a major public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Nigeria. In sub-Saharan Africa, the convergence of HBV and tuberculosis (TB) presents a dual epidemic, complicating disease management due to the hepatotoxic nature of anti-TB therapy. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of HBV infection and associated liver dysfunction among individuals infected with tuberculosis in Oyo State, Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from February 2024 to August 2024 among 216 individuals infected with TB attending selected DOT clinics in Oyo state, Nigeria. Participants were screened for HBV using the HBV-5 panel rapid diagnostic kit. Liver function was evaluated via serum liver enzymes using the Randox Kit. HBsAg-positive samples were further tested for HBV-DNA using nested PCR targeting the S and P gene of the HBV genome. All socio-demographic, clinical and serological data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0. Associations between variables and HBsAg positivity were assessed analyzed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression methods. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe overall prevalence of HBV (HBsAg) was 11.6% (25/216), highest in the 30–44 age groups (4.6%). Among males (n = 128), 14 (6.5%) were HBsAg-positive, while females (n = 88), 11 (5.1%) were positive. Significant associations were found with household history of hepatitis (p = 0.006), body tattoos (p = 0.043), body piercing (p = 0.035), and smoking (p = 0.041). HBV-DNA was detected in 64.0% (16/25) of HBsAg-positive individuals, despite universal HBeAg negativity. Among the HBsAg-positive cases, 48.0% had elevated AST, while ALT remained normal. Elevated conjugated bilirubin was observed in 52.0%, and total bilirubin in 4.0%.ConclusionA notable burden of active HBV infection and liver dysfunction exists among individuals infected with tuberculosis. Routine HBV screening and liver function monitoring should be integrated into TB care. Further studies on occult HBV infection, genotyping, and longitudinal liver assessments are recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12171-z.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus, Tuberculosis, Co-infection, HBV-DNA, Liver dysfunction, Nigeria

Introduction

Co-infection with tuberculosis and hepatitis B virus is an emerging clinical and public health concern, particularly in regions where both pathogens are highly endemic, such as sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Tuberculosis is ranked among the top 10 causes of death globally and the leading cause of death from a single infectious disease agent [2]. In 2022, there were 7.5 million newly diagnosed cases of tuberculosis worldwide, the largest number of cases reported in a single year since WHO began monitoring tuberculosis globally in the mid-1990s and 1.30 million recorded death cases. Nigeria ranks sixth among the 30 high TB burden countries, contributing 4.6% to the global burden and has the highest burden in Africa [2]. Middle-income nations like Nigeria have higher rates of the disease because of factors like poverty, unhealthy lifestyles, a high rate of HIV infection, inadequate early TB screening, and delayed treatment with approved anti-TB medications [3].

In Nigeria, HBV is highly endemic with an estimated 13.6% of its population infected, contributing significantly to the 75 million HBV cases across Africa [4]. About 90% of Nigerians with chronic HBV are unaware of their status, mainly due to limited resources, low awareness, and insufficient public health prioritization [5].

HBV infection can lead to various liver diseases, including acute and chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis and liver failure [6]. Reported prevalence of hepatitis B virus infection among tuberculosis cases varies considerably worldwide, ranging from 0.5% to 44% across different geographic regions, with the highest rates observed in Africa according to recent systematic reviews [7, 8]. In Nigeria, the prevalence varies considerably by location and study, with recent reports indicating rates between 8% and 15% [9–12].

HBV-TB coinfection significantly complicates the effective treatment of tuberculosis, as both infections affect liver function and share similar clinical symptoms such as fatigue and liver enlargement as reported in both local and international studies [12, 13]. First-line anti-TB drugs like isoniazid, rifampicin, and pyrazinamide are known to cause hepatotoxicity, and their effects are exacerbated in the presence of chronic HBV infection. This dual impact on the liver heightens the risk of drug-induced liver injury (DILI), manifesting in elevated liver enzymes, abnormal bilirubin levels, and, in severe instances, acute hepatic failure. Such complications often result in delayed or discontinued treatment, reduced patient compliance, and increased rates of morbidity and mortality [14].

Despite the clinical risks of HBV-TB co-infection, routine HBV screening among TB patients remains limited, and standardized management protocols are lacking. Addressing this gap is critical to improve detection, optimize care, and reduce adverse treatment outcomes. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of HBV infection and liver dysfunction among individuals with tuberculosis attending DOT clinics in Oyo state, Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study setting, design and population

This study was conducted across selected Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) clinics in various local government areas of Oyo State, Southwestern Nigeria. A cross-sectional design was adopted to evaluate consenting individuals with clinically and laboratory-confirmed tuberculosis who were receiving treatment at the participating DOT centers. Data collection was conducted over six-months, from February to August 2024. The sample size was calculated using a prevalence estimate of 14.9% for HBV among individuals infected with TB, as reported by Imoru et al. (2018). Incorporating a 10% margin for potential non-responses, a total of 216 participants were recruited. Information on socio-demographic variables, clinical history, and behavioral risk factors was obtained using structured, interviewer-administered questionnaires, with support from trained healthcare workers operating within the DOT facilities.

Blood samples collection and serological testing

Five milliters of venous blood were collected into serum-separating tubes, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for five minute and the serum was subsequently used for analysis. HBV serological markers (HBsAg, HBsAb, HBeAg, HBeAb, and HBcAb) were detected using the HBV 5-panel rapid test kit (Solid Diagnostics, China). HBsAg-positive samples were assayed for liver function markers, including ALT, AST, total and conjugated bilirubin, using Randox diagnostic reagents (Randox Laboratories Ltd., UK), following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Genomic DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from serum samples that tested positive for HBsAg using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). Protease and lysis buffer were applied to 200 µL of serum, followed by two sequential wash steps. DNA was eluted in 50 µL of elution buffer and stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Molecular detection of HBV-DNA

The presence of HBV DNA in HBsAg-positive samples was confirmed using nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting conserved regions of the S and P genes of the HBV genome. DNA amplification was performed to enhance detection sensitivity and specificity. Primers used for both PCR rounds were synthesized by Inqaba Biotechnical Industries Ltd. (Pretoria, South Africa), and the primer sequences were adopted as previously described [15]. In the first-round PCR, outer primers HBPr134 (5’-TGCTGCTATGCCTCATCTTC-3’) and HBPr135 (5’-CAGAGACAAAAGAAAATTGG-3’) were used. Each 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10x PCR buffer, 25 mM MgCl₂, 20 mM dNTPs, 0.5 µL each of the forward and reverse primers, 0.2 µL of Taq polymerase, 5 µL of DNA template, and nuclease-free water to volume. Thermal cycling was performed on an Eppendorf ™ Mastercycler X50 thermal cycler with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

A second-round (nested) PCR was conducted using 3 µL of the first-round product as template, with inner primers HBPr75 (5’-CAAGGTTATGTTGCCCGTTTGTCC-3’) and HBPr94 (5’-GGTATAAAGGGACTCACGATG-3’). The thermal cycling conditions remained the same, with the cycle number increased to 40 for enhanced sensitivity.

Positive (HBV plasmid DNA) and negative (nuclease-free water) controls were included in each run. PCR products were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide and visualized on a Gel documentation system (Azure 200, Dublin, CA). A distinct 340 bp band confirmed the presence of HBV DNA.

Statistical analysis

Data from the investigation were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 25.0. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate, with a 95% confidence interval. Binary logistic regression was performed to determine the association between selected risk factors and HBsAg positivity, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Both crude and adjusted ORs were calculated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, the prevalence of HBsAg among individuals infected with tuberculosis was 11.6%, indicating high HBV endemicity according to WHO classification (>8%) [16]. This finding is consistent with a recent report from Kwara State (10.5%) [10], but higher than rates reported in Jos (5.6%) [11] and a pooled review (9.5%) [17]. However, it is lower than the 14.9% reported in Sokoto [9].

A cross-country comparison shows that the rate observed in this study exceeds that of Ethiopia (3.4%) [18] and Sudan (9.5%) [19], but remains below the 25.6% reported in Brazil [20]. These prevalence differences may be attributed to variations in diagnostic methods, sample size, geography, and population-specific risk exposures [18].

The 30–44-year age group had the highest HBV prevalence (4.6%), echoing similar trends reported in Romania [21]. Though not statistically significant, males showed higher HBsAg positivity, consistent with national data linking male practices such as circumcision and use of shared sharp instruments to increased risk [22]. Married individuals also had higher prevalence, possibly due to unprotected sexual contact [23].

In this study, household history of hepatitis, body tattoos, piercings, and smoking were statistically significant associations with HBV positivity. Similar associations have been reported in Ethiopia, Egypt, and other settings [24–26]. Household history of hepatitis may reflect repeated exposure to infected family members and serves as a possible route for both vertical and horizontal HBV transmission, particularly in settings with shared items or inadequate infection-control practices [27]. However, the specific family member who was HBV-positive was not documented.

Body modification practices such as tattooing and piercing breach the skin barrier and are recognized routes for blood-borne pathogen transmission, particularly when non-sterile instruments or unregulated procedures are involved. Several studies and reviews have demonstrated a consistent association between these practices and HBV infection, including outbreaks and elevated seroprevalence among individuals with tattoos or piercings [24–26, 28]. Smoking may indirectly increase infection risk by impairing immune response and reducing vaccine efficacy, as evidenced by studies linking tobacco use to lower post-vaccination anti-HBs titers [29, 30].

Other exposures that did not reach statistical significance such as history of blood transfusion, prior surgery and multiple sexual partners were still observed at relatively high frequencies and remain biologically plausible routes of HBV acquisition. Blood transfusion and invasive procedures continue to pose risks in regions where screening or infection-control measures are suboptimal [31], while sexual contact remains a major source of adult HBV transmission globally [32]. These frequent but non-significant exposures warrant consideration as potential contributors to community transmission. These findings highlight the importance of maintaining sterile and safe practices and strengthening public health education to reduce HBV transmission risks.

The presence of HBsAb in 9.3% of HBsAg-negative individuals indicates prior vaccination or natural immunity. None of the participants tested positive for HBeAg, suggesting low viral replication [33]. HBeAb positivity (11.1%) implies reduced infectivity. HBcAb was detected in 16.7%, which indicates that a significant proportion of the participants had been exposed to HBV at some point, it’s presence in HBsAg-negative individuals may indicate a resolved HBV infection or occult HBV infection (OBI) where the virus persists at low levels despite the absence of detectable HBsAg [34].

Vaccination coverage in this study was notably low (1.9%), which may contribute to the continued transmission of HBV among individuals with tuberculosis. This rate is substantially lower than the 17.4% coverage previously reported in Northern Nigeria [35] and may reflect regional disparities in awareness or access to vaccination services [36]. Although the exact timing of vaccination could not be ascertained, the few vaccinated participants reported receiving the adult catch-up series (a full three-dose schedule) rather than the infant birth-dose schedule. The limited uptake underscores the need to strengthen HBV vaccination coverage and awareness in this population, particularly through targeted catch-up programs for adults and high-risk groups, in alignment with current WHO and national immunization recommendations [37]. Furthermore, novel vaccine technologies particularly circular RNAs (circRNAs) platforms are being explored and could provide additional options for HBV prevention in the future. CircRNAs vaccines form a closed-loop structure, making them naturally more stable and less susceptible to enzymatic breakdown than conventional linear mRNA vaccines [38]. This improved stability can support prolonged antigen expression, potentially reduce the number of doses required, and ease handling and storage demands, including reduced dependence on strict cold-chain systems [39]. Although circRNAs-based vaccines are still in early development, their potential benefits make them a promising option for enhancing HBV immunization, especially in regions where logistical barriers limit vaccine delivery [40].

HBV DNA was detected in 64% of HBsAg-positive participants, confirming active replication. This finding underscores the risk of liver injury in co-infected individuals undergoing anti-TB therapy [41, 42]. The remaining 36% were DNA-negative, likely representing inactive carriers or occult infections [43, 44].

HBsAg remains the principal marker for determining current HBV infection status, while HBV DNA quantification provides a more direct measure of viral replication and infectivity. The inclusion of anti-HBc aids in distinguishing previous exposure from active infection, whereas HBeAg and anti-HBe profiles serve as indicators of viral replication phase and immune control [45]. In this study, the combination of serological markers and molecular detection improved diagnostic accuracy and reduced the likelihood of misclassification. This integrated diagnostic approach aligns with current global recommendations for accurate characterization of HBV infection status among co-infected populations [46].

Elevated AST levels were observed in 48% of HBsAg-positive cases, while ALT levels remained within the normal range across all cases. Mild increases in conjugated (52%) and total bilirubin (4%) were also observed but were not statistically significant. The predominance of AST elevation with normal ALT is atypical for viral hepatitis, where ALT usually predominates [47]. The pattern observed in this study may indicate mild hepatocellular stress, extra-hepatic enzyme contribution or transient drug-related effects [48–50]. The standard treatment for newly diagnosed TB cases consists of a six-month regimen involving four medications: isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide, of which isoniazid, rifampicin, and pyrazinamide are known to be hepatotoxic and may cause drug-induced liver injury (DILI) [51]. In this study, all participants had received anti-TB therapy for at least two months before the screening exercise, which may have contributed to the observed enzyme elevations. However, other potential causes such as underlying HBV infection, metabolic liver changes, alcohol intake, or metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) cannot be excluded without further evaluation, which would require serial liver tests, CK, GGT/ALP, HBV viral load assessment and imaging or histology where indicated [52, 53]. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive evaluation, including viral load testing, lifestyle assessment and liver biopsy, in order to ascertain the causes of hepatic abnormalities in TB cases with HBV co-infection.

Severe complications may occur in individuals on anti-TB therapy with chronic HBV co-infection, including liver injury, cirrhosis, hepatic failure, poor treatment outcomes and increased mortality [13, 54]. The hepatotoxic effects of anti-TB drugs can further exacerbate underlying liver disease, resulting in treatment interruption or hepatic decompensation. Furthermore, HBV/TB co-infection may aggravate disease progression, trigger reactivation of latent infections, and increase the risk of complications during immunologically sensitive conditions such as pregnancy [55]. These risks highlight the need for routine HBV screening and vigilant hepatic monitoring during TB management.

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations that is noteworthy for discussion. The sample size was relatively limited, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect weaker associations between HBV infection and potential risk factors. The timing and completion of HBV vaccination could not be verified, although all self-reported vaccinated individuals showed serological patterns consistent with vaccine-induced immunity. Assessment of hepatic function was limited to serum enzyme and bilirubin measurements without imaging or histological confirmation. Consequently, the observed hepatic alterations could not be conclusively attributed to anti-tuberculosis therapy alone, as other underlying or coexisting factors may have contributed to liver dysfunction. These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the study findings.

Conclusion

This study underscores the clinical and public health importance of HBV–TB co-infection in Nigeria. The detection of active HBV replication among individuals with tuberculosis highlights a potential risk for hepatic complications during anti-TB therapy. Our findings emphasize the need for integrated HBV screening, improved vaccination coverage and closer monitoring of liver function within TB management programs to enhance treatment safety and mitigate the burden of co-infection in this population.
