Buprenorphine Products for the Treatment of Pain: Relative Risk of Abuse and Related Clinical Outcomes Compared to Full μ-Opioid Agonist Medications
Authors: Jody L. Green, Taryn Dailey-Govoni, Suzanne K. Vosburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe objective of this study was to utilize real-world data from National Poison Data System (NPDS) to evaluate the relative risk of intentional use and subsequent outcomes following exposures to buprenorphine buccal film (BBF) compared to immediate-release (IR) and extended-release (ER) full agonist opioid (FAO) formulations, and buprenorphine transdermal patch (BTP).MethodsA cross-sectional study design compared exposures that involved BBF to those that involved IR FAO, ER FAO, and BTP using real-world data from NPDS. Data included individuals age 18 years and older involved in exposures managed by US poison centers from 2020 to 2023. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize study groups, exposures, and outcomes. Relative risk was calculated for exposure reasons and clinical outcomes using BBF as reference group.ResultsDataset included 276 BBF, 43,322 IR FAO, 2453 ER FAO, and 134 BTP exposures. Compared to BBF, significantly higher risks were found for both IR FAO and ER FAO for intentional abuse, intentional suspected suicide, significant medical outcome, hospital admission, and treated/evaluated and released level of care.ConclusionFindings from this study suggest that, compared to FAO, exposures to BBF managed by US poison centers were less likely to involve intentional abuse or suspected suicide, have a decreased risk of resulting in a life-threatening effect or death, and have a lower likelihood of subsequent hospital admission and emergency department visits. Along with published guidelines and medication labels (including boxed warnings), relative risks of intentional exposures and associated clinical outcomes should be considered when determining opioid therapy for pain management.

Introduction

Buprenorphine has been approved in the United States (US) for the treatment of pain as an intravenous/intramuscular injection (Buprenex®, Indivior Pharmaceuticals, Inc.) since 1982, as a transdermal patch (Butrans®, Purdue Pharma LP and generic manufacturers) since 2010, and as a buccal film (Belbuca®, Collegium Pharmaceutical, Inc.) since 2015. The buprenorphine transdermal patch and the buprenorphine buccal film are partial μ-opioid agonists approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the management of severe and persistent pain that requires an extended treatment period with a daily opioid analgesic and for which alternative treatment options are inadequate [1, 2]. Several published studies have provided supportive evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of buprenorphine for the treatment of pain [3–10]. However, like all prescription opioid medications, the buprenorphine label includes a boxed warning for risk of addiction, abuse, and misuse which can lead to overdose and death [1, 2].

As efforts continue to curb prescription opioid abuse, the use of buprenorphine for analgesia is on the rise [11] and has been included in recent pain management guidelines. The 2022 updated Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Guideline for Prescribing Opioids for Chronic Pain included a recommendation to consider buprenorphine for tapering patients who have continued to use high doses of opioids without seeing benefits, who have been unable to taper, and who do not meet the criteria for opioid use disorder (OUD) [12]. Another 2022 guideline from the US Department of Veterans Affairs and US Department of Defense on the use of opioids for chronic pain recommended consideration of buprenorphine instead of full agonist opioids (FAO) for patients prescribed daily opioids, due to the lower risk for overdose and misuse [13]. Additionally, the American Society of Interventional Pain Physicians published updated opioid guidelines in 2023, recommending that prescribers consider buprenorphine as a viable alternative to FAOs in moderate to severe chronic pain therapy [14]. These recommendations are supported by the pharmacological profile of buprenorphine, resulting in potent analgesia, a ceiling effect on respiratory depression and euphoria, and reduced likelihood of other adverse events commonly observed with conventional opioid use [15–18]. Patient tolerability, lower risk of constipation, and reduced risk of respiratory depression have been noted as advantages for buprenorphine compared to most other opioid class medications used in the treatment of pain [15–18].

Considering buprenorphine’s partial agonist mechanism of action, it would be expected to have a lower abuse risk profile compared to a FAO. To date, only two epidemiological studies have directly compared post-market data on abuse/misuse and associated clinical outcomes between buprenorphine products for the treatment of pain and FAO medications [19, 20]. Both published studies were conducted prior to the 2015 approval of buprenorphine buccal film, which is the most recent buprenorphine product approved for the treatment of pain. In one study, quarterly rates from 2011 through 2013 of abuse and diversion of the buprenorphine transdermal patch were compared with other buprenorphine products (sublingual tablets, sublingual film), fentanyl patches, extended-release (ER) opioid tablets/capsules, and ER tramadol using data from the Researched Abuse, Diversion and Addiction-Related Surveillance (RADARS) System Poison Center, Drug Diversion, Treatment Programs Combined (Opioid Treatment and Survey of Key Informants’ Patients programs), and College Survey Programs [19]. The buprenorphine transdermal patch was abused and diverted at lower rates than other opioid groups in all data sources using either population-adjusted rates or prescription dispensed-adjusted rates. The second study was a retrospective 24-month cohort study using National Poison Data System (NPDS) data from July 2012 through June 2014 to compare rates of intentional abuse, suspected suicidal intent, and fatalities between buprenorphine transdermal patch, ER morphine, ER oxycodone, fentanyl patch, ER oxymorphone, and methadone tablets/capsules [20]. Absolute numbers and prescription-adjusted rates of intentional abuse and suspected suicidal intent with the buprenorphine transdermal patch were significantly lower (p < 0.0001) than all other ER opioid analgesics studied. These studies offer real-world evidence supportive of lower risk of abuse with buprenorphine compared to FAOs; however, they do not include the buprenorphine buccal film and use data that are now over 10 years old, and hence may not represent the current US landscape of intentional abuse and misuse of opioid pain medications.

The objective of this study was to utilize real-world data from the NPDS to evaluate the relative risk of intentional use (abuse, misuse, suspected suicide), medical outcomes, and level of healthcare utilized associated with exposures to buprenorphine buccal film compared to immediate-release (IR) FAO formulations, ER FAO formulations, and buprenorphine transdermal patch.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional study of individuals aged 12 years and older managed by US poison centers from 1 January 2020 through 31 December 2023. Study groups included (1) buprenorphine buccal film (BBF), (2) IR FAO opioids (solid oral dosage forms of brand and generic IR formulations of oxycodone, hydrocodone, oxymorphone, hydromorphone, and tapentadol), and (3) ER FAO opioids (solid oral dosage forms of brand and generic ER formulations of oxycodone, hydrocodone, oxymorphone). The BTP group served as a control, and the findings were expected to be similar to BBF considering they contain the same active ingredient (buprenorphine) and the focus of this study was on the potential differences for buprenorphine as a partial agonist. The formulations included in the ER FAO study group are the most frequently prescribed ER opioids in the US except for morphine, which was not included due to limitations within the NPDS dataset and because inclusion would have resulted in significant underreporting. Codeine was not included, as there is not an ER formulation commonly used in the USA. Methadone was excluded because it is primarily used to treat substance use disorder, and our study focused on opioids indicated for pain; furthermore, distinction between ER and IR formulations is not well documented in NPDS. This study involved secondary research of existing de-identified data and is considered non-human subject research, hence exempt from institutional review board (IRB) review. The authors had an agreement with America’s Poison Centers to analyze and publish the aggregate data.

National Poison Data System (NPDS)

NPDS is maintained by America’s Poison Centers (APC) and is the data repository for case management records for the 55 regional poison centers in the United States. The primary role of a poison center is to provide medical management to the public and healthcare providers (24 h a day/7 days a week/365 days a year) free of charge. This service is provided nationwide through a toll-free number which connects the caller to the regional poison center that serves their area. Each poison center uses a standardized electronic case record and submits de-identified case data to NPDS after providing necessary poison exposure management and information services to callers [21].

Calls to poison centers are managed by healthcare professionals who have received specialized toxicology training [22]. Poison center case management includes managing the initial contact as well as follow-up calls to monitor case progress and medical outcome, with all data recorded in the unique case record. Case records consist of predefined categorical, standardized fields which are coded according to the NPDS Coding Users’ Manual. All field definitions are uniform across all regional poison centers for data upload in NPDS. Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe patient demographics (age, gender), exposure characteristics (exposure reason, number of substances reported), and patient outcomes (medical outcome and level of healthcare received). The frequency of health outcomes between two different exposure groups can be compared by calculating a risk ratio, also referred to as relative risk. A risk ratio expresses how many times more probable a specific outcome is, given a certain exposure, compared to a reference exposure, and is calculated by dividing the proportion of the event occurring in the exposed group by the proportion of the event occurring in the reference group. A risk ratio of 1.0 indicates no difference in the risk of the outcome between the two groups. A risk ratio greater than 1.0 indicates an increased risk of the outcome in the exposed group relative to the reference group, and a risk ratio less than 1.0 indicates a decreased risk of the outcome in the exposed group relative to the reference group. Statistical significance is reached if the confidence interval (CI) does not include 1.0. Relative risk of intentional exposures (abuse, misuse, and suspected suicide), significant medical outcome (life-threatening effect or death), and level of healthcare received (hospital admission and treated/evaluated and released) were calculated using BBF as the reference group. All analyses were performed in SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.3 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

The proportion of intentional exposures was lowest among the buprenorphine groups (BTP 27.2%; BBF 23.1%) and highest among the FAO groups (IR FAO 63.5%; ER FAO 57.3%) (Table 2). Compared to BBF (4.0%) and BTP (3.7%), the proportion of intentional abuse was more than twice as high in the IR FAO group (9.9%) and more than four times as high in the ER FAO group (16.7%). Intentional misuse was similar across the study groups. The FAO groups had the highest proportion of intentional suspected suicide exposures (IR FAO 43.6%; ER FAO 30.2%), while the BTP and BBF groups were 9.0% and 12.3%, respectively. The most reported exposure reason for the BBF group was unintentional (54.3%), including 45.7% of exposures coded as therapeutic errors. Adverse reactions were substantially more likely to be reported following BTP exposures (33.6%) than for BBF (15.2%), IR FAO (4.3%), and ER FAO (3.5%). IR FAO and ER FAO groups included higher proportions of exposures that involved multiple substances (56.3% and 51.0%, respectively) than the BBF group (30.4%) and the BTP group (30.6%).

Major effect (life-threatening event) was reported in 3.3% of BBF cases, 9.0% of BTP cases, 12.2% of IR FAO cases, and 17.4% of ER FAO cases (Table 3). No deaths were reported for BBF exposures; however, there was one death following BTP exposure, nine deaths following ER FAO exposure, and 230 deaths following IR FAO exposure. Discussion

Of cases managed by US poison centers, buprenorphine products indicated for the treatment of pain are significantly less likely to involve intentional abuse and suspected suicide, or to result in significant medical outcomes, hospitalization, or emergency department visits (treated, evaluated, and released) than exposures that involved FAO, both IR and ER formulations. The most significant differences were found between BBF and ER FAO study groups, with exposures involving ER FAO being over four times as likely to be due to intentional abuse, 2.5 times as likely to be suspected suicide, 5.4 times as likely to result in a significant clinical medical outcome (life-threatening event or death), nearly twice as likely to result in hospital admission, and 1.6 times as likely to result in emergency department visit. There were no significant differences for intentional misuse (exposure resulting from improper or incorrect use that is not motivated by psychotropic effects), suggesting the intentionality of FAO exposures is motivated by their euphoric effects, which for buprenorphine are limited by its pharmacological profile. Differences in sex and age between study groups may suggest a differential bias in product availability of preference and should be further explored.

The proportion of exposures deemed adverse reactions were twice as likely to be reported following BTP exposures (33.6%) compared to BBF (15.2%), and almost 10 times as likely compared to IR FAO (4.3%) and ER FAO (3.5%). It is unknown whether these adverse reactions were related to the drug delivery systems (transdermal patch and buccal film versus oral tablets) or the active ingredient, highlighting the need for additional studies to better understand this finding.

This evaluation of relative risks based on real-world data supports current pain treatment guidelines from CDC [12], US Department of Veterans Affairs and US Department of Defense [13], and American Society for Interventional Pain Physicians [14], which recommend consideration of buprenorphine products for the treatment of pain based upon efficacy as well as to minimize risks of abuse and associated clinical outcomes associated with FAO therapies. Underlying evidence pertaining to these guidelines was well summarized in a published review which concluded that buprenorphine has the ability to induce analgesia at escalating doses, while exhibiting a plateaued effect on respiratory depression, euphoria, gastrointestinal motility, and addictive potential [18].

This study also supports previous research that demonstrated lower abuse potential with BTP compared to FAO therapies [19, 20] but was published prior to the availability of BBF. This study included real-world experience with BBF since its FDA approval in 2015 and, as expected, the profiles were similar for BBF and BTP, with one exception—medical outcome. Exposures that involved BTP were three times as likely to result in a significant medical outcome (life-threatening event or death) as exposures that involved BBF. This study was unable to determine the reason for this difference, but possible explanations could be the difference in the delivery systems (transdermal patch versus buccal film), dosage strengths contained in the formulations, and route of administration.

Limitations

This study is limited in that NPDS data are self-reported although recorded and vetted by a healthcare professional. NPDS data include only what is reported to poison centers and do not capture all exposures, hence the evaluation of relative risk rather than absolute rates. It is unknown where the individuals procured the medications used in these exposures; they may have had their own prescription or obtained the medication from an illicit source. Therefore, the study is not representative of patients receiving a prescription but rather those who have had first-hand experience with the medication that resulted in a need to contact a US poison center for medical advice and management. NPDS data are also limited in that the distinction between ER and IR morphine is often not documented; hence morphine was not included in this study.

Conclusions

Opioid medications for the treatment of pain provide patient benefit with risks best mitigated by prescribing formulations associated with the lowest amount of risk for intentional abuse and serious clinical outcomes. The findings from this novel study suggest that, compared to FAO, exposures to BBF managed by US poison centers are less likely to involve intentional abuse or suspected suicide, have a decreased risk of resulting in a life-threatening effect or death, and have a lower likelihood of subsequent hospital admission and emergency department visits. Along with published guidelines and medication labels (including boxed warnings), the relative risks of intentional exposures and associated clinical outcomes should be considered when determining opioid therapy regimens for pain management.
