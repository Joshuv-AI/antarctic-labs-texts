Invariant Differential Operators on the Heisenberg Group
M.E. Egwe

Abstract
In this paper, we elucidate certain properties of the ( 2 ​ n + 1 ) (2n+1) -dimensional Heisenberg group, and establish some theorems on the invariant differential operators on the group.

Preliminaries

The Heisenberg group (of order
n
),
I\!\!H_{n}
is a
noncommutative nilpotent Lie group whose underlying manifold is
I\!\!\!C^{n}\times\mathbb{I\!\!R}

with coordinates
(z,t)=(z_{1},z_{2},...,z_{n},t)
and group law given by

(z,t)(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(z+z^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+2\Im m\;z.z^{\prime})\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;z.z^{\prime}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}\bar{z}_{j}\;\;\;\;z\in I\!\!\!C^{n},\;\;t\in\mathbb{I\!\!R}.

Setting
z_{j}=x_{j}+y_{j}
, then
(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n},y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n},t)
forms a real coordinate
system for
I\!\!H_{n}
.
In this coordinate system, we define the following vector fields:

X_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+2y_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\;\;Y_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}-2x_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\;\;T=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}.

It is clear from
[
13
]
that
\{X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n},Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{n},T\}
is a basis for the left invariant vector fields on
I\!\!H_{n}.
These vector fields span the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{n}
of
I\!\!H_{n}
and
the following commutation relations hold:

[Y_{j},X_{k}]=4\delta_{jk}T,\;\;\;[Y_{j},Y_{k}]=[X_{j},X_{k}]=[X_{j},T]=[Y_{j},T]=0.

Similarly, we obtain the complex vector fields by setting

\left.\begin{array}[]{lr}Z_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}-iY_{j})=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}+i\bar{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\\
\\
\bar{Z}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}+iY_{j})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_{j}}-iz\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\end{array}\right\}.

[formula]

In the complex coordinate, we also have the commutation relations

[Z_{j},\bar{Z}_{k}]=-2\delta_{jk}T,\;\;[Z_{j},Z_{j}]=[\bar{Z}_{k},\bar{Z}_{k}]=[Z_{j},T]=[\bar{Z},T]=0.

The Haar measure on
I\!\!H_{n}
is the Lebesgue measure

dzd\bar{z}dt
on
I\!\!\!C^{n}\times\mathbb{I\!\!R}

[
14
]
. In particular, for
n=1
, we obtain the
3
-dimensional Heisenberg group
I\!\!H_{1}\cong\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{3}
(since
I\!\!\!C^{n}\cong\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{2n})
. Hence
I\!\!H_{n}
may also be referred to as
(2n+1)
-dimensional Heisenberg group.

For a real non-zero
\lambda
, let
\cal H^{\lambda}

be the Hilbert space of entire functions
F
on
I\!\!\!C^{r}
such that

\int_{I\!\!\!C^{r}}e^{-2|\lambda||z|^{2}}|F(z)|^{2}dz=|\lambda|^{-r}\|F\|^{2}_{\cal H^{\lambda}}

is finite. The monomials
z^{n}=z_{1}^{n_{1}}...z_{r}^{n_{r}},(n_{j}\in I\!\!\!N,
and
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots n_{r}
) are orthogonal
in
\cal H^{\lambda}
and the functions

\phi_{n}^{\lambda}(z)=(n!)^{-1/2}(\frac{2}{\pi})^{r/2}(2|\lambda|z)^{n}\;\;\;(n!=n_{1}!,...,n_{r}!)

form an orthonormal basis for
\cal H^{\lambda}
.

The group
I\!\!H_{n}
acts on
\cal H^{\lambda}
for positive
\lambda
by

U_{(z,t)}^{\lambda}F(w)=e^{-i\lambda t+\lambda(2{\langle w,z\rangle-|z|^{2}})}F(w-z).

and for negative
\lambda
by

U_{(z,t)}^{\lambda}F(w)=e^{-i\lambda t-\lambda(2{\langle w,\bar{z}\rangle-|z|^{2}})}F(w-\bar{z}).

For
\lambda=0
we get the one dimensional representations

\chi_{w}(z,t)=e^{-iRe\langle w,z\rangle}

These are the irreducible unitary representations of
I\!\!H_{n}
up to equivalence.

One significant structure that accompanies the Heisenberg group is the family of dilations

\delta_{\pm\lambda}(z,t)=(\pm\lambda z,\pm\lambda^{2}t),\;\;\lambda>0

This family is an automorphism of
I\!\!H_{n}.
Now, if
\sigma:I\!\!\!C\rightarrow I\!\!\!C
is an automorphism, there exists an induced automorphism,
\widetilde{\sigma}\in Aut(I\!\!H_{n}),
such that

\widetilde{\sigma}(z,t)=(\sigma z,t).

For simplicity, assume that
\widetilde{\sigma}
and
\sigma
coincide. Thus we may simply assume that if
\sigma\in Aut(I\!\!H_{n}),
we have
\sigma(z,t)=(\sigma z,t).

Differential Operators

Definition 2.1
.

A linear partial differential operator with smooth coefficient (of order m) is an operator of the form

Lf=\sum_{|\alpha|=m}a_{\alpha}\partial^{\alpha}f,\;\;\;a_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}).

If
a_{\alpha}^{\prime}s
are constants, we call
L
a constant - coefficient operator.

Next, let
G
be a real or complex Lie group and
K=\mathbb{I\!\!R}
or
I\!\!\!C
. Let
C^{\infty}(G;K)
denote the K-valued
C^{\infty}
-functions on
G
. A left invariant differential operator on
G
is a linear map

D:C^{\infty}(G;K)\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(G;K),

so that

(i)

if
g\in G
, then
D\circ L_{g}=L_{g}\circ D,\;\;(L_{g}f(x))=f(g^{-1}x),\;\;f\in C^{\infty}(G;K);

(ii)

if
g\in G
, then there is a neighbourhood
U
of
g
and a system of coordinates
\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
(holomorphic if
K=I\!\!\!C)
so that

Df|_{{}_{U}}=\sum_{|i|=m}a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}\frac{\partial^{i_{1}+\dots+i_{n}}}{\partial x^{i_{1}}_{1}+\cdots+\partial x^{i_{n}}_{n}}f,

f\in C^{\infty}(G;K)
, where
a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}\in C^{\infty}(U;K)
and depends only on
D
and
U
.

An operator that occurs as an analogue (for the Heisenberg group) of the Laplacian

\Delta=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x_{j}}}
on
\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}
is denoted by
{\cal L}_{\alpha}

where
\alpha
is a parameter and defined by

{\cal L}_{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Z_{j}\bar{Z}_{j}+\bar{Z}_{j}Z_{j})+i\alpha T,

[formula]

where

{Z}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}+Y_{j})\;\mbox{and}\;\bar{Z}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{j}-Y_{j}),

so that
{\cal L}_{\alpha}
can be written as

{\cal L}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{4}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}^{2}+Y_{j}^{2})+i\alpha T.

{\cal L}_{\alpha}
is called the
sublaplacian.

{\cal L}_{\alpha}
satisfies symmetry properties analogous to those of
\Delta
on
\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}.
It is left-invariant on
I\!\!H_{n},
of degree
2
with respect to the dilation automorphism of
I\!\!H_{n}
and is invariant under unitary rotations(

A differential operator
L
on
\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}
is said to be locally solvable at
x\in\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}
if for every
f\in I\!\!\!C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}),
there is a function (or a distribution)
u
and a neighbouhood
U

x
such that
Lu=f
holds (in the sense of distribution) on U. Not all partial differential operators are solvable. For example, in
[

{\cal L}_{\alpha},\;\;\alpha=0
) of the form

\displaystyle\Delta_{I\!\!H_{n}}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{j=1}X_{j}\circ X_{j}+Y_{j}\circ Y_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{j=1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}_{j}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}_{j}}+4y_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}\partial t}-4x_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{j}\partial t}+4(x^{2}_{j}+y^{2}_{j})\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\hskip 36.135pt(3.2)

can be expressed in the factorized form

AA^{\dagger}

where

A=\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+(2y-2ix)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)\mbox{and}\;A^{{\dagger}}=\left(\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+(2y+2ix)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right).

[formula]

Consequently, it was shown that this operator is nowhere solvable and a trivial group-invariant solution was constructed on the group.

In this paper, we shall give the necessary and sufficient condition for local solvability of the factors in (3.3).

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{I\!\!R}\backslash\{0\}=\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{*}
. Consider the representations
\pi_{\lambda}
of
I\!\!H_{n}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n})

[\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,t)\phi](u)=e^{i\lambda(t+(u+\frac{x}{2})y)}\phi(u+x),\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}).

If
f
is an integrable function on
I\!\!H_{n}
, then the integrated representation is given by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}[\pi_{\lambda}(f)\phi](u)&=&\displaystyle\int_{{}_{I\!\!H_{n}}}f(x,y,t)e^{i\lambda(t+(u+\frac{x}{2})y)}\phi(u+x)dxdydt\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{{}_{\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}}}\bar{{\cal F}}_{y,t}f(x,\lambda(u+\frac{x}{2}),\lambda)\phi(x+u)dx\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{{}_{\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}}}\bar{{\cal F}}_{y,t}f(v-u,\lambda(v+u)/2,\lambda)\phi(u)du\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{{}_{\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}}}K^{\lambda}_{f}(v,u)\phi(u)du;\end{array}

here
\bar{{\cal F}}_{y,t}f
denotes the partial Euclidean Fourier co-transform in the indicated variables.

Now recall that the irreducible unitary representations of
I\!\!H_{n}
are given by:

(i)

Finite-dimensional:
V=I\!\!\!C
, and the representations are parameterized by
(a,b)\in\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{2n}
with

\pi_{(a,b)}(X_{j})=ia_{j},\;\;\pi_{(a,b)}(Y_{j})=ib_{j},\;\mbox{and}\;\pi(T)=0.

(ii)

Infinite-dimensional:
V=L^{2}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n})
, and the representations are parameterized by
\lambda\in\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{*}
with

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\pi_{\lambda}(X_{j})&=&\displaystyle|\lambda|^{1/2}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}},\pi_{\lambda}(Y_{j})=i|\lambda|^{1/2}(sgn\;\lambda)x_{j},\;\;\mbox{and}\;\pi_{\lambda}(T)=i\lambda.\end{array}

[formula]

Here,
X,Y\mbox{and}\;\;T
are vector fields and
j=1,2,\cdots,n.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\pi_{\lambda}
be a representation of
{\cal U}(\mathfrak{H}_{n})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n}),

and let
P
be a homogeneous left invariant differential operator on the Heisenberg group
I\!\!H_{n}
. Then
P
is locally solvable if
\pi_{\lambda}(P)
has a bounded left inverse for every infinite dimensional irreducible unitary representation
\pi
of
I\!\!H_{n}
.

Proof:
It suffices to prove that there exists
N>0
such that for every
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(I\!\!H_{n})

Pu=T^{N}_{v},

[formula]

has a solution
u\in C^{\infty}(I\!\!H_{n}).

Since
T=\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
is in suitable coordinates, one may obtain
\nu_{1}\in C^{\infty}(I\!\!H_{n})
satisfying

T^{N}_{\nu_{1}}=f

by integrating
f\;N
times in
t
. Now if
U\subset C^{\infty}(I\!\!H_{n})
has compact closure, choose
\nu\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I\!\!H_{n})
such that
\nu\equiv\nu_{1}
on
U
. Then if
u\in C^{\infty}(I\!\!H_{n})
satisfies (3.7),

Pu=T^{N}_{\nu_{1}}=f\in U.

We now prove the theorem by constructing a solution of (3.8), using the Plancherel theorem for
I\!\!H_{n}
. If
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I\!\!H_{n})
, and
\pi_{\lambda}
is an infinite dimensional irreducible unitary representation of
I\!\!H_{n}
, then the operator

\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)=\int_{I\!\!H_{n}}\varphi(x)\pi_{\lambda}(x)dx

is of trace class. In particular, if
\{e_{i}\}
is any orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{n})
, then

tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi))=\sum_{i}(\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)e_{i},e_{i})<\infty.

Now suppose
P
is homogeneous of degree
\alpha>0
. Then

\pi_{\lambda}(P)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}|\lambda|^{\alpha/2}\pi_{\lambda}(P_{*}),\;\;\lambda>0\\
\\
|\lambda|^{\alpha/2}{\pi_{\lambda}}^{-1}(P),\;\;\lambda<0\end{array}\right.

[formula]

by (3.8).
The hypothesis of the theorem is then that
\pi_{\lambda}(P)
and
{\pi_{\lambda}}^{-1}(P)
have bounded inverses, to be denoted
B_{1}
and
B_{-1}
. Following [102], we first reduce to the case where
B_{1}
and
B_{-1}
are of trace class. This is accomplished by replacing
P
by
PQ
, where

Q=(\displaystyle\sum_{j}X^{2}_{j}+Y^{2}_{j})^{N^{\prime}}

for large
N^{\prime}
. Indeed from [102, Lemma 3.3], we have that
\pi_{1}(Q)^{-1}
is of trace class. Since the product of a bounded operator and an operator of trace class is again of trace class, the inverse of
\pi_{1}(PQ)=\pi_{1}(P)\pi_{1}(Q)
is of trace class.
PQ
again satisfies the hypothesis of the theorem and the local solvability of
PQ
implies that of
P
. Replacing
P
by
PQ
if necessary, we may assume that
B_{1}
and
B_{-1}
are of trace class. To solve (3.8), let
u
be the linear mapping

u:C^{\infty}_{0}(I\!\!H_{n})\longrightarrow I\!\!\!C

defined by

\varphi\longrightarrow u(\varphi)=\int_{{}_{\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{*}}}(i\lambda)^{N}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi))(\pi_{\lambda}(P))^{-1})|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

[formula]

for some large
N
, where
\tilde{\nu}(x)=\nu(x^{-1})
. We shall first show that
u
is an
L^{2}-
function by proving

|u(\varphi)|\leq C\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.

[formula]

By (3.9), the absolute value of the right-hand side of (3.10) is bounded by

\displaystyle|\int_{\lambda>0}|\lambda|^{N-\alpha/2}|(tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{1}))||\lambda|^{n}d\lambda|

+|\int_{\lambda<0}|\lambda|^{N-\alpha/2}|tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{-1})||\lambda|^{n}|d\lambda|.

[formula]

If
\alpha>0
is arbitrary, then

\displaystyle|\int_{\lambda>0}|\lambda|^{N-\alpha/2}|(tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{1}))||\lambda|^{n}d\lambda|

\leq\int_{0<\lambda<\alpha}|\lambda|^{N-\alpha/2}|tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{1}))||\lambda|^{n}|d\lambda|

+|\int_{\alpha\leq\lambda<\infty}|\lambda|^{N-\alpha/2}|tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{1}))|\lambda|^{n}|d\lambda|.

[formula]

The generalized Schwartz inequality for arbitrary operators
C,D
of trace class states that

|tr(CD)|\leq(tr(CC^{*}))^{\frac{1}{2}}(tr(DD^{*}))^{\frac{1}{2}}.

[formula]

The first integral on the right of (3.13) is bounded by

\left[\int^{a}_{0}|\lambda|^{2(N-\alpha/2)+n}d\lambda\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\int^{\lambda}_{0}|tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{1})|^{2}|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

[formula]

by the Standard Schwartz inequality. If
2(N-\alpha/2)+n>-1
i.e., if

N>(-(n+1)+\alpha)/2,

then the first factor in (3.15) is finite, say bounded by
C>0
. By (3.14), (3.15) is bounded by

C\left[tr(B_{1}B^{*}_{1})\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\int^{\lambda}_{0}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi^{*})\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})^{*})|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

[formula]

Now recall that the group convolution of, say, two functions
\psi,\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}
is defined as

(\psi*\chi)(g)=\int\psi(x^{-1})\chi(xg)dx.

[formula]

Recall from 1.4.6 that

\pi_{\lambda}(\psi*\chi)=\pi_{\lambda}(\psi)\pi_{\lambda}(\chi).

Hence (3.16) is bounded by

C^{\prime}\left|\int^{\lambda}_{0}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu}*\varphi)\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu}*\varphi)^{*})|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}=C^{\prime}\|\tilde{\nu}*\varphi\|_{L^{2}}

by Plancherel formula. By the general Hausdorff-Young inequality,

\|\tilde{\nu}*\varphi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\tilde{\nu}\|_{L^{1}}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.

Estimation of the second integral on the right-hand side of (3.13) is similar except that (3.15) is replaced by

\left[\int^{\infty}_{a}|\lambda|^{2(N-\alpha/2)+n-N^{\prime}}d\lambda\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\left[\int^{\lambda}_{0}|tr(\lambda^{N^{\prime}}\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)B_{1})||\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

[formula]

Now
(i\lambda)^{N^{\prime}}=\pi_{\lambda}(T^{N^{\prime}})
and
\pi_{\lambda}(T^{N^{\prime}})\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})=\pi_{\lambda}(\widetilde{T^{N^{\prime}}}\nu).

If
N^{\prime}
is chosen so large that the first factor is finite, (3.18) is bounded by

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}\left\{\int^{\lambda}_{0}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\widetilde{T^{N^{\prime}}}\nu)\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)^{*}\pi_{\lambda}(\widetilde{T^{N^{\prime}}}\nu)^{*})|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}}

=C^{\prime\prime}\|\widetilde{T^{N^{\prime}}}\nu*\varphi\|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.

Combining these estimates and similar ones for the other integral in (3.12) gives (3.11). This argument is very similar to that given in
[
18
]
.

To show that the
L^{2}
function
u
is actually smooth it suffices by Sobolev’s Lemma, to show that all distributional derivatives of
u
are in
L^{2}
. We shall show that if
D
is any left invariant differential operator of
I\!\!H_{n}
, then

|u(D\varphi)|\leq C_{D}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}},\;\;\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I\!\!H_{n}).

[formula]

To prove (3.19), one proceeds as above, but with
\varphi
replaced by
D\varphi
.

Since

tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(D\varphi)\pi_{\lambda}(D\varphi)^{*}\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})^{*})=tr(\pi_{\lambda}(D\varphi)^{*}\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})^{*}\pi_{\lambda}(D\varphi)),

\displaystyle\int_{0<\lambda<a}|\lambda|^{N}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(D\varphi)(\pi_{\lambda}(P))^{-1})|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\leq C\|D\varphi*\tilde{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}=C\|\varphi*D^{r}\tilde{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\int_{0\leq\lambda<\infty}|\lambda|^{N}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(D\varphi)(\pi_{\lambda}(P))^{-1})|\lambda|^{n}d\lambda

\leq C\|D\varphi*T^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}=C\|\varphi*D^{r}T^{N^{\prime}}\tilde{\nu}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}},\;\;r\in\mathbb{I\!\!R}^{+}.

The estimates for
\lambda<0
are the same. This completes the proof that
u
is
C^{\infty}
function.

Finally, a formal calculation in
[
19
]
[
12
]
[
1
]
shows that
u
satisfies (3.8) i.e., that

u(P^{\tau}\varphi)=T^{N}\nu(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(I\!\!H_{n})
, where
P^{\tau}
is the formal transpose of
P
. Indeed, since

\pi_{\lambda}(P^{\tau}\varphi)=\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi)\pi_{\lambda}(P),

and

(i\lambda)^{N}\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})=\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(T^{N})=\pi_{\lambda}(\widetilde{T^{N}}\nu),

\begin{array}[]{rcl}u(P^{\tau}\varphi)&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}(i\lambda)^{N}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\tilde{\nu})\pi_{\lambda}(P^{\tau}\varphi)(\pi(P))^{-1})|\lambda|^{N}d\lambda\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}tr(\pi_{\lambda}(\widetilde{T^{N}}\nu)\pi_{\lambda}(\varphi))|\lambda|^{N}d\lambda\\
\\
&=&\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{*}}tr(T^{N}\nu*\varphi))|\lambda|^{N}d\lambda\\
\\
&=&(T^{N}\nu*\varphi)|\lambda|^{N}d\lambda\\
&=&(\widetilde{T^{N}}\nu*\varphi)(0)\;\;\;\;\;\;\mbox{(by the Plancherel theorm,)}\\
&=&T^{N}\nu(\varphi),\;\;\;\;\;\;;\;\;\;\;\;\;\mbox{(by (3.17))}.\end{array}

This completes the proof of the theorem.
