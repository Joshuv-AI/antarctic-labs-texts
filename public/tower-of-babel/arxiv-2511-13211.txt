3DAlign-DAER: Dynamic Attention Policy and Efficient Retrieval Strategy for Fine-grained 3D-Text Alignment at Scale
Yijia Fan, Jusheng Zhang, Kaitong Cai, Jing Yang, Jian Wang, Keze Wang

Abstract
Despite recent advancements in 3D-text cross-modal alignment, existing state-of-the-art methods still struggle to align fine-grained textual semantics with detailed geometric structures, and their alignment performance degrades significantly when scaling to large-scale 3D databases. To overcome this limitation, we introduce 3DAlign-DAER , a unified framework designed to align text and 3D geometry via the proposed dynamic attention policy and the efficient retrieval strategy, capturing subtle correspondences for diverse cross-modal retrieval and classification tasks. Specifically , during the training, our proposed dynamic attention policy (DAP) employs the Hierarchical Attention Fusion (HAF) module to represent the alignment as learnable fine-grained token-to-point attentions. To optimize these attentions across different tasks and geometric hierarchies, our DAP further exploits the Monte Carlo tree search to dynamically calibrate HAF attention weights via a hybrid reward signal and further enhances the alignment between textual descriptions and local 3D geometry. During the inference, our 3DAlign-DAER introduces an Efficient Retrieval Strategy (ERS) to leverage efficient hierarchical searching in the large-scale embedding spaces, outperforming traditional methods ( e.g. , KNN) in accuracy and efficiency. Furthermore , to facilitate text-3D alignment research and train our 3DAlign-DAER, we construct Align3D-2M , a large-scale dataset featuring 2M text-3D pairs, to provide sufficient fine-grained cross-modal annotations. Extensive and comprehensive experiments demonstrate the superior performance of our 3DAlign-DAER on diverse benchmarks. We will release our codes, models, and datasets.

To address these issues, we introduce our open-source dataset
Align3D-2M
.
Constructed using our customized parallel rendering and annotation pipelines, it comprises 2 million curated text-3D pairs featuring fine-grained text-geometry correspondences. The significant scale and high quality of alignment within Align3D-2M enable the development and rigorous evaluation of models targeting complex real-world text-3D relationships for learning fine-grained alignment at scale. We further introduce our
3DAlign-DAER
framework to achieve fine-grained alignment and scalability, fully exploiting the potential of our Align3D-2M dataset. The central innovation of our 3DAlign-DAER lies in employing a Dynamic Attention Policy (DAP) to modulate the fine-grained token-to-point attentions learned by our Hierarchical Attention Fusion (HAF) module. Guided by a tailored hybrid reward from Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)
(
38
)
, our DAP iteratively calibrates attention weights, ensuring precise correspondence between textual descriptions and local geometric details. Extensive and comprehensive results demonstrate that the alignment quality of our 3DAlign-DAER is significantly enhanced through the dynamic attention refinement of DAP. Thus, 3DAlign-DAER advances in achieving robust retrieval performance at scale by making features more discriminative against hard distractors.

Specifically, during the training phase, our DAP employs an adaptive online search by using MCTS on attention maps from the HAF module. For each training sample, rather than relying on fixed attention weights, our DAP determines the optimal modulation strategy for the HAF weights. Our DAP is guided by our designed hybrid reward signal, which incorporates both dense feedback from the reduction in contrastive loss and sparse feedback based on retrieval performance on a validation set. By actively exploring how to enhance or suppress specific text token-to-3D patch associations based on this reward, our DAP forces the model to learn the precise and robust fine-grained correspondences necessary for challenging alignments.

To achieve efficient inference, especially when tackling large-scale sparse data retrieval , we design an
Efficient Retrieval Strategy (ERS)
for speed and accuracy. Our ERS leverages the highly discriminative and well-aligned representations produced by the training process of 3DAlign-DAER, which incorporates dynamic attention refinement in DAP. It constructs semantic and spatial hierarchies over the embedding space based on the learned representations, enabling an efficient tree-based search strategy to locate Top-K matches rapidly. This approach significantly boosts retrieval performance on large-scale datasets, overcoming the speed and accuracy limitations of traditional KNN, ANN, or Greedy Search techniques.
Comprehensive experiments show that our 3DAlign-DAER achieves SOTA on zero-shot classification, cross-modal retrieval (including large-scale), and few-shot learning, significantly outperforming strong baselines.

main

Methodology

Align3D-2M Dataset

To enable 3D model retrieval based on open-text instructions, we constructed a large-scale multimodal 3D dataset named Align3D-2M. During the dataset construction process, we have integrated multiple publicly available 3D model repositories, such as Objaverse-XL, ShapeNet
(
2
)
, Objaverse 1.0
(
5
)
, ObjectNet3D
(
45
)
, Pix3D
(
37
)
, and 3D_FUTURE
(
8
)
, and developed a unique data cleaning and standardization pipeline. Public repositories like ObjaverseXL, while vast, often contain raw, uncleaned metadata and include non-descriptive strings (e.g., “a591c555d?hl=ru”) unsuitable for detailed cross-modal training. Our primary goal is to create a large-scale dataset with consistent, semantically rich text annotations specifically tailored to effectively train advanced text-to-3D alignment models like our proposed 3DAlign-DAER. 3
for details about the source datasets.

Names and Sizes of Different Source Datasets Comprising Align3D-2M. Please note that we only selected relevant subsets or high-quality portions from each source.

To overcome the limitations of inconsistent or noisy source metadata and generate suitable training pairs, we first extract diverse 3D objects along with their available metadata (such as category labels and geometric attributes) from the integrated sources. For each object, we render a standard frontal view image. Subsequently, we utilize the multimodal capabilities of GPT-4o
(
24
)
, providing it with both the rendered frontal image and the extracted metadata. Guided by prompt strategies incorporating this information and including randomization parameters to ensure diversity, GPT-4o is leveraged to batch-produce initial text descriptions designed for better cross-modal understanding. This process initially generated about 2 million descriptions.

To refine these descriptions, we employ a combination of automated and human reviews. Initially, a language model (a BERT-based
(
6
;
18
)
classifier) performs preliminary screening, filtering out descriptions with obvious issues like grammatical errors, excessive generalization, or those clearly not describing 3D objects (filtering approx. 2.7%). To verify the quality and suitability of the generated descriptions for training, we hired around 50 annotators who conducted a random sample review of about 10% of the remaining descriptions. They focus on evaluating the accuracy, specificity, and relevance of the description to the 3D object based on predefined criteria. This multi-stage process aims to ensure a baseline level of quality and consistency across the large generated dataset, resulting in about 2 million description-object pairs deemed suitable for training our 3DAlign-DAER. Specific review criteria and statistics are detailed in the Appendix “Details on Align3D-2M Text Annotation and Data Curation”.

Model Architecture

Our 3DAlign-DAER comprises four key components: a text encoder, a 3D encoder, the Hierarchical Attention Fusion (HAF) module that incorporates MCTS-driven attention modulation according to our proposed Dynamic Attention Policy (DAP), and a contrastive learning objective.

Text Encoder

We employ a Transformer-based architecture to process input natural language descriptions. Given an input text sequence, the encoder generates token-level features projected into a shared
d
-dimensional embedding space:

\mathbf{F}_{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
, where
T
is the number of text tokens and
d
is the feature dimension.

3D Encoder

The 3D encoder processes point cloud data, extracting geometric and spatial information. We utilize a hierarchical architecture, potentially inspired by PointNet++, involving point set sampling, local feature aggregation, and global information fusion. This transforms the 3D geometry into features compatible with the text features:
\mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N
is the number of sampled points. Both
\mathbf{F}_{\text{text}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}
reside in the same
d
-dimensional space.

Hierarchical Attention Fusion (HAF) Module

The HAF module establishes fine-grained correspondences using cross-attention. Text and 3D features are linearly projected to Query (
\mathbf{Q}
), Key (
\mathbf{K}
), and Value (
\mathbf{V}
) matrices using learnable weights
\mathbf{W}_{Q},\mathbf{W}_{K}^{\text{3D}},\mathbf{W}_{V}^{\text{3D}},\mathbf{W}_{V}^{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. For text-to-3D attention:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{\text{text}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{\text{text}}\mathbf{W}_{Q}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Q}_{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d})

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{K}_{\text{3D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d})

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}\mathbf{W}_{V}^{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d})

(3)

For 3D-to-text attention (used in aggregation), text values are:

\mathbf{V}_{\text{text}}=\mathbf{F}_{\text{text}}\mathbf{W}_{V}^{\text{text}}\quad(\mathbf{V}_{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d})

(4)

An initial cross-attention weight matrix is computed via scaled dot-product attention:

\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{\text{text}}\mathbf{K}_{\text{3D}}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}

(5)

The
\text{softmax}(\cdot)
is applied row-wise. This
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
serves as the starting point for MCTS refinement.

MCTS-driven Dynamic Attention Policy

To refine the initial alignment
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
, our DAP introduces MCTS to dynamically modulate the attention weights, searching for an optimized distribution
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
that maximizes a reward signal.

State (
s
):
Represents the current attention matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}
. The root state corresponds to
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
.

Action (
a
):
A modification operation
a:\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{T\times N}
, yielding
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}=\text{Normalize}(\text{op}(\mathbf{A},\Delta,\mathbf{M}))
. Here,
op
is the operation (e.g., addition),
\Delta
is the magnitude,
\mathbf{M}
is a binary mask, and
Normalize
re-applies row-wise softmax. Actions include enhancing or suppressing attention in
\mathbf{M}
.

Reward (
R
):
Evaluates the quality of reaching a state, incorporating a dense internal loss-oriented reward and a sparse external retrieval-oriented reward:

R_{\text{total}}=\alpha\cdot R_{\text{internal}}+(1-\alpha)\cdot R_{\text{external}}

(6)

where
\alpha\in[0,1]
is a balance factor.
R_{\text{internal}}
denotes the decrements in the contrastive loss (see §
Contrastive Learning Objective
) after each MCTS action.
R_{\text{external}}
denotes the performance score of the final downstream retrieval task based on metrics (
i.e.
, weighted Recall@K and mAP). These scores can be efficiently approximated during MCTS rollouts.

MCTS iteratively builds a
search tree
via four steps:

Selection:
Traverse the tree from the root by selecting child nodes maximizing the UCT score :

\text{UCT}(s,a)=\overline{Q}(s,a)+c\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2\ln N(s)}{N(s,a)+\epsilon}}

(7)

where
\overline{Q}(s,a)
is the average reward after action
a
from state
s
,
N(s)
and
N(s,a)
are visit counts,
c
is the exploration constant, and
\epsilon
prevents division by zero. Selection stops at an expandable leaf node.

Expansion:
Add one or more child nodes corresponding to valid actions
a\in C(s)
applicable to the leaf node’s state
s
.

Simulation (Rollout):
Estimate the reward from a new node
s^{\prime}
by simulating subsequent actions using a fast policy
\pi_{\text{rollout}}
for a fixed depth
d
:
\hat{R}=\text{Simulate}(s^{\prime},\pi_{\text{rollout}},d)
. The reward is calculated via Eq.
6
.

Backpropagation:
Propagate the estimated reward
\hat{R}
up the path from the expanded node to the root, updating visit counts
N(s),N(s,a)
and average rewards
\overline{Q}(s,a)
using incremental mean updates:

\displaystyle N(s)

\displaystyle\leftarrow N(s)+1

\displaystyle N(s,a)

\displaystyle\leftarrow N(s,a)+1

\displaystyle\overline{Q}(s,a)

\displaystyle\leftarrow\overline{Q}(s,a)+\frac{\hat{R}-\overline{Q}(s,a)}{N(s,a)}

(8)

After a fixed budget, the action corresponding to the most promising path from the root yields
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
.

Attention-Guided Feature Aggregation

The optimized attention
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
guides cross-modal feature aggregation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\text{text}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{text}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d})

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}^{\top}\cdot\mathbf{V}_{\text{text}}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{3D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d})

(10)

These features represent each modality informed by the other. Global embeddings
\mathbf{E}_{\text{text}},\mathbf{E}_{\text{3D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
are obtained by pooling (
\mathrm{Pool}
) and non-linear transformations (
g_{\text{text}},g_{\text{3D}}
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{text}}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{text}}(\mathrm{Pool}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{text}}))\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle=g_{\text{3D}}(\mathrm{Pool}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{3D}}))

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{text}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\text{text}}/\|\mathbf{E}_{\text{text}}\|_{2}\quad

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{3D}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\text{3D}}/\|\mathbf{E}_{\text{3D}}\|_{2}

(12)

Contrastive Learning Objective

Using the InfoNCE loss
(
41
)
, the text-to-3D loss (
\mathcal{L}_{\text{t2v}}
) is for a batch of
B
pairs:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{t2v}}=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\log\frac{\exp\left(\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{text},i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{3D},i}/\tau\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{B}\exp\left(\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{text},i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{3D},j}/\tau\right)}

(13)

where
\tau
is a temperature parameter. A symmetric loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{v2t}}
is for 3D-to-text alignment. The final bidirectional loss is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{bidirectional}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{t2v}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{v2t}}

(14)

Minimizing this loss aligns positive pairs and separates negative pairs in the embedding space.

Overall Loss Function

Our overall training objective in Phase 2 is a bidirectional cross-modality contrastive loss computed with MCTS-refined attentions, with additional regularization:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{bidirectional}}+\gamma\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}

(15)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bidirectional}}
uses embeddings derived from
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}
includes regularization terms (e.g., weight decay) weighted by
\gamma
. The MCTS reward (Eq.
6
) guides the
search
for the optimal attention matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
, which subsequently influences
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bidirectional}}
, rather than directly contributing to the gradient via backpropagation in this formulation.

Training Algorithm

We introduce our two-stage training algorithm of 3DAlign-DAER (details in Appendix), which contains two phases:

1
st
-Phase:

Contrastive Pre-training
. We pretrain only with
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bidirectional}}
to warmup and obtain the initial attention
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
(Eq.
5
). This establishes a baseline alignment, potentially fine-tuning encoders and training projection heads.

2
nd
-Phase:

MCTS-Guided Optimization
. MCTS is introduced to refine attention. The training loop involves: (1) Forward pass for
\mathbf{F}_{\text{text}},\mathbf{F}_{\text{3D}}
. (2) Compute
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
. (3) Run MCTS search starting from
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
to find
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
, using the current model for reward evaluation. (4) Generate final embeddings
\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{text}},\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{3D}}
using
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
. (5) Compute
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bidirectional}}
(Eq.
14
). (6) Backpropagate the loss to update model parameters. (7) Update MCTS statistics. MCTS search is performed periodically (e.g., every
k=10
steps).

ERS for Efficient Inference

During inference, the full MCTS is computationally expensive. We propose ERS, a lightweight hierarchical search strategy for efficient retrieval. It navigates a pre-computed or dynamically built hierarchical index over the 3D database. When retrieving for a query text embedding
\mathbf{q}=\hat{\mathbf{E}}_{\text{text}}
, ERS selects child nodes
a
(representing sub-category
s_{a}
) using a modified UCT-like score:

\begin{split}&\text{UCT}_{\text{Lite}}(s,a)=\lambda_{1}\cdot\text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\bm{\mu}_{s_{a}})\\
&+\lambda_{2}\cdot\frac{N_{\text{success}}(s,a)}{N(s,a)+\epsilon}+\lambda_{3}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(s)}{N(s,a)+\epsilon}}\end{split}

(16)

where
\text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\bm{\mu}_{s_{a}})
is the cosine similarity to the child’s representative embedding
\bm{\mu}_{s_{a}}
,
N_{\text{success}}
counts past retrieval successes through
a
,
N
are visit counts,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
are weights balancing similarity, historical success, and exploration, and
\epsilon
prevents division by zero. This efficiently prunes the search space.

In this paper, we presented the 3DAlign-DAER framework for fine-grained alignment and large-scale sparse data retrieval in 3D-text cross-modal understanding. Appendix I
Details on Align3D-2M Text Annotation and Data Curation

This section provides further details on the pipeline used to generate and refine the textual descriptions paired with the 3D models in the Align3D-2M dataset, as described in the main paper. The primary motivation for creating Align3D-2M was to construct a large-scale, high-quality dataset
specifically designed for pre-training
advanced 3D-text alignment models like our proposed 3DAlign-DAER. This curated dataset addresses the limitations of relying solely on raw, often noisy, metadata from existing large repositories and provides the necessary data fidelity for learning fine-grained cross-modal correspondences. We elaborate on the source data integration, the automated pre-filtering, and the human review process, including quality control measures and observed rejection rates, potential biases, and the 3D model data cleaning steps.

Source Data Integration Strategy

Align3D-2M integrates data from multiple diverse, publicly available 3D model repositories. As shown in Figure 3 in the main paper, the primary sources include large-scale collections like Objaverse-XL and Objaverse 1.0, more structured academic datasets like ShapeNet and ObjectNet3D, and datasets with richer scene context or specific object types like Pix3D and 3D-FUTURE.

Our integration strategy was not a simple aggregation. Instead, we performed a
curated selection
process:

•

We focused on extracting
single, well-defined 3D objects
from these sources, excluding complex scenes or assemblies where text-object alignment would be ambiguous for this stage of pre-training.

•

For very large sources like Objaverse-XL, we aimed to select a diverse and representative subset, potentially filtering based on metadata quality or object complexity indicators available in the source.

•

From datasets like ShapeNet, we leveraged its canonical categorization and cleaner geometry.

•

The goal was to harness the complementary strengths of each source – scale from Objaverse, structure from ShapeNet, potential real-world grounding hints from others – to create a unified pool that is larger and more diverse than any single source, while maintaining a focus on clear object-level representation suitable for pre-training alignment models.

•

This curated pool formed the basis for the subsequent text generation and 3D model cleaning steps.

Text Annotation Generation and Automated Pre-filtering

As mentioned in the main paper, initial text descriptions were generated using GPT-4o, prompted with rendered frontal views and metadata for each selected 3D object. This process yielded approximately 2 million initial descriptions.

Before human review, an automated pre-filtering step was crucial for efficiency and quality improvement. We employed a classifier based on fine-tuned BERT models. This classifier was trained to identify and discard descriptions exhibiting specific undesirable characteristics:

•

Grammatical Errors:
Sentences with significant grammatical flaws that impede understanding.

•

Excessive Generalization:
Descriptions that were too vague or generic to be informative (e.g., ”a 3D object”, ”model”, ”item”).

•

Irrelevance:
Text that clearly did not describe the visual or functional aspects of the corresponding 3D object, or contained unrelated information.

This automated process flagged and removed approximately 2.7% of the initially generated descriptions, resulting in approximately 1.94 million pairs, allowing the subsequent human review phase to focus on a higher-quality subset.

Human Review Process and Quality Control

The human review phase served as a critical quality validation step. We adopted a statistical sampling approach combined with detailed annotation guidelines.

Annotator Setup and Training:
We hired and onboarded a team of approximately 50 annotators. Annotators received training on the task objectives and specific review criteria (detailed below) to ensure consistency. They likely interacted with an interface displaying the rendered image, potentially a 3D view or rotation controls for the object, and the corresponding text description generated after automated filtering.

Sampling Strategy:
Given the scale of the dataset (approx. 1.94 million items post-filtering), reviewing every description manually was infeasible. We employed a random sampling strategy, selecting approximately 10% of the filtered object-description pairs for human review (approx. 208,000 pairs). This sample size was chosen to provide statistical confidence in the overall quality of the dataset. The random sampling aimed to cover the diversity of object categories and styles present in the source datasets.

Annotation Task and Criteria:
Annotators evaluated the sampled descriptions based on several predefined criteria, flagging items for rejection if they failed to meet standards:

•

Accuracy:
Does the description contain factually incorrect information about the object’s appearance (e.g., color, shape, parts) or stated function (if applicable)?

•

Specificity:
Does the description provide enough detail to distinguish the object from other similar objects? Overly generic descriptions were rejected.

•

Clarity and Fluency:
Is the description grammatically correct, well-structured, and easy for a human to understand? Significantly confusing or poorly written text led to rejection.

•

Relevance:
Does the description focus appropriately on the 3D object? Descriptions dwelling excessively on abstract interpretations or unrelated concepts were rejected.

Annotators used these criteria to mark descriptions as either ”Accept” or ”Reject”.

Quality Control and Validation Outcome:
The review process spanned approximately three weeks. Standard quality control measures like spot-checking and monitoring inter-annotator agreement were potentially employed. Within the human-reviewed 10% sample, the observed rejection rate based on the above criteria was approximately 4.5%. This relatively low rejection rate, after the automated pre-filtering, provided strong evidence for the high quality of the remaining descriptions. This positive validation from the large sample gave us the confidence to accept the entire set of approx. 1.94 million descriptions that passed the initial automated filtering, forming the final Align3D-2M text corpus (rounded to 2M for naming). This rigorous process justifies our claim of a ”high-quality” dataset despite not manually reviewing every single pair.

Discussion of Potential Biases and Mitigation

We acknowledge potential sources of bias in the annotation pipeline:

•

LLM Generation Bias:
Large language models like GPT-4o can reflect biases present in their training data. This might manifest as generating more descriptions for common objects, using stereotypical language, or overlooking features of less common object types. Our mitigation strategy involved using diverse 3D model sources (Objaverse, ShapeNet, etc.) and incorporating randomization in the prompting strategies to encourage more varied outputs.

•

Human Annotation Bias:
Human judgment can be subjective. Annotators might have different interpretations of criteria like ”specificity” or unconscious biases related to object aesthetics or types. Mitigation included providing clear guidelines and criteria, training the annotators, and using a relatively large pool of annotators ( 50) to average out individual biases. The statistical sampling approach also ensures that the final validation is not overly reliant on any single annotator’s judgment across the entire dataset.

•

Sampling Bias:
While the 10% sample was chosen randomly, there’s always a small statistical chance it might not perfectly represent the full distribution of edge cases or quality variations in the filtered dataset. The large size of the sample (approx. 208,000 items) aimed to minimize this risk.

3D Model Data Cleaning and Standardization

Beyond text annotation, curating the 3D models themselves involved a data cleaning and standardization pipeline applied to the objects selected from the integrated sources (as described in Section
I
). The process included:

•

Format Conversion:
Standardizing models into accessible formats. As noted in the main paper, we developed a GPU-accelerated method to efficiently convert formats like GLB and OBJ into point clouds for processing by our 3D encoder.

•

Filtering and Deduplication:
Initial filtering removed unusable or very low-quality assets (e.g., files that failed to load, models with extremely low polygon counts). Deduplication steps, potentially based on file hashes or geometric similarity checks, were applied to reduce redundancy within the combined pool. The selection of specific subsets or ”high-quality portions” from sources also implies that a quality threshold was applied during integration.

•

Potential Normalization (Implied):
Standard practices often include normalizing object orientation (e.g., aligning to a canonical upright position) and scale (e.g., fitting within a unit bounding box) to ensure consistency for model training, although the specific normalization strategy would depend on the chosen 3D encoder’s requirements.

This curation aimed to provide a large-scale yet reasonably clean and standardized set of 3D assets paired with high-quality text for multimodal pre-training.

Crucially, Align3D-2M is constructed exclusively as a pre-training dataset.
To maintain the integrity of standard evaluation protocols and prevent data leakage,
Align3D-2M explicitly excludes any 3D models or text descriptions that are part of the established test sets
of commonly used benchmarks such as the ModelNet40 test split, the ScanObjectNN test split, or the Text2Shape test set. All evaluations reported in our paper using these benchmarks are performed on their standard, unseen test partitions.

Dataset Contribution and Impact

Align3D-2M was intentionally constructed to overcome limitations found in existing large-scale 3D repositories, particularly regarding the quality and detail of associated text. While primarily developed to enable our 3DAlign-DAER method to achieve SOTA performance by providing sufficiently detailed and well-aligned text-3D pairs
for pre-training
, we recognize its broader utility. The meticulous curation process, resulting in cleaner models and significantly improved textual descriptions compared to raw source metadata (as evidenced by the performance drop when training 3DAlign-DAER directly on raw Objaverse-XL data), was instrumental in enabling our model to learn fine-grained alignments and achieve state-of-the-art results. This comparison highlights that both the novel methodology of 3DAlign-DAER and the high-quality nature of the Align3D-2M pre-training dataset are crucial factors contributing to the achieved results. Consequently, we plan to release the Align3D-2M dataset and the associated pre-trained model embeddings derived from it to the research community, serving as a valuable resource to advance research in the field.

Appendix J
Theoretical Analysis of MCTS Computational Overhead

While Section
F
empirically quantifies the computational overhead introduced by the MCTS-driven attention optimization during training (observing approximately an 11.6% increase in training time relative to the baseline without MCTS), this section provides a theoretical justification for why this overhead remains manageable and does not become prohibitive. The manageable cost stems from several key aspects of our approach, which effectively constrain the complexity of the MCTS search:

High-Quality Initialization Prunes the Search Space

The MCTS search for the optimal attention matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
does not begin from a random or uniform state. Instead, it starts from
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
(Eq.
5
), which is computed by the Hierarchical Attention Fusion (HAF) module (Sec.
Hierarchical Attention Fusion (HAF) Module
). The HAF module, based on standard cross-attention mechanisms and potentially benefiting from the pre-training in Phase 1 (Sec.
Training Algorithm
), already provides a semantically meaningful, albeit potentially coarse, initial alignment between text tokens and 3D point features. Therefore, MCTS operates primarily as a
refinement
mechanism rather than a full discovery process. The search is effectively guided toward promising regions of the attention space centered around this strong initial ”root” state, significantly reducing the effective volume of the space that needs to be explored compared to starting from scratch.

Constrained Action Space Focusing on Local Refinements

The actions defined within our MCTS framework (Sec.
MCTS-driven Dynamic Attention Policy
, Action paragraph) involve applying relatively small modifications (
\Delta=\pm 0.1
) to a limited subset (e.g., 10% mask
\mathbf{M}
) of the pre-softmax attention scores. This means each step in the MCTS tree explores relatively localized adjustments to the attention distribution. The search focuses on
fine-tuning
specific token-point associations rather than exploring drastically different global attention patterns. This constrained nature of actions prevents the search from branching out too aggressively into vastly dissimilar attention configurations at each step, keeping the exploration focused and computationally tractable.

Bounded Search Complexity via Explicit Limits

The complexity of the MCTS search is explicitly controlled by hyperparameters defined in Appendix
B
.

•

The
search budget
is limited to a modest 100 simulations per MCTS instance. This directly caps the number of nodes expanded and evaluated within the search tree, preventing uncontrolled growth.

•

The
simulation (rollout) depth
is fixed at a shallow
d=5
steps. This ensures that the reward estimation phase remains computationally inexpensive, relying on short-term projections rather than deep, complex simulations.

These limits are consistent with the goal of local refinement rather than an exhaustive global search, ensuring the MCTS component completes within a reasonable time frame during training iterations.

Amortized Cost through Periodic Execution

The MCTS optimization is not performed at every single training step. Instead, it is executed periodically, every
k=10
steps (Appendix
B
). This
amortizes the computational cost
of the MCTS search over multiple gradient update steps. For the steps where MCTS is not run, the training proceeds using the previously found
\mathbf{A}_{\text{optimized}}
(or
\mathbf{A}_{\text{initial}}
early on), incurring only the standard overhead of the forward and backward passes.

Efficient Simulation Policy

The use of a simple random rollout policy (
\pi_{\text{rollout}}
) during the simulation phase (Sec.
MCTS-driven Dynamic Attention Policy
, Simulation step) further contributes to efficiency. While potentially less accurate than a learned policy, it avoids the significant computational cost associated with invoking a complex policy network repeatedly during the rollouts.

In summary, the computational overhead of the MCTS attention optimization in 3DAlign-DAER is theoretically kept manageable due to a combination of factors: starting the search from a strong initial alignment provided by HAF, exploring a constrained space of local refinements, imposing strict limits on the search budget and depth, amortizing the cost through periodic execution, and utilizing an efficient simulation strategy. These design choices ensure that while MCTS introduces a measurable overhead, it remains a practical approach, offering a justifiable trade-off for the significant improvements in fine-grained alignment and downstream task performance demonstrated in our experiments.
