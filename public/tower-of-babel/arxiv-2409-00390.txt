Abstract
. We develop the bialgebra theory for two classes of non-associative algebras: nearly associative algebras and L ​ R LR -algebras. In particular, building on recent studies that reveal connections between these algebraic structures, we establish that nearly associative bialgebras and L ​ R LR -bialgebras are, in fact, equivalent concepts. We also provide a characterization of these bialgebra classes based on the coproduct. Moreover, since the development of nearly associative bialgebras — and by extension, L ​ R LR -bialgebras — requires the framework of nearly associative L L -algebras, we introduce this class of non-associative algebras and explore their fundamental properties. Furthermore, we identify and characterize a special class of nearly associative bialgebras, the coboundary nearly associative bialgebras, which provides a natural framework for studying the Yang-Baxter equation (YBE) within this context.

1.
Introduction

A nearly associative algebra is a non-associative algebra, i.e., a not necessarily associative algebra, over a field
\mathbb{K}
satisfying the multilinear identity in three variables:
(xy)z=y(zx)
. This class of non-associative algebras was first approached in
[
12
]
and, then, has been deeply studied in
[
8
]
. Further information on this class can be found in
[
1
]
.
Another significant class of algebras is the one of
LR
-algebras, also known as bicommutative algebras. These algebras are non-associative algebras over a field
\mathbb{K}
satisfying both right-commutativity and left-commutativity, i.e., the identities
(xy)z=(xz)y
and
x(yz)=y(xz)
. In particular, a non-associative algebra is called an
R
-algebra if it satisfies only right-commutativity, and an
L
-algebra if it satisfies only left-commutativity.
One-sided commutative algebras appeared first in the paper
[
10
]
due to Cayley in 1857, and they emerge in contexts such as the study of hydrodynamic type equations
[
5
]
. In recent years, the theory of
LR
-algebras has been intensively studied (see for example
[
9
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
17
]
).
Even though in general the above two classes of non-associative algebras are distinct, the authors in
[
8
]
show a connection between them. They prove that if
A
is a non-associative algebra equipped with an invariant, non-degenerate, and symmetric bilinear form (referred to as quadratic algebra), then
A
is nearly associative if and only if it is an
LR
-algebra.

Bialgebras are algebraic structures that arise from a combination of a multiplication and a comultiplication, subject to specific compatibility conditions. Notable examples of bialgebras include associative bialgebras and Lie bialgebras.
Drinfeld’s introduction of Lie bialgebras
[
15
]
has had a significant impact, particularly in the context of integrable models, where they are intimately connected to the classical Yang-Baxter equation. For associative algebras, Joni and Rota
[
18
]
introduced the concept of an infinitesimal bialgebra to provide a formal framework for the calculus of divided differences. Subsequently, Aguiar expanded this idea into a comprehensive theory of infinitesimal bialgebras, drawing parallels with the well-established theory of Lie bialgebras
[
2
,
3
]
.
Zhelyabin generalized Drinfeld’s construction to an arbitrary variety of non-associative algebras and studied the Jordan case (cf.
[
19
]
). We also refer the interested reader to
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
11
]
for more bialgebra structure results on other classes of non-associative algebras.

The goal of this paper is to develop a bialgebra theory specifically for nearly associative algebras.
We use Drinfeld’s construction of the double of a Lie algebra, generalized by Zhelyabin to any non-associative algebra, to define the class of nearly associative bialgebras. But, since double of algebras are quadratic algebras, the notions of nearly associative bialgebra and
LR
-bialgebra are equivalent, effectively extending the theory to the class of
LR
-algebras as well. We further broaden the theory by proving the equivalence of nearly associative bialgebras with
R
-bialgebras and
L
-bialgebras.
Moreover, since such a theory is only feasible in the context of nearly associative
L
-algebras, i.e., nearly associative algebras satisfying left-commutativity, we introduce this class of algebras and investigate some of its fundamental properties.
Furthermore, given the importance of the classical Yang-Baxter equation and coboundary Lie bialgebras, we define and explore nearly associative analogs of these concepts.
It should be noted that in
[
12
]
there is an attempt at a definition of nearly associative bialgebras using the notion of matched pairs of nearly associative algebras.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
provides the necessary background on nearly associative algebras and
LR
-algebras, covering basic definitions and properties. In Section
3
, we present the representation theory of nearly associative algebras. We specifically define both the adjoint and coadjoint representations, establish necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of the coadjoint representation, and present a general result applicable to any non-associative algebra (Corollary
3.15
).
Section
4
is devoted to the class of nearly associative
L
-algebras and presents some of their structural properties.
In Section
5
, we discuss nearly coassociative coalgebras,
L
-coalgebras, and
R
-coalgebras, along with their properties.
In Section
6
, we introduce nearly associative bialgebras through the double construction of nearly associative
L
-algebras in the sense of Drinfeld. We establish the equivalence between nearly associative bialgebras and the concepts of
R
-bialgebra,
L
-bialgebra, and
LR
-bialgebra, and provide a characterization of nearly associative bialgebras based on the coproduct (Theorem
6.5
). Finally, Section
7
is dedicated to coboundary nearly associative bialgebras, which serve as a natural framework for studying analogs of the classical Yang-Baxter equation and
r
-matrices in this context.

Throughout this paper, all vector spaces are assumed to be finite-dimensional over field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic zero.
We will always work with non-associative algebras, which we sometimes refer to just as algebras for simplicity.

\displaystyle\asso(x,y,z)=2[y,zx],

.

The following gives a characterization of flexible nearly associative algebras.

Recall that an algebra

\displaystyle[x,y]=-[y,x],

Definition 2.5
.

A non-associative algebra

Recall that a derived series of an algebra

The following proposition characterizes the nearly associative algebras.

(

A non-associative algebra

(2)

Remark 2.9

Any associative

Given an algebra

The opposite algebra of a nearly associative algebra is still a nearly associative algebra. Moreover, the opposite algebra of an

Now, we present an example of a six-dimensional nearly associative algebra that is not an

Example 2.11
.

Let

be a vector space of dimension six with basis

If we equipped

\displaystyle\qquad e_{1}e_{2}=e_{3},\ \ e_{2}e_{1}=-e_{4},\ \ e_{2}e_{2}=e_{5},\ \ e_{1}e_{5}=-e_{6},\ \ e_{5}e_{1}=e_{2}e_{3}=e_{4}e_{2}=e_{6},\ \ e_{2}e_{4}=-2e_{6},\ \ e_{3}e_{2}=2e_{6},

Let

be a vector space of dimension three with basis

If we equipped

Lemma 2.13
.

Consider a nearly associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
.
Then
\mathcal{A}
is an
R
-algebra if and only if it is an

-algebra.

Proof.

Let us suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is an

-algebra. Since
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative algebra, we obtain, for all

\begin{split}x(yz)=(zx)y=(zy)x=y(xz).\end{split}

Then
\mathcal{A}
is an

-algebra. Thus, we have shown that if
\mathcal{A}
is an

-algebra, then it is also an

-algebra. As a consequence, by the opposite algebra property in Remark
2.10
, the converse holds as well.
∎

Definition 2.14
.

Let

(1)

\displaystyle B(x,y)=B(y,x)

(3)

\displaystyle B(xy,z)=B(x,yz)

A non-associative algebra

The following theorem proved by the authors in
[
8
]
characterizes quadratic nearly associative algebras.

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
(A,B_{A})
be a quadratic algebra. Then
A
is a nearly associative algebra if and only if
A
is an
LR
-algebra.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
(A,B_{A})
be a quadratic algebra. Then the three conditions are equivalent:

(1)

A
is an
R
-algebra;

(2)

A
is an
L
-algebra;

(3)

A
is a nearly associative algebra.

Proof.

First, we show the equivalence between Condition (1) and (2). We assume that
A
is an
R
-algebra. Since
B_{A}
is invariant, we get, for all
x,y,z,t\in A

\begin{split}0=B_{A}((xy)z-(xz)y,t)=B_{A}(x,y(zt))-B_{A}(x,z(yt))=B_{A}(x,y(zt)-z(yt)).\end{split}

Since
B_{A}
is non-degenerate, it follows that
y(zt)-z(yt)=0
for all
y,z,t\in A
, that is,
A
is an
L
-algebra. Thus, we have shown that Condition (1) implies (2).
As a consequence, by the opposite algebra property in Remark
2.10
, we have also that Condition (2) implies (1).

Now, let us consider a nearly associative algebra
A
. Since
(A,B_{A})
is a quadratic algebra, by Theorem
2.16
we infer that
A
is an
LR
-algebra, so it is an
R
-algebra as well as an
L
-algebra. Thus, it is proven that Condition (3) implies (1) and (2).

Now, it is enough to show that Condition (1) implies (3). Suppose that
A
is an
R
-algebra. We proved already that Condition (1) implies (2), so
A
is also an
L
-algebra, that is,
A
is an
LR
-algebra.
By Theorem
2.16
we conclude that
A
is a nearly associative algebra, completing the proof.
∎

Remark 2.18

3.
Representation of nearly associative algebras

In this section, we introduce the concept of representation of a nearly associative algebra. We define the adjoint and coadjoint representations. We establish the conditions for the existence of the coadjoint representation, and provide a general result that applies to any non-associative algebra.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a non-associative algebra satisfying conditions
(P_{1}),\ldots,(P_{n})
,
V
be a vector space,
and
\displaystyle r,l:{A}\longrightarrow\mbox{End }(V)
be a pair of linear maps.
On the vector space sum
A\oplus V
we consider the following
product:

(3.10)

\begin{split}(x+u)\ast(y+v):=x\cdot y+l(x)(v)+r(y)(u),\end{split}

for
x,y\in A
and
u,v\in V
. We call
(V,r,l)
an
A
-bimodule
if
(A\oplus V,\ast)
is an algebra that also satisfies the conditions
(P_{1}),\ldots,(P_{n}).
In this case,
(r,l)
is called a

representation
of
A
in
V
relative to the
properties
(P_{1}),\ldots,(P_{n}).

Accordingly to the above definition, we define a representation of a nearly associative algebra as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra,
V
be a vector space, and
\displaystyle r,l:{A}\longrightarrow\mbox{End }(V)
be a pair of linear maps. Then
\displaystyle(r,l)
is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
V
if
(A\oplus V,\ast)
, where
\ast
is the product defined by (
3.10
), is a nearly associative algebra.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra,
V
be a vector space,
and
\displaystyle l,r:{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow\mbox{End }(V)
be linear maps.
The pair
\displaystyle(r,l)
is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
V

if and only if, for all
x,y\in{\mathcal{A}}
,

(3.11)

\displaystyle l(x)l(y)=r(y)r(x),

(3.12)

\displaystyle l(x)r(y)=l(yx),

(3.13)

\displaystyle r(x)l(y)=r(xy).

Proof.

First, suppose that the pair
\displaystyle(r,l)
is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
V
. Then
(A\oplus V,\ast)
, where
\ast
is the product defined by (
3.10
), is a nearly associative algebra. Thus,
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and
v\in V,

\begin{split}l(x)l(y)(v)&=x\ast(l(y)(v))=x\ast(y\ast v)=(v\ast x)\ast y=(r(x)(v))\ast y=r(y)r(x)(v),\end{split}

\begin{split}l(x)r(y)(v)&=x\ast(r(y)(v))=x\ast(v\ast y)=(y\ast x)\ast v=(yx)\ast v=l(yx)(v)\end{split}

\begin{split}r(x)l(y)(v)&=l(y)(v)\ast x=(y\ast v)\ast x=v\ast(x\ast y)=v\ast(xy)=r(xy)(v).\end{split}

Thus the Conditions (
3.11
)-(
3.13
) are satisfied.

Conversely, assume that Conditions (
3.11
)-(
3.13
) are satisfied. Then, for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
and
u,v,w\in V
, we have

\displaystyle\Big((x+u)\ast(y+v)\Big)\ast(z+w)

\displaystyle=\Big(xy+l(x)(v)+r(y)(u)\Big)\ast(z+w)=(xy)z+l(xy)(w)+r(z)l(x)(v)+r(z)r(y)(u)

\displaystyle=y(zx)+l(y)r(x)(w)+r(zx)(v)+l(y)l(z)(u)=(y+v)\ast\Big(zx+l(z)(u)+r(x)(w)\Big)

\displaystyle=(y+v)\ast\Big((z+w)\ast(x+u)\Big).

Thus,
(\mathcal{A}\oplus V,\ast)
is a nearly associative algebra and
(r,l)
is a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
V
as required.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra and consider the operators of left and right multiplications

is a representation of

The proof is a direct consequence of Proposition

Let

and
r^{\ast}
are the dual map of
l
and
r
, respectively,
(r^{\ast},l^{\ast})
is not necessarily a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
V^{\ast}
.

Remark 3.6
.

In
[
12
]
the authors use a different definition for the dual maps in the context of nearly associative algebras. They define the dual maps

\displaystyle l^{\ast}:A\longrightarrow\End(V^{\ast})
and
\displaystyle r^{\ast}:A\longrightarrow\End(V^{\ast})
of
l
and
r
, respectively, by
l^{\ast}(x)(f):=fl(x)
and
r^{\ast}(x)(f):=fr(x)
for
x\in A,f\in{V}^{\ast}
, respectively.
However, in many classes of algebras, the pair
\displaystyle(r^{\ast},l^{\ast})
so defined is not a representation of
A
in
V^{\ast}
. For example, given
({\mathfrak{g}},[-,-])
a Lie algebra and the map

\displaystyle\mbox{ad}:{\mathfrak{g}}\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{g}l}({\mathfrak{g}})
defined by

\displaystyle\ad_{x}(y)=(\ad x)(y):=[x,y],

for
x,y\in{\mathfrak{g}}
. If we consider
L:=\ad
and
R:=-\ad
, then
(R,L)
is a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
in
\mathfrak{g}
. However,
if
\rmL^{\ast}(x)(f)=fL(x)
and
\rmR^{\ast}(x)(f)=fR(x)=-fL(x)
for all
x\in{\mathfrak{g}}
and
f\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\ast}
, then
({\mathfrak{g}}^{\ast},\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is not a
{\mathfrak{g}}
-bimodule, except in case of
2
-nilpotent algebras.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra and
(\rmR,\rmL)
be its adjoint representation.
Then the pair
(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
if and only if

(3.15)

\begin{split}\rmL_{xy}=\rmR_{yx},\end{split}

whenever
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. In these conditions, the representation

(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is called the coadjoint representation (or co-regular representation) of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and
f\in{V}^{\ast}
. By Remark
2.2
, we get,

\displaystyle\rmL^{\ast}_{x}\rmL^{\ast}_{y}(f)=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(f\rmR_{y})=f\rmR_{y}\rmR_{x}\overset{\eqref{eq:lrm1}}{=}f\rmL_{x}\rmL_{y}=\rmR^{\ast}_{y}(f\rmL_{x})=\rmR^{\ast}_{y}\rmR^{\ast}_{x}(f).

More, we have
\rmL^{\ast}_{yx}(f)=f\rmR_{yx}
and

\displaystyle\rmL^{\ast}_{x}\rmR^{\ast}_{y}(f)=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(f\rmL_{y})=f\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}\overset{\eqref{eq:lr2}}{=}f\rmL_{xy}.

Thus,
\rmL^{\ast}_{x}\rmR^{\ast}_{y}(f)=\rmL^{\ast}_{yx}(f)
if and only if Condition (
3.15
) holds.

Finally, we have
\rmR^{\ast}_{xy}(f)=f\rmL_{xy}
and

\displaystyle\rmR^{\ast}_{x}\rmL^{\ast}_{y}(f)=\rmR^{\ast}_{x}(f\rmR_{y})=f\rmR_{y}\rmL_{x}\overset{\eqref{eq:lr3}}{=}f\rmR_{yx}.

Again,
\rmR^{\ast}_{x}\rmL^{\ast}_{y}(f)=\rmR^{\ast}_{xy}(f)
if and only if Condition (
3.15
) holds.

Therefore, the pair
(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
if and only if Condition (
3.15
) is satisfied.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra. The condition
\rmL_{xy}=\rmR_{yx}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
is equivalent to the
condition
\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}=\rmR_{y}\rmL_{x}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
, which means,
(xy)z=z(yx)

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra and
(\rmR,\rmL)
be its adjoint representation. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
;

(2)

\mathcal{A}
is an
L
-algebra;

(3)

\mathcal{A}
is an
R
-algebra.

Proof.

Suppose first that
(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
. Then by Lemma
3.7

\rmL_{xy}=\rmR_{yx}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
.
Thus it follows that
x(yz)=(zy)x=y(xz)
, for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}
is an
L
-algebra.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is an
L
-algebra. Then

(xy)z=(xz)y=z(yx)
, for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
, that is
\rmL_{xy}=\rmR_{yx}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus, by Lemma
3.7

(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
. As a consequence, the equivalence of Conditions (1) and (2) is proved.

Finally, the equivalence of Conditions (2) and (3) comes from Lemma
2.13
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative algebra. Then the pair
(\rmR,\rmL)
is a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}
, and
(\mathcal{A},\rmR,\rmL)
is a
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule.
Applying Proposition
3.9
, if
\mathcal{A}
is an
L
-algebra or an
R
-algebra, then the pair
(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
, thus
(\mathcal{A}^{\ast},\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is also a
\mathcal{A}
-bimodule.

As a consequence of Theorem
2.16
and Proposition
3.9
, we have the following.

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},B_{\mathcal{A}})
be a quadratic nearly associative algebra and
(\rmR,\rmL)
be its adjoint representation. Then the pair
(\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
is a
representation of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
.

We conclude this section by proving a general result that holds for any non-associative algebra
A
and generalize the above result.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
A
be an algebra,
\rmL
and
\rmR
be the operators of left and right multiplication, respectively. If
(A,B_{A})
is a quadratic algebra, then there exists an isomorphism
\Phi:A\longrightarrow A^{\ast}
such that
\Phi(\rmL_{x}(a))=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
and
\Phi(\rmR_{x}(a))=\rmR^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
for all
x,a\in A
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\Phi:A\longrightarrow A^{\ast}
defined by
\Phi(a):=B_{A}(a,-)
, for
a\in A
.
Since
B_{A}
is non-degenerate, the map
\Phi
is an isomorphism of vector space. Moreover, for any
x,a,b\in A

\Phi(\rmL_{x}(a))(b)=B_{A}(xa,b)=B_{A}(bx,a)=B_{A}(a,\rmR_{x}(b))=\Phi(a)(\rmR_{x}(b))=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))(b),

that is,
\Phi(\rmL_{x}(a))=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
for all
x,a\in A
. Similarly, it follows that
\Phi(\rmR_{x}(a))=\rmR^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
for all
x,a\in A
, as required.
∎

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
A
be an algebra, and
\rmL
and
\rmR
be the operators of left and right multiplication, respectively. Then there exists a bilinear form
B_{A}:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
such that
(A,B_{A})
is quadratic if and only if there exists an isomorphism
\Phi:A\longrightarrow A^{\ast}
such that
\Phi(\rmL_{x}(a))=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
and
\Phi(\rmR_{x}(a))=\rmR^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
whenever
x,a\in A
.

Proof.

If
(A,B_{A})
is a quadratic algebra, the desired conclusion follows from Proposition
3.12
.

Conversely, suppose that there exists an isomorphism
\Phi:A\longrightarrow A^{\ast}
such that
\Phi(\rmL_{x}(a))=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
and
\Phi(\rmR_{x}(a))=\rmR^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(a))
for all
x,a\in A
. Let us consider the map
T:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
defined by
T(x,y):=\Phi(x)(y)
for
x,y\in A
. Since
\Phi
is an isomorphism,
T
is a non-degenerate bilinear form. Also, for all
x,y,z\in A
, we have that

T(xy,z)=\Phi(xy)(z)=\Phi(\rmR_{y}(x))(z)=\rmR^{\ast}_{y}(\Phi(x))(z)=\Phi(x)(\rmL_{y}(z))=\Phi(x)(yz)=T(x,yz),

that is,
T
is invariant. More,
T
satisfies the following property: for all
x,y,z\in A

(3.16)

T(xy,z)=T(y,zx).

In fact, for all
x,y,z\in A

T(xy,z)=\Phi(xy)(z)=\Phi(\rmL_{x}(y))(z)=\rmL^{\ast}_{x}(\Phi(y))(z)=\Phi(y)(\rmR_{x}(z))=\Phi(y)(zx)=T(y,zx).

Now, let us consider the following two new bilinear forms
T_{s}:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
and
T_{a}:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
defined by

T_{s}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(T(x,y)+T(y,x)\Big)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad T_{a}(x,y):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(T(x,y)-T(y,x)\Big),

for
x,y\in A
, respectively. Clearly,
T=T_{s}+T_{a}
, with
T_{s}
symmetric and
T_{a}
antisymmetric (i.e.,
T_{a}(x,y)=-T_{a}(y,x)
for all
x,y\in A
). Now, since
T
is invariant and satisfy the property (
3.16
), for all
x,y,z\in A
we have

T_{s}(xy,z)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(T(xy,z)+T(z,xy)\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(T(x,yz)+T(yz,x)\Big)=T_{s}(x,yz),

thus
T_{s}
is a symmetric invariant bilinear form. Similarly,
T_{a}
is an antisymmetric invariant bilinear form.
Moreover,
for all
x,y,z\in A

T_{a}(xy,z)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(T(xy,z)-T(z,xy)\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(T(y,zx)-T(zx,y)\Big)=T_{a}(y,zx).

As a consequence, for all
x,y,z\in A

T_{a}(xy,z)=T_{a}(y,zx)=T_{a}(yz,x)=-T_{a}(x,yz)=-T_{a}(xy,z).

Thus, since
\mathbb{K}
is of characteristic different from 2, for all
x,y,z\in A
, it follows that

(3.17)

T_{a}(xy,z)=0.

If
N:=\{x\in A\ |\ T_{a}(x,y)=0\mbox{ for all }y\in A\}
, then by (
3.17
) we have
A^{2}\subseteq N
, and as a consequence
N
is a two-sided ideal of
A
. Now, let
W:=\{x\in A\ |\ T_{s}(x,y)=0\mbox{ for all }y\in A\}
.
Since
T
is non-degenerate,
N\cap W=\{0\}
.
Then there exists a vector subspace
V\subseteq A
such that
A=W\oplus V
. Clearly,
N\subseteq V.

Moreover, since
A^{2}\subseteq N\subseteq V
,
V
is also a two-sided ideal of
A
and
{T_{s}}\,|_{V\times V}
is non-degenerate.

Consider a symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form
H:W\times W\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
. Then we can define the following bilinear form
\widetilde{H}:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
such that

\widetilde{H}|_{W\times W}=H
and
\widetilde{H}(V,A)=\widetilde{H}(A,V)=\{0\}
.
Clearly,
\widetilde{H}
is symmetric. Now, we consider the bilinear form

B_{A}:=T_{s}+\widetilde{H}:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}.

Since
T_{s}
and
\widetilde{H}

are symmetric, then
B_{A}
is also symmetric. Moreover, given
w\in W
and
v\in V
, if
B_{A}(w+v,x)=0
for all
x\in A
, then
\widetilde{H}(w,x)+T_{s}(v,x)=0
. Now, if
x\in W
, then
T_{s}(v,x)=0
. So, it follows that
\widetilde{H}(w,x)=0
, and then
H(w,x)=0
. Since
H
is non-degenerate, it follows
w=0
. Now, if suppose that
x\in V
, then
\widetilde{H}(w,x)=0
. Thus, it follows that
T_{S}(v,x)=0
. Since
{T_{s}}\,|_{V\times V}
is non-degenerate, it follows that
v=0
. Thus
B_{A}
is non-degenerate.

Finally, since
A^{2}\subseteq V
,
\widetilde{H}(V,A)=\widetilde{H}(A,V)=\{0\}
and
T_{s}
is invariant, we have
B_{A}(xy,z)=T_{s}(xy,z)=T_{s}(x,yz)=B_{A}(x,yz)
for all
x,y,z\in A
.
Then
B_{A}
is invariant. Thus
(A,B_{A})
is a quadratic algebra as required.
∎

Definition 3.14
.

Let
A
be a non-associative algebra,
(V_{1},r_{1},l_{1})
and
(V_{2},r_{2},l_{2})
be two
A
-bimodules.
A linear map
\displaystyle\Phi:V_{1}\longrightarrow V_{2}
is a
morphism
of
A
-bimodules
if for all
x\in A,v_{1}\in V_{1}
,

(3.18)

\begin{split}\Phi(r_{1}(x)(v_{1}))=r_{2}(x)(\Phi(v_{1}))\mbox{ and }\Phi(l_{1}(x)(v_{1}))=l_{2}(x)(\Phi(v_{1})).\end{split}

More,
\displaystyle\Phi
is called an
isomorphism
of
A
-bimodules if it is also bijective.

A consequence of the previous theorem is the following result for
A
-bimodules.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
A
be an algebra,
\rmL
and
\rmR
be the operators of left and right multiplication, respectively. Then there exists a bilinear form
B_{A}:A\times A\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
such that
(A,B_{A})
is quadratic if and only if
(A,\rmR,\rmL)
and
(A^{\ast},\rmR^{\ast},\rmL^{\ast})
are isomorphic
A
-bimodules.

4.
Nearly associative
L
-algebras

In this section, we introduce the class of nearly associative
L
-algebras, which we show to be the appropriate class for presenting the notion of bialgebra in our context. Furthermore, we highlight some interesting properties of this class of algebras.

Definition 4.1
.

A non-associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
is called
nearly associative
L
-algebra
(or simply
NAL
-algebra
for short) if it is a nearly associative algebra and an
L
-algebra simultaneously, i.e., the following conditions are both satisfied

\displaystyle(xy)z=y(zx),

\displaystyle x(yz)=y(xz),

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 4.2
.

The class of nearly associative
L
-algebras is a proper subclass of the classes of nearly associative algebras and
L
-algebras. In fact, not all nearly associative algebras are also
L
-algebras (e.g., the algebra in Example
2.11
), and vice versa, not all
L
-algebras are nearly associative algebras (e.g., the algebra in Example
2.12
).

Example 4.3
.

Let
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-]_{\mathfrak{g}})
be the Heisenberg Lie algebra of dimension three, i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}
is spanned by
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
such that
[e_{1},e_{2}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=e_{3}
,
[e_{1},e_{3}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=[e_{2},e_{3}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=0
, and consider the symmetric bilinear map
\varphi:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
such that

\varphi(e_{1},e_{1})=\varphi(e_{1},e_{2})=\varphi(e_{2},e_{2})=1,\quad\varphi(e_{1},e_{3})=\varphi(e_{2},e_{3})=\varphi(e_{3},e_{3})=0.

Then by
[
8
, Example 3.15]
the vector space
\mathcal{A}:=\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathbb{K}
endowed with the product
\cdot:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}
defined by

(x+v)\cdot(y+w):=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\varphi(x,y),

for
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
,
v,w\in\mathbb{K}
, is a four-dimensional nearly associative
L
-algebra that is in particular associative.

Remark 4.4
.

By
[
8
, Corollary 3.11]
any associative
LR
-algebra is a nearly associative
L
-algebra. However, not all nearly associative algebras that are associative are also
LR
-algebras (cf.
[
8
, Example 8.24]
).

Now, we give an example of a six-dimensional nearly associative
L
-algebra that is not associative.

Example 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a vector space of dimension six with basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4},e_{5},e_{6}\}
.
If we equipped
\mathcal{A}
with the linear product defined by

\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}=e_{3},\quad e_{2}e_{1}=e_{4},\quad e_{2}e_{2}=e_{5},\quad e_{1}e_{5}=e_{5}e_{1}=e_{2}e_{3}=e_{4}e_{2}=e_{6},\quad e_{2}e_{4}=e_{3}e_{2}=2e_{6},

and all other products equal to
0
, then
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative algebra which is not associative (cf.
[
8
, Example 5.2]
). Also,
\mathcal{A}
is an
L
-algebra. In fact, by easy calculation we get that
e_{i}(e_{j}e_{k})=0
if either one of the indices
i,j,k
is not in
\{1,2\}
or two of the indices
i,j,k
are equal to
1
, and
e_{1}(e_{2}e_{2})=e_{6}=e_{2}(e_{1}e_{2}).

Thus
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative
L
-algebra.

Remark 4.6
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative
L
-algebra, then by Lemma
2.13

\mathcal{A}
is an
R
-algebra, and as a consequence
\mathcal{A}
is
LR
-algebra, i.e.,
(xy)z=(xz)y
for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 4.7
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative
L
-algebra, then

(4.19)

\displaystyle\rmL_{x}\rmL_{y}=\rmL_{y}\rmL_{x}=\rmR_{x}\rmR_{y}=\rmR_{y}\rmR_{x},

(4.20)

\displaystyle\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}=\rmL_{xy}=\rmR_{yx}=\rmR_{y}\rmL_{x},

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

By the above remark
\mathcal{A}
is an
LR
-algebra, then
\rmL_{x}\rmL_{y}=\rmL_{y}\rmL_{x}
and
\rmR_{x}\rmR_{y}=\rmR_{y}\rmR_{x}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Also,
\mathcal{A}
is in particular a nearly associative algebra, then by Condition (
2.4
) we have that
\rmL_{y}\rmL_{x}=\rmR_{x}\rmR_{y}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus, Conditions (
4.19
) are satisfied.

Now, we prove Conditions (
4.20
). For all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
,

\rmL_{xy}(z)=(xy)z=y(zx)=z(yx)=\rmR_{yx}(z).

Also, since
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative algebra, from Conditions (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) we have that
\rmL_{xy}=\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}
and
\rmR_{yx}=\rmR_{y}\rmL_{x}
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus Conditions (
4.20
) hold.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

As a direct consequence of Proposition
4.7
, we have that if
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative
L
-algebra, then

(4.21)

(xy)z=z(yx),

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.

The following proposition, concerning also the products defined in (
2.1
), shows some direct consequences of the definition of nearly associative
L
-algebras.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative
L
-algebra. Then:

(1)

\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}\subseteq Z(\mathcal{A}^{-})
;

(2)

[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}]\subseteq\ann(\mathcal{A}^{+})
;

(3)

\displaystyle\asso^{+}(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{2}\asso(x,y,z)=[y,zx]
, for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
. Then by Remark
4.8
we have

\displaystyle[x\bullet y,z]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:A-A+}}{=}\frac{1}{4}\Big((xy)z+(yx)z-z(xy)-z(yx)\Big)\overset{\eqref{eq: quasi-comm}}{=}\frac{1}{4}\Big((xy)z+(yx)z-(yx)z-(xy)z\Big)=0,

and

\displaystyle[x,y]\bullet z

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:A-A+}}{=}\frac{1}{4}\Big((xy)z-(yx)z+z(xy)-z(yx)\Big)\overset{\eqref{eq: quasi-comm}}{=}\frac{1}{4}\Big((xy)z-(yx)z+(yx)z-(xy)z\Big)=0.

Thus Conditions (1) and (2) are satisfied. Moreover, by Remark
4.8
and Proposition
2.3

\displaystyle\asso^{+}(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=(x\bullet y)\bullet z-x\bullet(y\bullet z)\overset{\eqref{eq:A-A+}}{=}\frac{1}{4}\Big((xy)z+(yx)z+z(xy)+z(yx)-x(yz)-x(zy)-(yz)x-(zy)x\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big((xy)z-x(yz)+z(yx)-(zy)x\Big)\overset{\eqref{eq: quasi-comm}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\Big((xy)z-x(yz)\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\asso(x,y,z)\overset{\eqref{eq:condit1}}{=}[y,zx].

∎

As a direct consequence of (2) of Proposition
2.7
and (1) of Proposition
4.9
we get the following.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative
L
-algebra. Then:

(1)

\asso^{+}(x\bullet t,y,z)=\asso^{+}(x,y\bullet t,z)=\asso^{+}(x,y,z\bullet t)=0
, for all

;

(2)

(\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A},\bullet)
is an associative subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}^{+}
.

As a consequence of Proposition
2.4
and Condition (1) of Proposition
4.9
we have the following.

Corollary 4.11
.

Any nearly associative
L
-algebra is flexible.

As a result of Proposition
2.6
and Conditions (1) and (2) of Proposition

, we derive the following.

Corollary 4.12
.

Any nearly associative
L
-algebra is a Lie-Poisson-Jordan admissible algebra.

Recall that a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-])
is
3
-nilpotent if
[[[x,y],z],t]=0
for all
x,y,z,t\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then by (2) of Proposition
2.7
, (1) of Proposition
4.9
and Corollary
4.12
we get the following.

Corollary 4.13
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative
L
-algebra, then
\mathcal{A}^{-}
is a
3
-nilpotent Lie algebra.

Recall that a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-])
is
2
-nilpotent if
[[x,y],z]=0
for all
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then we obtain the following as a direct consequence of Condition (3) of Proposition
4.9
and Corollary
4.12
.

Corollary 4.14
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative
L
-algebra. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{A}
is an associative algebra;

(2)

\mathcal{A}^{+}
is a commutative associative algebra;

(3)

\mathcal{A}^{-}
is a
2
-nilpotent Lie algebra.

Next, we present another property of the underlying Lie algebra
\mathcal{A}^{-}
of a nearly associative
L
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
. To this end, we recall the definition of a central extension of a Lie algebra.
Let
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-]_{\mathfrak{g}})
be a Lie algebra,
V
be a vector space, and
\omega:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow V
a bilinear map. Then the vector space
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathfrak{g}\oplus V
endowed with the product defined by

[x+v,y+w]_{\mathfrak{h}}:=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\omega(x,y),

for
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and
v,w\in V
, is a Lie algebra if and only if
\omega
is a 2-cocycle of
\mathfrak{g}
, i.e., for
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
,

\omega(x,y)=-\omega(y,x),

\omega([x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}},z)+\omega([y,z]_{\mathfrak{g}},x)+\omega([z,x]_{\mathfrak{g}},y)=0.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
is called the
central extension
of
\mathfrak{g}
by
V
by means of
\omega
.
Remark that
\mathfrak{h}
is called “central” because
V\subseteq Z(\mathfrak{h}).

Also, it is referred to as an “extension” since it fits into the short exact sequence
V\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{h}\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{g}.

Indeed, the cocycle
\omega
arises from the canonical projection
\pi:\mathfrak{h}\longrightarrow V
via the relation
\omega(x,y)=\pi([x,y]).

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a nearly associative
L
-algebra. Then
(\mathcal{A}^{-},[-,-])
is the central extension of a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-]_{\mathfrak{g}})
by means of a
2
-cocycle
\omega
.

Proof.

There exists a vector subspace
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{-}
such that
\mathcal{A}^{-}=\mathfrak{g}\oplus(\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A})
as vector spaces. Since by (1) of Proposition
4.9

\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}\subseteq Z(\mathcal{A}^{-})
, we may have for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and
a,b\in\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}

[x+a,y+b]:=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\omega(x,y),

where
[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\omega(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}
.
We claim that
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-]_{\mathfrak{g}})
is a Lie algebra and
\omega:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}
is a
2
-cocycle, and this will complete the proof.
Since
(\mathcal{A}^{-},[-,-])
is a Lie algebra,
for
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and
a,b\in\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\textasciidieresis[x+a,y+b]=-[y+b,x+a]\;\Longrightarrow[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\omega(x,y)=-[y,x]_{\mathfrak{g}}-\omega(y,x),

and consequently
[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}=-[y,x]_{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\omega(x,y)=-\omega(y,x)
. Also, for
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
and
a,b,c\in\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}
, from

\displaystyle\textasciidieresis[x+a,[y+b,z+c]]+[y+b,[z+c,x+a]]+[z+c,[x+a,y+b]]=0,

we get

\displaystyle[x,[y,z]_{\mathfrak{g}}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+[y,[z,x]_{\mathfrak{g}}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+[z,[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\omega(x,[y,z]_{\mathfrak{g}})+\omega(y,[z,x]_{\mathfrak{g}})+\omega(z,[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}})=0.

Thus
(\mathfrak{g},[-,-]_{\mathfrak{g}})
satisfies the Jacobi identity and
\omega([x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}},z)+\omega([y,z]_{\mathfrak{g}},x)+\omega([z,x]_{\mathfrak{g}},y)=0
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 4.16
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
in the Proposition
4.15
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{A}^{-}/(\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A})
.

We conclude this section with a characterization of nearly associative
L
-algebras.

Proposition 4.17
.

An algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a nearly associative
L
-algebra if and only if the following four conditions hold:

(1)

\mathcal{A}^{-}
is a Lie algebra;

(2)

\mathcal{A}\bullet\mathcal{A}\subseteq Z(\mathcal{A}^{-})
;

(3)

[\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}]\subseteq\ann(\mathcal{A}^{+})
;

(4)

\asso^{+}(x,y,z)=[y,zx]
, for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

By Propositions
2.6
and
4.9
, it is clear that if
\mathcal{A}
is a
NAL
-algebra then the four conditions above are satisfied.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies these four conditions. First notice that from Condition (2) it follows that
[y,xz]=-[y,zx]
for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
, then

[y,[z,x]]=\frac{1}{2}\Big([y,zx]-[y,xz]\Big)=[y,zx].

Thus, by Condition (4), we have for
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}

(4.22)

\asso^{+}(x,y,z)=[y,[z,x]],

which ensures the second condition of Proposition
2.7
.
Moreover, from Conditions (2) and (3) we have that
[x,y]\bullet z+[x\bullet y,z]+[x,z]\bullet y+[x\bullet z,y]=0,
for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
, that guarantees the third condition of Proposition
2.7
.
Also, by Condition (1)
\mathcal{A}^{-}
is a Lie algebra. Thus, the three conditions of Proposition
2.7
are satisfied and, as a consequence,
A
is a nearly associative algebra.

It remains to prove that
\mathcal{A}
is also an
L
-algebra. From Conditions (2) and (3) and using (
2.2
),
for every
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
we have

\displaystyle x(yz)

\displaystyle=x[y,z]+x(y\bullet z)=[x,[y,z]]+x\bullet[y,z]+[x,y\bullet z]+x\bullet(y\bullet z)=[x,[y,z]]+x\bullet(y\bullet z),

and, by switching

with

, we also have
y(xz)=[y,[x,z]]+y\bullet(x\bullet z)
.
Since
[-,-]
is anti-commutative and
\bullet
is commutative, using Condition (
4.22
), we have

\displaystyle x(yz)-y(xz)

\displaystyle=[x,[y,z]]+x\bullet(y\bullet z)-[y,[x,z]]-y\bullet(x\bullet z)=[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+(y\bullet z)\bullet x-y\bullet(z\bullet x)

\displaystyle=[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+\asso^{+}(y,z,x)\overset{\eqref{eq:1ab}}{=}[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+[z,[x,y]].

Therefore, since
[-,-]
is a Lie structure on

\mathcal{A}
, we get

for all

, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}
is an

∎

5.
Nearly coassociative coalgebras,
L
-coalgebras and
R
-coalgebras

This section aims to introduce the notions of nearly coassociative coalgebras,
L
-coalgebras and
R
-coalgebras and present some of their properties.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an algebra and
\Delta_{A}:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
be a linear map called
comultiplication
. Then the pair
(A,\Delta_{A})
is called a
coalgebra
. In the following, we use Sweedler’s notation, so we write
\Delta_{A}(x)=\sum_{(x)}x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}
,
where
x_{(1)}
and
x_{(2)}
are elements in
A
.

In the following, denote by
\displaystyle\tau:A\otimes A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
the linear map (the so-called
twist map
) given by
\tau(x\otimes y):=y\otimes x
, for
\displaystyle x,y\in A
, by

\displaystyle\xi:A\otimes A\otimes A\longrightarrow A\otimes A\otimes A
the linear cycle map defined by
\xi(x\otimes y\otimes z):=y\otimes z\otimes x
, for
\displaystyle x,y,z\in A
, and by
\mbox{I}:A\longrightarrow A
the identity map on
A
.

Let us denote by
\displaystyle m:A\otimes A\longrightarrow A
the bilinear product on
A
, that is,
m(x\otimes y):=x\cdot y,\,
for
\displaystyle x,y\in A
, i.e.,
(A,m=\cdot)
. Then the condition in the definition of nearly associative algebra may be written as:

\begin{split}m(m\otimes\mbox{I})&=m(\mbox{I}\otimes m)\xi.\end{split}

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a vector space provided with a comultiplication
\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is a
nearly coassociative coalgebra
if

\begin{split}(\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}&=\xi(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}.\end{split}

Similarly, the condition of
L
-algebra may be written as:

\begin{split}m(\mbox{I}\otimes m)&=m(\mbox{I}\otimes m)(\tau\otimes\mbox{I}),\end{split}

and the condition of
R
-algebra may be written as:

\begin{split}m(m\otimes\mbox{I})&=m(m\otimes\mbox{I})(\mbox{I}\otimes\tau).\end{split}

Definition 5.2
.

Let
A
be a vector space provided with a comultiplication
\displaystyle\Delta_{A}:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
. We say that the pair
(A,\Delta_{A})
is an:

•

L
-coalgebra
if

(5.23)

\begin{split}(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{A})\Delta_{A}&=(\tau\otimes\mbox{I})(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{A})\Delta_{A}.\end{split}

•

R
-coalgebra
if

(5.24)

\begin{split}(\Delta_{A}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{A}&=(\mbox{I}\otimes\tau)(\Delta_{A}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{A}.\end{split}

•

LR
-coalgebra
if it is an
L
-coalgebra and an
R
-coalgebra simultaneously, i.e., Conditions (
5.23
) and (
5.24
) are both satisfied.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a non-associative algebra, and assume a non-associative algebra structure in the dual vector space
(A^{*},\star)
. In the following, we denote
f(x)
by either
\langle f,x\rangle\,
or
\langle x,f\rangle
, for
x\in A
and
f\in A^{\ast}
. Moreover, we identify the finite dimensional algebra
A
and its bi-dual space
A^{**}=(A^{*})^{*}
by the isomorphism of vector space

A\longrightarrow(A^{*})^{*}
given by
x\mapsto\langle x,-\rangle.

There is a natural way to define a coalgebra structure on
A
from the algebra structure of its dual
A^{*}
.
From the multiplication

\displaystyle\star:A^{\ast}\otimes A^{\ast}\longrightarrow A^{\ast}
on
\displaystyle A^{\ast}
we infer a comultiplication
\displaystyle\Delta_{A}:={}^{t}\hskip-1.42271pt\star:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
on
A
(using the identification between
A
and the bi-dual
A^{**}=(A^{*})^{*}
)
given by the following relation:

(5.25)

\begin{split}\langle\Delta_{A}(x),f\otimes g\rangle&=\langle x,f\star g\rangle,\end{split}

for
x\in A
and
f,g\in A^{\ast}
. On the other way around, we may also endow the dual of a coalgebra with an algebra structure. Given a coalgebra
(A,\Delta_{A})
, then
(A^{\ast},\star)
is an algebra where the multiplication
\star:A^{\ast}\otimes A^{\ast}\longrightarrow A^{\ast}
comes from (
5.25
).

Similarly, we can also consider a coalgebra structure on the dual
A^{*}
from the algebra structure of
A
, by taking into account the
comultiplication
\displaystyle\Delta_{A^{\ast}}:A^{\ast}\longrightarrow A^{\ast}\otimes A^{\ast}
on
A^{\ast}
given by

\begin{split}\langle\Delta_{A^{\ast}}(f),x\otimes y\rangle&:=\langle f,x\cdot y\rangle,\end{split}

for
x,y\in A
and
f\in A^{\ast}
.

Actually, we can establish the next result.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(A,\Delta_{A})
be a coalgebra and
(A^{*},\star)
be an algebra, where

\Delta_{A}
and
\star
are connected by the relation (
5.25
). Then we have the following assertions.

(1)

(A,\Delta_{A})
is a nearly coassociative coalgebra if and only if
(A^{*},\star)
is a nearly associative algebra.

(2)

(A,\Delta_{A})
is an
L
-coalgebra if and only if
(A^{*},\star)
is an
L
-algebra.

(3)

(A,\Delta_{A})
is an
R
-coalgebra if and only if
(A^{*},\star)
is an
R
-algebra.

Proof.

It follows from straightforward calculations.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
2.13
and Proposition
5.3
we have the following.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})
be a nearly coassociative coalgebra. Then
(\mathcal{A},\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})
is an
L
-coalgebra if and only if it is an
R
-coalgebra.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a vector space provided with a comultiplication
\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is a
nearly coassociative
L
-coalgebra
if it is a nearly coassociative coalgebra and an
L
-coalgebra simultaneously, i.e., the following conditions are both satisfied

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\xi(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}},

\displaystyle(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=(\tau\otimes\mbox{I})(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}.

Remark 5.6
.

If
(\mathcal{A},\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})
is a nearly coassociative

-coalgebra, then as a consequence of Corollary

it follows that

is also an
R
-coalgebra.

Example 5.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a vector space of dimension four with basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
and consider the comultiplication

Then

is a nearly coassociative

Let

6.
Nearly associative bialgebras

This section aims to introduce the notion of nearly associative bialgebra and give a characterization. Moreover, we show that it is equivalent to the concept of
R
-bialgebra,
L
-bialgebra, and then
LR
-bialgebra.

Drinfeld introduced the notion of Lie bialgebra in
[
15
]
to study solutions to the classical Yang-Baxter equation.
After that, Zhelyabin generalized this construction to an arbitrary variety
[
19
]
. In the following, we use this construction to define the class of nearly associative bialgebras.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be a non-associative algebra, and assume a non-associative algebra structure in the dual vector space
(A^{*},\star)
.
On the vector space
\rmD(A):=A\oplus A^{*}
, we consider the following product:

(6.26)

(x+f)\diamond(y+g):=\underbrace{x\cdot y+\rmL_{f}^{*}(y)+\rmR_{g}^{*}(x)}_{\in\;A}+\underbrace{f\star g+\rmL_{x}^{*}(g)+\rmR_{y}^{*}(f)}_{\in\;A^{*}},

for
x,y\in A,f,g\in A^{*}
.
Remark that the elements
\rmL_{f}^{*}(y)
and
\rmR_{g}^{*}(x)
belong to
A
, using the identification between
A
and
(A^{*})^{*}
. Indeed, for
h\in A^{*}
we have

\rmL_{f}^{*}(y)(h)=\langle y,\rmR_{f}(h)\rangle=\langle y,h\star f\rangle\;\mbox{ and }\;\rmR_{g}^{*}(x)(h)=\langle x,\rmL_{g}(h)\rangle=\langle x,g\star h\rangle.

Thus
(\rmD(A),\diamond)
is an algebra, and both
(A,\cdot)
and
({A}^{*},\star)
are its subalgebras.

Now, let us consider

B:\rmD(A)\times\rmD(A)\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
given by

(6.27)

B(x+f,y+g):=\langle f,y\rangle+\langle g,x\rangle,

for
x,y\in A,f,g\in A^{*}
.
It is easy to see that
B
is a symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form such that

B\Big((x+f)\diamond(y+g),z+l\Big)=B\Big(x+f,(y+g)\diamond(z+l)\Big),

whenever
x,y,z\in A,f,g,l\in A^{*}
. Hence
(\rmD(A),\diamond,B)
is a quadratic algebra.

Now, we recall the definition of bialgebras for an arbitrary variety presented by Zhelyabin in
[
19
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

Suppose that
M
is a variety of algebras, and
(A,\cdot)
and
(A^{*},\star)
are algebras in
M
, where
A^{*}
is the dual vector space of
A
. Then
(A,\cdot,\Delta_{A})
, where
\Delta_{A}={}^{t}\hskip-1.42271pt\star
, is called an
M
-bialgebra
(or a
bialgebra of type
M
)
if the algebra
(\rmD(A),\diamond)
belongs to

M
.

In the context of quadratic algebras, by Theorem
2.16
, the notions of nearly associative algebra and
LR
-algebra are equivalent. Thus, since
(\rmD(A),\diamond,B)
, where
B
is the the bilinear form defined by (
6.27
), is a quadratic algebra such that
(A,\cdot)
and
(A^{*},\star)
are both subalgebras of
(\rmD(A),\diamond)
, the notion of nearly associative bialgebra only makes sense if
(A,\cdot)
and
(A^{*},\star)
are both nearly associative algebras and
LR
-algebras.
Moreover, by Lemma
2.13
, the notions of nearly associative
L
-algebra and nearly associative
R
-algebra are equivalent. Then the notion of nearly associative bialgebra makes sense if
(A,\cdot)
and
(A^{*},\star)
are nearly associative
L
-algebras (
NAL
-algebras), or equivalently, nearly associative
R
-algebras.
Let us write the definition of bialgebra that we will use specifically for the case of nearly associative algebras.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
and
(\mathcal{A}^{*},\star)
be nearly associative
L
-algebras, where
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the dual vector space of
\mathcal{A}
. Then
(\mathcal{A},\cdot,\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})
, where
\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}={}^{t}\hskip-1.42271pt\star
, is called
nearly associative bialgebra

if
(\rmD(\mathcal{A}),\diamond)
is a nearly associative algebra.

Remark 6.3
.

Since
(\rmD(\mathcal{A}),\diamond,B)
is a quadratic algebra, where the bilinear form
B
is defined by (
6.27
), by Theorem
2.16

(\rmD(\mathcal{A}),\diamond)
is a nearly associative algebra if and only if
(\rmD(\mathcal{A}),\diamond)
is an
L
-algebra if and only if
(\rmD(\mathcal{A}),\diamond)
is an
R
-algebra. So, the concepts of the nearly associative bialgebra,
R
-bialgebra,
L
-bialgebra, and then
LR
-bialgebra are all equivalent.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
and
(\mathcal{A}^{*},\star)
be two nearly associative
L
-algebras (where
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the dual vector space of
\mathcal{A}
). Then
(\mathcal{A},\cdot,\Delta_{\mathcal{A}})
, where
\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}={}^{t}\hskip-1.42271pt\star
, is a nearly associative bialgebra if and only if the following conditions hold: for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}

(6.28)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x\cdot y)=\;(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=\;\tau(\rmL_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),

(6.29)

\displaystyle(

\displaystyle\rmR_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{x})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=\;(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=\;\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+\tau(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y).

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and
f,g\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
. We claim that

(6.30)

\displaystyle(x\diamond y)\diamond f=(x\diamond f)\diamond y\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x\cdot y)=(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),

(6.31)

\displaystyle(f\diamond x)\diamond y=(f\diamond y)\diamond x\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;(\rmR_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{x})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),

(6.32)

\displaystyle(f\diamond g)\diamond x=(f\diamond x)\diamond g\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x\cdot y)=\tau(\rmL_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),

(6.33)

\displaystyle(x\diamond f)\diamond g=(x\diamond g)\diamond f\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+\tau(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y).

This will prove that
(\rmD(\mathcal{A}),\diamond)
is an

and Remark

First, we show the Equivalence (
6.30
).
For all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and

\displaystyle(x\diamond y)\diamond f=(x\diamond f)\diamond y

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\rmR^{*}_{f}(x\cdot y)+\rmL^{*}_{x\cdot y}(f)=\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y+\rmL^{*}_{L^{*}_{x}(f)}(y)+\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmL^{*}_{x}(f))

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\begin{cases}\rmR^{*}_{f}(x\cdot y)=\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y+\rmL^{*}_{L^{*}_{x}(f)}(y)\in\mathcal{A}\\
\rmL^{*}_{x\cdot y}(f)=\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmL^{*}_{x}(f))\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\end{cases}.

Now, for all
z\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\langle\rmL^{*}_{x\cdot y}(f),z\rangle=\langle\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmL^{*}_{x}(f)),z\rangle\Longleftrightarrow\langle\rmR_{x\cdot y}(z),f\rangle=\langle\rmL_{y}(z),\rmL^{*}_{x}(f)\rangle\Longleftrightarrow\langle\rmR_{x\cdot y}(z),f\rangle=\langle\rmR_{x}(\rmL_{y}(z)),f\rangle

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\langle z\cdot(x\cdot y),f\rangle=\langle(y\cdot z)\cdot x,f\rangle\Longleftrightarrow z\cdot(x\cdot y)=(y\cdot z)\cdot x,

i.e.,
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
is a nearly associative algebra.
Moreover, for all
h\in\mathcal{A}^{*}

(6.34)

\langle\rmR^{*}_{f}(x\cdot y),h\rangle=\langle\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y,h\rangle+\langle\rmL^{*}_{L^{*}_{x}(f)}(y),h\rangle.

The left-hand side of (
6.34
) is
\langle\rmR^{*}_{f}(x\cdot y),h\rangle=\langle x\cdot y,f\star h\rangle=\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x\cdot y),f\otimes h\rangle
. On the right-hand, we get

\displaystyle\langle\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y,h\rangle+\langle\rmL^{*}_{L^{*}_{x}(f)}(y),h\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y,h\rangle+\langle\rmL^{*}_{f\rmR_{x}}(y),h\rangle=\langle\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y,h\rangle+\langle y,h\star f\rmR_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(h),\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\otimes y\rangle+\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),h\otimes f\rmR_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{(h)}h_{(1)}\otimes h_{(2)},\rmR^{*}_{f}(x)\otimes y\rangle+\langle\sum_{(y)}y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rmR_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(h)}\langle x,f\star h_{(1)}\rangle\,\langle y,h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)},h\rangle\,\langle y_{(2)}\cdot x,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(h)}\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x),f\otimes h_{(1)}\rangle\,\langle y,h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle\tau(y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)}\cdot x),f\otimes h\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(1)},f\rangle\,\langle x_{(2)}\otimes y,\sum_{(h)}h_{(1)}\otimes h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle\tau(y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)}\cdot x),f\otimes h\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(1)},f\rangle\,\langle x_{(2)}\otimes y,\Delta_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(h)\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle\tau(y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)}\cdot x),f\otimes h\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(1)},f\rangle\,\langle x_{(2)}\cdot y,h\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle\tau(y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)}\cdot x),f\otimes h\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{(x)}x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}\cdot y,f\otimes h\rangle+\langle\sum_{(y)}\tau(y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)}\cdot x),f\otimes h\rangle.

Therefore, Equation (
6.34
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x\cdot y)=\sum_{(x)}x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}\cdot y+\tau\big(\sum_{(y)}y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)}\cdot x\big)\Longleftrightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x\cdot y)=(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y).

This proves the Equivalence (
6.30
), as required.

Now, let us show Equivalence (
6.31
). For all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and
f\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, we have

(f\diamond x)\diamond y=\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\diamond y+R^{*}_{x}(f)\diamond y=\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{x}(f)}(y)+\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmR^{*}_{x}(f)),

and similar for
(f\diamond y)\diamond x
just by exchanging
x
with
y
. Thus,

\displaystyle(f\diamond x)\diamond y=(f\diamond y)\diamond x\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\begin{cases}\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{x}(f)}(y)=\rmL^{*}_{f}(y)\cdot x+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{y}(f)}(x)\in\mathcal{A}\\
\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmR^{*}_{x}(f))=\rmR^{*}_{x}(\rmR^{*}_{y}(f))\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\end{cases}

Now, for all
z\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\langle\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmR^{*}_{x}(f)),z\rangle=\langle\rmL_{y}(z),\rmR^{*}_{x}(f)\rangle=\langle\rmL_{x}(\rmL_{y}(z)),f\rangle=\langle x\cdot(y\cdot z),f\rangle,

and similarly
\langle\rmR^{*}_{x}(\rmR^{*}_{y}(f)),z\rangle=\langle y\cdot(x\cdot z),f\rangle
. Thus,

\rmR^{*}_{y}(\rmR^{*}_{x}(f))=\rmR^{*}_{x}(\rmR^{*}_{y}(f))\;\Longleftrightarrow\;x\cdot(y\cdot z)=y\cdot(x\cdot z)

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{A}
and
f\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, i.e.,
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
is an
L
-algebra.
Moreover, for all
h\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
we get

\begin{split}\langle\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{x}(f)}(y),h\rangle&=\langle\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y,h\rangle+\langle\rmL^{*}_{f\rmL_{x}}(y),h\rangle=\langle\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y,h\rangle+\langle y,h\star f\rmL_{x}\rangle\\
&=\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(h),\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\otimes y\rangle+\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),h\otimes f\rmL_{x}\rangle\\
&=\langle\sum_{(h)}h_{(1)}\otimes h_{(2)},\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\otimes y\rangle+\langle\sum_{(y)}y_{(1)}\otimes y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rmL_{x}\rangle\\
&=\sum_{(h)}\langle x,h_{(1)}\star f\rangle\,\langle y,h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)},h\rangle\,\langle x\cdot y_{(2)},f\rangle\\
&=\sum_{(h)}\langle\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x),h_{(1)}\otimes f\rangle\,\langle y,h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)}\otimes x\cdot y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle\\
&=\sum_{(h)}\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(1)},h_{(1)}\rangle\,\langle x_{(2)},f\rangle\,\langle y,h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)}\otimes x\cdot y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle\\
&=\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(2)},f\rangle\,\langle x_{(1)}\otimes y,\sum_{(h)}h_{(1)}\otimes h_{(2)}\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)}\otimes x\cdot y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle\\
&=\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(2)},f\rangle\,\langle x_{(1)}\otimes y,\Delta_{\mathcal{A}^{*}}(h)\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)}\otimes x\cdot y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle\\
&=\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(2)},f\rangle\,\langle x_{(1)}\cdot y,h\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)}\otimes x\cdot y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle\\
&=\langle\sum_{(x)}x_{(1)}\cdot y\otimes x_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle+\sum_{(y)}\langle y_{(1)}\otimes x\cdot y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle.\end{split}

Doing similar calculations, just by exchanging
x
with
y
, we obtain that

\langle\rmL^{*}_{f}(y)\cdot x+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{y}(f)}(x),h\rangle=\langle\sum_{(y)}y_{(1)}\cdot x\otimes y_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle+\sum_{(x)}\langle x_{(1)}\otimes y\cdot x_{(2)},h\otimes f\rangle.

Thus, we conclude that, for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
,
f\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\rmL^{*}_{f}(x)\cdot y+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{x}(f)}(y)=\rmL^{*}_{f}(y)\cdot x+\rmL^{*}_{R^{*}_{y}(f)}(x)\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{x})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y)=(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(x)+(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}(y),

which proves Equivalence (
6.31
), as desired. The two remaining equivalences are proved similarly to those shown above, finishing the proof.
∎

7.
Coboundary nearly associative bialgebras,
LR
-Yang-Baxter equation, and
r-
matrices

In the previous section, we studied nearly associative bialgebras in general, now we focus on a special class of them, namely coboundary nearly associative bialgebras. This class gives us a natural framework to investigate the analog of the Yang-Baxter equation and the matrix
r
in our context.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an algebra and consider
r=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes b_{i}\in A\otimes A
. Since in general in our structure we do not have the unit, we write directly the following elements in
A\otimes A\otimes A
(as in
[
19
]
):

\displaystyle r_{12}r_{23}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j},

\displaystyle r_{13}r_{12}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i},

\displaystyle r_{23}r_{13}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{j}\otimes a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot b_{j}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{LR}(r):=r_{12}r_{23}-r_{13}r_{12}-r_{23}r_{13}.

Recall that
\displaystyle r\in A\otimes A
is
skew-symmetric
if
\displaystyle r+\tau(r)=0
, where
\tau:A\otimes A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
is the twisted map.
Now, we define the comultiplication
\displaystyle\Delta_{r}:A\longrightarrow A\otimes A
by, for
x\in A
,

(7.35)

\begin{split}\Delta_{r}(x)&:=(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I}-\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big).\end{split}

Also, for
x\in A
, we consider the operator
\ad_{x}:=\rmL_{x}-\rmR_{x}
of
A
, i.e.,
\ad_{x}(y)=x\cdot y-y\cdot x
for all
y\in A
. Moreover, as in Section
5
, we denote the product “
\cdot
” of
A
also by
m:A\otimes A\longrightarrow A
, that is,
m(x\otimes y):=x\cdot y,\,
for
x,y\in A
, i.e.,
(A,m=\cdot)
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
(A,m=\cdot)
be an algebra and
r\in A\otimes A
be skew-symmetric. If
\Delta_{r}
is the comultiplication defined by (
7.35
), then
(A,\Delta_{r})
is a nearly coassociative coalgebra if and only if, for all
x\in A
,

\Big(\big(m(\ad_{x}\otimes\mbox{I})\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}+m\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x}\big)(\mbox{I}\otimes\xi)-\big(\mbox{I}\otimes m(\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})\otimes\mbox{I}+\mbox{I}\otimes m\otimes\ad_{x}\big)\Big)(r\otimes r)=0.

Proof.

Let
x\in A
, by (
7.35
) we have

\displaystyle(\Delta_{r}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{r}\otimes\mbox{I})\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\Delta_{r}(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{i}-\Delta_{r}(a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(((x\cdot a_{i})\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(x\cdot a_{i}))\otimes b_{i}-(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)+a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)

and

\displaystyle\xi(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{r})\Delta_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=\xi(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{r})\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)\Big)=\xi\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes\Delta_{r}(b_{i})-a_{i}\otimes\Delta_{r}(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\xi\Big(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}-(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot b_{i})-a_{i}\otimes((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}+a_{i}\otimes a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big((b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot b_{i})\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})-((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes a_{i}+a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))\otimes a_{i}\Big).

Then by Definition
5.1

(A,\Delta_{r})
is a nearly coassociative coalgebra (i.e.,
(\Delta_{r}\otimes\mbox{I})\Delta_{r}=\xi(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{r})\Delta_{r}
) if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(((x\cdot a_{i})\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}+((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes a_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(-(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)-(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})\Big)

(7.36)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(x\cdot a_{i}))\otimes b_{i}-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))\otimes a_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)+a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot b_{i})\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})\Big)=0.

Now, since
\tau(r)=-r
, we get that Condition (
7.36
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(((x\cdot a_{i})\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}-((a_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big((a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})-(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x))\otimes b_{i}-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(x\cdot a_{i}))\otimes b_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})\Big)=0.

Thus,
(A,\Delta_{r})
is a nearly coassociative coalgebra if and only if

\displaystyle\Big(\big(m(\ad_{x}\otimes\mbox{I})\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}+m\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x}\big)(\mbox{I}\otimes\xi)-\big(\mbox{I}\otimes m(\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})\otimes\mbox{I}+\mbox{I}\otimes m\otimes\ad_{x}\big)\Big)(r\otimes r)=0,

as required.
∎

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
be a nearly associative
L
-algebra and
r\in\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
be skew-symmetric. If
\Delta_{r}
is the comultiplication defined by (
7.35
), then we have the following assertions:

(1)

(2)

(\mathcal{A},\Delta_{r})
is an

\big((\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})-(\mbox{I}\otimes\tau)(\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\big)\mathbf{LR}(r)=0.

. By (

) we have

\displaystyle(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{r})\Delta_{r}(x)

\displaystyle=(\mbox{I}\otimes\Delta_{r})\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes\Delta_{r}(b_{i})-a_{i}\otimes\Delta_{r}(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}-(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot b_{i})-a_{i}\otimes((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}+a_{i}\otimes a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))\Big).

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(a_{i}\otimes a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))-a_{j}\otimes a_{i}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))\Big)

(7.37)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(a_{j}\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{j}\cdot b_{i})-(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{j}-a_{i}\otimes((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\Big)=0.

\displaystyle\big(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}-(\tau\otimes\mbox{I})(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})\big)(r_{12}r_{23}-r_{23}r_{13}-r_{13}r_{12})=0,

Theorem 7.10
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
be a nearly associative
L
-algebra and
r\in\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{A}
be skew-symmetric. If
\Delta_{r}
is the comultiplication defined by (
7.35
), then the triple
(\mathcal{A},\cdot,\Delta_{r})
is a coboundary nearly associative bialgebra if and only if the following conditions hold:

(1)

\big((\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})-(\tau\otimes\mbox{I})(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})\big)\mathbf{LR}(r)=0
,

(2)

\Big((m(\ad_{x}\otimes\mbox{I})\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}+m\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})(\mbox{I}\otimes\xi)-(\mbox{I}\otimes m(\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})\otimes\mbox{I}+\mbox{I}\otimes m\otimes\ad_{x})\Big)(r\otimes r)=0
,

(3)

(\rmL_{x}\otimes\rmR_{y}-\rmR_{x}\otimes\rmL_{y})(r)=0
,

(4)

(\ad_{y}\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}-\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x}\ad_{y})(r)=0
,

(5)

(\rmL_{y}\otimes\rmL_{x}-\rmR_{y}\otimes\rmR_{x})(r)=0
,

(6)

(\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}-\tau)(\ad_{x\cdot y}\otimes\mbox{I})(r)=0
,

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.5
, it is enough to prove that the Conditions (1)-(6) are satisfied if and only if
(\mathcal{A},\Delta_{r})
is a nearly coassociative
L
-coalgebra, and the Conditions (
6.28
) and (
6.29
) hold.

By Propositions
7.1
and
7.3

(\mathcal{A},\Delta_{r})
is a nearly coassociative
L
-coalgebra if and only if Conditions (1) and (2) are satisfied. Thus, we have just to prove that the Conditions (3)-(6) are satisfied if and only if the Conditions (
6.28
) and (
6.29
) hold.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
and suppose that the Conditions (
6.28
) and (
6.29
) are satisfied.
By definition of
\Delta_{r}
and since
\tau(r)=-r
, we have that

\displaystyle(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\Delta_{r}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot y)-a_{i}\otimes\big((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot y\big)-(a_{i}\cdot x)\otimes(y\cdot b_{i})+\big((a_{i}\cdot y)\cdot x\big)\otimes b_{i}\Big)

and

\displaystyle\tau(\rmL_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(x)+(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(-a_{i}\otimes\big(y\cdot(x\cdot b_{i})\big)+(a_{i}\cdot x)\otimes(y\cdot b_{i})+\big(x\cdot(y\cdot a_{i})\big)\otimes b_{i}-(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot y)\Big).

Since
\mathcal{A}
is an
NAL
-algebra, we have

\displaystyle(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\Delta_{r}(y)=\tau(\rmL_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(x)+(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot y)-(a_{i}\cdot x)\otimes(y\cdot b_{i})\big)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;2(\rmL_{x}\otimes\rmR_{y}-\rmR_{x}\otimes\rmL_{y})(r)=0.

Since the characteristic of
\mathbb{K}
is different from
2
, Condition (3) is satisfied.

Recall that by Remark
4.6

\mathcal{A}
is also
\rmR
-algebra. Now, since

\Delta_{r}(x\cdot y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big(y\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x)\big)\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes\big((y\cdot b_{i})\cdot x\big)\Big)
,
by Condition (3) and using Proposition
4.7
it follows that

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}(x\cdot y)=(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x})\Delta_{r}(y)

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big(y\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x)\big)\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes\big((y\cdot b_{i})\cdot x\big)+a_{i}\otimes\big((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot y\big)-\big((a_{i}\cdot y)\cdot x\big)\otimes b_{i}\Big)=0

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big(\big(y\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x)\big)-\big((a_{i}\cdot y)\cdot x\big)\big)\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes\big(\big((y\cdot b_{i})\cdot x\big)-\big((b_{i}\cdot x)\cdot y\big)\big)\Big)=0

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\big((\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}-\rmR_{x}\rmR_{y})\otimes\mbox{I}-\mbox{I}\otimes(\rmR_{x}\rmL_{y}-\rmR_{x}\rmR_{y})\big)(r)=0

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\big((\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}-\rmR_{y}\rmR_{x})\otimes\mbox{I}-\mbox{I}\otimes(\rmR_{x}\rmL_{y}-\rmR_{x}\rmR_{y})\big)(r)=0

\displaystyle\;\Longleftrightarrow\;(\ad_{y}\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}-\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x}\ad_{y})(r)=0.

Thus, Condition (4) holds.

Now, by the definition of
\Delta_{r}
and since
\tau(r)=-r
, it follows that

\displaystyle(\rmR_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(x)+(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{x})\Delta_{r}(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(((x\cdot a_{i})\cdot y)\otimes b_{i}-(a_{i}\cdot y)\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)+(y\cdot a_{i})\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})-a_{i}\otimes(x\cdot(b_{i}\cdot y))\Big),

\displaystyle\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+\tau(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((y\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x))\otimes b_{i}-(y\cdot a_{i})\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})+(a_{i}\cdot y)\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)-a_{i}\otimes((y\cdot b_{i})\cdot x)\Big),

and

\displaystyle(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(y\cdot b_{i})-a_{i}\otimes(y\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x))+((y\cdot a_{i})\cdot x)\otimes b_{i}-(a_{i}\cdot x)\otimes(b_{i}\cdot y)\Big).

Since
\mathcal{A}
is an
NAL
-algebra, it follows that

\displaystyle(\rmR_{y}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(x)+(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{x})\Delta_{r}(y)=\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+\tau(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)\;

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big((y\cdot a_{i})\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})-(a_{i}\cdot y)\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;2(\rmL_{y}\otimes\rmL_{x}-\rmR_{y}\otimes\rmR_{x})(r)=0.

Since the characteristic of
\mathbb{K}
is different from
2
, Condition (5) is satisfied.

Now, by Condition (5) and using Proposition
4.7
we have

\displaystyle\tau(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+\tau(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)=(\mbox{I}\otimes\rmL_{y})\Delta_{r}(x)+(\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{ I})\Delta_{r}(y)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big(y\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x)-(y\cdot a_{i})\cdot x\big)\otimes b_{i}+a_{i}\otimes\big(y\cdot(b_{i}\cdot x)-(y\cdot b_{i})\cdot x\big)\Big)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;\big((\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}-\rmR_{x}\rmL_{y})\otimes\mbox{I}+\mbox{I}\otimes(\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}-\rmR_{x}\rmL_{y})\big)(r)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;(\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}-\tau)((\rmL_{y}\rmR_{x}-\rmR_{x}\rmL_{y})\otimes\mbox{I})(r)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;(\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}-\tau)\big((\rmL_{x\cdot y}-\rmR_{x\cdot y})\otimes\mbox{I}\big)(r)=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\;(\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}-\tau)(\ad_{x\cdot y}\otimes\mbox{I})(r)=0.

Thus, Condition (6) is satisfied. So, we proved that if the Conditions (
6.28
) and (
6.29
) are satisfied, then the Conditions (3)-(6) hold. The converse is proven with similar arguments, and this completes the proof.
∎

,

\begin{split}&\big((\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})-(\tau\otimes\mbox{I})(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})\big)\mathbf{LR}(r)=\\
&\quad\quad\quad=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}-\big(x\cdot(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\big)\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}-(x\cdot a_{j})\otimes a_{i}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot b_{j})\Big)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(-(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes(x\cdot a_{i})\otimes b_{j}+b_{j}\otimes\big(x\cdot(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\big)\otimes b_{i}+a_{i}\otimes(x\cdot a_{j})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot b_{j})\Big).\end{split}

Consequently, since
r
is skew-symmetric, by Remark
7.18
, and taking into account (
3.14
), for all
\displaystyle f,g,h\in\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
,
x\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\begin{split}(f\otimes g\otimes h)&\Big(\big((\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})-(\tau\otimes\mbox{I})(\rmL_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I})\big)\mathbf{LR}(r)\Big)=\\
=&\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\Bigl\langle g,f(x\cdot a_{i})h(b_{j})(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle f,g(b_{j})h(b_{i})\big(x\cdot(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\big)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle h,f(x\cdot a_{j})g(a_{i})(b_{i}\cdot b_{j})\Bigr\rangle\Big)\\
&+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(-\Bigl\langle f,g(x\cdot a_{i})h(b_{j})(b_{i}\cdot a_{j})\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle g,f(b_{j})h(b_{i})\big(x\cdot(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\big)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle h,f(a_{i})g(x\cdot a_{j})(b_{i}\cdot b_{j})\Bigr\rangle\Big)\\
=&-\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(f))\mathcal{R}(h)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(f),\mathcal{R}(h)\mathcal{R}(g)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle h,\mathcal{R}(g)\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle\\
&+\Bigl\langle f,\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(g))\mathcal{R}(h)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(h)\mathcal{R}(f)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle h,\mathcal{R}(f)\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(g))\Bigr\rangle.\end{split}

Then (1) of Theorem
7.10
is equivalent to the condition

\begin{split}&-\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(f))\mathcal{R}(h)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(f),\mathcal{R}(h)\mathcal{R}(g)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle h,\mathcal{R}(g)\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle\\
&+\Bigl\langle f,\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(g))\mathcal{R}(h)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(h)\mathcal{R}(f)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle h,\mathcal{R}(f)\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(g))\Bigr\rangle=0.\end{split}

Now we deal with Condition (2) of Theorem
7.10
. For
x\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle\Big((m

\displaystyle(\ad_{x}\otimes\mbox{I})\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}+m\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})(\mbox{I}\otimes\xi)-(\mbox{I}\otimes m(\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})\otimes\mbox{I}+\mbox{I}\otimes m\otimes\ad_{x})\Big)(r\otimes r)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(((x\cdot a_{i})\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}-((a_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes b_{i}+(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})-(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\otimes b_{j}\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x))\otimes b_{i}-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot(x\cdot a_{i}))\otimes b_{i}+a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot x)-a_{j}\otimes(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\otimes(x\cdot b_{i})\Big).

Consequently, for all
\displaystyle f,g,h\in\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
,
x\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\displaystyle(f\otimes

\displaystyle g\otimes h)\Big(\Big((m(\ad_{x}\otimes\mbox{I})\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\mbox{I}+m\otimes\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})(\mbox{I}\otimes\xi)-(\mbox{I}\otimes m(\mbox{I}\otimes\ad_{x})\otimes\mbox{I}+\mbox{I}\otimes m\otimes\ad_{x})\Big)(r\otimes r)\Big)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\Bigl\langle f,g(b_{j})h(b_{i})((x\cdot a_{i})\cdot a_{j})\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle f,g(b_{j})h(b_{i})((a_{i}\cdot x)\cdot a_{j})\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle f,g(b_{j})h(x\cdot b_{i})(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle f,g(b_{j})h(b_{i}\cdot x)(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})\Bigr\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\Big(\Bigl\langle g,f(a_{j})h(b_{i})(b_{j}\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x))\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle g,f(a_{j})h(b_{i})(b_{j}\cdot(x\cdot a_{i}))\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle g,f(a_{j})h(b_{i}\cdot x)(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle g,f(a_{j})h(x\cdot b_{i})(b_{j}\cdot a_{i})\Bigr\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl\langle f,\ad_{x}(\mathcal{R}(h))\mathcal{R}(g)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle f,\mathcal{R}(\ad_{x}^{\ast}(h))\mathcal{R}(g)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}(f)\ad_{x}(\mathcal{R}(h))\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}(f)\mathcal{R}(\ad_{x}^{\ast}(h))\Bigr\rangle.

Thus Condition (2) of Theorem
7.10
is equivalent to

\Bigl\langle f,\ad_{x}(\mathcal{R}(h))\mathcal{R}(g)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle f,\mathcal{R}(\ad_{x}^{\ast}(h))\mathcal{R}(g)\Bigr\rangle+\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}(f)\ad_{x}(\mathcal{R}(h))\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}(f)\mathcal{R}(\ad_{x}^{\ast}(h))\Bigr\rangle=0.

Now, to rewrite Condition (3) of Theorem
7.10
in terms of
\mathcal{R}
, we recall that,
for
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
,

\begin{split}&\big((\rmL_{x}\otimes\rmR_{y})-(\rmR_{x}\otimes\rmL_{y})\big)(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big((x\cdot a_{i})\otimes(b_{i}\cdot y)-(a_{i}\cdot x)\otimes(y\cdot b_{i})\big).\end{split}

Hence, for all
\displaystyle f,g\in\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
,

\begin{split}&(f\otimes g)\Big(\big((\rmL_{x}\otimes\rmR_{y})-(\rmR_{x}\otimes\rmL_{y})\big)(r)\Big)=\Bigl\langle\rmL_{y}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\rmR_{y}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle.\end{split}

Then Condition (3) of Theorem
7.10
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\rmL_{y}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\rmR_{y}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle=0,

Similarly, Condition (5) of Theorem
7.10
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Bigl\langle\rmR_{x}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(\rmR_{y}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle\rmL_{x}^{\ast}(g),\mathcal{R}(\rmL_{y}^{\ast}(f))\Bigr\rangle=0.

Now to express Condition (4) of Theorem
7.10
in terms of
\mathcal{R}
, we have
for
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
,

\begin{split}&(\ad_{y}\rmR_{x}\otimes\mbox{I}-\mbox{I}\otimes\rmR_{x}\ad_{y})(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\big(y\cdot(a_{i}\cdot x)\big)\otimes b_{i}-\big((a_{i}\cdot x)\cdot y\big)\otimes b_{i}-a_{i}\otimes\big((y\cdot b_{i})\cdot x\big)+a_{i}\otimes\big((b_{i}\cdot y)\cdot x\big)\Big).\end{split}

Then, for all

Thus (4) of Theorem
7.10
is equivalent to the condition

\Bigl\langle f,\mathcal{R}\big(\ad_{y}^{\ast}L_{x}^{\ast}(g)\big)\Bigr\rangle-\Bigl\langle g,\mathcal{R}\big(\rmL_{x}^{\ast}\ad_{y}^{\ast}(f)\big)\Bigr\rangle=0.

Finally, we deal with Condition (6) of Theorem
7.10
. Since for

completing the proof.
