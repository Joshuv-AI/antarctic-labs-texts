Cardio-Obstetric Shock
Authors: Sayyad Kyazimzade, Jan M. Griffin, Jessica L. Atkins, Gregory R. Jackson, Chakradhari Inampudi, Brian A. Houston, Christopher Goodier, Loren Francis, Lucas Witer, Bhavadharini Ramu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Cardiogenic shock in pregnancy is rare but is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Timely recognition with multidisciplinary management is necessary for optimal maternal and fetal outcomes. Here we present a case of cardiogenic shock in the antepartum period managed with mechanical circulatory support as a bridge to pregnancy viability.

History of presentation

A 26-year-old pregnant female, G2P1001, presented at 19 weeks gestation with a 2-week history of progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and 1 episode of near syncope. Past medical history

She had a known history of untreated hypothyroidism, morbid obesity (body mass index of 46 kg/m2), pre-eclampsia in her first pregnancy, and remote COVID-19 infection. Her father suffered sudden death in his sleep and paternal grandmother had heart failure.

Differential diagnosis

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) was the most likely etiology. Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) typically presents later in pregnancy, whereas spontaneous coronary artery dissection or other cardiomyopathies were unlikely based on her clinical presentation.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram (Figure 1) showed sinus tachycardia and left bundle branch block. Transthoracic echocardiography showed left ventricular ejection fraction of 35% (Video 1), a dilated left ventricle (LV) with an end-diastolic dimension of 6.5 cm, global hypokinesis, normal right ventricular (RV) function, and an estimated RV systolic pressure of 28 mm Hg. Chest x-ray showed pulmonary edema. Her thyroid-stimulating hormone was mildly elevated at 6.15 mIU/L (upper-limit of normal: 4.94 mIU/L) and free T4 was normal at 0.89 ng/dL (normal range: 0.70-1.48 ng/dL). Management

The patient underwent initial optimization with intravenous diuresis with clinical improvement. At 21 weeks gestation, with increasing hemodynamic demands of her advancing pregnancy, she progressed to cardiogenic shock (CS) requiring inotropic support. Repeat echocardiogram showed LV ejection fraction <20%. Right heart catheterization on inotropic support showed persistent LV predominant CS with elevated filling pressures (right atrial pressure of 13 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 27 mm Hg, thermodilution cardiac index of 1.7 L/min/m2, pulmonary artery pulsatility index of 1.6, and cardiac power output of 0.8 W). Multidisciplinary discussions and shared decision-making occurred throughout the hospital course, involving maternal-fetal-medicine (MFM), advanced heart failure cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, cardiac/obstetric anesthesia, neonatology, the patient, and her family. Counseling was provided regarding the high risk of continuing pregnancy, and she wished to proceed with pregnancy. Because of persistent CS, temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) was offered with a percutaneous microaxial flow pump (Impella 5.5, Abiomed) implanted via the axillary artery to facilitate ongoing fetal development and planned delivery at 24 to 26 weeks gestation.

This time frame was chosen based on discussion with MFM/neonatology and the Society of Maternal-Fetal Medicine’s consensus statement on peri-viable birth which states that optimal neonatal outcomes in the peri-viable period (20 weeks to 25 weeks and 6 days) were observed in the neonates born after 24 weeks and closer to 26 weeks gestation.1 Fetal heart tones were monitored throughout the implantation procedure with the MFM-neonatology team on stand-by. A simultaneous advanced heart failure therapies evaluation was launched, and she was approved for destination therapy left ventricular assist device (LVAD) post-partum pending cardiac function (morbid obesity precluded heart transplantation).

Despite Impella support, she suffered 2 episodes of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia due to inadequate LV unloading as evidenced by persistently elevated filling pressures and dilated LV on echocardiogram. To offer complete hemodynamic support and allow for a safe delivery, she was placed on central venoarterial-extracorporeal life support (VA-ECLS) via median sternotomy with the Impella 5.5 for LV decompression. Central cannulation was pursued due to her body habitus, unfavorable femoral vasculature with risk of limb ischemia, anticipated prolonged need for support, and to limit immobility. With VA-ECLS support, her hemodynamics improved, allowing extubation, weaning of vasoactive medications, and physical rehabilitation. Because of relative stability, the decision was made to continue VA-ECLS support to allow further fetal maturity. She underwent a planned cesarean section at 25 weeks 4 days gestation under general anesthesia. Systemic heparin was held for 6 hours before planned cesarean section while the Impella heparin purge was continued.

Because of persistent bleeding in the immediate postoperative phase, bilateral uterine artery embolization was performed. Despite this, she developed hemorrhagic shock due to refractory hemorrhage at the transverse uterine incision site requiring massive transfusion, laparotomy, and ultimately hysterectomy, delaying resumption of systemic heparin by >24 hours. She also developed bacteremia secondary to abdominal wound infection/dehiscence requiring antibiotics. We did not add bromocriptine in the post-partum period considering her etiology was likely DCM, her MCS dependence, and the associated thrombotic risk.

Over the next 2 weeks, with improvement in hemodynamics, she was decannulated from VA-ECLS to axillary Impella 5.5 support. However, with progressive RV dysfunction, temporary RV support was provided with percutaneous dual lumen cannulation (ProtekDuo, LivaNova PLC) and centrifugal extracorporeal circulatory support pump (Centrimag, Abbott). With persistent MCS dependence, she underwent placement of durable LVAD (HeartMate 3, Abbott) 1 month postpartum. Two weeks later, her RV support was successfully decannulated, allowing transfer to the floor and subsequent discharge to a rehabilitation facility before returning home. Her baby was also discharged home.

CS is characterized by low cardiac output, elevated filling pressures, and compensatory vasoconstriction manifesting as systemic hypoperfusion and multisystem organ failure.2 The most common cause of CS in pregnancy is PPCM, an idiopathic DCM presenting with LV systolic dysfunction in the later stages of pregnancy or early postpartum where no other cause is identified.3 Based on our patient’s presentation with CS during the early hemodynamic demands of pregnancy when cardiac output is rising to maximal levels, and before the typical presentation of PPCM, we believe her presentation was most consistent with DCM.

There are no consensus documents or guidelines on managing CS in pregnancy, particularly when occurring before fetal viability. The management of CS in pregnancy requires a high degree of suspicion and prompt diagnosis including hemodynamic assessment to allow phenotyping of shock stage, severity, and ventricular predominance.2 Such practice can guide early initiation of tailored medical and mechanical management strategies because time is of the essence in ensuring optimal maternal and fetal outcomes. The ability to offer MCS early is critical in CS in pregnancy, especially when inotropic therapy cannot maintain adequate perfusion. Temporary MCS serves as a viable bridge strategy during pregnancy for such patients.

Previously published systematic reviews and case series have primarily reported on CS occurring in later pregnancy or postpartum.4, 5, 6, 7 Our case is unique in describing our multidisciplinary approach in managing CS in early pregnancy, before a high likelihood of fetal survival, with the early initiation of MCS as a bridge to durable LVAD and showing that favorable outcomes for both mother and child can be achieved.

Follow-up

At the 8-month follow-up, the mother was doing well on durable LVAD support. Her baby is growing appropriately.

Conclusions

In managing CS during a previable phase of pregnancy, a multidisciplinary approach is critical to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes. Prompt hemodynamic assessment and adequate circulatory support can facilitate a safe delivery plan.
