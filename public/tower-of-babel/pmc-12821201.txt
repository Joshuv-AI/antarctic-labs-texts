Deep learning-based seed variety classification: a case study in maize
Authors: Omidali Akbarpour, Samaneh Akbari, Dimitrios Fanourakis, Amin Taheri-Garavand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Distinguishing among maize seed varieties is often challenging because of their morphological similarity and genetic diversity. Traditional seed identification methods frequently fail to balance accuracy, speed, and practicality, creating the need for innovative solutions. Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly in deep learning, provide promising alternatives for the precise classification of crop varieties. This study aimed to classify five maize seed genotypes (KSC201, KSC260, KSC400, KSC703, and KSC705) using only RGB digital images acquired under standardized lighting conditions. Image analysis and model training were performed in Python (Colab environment). The proposed convolutional neural network (CNN) model demonstrated excellent performance across all classes, achieving near-perfect accuracy, recall, and specificity for KSC260 and KSC703. The precision–recall analysis showed that KSC705 exhibited almost perfect classification with an average precision of 0.99, while KSC201 achieved 0.96. The overall model performance reached an accuracy of 96.67%, sensitivity of 96.67%, precision of 96.74%, and specificity of 99.18%. Results from the confusion matrix confirmed that the CNN effectively distinguished among all five maize genotypes, and the high area under the curve (AUC) values indicated almost perfect prediction capability. These findings demonstrate that CNN-based image analysis provides a reliable and accurate approach for seed variety identification, comparable to the performance of advanced architectures such as ConvNeXt_Tiny in other agricultural applications.

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is a cornerstone of global agriculture, ranking first among cereal crops with an annual production exceeding 1.2 billion tons [1]. Beyond its role as a staple food and feed crop, maize also supports a broad range of industrial sectors, providing raw materials for starch, oil, sweeteners, and biofuels. The continuous global demand for high-yielding hybrids underscores the importance of maintaining genetic purity in seed production systems. Even minor genetic admixtures can compromise hybrid performance, reduce yield, and cause economic losses across the seed value chain [2].

Two main types of contamination in maize seed fields are genetic and mechanical. Genetic contamination occurs through cross-pollination with nearby compatible plants or accidental self-pollination from poor detasseling. Mechanical contamination results from mixing seeds of different varieties [3]. In contrast, intentional seed adulteration, namely the mixing or mislabeling of lower-value seeds as premium hybrids, can occur for economic gain and poses serious challenges for seed certification and farmer trust. In both cases, the resulting reduction in genetic purity undermines breeding programs and damages market confidence [4]. Therefore, accurate identification and classification of maize seed varieties is essential for maintaining varietal integrity, ensuring reliable field performance, and safeguarding the seed industry’s credibility [5].

Traditional methods used for hybrid identification (e.g., morphological characterization, molecular marker analysis, or near-infrared spectroscopy) can achieve high precision but are often labor-intensive, time-consuming, and dependent on specialized laboratory facilities [6, 7]. These drawbacks limit their applicability for large-scale or on-site verification. Consequently, there is a strong need for rapid, non-destructive, and cost-efficient classification methods capable of supporting seed purity control at various stages of production and distribution.

Recent progress in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning and computer vision, has provided powerful new tools for agricultural image analysis [8–11]. Among these, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated remarkable performance in image recognition tasks by automatically learning discriminative features without the need for manual extraction [12–14]. CNN-based models have already achieved classification accuracies above 90% for various seeds, including wheat, rapeseed, and chickpea [13, 15, 16]. Compared with conventional machine-learning algorithms such as support vector machines or artificial neural networks, CNNs offer improved feature generalization and robustness under diverse imaging conditions.

However, most existing research either focuses on single seed types or relies on advanced imaging techniques such as hyperspectral or near-infrared imaging, which, despite their precision, require expensive equipment and complex preprocessing pipelines [17–20]. In contrast, RGB imaging provides a simple, non-invasive, and highly cost-effective alternative, as it relies on readily available digital cameras or even smartphone sensors [12, 13, 21–23]. When combined with deep learning, RGB-based image classification offers a practical route toward scalable, field-applicable, and economically viable solutions for seed purity assessment.

Despite recent progress, comprehensive studies applying CNN architectures to distinguish multiple maize hybrids using standard RGB images under controlled illumination remain limited, and the comparative performance of CNN models in classifying visually similar genotypes has not been systematically evaluated. Existing RGB-based seed-classification studies in other crops often rely on small datasets, few cultivars, or non-standardized imaging conditions. In contrast, the present work combines (i) a five-cultivar maize dataset acquired under rigorously controlled illumination, (ii) extensive stratified sampling with independence validation to avoid data leakage, and (iii) an ablation-based comparison with lighter architectures, thereby providing a more transparent and robust evaluation framework than most previous studies. Although the CNN architecture follows established design principles and is not intended as a methodological innovation, the novelty of this work lies in developing a reliable, low-cost, and fully reproducible workflow for maize seed classification using standard RGB imaging. By integrating controlled illumination, rigorous sampling, independence validation, and systematic ablation analysis, the study offers a practical and deployable solution tailored to seed-quality applications, advancing the automation of hybrid identification and supporting digitalization in breeding and certification workflows.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop and validate a CNN-based framework for accurately classifying commercial maize seed varieties using non-destructive RGB imaging. The proposed system aims to provide a rapid and cost-efficient alternative to conventional laboratory methods, thereby improving the accuracy of hybrid identification, supporting the maintenance of genetic purity, and enhancing transparency and efficiency within modern seed production workflows.

Materials and methods

Seed samples and image acquisition

Seeds of five maize (Zea mays L.) cultivars (KSC201, KSC260, KSC400, KSC703, and KSC705) were used in this study. All seed samples, harvested less than six months prior to the experiment, were obtained from the Institute of Seed and Seedling Improvement and Production (Karaj, Iran). These cultivars represent high-yielding, commercially important hybrids widely adopted by both farmers and researchers. The five genotypes were selected based on distinct seed morphological characteristics, including differences in seed shape, size, color tone, and surface texture, ensuring sufficient phenotypic variability for reliable model training and testing. All seeds were obtained from a single production batch and stored under controlled laboratory conditions (5 °C, 40% relative humidity) to ensure uniform moisture content prior to imaging. At the end of the present experiment, seed moisture content was determined by oven-drying at 103 °C for 17 h and was consistent across cultivars [11.8 ± 0.5% (wet basis)]. Seeds were imaged on their flattest surface to ensure consistent orientation across samples.

Following verification of morphological integrity and the absence of visible defects, the seeds were transferred to the Faculty of Agriculture, Lorestan University (Iran) for digital image acquisition. A custom imaging station was designed to maintain consistent and well-defined illumination conditions. The system consisted of three main components: (i) a photographic platform, (ii) a digital camera, and (iii) a computer equipped with image-processing software (Fig. 1). An artificial lighting system (4000 K white light-emitting diode, 0.4 m; Philips, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) was installed at the upper interior of the station to ensure stable, uniform illumination. The inner walls were painted white to enhance reflectivity and promote even light distribution.

Images were captured through a centrally positioned aperture located at the upper panel of the imaging station. The camera-to-sample distance was fixed at 40 cm, and the camera was mounted vertically to standardize image geometry. Samples were inserted manually through a door that provided access to the imaging chamber. To prevent external light interference, the door remained closed during image capture, and all operations were conducted in a dark room. Each seed sample was placed on a green background in a consistent orientation and imaged from directly above, with the camera’s image plane kept parallel to the sample surface.

Two-dimensional (2D) images were recorded using a charge-coupled device (CCD) digital camera (PowerShot G15, 12 megapixels; Canon, Tokyo, Japan). Across all captures, camera settings were identical: resolution = 12.1 megapixels, aperture (f-stop) = 2.8, shutter speed = 1/100 s, ISO = 800, with flash and zoom disabled. All images were acquired in the RGB color space and saved in JPEG format with a native resolution of 4000 × 3000 pixels.

The ISO setting was fixed at 800 to ensure consistent exposure across all captures. Under the controlled lighting conditions of the imaging station (Fig. 1), this ISO level did not introduce visible noise or graininess, as confirmed by visual inspection of all raw images. Moreover, CNNs are generally robust to mild sensor noise, particularly when data augmentation techniques are applied.

Image processing and dataset preparation

For each capture, 10–15 maize seeds were arranged against a green background to enhance visual contrast. The number of seeds per image was adjusted depending on kernel size and shape to prevent overlap and maintain full visibility of individual seeds, thereby ensuring consistent segmentation quality across cultivars. The sheet containing the seeds was placed within the imaging system, and high-resolution digital photographs were obtained under the standardized conditions described above. Following acquisition, the colored background was digitally removed, and the images were segmented into individual seed images (Fig. 2). Background removal was performed using color-thresholding in the HSV (Hue–Saturation–Value) color space to isolate the green background from the seed regions. Pixels corresponding to the background were masked and removed, after which contour detection was used to identify individual seed boundaries. Each detected contour was enclosed within a bounding box, and the corresponding region was cropped to produce a single-seed image for downstream analysis. Similar segmentation-based workflows for isolating individual agricultural units prior to classification have been reported in recent CNN studies [24]. Each segmented image was subsequently resized and uploaded to Google Colab for preprocessing and further analysis.

To enhance dataset diversity and improve model generalization, data augmentation techniques, including rotation (0–40°), zooming (± 20%), width/height shifts (± 15%), cropping, and horizontal flipping, were applied during preprocessing using the Keras ImageDataGenerator. These stochastic transformations simulated realistic variation in seed orientation, scale, and position, helping the CNN learn invariant morphological features and reduce overfitting. The complete list of augmentation parameters and their ranges is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Comparable augmentation strategies have been successfully applied in quality assessment pipelines for cocoa beans using multi-output learning models [25]. Each original image generated approximately five augmented variants through stochastic transformation, maintaining a balanced ratio between original and synthetic samples to prevent bias during model training. A detailed overview of the dataset composition, including the number of original and augmented images per cultivar, is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Individual seed images were prepared, corresponding to the five maize cultivars used. Each image was labeled according to its respective cultivar.

To minimize sampling bias and to ensure rigorous model evaluation, a two-stage stratified splitting procedure was implemented. Initially, 15% of the complete dataset was allocated as a fixed and permanently unseen test set, with stratified sampling applied to preserve the proportional representation of all five genotypes. By doing so, the final performance metrics were computed on an independent and non-overlapping subset that was not exposed at any stage of training or hyperparameter optimization. The remaining 85% of the dataset was subjected to 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Within each fold, stratification was enforced so that identical genotype distributions were maintained, thereby preventing class imbalance and ensuring that all five cultivars were proportionally represented in both the training and validation subsets. Through this procedure, five independent and compositionally balanced train/validation partitions were generated, each comprising 80 and 20% of the cross-validation pool, respectively.

To ensure methodological transparency, the total number of original and augmented images per genotype was carefully documented, and the high consistency observed across cross-validation outcomes was taken as evidence that no sampling leakage or systematic bias had occurred. These results indicate that the stratified allocation scheme effectively preserved independence among data partitions while supporting stable and generalizable model learning. All images were resized to a uniform dimension, and pixel intensity values were normalized to the range [0, 1] before being used as input data for the CNN. All segmented seed images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels prior to model input. Similar preprocessing steps, including standardized resizing and normalization, have been implemented in recent hyperspectral imaging studies on table grapes [26]. Representative seed images of the five cultivars are presented in Fig. 3. For interpretability purposes, each representative seed image was further processed using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and saliency analysis, generating heatmap-based and pixel-sensitivity visualizations that highlight the morphological regions most influential to CNN decision-making (Fig. 3).

All images were saved in high-quality JPEG format (minimal compression) to ensure efficient storage without noticeably affecting fine surface details. Previous studies have shown that CNNs remain robust to mild JPEG compression, particularly when combined with data augmentation. Visual inspection confirmed that no visible artifacts interfered with seed surface texture, edge definition, or color-tone characteristics.

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture

The convolutional neural network (CNN) model was developed in Python using the TensorFlow and Keras libraries, selected for their flexibility and efficiency in deep learning applications. The overall architecture, which comprises three core components (i.e., input, hidden, and output layers), is depicted schematically in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig. 4, the model consists of three sequential convolutional blocks (Conv2D–BatchNorm–ReLU–MaxPooling–Dropout), followed by a flattening layer, a fully connected dense layer with 256 neurons, and a final softmax output layer corresponding to the five maize genotypes. Figure 5 summarizes the complete experimental and modelling workflow, illustrating all stages from raw image acquisition to final model evaluation. All code was written in Python 3.10 using TensorFlow 2.12 and Keras 3.0 libraries.

These blocks extract progressively deeper features related to edges, texture, color gradients, and shape patterns. (iv) Output layer: A 5-neuron softmax layer corresponding to the five maize genotypes, producing class-probability predictions. Together, these components form a compact, computationally efficient architecture optimized for real-time maize seed classification

Dropout layers

These layers randomly deactivate a fraction of the neurons during training to reduce overfitting and improve the model’s generalization ability. By preventing excessive co-adaptation of neurons, dropout forces the network to learn more robust and independent feature representations. In this study, a dropout rate of 0.5 was applied after each convolutional block to enhance training stability and prevent overfitting. Dropout has likewise been applied to improve generalization in CNN architectures used for soil-type classification and plant disease detection [24, 32].

Flatten and dense layers

The Flatten layer converts the two-dimensional feature maps generated by the convolutional blocks into a one-dimensional vector, which serves as input to the fully connected (Dense) layers. These Dense layers perform high-level reasoning and classification by combining features learned in earlier layers. The final CNN architecture consisted of 3,795,985 parameters, of which 1,265,189 were trainable. Model optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer, which adapts learning rates dynamically and incorporates momentum to accelerate convergence and improve stability during training. Training was performed on an NVIDIA T4 GPU, which provides sufficient memory and computational throughput for medium-sized CNN architectures while enabling efficient batch processing and rapid convergence. This hardware is widely used for deep learning tasks requiring fast iteration and reproducible training performance. Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, dropout rate, and number of epochs) were optimized using preliminary grid-based experiments, with the final configuration selected based on the lowest validation loss and highest validation accuracy. Dense-layer configurations similar to those used here have been implemented in recent agricultural CNN applications, such as tea leaf disease classification [27].

Training and evaluation

The CNN was trained for 50 epochs using a learning rate of 0.00001 and a batch size of 32. The training duration was set to 50 epochs because validation accuracy plateaued beyond this point, and additional epochs did not yield further improvements, consistent with standard practice for medium-sized CNN architectures. To prevent overfitting and preserve the optimal model weights, early stopping and model checkpointing were employed during training.

In addition to the single hold-out validation approach, a 5-fold cross-validation scheme was also conducted to confirm the model’s robustness. The dataset was randomly partitioned into five equal folds, with each fold used once as a validation set while the remaining four served for training. The average performance across all folds exhibited minimal variation (< 1%), confirming the stability and generalization capability of the CNN (Supplementary Table S3).

All computational experiments were conducted in Google Colab Pro + using an NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB VRAM) and 83 GB system RAM. The model was implemented in Python 3.10 with TensorFlow 2.12 and Keras 3.0. The complete list of hyperparameters (including learning rate, batch size, optimizer parameters, dropout configuration, kernel sizes, stride values, and regularization settings) is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Evaluation metrics

Several standard performance metrics were employed to comprehensively evaluate the classification performance of the CNN model. These included five conventional statistical measures, namely accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score, and five additional indicators: the false positive rate (FPR), class-specific accuracy, Precision–Recall (PR) curves, confusion matrix, and the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC). The formal mathematical definitions of all evaluation metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Model performance across the five maize cultivars (KSC201, KSC260, KSC400, KSC703, and KSC705) was further evaluated using Precision–Recall (PR) curves and Average Precision (AP) scores. Precision and recall were calculated across varying thresholds to generate PR curves, while AP values summarized the overall performance for each class. Evaluation on a held-out test set ensured model generalizability and enabled the identification of class-specific strengths and weaknesses.

The confusion matrix provides a tabular summary of the model’s predictions, showing true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) for each class. It enables a detailed evaluation of classification accuracy and error distribution across all categories.

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the corresponding Area Under the Curve (AUC) were used to assess the model’s ability to discriminate between classes. The ROC curve plots the True Positive Rate (TPR) against the False Positive Rate (FPR). A higher AUC value indicates stronger classification performance, with points above the diagonal line representing favorable outcomes where sensitivity exceeds the FPR [33].

Statistical analysis

All model performance metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, false positive rate (FPR), average precision (AP), and area under the ROC curve (AUC), were computed using built-in evaluation functions in TensorFlow/Keras and scikit-learn. To assess the robustness and statistical reliability of the model, a 5-fold cross-validation procedure was implemented, and the mean performance across folds was reported. Variability between folds is expressed as standard deviation (± SD).

For the precision–recall (PR) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, AP and AUC values were calculated using scikit-learn’s precision_recall_curve and roc_auc_score functions. Confusion matrix values represent raw counts of true and misclassified samples.

Where applicable, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated for key performance indicators (overall accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity) using a non-parametric bootstrap resampling approach (1000 iterations). Statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.10 using NumPy, SciPy, and scikit-learn libraries.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the successful application of a convolutional neural network (CNN) for the automated classification of maize (Zea mays L.) seed genotypes using RGB imagery. The model achieved outstanding overall accuracy and generalization capability, confirming the potential of deep learning–based image analysis for seed purity verification and cultivar identification.

Model performance and generalization

The high test accuracy (96.67%) and balanced validation accuracy (94.45%) reveal that the model effectively generalized to unseen data without evidence of overfitting. The slightly higher accuracy on the test set compared with the training and validation sets is attributable to the extensive use of regularization and augmentation during training, which increases the difficulty of the training samples. In contrast, the test images are unaugmented and therefore represent an easier classification domain, making such performance differences expected and not indicative of overfitting. The convergence between training and validation losses, together with the near-identical performance metrics across datasets (Figs. 6 and 7), indicates stable optimization and proper learning capacity. Although the dataset was acquired under controlled imaging conditions, model robustness was indirectly assessed through extensive augmentation, which simulated a range of environmental disturbances such as variable angles, zoom levels, and positional shifts. The high validation and test accuracies suggest that these perturbations did not compromise classification performance, indicating resilience to moderate variation in imaging conditions. The balanced dataset structure and independence validation procedures (stratified sampling + feature distribution analysis) likely contributed to the stable convergence and high generalization accuracy observed in all genotypes. Such outcomes are consistent with previous deep learning studies on agricultural seed imaging [12, 13, 15, 34–36] that reported comparable accuracies (94–98%) when CNNs were implemented under controlled lighting conditions [25].

Compared with traditional machine-learning approaches such as support vector machines (SVMs) or random forests, CNNs can automatically extract spatial and spectral features from raw images, eliminating the need for hand-crafted descriptors. This advantage explains the high discriminative ability observed in the present model, which could distinguish among five visually similar maize hybrids with minimal confusion. The accuracy obtained in this study (96.67%) is comparable to, or slightly exceeds, those reported for similar RGB-based models applied to chickpea, rice, and wheat seeds (93–96%; Table S8). This highlights the general applicability of the proposed framework to other crops where phenotype-based discrimination remains challenging.

Evaluation of class-wise performance

The confusion-matrix analysis (Fig. 9) further supports the robustness of the CNN architecture. The model correctly classified nearly all samples of KSC260, KSC400, and KSC705, while minor misclassifications occurred mainly between KSC201 and KSC703. The slightly lower recall observed for KSC201 may be linked to its intermediate kernel size and color intensity, which visually overlaps with KSC703. Such overlaps highlight the biological realism of the model’s confusions and underscore the value of high-resolution, standardized imaging for capturing subtle morphological cues. These two cultivars share certain morphological and chromatic similarities that likely contributed to occasional overlap in feature space. This behavior is consistent with the interpretability visualizations (Fig. 3), where Grad-CAM and saliency maps highlight overlapping morphological regions between these two cultivars. Nevertheless, recall and specificity remained above 96% for all genotypes, showing that even the most challenging classes were reliably recognized. As summarized in Table 1, precision, recall, and F1-scores remained above 93% across classes, confirming consistent recognition accuracy.

The Precision–Recall (PR) curves (Fig. 8) reinforced these results, as all classes exhibited average precision (AP) values above 0.95, with four approaching 1.0. This pattern confirms that the CNN not only achieved high accuracy but also maintained high precision across thresholds, minimizing false positives. Similar behavior was observed in previous CNN-based grain classification studies, including those of [16, 37] on wheat and [13] on chickpea, where AP scores above 0.95 were indicative of strong feature, separability.

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values (Fig. 10) provide complementary evidence of model reliability. Four genotypes achieved perfect AUC values of 1.0, and the fifth reached 0.99, indicating nearly complete separation between classes. These results surpass or match many earlier applications of CNNs for crop seed discrimination, which commonly report AUC values between 0.96 and 0.99 [28, 38].

Architectural design and regularization effects

The success of the model can be attributed to its well-designed architecture, which combined multiple convolutional blocks with batch normalization, max pooling, and dropout layers (Fig. 4). This structure ensured effective feature extraction, hierarchical abstraction, and strong regularization. A full layer-by-layer summary of the CNN architecture is provided in Supplementary Table S7. The mathematical formulations governing convolution, pooling, and activation operations (Supplementary Table S5) ensured the consistent implementation of the CNN architecture and contributed to its stable convergence behavior. The inclusion of dropout layers (rate = 0.5) was particularly beneficial in mitigating overfitting, as indicated by the close correspondence between training and validation losses at the end of training (Fig. 6;14 and 0.16, respectively). The selected dropout rate (0.5) and five-block architecture provided the best trade-off between bias and variance, preventing overfitting while maintaining sufficient feature diversity. Given the size of the dataset and the need for computational efficiency, a lightweight CNN was preferred over heavier SOTA models, which often require millions of additional parameters and significantly longer training times. Shallower networks underfit, whereas deeper ones showed marginal gains but higher computational demand (Supplementary Table S9). Ablation results presented in Supplementary Table S9 also provide a quantitative comparison with lighter architectures, showing that shallower or reduced-parameter models resulted in noticeably lower accuracy (–2.5% to − 4.1%), confirming that the selected network offers a superior balance between computational efficiency and classification performance. A detailed comparison of model complexity, including parameter counts and computational requirements relative to related CNN architectures, is provided in Supplementary Table S10.

The architecture’s parameter counts (Table 2; 3.8 million, with 1.26 million trainable) strikes a balance between complexity and computational efficiency. Similar-scale architectures have proven effective in small to medium-sized agricultural image datasets, enabling accurate feature representation without excessive training time or resource consumption [28, 38, 39]. The present results corroborate that moderately deep CNNs can outperform more complex networks when dataset size, image quality, and augmentation are properly optimized.

Role of image acquisition and data augmentation

Model performance is closely tied to the quality and consistency of image acquisition. The custom imaging station used in this study (Fig. 1), which provided uniform illumination and consistent geometry, minimized confounding variation caused by shadows, reflections, and color distortions (see also [12, 14]). Controlled imaging has been repeatedly shown to enhance CNN reliability in plant phenotyping and seed-classification tasks [13, 18, 40].

Additionally, the application of extensive data-augmentation techniques (i.e., rotation, zooming, translation, and flipping) proved crucial in improving model generalization. By artificially increasing dataset diversity, augmentation enabled the CNN to learn invariant representations of seed morphology and surface texture, thereby reducing sensitivity to orientation or slight lighting differences.

Generalization to variable real-world conditions

The dataset used in this study was acquired under uniform lighting, camera distance, and background conditions. While this controlled setup minimizes noise and enables consistent feature extraction [41], it may also limit the model’s generalization capacity in real-world environments where illumination, shadows, seed orientation, and background texture are more variable. Models trained exclusively on homogeneous datasets may overfit to background-specific or lighting-specific cues instead of learning fully intrinsic seed features. Similar limitations associated with controlled-image acquisition settings have been noted in other horticultural imaging studies, including those using RGB-based machine-learning models for postharvest quality assessment [23]. To address this, future work should evaluate the model under diverse acquisition settings, including variable light spectra and intensities, different imaging platforms (smartphone and low-cost sensors), and heterogeneous backgrounds. Data-augmentation strategies and domain-adaptation methods can also be incorporated to improve robustness and ensure high performance across realistic field or processing-line conditions.

Comparison with previous approaches

When benchmarked against previous studies on maize seed classification, the developed model shows competitive or superior performance. Unlike many previous RGB-based seed classification studies, which often rely on smaller datasets or limited validation procedures, the present study integrates stratified sampling, independence testing, and ablation-based architectural analysis, offering a more rigorous and reproducible evaluation of CNN performance. Earlier works using conventional image analysis and SVM classifiers typically reported accuracies around 90–93%, largely constrained by the limited ability of hand-engineered features to capture complex spatial patterns [39, 42]. Deep CNNs, by contrast, can extract multi-level spatial information automatically, resulting in higher accuracy and robustness.

Furthermore, unlike hyperspectral or near-infrared imaging methods that require expensive instrumentation, the present approach relies solely on standard RGB imaging (Figs. 2 and 3), which is cost-effective and easily adaptable for routine use in seed laboratories. This distinction significantly enhances its practical value for breeders, certification agencies, and seed producers seeking affordable quality-control solutions. While transformer-based architectures and hybrid CNN-SVM models are emerging for plant phenotyping tasks, their computational demand and data requirements often exceed what is feasible for seed laboratories. For similar reasons, heavy deep-learning architectures such as ResNet50, EfficientNet, ViT, or ConvNeXt-Tiny were not evaluated here, as they typically require substantially larger datasets and computational resources and are less suited for lightweight, real-time deployment in seed laboratories. The present CNN achieves comparable accuracy with substantially lower complexity (Supplementary Table S9), suggesting that compact architectures remain advantageous for real-world deployment. A comparative summary of related CNN-based seed classification studies, including both RGB and hyperspectral imaging approaches, is presented in Supplementary Table S8.

Practical implications and future directions

The consistent high accuracy and near-perfect AUC values (Table 1) confirm that CNN-based image analysis can serve as a reliable, rapid, and non-destructive tool for maize genotype verification. The approach could be readily extended to other crops or integrated into automated seed-sorting and quality-assessment systems. Combined with edge-computing platforms or mobile imaging devices, such models could enable on-site varietal testing in breeding programs and commercial seed production. The model’s lightweight architecture and low inference time (~ 0.04 s per image) make it suitable for deployment on embedded or mobile platforms such as smartphones, Raspberry Pi, or dedicated edge-AI modules, enabling real-time, on-site seed verification.

However, several limitations merit consideration. First, the current dataset was acquired under controlled laboratory conditions. Therefore, performance under variable field lighting or different imaging devices remains to be evaluated. Second, the study used only five genotypes, and expanding the number of cultivars would provide a more comprehensive assessment of scalability. Interpretability has been strengthened through the inclusion of Grad-CAM and saliency-map visualizations (Fig. 3), which reveal the morphological regions most influential to CNN decision-making. These visualizations improve model transparency. Future studies may incorporate additional explainability techniques to further enhance interpretability. A minor limitation is the use of JPEG files. However, as detailed in the Image Acquisition section, compression was minimal and did not affect feature extraction, as indicated by the high model performance. Although ISO 800 can introduce digital noise, the controlled illumination, maximum-quality image capture, and consistent high model performance indicate that noise did not adversely affect feature extraction. Future research could also explore hybrid models that integrate spectral or morphological features with CNN-extracted deep features, potentially enhancing accuracy in challenging classification scenarios [43]. Future work could include benchmarking the proposed model against larger state-of-the-art architectures such as ResNet, EfficientNet, ViT, or ConvNeXt to further quantify performance trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost.

Beyond laboratory applications, the developed CNN framework could be adapted for lightweight deployment on mobile or edge-computing platforms. Implementations using TensorFlow Lite or ONNX would enable real-time seed classification directly on portable devices without requiring high-end GPUs. In practical terms, the trained model required approximately 35 min for full convergence on an NVIDIA T4 GPU and achieved an average inference time of ≈ 0.04 s per image, confirming its suitability for rapid on-site applications. Such deployment could facilitate on-site seed verification in breeding stations, seed-processing plants, and even farm-level operations, thereby bridging the gap between research models and practical field usability. When integrated with blockchain or traceability systems, such CNN-based image diagnostics could serve as digital checkpoints within seed certification pipelines, ensuring variety authenticity from breeding stations to commercial distribution. Future implementations could link model outputs to centralized seed-registry databases, enabling automated labeling and verification within national or international certification frameworks. Such approaches also reduce laboratory waste and energy consumption by minimizing the need for physical testing. In addition, transfer learning strategies could be employed to adapt the model for other crop species, supporting multi-crop scalability while reducing the need for large, crop-specific datasets. Future work should include explicit robustness testing under varying illumination, camera devices, and seed orientations to further validate real-world applicability. In the present study, interpretability was enhanced through Grad-CAM and saliency-map visualizations (Fig. 3), and additional explainability methods will be integrated in future work to further improve model transparency and deployment readiness.

Overall significance

Despite its strong performance, this study was conducted under controlled imaging conditions. Future investigations should test model robustness under variable illumination, seed orientation, and camera devices to ensure scalability. Furthermore, incorporating spectral or textural indices may further improve discrimination in visually similar genotypes.

In summary, the developed CNN achieved exceptional performance in classifying maize seed genotypes using simple RGB imagery. The model combined architectural efficiency, effective regularization, and high generalization capacity. Its accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values (summarized in Tables 1 and 2) collectively demonstrate its reliability for genotype discrimination. These findings provide a strong foundation for the broader adoption of deep learning in seed purity analysis and other precision-agriculture applications that demand fast, objective, and cost-effective decision support. Beyond laboratory use, the framework can be easily integrated into automated seed-sorting systems or mobile-based imaging platforms, enabling real-time variety verification at seed production and distribution centers. Such adaptability can substantially reduce the time, labor and cost associated with conventional seed certification processes. Collectively, these findings emphasize that accessible RGB-based CNN frameworks can complement molecular and spectral methods, offering a low-cost pathway for digital seed certification. By relying solely on RGB imaging and open-source software, the developed framework supports sustainable and cost-effective digitalization of seed quality control, aligning with global goals for precision agriculture and resource efficiency.

Comparison with state-of-the-art models

The performance of the developed CNN model aligns closely with or surpasses results reported in recent state-of-the-art agricultural imaging studies. For example, the CAS-CNN and modified MobileNetV2 architectures used for cashew nut and fruit disease detection achieved classification accuracies between 94 and 98% [24], while enhanced SOTA CNN models for tea leaf disease identification reached similar levels of precision and recall [27]. Likewise, multi-output learning frameworks applied to cocoa bean quality assessment [25] and chained machine learning (ML) approaches used in hyperspectral table grape evaluation [26] reported high overall accuracies but also highlighted challenges in class separability for visually similar classes.

In comparison, the proposed CNN achieved a test accuracy of 96.67% alongside near-perfect class-wise AUC values (0.99–1.00), demonstrating that the model maintains highly balanced precision, recall, and specificity across all five maize genotypes. These results indicate that the architecture provides state-of-the-art classification performance while relying solely on a lightweight RGB image–based pipeline, underscoring its efficiency and suitability for real-world, resource-constrained seed identification applications. A direct performance comparison with widely used pretrained CNN architectures (e.g., VGG16, MobileNet, ResNet50) was not included in this study. We acknowledge this as a methodological limitation. Nonetheless, the obtained performance metrics are consistent with or exceed those reported in comparable seed-classification studies, where deep learning models typically achieve 85–95% accuracy under similar imaging conditions and problem complexity [39, 44]. To further strengthen the methodological rigor, future work will incorporate a systematic transfer-learning benchmark using multiple pretrained networks. This will allow quantitative assessment of the trade-off between inference accuracy, computational cost, and deploy ability on low-power agricultural devices, thereby providing a more comprehensive validation of the proposed architecture.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed and validated a convolutional neural network (CNN) model capable of accurately classifying five maize seed genotypes using digital image analysis. The proposed model achieved excellent performance, effectively distinguishing morphologically similar genotypes with high precision (96.74%), sensitivity (96.67%), and specificity (99.18%). The balanced F1-scores and nearly perfect ROC–AUC values confirm the strong generalization ability of the model across all classes. Compared with traditional machine learning approaches, the CNN framework provides substantial improvements in accuracy, automation, and efficiency while eliminating the need for costly laboratory-based genetic testing or manual feature extraction.

The robustness and reproducibility of the model highlight its potential integration into intelligent sorting systems, automated seed certification, and quality-assurance programs, where rapid and reliable variety identification is critical. Future studies should aim to enhance scalability and field applicability by integrating lightweight architectures such as ConvNeXt or MobileNet, transfer learning techniques, or additional imaging modalities including hyperspectral imaging.

Overall, this study demonstrates that CNN-based deep learning provides a transformative and cost-efficient solution for automated maize seed classification. The developed framework supports the digital transformation of seed production, ensuring genetic purity and operational transparency in hybrid breeding programs. Integrating such models into IoT-enabled and blockchain-supported certification pipelines will enable real-time, traceable, and scalable seed verification across the production chain. This aligns with global sustainability goals promoting low-cost, low-carbon digital agriculture.
