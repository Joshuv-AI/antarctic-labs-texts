Enhancing medical image security with quantum DNA dual encryption
Authors: Saba Inam, Shamsa Kanwal, Rehana Amir, Shaha Al-Otaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnsuring patient secrecy and maintaining data integrity are critical in healthcare information security. With increasing demands for stronger protection in the management and storage of medical images, this study introduces an innovative framework utilizing chaos-based quantum encryption for enhanced security. This approach combines a DNA codec with the QFBT (Quantum Fibonacci Transform), creating a unique dual-encryption method for quantum color images. The process begins with converting a standard color image to quantum data through GQIR (Generalized Quantum Image Representation) technique. A DNA codec then encodes and decodes the pixel color data of the quantum image, achieving pixel-level diffusion. Finally, the QFBT is applied to scramble the image’s spatial data. The resulting transformed image is then used as the key matrix in quantum XOR functions. Findings from the experiment indicate that both the correlation and peak value of the encrypted image histograms show strong similarity. The mean NPCR (Normalized Pixel Change Rate) for the RGB channels is 99.63%, while UACI (Uniform Average Change Intensity) is 33.43%. Additionally, the mean information entropy is approximately 7.9992. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of QDIES in providing robust security for medical image data.Keywords: Quantum Fibonacci transform, DNA codec, Quantum adders, Quantum circuits, Security analysis, Quantum image encryptionSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

With the ongoing innovations in communication technology and networks, videos and images have emerged as primary mediums for data dissemination and exchange. However, ensuring the sanctuary of these media has become a crucial area of research1. As a result, numerous scholars have extensively studied the encryption and safeguarding of conventional digital images2. Consequently, computerized images have been integrated with quantum processing structure for the purpose of QIP (Quantum image processing)3. Quantum image processing, a subfield of quantum information, is dedicated to designing quantum protocols and techniques for storing, enhancing, and retrieving visual data. Although still in its early stages, this field has already made significant progress in areas such as quantum image steganography and disambiguation4 quantum image encryption5, and quantum image watermarking6.

Image encryption typically encompasses both conventional image encryption techniques and quantum encryption methods. One such approach is DNA encoding, offers benefits like parallel computing capabilities and significant computational power7. Its distinct complementary DNA rules are extensively utilized in various traditional encryption algorithms.

A study by Chen et al.8 introduced DNA-encoded hydrogels as an innovative bioink for 3D bioprinting of cartilage organoids, overcoming limitations associated with traditional biomaterials. Leveraging the programmability of DNA, these hydrogels offer reversible cross-linking and tunable mechanical and structural properties. This enables enhanced control over cellular behavior and more accurate recapitulation of cartilage tissue features. The work underscores the promise of DNA-encoded hydrogels in regenerative medicine and related biomedical fields, while also acknowledging existing challenges that warrant further investigation.

Safdar et al.9 proposed a hybrid image encryption method integrating finite ring theory with DNA encoding. Using Quasi-Galois rings, the approach generates S-boxes for substitution and permutation, while additional ring transformations enhance pixel shuffling. DNA sequences further boost randomness and robustness.

A new cryptosystem combining a 6D chaotic system with DNA techniques is proposed10, where a hash-based key ties the cipher to the original image. The method involves pixel partitioning, scrambling, DNA coding/computing, and diffusion. Experiments confirm its strong defense against common attacks and improved security over several existing methods.

Quantum computing3 offers significant computational and decryption power due to its unique features like quantum parallelism and entanglement. This advanced capability often challenges conventional image encryption methods, making them vulnerable. As a result, quantum image encryption algorithms are becoming increasingly essential in the realm of information security. Converting classical images into quantum images is a crucial step in quantum image encryption. The methods used to prepare different quantum image representations can vary. Researchers have proposed numerous schemes for representing quantum images.

In 2003, Bose and Venegas-Andraca11 introduced the concept of using quantum mechanics to store, transform, and retrieve images. The primary function of this approach is to convert the image’s spatial information into the intensity of a single quantum bit. However, it does not take advantage of the quantum capabilities of entanglement and superposition. In 2005, Latorre12 presented another example of quantum image representation, called Real-Ket. This model stores images through continuous quadratic fractioning. In 2011, Le et al.13 introduced the FRQI (Flexible Representation of Quantum Images) model which encrypts both color and spatial data of image’s pixels into quantum states. Zhang et al.14 demonstrated the NEQR (Novel Enhanced Quantum Representation) model in 2013, emerged from FRQI model and leverages quantum bits. In 2015, Jiang et al.15 put forward the GQIR (Generalized Quantum Image Representation) model, which addressed the limitations of earlier models that could only handle images with fixed sizes and color depths. This advancement allowed for the representation of images with any color depth and size. As a result, several innovative image encryption techniques have been developed based on these quantum models.

Image encryption often relies on the effective scrambling properties of the Fibonacci transformation16 that leverages the mathematical properties of the Fibonacci sequence to enhance image encryption. Jiang et al. applied classical pixel shuffling techniques, including Fibonacci, Arnold, and Hilbert, to the domain of quantum image encryption in 2014. Inam et al.17 developed a quantum color image encryption scheme using a 4D hyperchaotic Lorenz system integrated with a Fibonacci transform in 2025. By representing images as quantum data via the GQIR technique, the method generates highly unpredictable keys and applies quantum-based scrambling and diffusion. Zhao et al.18 presented a color image encoding strategy that combines oscillatory quantum walks with a controlled Rubik’s cube mechanism in 2022. Zhu et al.19 introduced a mixed double perturbation MQIR quantum image encoding strategy centralized on random permutation in 2024. Their experiments demonstrated that algorithm offers a vast key space and computationally efficient intricacy, adapting it well suited for unequal-sided quantum images.

In our paper, we present a quantum image encryption approach which integrates DNA encoding technology with the QFBT. The process begins by employing the GQIR technique to convert conventional color images to quantum images, while color data of pixels is encoded using DNA encoding methods. Then the QFBT is employed to scramble pixel positions, thereby strengthening the encryption through spatial diffusion. The resulting encrypted image is then used as a key matrix for quantum XOR operations, completing the encryption process with enhanced confusion and cryptographic robustness. For decryption, we develop a technique that leverages DNA decoding principles alongside the reversible nature of quantum circuits. Simulation results demonstrate that QDIES achieves robust encryption with strong resistance to attacks.

Unlike prior quantum image encryption schemes that use either DNA encoding or Fibonacci transform separately, QDIES is the first to integrate DNA encoding decoding with Quantum Fibonacci Transform (QFBT) within a unified quantum circuit, enabling simultaneous pixel scrambling and diffusion. We formally prove that QDIES reduces the computational space complexity from (classical DNA encryption) to using quantum superposition, making it scalable for large images.

Workflow of encryption and decryption algorithm

To safeguard images, a multi-stage encryption process is recommended. Initially, a plain image is transformed into a quantum image, and color data is transformed into binary sequence based on specific principle (Rule 1). This binary sequence is then fed into a quantum DNA codec to perform encoding functions on the quantum image. Decoding is achieved by applying the RULE 4, which involves inverting both high and low qubits simultaneously. The encoded image then undergoes scrambling through the QFBT to disrupt pixel relationships. For the diffusion stage, we perform a quantum XOR operation between this scrambled image and the original quantum image, creating a new intermediate cipher image. To further strengthen security, this XOR-processed image is subjected to additional encryption through quantum DNA encoding using rule 8. Differential attack analysis

The efficiency of various image encryption algorithms can be quantified through differential attacks. This involves determining the degree of impact of plaintext alteration on the resultant ciphertext.

Sensitivity to plaintext attacks

Encryption algorithms are supposed to have a high level of plaintext sensitivity, whereby slight changes in the plaintext, such as altering a bit of a pixel, will have drastic effects on the ciphertext. In order to determine the effect of QDIES, two bits of pixels in an image having dimensions of 512 × 512 were altered in the top-left corner and the bottom-right corner shown in Fig. 11. The two altered images looked identical to the original image. After subjecting all the three images to encryption, the ciphertexts generated along with the difference images showed noise-like features. This implies that there is a great impact of minor alterations of plaintext on ciphertext. Computational complexity analysis

While security remains the primary concern, computational efficiency is equally essential for the practical deployment of image encryption systems. Computational complexity analysis focuses on both time and space requirements. The proposed QDIES scheme achieves substantial performance improvements through three key optimizations. The Generalized Quantum Image Representation (GQIR) efficiently encodes classical image data into quantum states, enabling parallel processing of pixel information. DNA encoding/decoding operations are integrated with the Quantum Fibonacci Transform (QFBT), which operates on pixel blocks (64-bit vectors) instead of individual pixels. Additionally, all operations utilize relative coordinates, avoiding padding overhead for non-square images.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel colored quantum image encryption technique which integrates DNA codecs with Quantum Fibonacci Transform (QFBT). The algorithm successfully achieves dual scrambling of both color and spatial position, significantly enhancing encryption efficiency. We perform a comprehensive comparative analysis between plaintext and ciphertext images, evaluating six key facets: runtime complexity, histogram distribution, correlation coefficients, key vulnerability, entropy information, and resistance to variation. Imitation results demonstrate that the suggested approach exhibits superior encryption performance, robust security, and sturdy resilience against attacks. Our work is based on quantum circuit-level modeling validated through simulations using Qiskit under ideal noise-free conditions. For a RGB image, the QDIES scheme requires 19 qubits and CNOT gates. On current NISQ devices, issues such as decoherence, gate infidelity, and connectivity constraints may affect practical deployment. Thus, while our approach offers a strong theoretical basis for future fault-tolerant quantum computers, near-term implementation would require error mitigation strategies. This work serves as a simulation-validated framework to guide future quantum image encryption research. Future work will focus on further optimization and research to enhance the algorithm’s computational efficiency and overall performance.
