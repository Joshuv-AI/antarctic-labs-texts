Electroelastic guided wave dispersion in piezoelectric plates: spectral methods and laser-ultrasound experiments
D. A. Kiefer, G. Watzl, K. Burgholzer, M. Ryzy, C. Grünsteidl

Abstract
Electroelastic waves in piezoelectric media are widely used in sensing and filtering applications. Despite extensive research, computing the guided wave dispersion remains challenging. This paper presents semi-analytical approaches based on spectral methods to efficiently and reliably compute dispersion curves. We systematically assess the impact of electrical boundary conditions on a 128° Y-cut LiNbO 3 \text{LiNbO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} wafer, examining open-open, open-shorted and shorted-shorted surfaces configurations. Multi-modal dispersion maps obtained from laser-ultrasonic experiments for each boundary condition exhibit excellent agreement with the computational predictions. A straightforward implementation of the spectral collocation method is made available as GEW piezo plate ( https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.14205789 ), while the spectral element method will be integrated to GEWtool ( http://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.10114243 ). Therewith, we aim to make advanced semi-analytical techniques more accessible to physicists and engineers relying on dispersion analysis.



I
Introduction

In a waveguide, waves are constrained to travel along a defined path due to boundaries that channel the energy
Auld 1973a
. Guided elastic waves have applications in non-destructive testing
Wilcox, Lowe, and Cawley 2001
, material characterization
Clorennec, Prada, and Royer 2007
;
Thelen et al. 2021
;
Grabec et al. 2024
;
Watzl et al. 2025
and sensing
Cegla, Cawley, and Lowe 2005
;
Kiefer, Benkert, and Rupitsch 2022
.
If the waveguide’s material is piezoelectric, the mechanical and electrical fields are coupled, resulting in electroelastic waves
Auld 1973b
;
Auld 1973a
;
Kuang 2014
;
Kyame 1949
;
Epstein 1973
.
These have found widespread application in micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS), e.g., as surface acoustic wave (SAW) devices for signal processing and sensing
Mandal and Banerjee 2022
. In other devices, like bulk acoustic wave filters, spurious guided modes need to be avoided
Kokkonen et al. 2010
.
Due to technological advances in materials, thin film technology and further development of MEMS devices, application-driven research on
guided waves in piezoelectric plates is still very active today
Yao et al. 2024
;
Lu and Ren 2023
;
Setiawan, Kadota, and Tanaka 2021
;
Caliendo and Hamidullah 2017
.

Electroelastic waves in piezoelectric media are well studied. The theoretical fundamentals are covered in classic textbooks
Auld 1973b
;
Auld 1973a
. Initial work describes the propagation of bulk waves
Kyame 1949
;
Epstein 1973
. Work on modeling guided waves in piezoelectric plates was pioneered by Tiersten
Tiersten 1963
, who considered metallized surfaces while restricting propagation to two specific directions. Later, fundamental modes propagating along principal directions with open (unmetallized) and shorted (metallized) surfaces were studied by Joshi et al.
Joshi and Jin 1991a
and validated experimentally with interdigital transducers. Syngellakis and Lee
Syngellakis and Lee 1993
extended the modeling to arbitrary anisotropy and propagation direction and computed the dispersion of electrically shorted higher-order modes. Electrically open modes at different propagation directions were computed by Kuznetsova et al.
Kuznetsova et al. 2004
.
Sun et al.
Sun et al. 1998
presented solutions for open-open, open-shorted and shorted-shorted surfaces. A periodic grating of open and shorted regions can be used to design phononic crystals with tunable band gaps
Vasseur et al. 2018
. Guo et al.
Guo, Chen, and Zhang 2009
analyzed multilayered piezoelectric plates based on a state-space formalism. Lastly, Zhu et al.
Zhu et al. 2020
accounted for the electrical losses in the metallized surfaces of plates.

Root-searching of the characteristic equations was used in all previously mentioned studies (except the phononic crystal) to compute dispersion curves. Although this method is known to be slow and to miss solutions, it is still prevalent today. A software based on root-searching was made available in 1990 by Adler
Adler et al. 1990
and is still actively being used
Caliendo et al. 2023
;
Ageikin et al. 2023
. An alternative treatment of the transcendental characteristic equations are analytic approximations for limiting cases
Shuvalov and Le Clezio 2010
.

In contrast to root-searching of the characteristic equations, semi-analytical methods discretize the waveguide cross section and then solve a standard algebraic eigenvalue problem to reliably and efficiently obtain solutions. Various discretization methods have been used for semi-analytical models of piezoelectric waveguides. Finite Elements were used very early by Lagasse
Lagasse 1973
for ridge and wedge guides, while Cortes et al.
Cortes, Datta, and Mukdadi 2008
treated multi-layered plates. Spectral methods are based on a global function basis to expand the field and are particularly well-suited for layered structures. In this context, Laguerre polynomials were used for surface waves
Datta and Hunsinger 1978
;
Lefebvre et al. 1998
and Legendre polynomials
Zhang et al. 2019
for plates. Laude et al.
Laude, Assouar, and Hou 2010
used a Fourier series representation but encountered difficulties due to the non-vanishing electrical field outside the plate. Despite the advantages of semi-analytical methods, their wide-spread adoption is hindered by the lack of publicly available implementations and the relatively high level of knowledge on numerical computing required to implement them.

From an experimental point of view, laser-based ultrasound (LUS) has proven to be a valuable tool to precisely resolve the multi-modal dispersion curves of plates
Thelen et al. 2021
;
Pierce et al. 1997
;
Schöpfer et al. 2013
;
Rohringer et al. 2019
;
Claes et al. 2021
and to access specific modes in the response spectrum
Prada, Balogun, and Murray 2005
;
Clorennec, Prada, and Royer 2007
;
Watzl et al. 2022
;
Ryzy et al. 2023
;
Watzl et al. 2025
;
Kiefer, Mezil, and Prada 2023
.
Importantly, the contact-free operation of LUS allows for flexible scanning of the wave field under various environmental and boundary conditions. This is particularly valuable for model validation and for inverse characterization of material parameters.
We are aware of two studies
Yang and Tsai 2006
;
Chen, Zhao, and Pan 2011
that present LUS measurements of piezoelectric plates, but the effect of electrical BCs was not examined therein.

In this work, we present a semi-analytical model for guided electroelastic waves in piezoelectric plates based on spectral methods. Our focus lies on the highly instructive spectral collocation method (SCM)
Trefethen 2000
;
Fornberg 1996
;
Weideman and Reddy 2000
.
The SCM was previously shown to be efficient and robust to compute dispersion of purely elastic waves
Adamou and Craster 2004
;
Hernando Quintanilla, Lowe, and Craster 2015
.
Its main advantage is the straight-forward implementation based on the underlying partial differential equations in their conventional strong form.
The SCM code by Kiefer
Kiefer 2022
is extended to piezoelectric plates and we make our implementation publicly available under the name
GEW piezo plate

Kiefer and Grünsteidl 2024
.
As an alternative, highly related method we also introduce the spectral element method (SEM)
Fichtner 2010
;
Gravenkamp, Song, and Prager 2012
in Appendix
B
. While it is numerically superior to the SCM, it is at the same time conceptually more intricate. Our SEM implementation will be provided in
GEWtool

Kiefer 2023
, a software for guided wave analysis.
Overall, we hope to demonstrate with this contribution the power of spectral methods, the simplicity of SCM, and contribute to a wider adoption of semi-analytical methods for dispersion computations.

Furthermore, we validate our model with LUS experiments and contribute a compilation of multi-modal guided wave spectra in
\text{LiNbO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
wafers, including two different propagation directions and three different electrical BCs.

VII
Conclusion

We presented the SCM to calculate dispersion curves and mode shapes for guided electroelastic waves in piezoelectric plates. LUS experiments were conducted to measure dispersion curves on a
\text{LiNbO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
wafer. Comparison of theory and experiment for different directions and electrical BCs showed excellent agreement. From these results, material characterization based on an inverse problem seems feasible.
A need for new characterization techniques is especially present in the case of deposited thin films and at high frequencies. The properties of thin films often differ from the bulk material, and depend on the growing process and layer thickness.
We consider the possibility to measure a sample under different electrical BCs especially promising to disentangle elastic, piezoelectric and electric material properties.

The SCM can be extended to multi-layered plates by the approach presented in Ref.
Hernando Quintanilla, Lowe, and Craster 2015
. This is particularly interesting for MEMS devices, which often consist of layered structures.

The model could then be used in combination with LUS measurements on different production steps of a stacked MEMS device, e.g., on the pure substrate of a SAW device and then on the deposited stack, including electrodes.
Previous work has proven that LUS can indeed be used to measure guided waves and resonances in the GHz range for material characterization purposes
Ryzy et al. 2023
;
Grünsteidl et al. 2020
.
With the optical spot sizes that are achievable in practice, effective generation and detection of guided waves down to
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
wavelength is feasible.

As an alternative to SCM we discussed the SEM in Appendix
B
. It is based on the weak-form of the waveguide problem and is numerically superior to SCM but also somewhat more difficult to understand. An implementation of the SEM will be available in
GEWtool

Kiefer 2023
for multi-layered piezoelectric plates.

Appendix B
Spectral element discretization

The spectral element method (SEM) is numerically superior to SCM because it (i) conserves the hermitian symmetry of the problem, (ii) preserves the regularity of the operators and (iii) converges faster. These properties also facilitate the postprocessing, e.g., computation of group velocities
Finnveden 2004
and zero-group-velocity points
Kiefer et al. 2023
as well as mode-sorting
Gravenkamp, Plestenjak, and Kiefer 2023
. SEM is akin to the finite element method but uses high-order polynomials defined on the entire domain as a basis, i.e., no meshing is necessary. In this section we sketch the SEM-discretization of the waveguide problem without providing theoretical details, for which we rather refer to the literature
Hughes 2012
;
Gravenkamp, Song, and Prager 2012
.

First, the boundary-value problem (
10
) and (
12
) needs to be cast into the corresponding weak form. To this end we introduce appropriate test functions
\psi(z)
corresponding to
\Psi(z)
, i.e., as a four-component vector function.
Multiplying (
10
) with
\psi^{T}(z)
from the left, integrating over the domain and performing a partial integration of the terms in
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
leads to the
weak form of the waveguide problem
:

\displaystyle\int_{-h/2}^{+h/2}\psi^{T}

\displaystyle\left[(\mathup{i}\mkern 1.0muk)^{2}\mathcal{A}+\mathup{i}\mkern 1.0muk\mathcal{B}^{T}\partial_{z}+\omega^{2}\mathcal{M}\right]\Psi\,\mathup{d}z

\displaystyle-\int_{-h/2}^{+h/2}\partial_{z}\psi^{T}\left[\mathup{i}\mkern 1.0muk\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}\partial_{z}\right]\Psi\,\mathup{d}z

\displaystyle+\left.\left[\psi^{T}(-\mathup{i}\mkern 1.0muk\mathcal{V})\Psi\right]\right|_{-h/2}^{+h/2}=0\,.

(21)

Thereby we have incorporated the Neumann BC from (
12
) into the bracketed boundary term.

Second, discretization is performed by approximating

\displaystyle\Psi(z)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j}\underline{\Psi}_{j}P_{j}(z)\,,

\displaystyle\psi(z)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i}\underline{\psi}_{i}P_{i}(z)\,,

(22)

as a weighted superposition of
N
known polynomials
P_{j}(z)
with
j\in\{1...N\}
and 4-component vector coefficients
\underline{\Psi}_{j}
,
\underline{\psi}_{i}
. We choose Lagrange polynomials
P_{j}(z)
defined on the standard Gauß-Lobatto-Legendre points
Hughes 2012
.

Accounting for (
22
) in (
B
), the equation must hold for arbitrary
\underline{\psi}_{i}
, so that we can write the discrete system

\displaystyle[\,(\mathup{i}\mkern 1.0muk)^{2}\underbrace{\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathrm{PP}}_{L_{2}}+\mathup{i}\mkern 1.0muk\underbrace{(\mathcal{B}^{T}\otimes\mathrm{PD}-\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathrm{PD}^{T}-\mathcal{V}\otimes\mathrm{BB})}_{L_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{-\mathcal{C}\otimes\mathrm{DD}}_{L_{0}}+\omega^{2}\underbrace{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathrm{PP}}_{M}\,]\Psi_{\mathup{d}}=0\,,

(23)

where
\Psi_{\mathup{d}}=[\underline{\Psi}_{j}]
,
j\in\{1...N\}
, and the
N\times N
-matrices

\displaystyle\mathrm{PP}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{-h/2}^{+h/2}P_{i}P_{j}\,\mathup{d}z\,,

(24a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PD}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{-h/2}^{+h/2}P_{i}\partial_{z}P_{j}\,\mathup{d}z\,,

(24b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{DD}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{-h/2}^{+h/2}\partial_{z}P_{i}\partial_{z}P_{j}\,\mathup{d}z\,,

(24c)

\displaystyle\mathrm{BB}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\left.\left[P_{i}P_{j}\right]\right|_{-h/2}^{+h/2}\,.

(24d)

Note that in (
B
) we have made use of the Kronecker product between
\mathrm{PP}
and
\mathcal{A}
(and similar for other terms), defined in block form as
\mathrm{PP}\otimes\mathcal{A}=[\mathrm{PP}_{ij}\,\mathcal{A}]
.
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathrm{PP}
is a permutation thereof that leads to an equally valid linear system. As usual, the matrices (
24
) are computed by numerical integration of the known basis polynomials. Dirichlet BCs, e.g., electrically shorted surfaces, can be implemented by removing the corresponding degree of freedom, as was done in Sec.
III
. The presented SEM procedure is implemented in
GEWtool

Kiefer 2023
. A validation and convergence analysis can be found in Appendix
A
.
