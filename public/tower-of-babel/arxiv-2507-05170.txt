Predictive posteriors under hidden confounding
Carlos García Meixide, David Ríos Insua, [0.5ex] Instituto de Ciencias Matemáticas, CSIC, [-0.5ex] Departamento de Matemáticas, Universidad Autónoma de Madrid

Abstract
Predicting outcomes in external domains is challenging due to hidden confounders that influence both predictors and outcomes, complicating generalization under distribution shifts. Traditional methods often rely on stringent assumptions or overly conservative regularization, compromising estimation and predictive accuracy. Generative Invariance (GI) is a novel framework that facilitates predictions in unseen domains without requiring hyperparameter tuning or knowledge of specific distribution shifts. However, the available frequentist version of GI does not always enable identification and lacks uncertainty quantification for its predictions. This paper develops a Bayesian formulation that extends GI with well-calibrated external predictions and facilitates causal discovery. We present theoretical guarantees showing that prior distributions assign asymptotic meaning to the number of distinct datasets that could be observed. Simulations and an application case highlight the remarkable empirical coverage behavior of our approach, nearly unchanged when transitioning from low- to moderate-dimensional settings.



1
Introduction

In recent years, there has been a surge of interest in machine learning methods that demonstrate robust performance even when there are shifts in the distribution between training and test data
[
Wong et al. 2021
,
Miller et al. 2020
]
. Much of the existing research in this area focuses on point predictions, often necessary for communication and traditional practices, requiring the definition of an appropriate loss function for evaluation,
typically squared error loss. In contrast, probabilistic predictions offer a detailed quantification of uncertainty for future outcomes and are typically more relevant for risk management and decision support purposes, though they are more complex to construct and communicate.

This paper focuses on creating probabilistic predictions for a response variable
Y\in\mathbb{R}
based on a set of covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, therefore yielding prediction intervals. When a machine learning strategy is trained on a labeled source dataset, it can be expected to perform well on an unlabeled target dataset drawn from the same distribution, provided that generalization guarantees hold
[
Boucheron et al. 2013
]
. However, numerous applications often experience distribution shifts, challenging the standard independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption and complicating the use of existing prediction methods.

The majority of research addressing distribution shifts has concentrated on point prediction in regression or classification. One notable strategy is
distributionally robust optimization
, introduced by
Sinha et al. 2020
, which involves minimax optimization over distributions within a certain distance or divergence from the observed data distribution. Another important approach, initiated by
Rothenhäusler et al. 2021
, employs a causality-inspired framework to leverage the underlying data structure, thereby improving prediction stability amidst distributional shifts. However, the regularization scheme proposed by the authors prevents the causal parameters from being the actual probability limit of the estimators they introduce; i.e., they are not asymptotically unbiased.

Another framework for quantifying uncertainty of any predictive algorithm is
conformal prediction
. Recent developments in this literature have shown a growing interest in robustness, particularly in constructing robust prediction intervals for a fixed marginal coverage level rather than estimating full distributions
[
Tibshirani et al. 2019
]
. In addition, causality has recently been related to a certain risk minimization problem with a corresponding notion of robustness
[
Bühlmann 2020
]
. To our knowledge, only two studies address robust probabilistic prediction in the causality literature:
Kook et al. 2022
proposed
distributional anchor regression
for conditional transformation models as an extension of the above mentioned; and
Henzi et al. 2024
analysed invariance for probabilistic predictions penalizing empirical risk minimization with the variance of the scoring rule computed across environments.

Ai and Ren 2024
argue that distribution shifts comprise both “covariate” and “
Y|X
” types of shifts.
Covariate shifts refer to differences in the marginal distribution of
X
between training and target environments, due e.g. to age or gender distributional structure variations.
Y|X
shifts involve changes in the conditional relationship between the outcome and covariates, potentially due to unobserved confounders or noncompliance
[
Robins 1989
]
.

Ai and Ren 2024
suggest reweighting training samples based on estimated covariate shift and adjusting confidence levels to accommodate the worst-case
Y|X
shift. Importantly, both types of shifts are fundamentally different: while covariate shifts can be identifiable from data, the type
Y|X
itself is not inherently identifiable.

Work introduced in
Meixide and
Insua 2025
formulates linear algebra hypotheses that enable identification of causal parameters through the so-called Generative Invariance (GI), a projection estimand that adapts predictions to unseen domains without requiring knowledge of the specific test distribution shifts or hyperparameter tuning. GI achieves identifiability under mild, verifiable conditions and accommodates the influence of unobserved confounders across heterogeneous training environments through a parametric component. By redefining the role of invariance and avoiding the over-conservatism of classical instrumental variable (IV) methods
[
Angrist et al. 1996
]
, GI ensures optimal predictions while preserving unbiased estimation of causal parameters, marking a fundamental shift from the traditional view of predictive robustness as a minimax optimization problem, as GI does not require introducing bias into estimators of causal parameters. This is made possible by a novel mechanism that overpasses regularization techniques typically used in robustness frameworks
[
Rothenhäusler et al. 2021
,
Shen et al. 2025
]
. In this regard, GI is the first method exploiting distributional information from unlabeled data in the target domain to provide asymptotically unbiased domain adaptation.

This paper builds upon GI to enable further exploration and applicability of its core concepts bringing in Bayesian methods. Frequentist GI facilitates the derivation of an asymptotic normality result for causal parameters but does not say anything about uncertainty at the predictive scale on
Y
. Here we shall provide principled uncertainty quantification for GI, which will be crucial in at least two important respects.

First, we open the door towards credible intervals for causal relationships under hidden confounding and, therefore, enable simultaneous causal discovery and calibrated predictions in this setting: to the best of our knowledge, we deliver the first computationally feasible hypothesis test of a covariate being in the parental set of a target response in a linear structural equation model.
Note that obtaining credible statements for existing causal discovery methods is often difficult as one would need to determine the distribution of causal effects estimators after having searched and estimated a graphical structure of the model.
Peters et al. 2016
were able to control against false positive causal selection gathering all submodels that are statistically invariant across environments in a suitable sense. Pursuing this thread further,
Fan et al. 2024
obtain a finite sample variable selection property and its asymptotic relaxation in high dimensions. Two important limitations of this methodological family is having to assume no hidden confounding and the lack of computational scalability due to an exponentially increasing number of hypothesis tests to be performed, apart from being statistical power not well understood in their context.

Second, while classical definitions frame identifiability as a binary property (a parameter is either identified or not), we are the first to explicitly articulate the empirical manifestation of weak identifiability as a gradual phenomenon, intimately connected to ill-posedness of inverse problems, as extensively studied recently by
Ghassami et al. 2025
and
Bennett et al. 2023
. In such settings—common in nonparametric IV models or proximal causal inference—the underlying parameter must solve a conditional moment restriction involving a compact or nearly singular integral operator. The inversion problem becomes ill-posed and—although the model remains formally identifiable—variance inflates, confidence intervals widen, and estimation becomes unstable. In our framework, this will manifest as the prior progressively taking control of the posterior as identifiability blows up. As we will see, a posterior contracting toward the prior is reflecting violation of identification conditions revealing the regularized nature of Bayesian posteriors. This is in contrast to frequentist estimators, which exhibit exploding variances and numerical instability in the face of non-invertible or ill-conditioned design matrices. We make this transition from identifiability to non-identifiability visible and quantifiable, showing how Bayesian inference interpolates smoothly across this boundary.

We emphasize that IV should not be interpreted as an oracle for our method: IV is the causal truth when the goal is (asymptotically unbiased) estimation of causal parameters. However, for prediction we are rather interested in the whole conditional distribution of the response given the covariates. This includes the IV structural function plus the conditional mean of the residuals given the covariates. It is precisely this second component that
Meixide and
Insua 2025
successfully identified and incorporated in a principled manner, enabling an exact representation of the effect of hidden confounders on the response (when this underlying dependence is truly linear).

Our method calibrates well even when prior information is not available: simulations have been performed using a frequentist ground truth— see Section
6.1
, where it is clearly stated that the parameters in our simulated numerical experiments are deterministic. There is no substantive prior knowledge: aside from the empirical Bayes shrinkage on environment-specific covariate means we propose, the remaining distributions were chosen for technical convenience, following Bayesian ridge regression, and do not reflect genuine beliefs. The key message is that the prior is not used to incorporate subjective information—it simply absorbs the effects of poor identifiability. This is analogous to the well known example of Gaussian priors emerging as
l_{2}
penalties in ridge regression: while not representing expert domain knowledge, they entail important technical consequences in terms of regularization.

1.1
Contributions and related work

We offer the first principled, unified, and practical solution to two longstanding and challenging problems simultaneously: conducting hypothesis testing for causal parenting and calibrating probabilistic predictions in the presence of hidden confounding.

Wu et al. 2025
introduce a hierarchical generative model that encodes the indices of invariant predictors as a latent Bernoulli vector and, then perform posterior inference—exact or variational—to identify features whose conditional relationship with the outcome remains stable across environments. Nonetheless, as
Wu et al. 2025
’s framework exploits the original invariance assumption in
Peters et al. 2016
—namely that the conditional distribution of the outcome given the truly invariant covariates is identical across environments—it inherits the same vulnerability: when hidden confounders induce violations of this assumption (e.g., the conditional model is misspecified because of omitting covariates), the method can still select non-causal features or discard causal ones. Likewise,
Madaleno et al. 2025
reformulate Invariant Causal Prediction in a Bayesian setting—embedding per-environment coefficients in a hierarchical prior and using sparsity induced priors to screen for invariant parents—yet it still rests on the invariance assumption.
Holovchak et al. 2025
seek to recover interventional distributions, which are not designed for predictive purposes and do not provide built-in facilities for formal hypothesis testing. Our approach, on the other hand, supplies well-calibrated Bayesian posteriors for both causal coefficients and outcome predictions, yielding credible intervals and a causal discovery procedure that remain valid under hidden confounding with a shifted test environment.

We extensively evaluate the empirical coverage properties of our approach across a range of scenarios, varying both sample size (four levels) and covariate dimension (three levels). In all settings, the coverage achieved by our method closely matches the nominal theoretical level. In contrast, even the best-case performance of the baseline—Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)—is comparable to the worst-case performance of our approach.

The paper is structured as follows.
Section 2 introduces the problem illustrating
why traditional methods for external validity under hidden confounding fail, and sketches the GI solution to support our later discussion.
A Bayesian solution for the problem is delivered in two stages
within Section 3: an initial simpler setting facilitates structural understanding used to analyse
more complex settings later on. A pioneering Bayesian selector of causal variables under hidden confounding is presented in Section 4. Next, posterior consistency and contraction rates for our estimator are derived in Section 5. These ideas
are empirically corroborated in Section 6, first with two simulation setups
and then with a medical case study concerning lifestyle factors affecting the body
mass index (BMI). Finally, Section 7 poses some open research directions. We defer all proofs to an appendix.

2
Problem formulation

We use the basic setup introduced in
Meixide and
Insua 2025

to motivate the problem of interest.
Consider analyzing the relationship between the random variables
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
, formulated
through the structural model

X\leftarrow(1-\theta)X_{0}+\theta X_{1}\textrm{ and }Y\leftarrow\beta^{T}_{*}X+\varepsilon_{Y},

(1)

where
\theta\in\{0,1\}
is a binary indicator distinguishing between training (
\theta=1
) and testing (
\theta=0
) regimes.

X_{0}
and
X_{1}
are independent
p
-dimensional random vectors. The error term
\varepsilon_{Y}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{Y}|\theta]=0
, but otherwise may exhibit dependence with
X_{0}
or
X_{1}
. Upon conditioning on
\theta
, we relax the traditional independence assumptions between the components of
(X^{1},\ldots,X^{p})
and
\varepsilon_{Y}
. This departure from conventional approaches accounts for potential hidden confounders, with the endogeneity between
X
and
Y
attributed to unobserved common causes impacting the
error term. Using training data
{D}_{1}=\{(X_{1,1},Y_{1,1}),\ldots,(X_{1,n},Y_{1,n})\}
, obtained when
\theta=1
, our primary objective is thus to develop predictions for a test set
{D}_{0}=\{X_{0,1},\ldots,X_{0,{n_{0}}}\}
, corresponding to
\theta=0
. Note that model (
1
) assumes only that the covariates distribution may differ between training and testing environments, without imposing any specific structure on the nature of this shift (e.g., additive), as is commonly assumed in the literature.

Following Theorem 3.2 in
Meixide and
Insua 2025
, the setup (1) enables frequentist identification of the causal parameter
\beta_{*}
if
\mathbb{E}[X\mid\theta=1]=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]:=\mu\neq 0
in the one dimensional setting without requiring heterogeneous training sources,
showing that the minimizer
(\beta_{opt},K_{opt})
of the population least squares risk

(\beta,K)\longmapsto\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-\beta X-K(X-\mathbb{E}X))^{2}\mid\theta=1\right]

exists, is unique and attained at
\left(\beta_{*},\frac{K_{*}}{\operatorname{Var}X_{0}}\right)
with
K_{*}:=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_{Y},X_{0})=\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_{Y},X_{1})
under model (
1
) and inner-product invariance conditions
[
Rothenhäusler et al. 2019
]
. Therefore, the conditional expectation of
Y_{0}
given
X_{0}
becomes identified despite the presence of hidden confounders. A
p
-dimensional version of this identifiability result is provided in Theorem 3.4 therein, which states that if the population mean vectors of the covariate distributions corresponding to different data environments span
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, then
\beta_{*}
and
K_{*}
are identifiable.

n=1000
) serve for training purposes to fit a standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear model. Samples from a different test

3
Generative Invariance: a Bayesian perspective

This section provides a Bayesian analysis of GI
in two steps considering first a single-predictor, single-training source setup, providing
a Bayesian formulation of the least-squares problem in
Meixide and
Insua 2025
as well as analytic expressions
for the required conditional posterior distributions,
serving to
derive theoretical insights while preserving the notation streamlined.
We then consider the multiple predictor, multiple test environment setup, formulating the model, discussing computational implementation, and illustrating relevant applications.

3.1
Single predictor, single test environment

Let us provide first a Bayesian analysis of the basic setup (1) to obtain the posterior predictive distributions required to issue predictions for
\theta=0
.
We have available training

{\cal D}_{1}
and testing

{\cal D}_{0}
data,
with our objective being to predict the (missing) labels

\{Y_{0,1},...,Y_{0,n_{0}}\}
in this testing environment.
In the training environment, let us formulate

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\beta X_{i}+K(X_{i}-\mu)+\varepsilon^{\prime}_{Y,i},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}_{Y,i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{Y}^{2}),

\displaystyle X_{i}\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2}_{X}),

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n

Complete this with standard normal-inverse gamma priors

[
French and Ríos Insua 2000
]
from Bayesian linear regression models:

\displaystyle\mu|\sigma^{2}_{\mu}\sim N(\hat{\mu},\tau_{\mu}^{2}\sigma_{\mu}^{2}),

\displaystyle\beta,K|\sigma^{2}_{Y}\sim N(0,\tau_{Y}^{2}\sigma_{Y}^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\sim\operatorname{Inv-Gamma}(a_{\mu},b_{\mu}),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{X}\sim\operatorname{Inv-Gamma}(a_{X},b_{X})

\displaystyle\sigma_{Y}^{2}\sim\operatorname{Inv-Gamma}(a_{Y},b_{Y}),

\displaystyle\tau_{Y}^{2}\sim\operatorname{Inv-Gamma}(a_{\tau},b_{\tau})

\displaystyle\tau_{\mu}^{2}\sim\operatorname{Inv-Gamma}(a_{\tau_{\mu}},b_{\tau_{\mu}})

with
\hat{\mu}
the sample average of the covariate. Then, the log-posterior is calculated as

\displaystyle\log p(\beta,K,\mu,\sigma_{\mu},\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y},\tau_{\mu},\tau_{Y}|{D}_{1})\propto

\displaystyle\log p(Y|X,\beta,K,\mu,\sigma_{Y})+\log p(X|\mu,\sigma_{X})+

\displaystyle\log p(\beta|\sigma_{Y},\tau_{Y})+\log p(K|\sigma_{Y},\tau_{Y})+\log p(\mu|\sigma_{\mu})+

\displaystyle\log p(\sigma_{X})+\log p(\sigma_{Y})+\log p(\tau_{Y})+\log p(\tau_{\mu})

\displaystyle=-\frac{n}{2}\log\sigma_{Y}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\beta X_{i}-K(X_{i}-\mu))^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{2}\log\sigma_{X}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{X}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log(\sigma_{Y}^{2}\tau_{Y}^{2})-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}\tau_{Y}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\log(\sigma_{Y}^{2}\tau^{2})-\frac{K^{2}}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}\tau_{Y}^{2}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\log\sigma_{\mu}^{2}-\frac{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mu}^{2}}-(a_{X}+1)\log\sigma_{X}^{2}-\frac{b_{X}}{\sigma_{X}^{2}}

\displaystyle-(a_{Y}+1)\log\sigma_{Y}^{2}-\frac{b_{Y}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}-(a_{\tau}+1)\log\tau^{2}-\frac{b_{\tau}}{\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle-(a_{\mu}+1)\log\sigma_{\mu}^{2}-\frac{b_{\mu}}{\sigma_{\mu}^{2}}-(a_{\tau_{\mu}}+1)\log\tau_{\mu}^{2}-\frac{b_{\mu}}{\tau_{\mu}^{2}}

Using standard arguments, the marginal posteriors for the variances
\tau_{Y}^{2},\tau_{X}^{2},\sigma_{Y}^{2},\sigma_{X}^{2},\sigma_{\mu}^{2}
, are
inverse-gamma. In turn, observe that given
\mu
and the other
\tau^{2}
,
\sigma^{2}
parameters, the joint marginal
posterior of
\beta
and
K
is

\displaystyle\log p(\beta,K\mid\mu,\tau_{Y},\tau_{X},\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{X},\sigma_{\mu},{D}_{1})\propto-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{\tau_{X}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{K^{2}}{2\sigma_{Y}^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mu)^{2}-\frac{1}{\tau_{Y}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-2\beta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}X_{i}\right)-2K\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right)

\displaystyle+2\beta K\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mu)\right),

which corresponds to a bivariate normal distribution and, hence, the marginals of
\beta
and
K
, given the remaining
parameters, are also normal. This derivation serves to show that an MCMC sampler is easily derived (we shall use
Rstan
, but understanding its structure can be useful for potential specific implementations crafted towards our estimator). On the other hand, this joint marginal posterior facilitates understanding the relation between
\beta
and
K
, later used to discuss identifiability.
Finally, the marginal posterior of
\mu
given the other
parameters is normal as well,
so that a Gibbs
sampler may be implemented to obtain a sample from the posterior and, consequently, from the
predictive distribution of the targeted posterior missing labels.

Frequentist GI heavily relies on a key assumption: the linear independence of the environment-wise population mean vectors of the covariates. The further the identifiability condition is from being violated, the weaker the influence of the priors on
\beta
and
K
.
However, in settings close to linear dependence—where the frequentist method may encounter numerical issues due to matrix non-invertibility—our Bayesian formulation instead applies a controlled shrinkage toward external input, thereby avoiding the computational instability derived from a theoretical violation of identifiability.

, when
\mu=0
, the term
K(X_{i}-\mu)
collapses to

making
\beta

unidentifiable since
\beta X_{i}+KX_{i}
could be satisfied by infinitely many combinations of that sum to the same total. Figure

\lambda

3.2
Multiple predictors, multiple test environments

Consider now the more complex case with
E
training environments and
p
covariates. Let
\mathbb{P}_{e}
,
e=1,\ldots,E
, denote the law giving rise to each data source. The sample from the fully observed environments is, therefore,

\left(X_{e,1},Y_{e,1}\right),\ldots,\left(X_{e,n_{e}},Y_{e,n_{e}}\right)\sim\mathbb{P}_{e},\quad e=1,\ldots,E

which we condensate through
{D}_{E}=\{(Z_{1},X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots(Z_{m},X_{m},Y_{m})\}
, where
Z_{i}
is a dummy indicator denoting from which environment did the
i
-th observation come from and
m=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{E}
. Recall the test sample is
\left(X_{0,1},Y_{0,1}\right),\ldots,\left(X_{0,n_{0}},Y_{0,n_{0}}\right)\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}
, from which just the covariates are observed and we want to predict the labels.

Assuming that the model coefficients are a priori normally distributed with mean zero and common variance, we arrive to a situation similar to a Bayesian version of the usual ridge regression procedure
[
Tew et al. 2023
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid\left(X_{i},Z_{i},\text{parameters}\right)\sim N\left(\beta^{T}X_{i}+K^{T}(X_{i}-\mu^{Z_{i}}),\sigma_{Y}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle X_{i}\mid(Z_{i},\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{E},\hat{\Sigma}_{Z_{i}})\sim N_{p}(\mu^{Z_{i}},\hat{\Sigma}_{Z_{i}}),\quad i=1,\dots,m

where
\hat{\Sigma}_{Z_{i}}
is estimated by computing
the covariance of the matrix containing the covariate samples in the environment
Z_{i}
.
Note that an intercept is automatically included in the model by adding a column of
n
ones to the design matrix
X
. In this case, if the predictors were originally in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, now
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
so that we need
Z\geq p+1
data environments to identify
\beta
and the new ingredient
K
. For instance, in a one-dimensional example, including an intercept just involves having to observe data from two different environments instead of one single dataset. There are no other constraints. Also, observe that the “correction” term has expectation
\mathbb{E}\left(K^{T}\left(X-\mu^{Z}\right)\mid Z\right)=K^{T}\mathbb{E}\left(\left(X-\mu^{Z}\right)\mid Z\right)=K^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}(X\mid Z)-\mu^{Z}\right)=K^{T}\left(\mu^{Z}-\mu^{Z}\right)=0
, so it does not have anything to do with the intercept.

The priors are specified as

\displaystyle\mu^{1},\dots,\mu^{E}

\displaystyle\sim N_{p}(\hat{\mu},{\Sigma_{\mu}}),

\displaystyle\beta,K\mid\tau^{2},\sigma_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim N_{p}\left(0,\tau^{2}\sigma_{Y}^{2}I_{p}\right),

\displaystyle\sigma_{Y}^{2}\sim\sigma_{Y}^{-2}d\sigma_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle,\tau^{2}\sim\pi(\tau^{2})\,d\tau^{2}.

with
\Sigma_{\mu}
built upon a correlation matrix
R
with prior distribution
R\sim\text{LKJ}(2)

[
Lewandowski et al. 2009
]
, which has a single shape parameter
\eta
so that the probability density function for a
p\times p
matrix
R
is
\propto[\operatorname{det}({R})]^{\eta-1}
, being the default prior assigned by Stan to correlation matrices. In addition,
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
,
\beta
and
K
are independent conditional on
\tau^{2}
. When there is not strong prior knowledge about how much the regression coefficients should be shrunk, we can use a beta-prime distribution as a default prior for
\tau^{2}
, also known as the inverted-beta and Pearson Type VI distribution
[
Polson and Scott 2012
]
, with density

\pi\left(\tau^{2}\right)=\frac{\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{a-1}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-a-b}}{B(a,b)},a>0,b>0,

where
B(a,b)
represents the beta function and
a=b=1/2
is usually set, leading to the standard half-Cauchy prior. The latter is particularly useful as a weakly informative prior for scale parameters because of its heavy tails.

We emphasize though that the specification of priors is not the central contribution of this work. In fact, it will be shown in Section
5
that the prior affects the contraction rate only as a multiplicative constant, not through the exponential term.

In the canonical Bayesian linear model, the posterior mode (and mean) is equivalent to the frequentist ridge estimate with penalty
\lambda=1/\tau^{2}
. In our case, we shall express the population heterogeneity leading to each environment as sampled from a common prior distribution to get a beneficial shrinkage effect.
N_{p}(\hat{\mu},{\Sigma_{\mu}})
is thus viewed as an empirical Bayes distribution characterizing the variability of the covariate means induced by naturally occurring environments. It is useful to think of the first step of the data generating process as the covariate means being drawn from a common prior. Note though that we just plug the empirical mean and not the variance of this distribution, to avoid a problem
frequently occurring in empirical Bayes estimation
[
Law et al. 2023
]
: for
simplicity, assume that
p=1
and all the environments gave rise to the same number
n
of observations; then, it is possible to build, via maximum likelihood, an estimator for the Gaussian prior variance as
\max\left\{\frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}\left(\hat{\mu}^{e}-\hat{\mu}\right)^{2}-\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{n},0\right\}

where
\hat{\sigma}
is a consistent estimator of an asymptotic variance. If the previous max collapses
to 0, then the resulting posterior is a point mass and the empirical Bayes posterior predictive region is a singleton. We escape from this problem by associating a hyperprior to this parameter.

We could derive the explicit expression for the conditional posterior distributions of
\beta
and
K
given
{D}_{E}
, as
we did in Section
3.1
, but it would not provide additional insights. Therefore the model is directly implemented in
Rstan

[
Guo et al. 2020
]
.
For this, let us obtain the predictive distribution of
Y_{0}
conditional on
X_{0}
.
Meixide and
Insua 2025
show that we recover the test distribution under inner product invariance
[
Rothenhäusler et al. 2019
]
, error Gaussianity and the so-called
causal Mahalanobis conditions
as

\begin{bmatrix}X_{0}\\
\beta^{T}_{*}X_{0}+K_{*}^{T}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\left(X_{0}-\mathbb{E}X_{0}\right)+\xi S_{0}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}

with
S_{0}=\sqrt{\sigma_{Y}^{2}-K_{*}^{T}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}K_{*}}
and
\xi\sim N(0,1)
independent of
X_{0}

Denoting by
w=(\beta,K)
, the predictive distribution for
f=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{0}|X_{0},w\right]=\beta^{T}X_{0}+K^{T}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\left(X_{0}-\mathbb{E}X_{0}\right)
is given by averaging it with respect to the posterior as

\displaystyle p\left(f\mid X_{0},{D}_{E}\right)

\displaystyle=\int p\left(f\mid X_{0},{w}\right)p({w}\mid{D}_{E})d{w},

from which we obtain the predictive posterior by adding randomly sampled
N(0,S^{2}_{0})

An example of a case where the predictive posterior has a closed form expression is given by trivially pooling all the observations into one single training source (denoted by

) without considering the dataset indicators and assuming a fixed-design linear model

is the prior covariance matrix of

. The posterior predictive distribution is

p\left(Y_{0}\mid X_{0},D\right)=N\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}X_{0}^{\top}A^{-1}X^{T}Y,X_{0}^{\top}A^{-1}X_{0}+\sigma^{2}_{Y}\right)

and we use it to build a credible interval

by considering, for instance, its
\frac{\alpha}{2}

quantiles. In our more complex case, where

4
Causal discovery under hidden confounding

A type I error guarantee
[
Peters et al. 2016
]
for causal discovery is known to hold under the invariance hypothesis, which allows exploiting that the conditional distribution of the response given the complete set of corresponding direct causal predictors must remain identical under interventions on variables other than the target variable. However, the presence of hidden variables (the “
Y|X
shift”) breaks invariance.
Peters et al. 2016
obtain the intersection
\mathcal{\hat{S}}
of all the subsets of covariates not rejecting the null hypothesis of invariance through a test able to control the proportion of false positive selections:
\mathbb{P}[\hat{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq\mathrm{pa}(Y)]\geq 1-\alpha
where
\mathrm{pa}(Y)=\{1\leq j\leq p:\beta^{j}_{*}\neq 0\}
. Taking the intersection makes their procedure conservative in terms of power, which, on another note, is highly dependent on the structure of the observed environments.

In turn, BGI is able to correctly estimate the posterior distribution of the causal parameters
\beta_{*}
and
K_{*}
, and, therefore, automatically enables for hypothesis testing of one component
\beta^{j}_{*}
being zero,
j=1,...,p
. Since our method provides the posterior distribution
p(\beta^{j}_{*}|D_{E})
for each coefficient, we can construct a
(1-\alpha)
credible region
\hat{C}_{\alpha}
such that:

\mathbb{P}(\beta^{j}_{*}\in\hat{C}_{\alpha}|D_{E})=1-\alpha
.
Given
\hat{C}_{\alpha}
, we can assess whether
\beta^{j}_{*}=0
is plausible by checking whether
0
lies within
\hat{C}_{\alpha}
.

The solution we propose to this problem is also more natural because the invariance hypothesis- which, we emphasize, is not required in our approach- is always formulated based on the
observed
environments
\mathcal{E}
: a subset of covariates
S\subset\{1,\ldots,p\}
fulfills the null hypothesis
H_{0,S}(\mathcal{E})
- “invariance”- iff
\mathcal{L}\left(Y^{e}\mid X_{S}^{e}\right)
(the conditional distribution of the response given the covariates in
S
) is the same for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
. This may seem a bit contradictory
as hypotheses should be assertions that hold or not pre-empirically, begging the following paradox: knowing which environments are observed or not turns out to be something known in the population world, prior to sampling– what
\mathcal{E}
is becomes an aspect of the underlying distribution before we even have sampled from it. We solve this issue automatically: given a sample
\beta^{j}_{1},\ldots,\beta^{j}_{N}
from the posterior of
\beta^{j}_{*}
we decide that
j
is a causal parent of
Y
when

\displaystyle\min\left\{\left|\left\{i:\beta^{j}_{{i}}<0\right\}\right|,\left|\left\{i:\beta^{j}_{{i}}>0\right\}\right|\right\}<\alpha N,

interpreted as the posterior distribution being sufficiently shifted away from zero, either above or below.

We apply the proposed method to select the parent variables of BMI in a clinical study in Section
6.2
.

5
Asymptotics

This section develops the large sample theory for our proposed estimator. We begin by introducing the notation and generative model shared across environments, along with the assumptions necessary for the Bayesian framework. These components enable us to establish posterior consistency and derive exponential contraction rates for the posterior distribution over the shared parameters
(\beta,K)
. Notably, our results highlight two key phenomena often left unexplained by frequentist theory: robustness to near-violations of identifiability, and the role of natural variability across environments in shaping asymptotic behavior. We conclude with a discussion of asymptotic normality.

Let
D_{n,E}=\{(X_{ei},Y_{ei})\,:\,e=1,\dots,E,\;i=1,\dots,n\}
be i.i.d. within each
environment; to streamline notation, we assume the same sample size across environments, setting
n_{e}=n
for each
e

e
,

\displaystyle Y_{ei}\mid X_{ei},w,\vartheta_{e}\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\beta^{\!\top}X_{ei}+K^{\!\top}\Sigma_{e}^{-1}(X_{ei}-\mu_{e}),\sigma_{Y,e}^{2}\bigr),

\displaystyle X_{ei}\mid\vartheta_{e}\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}_{p}(\mu_{e},\Sigma_{e})

where
w=(\beta,K)\in\mathbb{R}^{2p+1}
is shared,
while the nuisance
\vartheta_{e}=(\mu_{e},\Sigma_{e},\sigma_{Y,e}^{2})
varies with
e
. In addition, the theorems in this section require the following hypotheses.

Assumption 1

(Coefficient invariance and underlying truth)
.

There exists a unique
w^{\star}=(\beta_{\star},K_{\star})
and environment-specific

\vartheta_{e}^{\star}
generating the data according to model
(M)

for all
e
.

The crucial component in Assumption 1 is the identifiability of
w^{\star}
, which follows from the linear independence
condition on the environment covariate means in
Meixide and
Insua 2025
.

Assumption 2

(Priors)
.

(i)
The prior
p(w)
is absolutely continuous and assigns positive mass
to every neighbourhood of
w^{\star}
.

(ii)
Conditioned on
w
, the nuisances

\vartheta_{1},\dots,\vartheta_{E}
are
a priori
independent with densities placing positive mass around each
\vartheta_{e}^{\star}
.

Assumption 3

(Finite KL variance)
.

For every
e
and
w
, the log–likelihood ratio per observation

\ell_{ei}(w)=\log\!\bigl\{p(Y_{ei},X_{ei}\mid w^{\star},\vartheta_{e}^{\star})/p(Y_{ei},X_{ei}\mid w,\vartheta_{e}^{\star})\bigr\}

satisfies
\operatorname{Var}[\ell_{ei}(w)]<\infty
.

Denote
k_{e}(w):=\mathbb{E}[\ell_{ei}(w)]
the environment-wise gaps. Write the expected likelihood ratios as KL divergences and define

k_{\min}:=\min_{w\neq w^{\star}}\frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(p_{e}(\cdot\mid w^{\star})\,\|\,p_{e}(\cdot\mid w)\bigr)

We start by establishing the posterior consistency of
(\beta,K)
, so that credible sets shrink to neighborhoods of
w^{\star}
meaning that any posterior credible region of fixed nominal level will eventually cover only points arbitrarily close to
w^{\star}
. We recall that on general metric spaces, posterior consistency is equivalent to weak convergence of the posterior distribution to the Dirac measure at the true parameter value
w^{\star}

[
Ghosal and Van der Vaart 2017
, Proposition 6.2]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Pi_{n}(\cdot\mid D_{n,E})
be the BGI posterior distribution. Under Assumptions
1
–
3
,

\Pi_{n}(\cdot\mid D_{n,E})\rightsquigarrow\delta_{w^{*}}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{P}\text{-probability, as }n\to\infty,

where
\rightsquigarrow
denotes weak convergence of measures and
\delta_{w^{*}}
is the Dirac measure at
w^{*}
.

The posterior distribution is itself a random object because it depends on the observed data. Therefore, when we say that the posterior is consistent, we mean that it converges to a point mass at the true parameter value both in a measure-theoretic and probabilistic sense. Specifically, posterior consistency means weak convergence (outer convergence) of the posterior distribution to the Dirac measure at the true parameter
w^{\star}
, in probability (inner convergence) under the true data-generating distribution
\mathbb{P}
. The weak convergence describes the limiting behavior of the posterior as a probability measure, while the convergence in probability reflects the randomness induced by the data because the posterior itself is a random object. As a consequence of Theorem
1
, point estimates (e.g. the posterior mean or mode) are consistent: they converge in probability to
(\beta_{\star},K_{\star})
.

In consonance with the theoretical properties in
Wu et al. 2025
, the next result entails that the posterior of
w
contracts exponentially
in
n
and
E
, with a rate that improves under greater environment heterogeneity. Importantly, this is a phenomenon that the frequentist framework for causal robustness has not been able to satisfactorily account for
[
Shen et al. 2025
]
.

Theorem 2

(Exponential posterior contraction rate)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
,

TV\!\bigl\{p(w\mid D_{n,E}),\delta_{w^{\star}}\bigr\}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\bigl(e^{-\kappa\,k_{\min}\,nE}\bigr)

for every
\kappa\in(0,1)
.

Remark 1
.

During the proof of Theorem
2
, the impact of the prior pushing most probability into the neighbourhood of
w^{\star}
has a scaling effect over the whole rate. This means that the influence of the prior is entirely outside the exponent involved in the contraction rate.

Finally, the well-known asymptotic properties of posterior distributions in Gaussian linear models
[
Ghosal et al. 2000

Bontemps 2011

reveal that the posterior distribution of

converges weakly to a normal distribution centered on
w^{\star}
. When the covariate dimension

[

and because the priors over
\beta,K,\tau
are absolutely continuous.

7
Discussion

We have introduced a new framework that delivers calibrated probabilistic predictions and credible causal discovery simultaneously, even when distribution shifts and hidden confounders undermine conventional methods. Unlike classical IV or minimax-robust approaches—which either sacrifice unbiasedness through regularization or offer no uncertainty quantification—BGI uses a hierarchical prior to fuse information from multiple training environments and unlabeled target data, yielding fully Bayesian posteriors for both causal parameters and future outcomes. We prove that these priors endow the model with rigorous asymptotic guarantees, turning the number of distinct datasets into a meaningful measure of precision, and show how BGI enables the first practical hypothesis test for causal parenthood under hidden confounding. Extensive simulations and a real medical case study confirm that BGI maintains almost nominal coverage from low to moderate dimensions, while ordinary least squares and classical IV fail to generalize.

Regression problems involving heterogeneous datasets with the same structure but originating from different sources—such as multiple countries, demographics, or experimental groups—pose challenges like varying data distributions, cultural biases, or contextual differences. For instance, predicting housing prices unbiasedly in a new territory could use our approach fed with estate data from different cities or nations, where local economic conditions or regulations introduce shifts in feature relationships. In clinical research, patient response data from different hospitals or trials may follow the same scheme but differ in underlying population characteristics. Our approach integrates these heterogeneous data sources in an optimal way; on top of this, its Bayesian character provides inference with well-calibrated uncertainty quantification, as our simulations show, and valid hypothesis testing.

The interpretation of causal identification within the Bayesian framework remains an open and, to some extent, philosophical question.
Gustafson 2005
argue that non-identifiability manifests as posterior flatness around causal parameters. Foundational work on Bayesian inference for the Local Average Treatment Effect
[
Imbens and Rubin 1997
]
adopts what is known as the
phenomenological Bayes
approach, which views sensitivity to prior specifications as a diagnostic tool for assessing which identifying assumptions can be reasonably imposed or relaxed in IV-like settings. In
Robust Statistics
,
Huber and Ronchetti 2009
write: “Instead of worrying about things not under his control, the robust Bayesian is merely concerned with inaccuracies of specification.”
[
Wolpert 2004
]
echoes this view: “In some sense, I believe that this is the fundamentally correct paradigm for statistics—admit that the prior (and model and utility) are inaccurately specified, and find the range of implied conclusions.” While this is a critical issue for the future of causal inference, the focus of this paper has been to develop a principled methodology that enables reliable prediction and uncertainty quantification in partially observed domains—without compromising the consistency of parameter estimation in the pursuit of robustness. This is essential to ensure the validity of the causal parenthood hypothesis test we introduce.

Classical Bayesian literature has long acknowledged the role of prior distributions in resolving identifiability issues, particularly in models where the likelihood is weakly informative or structurally deficient. For example,
Richardson and Green 1997
developed a fully Bayesian treatment for mixture models with an unknown number of components, where identifiability is inherently limited by symmetry and label-switching. Their approach, using reversible jump MCMC, shows how prior structure—especially hierarchical priors—can regularize inference and enable coherent uncertainty quantification, even when classical estimators would be ill-posed or degenerate. Similarly,
Müller and Insua 1998
addressed the problem of designing efficient MCMC algorithms for inference with feedforward neural networks, where identifiability is lost due to the overparameterization and complex posterior geometry of non-linear architectures.

This perspective directly relates to the main problem of our paper: the parameter
\mu
(prior mean of the distribution naturally leading to the different covariate averages accross environments) enters into the model not as a nuisance to be estimated and fixed but as a random quantity. In the frequentist setting, lack of identifiability—e.g., when
\mu\to 0
—results in non-invertible design matrices and unstable estimators. We recall Figure
2
, where the posterior of
\beta
shrinks towards its prior mean as identifiability deteriorates. While this introduces some dependence on the prior, it avoids the pathologies of the frequentist alternative. Again, this exemplifies how Bayesian methods can express the blurry nature of identifiability through the introduction of priors
[
Gustafson 2005
]
. The cost, of course, is that the results will depend to some degree on the chosen prior structure.
The adversarial machine learning (AML) arena
[
Cinà et al. 2023
]
considers situations in which an adversary modifies the operational data distribution in pursue of some objective, thereby inducing a distribution shift. Bayesian approaches
[
Rios Insua et al. 2023
]
seem natural to model the available partial information about how the attacker might have perturbed such adistribution. Moreover, as attackers interact with an ML system, they learn how to modify their attacks inducing further shifts. In this context, the
attacker
seeks to manipulate instances to fool an ML algorithm in search of one or more objectives depending on the application
[
Rios Insua et al. 2023
]
, therefore inducing a distribution shift.

A natural extension of this work involves modeling adversarial attacks in machine learning scenarios where the attacker manipulates only the covariates with the goal of steering the posterior predictive mean toward a desired target value. Under a Bayesian linear regression model with a normal-inverse-gamma prior, this reduces to a constrained optimization problem in which the attacker perturbs the input data within a fixed
L_{2}
norm budget to shift the predictive mean. The attack is feasible when the desired shift lies within a ball determined by the posterior mean of the regression coefficients, and the optimal perturbation aligns with the direction of those coefficients. If the attacker’s target is uncertain and modeled probabilistically, this uncertainty induces a distribution over the perturbations and, consequently, over the manipulated inputs. This distribution can then be used to update prior beliefs about the regression coefficients and other model parameters. A promising next step would be to extend this framework to BGI and investigate how such adversarial perturbations can inform or refine prior distributions in that setting.

Another promising direction consists on relaxing the assumption that the distribution of confounders and their influence on observed variables remains constant across environments. Similar to our approach, where environment means are naturally sampled from a prior rather than relying on frequentist estimators, thereby easing strict frequentist identification conditions, each environment could be assigned a distinct
K^{z}_{*}
, all shrunk through a prior. More broadly, further investigation into relaxing identifiability conditions is needed and would have a significant impact on the field of causal representation learning, which currently relies on the assumption that the number of environments must at least equal the number of latent nodes
[
Jin and Syrgkanis 2024
]
.

Additional theoretical questions remain open for future investigation. First, when generalizing to a new domain we
should check whether Generative Invariance achieve a marginal PAC guarantee
[
Zhang and Candès 2024
]
.
Specifically, for
(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}
,
we would like to establish whether

\mathbb{P}\left\{Y_{n+1}\notin\hat{C}_{n}\left(X_{n+1}\right)\mid D_{E}\right\}\leq\alpha+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

with high probability
or even a stronger guarantee conditional on
X_{n+1}
. It would be interesting to achieve these guarantees using the martingale posterior framework
[
Fong et al. 2024
]
. This approach could be particularly powerful as it generalizes traditional Bayesian posteriors while eliminating the need for explicit likelihood-prior construction.

where

S_{n,E}(w)=\sum_{e=1}^{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{ei}(w)\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{ei}(w)\;=\;\log p_{e}\bigl(Y_{ei}\mid X_{ei},w^{\star},\vartheta_{e}^{\star}\bigr)-\log p_{e}\bigl(Y_{ei}\mid X_{ei},w,\vartheta_{e}^{\star}\bigr).

By Assumption
1
–
3
, for each
w\neq w^{\star}

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\ell_{ei}(w)\bigr]=k_{e}(w)\geq 0
with strict inequality
for some
e
, and
\ell_{ei}(w)
has finite variance. We proceed following the same case analysis—branching into the regimes of increasing
n
and increasing
E
—as in
Wu et al. 2025
.

First, suppose
n\to\infty
with
E
fixed. Treating
\{\,\sum_{e}\ell_{ei}(w)\}_{i=1}^{n}
as an i.i.d. sequence with mean

\sum_{e}k_{e}(w)=:E\,k(w)>0
for
w\neq w^{\star}
, the strong law of large numbers (SLLN) gives

\frac{1}{n}S_{n,E}(w)\;\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\;Ek(w),\quad\frac{1}{n}S_{n,E}(w^{\star})\;\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\;0.

Dividing numerator and denominator of the posterior at
w^{\star}
by

p(w^{\star})\exp\{-S_{n,E}(w^{\star})\}
yields

p\bigl(w^{\star}\mid D_{n,E}\bigr)=\frac{1}{1+\displaystyle\sum_{w\neq w^{\star}}\frac{p(w)}{p(w^{\star})}\exp\!\{\,S_{n,E}(w^{\star})-S_{n,E}(w)\}}.

Since for each
w\neq w^{\star}
,

\frac{1}{n}\bigl[S_{n,E}(w^{\star})-S_{n,E}(w)\bigr]\;\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\;-E\,k(w)<0,

Lemma 2 of
Wu et al. 2025
gives

\exp\{S_{n,E}(w^{\star})-S_{n,E}(w)\}\to 0
in probability,
and hence
p(w^{\star}\mid D_{n,E})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}1
.

Second, suppose
E\to\infty
with
n
fixed. An entirely analogous argument applies by viewing

\{\sum_{i}\ell_{ei}(w)\}_{e=1}^{E}
as i.i.d. with mean
n\,k(w)
,
and again invoking SLLN and Lemma 2 of
Wu et al. 2025
.

Consequently, since in either regime—
n\to\infty
or
E\to\infty
—we have

p(w^{\star}\mid D_{n,E})\to 1
in probability, it follows that

p(w^{\star}\mid D_{n,E})\xrightarrow{P}1
whenever
nE\to\infty
.
\square

where
P_{\max}:=\sup_{w\in B_{r_{n}}^{c}}p(w)

is the minimum prior density in a small ball around the invariant
