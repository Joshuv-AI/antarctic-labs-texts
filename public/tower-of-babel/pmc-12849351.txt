Developing a screening-guided volunteer-supported in-home respite services model: a modified online Delphi study
Authors: Linda Dong-Ling Wang, Jeff Yiu, Nga-Yan Hui, Matthew Guocheng Feng, Vivian W. Q. Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal population ageing is intensifying the demand for sustainable, person-centered care models that enable older adults to age in place. In-home respite services, particularly those supported by volunteers, offer critical support for family caregivers, especially those caring for homebound older adults. Successful implementation requires stakeholder-informed co-design. This study aimed to establish expert consensus on a screening-guided, volunteer-supported in-home respite service model.MethodsWe conducted a modified online Delphi study to co-design the service model. A panel of 17 experts, including international researchers and Hong Kong-based NGO practitioners, participated in two Delphi rounds. Participants rated the model across four domains (appropriateness, completeness, clarity, and applicability) using 9-point Likert scale, with consensus defined as ≥ 75% of ratings ≥ 7. Qualitative feedback was analyzed using thematical analysis.ResultsThe final model incorporates a structured 4-level risk triage and needs assessment process to guide volunteer matching and services provision within a collaborative framework. Moderate overall consensus was achieved after two Delphi rounds, with strong endorsement for the model’s clarity and appropriateness. However, significant divergence emerged among stakeholder subgroups, reflecting tensions between methodological rigor and practical feasibility. The model was perceived as feasible and acceptable for supporting family caregivers. The co-design process underscored the importance of stakeholder engagement in tailoring services to local community needs.ConclusionThis study presents a co-designed, risk-triaged, volunteer-supported in-home respite service model that aligns with ageing in place policies and addresses growing caregiver support needs in ageing societies. By integrating international research perspectives and local practice-based expertise, and by transparently documenting consensus and divergence, this work offers actionable insights for developing, adapting, and scaling respite care services internationally. Future research should evaluate the model’s generalizability, long-term outcomes, and sustainability across diverse populations and settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13937-6.

Background

Globally, the population of persons aged 65 and over is expanding more rapidly than any other demographic, a trend driven by increased life expectancy and population ageing [1]. This demographic shift has not been accompanied by a corresponding growth in the healthcare workforce or trained caregivers [2, 3]. Moreover, the policy shift towards “ageing in place” [4, 5] could lead to reduced investment in formal health services [6], which could further constrain the supply of formal caregivers.

Family caregivers, particularly spouses or adult children, are the primary source of support for most older adults. However, this role places family caregivers at high risk for significant challenges, including deteriorating mental [7] and physical health [8], financial hardship, and social isolation [9].

Pearlin’s stress model [10, 11] provides the overarching conceptual foundation for this study, framing caregiver support as an intervention that targets primary and secondary stressors while bolstering protective mediators. This model offers a useful structure for understanding caregiver challenges, categorizing them into stressors, mediators, and manifestations. Within this model, primary stressors arise directly from the care recipient’s needs, while secondary stressors result from the ripple effects of these primary demands [11]. Research confirms that primary stressors, such as the complexity of care tasks, and secondary stressors, like financial strain, are significantly linked to caregiving burden [12–14]. Mediators, such as coping mechanisms and social support, influence a caregiver’s ability to manage stress, with advanced age, lower education, and poor health identified as key vulnerability factors [15]. The ultimate manifestations of this process are adverse outcomes for both parties, encompassing heightened anxiety, worsened mental health, and physical decline for caregivers and care recipients alike [9, 16, 17].

Adequate support for caregivers strengthens their ability to cope and reduces the negative manifestations of stress. Common forms of such support include caregiving training, financial assistance, respite care, peer support, and psychosocial interventions [18–20]. Among these, respite care- providing temporary care for the recipient to relieve the caregiver – is critical, as it allows caregivers time to rest and attend to personal or social obligations [21, 22]. While respite care can be offered in residential facilities or day care centers [23], in-home services are uniquely advantageous as they are provided in the care recipient’s own residence. This aligns with a demonstrated preference among care recipients to remain in the familiar and secure environment of their own homes [5, 24, 25]. Furthermore, this trusted setting also reassures caregivers and fosters a greater sense of control, thereby encouraging continued service utilization [26].

Although research indicates that respite services, including in-home models, can reduce caregiver burden, role strain, and improve life satisfaction and quality of life [24, 27–29], with benefits sometimes evident in as little as 14 days [30], the overall effects on well-being are often inconsistent or modest [31, 32]. The existing evidence base frequently prioritizes efficacy through randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental designs [24] or analyzes service dosage [33]. A critical limitation of this approach is its reliance on predetermined interventions [24, 32], which fails to address the core challenge of accurately identifying family caregivers and assessing their heterogeneous needs [34, 35], ultimately leading to inefficient resource allocation.

Hong Kong faces a particularly severe population ageing trend, with its elderly population (age 65 and above) projected to increase from 15.3% to 35.9% between 2015 and 2064 [36]. In line with its “ageing in place” policy [37], the government’s approach increasingly relies on family caregivers, underscoring the critical need to enhance community-based support for these individuals, especially those tending to homebound older adults.

Currently, respite care in Hong Kong is predominantly delivered via day care centers [38]. Paradoxically, while these centers previously faced a shortage of vacancies [39], they now report significant underutilization, with a post-COVID-19 utilization rate of around 50% [40]. This trend can be understood within the local cultural context, where filial piety strongly motivates family care as a duty [41–44] and caregiving is often viewed as a private, “internal” family affair, creating a reluctance to seek external help [45, 46]. This suggests that conventional day respite models may be unsuitable for many local caregivers, highlighting a potential niche for the unique advantages of in-home respite services.

Present study

In response to the aforementioned needs, the Hong Kong Jockey Club Charities Fund funded the “Stand-by U” Caregivers Community Support Project to develop a screening-guided in-home respite service model. This project aims to triage caregivers of homebound older adults by level of need, deliver tailored respite services, and evaluate effectiveness using mixed methods. Launched in April 2024 as the first large-scale program of its kind in Hong Kong, “Stand-by U” is a collaborative initiative with five partner non-governmental organizations (NGOs). The service is delivered by trained volunteers (Vs) and substitute caregivers (SCs), who are recruited and managed by the partner NGOs and receive training from the Hong Kong Association of Gerontology (HKAG).

The initial service model (Fig. 1) was developed by the research team based on a prior pilot project and literature review. In this model, caregivers (CGs) are screened by NGO social workers using a multidimensional screening tool and assigned to one of three risk tiers: low, moderate, or high. Cases identified as high-risk — characterized by aggressive verbal behavior, domestic violence, or caregiver ideation of self-harm or harm to others — are excluded from the program and immediately referred to specialized professional services. Eligible CGs receive tiered support: those at low risk are allocated 12h of respite over six months, while those at moderate risk receive 36h. A mandatory advance booking system is implemented for all in-home respite sessions to aid logistical planning and improve service effectiveness and user satisfaction [47, 48]. Further operational specifics are available in the project protocol [49].

The decision to utilize trained volunteers is a strategic response to the shortage of professional caregiver in Hong Kong, supported by evidence from other local, volunteer-based elderly care interventions [50]. While a small body of research has explored the use of volunteers in respite care [51–53], it has primarily examined effects on the volunteers themselves, with limited focus on the outcomes for caregivers and care recipients [52, 53]. This study aims to address this gap.

In a densely populated and socioeconomically diverse city like Hong Kong, stratifying service users is essential for appropriate service provision and efficient resource allocation. Our service flow incorporates an initial screening process to triage caregivers by need level, which in turn determines the types and intensity of services they receive. However, the effective implementation of such a model requires consensus among all stakeholder groups involved in its design and delivery. A well-documented “gap” exists between academic and service-practice stakeholders, with management literature highlighting fundamental differences in goals, communication styles [54], and the challenge of reconciling research rigor with practical relevance [55].

Co-design plays a critical role in bridging this gap. Moving beyond mere consultation, it actively involves stakeholders throughout the design process to ensure the resulting service is not only evidence-based but also contextually relevant, practically feasible, and aligned with the real-world needs and preferences of its end-users [56, 57].

Furthermore, Pearlin’s stress process model directly informed our service design and evaluation approach. It underscored the necessity of a risk-stratified screening process to systematically identify varying levels of caregiver stressors and needs. The model’s focus on mediators highlighted the critical role of tailored support. This logic shaped the core objective of this study: to co-design a service model whose scientific merit (completeness in addressing the stress process) and practical value (applicability in delivering tailored support) could be rigorously appraised by key stakeholders.

Consequently, this study has two primary aims: first, to foster dialogue and build consensus between different stakeholder groups, and second, to co-design a finalized service flow that best meets their collective objectives. To achieve these aims, we employed a modified online Delphi approach to gather and synthesize expert opinions.

Methods

The Delphi method is an iterative, structured group facilitation technique that utilizes multiple rounds of questionnaires to gather expert opinions and build consensus on a specific issue [58, 59]. It is valued for generating in-depth insights from diverse stakeholders and has been widely applied to develop best-practice and guidance across wide fields [60, 61].

This study employed an online Delphi approach to overcome logistical barriers, such as the need for a physical meeting location [61], and to facilitate collaborations with international experts [62]. In each round, the expert panel provided opinions on the service model. Controlled feedback was then supplied to participants to inform their judgment in subsequent rounds [63, 64]. This feedback, presented in an anonymized format to minimize bias, included a written report with aggregated statistics, such as means, measures of central tendency, and dispersion of rating scores, along with a thematic analysis of participant comments.

Evidence suggests that most significant consensus improvements typically occur within the first two rounds, with diminishing returns in subsequent rounds [65, 67]. Furthermore, additional rounds can increase participant fatigue and reduce response rates [66]. Therefore, a two-round online Delphi design was adopted, with provisions for additional rounds only if consensus was not achieved.

Panel selection

In Delphi studies, participants are typically recruited via purposive or convenience sampling [67]. While there is no definitive consensus on the optimal panel size, with expert panels varying widely across studies [68, 69], a trend in health research favors smaller, highly specialized panels [70]. Such panels, often composed of experts with specific expertise [70] or similar training levels [71] while still representing diverse professional groups, are considered capable of yielding a more valid consensus [72, 73]. A minimum of 12 participants is generally deemed sufficient for a Delphi study [74].

In accordance with this approach, we recruited a panel of 17 research- and practice-based experts, categorized into three distinct groups. The first group included international and local scholars in gerontology with specific expertise in caregiving research. The second and third groups comprised team leaders and frontline social workers from the partner NGOs responsible for implementing the “Stand-by U” Project’s in-home respite services. Eligibility criteria for all participants included being aged 18 or above, proficiency in both English and Chinese, and holding at least a Bachelor’s degree. Scholars were additionally required to have a minimum of eight years of relevant experience in caregiving or gerontology. Participants were recruited through professional networks and convenience sampling (Table 1). Potential participants were invited via email which detailed the study’s aims and procedures. Those who agreed to participate provided informed consent electronically via a hyperlink.

Procedures and measures

This study received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of The University of Hong Kong (Reference number: EA240374). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study.

Prior to the first survey round, an online discussion via Zoom was conducted by the project’s principal investigator (VWQL), who served as moderator. The session presented the in-home respite care project and the initial service model to the expert panel. Following the presentation, panel members were invited to raise and discuss potential issues or concerns regarding the model.

For Round 1, participants were invited via email to complete a study-specific online questionnaire on the Qualtrics XM platform. Using a 9-point Likert scale, they evaluated the service model across four key dimensions: appropriateness, completeness, clarity, and applicability. For each dimension, participants were also invited to provide qualitative comments to elaborate on their ratings.

These four dimensions were deductively developed by the research team to form a balanced appraisal framework that explicitly captures the core and often divergent priorities of research-based and practice-based stakeholders. This approach ensured the service model was evaluated from both scientific and implementation perspectives. Specifically, completeness pertained to scientific rigor (a primary concern for academics), applicability addressed practical implementation (a key focus for practitioners), appropriateness assessed the perceived suitability of the procedures for achieving service aims, and clarity evaluated the comprehensibility of the procedures. This appraisal framework facilitated a systematic multi-angled evaluation critical for assessing real-world implementation potential.

Following the analysis of Round 1 data, the initial service flow (Fig. 1) was revised. A key modification was the expansion of the risk-triage system from three to four tiers (Fig. 2), with services subsequently targeted only to caregivers identified as having Moderate or Mild needs. The revised model, accompanied by controlled feedback summarizing the Round 1 results, was distributed to the panel for review. Participants were then invited to complete a second-round online survey re-evaluate the updated model.

Upon closing the second survey, a final online discussion was convened. The same moderator presented a summary of the Round 2 findings and addressed the collective feedback. Participants were again encouraged to provide final comments on the refined service model.

Data analysis

Quantitative data from the online surveys were exported from the Qualtrics platform, and descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS. To identify statistically significant (p < .05) changes in ratings between the two rounds, mean comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

In line with a priori criteria, ratings of 7 to 9 on the 9-point scale were classified as “critical” scores, indicating strong agreement with the service flow [75–77]. Consensus for an item was defined as the percentage of experts assigning a critical score. While standardized thresholds for consensus vary in the literature, ranging from 51% [78] to 80% [79], a median threshold of 75% was adopted for this study, as supported by two major reviews of Delphi methodology [80, 81]. This threshold has also been shown to predict the stability of consensus in subsequent rounds [82], making it a practical indicator for determining the need for further iterations.

For the qualitative data, a thematic analysis was performed on the transcripts from the discussion sessions and the open-ended comments from the questionnaires. The analysis involved generating initial codes from the data, which were subsequently grouped into themes that were defined and named [83, 84]. Representative participant extracts were selected to illustrate and substantiate each theme in the final analytical report.

Results

Of the 19 experts initially approached, 17 agreed to participate the online Delphi study (response rate 89.5%). Participant retention was high across both rounds: all 17 panelists completed the first-round questionnaire and 16 completed both rounds, representing a 94% retention rate.

Consensus ratings

As shown in Table 2, a decrease in both critical agreement percentages and mean ratings was observed between Delphi rounds. “Clarity” consistently received the highest mean ratings (R1: 7.19; R2: 7.13), while “Applicability” received the lowest (R1: 6.38; R2: 6.25). The largest decline in critical agreement was for Clarity (-12.5%), which fell from 87.5% to 75.0%. Final ratings across the participant groups ranged from 6.25 to 7.13 out of 9 (Critical ratings: 62.5% to 75.0%), indicating a moderate consensus was achieved. Concurrently, increased standardized deviations (SDs) across all dimensions in Round 2 (e.g., Appropriateness SD increased from 0.71 to 1.59) indicate greater rating dispersion, reflecting more divergent views among stakeholders following the model refinement.

Table 4 presents the results of stakeholder-specific subgroup analysis. While the sample size precludes formal statistical testing of effect sizes, divergent consensus patterns among the stakeholder groups are readily apparent. Scholar (Academic) stakeholders provided consistently high ratings (ranging from 6.75 to 8.5 out of 9) across all dimensions. They demonstrated strong consensus, with critical ratings at or above 75% for all four dimensions in both rounds, reaching full endorsement (100%) for Appropriateness, Clarity, and Applicability in R2. NGO team leaders also showed stable, high endorsement, with critical ratings of 75–100% in R1 and full endorsement across all dimensions in R2. In contrast, social workers, the frontline implementers of the project, provided lower initial ratings in R1, which then declined further across all dimensions in R2. The most pronounced decrease was observed for Applicability, where the critical rating fell from 83.3% in R1 to 33.3% in R2.

Qualitative results

Given the small sample size and the limited number of responses in this optional component of the survey, a single coder (JY) performed the qualitative coding. All other co-authors reviewed and endorsed the coding. Thematic analysis of the qualitative data yielded two key themes: (1) Assessment Burden and (2) Expansion of Service Provision.

Assessment burden

Although the comprehensiveness of the assessment tools was acknowledged, NGO team leaders and social workers frequently characterized them as overly complex, detailed, and administratively frequent. They contended that the assessments imposed a significant time and response burden on both staff and service users. This concern is encapsulated in the following comment from a social worker, whose quantitative ratings were notably low:Assessment tools had too many unnecessary questions with low linkage to service provision. The time used to conduct the assessments was too long, and the participant could provide careless answers due to lack of interest. This used up both the participant’s and the social worker’s time, and it could take several hours in total to complete all the assessments

The expert panel identified two main primary factors contributing to the assessment length: the sheer volume of questions and the complexity of certain items, which required social workers to provide additional explanations to service users. To mitigate this burden, the panel proposed several solutions. First, they recommended streamlining the tools by retaining only the most critical and relevant questions, as some items were perceived as uninformative for service planning. Second, they suggested revising questions to be more engaging or easier to comprehend. Third, they advised reducing the frequency of follow-up assessments, particularly the post-service re-assessments. As one social worker noted:It is hard to contact and follow-up with the participants 3, 6, or 12 months after the service. From a research perspective, follow-up assessments are essential for evaluating longitudinal outcomes for caregivers and recipients post-intervention. However, participants questioned the utility of reassessments at 6 and 12 months, noting the difficulty in attributing any observed changes solely to the intervention. This perspective highlighted the practical and methodological limitations of long-term follow-ups, a concern echoed by both academic and practitioner panelists. Ultimately, the expert panel argued that the burden imposed by these extensive assessment risks compromising data quality, discouraging ongoing participation, and creating significant logistical challenges for frontline social workers.

Expansion of service provision

Feedback from NGO team leaders and frontline social workers primarily focused on enhancing the flexibility and scope of service delivery. Two team leaders contended that the risk screening tool might, in some instances, fail to capture the full complexity of a caregiving situation, potentially leading to misalignment between assessed risk and service assignment. Consequently, NGO partners advocated for greater discretion to override screening results, allowing them to assign services based on professional judgment. They argued this flexibility would enable service provision to some low-risk and high-risk cases that would otherwise be referred out, thereby increasing the program’s coverage.

In contrast, academic experts proposed diversifying the types of support offered. One scholar expert, who was also a family caregiver, recommended establishing an “immediate respite” or crisis intervention” component for emergencies. Other suggestions included expanding the duties of volunteers and substitute caregivers to include leisure and entertainment activities, or even educational support such as technology assistance or guidance on legal and financial matters for care recipients and caregivers.

During the second-round discussion, the moderator addressed these suggestions and the associated rating changes. It was clarified that the “Stand-by U” project serves a dual purpose as both a service initiative and an evidence-based research study. The objective is to rigorously evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of peer-supported in-home respite care to inform its potential scaling in Hong Kong. Many of the standardized, validated assessment scales are essential for measuring impact and formulating personalized care plans; therefore, removing items or altering wording was deemed methodologically unsound. Through this detailed dialogue, the research team successfully addressed panel concerns, particularly those of social workers, leading to a final consensus on the service flow. To support implementation, dedicated training workshops will be provided to social workers to build proficiency with the assessment tools and enhance interviewing and data collection techniques.

Several proposed expansions to service provision were deemed unfeasible or beyond the project’s scope. First, the standardized screening tool serves to ensure objective and consistent assessment of caregiver needs. Permitting NGOs to override results based on subjective judgments would compromise a key element of study design; this rationale was clearly communicated to NGO partners during discussions. However, a limited flexibility mechanism was established: where unique family circumstances observed during home visits make respite services critically necessary for caregivers outside the eligible risk tiers, or where service hour extensions are warranted, social workers may request an exception by providing detailed justification on the assessment form for team leader approval.

Second, the scope of activities during respite sessions is necessarily limited by the training curriculum for volunteers and substitute caregivers, which focuses on foundational caregiving skills. Third, the provision of “emergency” respite is logistically constrained by the volunteer-based model, which cannot guarantee immediate, on-call availability. As a solution, the project will strengthen its referral pathways to specialized professional services to ensure emergency needs can be appropriately addressed.

Following these clarifications, the expert panel endorsed the final service flow and agreed to adhere to the established procedures for assessment and service delivery.

Discussion

The present study details the co-design process for a volunteer-supported, risk-stratified in-home respite service model targeting family caregivers of homebound older adults within a Chinese context. This model innovatively integrates a screening-guided triage system to deliver peer support via trained volunteers within the home setting. Using a two-round Delphi approach, moderate overall consensus was achieved on the service flow. While quantitative ratings reflected broad endorsement of the model’s clarity and appropriateness, qualitative feedback revealed significant divergence among stakeholder subgroups, most notably among frontline social workers.

Our findings indicate that the clarity of the service flow was consistently rated highest across stakeholder groups, suggesting the model is conceptually sound and holds potential for future scaling. Both academic scholars and NGO team leaders remained moderately to strongly supportive throughout the process. In contrast, consensus among frontline social workers, the primary implementers, significantly declined in the second round. This was most pronounced for ratings of appropriateness and applicability, dimensions directly tied to practical implementation. This divergence underscores a fundamental tension between the research rigor and methodological controls valued by academics, and the flexibility, feasibility, and practical relevance prioritized by frontline practitioners.

The qualitative analysis identified two principal implementation challenges: assessment burden and service provision flexibility. Social workers and NGO team leaders indicated that the comprehensive assessment tools created excessive time and response demands for both staff and service users. This raised concerns about participant fatigue, potential compromises to data quality, and logistical difficulties in longitudinal follow-up. These findings align with established literature on response burden in health service research, which documents its association with lower participation rates [85, 86] and less reliable data [87].

The divergent ratings observed during the Delphi process reflect the inherent tensions in service design between academic and practitioner priorities. Academic stakeholders emphasized evidence rigor and supported retaining detailed assessments to robustly evaluate service effectiveness. Their perspective was largely theoretical and macro-level, focusing on the model’s logical coherence, comprehensiveness, and scientific validity. In contrast, frontline social workers, as direct implementers, evaluated the model through the lens of practical operability. Their lower ratings, particularly regarding applicability, stemmed from direct experience with operational challenges such as assessment burden. NGO team leaders, while also practitioners, occupied a management role that balanced frontline realities with broader organizational objectives like resource allocation and program goals; for them, excessive service flexibility posed potential management and budgetary challenges.

To alleviate assessment burden, the expert panel proposed streamlining core questions, simplifying item wording, and reducing the frequency of follow-up assessments. Some participants specifically questioned the value of reassessments at 6- and 12-months post-intervention, noting the difficulty in attributing outcomes solely to the intervention. While acknowledging this methodological limitation, the research team emphasized the importance of documenting the longitudinal caregiving trajectory, such as decisions to continue home care or transition to alternative arrangements, as a valuable outcome in itself. The explicit concerns raised by social workers regarding feasibility and burden have direct implications for future implementation. To translate the co-designed model into scalable practice, specific mitigation strategies are required. These include: (1) implementing co-training workshops for researchers and social workers to foster shared understanding of assessment purposes; (2) adapting workflows by integrating key assessment items into initial client engagement conversations to reduce redundancy and perceived burden; and (3) establishing a structured feedback channel for frontline staff to report practical obstacles in real-time, enabling iterative model refinement. Such proactive strategies are crucial for addressing the applicability gap and building frontline buy-in, which is essential for successful scale-up.

Regarding service provision, practice-based stakeholders expressed concern that the risk screening tool might not always align services effectively with complex client needs, advocating for greater flexibility in case allocation to extend coverage across risk levels. Academic experts, conversely, proposed expanding service scope to include emergency respite, crisis intervention, and a wider range of in-home activities.

During the second-round deliberations, several of these suggestions were determined to be currently unfeasible. Granting NGOs full discretion to override screening outcomes would compromise research integrity by removing a key experimental control. Similarly, expanding volunteer activities was constrained by existing training protocols, which focus on core caregiving competencies. The implementation of on-demand emergency respite was also deemed impractical within a volunteer-supported model lacking formal shift structures. Despite these limitations, the expert panel ultimately endorsed the service flow and committed to following the established assessment and service delivery procedures.

This tension between aspirations for service expansion and pragmatic operational constraints is well-documented in international implementation research [88, 89], underscoring the critical need to align service design with available resources and practical realities. Quantitative analysis confirmed that despite stakeholder concerns and model refinements following Round 1, no statistically significant shifts in overall ratings occurred between rounds. This rating stability suggests that while operational challenges were acknowledged, the core service model maintained its legitimacy and foundational validity among stakeholders.

International contributions and implications

This study offers several contributions with relevance for international audiences engaged in health service design and evaluation:

Model Co-Design through structured stakeholder engagement

The implementation of a tripartite Delphi panel—incorporating academic researchers, NGO management, and frontline practitioners—demonstrates a replicable approach for integrating diverse expertise into service development. The detailed documentation of both consensus achievement and subgroup divergences provides transferable insights for developing culturally adapted interventions across different health systems.

Navigating Assessment Burden and Research Rigor: The identified tension between comprehensive assessment protocols and practical service delivery reflects a universal challenge in evidence-based practice. Our findings substantiate the need to position rigorous assessment as an essential investment in service quality and personalization, while simultaneously highlighting the global risk of participant disengagement and implementation resistance when burden becomes excessive. The data collected serve dual purposes: generating research evidence while directly informing individual care planning and community service referrals.

Balancing Standardization with Adaptive Flexibility: The established process for managing exceptional cases, requiring documented justification and managerial approval, represents an innovative approach to reconciling methodological rigor with practical responsiveness. This negotiated flexibility addresses a fundamental challenge in implementation science: maintaining intervention fidelity while accommodating complex real-world circumstances. The inclusion of NGOs leaders provides crucial managerial perspective, highlighting how standardization supports effective resource management while controlled adaptability maintains service relevance.

Delphi methodology as a Bridge across Research-Practice divides

The modified online Delphi process served not only as a consensus-building mechanism but also as an effective platform for reconciling academic and practitioner priorities. This methodological approach offers valuable guidance for international initiatives seeking to develop collaborative, contextually appropriate health service innovation through meaningful stakeholder engagement.

Implications for policy

The co-designed, risk-triaged, volunteer-supported in-home respite service model directly advances Ageing in Place policy objectives by delivering tailored, community-based support that helps older adults remain in their homes while addressing the practical challenges faced by family caregivers. Widespread adoption of such evidence-informed, collaboratively developed models can assist policymakers in building more sustainable and responsive long-term care systems capable of meeting the demands of ageing populations.

Limitations and future directions

While this Delphi study maintained high participant retention and achieved robust qualitative engagement, several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, although the expert panel included international scholars, all practice-based participants were drawn from local NGOs involved in this specific project. This may limit the transferability of findings to other contexts and introduces potential biases, including the influence of prior working relationships on group dynamics. The panel’s demographic composition, featuring a majority of participants under 50 years of age, may also have shaped perspectives on feasibility and applicability; however, this accurately reflects the age distribution typical of frontline service delivery roles in the sector.

Second, the context-specific nature of the panel may reflect Hong Kong’s unique socio-environmental context. For instance, the city’s dense urban geography and comprehensive public transportation system facilitate volunteer mobility and make home visits logistically feasible and cost-effective, an infrastructural advantage not universally available. Furthermore, the model relies on a strong, culturally embedded tradition of volunteering, which may not be prevalent or socially supported in other societies.

Third, it should be acknowledged that the principal investigator served as the moderator for the discussion sessions. While this arrangement may raise concerns about potential influence on participant responses, the structured nature of the Delphi method, featuring anonymous rating rounds and controlled feedback, substantially mitigates such risks. The moderator primarily acted as a facilitator to present aggregated results and stimulate balanced discussion. Therefore, while this limitation is noted, it is unlikely to have materially compromised the validity of the consensus outcomes.

Future research should examine the model’s generalizability and adaptability across different geographic and cultural contexts, particularly in regions with logistical constraints or less developed volunteer ecosystems. Additionally, longitudinal evaluation of service outcomes and implementation sustainability, particularly concerning assessment burden, staff engagement, and the long-term retention of volunteers, is essential to determine the model’s effectiveness and feasibility in diverse settings. Future implementation phases should prioritize and evaluate the proposed mitigation strategies (e.g., co-training, workflow adaptation) to directly address the feasibility concerns raised in this study and enhance the model’s scalability.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the field by presenting a co-designed, screening-guided volunteer-supported in-home respite service model that has undergone critical appraisal by multiple stakeholder groups. By documenting both areas of consensus and divergence, and by navigating the balance between methodological rigor and practical implementation, our findings offer actionable guidance for the global health services research community. These insights are particularly relevant given rapidly ageing population and the escalating demand for sustainable, person-centered caregiver support. The methodologies and lessons derived from this research have broad applicability for developing, evaluating, and scaling innovative respite services that address the complex needs of family caregivers in ageing societies worldwide.
