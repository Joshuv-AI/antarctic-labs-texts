Quantum thermodynamics of continuous feedback control
Kacper Prech, Joël Aschwanden, Partick P. Potts

Abstract
The laws of thermodynamics are a cornerstone for describing nanoscale and open quantum systems. However, formulating these laws for systems under continuous feedback control and under experimentally relevant conditions is challenging. In this work, we lay out a formalism for the laws of thermodynamics in an open quantum system under continuous measurement and feedback described by a Quantum Fokker Planck Master Equation. We derive expressions for work, heat, and measurement-induced energy changes, and we investigate entropy production and fluctuation theorems. We illustrate our results with a continuous version of a measurement-driven Szilard engine, as well as a work extraction scheme in a two-level system under bang-bang control. Our results provide insights into the energetics as well as the irreversibility of classical and quantum systems under continuous feedback control.

Introduction

Macroscale devices such as as engines and refrigerators are governed by the laws of thermodynamics
de Groot and Mazur 2007
. The first law of thermodynamics states that the total energy change can be separated into work and heat. Work is an energy change mediated by degrees of freedom that we have control over, meaning that it can be harvested from or supplied to the device externally, whereas heat originates from interactions with an environment, which we cannot control. The second law states that the entropy production, which depends on the heat, cannot be negative, restricting which processes the system can undergo.

Recently, a powerful approach to describing continuous feedback in experimentally realistic conditions has been derived: the Quantum Fokker-Planck Master Equation (QFPME)

II
Quantum Fokker Planck Master Equation

\mathcal{L}(D)

The general setup of the QFPME is illustrated in Fig.
1
. We consider a continuous measurement of the Hermitian observable
\hat{A}

Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Bednorz et al. 2012
in an open quantum system. Feedback is controlled by an instantaneous measurement outcome
D
of a detector with a bandwidth
\gamma
, which filters out high frequency noise from the outcomes of the continuous measurement
Wheatley et al. 2010
;
Sarovar et al. 2004
;
Sarovar et al. 2005
;
Feng et al. 2011
;
Liu et al. 2010
;
Warszawski and Wiseman 2002a
;
Warszawski and Wiseman 2002b
, thus introducing a delay.

The time evolution of the joint state
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)
of the system and the measurement outcome follows the QFPME
Annby-Andersson et al. 2022
:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)&=\mathcal{L}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)+\lambda\mathcal{D}[\hat{A}]\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\\
&-\gamma\partial_{D}\mathcal{A}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial^{2}_{D}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D),\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathcal{A}(D)\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{A}-D,\hat{\rho}\}
and
\mathcal{D}[\hat{O}]\hat{\rho}=\hat{O}\hat{\rho}\hat{O}^{\dagger}-\{\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{O},\hat{\rho}\}/2
.
The first term on the right hand side of Eq. (
1
) describes feedback with a
D
-dependent Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}(D)
. The second term is a dephasing in the eigenbasis of the observable
\hat{A}
with a rate
\lambda
, which is the effect of the measurement backaction. The measurement rate
\lambda
quantifies its strength and invasiveness, with
\lambda\to\infty
corresponding to a continuous projective measurement. The third and fourth terms, called drift and diffusion, describe the time-evolution of the detector’s measurement outcome.

In many situations of practical interest, where the system is weakly coupled to the environment, the superoperator
\mathcal{L}(D)
corresponds to a Lindblad master equation
Pusz and Woronowicz 1978
;
Alicki 2001
;
Potts et al. 2021
;
Albash et al. 2012

\mathcal{L}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}=-i[\hat{H}_{t}(D),\hat{\rho}_{t}]+\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{\rho}_{t},

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}
describes interaction with the environment and
\hat{H}_{t}(D)
is a
D
-dependent feedback Hamiltonian, which may also optionally include an explicit time dependence not resulting from feedback. Crucially, the Hamiltonian may be an arbitrary function of the measurement outcome. In this work, we will consider linear feedback, with
\hat{H}(D)\propto D
, as well as threshold-like feedback, where, e.g.,
\hat{H}_{+}
and
\hat{H}_{-}
are applied when
D\geq D_{\rm th}
and
D<D_{\rm th}
respectively. Threshold-like feedback is also referred to as bang-bang control and is useful, for instance, for optimizing protocols for work extraction in driven open quantum systems
Cavina et al. 2018
and quantum error correcting
Sarovar et al. 2004
.
In the QFPME-formalism (
1
), the distribution of the measurement outcome at time
t
is given by
P_{t}(D)=\text{Tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\}
. The quantum state of the system without any knowledge of the measurement outcome is given by
\hat{\rho}_{t}=\int dD\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)
, whereas the quantum state conditioned on the outcome
D
is obtained by
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)/P_{t}(D)
. Expectation values are computed by averaging over the measurement outcomes, as well as the quantum mechanical ensemble:

\left\langle\hat{O}(D)\right\rangle:=\int dD\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{O}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\}.

(3)

This definition will be essential to evaluate energy terms in the QFPME.

We emphasize that the QFPME can also be applied to classical (incoherent) systems that are measured with a finite bandwidth. In this case
[\hat{A},\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)]=0
, i.e., there is no measurement backaction.

III
The first law of thermodynamics

In this section we present our investigation of the first law of thermodynamics for systems described by the QFPME. We begin with a brief summary of how heat and work are defined in Lindblad master equations and then proceed with our results for feedback controlled systems and their derivation. Our findings are then illustrated with examples.

III.1
Power and heat current in the Lindblad master equation

Here we consider open quantum systems without any feedback control that evolve in time under the Lindblad master equation
\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{t}=\mathcal{L}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}
, i.e., according to Eq. (
2
) but treating
D
as a fixed parameter instead of a dynamical variable. The definitions of heat and work in Lindblad master equations have been debated in the literature
Novotný 2002
;
Levy and Kosloff 2014
;
Trushechkin and Volovich 2016
;
Hofer et al. 2017
;
González et al. 2017
;
De Chiara et al. 2018
;
Hewgill et al. 2021
. Due to the approximations made in deriving master equations, the laws of thermodynamics may generally not be guaranteed. It is however always possible to obtain a thermodynamically consistent framework
Potts et al. 2021
. In this work, we focus on two particularly simple thermodynamic frameworks that cover many applications. The first framework
Pusz and Woronowicz 1978
;
Alicki 2001
is obtained by defining the internal energy as

U\equiv\left\langle\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\},

(4)

the power as the change in energy due to the change in the Hamiltonian,

P=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{t}\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\}=\left\langle\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle,

(5)

and the heat current as the change in energy due to the change in the density matrix,

J=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{H}_{t}(D)\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{t}\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{H}_{t}(D)\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{\rho}_{t}\}=\left\langle\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle,

(6)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}^{\dagger}
is the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}
.
We note that the first law of thermodynamics,
\partial_{t}U=P+J
, is valid by construction.

The second framework relies on the thermodynamic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm TD}
introduced in Ref.
Potts et al. 2021
. In particular, there are scenarios where a thermodynamically consistent framework can be obtained with a time-independent thermodynamic Hamiltonian obeying

\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)=i[\hat{H}_{t}(D),\hat{H}_{\rm TD}(D)],

(7)

an example is presented in Sec.
III.6
below.
In this framework, the internal energy and heat current are defined with the help of
\hat{H}_{\rm TD}(D)
:

U\equiv\left\langle\hat{H}_{\rm TD}(D)\right\rangle,\hskip 14.22636ptJ=\left\langle\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}^{\dagger}\hat{H}_{\rm TD}(D)\right\rangle,

(8)

while the power is still given by Eq. (
5
).
Again, the first law of thermodynamics is obeyed, due to Eq. (
7
).

III.2
The first law for the QFPME

For quantum systems under continuous measurement and feedback described by the QFPME (
1
), the rate of change of the average energy can be separated into power
P
, heat current
J
, and the energy flow due to the measurement
\dot{E}_{\rm M}
:

\partial_{t}U=\partial_{t}\int{dD}\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{U}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\}=P+J+\dot{E}_{\rm M},

(9)

where the operator
\hat{U}(D)
depends on the thermodynamic framework (in this work, it is equal to
\hat{H}_{t}(D)
or
\hat{H}_{\rm TD}(D)
). The power is given by

P=\gamma\left\langle\mathcal{A}(D)\partial_{D}\hat{U}(D)\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\left\langle\partial^{2}_{D}\hat{U}(D)\right\rangle+\left\langle\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle,

(10)

and the heat current reads

J=\left\langle\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}^{\dagger}\hat{U}(D)\right\rangle.

(11)

Finally, the energy flow due to the measurement reads

\dot{E}_{\rm M}=\lambda\left\langle\mathcal{D}[\hat{A}]\hat{U}(D)\right\rangle.

(12)

The average values above are defined according to Eq. (
3
). Equation (
9
) constitutes the first law of thermodynamics for open quantum systems under continuous feedback control in analogy to Eq. (
4
). A derivation and justification is presented in the subsection below.

The power
P
consists of the three terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
10
). The first and the second one are a direct result of the drift and diffusion terms describing how the (thermodynamic) Hamiltonian changes in time due to the feedback. Importantly, the second term involving the second derivative with respect to
D
plays a role for nonlinear feedback. The third term in power as well as the heat current are equivalent to the expressions in the absence of feedback. The measurement energy rate
\dot{E}_{\rm M}
(
12
) quantifies the change in energy due to the continuous measurement of
\hat{A}
as a result of the measured operator not commuting with the Hamiltonian. This term is also known as
quantum heat

(
Elouard et al. 2017b
)
.

III.3
Stochastic energetics in the QFPME

While the first law in Eq. (
9
) can be derived from the QFPME directly, the interpretation of heat and work is particularly illuminating in the stochastic formulation of the QFPME
Annby-Andersson et al. 2022
. In this formulation, the central quantity is the density matrix conditioned on all previous measurement outcomes
\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
. Its time-evolution is determined by the Belavkin equation, a stochastic differential equation

d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}=\mathcal{L}(D)\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}dt+\lambda\mathcal{D}[A]\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}dt+\sqrt{\lambda}\{\hat{A}-\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\rm c},\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}\}dW,

(13)

where
\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\rm c}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{A}\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}\}
and
dW
is a Wiener increment with the expectation value
\mathbb{E}[dW]=0
and
dW^{2}=dt

Jacobs 2010
.

The measurement outcome is determined by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, described by the stochastic differential equation
Annby-Andersson et al. 2022

dD_{\rm c}=\gamma\left(\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\rm c}-D_{\rm c}\right)dt+\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}dW,

(14)

where we use the subscript ’c’ to denote that this is the measurement outcome conditioned on all previous measurement outcomes and not the argument in a distribution. In the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (
14
), the terms on the right-hand site are called drift and diffusion, respectively. The drift describes how
D_{\rm c}
approaches the conditional expectation value of
\hat{A}
, wheres the diffusive part corresponds to the noise in the measurement outcome.

The left-hand side of Eq. (
9
), can then be equivalently expressed as

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\left\langle\hat{U}\right\rangle&=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{Tr}\left\{\frac{d(\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_{\rm c})}{dt}\right\}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{Tr}\left\{\frac{d\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}+\hat{U}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}+d\hat{U}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}}{dt}\right\}\right],\end{split}

(15)

where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the average over all trajectories the measurement outcome can take. For brevity of notation we have dropped the
D
-dependence in
\hat{U}\equiv\hat{U}(D_{\rm c})
, and we write
d\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}=(d\hat{U})\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
. We note that since
\hat{U}
depends on
D_{\rm c}
, it is also a stochastic quantity. However, we refrain from giving it a subscript since it only depends on the current measurement outcome, not its full history in contrast to
\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
and
D_{\rm c}
.

To calculate the terms appearing in Eq. (
15
), we find the differential of the internal energy operator
\hat{U}
, which describes another Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

d\hat{U}=\left[\partial_{t}\hat{U}+\gamma\left(\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\rm c}-D_{\rm c}\right)\partial_{D}\hat{U}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial^{2}_{D}\hat{U}\right]dt+\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}\partial_{D}\hat{U}dW,

(16)

where again
\hat{U}
and its derivatives are evaluated at
D_{\rm c}
. A derivation of this equation can be found in App.
A.1
.
Since both
d\hat{U}
and
d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
include the Wiener increment
dW
, and since
dW^{2}=dt
, the term
d\hat{U}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
is linear in
dt
and thus contributes to Eq. (
15
).
Combining the Belavkin equation (
13
) with the differential in Eq. (
16
) allows us to obtain the expectation values of all three differential terms in Eq. (
15
), which are given by Eqs. (
57
), (
59
), and (
61
), respectively; see App.
A.2
for details.

When
\hat{U}(D)=\hat{H}_{t}(D)
, the differential terms in Eq. (
15
) have a particularly transparent interpretation. Just like in the absence of feedback, we may use Eq. (
16
) to identify energy changes due to the change in Hamiltonian as work and energy changes due to changes in the density matrix as heat. In this approach, the term including
d\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
contributes to work, while the term
\hat{U}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
contributes to heat. As we discuss in more detail below, the remaining term including
d\hat{U}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}
also contributes to work.

We first consider the heat-like contributions to the energy change

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{H}_{t}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}\}]}{dt}=J+\dot{E}_{\rm M},

(17)

which includes the heat current
J
(
11
) and the measurement energy rate
\dot{E}_{\rm M}
(
12
). This identification motivates the term
quantum heat

Elouard et al. 2017b
. However, it has been shown that the energy contribution from the measurement cannot always be interpreted as heat but sometimes rather acts as a source of work
Jacobs 2009
;
Elouard et al. 2017b
;
Elouard et al. 2024
. We further note that in the thermodynamic framework where
\hat{U}(D)=\hat{H}_{\rm TD}(D)
, the power due to the explicit time-dependence of the Hamiltonian,
\left\langle\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle
, is included in
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{H}_{\rm TD}d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}\}]/dt
.
This further illustrates that care has to be taken in identifying energy flows as heat or work.

The power reads

P=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}\{d\hat{H}_{t}(\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}+d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}\}]}{dt}.

(18)

This can be motivated by considering the feedback control in infinitesimal time increments. In each increment, a measurement is performed which updates the density matrix and the measurement outcome. Then, the Hamiltonian is updated by feeding back the new measurement outcome. These two steps can be illustrated as:

\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{H}_{t}\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}\}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{H}_{t}(\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}+d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c})\}\rightarrow\mathrm{Tr}\{(\hat{H}_{t}+d\hat{H}_{t})(\hat{\rho}_{\rm c}+d\hat{\rho}_{\rm c})\}.

(19)

Work is performed in the second step, where the Hamiltonian is changed, resulting in the power given in Eq. (
18
), see also App.
A.2
for details.

III.4
Applicability to classical systems

Our results are equally applicable to classical systems under continuous feedback control. Suppose a classical system can occupy a set of microstates (configurations)
\{a\}
with corresponding
D
-dependent energies
\omega_{a}(D)
. We can define the joint probability of the system
a
and the measurement outcome
D
at a given time as
p_{a}(D)
.

Formally,
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)=\sum_{a}p_{a}(D)|a\rangle\langle a|
and
\hat{H}_{t}(D)=\sum_{a}\omega_{a}(D)|a\rangle\langle a|
, where
\langle a|a^{{}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{a,a^{{}^{\prime}}}
, and we can write the eigendecomposition of the observable as
\hat{A}=\sum_{a}\xi_{a}|a\rangle\langle a|
.

Then, the measurement energy
\dot{E}_{\rm M}
vanishes. This is expected because there is no measurement backaction. The three terms that appear in the power
P
in Eq. (
10
) [with
\hat{U}(D)=\hat{H}_{t}(D)
] can simply be found using the following expressions:

\begin{split}&\left\langle\partial^{2}_{D}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle=\sum_{a}\int dDp_{a}(D)\partial_{D}^{2}\omega_{a}(D),\\
&\left\langle\mathcal{A}(D)\partial_{D}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle=\sum_{a}\int dDp_{a}(D)(a-D)\partial_{D}\omega_{a}(D),\\
&\text{and}\quad\left\langle\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{t}(D)\right\rangle=\sum_{a}\int dDp_{a}(D)\partial_{t}\omega_{a}(D).\\
\end{split}

(20)

Notably, the nonlinearity of feedback also has consequences for the energetics of classical systems.

Annby-Andersson et al. 2022

|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle

\omega
(hereafter we set
\hbar=1

D

\hat{U}(D)=\hat{H}(D)]

\hat{H}(D)=\theta(D)\omega|0\rangle\langle 0|+(1-\theta(D))\omega|1\rangle\langle 1|,

(21)

where
\theta(D)

\hat{A}=\hat{\sigma}_{z}=|1\rangle\langle 1|-|0\rangle\langle 0|
, which means that the sign of
D
indicates which state is occupied. Since
[\hat{A},\hat{H}]=0
, the system remains diagonal in the energy eigenbasis. The system is weakly coupled to a bosonic reservoir with the Bose-Einstein distribution
n_{\rm B}=\left[\exp{(\frac{\omega}{k_{\rm B}T})-1}\right]^{-1}
, where
k_{\rm B}
and
T
are the Boltzmann constant and temperature of the reservoir, respectively. The Liouvillian in the QFPME is given by (note that
[\hat{H}(D),\hat{\rho}(D)]=0
)

\mathcal{L}(D)=\theta(D)\mathcal{L}_{+}+(1-\theta(D))\mathcal{L}_{-},

(22)

where

\mathcal{L}_{+}\hat{\rho}=\kappa n_{\rm B}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}]\hat{\rho}+\kappa(n_{\rm B}+1)\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}

(23)

and

\mathcal{L}_{-}\hat{\rho}=\kappa n_{\rm B}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}+\kappa(n_{\rm B}+1)\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}]\hat{\rho}.

(24)

Here,
\hat{\sigma}=|0\rangle\langle 1|
and
\kappa
is characterizes the coupling strength to the environment.

In this setup, work is extracted in the following way: starting in the ground state, the system can be excited with rate
\kappa n_{\rm B}
. This state of change is ideally detected through the measurement outcome
D
changing sign. When this happens, the ground and excited energy levels are swapped through feedback. The system is then again in its ground state, ready absorb the next quantum of energy
\omega

However, measurement inaccuracy and the detector’s delay may result in heating instead.

For the power, we find from Eq. (
10

,
p_{1}(D)=\langle 1|\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)|1\rangle
, and
P(D)=p_{0}(D)+p_{1}(D)
.
For the heat current, we find from Eq. (
11

J=\omega\kappa n_{\rm B}(1-\eta)-\omega\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})\eta,

(26)

where

\eta=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{0}(D)dD+\int_{-\infty}^{0}p_{1}(D)dD;

(27)

denotes the error probability, i.e., the probability that the sign of
D
does not correspond to the state the system is in.
The smaller the error probability, the more heat is absorbed by the system and extracted as power via the feedback mechanism.
Since
[\hat{A},\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)]=0
, the measurement energy vanishes as expected,
\dot{E}_{\rm M}=0
. Details can be found in App. (
B.1
).

The steady-state
P(D)
that the measurement outcome takes the value

25

. As anticipated, increasing the measurement strength

is, the faster the detector and the more work is extracted. In the limit
\gamma\gg\kappa
, the measurement outcome
D

III.6
Example 2: Continuous-measurement-driven engine

To illustrate our results in the quantum regime, we investigate an engine that is driven by a continuous measurement, i.e., the measurement energy flow
\dot{E}_{\rm M}
is converted into work. The considered engine is a continuous version of the engine introduced in Ref.
Elouard et al. 2017a
, where a projective measurement increases the energy of a qubit, which is then extracted as work by applying a unitary rotation.

We consider a qubit with energy splitting
\omega
that is coherently driven with a strength proportional to the measurement outcome

\hat{H}_{\rm lab}(D)=\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+igD\left(e^{i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}-e^{-i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\right).

(28)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{x,y,z}
are standard Pauli matrices, and
\hat{\sigma}=(\hat{\sigma}_{x}+i\hat{\sigma}_{y})/2
. The subscript
"\mathrm{lab}"
signifies a laboratory frame.
The setup is shown in Fig.
1
(”example 2”). Here we consider weak drives, i.e.,
g\ll\omega
, which allows for a thermodynamically consistent description with the thermodynamic Hamiltonian

\hat{U}=\hat{H}_{\rm TD}=\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}.

(29)

The internal energy of the system is thus fully determined by the projection of its Bloch vector on the
z

In a frame rotating with
\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}
, the Hamiltonian is given by (see App.
C.1
for details)

\hat{H}(D)=gD\hat{\sigma}_{y},

(30)

which corresponds to a rotation around the
y
-axis on the Bloch sphere with a direction set by the sign of
D
. We continuously measure the observable
\hat{A}=\hat{\sigma}_{x}
, which means the measurement outcome
D
indicates a position of the quantum state on the
x
-axis of the Bloch sphere. If the sign of
D
and the actual position on the
x
-axis are in agreement, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
30
) drives the system towards the ground state of
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
, extracting work. However, if they are in disagreement, we supply work by driving towards the excited eigenstate of
\hat{\sigma}_{z}
. The time evolution of
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)
is described by the QFPME (
1
), where the system is additionally weakly coupled to a bosonic reservoir with Bose-Einstein distribution
n_{\rm B}
. Its effect is described by the superoperator

\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{\rho}=\kappa n_{\rm B}\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}+\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}]\hat{\rho}.

(31)

The power extracted in this engine can be computed from Eq. (
10
) and reads (note that
\hat{H}_{\rm TD}

)

(32)

where
\langle\bullet\rangle_{\rm lab}

\dot{E}_{\rm M}=-\lambda\omega\left\langle\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right\rangle,

(33)

and the heat current reads

J=-\frac{\omega}{2}\kappa\left[1+(2n_{B}+1)\langle\hat{\sigma}_{z}\rangle\right],

(34)

see App.
C.2
for details.

Generally, the extracted power,

\lambda
increases. This is a consequence of the fact that the measurement energy flow, the fuel of this engine, increases for stronger measurements. Indeed, in the absence of a reservoir (
\kappa=0

in the steady state. The presence of an environment generally has a detrimental effect on the extracted power. For very weak measurements (

IV
Fluctuation theorems and the second law of thermodynamics

In the second part of our work, we derive a novel FT and a second law of thermodynamics using the feedback-control formalism of the QFPME (
1
). After a brief introduction to FTs for feedback-controlled systems based on the existing literature, we introduce the FT derived from the QFPME for classical systems and contrast it to previous results. We then demonstrate how our results can be extended to the quantum regime.

Beginning with systems without feedback control, the FT, which was first introduced for classical systems
Seifert 2005
;
Jarzynski 1997a
;
Jarzynski 1997b
;
Crooks 1999
;
Crooks 2000
and later generalized to open quantum systems
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
;
Manzano et al. 2015
;
Manzano et al. 2018
;
Manzano and Zambrini 2022
;
Campisi et al. 2009
, is given by

\frac{P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mu}]}{P[\mu]}=e^{-\sigma[\mu]}\implies\left\langle e^{-\sigma}\right\rangle=1,

(35)

where the equality on the left is called the detailed FT, and the equality on the right, implied by the detailed FT, is the integral FT. The key element is the stochastic entropy production
\sigma[\mu]
along a single trajectory
\mu
. In classical systems, we consider phase-space trajectories
Seifert 2005
;
Jarzynski 1997a
;
Jarzynski 1997b
;
Crooks 1999
;
Crooks 2000
and in open quantum systems quantum-jump trajectories
Horowitz 2012
;
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
;
Manzano et al. 2015
;
Manzano et al. 2018
;
Manzano and Zambrini 2022
;
Breuer 2003
;
Hekking and Pekola 2013
associated with the unraveling of a Lindblad master equation. Here,
\bar{\mu}
is obtained by time-reversing
\mu
, and the subscript
"\textrm{B}"
signifies a backward version of the experiment, where any time-dependent parameter is time reversed. The FT implies that the probability of the time-reversed trajectory
\bar{\mu}
occurring is exponentially suppressed by the stochastic entropy production
\sigma[\mu]
. Importantly, the second law of thermodynamics,

\langle\sigma\rangle=\sum_{\mu}P[\mu]\sigma[\mu]\geq 0,

(36)

which states that the average entropy production cannot be negative, follows from the FT, which can, therefore, be seen as a generalization of the second law.

Under feedback control, Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
) no longer apply, and the average entropy production in the system can become negative. In the modern formulation of information thermodynamics
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2010
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Prech and Potts 2024
;
Yada et al. 2022
;
Horowitz and Vaikuntanathan 2010
;
Gong et al. 2016
, the stochastic entropy production
\sigma[\mu]
is viewed on an equal footing to an information term
I[\mu]
, giving rise to modified versions of the FTs and the second law,

\frac{P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mu}]}{P[\mu]}=e^{-\sigma[\mu]-I[\mu]}\implies\left\langle e^{-\sigma-I}\right\rangle=1\implies\langle\sigma\rangle\geq-\langle I\rangle,

(37)

where now
\mu
is a joint trajectory of the system and the measurement outcomes used for feedback, and the information term
I[\mu]
arises from correlations between the trajectory of the system and the measurement outcomes.
These relations have been derived for both classical
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2010
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Horowitz and Vaikuntanathan 2010
and quantum systems
Prech and Potts 2024
;
Yada et al. 2022
, and different information terms are known: transfer entropy
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
or coarse-grained entropy
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
for classical systems and quantum-classical transfer entropy
Yada et al. 2022
or coarse-grained entropy
Prech and Potts 2024
for quantum systems. Below we present relations in the spirit of Eq. (
37
) that we obtained for systems described by the QFPME.

IV.1
Classical systems

IV.1.1
Fluctuation theorems and the second law

We start with presenting our results for a classical system, which may occupy a set of microstates (configurations)
\{a\}
.
Let us denote as
a_{n}
the microstate of the system at the time
t_{n}=n\delta t
for
n=0,1,...,N
, where
N=\tau/\delta t
and
\tau
is the duration of the experiment. We continuously monitor the state of the system. In the absence of measurement and feedback control, the trajectory
\mu
is given by the tuple
\mathbf{a}:=(a_{0},a_{1},...,a_{N})
. It becomes continuous in the limit
\delta t\to 0
for fixed
\tau
.
In the same way, we can define a trajectory of the detector’s measurement outcomes
\mathbf{D}
, such that the trajectory
\mu=(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D})
includes both the system states and the measurement outcomes. We assume that the state of the system
a
is monitored, i.e.
\hat{A}=\sum_{a}a|a\rangle\langle a|
.

From the QFPME, we derived the novel detailed and integral FTs,

\frac{P[\bar{\mathbf{a}},\bar{\mathbf{D}}]}{P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}=e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}\implies\left\langle e^{-\sigma-\sigma_{\rm m}}\right\rangle=1,

(38)

which form a counterpart to Eq. (
37
). Here,
\bar{\mathbf{a}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{D}}
are time-revered versions of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{D}
. The left-hand side of the detailed FT in Eq. (
38
) is the ratio of probabilities that the system and detector follow specific trajectories and their time-reversed versions.
The term
\sigma_{\rm m}
, which appears instead of the information term
I
and originates from feedback control, is named by us “measurement entropy” and is implicitly defined as

e^{-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}:=\frac{P_{\rm m}[\bar{\mathbf{D}}|\bar{\mathbf{a}}]}{P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]},

(39)

where
P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
is the probability of obtaining the trajectory of measurement outcomes
\mathbf{D}
when the system’s trajectory is fixed to be
\mathbf{a}
.
It is completely determined by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with a time-dependent drift term given by
\mathbf{a}
, see App.
D.1
.
We find that the measurement entropy can be cast into a stochastic Itô integral

\sigma_{\rm m}=\frac{8\lambda}{\gamma}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}(a_{t}-D_{t})dD_{t}\right)-\gamma\tau-\ln\frac{P_{\rm ini}[D_{\tau}|a_{\tau}]}{P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]}

(40)

where
a_{t}
is the state of the system at the time
t
, and
dD_{t}
is given by the stochastic Itô differential equation (
14
) but with
a_{t}
replacing the expectation value of
\hat{A}
:

dD_{t}=\gamma(a_{t}-D_{t})dt+\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{\lambda}}dW.

(41)

The time-discrete representation of Eq. (
40
) is presented in Eq. (
115
). The last term in Eq. (
40
) is determined by the initial
P_{\rm ini}
probability distribution of the measurement outcomes. The asymmetry between
P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
and
P_{\rm m}[\bar{\mathbf{D}}|\bar{\mathbf{a}}]
, which results in a non-vanishing measurement entropy
\sigma_{\rm m}
, arises from the detector’s delay due to its finite bandwidth and is an essential feature of feedback control described by the QFPME formalism. The measurement entropy captures the fact that it is more likely to observe measurement outcomes that lag behind than measurement outcomes that anticipate the behavior of the system. Details about the general setting of feedback control based on the QFPME are presented in App.
D.1
, and Eqs. (
38
) and (
40
) are derived in App. (
D.2
).

Similarly to the second law in Eq. (
37
), from the FT in Eq. (
38
), we obtain the second law of thermodynamics for QFPME-based feedback by applying Jensen’s inequality:

\left\langle\sigma\right\rangle\geq-\left\langle\sigma_{\rm m}\right\rangle

(42)

We find that the rate of change of the average of the measurement entropy can be expressed as

\partial_{t}\langle\sigma_{\rm m}\rangle=8\lambda\left(\left\langle(D_{t}-a_{t})^{2}\right\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{8\lambda}\right).

(43)

The first term denotes the mean squared error of the measurement outcome. The second term is the mean squared error of the measurement outcome if the system remains in a fixed state
a
. In this case, the distribution of the measurement outcome tends to a Gaussian with mean
a
and variance
\gamma/(8\lambda)
. The measurement entropy thus quantifies the excess mean squared error, which arises due to the dynamics of the system, which is only captured by the measurement outcome with a delay. Equation. (
43
) is derived in App.
D.3
.

IV.1.2
Comparison with other approaches to feedback control

We wish to highlight conceptual differences between the QFPME-based feedback and the formalism of Refs.
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Horowitz and Vaikuntanathan 2010
which result in the FTs discussed in the beginning of this section. In deriving these FTs, the main challenge is that measurement and feedback cannot be time-reversed, since measurement occurs before feedback. This requires the choice of a backward experiment that is not equal to the time-reversed forward experiment. As shown in Ref.
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
, different choices leading to different information quantifiers are possible.

For the QFPME, the feedback is instantaneous and therefore time-reversing the measurement outcome results in the time-reversed feedback protocol. Causality in the QFPME is not enforced by employing feedback after the measurement outcome is obtained, but rather by the finite reaction time of the detector, which ensures that the measurement outcome reacts to changes in the system with a finite delay. For this reason, an experiment described by the QFPME can be time-reversed and no auxiliary backward experiment needs to be introduced.

We further note that previous works typically assume that the measurement is time-reversal symmetric
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Horowitz and Vaikuntanathan 2010
, i.e., that
P_{\rm m}[\bar{\mathbf{D}}|\bar{\mathbf{a}}]=P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
such that the measurement entropy vanishes. For an example of how the measurement entropy may enter previous FTs in the presence of measurement and feedback, see the supplemental material of
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
.

IV.1.3
Coarse graining

Since the idea behind measurement and feedback protocols is to gather and use information about the system in the form of the trajectory of measurement outcomes,
\mathbf{D}
, and not the systems’s trajectory, we may only have access to
\mathbf{D}
without any knowledge of
\mathbf{a}
. Motivated by this scenario, we provide a FT for just the detector’s trajectory using coarse graining.
Let us define the effective entropy production coarse-grained over
\mathbf{a}
, the inaccessible part of the trajectory:

e^{-\Sigma[\mathbf{D}]}:=\int d\mathbf{a}e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}e^{-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{D}],

(44)

where
P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{D}]=P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]/P[\mathbf{D}]
is the conditional probability, with
P[\mathbf{D}]=\int d\mathbf{a}P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]
. Then, the coarse-grained counterpart to Eq. (
38
) holds,

P[\mathbf{D}]e^{-\Sigma[\mathbf{D}]}=P[\bar{\mathbf{D}}]\implies\langle e^{-\Sigma[\mathbf{D}]}\rangle=1,

(45)

which depends only on
\mathbf{D}
.
For details, see App.
D.4
.

IV.3
Quantum systems

IV.3.1
Fluctuation theorems

Having presented the FTs in Eq. (
38
) and the second law in Eq. (
42
) for classical systems, we now show their counterparts for quantum dynamics. For open quantum systems described the Lindblad master equation

\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{t}=-i[\hat{H}_{t},\hat{\rho}_{t}]+\sum_{k}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}_{k}]\hat{\rho}_{t},

(48)

where
\hat{L}_{k}
are Lindblad jump operators, a trajectory of the system is typically defined via the so-called quantum-jump unraveling of the master equation
Manzano et al. 2015
;
Manzano et al. 2018
;
Manzano and Zambrini 2022
;
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
, which enters the probabilities in the FT without feedback control [see Eq. (
35
)]. It includes outcomes of the projective measurements onto the eigenstates of the initial and final states of the quantum systems as well as a list of jumps,
(k_{1},k_{2},...,k_{M})
, together with their corresponding times,
(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{M})
, which we collectively denote by
\Gamma
.

The quantum system is continuously measured with the observable
\hat{A}=\sum_{a}a|a\rangle\langle a|

(
Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Bednorz et al. 2012
)
, and feedback at the time
t
is implemented with the measurement outcome
D_{t}
, just as we have considered for the classical system. However, the difference in the quantum regime is that the system may be in a superposition of eigenstates of the observable
\hat{A}
. To define a trajectory for the quantum system, we combine the above-introduced unraveling with trajectories along a closed time contour
Hofer and Clerk 2016
. See App.
F
for details. In this method, identity operators
\hat{I}=\sum_{a}|a\rangle\langle a|
are inserted at every step
\delta t
in the forward-in-time and backwards-in-time branches of the closed time contour. Upon performing the Keldysh rotation, the trajectory can be phrased in terms of a classical trajectory,
\mathbf{a}_{\rm c}:=(a_{0}^{\rm c},a_{1}^{\rm c},...,a_{N}^{\rm c})
, related to diagonal elements of the density matrix, and a quantum trajectory
\mathbf{a}_{\rm q}:=(a_{0}^{\rm q},a_{1}^{\rm q},...,a_{N}^{\rm q})
, associated to superposition states. The quasi-probability distribution of the trajectory, is then expressed as
P[\Gamma,\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\mathbf{a}_{\rm q},\bf{D}]
. Importantly, it does not have to be positive and can be even complex. However, it is still normalized.

With this formulation, we find the novel FTs for open quantum systems under QFPME-base feedback control:

\frac{P[\bar{\Gamma},\bar{\bf{a}}_{\rm c},\bar{\bf{D}}]}{P[\Gamma,\bf{a}_{\rm c},\bf{D}]}=e^{-\sigma[\Gamma,\bf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\bf{D}]}\implies\left\langle e^{-\sigma-\sigma_{\rm m}}\right\rangle=1,

(49)

Here, crucially, the stochastic entropy production
\sigma[\Gamma,\bf{D}]
depends on the unraveling trajectory
\Gamma
, which encompasses all quantum jumps, and the measurement entropy
\sigma_{\rm m}[\bf{a}_{\rm c},\bf{D}]
depends on the classical trajectory
\bf{a}_{\rm c}
but not the quantum trajectory. This allows us to integrate out the quantum trajectory and obtain the quasi-probability distribution
P[\Gamma,\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\mathbf{D}]=\int d\mathbf{a}_{\rm q}P[\Gamma,\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\mathbf{a}_{\rm q},\mathbf{D}]
, which is real valued but may take on negative values. In the FT, the meaning of
\bar{\Gamma},\bar{\bf{a}}_{\rm c}
, and
\bar{\bf{D}}
is the time-reversed versions of
\bar{\Gamma},\bar{\bf{a}}_{\rm c}
, and
\bar{\bf{D}}
, respectively. In the quantum case, we find the expression for the stochastic measurement entropy,

\sigma_{\rm m}=\frac{8\lambda}{\gamma}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}(a_{t}^{\rm c}-D_{t})dD_{t}\right)-\gamma\tau-\ln\frac{P_{\rm ini}[D_{\tau}]}{P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}]},

(50)

where the classical Keldysh path
\bf{a}_{\rm c}
replaces the system’s trajectory
\bf{a}
[cf. Eq. (
40
)]. See App.
F
for derivations.

In Sec.
IV.1.2
, we discussed conceptual differences between our FT for QFPME-based feedback control and the approaches of Refs.
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
;
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Horowitz and Vaikuntanathan 2010
for classical systems. We note that the latter have been generalized to open quantum systems in Refs.
Prech and Potts 2024
;
Yada et al. 2022
. The same differences are present between our results in the quantum regime and Refs.
Prech and Potts 2024
;
Yada et al. 2022
.

IV.4
Coarse-graining and the second law

Since the quasi-probability distribution
P[\Gamma,\bf{a}_{\rm c},\bf{D}]
does not need to be positive, the second law does not follow from the integral FT in Eq. (
49
) anymore.
However,
P[\Gamma,\mathbf{D}]=\int d\mathbf{a}_{\rm c}P[\Gamma,\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\mathbf{D}]
represents a valid probability distribution. Therefore, by defining the measurement entropy coarse-grained over the inaccessible classical trajectory
\mathbf{a}_{\rm c}
,

e^{-\sigma_{\rm m,cg}[\mathbf{D}]}:=\int d\mathbf{a}_{\rm c}~e^{-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\bf{D}]}P[\mathbf{a}_{\rm c}|\Gamma,\mathbf{D}],

(51)

where
P[\mathbf{a}_{\rm c}|\Gamma,\mathbf{D}]=P[\Gamma,\mathbf{a}_{\rm c},\mathbf{D}]/P[\Gamma,\mathbf{D}]
,
we obtain the FT

\frac{P[\bar{\Gamma},\bar{\bf{D}}]}{P[\Gamma,\bf{D}]}=e^{-\sigma[\Gamma,\bf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m,cg}[\mathbf{D}]}\implies\left\langle e^{-\sigma-\sigma_{\rm m,cg}}\right\rangle=1.

(52)

Both
\Gamma
and
\mathbf{D}
are experimentally accessible, and the probabilities on the left-hand side are nonnegative and normalized, which makes this relation the quantum analogue for the classical result [see Eq. (
38
)]. Importantly, the coarse-grained measurement entropy
\sigma_{\rm m,cg}[\mathbf{D}]
depends solely on the trajectory of the detector. The second law follows from the right-hand side of Eq. (
52
):

\left\langle\sigma\right\rangle\geq-\left\langle\sigma_{\rm m,cg}\right\rangle.

(53)

V
Discussion and conclusions

Definitions of work and heat have been well studied in classical and quantum systems without feedback control, but such a formulation has been lacking for continuously measured systems under arbitrary (linear but also nonlinear) feedback protocols. Relying on the QFPME, which describes the time evolution of the joint state of a system and a detector with finite bandwidth, we have derived expressions for the power, heat current, and measurement energy rate with the aid of methods from stochastic calculus. We have applied these definitions to investigate the performance of two different work-extraction toy models: a two-level system under bang-bang control, which is an example of a class of threshold-like feedback protocols, and a continuous-measurement-driven engine, where the quantum measurement provides a source of energy by backaction on the quantum state of the qubit. Since measurement and feedback constitutes a powerful tool to control nanoscale systems, we expect that the first law of thermodynamics presented here will be useful in future investigations of such setups.

The second part of this manuscript is concerned with the second law and FTs for systems that follow the QFPME. While in the existing literature, FTs have been extended to feedback controlled systems in classical and quantum regimes by including information terms that quantify correlations between measurement outcomes and trajectories followed by the system, a common assumption in these studies is a symmetry between forward and backward experiments in the conditional probability of measurement outcomes (
P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
). As evidenced, this assumption is naturally broken in systems described with the QFPME-formalism, which is a consequence of the finite bandwidth of the detector, a relevant characteristic of experiments. Using the QFPME-framework (as well as Keldysh quasiprobabilities in the quantum regime), we derive the corresponding FTs, where the information term is replaced with the measurement entropy that quantifies the asymmetry in distributions of measurement outcomes.
Our results are illustrated on both previously used toy models and they shed insight into the role of the measurement in the irreversibility of feedback experiments.

We anticipate the presented framework to be applied to a variety of classical and quantum devices to assess their thermodynamic performance and obtain insights into their limitations. In particular, we unlock this possibility for models where nonlinear protocols may be leveraged to optimize cooling or any other goal. Furthermore, extending the presented framework to different types of detector filter (e.g., a high-pass frequency filter) constitutes an intriguing avenue to be explored.

\left\langle\partial_{D}^{2}\hat{H}\right\rangle=\omega\left\langle\partial_{D}\delta(D)\left(|0\rangle\langle 0|-|1\rangle\langle 1|\right)\right\rangle=-\omega\left(\partial_{D}\langle 0|\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)|0\rangle|_{D=0}-\partial_{D}\langle 1|\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)|1\rangle|_{D=0}\right)=-\omega\left[\partial_{D}p_{0}(D)|_{D=0}-\partial_{D}p_{1}(D)|_{D=0}\right],

(65)

where we have used the relation
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dDf(D)\partial_{D}\delta(D)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dD\delta(D)\partial_{D}f(D)
for a distribution
f(\cdot)
. Here, we have also introduced
p_{0}(D)=\langle 0|\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)|0\rangle
and
p_{1}(D)=\langle 1|\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)|1\rangle
for brevity of notation. Inserting the above drift and diffusion terms into Eq. (
10
) results in the power in Eq. (
25
).

As far as the heat current is concerned, we start from Eq. (
11
). The system absorbs and ejects energy quanta
\omega
with rates
\kappa n_{\rm B}
and
\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})
. When
D\geq 0
,
|0\rangle
and
|1\rangle
are the excited and the ground state, respectively, and the contribution to the heat current is given by
\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}dD\left(\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{1}(D)-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{0}(D)\right)
. When
D<0
, the expression is analogous, but the states are switched. Therefore, the expression for heat current reads

\begin{split}J&=\omega\int_{0}^{\infty}dD\left(\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{1}(D)-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{0}(D)\right)+\omega\int_{-\infty}^{0}dD\left(\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{0}(D)-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{1}(D)\right)\\
&=\omega\kappa n_{\rm B}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}dDp_{1}(D)+\int_{-\infty}^{0}dDp_{0}(D)\right)-\omega\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}dDp_{0}(D)+\omega\int_{-\infty}^{0}dDp_{1}(D)\right),\end{split}

(66)

Two integrals in the bracket after
\kappa n_{\rm B}
is the total probability that
D
shows the correct state of the system, whereas the two integrals in the bracket after
\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})
is the total probability that they do not. The latter is the error probability
\eta
in Eq. (
27
) in the main text, resulting in Eq. (
26
).

B.2
Steady-state solution

From the QFPME (
1
) with the Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}(D)
(
22
), we obtain a pair of coupled differential equations,

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}p_{0}(D)=\mathcal{J}p_{0}(D)+\gamma\partial_{D}p_{0}(D)+\theta(D)\left(\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{1}(D)-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{0}(D)\right)+(1-\theta(D))\left(\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{1}(D)-\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{0}(D)\right),\\
&\partial_{t}p_{1}(D)=\mathcal{J}p_{1}(D)-\gamma\partial_{D}p_{1}(D)+\theta(D)\left(\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{0}(D)-\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{1}(D)\right)+(1-\theta(D))\left(\kappa n_{\rm B}p_{0}(D)-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})p_{1}(D)\right),\\
\end{cases}

(67)

where we have introduced the differential operator
\mathcal{J}\cdot=\gamma\partial_{D}(D\,\cdot)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial_{D}(\cdot)
. It is convenient to define
q_{+}(D)=p_{0}(D)+p_{-}(D)
and
q_{-}(D)=p_{0}(D)-p_{-}(D)
, which allows us to rewrite Eq. (
67
) as

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}q_{+}(D)=\mathcal{J}q_{+}(D)+\gamma\partial_{D}q_{-}(D),\\
&\partial_{t}q_{-}(D)=\mathcal{J}q_{-}(D)+\gamma\partial_{D}q_{+}(D)-(\kappa n_{\rm B}-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B}))q_{+}-(\kappa n_{\rm B}+\kappa(1+n_{\rm B}))q_{-}+2\theta(D)(\kappa n_{\rm B}-\kappa(1+n_{\rm B}))q_{+}(D).\\
\end{cases}

(68)

To make progress, we introduce the functions

G_{n}=G_{n}(D)=\frac{e^{-\frac{D^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}\mathrm{He}_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)

(69)

for
n\geq 0
, where

\mathrm{He}_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)=\left(\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{H}_{n}\left(\frac{D}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\right)

(70)

are generalized Hermite polynomials with
\sigma=\frac{\gamma}{8\lambda}
and
\mathrm{H}_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\partial^{n}_{x}e^{-x^{2}}
are standard physicist’s Hermite polynomials.
The functions
G_{n}
fulfill two useful properties:

\mathcal{J}G_{n}=-\gamma nG_{n}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{D}G_{n}=-\frac{1}{\sigma}G_{n+1},

(71)

which follow from two identities obeyed by the generalized Hermite polynomials,
\partial_{D}\mathrm{He}_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)=n\mathrm{He}_{n-1}^{[\sigma]}(D)
and
\mathrm{He}_{n+1}^{[\sigma]}(D)=2D\mathrm{He}_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)-\partial_{D}\mathrm{He}_{n-1}^{[\sigma]}(D)
.
Next, we expand
q_{\pm}(D)
as

q_{\pm}(D)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{n}^{\pm}G_{n}(D),

(72)

where the coefficients
q_{n}^{\pm}
do not explicitly depend on
D
. We insert this expansion into Eq. (
68
) and consider the steady-state condition by setting the time-derivatives on the left-hand sides to zero.
Next, we multiply both equations (
68
) by
\mathrm{He}_{m}^{[\sigma]}(D)
and integrate over
D
from
-\infty
to
\infty
.
Using the orthonormality of the generalized Hermite polynomials,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}He_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)He_{m}^{[\sigma]}(D)\frac{e^{-\frac{D^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}dD=\delta_{n,m}n!\sigma^{n},

(73)

and the relation

\int_{0}^{\infty}He_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)He_{m}^{[\sigma]}(D)\frac{e^{-\frac{D^{2}}{2\sigma}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}dD=\begin{cases}&\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{m}m!\quad\text{for $n=m$},\\
&0\quad\text{for $n+m$ even},\\
&\frac{(-1)^{(m+n-1)/2}\sqrt{\sigma^{m+n}}m!!(n-1)!!}{\sqrt{2\pi}(m-n)}\quad\text{for $n$ even and $m$ odd},\\
&\frac{(-1)^{(m+n-1)/2}\sqrt{\sigma^{m+n}}n!!(m-1)!!}{\sqrt{2\pi}(n-m)}\quad\text{for $n$ odd and $m$ even},\\
\end{cases}

(74)

with
(-1)!!=1
,
we find a set of linear equations:

\begin{cases}&0=-\gamma mq_{m}^{+}-\frac{\gamma}{\sigma}q_{m-1}^{-}\\
&0=-\gamma mq_{m}^{-}-\frac{\gamma}{\sigma}q_{m-1}^{+}-\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})q_{m}^{-}-2\kappa\mathcal{C}_{m}.\\
\end{cases}

(75)

Here,

\mathcal{C}_{m}=\begin{cases}\sum_{n=0,2,...}q_{n}^{+}\frac{(-1)^{(m+n-1)/2}\sqrt{\sigma^{m+n}}m!!(n-1)!!}{\sqrt{2\pi}(m-n)m!\sigma^{m}}\quad\text{for $m$ odd},\\
\sum_{n=1,3,...}q_{n}^{+}\frac{(-1)^{(m+n-1)/2}\sqrt{\sigma^{m+n}}n!!(m-1)!!}{\sqrt{2\pi}(n-m)m!\sigma^{m}}\quad\text{for $m$ even},\\
\end{cases}

(76)

where
q^{\pm}_{n}=0
for
n<0
. We can notice that in Eq. (
75
), elements
q_{m}^{-}
with even index
m
are coupled only to elements
q_{m^{\prime}}^{+}
with odd index
m^{\prime}
and vice-versa. For even
m
’s and odd
m^{\prime}
’s,
q_{m}^{-}=q_{m^{\prime}}^{+}=0
is a solution. The same does not hold for odd
m
’s and even
m^{\prime}
’s, because from the second line in Eq. (
75
) with
m=1
, we would have
q_{0}^{+}=0
. However, we can show that
q_{0}^{+}=1
, which follows from normalization of probability:

1=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}q_{+}(D)dD=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{n}^{+}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{n}(D)dD=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{n}^{+}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G_{n}(D)\mathrm{He}_{0}^{[\sigma]}(D)dD=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q_{n}^{+}\delta_{n,0}n!\sigma^{n}=q_{0}^{+},

(77)

where we inserted Eq. (
72
) to obtain the second equality and used
\mathrm{He}_{0}^{[\sigma]}(D)=1
in the third equality and Eq. (
73
) in the fourth equality.
Therefore, we obtain a set of linear equations for
q^{-}_{m}
and
q^{-}_{m^{\prime}}
terms with only odd
m
’s and even
{m^{\prime}}
:

\begin{cases}&0=-\gamma(2\ell)q_{2\ell}^{+}-\frac{\gamma}{\sigma}q_{2\ell-1}^{-}\\
&0=-\gamma((2\ell+1)q_{2\ell+1}^{-}-\frac{\gamma}{\sigma}q_{2\ell}^{+}-\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})q_{2\ell+1}^{-}-2\kappa\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{+}_{2k}\frac{(-1)^{\ell+k}\sqrt{\sigma^{-2\ell+2k-1}}(2\ell+1)!!(2k-1)!!}{\sqrt{2\pi}(2\ell-2k+1)(2\ell+1)!}.\\
\end{cases}

(78)

To solve them, we make a truncation by considering the first
L
equations with
\ell=0,2,...,L
. This means we set
q^{+}_{2\ell}=q^{-}_{2\ell+1}=0
for
\ell>L
. The truncation is justified, because Eq. (
72
) converges for a sufficiently large
L
. This way we obtain
2L+1
equations (the first line in Eq. (
78
) with
\ell=0
yields
0=0
) with
2L+1
variables, which is a solvable system of equations. The probability
P(D)
is then found using Eq. (
72
).
The functions
G_{n}
are either even or odd depending on the index,
G_{n}(-D)=(-1)^{n}G_{n}(D)
, which results in
q_{+}(-D)=q_{+}(D)
and
q_{-}(-D)=q_{-}(D)
. The occupation probabilities are given by
p_{0}(D)=(q_{+}(D)+q_{-}(D))/2
and
p_{1}(D)=(q_{+}(D)-q_{-}(D))/2
. They obey the relations
p_{0}(-D)=p_{1}(D)
and
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dDp_{0}(D)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dDp_{1}(D)=1/2
.
The power
P
in Eq. (
25
) can be obtained with the expression

P=-\omega\gamma\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}q^{+}_{2\ell}G_{2\ell}(0)-\omega\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}q^{-}_{2\ell+1}\partial_{D}G_{2\ell+1}(D)|_{D=0}=-\omega\gamma\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}q^{+}_{2\ell}G_{2\ell}(0)+\omega\gamma\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}q_{2\ell+1}^{-}G_{2\ell+2}(0),

(79)

where we have applied
\partial_{D}G_{n}(D)=-(1/\sigma)G_{n+1}(D)
.

Appendix C
Continuous-measurement-driven heat engine

In this section, we provide details about the continuous-measurement-driven quantum heat engine in Sec.
IV.2
.

C.1
Rotating frame

Let us denote the density matrix in the laboratory frame and in the rotating frame as
\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)
and
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)=\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}
, respectively. Here,
\hat{V}=e^{it\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}}
. The laboratory-frame density matrix
\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)
follows the QFPME (
1
) with the laboratory-frame Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm lab}
(
28
), and the observable
\hat{A}_{\rm lab}=\hat{V}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{x}\hat{V}
is continuously measured. In the rotating frame, the equation of motion reads

\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)=\partial_{t}\left(\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right)=\partial_{t}\hat{V}\left(\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right)+\left(\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\partial_{t}\hat{V}^{\dagger}+\hat{V}\left(\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=i\frac{\omega}{2}[\hat{\sigma}_{z},\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)]+\hat{V}\left(\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger},

(80)

where we used
\partial_{t}\hat{V}=i\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\hat{V}
and
\partial_{t}\hat{V}^{\dagger}=-i\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{z}
. For
\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)
, we can substitute the QFPME (
1
), which we write here for convenience:

\partial_{t}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)=-i[\hat{H}_{\rm lab},\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)]+\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)+\lambda\mathcal{D}[\hat{A}_{\rm lab}]\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)-\gamma\partial_{D}\mathcal{A}_{\rm lab}(D)\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial^{2}_{D}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D).

(81)

We now compute
\hat{V}\cdot\hat{V}^{\dagger}
applied to each term on the right-hand side. The first term results in

\hat{V}\left(-i[\hat{H}_{\rm lab},\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)]\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=-i[\hat{V}\hat{H}_{\rm lab}\hat{V}^{\dagger},\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}]=-i\frac{\omega}{2}[\hat{\sigma}_{z},\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)]-i[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)],

(82)

where we used
\hat{V}\hat{H}_{\rm lab}\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+gD\hat{\sigma}_{y}=\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+\hat{H}
, with
\hat{H}
being the Hamiltonian (
30
) in the rotating frame. Notice that the term
-i\frac{\omega}{2}[\hat{\sigma}_{z},\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)]
in Eq. (
82
) cancels with the first term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
80
). From the second term in Eq. (
81
), where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}
is given in Eq. (
31
), we obtain

\hat{V}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D).

(83)

Here, we used the relations
\hat{V}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}\hat{V}=\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}
,
\hat{V}\hat{\sigma}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{V}=\hat{\sigma}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}
,
\hat{V}\hat{\sigma}\hat{V}=e^{-i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}
, and
\hat{V}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{V}=e^{i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}
. From the third term in Eq. (
81
) we have

\hat{V}\left(\lambda\mathcal{D}[\hat{A}_{\rm lab}]\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\lambda\mathcal{D}[\hat{V}\hat{A}_{\rm lab}\hat{V}^{\dagger}]\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\lambda\mathcal{D}[\hat{A}]\hat{\rho}_{t}(D).

(84)

From the fourth term in Eq. (
81
) we find

\hat{V}\left(-\gamma\partial_{D}\mathcal{A}_{\rm lab}(D)\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\partial_{D}\{\hat{V}\hat{A}_{\rm lab}\hat{V}^{\dagger}-D,\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\partial_{D}\{\hat{A}-D,\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\}=-\gamma\partial_{D}\mathcal{A}(D)\hat{\rho}_{t}(D),

(85)

where we used
\hat{V}\hat{A}_{\rm lab}\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\hat{A}
.
Lastly, the fifth term in Eq. (
81
) yields

\hat{V}\left(\partial_{D}^{2}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}=\partial_{D}^{2}\left(\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right)=\partial_{D}^{2}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D).

(86)

Collecting together Eqs. (
82
)-(
86
) leads to the QFPME (
1
) describing the time evolution of the density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)
in the rotating frame, where the measured observable is
\hat{A}=\hat{\sigma}_{x}
, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is given in Eq. (
30
), and the superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{\rm B}
describing the effect of the reservoir is given in Eq. (
31
).

C.2
Power, heat, and measurement energy

All expressions that appear in the first law of thermodynamics in Eq. (
9
) are in the laboratory frame, which means they are evaluated with respect to
\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)
. However, we would like to compute them in the rotating frame, with respect to
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)=\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}
with
\hat{V}=e^{it\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}}
, which follows the QFPME with the time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
(
30
). To this end, for the expectation value of
\hat{O}
in the laboratory frame, we write the corresponding expression in the rotating frame:

\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\rm lab}:=\int dD\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{O}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\}=\int dD\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{V}\hat{O}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\hat{V}\hat{\varrho}_{t}(D)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\}=\int dD\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{V}\hat{O}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\}=:\left\langle\hat{V}\hat{O}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle,

(87)

which can be employed to find the power (
10
), heat current (
11
), and measurement energy (
12
). Below, we compute each element that appears in these quantities.
The thermodynamic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\rm TD}=\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}
[see Eq. (
29
)] is independent of
D
and therefore the only term contributing to the power is

P=\left\langle\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{\rm lab}\right\rangle_{\rm lab}
, where
\hat{H}_{\rm lab}=\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+igD\left(e^{i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}-e^{-i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\right)
[see Eq. (
28
)]. Using Eq. (
87
), we find

P=\left\langle\hat{V}\left(\partial_{t}\hat{H}_{\rm lab}\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=-g\omega\left\langle D\hat{V}\left(e^{i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}+e^{-i\omega t}\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=-\omega g\left\langle D(\hat{\sigma}+\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger})\right\rangle=-\omega g\left\langle D\hat{\sigma}_{x}\right\rangle,

(88)

The above equation is the power in Eq. (
32
).
For the measurement energy (
12
) we obtain

\dot{E}_{\rm M}=\left\langle\hat{V}\left(\lambda\mathcal{D}[\hat{A}_{\rm lab}]\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=\lambda\left\langle\mathcal{D}[\hat{V}\hat{A}_{\rm lab}\hat{V}^{\dagger}]\hat{V}\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=\lambda\left\langle\mathcal{D}[\hat{\sigma}_{x}]\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right)\right\rangle=-\lambda\omega\left\langle\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right\rangle,

(89)

which recovers Eq. (
33
).
For the heat current (
11
), we find

\begin{split}J=&\left\langle\hat{V}\left(\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{\rm B}\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=\left\langle\hat{V}\left(\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\sigma}]\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{V}\left(\kappa n_{\rm B}\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}]\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\right)\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle=\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})\left\langle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\sigma}]\hat{V}\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle+\kappa n_{\rm B}\left\langle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}]\hat{V}\hat{H}_{\rm TD}\hat{V}^{\dagger}\right\rangle\\
=&\kappa(1+n_{\rm B})\left\langle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\sigma}]\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right)\right\rangle+\kappa n_{\rm B}\left\langle\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{\sigma}^{\dagger}]\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right)\right\rangle=-\frac{\omega}{2}\kappa-\frac{\omega}{2}\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})\langle\hat{\sigma}_{z}\rangle,\end{split}

(90)

which is Eq. (
34
).

C.3
Steady-state solution

The joint state of the quantum system and the measurement outcome
\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)
can be expressed as

\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)=\frac{1}{2}\left(P_{t}(D)\hat{I}+a_{x,t}(D)\hat{\sigma}_{x}+a_{y,t}(D)\hat{\sigma}_{y}+a_{z,t}(D)\hat{\sigma}_{z}\right),

(91)

where
\hat{I}
is the identity matrix and
(a_{x},a_{y},a_{z})
is the Bloch vector for a given
D
at the time
t
, where
a_{j}=a_{j,t}(D)
and
P_{t}(D)=P
for brevity (not to be confused with power).
Using the relation
a_{j}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\sigma}_{j}\hat{\rho}_{t}(D)\}
for
j\in\{x,y,z\}
, the QFPME for the continuous-measurement-driven heat engine leads to a set of coupled differential equations,

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}P=\gamma\partial_{D}(DP-a_{x})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial_{D}^{2}P,\\
&\partial_{t}a_{x}=2gDa_{z}+\gamma\partial_{D}(Da_{x}-P)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial_{D}^{2}a_{x}-\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{2}a_{x},\\
&\partial_{t}a_{y}=-2\lambda a_{y}+\gamma\partial_{D}(Da_{y})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial_{D}^{2}a_{y}-\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{2}a_{y},\\
&\partial_{t}a_{z}=-2gDa_{x}-2\lambda a_{z}+\gamma\partial_{D}(Da_{z}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial_{D}^{2}a_{z}-\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})b_{z}-\kappa P.\\
\end{cases}

(92)

Since we are concerned with the steady state, we may set the left-hand sides to zero. Using the differential operator
\mathcal{J}\cdot=\gamma\partial_{D}(D\,\cdot)+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{8\lambda}\partial_{D}(\cdot)
, which has already been introduced, we can simplify the four equations above to

\begin{cases}&0=\mathcal{J}P-\gamma\partial_{D}a_{x},\\
&0=2gDa_{z}+\mathcal{J}a_{x}-\gamma\partial_{D}P-\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{2}a_{x},\\
&0=-2\lambda a_{y}+\mathcal{J}a_{y}-\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{2}a_{y},\\
&0=-2gDa_{x}-2\lambda a_{z}+\mathcal{J}a_{z}-\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})b_{z}-\kappa P.\\
\end{cases}

(93)

Next, we expand the Bloch coefficients using the functions
G_{n}
defined in Eq. (
69
):

P=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}G_{n}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad a_{j}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^{(j)}_{n}G_{n},

(94)

which we insert into Eq. (
93
). We note that
p_{n}
and
a_{n}^{(j)}
do not depend on
D
. We apply the relations in Eq. (
71
) and multiply each equation by
\mathrm{He}_{m}^{[\sigma]}(D)
.
Integrating over
D
from
-\infty
to
\infty
and utilizing the identities

\partial_{D}\mathrm{He}_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)=n\mathrm{He}_{n-1}^{[\sigma]}(D)
and
\mathrm{He}_{n+1}^{[\sigma]}(D)=2D\mathrm{He}_{n}^{[\sigma]}(D)-\partial_{D}\mathrm{He}_{n-1}^{[\sigma]}(D)
as well as the orthonormality (
73
) of Hermite polynomials leads to a recursive scheme

\begin{cases}&0=mp_{m}-\frac{1}{\sigma}a_{m-1}^{(x)},\\
&0=-\left(2\lambda+\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{2}\right)a_{m}^{(y)}-\gamma ma_{m}^{(y)},\\
&0=ga_{m-1}^{(z)}+g(m+1)\sigma a_{m+1}^{(z)}-\frac{\gamma}{2}ma_{m}^{(x)}+\frac{\gamma}{2\sigma}p_{m-1}-\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{4}b_{m}^{(x)},\\
&0=-ga_{m-1}^{(x)}-g(m+1)\sigma a_{m+1}^{(x)}-\lambda a_{m}^{(z)}-\frac{\gamma}{2}ma_{m}^{(z)}-a_{m}^{(z)}\frac{\kappa(1+2n_{\rm B})}{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2}p_{m},\\
\end{cases}

(95)

where we set
a^{(j)}_{m}=0
and
p_{m}=0
when
m<0
.
From the second equation we immediately see that
a_{m}^{(y)}=0

Appendix D
Fluctuation theorems for classical systems

D.1
General setting

When introducing the general setting of the QFPME-based feedback control, we largely follow the notation of Refs.
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
.
At the start of the forward experiment, the system and detector are prepared in the states
a_{0}
and
D_{0}
according the initial joint distribution
P_{\rm ini}[a_{0},D_{0}]
. The system is driven with a protocol
\mathbf{\Lambda}:=(\Lambda_{1},...,\Lambda_{N})
such that the probability
P[a_{n}|a_{n-1},\Lambda_{n}]
of transition
a_{n-1}\to a_{n}
depends on
\Lambda_{n}
, where
\mathbf{\Lambda}
encompasses all settings of experimental ”knobs”. Feedback control is implemented by making the protocol
\mathbf{\Lambda}
dependent on the trajectory
\mathbf{D}:=(D_{0},D_{1},...,D_{N},D_{N+1})
of the detector such that
\Lambda_{n}(\mathbf{D})=\Lambda(D_{n})
depends only on
D_{n}
, which is required to describe the dynamics of the system with the QFPME formalism. We thereby ignore an explicit time-dependence in the protocol for simplicity. Here, each
D_{n\geq 1}
is contingent upon
(a_{0},...,a_{n-1})
and
(D_{0},...,D_{n-1})
, which is characterized by the probability
P_{\rm m}[D_{n}|(a_{0},...,a_{n-1}),(D_{0},...,D_{n-1})]
. A general way to write the joint probability of the system’s and measurement outcome’s trajectories is given by [cf. Eq. (47) in Ref.
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
and Eq. (5) in Ref.
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
]

\begin{split}P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]=&P_{\rm ini}[a_{0},D_{0}]P[D_{1}|(a_{0}),(D_{0})]P[a_{1}|a_{0},\Lambda(D_{1})]\\
&\times P[D_{2}|(a_{0},a_{1}),(D_{0},D_{1})]P[a_{2}|a_{1},\Lambda(D_{2})]\times...\\
&\times P[a_{N}|a_{N-1},\Lambda(D_{N})]P[D_{N+1}|(a_{0},...,a_{N}),(D_{0},...,D_{N})]\\
=:&P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{\Lambda}(\mathbf{D})],\end{split}

(101)

where we grouped together

P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{\Lambda}(\mathbf{D})]=P_{\rm ini}[a_{0}]P[a_{1}|a_{0},\Lambda(D_{1})]...P[a_{N}|a_{N-1},\Lambda(D_{N})]

(102)

and

P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]=P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]P[D_{1}|a_{0},D_{0}]...P[D_{N+1}|(a_{0},a_{1},...,a_{N})],

(103)

with
P_{\rm ini}[a_{0},D_{0}]=P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]P_{\rm ini}[a_{0}]
and
P_{\rm ini}[a_{0}]
being a marginal distribution.
The probability
P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
is the probability of recording the measurement outcomes’ trajectory
\mathbf{D}
given the fixed system’s trajectory
\mathbf{a}
, which is signified by the subscript ”m”. We stress that due to feedback control, it is different from
P[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]=P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]/P[\mathbf{a}]
, where
P[\mathbf{a}]=\int d\mathbf{D}P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]
is the marginal distribution
Sagawa and Ueda 2012
;
Potts and Samuelsson 2018
.
As demonstrated below, the probability
P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
is given by

P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]=P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\prod_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{\gamma\delta t}e^{-2\lambda\delta t\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}\right)^{2}}.

(104)

The probability in Eq. (
101
) is normalized,
\int d\mathbf{D}d\mathbf{a}P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]=1
, which can be shown by integrating in the order
D_{N+1}\to a_{N}\to D_{N}\to...\to a_{0}\to D_{0}
. The ensemble average of any trajectory-dependent quantity
O(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D})
is obtained with

\left\langle O\right\rangle=\int d\mathbf{D}d\mathbf{a}O(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D})P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}].

(105)

Equation (
104
) is derived as follows. In the formalism of the QFPME, the state of the detector at the time
t
reads
Annby-Andersson et al. 2022

D(t)=\gamma\int_{-\infty}^{t}dse^{-\gamma(t-s)}z(s),

(106)

where
z(s)
is the outcome of the continuous Gaussian measurement
Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Bednorz et al. 2012
at the time
s
. The integral represents the low-pass filter with the bandwidth
\gamma
. In the time-discrete notation, it reads

D_{n}=\gamma\delta t\sum_{k=0}^{n}e^{-\gamma(n-k)\delta t}z_{k}.

(107)

In the Gaussian measurement for classical incoherent systems, the probability of the outcome
z
given the state of the system
a
is given by
Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Bednorz et al. 2012

P[z|a]=\sqrt{\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}}e^{-2\lambda\delta t(z-a)^{2}},

(108)

which is a Gaussian distribution centered at
a
. Its variance is given by
1/(4\lambda\delta t)
, which means increasing
\lambda
results in a more precise measurement. The probability of obtaining a tuple of outcomes
\mathbf{z}=(z_{0},z_{1},...,z_{N+1})
given the trajectory of the system
\mathbf{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},...,a_{N})
reads

P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{a}]=P_{\rm ini,z}[z_{0}|a_{0}]\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\prod_{n=0}^{N}e^{-2\lambda\delta t(z_{n+1}-a_{n})^{2}},

(109)

where
P_{\rm ini,z}[z_{0}|a_{0}]
is the probability of the initial
z_{0}
before the start of the experiment. Using Eq. (
107
), the probability of the trajectory of the detector
\mathbf{D}
is obtained through

\begin{split}P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]&=\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\int dz_{N+1}...dz_{0}\prod_{r=0}^{N+1}\delta\left(D_{r}-\gamma\delta t\sum_{k=0}^{r}e^{-\gamma\delta t(r-k)}z_{k}\right)P_{\rm ini,z}[z_{0}|a_{0}]\prod_{n=0}^{N}e^{-2\lambda\delta t}(z_{n+1}-a_{n})^{2}\\
&=P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\prod_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{\gamma\delta t}e^{-2\lambda\delta t\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}\right)^{2}},\end{split}

(110)

where we recognize
P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]:=P_{\rm ini,z}[D_{0}/(\gamma\delta t)|a_{0}]/(\gamma\delta t)
as the initial probability of the detector before the start of the experiment. Therefore, we recover
P_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
in Eq. (
104
).

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{n}[D_{n}]&=\gamma\delta t\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n-1}}{\gamma\delta t}+a_{n-1}\right)=e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n-1}+\gamma\delta ta_{n-1}\\
&=(1-\gamma\delta t)D_{n-1}+\gamma\delta ta_{n-1},\end{split}

(126)

and

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{n}[D_{n}^{2}]&=(\gamma\delta t)^{2}\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n-1}}{\gamma\delta t}+a_{n-1}\right)^{2}+\frac{(\gamma\delta t)^{2}}{4\lambda\delta t}\\
&=(D_{n-1}^{2}-2\gamma\delta tD_{n-1}^{2}+2\gamma\delta tD_{n-1}a_{n-1})+\frac{\gamma^{2}\delta t}{4\lambda},\end{split}

(127)

respectively, where we kept terms up to the first order in
\delta t
.
Applying these calculations to Eq. (
124
) leads to

\begin{split}-\mathcal{I}_{n}[D_{n}^{2}]+\mathcal{I}_{n}[2D_{n}a_{n-1}]=&-D_{n-1}^{2}+2D_{n-1}a_{n-1}-\frac{\gamma^{2}\delta t}{4\lambda}\\
&+2\gamma\delta t(D_{n-1}-a_{n-1})^{2}.\end{split}

(128)

We now combine the first two elements in the equation above with
2(a_{n-2}-a_{n-1})D_{n-1}
, resulting in

2(a_{n-2}-a_{n-1})D_{n-1}-D_{n-1}^{2}+2a_{n-1}D_{n-1}=-D_{n-1}^{2}+2a_{n-2}D_{n-1},

(129)

which leads to a recursive scheme when we repeatedly apply this reasoning to the sum in Eq. (
124
) starting from
n=1
. At the end, the first two terms in Eq. (
128
) for
n=N+1
are
-D_{0}^{2}+2a_{0}D_{0}
, which cancel out in Eq. (
124
). Therefore, the only remaining elements are the last two terms in Eq. (
128
), and in the limit
\delta t\to 0
, we obtain

\langle\sigma_{\rm m}\rangle=-\gamma\tau+8\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\langle(D_{n}-a_{n})^{2}\rangle-\left\langle\ln\frac{P_{\rm ini,B}[D_{N+1}|a_{N}]}{P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}|a_{0}]}\right\rangle,

(130)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
43
).

D.4
Coarse-graining

We have the marginal probability distributions
P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mathbf{D}}]=\int d\bar{\mathbf{a}}P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mathbf{a}},\bar{\mathbf{D}}]
and
P[\mathbf{D}]=\int d\mathbf{a}P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]
.
Our starting point is Eq. (
38
):

P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mathbf{a}},\bar{\mathbf{D}}]=P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}.

(131)

Inserting
P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{D}]=P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]/P[\mathbf{D}]
results in

P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mathbf{a}},\bar{\mathbf{D}}]=P[\mathbf{D}]P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{D}]e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}.

(132)

Upon integrating out the system’ trajectory we obtain

P_{\rm B}[\bar{\mathbf{D}}]=P[\mathbf{D}]\int d\mathbf{a}P[\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{D}]e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}=P[\mathbf{D}]e^{-\Sigma[\mathbf{D}]},

(133)

where we used the definition of the coarse-grained
\Sigma[\mathbf{D}]
in Eq. (
44
), which concludes the derivation of the detailed FT in Eq. (

). Further integrating of the detector’s trajectory leads to the integral FT in Eq. (

).

E.2
FT for

The trajectories
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{D}
uniquely determine
m[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]

P[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a},m]=\delta(m[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}]-m)P[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}].

(142)

From the detailed FT in Eq. (
38

m[\bar{\mathbf{a}},\bar{\mathbf{D}}]=-m[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]
, it follows that (here we drop the subscript ”B”)

P[\bar{\mathbf{D}},\bar{\mathbf{a}},-m]=e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}]}P[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a},m].

(143)

We define the joint probability of
m

\mathbf{a}

P[\mathbf{a},m]=\int d\mathbf{D}P[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D},m]

P[\bar{\mathbf{a}},-m]=P[\mathbf{a},m]\int d\mathbf{D}e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}P[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a},m],

(144)

where the conditional probability
P[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a},m]=P[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a},m]/P[\mathbf{a},m]
can be calculated as

P[\mathbf{D}|m,\mathbf{a}]=\frac{P[\mathbf{D},m|\mathbf{a}]}{\int dDP[\mathbf{D},m|\mathbf{a}]},

(145)

with
P[\mathbf{D},m|\mathbf{a}]=\delta(m[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}]-m)P[\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{a}]
.
The stochastic entropy production in Eq. (
144
) is given by
e^{-\sigma[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{D}]}=e^{m\omega/(k_{\rm B}T)}
, which does not explicitly depend on
\mathbf{D}
and, for this reason, can be taken in front of the integral.

In the long-time limit, we can approximate

e^{-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}]}=\prod_{n=1}^{N}e^{-\frac{8\lambda}{\gamma}D_{n}(a_{n-1}-a_{n})},

(146)

which can be seen from Eq. (
124

m=m_{+}-m_{-}
, where
m_{+}
and
m_{-}
correspond to a total number of quanta flowing into and out of the system. We assume that the total number of jumps,
M=m_{+}+m_{-}
, is small comparing to the number of steps
N
and that all jumps are significantly separated in time. In each step
n
, when no jump occurs (
a_{n-1}-a_{n}=0
), no heat exchange happens, and the corresponding element in the product in Eq. (
146
) is simply
1
regardless of the value of
D_{n}
.
When a jump takes place,
a_{n-1}-a_{n}=2a_{n-1}=\pm 2
. If the signs of
a_{n-1}
and
D_{n}
are the same, the increment of entropy production is

m_{+}
(the system transitions from the ground to the excited state). But if they are different, the increment of entropy production is
\omega/(k_{\rm B}T)
, and the jump contributes to
m_{-}
(the system transitions from the excited to the ground state). Since consecutive jumps happen in distant steps
n
, and the detector is much faster than the rate of jumps, for each step with a jump, we can approximate
P[D_{n}|a_{n-1}]=\sqrt{\frac{4\lambda}{\pi\gamma}}e^{-\frac{4\lambda}{\gamma}(D_{n}-a_{n-1})^{2}}
from Eq. (
123

\int d\mathbf{D}P[\mathbf{D},m|\mathbf{a}]=\binom{M}{m_{+}}(1-\eta)^{m_{+}}\eta^{m_{-}},

(147)

where, for instance, if the jump from
a_{n-1}=1
to
a_{n}=-1
contributes to
m_{-}

\int_{-\infty}^{0}dD_{n}P[D_{n}|1]=\left[1-\text{Erf}\left(2\sqrt{\lambda/\gamma}\right)\right]/2=\eta
, and the prefactor accounts for the number of combinations that the steps contributing to
m_{+}
and
m_{-}
can be arranged in.

Now, the product of the probability and the measurement entropy for the step with a jump from
a_{n-1}
to
a_{n}=-a_{n-1}
is given by
\sqrt{\frac{4\lambda}{\pi\gamma}}e^{-\frac{4\lambda}{\gamma}(D_{n}-a_{n-1})^{2}}e^{-\frac{8\lambda}{\gamma}D_{n}(a_{n-1}-a_{n})}=\sqrt{\frac{4\lambda}{\pi\gamma}}e^{-\frac{4\lambda}{\gamma}(D_{n}+a_{n-1})^{2}}

P[D_{n}|-a_{n-1}]
. Therefore, we find

\int d\mathbf{D}e^{-\sigma_{\rm m}[\mathbf{D},\mathbf{a}]}P[\mathbf{D},m|\mathbf{a}]=\binom{M}{m_{+}}\eta^{m_{+}}(1-\eta)^{m_{-}},

(148)

since the roles of
\eta
and
1-\eta
are interchanged with respect to Eq. (
147

Combining Eqs. (
144
), (
145
), (
147
), and (
148
) leads to

\begin{split}P[\bar{\mathbf{a}},-m]=&P[\mathbf{a},m]e^{m\omega/(k_{\rm B}T)}\frac{\binom{M}{m_{+}}\eta^{m_{+}}(1-\eta)^{m_{-}}}{\binom{M}{m_{+}}(1-\eta)^{m_{+}}\eta^{m_{-}}}\\
=&P[\mathbf{a},m]e^{m\omega/(k_{\rm B}T)}\left(\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\right)^{m_{+}-m_{-}}\\
=&P[\mathbf{a},m]e^{m\left[\omega/(k_{\rm B}T)-\log{\left(\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}\right)}\right]}.\end{split}

(149)

Upon integrating out
\mathbf{a}
from both sides, we obtain the FT for

in Eq. (
47
).

F.1
Forward trajectory

At the time
t=0
, the system is prepared in the initial state

\hat{\rho}_{\rm ini}=\sum_{v_{\rm i}}p_{v_{\rm i}}|v_{\rm i}\rangle\langle v_{\rm i}|
.
The probability of the trajectory of the measurement outcomes,
\mathbf{D}=(D_{0},D_{1},...,D_{N+1})
, is given by
Annby-Andersson et al. 2022

P[\mathbf{D}]=P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}]\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\delta t}\right)^{N+1}\text{Tr}\left\{\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{D_{N+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{N}}{\gamma\delta t}\right)e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{N})\delta t}...e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{1})\delta t}\mathcal{K}\left(\frac{D_{1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{0}}{\gamma\delta t}\right)\hat{\rho}_{\rm ini}\right\},

(150)

where
P_{\rm ini}
is the initial distribution of the measurement outcome, and the measurement operator of the continuous Gaussian measurement is defined as
\mathcal{K}(z)\hat{\rho}=K(z)\hat{\rho}K(z)
, with the Hermitian operator
K(z)=\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\lambda\delta t(z-\hat{A})^{2}}

Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Bednorz et al. 2012
. The superoperator
e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{n})\delta t}
realizes a feedback step. Now, we insert the identity operator
\hat{I}=\sum_{a_{n}}|a_{n}\rangle\langle a_{n}|
after the feedback superoperator corresponding to
D_{n}
in two branches, which we here denote with
L
and
R
, defining the Keldysh trajectory
Hofer 2017
;
Nazarov and Kindermann 2003
;
Hofer and Clerk 2016
;
Clerk 2011
.
The quasi-probability of the trajectory reads

\begin{split}P[\mathbf{a}^{L},\mathbf{a}^{R},\mathbf{D}]=&\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\delta t}\right)^{N+1}P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}]\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{a}^{L},\mathbf{a}^{R},\mathbf{D})\\
&\exp{\left[-\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{L}\right)^{2}\right]}\exp{\left[-\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{R}\right)^{2}\right]},\end{split}

(151)

where

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{a}^{L},\mathbf{a}^{R},\mathbf{D})=\langle a_{N}^{L}|e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{N})\delta t}\left[|a_{N-1}^{L}\rangle...|a_{1}^{L}\rangle\langle a_{1}^{L}|e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{1})\delta t}\left[|a_{0}^{L}\rangle\langle a_{0}^{R}|\right]|a_{1}^{R}\rangle\langle a_{1}^{R}|...\langle a_{N-1}^{R}|\right]|a_{N}^{R}\rangle\times\delta_{a^{L}_{N},a^{R}_{N}}\times\langle a_{0}^{L}|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ini}|a_{0}^{R}\rangle

(152)

and
\delta_{a^{L}_{N},a^{R}_{N}}
is the Kronecker delta.
We further introduce the quantum-jump unraveling
Manzano et al. 2015
;
Manzano et al. 2018
;
Manzano and Zambrini 2022
;
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
. Each term
e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{n})\delta t}
can be expanded as

e^{\mathcal{L}(D_{n})\delta t}|a^{L}_{n-1}\rangle\langle a^{R}_{n-1}|=e^{-i\delta t\hat{H}_{\rm eff}}|a^{L}_{n-1}\rangle\langle a^{R}_{n-1}|e^{i\delta t\hat{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}}+\delta t\sum_{k}\hat{L}_{k}|a^{L}_{n-1}\rangle\langle a^{R}_{n-1}|\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}=:\mathcal{J}_{0}|a^{L}_{n-1}\rangle\langle a^{R}_{n-1}|+\sum_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}|a^{L}_{n-1}\rangle\langle a^{R}_{n-1}|+,

(153)

where we used superoperators for convenience, with
\mathcal{J}_{k}
corresponding to the quantum jump
k
and
\mathcal{J}_{0}
describing the non-Hermitian evolution with the effective Hamilonian
\hat{H}_{\rm eff}=\hat{H}_{\rm eff}(D_{n})=\hat{H}(D_{n})-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}(D_{n})\hat{L}_{k}(D_{n})
. The unraveling trajectory
\Gamma
encompasses monitoring whether and if yes, which jump occurred in each time step, as well as the outcomes of the projective measurements at the initial time
0
and the final time
\tau
, which is known as the two-point measurement scheme. In the two-point measurement scheme, we project onto
|v_{\rm i}\rangle\langle v_{\rm i}|
, the eigenstates of the initial state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm ini}
, at the beginning. At the end we project onto
|v_{\rm f}\rangle\langle v_{\rm f}|
, the eigenstates of the final state of the system average over all measurement outcomes,

\hat{\rho}_{\rm fin}=\sum_{v_{\rm f}}p_{v_{\rm f}}|v_{\rm f}\rangle\langle v_{\rm f}|
.
Then, the quasi-probability distribution of the joint trajectory of the Keldysh path, unraveling, and detector is given by

\begin{split}P[\Gamma,\mathbf{a}^{L},\mathbf{a}^{R},\mathbf{D}]=&\left(\frac{2\lambda\delta t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac{N+1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\delta t}\right)^{N+1}P_{\rm ini}[D_{0}]\mathcal{A}(\Gamma,\mathbf{a}^{L},\mathbf{a}^{R},\mathbf{D})\\
&\exp{\left[-\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{L}\right)^{2}\right]}\exp{\left[-\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{R}\right)^{2}\right]},\end{split}

(154)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Gamma,\mathbf{a}^{L},\mathbf{a}^{R},\mathbf{D})=p_{v_{\rm i}}\langle a_{N}^{L}|\mathcal{J}_{k_{N}}(D_{N})\left[|a_{N-1}^{L}\rangle...|a_{1}^{L}\rangle\langle a_{1}^{L}|\mathcal{J}_{k_{1}}(D_{1})\left[|a_{0}^{L}\rangle\langle a_{0}^{R}|\right]|a_{1}^{R}\rangle\langle a_{1}^{R}|...\langle a_{N-1}^{R}|\right]|a_{N}^{R}\rangle\times\langle a_{0}^{L}|v_{\rm i}\rangle\langle v_{\rm i}|a^{R}_{0}\rangle\times\langle v_{\rm f}|a^{L}_{N}\rangle\langle a_{N}^{R}|v_{\rm f}\rangle,

(155)

with
k_{n}
denoting the jump or non-Hermitian evolution governed by the measurement outcome
D_{n}
.
Next, we perform the Keldysh rotation, introducing the classical fields
a_{n}^{\rm c}=(a_{n}^{L}+a_{n}^{R})/2
and quantum fields
a_{n}^{\rm q}=(a_{n}^{L}-a_{n}^{R})/2
. As a result, two last terms in Eq. (
154
) can we expressed as

\begin{split}&\exp{\left[-\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{L}\right)^{2}\right]}\exp{\left[-\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{R}\right)^{2}\right]}\\
&=\exp{\left[-2\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(\frac{D_{n+1}-e^{-\gamma\delta t}D_{n}}{\gamma\delta t}-a_{n}^{\rm c}\right)^{2}\right]}\exp{\left[-2\lambda\delta t\sum_{n=0}^{N}\left(a_{n}^{\rm q}\right)^{2}\right]}.\end{split}

(156)

We can notice that the classical fields
a_{n}^{\rm c}
play the same role as the system’s states
a_{n}
in the probability in Eq. (
104
) for the classical systems. The quantum fields
a_{n}^{\rm q}
, on the other hand, introduce dephasing, which grows in strength with the distance
|a_{n}^{L}-a_{n}^{R}|
between the two Keldysh branches.

F.2
Backward trajectory

In the backward experiment, signified by subscript ”B”, we perform measurement and feedback just like in the forward one, but the measurement operator is
\sum_{a}a|\tilde{a}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}|
, where
|\tilde{a}\rangle=\Theta|a\rangle
, with
\Theta
denoting the anti-Hermitian time-reversal operator, and we prepare the initial quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm ini,B}=\sum_{v_{\rm f}}p_{v_{\rm f}}|\tilde{v}_{\rm f}\rangle\langle\tilde{v}_{\rm f}|
. The time-reversed versions of the Keldysh and detector’s trajectories are given by
\bar{a}^{\ell}_{n}=a^{\ell}_{N-n}
(for
\ell=L,R
) and
\bar{D}_{n}=D_{N-n+1}
, respectively, where the Keldysh paths are defined with respect to the identity operators
\sum_{a}|\tilde{a}\rangle\langle\tilde{a}|
. Under the Keldysh rotation, we have
\bar{a}_{n}^{\rm c}=a_{N-n}^{\rm c}
and
\bar{a}_{n}^{\rm q}=a_{N-n}^{\rm q}
. As far as the time-reversed version of the quantum-jump unraveling,
\bar{\Gamma}
, we use the standard procedure
Manzano et al. 2015
;
Manzano et al. 2018
;
Manzano and Zambrini 2022
;
Horowitz and Parrondo 2013
: The outcomes of the two-point-measurement scheme are interchanged, with projectors onto
|\tilde{v}_{\rm f}\rangle\langle\tilde{v}_{\rm f}|
at the initial time and onto
|\tilde{v}_{\rm i}\rangle\langle\tilde{v}_{\rm i}|
at the final time. Driving that implements feedback is performed under the time-reversal operator, i.e., the Hamiltonian and jump operators become
\Theta\hat{H}\Theta^{-1}
and
\Theta\hat{L}_{k}\Theta^{-1}
, respectively. For the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, this results is
\Theta\hat{H}\Theta^{-1}-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{k}\Theta\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{L}_{k}\Theta^{-1}=\Theta\hat{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}\Theta^{-1}
, where we used
\Theta i=-i\Theta
.
The backward-trajectory jump superoperators are defined as (for
k\neq 0
)

\bar{\mathcal{J}}_{k_{n}}\hat{\rho}=\Theta\hat{L}_{k^{\prime}_{N+1-n}}\Theta^{-1}\hat{\rho}\Theta\hat{L}_{k^{\prime}_{N+1-n}}^{\dagger}\Theta^{-1}=e^{-\sigma_{k_{N+1-n}}}\Theta\hat{L}^{\dagger}_{k_{N+1-n}}\Theta^{-1}\hat{\rho}\Theta\hat{L}_{k_{N+1-n}}\Theta^{-1},

(157)

where for each
\hat{L}_{k}
, we can uniquely identify
\hat{L}_{k^{\prime}}=e^{-\sigma_{k}/2}\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}
, with
\sigma_{k}
being the stochastic entropy increment due to dissipation, associated with the quantum jump
k
. For
