Body composition mediates the association between fatty acids and NAFLD risk: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Qishan Yang, Xinming Xu, Yue Chen, Zhicheng Zhang, Berty Ruping Song, Liang Sun, Xiang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate how body composition, particularly adiposity distribution, mediates the association between circulating fatty acids (FAs) and the incidence of severe non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).MethodsIn the UK Biobank cohort, plasma FAs were measured using nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy in 107,593 participants at baseline. Cox regression models were utilized to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between FAs and the incidence of severe NAFLD. Additionally, mediation analyses were conducted to quantify the mediating effects of different body composition indices in these associations. Cross-sectional association of FAs with metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) were also explored by multivariate logistic regression.ResultsDuring an average follow-up period of 13.1 years, 1,480 severe NAFLD cases were identified. In multivariable analyses, the HRs per SD and CIs were 0.76 (95% CI: 0.68–0.86) for plasma polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), 1.28 (95% CI: 1.12–1.46) for monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), and 1.20 (95% CI: 1.10–1.32) for saturated fatty acids (SFA) (all P < 0.001). Results for MAFLD and MASLD were comparable. SFA and MUFA were positively correlated, while PUFA was inversely correlated to all body composition indices. Mediation analysis revealed that central obesity indices, such as waist circumference and body roundness index, mediated 19–36% of the associations between FAs and severe NAFLD (all P < 0.001). Although other body composition indices also exhibited significant mediating effects, their contributions were relatively smaller. Specifically, leg fat mass index mediated around 13–26% and arm fat mass index mediated around 9–21% of the association between FAs and severe NAFLD.ConclusionThis study highlights that body compositions, particularly central obesity, may partially mediate the relationship between plasma FAs and the incidence of severe NAFLD, suggesting that dietary interventions, when integrated with broader strategies to reduce central adiposity, could be critical for preventing NAFLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01119-3.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), characterized by hepatic steatosis, is closely associated with metabolic disorders such as obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and metabolic syndrome (MS). NAFLD affects approximately 30% of the global population, with a regional prevalence of 30.2% in Europe, and 11% in England [1]. This high prevalence underscores its significance as a primary public health concern, while lifestyle modification, including dietary changes, could be the cornerstone of NAFLD prevention and management.

Emerging evidence highlights a potential complex interplay between fatty acids (FAs), lipid metabolism, and body composition in the development and progression of NAFLD [2]. Body fat distribution and lean mass exhibit distinct metabolic implications and are differentially associated with the risk of metabolic diseases. For instance, observational studies have shown that higher body fat mass and lower leg fat-to-trunk fat ratios are linked to a higher risk of NAFLD [3], whereas leg fat and lean body mass are inversely associated with NAFLD and T2D [4, 5]. Meanwhile, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) have been associated with a lowered risk of NAFLD, while saturated fatty acids (SFAs) and monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) are linked to an elevated risk [6–9]. Additionally, an observational study reported that higher dietary PUFA intake was inversely associated with body fat [10], while an intervention study demonstrated that excessive SFA intake promotes greater visceral adipose tissue (VAT) accumulation than does excessive intake of unsaturated FAs [11]. Thus far, the relationship between MUFAs and body composition is less understood. Moreover, the role of body composition in the association between circulating FAs and NAFLD remains unclear.

At present, two new nomenclatures for NAFLD, namely metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) have been recently introduced [12]. These updated definitions incorporate metabolic risk factors into their diagnostic criteria, offering a more precise reflection of the disease’s etiology. However, studies exploring the association between circulating FAs and these new classifications of fatty liver disease remain scarce.

This study hypothesizes that body composition (including whole body, central, trunk, leg, arm fat, and fat-free mass) modulates the relationship between circulating FAs and NAFLD. Furthermore, it investigates the association of circulating FAs with MAFLD and MASLD, providing a more comprehensive assessment of their relationships with fatty liver diseases.

Methods

Study population

The present study was based on the UK Biobank (UKB) cohort. Detailed descriptions of the UKB cohort design and methods can be found on the UKB website (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/). In brief, the UKB is an ongoing prospective cohort study involving 502,389 participants aged 40–69 years from England, Wales, and Scotland recruited between 2006 and 2010. All participants provided written informed consent, completed touchscreen questionnaires and verbal interviews, underwent anthropometric measurements, and provided blood samples. Ethical approval for the UKB was granted by the Northwest Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee (reference no.16/NW/0274).

Among the recruited participants, a total of 107,593 participants were finally included in the current analysis (flow chart detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1). Exclusions included those with missing nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) plasma fatty acid data (n = 384,457), as well as those with prevalent severe NAFLD, HBV/HCV infection, liver-damaging drug use, alcohol-related diseases at baseline [7, 13], liver cirrhosis, liver cancer, and other liver-related diseases [7, 14](n = 3,386), or liver enzyme levels considered as outliers according to the Studentized Deviate Many-Outlier procedure [15] (n = 6,953).

Plasma fatty acid measurements

A high-throughput NMR metabolomics platform developed by Nightingale Health Ltd was used to perform metabolomic profiling on baseline plasma samples from a randomly selected subset of around 120,000 UKB participants. Detailed descriptions of plasma FA measurements and quality control measures can be found at https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/label.cgi?id=220, and details of the Nightingale Health NMR biomarker platform were described in a previous study [16]. FAs, including linoleic acid (LA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-6 PUFA), omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFA), total PUFA, total SFA, and total MUFA, expressed as percentages of total FA, were adopted in the current analysis. During the first repeated assessment visit (2012–2013), approximately 5,000 participants who provided plasma samples at baseline were reinvited to provide additional plasma samples. The fatty acid profiles from these repeated measurements demonstrated moderate correlations (ranging from 0.5 to 0.6) with their corresponding baseline values [2], indicating stability in the plasma FA levels over time.

Data collection

Sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, including age, sex, ethnicity, education, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and dietary intake were collected via a self-administered touchscreen questionnaire at baseline. Years of education were defined as follows: no qualifications (7 years), CSEs (10 years), O-levels/GCSEs (10 years), A levels/AS levels (13 years), other professional qualifications (15 years), NVQ or HNC (19 years), and a college or university degree (20 years). The Townsend Deprivation Index was calculated, assigning each participant a score based on the output area corresponding to their postcode. Physical activity was quantified by summing metabolic equivalent (MET) hours per week for all activities, including walking and moderate and vigorous exercise. Sedentary time was calculated by combining the hours spent per day watching TV and using a computer. Participants were asked to report the frequency of consumption of the following food, vegetables (raw and cooked), fresh fruit, red meat (unprocessed beef, pork, and lamb/mutton), processed meat, poultry, fish (oily and non-oily), and cheese on a weekly or daily basis over the past 12 months, as part of a short touchscreen questionnaire. Details on serving sizes and categorization calculations have been reported elsewhere [17].

Prevalent cardiovascular diseases (CVD) (including coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and composite CVD, as specified in Supplementary Table 1), and T2D were identified through self-report, first occurrence (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/first_occurrences_outcomes.pdf), and derived from ICD-10 codes mapped to hospital admissions records and primary care data (Supplementary Table 1). Hypertension was defined as having one of the following conditions: self-reported hypertension during nurse interviews, self-reported questionnaires regarding high blood pressure diagnosed by doctor, and hospital data (Supplementary Table 1). Non-fasting venous blood samples were collected and, after minimal processing at the assessment centers, were shipped to a central facility for further processing and long-term storage at − 80 °C. Biochemical biomarkers, such as gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) were measured in baseline blood samples at a central UK Biobank laboratory (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=17518).

Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference (WC) were conducted using calibrated instruments following standard protocols (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=100010). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Tanita BC418MA whole body composition analyzer was used for whole body bioimpedance measures, in which the body fat mass, trunk fat mass, arm fat mass (averaged from left and right arm fat mass), and leg fat mass (averaged from left and right leg fat mass) were adopted in the current analysis.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measurements of abdominal fat were performed using a Siemens MAGNETOM Aera 1.5T MRI scanner as part of the UKB imaging study, a population-based sub-study of the larger UKB involving 100,000 participants. The sub-study began in April 2014 and continued through May 2020, providing detailed imaging data for liver and abdominal composition. A 6-minute dual echo Dixon VIBE protocol was used to acquire water and fat separated volumetric data covering the region from neck to knees. Liver fat content was measured using a multiecho Dixon acquisition, which quantifies whole liver proton density fat fraction (WL-PDFF) by separating water and fat signals. VAT volume and abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (ASAT) volume were calculated in liters (L) and were derived using AMRA Profiler Research [18], a validated tool demonstrating high accuracy in quantifying body fat volumes (Pearson’s r = 0.97, p < 0.001 compared to the gold-standard method SliceOmatic) [19]. Total trunk fat volume (L) was calculated as the sum of VAT and ASAT volume (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/label.cgi?id=105).

Anthropometric composition indices

To provide a more comprehensive analysis of body fat within overall body composition, this study utilized the fat mass index [20]. The body fat mass index (FMI) was derived by dividing body fat mass by height in meters squared (kg/m²) to assess fat mass relative to height. The fat free mass index (FFMI), trunk fat mass index (TFMI), arm fat mass index (AFMI), and leg fat mass index (LFMI) were computed in the same manner as FMI. Genetic data and SNPs related to NAFLD

Genotyping process and quality control within the UKB was detailed in previous studies and was performed in two arrays: the UK BiLEVE axiom and the UKB axiom, covering approximately 500,000 participants [22, 23]. For this study, seven single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within GCKR, LPL, TRIB1, FTO, MAU2/TM6SF2, APOE, and PNPLA3 genes, were selected. The effect sizes (β-coefficients) of these SNPs were obtained from a meta-analysis of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and used to calculate the genetic risk score (GRS) for NAFLD as below: GRS = 0.12 × GCKR + 0.08 × LPL + 0.12 × TRIBI + 0.05 × FTO + 0.41× MAU2/TM6SF2 + 0.15 × APOE + 0.35 × PNPLA3 [24]. GRS was categorized as high (quintile 5), intermediate (quintiles 2–4), or low (quintile 1).

Outcomes

According to previous studies, severe NAFLD was defined using hospitalization admission records and death data via the presence of either ICD-10 code K76.0 (fatty liver, not elsewhere classified) or K75.8 (other specified inflammatory liver diseases) [25]. Linked data from Health Episode Statistics (for England and Wales) and Scottish Morbidity Records (for Scotland) were utilized to gather information regarding hospital admissions and diagnoses. Date and cause of death were extracted from death certificates archived by the National Health Service Information Centre (for England and Wales), and the National Health Service Central Register Scotland (for Scotland). The follow-up period for each participant was calculated from baseline to the earliest occurrence of one of the following events: first occurrence of severe NAFLD, death, or the end of the study period [October 31, 2022 for England, July 31, 2021 for Scotland, and February 28, 2018 for Wales]. WL-PDFF measurements from the UKB imaging study (April 2014-May 2020) were included in the sensitivity analysis to capture additional cases that might not have been clinically diagnosed [18]. NAFLD was diagnosed by WL-PDFF ≥ 5% [7], and for NAFLD cases identified via WL-PDFF, the imaging date was referred to as the diagnosis date. Further details regarding the exclusion criteria applied in the sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

We further adopted two new terms MAFLD and MASLD in the analysis to better capture the association between FA and newly differentiated fatty liver disease definitions. MAFLD is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis along with at least one of the following criteria: overweight or obese, T2D, or metabolic dysregulation in lean or normal-weight individuals [12]. Based on these criteria, individuals are further categorized into MAFLD with overweight/obesity (MAFLD-O), MAFLD with T2D (MAFLD-T2D), and MAFLD in lean or normal-weight adults (MAFLD-lean). Meanwhile, MASLD is identified as hepatic steatosis with no other causes, accompanied by at least one sign of metabolic dysregulation [12, 26, 27], as described in Supplementary. Patients with MASLD who consume alcohol in amounts exceeding 140–350 g per week for women or 210–420 g for men are further classified as having metabolic liver disease associated with alcohol intake (MetALD). Statistical analysis

The correlations of plasma FAs with body composition indices and dietary factors were assessed using partial Spearman correlation coefficients (r). Cox regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for severe NAFLD events associated with the log-transformed and standardized levels of plasma FAs. Three models were constructed to adjust for potential confounders. Model 1 was adjusted for age (continuous), sex, ethnicity (White, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, Mixed, others), years of education (continuous), Townsend Deprivation Index (continuous), smoking status (never or occasional, former, current regular), alcohol consumption (never or occasional, former, current regular), physical activity (MET-hour/week), sedentary time (hour/day), as well as baseline CVD, T2D, and hypertension status (yes/no). On top of Model 1, Model 2 was additionally adjusted for the weekly dietary intake frequency (continuous) for bread (slices per week), cereal (bowls per week), vegetables (tablespoons per week), fresh fruit (pieces per week), and red meat, processed meat, poultry, and fish (all as frequency per week). Model 3, building on Model 2, was further adjusted for BMI (continuous) and other FAs. The proportional hazards assumption in the Cox regression model was tested using the Schoenfeld residuals method and was not violated. Potential nonlinear associations were also assessed by using restricted cubic splines with three knots (5%, 50%, and 95%). Multiple imputation by chained equations was used to impute missing values in the covariates using “MICE” package in R.

Causal mediation analysis was conducted via “CMAverse” package to evaluate to which extent the association between plasma FAs and severe NAFLD was mediated by body composition measures related to obesity and metabolic health, including WC, BMI, BRI, FMI, FFMI, TFMI, LFMI, and AFMI.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, age (< 60 and ≥ 60 years), BMI (< 25 and ≥ 25 kg/m²), WC (< 102 cm and ≥ 102 cm for men; <88 cm and ≥ 88 cm for women), body fat percentage (< 25% and ≥ 25% for men; <36% and ≥ 36% for women), MET (< 150 and ≥ 150 h/week), and sedentary time (< 4 and ≥ 4 h/day). HRs and 95% CIs were estimated for log-transformed and standard deviation increases in FAs within these subgroups. Multiplicative interaction terms were used to evaluate interactions between FA levels and subgroup variables in relation to severe NAFLD, as well as between FA levels and the genetic risk score for NAFLD.

Four sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the study findings. First, participants with baseline CVD or T2D were excluded to determine whether the associations between FAs and severe NAFLD were independent of these prevalent cardiometabolic conditions. Second, a WL-PDFF ≥ 5% was used to capture additional NAFLD cases that might not have been clinically diagnosed, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the disease. Third, multivariate logistic regression was utilized to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for various baseline definitions of fatty liver disease (MAFLD, MAFLD-O, MAFLD-T2D, MAFLD-lean, MASLD, and MetALD) in relation to quintiles of plasma FAs, with each FA analyzed in a separate model. Furthermore, to address the issue of multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. Fourth, mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the mediating effects of VAT, ASAT, and trunk fat volumes derived from abdominal MRI on the association between plasma FAs and NAFLD cases (combination of severe NAFLD and WL-PDFF ≥ 5% cases). All analyses were performed by R Studio version 4.3.2. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Among the 107,593 participants, the mean (SD) age was 56.5 (8.09) years, 54.5% were women, and the mean (SD) BMI was 27.4 (4.75) kg/m2. The characteristics of participants according to severe NAFLD status are shown in Table 1. In brief, participants who developed severe NAFLD had higher deprivation scores, lower education attainment, and exhibited more unhealthy lifestyle habits, including lower physical activity, higher sedentary time, and current smoking. These participants also had higher BMI, body fat measures, higher processed and red meat intake, and lower plasma PUFA but higher MUFA and SFA levels.

PUFA levels inversely correlated with SFA (r = − 0.69, P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2) and MUFA levels (r = − 0.85, P < 0.001). Additionally, n-6 PUFAs showed stronger negative correlations with BMI, WC, FMI, and FFMI (r = − 0.21 to − 0.33, all P < 0.001) compared to n-3 PUFAs (r = − 0.06 to − 0.24, all P < 0.001), while MUFA and SFA showed a positive correlation with these body metrics (r = 0.13 to 0.38, all P < 0.001). Food composition also varied with FA types, with n-6 PUFA positively correlated with bread and cereal intake (r = 0.01 to 0.03, all P < 0.001), n-3 PUFA with fish intake (r = 0.33), SFA with red meat and cheese intake (r = 0.13 to 0.38, all P < 0.001), and MUFA with processed meat intake (r = 0.06).

During a mean follow-up period of 13.1 (SD 2.05) years, 1,484 severe NAFLD cases were identified. Cox regression analysis revealed that higher plasma levels of LA, DHA, n-6 PUFA, n-3 PUFA, and total PUFA were associated with a lower incidence of severe NAFLD (HRs range: 0.66–0.88, all P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Conversely, SFA and MUFA were positively associated with severe NAFLD risk (HRs range: 1.40–1.55, all P < 0.001). Most of these results remained robust after adjusting for other FAs in Model 3. Specifically, n-6 PUFA was associated with a 23% lower risk (HR: 0.77, 95% CI: 0.69–0.86), and PUFA with a 24% lower incidence of severe NAFLD (HR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.68–0.86). Conversely, SFA was associated with a 20% (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.32) and MUFA with a 28% (HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.12–1.46) higher risk. However, the association of DHA and n-3 PUFA with severe NAFLD was attenuated. Restricted cubic spline also detected a slight U-shape curve between total PUFA and severe NAFLD (PNonlinear < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Mediation analysis revealed that all body composition metrics were mediators linking FAs with severe NAFLD (Fig. 2). Notably, markers of central obesity, including WC and BRI, mediated approximately 30.0% of the inverse association between PUFA and severe NAFLD, and 21.2% of SFA’s positive association, and 36.5% of MUFA’s positive association with severe NAFLD. LFMI played slightly greater mediating roles than AFMI, mediating approximately 19.8% of the PUFA-NAFLD association, 12.6% for SFA, and 24.0% for MUFA. Furthermore, FFMI also mediated 17.3% of PUFA’s, 13.7% of SFA’s, and 19.8% of MUFA’s association with severe NAFLD.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study of 107,593 participants followed for an average of 13.1 years showed that plasma PUFA levels were inversely, while SFA and MUFA levels were positively associated with the incidence of severe NAFLD. Similar results were observed in cross-sectional associations of FAs with MAFLD and MASLD. Mediation analyses further revealed that all body composition indices played a significant role in mediating the association between plasma FAs and severe NAFLD, with markers of central obesity, including WC and BRI, identified as the most contributable mediators.

The findings of this study aligned with several cross-sectional studies and a prospective UKB study, which identified similar associations between FAs and NAFLD in Western populations [2, 7–9] and further explored how body composition influences this association. Previous studies considered the adjustment of BMI, a proxy of obesity, in the association between FAs and NAFLD [2, 7]. However, BMI itself cannot adequately reflect either body fat distribution or fat-free mass [29]. It has become widely recognized that the association between obesity and metabolic diseases is influenced more by the distribution of body fat than by overall fat mass [30]. For instance, VAT, reflected by WC and BRI, is more sensitive to lipolysis than subcutaneous and peripheral fats and is strongly linked to an increased risk of insulin resistance and metabolic diseases [31, 32]. Furthermore, VAT lipolysis drains free fatty acids (FFA) directly through the portal circulation to the liver, which may elevate hepatic denovo lipogenesis (DNL), leading to increased hepatic fat storage [33]. On the other hand, adipocytes from leg adipose tissues are more sensitive to the suppression of lipolysis and more resistant to lipolytic stimuli than VAT [34]. This study provided a more comprehensive examination of how body fat distribution, including upper and lower areas of the body and fat-free mass, which have different metabolic implications, mediate the relationship between plasma FAs and severe NAFLD.

In this study, SFA and MUFA were positively correlated with all body composition indices, especially with WC and BRI. WC and BRI mediated 19–21% of the association between SFA and severe NAFLD, and 33–36% of the association between MUFA and severe NAFLD. Sensitivity analysis in participants with abdominal MRI data yielded consistent results, with VAT mediating 53% of the association between SFA and NAFLD events and 28% of the association for MUFA. Similarly, ASAT mediated 30% of SFA’s association and 13% of MUFA’s association with NAFLD events. SFA levels may contribute to intrahepatic fat storage by enhancing liver DNL via activation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) [35]. The palmitoleic and oleic acids produced from DNL are the most abundant FAs present in triacylglycerides (TAGs), phospholipids, and cholesterol esters [33], which may further explain why elevated plasma levels of MUFA were associated with a higher incidence of severe NAFLD. SFA is also positively associated with increased 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}-hsd1 gene and protein expression, a regulator of cortisol activity, which may lead to greater VAT accumulation [11]. Intervention studies suggest diets enriched with MUFA may have beneficial effects on preventing central fat distribution and obesity prevention [36–38]. However, previous studies have also noted that circulating MUFA levels may not reliably reflect dietary intake but are instead affected to a greater extent by factors relating to endogenous lipogenesis, such as SFA consumption and desaturase activity [39]. Furthermore, in the prospective UKB study by Zhuang et al. [2], plasma MUFA was inversely associated with serum HDL-cholesterol levels, and HDL-cholesterol further mediated 32.6% of the positive association between MUFA and severe NAFLD in that study, indicating that higher plasma MUFA levels may have a promoting effect on dyslipidemia, but further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms.

All PUFAs were inversely correlated with central fat indices and other body composition indices. This aligns with previous observational studies showing that elevated circulation of n-3 PUFA is associated with lower liver fat accumulation and improved lipid profiles, such as elevated HDL-cholesterol and lowered LDL-cholesterol and TAG levels in individuals with NAFLD [2, 40–43]. Moreover, a randomized controlled trial in individuals with abdominal obesity, defined as WC >88 cm (for women) or >102 cm (for men), also found that compared to SFA intake, higher n-6 PUFA intake reduced liver fat measured by MRI and modestly improved metabolic status without weight loss [44]. Findings from experimental studies further indicated that n-6 and n-3 PUFAs can suppress SREBP-1c activity and activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α (PPARα), facilitating plasma TAG clearance and improving hepatic lipid metabolism [44]. Interestingly, observational studies showed that plant-derived LA and ALA may have a stronger impact on body fat and lean fat mass compared to marine-derived EPA and DHA [45], which supports the findings in this study where n-6 PUFA is more inversely correlated to FMI (r = − 0.29) than n-3 PUFA (r = − 0.06). Furthermore, n-6 PUFA were inversely associated with FFMI (r = − 0.23) in this study, and 18–19% of the association between LA or n-6 PUFA and severe NAFLD was mediated by lowered FFMI. Given that previous studies have also shown that weight loss is often accompanied by a reduction in lean body mass [46], the inverse association observed between n-6 PUFA and FFMI in the present study may be explained by the inverse correlation between n-6 PUFA and BMI/FMI. However, more research is needed to further explore the effect of different PUFAs on body composition indices.

GRS-based stratification did not reveal any significant interaction between FA profiles and genetic predisposition to NAFLD, indicating that the observed associations between FA levels and NAFLD in this study persisted regardless of genetic susceptibility as quantified by the GRS. This finding underscores the potential for broad applicable interventions targeting FA metabolism to reduce NAFLD risk across diverse genetic backgrounds. Furthermore, the mediating role of WC and BRI, explaining 19% to 36% of the association between FA levels and NAFLD, suggests that interventions targeted at reducing central fat accumulation could modulate the associations between FAs and NAFLD risk. Evidence from a randomized controlled trial showed that abdominal endurance exercise in men led to greater reductions in trunk fat compared to treadmill running, implying that regional fat loss through targeted exercise is feasible [47]. These findings collectively raise the possibility that localized endurance training, when combined with PUFA supplementation, may offer an enhanced approach to NAFLD prevention. Certainly, future interventional studies are warranted to explore this potentially synergistic strategy.

The present study has several strengths besides utilizing data from a large, nationally representative cohort and its prospective design. We carefully controlled for various potential confounding factors, thereby minimizing residual confounding effects. The study investigated associations with different subtypes of FAs and conducted several sensitivity analyses to enhance the understanding of the relationship between FAs and severe NAFLD while ensuring the robustness of the findings. The association of FAs was tested on multiple definitions of fatty liver diseases, providing compelling evidence of their impact on fatty liver diseases with different etiologies. Further, this study also explored the mediation effect of multiple body fat distribution, including trunk and limb fat mass, and fat-free mass index, thereby providing a wholesome understanding of how body composition interacts with FA’s relationship to severe NAFLD. Additionally, mediation effects of abdominal fat measures such as VAT and ASAT obtained from MRI scans were included in the sensitivity analyses to offer a more accurate representation of central obesity and its role in the association between FA and NAFLD. This study also has several limitations. First, all plasma FA and body composition measures were conducted once at baseline. The inability to assess changes in these measures over time hindered the understanding of how these factors might influence the development and progression of severe NAFLD. Second, the dietary questionnaire was self-reported and only assessed the frequency of intake of major food groups, which might not accurately capture the detailed nutritional quality or specific quantities of food consumed. Third, severe NAFLD cases were primarily identified through hospital admission records and death registries, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence of NAFLD. However, we also adopted two definitions, MAFLD and MASLD, in our sensitivity analysis. The prevalence of MAFLD was 37%, while the prevalence of MASLD, calculated by combining MASLD and its subtype MetALD, was approximately 37%, which is more comparable to the regional prevalence of NAFLD in Europe. Lastly, the UKB population predominantly consisted of well-educated white British individuals who belonged to higher socioeconomic status groups, which might restrict the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations.

Conclusion

This study underscored the intricate relationship between FAs and the risk of developing severe NAFLD, particularly focusing on the mediating influence of body composition. Measures associated with central obesity, such as WC and BRI, demonstrated relatively stronger mediation effects than the other body compositions, such as TFMI, AFMI, LFMI, and FFMI. Furthermore, the analysis confirmed the link between FAs and NAFLD across various definitions of fatty liver disease, including MAFLD and MASLD. Understanding these underlying mechanisms is crucial for developing effective lifestyle strategies aimed at reducing NAFLD risk. Public health efforts emphasizing targeted fat reduction, such as localized endurance exercise combined with PUFA-rich dietary interventions, may offer a promising and scalable approach to NAFLD prevention. Further research is essential to explore these associations in-depth and evaluate their potential clinical and population-level applications.
