Perivascular space, brain functional connectivity and sleep: a healthy aging population study
Authors: Nien-Chu Shih, Joey A. Contreras, Wendy J. Mack, Jeiran Choupan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perivascular space (PVS) surrounds cortical perforating vessels as part of the brain clearance system. Sleep affects both brain clearance and functional connectivity (FC), but impacts of PVS on FC remains unclear. We utilized T1-W and resting state-fMRI data, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and NIH cognitive tests from 512 health aging (HCP-Aging). Basal ganglia (BG)-PVS was positively correlated with FC in the right anterior medial temporal gyrus (aMTG) and right temporal regions, while centrum-semiovale (CSO)-PVS was positively correlated with FC in the left hippocampus and right frontal regions. In early middle-aged, increased CSO-PVS showed higher hippocampal FC and better cognition. Individuals with longer time spent in bed had larger BG-PVS linked to higher FC in the right aMTG. Additionally, older adults with better sleep quality had larger BG-PVS linked to higher FC in the right aMTG. This suggests that PVS morphology may reflect changes in neural connections involved in memory-related regions. This study is based on the same cohort as our previous work; however, it extends the investigation by incorporating functional connectivity analyses to provide novel insights beyond regional activity.

Introduction

The brain clearance system is a highly specialized fluid transport systems that facilitate the exchange of interstitial fluid and regulates cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics within the brain1. Dysfunction of the brain clearance system has been associated with decline in cognitive function and degeneration of nerve cells2. Previous studies have shown that the brain clearance system may play a role in the development and progression of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease1.

Perivascular spaces (PVS), an important segment of the brain clearance system, are CSF-filled spaces surrounding small blood vessels in the brain and play a crucial role in brain clearance of amyloid-β and other metabolic waste3,4. Enlarged PVS are common in the elderly, and advanced age itself is considered a significant risk factor for enlargement of PVS. Some studies have linked the presence of enlarged PVS to an increased risk of cognitive decline, dementia, and cerebral small vessel disease1. Previous research suggests that waste clearance occurs most readily during deep sleep, as evidenced by the movement of molecular tracers from the cisterna magna CSF, along periarterial spaces, and through brain parenchyma5. Our team has made attempts to investigate PVS in cognitive decline and the link between PVS and sleep. Among all the results, we notably observed ra eduction in PVS volume fraction, measured by structural MRI, in the anterior superior medial temporal gyrus (asMTG) of individuals with mild cognitive impairment when compared to cognitively normal aging controls6. In a separate study, to our surprise, we found that healthy older adults who had better sleep quality had larger PVS volume fraction7.

In addition, early amyloid-β deposition has been associated with reduced or aberrant connectivity within the Default Mode Network (DMN) in cognitively healthy individuals8,9. These findings suggest that dysfunction of the brain’s clearance system may not only affect local metabolism but may also further alter network synchronization across brain regions. The mechanism between PVS and functional connectivity (FC) is still unclear. However, the link between PVS and FC may contribute to the integration of vascular and neural processes that are crucial for cognitive function. At the vascular level, an enlarged PVS is often considered an indicator of impaired microvascular function and decreased vessel wall compliance, which may disrupt neurovascular coupling10. Previous research found that vasomotion regulates local cerebral blood flow (CBF) and cerebral blood volume (CBV) by the smooth muscle in the vessel walls, thereby influencing the magnitude and dynamics of the Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) signals and the synchronization10,11. When the PVS is enlarged or dysfunctional, it may change the mechanical properties of peripheral blood vessels12, reduce the ability of blood vessels to respond to neural demands, and lead to an imbalance in neurovascular coupling. Such alterations in neurovascular regulation may extend beyond local regions and influence distal connectivity through cortical–subcortical pathways or white matter tracts13,14. Association between impaired vasomotion patterns and cross-network dysfunction has been observed in conditions such as AD, stroke, and hypertension15–17. Therefore, PVS is not simply a manifestation of static structural abnormalities but may represent a dynamic imbalance of blood vessels that affects functional connectivity. To better understand the role of the brain’s clearance system, this study aims to investigate how poor sleep and structural changes in PVS influence brain functional neuronal activity.

In aging and neurodegenerative diseases like AD, disruptions in sleep are often accompanied by changes in regional brain activity within the DMN18, particularly reduced BOLD signal amplitude in frontal regions, assessed by analyzing fMRI data. The DMN is involved in self-referential thinking, memory consolidation, and internal cognition, usually active at rest and inactive at work19. fMRI utilizes the BOLD signal as an indirect measure of neuronal activity11,20. The BOLD signal detects changes in the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated hemoglobin in the blood11. When neuronal activity leads to increased metabolic demand, it promotes a localized increase in CBF that overcompensates for oxygen consumption, resulting in a relative decrease in deoxygenated hemoglobin and a corresponding BOLD signal change20.

Furthermore, long-term sleep deprivation has been shown to decrease DMN activity and connectivity, with poor sleep quality similarly associated with weaker DMN function21,22. Notably, previous studies have indicated that one night of sleep deprivation upregulates connectivity in the DMN and impairs the frontal–parietal functional network23,24. Moreover, sleep disorders such as insomnia and daytime sleepiness result in a reduction and disturbance of DMN functional connectivity25,26. These findings highlight that sleep behavior may influence cognitive performance by altering brain connectivity patterns and that sleep quality and patterns play an integral role in modulating brain activity.

Despite findings on how sleep affects both brain clearance systems and DMN function, the link between sleep behavior, PVS, and FC in healthy individuals remains understudied. The present analysis builds upon an existing dataset previously examined at the regional level. While the cohort remains the same7, the current study adopts a different analytical focus, shifting toward functional connectivity to explore broader network dynamics. As this extension was motivated by emerging questions rather than predefined hypotheses, the findings should be considered exploratory in nature.

Results

Clinical and imaging characteristics of study participants

Participant demographic and cognitive data are summarized in Table 1. In total, 252 early middle-aged participants (97 men and 155 women; age range 36–54 years), 111 middle-aged participants (50 men and 61 women; age range 55–65 years), and 150 older participants (68 men and 82 women; age range above 65 years) were included in this study. There were no significant differences in sex, BMI, years of education, or general cognitive performances (NIH total cognition composite score, fluid cognition composite score, crystallized cognition composite score) among the three groups (all p > 0.3). Compared with early middle-aged and middle-aged participants, older participants performed significantly worse on MoCA (p < 0.001) and NIH working memory tests (p < 0.001). Older participants had significantly larger BG-PVS volume (p < 0.001) and CSO-PVS volume (p < 0.001) than early middle-aged and middle-aged participants. In contrast, early middle-aged participants had significantly larger BG volume (p < 0.0001) and CSO volume (p < 0.0001) than middle-aged and older participants. Compared with early middle-aged and middle-aged participants, older participants had longer PSIQ self-rated sleep duration (p = 0.045), and a lower score for sleep medication (p = 0.004). The relationship between sleep and FC

Our next objective was to investigate whether sleep was associated with PVS-related FCs. These PVS-related FCs and PSQI sleep components did not show any significant correlation. (Table 4). To further investigate the effect of sleep quality on these PVS-related FCs, we grouped the participants based on the total PSQI score into a poor sleep group (score > 5) and a good sleep group (score <= 5). Right aMTG FC did not differ significantly between the two sleep groups (p = 0.76). As well, left hippocampus FC did not differ significantly between poor and good sleep groups (Supplementary Fig. 2A, p = 0.93). Age-related relationship among PVS, FC, and sleep

Despite the lack of a statistically significant correlation between sleep and FC in the overall sample in this study, we further explored whether these associations differed over age groups. The correlation between PVS and sleep parameters varied across different age groups according to our previous research7, and we found a similar pattern in this study (Supplementary Table 1). Although these observations stem from the same cohort, they are not presented as an independent replication. Rather, our intention was to assess the robustness of the previously observed PVS–sleep relationship under a refined age stratification, in which the original middle-aged group was separated into early and late middle-aged groups. This consistency indicates that the age-related differences in the PVS–sleep relationship are not merely a consequence of the original grouping method. In the early middle-aged group, mediation analysis revealed a significant mediation effect of time in bed on right aMTG FC through BG-PVS volume fraction (Fig. 3c, average causal mediation effect (95% CI) = 0.0011 (0.000089, 0.00), p = 0.027). In addition, there was a marginally significant mediation effect of time in bed on hippocampus FC through CSO-PVS volume fraction (Fig. 3d, average causal mediation effect (95% CI) = 0.0006 (−1.19e−05, 0.00), p = 0.058). Furthermore, in the older group, the result indicated a significant mediation effect of sleep quality scores on right aMTG FC through BG-PVS volume fraction (Fig. 3e, average causal mediation effect (95% CI) = −0.0109 (−0.025, 0.00), p = 0.009).

Discussion

This study is the first to explore the relationship between PVS and FC. Our results indicate that people who have larger PVS in the BG showed higher FC between the right aMTG and right temporal pole and insular cortex. People who had larger CSO-PVS showed higher FC between the left hippocampus and the right frontal regions: right pars triangularis, right pars opercularis, and right frontal operculum cortex. In addition, the relationship between PVS, FC, and cognition varied across the different age groups. In early middle-aged individuals (age: 36-55 years old), the connection between PVS and MoCA scores is mediated by PVS associations on FC of the hippocampus. Early middle-aged individuals who had larger CSO-PVS volume fraction showed higher hippocampus FC, which was associated with better cognitive performance. However, we did not find any correlation between these FC and cognition in the middle-aged group (age: 55–65 years old) and the older group (age above 55 years old).

While the details of mechanisms linking PVS and FC require further clarification, our results suggest that they may jointly reflect an integrated regulatory process between the vascular and nervous systems. The enlargement of PVS may not only be a passive indicator of microvascular burden but may also be accompanied by adaptive changes at the neural network level to maintain efficient information transmission under vascular or metabolic stress. In other words, the PVS-FC association may be viewed as a vascular-neural co-regulatory phenomenon, reflecting the dynamic network reorganization of the brain to maintain cognitive stability under early vascular stress. This view is consistent with recent literature suggesting that the decline in vascular and cerebral glymphatic function during midlife triggers compensatory neural network reorganization28–30.

In addition to vascular mechanisms, PVS is also an important channel for the circulation of the brain's glymphatic system and CSF. Its functional changes may affect metabolic homeostasis and neural network activity in the brain. A previous study reported that synchronized neuronal oscillations, particularly during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, Slow-wave activity promotes the exchange of CSF with PVS, enhancing the clearance of waste, including amyloid-β proteins31,32. However, when PVS enlarges due to impaired clearance or fluid accumulation, it may reflect an early manifestation of brain glymphatic dysfunction and neuroinflammation13,33.

In this study, although no significant direct interaction between sleep quality and the PVS–FC relationship was observed in the overall sample, stratified analyses suggested that sleep may play a differential role across life stages. Our results indicated that early middle-aged people with longer time in bed had a larger BG-PVS volume fraction that was associated with higher levels of functional connectivity on the right aMTG. On the other hand, older people with better sleep quality had a larger BG-PVS volume fraction that was associated with higher levels of FC on the right aMTG. These findings potentially indicate that changes in fluid dynamics or lymphatic efficiency are affecting network integration. However, we did not find any correlation between PVS and sleep in the middle-aged group, suggesting that sleep-related effects may gradually diminish with declining neuroplasticity and vascular regulation. These results suggest that despite the enlarged PVS being associated with pathology1, PVS structural changes may not simply represent pathology, but may also be a compensatory response to changes in brain fluid dynamics. Its impact on FC may therefore show different directions at different age stages.

Furthermore, although there is no direct evidence that increased CSF is associated with enhanced FC in the brain, some hypotheses support this possibility: (1) Increased CSF flow can enhance waste clearance in the brain 34, reduce neuroinflammation, and promote better functional connection, as efficient waste removal during periods of high metabolic activity is critical to maintaining a clean and functional neural environment; (2) CSF plays a key role in distributing nutrients to different brain regions 35, and enhanced CSF dynamics can Improve nutrient delivery and support synaptic activity and communication of neural circuits, thereby enhancing functional connectivity; (3) Some studies have shown that CSF dynamics may indirectly influence important brain areas related to memory, visual construction, verbal fluency, and cognition, by maintaining metabolic homeostasis and ISF balance within the brain. Changes in CSF circulation may alter FC patterns in these regions, particularly when glymphatic clearance or inflammatory responses are disrupted 36,37. Taken together, enlargement of PVS may affect the brain’s FC and overall cognitive health through multiple mechanisms.

Our study also showed that the association between PVS and FC was not widespread throughout the brain but was primarily concentrated in core integration regions involved in language and memory processing, such as the anterior medial temporal gyrus (aMTG), temporal pole, hippocampus, and inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) 38–41. In our results, we found that individuals with larger BG-PVS showed higher FC in the right aMTG and a cluster in right temporal regions (temporal pole and insula cortex), while individuals with larger CSO-PVS showed higher FC between left hippocampus and right frontal region (right pars triangularis, right pars opercularis. These regions are located at the intersection of the DMN and the semantic network, possessing a high degree of cross-domain integration, and are considered key hubs for maintaining high-level cognition, such as semantic retrieval, memory integration, and internal thought42,43. Previous studies have also shown that the MTG and temporal pole are connected via the uncinate fasciculus44, and that functional connectivity between the hippocampus and IFG regions is often associated with the integration of memory and language39. Therefore, the observed higher PVS burden was associated with higher FC in these regions in this study, which may not simply reflect local metabolic load but rather functional compensatory mechanisms activated by the brain to maintain cognition.

One study showed that healthy older adults with higher BG-PVS burden showed higher connectivity coupling between the retrosplenial temporal network and the cingulo-parietal network compared to those without higher PVS burden37. This suggests that even in the presence of microvascular changes, the brain may maintain cognitive stability by strengthening connectivity between key networks. However, this compensatory effect appears to be age-limited. In our study, we found that this FC was primarily present in early middle-aged individuals (ages 36–55) and gradually diminished in late middle age and later life. This observation supports a “compensation-to-decline” transition model: in early middle age, neural networks still possess plasticity to reallocate resources and increase functional connectivity45,46. Therefore, the PVS may trigger moderate network reorganization to maintain language and memory function.

However, this mechanism may no longer play a protective role with increasing age. Another longitudinal study showed that higher PVS burden in the BG was positively associated with declines in language, information processing, executive functions, and contextual memory47, suggesting that when PVS further increases, it may no longer be an adaptive change, but rather reflect chronic microvascular damage and network degeneration. In summary, the interaction between PVS and FC may have different functional implications at different life stages; it may represent compensatory adjustments in early middle age, while in later life it may indicate neural network degeneration or functional imbalance.

Notably, this study observed significant associations with PVS–FC only for MoCA scores, not for other single cognitive measures. This phenomenon may be related to the MoCA’s global sensitivity. The MoCA covers multiple cognitive domains, including attention, executive function, language, memory, and visuospatial ability, and has been shown to be more capable of detecting early or network-level cognitive changes48. In contrast, other subtests often target single functional modules and may not reflect the global neural network modulation involved in PVS and functional connectivity changes. Furthermore, the PVS–FC associations observed in this study primarily involved language- and memory-related regions (such as the aMTG and hippocampus), which have been shown to be involved in cross-domain semantic integration and memory retrieval14.

This study still needs to note some limitations. First, although the current analysis builds on an existing dataset, it was not preregistered for age-specific stratification, and the age group subdivision was finalized post-hoc. As such, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in independent cohorts with predefined analytic plans. Second, the fMRI ROIs are located in gray matter, further away from the studied PVS presentation regions. The integration of interpretations between white matter structural changes and gray matter neural activity is a major challenge of this study, and further tracer studies are required. Additionally, the relationship between PVS and FC was only observed at one time point. Moreover, although FC was assessed at only one time point, it should also be acknowledged that functional connectivity can be acutely modulated by recent sleep history. Prior research has shown that insufficient sleep or short-term fluctuations in sleep quality can alter large-scale network organization and resting-state connectivity patterns49,50. Although general sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI, acute sleep parameters such as sleep duration or sleep disruption immediately prior to the scan were not systematically captured. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether part of the observed FC variability may reflect transient sleep-related influences rather than stable neural characteristics. Therefore, future research needs to plan longitudinal studies to accurately investigate the long-term impact of alterations in PVS on FC changes. Although subjective sleep measures such as the PSQI provide valuable information about an individual’s perceived sleep quality, they do not directly correspond to objective physiological indices such as slow-wave activity or deep sleep architecture. Thus, the sleep–PVS–FC relationships observed here should be interpreted within the context of subjective sleep experience. The two PSQI components associated with greater PVS burden and higher FC, sleep quality, and time in bed, reflect different aspects of sleep behavior. Self-reported sleep quality may capture perceptions of restfulness and stress linked to vascular and glymphatic regulation, whereas longer time in bed may reflect lifestyle patterns such as lower daytime activity or compensatory rest. These distinctions suggest that subjective sleep measures may relate to PVS burden and FC through behavioral or psychological pathways rather than specific sleep-stage mechanisms not assessed in this study. Future work combining subjective and objective sleep metrics will help clarify how multiple dimensions of sleep influence PVS structure and functional connectivity. Furthermore, the majority of the participants in the study were white or African American, so these findings might not apply to other populations.

In conclusion, our results demonstrated that BG-PVS volume fraction was positively associated with FC of the right aMTG and a cluster in temporal regions; CSO-PVS volume fraction was positively associated with FC of the left hippocampus and a cluster in frontal IFG regions. In addition, FC in the studied seed regions plays a critical role in mediating the relationship between PVS and cognitive ability. Furthermore, despite sleep not directly affecting the studied FCs, PVS in BG and CSO mediated the interaction between time in bed and FCs in the early middle-aged population; while in the older population, only BG-PVS mediated the interaction between sleep quality and right aMTG FCs. This study could be used as a foundation for future research relating to PVS, FC, and sleep behavior. The findings of this study provide a greater understanding of how PVS alternation influences brain FC in older, cognitively healthy individuals, and open a new area of PVS research (structure-to-function) to investigation.

Methods

Participants

Structural MRI and resting state functional MRI (fMRI) data from 512 cognitively healthy individuals aged 36-100 years from the HCP-Aging Lifespan Release 2.027 were examined. Participants had no diagnosed neurological or major psychiatric disorders at recruitment. In this study, for each participant we used their information on age, sex, years of education, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire, cognitive performance measured by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), NIH toolbox working memory tests, and NIH toolbox cognition battery (total cognition composite score, total fluid cognition composite score, and crystallized cognition composite score).

While the dataset excluded individuals with diagnosed conditions, it did not exclude based on sleep behavior. MoCA scores between 20 and 30 were classified as healthy aging, despite scores below 25 typically reflecting mild cognitive impairment. All exclusion criteria for the current analyses on HCP-Aging data are following our previous study7. We further excluded one participant who had missing fMRI data. Therefore, 512 participants were included in the analyses.

We categorized participants into early middle-aged (36–55 years old, N = 252), middle-aged adults (56–65 years old, N = 111), and older adults (above 65 years old, N = 149), based on the age range commonly used in previous lifespan research51–54. This was primarily intended to more accurately capture the nonlinear trends in vascular and functional changes during aging. This grouping was established prior to statistical analysis based on research findings on changes in cerebral vascular compliance and cerebral glymphatic efficiency in the mid-to-late 50s28,55. Therefore, this subdivision was intended to capture potential age-related variations more precisely in network dynamics rather than to generate effects. The demographic information of the participants is shown in Table 1.

MRI Acquisition and pre-processing

All participants were scanned using a customized Siemens 3 T Prisma scanner housed at Washington University in St. Louis, using a standard 32-channel Siemens receive head coil. The T1-weighted image was acquired with repetition time (TR)/inversion time (TI) = 2500/1000 ms, time to echo (TE) = 1.8/3.6/5.4/7.2 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 240 × 166 mm, and the T2-weighted image with TR = 3200 ms, TE = 564 ms, FOV = 256 × 240 × 166 mm. Structural T1-weighted MPRAGE and T2-weighted SPACE images were preprocessed in the LONI pipeline using the HCP minimal processing pipeline version 4.0.1 and FreeSurfer version 6. The preprocessing included gradient nonlinearity corrections, registering structural images together, aligning to native space anterior commissure-posterior commissure, and registering to Montreal Neurological Institute space (MNI space) using FSL’s FNIRT. Native space images were used to generate individual regional subcortical PVS features for white and pial surfaces with FreeSurfer. Detailed preprocessing methods are described in a prior publication56.

Resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) scans were acquired with a 2D multiband (MB) gradient-recalled echo (GRE) echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (MB8, TR/TE = 800/37 ms, flip angle = 52°) and 2.0 mm isotropic voxels covering the whole brain (72 oblique-axial slices). Functional scans were acquired in pairs of two runs, with opposite phase encoding polarity so that the fMRI data in aggregate is not biased toward a particular phase encoding polarity. The HCP-A fMRI data used in this study were anterior-to-posterior (AP) and posterior-to-anterior (PA) phase encoding directions and were averaged through the default processing pipeline via CONN toolbox57 (release 20.b).

Participants acquired a total 26 min of resting state scans of four runs of 6.5 minutes each. During rs-fMRI scanning, participants view a small white fixation crosshair on a black background. Participants were instructed to stay still, stay awake, and blink normally while looking at the fixation crosshair.

PVS segmentation

Nowadays, MRI-visible PVS (size: 3 mm in diameter) can be detected in healthy individuals and does not necessarily indicate pathological enlargement. Several neuroimaging studies have shown that the amount of PVS is higher in the elderly and increases with age1. Higher PVS burden is more visible on the basal ganglia (BG) and centrum semiovale (CSO) of MRI, we chose these two areas as our PVS regions of interest (ROIs). The BG and CSO were segmented by FreeSurfer using an atlas-based approach, and brain volume and white matter masks were derived from the Desikan-Killiany atlas. PVS segmentation was performed as explained in Sepehrband et al58. In brief, T1w and T2w images were adaptively filtered to remove the high-frequency noise and then co-registered and combined to obtain enhanced PVS contrast (EPC). EPC is shown to provide superior visibility of PVS compared with T1w or T2w alone (Supplementary Fig. 1)58. PVSs were segmented from EPC images by applying the Frangi filter using Quantitative Imaging Toolkit (QIT)59 and a vesselness threshold of 1.558, which was optimized for the HCP data. To verify the accuracy of PVS segmentation, PVS segmentation quality control was performed by four trained analysts. Detailed quality control methods and evaluation criteria are described in a prior publication58.

Functional MRI preprocessing, denoising, and analysis

All functional imaging data were processed with the CONN toolbox57. Functional and anatomical data were preprocessed via SPM60 (release 12.12.6) using a preprocessing pipeline61 including realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, slice timing correction, direct MNI-space normalization, and smoothing. The functional data were smoothed using spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full-width half-maximum (FWHM). Subsequently, functional data were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline62, which applies linear regression and bandpass frequency filtering to remove the effects of unwanted motion and other artifacts from the BOLD signal.

We conducted seed-to-voxel analysis to examine the association between PVS volume fraction and the whole-brain FC of brain regions involved in DMN, bilateral anterior medial temporal gyrus (aMTG), bilateral parahippocampal gyrus, and bilateral hippocampus. For the selection of seeds of functional connectivity, besides default mode network regions (medial prefrontal cortex, bilateral paracentral lobule, posterior cingulate cortex), we also examined the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus, which are related to memory consolidation during sleep41. In our prior research, we observed a notable reduction in PVS volume fraction in the asMTG of individuals with MCI compared to cognitively normal aging cohorts6, therefore, we selected the aMTG as one of the regions of interest (ROI). In this study, FC was defined as the correlation coefficient between the time series of specific seed regions and voxel clusters throughout the brain, reflecting the synchronization of neural activity across regions.

ROI FC maps were regressed on PVS volume fraction in a general linear model, controlling for age and sex. Voxel-level threshold p < 0.001 and cluster-size p-FDR < 0.05 were used, which were adjusted by FDR for multiple comparison correction.

Sleep parameters

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a 19-item self-rated questionnaire used to assess subjective sleep quality over the past month63. Grouping all 19 items into 7 component scores, each component is scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep. Sleep efficiency is calculated as the total sleep time divided by the time spent in bed, resulting in a score of 0 for a proportion greater than 85%, 1 for 75–84%, 2 for 65–74%, and 3 for less than 65%. Sleep quality is subjectively rated as very good (0), fairly good (1), fairly poor (2), or very poor (3). Sleep duration was calculated by subtracting the time participants fell asleep from the time they woke up. The duration of sleep is scored as 0 for more than 7 hours, 1 for 6–7 hours, 2 for 5–6 hours, and 3 for less than 5 hours. Sleep latency, or the time taken to fall asleep, is scored as 0 for less than 15 minutes, 1 for 16–30 minutes, 2 for 31-60 minutes, and 3 for more than 60 minutes. Sleep medication use is evaluated based on frequency, with scores of 0 for no usage, 1 for usage less than once a week, 2 for once or twice weekly, and 3 for three or more times per week. Daytime dysfunction, which assesses the ability to stay awake and maintain enthusiasm, is scored as 0 for no problems, 1 for very slight problems, 2 for somewhat of a problem, and 3 for a very big problem. Time in bed was calculated by subtracting the participants’ usual bedtime from their usual wake-up time. The PSQI total score, ranging from 0 to 21, is the sum of the above components, with higher scores indicating worse overall sleep quality.

Statistics

We used the fraction of the ROI volume that is occupied by the PVS (referred to as volume fraction) as our measure to calculate the PVS burden in the BG and the CSO. Owing to the non-normal distribution of the PVS in the BG, the log-transformed value of the volume fraction of the PVS was used. The one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were used to compare sex, BMI, years of education, and cognitive performances between different age groups, and the results are shown in Table 1. FC values were extracted from the significant clusters obtained on the CONN toolbox results.

We aimed to test whether (1) FCs were associated with PVS volume fraction, (2) FC patterns that were significantly associated with PVS volume fraction in the main analyses were also correlated with global cognition and memory performance, and (3) the impact of sleep on PVS and these associated FCs. Therefore, correlation analyses were performed between FC values and (1) PVS volume fraction, (2) cognitive scores, and (3) sleep measurements in the whole sample and separately in early middle-aged, middle-aged, and older participants. Pearson Correlation was used for continuous variables, and Spearman Correlation was used for ordinal variables. Furthermore, to investigate if sleep impacts the relationships between PVS volume fraction and FC, we applied interaction testing on a regression model.

To further explore the indirect effects of PVS on cognitive performance mediated through FC or the indirect effects of sleep on FC mediated through PVS, we utilized the mediation package in R to construct a mediation model. A 95% confidence interval was reported to determine the significance of the indirect effects, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. All regression and mediation analyses controlled for age, sex, and years of education, and considered statistically significant at p-values < 0.05. FDR p-value was also performed for multiple comparison correction. In this study, multiple hypothesis tests were performed across PVS, FC, and sleep. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied at an alpha threshold of 0.05 to identify significant results while controlling for the proportion of false positives. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3).
