CHORDONOMICON: A Dataset of 666,000 Songs and their Chord Progressions
Spyridon Kantarelis, Ioannis Liolitsas, Konstantinos Thomas, Vassilis Lyberatos, Edmund Dervakos, Giorgos Stamou, Artificial Intelligence, Learning Systems Laboratory

Abstract
Chord progressions encapsulate important information about music, pertaining to its structure and conveyed emotions. They serve as the backbone of musical composition, and in many cases, they are the sole information required for a musician to play along and follow the music. Despite their importance, chord progressions as a data domain remain underexplored — existing datasets lack the scale, structural annotation, and metadata diversity required for rigorous evaluation of music understanding models. In this work, we present Chordonomicon , the largest dataset of its kind, containing over 666,000 song-level symbolic chord progressions, annotated with structural parts (verse, chorus, bridge, etc.), genre, and release date, created by scraping various sources of user-generated progressions and associated metadata, showing strong similarity to well-established prior datasets. Beyond the dataset itself, we propose a reproducible benchmark suite for next chord prediction, evaluating three sequence modeling architectures (RNN, GRU, LSTM) across multiple context window sizes and data scales under strict exact-match evaluation. Our experiments reveal that structural part annotations consistently improve prediction performance. Chordonomicon is released as an open benchmark, providing split methodology, baselines, and evaluation protocols to enable fair and reproducible comparison for future work on chord prediction, classification, generation, and beyond.

Introduction

Large, publicly available, well structured datasets have been crucial for technological progress in recent decades. They allow for wide-spread experimentation and serve as a common basis for comparison, reproducibility, and evaluation. In the domain of music, the development of such datasets has been extremely slow compared to other domains, hindered by issues such as copyrights, unavailability of audio, lack of symbolic representations, and inadequate metadata. In the era of large language models and text-driven technologies, chord progression datasets have become especially valuable, as they provide structured, text-based representations of music that are easy to process and integrate into modern AI workflows. Among various symbolic formats, chord progressions best capture the harmonic structure of a song—the foundation upon which melodies are constructed and a central element in musical composition. Dataset

Chordonomicon is a symbolic dataset containing exclusively chord progressions and associated metadata — no audio, lyrics, or song titles are included, as these constitute copyrightable material unsuitable for open research datasets. 2.1
Data Gathering and Curation

The
Chordonomicon
dataset was created by carefully collecting, processing, and standardizing data to ensure high-quality, uniform song-level chord progressions and metadata suitable for machine learning (see
1
). We leveraged web scraping techniques to extract song chord data from the Ultimate Guitar (UG) platform. By adhering to UG’s robots.txt, Terms of Service, and established web accessibility protocols (see
Ethics Statement
), we obtained the site’s XML sitemap and filtered the URLs containing the “
-chords-
” substring, which denotes song chord pages. This yielded a dataset of 1.4 million song chord pages, encompassing multiple interpretations (versions) per song. We then collected the raw content, user ratings, vote counts, artist IDs, song IDs, artist names, and song titles from each of these URLs. Utilizing the song and artist IDs, along with fuzzy string matching on the song and artist names, we consolidated this data into 793,362 unique songs from 93,872 unique artists. Duplicate songs were consolidated using Levenshtein distance-based fuzzy matching with a similarity threshold of 0.85, handling common user-generated inconsistencies such as missing articles, version annotations, and spelling errors (e.g., "Hotel California" vs. "The Hotel California"). For songs with multiple chord versions, we retained the best-rated version, determined by a weighted average of user rating and vote count percentage. The raw HTML files were parsed to extract full chord progressions for each song. To ensure consistency, consecutive identical chords were collapsed into a single token (e.g., ’A G G A’ → ’A G A’), as repetition carries no additional harmonic information. Songs with fewer than four chords were excluded as they lack sufficient harmonic content for meaningful analysis. All files were standardized to UTF-8 encoding, with any songs containing non-standard or non-Latin chord encodings (e.g., Cyrillic characters) removed to ensure uniform representation. All associated metadata were transformed into a unified format to eliminate cross-source ambiguities.

The remaining dataset underwent expert curation over a period of approximately three months by two specialists holding advanced degrees in Western tonal harmony. Western tonal music provides a well-established, theoretical framework defining all chords derivable from major, harmonic minor, and melodic minor scales, offering an unambiguous criterion for harmonic validity. For each chord, experts verified: (i) the presence of an identifiable tonic note (A through G), (ii) the correctness of any alterations (sharps and flats), and (iii) that the chord quality is consistent with Western tonal structures. Progressions containing notationally invalid symbols — such as entries lacking a tonic note entirely (e.g., "add#9"), numerals misused as chords (e.g., "12"), or locale-specific notations incompatible with standard Western notation (e.g., "fis") — were removed entirely. This process ensured that every chord in Chordonomicon is unambiguous, theoretically grounded, and convertible to standardized machine-readable formats. This rigorous curation process resulted in the removal of over 100,000 songs — approximately 13% of the consolidated dataset — yielding a final collection of 679,807 unique, fully curated song-level chord progressions, harmonically accurate, semantically rich, and convertible to standardized machine-readable formats.

All chord progressions are provided in the FHO syntax, with conversion scripts provided for the Harte chord syntax — the de facto standard for chord representation in MIR, widely adopted in prominent datasets such as the McGill Billboard corpus
Burgoyne et al. (2011)
. Crucially, the successful conversion of every chord in Chordonomicon to Harte syntax serves as an additional validation of dataset quality — every entry, including locale-specific and non-standard notations encountered during curation, can be unambiguously mapped to a standard Harte representation (e.g., "SOL5" → "G:(3)", "hmin" → "B:min"), formally confirming that the dataset contains exclusively well-formed, theoretically grounded harmonic content.

Structural part annotations are a defining feature of Chordonomicon and, as our experiments demonstrate, a critical factor in chord prediction performance. We employed eight distinct part categories:
Intro, Verse, Chorus, Bridge, Interlude, Solo, Instrumental
, and
Outro
, resolving approximately 3,300 unique user-generated part name variants to their canonical category (e.g., "Refrain" → "Chorus", "Verse 1" → "Verse"). Songs containing any unclassifiable part label were excluded from structural annotation, retaining only their chord progression — a conservative approach that ensures reliability: 397,580 songs (58.5% of the dataset) carry full structural annotations. Release dates and genre metadata were collected via the Spotify Web API, verified by matching track and artist names across both sources, with Spotify IDs provided where available.

2.3
Chords

A music chord is a group of notes played together. In Western music, chords are typically built on intervals of thirds (tertian harmony), with triads (three notes) forming the foundation and extended chords comprising four to seven notes. An exception is the power chord (two notes a fifth apart) commonly employed in rock and metal music. When the notes of a chord are reordered, placing a non-root note in the bass, the result is an inverted chord. Our dataset comprises 51,994,634 chord tokens, with 749 unique chord types and 1,464,392 inverted chord instances.

The chord distribution, illustrated in Figure
3
and closely mirroring the ground-truth reference distributions of established chord recognition datasets
Burgoyne et al. (2011)
, reveals a clear dominance of natural triads, particularly chords in the C and G major families. This is not a sampling artifact but a well-understood phenomenon musicologically. First, G, C, D, and A are open chord shapes on guitar, requiring minimal finger placement and making them the default vocabulary of beginner and intermediate players. Second, the widespread use of capos systematically shifts transcriptions toward guitar-friendly positions regardless of the song’s original tonality. Since Chordonomicon is sourced from a guitar-oriented platform, this distribution authentically reflects how popular music is actually performed and transcribed by guitarists worldwide, rather than how it is theoretically composed. Additionally, non-professional transcribers often simplify complex harmonies to their triad equivalents, contributing to the dominance of simple chord forms.

Next Chord Prediction Benchmark

Next chord prediction is a task where given an incomplete chord progression, we have to predict the next chord in the sequence. Formally, given a sequence of chords
(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{k})
we predict
P(y_{k}|(y_{1},\dots,y_{k-1})
, which means that this task is essentially language modeling. We focus on next chord prediction as a canonical and well-defined task that enables controlled evaluation across models and data scales. While the dataset supports a broader range of tasks (e.g., generation, classification, and structure prediction), we leave their systematic benchmarking to future work. The experimental framework was designed to assess how training set size, sequence length, chord representation, and structural information influence model performances. The code for replicating all benchmark experiments is publicly available at
https://github.com/spyroskantarelis/chordonomicon
.

3.1
Experimental Framework

For the chord representation, we used two approaches: a
single-token
representation, where each unique chord symbol (e.g., Cmaj7, Am, G/B) was treated as an atomic token, and a
three-tokens
representation, where each chord was decomposed into three components. More specifically the Root: the pitch class of the chord (e.g., C, Eb); Quality + Extensions: the chord type and added tones (e.g., maj7, min, sus4, add13); and Bass: the bass note when specified (e.g., the E in C/E), or N for none. For the structural information, we created three large subsets of our dataset:
Filtered dataset:
Contains all songs with at least one metadata field (genre or decade), with structural labels removed; suitable for evaluation on entire songs (472483 rows).
Labeled-song dataset:
Subset of the filtered dataset containing only songs with structural labels (296179 rows), later kept without labels for evaluation.
Segmented dataset:
Each entry corresponds to a single labeled section (e.g., a single verse or chorus - 2009699 rows). Labels and associated chord sequences are preserved for section-level modeling and evaluation.

For the tasks three architectures were considered, the vanilla recurrent neural networks (RNN), the gated recurrent units (GRU), the long short-term memory networks (LSTM). Transformer-based architectures are left for future work, as their effective application to chord sequences would require investigation of alternative tokenization strategies and data representations suited to the limited harmonic vocabulary. The models receive sequences of either encoded chords (1-token) or encoded chord components (3-tokens) and uses an embeddings layer to create meaningful embeddings to be processed by the main module. The module’s output is then collected by fully connected layers, with either one head per chord or 3 heads, one per chord component. For the 3-tokens representation the heads of the components are then combined to determine which chord from the known vocabulary fits best. The multi-head approach allows the model to leverage both component-level and full-chord supervision simultaneously.

Models were trained using cross-entropy loss, with the total loss of the 3-tokens approach being the summation of the component and full chord losses. Hyperparameters were as follows: hidden dimension 256, one main module layer, batch size of 4096, learning rate 0.005, a maximum of 200 epochs and embedding dimension of 128 for the chords and 16 for each component, resulting to approximately 449k–1.3m parameters depending on model and tokenization. Training was performed on the segmented dataset, while evaluation was performed on the filtered dataset, the labeled-song dataset, and the segmented dataset after filtering songs that had at least one part belong to the training or validation dataset. To improve performance and generalization, added mechanisms were employed. Early stopping in combination with learning rate scheduling was used to avoid overfitting, while a stratified splitting ensured training validation and test sets had no overlapping songs and maintained label distributions. Also, during all phases, to increase robustness to variable-length sequences, sequences were cut randomly when sampling while ensuring the target chord followed at least 4 chords. All experiments were conducted locally on a consumer-grade laptop equipped with an Intel i7-9750H CPU and an NVIDIA GTX 1650 GPU, with each training run lasting between 10 minutes and 2 hours. Across approximately 270 training runs, result variance remained consistently below 1%, confirming statistical stability. We estimate total energy consumption at approximately 9–18 kWh, corresponding to a carbon footprint of roughly 3.5–9 kg CO
2
based on an average grid carbon intensity of 300–500g CO
2
/kWh.

3.2
Results

The chord prediction experiments consist of two main analyses. First, we investigated how the size of the training dataset influences model performance, as shown in Figure
5
. Performance improves dramatically as the sample size grown from a few thousand entries (specifically 5,000) to 200,000, with only minor gains beyond that up to 1,000,000 samples. RNN consistently performs worse than the other models. Table
2
summarizes the average results over multiple runs using the best performing sample size (1,000,000 samples). When tested with whole song sequences that have part labels, accuracy decreases and loss increases. Performance drops further when tested on datasets missing structural labels. The 3-tokens representation generally outperforms the 1-token representation, except for the vanilla RNN. In terms of accuracy, the LSTM achieves the best results, while the GRU shows slightly better performance in loss despite a minor gap in accuracy. This suggests that next chord prediction in this setting is dominated by strong local dependencies and relatively low-entropy transitions, where the inductive biases of recurrent models remain competitive or advantageous.

Secondly, we investigated the impact of structural information and sequence length on next chord prediction by evaluating all architectures across the three dataset subsets. Figure
6
presents the cumulative mean loss versus sequence length across all conditions. LSTM consistently achieves the lowest loss while RNN clearly underperforms, with GRU sitting between the two — particularly benefiting from the 3-token representation over the 1-token approach. Performance stabilizes at approximately 10–20 chords of context, suggesting this as the effective context window for next chord prediction. Most notably, loss decreases progressively from the filtered subset to the labeled subset to the segmented subset, demonstrating that structural information consistently reduces prediction uncertainty across all architectures and representation choices. Our findings agree with those in
Korzeniowski et al. (2018)
, confirming both the relative ordering of model performances and the effective context window of approximately 10–20 chords.

3.3
Discussion

Across all architectures, performance improves steeply with training data size until approximately 200,000 samples, after which gains plateau, indicating that the dominant harmonic patterns in the corpus are captured well before exhausting the full dataset, with additional samples providing diminishing returns. A similar saturation effect is observed with sequence length, where performance stabilizes at approximately 10–20 chords, confirming that harmonic predictability is concentrated locally. The segmented subset directly exploits this locality; by treating each structural part as an independent sequence, models operate within a harmonically coherent context window, which explains its consistent performance advantage over the labeled and filtered subsets. The 3-token representation consistently outperforms the 1-token approach across all conditions, as decomposing each chord into its root, quality, and inversion allows models to internalize relationships between chord components.

When models are evaluated on entire songs without part segmentation, overall performance degrades. Interestingly, although accuracy declines, loss continues to decrease, suggesting that improvements in the objective may reflect increased confidence or calibration effects rather than gains in classification accuracy.
This occurs because crossing a part boundary effectively resets the harmonic context: the tonal patterns governing one section do not carry over to the next, and sequences spanning multiple parts blend incompatible harmonic contexts. The gradual confidence gain reflects the model adapting to the new local context after the boundary, rather than genuine predictive improvement. This reinforces the importance of part-level structure in symbolic chord modeling.

4
Limitations

Chordonomicon has several limitations inherent to its source. Timing and beat information is absent, as Ultimate Guitar transcriptions do not encode temporal structure. Guitar-idiomatic patterns (simplified voicings and capo usage) are present, reflecting authentic popular music performance practice. As the dataset is derived from user-generated content, it inevitably contains noise and simplified harmonic representations. Rather than attempting to fully eliminate such variability, we view it as reflecting real-world transcription practices. The scale of the dataset helps mitigate individual errors, though we acknowledge that systematic biases (e.g., guitar-centric chord distributions) remain. The equal temperament nature of the guitar means microtonal and non-Western harmonic systems are outside the dataset’s scope. Each song’s tonal key was excluded as its assignment is a globally interpretive judgment, unlike individual chord validity which is locally verifiable; transposition scripts are provided for key normalization. N.C. (no chord) markers were excluded as they carry no harmonic information.

5
Conclusion

We present the
Chordonomicon
, the largest dataset of chord progressions, comprising over 666,000 meticulously curated user-generated progressions, along with uniformly formatted structural information and additional metadata, addressing critical gaps in data availability and consistency for chord-based MIR tasks. Its standardized formatting and integration potential with music ontologies open new possibilities for hybrid AI systems and advanced harmonic analysis. Additionally, we offer insights into its corpus and demonstrate baseline performance on a proposed evaluation framework for next chord prediction, investigating different model architectures, representations, sample sizes, sequence length, and structural information. Results highlight the positive effect of structural information.

Finally,
Chordonomicon
offers a unique blend of harmonic, structural, and metadata-rich representations on a very large scale, making it an invaluable tool for AI researchers across domains. We envision this dataset as a foundation for advancing chord-based generative modeling, classification tasks, and hybrid AI systems. We encourage the research community to build upon our preliminary benchmarks, explore novel architectures, and deepen our understanding of chord progressions in music theory and computational creativity.

Ethics Statement

Data Harvesting Legality and Ethicality

1.
Web Crawler Permissions
: Ultimate Guitar (hereafter “UG") does not flag "tabs.ultimate-guitar.com/tab/*" as “Disallowed” in their /robots.txt file. This indicates that UG permits automated crawlers to access and index their tablature and chord pages, aligning with established web protocols for content accessibility.
2.
Terms of Service Compliance
: A thorough examination of UG’s Terms of Service (TOS) reveals no obvious prohibition against automated content crawling or scraping. This absence of such restrictions further supports the permissibility of data collection for research purposes.
3.
Legal Precedents
: U.S. courts have consistently ruled in favor of web-scraping publicly accessible data, particularly for research purposes. Notably, in Sandvig v. Barr (D.D.C. 2020), the court held that such activities are protected under the First Amendment and do not violate the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA). Additionally, the hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp. (9th Circuit, 2019) decision further reinforced the legality of scraping publicly available data.

Intellectual Property Considerations

1.
User-Generated Content Ownership
: UG’s TOS explicitly states that “Ultimate Guitar does not claim any ownership rights in User Generated Content that you transmit, submit, display or publish on, through or in connection with the Service". And further clarifies that “User Generated Content includes, without limitation, tablatures (text or electronic)…". This clause clearly delineates the ownership status of the content we’re collecting.
2.
Non-Copyrightable Elements
: Legal precedents consistently support the notion that chord progressions are not copyrightable. Cases such as Granite Music Corp. v. United Artists Corp. (1977) and Swirsky v. Carey (2004) have established that chord progressions are fundamental building blocks of music, akin to "common musical property", since many songs share the same or similar chord progressions. This legal stance corroborates that our dataset solely comprises of non-copyrightable elements.
3.
Data Processing
: To further ensure ethical and legal compliance, our data collection process excludes copyrightable elements such as lyrics, song titles, and artist names. We retain only the chord progressions, the associated song structure information (e.g., intro, verse, chorus), release year, the genres of the relevant artist and Spotify IDs of track and artist (non-copyrightable alphanumeric strings).

In conclusion, by adhering to web crawling conventions, respecting Terms of Service, focusing on non-copyrightable elements, and following well-established precedents in data collection and licensing, our approach to creating this dataset stands on solid legal and ethical ground. Beyond legal compliance, we recognize the importance of respecting the creative efforts of the Ultimate Guitar community, whose voluntary transcription work made this dataset possible. While the anonymous nature of user-generated contributions makes individual consent and attribution infeasible, we explicitly acknowledge the UG community’s indirect but essential role in enabling this research. The potential scientific and cultural value of this research further justifies its creation and use within the bounds of fair use and academic freedom.
