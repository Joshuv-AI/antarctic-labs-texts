TRACE: Temporal Reasoning via Agentic Context Evolution for Streaming Electronic Health Records (EHRs)
Zhan Qu, Michael Färber

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) encode extensive medical knowledge but struggle to apply it reliably to longitudinal patient trajectories, where evolving clinical states, irregular timing, and heterogeneous events degrade performance over time. Existing adaptation strategies rely on fine-tuning or retrieval-based augmentation, which introduce computational overhead, privacy constraints, or instability under long contexts. We introduce TRACE ( T emporal R easoning via A gentic C ontext E volution), a framework that enables temporal clinical reasoning with frozen LLMs by explicitly structuring and maintaining context rather than extending context windows or updating parameters. TRACE operates over a dual-memory architecture consisting of a static Global Protocol encoding institutional clinical rules and a dynamic Individual Protocol tracking patient-specific state. Four agentic components, Router, Reasoner, Auditor, and Steward, coordinate over this structured memory to support temporal inference and state evolution. The framework maintains bounded inference cost via structured state compression and selectively audits safety-critical clinical decisions. Evaluated on longitudinal clinical event streams from MIMIC-IV, TRACE significantly improves next-event prediction accuracy, protocol adherence, and clinical safety over long-context and retrieval-augmented baselines, while producing interpretable and auditable reasoning traces. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong performance on static medical tasks, including diagnostic question answering, clinical summarization, and radiological reporting
(
19
;
13
)
. However, deploying LLMs for
longitudinal clinical reasoning
remains a fundamental challenge. Real-world patient care unfolds as a continuous,
non-stationary event stream characterized by irregular temporal intervals, heterogeneous modalities, and evolving physiological states
(
22
;
25
)
. In this setting, models must reason not only over isolated observations, but over extended trajectories in which early decisions, latent conditions, and institutional constraints remain clinically relevant over time.

A core difficulty arises from the mismatch between how LLMs process context and how clinical reasoning operates in practice. Standard Transformers rely on a fixed context window and quadratic self-attention, making long-horizon inference computationally infeasible and brittle to context dilution. As trajectories grow, models increasingly exhibit
context collapse
and
lost-in-the-middle
behavior
(
11
;
16
)
, where critical early information (e.g., admission diagnoses or chronic contraindications) is overshadowed by recent but less relevant events. Simply extending context length or increasing model size does not resolve this structural limitation.

Clinical reasoning further depends on conditional, context-sensitive heuristics that are rarely explicit in pretraining data
(
17
)
. Examples include interpreting lab trends relative to patient-specific baselines, enforcing contraindications, or adhering to institution-level formularies and protocols. Such heuristics are neither purely parametric nor reliably recoverable through semantic similarity alone; they must be
explicitly represented, selectively activated, and consistently applied
as the patient trajectory evolves.

Existing adaptation strategies exhibit complementary but insufficient solutions. Fine-tuning can specialize a model to a clinical domain, but incurs high computational cost, complicates deployment on privacy-sensitive data, and lacks robustness to evolving protocols
(
30
;
31
;
6
)
. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) avoids parameter updates but relies on vector similarity, which often retrieves clinically irrelevant or temporally inappropriate information
(
3
;
24
;
25
)
. Long-context models can ingest extensive histories, but suffer from severe efficiency and reliability degradation in long-horizon settings
(
10
;
9
;
22
)
. These limitations suggest that robust clinical reasoning requires neither continual parameter updates nor unbounded context windows, but a mechanism for
structuring, maintaining, and updating the right context over time
.

We propose a different paradigm: treating longitudinal clinical reasoning as a problem of
continuous context optimization
. We introduce
TRACE
(
T
emporal
R
easoning via
A
gentic
C
ontext
E
volution),
a framework that transforms a frozen LLM into a coordinated, multi-agent system operating over a compact, structured cognitive state. Rather than relying on a monolithic context window or noisy retrieval, TRACE maintains a
Dual-Memory Architecture
consisting of: (i) a static
Global Protocol
encoding institution-level clinical guidelines, and (ii) a dynamic
Individual Protocol
that tracks the evolving patient state in a structured, machine-readable form.

At inference time, TRACE executes a lightweight agentic loop comprising four roles. A
Router
performs deterministic, trigger-based selection of relevant global rules. A
Reasoner
generates clinically grounded predictions conditioned on the selected protocols and patient state. An
Auditor
selectively verifies safety-critical or high-uncertainty actions, enforcing contraindications without incurring constant overhead. Finally, a
Steward
maintains a bounded context budget through a structured state compression mechanism (
Mitosis
), ensuring that inference cost remains constant with respect to trajectory length.

To enable domain adaptation without test-time learning or parameter updates, TRACE employs a separate offline
Reflector
agent. During an offline protocol induction phase, the Reflector analyzes model failures on historical data and synthesizes explicit, generalizable rules that are incorporated into the Global Protocol. Once induced, the Global Protocol is frozen and reused across all downstream patient trajectories, ensuring a clean separation between protocol induction and deployment-time inference.

We evaluate TRACE on longitudinal event streams derived from MIMIC-IV
(
8
)
using a prequential evaluation protocol that strictly separates past learning from future prediction. Across multiple open-source LLMs, TRACE substantially improves predictive accuracy, protocol adherence, and clinical safety compared to long-context, retrieval-augmented, and monolithic agent baselines, while maintaining bounded inference cost and fully transparent, human-readable reasoning traces. Our main contributions:

•

A context-centric view of longitudinal clinical reasoning.

We formulate clinical decision-making over patient trajectories as a problem of continuous context evolution, rather than static sequence modeling or unbounded context accumulation.

•

TRACE: a dual-memory, agentic framework for clinical streams.

We propose TRACE, a multi-agent system that explicitly separates institutional clinical knowledge from patient-specific state, enabling scalable, interpretable reasoning over long and irregular clinical trajectories without parameter updates.

•

Offline protocol induction via reflective rule synthesis.

We introduce a Reflector agent that distills generalizable, institution-specific clinical rules from model failures in an offline setting, avoiding fine-tuning and test-time learning.

•

A safety-aware inference loop with bounded inference cost.

TRACE integrates structured state updates and conditional safety auditing to maintain reliable clinical behavior while keeping inference cost independent of trajectory length.

•

Extensive empirical evaluation on longitudinal EHR data.

We evaluate TRACE on event streams derived from MIMIC-IV and demonstrate consistent improvements in predictive accuracy, protocol adherence, and clinical safety over long-context and retrieval-augmented baselines.

Related Work

Adaptive Context and Prompt Evolution for Post-Training LLM Improvement

Recent work has explored improving large language models (LLMs)
without parameter updates
by iteratively modifying textual artifacts such as prompts, system instructions, or external memory. These approaches differ in whether adaptation occurs offline or at inference time, and in whether updates target short-lived prompts or persistent external memory. Reflection-based methods show that LLMs can critique and revise their own outputs to improve performance, either within a single instance
(
12
)
or across episodes in sequential decision-making
(
18
)
. In parallel, prompt optimization methods treat prompts as optimizable objects and apply black-box or evolutionary search in prompt space, including Automatic Prompt Engineer (APE)
(
29
)
, OPRO
(
26
)
, and PromptBreeder
(
5
)
. These approaches typically operate offline on fixed evaluation sets and do not address continual adaptation or long-horizon stability. Related systems such as MemGPT
(
14
)
focus on managing long-context interactions via external memory, but primarily address memory access and control rather than learning transferable problem-solving knowledge.

Dynamic Cheatsheet (DC)
(
20
)
explicitly frames inference as a
test-time learning
process, in which an LLM maintains and curates a persistent external memory to improve future performance. GEPA
(
1
)
advances a complementary direction by optimizing prompts for compound LLM systems through reflective edits and Pareto-based evolutionary selection, positioning prompt evolution as an alternative to reinforcement learning for system-level optimization. However, iterative prompts and memory rewriting may degrade over long horizons, particularly when updates are unstructured or overwrite earlier information, leading to context drift or loss of critical constraints. Agentic Context Engineering (ACE)
(
27
)
addresses these limitations by evolving structured contexts via incremental updates and deterministic merging, supporting both offline prompt optimization and online test-time adaptation. Together, these methods form a coherent research field on post-training LLM self-improvement via evolving textual artifacts, differing primarily in their optimization targets and adaptation timescales.

Medical Foundation Models and Longitudinal EHR Reasoning

Recent medical foundation models have demonstrated strong performance on static and short-horizon clinical tasks, including diagnostic reasoning and clinical question answering
(
19
;
13
;
23
)
. Domain-aligned variants such as
Meditron further improves medical factuality and robustness through large-scale pretraining on curated clinical corpora
(
2
)
. Beyond text-centric models, recent work has explored foundation models trained directly on structured medical events. COMET, for example, applies scaling laws to heterogeneous EHR sequences and demonstrates strong zero-shot performance across a range of clinical prediction tasks
(
21
)
. To support longitudinal reasoning, a growing body of work models Electronic Health Records (EHRs) as long temporal sequences of visits or events. Recent studies evaluate long-context architectures on full patient histories containing tens of thousands of events
(
22
)
, while representation-focused approaches such as GT-BEHRT
(
15
)
and AutoDP
(
4
)
improve predictive performance by modeling temporal dependencies, task structure, or concept relations. In parallel, several works augment longitudinal EHR models with retrieval mechanisms to incorporate external or historical clinical knowledge, including RAM-EHR
(
25
)
and multimodal medical RAG systems
(
24
)
. Other efforts explore generative modeling of longitudinal EHRs, including diffusion-based approaches for synthesizing realistic multimodal patient trajectories
(
7
;
28
)
. Despite substantial progress, existing approaches predominantly treat patient history as a flat or weakly structured sequence and rely on longer contexts, retrieval, or larger models to manage temporal dependencies, leaving open the problem of how to support reliable, interpretable, and policy-aware clinical reasoning over unbounded event streams without continually expanding context windows or retraining model parameters.

3
Methodology

We formulate longitudinal clinical reasoning as a
continuous context optimization
problem over a potentially unbounded event stream. A patient trajectory is modeled as a sequence of event sets
\mathcal{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{T})
, where each
E_{t}
represents a bundle of clinical observations, interventions, and measurements at timestep
t
. At each timestep, the model must estimate
P(Y_{t+1}|E_{1:t})
where
Y_{t+1}
denotes the next clinical intervention or patient state.

Standard Transformer architectures scale poorly to long-horizon clinical trajectories due to the quadratic
O(t^{2})
computational and memory complexity of self-attention and the fixed context window
L_{\text{max}}
. Consequently, naïvely extending the context length becomes computationally infeasible and empirically brittle, particularly when modeling long-term temporal dependencies in irregular clinical data.

To address this limitation, TRACE reformulates longitudinal reasoning by maintaining a compact, high-density cognitive state rather than relying on massive context windows or ad hoc vector retrieval. Specifically, TRACE decouples clinical context into two complementary memory streams: a static, institution-level
Global Protocol
encoding clinical guidelines and shared medical knowledge, and a dynamic, patient-specific
Individual Protocol
that evolves over time. These memory streams are jointly managed by a multi-agent controller comprising a Router, Reasoner, Auditor, and Steward, enabling continuous context optimization over an effectively infinite horizon. In addition, TRACE employs a separate Reflector agent used exclusively during offline protocol induction to induce and refine the Global Protocol.

3.1
Dual-Memory Architecture

TRACE maintains the inference state
S_{t}=(\mathcal{P}_{G},\mathcal{P}_{I,t},E_{\text{buff}})
, where
E_{\text{buff}}
is a short rolling buffer of recent raw tokens.

1. Global Protocol (
\mathcal{P}_{G}
): The Institutional Playbook.

\mathcal{P}_{G}
serves as the “Long-Term Semantic Memory” containing transferable medical heuristics (e.g., sepsis bundles, insulin sliding scales). Unlike vector-based RAG which suffers from retrieval noise,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
is structured as a
Key-Value Cheatsheet

(
20
)
. Keys correspond to clinical triggers (e.g.,
[TRIGGER: LACTATE > 4]
), and values contain precise action guidelines. This memory is learned offline and frozen during inference (see Sec.
3.4
).

2. Individual Protocol (
\mathcal{P}_{I,t}
): The Patient State.

\mathcal{P}_{I,t}
is a dynamic, evolving JSON object representing the specific patient’s current physiological status. It functions as a
structured state-space model
, tracking active latent variables rather than summarizing past tokens:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{I,t}=\{

\displaystyle\text{"active\_problems"}:[d_{1},d_{2}\dots],

\displaystyle\text{"current\_meds"}:[m_{1},m_{2}\dots],

\displaystyle\text{"trajectory"}:[\text{trend}_{1},\dots]\}

3.2
Offline Protocol Induction via the Reflector

TRACE incorporates a dedicated
Reflector
agent responsible for inducing
and refining the Global Protocol
\mathcal{P}_{G}
during offline protocol induction. The Reflector operates exclusively offline and is never invoked during inference.

Given a failed prediction at timestep
t
, the Reflector is provided with the
patient trajectory
E_{1:t}
, the model’s predicted action
\widehat{Y}_{t+1}
,
and the ground-truth intervention
Y^{*}_{t+1}
. Its objective is to determine
whether the error arises from missing institutional knowledge or an underspecified
clinical rule.

When such a gap is identified, the Reflector synthesizes a new generalizable rule
of the form:

\texttt{IF [trigger] THEN [action]},

which is categorized and appended to the Global Protocol
\mathcal{P}_{G}
. Once
learned,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
is fixed and reused across all downstream patient
trajectories.

3.3
The TRACE Inference Loop

At each timestep
t
, TRACE executes a coordinated loop designed to minimize computational cost while maximizing safety. This loop operates solely over inference-time agents (Router, Reasoner, Auditor, Steward); the Reflector is not active during deployment.

Step 1: Trigger-Guided Routing.

The
Router
selects a subset of protocol entries from
\mathcal{P}_{G}
relevant to the current state given
E_{t}
and
\mathcal{P}_{I,t}
:

C_{t}=\text{Router}(E_{t},\mathcal{P}_{I,t},\mathcal{P}_{G}).

The Router is prompted to match observed triggers in
E_{t}
(e.g., hypotension) to protocol keys in
\mathcal{P}_{G}
, producing a compact set of candidate rules for downstream reasoning.

Step 2: Predictive Reasoning.

The
Reasoner
generates the prediction
\widehat{Y}_{t+1}
using the context. It is prompted to explicitly cite rules from
C_{t}
to justify its actions:

\widehat{Y}_{t+1},\text{logprobs}=\text{LLM}(\text{Prompt}\oplus C_{t}\oplus\mathcal{P}_{I,t}\oplus E_{\text{buff}})

Step 3: Trigger-Based Auditing for Computational Efficiency.

To reduce the inference cost of reflective verification, the
Auditor
is conditional. We define an uncertainty proxy
U(\widehat{Y}_{t+1})
from the average token log-probability of the generated output. The Auditor is triggered only if:

1.

Uncertainty:

U(\widehat{Y}_{t+1})>\tau_{\text{uncertainty}}
.

2.

Safety Criticality:

\widehat{Y}_{t+1}\cap\mathcal{V}_{\text{risk}}\neq\emptyset
(where
\mathcal{V}_{\text{risk}}
is a vocabulary of high-stakes interventions).

When triggered, the Auditor validates
\widehat{Y}_{t+1}
against (i)
\mathcal{P}_{G}
and (ii) explicit safety constraints derived from the structured patient state
\mathcal{P}_{I,t}
(e.g., allergies, active problems).

Step 4: The Steward and Protocol Mitosis.

The
Steward
manages the context budget via
Mitosis
, a structured state compression algorithm. When
|E_{\text{buff}}|>L_{\text{limit}}
, the Steward absorbs the raw information into the structured state:

\mathcal{P}_{I,t+1}=\text{Update}(\mathcal{P}_{I,t},E_{\text{buff}})

Unlike text summarization, Mitosis performs discrete state updates (e.g., removing a medication from
current_meds
upon a stop order). The raw buffer
E_{\text{buff}}
is then flushed.
This keeps the prompt budget bounded and independent of the trajectory length
T
, while preserving the state fidelity required for clinical safety.

3.4
Optimization Strategy: Two-Phase Evolution

To prevent data contamination and ensure robust generalization, we adopt a two-phase learning strategy inspired by GEPA
(
1
)
.

Phase I: Offline Global Learning (Protocol Induction).

We execute TRACE on the training set. When the model fails to predict a ground-truth intervention
Y^{*}_{t+1}
, the
Reflector
analyzes the error. If the error stems from missing knowledge (e.g., institutional dosage limits), the Reflector synthesizes a new rule
r
and appends it to
\mathcal{P}_{G}
. This phase induces a high-quality ”Medical Playbook” from data.

Phase II: Online Deployment (Test-Time Adaptation).

During evaluation on the test set,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
is
frozen
. The system adapts solely via the Individual Protocol
\mathcal{P}_{I}
. This strictly evaluates the system’s ability to maintain patient-specific context and apply learned rules to unseen trajectories without parameter updates.

4
Data Construction

We transform the relational structure of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) into a linearized, semi-structured event stream suitable for long-horizon agentic reasoning. Raw EHR data are inherently heterogeneous and temporally sparse, spanning laboratory measurements, medication orders, diagnoses, and procedures recorded at irregular intervals. Directly modeling such relational tables as a flat sequence leads to long, low-density contexts and poor temporal alignment. To address this, we construct compact
event bundles
that aggregate clinically related observations while preserving temporal ordering.

4.1
Source Dataset

We use MIMIC-IV v2.2
(
8
)
, a large-scale, deidentified critical care database derived from admissions to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. From the relational schema, we extract diagnoses, medications, laboratory results, and procedures associated with each ICU admission. Diagnoses and procedures are represented using normalized ICD codes enriched with their textual descriptions. Medication data are parsed into explicit start and stop events with associated metadata such as dosage and route. Laboratory measurements are paired with reference ranges to support semantic interpretation.

4.2
Event Normalization and Semantic Discretization

To maximize information density per token and reduce numerical noise, continuous clinical variables are discretized into semantic categories relative to reference ranges or patient-specific baselines (e.g.,
Low
,
Normal
,
High
). Normal laboratory results are aggregated when possible (e.g.,
CMP: All Normal
), while abnormal values are explicitly reported together with their measured magnitude (e.g.,
Creatinine: 2.1 (High)
). This semantic discretization preserves clinically salient deviations while substantially reducing token usage.

4.3
Event Bundle Construction

Clinical events are sorted chronologically within each admission and grouped into
Event Bundles
based on temporal proximity. Events occurring within a fixed one-hour window are coalesced into a single bundle
E_{t}
, which summarizes all observations, interventions, and measurements recorded during that interval. This aggregation avoids excessive fragmentation of closely related events while maintaining a clinically meaningful temporal resolution.

To handle extended periods of clinical silence, we insert explicit time-delta tokens (e.g.,
[TIME_DELTA: +12 hours]
) whenever the gap between consecutive bundles exceeds a predefined threshold. These tokens allow the model to reason about elapsed time without processing redundant empty steps, yielding more compact and interpretable trajectories.

4.4
Serialized Clinical Streams

The above steps define a deterministic transformation

\phi:\mathcal{D}_{\text{relational}}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{\text{text}},

which maps raw relational EHR tables to a serialized event stream
\mathcal{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{T})
. Each event bundle is rendered as a compact textual summary with structured sections corresponding to diagnoses, medications, laboratory results, and procedures. The resulting representation serves as the sole input to the TRACE inference loop described in Section
3
, enabling consistent processing across models and experimental conditions.

Across the evaluation cohort, this construction yields concise patient trajectories with a manageable number of interaction steps per ICU stay; detailed statistics on trajectory length and token counts are reported in Section X.

5
Experimental Setup

We evaluate TRACE in a streaming clinical decision-making setting using serialized event bundles constructed as described in Section
4
. All experiments follow a prequential (predict-then-update) protocol to prevent temporal leakage and reflect realistic deployment conditions.

Evaluation Protocol.

Evaluation proceeds in two phases. In Phase I (offline protocol induction), TRACE is applied to a training cohort of
N=100{,}804
patients. At each timestep
t
, the system predicts
\widehat{Y}_{t+1}
given the observed history; when the prediction does not match the ground-truth action
Y^{*}_{t+1}
, the Reflector agent analyzes the failure and, when appropriate, synthesizes a new generalizable rule that is appended to the Global Protocol
\mathcal{P}_{G}
. After processing the full training cohort,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
is frozen. In Phase II (streaming inference), we evaluate on a held-out test set of
N=100{,}863
patients. Each trajectory is processed sequentially, one event bundle at a time: the system predicts
\widehat{Y}_{t+1}
, the prediction is scored against
Y^{*}_{t+1}
, and the Individual Protocol
\mathcal{P}_{I,t}
is updated via the Steward using the observed outcome. No parameter updates or
\mathcal{P}_{G}
modifications occur during this phase.

Dataset Statistics.

After normalization and event bundle construction (Sec.
4
), the corpus contains approximately 5.0M event bundles per phase, comprising over 43M clinical events. Each bundle aggregates events recorded within a one-hour window and contains on average 8.6 events, primarily laboratory measurements (4.3 per bundle) and medication orders (3.2 per bundle), with diagnoses and procedures occurring less frequently. Patient trajectories are long-horizon, with an average of 48–49 event bundles per ICU admission and approximately 420 events per patient after aggregation and discretization. In Phase I, TRACE induces a Global Protocol containing 441 rules. The resulting protocol is compact (21 words on average per rule), human-readable, and explicitly editable, allowing clinicians or system designers to inspect, modify, or extend institutional heuristics without retraining the underlying model.

Backbone Models.

TRACE is tested on 5 open-source LLMs spanning general-purpose and domain-specific settings: Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct, Mixtral-8x22B-v0.1, Meditron-70B, and DeepSeek-V3.1. All agent roles (Router, Reasoner, Auditor, Steward, and Reflector) are instantiated as prompted LLM calls using the same backbone within a given experiment.

Prompt Structure and Context Budget.

Inference is conducted under a fixed prompt budget with predefined token allocations for system instructions, retrieved Global Protocol entries, the structured Individual Protocol, and a short rolling buffer of recent event bundles. This design ensures that prompt length remains bounded and independent of trajectory length. Prompt templates are shared across models and are provided in Appendix
A
.

Baselines.

We compare TRACE against three baseline approaches that represent common strategies for longitudinal clinical reasoning with LLMs: (i) a long-context clinical LLM that ingests the raw serialized history up to the context limit; (ii) a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system using a Contriever-based retriever to select top-
K
historical segments from the patient’s past; and (iii) a monolithic agent (MemGPT-style) that maintains a single free-text summary of patient state without separating institutional knowledge from individual context. All baselines receive the same event bundle inputs, use identical backbone models where applicable, and operate under the same fixed prompt budget during inference.

Metrics.

We report four metrics:
(i) Recall@5
for next-step prediction of medications, lab orders, and procedures;
(ii) Protocol Adherence
, the fraction of timesteps where predictions explicitly cite an activated Global Protocol rule;
(iii) Clinical Equivalence (1–5)
, a GPT-4o judged measure of plan-level clinical acceptability allowing reasonable alternatives; and
(iv) Auditor Activation Rate
, the fraction of timesteps escalated to conditional verification due to uncertainty or safety-critical actions.

Results

We report main results on the held-out test cohort in Table
1
. Evaluation is performed over serialized event-stream trajectories constructed per ICU admission (Sec.
4
) using the prequential streaming protocol described in Sec.
5
. Performance is measured using Recall@5 for next-step prediction of medications, lab orders, and procedures, together with a GPT-4o judged Clinical Equivalence score (1–5). For TRACE, we additionally report Protocol Adherence and the Auditor Activation Rate, which measures how often the system escalates a decision to conditional verification.

6.1
Main Results: Predictive Performance and Clinical Quality

TRACE substantially improves Recall@5 over all baselines.

The long-context baseline achieves Recall@5 of 0.3192 (medications), 0.2119 (lab orders), and 0.3511 (procedures). Static RAG and the monolithic agent perform comparably on medications (0.3221 and 0.3224), but degrade markedly on lab orders (0.1245 and 0.1360) and procedures (0.2827 and 0.3313). This pattern highlights a core challenge of longitudinal clinical streams: temporally appropriate ordering depends on localized triggers and evolving state, which similarity-based retrieval and free-text summaries often fail to preserve.

Across backbone models, TRACE consistently improves Recall@5 for medications (0.5327–
0.5986
) and lab orders (0.4176–
0.5727
). Procedure prediction also improves, with TRACE achieving up to
0.4219
(Meditron-70B) and strong performance across other backbones (0.3089–0.4184). These results indicate that explicit state tracking and trigger-guided protocol routing improve exact-match prediction across multiple clinical action types, with particularly large gains for medication and lab ordering.

Clinical equivalence improves even when exact matches remain difficult.

Exact-match Recall@5 can underestimate clinically valid decisions, as recorded action sequences may omit acceptable alternatives (e.g., class-equivalent medications or alternative diagnostic workups). The Clinical Equivalence score complements Recall@5 by assessing plan-level clinical acceptability. TRACE improves equivalence from 2.95 (long-context) and 3.28 (monolithic agent) to 3.56–
3.96
across backbones. Notably, DeepSeek-V3.1 attains the highest equivalence score (
3.96
) despite only moderate Recall@5, suggesting that TRACE often generates clinically reasonable alternatives that differ from the exact recorded tokens.

High protocol adherence reflects explicit institutional grounding.

TRACE maintains consistently high Protocol Adherence (92.1–
94.7
%), demonstrating that the Router reliably activates relevant Global Protocol entries and that the Reasoner grounds its predictions in explicitly cited rules under a bounded prompt budget. We do not report adherence for baselines, as they do not operate over an explicit, citeable protocol representation.

Auditor activation remains conditional rather than constant.

Table
1
reports the
Auditor Activation Rate
, defined as the fraction of timesteps where TRACE escalates a prediction to the Auditor due to high uncertainty or safety-critical action types. Activation rates range from
5.15%
(Mixtral-8x22B) to 9.42% (Llama-3.1-70B), indicating that verification is invoked on a minority of steps rather than continuously. This metric reflects verification frequency and computational overhead, not the rate of contraindicated recommendations.

With the sepsis protocol fixed, TRACE incrementally predicts the next required
bundle as prior actions are observed and incorporated into state.

This example shows a short ICU trajectory in which a sepsis protocol is executed across multiple timesteps using a serialized event stream. At
t=0
, an abnormal lactate measurement is observed, after which the system predicts the set of protocol-relevant actions, including fluid resuscitation and antibiotics. The prediction at this stage reflects recognition of a sepsis pattern rather than a direct reaction to a single event. As the trajectory progresses, observed interventions are incorporated step by step. After fluid resuscitation is recorded at
t=1
, the next predicted bundle advances to blood cultures, reflecting that fluids have already been administered while cultures have not yet occurred. At
t=2
, once blood cultures are observed, the predicted next action becomes initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Previously completed interventions are not re-predicted, and the predicted actions change only as the execution state of the protocol changes.

After all protocol-specified interventions are observed, the system produces no further predictions. Overall, the example illustrates stepwise execution of a multi-action clinical protocol over a longitudinal event stream, where each prediction depends on the cumulative set of actions already taken rather than on isolated recent events.

7
Conclusion

We introduced TRACE, a framework for long-horizon reasoning with frozen large language models that replaces unbounded context accumulation with explicit, structured state. Empirically, our results show that performance gains are primarily driven by the dual-memory design, which separates a static Global Protocol from a dynamic, structured Individual Protocol. Ablation studies indicate that removing either component substantially degrades Recall@5 and protocol adherence, highlighting the importance of explicitly representing both institutional rules and evolving state.

The agentic inference loop supports this structure by enabling trigger-based protocol selection and state-aware progression across timesteps, while conditional auditing stabilizes predictions without constant verification. Together, these components enable reliable reasoning over long event streams with bounded inference cost. Although evaluated in the clinical domain, the design addresses a general challenge in sequential decision-making with LLMs where relevant context must be preserved and updated over extended horizons.

Impact Statement

This work proposes TRACE, a framework for improving the reliability and transparency of LLMs in long-horizon, non-stationary reasoning settings by structuring and maintaining context explicitly rather than updating model parameters. While our experiments focus on the clinical domain, the primary contribution is methodological: TRACE introduces a general approach for deploying frozen LLMs in complex real-world environments where decisions must be made sequentially under evolving constraints.

From a positive impact perspective, TRACE has the potential to improve the safety, interpretability, and auditability of LLM-based decision-support systems. By representing both institutional knowledge (Global Protocol) and instance-specific state (Individual Protocol) in human-readable and editable form, the framework enables external inspection, correction, and alignment with domain-specific rules. This transparency is particularly important in high-stakes domains, where opaque or untraceable model behavior can lead to downstream harm. More broadly, the proposed design pattern may benefit applications beyond healthcare, including scientific workflows, software maintenance, and operational decision support.

At the same time, this work has important limitations. Our empirical evaluation relies on MIMIC-IV, a deidentified intensive care unit dataset with restricted access requirements. As a result, reproducibility is limited to researchers who are able to obtain appropriate data use approvals, and the dataset reflects the practices and patient population of a single healthcare system. Moreover, TRACE is not intended to function as an autonomous clinical decision-maker, and any deployment in real-world clinical settings would require rigorous validation, governance, and human oversight.

Finally, like all LLM-based systems, TRACE may inherit biases present in its LLM backbones and training data. While the framework emphasizes structured reasoning and explicit rule grounding, it does not eliminate the risk of biased or inappropriate predictions. We therefore emphasize that TRACE should be viewed as a research contribution toward safer and more interpretable LLM systems, rather than as a ready-to-deploy solution, and that careful domain-specific evaluation remains essential before use in practice.
