Diffusion-Based Hypothesis Testing and Change-Point Detection
Sean Moushegian, Taposh Banerjee, Vahid Tarokh

Abstract
Score-based methods have recently seen increasing popularity in modeling and generation. Methods have been constructed to perform hypothesis testing and change-point detection with score functions, but these methods are in general not as powerful as their likelihood-based peers. Recent works consider generalizing the score-based Fisher divergence into a diffusion-divergence by transforming score functions via multiplication with a matrix-valued function or a weight matrix. In this paper, we extend the score-based hypothesis test and change-point detection stopping rule into their diffusion-based analogs. Additionally, we theoretically quantify the performance of these diffusion-based algorithms and study scenarios where optimal performance is achievable. We propose a method of numerically optimizing the weight matrix and present numerical simulations to illustrate the advantages of diffusion-based algorithms.

II
Score-Based Models

In this section, we provide background on score-based models, which utilize the score
\nabla_{X}\log p(X)
of a distribution
P
.
We begin by defining the Fisher divergence between two distributions:

Definition II.1

(Fisher Divergence)
.

The Fisher divergence between distributions
P
and
Q
is defined to be:

\mathbb{D}_{\texttt{{F}}}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{{X}}\log p(X)-\nabla_{{X}}\log q(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right].

We next define the Hyvärinen score:

Definition II.2

(Hyvärinen Score)
.

We define the Hyvärinen score of
X
with respect to distribution
Q
as:

\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{{H}}}(X,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla\log q(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\Delta_{X}\log q(X),

(1)

where
\Delta_{X}
is the Laplacian operator:
\Delta_{X}f(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\partial^{2}f(X)}/{\partial X_{i}^{2}}
.

Lemma II.3

(Hyvärinen’s Theorem)
.

Let
s(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Under the assumption that
\nabla_{X}\log p(X)
and
s(X)
are differentiable with a continuous derivative, that
\nabla_{X}\log p(X)\rightarrow 0
and
s(X)\rightarrow 0
as
\|X\|\rightarrow\infty
, and that both
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|\nabla_{X}\log p(X)\|^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|s(X)\|^{2}]
are finite, we have that:

\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{X}\log p(X)-s(X)\|^{2}\bigg]=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{X}\log p(X)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|s(X)\|^{2}+tr(\nabla_{X}s(X))\bigg].

(2)

If for some distribution
Q
with density
q(X)
we set
s(X)=\nabla_{X}\log q(X)
, then we have that

\mathbb{D}_{\texttt{{F}}}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{X}\log p(X)\|^{2}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{{H}}}(X,Q)\bigg].

(3)

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
.
∎

If we do not know the density or score function of some distribution
P
, but do have a dataset
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of i.i.d. samples of
P
, we can use score matching (
[
11
,
12
,
20
]
) to estimate
\nabla_{X}\log p(X)
by
s_{\theta}(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\theta
parameterizes
s
.
Defining

J_{\theta}(X)=\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\|s_{\theta}(X)\|^{2}+tr(\nabla_{X}s_{\theta}(X))\bigg),

(4)

score matching optimizes

\min_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{\theta}(X_{i}).

(5)

We note that in the limit of large
n
,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{\theta}(X_{i})
converges to
\mathbb{E}_{P}[J_{\theta}(X)]
,
and that if there exists a distribution
Q_{\theta}
for which
s_{\theta}(X)=\nabla_{X}\log q_{\theta}(X)
, then
\mathbb{E}_{P}[J_{\theta}(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{{H}}}(X,Q_{\theta})]
. In this case, score matching produces the
\theta
which minimizes
\mathbb{D}_{\texttt{{F}}}(P\|Q_{\theta})
by (
3
). We note that score matching is analogous to maximum likelihood estimation when
\mathbb{D}_{\texttt{{F}}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{{H}}}
are replaced by
\mathbb{D}_{\texttt{{KL}}}
and negative-log-likelihood, respectively.

III
Score-Based Hypothesis Testing and Change-Point Detection

We now present background on hypothesis testing and change-point detection, which we shall refer to as our
detection tasks
. Both detection tasks require us to discern between two competing hypotheses: hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
, under which the data was generated by
P_{\infty}
, and hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, under which the data was generated by
P_{1}
. For both tasks, we discuss LLR-based and score-based solutions.

In this section and throughout the paper, we shall make the following assumptions regarding
P_{\infty}
and
P_{1}
:

Assumption III.1
.

We assume that:

1.

P_{\infty}
and
P_{1}
are both supported on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

2.

P_{\infty}\neq P_{1}
,

3.

P_{\infty},P_{1}
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and

4.

The densities
p_{\infty},p_{1}
are twice differentiable with continuous derivatives.

III-A

Simple Hypothesis Testing

Suppose
n
samples
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are drawn independently from some distribution
P_{*}
: under the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
,
P_{*}=P_{\infty}
and under the alternate hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
P_{*}=P_{1}
.
The task of simple hypothesis testing (hereafter referred to as hypothesis testing) is to decide whether or not to reject
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.

We define a
test

T
to be a function that maps data
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
to a decision:
T((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})\in\{0,1\}
. We reject
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
if
T((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=1
, and fail to reject
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
otherwise. A type I (type II) error occurs when the test incorrectly rejects (incorrectly fails to reject)
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. We denote the probabilities of type I and type II error as

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[T((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=1],

(6)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\beta_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{1}[T((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=0],

(7)

respectively. We refer to
n
as the
batch size
of test
T
.

The work of this paper is motivated by the Neyman-Pearson regime, in which one cannot tolerate a probability of type I error in excess of some threshold
\overline{\alpha}
. Thus, we wish to find a test
T
that is a solution of (or at least
approximates
a solution of):

\min_{T}\beta_{n}(T)\;\text{ subject to }\;\alpha_{n}(T)\leq\overline{\alpha}.

(8)

The
type II error exponent
describes the relationship between batch size and type II error probability for large
n
.

Definition III.2

(Type II Error Exponent)
.

For a test
T
, the type II error exponent
E
is given by

E=-\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(\beta_{n}(T))}{n}.

(9)

We refer to the type II error exponent of any test
T
as
\mathcal{B}(T)
.

We next proceed to consider two solutions to the problem of hypothesis testing.

III-A
1
Log-Likelihood Ratio Test

When one has complete knowledge of the densities of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
, one can use the log-likelihood ratio test:

T_{\texttt{{KL}}}^{c}((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X_{i})<c\\
1&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

(10)

where

Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)=\log p_{1}(X)-\log p_{\infty}(X),

(11)

and where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is some pre-determined threshold. Under the assumption that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X_{i})\neq c
almost surely, the Neyman-Pearson Lemma proves that the log-likelihood ratio test of (
10
) is optimal under the objective of (
8
) for all choices of
\overline{\alpha}\in(0,1)
and for all batch sizes
n\in\mathbb{N}
(
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
).

III-A
2
Hyvärinen Score Test

In
[
16
]
, a score-based hypothesis test which utilizes the Hyvärinen score was proposed. It is defined as

T_{\texttt{{F}}}^{c}((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X_{i})<c\\
1&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

(12)

where

Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X)=\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{{H}}}(X,P_{\infty})-\mathcal{S}_{\texttt{{H}}}(X,P_{1}),

(13)

and where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is some pre-determined threshold.

A composite hypothesis test was proposed in
[
16
]
for the case where only the null distribution is precisely known. Furthermore,
[
16
]
derived limiting distributions on
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X_{i})
for
X_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}P_{\infty}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and provides conditions under which this limiting distribution (after scaling by
\sqrt{n}
and shifting) is the standard normal distribution.

III-B

Online Change-Point Detection

We next consider a problem in which we must detect between
\mathcal{H}_{\infty},\mathcal{H}_{1}
under temporal constraints.
Suppose we are given probability distributions
P_{\infty},P_{1}
and a natural number
\nu\in\mathbb{N}
. A data-stream
(X_{t})^{\infty}_{i=1}
indexed by time
i\in\mathbb{N}
can be generated by the following process:

X_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\begin{cases}P_{\infty}&\text{ if }i<\nu,\\
P_{1}&\text{ if }i\geq\nu.\end{cases}

(14)

We observe the data stream in real-time: at any time
i
, we can see past and present data,
(X_{j})_{j\leq i}
, but cannot see future data,
(X_{j})_{j>i}
. Our objective is to raise an alarm as soon as possible after time
\nu
, but we wish to avoid prematurely raising the alarm before time
\nu
.

While hypothesis testing used a
function

T
, here we employ a
stopping rule

R
.
A
false alarm
is the event where
R
detects the change-point prematurely:
R<\nu
. In hypothesis testing, we considered the probability of type I error; in this setting, for any non-trivial stopping rule, the probability of a false alarm depends upon
\nu
and can be made arbitrarily close to one (zero) for high (low) values of
\nu
, so we instead consider the
mean time to false alarm
, a term which we shall use interchangeably with
average run length
. This quantity is given by:

\text{ARL}(\tau)=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau].

(15)

Most non-trivial stopping rules will accumulate evidence of a change for some duration of time after
\nu
. In this setting, we consider the mean time to alarm following
\nu
rather than the probability of instantaneous detection.
We define the worst-case average detection delay (
[
26
]
), which we shall also refer to as expected detection delay, by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{WADD}}}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\nu\geq 1}\esssup\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(\tau-\nu+1)^{+}|\mathcal{F}_{\nu-1}],

(16)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
refers to the expectation over data streams following (
14
) for a particular
\nu
. We seek to minimize the expected detection delay subject to a constraint on acceptable average run length
\overline{\gamma}
, as in
[
17
]
. Thus, we wish to find a stopping rule that is a solution of (or at least
approximates
a solution of):

\min_{\tau}\;\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{WADD}}}(\tau)\;\text{ subject to }\;\text{ARL}(\tau)\geq\overline{\gamma}.

(17)

Next, we consider one likelihood-based and one score-based solution to change-point detection.

III-B
1
Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) Algorithm

The Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) algorithm (
[
27
,
26
,
28
]
) is a popular solution to the problem of change-point detection which is optimal under the objective of (
17
).
The algorithm defines an
instantaneous detection score
,
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)
, which follows (
11
). It further defines a
cumulative detection score
by

Y_{\texttt{{KL}}}(t)=\begin{cases}0&t=0\\
\max(0,Y_{\texttt{{KL}}}(t-1)+Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X_{t}))&t>0,\end{cases}

(18)

and a
stopping rule
given by

\tau_{\texttt{{KL}}}^{c}=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:Y_{\texttt{{KL}}}(t)\geq c\}

(19)

for some choice of threshold
c\geq 0
.

III-B
2
Score-Based Cumulative Sum (SCUSUM) Algorithm

The Score-Based Cumulative Sum (SCUSUM) algorithm
[
17
]
replaces log-likelihood ratios by differences of Hyvärinen scores.
With instantaneous detection score
Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X)
defined as in (
13
), the SCUSUM algorithm defines a cumulative detection score as

Y_{\texttt{{F}}}(t)=\begin{cases}0&t=0\\
\max(0,Y_{\texttt{{F}}}(t-1)+Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X_{t}))&t>0,\end{cases}

(20)

and defines a stopping rule as

\tau_{\texttt{{F}}}^{c}=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:Y_{\texttt{{F}}}(t)\geq c\}

(21)

for some choice of stopping threshold
c>0
.

Remark III.3
.

The presentation of SCUSUM in
[
17
]
includes a scaling factor
\lambda>0
in the definition of
Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X)
. For the limited purposes of this presentation, we can omit
\lambda
without loss of generality.

The pre-change drift
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X)]
and post-change drift
\mathbb{E}_{1}[Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(X)]
were calculated in
[
17
]
, where it was further demonstrated that the pre-change drift is strictly negative and the post-change drift strictly positive under certain conditions. Furthermore,
[
17
]
provided theorems which bound the mean time to false alarm (
15
) and detection delay (
16
) for any given choice of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
.

IV
Diffusion-Based Detection

We have presented the Fisher divergence as well as a score-based test and stopping rule. In this section, we first define the diffusion divergence, which generalizes the Fisher divergence.
We next propose a diffusion-based test and stopping rule, generalizing the score-based algorithms introduced in Section
II
. We conclude by providing theorems which bound the properties of the proposed diffusion-based test and algorithm.

Though the score-based methods are special cases of the diffusion-based ones (and though the diffusion-based methods still utilize the score
\nabla\log p(X)
), we shall refer to the Fisher divergence-based methods as
score-based
and the methods utilizing
m(X)
as
diffusion-based
for clarity.

IV-A

Diffusion Divergence

A generalization of the Fisher divergence was studied in
[
18
]
and applied to change-point detection in
[
19
]
. This generalization, called the
diffusion divergence
, transforms the difference between scores in Definition
II.1
by multiplication with some matrix-valued function
m(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times w}
:

Definition IV.1

(Diffusion Divergence)
.

The diffusion divergence between distributions
P
and
Q
is defined to be:

\mathbb{D}_{m}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|m^{T}(X)\big(\nabla_{{X}}\log p(X)-\nabla_{{X}}\log q(X)\big)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

for some matrix-valued function
m(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times w}
.

We impose conditions upon
m(X)
in Section
IV
.
For ease of notation, we denote the
function

m(X)
simply as
m
in the subscript of
\mathbb{D}
.

We next define the
diffusion-Hyvärinen score
, which generalizes the Hyvärinen score of Definition
II.2
.

Definition IV.2

(Diffusion-Hyvärinen Score)
.

\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,P)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|m^{T}(X)(\nabla_{X}\log p(X))\right\|_{2}^{2}+\nabla\cdot m(X)m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X),

where we denote the divergence of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Jacobian
J_{f}
as
\nabla\cdot f(X)=tr(J_{f})=\sum_{i}{\partial f_{i}(X)}/{\partial X_{i}}
.

(22)

For distributions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,P)-\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,Q)]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,P)-\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,Q)]

IV-B

Diffusion-based Detection

We begin by imposing mild regularity conditions on

, which mirror the regularity conditions presented in

Assumption IV.5

(Diffusion-Hyvärinen Regularity Conditions

]
)

For
(R,S)\in\{(P_{\infty},P_{1}),(P_{1},P_{\infty})\}
, we assume that:

1.

m(X)
and
\nabla\log s(X)
are differentiable with continuous derivatives,

2.

\mathbb{E}_{R}[\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log s(X)\|^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{R}[\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log r(X)\|^{2}]<\infty
, and

3.

s(X)m(X)m^{T}(X)\nabla\log r(X)\rightarrow 0
as
\|X\|\rightarrow\infty
.

Lemma IV.6

(Finite Divergences)
.

Part
2
of Assumption
IV.5
implies that
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})<\infty
and
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})<\infty
.

.
∎

As an expectation over a norm, the diffusion divergence is strictly nonnegative for all distributions. We next provide an assumption that the diffusion divergence is nonzero, which we shall assume everywhere this paper:

Assumption IV.7

(Positive Divergences)
.

We assume that
m(X)
is chosen such that

\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})>0\text{ and }\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})>0.

Remark IV.8
.

The work of
[
18
]
uses the diffusion divergence to generalize score matching, which requires the diffusion divergence to satisfy
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P\|Q)=0\iff P=Q
for all
P,Q
. For noninvertible
m(X)
, there might exist
P,Q
for which
\nabla\log p(X)-\nabla\log q(X)
lies in the kernel of
m(X)
almost everywhere (causing
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P\|Q)=0
for
P\neq Q
), and hence
[
18
]
imposed a condition of invertability on
m(X)
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Throughout this paper, all diffusion divergences take arguments only from
\{P_{\infty},P_{1}\}
. Therefore, we require only Assumption
IV.7
and can therefore consider
m(X)
which is not invertible. Hence, Assumption
IV.7
permits
m(X)
to be non-square (
w\neq d
) and the theorems of this paper are given for arbitrary
w
.

Next, we generalize the test statistic of
[
16
]
and the instantaneous detection score of
[
17
]
to include
m(X)
.

Definition IV.9

(Diffusion Test Statistic and Instantaneous Detection Score)
.

For any choice of
m(X)
that satisfies Assumption

Z_{m}(X)=\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,P_{\infty})-\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,P_{1}),

(25)

where

follows Definition

.

Note that

is a function of its subscript unlike

and
Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(\cdot)

We next propose a diffusion-based hypothesis test.

Definition IV.10

(Diffusion Hypothesis Test)
.

We define

T_{m}^{c}((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})<c\\
1&\text{ else}\end{cases}

(26)

for some choice of stopping threshold
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

We further propose the diffusion change-point detection stopping rule.

Definition IV.11

(Diffusion Change-Point Detection Stopping Rule)
.

We define

Y_{m}(t)=\begin{cases}0&t=0\\
\max(0,Y_{m}(t-1)+Z_{m}(X_{t}))&t>0,\end{cases}

(27)

and

\tau_{m}^{c}=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}:Y_{m}(t)\geq c\}

(28)

for some choice of stopping threshold
c>0
.

For each of our detection tasks, we have generalized one unique score-based detection algorithm into a collection of diffusion-based algorithms, each of which is different in both its choice of

V
Optimization of the Diffusion-Matrix Function

In this section, we propose a method of finding the optimal
m(X)
.

V-A

Objective Function: Change-Point Detection

Under the objective presented in (
17
), we wish to minimize the detection delay (
16
) subject to a constraint on the average run length (
15
). Denoting our constraint on the average run length as
\overline{\gamma}
, we can set a stopping threshold to
c=\log\overline{\gamma}
; by Theorem
IV.13
the constraint of (
17
) (that ARL
(\tau)\geq\overline{\gamma}
) will be satisfied so long as
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]\leq 1
.

The expected detection delay is a function of stopping threshold and
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
. Since we have already set the stopping threshold
c=\log\overline{\gamma}
, we minimize (
32
) by maximizing
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
. This produces the objective:

\max_{m}\;\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})\;\text{ s.t. }\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]=1.

(33)

For any
m(X)
satisfying Assumption
IV.5
, and any
k>0
, it is easy to see that
Z_{km}(X)=k^{2}Z_{m}(X)
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and that
\mathbb{D}_{km}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})=k^{2}\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
. Thus, if we were to optimize by simply maximizing
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
, then for any
m(X)
,
\mathbb{D}_{km}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})>\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
whenever
k>1
. Thus, some constraint is necessary for a maximum of (
33
) to exist. Furthermore, in change-point detection (hypothesis testing), a stopping rule (test) using instantaneous detection score (statistic)
Z_{m}(\cdot)
and threshold
k
is identical to another stopping rule (test) using instantaneous detection score (statistic)
k^{2}Z_{m}(\cdot)
and threshold
k^{2}c
– adjusting the scale
k
does not actually change the algorithm (scale-invariance over the densities of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
is an advantage of score-based methods but scale-invariance over
m
frustrates this optimization). Optimization over only
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
may simply lead to a larger scaling on
m(X)
, and this optimization would neither improve the test nor converge to any limit.
Thus, some constraint on the scale of
m(X)
is necessary, and the condition
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]\leq 1
plays this role. Though the constraint was introduced as a proof technique, it is in fact essential to the optimization process.

V-B

Objective Function: Hypothesis Testing

We wish to find a diffusion hypothesis test between
P_{\infty},P_{1}
which performs reasonably well
for all batch sizes
n
. The popular objective function of (
8
), however, is explicitly a function of a particular batch size
n
. We modify the objective of (
8
) to better account for the variable nature of the batch size:

\max_{T}\;\mathcal{B}(T)\;\text{ subject to }\;\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}(T)\leq\overline{\alpha},

(34)

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
is defined by Definition
III.2
. Though the objective of (
34
) considers the performance of a test for large
n
, it does not depend upon any one particular choice of
n
.

For any
m(X)
following Assumption
IV.5
and satisfying
\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp(-Z_{m}(X))]\leq 1
, Theorem
IV.12
provides that
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})
is a lower-bound on the error exponent of some particular
T_{m}^{c}
which obeys
any constraint
on asymptotic type I error probability.
For any choice of
\overline{\alpha}
, we maximizing the type II error exponent of a test by maximizing its lower bound,
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})
, while enforcing
\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp(-Z_{m}(X))]=1
to satisfy the condition of the Theorem
IV.12
.

Thus, we propose that the following objective optimizes
m(X)
for the problem of hypothesis testing:

\max_{m}\;\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})\;\text{ s.t. }\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp(-Z_{m}(X))]=1.

(35)

Remark V.1
.

Recalling Definition
IV.9
, we observe that the objective of (
33
) takes the form of (
35
) when the roles of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
are interchanged.

V-C

Optimality of Diffusion-Based Hypothesis Testing and Change-Point Detection

We first demonstrate that for a special case of two Gaussian distributions with a common covariance matrix,
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(\cdot)=Z_{m}(\cdot)
for a particular choice of
m(X)
:

Theorem V.2
.

Consider vectors
\mu_{\infty},\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and positive definite matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let
P_{\infty}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\infty},V)
and
P_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},V)
, and define
M^{*}=V^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Then,

\forall X\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\;Z_{M^{*}}(X)=Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X).

(36)

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
.
∎

In general, we do not expect any particular choice of
m(X)
to exactly recover the LLR-based tests. We demonstrate using another pair of Gaussians that there does not always exist an
m(X)
such that
Z_{m}(\cdot)=Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(\cdot)
with probability one.

Theorem V.3
.

There exists a pair of distributions
P_{\infty},P_{1}
such that for all
m(X)
,

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[Z_{m}(X)\neq Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)]>0\;\text{ and }\;\mathbb{P}_{1}[Z_{m}(X)\neq Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)]>0.

Proof.

The proof is given in Appendix
B
.
∎

Remark V.4
.

For any
P_{\infty},P_{1}
,
Z_{m}(\cdot)=Z_{\texttt{{F}}}(\cdot)
if
m(X)=I
.

It is possible to bound the performance of the
best
diffusion-based algorithm – the algorithm corresponding to the
best
choice of
m(X)
– in terms of the performance of the score-based and LLR-based algorithms. By Remark
V.4
, we observe that the best diffusion-based algorithms can perform no worse than their score-based peers (though diffusion algorithms corresponding to a poorly-chosen
m(X)
can perform arbitrarily poorly). The diffusion-based algorithms can never outperform their LLR-based counterparts, however, as the latter are provably optimal under (
8
) and (
17
).

While we know that CUSUM (
19
) is optimal under the metric of (
17
), in general there can be stopping rules distinct from CUSUM which match the performance (
[
30
,
31
]
) of CUSUM. Therefore, while for some choices of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
we do not know whether LLR-like performance is attainable (such as for the
P_{\infty},P_{1}
of Theorem
V.3
), we do know that this optimal performance is attainable for other choices of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
(such as those of Theorem
V.2
).

In general, and especially for high-dimensional
P_{\infty},P_{1}
, the best possible
m(X)
will not be analytically identifiable. In the general case, we simply wish to find an
m(X)
that approximates a solution to (
33
), (
35
). In the next section, we turn to machine learning to help with this approximation.

V-D

Numerical Optimization

We propose a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) network
m:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times w}
to approximate the best possible matrix-valued function for a given
P_{\infty},P_{1}
.
The MLP
m(X)
can be trained to approximate a solution of (
33
) or (
35
).

While we wish to optimize the MLP via gradient descent, the method of gradient descent requires differentiability of the loss function. The constraints (
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]=1
of (
33
) and
\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp(-Z_{m}(X))]=1
of (
35
)) are not differentiable, so gradient descent cannot be applied directly to these objectives. We turn to the use of regularization to circumvent this issue.
We propose the loss functions
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{CPD}}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{HT}}}
to numerically optimize
m(X)
for change-point detection and hypothesis testing, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{CPD}}}(m)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})+\alpha\big[\log\mathbb{E}_{\infty}(\exp Z_{m}(X))\big]^{2},

(37)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{HT}}}(m)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})+\alpha\big[\log\mathbb{E}_{1}(\exp(-Z_{m}(X)))\big]^{2},

(38)

where
\alpha>0
is a training hyperparameter.
The condition
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]=1
of (
33
) (
\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp(-Z_{m}(X))]=1
of (
35
)) is satisfied if and only if
[\log\mathbb{E}_{\infty}(\exp Z_{m}(X))]^{2}=0
(
[\log\mathbb{E}_{1}(\exp(-Z_{m}(X)))]^{2}=0
). Therefore, though it does not strictly enforce the exact conditions, gradient descent over
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{CPD}}}(m)
or
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{{HT}}}(m)
causes the neural network
m(X)
to approximately satisfy the conditions of (
33
) or (
35
).

In this section, we implement and simulate the diffusion-based methods and compare their performances against those of their LLR-based and score-based analogs for both hypothesis testing and change-point detection.
We begin by introducing three model classes with analytical score functions.

VI-A

Model Classes

In this section, we shall use the notation of
p(X)
to refer to the density of some distribution
P
, and shall use the notation of
\tilde{p}
to refer to the
unnormalized
density of some distribution
P
. Two of our three model classes provide only an unnormalized density.

For each model class below, the simulations are performed with parameters chosen in light of
[
17
, Proposition 5]
to demonstrate cases where score-based methods noticeably underperform LLR-based methods. We provide details regarding parameters of our model classes in Appendix
A
.

VI-A
1
Gaussian Distribution

The multivariate Gaussian distribution is parameterized by a vector
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a positive-definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Its density ands score function are given by:

p(X)=\frac{\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}(X-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)\bigg)}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{d}|\Sigma|}},

(39)

\nabla\log p(X)=-\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu).

(40)

VI-A
2
Gauss-Bernoulli Restricted Boltzmann Machine

The Gauss-Bernoulli Restricted Boltzmann Machine (GB-RBM) (
[
14
]
) is simultaneously an energy-based model and a score-based model. Thus, it is a natural candidate for comparisons between LLR-based and score-based algorithms.

As presented in
[
14
, Equation 3]
, the conditional density of visible variable conditioned upon latent variable is a Gaussian distribution with diagonal covariance; here, we relax this condition and require only that it be positive definite. For this formulation of the GB-RBM with latent dimension
h
, the model is parameterized by vectors
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\phi\in\mathbb{R}^{h},W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times h}
, and positive definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

We first provide the free-energy of the GB-RBM

F(X)=\frac{1}{2}(X-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)-\mathbf{1}^{T}\text{Softplus}(\phi+W^{T}\Sigma^{-1}X),

(41)

from which the unnormalized density and score function of the GB-RBM can be expressed:

\tilde{p}(X)=\exp(-F(X)),

(42)

\nabla\log\tilde{p}(X)=-\nabla F(X)=-\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)+\Sigma^{-1}W\text{ Sigmoid}(\phi+W^{T}\Sigma^{-1}X).

(43)

VI-B

Quartic Exponential Model

We finally consider an exponential model class that is quartic (fourth-order) in
X
. This model is parameterized by
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and positive-definite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. The unnormalized density and score function of this model are:

\tilde{p}(X)=\exp\bigg(-(X^{\odot 2}-\mu)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(X^{\odot 2}-\mu)\bigg),

(44)

\nabla\log\tilde{p}(X)=-4\Sigma^{-1}(X^{\odot 2}-\mu)\odot X,

(45)

where
\odot
denotes element-wise multiplication and
X^{\odot 2}
denotes element-wise squaring. We note that if
\mu=0
, the density of (
44
) can be rewritten as

\tilde{p}(X)=\exp\bigg(-\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\Sigma_{i,j}X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2}\bigg).

(46)

VI-C

Implementation of Algorithms

VI-C
1
LLR-Based Algorithms

For model classes that provide normalized densities, we implement the LLR-based algorithms following their description in Section
II
. For model classes involving unnormalized densities, we must modify the test statistic and instantaneous detection score
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(\cdot)
to account for the lack of normalization. Define

C_{\infty}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{p}_{\infty}(X)dX\quad\text{ and }\quad C_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tilde{p}_{1}(X)dX.

(47)

Then, the LLR-based test statistic and instantaneous detection score is

\displaystyle Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p_{1}(X)}{p_{\infty}(X)}=\log\frac{\tilde{p}_{1}(X)}{\tilde{p}_{\infty}(X)}\frac{C_{\infty}}{C_{1}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\tilde{p}_{1}(X)}{\tilde{p}_{\infty}(X)}+\log\frac{C_{\infty}}{C_{1}},

(48)

which present a problem, as
C_{\infty}/C_{1}
is not known.
It can, however, be calculated from an expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}\bigg[\frac{\tilde{p}_{\infty}(X)}{\tilde{p}_{1}(X)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{1}\bigg[\frac{{p}_{\infty}(X)}{{p}_{1}(X)}\frac{C_{\infty}}{C_{1}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{1}(X)\frac{p_{\infty}(X)}{{p}_{1}(X)}\frac{C_{\infty}}{C_{1}}dX

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\infty}}{C_{1}}\int p_{\infty}(X)dX=\frac{C_{\infty}}{C_{1}},

which we can approximate with a sample mean:

\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{P}_{\infty},\tilde{P}_{1}}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\tilde{p}_{\infty}(X_{i})}{\tilde{p}_{1}(X_{i})}

(49)

for a dataset
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of i.i.d. samples of
P_{1}
.
Thus, for our LLR-based tests, we let

\hat{Z}_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)=\log\frac{\tilde{p}_{1}(X)}{\tilde{p}_{\infty}(X)}+\log\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{P}_{\infty},\tilde{P}_{1}}^{n}

(50)

play the role of
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)
. We note that the estimation of
\mathcal{R}_{\tilde{P}_{\infty},\tilde{P}_{1}}^{n}
requires a significant quantity
n
of data and that this estimation becomes generally intractable as the data dimension becomes large.

VI-C
2
Score-Based Algorithms

Score-based algorithms follow the implementation given in Section
II
. We note that for any unnormalized density
\tilde{p}
with normalizing constant
Z
:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p(X)

\displaystyle=\nabla\log\frac{\tilde{p}(X)}{Z}

\displaystyle=\nabla\log\tilde{p}(X)-\nabla\log Z=\nabla\log\tilde{p}(X)

(51)

and hence the score-based algorithms are invariant to the scale of
\tilde{p}_{\infty}(\cdot),\tilde{p}_{1}(\cdot)
.

VI-C
3
Diffusion-Based Algorithms

Like the score-based algorithms, diffusion-based algorithms are also scale invariant; hence, the diffusion-based algorithms do not need to account for scaling constants
C_{\infty},C_{1}
.

We simulate square matrix-valued functions. We let a feed-forward neural network play the role of
m(X)
, mapping data in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. We optimize this choice of
m(X)
following the loss function of (
37
). Details regarding the architecture of this neural network and its training are provided in Appendix
A
.

VI-D

Results

For both hypothesis testing and change-point detection, and for each of the three proposed model classes, we compare the performances of the diffusion-based algorithms against the performances of their score-based and LLR-based counterparts.

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a hypothesis test and a change-point detection stopping rule which utilize the diffusion divergence. We have studied the properties of these methods, calculating and bounding their performance metrics. We have developed an objective over the diffusion matrix function for hypothesis testing and change-point detection applications.
We have demonstrated that the performances of the best-possible diffusion algorithms are no worse than the performances of the score-based algorithms and no better than the performances of the LLR algorithms.
We have proposed a loss function for the training of
m(X)
and demonstrated the stability of this process by way of simulation. Finally, we have demonstrated the implementability of the diffusion-based algorithms in numerical simulations.

Lemma B.1
.

For completeness, we reproduce this Lemma and proof from
[
29
]
[Lemma 4].

For differentiable
g,h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}h(X)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(X)}{\partial X_{1}}dX_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty,b\rightarrow-\infty}g(a,X_{2},\cdots X_{n})h(a,X_{2},\cdots,X_{n})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad-g(b,X_{2},\cdots,X_{n})h(b,X_{2},\cdots,X_{n})

\displaystyle\;-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}g(X)\frac{\partial h(X)}{\partial X_{1}}dX_{1}

(55)

The same follows for all
X_{i}\neq X_{1}
.

Proof.

By the product rule:

\frac{\partial g(X)h(X)}{\partial X_{1}}=g(X)\frac{\partial h(X)}{\partial X_{1}}+h(X)\frac{\partial g(X)}{\partial X_{1}}

(56)

Rearranging:

h(X)\frac{\partial g(X)}{\partial X_{1}}=\frac{\partial g(X)h(X)}{\partial X_{1}}-g(X)\frac{\partial h(X)}{\partial X_{1}}

(57)

The result follows from integrating both sides of (
57
) over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
s(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a differentiable function with continuous derivatives and let
P
be a probability distribution with density
p
absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Let
(v)_{i}
denote the
i
-th element of vector
v
.

If

\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}]<\infty\;\text{ and }\;\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|s(X)\|^{2}]<\infty,

(58)

then

\mathbb{E}_{P}[|(\nabla\log p(X))_{i}(s(X))_{i}|^{2}]<\infty

(59)

for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

Proof.

By the definition of the norm, we can say that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\sum_{i}((\nabla\log p(X))_{i})^{2}\bigg]

(60)

so
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\|\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}]<\infty
implies that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[((\nabla\log p(X))_{i})^{2}]<\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d
. By similar reasoning, the assumptions of this Lemma imply that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[((s(X))_{i})^{2}]<\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq d
.

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[|(\nabla\log p(X))_{i}(s(X))_{i}|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{P}[((\nabla\log p(X))_{i})^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{P}[((s(X))_{i})^{2}]}

(61)

and thus the quantity of interest is bounded from above by finite terms.

For the special choice of
s(X)=\nabla\log q(X)
, then Assumption
IV.5
satisfies the assumptions of this Lemma, and this Lemma demonstrates that

\mathbb{E}_{P}[|(\nabla\log p(X))_{i}(\nabla\log q(X))_{i}|]<\infty

(62)

for all
1\leq i\leq d
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
IV.3
.

This proof follows the arguments of
[
29
]
[Theorem 1]. For completeness, we reproduce a modified version. For generality, we prove this theorem for arbitrary
s(X)
, but note that when we let
s(X)=\nabla\log q(X)
, then this theorem calculates a new form for
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P\|Q)
.

Let
s(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
be differentiable with a continuous derivative. We calculate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\big(\nabla\log p(X)-s(X)\big)\|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)s(X)\|^{2}-\underbrace{(m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X))^{T}m^{T}(X)s(X)}_{\text{Term 1}}\bigg].

(63)

We examine Term 1 in more detail:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[(m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X))^{T}m^{T}(X)s(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\nabla\log p(X))^{T}m(X)m^{T}(X)s(X)].

(64)

We define

f(X)=m(X)m^{T}(X)s(X).

(65)

Letting
v_{i}
and
(v)_{i}
denote the
i
-the element of a vector
v
, we can simply the expression of (
64
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\nabla\log p(X))^{T}f(X)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)(\nabla\log p(X))^{T}f(X)dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\nabla\log p(X))_{i}(f(X))_{i}dX

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)(\nabla\log p(X))_{i}(f(X))_{i}dX

(66)

We next choose to calculate the integrand of (
66
) for the case of
i=1
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)(\nabla\log p(X))_{1}(f(X))_{1}dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)\frac{\partial\log p(X)}{\partial X_{1}}(f(X))_{1}dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)\frac{\frac{\partial p(X)}{\partial X_{1}}}{p(X)}(f(X))_{1}dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\partial p(X)}{\partial X_{1}}(f(X))_{1}dX

(67)

By the result of Lemma
B.2
, we can invoke Fubini’s Theorem to expand the integral of (
67
) into a double integral:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\partial p(X)}{\partial X_{1}}(f(X))_{1}dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\underbrace{\bigg(\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\partial p(X)}{\partial X_{1}}(f(X))_{1}dX_{1}\bigg)}_{\text{Term 2}}d(X_{2},\cdots X_{n}).

(68)

Next, we apply Lemma
B.1
to Term 2, letting
p(X)
play the role of
g(X)
and
(f(X))_{1}
play the role of
h(X)
. The limit of Lemma
B.1
evaluates to zero by Part
3
of Assumption
IV.5
.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\bigg(0-\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}p(X)\frac{\partial(f(X))_{1}}{\partial X_{1}}dX_{1}\bigg)d(X_{2},\cdots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)\frac{\partial(f(X))_{1}}{\partial X_{1}}dX

(69)

where in the last step we collapse the double integral into one single integral over
X
.

Alltogether, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\nabla\log p(X))^{T}f(X)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)(\nabla\log p(X))_{i}(f(X))_{i}dX

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)\frac{\partial(f(X))_{i}}{\partial X_{i}}dX

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(X)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial(f(X))_{i}}{\partial X_{i}}dX

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\nabla\cdot f(X)]

(70)

Returning to (
B
), we substitute and arrive at:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)(\nabla\log p(X)-s(X))\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)s(X)\|^{2}-\nabla\cdot m(X)m^{T}(X)s(X)\bigg].

(71)

For the special case where
s(X)=\nabla\log q(X)
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{m}(P\|Q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)(\nabla\log p(X)-\nabla\log q(X))\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log q(X)\|^{2}-\nabla\cdot m(X)m^{T}(X)\nabla\log q(X)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{m}(x,Q)\bigg]

(72)

∎

.

This proof closely follows the arguments presented in
[
17

We know that

\mathbb{D}_{m}(P\|Q)=\mathbb{E}_{P}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)\nabla\log p(X)\|^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,Q)\bigg]

(73)

We observe that the first term inside the expectation does not depend upon

Proof of Lemma
IV.6
.

Letting

\overline{r}(X)=m^{T}(X)\nabla\log r(X),\quad\overline{s}(X)=m^{T}(X)\nabla\log s(X),

then
\mathbb{D}_{m}(R\|S)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{R}[\|\overline{r}(X)-\overline{s}(X)\|^{2}]
.
Then, by the triangle inequality,

\|\overline{r}(X)-\overline{s}(X)\|\leq\|\overline{r}(X)\|+\|\overline{s}(X)\|

Finally,

Proof of Theorem
IV.12
.

This proof follows the arguments of the Stein Diffusion Lemma as presented in
[
34
, Theorem 11.1]
(forward direction only).

Fix some arbitrarily small
\delta>0
and set
c=n(\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})-\delta)
. We recall Definition
IV.10
:

T_{m}^{c}((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})<c\\
1&\text{ else. }\end{cases}

(76)

Recalling (
6
), the type I error probability of
T_{m}^{c}
is given by

\alpha_{n}(T_{m}^{c})=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})\geq c\bigg].

(77)

By the law of large numbers, we know that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})\bigg|_{X_{i}\sim P_{\infty}}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[Z_{m}(X)]\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

(78)

and we have demonstrated in (
23
) that

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[Z_{m}(X)]=-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1}).

(79)

For the
\delta>0
previously chosen, the definition of convergence guarantees that for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists some
N
for which
n>N
implies that

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})\geq(\delta-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1}))\bigg]<\epsilon.

(80)

Rearranging the inequality and substituting
c=n(\delta-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty}))
, we can say that for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists an
N
such that
n>N
implies:

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})\geq c\bigg]<\epsilon.

(81)

Letting
\epsilon=\overline{\alpha}
establishes that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\alpha_{n}(T_{m}^{c})\leq\overline{\alpha}
for all
\overline{\alpha}\in(0,1)
.

Next, we calculate the type II error probability. Recalling (
7
), the type II error probability of
T_{m}^{c}
is given by:

\beta_{n}(T_{m}^{c})=\mathbb{P}_{1}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})<c\bigg].

(82)

We apply the Chernoff Bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{1}\bigg[\exp\bigg(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{m}(X_{i})\bigg)>\exp-c\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\inf_{\theta>0}e^{-\theta(-c)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[\theta\exp-Z_{m}(X_{i})]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq e^{c}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp-Z_{m}(X_{i})].

(83)

We assume that
\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp-Z_{m}(X_{i})]\leq 1
. Thus:

e^{c}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{1}[\exp-Z_{m}(X_{i})]\leq e^{c},

(84)

and

\beta_{n}(T_{m}^{c})\leq e^{c}=\exp\bigg(n(\delta-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1}))\bigg).

(85)

This result holds for all
\delta>0
. By Definition
III.2
, it follows that
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})\leq\mathcal{B}(T_{m}^{c})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
IV.13
.

This proof closely follows the arguments of Theorem 3 in
[
17
, Theorem 3]
.
We reproduce this proof with modification for completeness.
We prove in many steps:

Construction of a Random Walk and Martingale:

We first define

\delta=-\log(\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]).

(86)

From the condition of Theorem
IV.13
,
\delta\geq 0
. Next, define

\tilde{Z}_{m}(X)=Z_{m}(X)+\delta

(87)

where
Z_{m}(X)
follows Definition
IV.9
.
We further define

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{a}^{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=a}^{b}\tilde{Z}_{m}(X_{i})

\displaystyle\tilde{G}(n)

\displaystyle=\exp\tilde{W}_{1}^{n}=\exp\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{Z}_{m}(X_{i})\bigg)

We observe that
\tilde{W}
is a random walk that can take negative values.
Using Lemma
IV.4
and Jensen’s Inequality with respect to the convex function
-\log(\cdot)
:

\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})=-\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[Z_{m}(X)]>-\log E_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X)]=\delta.

(88)

We present Jensen’s Inequality as a strict inequality (it could achieve equality only if
Z_{m}(X)
is almost surely constant, but this would imply that
D_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})=\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})=0
, violating Assumption
IV.7
).
Using (
88
), we observe:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{Z}_{m}(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[Z_{m}(X)+\delta]

\displaystyle<-\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})+\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{\infty}\|P_{1})=0.

(89)

As
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{Z}_{m}(X)]<0
, we note that
\tilde{W}
is a random walk with a strictly negative drift.

We observe that

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{G}(n+1)|\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\tilde{G}(n)\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp(\tilde{Z}_{m}(X_{n+1})]\\
=\tilde{G}(n)e^{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X_{n+1})]=\tilde{G}(n),

(90)

and that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{G}(n)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\bigg[\exp\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Z_{m}(X_{i})+\delta)\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=e^{n\delta}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\exp Z_{m}(X_{i})]=1,

(91)

so
\tilde{G}
is a non-negative martingale with mean one under
P_{\infty}
.

Construction of Stopping Rule
\tilde{T}
:

Next, we define a stopping time:

\tilde{T}=\inf\bigg\{n\geq 1:\bigg(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\tilde{W}_{k}^{n}\bigg)\geq c\bigg\}

(92)

We note that
\tilde{T}
cannot be trivially calculated due to the strictly negative drift of the random walk given by
\tilde{W}_{k}^{n}
.

Construction of Stopping Rule
M
:

We define a sequence of stopping times:

\displaystyle\eta_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{t:\tilde{W}_{1}^{t}<0\bigg\},

\displaystyle\eta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{t>\eta_{k}:\tilde{W}_{\eta_{k}+1}^{t}<0\bigg\},

and

M=\inf\bigg\{k\geq 0:\eta_{k}<\infty\text{ and }\tilde{W}_{\eta_{k}+1}^{n}>c\text{ for some }n>\eta_{k}\bigg\}.

(93)

We can see that
M\leq\tilde{T}
. Since
\tilde{Z}_{m}(X)\geq Z_{m}(X)
, we know that
\tilde{T}\leq T
. Hence,
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[T]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[M]
.

Calculation of
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(M>k)
:

As an intermediate step in the calculation of
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[M]
, we first calculate
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(M>k)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[M>k]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{M>k\}}]=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{M>k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{M\geq k\}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbbm{1}_{\{M>k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{M\geq k\}}|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbb{P}(M\geq k+1|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}})\mathbbm{1}_{\{M\geq k\}}].

We next consider the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(M\geq k+1|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}})
.

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(M\geq k+1|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}})=1-\mathbb{P}_{\infty}(M\leq k|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}}).

(94)

We calculate
\mathbb{P}(M\leq k|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg(\tilde{W}_{\eta_{k}+1}^{n}>c\text{ for some }n>\eta_{k}\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg(\tilde{W}_{1}^{n}\text{ for some }n\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg(\bigg(\max_{n:n\leq t}\tilde{W}_{1}^{n}\bigg)\geq c\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg(\bigg(\max_{n:n\leq t}\exp(\tilde{W}_{1}^{n})\bigg)>e^{c}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\bigg(\bigg(\max_{n:n\leq t}\tilde{G}(n)\bigg)>e^{c}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{G}(t)]}{e^{c}}=e^{-c},

where in the first step we rely upon the i.i.d. (and hence stationary) nature of
X_{i}
and in the last step we invoke Doob’s submartingale inequality (
[
35
]
), noting that
\tilde{G}(t)
is a nonnegative martingale with mean one under
P_{\infty}
.

From (
94
), we know that
\mathbb{P}(M\geq k+1|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}})

=1-e^{-c}
and hence that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[M>k]=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[M\geq k+1|\mathcal{F}_{\eta_{k}}]\mathbbm{1}_{\{M\geq k\}}]

\displaystyle\quad\geq(1-e^{-c})\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[M>k-1]

\displaystyle\quad\geq(1-e^{-c})^{2}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[M>k-2]

\displaystyle\quad\geq\cdots\geq(1-e^{-c})^{k}.

We next sum a geometric series:

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[M]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M>k)\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-c})^{k}=\frac{1}{1-(1-e^{-c})}=e^{c}

(95)

and we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[T]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tilde{T}]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[M]\geq e^{c}.

(96)

∎

Proof of Theorem

This proof closely follows the arguments presented in

Proof of Theorem

Direct calculation gives:

\displaystyle Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(X-\mu_{1})^{T}V^{-1}(X-\mu_{1})

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}(X-\mu_{\infty})^{T}V^{-1}(X-\mu_{\infty}).

Without loss of generality, we can substitute

\mu_{\infty}
and substitute

in place of

In the same way, we calculate

Proof of Theorem
V.3
.

Consider
P_{\infty}=\mathcal{N}(0,1/4)
and
P_{1}=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Here,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d=1
. There does not exist any matrix-valued function
m(X):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d\times w}
such that
Z_{m}(X)=Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)
across all
X\in\mathbb{R}
. Define

\displaystyle P_{\infty}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\;P_{1}=\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(109)

These distributions are supported on
\mathbb{R}
. Suppose for contradiction that there exists some
m(X):\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that:

Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)=Z_{m}(X)\;\;\forall X\in\mathbb{R}.

(110)

where
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)
is defined in (
11
). Defining

u(X)=m^{2}(X),

(111)

we can see that
u(X)\geq 0
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}
. We shall demonstrate a contradiction by showing that
u(X)
cannot be positive for all
X\in\mathbb{R}
when
\sigma=1/2
.

To simplify notation, we denote

\displaystyle s_{\infty}(X)

\displaystyle=\nabla\log p_{\infty}(X),

(112)

\displaystyle s_{1}(X)

\displaystyle=\nabla\log p_{1}(X).

(113)

When
X,m(X)
are scalars, then
s_{\infty}(X),s_{1}(X)
are also scalars, and we can re-write the diffusion-based instantaneous detection score of Definition
IV.9
in simpler terms. We denote the derivatives of
u(X),s_{\infty}(X)
, and
s_{1}(X)
as
u^{\prime}(X),s_{\infty}^{\prime}(X)
, and
s_{1}^{\prime}(X)
, respectively.

\displaystyle Z_{\sqrt{u}}(X)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u(X)s_{\infty}^{2}(X)+\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\bigg(u(X)s_{\infty}(X)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{2}u(X)s_{1}^{2}(X)-\frac{\partial}{\partial X}\bigg(u(X)s_{1}(X)\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}u(X)\big(s_{\infty}^{2}(X)-s_{1}^{2}(X)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+u^{\prime}(X)\big(s_{\infty}(X)-s_{1}(X)\big)

\displaystyle\quad+u(X)\big(s_{\infty}^{\prime}(X)-s_{1}^{\prime}(X)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u(X)\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(s_{\infty}^{2}(X)-s_{1}^{2}(X))+(s_{\infty}^{\prime}(X)-s_{1}^{\prime}(X)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+u^{\prime}(X)\bigg(s_{\infty}(X)-s_{1}(X)\bigg).

(114)

We next calculate
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)
. We note that for a scalar Gaussian distribution
P=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma)
:

\log p(X)=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(X-\mu)^{2},

(115)

and so

\displaystyle\log p_{1}(X)-\log p_{\infty}(X)

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}X^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}X^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\log(\sigma)+\frac{X^{2}}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-1\bigg).

(116)

Setting
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)=Z_{m}(X)
, we arrive at the first-order ordinary differential equation (ODE):

u(X)\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(s_{\infty}^{2}(X)-s_{1}^{2}(X))+(s_{\infty}^{\prime}(X)-s_{1}^{\prime}(X))\bigg)+u^{\prime}(X)\bigg(s_{\infty}(X)-s_{1}(X)\bigg)=\log(\sigma)+\frac{X^{2}}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-1\bigg).

(117)

We calculate:

\displaystyle s_{\infty}(X)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}X,\;s_{1}(X)=-X,

(118)

and further observe that
s_{\infty}(X)-s_{1}(X)=(1-\sigma^{-2})X
, that
s_{\infty}^{\prime}(X)-s_{1}^{\prime}(X)=(1-\sigma^{-2})
, and that
s_{\infty}^{2}(X)-s_{1}^{2}(X)=(\sigma^{-4}-1)X^{2}
.
Plugging into (
117
):

\displaystyle u(X)\bigg(\frac{1}{2}X^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}-1\bigg)+\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle+u^{\prime}(X)\bigg(X\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=\log(\sigma)+\frac{X^{2}}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-1\bigg).

(119)

We next divide through by
(1-\sigma^{-2})X
, noting that this term has a zero at
X=0
. We shall take care not to integrate the resulting ODE through
X=0
. Furthermore, this also introduces a zero if
\sigma=1
, though in this case
p_{\infty}(X)=p_{1}(X)
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}
, rendering detection impossible.
Performing this division, we get that

u(X)k(X)+u^{\prime}(X)=r(X),

(120)

where

k(X)=\frac{\frac{1}{2}X^{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}-1\bigg)+\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}{X\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}=-\gamma X+\frac{1}{X},

(121)

r(X)=\frac{\log(\sigma)+\frac{X^{2}}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}-1\bigg)}{X\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)}=\frac{\delta}{X}-\frac{X}{2},

(122)

and where

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}}{1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}}\bigg),\quad\quad\delta=\bigg(\frac{\log\sigma}{1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}}\bigg).

(123)

We note that
\gamma,\delta
are independent of
X
and that they are positive for all
\sigma>0
.

We shall attempt to solve this ODE via the integrating factor method. We calculate that
\int k(X)dX=\int-\gamma X+X^{-1}dX=-\frac{\gamma}{2}X^{2}+\log|X|
(note that it is not necessary to include a constant of integration
C
, as any such constant would cancel in the step of (
125
)). We define

\displaystyle D(X)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(\int k(X)dX\bigg)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma}{2}X^{2}\bigg)|X|.

(124)

We recall the ODE of (
B
) and multiply through by
D(X)
:

D(X)u(X)k(X)+D(X)u^{\prime}(X)=D(X)r(X).

(125)

We observe that
\frac{\partial}{\partial X}D(X)=D(X)k(X)
and that
\frac{\partial}{\partial X}D(X)u(X)=D(X)k(X)u(X)+D(X)u^{\prime}(X)
, which is the left-hand-side of (
125
). We make this substitution to the left-hand-side and apply the Fundamental Theorems of Calculus:

D(X)u(X)-D(a)u(a)=\int_{a}^{X}D(y)r(y)dy.

(126)

We know by Lemma
IV.6
that the diffusion divergence is bounded. Recalling (
118
), we can express
\mathbb{D}_{m}(P_{1}\|P_{\infty})
as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{1}\bigg[\big\|m^{T}(X)(\nabla\log p_{1}(X)-\nabla\log p_{\infty}(X))\big\|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}X^{2}}u(X)X^{2}dX<\infty.

(127)

Consider
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
. For this choice of
\sigma
, we have that
\gamma=2.5>1
. Recalling that
u(X)\geq 0
for all
X
and noting that
e^{h(X)},X^{2},|X|\geq 0
for all
X,h(\cdot)
, we have that:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}X^{2}}u(X)X^{2}dX<\infty

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}X^{2}}u(X)X^{2}dX<\infty

(128)

as
\frac{\gamma}{2}>\frac{1}{2}
. Furthermore, for all
X\not\in[-1,1]
, we know that
X^{2}>|X|
. Thus, the convergence of (
B
) implies:

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}X^{2}}u(X)|X|dX<\infty.

(129)

We note that the integral of (
129
) is equivalent to
e^{-C}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}D(X)u(X)dX
. As all terms of the integrand are non-negative for all
X
, the convergence is absolute and implies that

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}D(X)u(X)=\lim_{X\rightarrow-\infty}D(X)u(X)=0.

(130)

Plugging (
130
) into (
126
), we get that for all
X<0
:

D(X)u(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{X}D(y)r(y)dy.

(131)

We now expand the integrand:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{X}D(y)r(y)dy

\displaystyle\quad=\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{X}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma}{2}y^{2}\bigg)\frac{|y|}{y}\delta\;dy}_{\text{term 1}}

\displaystyle\quad-\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{X}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\gamma}{2}y^{2}\bigg)|y|\frac{y}{2}\;dy}_{\text{term 2}}.

If
X<0
, then
y<0
and
\frac{|y|}{y}=-1
. Thus:

\text{term 1}=-\delta\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}}\Phi(X\sqrt{\gamma}),

(132)

where
\Phi
denotes the CDF of a standard scalar Gaussian with mean zero and standard deviation one. We next integrate term 2 using integration by parts, keeping in mind that
X<0\implies y<0
, and hence
y|y|=-y^{2}
:

term 2

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{X}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}y^{2}}\frac{(-y^{2})}{2}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\int_{-\infty}^{X}(-\gamma ye^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}y^{2}})(y)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\bigg([\gamma ye^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}y^{2}}]_{-\infty}^{X}-\int_{-\infty}^{X}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}y^{2}}dy\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\gamma}\bigg(Xe^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}X^{2}}-\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\gamma}}\Phi(X\sqrt{\gamma})\bigg).

(133)

We now know that

u(X)=\frac{1}{D(X)}\underbrace{\bigg(\zeta\Phi(X\sqrt{\gamma})-\frac{1}{2\gamma}Xe^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}X^{2}}\bigg)}_{\tilde{u}(X)},

(134)

where
\zeta=(-\delta-(2\gamma)^{-1})\sqrt{2\pi\gamma^{-1}}
.
We observe that
D(X)\geq 0
for all
X

u(X)
is equal to the sign of
\tilde{u}(X)
. Evaluating, we observe that
\tilde{u}(-1)\approx 0.054>0
but
\tilde{u}(-0.05)\approx-0.013<0

u(X)<0
for some
X\in\mathbb{R}

m(X):\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
can enforce that
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)=Z_{m}(X)
for all
X\in\mathbb{R}
.

Consider a more general case, where

. Letting
s_{P}(X)=\nabla\log p(X)

\mathcal{S}_{m}(X,P)=\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)s_{P}(X)\|^{2}+\nabla\cdot m(X)m^{T}(X)s_{P}(X).

(135)

Expanding the first term:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|m^{T}(X)

\displaystyle s_{P}(X)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|(m_{1}(X)s_{P}(X),\cdots m_{w}(X)s_{P}(X))^{T}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(m_{1}^{2}(X)s_{P}^{2}(X)+\cdots+m_{w}^{2}(X)s_{P}^{2}(X))

\displaystyle=

is scalar for any

Expanding the second term:

\displaystyle m(X)m^{T}(X)s_{P}(X)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\|m^{T}(X)\|^{2}s_{P}(X)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{w}m_{i}^{2}(X)\bigg)s_{P}(X).

(137)

In this case, scaling by

where

\tilde{m}(X)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{w}m_{i}^{2}(X)}.

(138)

We know that there cannot exist an

such that the diffusion test statistic matches the log-likelihood ratio.
Thus, for this choice of
P_{\infty},P_{1}
, for all matrix-valued functions

where

,

is not equal to
Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(\cdot)
.

Define
h(X)=|Z_{\texttt{{KL}}}(X)-Z_{m}(X)|
. We know that for all
m(X)
there exists at least one value

.
By Assumptions
III.1

IV.5

are supported on

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}[X\in(X^{*}-\delta,X^{*}+\delta)]>0

\mathbb{P}_{1}[X\in(X^{*}-\delta,X^{*}+\delta)]>0
