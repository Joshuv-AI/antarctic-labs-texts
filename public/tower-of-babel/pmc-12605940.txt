CPZ promotes the proliferation of gastric cancer by activating the Wnt pathway through binding with DVL2
Authors: Rongkui Luo, Wen Huang, Huimei Wang, Minying Deng, Zixiang Yu, Zhaomeng Cui, Huiyu Bao, Yufeng Liu, Chen Xu, Yingyong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is one of the most prevalent and heterogeneous malignancies worldwide, with treatment outcomes often hindered by delayed diagnosis and limited therapeutic options. Consequently, identifying novel targets for GC diagnosis and treatment is of significant clinical relevance. This study investigated the role of CPZ in GC progression and explored its molecular mechanisms and potential as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target.MethodsBioinformatics analysis and clinical pathological evaluations were conducted to assess CPZ expression in GC and its correlation with clinical parameters. In vivo and in vitro experiments, including CCK8 proliferation assays, flow cytometry, and xenograft mouse models, were employed to confirm the impact of CPZ on GC malignancy. Potential molecular mechanisms through which CPZ regulates GC cell biological functions were identified and validated using immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (IP-MS), co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), protein truncation assays, GST-pull down assays, and Western blotting. The CPZ inhibitor PF-573228 was identified through screening a small-molecule compound library.ResultsCPZ expression was significantly upregulated in GC (tumor tissues vs. normal tissues = 54.3% vs. 10.1%), correlating with unfavorable clinical pathological parameters and poor prognosis. CPZ directly interacts with DVL2 via its C-terminal domain (187-581aa). Its role in promoting tumorigenesis (both in vivo and in vitro) by fostering GC proliferation and regulation of cell cycle distribution is partially dependent on CPZ-DVL2 interaction, which activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. PF-573228 specifically inhibits CPZ-mediated GC cell proliferation and tumor growth in mouse models (approximately fivefold compared to the control group).ConclusionThis study highlights the potential of CPZ as a novel biomarker and prognostic predictor for GC, while also elucidating its role in GC progression. The identification of PF-573228 as a specific inhibitor of CPZ provides a basis for the development of new therapeutic strategies for GC diagnosis and intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03912-2.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Carboxypeptidase, Carboxypeptidase Z, Wnt/β-catenin, Xenograft

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal cancers worldwide, ranking sixth in incidence and third in mortality [24]. In China, GC represents a significant public health burden, with approximately 480,000 new cases and 370,000 deaths annually, placing it third in both incidence and mortality among all cancers [14]. The disease’s high incidence, poor prognosis, and cellular heterogeneity present substantial global health challenges. Most patients with GC are diagnosed at advanced stages, with early-stage GC comprising less than 10% of cases [14], and current treatments for advanced GC are suboptimal. While HER2, MET, and Claudin 18.2 are commonly used biomarkers for GC diagnosis and therapy [8, 16], many patients do not experience significant prognostic benefits, contributing to the persistently low 5-year survival rate [15]. Therefore, understanding the underlying mechanisms of GC is critical for identifying novel prognostic biomarkers and more effective therapeutic approaches.

Carboxypeptidases, a class of proteases found in animals, plants, and fungi, play essential roles in various physiological processes across mammalian organs, including food digestion and neuroendocrine peptide synthesis [20, 22]. Carboxypeptidase Z (CPZ), also known as metallocarbonylpeptidase Z, is highly expressed in the extracellular matrix and during early embryonic development [18, 19]. It is also detected in tissues such as the brain, lung, thymus, and kidney [22]. Emerging evidence suggests that CPZ may act as an oncogene in the progression of several cancers. For example, mutations in the CPZ gene are linked to neuroblastoma susceptibility [19]. In GC, bioinformatics analyses have identified CPZ overexpression as a potential diagnostic and prognostic marker, correlating with overall survival (OS) [12]. However, these findings require experimental validation to fully understand CPZ’s biological functions and mechanisms in GC. Furthermore, the Frizzled domain of CPZ interacts with the Wnt signaling pathway, which is critical to tumor progression, by directly binding to Wnt proteins [6]. Thus, CPZ may serve as a promising therapeutic target with clinical relevance.

This study investigated the differential expression of CPZ between GC and adjacent normal tissues and examined its association with clinical pathological features and prognosis. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that CPZ promotes GC cell proliferation by regulating cell cycle progression. Mechanistically, CPZ directly interacts with DVL2 and activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, facilitating GC cell proliferation. High-throughput drug screening identified PF-573228 as a potent inhibitor of CPZ-overexpressing GC cells. As a CPZ-specific inhibitor, PF-573228 binds selectively to CPZ, inhibiting GC cell proliferation and CPZ-mediated xenograft growth by suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Methods and materials

Bioinformatics analysis

In this study, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were utilized as primary public databases. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GC were analyzed using the limma software package (version 4.2.1). Clinical data for patients with GC were retrieved from the TCGA database. To assess the prognostic significance of these genes in GC, Cox regression analysis was performed.

Patients and specimens

Histological samples from 1113 patients with GC, collected between January 1, 2014, and January 31, 2020, were obtained from the Pathology Department archives of Zhongshan Hospital, affiliated with Fudan University. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Fudan University Zhongshan Hospital (Approval No. B2022-205R), and informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the EnVision two-step method, with diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogenic reagent. The results were interpreted by two senior pathologists through double-blind analysis. Staining intensity was graded on a scale from 0 to 3, where 0 indicated no staining, 1 indicated weak staining, 2 indicated moderate staining, and 3 indicated strong staining. The H-SCORE was calculated by summing the product of the percentage of positive cells and the staining intensity.

Cell culture and virus transfection

Human GC cell lines (MGC803, HGC27, BGC823, MKN45, AGS) and the human gastric mucosal cell line (GES-1) were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. These cell lines were cultured in DMEM (high glucose) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (penicillin, streptomycin, and amphotericin). Cells were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Lentiviruses carrying the target gene and an empty vector (negative control) were purchased from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). The target sequences for shRNAs are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blot (WB)

Total RNA was extracted from the cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a reverse transcription kit from Takara (Dalian, China). Quantification of cDNA levels was performed using the QuantStudio 5 instrument from Applied Biosystems, with β-actin serving as an internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2. For Western blotting (WB) analysis, total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor and 2% phosphatase inhibitor. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime).

CCK-8 cell proliferation assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well (100 μL), with three replicates per group. After overnight incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2–4 h. A blank control well was included. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan™ FC Microplate Photometer (Thermo Scientific). Each experiment was independently repeated three times, with three technical replicates per condition.

Cell cycle and apoptosis detection

To assess cell cycle distribution, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were serum-starved for 24 h. The cells were then fixed in 75% anhydrous ethanol and stored at 4 °C for 14–18 h. Afterward, the cell pellets were resuspended in 500 μL PI/RNase (BD Biosciences), stained at room temperature for 30 min, and analyzed. For apoptosis detection, cells were resuspended in 100 μL binding buffer (1 × 105 cells/sample), stained with 5 μL Annexin-V-FITC and 10 μL PI Staining Solution (BD Biosciences, NY, USA) in the dark for 25 min at room temperature, then diluted with 400 μL binding buffer. Cell cycle and apoptosis were analyzed using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.4, Tree Star Inc.).

Subcutaneous tumor formation experiment

Male NKG mice (6 weeks old, weighing 18–20 g) were purchased from the Animal Experimental Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai). The mice were randomly divided into two groups: CPZ-KDNC and CPZ-KD, with five mice per group, and appropriately labeled. Each mouse was injected with 5 × 106 cells (200 μL). Tumor dimensions (length, L, and width, W) were measured every 3 days post-tumor formation (approximately 7 days), along with body weights. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (L × W2)/2. All animal experiments followed protocols approved by the Experimental Animal Management Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (No. 2022-06-DJ-70).

Immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (IP-MS) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

To identify proteins interacting with CPZ, IP-MS experiments were performed by Orizymes Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Cells were lysed to obtain protein samples, which were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 10 μg of anti-flag antibody or IgG antibody. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) were used to bind the antibody and precipitate the protein. Magnetic bead-protein complexes were separated, eluted, and resolved using SDS-PAGE. Target proteins were detected by WB.

GST pull-down

Vector construction, protein expression, and purification of GST-CPZ and His-DVL2 were handled by Yeasen (Shanghai, China). GST-CPZ protein (200 μg) was incubated with anti-GST magnetic beads for 2 h, followed by the addition of His-DVL2 protein. The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Non-specific binding proteins were washed away, and the interaction between CPZ and DVL2 was analyzed by WB.

Compound screening

This study employed a small-molecule compound library consisting of 517 FDA-approved drugs sourced from Selleck Corporation. A detailed list of the compounds is provided in Supplementary Table S3. Initial screening aimed to identify potential inhibitors of CPZ-overexpressing (CPZ-OE) cells, using a compound concentration of 10 μM. Drugs exhibiting high sensitivity in CPZ-OE cells were further assessed. The screening criteria included compounds showing >80% inhibition in the CPZ-OE group, a significant difference in inhibition compared to the CPZ-OE negative control (CPZ-OENC) group (P < 0.05), and an inhibition ratio >2 in the CPZ-OE/CPZ-OENC comparison. Various concentration gradients were tested to determine the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of the most promising compounds.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The CM5 chip (Cytiva) was installed following the Biacore T200 instrument’s standard operating procedure. Recombinant CPZ protein was coupled to the CM5 chip, and PF-573228, diluted in PBS, was flowed through. The dissociation constant (KD) of PF-573228 binding to CPZ protein was determined using kinetic analysis in Biacore T200 software (version 1.0).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking simulations were performed using the HADDOCK 2.4 web server (https://wenmr.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4) to predict the binding mode and interaction sites of small-molecule compounds with target proteins, as well as to forecast protein–protein interactions. The structures of DVL2 and CPZ-binding partner proteins were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB IDs: 1856 and 8532). Active and passive residues for docking were defined based on known interaction interfaces and literature reports. The docking process included three main stages: rigid-body energy minimization, semi-flexible simulated annealing, and final explicit solvent refinement. Default HADDOCK parameters were used unless otherwise stated, with the HADDOCK score serving as the primary scoring function for ranking docking solutions. The top-ranked cluster with the lowest HADDOCK score and favorable interaction energy was selected for visualization and further analysis using PyMOL (version 2.5.0).

Clone formation assay

For proliferation assays, cells were plated at a density of 700 cells per well in 6-well plates. The following day, once the cells were adherent, the treatment group was exposed to PF-573228 at concentrations of 7.79 μM (MGC803) and 11.3 μM (HGC27), while the control group received medium containing DMSO. The media were refreshed every 3 days for 14 days. Cells were then fixed with formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Colony formation assays were performed in triplicate, and colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted after crystal violet staining. All data are presented as the mean ± SD of at least three independent experiments. The reproducibility of results was confirmed by obtaining consistent outcomes across independent experimental repeats.

Tumor xenograft mouse model

A subcutaneous xenograft tumor model was established in NKG mice. When tumors reached an average volume of 100 mm3, mice with uniform tumor sizes were selected and randomly assigned to one of four groups: CPZ-OENC control, CPZ-OENC treatment, CPZ-OE control, and CPZ-OE treatment, with five mice per group. The treatment began on Day 0, with drug administration starting immediately. Mice in the treatment groups received intraperitoneal injections of PF-573228 (5 mg/kg) 5 days per week [29], while controls were administered DMSO. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days to calculate tumor volume. After 35 days of treatment, mice were euthanized in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Tumors in mice should not exceed 2000 mm3, and the diameter of any dimension should be less than 20 mm; the weight of tumors should not exceed 10% of the weight of mice. All procedures were performed in accordance with the approved protocol, and the maximal tumor size/burden was not exceeded at any time. As for the euthanasia of animals, we placed the mice in a sealed laboratory animal room SMQ-1 (Tianhuan Technology Co., Ltd, China) containing carbon dioxide (CO2) and slowly injected CO2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and chart creation were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) and GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in continuous variables were analyzed using independent sample t tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and ANOVA. Categorical variables were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test or chi-square tests. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with logistic regression analysis to assess survival differences. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using Cox regression. All experiments were replicated three times to ensure reliability.

Results

Overexpression of CPZ indicates poor prognosis in patients with gastric cancer (GC)

To investigate the expression profile of CPZ in GC tissues, an analysis was performed on datasets comprising non-paired samples (375 GC tissues and 32 normal gastric mucosal tissues) and paired samples (27 matched pairs of GC and normal tissues) from the TCGA database. CPZ expression was significantly elevated in GC tissues compared to normal tissues, regardless of the sample type (P < 0.001 for non-paired and P = 0.019 for paired samples; Fig. 1A, B). ROC curve analysis indicated that CPZ exhibited high specificity, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for GC (Fig. 1C). Clinical feature analysis revealed significantly higher CPZ expression in stages II and III compared to stage I (P < 0.05), with a trend towards increased expression in stage IV (P = 0.07; Fig. 1D). The optimal cutoff value for CPZ expression (FPKM) was determined as 0.16 using the R language survivminer and survival packages. Patients with FPKM values above 0.16 were classified into the high-expression group, while those below this threshold were placed in the low-expression group. Survival analyses of the TCGA and GSE15459 datasets demonstrated that high CPZ expression correlated with poorer prognosis compared to low expression (P = 0.005 and P = 0.0027, respectively; Fig. 1E, F).

To further validate these results, immunohistochemical staining was performed on tumor tissues and matched normal tissues from 1113 patients with GC, with CPZ expression scored using the H-score method. CPZ expression was significantly higher in GC tumor tissues than in normal gastric mucosal tissues (54.3% vs. 10.1%, P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Classification of CPZ H-scores into high, low, and no expression groups revealed that CPZ expression increased with tumor infiltration depth (T stage), lymph node metastasis (N stage), postoperative pTNM stage, and tumor diameter (Table 1). Additionally, high CPZ expression was associated with clinically relevant subgroups, including HER2-negative/low expression, pMMR status, and absence of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection (Table 1). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated a significant correlation between CPZ expression and poor OS and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with GC (P < 0.0001; Fig. 2B, C).

Moreover, the diagnostic performance of CPZ was compared with existing GC markers, CEA and CA199, using tissue microarrays. As shown in Fig. 2D, the sensitivity and specificity of CPZ (AUC = 0.756) were comparable to CEA (AUC = 0.747) and superior to CA199 (AUC = 0.469), suggesting CPZ’s potential for differential diagnosis of GC. Together, these findings highlight CPZ’s pivotal role in GC progression and its potential as a prognostic marker.

CPZ promotes GC cell proliferation and regulates cell cycle distribution

To evaluate CPZ expression at the cellular level, qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 3A) were used to measure CPZ mRNA and protein levels in five GC cell lines (MKN45, MGC803, AGS, HGC27, BGC823) and the human gastric mucosal epithelial cell line GES-1. Compared to GES-1, all five GC cell lines exhibited significantly higher CPZ expression, with MGC803 and HGC27 showing the highest mRNA and protein levels, thus selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3A).

To investigate the biological role of CPZ in GC, CPZ-OE and CPZ-KD cell lines, along with their respective control groups, were established and validated for transfection efficiency (Fig. 3B). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that CPZ overexpression significantly enhanced GC cell proliferation in both MGC803 and HGC27 cells, while CPZ knockdown inhibited proliferation (Fig. 3C).

Flow cytometry analysis was performed to assess cell apoptosis and cell cycle progression. CPZ knockdown in HGC27 cells significantly increased both early and late apoptosis rates (P < 0.01 and P < 0.0001, respectively; Fig. 3D). Apoptosis analysis also revealed a significant increase in late apoptosis in CPZ-knockdown MGC803 cells compared to controls (P < 0.05; Fig. 3D). In CPZ-overexpressing MGC803 and HGC27 cells, a marked reduction in the G1 phase cell population was observed, accompanied by an increase in the S + G2 phase population. In contrast, CPZ knockdown inhibited the transition from the G1 phase to the S + G2 phase (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that CPZ inhibits GC cell apoptosis and facilitates progression from the G1 to the S + G2 phase, thereby promoting GC cell proliferation.

Knockdown of CPZ inhibits tumor proliferation in a subcutaneous mouse model

To further validate these in vitro findings, CPZ knockdown MGC803 cells and control cells were subcutaneously injected into NKG mice to assess the effect of CPZ on GC cell proliferation in vivo. As shown in Fig. 4A–C, tumors in the CPZ knockdown group grew more slowly and were significantly smaller than those in the control group. Immunohistochemical staining of tumor tissues revealed a significant reduction in CPZ expression in the CPZ-KD group compared to controls (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, the Ki-67 proliferation index was markedly lower in the CPZ-KD group (50 ± 8%) compared to the control group (90 ± 5%). These results suggest that CPZ knockdown inhibits GC cell proliferation in vivo.

Direct interaction between CPZ and DVL2

To investigate the molecular mechanism through which CPZ influences GC progression, IP-MS was initially employed to identify potential proteins interacting with CPZ. Candidate proteins were selected based on their exclusive peptide presence in CPZ-OE cells, absent in CPZ-OENC cells. Through extensive screening, 61 proteins potentially binding to CPZ were identified (Fig. 5A). Among the top 10 proteins, DVL2 exhibited the most significant difference (Fig. 5A). Co-IP analysis confirmed the co-precipitation of CPZ and DVL2, suggesting a direct interaction (Fig. 5B). This binding was further validated by glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays (Fig. 5C). To define the interaction domain, co-IP analysis with truncated mutants of CPZ and DVL2 was performed. The results indicated that DVL2 specifically binds to the N-terminal deletion mutant of CPZ (Flag-CPZ2-187-581aa) but not the C-terminal mutant (Flag-CPZ1-40-167aa) (Fig. 5D). Additionally, molecular docking analysis supported the specificity of the CPZ-DVL2 interaction (Fig. 5E; Supplementary Figure S1).

CPZ stimulates GC proliferation via interaction with DVL2

To evaluate the role of DVL2 in CPZ-mediated GC cell proliferation, DVL2 interference plasmids were co-transfected into CPZ-OE stable cell lines. CCK8 assays revealed that DVL2 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation induced by CPZ overexpression (Fig. 6A). Cell cycle analysis demonstrated that DVL2 knockdown reversed the stimulatory effect of CPZ overexpression on the transition from G1 to the S + G2 phases (Fig. 6B). Collectively, these results suggest that the CPZ-DVL2 interaction synergistically promotes GC cell proliferation and modulates cell cycle progression.

CPZ activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway via DVL2

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying CPZ regulation of GC proliferation, the potential signaling pathways modulated by CPZ were examined. Based on the above results, it was found that CPZ’s role in promoting GC proliferation and regulating cell cycle distribution partially depends on its interaction with DVL2. Given DVL2’s pivotal role in the Wnt signaling pathway [25, 27], WB analysis was performed to assess whether CPZ expression alters Wnt pathway activation in GC cell lines. The results showed that CPZ knockdown reduced β-catenin protein levels in MGC803 and HGC27 cells, while CPZ overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 7A, B), indicating CPZ’s involvement in activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in GC. To further validate the impact of the CPZ-DVL2 interaction on Wnt/β-catenin activation, DVL2 was knocked down in CPZ-OE HGC27 and MGC803 cells, resulting in a significant reduction in β-catenin levels (Fig. 7C, D). TOP/FOP Flash reporter assays showed that DVL2 knockdown reversed the Wnt pathway activation induced by CPZ overexpression (Fig. 7E). These results indicate that CPZ-mediated activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in GC cells is at least partially dependent on its interaction with DVL2.

Screening for CPZ inhibitors from compound libraries

To identify potential inhibitors of CPZ, a screening of 517 compounds was performed on CPZ-OE and CPZ-OENC cells. Seventy-one inhibitors were identified, each demonstrating >80% inhibition of proliferation in CPZ-OE cells and a >twofold higher inhibition rate in the CPZ-OE/CPZ-OENC comparison in GC cell lines (Fig. 8A). PF-573228, a Wnt pathway inhibitor, emerged as the most effective candidate, showing significantly stronger inhibitory activity in CPZ-OE cells compared to control cells (Fig. 8B). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis indicated that PF-573228 binds to CPZ with a KD value of 7.5 µM (Fig. 8C). Molecular docking studies revealed strong interactions between CPZ and PF-573228, mediated by two hydrogen bonds at residues 162H and 336N (Fig. 8D).

PF-573228 inhibits CPZ-mediated tumorigenicity in GC cells

Treatment with PF-573228 or DMSO in both CPZ-OENC and CPZ-OE cell lines showed that PF-573228 markedly suppressed colony formation, with a more potent effect in the CPZ-OE group (Fig. 8E). Co-IP assays confirmed that PF-573228 interferes with the interaction between CPZ and DVL2 (Fig. 8F). In vivo experiments demonstrated that PF-573228 inhibits xenograft growth in MGC803-CPZ-OENC and MGC803-CPZ-OE models at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Notably, the CPZ-OE group exhibited increased sensitivity to PF-573228 compared to the CPZ-OENC group (Fig. 9A–D), consistent with the colony formation assay results. These results suggest that CPZ may serve as a promising drug target for inhibiting GC growth, both in vitro and in vivo.

Discussion

GC is a highly prevalent malignancy globally, ranking sixth in incidence and third in mortality. In China, GC accounts for over 40% of new cases and deaths from this malignancy [17]. As understanding of GC advances, its management has evolved from a singular focus on surgery to a comprehensive treatment strategy that includes chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [3]. GC is characterized by substantial heterogeneity and a complex pathogenic profile. Recent advancements in the molecular classification of GC have led to the identification of key molecular markers that are crucial in clinical practice, offering more precise insights into GC management and prognosis [1]. However, only a small subset of these biomarkers and their corresponding targets have progressed to preclinical and clinical trial stages. Consequently, identifying effective therapeutic targets that can be addressed by drugs targeting tumor-driving pathways has become a critical area of focus in both tumor biomedical research and clinical translation, providing a theoretical foundation for personalized therapies. CPZ, a member of the carboxypeptidase subfamily, interacts with cysteine-rich domains (CRDs) [6]. Previous studies have suggested an oncogenic role for CPZ in various tumor types, although the precise mechanisms through which it exerts its effects remain unclear. In this study, bioinformatics analysis confirmed CPZ overexpression in patients with GC, supporting its potential as both a diagnostic biomarker and a prognostic indicator for GC, consistent with earlier findings [12]. When compared to established GC diagnostic markers such as CEA and CA199, CPZ demonstrated superior diagnostic performance, highlighting its potential for use in the differential diagnosis of GC. Immunohistochemical analysis further revealed elevated CPZ expression in histological samples from patients with GC, which correlated with adverse clinicopathological parameters, including tumor infiltration depth (T stage), lymph node metastasis, tumor size, and nerve invasion. Additionally, high CPZ expression was closely associated with HER2-negative/low expression status, pMMR status, and absence of EBV infection, factors that are crucial for clinical decision-making. Notably, patients with elevated CPZ levels generally exhibited a poorer prognosis.

Our in vivo experimental findings indicate that CPZ promotes GC cell proliferation by modulating cell cycle progression and inhibiting apoptosis. In line with these results, CPZ knockdown in a mouse subcutaneous tumor model effectively suppressed tumor growth and reduced tumor volume, further supporting the role of CPZ in GC progression. Previous studies have suggested potential molecular mechanisms underlying CPZ’s involvement in tumorigenesis, possibly through alterations in extracellular matrix composition that activate the Wnt pathway [12, 18]. Despite growing knowledge of CPZ’s biological functions and potential roles in various diseases, its specific mechanism in GC development remains to be fully elucidated.

WB analysis preliminarily confirmed the correlation between CPZ and the Wnt pathway in GC. Notably, CPZ regulates the expression of β-catenin, a key component in gastric tumorigenesis that controls cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [2, 13]. It is hypothesized that CPZ modulates the biological functions of GC cells by activating the downstream Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. To explore the specific molecular mechanism by which CPZ promotes GC proliferation, IP-MS, Co-IP, GST pull-down, and docking experiments were employed to identify and validate proteins interacting with CPZ. DVL (Disheveled), a critical player in embryonic development and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various cancers [10, 26]. Studies indicate that DVL-2 interacts with c-Jun and β-catenin to form a stable complex, leading to the transcriptional activation of Wnt target genes in the nucleus [4]. The accumulation and overexpression of DVL-2 can activate the Wnt signaling pathway, potentially contributing to colorectal cancer development [28]. Our results validate the direct binding between CPZ and DVL2 proteins, specifically through CPZ’s C-terminal domain (40-167aa). Knockdown of DVL2 significantly inhibited CPZ-induced cell proliferation and the transition to the S + G2 phase of the cell cycle, while concurrently suppressing β-catenin expression within the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings suggest that CPZ, through its interaction with DVL2, acts on β-catenin to synergistically promote GC cell proliferation by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Despite significant progress in identifying biomarkers as driver genes in molecular biology, only a small fraction has been validated in the laboratory and successfully translated into clinical practice [11]. Consequently, the development of effective GC biomarkers and novel therapeutic targets remains a major focus in tumor research. Through high-throughput compound library screening, PF-573228 was identified as an inhibitor of CPZ overexpression in GC cells. PF-573228 specifically binds to CPZ, inhibiting CPZ-mediated GC cell proliferation. Notably, PF-573228 is an ATP-competitive inhibitor of Focal Adhesion Kinase (FAK) [21] and can induce cell apoptosis [9]. Several studies have described interactions between FAK and the Wnt pathway. For example, FAK/PYK2 promotes the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by phosphorylating GSK3β, contributing to intestinal tumor development [5]. In diffuse GC, FAK activation can stimulate YAP-TAZ, PI3K-AKT, and β-catenin [7, 23].

This study demonstrates that PF-573228 effectively reverses the clonal formation of GC cells induced by CPZ overexpression, an effect confirmed in a mouse xenograft tumor model. Moreover, PF-573228 treatment blocks the interaction between CPZ and DVL2. These findings suggest that PF-573228 exerts specific inhibitory effects on CPZ, reducing Wnt/β-catenin signaling mediated by CPZ. Therefore, PF-573228 holds promise as a therapeutic target for patients with GC exhibiting elevated CPZ expression.

However, insufficient experimental evidence currently exists to establish PF-573228 as a CPZ-specific drug, a limitation this study has not fully addressed. To overcome this, future research will focus on optimizing detection methods and expanding validation models to further confirm PF-573228’s efficacy. Additionally, efforts will be made to screen for specific CPZ inhibitors, aiming to provide more reliable experimental evidence for research on this target.

Conclusion

In this study, CPZ emerges as a potent biomarker and prognostic indicator for patients with GC. It acts as an oncogene, promoting GC progression by enhancing cellular proliferation and accelerating the transition of the cell cycle from the G1 phase to the S + G2 phases. The mechanism underlying CPZ-induced proliferation involves its C-terminal domain (amino acids 40-167) directly interacting with DVL2, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Through high-throughput screening of small-molecule compound libraries and in vivo and in vitro validation, PF-573228 was identified as a specific anti-tumor agent targeting CPZ. This discovery paves the way for a promising therapeutic target and potential drug candidate for patients with GC exhibiting high CPZ expression.
