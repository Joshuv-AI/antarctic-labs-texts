High-temperature strength and microstructural evolution of Ti-6Al-4V alloy fabricated by wire Arc additive manufacturing under elevated temperature
Authors: Soobin Kim, Dong-Hyuck Kam, Kee-Ahn Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the high-temperature mechanical properties and compressive deformation behavior of Ti-6Al-4V fabricated by Wire Arc Additive Manufactured (WAAM), in comparison with its hot-rolled counterpart. The WAAM alloy exhibited a fine α-lath Widmanstätten structure containing a nano-film β phase, which developed through solid-state β→α transformation during cooling under a steep thermal gradient induced by rapid solidification and interpass thermal cycling. In contrast, the wrought alloy showed a bimodal microstructure with coarse equiaxed α and α + β lamellar colonies. Compression tests at RT, 300 °C, 500 °C, and 700 °C revealed that WAAM alloy retained higher yield strengths across all temperatures (RT: 1034.1 MPa, 300 °C: 845.7 MPa, 500 °C: 646.2 MPa, 700 °C: 320.4 MPa). This was attributed to the fine α-lath structure, high angle grain boundary (HAGB) fraction, and α/β interface strengthening. However, at 700 °C, accelerated kinking and α-lath fragmentation led to localized dynamic recrystallization (DRX) and rapid flow softening. These findings demonstrate the thermal stability and deformation resistance of WAAM alloy. Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM) has garnered considerable attention as an emerging fabrication technology. It enables the fabrication of geometrically complex components via layer-by-layer material deposition in accordance with computer-aided design (CAD) defined geometries. In contrast to conventional processes such as machining, casting, and forming, AM offers exceptional geometric design flexibility while significantly reducing material waste1–4. These advantages have positioned AM as a transformative manufacturing strategy, particularly in industries that demand lightweight, structurally optimized, and highly customized components in such as aerospace, automotive, and shipbuilding industries5–8.

Among the various AM techniques, wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) has gained increasing attention due to its high deposition rate and economic viability9,10. WAAM utilizes metallic wires as feedstock and employs an arc-based heat source to fabricate components nearly without part-size limitations11. In addition, its reliance on low-cost raw materials enables substantial cost reductions compared to conventional fabrication methods. While laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) remains the most widely used AM method, WAAM has demonstrated build speeds up to 10–20 times faster. This difference alone can significantly enhance throughput, especially for large-scale structure12,13.

Ti-6Al-4V is widely recognized as a preferred alloy for structural components operating under extreme service conditions, such as those encountered in aerospace and energy industries. Its wide adoption stems from its superior specific strength, thermal stability, oxidation resistance, and low density, which collectively provide a robust performance in harsh environments14–17. However, these advantages are offset by its limited machinability at ambient temperatures and the high cost of raw feedstock, both of which contribute to increased production costs and constrain its industrial scalability18–21. To address these limitations, WAAM has emerged as a viable alternative. This technique facilitates the fabrication of near-net-shape Ti-6Al-4V components by leveraging high deposition rates and reduced material waste. In contrast to conventional processes, WAAM minimizes the need for extensive machining and allows for the production of complex geometries with greater design freedom. Its ability to combine cost efficiency with manufacturing flexibility has led to growing interest in deploying WAAM for large-scale Ti-6Al-4V applications across various industries.

However, the WAAM process generates fundamentally different microstructures compared to conventional wrought Ti-6Al-4V, primarily due to rapid solidification and repeated thermal cycling during successive layer deposition22–25. The resulting microstructure typically consists of coarse prior β grains filled with aligned α-lath colonies, formed during the β to α transformation upon cooling. These microstructural features critically influence dislocation glide, slip transfer, and strain localization under load. Dislocations can glide across semicoherent α/β interfaces within lamellar colonies, allowing each colony to behave as a grain in the early stages of deformation. As deformation progresses, accumulated dislocation–interface interactions lead to localized strain accumulation and interfacial hardening, which restrict further slip transfer and promote sub-boundary formation26,27. The fine α-lath spacing shortens the mean free path of dislocations, resulting in higher strain hardening28. With increasing temperature, these interactions evolve from hardening-dominated behavior to thermally activated recovery and recrystallization processes, as dislocation climb and localized strain at colony boundaries facilitate the nucleation of dynamic recrystallization (DRX). In this context, these microstructural characteristics modulate the competitive activation of dynamic restoration mechanisms—dynamic recovery (DRV), continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX), and discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX)29,30. Despite extensive interest in process optimization, understanding of their temperature-dependent deformation behavior remains incomplete.

To ensure the reliable application of WAAM-fabricated Ti-6Al-4V alloys in high-temperature environments, it is essential to establish a comprehensive understanding of their mechanical and microstructural stability under elevated temperatures31–35. However, most previous studies on WAAM Ti-6Al-4V have primarily focused on optimizing process parameters to tailor the microstructure and improve room temperature mechanical properties. For instance, Wang et al.33 investigated the effects of deposition direction and dwell time on the evolution of β-grain morphology and α/β lamellar structures in WAAM Ti-6Al-4V alloys. Increasing the dwell time from 0s to 120s led to a transition from coarse columnar β grains to fine equiaxed grains and reduced the α-lath thickness from ~ 1.2 μm to ~ 0.6 μm, thereby improving the tensile strength (≈ 853 MPa) and elongation (≈ 15%). Similarly, Zhou et al.34 examined the effects of process conditions on the microstructure and tensile properties by systematically adjusting parameters such as deposition path, current, and travel speed. They reported that the co-directional deposition path produced a more uniform thermal history and suppressed excessive reheating compared with the reciprocating path, resulting in finer α-lath structures and improved strength-ductility balance. Maurya et al.35 further pursued grain refinement and mechanical enhancement by modifying the wire morphology used in WAAM processing. They developed a C-type filler wire with a flattened cross-section to reduce heat input and improve deposition stability, which, when combined with interlayer hammer peening, refined the β and α grains and enhanced the overall mechanical balance. While these studies clearly demonstrated that the microstructure and mechanical performance of WAAM Ti-6Al-4V can be tuned through process parameter control, they provide limited insight into how such modifications influence deformation behavior at elevated temperatures.

More recently, research has expanded beyond process optimization toward a deeper understanding of the thermomechanical behavior of WAAM-based Ti-6Al-4V alloys. Teng et al.36 compared TC4 and TC11 alloys fabricated by multi-wire WAAM, evaluating their RT compressive and wear properties. They reported that TC11 exhibited a higher compressive yield strength (~ 1088 MPa) and hardness than TC4 (~ 837 MPa), attributed to the refined lamellar microstructure and solid-solution strengthening from Mo and Zr additions. This study is referenced as a representative example focusing on compressive behavior. However, it does not address temperature-dependent restoration mechanisms (e.g., DRV and DRX) or compression-driven deformation at elevated temperatures. Fu et al.37 combined the WAAM process with in-situ forging and conducted isothermal compression tests at 800–950 °C (0.01–1 s− 1, ~ 60% reduction), establishing strain-dependent constitutive equations and hot-processing maps. They identified an optimal forging window 900–950 °C, where fine equiaxed grains were formed through CDRX. However, their study primarily focused on flow-stress modeling and process window definition, without addressing the interaction among interface-constrained slip, recovery, and recrystallization mechanisms that govern softening behavior. Shi et al.38 investigated the tensile anisotropy of WAAM-TC4 alloys between 20 °C and 600 °C across different build orientations (0°, 45°, 90°), revealing that phase-boundary slip and shear-localization bands dominated the deformation behavior. While this work provided valuable insights into anisotropy and interfacial deformation characteristics, it was limited to uniaxial tension.

Collectively, previous studies demonstrate an increasing research interest in the high temperature mechanical behavior of WAAM Ti-6Al-4V alloys. Although several investigations have examined room temperature compression and high temperature tension, systematic studies addressing the deformation mechanisms under elevated temperatures remain limited. In practical applications, titanium alloy components are subjected to combined tensile and compressive stresses together with thermal loading39. Among these, compressive deformation serves as a fundamental uniaxial mode for interpreting plastic responses under complex stress conditions, providing essential insight into thermally activated mechanisms such as recovery, dynamic recrystallization, creep, and fatigue. Nevertheless, most existing compression studies have been confined to room temperature, and the evolution of deformation mechanisms under high-temperature compression has not yet been fully clarified. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the compression-based deformation mechanisms of WAAM Ti-6Al-4V to advance the understanding of its thermomechanical stability under service-relevant conditions.

In this study, the compressive mechanical behavior of WAAM-fabricated Ti-6Al-4V was systematically compared with that of conventional wrought Ti-6Al-4V over a temperature range from room temperature (RT) to 700 °C. Post-deformation microstructural analyses were further conducted to elucidate the predominant deformation mechanisms as a function of temperature, thereby establishing a fundamental understanding of how the unique microstructural features inherent to WAAM influence high-temperature mechanical stability.

Results

Initial microstructural characterization

Figure 1 shows the optical microscopy images of the as-fabricated WAAM and the reference Wrought alloys. In the WAAM material (Fig. 1(a1)), coarse prior β grains (~ 0.5–1 mm) were observed, containing intragranular α-lath colonies forming a Widmanstätten structure (Fig. 1(a2)). These colonies preferentially formed along prior β grain boundaries via heterogeneous nucleation and growth during the β → α transformation. As the microstructural evolution in Ti alloys is highly sensitive to cooling rate32, α-phase nucleation initiates at β grain boundaries and proceeds along them as the temperature decreases below the β-transus (Tβ)41–44. The resulting α-laths exhibit specific crystallographic orientations governed by the Burgers orientation relationship (BOR), which maintains lattice correspondence and minimizes transformation strain during the diffusionless β→α transformation upon cooling under a steep thermal gradient45–48. In contrast, the Wrought alloy exhibited a bimodal microstructure consisting of coarse equiaxed α grains and transformed α + β colonies (Fig. 1(b), green arrows), a typical feature of hot-rolled Ti alloys49,50. In some regions, the residual β phase was found to form colonies together with the α phase, consistent with previously reported morphologies51 as well as those observed in this study.

Initial microstructures of Ti-6Al-4V alloys: (a1, a2) WAAM; (b) Wrought.

Figure 2 presents the X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis results of the WAAM and Wrought alloys. In the case of the WAAM, only the primary diffraction peaks corresponding to the α phase were detected, with no clearly discernible β phase peaks, which is attributed to the low fraction of residual β phase resulting from rapid cooling-induced α transformation52,53. In contrast, the Wrought alloy exhibited β phase peaks alongside those of the α phase, likely due to increased β fraction facilitated by enhanced diffusion of β-stabilizing elements during hot working. Furthermore, the sharper diffraction peaks observed in the Wrought indicate higher crystallinity and a larger grain size54.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis results comparing the phase constituents of WAAM and Wrought alloys.

EBSD analysis was performed to examine the crystallographic characteristics of the WAAM alloy (Fig. 3). The inverse pole figure (IPF) map (Fig. 3a) revealed a lamellar microstructure composed of α-laths with an average thickness of 1.33 ± 0.65 μm. High-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, > 15°) accounted for 80.8% of total boundaries (Fig. 3(b)), and the kernel average misorientation (KAM) map (Fig. 3c) showed increased misorientation near α-lath boundaries and intersection regions. The high HAGB fraction and localized dislocation accumulation—resulting from lattice misfit—are known to hinder dislocation motion and thereby can improve strength and creep resistance at both ambient and elevated temperatures55. The phase map (Fig. 3d) confirmed the dominance of the α phase (99.8%), consistent with XRD results. Although a small amount of residual β phase may exist due to repeated thermal cycling and rapid solidification, its volume fraction is likely below the detection limit of XRD.

EBSD and TEM-EDS analysis results of WAAM-fabricated Ti-6Al-4V alloys: (a) IPF map; (b) GB map; (c) KAM map; (d) Phase map; (e) STEM-EDS line scan result across α-lath boundaries.

To identify residual β phase, TEM–EDS analysis was conducted, as shown in Fig. 3e. The STEM image revealed α-laths, and EDS line profiling across their boundaries exhibited localized vanadium (V) enrichment. Given that V is a β-stabilizing element, this enrichment is indicative of thin β layers along the α-lath boundaries56–58. The β phase likely forms as nanoscale films encapsulating the α-laths, generating coherent α/β interfaces. These interfaces can serve as obstacles to dislocation motion, and the crystallographic mismatch between α and β phases further restricts slip propagation under high-temperature conditions. These nano-film β layers are expected to affect slip transfer and interfacial stability during subsequent high temperatures deformation.

Figure 4 presents the EBSD results of the Wrought alloy. The IPF map (Fig. 4a) showed equiaxed α grains with an average size of 10.8 ± 5.51 μm. As shown in Fig. 4b, low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, < 15°) comprised 66.5% of the total, while HAGBs accounted for 33.5%. The average KAM value was 1.14°, notably higher than that of WAAM. This is likely due to DRX (dynamic recovery) and DRV (dynamic recrystallization) occurring during hot rolling, which induce dislocation rearrangement and subgrain formation59,60. While these mechanisms reduce internal stress, repeated hot deformation may leave residual strains, contributing to the increased KAM values observed in the Wrought alloy.

EBSD analysis results of Wrought alloy: (a) IPF map; (b) GB map; (c) Phase map; (d) KAM map.

Compressive deformation behavior at various temperatures

Figure 5 compares the high-temperature compressive behaviors of WAAM and Wrought alloys. The engineering stress–strain curves at RT, 300 °C, 500 °C, and 700 °C are shown in Fig. 5a, while the corresponding true stress-strain curves are shown in Fig. 5b. Figure 5c summarizes the yield strengths (YS) extracted from the engineering curves. WAAM exhibited consistently higher YS and flow stress than Wrought across all temperatures, with a peak YS of 1034.1 MPa at RT. This enhanced strength is primarily due to the fine α-lath boundaries along with nano-film β phases formed during rapid solidification and repeated thermal cycling. These features result in α/β interfaces that act as barriers to dislocation motion, thereby promoting Hall–Petch-type strengthening. According to Semiatin et al.61, such interfaces significantly enhance flow stress in Ti-6Al-4V alloys with Widmanstätten or colony-type α microstructures, as explained by the Eshelby model.

(a) Engineering stress-strain curves under compression for the WAAM and Wrought Ti-6Al-4V alloys; (b) True stress-strain curves; (c) Comparison of compressive yield strength of Ti-6Al-4V alloys at various temperatures.

As the temperature increased, both materials exhibited typical thermal softening behavior, evidenced by a gradual decrease in peak flow stress. The WAAM alloy retained superior strength over the wrought counterpart even at 300 °C. However, the strain hardening rate declined with increasing temperature, primarily due to enhanced activation of slip systems promoting dislocation mobility. Additionally, at both RT and 300 °C, insufficient plastic strain accumulation was observed in both alloys, leading to premature fracture. At 500 °C, the yield strength of WAAM decreased to 646.2 MPa but remained higher than that of Wrought (530.6 MPa). WAAM showed pronounced work hardening (WH) behavior during early deformation due to dislocation accumulation, followed by gradual flow softening. In contrast, Wrought showed a more continuous and moderate decline in flow stress. Above 500 °C, WH competes with softening mechanisms such as DRV and DRX62. Li et al.62 reported that WH dominates initially, but DRV and DRX progressively take over with increasing strain via dislocation rearrangement and annihilation. At 700 °C, both alloys experienced a sharp drop in yield strength. The reduction in WAAM was particularly pronounced, narrowing the strength difference between the two alloys as softening became dominant.

Overall, WAAM demonstrated high strength at RT and intermediate temperatures (300 °C and 500 °C), primarily due to its fine α-lath structure, high fraction of HAGBs, and α/β interface-related microstructural features. However, its thermal stability significantly decreased above 700 °C due to rapid softening. In contrast, Wrought exhibited lower strength but more uniform deformation, which may be attributed to its coarse α-grain structure and a higher proportion of LAGBs.

Microstructural evolution during high-temperature deformation

Figure 6 shows SEM images of WAAM after compressive deformation at various temperatures. At RT and 300 °C, the α-lath lamellar structure was mostly retained, though kinking appeared at 300 °C due to dislocation glide and stress accumulation63. At 500 °C, more pronounced kinking, sub-boundary formation, and slip bands were observed, causing α-lath segmentation. At 700 °C, severe bending and fragmentation indicated accelerated softening. These morphological changes suggest temperature-dependent degradation of the lamellar structure, with further crystallographic evolution discussed in two paragraphs later.

SEM micrographs of the deformed microstructures of WAAM samples at various temperatures.

Figure 7 presents SEM images of the Wrought alloy. At RT and 300 °C, equiaxed α grains and α + β colonies were largely maintained, though cracks formed along the 45° shear plane, typically at α grain boundaries or α/β interfaces. At 500 °C, α grains elongated perpendicular to the loading axis, and kinking and fragmentation occurred in lamellar regions (blue arrows). At 700 °C, some colonies transformed into globularized grains, suggesting activation of CDRX.

SEM observation results of the deformed microstructure of Wrought at various temperatures.

Figure 8 presents EBSD analysis results of WAAM after compression at RT and 300 °C. The IPF maps show that the α-lath lamellar structure was largely preserved at both temperatures. However, at 300 °C, dislocation rearrangement led to substructure formation and an increased fraction of LAGBs within the α-laths (highlighted by yellow circles). The average KAM value increased from 0.55 in the initial microstructure to 1.67 after deformation at RT, indicating a significant rise in local lattice curvature. In the enlarged KAM map (Fig. 8a), misorientation is strongly concentrated along α-lath boundaries, suggesting that these interfaces act as effective barriers to dislocation glide and promote dislocation pile-up during deformation. At 300 °C (Fig. 8b), KAM values increased and intensified within the laths, suggesting enhanced dislocation accumulation and localized strain due to inward slip migration. GROD maps revealed pronounced intragranular misorientation at both temperatures, implying strain inhomogeneity and stress concentration that could have contributed to premature fracture.

Microstructural evolution of WAAM analyzed by EBSD after compression at room temperature and 300 °C.

Figure 9 presents EBSD analysis results of the Wrought alloy after compression at RT and 300 °C. The IPF and phase maps show that coarse equiaxed α grains and α + β lamellar colonies remained largely unchanged, with no notable variation in phase distribution. These observations suggest that plastic deformation was primarily accommodated through intragranular slip and grain boundary activity. The KAM maps reveal localized misorientation along grain boundaries and within lamellar colonies. At 300 °C, increased misorientation and slip bands formation were observed in coarse α grains (marked by red circles). GROD maps showed more scattered orientation deviation at RT, whereas at 300 °C, misorientation became more homogeneously distributed. Nevertheless, localized slip remained active in certain grains even at 300 °C.

Microstructural evolution of wroughtTi-6Al-4V analyzed by EBSD after compression at room temperature and 300 °C.

Figure 10 shows the EBSD results of WAAM alloy after compression at 500 °C and 700 °C. At 500 °C (Fig. 10a), misorientation at α-lath boundaries decreased, and the boundaries appeared more relaxed, likely due to dislocation migration and annihilation via DRV28,64. At 700 °C (Fig. 10b), this trend intensified: α-lath structures became indistinct, fragmentation occurred, and localized strain bands formed within certain laths (highlighted by blue arrows) as softening progressed. These results indicate that DRV dominated microstructural evolution at 500 °C, while accelerated softening at 700 °C led to severe lamellar degradation.

Microstructural evolution of WAAM analyzed by EBSD after compression at 500 °C and 700 °C.

Figure 11 shows the microstructure of Wrought after deformation at 500 °C and 700 °C. Above 500 °C, grains generally elongated perpendicular to the compression axis. At 500 °C (Fig. 11a), distinct LAGBs developed along pre-existing grain boundaries (indicated by arrows), attributed to dislocation accumulation and rearrangement during DRV (dynamic recovery). Subgrains formed adjacent to grain boundaries, and segmentation within α grains became evident, indicating localized stress redistribution and microstructural reorganization. At 700 °C (Fig. 11b), features of both DRV and DRX (dynamic recrystallization) were observed. EBSD maps revealed newly formed grains with partially globular or equiaxed morphologies, evolved from previously elongated α grains. This transformation resulted from enhanced dislocation mobility and annihilation, increasing the fraction of HAGBs and promoting a more homogeneous orientation distribution. These microstructural changes contributed to the more pronounced softening observed at 700 °C compared to 500 °C.

Microstructural evolution of Wrought analyzed by EBSD after compression at 500 °C and 700 °C.

Figure 12 presents the quantitative analysis of deformed, substructured, and recrystallized regions in WAAM and Wrought alloys, based on grain orientation spread (GOS) maps. In WAAM (Fig. 12a), the microstructure was dominated by substructured regions, especially at RT, due to dislocation accumulation along α-lath boundaries. This limited strain accommodation and caused early fracture. At 300 °C and 500 °C, the substructured fraction slightly decreased due to partial dislocation migration and annihilation. However, it remained predominant as thermal energy was insufficient to activate DRX. Notably, at 500 °C, the deformed fraction increased, suggesting ongoing strain accumulation with minimal DRX activity. In contrast, at 700 °C, a substantial increase in recrystallized fraction was observed, accompanied by a marked decrease in substructured regions, indicating full-scale DRX. These findings suggest that WAAM primarily accumulates strain at room to intermediate temperatures (300–500 °C), while DRX-driven recrystallization becomes dominant at 700 °C.

Recrystallized fraction of the Ti-6Al-4V alloys after deformation at various temperatures: (a) WAAM, and (b) Wrought.

For the Wrought alloy shown in Fig. 12b, the deformed fraction remained high across all temperatures. The substructured regions showed a gradual transition toward recrystallization, with a slight increase up to 500 °C followed by a decrease at 700 °C. This behavior reflects the combined effects of dislocation rearrangement during deformation and the alloy’s prior hot-working history. Due to its pre-existing high dislocation density and residual stress, the alloy retained a large deformed fraction from RT to 500 °C. At 500 °C, partial DRV occurred, but dislocations were not fully annihilated. Instead, they rearranged along grain boundaries and within grains to form LAGBs, leading to a slight increase in the substructure fraction. At 700 °C, sufficient thermal energy activated full DRX, resulting in increased recrystallized fraction and corresponding reductions in the deformed and substructured regions. These features, including α grains globularization, were consistent with SEM and EBSD results.

Discussion

Flow softening mechanism

In high-temperature compression tests above 500 °C, WAAM exhibited more pronounced flow softening than Wrought. At 500 °C, WAAM showed moderate softening. At 700 °C, peak stress was reached quickly, followed by a rapid stress drop. In contrast, Wrought alloy displayed a more gradual flow softening response at both temperatures. This difference is attributed to a combination of factors, including deformation-induced heating, initial dislocation structure, dynamic globularization, kinking of lamellar α, reduced Hall–Petch strengthening, shear band formation, and texture evolution. The WAAM alloy exhibited rapid flow softening during the early stage of high-temperature compression (ε < 0.1), which is closely linked to the kinking of the α-lath lamellar structure. Miller et al.66 suggested that texture evolution, dislocation substructures, and dynamic globularization alone cannot fully explain rapid softening at low strains, and proposed platelet kinking—triggered by localized stress concentration—as the primary mechanism. In this study, as shown in Fig. 6, kink formation was evident above 500 °C and became more severe at 700 °C, leading to α-lath segmentation. This likely resulted from local stress-induced buckling of the lamellae, which accelerated structural breakdown and softening. Such instability-driven deformation contributed to strain localization, consistent with buckling models associated with mechanical instability. EBSD analysis (Fig. 10) supported this interpretation, showing weakened α-lath boundaries due to DRV and evidence of kinked and segmented laths. While localized kinking was also observed in α + β lamellar regions of the Wrought alloy (Fig. 7), its lower frequency suggests a limited impact on the overall deformation behavior.

While the dominant softening behavior observed in this study is primarily attributed to kinking, the contribution of DRX (dynamic recrystallization) cannot be ruled out. DRX generally occurs via two mechanisms: CDRX (continuous dynamic recrystallization) and DDRX (discontinuous dynamic recrystallization). CDRX involves progressive subgrain rotation near grain boundaries, with LAGBs gradually transforming into HAGBs through dislocation accumulation and rearrangement67–69. In contrast, DDRX proceeds via the nucleation and growth of new grains, eliminating existing dislocations and subgrain boundaries in a discontinuous manner within the deformed matrix70,71. EBSD analysis (Figs. 10 and 11) confirmed that both alloys underwent full-scale DRX activation at 700 °C. To further investigate this behavior, IPF, GOS, and cumulative misorientation profiles for representative regions (Regions I–III in Figs. 10 and 11) are presented in Fig. 13 for WAAM and Wrought, respectively.

Recrystallization types and misorientation profiles after high-temperature compression: (a, b) WAAM, and (c) Wrought.

In WAAM (Fig. 13a, b), both DDRX and CDRX were observed. As shown in Fig. 13a, typical DDRX features were identified, including a low intra-grain LAGB fraction and fine recrystallized grains distributed within the deformed matrix. The point-to-point misorientation profile showed minimal orientation change inside non-recrystallized grains, followed by a steep increase at grain boundaries—characteristic of DDRX. Additionally, bridging LAGBs formed after grain boundary expansion (green arrow) further support DDRX activity72. Figure 13b simultaneously revealed CDRX behavior. Cumulative misorientation across an α-lath (point C to D) exceeded 15°, indicating progressive lattice rotation and the formation of recrystallized grains. These results indicate that, in WAAM at 700 °C, both DDRX and CDRX contributed to α-lath rearrangement and the formation of new micro-grains.

Conversely, the deformation behavior of the Wrought alloy was primarily governed by CDRX, as shown in Fig. 13c. The point-to-point misorientation profile revealed that misorientation within coarse grains exceeded 15°, along with clear subgrain rotation which is characteristic of typical CDRX. This behavior is attributed to the combined effects of dislocation generation, DRV, and LAGB formation during deformation, which facilitated progressive lattice rotation. In addition, the substantial recrystallized fraction observed in Fig. 12 likely contributed to softening by enabling extensive dislocation annihilation, thereby reducing the overall flow stress.

These observations indicate that both alloys experienced DRX at 700 °C, but the dominant sites and the extent of DRX-assisted softening differed depending on their α-phase morphology. Although both WAAM and Wrought alloys contain α-colony regions, their fractions and structural characteristics differ substantially. WAAM possesses a well-developed lamellar α-colony architecture, within which widespread kinking, fragmentation, and high dislocation density zones develop during high temperature deformation. These kink-induced fragmented regions act as favorable sites for boundary bulging, subgrain rotation, and DRX nucleation, thereby accelerating DRX progression. In contrast, the Wrought alloy is dominated by coarse primary α grains with only limited colony-type lamellae, so DRX mainly proceeds within these coarse α grains through CDRX. As a result, DRX also contributes to softening in the Wrought alloy, but its impact is smaller compared with WAAM because it is not amplified by extensive kink-induced deformation zones.

Such concurrent processes of grain boundary migration and subgrain rotation were strongly governed by the boundary character. In WAAM, the initial microstructure contained a substantially higher fraction of high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, > 15°) compared with the Wrought alloy, which exhibited a much larger proportion of low-angle boundaries (LAGBs, 2–5° and 5–15°). This boundary-character contrast has a direct mechanistic influence on the active DRX pathways. The abundant HAGBs in WAAM act as efficient dislocation sources and sinks73,74, promoting boundary bulging and migration and lowering the nucleation barrier for DDRX75,76. Their higher interfacial energy and reduced structural coherency provide favorable sites for dislocation absorption, emission, and boundary sliding, which accelerates the formation and growth of new grains76. In addition, the HAGB-rich network enhances boundary mobility in kink-affected regions, where localized instability further facilitates DDRX nucleation. In contrast, the Wrought alloy, which contains a significantly larger fraction of LAGBs, exhibits deformation governed by strong dislocation tangling, restricted boundary mobility, and high plastic compatibility between adjacent grains. These conditions raise the nucleation barrier for DDRX and instead favor the gradual subgrain rotation and the LAGB-to-HAGB transition characteristic of CDRX. Therefore, the coexistence of DDRX and CDRX in WAAM arises from the combined effects of its high HAGB density and widespread kink-induced instability, while CDRX dominates in the LAGB-governed Wrought alloy. At 700 °C, thermally activated boundary migration and dislocation climb further reduce the nucleation barrier for DDRX in HAGB-rich WAAM, whereas CDRX remains the primary recrystallization pathway in the Wrought alloy.

In addition to the recrystallization-related mechanisms discussed above, the stability of the nano-film β layers present along α-lath boundaries in the as-built WAAM alloy is also expected to influence the softening behavior at elevated temperatures. Previous studies on lamellar Ti-6Al-4V have shown that thin β ligaments located at α/β interfaces can undergo morphological changes during hot deformation. Zhang et al.77 reported that during low-strain rate compression at 900–950 °C, extensive α-lamella fragmentation and β-phase penetration along newly formed α/α boundaries break up the originally continuous β interlayers, indicating a substantial loss of interfacial coherency under elevated-temperature condition. Chong et al.78 observed through TEM that boundary migration, lamella subdivision, and recovery during hot compression progressively degrade α/β interface integrity, thereby reducing their effectiveness as obstacles to dislocation motion. Mironov et al.79 further demonstrated that kinking and strain localization within colony-α structures diminish α/β interfacial coherency and promote interface-related softening. Pilchak et al.80 further demonstrated that kinking, strain localization, and spheroidization of lamellae are accompanied by a loss α/β interfacial coherency, which promotes interface-related softening.

Considering these insights, the nano-film β layers in the WAAM alloy likely acted as effective slip barriers during the early stage of deformation. At 500 °C, the α/β interfaces remained relatively sharp and the microstructure was primarily governed by thermally activated recovery, while kinking continued to be active. These observations suggest that the β films largely retained their interfacial stability at this temperature. In contrast, at 700 °C, the extensive lamella fragmentation, boundary mobility, and DRX nucleation observed in EBSD imply a reduction in β-film coherency and morphological stability. Such interfacial degradation would have facilitated enhanced slip transfer and localized strain accommodation, thereby accelerating softening. Therefore, the temperature-dependent destabilization of the nano-film β layers provides an additional mechanistic explanation for the more pronounced softening behavior observed in the WAAM alloy compared with the Wrought alloy.

In addition to these microstructural effects, it is also important to consider the role of interstitial oxygen. The Wrought alloy contains a higher oxygen level (0.34 wt%) than the WAAM alloy (0.16 wt%). Previous studies have shown that interstitial oxygen increases lattice friction in polycrystalline titanium and markedly reduces screw-dislocation mobility, thereby raising the resistance to slip. It has also been reported that higher oxygen concentrations suppress recovery and subgrain development by limiting dislocation annihilation and slowing recrystallization kinetics81,82. Nurly et al.83 further demonstrated that higher oxygen contents in Ti-6Al-4V increase the resistance to high temperature softening by inhibiting diffusion assisted processes. These findings suggest that the relatively lower oxygen content of the WAAM alloy may have contributed to its more pronounced softening behavior at elevated temperatures. Although microstructural architecture remains the primary factor governing the deformation mechanisms identified in this study, the influence of oxygen on high temperature softening kinetics cannot be completely excluded.

Evolution of texture and deformation behavior with temperature

The EBSD IPF maps reveal clear temperature-dependent texture evolution in the WAAM alloy. In the as-built condition (Fig. 3), the microstructure exhibited a strong basal texture with α-laths aligned along the [0001] direction due to directional solidification. After deformation at RT (Fig. 8), the texture remained essentially unchanged, reflecting limited slip transfer across α/β interfaces and the strong geometric constraint of the aligned lamellae, which led to localized deformation and premature fracture at a strain of ~ 0.3. At 300 °C, the α-laths showed increased orientation diversity accompanied by higher KAM and intralath misorientation, indicating stress-induced lattice rotation and activation of non-basal < a > and < c + a > slip systems. At 500–700 °C (Fig. 10), the overall texture intensity progressively weakened while the basal component remained dominant. KAM and GROD maps showed partial relaxation of misorientation through thermally activated recovery and subgrain rotation, but pronounced kink-band fragmentation was still evident within the α-laths. Consequently, regions of reduced misorientation coexisted with areas of enhanced intralath rotation, showing that orientation randomization at elevated temperatures results from the combined influence of recovery, kinking, and early-stage DRX mechanisms.

The pole figures in Fig. 14a corroborate these observations. The as-built (0001) pole exhibited a sharply concentrated basal cluster characteristic of a strong < 0001 > fiber texture, and only minimal changes occurred after RT deformation. At 300 °C, moderate dispersion of the basal poles and slight rotation of prismatic and pyramidal poles indicated limited lattice rotation associated with non-basal slip. At 500 °C, the basal intensity decreased and the poles broadened asymmetrically, accompanied by increased dispersion of prismatic and pyramidal components. At 700 °C, the PFs became highly diffuse with multi-lobe patterns, consistent with progressive subgrain rotation, kink-assisted segmentation, and partial DRX. Together, these trends demonstrate that WAAM Ti-6Al-4V evolves from a strongly aligned lamellar structure to a recovery-dominated and partially recrystallized state through the combined action of kinking, recovery, and substructure evolution.

Texture analysis of Ti-6Al-4V alloys after compression at RT, 300 °C, 500 °C, and 700 °C, showing IPF maps and the corresponding {0001}, {100}, and {110} pole figures that reveal temperature-dependent changes in crystallographic orientation and texture evolution. (a) WAAM; (b) Wrought.

In contrast, the Wrought Ti-6Al-4V alloy exhibited only limited microstructural evolution under the same temperature conditions. The initial microstructure (Fig. 4) consisted of equiaxed α grains formed by hot rolling and partial recrystallization. After deformation at RT (Fig. 9), the IPF map remained nearly identical to the as-received state, confirming that deformation was mainly accommodated by localized slip before premature fracture at a strain of ~ 0.3. At 300 °C (Fig. 9), a slight increase in LAGBs and mild orientation gradients indicated the onset of lattice rotation and subgrain formation, although the overall grain orientations remained unchanged due to the still limited strain (~ 0.35). At 500 °C (Fig. 11), pronounced intragranular misorientation developed, reflecting enhanced dislocation rearrangement and subgrain rotation associated with thermally activated recovery. At 700 °C (Fig. 11), distinct equiaxed recrystallized grains with reduced KAM values confirmed that recrystallization became the dominant mechanism, producing a mixed structure of retained coarse grains and newly nucleated fine grains.

Pole figure analysis (Fig. 14b) supports these observations. In the initial state, the (0001), (100), and (110) poles exhibited broad diffuse distributions indicative of a weak, nearly random texture. After RT and 300 °C deformation, the overall pattern changed little, aside from band-shaped prismatic and pyramidal features reflecting directionally concentrated slip prior to early fracture. At 500 °C, broader and more irregular prismatic and pyramidal poles appeared due to increased intragranular misorientation and recovery-driven lattice rearrangement, although no strong preferred orientation developed. At 700 °C, the PFs showed a clear transition: the (0001) pole displayed increased central intensity, while prismatic and pyramidal poles formed continuous ring-shaped patterns. This texture redistribution is characteristic of high-temperature recrystallization and is consistent with EBSD evidence of subgrain rotation and the conversion of LAGBs into HAGBs. These results demonstrate that the Wrought alloy exhibits minimal texture reorganization at RT and 300 °C due to the limited strain, moderate recovery-dominated changes at 500 °C, and pronounced texture redistribution driven by full recrystallization at 700 °C.

The progressive weakening and randomization of texture in both alloys are consistent with the observed flow softening behavior and the increasing activity of recovery, CDRX, and partial DDRX at elevated temperatures. These results highlight the critical role of temperature-dependent substructure and grain boundary evolution in governing the deformation response of WAAM and Wrought Ti-6Al-4V.

Deformation mechanisms at various temperatures

Figure 15 (upper) schematically illustrates the temperature-dependent compressive behavior of the WAAM alloy. WAAM consistently exhibited higher compressive yield strength than its wrought counterpart; however, pronounced flow softening was observed at 700 °C (refer to Fig. 5). At RT, severe dislocation accumulation occurred at α-lath boundaries and intersections, leading to localized stress concentration and rapid fracture. At 300 °C, dislocations became more uniformly distributed within the α-laths, slightly enhancing strain accommodation; however, premature failure still occurred at strains around 0.35 due to residual stress localization. At 500 °C, external stresses were mainly accommodated through kinking of α-laths, accompanied by the activation of DRV and the formation of LAGBs via dislocation absorption and rearrangement at boundaries. At 700 °C, dynamic recrystallization (CDRX and DDRX) was fully activated, promoting new grain formation, while kink-induced fragmentation and breakdown of the lamellar structure became dominant. These microstructural evolutions indicate a temperature-dependent transition in deformation mechanisms—from dislocation accumulation and stress concentration to kink band formation, subgrain development, and ultimately recrystallization—resulting in rapid flow softening at elevated temperatures.

Schematic illustration of deformation mechanisms of WAAM and Wrought alloys at various temperatures.

Figure 15 (lower) schematically illustrates the compressive deformation behavior of the Wrought alloy. Compared to WAAM, Wrought exhibited lower flow stresses but showed more moderate softening at temperatures above 500 °C. At RT and 300 °C, intense dislocation accumulation occurred near α/β interfaces, resulting in localized strain inhomogeneity and premature fracture. Defects were primarily aligned along the 45° direction, corresponding to the maximum shear stress path. At 500 °C, kinking appeared within certain α + β lamellar regions, while coarse α grains elongated perpendicular to the compression axis to accommodate the applied load. DRV was also activated, promoting dislocation rearrangement into grain and subgrain boundaries. At 700 °C, elevated thermal energy and compressive strain facilitated dynamic globularization. Subgrain boundaries gradually transformed into HAGBs, and new grains nucleated and grew along the elongated α grain boundaries. This progressive microstructural evolution—driven by a combination of strain accumulation and DRX—accounts for the relatively moderate softening behavior observed in Wrought alloy.

Conclusions

In this study, a comparative investigation was performed to evaluate the high-temperature compressive property and deformation behavior of WAAM and Wrought alloys, with particular emphasis on the associated microstructural evolutions and flow softening mechanisms. The major findings can be summarized as follows.

The WAAM Ti-6Al-4V alloy exhibited significantly higher compressive yield strength and superior early-stage deformation resistance than the wrought Ti-6Al-4V at room and intermediate temperatures (300–500 °C). This enhanced strength was primarily attributed to its unique lamellar microstructure, consisting of fine α-laths, a high fraction of high angle grain boundaries, and well-defined α/β interfaces, which collectively acted to impede dislocation motion and localized plastic deformation.

The WAAM Ti-6Al-4V alloy exhibited significantly higher compressive yield strength and superior early-stage deformation resistance than the wrought Ti-6Al-4V at room and intermediate temperatures (300–500 °C). This enhanced strength was primarily attributed to its unique lamellar microstructure, consisting of fine α-laths, a high fraction of high angle grain boundaries, and well-defined α/β interfaces, which collectively acted to impede dislocation motion and localized plastic deformation.

At elevated temperatures above 700 °C, the WAAM Ti-6Al-4V alloy showed rapid flow softening behavior, resulting from the simultaneous activation of discontinuous dynamic recrystallization and continuous dynamic recrystallization, coupled with accelerated kinking, fragmentation of α-laths, and the eventual collapse of the pre-existing lamellar structure. This microstructural instability under high-temperature deformation underscores the necessity of further thermal or thermomechanical treatments to stabilize the microstructure.

This microstructural instability under high-temperature deformation underscores the necessity of further thermal or thermomechanical treatments to stabilize the microstructure.

In contrast, the wrought Ti-6Al-4V alloy, despite its lower strength due to coarse α grains and a high proportion of low angle grain boundaries, maintained a more gradual and stable softening response at elevated temperatures. This was facilitated by the progressive rearrangement of dislocations through DRV and the occurrence of dynamic globularization, which effectively delayed catastrophic softening.

In contrast, the wrought Ti-6Al-4V alloy, despite its lower strength due to coarse α grains and a high proportion of low angle grain boundaries, maintained a more gradual and stable softening response at elevated temperatures. This was facilitated by the progressive rearrangement of dislocations through DRV and the occurrence of dynamic globularization, which effectively delayed catastrophic softening.

Given that WAAM offers a cost-effective and scalable solution for fabricating large-scale or complex Ti-6Al-4V structures—especially in aerospace and energy applications where near-net shape capability and material buy-to-fly ratios are pivotal—this study provides essential foundational insights into the process–microstructure–performance relationships. It is thus expected to serve as a valuable guide for future efforts aimed at optimizing the design, certification, and reliable deployment of WAAM Ti-6Al-4V components under demanding service environments.

Methods

A Ti-6Al-4V bulk material was fabricated via wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) utilizing a cold metal transfer (CMT) arc mode integrated in a gas metal arc welding (GMAW) platform. A 1.2 mm diameter ER Ti-5 wire was employed as the feedstock, and the torch was maintained at a fixed vertical orientation (90 °) relative to the deposition surface. The process was conducted under previously optimized parameters33, including a current of 290 A, a wire feeding rate of 9 m/min, and a helium shielding gas flow rate of 20 L/min. A schematic illustration of the WAAM deposition process is shown in Fig. 16(a), and the detailed process parameters are summarized in Table 2. For comparative analysis, a hot-rolled Ti-6Al-4V plate (100 × 100 × 30 mm3, Sejin Titanium Co., Korea) was used as a wrought counterpart. These samples are hereafter denoted as WAAM and Wrought, respectively.

Chemical compositions of the materials were measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent), and the results are summarized in Table 3. The oxygen content was determined with an ONH analyzer (ELTRA), which resulted in measured oxygen contents of 0.16 ± 0.06 wt% for WAAM and 0.34 ± 0.09 wt% for Wrought. The oxygen content of the Wrought was approximately twice that of the WAAM. This difference is not considered an experimental deviation but rather a process-inherent characteristic, because WAAM is conducted under an inert shielding atmosphere that limits oxygen uptake, whereas repeated thermomechanical exposures during wrought processing naturally increase oxygen concentration83. In addition, the porosity of both materials was evaluated using the Archimedes method (ASTM B962). The measured bulk densities were 4.401 ± 0.003 g/cm3 for the WAAM alloy and 4.427 ± 0.002 g/cm³ for the Wrought alloy, corresponding to 99.34% and 99.93% of the theoretical density (4.43 g/cm3), respectively. These results indicate that the WAAM material contains a low porosity level of approximately 0.7%, comparable to that of the wrought counterpart.

Cylindrical compression specimens (Φ4 mm × 6 mm) were extracted using electrical discharge machining. As illustrated in Fig. 16(b), both WAAM and Wrought samples were sectioned perpendicular to their respective processing directions and collected from the central region of each material to ensure representative microstructural sampling. Compression tests were conducted at RT, 300 °C, 500 °C, and 700 °C using a servo-hydraulic testing system (MTS-810, USA) with a constant strain rate of 1 × 10− 3 s− 1 up to a true strain of 0.6. The thermal–mechanical loading sequence, including heating, holding, deformation, and post-deformation quenching, is schematically presented in Fig. 16(c). All tests were repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. To preserve the high-temperature deformation structures, all tested samples were immediately water-quenched.

Metallographic samples were mechanically ground with SiC abrasive papers (240–2000 grit) and polished sequentially using 1 μm diamond suspension and 0.04 μm colloidal silica. Kroll’s reagent was applied for chemical etching. Microstructural observations were conducted using an optical microscope (Olympus) and a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Tescan). Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD, Oxford Instruments) was employed to evaluate grain orientation and geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) distribution. For fine-scale β-phase analysis, transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL) was performed on thin foils prepared via focused ion beam (FIB, Thermo Fisher).
