Morel‐Lavallée Lesion: An Easily Missed Closed Debridement Injury in Blunt Trauma
Authors: Hidetaka Mochizuki, Hirohito Hirata, Tomohito Yoshihara, Takaomi Kobayashi, Masatsugu Tsukamoto, Tetsuji Uemura, Masaaki Mawatari, Tadatsugu Morimoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMorel‐Lavallée lesion (MLL) is a closed soft tissue injury following trauma that is often missed. If not treated appropriately, they can lead to infection and is difficult to treat. High‐energy trauma with shear forces should be treated with MLL in mind.

Introduction

A Morel‐Lavallée lesion (MLL) is a traumatic, closed, degloving injury first described by Victor Auguste Francois Morel‐Lavallee in 1863 [1].

MLLs commonly occur in high‐energy injuries where the skin and subcutaneous tissue are detached from the superficial fascia. Consequently, a space filled with serous fluid, blood, and necrotic fat is formed. MLLs have a significant impact on the management of orthopedic injuries from two perspectives. One is a diagnostic problem that can easily be missed [2]. This is because MLL is a rare condition, with poor skin findings in the early stages, and in cases of multiple traumas, MLL may not be as prominent as other prominent findings [3]. Another problem is that, if improperly managed, MLL can lead to soft tissue necrosis and infection [3, 4, 5]. Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment are crucial.

Here, we report a case of an MLL in the lumbosacral region following multiple trauma (lumbosacral vertebral, pelvic, and lower extremity fractures) that was missed and improperly treated surgically, resulting in necrosis and infection of the MLL site, which required a vastus lateralis muscle skin graft for difficult treatment.

This case report focuses on diagnostic and therapeutic issues. It also aims to strengthen the knowledge base regarding MLL by highlighting the need for early diagnosis and treatment using key characteristic imaging features important for improving prognosis.

Case Report

Case History and Examination

A 35‐year‐old male with no specific comorbidities was injured when he slipped and fell while riding a motorcycle.

His vital signs remained stable upon admission. Physical examination revealed abrasion of the lower back and deformity and swelling of the right lower leg. Physical examination revealed no pelvic mobility and no findings strongly suggestive of subcutaneous hematoma.

An examination of the images was performed, and the patient was diagnosed with fractures of the transverse process of the L5 lumbar vertebra, sacral coccyx, and right tibia and fibula and was admitted to the hospital on the same day.

Blood tests on admission showed a normal hemoglobin (Hb) level of 14.4 g/dL, but blood drawn about 4 h later showed a decreased Hb level of 8.2 g/dL, suggesting active bleeding, so a contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest and abdomen was performed, but no obvious source of bleeding was identified. However, examination revealed a hematoma around the sacral fracture site (Figure 1).

The sagittal plane computed tomography (CT) of the sacrum after the trauma. The white arrow indicates a fracture in the sacral region. The red arrow shows a subcutaneous hematoma extending to the buttock, indicating the presence of a Morel‐Lavallée lesion.

Emergency physicians determined that the anemia was due to bleeding from each fracture.

Blood transfusions were administered as needed for persistent anemia. After transfusion, the Hb levels increased but were not maintained, suggesting continued active bleeding. Therefore, careful follow‐up was performed.

Diagnosis

Three days after the injury, contemplative fracture surgery and posterior fixation were performed on the tibiofibular sacrococcygeal fractures.

Skin findings prior to posterior fixation of the sacrum showed no abrasions, but there were findings suggestive of extensive subcutaneous hemorrhage (Figure 2).

Image of the buttock skin at the time of initial surgery. Findings suggestive of subcutaneous hemorrhage over a large area, including the site of injury.

During surgery for the sacrococcygeal fracture, a large subcutaneous hematoma was observed; however, there was no active bleeding.

Posterior fixation was performed, and the skin closed without any problems. Upon retrospective review, we realized that we had missed the MLL because preoperative lumbar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed extensive subcutaneous hemorrhage in the lumbosacral region (Figure 3).

Postoperative radiographic images of the sacrum. The sacrum is posteriorly fixed, and an implant has been inserted.

The anemia improved postoperatively, and blood transfusion was not necessary on the day after surgery.

On the fourth postoperative day, there were findings of suspected subcutaneous bleeding around the wound. On the seventh postoperative day, erosion and necrosis were observed around the wound, which showed a tendency to enlarge (Figure 4a,b).

Lumbar spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sagittal image before surgery. T1‐weighted images show a low signal (a), and T2‐weighted images show a high signal (b), indicating a hematoma. The white arrow points to the Morel‐Lavallée lesion extending to the gluteal region.

In addition, approximately 2 weeks postoperatively, a bloody exudate appeared from the underlying layer of the buttock necrosis (Figure 5).

Image of the skin of the buttock approximately 2 weeks after sacral fracture surgery. The skin on the buttocks continued to bleed, and the area around the postoperative wound was necrotic.

Treatment

Approximately 3 weeks after the initial surgery, the patient was diagnosed with a wound infection and underwent hematoma and implant removal (Figure 6).

Intraoperative images of debridement and implant removal for Morel‐Lavallée lesion.

The gluteal muscle area was extensively resected because of necrosis. As skin closure was not possible, negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) was performed, and the patient was followed up with antimicrobial therapy.

Subsequently, methicillin‐sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) and methicillin‐susceptible Staphylococcus epidermidis (MSSE) were detected in wound cultures. Several additional debridement cycles were performed, and the signs of infection resolved approximately 6 weeks after the initial surgery. An extensive skin defect remained; therefore, a vast cutaneous valve was inserted simultaneously (Figure 7a,b). After implant removal, we did not perform a further implantation for the sacral fracture, and the patient was treated with conservative management. The patient was treated conservatively for a transverse process fracture of the L5 lumbar vertebra.

Image of the skin of the buttock. (a) Preoperative image before surgery performed about 6 weeks after the initial surgery. (b) Postoperative vastus lateralis skin graft.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

After a wide dorsal skin valve was created, the patient had no postoperative wound problems and was discharged from the hospital approximately 4 months after the initial surgery, with no wound problems during outpatient visits. As for the sacral coccyx, bony fusion was not achieved, and pain persisted; therefore, surgery was performed approximately 2 years after the initial surgery, and the caudal vertebra was removed. The L5 transverse process fracture of the lumbar vertebrae was treated conservatively without any problems, and the right tibiofibular fracture became pseudarthrosis postoperatively. Curettage and bone grafting were performed approximately 2 years after the initial surgery, and the patient continued to visit the hospital as an outpatient until 4 years after the initial surgery, but no wound problems occurred, and hospital visits were discontinued.

Discussion

Clinical Features and Imaging Diagnosis of MLL

MLL is a serious soft tissue injury. Clinically, patients with MLL present with skin erythema, soft tissue swelling, fluctuations, and hypermobility. However, it is estimated that one‐third of MLL is undiagnosed at the time of acute trauma because the skin lesions of MLL may not be visible early in the injury. As they are often missed, it is important to keep MLL in mind during high‐energy trauma to avoid missing them [6].

MLL was initially used to describe lesions over the greater trochanter; however, the term has been used to describe dropout‐type injuries, regardless of location. Vanhegan et al. investigated more than 200 MLL in a literature review and found a breakdown by region: the greater trochanter or hip (30.4%), thigh (20.1%), pelvis (18.6%), knee (15.7%), buttocks (6.4%), lumbosacral region (3.4%), abdomen (1.4%), calf or lower leg (1 0.5%), and head (0.5%) [7]. MLL is associated with fractures of bony prominences, such as the femoral greater trochanter, lumbosacral or pelvic area, scapula, knee, and elbow, owing to the high‐energy nature of injuries at these sites [8]. Of note the incidence of MLL associated with pelvic and acetabular fractures is likely to be higher than that previously reported, possibly because lesions of smaller volume are likely to be missed [8]. Clinicians should always suspect MLL when managing patients following shear injuries or direct blows to bony prominences (particularly the pelvis and greater trochanters) [3].

Ultrasonography, CT, and MRI can be useful for MLL diagnostic imaging, as described below.

On ultrasonography, MLL appears as a well‐defined fluid reservoir between the subcutaneous tissue and deep fascia, with highly echoic areas within the collection indicating fat [3, 9]. CT indicates fluid–fluid levels due to the sedimentation of blood and lymph fluid and varying amounts of debris within the lesion, including fat lobules, with or without peripheral capsules [3, 9]. MRI is the gold standard for the diagnosis and evaluation of MLL [10]. MRI may reveal perifascial detachment, fatty layer lacerations, and the development of multiple septa, in addition to fluid retention. The specific MRI and ultrasound findings of MLL depend on the time since injury onset [10].

The differential diagnoses included hemangiomas, fat necrosis, and subcutaneous hematomas. Injuries to the superior gluteal artery associated with pelvic fractures are the first diseases to be recalled in cases of extensive soft‐tissue necrosis of the lumbar back and buttocks. In this case, we also considered the differential diagnosis of a superior pudendal artery injury as the cause of the subcutaneous hematoma, but the angiogram was negative.

Management of MLL

MLL may increase the risk of perioperative infections and should not be overlooked by orthopedic surgeons [5, 8, 11, 12]. In MLL, the perforated structures of soft tissue blood vessels and lymphatic vessels are disrupted by high‐energy trauma with shear forces, resulting in characteristic reservoirs of blood, serous fluid, and necrotic fat between tissue layers. Bacterial colony formation has been reported in 46% of lesions sampled [8]. Evidence of bacterial contamination by aspiration of body fluids has also been reported in MLL despite the closed nature of the injury [8]. The presence of MLL was reported to be an independent and significant risk factor for postoperative surgical site infection after pelvic and acetabular surgery (odds ratio, 8.4; 95% confidence interval, 1.3–56.8; p = 0.029) [5]. Missed and inadequate treatment can exacerbate soft tissue injuries and increase the risk of infection.

The management of MLL is determined by the size, location, and onset of the injury and can range from percutaneous drainage to perfusion and open debridement.

Owing to the high rate of worsening necrosis and infection, large or symptomatic MLLs should be dissected and perfused through single or multiple incisions to reduce the risk of undesirable sequelae, especially if they are in close proximity to the planned surgical incision site [3].

Early surgical detachment of MLL may be performed to remove materials that serve as a medium for bacterial colony formation. Early percutaneous treatment or surgical dissection of MLL may also be performed to remove body fluids (substances that may serve as a medium for bacterial colony formation) and dead cavities.

From a literature review, Greenhill et al. developed a treatment algorithm based on the timing of MLL lesions and the presence of infection, fracture, or skin necrosis and recommended compression or aspiration for acute lesions and isolated lesions without infection, fracture, or skin necrosis [13].

There are also reports of the effectiveness of sclerotherapy using drugs such as talc and doxycycline for the surgical treatment of MLL [14]. Doxycycline is commonly used as a sclerotherapy agent to treat effusions, and while its use in treating MLL is uncommon, some reports have shown efficacy [15]. There have been reports of symptom resolution with sclerotherapy using doxycycline for MLL to the knee [16, 17].

Reminder to Clinicians

As we were unaware of the existence of this condition, we underestimated the subcutaneous hemorrhage to be due to bruising or fracture and missed the MLL, which was clearly visible in the images.

The initial diagnosis was incorrect, and the patient underwent invasive surgery, which resulted in soft tissue necrosis, infection, nail extraction, and cosmetic deformities due to skin valve formation, among other problems. In particular, with regard to the initial treatment after pelvic fractures, including sacral fractures, it may be desirable not to perform posterior fixation early but rather to perform surgery after confirming that hematoma removal has been performed in one phase and that no hematoma growth has occurred, or to consider an anterior approach in some cases.

Inappropriate diagnosis and improper management may lead to undesirable outcomes, and clinicians should always keep MLL in mind and repeatedly evaluate them when managing patients after shear injuries or direct blows to the pelvis or greater trochanter.

Conclusion

MLL is often missed in closed soft tissue injuries resulting from high‐energy trauma. However, MLL requires prompt and accurate diagnosis, it is often delayed or missed because of the focus on more serious injuries sustained during accidents. In this study, we experienced a case of MLL with multiple traumatic injuries due to a car accident. High‐energy trauma with shear forces should be treated with MLL in mind.
