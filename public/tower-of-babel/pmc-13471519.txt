Caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding sleep care for 0–3 year-old children: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sijia Li, Juan Yin, Juan Qi, Lei Peng, Heng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep care significantly impacts children’s development; however, caregivers’ knowledge and practices regarding sleep care remain understudied. This study aims to comprehensively assess caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) concerning sleep care for infants and toddlers aged 0–3 years.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was collected from July to October 2024 among caregivers at two maternal and child health hospitals (Wuxi and Xuzhou) in Jiangsu Province, China. A standardized questionnaire assessed caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding sleep care for children aged 0–3 years.ResultsA total of 2,009 valid responses were obtained, with 1,750 (87.11%) were female caregivers. The median (Q₁, Q₃) scores for knowledge, attitude, and practice were 8.00 (7.00, 9.00), 22.00 (20.00, 24.00), and 37.00 (33.00, 40.00), respectively. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed statistically significant positive correlations between caregivers’ knowledge and their attitudes (r = 0.119, P < 0.001) and practices (r = 0.167, P < 0.001), as well as attitude and practice (r = 0.258, P < 0.001). Logistic regression analysis revealed that higher knowledge scores [OR = 1.154, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.034–1.288, P < 0.001], positive attitudes (OR = 1.188, 95% CI: 1.136–1.243, P < 0.001), higher household income (OR = 1.634, 95% CI: 1.066–2.505, P = 0.024), and better child sleep quality (OR < 1, 95% CI: 0.102–0.928, P < 0.05) were independently associated with better sleep care practices. Structural equation modeling showed direct effects of knowledge on both the attitudes (β = 1.346, P < 0.001) and practices (β = 1.152, P < 0.001), as well as of attitude on practice (β = 0.492, P < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough caregivers demonstrated adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and generally sufficient sleep care practices, targeted educational interventions remain necessary, particularly for caregivers with lower education and income, to further optimize sleep care behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06958-5.Keywords: Caregivers; Infant; Toddler; Sleep care; Knowledge, Attitudes, Practice; Cross-Sectional Study

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process that plays an essential role in children’s physical and psychological development, significantly affecting daytime functioning, learning abilities, and overall health outcomes [1]. During the first three years of life, sleep patterns undergo dramatic changes as part of normal development, with recommended sleep duration ranging from 14 to 17 h per 24-hour period for newborns to 11–14 h for toddlers aged 1–2 years [2]. Research indicates that 25–50% of infants aged 6–12 months and up to 50% of toddlers experience problematic night waking and sleep disturbances [3].

Sleep disturbances in early childhood have been linked to cognitive delays, emotional regulation difficulties, and behavioral issues [4, 5]. Moreover, poor sleep quality during infancy and toddlerhood can have long-term implications for mental health and academic performance [6]. These findings underscore the critical importance of establishing healthy sleep patterns during the first three years of life.

Caregivers play a pivotal role in shaping children’s sleep patterns through their practices and routines [7, 8], which are in turn influenced by cultural contexts and parental attitudes. These factors contribute to variations in sleep patterns across populations [1]. Studies in Western countries have highlighted the role of parents in shaping infant sleep, demonstrating that consistent bedtime routines and reduced nighttime intervention are associated with better sleep outcomes [9, 10]. In China, limited research has explored caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding infant and toddler sleep. A recent study in Chongqing revealed low levels and structural gaps in parental sleep knowledge for children aged 0–3 years, which were significantly associated with poorer sleep outcomes in infants and toddlers [11]. Another population-based study in China found that parental involvement at sleep onset, such as feeding or rocking, was common and significantly associated with night awakenings and difficulty falling asleep in children under three years old [12].

The KAP survey is widely used as a diagnostic research tool, to assess a group’s comprehension, beliefs, and actions regarding a specific subject, particularly within the realm of health literacy, where it is based on the premise that knowledge positively influences attitudes, which in turn mold behaviors [13–15]. Within this framework, behavioral practices are considered the most observable and modifiable outcomes of caregiver knowledge and attitudes, making them a meaningful focus for analysis [16, 17]. In the context of this study, sleep-related practices refer to specific caregiver behaviors that support infant and toddler sleep, such as establishing consistent bedtime routines, putting the child to sleep at appropriate times, minimizing distractions in the sleep environment, responding calmly to night waking, and avoiding overstimulation before sleep [18, 19]. This methodology is particularly relevant for investigating sleep care practices in China, where traditional beliefs and modern parenting approaches often coexist, potentially influencing caregiving behaviors related to sleep. Understanding these factors is crucial for developing culturally appropriate interventions to improve infant sleep care.

Despite the recognized importance of caregiver involvement in infant sleep, research specifically examining Chinese caregivers’ KAP regarding sleep care for children aged 0–3 year remains scarce. Therefore, this study aims to systematically assess caregivers’ sleep-related knowledge, attitudes and practices for children 0–3 years in China, explore their inter-relationships, and generate evidence-based recommendations.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This cross-sectional study was conducted between July 1, 2024, and October 30, 2024, in two randomly selected cities in Jiangsu Province-Wuxi (Southern Jiangsu) and Xuzhou (Northern Jiangsu)-to ensure regional representativeness. The study population comprised caregivers of infants and toddlers aged 0–3 years. Infants were identified through physical examination records and linked to their community address. One community was randomly selected from each district, and eligible infants were recruited using systematic random sampling. Participants were included if they met these criteria: permanent residency in urban or rural areas (> 6 months), caregiving for full-term infants (gestational age 37–41 weeks), infants’ birth weight 2,500-4,000 g, and adequate comprehension abilities. Exclusion criteria were infants requiring neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission or illness within the previous two weeks. Caregivers received free sleep-related consultations and education, but no financial compensation was provided. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Wuxi Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. 2023-01-0921-39), and informed consent was secured from all participants prior to data collection.

Questionnaire introduction

The questionnaire (Supplementary file 1) was developed based on guidelines and existing literature regarding “Sleep in Chinese Children Aged 0–5 Years”. After initial design, the questionnaire was revised according to feedback from two senior experts specializing in pediatric developmental behavior and pediatric psychology, thereby ensured adequate content validity and relevance. The finalized questionnaire, administered in Chinese, comprised four sections: demographic information, knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions. The knowledge dimension included nine items related to infant and toddler sleep care. In addition, one judgment item (Question 6), asking whether Wuxi is the capital city of Jiangsu Province, was included as a quality-control question to assess respondents’ attentiveness and basic comprehension. Questionnaires with incorrect responses to this item were excluded during data cleaning. This item was not considered part of the knowledge construct and was therefore not included in knowledge score calculation or descriptive analyses. The attitude dimension contained six items assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1 point) to “strongly agree” (5 points), yielding a total score between 6 and 30. The practice dimension consisted of ten items evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from “always” (5 points) to “never” (1 point), leading to a total score between 10 and 50. For the purpose of this study, scores exceeding 70% of the maximum possible score in each section were considered indicative of adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices. This threshold was primarily informed by the distribution of scores observed in our pilot survey and is consistent with the common practice in KAP research of setting a cutoff point to dichotomize continuous scores for analysis [20, 21].

A pilot survey was conducted by distributing 100 copies of the questionnaire, of which 30 valid responses were collected. The relatively low validity rate was primarily due to incomplete or inconsistent responses and difficulties in understanding certain items. Based on this experience, we refined the questionnaire instructions, provided additional guidance to participants, and trained surveyors to supervise data collection more effectively for the subsequent formal survey. The theoretical sample size for the formal survey was calculated to be 384, based on α = 0.05, δ = 5%, and a conservative P = 0.5. To ensure sufficient statistical power even if 20% of the questionnaires were invalid, the sample size was adjusted using the formula n adjusted = n / (1–0.20), resulting in a final required sample size of 480. To further enhance the reliability and representativeness of the data, a total of 2,684 questionnaires were distributed in the formal survey. This number substantially exceeds the minimum requirement, and 2,009 valid responses were ultimately collected for analysis. The questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.755.

Wuxi and Xuzhou, two cities in Jiangsu Province, were randomly selected to ensure regional representativeness. Based on stratified district sampling, one community from each district was randomly selected, and eligible infants aged 0–3 years were systematically recruited using random number generation. Eligible infants aged 0–3 years were identified from health examination records in each selected community. Primary caregivers were then selected using systematic random sampling, with a random start and every kth entry invited to participate. The questionnaire was administered electronically via the Chinese online survey platform “Wenjuanxing.” A unique survey link was sent by trained investigators to the selected caregivers, who completed the questionnaire independently. Investigators provided brief instructions to ensure participants understood the procedure. This approach maintained the integrity of systematic randomization while using an online platform, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. All surveyors underwent standardized training conducted by the project leader, and only those who successfully passed the assessment were authorized to provide individualized guidance to participants during questionnaire completion.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: n = ( Z_(1-α/ 2)/ δ )^2 × p × (1- p ), where Z_(1- α /2) = 1.96 when α = 0.05, δ is the allowable error (5% in this study), and P represents the expected proportion of caregivers with adequate knowledge, attitudes, or practices. In the absence of prior data, we used p = 0.5, which provides the maximum required sample size as a conservative estimate. The theoretical sample size was 480, including an additional 20% to account for potential non-response or incomplete questionnaires.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed data or as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD for normally distributed data or median (Q1, Q3) for non-normally distributed data. Appropriate statistical methods were applied according to data distribution (see Results section). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Binary logistic regression was performed to identify demographic factors associated with adequate knowledge, positive attitudes, and proactive practices, using a 70% threshold to define outcomes. Variables that were significant in univariate analysis (P < 0.05) or identified in prior literature as potential confounders were included in the multivariate model. For categorical variables, the reference category (Ref.) is indicated in the tables. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to account for potential confounding effects of other covariates, providing a more accurate estimate of the independent association between each factor and the outcome. Comparisons of KAP dimension scores across different demographic groups were conducted using appropriate statistical tests. For continuous variables, an independent samples t-test was applied for two-group comparisons when the data followed a normal distribution. In cases where the data did not meet normality assumptions, the Mann-Whitney U test (also known as the Wilcoxon rank sum test) was employed for comparing two independent groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted for non-normally distributed data. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was utilized to examine the relationships between knowledge, attitude, and practice scores. Since normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (see above), Spearman’s correlation was applied for variables that did not meet the normality assumption, while Pearson’s correlation would be used for normally distributed variables. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using AMOS 26.0 to assess directional relationships among total KAP scores. Compared with correlation or regression, SEM allowed simultaneous modeling of direct and indirect effects, providing a more nuanced understanding of their interrelations. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the incremental fit index (IFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and the comparative fit index (CFI). RMSEA values < 0.05 indicate a close fit, while values < 0.08 are considered acceptable, based on established SEM guidelines [22, 23]. For IFI, TLI, and CFI, values ≥ 0.90 are generally regarded as indicative of acceptable model fit [22]. All P-values were reported to three decimal places, with a two-sided P-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Actual fit indices in this study were interpreted against these literature-supported reference criteria.

Results

Demographic information of participants

Out of the collected copies of the questionnaire, 51 were excluded due to lack of informed consent, 331 due to logical errors in responses to trap questions, and 303 due to incorrect age information, leaving 2,009 valid cases. Among caregivers, 87% were female and 86% were mothers; 57% were aged ≥ 31 years, 62% resided in rural areas, and 44% had completed high-school or associate-level education. Of the infants and toddlers, 53% were boys and 60% were 25–36 months old. Parents rated the sleep quality of 44% of the children as good, while 43% were perceived to have minor sleep problems. The median (IQR) scores knowledge, attitude, and practice were 8.00 (7.00, 9.00), 22.00 (20.00, 24.00), and 37.00 (33.00, 40.00), separately. Analyses of demographic characteristics showed that participants’ knowledge, attitude, and practice scores varied by residence, education, employment status, average monthly household per capita income, and child sleep problems (all P < 0.05). Meanwhile, knowledge scores varied significantly by gender, age, marital status, and relationship to the infant/toddler (all P < 0.05). Attitude scores varied significantly by age and child’s nighttime sleep quality (all P < 0.05). Practice scores varied significantly by age (months) of the infant/toddler and child’s nighttime sleep quality (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Distribution of responses to knowledge, attitude, and practice

The distribution of knowledge dimension revealed that the three items with the lowest correctness rates were K3 with 68.19%, K4 with 68.54%, and K10 with 80.24% (Table S1). Responses to the attitude dimension showed that 24.34% strongly agreed and 32.65% agreed that irregular sleep patterns in children significantly disrupt their daytime work and daily life (A5). Also, 19.86% strongly agreed and 33.85% agreed that nighttime crying is a challenging issue for them to handle (A4). On the other hand, 1.54% strongly disagreed and 5.53% disagreed that they are confident in dealing with their child’s nighttime crying (A6) (Table S1). 9.21% of caregivers reported that they always rock or feed their child to sleep immediately after feeds(P1). On the other hand, 8.71% never play soothing music or sing lullabies to help their child fall asleep (P5), and 7.81% never actively search online for information on cultivating healthy sleep habits in infants and toddlers (P10). Analysis of score distributions showed that approximately half of the participants achieved relatively higher scores in each of the knowledge, attitude, and practice dimensions, while the remaining participants demonstrated comparatively lower levels. Overall, the distribution patterns indicated moderate variability across the three dimensions (Table S1).

Correlations between KAP

Spearman correlation analysis indicated significant positive correlations between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.119, P < 0.001), as well as practice (r = 0.167, P < 0.001). Meanwhile, there was also correlation between attitude and practice (r = 0.258, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, caregivers of infants and toddlers aged 0–3 years generally demonstrated adequate sleep-related knowledge, relatively positive attitudes, and overall acceptable sleep care practices. These findings indicate that most caregivers in our sample already possess a basic understanding of child sleep health. However, despite satisfactory overall performance, specific aspects of sleep care practices—such as establishing consistent bedtime routines and promoting independent sleep—remain suboptimal. This discrepancy suggests that adequate knowledge and favorable attitudes do not always translate into optimal caregiving behaviors, a phenomenon frequently observed in early childhood health practices [24].

Consistent with previous research, gaps between health-related knowledge and actual caregiving practices are frequently reported in infant and toddler care contexts [24, 25]. Although caregivers understand general sleep principles, they often struggle to apply them in daily routines, particularly when managing nighttime awakenings or responding to infant crying. These challenges may be influenced by contextual factors such as caregiving burden, cultural beliefs regarding infant sleep, limited access to professional guidance, and variability in social or familial support [26, 27]. Therefore, improving sleep care practices may require not only information dissemination but also practical, skills-based guidance tailored to caregivers’ real-life caregiving situations.

Analysis of individual knowledge items further highlighted specific misconceptions that may adversely affect infant and toddler sleep management. The lowest accuracy rates were observed for items related to circadian rhythm establishment, differentiation between sleep duration and sleep quality, and the normality of mild movement or crying during light sleep. Misunderstanding these concepts may lead caregivers to adopt inappropriate sleep management strategies, such as prioritizing longer sleep duration over sleep continuity or intervening excessively during normal sleep phases. Previous studies have emphasized that misconceptions regarding infant sleep physiology may interfere with appropriate sleep management strategies and sleep consolidation in early childhood [27, 28]. These findings underscore the importance of targeted educational content addressing common misconceptions rather than relying solely on generalized sleep advice.

This gap in nuanced understanding reflects broader challenges in health literacy, where caregivers may grasp general concepts but struggle with specific, practical knowledge, as reported in previous studies on parental health literacy and caregiving behaviors [29, 30]. Additionally, although caregivers generally acknowledged the importance of sleep routines, their lack of confidence in managing nighttime crying highlights deficiencies in practical caregiving skills, which is consistent with previous findings on parental coping strategies and stress related to infant sleep problems [31, 32].

Correlation analyses demonstrated significant positive associations among knowledge, attitudes, and practices, suggesting interrelated patterns within caregivers’ sleep-related KAP. Structural equation modeling further supported this framework by indicating that knowledge was associated with both attitudes and practices, with attitudes partially mediating this relationship. These findings are consistent with the theoretical assumptions of the KAP model, which proposes that knowledge provides a foundation for attitudinal orientation and behavioral tendencies [33, 34]. However, the observed associations should be interpreted cautiously, as positive attitudes alone did not uniformly correspond to optimal practices. Similar challenges have been reported in previous studies of parental health behaviors, highlighting the complexity of translating cognition into action [35, 36]. Although these associations reached statistical significance, the correlation coefficients were relatively low, indicating small effect sizes. This suggests that knowledge and attitudes alone have limited predictive value for caregiving practices at the individual level, but may still be relevant at the population level in public health contexts.

Multivariate logistic regression analyses identified several sociodemographic factors associated with caregivers’ KAP levels. Higher educational attainment and household income were associated with better knowledge and practice scores, consistent with prior evidence linking socioeconomic resources to health literacy and caregiving capacity [37–39]. In contrast, younger caregivers and those caring for older toddlers were observed to have lower attitude scores. These associations should not be interpreted as evidence of age-related, developmental, or generational causality. Rather, they may reflect differences in caregiving experience, role adaptation, or evolving caregiving demands at different stages of early childhood. Similar associations have been reported in previous cross-sectional studies; however, longitudinal research is needed to clarify the temporal and developmental mechanisms underlying these relationships [40, 41].

Disparities in KAP scores between urban and rural caregivers were also observed, with rural caregivers consistently demonstrating lower levels of knowledge, attitudes, and practices. This pattern likely reflects broader inequalities in access to healthcare services, educational resources, and professional guidance, as documented in prior research on urban–rural health disparities [42, 43]. Addressing these inequalities may require context-specific interventions that consider structural and resource-related constraints in under-resourced settings.

From a public health perspective, these findings suggest that interventions aimed at improving infant and toddler sleep care should extend beyond knowledge-based education alone. Community-based programs incorporating practical skill training, culturally sensitive guidance, and family-centered approaches may be more effective in supporting caregivers [44, 45]. Integrating sleep care education into routine pediatric healthcare visits may also provide opportunities for timely and individualized support, as suggested by previous intervention studies in comparable contexts [46].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships among caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Second, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may have introduced recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of caregivers’ responses. Third, the sample was drawn from only two hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings. Additionally, online data collection via Wenjuanxing may have introduced coverage and self-selection biases, potentially leading to overrepresentation of caregivers with higher digital literacy or heightened concern about child sleep issues. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results and designing future studies [47, 48].

Conclusion

In conclusion, caregivers of infants and toddlers aged 0–3 years demonstrated generally adequate levels of sleep-related knowledge, attitudes, and sleep care practices; however, notable gaps: while most caregivers grasped basic sleep-related knowledge, specific misconceptions persisted (e.g., regarding daytime vs. nighttime sleep balance and sleep duration-quality relationships); practices were also uneven, with inadequate performance in fostering independent sleep habits and proactive use of sleep-promoting strategies. Notably, higher education levels, greater household income, and better child sleep quality were significantly associated with improved knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Targeted educational interventions focusing on translating knowledge and attitudes into practical sleep care behaviors are recommended, particularly for caregivers with lower educational attainment and income levels, to promote healthier sleep habits in young children.
